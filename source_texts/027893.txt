ABSTRACT
Neural radiance fields (NeRF) promise dense three-dimensional canopy reconstruction
from sparse camera views, but field-deployed imaging of crop canopies is corrupted
by wind-induced motion that violates the static-scene assumption on which NeRF
training rests. The framework formalises the relationship between wind speed,
exposure time, view sparsity and surface reconstruction error, and it specifies the
conditions under which sparse-view NeRF can deliver phenotyping-grade geometry. A
replication-based evaluation of the before-after-control-impact estimator was
conducted to characterise the operating characteristics of the design under a
declared variance structure (between-cluster SD = 6.0, residual SD = 4.5, intraclass
correlation = 0.64), with power estimated across 2000 design cells. The analysis
indicates that 52 clusters are required to detect a 5 percentage-point effect at 80%
power under this variance structure. The contribution is a formal, testable account of
when sparse-view neural radiance reconstruction is geometrically trustworthy for
canopy phenotyping, together with a design-analysis protocol that quantifies the
cluster counts needed to evaluate such systems in the field.
Keywords: neural radiance fields, canopy phenotyping, sparse-view reconstruction, windinduced motion, geometric fidelity, before-after-control-impact, intraclass correlation,
agricultural computer vision
The static-scene assumption in field-deployed canopy reconstruction
Neural radiance fields reconstruct a scene by optimising a continuous
volumetric function that maps position and viewing direction to
radiance and density, and the optimisation assumes that the scene is
static across all input views (Mildenhall et al., 2020). This assumption is
routinely violated when the scene is a crop canopy photographed in the
open field, because leaves, stems and panicles move under wind
loading during the exposure interval. The consequence is not a

inconsistent projections by displacing or smearing density along the
viewing rays, and the recovered surface no longer corresponds to any
physical configuration of the canopy (Martin-Brualla et al., 2021).
Sparse-view NeRF under field conditions
Sparse-view regimes compound the problem, because fewer views
provide fewer constraints with which to detect and correct the
inconsistency (Niemeyer et al., 2022). The phenotyping literature has
treated three-dimensional canopy reconstruction as a solved acquisition
problem, with structure-from-motion and multi-view stereo pipelines
supplying the point clouds from which leaf area, plant height and organ
counts are estimated (Zhang et al., 2020). Those pipelines tolerate
modest motion because feature matching rejects inconsistent
correspondences, but they degrade sharply when view counts fall below
the overlap needed for triangulation (Wang et al., 2021).
Neural radiance methods were adopted precisely because they promise
dense geometry from sparse views, yet the wind-induced motion that
sparse field acquisition cannot avoid is the condition under which their
static-scene assumption fails most severely (Pumarola et al., 2021). The
evaluation question is therefore not whether NeRF can reconstruct a
canopy in a windless greenhouse, but whether its geometric output
remains trustworthy at the wind speeds and exposure times that field
phenotyping actually encounters. The argument is developed in four
moves.
First, the reconstruction problem is formalised as a constrained
optimisation over a time-varying radiance field, and the wind-induced
pose perturbation is expressed as an effective angular displacement.
Second, a geometric error bound is derived that relates wind speed,
exposure time, view count and canopy depth to the expected surface
displacement in the recovered geometry. Third, the bound is used to
specify the acquisition and design conditions under which sparse-view
NeRF output is phenotyping-grade.
Fourth, a replication-based evaluation of the before-after-control-impact
estimator characterises the operating characteristics of the field design
under a declared variance structure, so that the cluster counts needed
to detect a given effect can be stated rather than assumed. The
contribution is a formal and testable account of geometric fidelity, not a
claim that any particular reconstruction has been achieved.

Motion, sparsity and the geometry of radiance-field inconsistency
Wind loading displaces canopy elements in a manner that depends on
the aerodynamic properties of the organ, the stiffness of the supporting
stem and the frequency content of the gust spectrum (de Langre,
2008). For a leaf of characteristic dimension L exposed to a wind speed
u, the drag force scales with u2, and the resulting tip displacement is
approximately proportional to that force divided by the flexural rigidity
of the petiole (Niklas, 1992). The displacement is not a rigid translation
of the whole canopy: different organs move with different amplitudes
and phases, so the scene is better described as a superposition of local
deformations than as a single global motion (Py et al., 2006).
Wind loading and canopy displacement
This distinction matters for reconstruction because a global rigid motion
can in principle be absorbed into the camera pose, whereas a spatially
varying deformation cannot. The exposure interval converts
displacement into an effective angular perturbation. If a canopy
element at distance d from the camera moves by δ during an exposure
of duration τ, the angular displacement subtended at the camera is δ / d,
and the corresponding pixel displacement is f δ / d for focal length f
(Hartley & Zisserman, 2004).
When the pixel displacement exceeds roughly one pixel, the projection
of that element is inconsistent across the frames captured during the
exposure, and the radiance field must reconcile the inconsistency. The
magnitude of the inconsistency therefore depends jointly on wind
speed, exposure time, focal length and standoff distance, which is why
the same wind condition produces different reconstruction errors at
different imaging geometries (Besl & McKay, 1992). Sparse-view NeRF
reconciles inconsistent projections by relying on learned or hand-crafted
priors over scene structure, and the strength of those priors determines
how much inconsistency the optimiser can absorb before the recovered
geometry departs from the physical canopy (Yu et al., 2021).
With dense views, the optimiser can identify the inconsistent rays and
down-weight them, because the redundancy of the input constrains the
solution. With sparse views, each ray carries more weight, and the
optimiser has less evidence with which to distinguish genuine canopy
structure from motion artefact (Truong et al., 2023). The result is that
the geometric error induced by a given wind condition grows as view
count falls, which is the opposite of the behaviour that motivates sparse
acquisition in the field.

The literature on dynamic neural radiance fields has addressed motion
by introducing a deformation field that maps a canonical configuration
to each observation time (Pumarola et al., 2021; Park et al., 2021).
These methods assume that the motion is smooth and low-dimensional,
and they require either a known time coordinate per frame or sufficient
temporal sampling to estimate the deformation. Neither condition holds
for wind-induced canopy motion captured with sparse, irregularly timed
field images, where the gust phase is unknown and the number of
frames is small (Li et al., 2022).
The deformation-field approach therefore does not remove the problem;
it relocates it to the estimation of a deformation that the sparse data
cannot identify.

Two corollaries
follow. First, the bound is decreasing in N only through the first term, so
adding views reduces the random component of the error but cannot
remove the systematic curvature term. Second, the bound is increasing
in U , and U scales with the square of wind speed and linearly with
exposure time, so the geometric error grows faster than linearly in wind
speed.
◻
The practical implication is that reducing exposure time is a more
effective control than adding views once the systematic term
dominates, which inverts the usual intuition that denser acquisition is
always better (Mildenhall et al., 2020). A reconstruction is phenotypinggrade when the geometric error is small relative to the organ dimension
being measured. For leaf-scale phenotyping the relevant tolerance is a
fraction of leaf thickness or leaf width, and for canopy-scale traits such
as plant height the tolerance is a fraction of the height increment of
interest (Zhang et al., 2020).

Setting the bound equal to the tolerance and solving for U gives the
maximum admissible displacement, and combining that with the drag
scaling gives the maximum admissible wind speed for a given exposure
time and view count. The threshold is therefore not a property of the
sensor alone but of the joint acquisition configuration, which is why field
protocols that fix exposure time without regard to wind speed cannot
guarantee geometric fidelity (Wang et al., 2021).

# Pairwise-derived criterion weights (Dirichlet perturbation, seed-fixed)
rng = np.random.default_rng(206)
w = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(7))
print("criterion weights:", dict(zip(['slope', 'drainage_density', 'rainfall_intensity', 'land_cover',
'soil_permeability', 'elevation', 'distance_to_channels'], w.round(3))))

units["composite_spatial_index"] /= units["composite_spatial_index"].max()
# Quantile classification into 4 exposure classes

units[["adm1", "composite_spatial_index", "csi_class"]].to_csv(
"composite_spatial_index_by_region.csv", index=False)

Operating characteristics of the before-after-control-impact design
Evaluating a reconstruction system in the field requires a design that
can detect a change in geometric fidelity attributable to the system
rather than to seasonal or site variation. The before-after-control-impact
design compares the change over time in a treated set of clusters with
the change over time in a control set, and its operating characteristics
depend on the between-cluster variance, the residual variance and the
intraclass correlation (Conner et al., 2016). The present analysis
characterises those operating characteristics under a declared variance
structure with between-cluster SD = 6.0, residual SD = 4.5 and
intraclass correlation = 0.64, and it estimates power across 2000
design cells.
No observational data are used or claimed: the analysis is a replicationbased evaluation of the estimator, and every quantity reported below is
a property of the design under the declared variance structure rather
than an estimate from field measurements.
The design parameters are the cluster counts 8, 16, 24 and 32 and the
effect sizes 3, 5 and 8 percentage points, with 2000 replications per
cell. The intraclass correlation of 0.64 is high by the standards of
agricultural field trials, and it implies that most of the variance in the
outcome is between clusters rather than within them (Killip et al.,
2004). Under such a structure the effective sample size is much smaller
than the nominal cluster count, and the power to detect a given effect
falls accordingly. The analysis therefore reports the cluster count
required to recover power, rather than assuming that a nominal sample
is adequate.
Table 1 reports the estimated power for the before-after-control-impact
interaction across the design grid. Power rises with effect size and with
cluster count, as expected, but the rate of rise is slow because the high
intraclass correlation discounts the information contributed by
additional clusters within the same site. At the smallest effect size of 3
percentage points, power remains below the conventional 80 per cent
threshold even at 32 clusters, which means that a field evaluation
designed to detect a 3 percentage-point improvement in geometric
fidelity would be underpowered at every cluster count in the grid. At 5
percentage points, power reaches 80 per cent only at the upper end of
the grid, and at 8 percentage points the threshold is crossed at
intermediate cluster counts.

Results
The perturbation bound and the design analysis answer different
questions, and their joint implication is the paper's substantive claim.
The bound says that geometric error under wind-induced motion is
dominated by a systematic term that scales with the square of wind
speed and with exposure time, and that adding views reduces only the
random term. The design analysis says that detecting a given

improvement in geometric fidelity in the field requires cluster counts
that are large relative to the grids typically used, because the high
intraclass correlation discounts within-site replication.
Acquisition conditions for geometric fidelity
Together they imply that the acquisition protocol and the evaluation
protocol must be designed against the same quantity: the systematic
error term that the bound identifies is the effect that the field design
must be powered to detect, and a design powered for a smaller effect
than the bound predicts is wasted. The practical consequence is a twostage protocol. In the first stage, the bound is evaluated for the
candidate acquisition configuration, using the wind-speed distribution at
the site, the exposure time of the sensor and the view count of the
capture protocol, to predict the geometric error that the configuration
will produce.
In the second stage, the field design is powered against that predicted
error, using the variance structure of the site to determine the cluster
count. If the predicted error is below the phenotyping tolerance, the
configuration is admissible and the design need only confirm it; if the
predicted error exceeds the tolerance, the configuration must be
changed before the field evaluation is worth conducting (Conner et al.,
2016). This ordering avoids the common failure in which a field trial is
powered for an effect that the acquisition protocol cannot produce.
Relation to existing evaluation practice
Existing evaluations of three-dimensional canopy reconstruction report
geometric accuracy against ground-truth scans in controlled conditions,
and they typically do not report the wind speed or exposure time at
which the scans were acquired (Zhang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021).
The bound derived here shows why that omission matters: the same
reconstruction pipeline can be phenotyping-grade at low wind speed
and fail at moderate wind speed, and without the acquisition conditions
the reported accuracy cannot be generalised to field deployment. The
design analysis adds a second reason for caution, because even a wellcontrolled accuracy study may be underpowered to detect the
difference between two acquisition configurations if the cluster count is
small relative to the intraclass correlation (Killip et al., 2004).
The contribution is therefore not a new reconstruction method but a
framework for evaluating whether a reconstruction method is
trustworthy under the conditions in which it will be used. The framework
also clarifies the role of dynamic neural radiance methods in
agricultural reconstruction. Those methods estimate a deformation field

Boundary conditions and threats to the geometric-fidelity claim
The bound rests on three assumptions that limit its scope. The
displacement field is assumed bounded and smoothly varying, which
holds for wind loading on a canopy but not for discrete events such as a
stem snapping or a leaf detaching during capture. The radiance field is
assumed Lipschitz in position, which holds for any field with bounded
spatial gradient but fails at the sharp occlusion boundaries that are
common in dense canopies.
The rendering Jacobian is assumed to have a smallest eigenvalue
bounded away from zero, which holds when the views constrain the
field in all directions but fails in the sparse-view regime where some
directions are observed by few rays (Yu et al., 2021). Where these
assumptions fail the bound is not valid, and the geometric error may
exceed it. The design analysis rests on a declared variance structure
rather than on measured variance.
The between-cluster SD of 6.0, residual SD of 4.5 and intraclass
correlation of 0.64 are the values under which the operating
characteristics were computed, and they are not estimates from any
particular site. A site with a lower intraclass correlation would require

prescription for any particular field trial.
The phenotyping-grade threshold depends on the trait being measured,
and the bound is expressed in absolute geometric error rather than in
trait-specific terms. A reconstruction that is adequate for plant height
may be inadequate for leaf angle, and the threshold must be set per
trait rather than once for the system (Zhang et al., 2020). The bound
also assumes that the wind-induced displacement is the dominant
source of geometric error, which is true when the canopy is the only
moving element but not when the camera platform itself moves, as it
does on a ground vehicle or an aerial platform (Wang et al., 2021).
Platform motion adds a global component that the bound does not
model, and it must be treated separately. Finally, the bound is an
expected-error bound rather than a worst-case bound, and it does not
guarantee that any particular reconstruction will fall within the
tolerance. A single capture under an unlucky gust may exceed the
bound, and a field protocol that relies on the bound must therefore
include a rejection criterion for individual captures rather than
assuming that every capture is admissible (Besl & McKay, 1992).
The rejection criterion is a design choice, and it interacts with the power
analysis because rejected captures reduce the effective sample and
therefore the power to detect the effect of interest.
Conclusion
Sparse-view neural radiance reconstruction is geometrically trustworthy
for canopy phenotyping only when the wind-induced displacement
during exposure is small relative to the organ dimension being
measured, and the bound derived here makes that condition explicit.
The bound separates a random term that adding views can reduce from
a systematic term that scales with the square of wind speed and with
exposure time and that adding views cannot remove, which inverts the
usual assumption that denser acquisition is always better.

underpowered for the effects that phenotyping evaluations typically
target. The contribution is a framework that links the acquisition
configuration to the geometric error it will produce and links that error
to the field design needed to detect it, so that the two can be designed
against the same quantity. The framework does not claim that any
particular reconstruction has been achieved, and it does not substitute
for field measurement; it specifies what a field measurement would
need to show in order to establish that a reconstruction is phenotypinggrade.
The next step is to apply the framework to a specific acquisition
configuration and a specific site, using the site's wind-speed distribution
and variance structure to compute the admissible exposure time and
the required cluster count, and to test the bound against measured
geometric error under controlled wind conditions.
