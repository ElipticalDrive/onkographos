Abstract
The Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) is a non-parametric density estimation
method, that is both a particle-based and a variational approach. Given some target distribution
π ∝ e−V and a prior, the SVGD provides samples distributed from π. This method is applicable
in many contexts, such as Bayesian inference, but the cost and run-time of the SVGD increases
with the number of samples generated. In this paper, we propose an optimal relaxation policy
(ORP) to accelerate the run-time of the SVGD. This relaxation strategy gives way to a new
numerical method which allows for particle enrichment, i.e. increasing the number of samples
during the run of the SVGD, which is then combined with a multi-level approach. The ORP
also allows us to establish an explicit expression, dependent on the dimension, for the number
of samples needed so that the time-average Kernelized Stein Discrepancy between the prior and
target distributions reaches a desired precision.
Key words. Stein Variational Gradient Descent, relaxation, multilevel, Bayesian inference

Introduction
Bayesian inference deals with sampling from a target posterior distribution, given a prior and a likelihood
function. When the likelihood function is known, an expression for the density of the posterior can be
found. Though the prior might follow an easy-to-sample-from distribution, like a Gaussian, sampling from
the posterior can be quite challenging. To tackle this problem, there are various sampling methods, such
as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), which is particle-based, or variational approaches, which seek to
minimize the Kullback Leibler (KL) divergence between the target and an approximate distribution.
A recent sampling method, called the Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD), combines the best of
both worlds from particle-based and variational methods. Introduced in the seminal paper [33], it starts by
sampling a fixed number of particles from the prior distribution, then deterministically moving them in the
direction that maximally decreases the KL divergence between the target and the current distribution until
the particles become distributed as samples of the target.
Given N ∈ N samples {X (j) }1≤j≤N in Rd , where d ≥ 1, the SVGD iteratively moves the samples by the
mapping X 7→ T (X) = X +ηϕ(X). Here, η is the step-size and ϕ is the vector field that maximally decreases
the Stein discrepancy between the target and the push-forward measure by T . Usually, this optimization

Since the seminal paper [33], many other works have followed. We will give an outline of some of these
contributions in the next paragraphs. Regarding theoretical work on the convergence, in [32], the asymptotic
properties and convergence of the SVGD are studied, showing that in the continuous time limit case and when
N → ∞, the SVGD dynamics are described by a mean-field limit, a nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation. In
this paper, this equation is observed to be a gradient flow of KL divergence under a special metric structure.
In [36], the authors prove the global existence and uniqueness of a solution to this mean-field equation, and
under certain conditions, the convergence of its solution to the target distribution. This paper also studies
the scaling, that is the many-particle, limit of the empirical measure. We also call the SVGD applied to the
many-particle limit the “continuous-SVGD”. The study of the long-time and many-particle case is continued
in [40]. Later, [8] finds improved asymptotic stability results. The paper [18] further explores the properties
of the mean-field equation and introduces the notion of the Stein geometry, discussing the choice of kernel
and giving a condition for exponential decay near the equilibrium in 1D. Later in [9], the exponential rate
of convergence in all dimensions is obtained by establishing the Stein-Log-Sobolev inequality for a certain
class of kernels. Using this inequality, the recent paper [10] studies the continuous-SVGD when the kernel’s
bandwidth goes to 0. This is a non-exhaustive list of continuous-SVGD references and some of the others
are summarized in [41] and [5]. Other recent theoretical works on gradient flows for sampling include [39],
in which the Stein Transport is introduced. Though similar to the SVGD, it has slightly different dynamics,
reaches convergence at finite time, and is connected to Fisher-Rao gradient flows. Also, [13] proposes a
variant of the SVGD that is a kernelized gradient flow of the chi-squared divergence and, in [12], gradient
flows based on the KL divergence are studied under several metrics, including the Stein metric.
As for the finite-particle convergence case, [32, 20, 28] provide some preliminary convergence results. The
first unified convergence result was found in [41], which is the convergence of the finite-particle empirical
measure to the target as both time t and the number of particles N go to infinity. Under mild assumptions,
anpexplicit error bound of the KSD is obtained and a rate of convergence is found to be of the form
1/ log(log(N )). The finite-particle convergence has also been studied in the context of Gaussian-SVGD in
[34], which is when the SVGD is projected to a family of Gaussian distributions. More recently, in [5], a
bound on the KSD in O( √1N ) is obtained for the continuous-time SVGD, then extended to the discrete-time
case. Next, using the relationship between Wasserstein convergence and the KSD from [26] and under some
additional hypotheses on the potential and type of kernel, [5] also shows Wasserstein convergence for the
non-Gaussian finite-particle case. Another finite-particle-based study is [24], which treats a variant of the
SVGD, called the regularized SVGD, which was first introduced in [25].
Aside from asymptotic studies and convergence results, many other variants of the SVGD have been
proposed. Non-exhaustively, there are works focusing on the kernels, such as [38], SVGD with importance
sampling in [22], and the mitotic SVGD in [31], which provides a particle-enrichment method. Accelerating

the SVGD is also a topic of interest. In certain cases, the repeated calculations of ∇V in the SVGD update
can be quite costly, leading to techniques such as Equation (9) in [33], gradient-free SVGD variants such as
[23, 45], and more recently the combination of Evolution Strategies with the SVGD in [7]. In other settings,
gradient-free methods might not be needed, but accelerations still desired. In [27], the SVGD is accelerated
with deep unfolding, in [16] and [11] by including second-order information, and in [42] by combining the
SVGD with Nesterov’s method. There are also multilevel SVGD variants, see for instance [3, 2, 44], which
will be discussed later.
Other works have been done on the SVGD in high dimensions. In particular, [47] shows that a “variance
collapse” phenomena is sometimes observed in the SVGD, in which the particles collapse to the modes of the
target. In [47], the authors note that sometimes the repulsive term in the SVGD update is small compared
to the driving term, which leads to a mode collapse of the particles and an underestimation of the variance,
hence the term “variance-collapse”. In particular, they show that high dimensions can exacerbate this problem, and under certain assumptions, find a negative correlation between the dimension of the problem and
the repulsive term. They then propose a message passing variant of the SVGD, called MP-SVGD, to deal
with high-dimensional cases. Since this paper, other message-passing SVGD methods have been proposed in
[43, 46]. Also tackling this variance-collapse problem, in [15], the authors propose a deterministic annealing
variant of the SVGD, named A-SVGD, which forces the particles to follow two phases: one repulsive in
which the particles must explore the space, and one exploitative in which the driving term becomes more
important. Later, [4] deals with the d > N regime, where the dimension is greater than the number of
particles. These authors suggest that a good estimation of the driving term requires a large number of
particles, so being in this d > N case could lead to variance collapse. They say that it could also be due
to a “deterministic bias” because the SVGD does not resample the particles during its run. The authors
propose a damped SVGD method, which similarly to [15], acts on the driving term, but differs in that the
damping term depends on the target distribution. More recently, in [14], the A-SVGD is applied to a high
dimensional problem of full-waveform inversion (FWI), and a PCA and clustering-based step is added after
the run of the SVGD to analyze the particles. In addition to FWIs, the SVGD has applications in a vast
number of fields.
In this work, we focus on sampling from distributions with a potential of the form V = VT + Vp . This
comes from the Bayesian inference motivation, where the likelihood function is of the form ∝ e−VT , and where
we write the prior as ∝ e−Vp . We are particularly interested in scenarios where a large number of samples
is desired. Of course, the more samples needed, the more costly the sampling, and the longer the SVGD
run-time. Therefore, in this work, we propose a method to accelerate the SVGD using an optimal relaxation
strategy, which is based on the theoretical results from [5]. Given a target precision of the continuoustime KSD average between the final samples and the target distribution, the relaxation strategy is chosen
to minimize the cost of running the SVGD while satisfying this precision goal at the end. Our method
notably provides an analytical expression for the required number of particles so that the time-averaged
KSD precision is attained. We also explore the relationship between this precision, the required number of
particles, and the dimension in a Gaussian setting, where analytical expressions can be derived for all the
terms involved. In addition, we propose a particle enrichment method to increase the number of particles
during the course of the SVGD, allowing the user to start with a lower, cheaper number of particles, before
finishing with a higher, more expensive amount. The optimal relaxation method and particle enrichment are
later coupled with a multi-level approach that is used in practice. In this multi-level method, we first select
a high value of KSD precision (low accuracy) and run the SVGD with relaxation. Once the samples have
reached a low-accuracy approximation of the target, we decrease the KSD precision and run the SVGD with
relaxation again, now starting from these updated samples. We repeat this process until a sufficiently low
KSD precision is employed, resulting in a better approximation. We call this method the Relaxed Multi-Level
Method. This method is illustrated with several numerical examples in varying dimensions. Speed-ups of up
to two orders of magnitude are obtained.
The paper is organized as follows. The optimal relaxation strategy is described in Section 2, the numerical
methodology, including the multi-level and particle enrichment methods are in Section 3, the numerical
results follow in Section 4, and the conclusion is in Section 5.

2
Relaxed SVGD
In this section, we introduce our relaxation strategy. The main idea is as follows. We introduce a relaxed
potential Vs = sVT + Vp and remark that when s = 0, it is equal to the prior’s potential, and when s = 1, it
is equal to the target’s. Therefore, we want to find an increasing sequence of J optimal steps si ∈ [0, 1] such
that s0 = 0 and sJ+1 = 1, where the sense of “optimal” will be specified later. With this step-sequence, we
thus obtain a sequence of potentials {Vsi }0≤i≤J+1 that go from the prior to the target potential. So, instead
of performing the SVGD directly from the prior to the target, we break up the SVGD drift into sub-drifts,
going from Vsi to Vsi+1 for 0 ≤ i ≤ J. The steps {si }1≤i≤J between s0 = 0 and sJ+1 = 1 are chosen
so that the continuous-time KSD average attains a desired precision and so that the cost of running the
SVGD is minimized. This brings us to an optimization problem, where the relaxation substeps {si }1≤i≤J
are the minimizers of the loss function Eq. (11). The construction of this minimization problem will follow
in Section 2.2, where the Optimal Relaxation Policy (ORP) of the substeps is presented. Before this, in
Section 2.1, we introduce some notation and study the upper bound of the KSD, derived in Banerjee et al.’s
work in [5].

Let us discuss this choice of κ. The cost of a SVGD iteration resides primarily in the following two areas:

multiplication has computational cost in O(N 2 ). We could also consider using sparse kernel matrices, making
the computational cost of the form O(N ). The sparsification of the SVGD is out of the scope of this work
but will be briefly discussed in Section 5. For these reasons, in practice, b = 2.

3
Numerical Methods
Before looking at the performance of our method in numerical tests, in this section let us give some details
on how we implement and use our methodology in practice. Starting with a brief discussion of the numerical
optimal relaxation method, we will also describe the particle enrichment method and introduce the multilevel approach mentioned in Section 1. This brings us to our Relaxed Multi-Level Method (RMLM).
3.1
Optimal Relaxation Policy in Practice
The ORP introduced in Section 2.2 relies on the evaluation of integral functions f0 , f1 , f2 , and f3 . If the
prior and target potentials are potentials of Gaussian distributions with diagonal covariance matrices, then
these integrals can be calculated exactly in all dimensions, see Section SM 1.3 and Section SM 1.4, and
the expressions depend exclusively on the means and covariances. For this reason, in our numerical tests,
if the prior and/or target potentials are not of Gaussian form, they are approximated as such for the sole
purpose of evaluating the loss function in the optimal step search. To find the Gaussian approximations,
we estimate the means and covariances of the prior and target distributions, which allow us to derive the
analytical expressions for the associated fk functions (1 ≤ k ≤ 3). For the prior, sometimes, as in the case
of a Gamma distribution, the mean and variance values are explicitly known. If not, we sample a large
number of particles from the prior, then calculate the empirical mean and covariance. For the target, we

3.2
Particle Enrichment
As seen in Section 2.2, as the si sub-steps increase, the number of necessary particles must also increase
during the run of the SVGD. We call the increasing of the particles mid-method “particle enrichment”.
Another work that proposes a particle enrichment method is [31]. In [31], after convergence of the SVGD,
the number of particles is doubled by concatenating the output particles with the particles at the previous
iteration. The SVGD is then run again until convergence, starting with the concatenated particles. This
process is repeated until a sufficiently high number of particles is reached.
Our enrichment method is based on K-nearest neighbor searches (K-NN). The main idea is to create
a partition of the samples, for instance using KD trees, and divide the set of samples into balls. Each ball
helps us estimate the local density. By assuming that within each K-ball, the mass of the points is roughly
the same, new points can be added by sampling uniformly inside each ball. To give an example of this
process, let us suppose that for the upcoming si−1 → si step, we need to increase the number of particles
to Ni > Ni−1 . We denote (X (1) , · · · , X (Ni−1 ) ) the current samples where each X (k) ∈ Rd . From these
samples, we construct a KD tree and query the tree for its K-nearest neighbors, where K is a user-defined
parameter. For a fixed X (k) with 1 ≤ k ≤ Ni−1 , let rk denote the maximal distance between X (k) and its
K-nearest neighbors. This is the radius of the ball centered in X (k) . For each dimension of X (k) , we sample

KD trees were first introduced in [6], and K-NN searches to approximate densities is widely used, see

algorithm used to split the hyperplane. As seen in [37], the construction cost of the standard KD tree, given
in [19], is in O(N log(N )), but other variants exist with different construction complexities. In particular,
Scipy’s implementation is based off of the variant described in [37]. The complexity of a nearest-neighbors
search is in O(log(N )), and because we need to perform m nearest-neighbor searches, the complexity is in
O(m log(N )). In practice, we find the KD tree construction and K-NN search to be cheap compared to the
SVGD itself. A survey of various K-nearest neighbors techniques going beyond KD trees can be found in
[17].
3.3
Multi-Level Method
Using the theoretical foundation developed for the optimal relaxation method in Section 2.2, we will now
introduce a multi-level variant that is used in practice. For ease of notation, we consider a scalar precision

number of particles N. The idea here is to choose a relatively high value of the precision, called ε1 , and

convergence. The results of this SVGD are not very precise, due to the high value of ε1 , but the resulting

This is not the first time a multi-level approach is combined with the SVGD. Other works using multilevel strategies are [3, 2], and [44]. These works deal with Bayesian inference in which the likelihood
function can be costly to evaluate. For instance, it might depend on solutions to PDEs and obtaining good
approximations of these solutions can be quite expensive. The main idea in these works is to start with a
cheaper, less accurate approximation of the likelihood and increase the accuracy at each level. The “levels”
in these works refer to the level of approximation of the likelihood or PDE solution, whereas in this work, our
levels correspond to the KSD precision, ε, which is decreased at each new level. In [3], the number of particles
at all levels is kept fixed and each level is associated with a discretization of the PDE solution that gives a
certain degree of accuracy. The results of this work are extended in [2] and applied to a high-dimensional
Bayesian test case for glacial ice models. The work [44] differs in that the number of particles vary at each
level and the authors use the SVGD with Multi-Level Monte Carlo-style methods and stochastic dynamical
systems to construct expectation estimators. All these works provide an analysis of the computational cost
and show that multi-level SVGD strategies can lower costs.

4
Numerical Results
In this section, we compare the performance of the Relaxed Multi-Level Method (RMLM) with the Classic
Method (CM) across a variety of tests. First of all, in Section 4.1 we perform Gaussian tests where the target
and prior distributions are both Gaussian in 1D, 2D, and 4D. As the target distribution is analytically known,
this allows us to calculate errors, in particular the errors on the mean and covariances. Since the method
is stochastic, due to sampling from the initial prior and during the particle enrichment process, we run the
test cases 32 times each in order to perform a statistical evaluation of errors and speed-ups. Let ∆µ(%)
denote the average relative error on the mean of the resulting particles and ∆σ(%) the average relative error
on their covariance matrix over the 32 runs. The relative errors are calculated using the Euclidean norm for
vectors and the Frobenius norm for matrices. In the 1D Gaussian case, we also study the link between the
KSD precision parameter ε and the speed-up factor of the RMLM, see Section 4.1.1. Next, in Section 4.2,

Conclusion
This work focuses on accelerating the SVGD through a novel optimal relaxation strategy based on preexisting
convergence results from [5]. In addition to an optimal sequence of relaxation sub-steps that break up the
SVGD drift into smaller and less costly problems, we obtain an analytical expression for the required number
of particles given the target distribution, the dimension, and the desired precision on the time-averaged
KSD. Based on these theoretical results, we propose a new numerical method called the Relaxed MultiLevel Method that combines the optimal relaxation strategy with a particle-enrichment and a multi-level
method. These additions allow us to run the SVGD during the early relaxation sub-steps with a lower
number of particles and at a higher value of KSD precision, before reaching the higher, required number of
particles and lower value of KSD precision at the final sub-step. The impact of this method is demonstrated
in practice with both Gaussian and non Gaussian statistical tests across a range of dimensions. Speed-ups
of up to two orders of magnitude are obtained. In addition, in the 1D Gaussian case, a numerical study of
the speed-up shows that as the value of KSD precision decreases, the speed-up increases. Our method is
also applied to a source detection inverse problem in 16D.
Let us note that as opposed to some SVGD acceleration methods which propose gradient-free methods,
in this work, we are interested in the cases where many particles might be required. This could be due to a
challenging target distribution, for example, one with a low variance. The cost of a single SVGD iteration
κ in our method is thus based on the cost of handling the kernel matrix, which can be of the order of the
square of the number of particles. It would be interesting to combine the Relaxed Multi-Level method with
a sparsification of the kernel matrix to further reduce costs. This is the subject of ongoing work but out of
the scope of this paper.
