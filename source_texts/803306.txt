You’ve surely heard about the disastrous incident involving Coldcard. According to rumors that are still unconfirmed, around 600 Bitcoin—worth 38 million U.S. dollars—were stolen. I feel very sorry for the person involved.
This proves once again that you shouldn’t generate random numbers with computers. While such pseudo-random numbers generated by computers are sufficient from a cryptographic standpoint—that’s only true in theory. Practice has shown time and again, even after decades, that it is impossible to generate sufficiently random numbers. If the entropy does not come from an external source, the keys can be recalculated sooner or later—it’s often simply a matter of time. Same applies if the entropy itself is dubious.
Currently, SeedSigner can generate seeds in the following two ways:
Using a photo (12 or 24 words)
50 dice rolls (12 words) or 99 dice rolls (24 words)
The question is quite simple: How robust is option 1, which is surely used frequently because it’s convenient? What scientific evidence do we have here compared to the dice rolls?
If we cannot prove that option 1 is at least as good as option 2, then I believe we should remove option 1 from the SeedSigner entirely. We should not focus on having a convenient method, but rather on having a single method that is absolutely robust.

Since two people already disliked my post with no reason given:
The entropy-from-photo generator uses multiple photos from the camera, but also the CPU serial number and the timestamp.
Why is it using that if we assume the camera returns enough entropy? That does not make sense.
Additionally, both the CPU serial number and the timestamp can't be considered as entropy. Yes, they are used additionally, but entropy has a definition. The CPU serial number is totally not random and static, stays always the same for the device.
Thus, the entropy-from-photo generator—from my understanding—mostly relies on 50 photos from the live view which is human-deterministic and the camera noise. People may only direct the SeedSigner to the wall, floor or to the window. Also we don't know what cameras are used. The results may not be reproducable.
At the end, I don't know how much entropy I would really get from this more complex algorithm. 100 bits? 80 bits? 60 bits? Option 2 on the other hand is way simpler and that dice rolls (with casino dice) create real entropy is proven by billions of dollars.

One possible way to strengthen the justification for the image-entropy method would be to photograph a known physical entropy source, for example a large set of freshly rolled dice, instead of an arbitrary scene.
Let $X$ be the actual image data that is hashed, and let $Y=f(X)$ be the sequence of visible dice results recoverable from that image by a fixed, deterministic ordering rule.
If all dice results are clearly distinguishishable in $X$, then the image representation contains at least the entropy present in $Y$.
For $n$ independent fair six-sided dice:
$$
H(Y)=n\log_2(6)
$$
Therefore, if the dice sequence remains recoverable from the actual image representation that is hashed:
$$
H(X)\ge H(Y)
$$
For 103 dice:
$$
103\log_2(6)\approx266.25\text{ bits}
$$
So the visible dice outcomes alone would provide about 266 bits of source entropy before hashing. Additional entropy may also be present in the random positions, orientations, spatial arrangement, imaging process, etc., but none of it needs to be credited in order to establish the lower bound from the visible dice.
This would avoid having to justify the image-entropy method mainly through assumptions about sensor noise or other difficult-to-quantify camera effects. The important requirement would instead be that the image-processing pipeline preserves enough information for all dice outcomes to remain distinguishishable in the data that is actually hashed.
I would be interested in whether this reasoning also holds cleanly in terms of min-entropy (including conditional min-entropy under a realistic attacker model), and whether there is any flaw in applying this argument to SeedSigner's actual image-entropy pipeline.

see: https://kdmukai-bot.github.io/seedsigner-ai-analysis/image-entropy/
tldr: I captured data using a modified build that took a burst of 10 images instead of just the one final image. The two most similar images in the burst were compared. Even when shooting a plain white wall, the measured difference between the images was 684,000 bits.
An image burst of a more normal, varied scene (a bookshelf) yielded 563,000 bits. FEWER differences than seen from the blank white wall.
Why would a real-world, complex scene produce LESS entropy (via this measurement method) than a blank white wall?!
That's your teaser to review the actual analysis.
It has a companion data and methodology doc that is meant to be consumed by AI to vet the claims, re-run the data analysis, or even build your own version of the capture harness I used to generate the images used in the analysis.
