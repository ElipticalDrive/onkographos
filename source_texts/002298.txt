Abstract
We present an algorithm for compositing a combination of density clouds and
contour surfaces used to represent a scalar function on a 3-D volume. The volume is divided into
convex polyhedra, at whose vertices the function is known, and the polyhedra are sorted in depth
before compositing. For data given at scattered 3-D points, we show that this sorting can be done
in O(n) time if we chose the tetrahedra in the Delaunay triangulation as the polyhedra. The
integrals for cloud opacity and visible cloud intensity along a ray through a convex polyhedron are
computed analytically, and this computation is coherent across the polyhedron’s projected area.

scalar function, scalar field, volume density, opacity, compositing, volume

Introduction
Three dimensional scalar functions can be visualized either as contour surfaces or as semi­
transparent volume density clouds. The function values are traditionally available at vertices on a
3-D integer cubic or rectangular lattice of voxels.
If only one contour surface is required, for example that of bone in medical imaging, the
simplest method is to find the set of all cubes exceeding the contour value, and project them all
[1,2], or just the facets on the boundary of the set [3], onto the picture plane. The image can then
be shaded using depth information, as described in [4],
Contour surfaces can also be polygonalized, by choosing vertices on those cube edges
which cross the contour [5-8]. The vertex normals can be interpolated from estimates of the
gradient of the function, and the polygons can then be shaded in a standard graphics pipeline. If
several transparent contours are desired, these polygons must be sorted [9], or ray tracing must be
used [10].
Ray tracing can also be used to render semi-transparent density clouds, by sampling the
scalar function at equally spaced points along the ray (usually by trilinear interpolation of the vertex
data), and compositing the color and opacity [10 - 13]. Alternatively, planes of the lattice can be
transformed, filtered, sampled at the image raster, and composited in order into the final image
[14]. Contour surface effects may be generated by density classification and gradient shading
methods applied directly to the lattice data [12 - 14], without first generating contour polygons.
There are also many problems where data is not available directly on a cubic lattice. In
finite element analysis, more general rectangular, prismatic, or tetrahedral elements may arise. The
geometry of a rectilinear lattice or finite element mesh may become distorted during a simulation of
elastic or plastic deformation. A rectilinear lattice may be mapped by a non-linear transformation to
a curvilinear lattice in order to represent a curved object. Finally, data values may be obtained at
scattered 3-D points which have not yet been connected by a mesh of edges.
Once a division of the volume into polyhedra is specified, with pointers to the polyhedron
adjacent across each face, it is possible to do ray tracing through an interpolated volume density.
However, the computation of the relevant polyhedron containing a sample along a ray is no longer
trivial, and simple trilinear interpolation is no longer applicable. The projection algorithm of
Dreben et al. [14] could also be generalized to a curvilinear lattice by appropriate multiple pass 3-D
non-linear transformations with anti-aliased resampling in one dimension at a time, as in [15].
However, the object may need to be subdivided into regions with different transformation
sequences, in order to avoid bottlenenecks [15]. Furthermore, this technique does not apply to
more general data sets.
Our goal was a method of rendering contour surfaces and density clouds in the same
image, which works on arbitrary arrangements of data vertices, and which exploits coherency
when coarse data are interpolated. We achieved this by sorting the polyhedra in depth, and then
compositing them [16] in depth order, either back to front, or front to back. We have written a
subroutine to scan convert and composite the density cloud inside a convex polyhedron, using
analytic integration for the color and opacity along the ray projecting to each pixel. We have also
written a surface shading, highlight, transparency, and compositing algorithm for polygons.
Finally, we have written a procedure to slice a convex polyhedron into two by a plane at a contour
level, and call, in the correct order, the cloud compositer twice, with, perhaps, the surface
compositer in between. By explicitly computing precise contour polygons, we are following a
very different philosophy from that in [12 - 14] where surface effects are computed directely from
nearby values of the given function.
The scan conversion of a polygon or polyhedron takes advantage of area coherence across
its projection. In addition, the analytic integration uses the coherence along the ray segment, so we
capitalize on volume coherence throughout each polyhedron. This is much more efficient than
tracing each ray separately from one polyhedron to the next, and the analytic integration is more
accurate as well as faster than interpolating and compositing multiple samples along a ray. Upson
and Keeler [13] discuss a compositing method using coherence across homogeneous segments
along the 1-D projection of the 2-D polygon in which a scan plane intersects a lattice cube.
However, they still sample along a ray rather than doing analytic integration, so they take
advantage of only one of the three dimensions of coherence.
Our volume coherence is particularly efficient where the polyhedra involved project to a
significant screen area. This occurs, when the viewpoint is close to the object or when the volume
subdivision is coarse. It is also applicable to irregular data, which many systems based on a
regular lattice [1 - 8, 12 - 14] cannot handle. However, for lattice data in which each voxel
projects to only a few pixels, the resampling and compositing method of [14] is faster.
Our technique requires that the input polyhedra be sorted in depth, so that whenever
ployhedron A obscures B, B comes later in the sort. Such a sort is trivial for a rectilinear lattice.
However, it is possible to fill a volume with a collection of convex polyhedra including three, A,
B, and C, such that A obscures B, B obscures C, and C obscures A. If such cycles occur, one of
the polyhedra must be subdivided.
In section 6 we present a simple algorithm which can detect such cycles if they occur, and
sort the polyhedra in time O (n) if they do not. We also show that the sort is always possible on
the Delaunay triangulation of a set of vertices. Therefore, on scattered data with no other preferred
triangulation, we can choose the Delaunay triangulation and be sure that cycles will not occur. In
the current system, we have not yet written a subdivision scheme, and hope that cycles will not
occur in practical data.
In the next section we describe our model for light propagation through the density clouds,
and in the following section, our method of computing analytically or with tables the integrals
resulting from the model. In section 4 we describe the algorithm for scan converting a density
cloud polyhedron, and in section 5, the one for rendering semi-transparent phong-shaded
polygons. Section 6 describes the depth sorting.
2.
Cloud Illumination Models
If a cloud is lit from above, the illumination is multiply scattered in all directions by the
cloud particles (droplets of water). Kajiya and Von Herzen [17] and Rushmeier and Torrence [18]
suggest methods of correctly accounting for this multiple scattering, but the computation required
is prohibitive.
A simpler single scattering model was suggested by Blinn [19]. Here the light reaching the
eye is scattered by only one particle, but may be occluded by others on the way to an from that
particle. For a rectilinear lattice, the "slab" method straight forwardly implements this single
scattering model. In a first pass, the light propagates from one layer of voxels to the next,
decreased by the layer's occlusion. A second pass then ray traces or composites the scattered light
to make a final image. The pictures in [17] were drawn in this way. If the directions of the light,
view, and the lattice axes relate favorably, the two passes may be combined into one [20]. Max
has also implemented the single scattering model for densities depending only on a single axis
component [21], or constant within a cloud volume [22].
For more general subdivisions, it should be possible to sort the polyhedra from the point of
view of the light source, and propagate the light from the front to the rear faces of each
polyhedron. However, this would require large amounts of intermediate illumination rasters to be
stored, as well as resampling along each ray, eliminating one dimension of coherence.
Alternatively, each polygonal face could be subdivided by all edges between it and the light source,
into subregions on which the illumination was represented analytically. This might again allow
analytic integration of the scattered light, but would greatly decrease the size of the volumes where
the computation was coherent. Instead, we chose to ignore the shadowing entirely, and only
occlude the light on the way to the viewer.
If we model ambient illumination shining equally from all directions, and not shadowed
along its path to any scattering particle, the result is the same as modelling glowing particles. This
is the density emitter model of Sabella, which assumes that the volume density p(x,y,z) glows
with an energy C p per unit length, and absorbs with an optical density of xp per unit length,
where C and x are constants for any fixed material..

4.
Scan converting and shading polyhedra
Our system handles both orthogonal and perspective projections.
For perspective
projection onto a screen at distance D from the eye, a 3-D point (X,Y,Z) transforms to the screen
coordinates (x,y,z,) where
x = DX/Z
y = DY/Z
z = -1/Z
(4)
This transformation takes planes to planes [23]. Therefore if z is interpolated linearly across a face
of a transformed polyhedron in screen coordinates and then Z is computed as -1/z, the result will
be the same as if the face of the original polyhedron were ray traced. This is necessary for the
integrals of the density cloud to be correctly computed.
Now consider a convex polyhedron V in screen coordinates, projected orthogonally onto a
convex region R in the (x,y) plane. The region R is covered twice by faces of V: once by those
facing the viewer, and once by those facing away.
If we scan convert R twice, linearly
interpolating z and the scalar function f for the front and back surfaces of V, we have the data
necessary to compute I and a as in the previous section. This is accomplished by a standard scan
line algorithm, stripped down to take advantage of the simplicity in the projection of a convex
polyhedron.
Edges of all polygons bounding V are entered into y buckets at the first scan line they
intersect, with a count FRONT set to 1 if the polygon is front - facing, and zero otherwise. Before
an edge is entered, the bucket is searched. If the edge has already been entered for another
polygon, the only action taken is to increment FRONT if the current polygon is front - facing. At
the end of the process, FRONT will be 0 or 2 for edges which project to the interior of R, and 1
for edges on the profile of V. Another pass through all the edges then changes the 2 values to 1,
and makes a second copy, with FRONT = 0, of each profile edge found having FRONT = 1. Two
x-sorted lists of edges are maintained from one scan line to the next: one for the front - facing
polygonal network, and one for the rear - facing. Since each network covers R exactly once, the
edges of a given network never cross each other. Therefore if the edges are entered correctly from
the y buckets into the x-sorted lists, the sort need never be checked thereafter. The x,z, and f
values are maintained incrementally for each edge from one scan line to the next.
Then, separately for the front and back networks, z and f are interpolated across the
intervals between edge intersections, and saved in separate pairs of scan line buffers. The integrals
(2) and (3) for I and a are then computed, assuming a further linear interpolation of f along the
length of the segment projecting to each pixel.
The relation between the front and back surfaces only comes into play in this last
integration step.
There is never any need to subdivide into smaller homogeneous spans
corresponding to the same front - and back - facing polygons. And since z and f are stored for the
front and back surfaces across the whole segment where the scanline intersects R, the
computations of I and a can be vectorized treating this whole segment homogeneously.
A less accurate but much faster alternative is to interpolate I and a directly across the
homogeneous regions onto which the same front - and back - facing polygons project. Since I is
0 and a is 1 along the profile, they need only be computed at the intersections of edge projections
in the interior of R. This scheme is particularly advantageous on systems like the IRIS 220 GTX,
which have hardware assist for linearly interpolating and compositing ”r g b a" images across
polygons. However, it less accurate, because I and a are non-linear functions of the cloud volume
thickness along a ray. Perhaps quadratic or cubic interpolation across bezier triangles [24,25]
would provide superior results.
The trilinear interpolation of f described above is ad-hoc, and depends on the orientation of
V and its projection onto the picture plane. For a general convex polyhedron, there is no obvious
way to interpolate values of f given at the vertices. However, if V is a tetrahedron, there is a
unique linear function f, independent of orientation, which matches the values at the vertices. If W
is any subvolume of V, obtained by slicing with contour planes, then the trilinear interpolation
described above for orthogonal projection will agree with f restricted to W.
But the
transformations (4) for perspective are non-linear, and destroy this property. Therefore, we allow
the user to specify a more accurate perspective option, which interpolates f linearly in world

5-Shading contour polgons
The contour polygons to be phong shaded are scan converted with the standard sort of
scan-line algorithm as described above, with the unit normals at the vertices also bilinearly
interpolated across the polygon. For rectangular lattices, the normal for a contour vertex can be
interpolated from the gradients of f at the vertices at the endpoints of the lattice edge containing the
contour vertex. The gradients at the lattice vertices can in turn be estimated from finite differences
of the values of f at adjacent vertices. For more irregular networks, the gradient of f is taken from
the gradient of the least squares linear approximation to f at the network vertex and its adjacent
neighbors. For curvilinear lattices the neighbors can be found from the lattice indices, but for more
general grids, pointers specifying the topology must be used.
For coarse subdivisions, the contour polygons may look blocky at their profiles, and mach
bands may appear along interior polygon edges, since phong shading is not C^. Max [25] gives a
method for subdividing the polygons into bezier triangles,.to give
shading, and for adding extra
polygons and vertices to smooth the profile. This method requires knowledge of the contour
surface topology, which is not directly available from our construction based on processing the
network polyhedra separately. Gallagher and Nagtegall [26] give a method of approximating the
contour surfaces with triangular or (preferably) quadrilateral bicubic patches across the interior of
each polyhedron. The patches are defined locally, without knowledge of the adjacent polyhedra,
so no topological data is required, but the global result is not C^. Nevertheless, generally smooth
surfaces and profiles result when these patches are subdivided into small polygons for rendering.
Unfortunately neither of these methods is applicable to our situation, because they can chop
the original polyhedron into non-convex pieces.
We use the phong scheme of bilinearly interpolating the normal vectors given at the vertices
and then renormalizing to a unit vector, but compute the highlights by two different methods. Let
N be the normal vector at a pixel, and H be the unit vector halfway between the viewing direction
V and the lighting direction L. Then the first method computes the highlight intensity I as
SC(H N)m, where S is the specular reflection coefficient, C is the color of the light, and m is an
exponent affecting the apparent size of the highlight. This method is particularly fast when H is a
constant, which is the case when the viewpoint and light are both at infinity. We always assume
the light is at infinity, but even for a perspective view with a finite viewpoint, we offer a fast
shading option assuming V is constant.
This assumption would result in constant highlights across flat polygons. For more
accurate highlights we have a second option, which computes the vector V separately for each
pixel. In this case, if there are multiple light sources Lj it is faster to compute a single vector R in
the direction at which a flat mirror normal to N would reflect the ray along V. According to
Whined [27], R can be computed as R = 2(V-N)N - V. Once R is known, we compute the
highlight intensity due to the i^ light source as I- = SC-(R-L-)n, where C- is the color of the i1^
light source, and n again determines the size of the highlight. If m = 4n, the two formulations give
approximately the same highlight.
For semi-transparent glass appearance on the contour surface, we use the same
transparency formulation as for the cloud density. The length a ray of unit direction V spends
inside a glass surface with normal N and thickness t is t/(V-N). If the glass has opacity x per unit
length, the transparency is exp( - xt/(V-N)). This becomes zero when V and N are perpendicular,
at the surface profile. Since we do not subdivide to get an accurate profile, the transparency could
instead become zero at a line in the interior of a polygon when some of the vertex normals point
towards the eye, and some point away. Therefore, we do not use the interpolated normals in this
computation, but instead interpolate the transparency computed at the vertices. This also saves
computing an exponential, dot product, and divide at every pixel.
6.
Depth-Sorting Polyhedral Subdivisions
List-priority algorithms have been used for hidden surface removal since the '60's
(Schumacher [27], Newell et al. [28]). The basic idea is to draw shaded filled polygons from back
to front into a framebuffer so that the front ones overwrite the ones behind them. Newell's
algorithm determined the priority order on a frame by frame basis by comparing the overlap of
polygons from a given point of view. Schumacher's algorithm precomputed a priority graph.
This graph was then topologically sorted and every polygon given a number. The sort was such
that once back-facing polygons were removed, the remaining polygons were always ordered from
back to front based on the preassigned numbers. Schumacher's algorithm also used separating
planes to partition convex clusters. This is a forerunner of binary space partition (BSP) trees [30].
A BSP-tree is a hierarchical partition of space. Each node in the tree is a plane which
divide space into two regions. Given a BSP-tree, a priority sort can be done in time proportional
to the number of nodes in the tree. To do this the tree is traversed; at each node the viewpoint is
compared against the separating plane and the two regions classified into front and back. The two
trees corresponding to these regions are then recursively traversed in back to front order. All these
methods allow a collection of polygons to be quickly sorted from back to front given any
viewpoint.
Most modern graphics hardware performs hidden surface elimination using a z-buffer so
these algrothims are not commonly used for that task. However, if polygons are to be drawn with
transparency, the list priority algorithms are still the best known way to draw the scene because
transparency calculations can be reduced to compositing operations if the surfaces are drawn in the
proper order.
We are interested in drawing a cell subdivision in three dimensions in back to front order to
perform an opacity projection of the subdivision. Each cell is assumed to be a convex polyhedron
with planar faces. We will also assume that the face adjacencies are given. That is, given a
polyhedron and one of its faces, we can find the polyhedron opposite the face in constant time.
Assume also that the plane equation of the face is available. For simplicity, we will also assume
the subdivision is space-filling so there are no holes.
To sort the cells in back to front order we first determine relative priorities of adjacent
polyhedra. This is done by comparing the viewpoint to the plane of the face dividing a pair of
adjacent polyhedra. Since the polyhedra are convex, this plane separates the polyhedra — that is,
each polyhedron must lie entirely on opposite sides of the plane. Furthermore, the forntmost
polyhedron is the polyhedron that is on the same side of the plane as the viewpoint. Relative
priorities can be constructed in time proportional to the total number of faces. These relative
priorities are stored in a directed graph. Note that the priority graph changes whenever the
viewpoint changes.
The second step topologically sorts the priority graph into a single priority list ordered from
back to front. The topological sort will always succeed as long as the directed graph does not
contain cycles. Furthermore, the sort can be performed in time proportional to the number of
edges in the graph (which equals the numbr of faces in the subdivision). As described in Knuth,
topological sorting can be done in linear time by keeping track of the number of active incoming
edges at every vertex in the graph. All the vertices with no incoming edges are placed in a queue.
Vertices are successively removed from this quiue and placed on the priority list. When they are
removed, all their neighbors along outgoing edges are examined. For each neighbor the count of
active incoming edges is decremented, and if the count goes to zero, then all the neighbors which
have priority over it have been output, so it is placed on the queue to be output.
If the queue becomes empty before all polygons are listed, there must be a cycle in the
graph. Otherwise, the algorithm will determine a priority sort in O(n) time. Note that a collection
of polyhedra which at some time were together in the queue are known not to overlap, and may be
rendered symultaneously. With multiply rendering processors, this can reduce the overlap tests
necessary to determine if a new polyhedron can be started on an available processor.
A special case of the general space subdivision is a triangulation. In 3D, a triangulation is a
decomposition of space into a set of tetrahedra. Tetrahedral subdivisions are nice because the
number of face adjacencies is always 4 and, as mentioned previously, they allow well-defined
interpolation formula.
A special triangulation is the Delaunay triangulation. This is the dual of a decomposition of
space into Voronoi polyhedra. The Voronoi diagram is defined given a set of points in space.
Each point is surrounded by a polyhedron defined as the locus of points which are nearer that point
than any other point in the set. The union of all these polyhedra are the Voronoi diagram [31, 32].
The vertices of the dual — the Delaunay triangulation — are the original set of points. Delaunay
triangulations are useful for several reasons. They can be computed in O (n log n) time in two- and
three-dimensions; The tetrahedra tend to be well-behaved and not long and skinny. Thus,
Delaunay triangulations are conveniently generated from point sets and these triangulations are
useful grids for finite element calculations or, when scattered data points are given without any
other preferred grid.
Delaunay triangulations also have another remarkable property recently proved by
Edelsbrunner [33]: "Given a viewpoint, the triangles comprising a Delaunay triangulation can
always be sorted in back to front order." This property makes these triangulations a useful
representation for volume rendering, since the topological sort described above will never find
cycles.
