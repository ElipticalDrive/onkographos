PERSPECTIVE is a branch of Geometry which
treats of the appearance of objects as distin­
guished from their actual shape.
Ordinary
perspective is the art practised by the draughts­
man and photographer of representing on a plane
surface (called the " plane of delineation")
objects as they would present themselves to the
eye if visible. " Things grow smaller in appear­
ance the further off they are.
Moreover this
lessening is progressive and uniform. There­
fore if objects of equal height are to be
represented as they " should " appear to the eye,
they must be drawn of different heights, the
nearer higher than the more remote; and the
same applies to width." *

So far as the construction goes, it is evident
by inspection that the perspective delineations
on X and P are similar in the mathematical
sense, differing only in size, and on all planes
parallel to X and P, as long as the distance O
to B is fixed. Also that when we photograph
or X-ray a body from the same side of it, the
photographic print ( + P) corresponds in the
matter of form and outline with the X-ray
negative ( - X ) ; while the photographic negative
( - P) is the proper analogue of the X-ray print
or the fluorescent screen ( + X ) . And again, in
the matter of perspective appearance of near and
far, it follows that those parts depicted (as for
example one of two overlapping bones) are to
be looked for as nearer the eye as were origin­
ally nearer the focus tube at the time of taking.
In the majority of cases in which X-ray photo­
graphs are taken, no visual comparison of the
objects radiographed is at the moment available;
vet the same geometrical principles of perspective
must apply.

That is to say, the photographic print agrees
in order of parts from one side to another with
the X-ray negative and both with the object
portrayed; and the photographic negative with
the X-ray print, and both the latter with the
original writing viewed in a plane mirror.
The practical consequences of the double
thesis (namely, (a) that the X-ray negative should
be discarded and (p) that parts of the object
portrayed are to be looked for perspectively in
the same order of distance as they were in
reference to the focus tube) if proved are entirely
opposed to the prevailing belief and usage.
Some of these consequences I have set out in a
former paper and will not now repeat.* But an
example may be taken from this Journal for
July last. Assuming that it is a print of the
right hand taken palm next the plate, the
excellent radiograph of Plate I. is perspectively
a representation of a left hand viewed through
its back—not, of course, the patient's real left
hand (which has no lesion), but a virtual, ideal,

In the translation into visible tones of a
generally invisible object by X-rays, certain
peculiarities of the transcribing agent remain.
If we call an ordinary drawing or photograph on
a plane P between O and B " graphic," then we
may appropriately call an X-ray representation
on a plane X beyond B " diagraphic." It will
be seen that the ambiguities as to relative
distance throw the interpreter back almost
entirely on the proper appreciation of perspective,

(1.) The absence of cross shading and cross
lighting takes away the impression of any relief
in an X-ray picture, the contrary of what holds
in the photographs of the child's hands in Plate I.
of the July Journal.
(2.) Owing to the
centrifugal
projection
opaque bodies eclipse other bodies both beyond
them and between them and the anticathodal
centre of emission; further, the overlapping out­
lines of silhouettes mutually disappear.
Thus
the coin in the illustration had one edge under
the upper transverse rod and the lower edge
over (nearer than) the middle transverse rod.
Nevertheless when two opaque bodies are known
to be of the same diameter, the one with the
apparently larger diameter must, according to the
laws of ordinary perspective, have been nearer
the radiographic centre at the time of taking.
(3.) Transparent bodies are seen one through
another indifferently; not only pictorially is the
furniture seen through the ghost, but the ghost
is also seen through the furniture.
In the
example shown the transverse rods were farther
from the focus tube than the bones across them.
Here again we are thrown back on perspective
laws alone to judge by as to relative distances;
whereas in ordinary vision or representation most
of our judgments as to relative distance are due
largely to the fact that nearer opaque reflecting
bodies hide parts of more remote ones, or if
transparent, at least obscure them.
(4.) The radiographic centre of emission not
being a mathematical point, but of sensible
dimensions, parts of the objects portrayed nearer
•to it came out less distinctly than parts closer
to the photographic plate—as would happen for
example if we focussed an ordinary camera for
the middle distance, but not for the foreground.
(5.) Except under artificial conditions, as when
we cover objects with some powder like bismuth
(as has been done with the wooden cube in
Fig. II.), or, perhaps, in the case of the dense
subperiosteal layer of bone in the shaft of a
long bone, in an X-ray photograph the repre­
sentation of surface contour is defective; rather,
are delineated mass and outline. Yet whereas
in ordinary delineation as a rule only the nearer
sides of things are represented, in an X-ray
delineation there is a possibility of depicting both

the back and front of an object at the same

(6.) Pictorially, so far as it is an enlargement,
an X-ray negative ( - X ) may be considered as
an image (on the plane of X ) of an enlarged
model of the objects depicted at a proportional
distance beyond (i.e., beyond X from O)—it is
thus an image of what lies behind the plane of
delineation in the same sense as an ordinary
drawing or pinhole photograph.
This paper is a plea for a fresh start in the
interpretation of X-ray photographs, on proper
perspective principles, by discarding the X-ray
print as such and using the X-ray negative viewed
from its face exclusively. T h e X-ray print has
come into sole use partly from a false analogy,
and partly from well-known technical exigencies.
So long as light tones are regarded as the
equivalent of the presence of actinic-rays of the
nature of light, and dark tones as the equivalent
of their absence, no doubt the X-ray print is
more correct than the X-ray negative; but it is
very easy to obtain secondary X-ray negatives
(correct in beginning the scale of tone at the
right end) by printing from the primary on
" carbon tissue," using only a single transfer.
