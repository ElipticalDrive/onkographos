Relightable Neural Assets, we introduce key adaptations that enable neural shading of volumetric cloud phenomena within a rasterization-based pipeline. In particular, we incorporate a per-pixel thickness parameter to
capture view-dependent opacity and replace the generalizing single light source with a sky illumination model,
allowing the network to learn complex atmospheric scattering effects. To achieve real-time performance, we

with a triplane feature encoding. This enables a fully rasterization-driven solution that reproduces volumetric
appearance without requiring ray marching or volume integration. We further describe a complete pipeline

Introduction
In the pursuit of increasing visual realism in real-time graphics, rendering volumetric clouds
remains a challenging problem. Modern game engines rely on a spectrum of approximations,
ranging from static skyboxes and billboards [Maciel and Shirley 1995] to impostors and dynamic
density fields [Schneider 2023]. While density-field approaches produce a more faithful cloud
appearance, they require volumetric rendering, which is computationally expensive.
Real-time volumetric rendering typically relies on ray marching, a process that incurs significant
computational cost due to repeated sampling along view rays. To achieve interactive performance,
existing systems often trade image quality for speed, for example, by evaluating the volume at
reduced resolution or using sparse sampling for distant regions [Hillaire 2016; Schneider 2023].
As a result, real-time cloud rendering often suffers from blur and loss of fine detail, making it a
delicate balance between performance and visual fidelity.
Recent advances in neural rendering have demonstrated strong potential for modeling complex
light transport in real time [Mullia et al. 2024; Zeltner et al. 2024]. However, these approaches
primarily target surfaces, focusing on reflectance and subsurface scattering, and do not directly
address fully volumetric phenomena such as clouds.
In this work, we introduce an adaptation of Relightable Neural Assets (RNA) [Mullia et al. 2024]
for real-time rendering of volumetric clouds within a rasterization-based pipeline. Our method
replaces explicit volumetric rendering with a neural shading model that reproduces volumetric
appearance on mesh geometry. To this end, we convert volumetric cloud assets into meshes and
generate training data using path-traced ground-truth renderings in Blender. The dataset captures
the cloud under diverse viewing and lighting conditions, including illumination from a physically
motivated sky model that accounts for atmospheric scattering. While RNA generalizes the light
interactions, our model bakes a single sky-illumination model as part of the training.
The resulting neural model learns to approximate complex light transport effects and is evaluated
at render time using standard rasterization outputs. By leveraging mesh representations, we
integrate seamlessly into the rasterization pipeline and utilize readily available information such as
G-buffers and depth. This design eliminates the need for ray marching while preserving volumetric
appearance, enabling sharper details than traditional real-time volumetric approaches and greater
view-dependent fidelity than billboard-based methods.
The main contributions of this paper are:
• We propose a neural shading model, inspired by Relightable Neural Assets, for real-time
rendering of volumetric clouds with realistic lighting.
• We present a workflow for converting volumetric cloud assets into mesh-based representations suitable for neural training.

2
Related Work
Cloud research in computer graphics is commonly studied from three complementary perspectives:
modeling, animation, and rendering [Goswami 2021]. Rendering in computer graphics has traditionally been dominated by physically-based approaches, which explicitly simulate light transport
to achieve high realism. While these methods provide strong physical grounding and predictive
accuracy, they are often computationally expensive, particularly for volumetric phenomena such
as clouds. In contrast, recent machine learning-based approaches, including neural rendering, aim
to approximate light transport from data, enabling efficient evaluation at runtime while capturing
complex appearance effects that are difficult to model analytically.
Building on this paradigm shift, our work extends Relightable Neural Assets [Mullia et al. 2024]
to the domain of volumetric cloud rendering. Accordingly, this section focuses on complementary
literature not extensively covered in their work, while highlighting key similarities and differences
where relevant throughout the paper.
2.1
Real-Time Cloud Rendering
Existing approaches for rendering clouds span a wide range of techniques, from heuristic approximations to physically-based methods. High-fidelity results typically require simulating light transport
through a participating medium, which is computationally expensive. In practice, real-time methods
rely on approximations, often limiting computations to single scattering or employing simplified
models of multiple scattering.
Billboard-based approaches [Maciel and Shirley 1995] approximate clouds using textured polygons and offer excellent performance. They have been widely used in real-time applications [Dobashi
et al. 2000; Harris and Lastra 2001; Wang 2003], producing visually plausible results. However, such
methods are inherently limited in view-dependent effects and lighting interactions. In contrast,
our mesh-based representation enables full parallax and more consistent lighting under changing
viewpoints.
Physically-based real-time cloud rendering is commonly achieved using ray marching over
density fields. For instance, Hillaire [2016] and Schneider [2023] present production-ready systems
for sky, atmosphere, and cloud rendering that rely on volumetric ray marching. While these methods
achieve high visual fidelity, their computational cost necessitates optimizations such as reduced
resolution and sparse sampling, often leading to blurred results. In contrast, our approach eliminates
ray marching entirely by learning the appearance of volumetric light transport through a neural
model applied to mesh geometry.
Duarte and Gomes [2017] propose a simulation-based pipeline that converts density fields into
meshes; however, these meshes are not used directly for rendering. Instead, they are voxelized and
used to derive volumetric fields, and the final rendering still relies on multi-scattering ray marching.
Our approach differs fundamentally by using the mesh as the primary rendering primitive.
For offline rendering, Kallweit et al. [2017] demonstrate neural methods for predicting radiance in
atmospheric clouds, achieving high-quality results at the cost of performance. Hu et al. [2023] build

2.2
Neural Rendering
Beyond RNA [Mullia et al. 2024], neural rendering has been widely explored for modeling appearance and light transport. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [Mildenhall et al. 2020] enable high-quality
novel view synthesis by encoding volumetric scenes into neural networks. However, NeRF-based
methods rely on ray marching through density fields and typically bake lighting into the representation, limiting relighting capabilities. Several extensions address relighting [Toschi et al. 2023; Xu
et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2024], but they retain the fundamental cost of volumetric rendering. Stadter
et al. [2024] extend NeRF-based approaches with neural volumetric level-of-detail representations
for path tracing, improving efficiency by adapting resolution based on distance. While effective,
these methods remain tied to volumetric representations and ray-based integration.
Condor et al. [2025] propose representing scattering and emissive volumes as compact threedimensional Gaussian or Epanechnikov volumetric primitives that can then be rendered through
ray tracing. However, their approach is not primarily targeting real-time rendering.
Neural methods have also been used to replace or augment BVH-based ray queries in ray tracing.
Weier et al. [2024] introduce neural nodes in a BVH-like hierarchy that can be queried for visibility,
intersection depth, normals, and appearance attributes. Fujieda et al. [2025] similarly introduce
per-object neural intersection functions that replace bottom-level BVH traversal. These works
primarily address geometry compression and ray-intersection queries rather than learning complex
shading or relightable light transport.
In contrast to prior neural and volumetric approaches, our method combines a neural shading
model with mesh-based rasterization, enabling real-time rendering of volumetric-looking clouds
without explicit volumetric integration.
3
Method
Our method consists of three stages: dataset generation, training, and real-time rendering (see
Figure 1). At its core is a neural shading model that learns to approximate the complex light
scattering within clouds, as well as their interaction with atmospheric illumination. The model
takes scene-dependent features as input and evaluates a multilayer perceptron (MLP), composed
of fully connected layers, to predict the final pixel color. In this way, volumetric appearance
is reproduced through learned shading on mesh geometry. The details of the neural model are
described in the following sections.
To train the model, we generate supervised data using Blender [Blender Online Community
2025]. This includes physically-based, path-traced renderings of clouds under varying viewing and
lighting conditions, as well as auxiliary scene information required as input to the neural model.
In addition, Blender is used to extract a mesh representation from the volumetric density field of
the cloud asset. Generating this dataset requires a carefully designed rendering setup, which we
describe in detail below.

3.1
Neural Model
Our neural model is based on the work of Mullia et al. [2024] with some modifications to better suit
volumetric clouds. Although their model handles subsurface scattering, it is primarily designed for
surfaces. To better capture the volumetric nature of clouds, we introduce a thickness parameter
that provides additional context about the distance that light travels through the cloud at a given
point and direction. Additionally, we add transparency as an output feature of the model to better
represent the semi-transparent nature of clouds. The rest of the inputs and outputs of the model
remain similar to the original RNA model, except for the normal and view directions. RNA uses the
normal direction as input to the model. In our model, however, we are not using it as it makes little
sense for a cloud. Even though we could extract it from the mesh, it does not contribute to how
photons scatter when they enter the cloud. The view direction is also left out, and the effects of
that are discussed in the results.
The complete architecture of our neural model is illustrated in Figure 2. The shading point p is
encoded using a triplane representation: the point is projected onto three orthogonal planes, and
the features 𝐹𝑋 𝑌 , 𝐹𝑌 𝑍 , and 𝐹𝑋 𝑍 are sampled from each plane using bilinear interpolation and finally
summed together [Chan et al. 2022]. While Mullia et al. [2024] use a 3D vector to represent the
light direction, we use a 2D parametrization of the hemisphere using spherical coordinates 𝜃 and
𝜙 for azimuth and elevation angles, respectively. These angles map to the sun direction in a sky
model, which will be the light source for the shading. This is in contrast to RNA, which considers
only a single directional light source. The final input to the model is the thickness parameter 𝛿,
which is the thickness of the cloud along the view direction at point p.
These inputs are concatenated and passed to an MLP that outputs two RGB colors c𝐷 and c𝐼 . The
color c𝐷 represents the color of the shading point when it is directly lit by the light source, while c𝐼
represents the indirect shading color when the shading point is occluded from the light source. This
is the same approach used by Mullia et al. [2024]. However, we also output a transparency value
𝛼 that represent how transparent the cloud is at the shading point for the given view direction.
For activation functions, we use rectified linear units, or ReLU, on all the hidden layers. On the
output layer, we use an exponential activation function with an offset, 𝑔(𝑥) = 𝑒 𝑥−3 , as proposed
by Zeltner et al. [2024].
In Section 4, we show an ablative study that demonstrates the importance of the thickness
parameter, the split RGB outputs (𝑐𝐷 and 𝑐𝐼 ), and different configurations for the MLP network.

Mesh extraction. We begin by converting the volumetric cloud asset into a mesh representation. Cloud assets are typically provided as density fields; in our case, we use the OpenVDB
format [Museth 2013]. The conversion from volume to mesh is performed using Blender’s Volume
to Mesh modifier, which internally relies on the OpenVDB toolkit. While various methods exist
for extracting isosurfaces [Kobbelt et al. 2001; Lorensen and Cline 1987; Schaefer et al. 2007], the
specifics of this process are not the focus of this work.
The resulting mesh can be highly detailed depending on the input density field and the algorithm
used. For our dataset, the extracted mesh contains approximately 5.1 million triangles. To study
performance trade-offs, we also generate a simplified version using Blender’s Decimate modifier,
reducing the mesh to 83,995 triangles. The neural model is trained using the high-resolution mesh,
while both versions are evaluated at runtime. This demonstrates that inference can be performed
on lower-resolution meshes, provided they approximate the same geometry and yield consistent
positional features (e.g., for thickness and triplane encoding).
Rendering setup. Training data are generated by rendering the mesh from multiple viewpoints
under varying lighting conditions. We use Blender’s View Layers and Render Passes to extract both
ground-truth images and auxiliary scene information, such as world-space positions.
To capture both volumetric appearance and surface-based features, we maintain multiple instances of the cloud mesh across different collections with distinct materials. For ground-truth
rendering, we use the Principled Volume shader to simulate volumetric light transport. For feature
extraction, we use a diffuse surface material (Principled BSDF ) to obtain geometric information.
Multi-scene configuration. We employ three separate scenes to capture all required data in a
single rendering pipeline:
• Front: Renders the volumetric cloud using a Nishita Sky Texture [Nishita et al. 1993] for
illumination, and outputs both color and surface position.

Dataset size. Unlike RNA, which varies lighting per pixel within a single render, the standard
Blender pipeline requires separate renders for each lighting condition. To compensate, we generate
1,000 frames (compared to 400 in RNA), increasing diversity in viewing and illumination conditions.
3.3
Dataset Processing
Before training, the generated data are processed to match the input representation of the neural
model.
Position normalization and thickness. Using the bounding box of the cloud, all world-space
positions are normalized to a consistent coordinate range. To compute per-pixel thickness, we
mirror the positions captured by the back camera and compute the difference with the corresponding
front-facing positions. This yields a thickness value that approximates the accumulated density
along the view direction.
View direction. Unlike RNA [Mullia et al. 2024], our model does not use the view direction as an
input. However, for comparison purposes, we store the camera position for each frame. We assume
that clouds are observed at a distance, allowing us to approximate the view direction as constant
across the image. This contrasts with RNA, where the view direction is defined per pixel.
This assumption should not pose any problems since this parameter is only used for the evaluation
of the impact of the view direction, and we can control the evaluation dataset to not include any
close-up images.

Visibility. The diffuse direct light pass from the visibility scene is used to determine whether
a point is directly or indirectly illuminated, corresponding to the outputs c𝐷 and c𝐼 . In RNA,
this visibility is computed via ray tracing. In our case, the rendered diffuse direct pass contains
continuous values and color information, and is therefore not directly suitable for binary visibility
classification.
To address this, we convert the diffuse direct pass to grayscale using the luminosity method. The
resulting values are then processed using a smoothstep function (cubic Hermite interpolation) to
produce soft visibility values. We empirically set the lower and upper thresholds to 0.0 and 0.1,
respectively. This formulation enables smooth interpolation between c𝐷 and c𝐼 , which is particularly
important for clouds with soft shadow boundaries.
Data filtering. Finally, we remove fully transparent pixels from the dataset, as they do not
contribute to the training signal. All processing steps are implemented using NumPy and PyTorch.
3.4
Training
We train the neural shading model using PyTorch [Paszke et al. 2019]. For each sample, the 3D
position p is encoded using a triplane representation, from which feature vectors are extracted
via bilinear interpolation. These features are concatenated with the sun direction (𝜃, 𝜙) and the
thickness parameter 𝛿, forming the input to the MLP. The network predicts two RGB values, c𝐷 and
c𝐼 , corresponding to directly and indirectly illuminated components, as well as a transparency value
𝛼. The triplane feature maps are implemented as learnable Parameter tensors in PyTorch. Using the
precomputed visibility values, we interpolate between c𝐷 and c𝐼 to obtain the final predicted color
c.̃ The predicted output (c̃, 𝛼) is then compared to the ground-truth RGBA values to compute the
training loss.
Following RNA [Mullia et al. 2024], we apply a logarithmic transformation log(𝑥 + 1) to both
predictions and ground truth prior to computing the ℓ2 loss. This stabilizes training and improves
reconstruction of high dynamic range values. We train the network for 250 epochs using the Adam
optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10−3 , which is halved every 50 epochs. The batch size is
set to 10242 samples, corresponding approximately to one frame of training data (but not exactly,
since fully transparent pixels are filtered). Training is performed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, giving
a total training time of about 20 minutes.
3.5
Renderer
Real-time rendering is implemented in a custom Vulkan framework using a sequence of render
passes. The scene is first rendered with all opaque geometry and the Nishita sky model, which is
required for consistency with the training data. While other sky models could be used, they would
require retraining to ensure consistency between training and inference.
After rendering the base scene, clouds are processed through a series of passes that reconstruct
the inputs required by the neural model and perform neural shading within a rasterization pipeline.
3.5.1 Neural Model and Inference. The trained neural model is integrated into Vulkan using the
cooperative vector extension, enabling execution on tensor cores from any shader stage [The
Khronos Group, Inc. 2024]. The MLP weights are stored in 16-bit floating-point precision, as
required by tensor core operations.
The triplane encoding is stored as 32-bit floating-point RGBA textures. Each texture encodes
four feature channels, and with three planes (XY, XZ, YZ) and eight features per plane, a total of six
textures are used at a resolution of 512 × 512. Using textures allows efficient bilinear interpolation
during feature lookup, consistent with the training setup.

3.5.2 Cloud Render Passes. The rendering pipeline for clouds is illustrated in Figure 3. It consists
of several pre-passes followed by a final shading pass.
Back-face position pre-pass. To compute the thickness parameter, we first render the back faces
of the cloud mesh. This pre-pass uses front-face culling and a greater-than depth test to capture the
furthest visible surface. The resulting positions are stored in a 16-bit RGB attachment, normalized
to the cloud’s bounding box.
Front-face depth pre-pass. Since the neural model shades only the visible surface, we perform a
Front-Face Depth Pre-Pass using back-face culling and a less-or-equal depth test. This ensures that
only the closest surface contributes during the final shading pass, which loads the same depth
buffer and compares to it.
Shadow pre-pass. To determine direct and indirect illumination, we generate a shadow map
from the sun direction defined by the Nishita sky model. The MLP outputs two colors, c𝐷 and c𝐼 ,
corresponding to directly and indirectly lit regions. We compute a shadow factor using percentage

4
Results
We evaluate our method through a series of ablation studies analyzing both model design and
rendering performance. Quantitative results are reported using image quality metrics and frame
times. Image quality is evaluated in PyTorch by comparing predicted images against a held-out
test set. This setup ensures pixel-perfect alignment between predictions and ground truth, which
would be difficult to achieve within our Vulkan renderer. Consequently, the reported image metrics
isolate the performance of the neural model and do not include errors introduced by rasterization
or mesh approximation.
Since the ground-truth data are path-traced HDR images (OpenEXR), we initially considered
HDR-specific metrics such as mPSNR [Munkberg et al. 2006] and FLIP-HDR [Andersson et al. 2021].
However, these metrics rely on multiple exposure levels, which in our case introduced instability
due to over- and under-exposed samples. Instead, we apply a fixed exposure and clamp values to
[0, 1], after which we report PSNR and FLIP-LDR [Andersson et al. 2020]. For FLIP, inferred images
are composited over a black background to account for the lack of alpha support.
Performance is measured in our Vulkan renderer using GPU timestamps to isolate different
pipeline stages. All frame times are reported in milliseconds with two significant digits. Experiments
were conducted on a system with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 GPU and an AMD Ryzen 9 5950X
CPU (Windows 11, NVIDIA GPU driver version 581.80, Vulkan 1.4.328).
4.1
Ablation Study
We evaluate a range of MLP architectures, varying both depth (2–4 hidden layers) and width
(16–512 neurons). Results are summarized in Table 2. Architectures are denoted as 𝐻 × 𝑁, where 𝐻
is the number of hidden layers and 𝑁 the number of neurons per layer. The triplane encoding is
kept fixed across all experiments (resolution 512 × 512, 8 features).
We observe that increasing model capacity improves image quality, although with diminishing
returns beyond 4 × 128. In particular, the jump from 4 × 256 to 4 × 512 yields negligible improvement
while incurring a substantial performance cost. Performance is strongly influenced by both network
size and mesh complexity. Larger MLPs introduce significant register pressure, leading to sharp
increases in frame time beyond 128 neurons. Additionally, mesh resolution has a major impact on
performance, with high-poly meshes consistently dominating rendering cost.
Based on this trade-off, the 4×64 architecture provides the best balance between quality (22.315 dB
PSNR) and performance (3.7 ms), and is used in subsequent experiments. Compared to RNA [Mullia
et al. 2024], we remove the view direction from the model inputs. Empirically, this leads to slightly
improved results (22.315 dB vs. 22.141 dB for 4 × 64) and reduced model complexity, suggesting
that an explicit view direction input is less critical for cloud appearance in our setting. However,
because of the way it is calculated, the thickness parameter still retains view dependency.

4.1.1 Thickness. We evaluate the impact of the proposed thickness parameter, which distinguishes
our model from RNA. Results are shown in Table 3. Including thickness significantly improves image
quality across all tested architectures (e.g., from 18.133 dB to 22.315 dB for 4 × 64), demonstrating
its importance for capturing volumetric effects. Without thickness, the model fails to correctly
account for accumulated density along the view direction, leading to inconsistent transparency
across viewpoints.
This behavior is illustrated in Figure 4, where the model without thickness produces erroneous
semi-transparent regions that persist across views. These artefacts manifest as incorrect dark bands
and reduced opacity in dense regions. Although thickness improves results substantially, we observe
that opacity does not saturate sufficiently toward the cloud interior. This leads to visible contouring
artefacts when compositing with the background. To address this, we apply a smoothstep function
to the thickness parameter prior to inference. As shown in Figure 5, this results in better opacity
behavior and improved visual consistency.
4.1.2 Visibility. Following RNA [Mullia et al. 2024], we evaluate a visibility mechanism that predicts
separate RGB outputs for directly and indirectly lit regions. Unlike RNA, our visibility test does
not rely on ray tracing but is adapted to our rasterization-based pipeline. Without visibility, the
model struggles to reproduce self-shadowing effects, resulting in overly smooth shading and loss of
fine details. Introducing visibility improves the reconstruction of shadowed regions and enhances
local contrast, as shown in Figure 6. Quantitatively, the inclusion of visibility yields a modest
improvement (22.315 dB vs. 22.134 dB for 4 × 64), but the qualitative gains are more pronounced.
Some failure cases remain, particularly in regions with soft shadow transitions, indicating limitations
of the binary visibility formulation for highly diffuse media.

4.2
Triplane Encoding
Consistent with RNA, the triplane encoding captures geometric features of the cloud. However, we
observe that high-frequency components in the triplane representation can introduce noise in the
final rendering. To mitigate this, we apply filtering to the triplane features using mipmapping. This
allows efficient, hardware-supported control over feature smoothness during rendering.
As shown in Figure 7, increasing the mipmap level reduces high-frequency artefacts and produces
visually smoother results. While this filtering decreases PSNR (20.031 dB vs. 22.315 dB for 4 × 64),
the perceptual quality improves, highlighting a mismatch between standard metrics and perceived
realism. This observation aligns with prior work [Sun et al. 2024], suggesting that controlled
smoothing of learned features can improve visual quality.
4.3
Final Rendering
Figure 8 and 9 present real-time renderings under varying lighting and viewing conditions. These
results use the 4 × 64 model, mipmap level 4 for the triplane encoding, and smoothed thickness. Our
method produces volumetric-looking clouds with realistic lighting while maintaining real-time
performance. Notably, the approach avoids volumetric integration entirely, yet retains key visual
characteristics such as soft self-shadowing and view-dependent appearance. Additional results,
including low-polygon meshes and dynamic sequences, are provided in the supplemental material.

For the triplane encoding, we observe a discrepancy between quantitative metrics and perceived
image quality. While smoothing the triplane features reduces PSNR, it improves visual coherence
by suppressing high-frequency artefacts. This suggests that standard pixel-wise loss functions
are insufficient for capturing perceptual quality in this setting, motivating the use of perceptual
or frequency-aware losses in future work. The observed noise in the triplane representation can
be attributed to feature collisions: multiple points along a viewing direction may project to the
same coordinates in a given plane (e.g., the XY plane), forcing the network to encode competing
information in shared features. This ambiguity introduces high-frequency artefacts in the learned
representation. Potential solutions include increasing feature dimensionality, adopting alternative
encodings, or incorporating view-dependent features.
Although our method targets rasterization-based rendering, it can be extended to ray tracing. The
position input is directly available from primary intersections, while thickness could be calculated
by tracking entry and exit points along rays (e.g., via any-hit shaders). Similarly, visibility could
be computed using ray tracing; however, for clouds, a soft visibility formulation would likely be
more appropriate than the binary approach used in RNA, suggesting the integration of soft shadow
techniques.
5.1
Limitations and Future Work
A primary limitation of our approach is the dependence on mesh complexity. High triangle counts
significantly impact performance, as demonstrated in our results. While we show that lowerresolution meshes can be used at runtime without retraining, further work is needed to optimize
asset generation with performance constraints in mind.
We also observe artefacts such as sharp edges and seams in certain regions. These can arise from
discontinuities in the visibility blending between 𝑐𝐷 and 𝑐𝐼 , as well as from abrupt changes in the
triplane encoding. Addressing these issues may require improved blending strategies or smoother
feature representations.
The thickness as an approximation for the accumulated density along the viewing direction
assumes homogeneous density. This is a limitation currently. Also, the thickness is the geometrical
distance from the first surface to the last surface, which can lead to some failure cases. For instance,
if there are two small sections of the volume located far apart from each other, but aligned with the
view direction they are overlapping. Then the calculated thickness would still be high, even though
the accumulated density likely should be low.
Our training pipeline is constrained by the lack of access to the modified Blender Cycles renderer
used in RNA [Mullia et al. 2024]. As a result, we rely on a less efficient data generation process,
requiring a larger dataset and limiting control over per-pixel lighting variation. Improving data
efficiency or enabling more flexible rendering control would be beneficial.
Finally, our evaluation is limited to a single static cloud asset. While extending the approach
to multiple assets is straightforward, generating the required path-traced training data is timeconsuming. Future work could explore more scalable data generation, as well as extensions to
dynamic or continuous cloudscapes, enabling temporally coherent neural rendering of evolving
volumetric phenomena.
6
Conclusions
In this work, we presented a neural shading approach for rendering volumetric clouds in real
time without requiring explicit volumetric rendering. Our method demonstrates that volumetric
appearance can be effectively approximated on mesh geometry, enabling efficient rendering while
