Adaptive Sampling
What does Adaptive Sampling Do?
Adaptive sampling is an intelligent rendering optimization technique that dynamically adjusts the number of samples per pixel based on the complexity and noise levels in different areas of the image.
Instead of using a fixed number of samples for every pixel (like traditional brute-force sampling), adaptive sampling analyzes each pixel's convergence and allocates more computational resources where they're needed most.
How It Works:
Initial Sampling: The renderer starts with a minimum number of samples per pixel across the entire image.
Variance Analysis: After each sampling pass, the algorithm calculates the statistical variance or noise level for each pixel. This is typically done by comparing the current pixel value against previous samples.
Threshold Testing: Each pixel's noise level is compared against a user-defined threshold. If the noise is below the threshold, the pixel is considered "converged" and sampling stops for that pixel.
Selective Refinement: Pixels that haven't converged continue to receive additional samples, up to a maximum sample limit.
The algorithm focuses computational effort on noisy areas like:
Complex reflections and refractions
Caustics and indirect lighting
Motion blur and depth of field edges
Areas with high geometric detail
Convergence Criteria:
The algorithm uses statistical measures like:
Standard deviation of sample values
Relative error between sample batches
Luminance variance thresholds
Benefits:
Dramatically reduced render times (often 2-10x faster)
Automatic quality optimization without user intervention
Clean results in difficult lighting scenarios
Efficient resource allocation
Implementation Details:
The algorithm typically uses progressive sampling patterns (like blue noise or stratified sampling) and can work across multiple render buffers simultaneously, analyzing not just color but also normal maps, depth, and other render passes to make informed decisions about where additional sampling is needed.
This approach makes physically-based rendering practical by avoiding wasted computation on areas that don't need high sample counts.

Filter Radius
In adaptive sampling, the filter radius serves several crucial functions for determining sampling convergence and quality assessment:
Neighborhood Analysis: The filter radius defines the area around each pixel that the algorithm examines when calculating variance and noise metrics. Instead of looking at a single pixel in isolation, it considers a small neighborhood of surrounding pixels to make more informed sampling decisions.
Variance Calculation: The filter radius determines how the algorithm calculates statistical variance for convergence testing. A larger radius means the variance calculation includes more neighboring pixels, which can provide a more stable assessment of whether an area has converged, but may miss fine details that need more sampling.
Noise Detection: The filter helps distinguish between actual scene detail and rendering noise. By analyzing pixel values within the filter radius, the algorithm can better determine whether variations are due to legitimate scene features (edges, textures, lighting changes) or sampling noise that needs more refinement.
Edge Preservation: The filter radius affects how the algorithm handles edges and fine details. A smaller radius is more sensitive to local variations and will continue sampling longer at sharp edges and fine details. A larger radius may stop sampling too early near important edges, leading to aliasing.
Sample Redistribution: Some adaptive sampling implementations use the filter radius to determine how to redistribute samples spatially. Areas within the filter radius of high-variance pixels may receive additional samples even if they individually appear converged.
Filtering Integration: The filter radius often works in conjunction with the final image reconstruction filter, helping ensure that the adaptive sampling decisions are compatible with how the final image will be filtered and anti-aliased.
Typical Values: Filter radii are usually small (0.5 to 2.0 pixels) to maintain detail sensitivity while providing enough neighborhood context for stable variance calculations.
The filter radius essentially balances between sampling efficiency and detail preservation in the adaptive sampling algorithm.
Sample Weighting
Sample weighting in adaptive sampling is a technique that assigns different importance values to individual samples based on various factors, allowing the renderer to make more informed decisions about sample distribution and convergence.
Instead of treating all samples equally, the algorithm assigns weights that reflect each sample's contribution to the final pixel value and its reliability for convergence assessment.
Weighting Factors:
Temporal Weighting: More recent samples typically receive higher weights than older ones, as they represent the most current estimate of the pixel's true value. This helps the algorithm respond quickly to convergence.
Spatial Weighting: Samples are weighted based on their distance from the pixel center, often using filter kernels like Gaussian, Mitchell, or box filters. Samples closer to the pixel center get higher weights.
Quality Weighting: Samples that contribute to areas with higher variance or complexity may receive different weights. For example, samples from difficult lighting conditions (caustics, indirect illumination) might be weighted differently than samples from simple direct lighting.
Variance-Based Weighting: Samples that contribute to high-variance pixels may be weighted more heavily in convergence calculations, ensuring that noisy areas continue to receive attention.
Applications in Convergence:
Weighted Variance Calculation: When determining if a pixel has converged, the algorithm uses weighted statistics rather than simple averages. This provides a more accurate assessment of actual noise levels.
Sample History: The weighting system maintains a history of sample contributions, allowing the algorithm to make better decisions about when to stop sampling based on the weighted contribution of new samples.
Filter Integration: Sample weights work with reconstruction filters to ensure that the adaptive sampling decisions align with how the final image will be filtered and anti-aliased.
Importance Sampling: Weights can be used to bias sampling toward more important light paths, improving convergence rates for complex lighting scenarios.
Benefits: Sample weighting makes adaptive sampling more robust and efficient by ensuring that convergence decisions are based on statistically meaningful data rather than treating all samples equally, leading to better quality-to-time ratios in the final render.
How does Adaptive Sampling work with Reconstruction Filters?
Adaptive sampling has a complex relationship with reconstruction filters because it creates irregular sampling patterns that traditional filters weren't designed to handle. Here's how they interact:
Non-Uniform Sample Distribution: Traditional reconstruction filters assume uniform sample spacing across pixels. Adaptive sampling breaks this assumption by creating areas with vastly different sample densities - some pixels might have 4 samples while neighboring pixels have 64 samples.
Filter Kernel Complications: Standard reconstruction filters (Mitchell, Lanczos, Gaussian, etc.) use fixed kernel sizes that assume consistent sample spacing. When adaptive sampling creates irregular patterns, the filter may:
Under-sample in low-density areas, causing aliasing
Over-blur in high-density areas, losing detail
Create visible boundaries between differently-sampled regions
Weighted Filtering: Modern implementations address this by incorporating sample weights into the reconstruction process. The filter considers not just the sample values but also their confidence levels and density when computing the final pixel value.
Multi-Resolution Challenges: Adaptive sampling essentially creates a multi-resolution sampling grid within the same image. Reconstruction filters must handle this gracefully to avoid artifacts like:
Visible "sampling boundaries" where different sample densities meet
Inconsistent blur characteristics across the image
Moiré patterns from interference between sampling and filtering
Filter Adaptation Strategies:
Density-Aware Filtering: Some systems adjust the filter kernel size based on local sample density, using smaller kernels in high-density areas and larger ones in sparse areas.
Variance-Guided Filtering: The filter strength can be modulated by the local variance - areas that needed more samples (high variance) may use different filtering characteristics than converged areas.
Hierarchical Filtering: Some implementations use multi-scale filtering approaches that can handle the irregular sampling patterns more gracefully.
Quality vs. Performance Trade-offs: The interaction between adaptive sampling and reconstruction filtering requires careful tuning. Aggressive adaptive sampling can speed up rendering but may require more sophisticated (and slower) reconstruction filters to maintain image quality, potentially negating some performance gains.
This is why adaptive sampling implementations often include specialized reconstruction methods rather than relying solely on traditional filtering approaches.
