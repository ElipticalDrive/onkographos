Darkest Skies: Where You Can Still See the Milky Way
A Sky Glow Model of the World and the United Kingdom from VIIRS
Night Lights, Checked Against Citizen Sky Meter Readings

Abstract
Artificial light scattered in the atmosphere brightens the night sky and hides the Milky Way
from much of humanity. I build a simple, open sky glow model from the Earth Observation
Group’s annual VIIRS night lights for 2023, read for all 246 countries from WorldPop’s harmonised 3 arc second files, and use it to map artificial zenith brightness for the world at 2.5
arc minutes and for the United Kingdom at 30 arc seconds. Each lit cell’s light spreads with
a power of distance; the exponent and the scale are fitted to 5,775 Globe at Night Sky Quality
Meter readings from 2019 to 2025, and in spatial cross-validation the model misses a typical
reading by 0.49 magnitudes per square arcsecond (correlation 0.79). With WorldPop’s 2023
population and the thresholds of the 2016 World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness, 52%
of the world’s people live where artificial glow hides the Milky Way, against 36% in the atlas
for 2014; the two agree closely on the ranking of 148 countries (Spearman’s ρ = 0.94). In the
United Kingdom the share is 85% of people but only 20% of land. The darkest areas are the
Scottish islands and Highlands; the nearest Milky Way sky is 35 km from central London. Radiance seen from space fell 13.5% over Great Britain between 2015 and 2023, a change that the
sensor’s blindness to blue LED light probably exaggerates.
Keywords: light pollution; night sky brightness; sky glow; Milky Way; VIIRS; night lights;
Globe at Night; Sky Quality Meter; United Kingdom; data visualisation
1 Introduction
Away from towns, on a clear night with no Moon, the Milky Way is the brightest diffuse feature of
the sky. It fades quickly once artificial light is added: light leaving streets and buildings is scattered
back down by air molecules and aerosols and raises the brightness of the whole sky, so that the
faint, extended glow of the Galaxy no longer stands out. The World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky
Brightness (Falchi et al. 2016) put numbers on this for the whole planet. Using satellite night
lights from 2014, a radiative transfer model of how light propagates in the atmosphere (Cinzano
and Falchi 2012) and calibration against sky brightness measurements, it found that more than a
third of humanity cannot see the Milky Way from where they live.
Ten years on, the question is worth asking again with open, current data, and at a finer scale for
one country. This note does three things. It builds a deliberately simple sky glow model from the
2023 VIIRS annual night lights, so that every step can be rerun from open files; it tests the model
against thousands of hand-held sky meter readings collected by volunteers; and it uses the model
to answer plain questions for the world and the United Kingdom: who can still see the Milky Way
from home, where the darkest places are, and how far city dwellers must go for a dark sky. The
results are published with an interactive map at https://maps.tristanhotham.com/dark-skies/.

2 Data
2.1 Night lights
The Earth Observation Group (Colorado School of Mines) produces annual composites of the
VIIRS Day/Night Band from cloud-free monthly averages, with background noise, gas flares and
volcanoes removed (VNL version 2; Elvidge et al. 2021). The sensor passes at about 1:30 am
local time and measures radiance in nW/cm2 /sr between roughly 500 and 900 nm. WorldPop
republishes these composites per country, resampled to 3 arc seconds, as part of its harmonised
covariates (Woods et al. 2024), under CC BY 4.0. I read all 246 country files for 2023 in a web
browser and reduced them there to a world grid of 2.5 arc minutes (the mean radiance of the
valid pixels in each cell and their number), and the files for the United Kingdom, Ireland and the
Isle of Man for every year from 2015 to 2023 to a grid of 30 arc seconds.
2.2 Population
People are taken from WorldPop’s 2023 population grid at 30 arc seconds (Global2 release R2025A,
constrained to built-up areas; Bondarenko et al. 2025), summed to the two model grids. The world
total is 8.06 billion.
2.3 Sky meter readings
Globe at Night, run by NOIRLab, has collected observations of the night sky from volunteers since
2006 and publishes them under CC BY 4.0. Some observers report a reading from a Sky Quality
Meter, a hand-held photometer that gives the brightness of the sky overhead in magnitudes per
square arcsecond. I kept readings between 15 and 22.4 magnitudes made under a sky reported
as clear, between 8 pm and 4 am local time, with valid coordinates: 30,237 readings from 2006 to
2025, of which 6,653 were made in 2019 to 2025 with the standard Globe at Night form.
2.4 Places and boundaries
Dark Sky Places are taken from the table in the English Wikipedia article “Dark-sky preserve” (CC
BY-SA 4.0), placed with the coordinates of each place’s own article (171 of 220), plus Wikidata
items of dark sky place classes not in the table. Towns and villages come from OS Open Names
for Great Britain and GeoNames for Northern Ireland; cities from GeoNames. Country outlines
are Natural Earth; UK counties, Scottish council areas and Northern Ireland districts come from
Oﬀice for National Statistics boundaries.
2.5 Data not used
The atlas of Falchi et al. (2016) is distributed by GFZ Data Services under a non-commercial
licence, and its download carries a note that further distribution is prohibited; I therefore use only
the figures printed in the paper (its Tables 1 and 2) for comparison. I found no open licence for
the Night Blight maps of England published by CPRE in 2016, and did not use them.

3 Methods
3.1 A kernel model of sky glow
For each lit cell j, upward light Ej is its mean radiance times its lit area. The artificial brightness
of the sky overhead at a place x is

where dxj is the distance in km, d0 = 1 km keeps the kernel finite next to a source, and the sum
runs over all cells within 250 km. This is the power law of distance behind Walker’s (1977) rule of
thumb, not a radiative transfer model: it ignores terrain, altitude, aerosols, the angular emission
of lamps and their colour. The convolution is computed with fast Fourier transforms in latitude
bands of two degrees, with the east to west cell width of the band’s central latitude. On the 2.5
arc minute grid the kernel between two cells is the mean over 5 × 5 sub-points of each, so that a
cell’s value is its average brightness. For the UK grid, light from cells outside the United Kingdom,
Ireland and the Isle of Man is convolved on the world grid and added.
3.2 Calibration
The exponent α (tried at 2.0, 2.3, 2.6 and 2.9), an optional exponential fall-off (none, 50 or 100 km)
and the scale k were fitted to the Globe at Night readings of 2019 to 2025. Model brightness is
turned into a total sky brightness by adding the natural sky of 174 µcd/m2 (22.0 mag/arcsec2 , as
in Falchi et al. 2016). I fitted k by least absolute error in magnitudes, which resists the outliers that
hand-held readings contain (thin cloud, moonlight, a nearby lamp), and only to readings darker
than 18.0 magnitudes, because cells of 4.6 km cannot represent the brightest city-centre streets.
The best form was chosen by five-fold spatial cross-validation in blocks of ten degrees.
3.3 Thresholds and exposure
Sky classes follow Falchi et al. (2016): artificial brightness of at most 1.74 µcd/m2 (1% of the
natural sky) is pristine; above 13.9 (8%) the sky is light polluted; above 87 (half the natural sky);
above 688 the Milky Way is hidden; and above 3,000 the eye cannot fully adapt to the dark. People
and land are counted in each class, on the 2.5 arc minute grid for countries and on the 30 arc
second grid for the United Kingdom.
3.4 Distances and change
For every city of a million or more people (GeoNames points, merging places within 25 km of a
larger one), I found the nearest land cell below the Milky Way threshold, and the nearest below
the light pollution threshold. For UK cities the search uses the 1 km grid. To describe change, the
UK model was run with the lights of 2015 and of 2023 against the same 2023 population.

4 Results
4.1 How well the model fits
The best form has α = 2.3 and no extra fall-off with distance. In cross-validation it misses readings
darker than 18.0 magnitudes by 0.49 magnitudes on average (mean bias 0.09; root mean square
0.75; correlation 0.79; Figure 1). Over all readings, including the bright ones, the root mean square
error is 1.08: the model badly underestimates the brightest city centres. Readings from 2012 to
2018, which were not used in the fit, are matched about as well (correlation 0.75). On the 72
readings inside the United Kingdom, the 1 km model misses by 0.39 magnitudes (correlation
0.87).
4.2 The world
By the model, 52% of the world’s people (about 4.2 billion) live under skies that hide the Milky
Way, 93% under light polluted skies and 26% where the eye cannot fully adapt to the dark (Figure
2). The Milky Way is hidden over 3% of the land between 75° N and 60° S, and 40% of that land is
light polluted. The atlas of Falchi et al. (2016) gave 36%, 83% and 14% of people and 22% of land.
Country rankings agree closely with the atlas (Figure 3, Table 1): Spearman’s ρ = 0.94 across 148
countries of a million or more people, with my shares higher by 11 percentage points on average.
In 82 of 160 such countries most people cannot see the Milky Way from home, and in 16 more
than nine in ten cannot, among them Hong Kong, Singapore, the Gulf states, Israel and Egypt.
In Eritrea, the Central African Republic, South Sudan, Sierra Leone and Ethiopia nearly everyone
can. Ukraine is one of the few large countries where the 2023 figure is well below the atlas (19%
against 30%).

4.3 The United Kingdom
At 1 km, 85% of the United Kingdom’s people live under a Milky Way hiding sky, about 57.7
million, but only 20% of its land does (Figure 4, Table 2). The same population on the 4.6 km
world grid gives 82%, a measure of how much the coarse grid understates exposure. The atlas
gave 77% of people for 2014.
The darkest areas, by median sky over their land, are the Western Isles (Na h-Eileanan Siar, 21.98
mag/arcsec2 ), Highland, Orkney, Shetland and Argyll and Bute; in England they are Northumberland (21.59), Devon, Cumbria, Cornwall and Dorset. The brightest are Greater London (18.18),
Glasgow City and the West Midlands. In 183 of 359 local authority areas fewer than one resident
in a hundred can see the Milky Way from home. The Milky Way should be visible from the centre
of 3% of cities in OS Open Names, 37% of towns, 67% of villages and 82% of hamlets. The town
with the darkest sky is Hugh Town in the Isles of Scilly (21.82). From central London the nearest
Milky Way sky is 35 km away, near Toys Hill in Kent; from Birmingham 25 km, Manchester 22 km
and Glasgow 15 km.
All 17 UK Dark Sky Places that could be placed lie below the Milky Way threshold in the model,
from the Isle of Coll (21.95) to Moore’s Reserve in the South Downs (20.64). Worldwide, 94% of
placed Dark Sky Places do.
4.4 Change in the UK, 2015 to 2023
Total VIIRS radiance over Great Britain fell by 13.5% between 2015 and 2023, while over Ireland
it rose by 10.3% (Figure 6). The largest falls were in Tyne and Wear (43.8%), Inverclyde and
Northumberland; radiance rose in 19 of 98 areas, most in the Western Isles and Cornwall. With
2015 lights the model puts 87% of UK residents under a Milky Way hiding sky, with 2023 lights
85%; 66% of land has a sky at least 5% dimmer and 15% at least 5% brighter.
5 Discussion
A model this simple reproduces most of the variation in measured sky brightness outside city
centres, and its country ranking is close to that of a far more elaborate atlas. Its level is higher.
Several differences pull in that direction: lights from 2023 rather than 2014; a population grid constrained to built-up areas, which places people under their town’s lights; and calibration readings

The fall in the UK’s radiance seen from space is consistent with the wholesale replacement of
orange sodium street lamps with white LEDs, which are often dimmer and better shielded. But
VIIRS sees little light below 500 nm, where white LEDs emit much of their energy and where light
scatters most strongly in the air, so a street can look darker from space and brighter to the eye.
Volunteers’ counts of visible stars point the other way: Kyba et al. (2023) found from Globe at
Night reports that the visibility of stars fell rapidly between 2011 and 2022, equivalent to a sky
brightening of about 10% a year. The satellite record should therefore not be read as evidence that
British skies have become darker.

6 Limitations
The kernel ignores terrain shading, altitude, air clarity, the angle and colour of emission, snow
and cloud. The calibration readings are concentrated in North America and Europe and are noisy;
distinctions near natural darkness (the 1% and 8% thresholds) are within that noise, so the Milky
Way threshold carries most weight here. The model underestimates the brightest city centres; I
report them as “never truly dark” rather than with precise values. Kosovo has no light file of its
own in the WorldPop set and is excluded from country results. Dark Sky Places are points at the
centres of large areas.
7 Conclusion
Open satellite lights, an open population grid, citizen sky meter readings and a two-parameter
model are enough to map where the Milky Way can still be seen, to within about half a magnitude outside city centres. By this model about half of the world’s people and 85% of the United
Kingdom’s cannot see it from home, though in the UK it remains visible over 80% of the land, and
from most large British cities a sky that shows it lies within an hour’s drive.
