# Opacity-Based Occlusion Culling for 3D Gaussian Splatting

Matteo Giannone1 [,](https://orcid.org/0009-0005-1689-7398) Mohamed Ibrahim[1](https://orcid.org/0000-0002-3559-3761) and Yang Liu[1](https://orcid.org/0009-0003-7469-8608)

1Huawei Canada

### Abstract

We present an occlusion culling pipeline for 3DGS that reduces rendering cost while preserving visual fidelity. Our two-stage framework combines a coarse stage—which uses an opacity volume and hierarchical occlusion maps to cull Gaussians invisible from the current viewpoint—with a fine stage that partitions Gaussians into depth-ordered batches and discards those projecting to fully opaque pixels. Early culling prior to sorting reduces the workload for downstream sorting, projection, and blending, cutting per-frame computation and memory bandwidth. The pipeline scales to millions of Gaussians, making it practical for real-time 3DGS rendering.

## 1. Introduction

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [KKLD23] represents scenes as collections of anisotropic Gaussian ellipsoids optimized to match a set of input images, and has gained popularity for its real-time rendering speed and high visual quality. However, the blending stage remains a major bottleneck, consuming over 70% of rendering time [LKLK25], largely because many Gaussians that contribute negligibly to the final image are still transformed, sorted, and passed to the blending stage. Culling such Gaussians early ideally before sorting—offers significant performance headroom. We propose a hierarchical occlusion culling method based on an opacity volume representation of the scene, enabling substantial rendering efficiency gains for 3DGS pipelines.

## 2. Related Work

Methods optimizing 3DGS through the use of occlusion information is fairly uncommon. OccluGaussian [LTL∗ 25] introduces an occlusion-aware scene division strategy that clusters cameras and partitions large environments to avoid occlusion-agnostic regioning, improving both reconstruction quality and rendering scalability. ODA-GS [LYH∗ 24] focuses on the optimization stage, incorporating occlusion- and distortion-aware modules that selectively accumulate gradients from visible Gaussians, preventing gradient dilution in dense, overlapping scenes. Both approaches reduce wasted computation: OccluGaussian by limiting region scope and camera correlation, and ODA-GS by restricting gradient contributions to non-occluded elements. A complementary line of work adopts probabilistic, density-based culling for extremely large particle datasets [IRR∗ 22], featuring the use of a coarse 3D density volume and a hierarchical depth-confidence pyramid.

## 3. Method

Our method introduces a once-per-scene preprocessing step that constructs the data structures required by two subsequent culling stages: a coarse stage applied before sorting, and a fine stage integrated into fragment processing.

The pre-processing step constructs a view-independent opacity volume that encloses all Gaussians in the scene, storing the accumulated opacity of all Gaussians within each voxel. For each voxel, we sample multiple points and compute, for each overlapping Gaussian, an opacity value weighted by its density across those points. The final value stored in each voxel is 1−∏i (1−α*i*), where α*i* is the density-weighted opacity of Gaussian i.

With the opacity volume completed, we use its information to create a Chebyshev distance map [DK19] which will be used to improve the speed of the coarse culling stage. Furthermore, we convert the volume to a sparse 3D texture to reduce VRAM usage by only storing 323 voxel regions if at least one voxel within contains an opacity greater than 0.01.

Coarse culling begins by rendering the opacity volume from the current viewpoint to produce a depth map. A ray is cast from the camera through each texel, traversing the opacity volume in steps; at each step, the sampled opacity is clamped to 0.99 to prevent numerical saturation, and the accumulated opacity is updated via the standard over-operator: accumulated+ = opacity ∗ (1.0 − accumulated). Traversal terminates when the accumulated opacity exceeds a threshold α*t* = 0.999999, at which point the current depth is written to the depth map. Empty regions are skipped using the distance map from the preprocessing step, accelerating traversal. Fine culling operates at the fragment processing stage. Gaussians are rendered in multiple batches that respect the sorting order (all Gaussians in a given batch lie in front of those in subsequent batches). This allows the accumulated opacity from a batch's output color attachment to be used to construct a fragment mask in the depth attachment, enabling fragment-level culling via the early depth test and thereby reducing both fragment shader invocations and blending computations. Batch boundaries are determined using the depth texture produced by the coarse culling stage: we construct a depth histogram and select depths at specific quantiles to partition the Gaussians into batches. In practice, we find that rendering in 2 batches (using a 16-bin histogram and splitting at the first quartile) yields meaningful performance gains with minimal overhead.

#### 4. Results

The performance gains of our culling method are most pronounced in scenes with significant occlusion. In scenes with little occlusion, the overhead of computing occlusion may result in a slight reduction in FPS; however, the method remains accurate and does not cull Gaussians that should remain visible.

For a comprehensive evaluation, Table 1 reports performance across two categories of scenes: those with few redundant Gaussians (MipNeRF360 [BMV∗ 22]), and those with many redundant Gaussians, namely CombinedInteriorGS—constructed by merging scenes 200, 640, 791, and 950 from the InteriorGS [SRT25] dataset into a single scene—and Classbuilding A [LTL∗ 25]. All experiments are conducted on a custom 3DGS renderer based on VKGS [Par24]. We average the results across all scenes for the MipNerf360 dataset. The results demonstrate that our method effectively culls occluded Gaussians without degrading image quality, yielding substantial FPS improvements in occlusion-heavy scenes, while incurring a modest FPS reduction in scenes with little occlusion. Regarding memory overhead, the opacity volume occupies less than 20 MB of VRAM as a 2563 sparse volume for the MipNeRF360 scenes and CombinedInteriorGS, and approximately 100 MB as a 5123 sparse volume for the Classbuilding A dataset.

#### 5. Conclusions and future work

In this work, we present an occlusion culling framework for 3DGS that improves rendering efficiency without requiring any modifications to pre-existing datasets. Our method significantly improves rendering speeds for scenes with a large number of occluded Gaussians while preserving image quality, at the cost of increased VRAM usage. Several directions remain open for future work. Alternative scene opacity representations could reduce VRAM consumption or more efficiently construct level 0 of the HOM. Furthermore, fitting the opacity volume to a subset of the scene (rather than the scene as a whole) could better utilize volume resolution, as large Gaussians near the scene boundaries often waste it.
