Abstract
Multi-view tomographic background-oriented Schlieren (TBOS) has become an
important optical technique for measuring 3D density-varying flow fields. Reconstructing the 3D density field from TBOS measurements is a typical inverse
problem, which becomes severely ill-posed with a limited number of views. Recent
studies have shown that implicit neural representation (INR) exhibits significant
advantages over voxel-based reconstruction algorithms for TBOS under such conditions. However, systematic parametric studies of INR remain scarce and the
conservative sampling strategies employed during INR training produce redundant sampling points and extra network overhead, reducing both efficiency and
accuracy. Based on the neural deflection field (NeDF) developed in our previous
work, a sequential parametric study of the network is carried out using synthetic
BOS images, revealing that the network depth dominates the reconstruction
1
accuracy and that the positional encoding lowers the network scale required to
reach the attainable accuracy rather than raising its upper limit. On this basis,
to improve network training efficiency and reconstruction quality, a lightweight
design with an adaptive sampling strategy is developed, which uses a single network to progressively concentrate the sampling points toward the key flow regions
as training proceeds. Finally, a turbulent helium jet experiment is conducted, in
which the proposed lightweight NeDF achieves the best cross-view generalization
among all the methods compared. This study provides an effective solution for
coping with this ill-posed reconstruction challenge and achieving high-accuracy,
high-efficiency TBOS flow diagnostics.

1 Introduction
In high-speed aerodynamics and combustion science, accurately characterizing the
spatial structure and transient behavior of 3D density-varying flows is essential for
revealing the underlying physical mechanisms and for advancing engineering design.
Typical examples include the shock/boundary layer interaction around hypersonic
vehicles (Wu et al. 2019; Takahashi et al. 2024) and the strongly non-uniform temperature and density structures in turbulent flames, whose evolution directly governs
combustion efficiency and instability (Grauer et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2020). There is
therefore a pressing need for non-intrusive optical diagnostics that combine high spatiotemporal resolution with quantitative 3D density measurement (Galley et al. 2011;
Panda and Seasholtz 2002).
The Gladstone–Dale relation, n = 1 + Kρ, establishes a linear link between the
refractive index n of a fluid medium and its density ρ, where K is the Gladstone–Dale
constant, so that optical methods based on refractive index variation provide an effective route to diagnosing 3D density-varying flows. Compared with classical Schlieren
techniques (Alvarez-Herrera et al. 2009; Narayan et al. 2018), the background-oriented
Schlieren (BOS) technique proposed around the year 2000 (Dalziel et al. 2000; Richard
and Raffel 2001; Meier 2002) characterizes the refractive index field of a non-uniform
flow through light deflection, and has become an important tool for the visualization
and quantitative measurement of density-varying flows due to its simple setup, flexible field of view, and ease of calibration (Hargather and Settles 2012; Mizukaki et al.
2014; Fisher et al. 2019). Combined with axisymmetric reconstruction tools such as
the Abel inversion, single-view BOS has been applied to axisymmetric jets (Tan et al.
2015; Xiong et al. 2020) and flames (Choudhury and Joarder 2022). For more general 3D flows, BOS is combined with computed tomography to form the tomographic
BOS (TBOS) technique, achieving quantitative reconstruction of the 3D density field
(Atcheson et al. 2008; Nicolas et al. 2016).
Under ideal conditions with a sufficient camera array, TBOS can accurately reconstruct the 3D instantaneous density field. In typical application scenarios such as
aero-engine combustors and hypersonic wind tunnels, however, harsh experimental
2
conditions restrict TBOS measurements to a limited number of views (Li et al. 2024a;
Schmidt et al. 2025), making the reconstruction a severely ill-posed inverse problem
(Hansen 1998). Traditional tomographic algorithms (Gordon et al. 1970; Axelsson
1987; Kak and Slaney 2001) discretize the 3D flow into a large number of independent
voxels and invert the refractive index gradient field by solving a large system of linear equations, which becomes severely underdetermined and leads to a sharp drop in
reconstruction quality. The optimization of TBOS reconstruction algorithms has therefore attracted much attention (Jia et al. 2024; Lang et al. 2017). For example, mask
constraints (Nicolas et al. 2016) and the wavelet-based posterior support constraint
built on them (Li et al. 2024b) suppress artifacts and improve the reconstruction of
jet and swirl flame structures. Both, however, require additional processing outside
the reconstruction itself, and residual artifacts still persist.
With the rapid development of deep learning, researchers have begun to exploit the
universal approximation capability of neural networks (Hornik et al. 1989) for BOSbased flow reconstruction. Early attempts combined BOS with the physics-informed
neural network (PINN) framework, either inferring continuous velocity and pressure
fields from previously reconstructed temperature fields (Cai et al. 2021) or representing the flow with a coordinate neural network and introducing the governing equations
as constraints to reconstruct single-view supersonic flows (Molnar et al. 2023). Such
approaches, however, either depend heavily on the accuracy of the initial reconstruction or converge slowly and are difficult to train. In recent years, implicit neural
representation (INR) (Mescheder et al. 2019; Park et al. 2019) has achieved major
breakthroughs in 3D reconstruction: a neural network parameterizes the scene as a
continuous function of the spatial coordinates, so that the inverse problem is solved
directly in a continuous space, mitigating this ill-posedness.
In computer vision, Mildenhall et al. (2022) proposed the neural radiance field
(NeRF), which uses a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to map a spatial scene to its
images and applies frequency encoding to enhance the network’s ability to represent
high-frequency spatial structure. Inspired by this, He et al. (2025) proposed the neural
refractive index field (NeRIF) and achieved simultaneous reconstruction of the refractive index and the refractive index gradient of a turbulent Bunsen flame. Molnar and
Grauer (2025) extended their neural-implicit reconstruction technique (NIRT) (Molnar et al. 2024) to the temporal dimension, building a unified spatiotemporal neural
representation and achieving time-resolved TBOS reconstruction of a high-speed compressible jet. Li et al. (2024a) developed the NeDF model and achieved high-accuracy
reconstruction of the 3D refractive index gradient field of a swirl flame. These methods
use the intrinsic smoothness of the neural network as an implicit regularizer, overcome
the limitations of traditional voxel discretization, greatly alleviate the ill-posedness
of the reconstruction problem, and yield results far superior to those of voxel-based
methods. However, the key hyperparameters of these models (such as the number of
layers and nodes, the activation function, the regularization method, and the encoding
scheme) are usually chosen empirically. For example, Molnar and Grauer (2025) notes
that more complex flow fields require more complex network architectures but does
not specify how to choose them. Li et al. (2024a) and Lu et al. (2026) examined the
influence of basic network hyperparameters such as the number of layers and nodes,
3
but did not investigate the other hyperparameters in depth. Moreover, among existing
studies, only Molnar and Grauer (2025) adopted TV regularization, whereas the other
models used no explicit smoothness regularization in either synthetic or experimental
settings. Most current models also directly inherit the encoding schemes developed in
computer vision: Molnar and Grauer (2025) and He et al. (2025) adopted frequency
encoding, while Li et al. (2024a) and Lu et al. (2026) chose multiresolution hash encoding (Müller et al. 2022). The actual role of the encoding scheme, and whether it is
coupled with the network scale, remains unclear. Given the many hyperparameters
of INR, empirical exploration alone can hardly realize their full potential. A systematic parametric study is therefore needed to clarify the role of each factor in TBOS
reconstruction and to provide practical guidance for hyperparameter selection.
In INR, besides the hyperparameter settings of the network and its training process, ray rendering is also a key factor affecting the final result. NeRF uses stratified
sampling in place of deterministic integration to perform ray rendering, avoiding the
discretization error introduced by voxel grids, and it further adopts a hierarchical sampling strategy so that the sampling points concentrate more on the region of interest
during rendering, improving training efficiency and reconstruction quality. Inspired by
this, Li et al. (2024a) directly adopted these methods for network training. However,
the hierarchical sampling strategy is built on a dual-network architecture, which makes
its training process considerably more complex than that of conventional INR. Moreover, this strategy distributes sampling points across almost the entire reconstruction
space, which is inconsistent with the physical fact that the refractive index gradient
concentrates in local regions such as shear layers and shocks, producing a large number
of redundant sampling points and limiting the network’s ability to resolve fine highfrequency structures. Lu et al. (2026) instead introduced an occupancy grid (Müller
et al. 2022) outside the network to accelerate the sampling, which confines the points
to the flow region but leaves them uniformly distributed within it rather than concentrated on the shear layers that dominate the deflection. He et al. (2025), in turn,
progressively increases the number of sampling intervals along each ray as training
proceeds instead of using hierarchical refinement, but this only relieves the resource
pressure in the early training stage and leaves the distribution of the sampling points
in the later stages unresolved. Therefore, a lightweight sampling strategy tailored to
TBOS flow measurement is needed to ensure both the efficiency and the accuracy of
the training process.
Motivated by these gaps, this paper conducts a sequential parametric study of the
NeDF architecture developed by our group and proposes a single-network adaptive
sampling strategy for a lightweight design. The resulting model is then validated on
synthetic images and on a laboratory turbulent helium jet experiment. The remainder
of this paper is organized as follows. Sec. 2 describes the TBOS measurement principle,
the synthetic and experimental datasets, and the NeDF framework. Sec. 3 presents
the parametric study, the lightweight design based on adaptive sampling, and the
synthetic and experimental validation results. Sec. 4 concludes the paper.

path of Cam. 1 as an example (red line in Fig. 1): a ray travels in a straight line in
a uniform medium but deflects continuously in a non-uniform refractive index field
(green solid line in Fig. 1), and its path satisfies the differential equation:

In a TBOS experiment, each camera acquires two BOS images (Fig. 2a): a reference
image without any flow disturbance, recording the original positions of the background

The above procedure constitutes the forward model of TBOS measurement,
whereas solving for the refractive index gradient from the deflection data is its inverse
problem. Since a single view provides insufficient information for general 3D flows,
multiple cameras, two of which are marked as Cam. 1 and Cam. 2 in Fig. 1, capture the background displacement synchronously from different views, and the 3D
refractive index gradient field is then inverted with a tomographic reconstruction algorithm. In this work, the reconstructed quantity is the refractive index gradient field
itself, from which the refractive index can be recovered by standard Poisson-equation
post-processing and the density then follows from the Gladstone–Dale relation.

To evaluate the reconstruction methods studied in this work quantitatively, we obtain
the flow field of a swirl premixed flame inside an aero-engine combustor by large-eddy
simulation (LES), identical to that used in Li et al. (2024a). A high-fidelity non-linear
ray-tracing platform (Rajendran et al. 2019) is then used to mimic the optical layout
of a real experiment, ultimately generating multi-view synthetic BOS images. Following the arc-shaped arrangement sketched in Fig. 1, 11 cameras are placed uniformly
over an arc of 155◦ , with an adjacent spacing of ∆θ = 15.5◦ . The detailed parameters are listed in Table 1, where DCB and DCO denote the perpendicular distances
from the camera lens to the background board and to the center of the measurement

double-precision third-order spline spatial interpolation and a fourth-order Runge–
Kutta solver for the ray equation (Eq. 1), so as to reproduce the real BOS imaging

2.3 Experimental setup
In addition to the synthetic case, we perform a laboratory TBOS measurement of a turbulent helium jet to verify the effectiveness and robustness of the algorithm under real
experimental conditions, which introduce calibration errors and measurement noise
that synthetic data cannot reproduce. In this flow, the large molecular-weight difference between helium and the ambient air, together with turbulent entrainment and
mixing in the shear layer, produces pronounced density variation. In the experiment,
the physical sizes of the cameras, the light sources, and the retro-reflective background
boards limit the number of views that can be arranged around the flow. A ring-shaped,
uniform arrangement of 11 cameras with an adjacent spacing of about 33◦ is therefore
adopted, under which the observable views remain limited, as in the synthetic study.
Of these 11 acquired views, one is held out for validation and the remaining 10 are
used for reconstruction (Sec. 3.4). A retro-reflective background board is placed on
the optical axis of each camera at a distance DCB from the lens to ensure sufficient

brightness of the BOS images. We use 11 Hikvision MV-CS050-10GM-PRO industrial cameras with matched SA5020M-10MP fixed-focus lenses, synchronized with a
home-built short-exposure overdriven LED light source. Helium is discharged vertically from a round nozzle of exit diameter D = 8 mm. The reconstruction targets
a single instantaneous frame acquired synchronously by the 11 cameras. The other
detailed parameters are listed in Table 2.

2.4 NeDF framework
To invert the spatial distribution of the flow from the light-deflection information, one
must construct the forward model corresponding to Eq. 2 and solve the associated
8
inverse problem. Unlike classical reconstruction algorithms, which discretize the reconstruction space into voxels, NeDF represents the flow implicitly and continuously by
means of an MLP. Its solution procedure comprises the three functional blocks of the
Module panel in Fig. 4: the network block (Net ), the network-training block (Train ),
and the sampling-point refinement block (Fine ). Assembling these blocks in different ways yields the three training pipelines shown in the remaining panels of Fig. 4.
The Uniform and Hierarchical pipelines, described in this section, correspond to the
baseline INR training process and the original NeDF, respectively, while the Adaptive
pipeline implements the sampling strategy proposed in this work and is detailed in
Sec. 3.2.
Fig. 4 Modular training pipelines of NeDF under the uniform, hierarchical, and adaptive sampling
strategies, with the Net, Train and Fine blocks of the Module panel shared by the three pipelines
The Net block is shown by the blue box in Fig. 4. Its core is an MLP that maps
spatial coordinates to the refractive index gradient of the flow, Fθ (p) = ∇n, where θ
denotes the learnable network parameters, the input p is the 3D position of a point in
the flow, and the output is the refractive index gradient vector at that point, whose
components are denoted (∇n)u , u ∈ {x, y, z}. The symbol σ denotes the non-linear
activation function, and before training θ is initialized with the random-initialization
strategy appropriate to the chosen activation function (Glorot and Bengio 2010; He
et al. 2015; Sitzmann et al. 2020). PE denotes the positional encoding: owing to
spectral bias, an MLP preferentially learns low-frequency functions, so the input coordinates are first lifted to a higher-dimensional space to enhance the network’s ability
to capture high-frequency flow structure (Rahaman et al. 2019; Mildenhall et al. 2022).
Two encoding schemes are examined in this research. The analytic frequency encoding,
adopted by NeRF (Mildenhall et al. 2022) and most INR-based methods, maps each
input coordinate into a high-dimensional vector through a set of multi-scale sinusoids.
The learnable multiresolution hash encoding (Müller et al. 2022) instead interpolates
trainable feature vectors from multi-scale grids, shifting much of the representational
burden from the MLP onto the feature table, so that the MLP itself can remain shallow. Their detailed formulations can be found in the original papers. In this work,
9
the frequency encoding uses Lf = 16 frequency levels, and the hash encoding uses
Lh = 16 resolution levels with F = 2 features per level, a table size of T = 219 , and
grid resolutions from Nmin = 16 to Nmax = 524288. While hash encoding was already
adopted in the original NeDF, the network scale also enhances the representational
capacity, and whether the two are coupled has not been quantified. This is examined
in the parametric study of Sec. 3.1.
The Train block is shown by the green box in Fig. 4, with a single ray as the
basic processing unit. Hereafter, one training iteration i updates the network with
one batch of rays of fixed size drawn from a single view, and one epoch denotes a
full cycle over all training views, with one iteration per view. N sampling points are
placed uniformly along each ray and passed to the MLP, with each point randomly
jittered within its assigned interval at every iteration (i.e. stratified sampling) to avoid
overfitting to fixed sampling locations. Integrating the network outputs along the ray
by the trapezoidal rule, with the quadrature weight of each point determined by the
spacing to its neighboring points, gives the predicted deflection ε̂u . Comparing it with
the known εu over all rays of the batch yields a mean-squared-error (MSE) loss:

NeDF therefore introduces the Fine block shown by the orange box in Fig. 4.
Based on the network outputs at the N coarse points, it generates M fine points
concentrated on the flow region along each ray: a sampling probability density function
(PDF) proportional to the predicted gradient magnitude |∇n| is defined, augmented
with a small uniform offset that keeps the probability nonzero over the entire ray, and
accumulated along the ray into a cumulative distribution function (CDF), from which
the fine points are drawn by inverse-transform sampling.
As shown by the Hierarchical panel in Fig. 4, the hierarchical sampling strategy
of the original NeDF (Li et al. 2024a) adds the Fine block through a coarse–fine
dual-network structure: in each iteration, the N uniformly placed coarse points first
10
go through the same pipeline as the Uniform panel with a coarse network MLPc .
The Fine block then generates fine points from the coarse output, and the merged
coarse and fine points are passed to a fine network MLPf , the two networks jointly
computing the loss. This densifies the sampling locally and improves accuracy, but
increases model complexity and training cost.
The pipeline above involves several hyperparameters, namely the network depth
and width, the activation function, and the positional encoding scheme, whose influence on TBOS reconstruction, together with that of the noise and regularization
conditions, has not been systematically quantified. We therefore carry out a parametric study on these factors in the 2D setting, with the case settings listed in Table 3.

3 Results and discussion
This section first presents the 2D parametric study (Sec. 3.1) and the lightweight
design based on adaptive sampling with its 2D assessment (Sec. 3.2). The resulting
lightweight configuration is then validated on the 3D synthetic BOS images (Sec. 3.3)
and finally on the helium jet experiment (Sec. 3.4). Because of the inherent randomness of stochastic gradient descent and network initialization, all quantitative metrics
reported below (curves and tabulated data) are obtained from six independent training
runs, whereas the reconstructed flow-field images shown are representative single-run
results.
3.1 Parametric study
We first analyze, under the hierarchical sampling strategy, how the network scale
(number of layers and nodes) affects reconstruction performance, with the other factors fixed at the baseline settings listed in the first row of Table 3. Fig. 5 shows the
reconstruction quality, quantified by the root-mean-square error (RMSE) against the
CFD ground truth, and the training time as functions of the number of layers and
nodes. When the network is small (e.g. 2 layers, 24 nodes), the training time is shortest, but the ability to capture flow details is limited, and the accuracy is poor. As the
network grows, the reconstruction quality improves rapidly, but the training time also
increases. Beyond a certain threshold, the accuracy gain saturates and even reverses
slightly for the largest networks, an early indication of overfitting, while the training
time keeps rising, so a trade-off between performance and efficiency is required.
Further analysis shows that the number of layers is the more decisive factor. Along
the 2-layer and 11-layer slices of the two surfaces, the node count affects the accuracy
comparatively little and inflates the time mainly when the network is deep. Along the
24 - and 29 -node slices, by contrast, the layer count clearly affects both quality and time.
Therefore, when tuning hyperparameters, one should control the overall reconstruction
quality mainly through the number of layers and then fine-tune the number of nodes for
a better result. Finally, we select three representative combinations along the accuracytime trade-off, namely the smallest network, an intermediate configuration and the
configuration beyond which the accuracy gain saturates while the time keeps growing,
marked by the red circles in Fig. 5, and compare their reconstructions with the CFD
ground truth in Fig. 6a. For the TBOS reconstruction problem, when the network
is small (2 layers, 24 nodes) the model tends to learn low-frequency information and
captures only a coarse outline. As the network grows (4 layers, 26 nodes), the ability
to resolve flow details strengthens and the basic structure is recovered, though details
are insufficient. At 7 layers with 27 nodes the model approaches its performance limit
and most flow details are recovered.
Having determined the best network scale (7 layers, 27 nodes), we compare four
common activation functions (LeakyReLU, ReLU, Tanh, and Sine), corresponding to
the four cases in Fig. 6b. ReLU and LeakyReLU give very close results with a small
difference in RMSE. Their difference appears mainly in the flame center, where ReLU

Finally, we analyze the performance of two encodings (frequency encoding and multiresolution hash encoding), with no encoding as the baseline. The results are shown in
Fig. 7. Without any encoding, the reconstruction error of the shallow networks is very

the network-scale analysis above. Frequency encoding eases this requirement, as the
shallow networks give clearly lower errors and the attainable accuracy is approached
at a smaller depth than without encoding. Even so, a fairly deep network is still needed
for frequency encoding to reach its optimum. Multiresolution hash encoding almost
removes the depth requirement: with only 2 layers the network already reaches the
accuracy that the other two encodings attain only with deep networks, allowing NeDF
to be built with an extremely shallow MLP. Notably, encoding lowers the network scale
required to reach the attainable accuracy rather than raising its upper limit. For hash
encoding in particular, enlarging the network further degrades the reconstruction (Fig.
7), even though the training loss continues to decrease (not shown). This pattern, a
falling training loss accompanied by a rising reconstruction error, points to overfitting
of the deflection data by the enlarged MLP rather than to optimization difficulty.

INR-based TBOS reconstruction. The network depth should be set first according to
the adopted encoding scheme, the node count can then be fine-tuned, LeakyReLU is

the measurements are affected by noise or similar disturbances.
3.2 Lightweight design based on adaptive sampling
The lightweight design builds directly on the conclusions of the parametric study.
The network settings identified in Sec. 3.1 are inherited here, except that the network
scale is greatly reduced, since hash encoding allows a network of only two layers to
reach the attainable accuracy. With the network side settled, the remaining room for
improving the training efficiency lies in the sampling process along the rays. Although
the hierarchical strategy (Fig. 4) concentrates part of the points on the flow region, it
performs the “sample-predict-loss” process twice per iteration, roughly doubling the
number of network evaluations and the per-step cost, and its uniformly distributed
coarse points still partly fall outside the flow. We therefore embed the Fine block
directly into the base training framework, achieving coarse-to-fine densification within
a single network and structurally eliminating the dual-network overhead, as shown by
the Adaptive panel in Fig. 4.
During the initial epochs, the N sampling points of each ray are distributed uniformly, and the training proceeds exactly as in the Uniform panel, so that the network
captures the global structure of the flow before any concentration begins. The Fine
block is then activated, with the activation epoch reported together with the training
parameters of each case, and from this point on the network output at the current
sampling points serves two tasks in every epoch. First, it is integrated along the ray
to compute the loss and update the network parameters. Second, the same output
is used to construct the sampling PDF, which is accumulated into the CDF, and
inverse-transform sampling then redistributes all N points of the ray toward the flow
region. The redistributed points do not join the current training epoch but serve as
the sampling coordinates of that ray in its next training iteration, with the number
of points per ray kept constant. This densification is therefore distributed across the
training epochs: the same N points start uniformly and progressively migrate toward
the key flow regions from one epoch to the next. Fig. 9 visualizes this migration of the
sampling points during 3D training. This differs fundamentally from the Hierarchical
panel, in which every iteration restarts from uniformly distributed coarse points and
generates its fine points afresh, so that the densification lives only within that single
iteration and carries no evolution across epochs. By restructuring the training logic
rather than introducing any additional module, the adaptive strategy enables a single
network to accomplish both sampling densification and training without extra forward
evaluations.
Accordingly, the lightweight study of the sampling strategy and the number of
sampling points is first carried out in the 2D setting. Specifically, a 2-layer network with
128 (27 ) nodes and hash encoding is adopted, together with the LeakyReLU activation
and its corresponding initialization strategy, and no TV regularization is applied. For
the adaptive strategy, the Fine block is activated after a warm-up of 10000 iterations.
The three strategies are compared at the same total number of sampling points per
ray, with the hierarchical strategy splitting them into coarse and fine points in a 2:1
ratio. The comparison is shown in Fig. 8. When the sampling points are few, the errors

the adaptive strategy holds the largest advantage, which becomes decisive in the more

3.3 3D synthetic validation
To further verify the parametric conclusions and the lightweight design, we next carry
out 3D tomographic reconstruction validation on the multi-view synthetic BOS images
of the swirl flame generated in Sec. 2.2. To test performance with an extremely shallow
network, the 3D reconstruction keeps the hash encoding and the per-layer node count
of the original NeDF (Li et al. 2024a), i.e. 44 nodes. The depth, however, is reduced
from the original 5 layers to 2, since hash encoding greatly lowers the depth requirement identified in Sec. 3.1 and the node count is secondary. To better demonstrate
the advantage of the adaptive sampling strategy, we adopt the lightweight sampling
budget of Sec. 3.2 and use only 96 sampling points per ray, half the number used in
the original NeDF. The learning rate during training is 0.02, halved every 500 epochs,
for a total of 2500 epochs, with each iteration processing a randomly drawn batch of
2048 rays, and for the adaptive strategy the Fine block is activated after a warm-up
of 100 epochs. All three sampling strategies are compared under this same network,
encoding scheme and per-ray sampling budget, so that the differences reported below
can be attributed to the sampling strategy alone.

This section further verifies the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed
lightweight reconstruction method using TBOS experimental data from a turbulent
helium jet. The BOS images are first processed by cross-correlation to obtain the displacement fields of the 11 views, whose magnitude distributions are shown in Fig. 11.

comparison. Therefore, to check the reconstruction consistency on an unseen view, one
randomly selected view (Fig. 11f) is excluded from every reconstruction and reserved
solely for validation. The remaining 10 views serve as the common input for all the
methods compared, either as the training set of NeDF or as the projection data of
the voxel-based algorithms. The experimental data are inevitably affected by cameracalibration error and measurement noise, and the reconstruction algorithm itself
may introduce artifacts. For this reason, in addition to comparing the various baseline algorithms, we also examine how TV regularization improves the reconstruction
quality.

the flow, improving the quality. As shown in Fig. 12e–g and Fig. 13e–g, the differences
among the three sampling strategies are amplified by the small per-ray sampling budget adopted here. With uniform sampling, most points fall outside the jet, so only a
rough flow structure is recovered and obvious artifacts remain. Hierarchical sampling
improves the details and reduces the artifacts to some extent, but its coarse points are
still spread uniformly over the whole domain, so the sampling budget actually spent
on the flow remains limited. Adaptive sampling instead concentrates nearly the entire
budget on the flow region, giving the clearest flow field among the three and strongly
suppressing the artifacts outside it. The concentration brings an additional benefit
under noisy experimental conditions. When the non-flow region is densely sampled, the
network tends to fit part of the measurement noise there, imprinting spurious structures on the background. By leaving few sampling points in that region, the adaptive
strategy suppresses this route of noise propagation to some extent.
This comparison reveals an instructive analogy between the two frameworks. When
the sampling points are distributed nearly uniformly, the network reconstruction
resembles the voxel-based result, albeit with fewer artifacts owing to the intrinsic
20
smoothness of the network. The hierarchical and adaptive strategies, in turn, act like
the mask in the voxel framework by focusing the reconstruction on the flow region.
Unlike the mask, which requires additional pre-processing, or the hierarchical strategy,
which requires a dual-network structure, the adaptive strategy achieves this focusing
at almost no extra cost. None of these, however, can fully suppress the experimental
noise and the few residual artifacts, so regularization must be introduced.
In the voxel framework, adding TV regularization further reduces the experimental
noise and gives the best voxel-based result. Even so, regularization only reduces the
noise and can hardly remove the artifacts inside the flow. Fig. 12h and Fig. 13h show
that introducing TV regularization in the NeDF framework, although it slightly lowers
the overall magnitude, joins the fragmented fine structures inside the flow into a
more continuous field and removes most of the remaining noise, improving the overall
quality.

4 Conclusions
To address the underdetermination of TBOS flow reconstruction, a sequential parametric study and a lightweight redesign of the NeDF framework have been conducted
in this paper. The parametric study shows that increasing the network scale improves
reconstruction quality at the cost of training time, with the number of layers being
the decisive factor, and that the accuracy gain saturates beyond a moderate scale.
LeakyReLU is the most robust activation choice, and TV regularization should be
reserved for noisy measurements. The positional encodings enhance the representational capability of the network to different degrees, yet without raising the upper limit
of the attainable accuracy, and hash encoding in particular supports an extremely
shallow network.

Guided by these findings, a lightweight design based on an adaptive sampling
strategy is developed. Inheriting the hash-encoded shallow network identified by the
parametric study, the adaptive strategy progressively concentrates the sampling points
on the flow region within a single network. In the 3D synthetic validation, an extremely
lightweight configuration of only 2 layers, 44 nodes and 96 sampling points per ray
attains the best RMSE, SSIM and PSNR with the highest run-to-run robustness and
a markedly shorter training time to reach a given accuracy. The helium jet experiment
further shows that the proposed method, with the same lightweight configuration
and combined with TV regularization, copes well with real calibration errors and
measurement noise and achieves the best cross-view generalization among all methods
compared.
Although the proposed method achieves marked progress in TBOS reconstruction,
some limitations and directions remain for further exploration. The present method
improves the utilization of the measurement information through a shallow network
and an optimized sampling strategy, but it does not fundamentally remove the underlying information deficiency. Future work could combine the present lightweight design
with a temporal representation and the governing flow equations, exploiting the spatiotemporal continuity of the flow to achieve efficient time-resolved reconstruction of
dynamic flows.
