Abstract
Deploying 3D point-cloud algorithms on embedded or robotic platforms is a pervasive engineering
challenge: volumetric convolution and neighborhood-based PCA, two staples of point-cloud processing,
demand compute and memory far exceeding embedded budgets. This paper addresses this challenge
through two general-purpose algorithmic decompositions, validated on a concrete case study - rebarmesh intersection detection from TLS scans. A training-free voxel-based dual-configuration 3D
convolution method detects rebar-mesh intersections from TLS point clouds with manual-survey
accuracy, but its workstation implementation (18-20 s, 2-3 GB) precludes embedded deployment. We
prove the dual-configuration kernels are axis-separable and decompose them into eight shared-result
1D convolutions, a 36-fold MAC reduction with exact equivalence. We replace point-wise KD-tree
orientation queries with a single-pass integral-statistics scheme (per-cell bincount moments plus
vectorized 3x3 aggregation), yielding 30-50x compiled and ~280x Python speedup. On a 4-core Linux VM
simulating embedded hardware, the pipeline processes a 1.34-million-point scan at 5 mm in 1-3 s (6-15x
speedup, 20-30x memory reduction) while reproducing baseline detection (96 nodes, 9x11 mesh, ~152
mm spacing). Repeated tests report spacing biases of -0.27 mm and -0.21 mm (0.17% and 0.14%),
outperforming the workstation; runtime falls to 1.14-1.17 s with 133 MB peak memory. Error
decomposition shows the convolution template adds only a small inherent Z-axis bias (-1.68 mm), while
the -25 to -27 mm residual cover bias stems from RANSAC plane-fitting errors. Both decompositions
generalize to any axis-separable 3D kernel and any point-cloud task requiring local neighborhood
statistics, including pipe, cable, and vessel tracking.

1. Introduction
Point-cloud processing algorithms validated on workstations rarely transfer directly to embedded or
robotic platforms, which offer only 1-4 cores and 0.5-8 GB without Open3D or large-scale BLAS. This gap
affects diverse applications - from tunnel and pipeline inspection to cable and vessel tracking - where 3D
convolution and local-neighborhood PCA are computational staples [1][2]. As a concrete and demanding
case study, autonomous inspection robots in utility tunnels, construction sites, and mining must verify
the as-built tying state of rebar meshes (spacing and concrete cover) before concrete pouring [3][4][5].
A prior voxel-based dual-configuration 3D convolutional method detects rebar intersections from TLS
point clouds within ~0.3% of manual surveys, but was designed for workstations (i7-10700, 64 GB),
needing ~18-20 s per scan and 2-3 GB.
The portability gap is stark: on a 4-core Linux VM (7.7 GB) simulating embedded hardware, 1 mm voxel
processing took over 600 s - two orders slower than the workstation - and even at 5 mm real-time
operation was unsustainable. The bottleneck is purely algorithmic: dense 3D convolution and point-wise
neighborhood PCA account for 93% of runtime, a pattern not specific to rebar detection [6][7] but
characteristic of many voxel-based point-cloud pipelines.
This paper does not propose a new detection algorithm; rather, it introduces two general-purpose
computational decompositions for point-cloud processing and validates them on the rebar-detection
case: a provably exact separable-convolution equivalence and a controllable-error integral-statistics
replacement for neighborhood PCA. Both techniques are applicable to any axis-separable 3D kernel and
any task requiring local point-cloud statistics, with cylindrical targets such as pipes, cables, and blood
vessels as natural extensions [8].
The contributions of this paper are threefold:
(1) We prove the dual-configuration 3D convolution kernel is axis-separable, deriving a sharedintermediate decomposition that cuts per-voxel MACs from 3456 to 96 (36x) via eight 1D FIR passes;
(2) We reformulate local PCA orientation as integral statistics: per-cell moments accumulated in one
O(N) pass and neighborhoods aggregated via vectorized indexing, eliminating ~8000 interpreter loops;
expected 30-50x compiled (~280x Python) speedup.
(3) We provide a C99 reference implementation and VM deployment analysis, projecting ~16 MB
memory and sub-second to 3 s runtime at 3 mm voxel resolution on a 4-core VM, confirming feasibility
for resource-constrained robotic platforms.
2.Related Work
Point-cloud processing on embedded platforms is an active but fragmented field. In construction quality
monitoring, automated rebar spacing and cover measurement has shifted from manual and
2
electromagnetic methods [5] to 3D point cloud-based approaches [9], including LiDAR-based evaluation
[5], Rebar-YOLOv8-seg segmentation [10], and 3D keypoint detection for robotic integration [11].
Beyond construction, embedded point-cloud processing has targeted depth filtering and autonomousdriving ground segmentation [12]. Separable convolution [13,14][15][16] reduces a k-dimensional kernel
to k one-dimensional convolutions, but most 3D kernels (PointNet [17], KPConv [18]) lack an axisseparable structure. Eschmann et al. [19] used UAVs for inspection with ground-station processing;
embedded real-time point-cloud processing for cylindrical targets - rebar, pipes, cables - remains largely
unreported [20]. This paper takes the training-free dual-configuration template matching method as a
representative baseline, focusing on general computational decompositions for embedded deployment
without modifying the detection algorithm.
3.Computational Profiling and Optimization Strategy
3.1 Baseline Pipeline Overview
The baseline pipeline fits the formwork plane via RANSAC [21], extracts outer-layer rebar points,
estimates in-plane mesh orientation via local PCA, rotates and aligns the cloud, rasterizes it into a binary
occupancy grid, performs dual-configuration 3D convolution, and applies thresholding, NMS, and subvoxel refinement to output intersection coordinates with spacing and cover. On the workstation (i710700, 2.90 GHz, 64 GB), it processes ~1.3 million points at 5 mm in ~19 s, detecting 96 intersections in
9x11 with ~152 mm mean spacing.

The theoretical 36× reduction in MAC count is only a lower bound on the actual speedup. In practice, 1D
convolutions access contiguous memory along the filtering axis, achieving far higher CPU cache-line
utilization than the strided access pattern of 3D convolution. In practice, the effective speedup ranges
from 10x to 20x (depending on the voxel grid size and cache hierarchy), falling below the 36x theoretical
MAC reduction because the eight separate 1D passes increase total memory traffic by approximately 35x compared to a single dense 3D pass.
Unlike approximate acceleration methods such as low-rank approximation, pruning, and quantization,
the separable decomposition is mathematically exact. The proof of Theorem 1 shows that the output of
the eight 1D convolution chains is equivalent to that of the dense 3D convolution, differing only by the
rounding error introduced by the reassociation of floating-point additions (no more than 1 ULP in
float32). Consequently, all downstream operations—response normalization, thresholding, NMS, and
sub-voxel refinement—operate on the same response field and produce identical node coordinates.
Accuracy is preserved within floating-point rounding tolerance.
5 Vectorized Integral-Statistics Principal Component Analysis
5.1 Baseline Bottleneck
The baseline orientation estimation samples 8000 points from the outer-layer rebar point cloud; for
each sample, it queries all neighbors within radius via a KD-tree, constructs a 2 × 2 covariance matrix,
and solves for the principal eigenvector. Although the KD-tree query and the eigendecomposition are
7
each implemented in compiled C code, the 8000 interpreter-to-native-code transitions—each
accompanied by Python object creation, argument marshaling, and temporary array allocation—
together consume 5.6 , even exceeding the KD-tree search itself. This is a classic pathology of
interpreted scientific computing: when the per-call native workload is small, the per-call foreignfunction-interface overhead dominates.

6 Embedded Implementation and Hardware Budget
6.1 C99 Reference Implementation
To verify deployability on resource-constrained platforms, the implementation replaces all
Python/NumPy with C equivalents: struct-based LAS parsing, array-based RANSAC, counting-sort grid
hashing, 1D FIR convolution, closed-form 2x2 eigendecomposition, Rodrigues rotation, Euclidean NMS,
and weighted-centroid refinement, all in float32 to match microcontroller FPU hardware. Crossvalidated against Python, it agrees on node count (within 1) and spacing.

The virtual machine (4-core CPU, 7.7 GB RAM) provides sufficient resources for the optimized pipeline,
achieving 1.14-1.17 s at 5 mm voxel resolution. The 3 mm configuration requires approximately 3.1
billion MACs, or 3.2 s of pure computation if all data resides in single-cycle SRAM. However, the 16 MB
working set exceeds the 16 KB L1 data cache by three orders of magnitude and must reside in external
SDRAM via the FMC. SDRAM random-access latency is 70-100 ns per access (versus ~1 ns for internal
SRAM), and the strided 1D convolution access pattern causes frequent row-open/row-close overhead.
With an estimated 90-95% L1 cache miss rate and SDRAM row-cycle overhead, effective throughput
drops to ~100-150 million MACs/s, extending compute time to 20-80 s; including I/O, RANSAC, and NMS
overhead, total estimated execution is 30-120 s. While this precludes real-time operation, it remains
practical for periodic inspection at 2-5 min scan intervals.

8 Discussion and Conclusions
The two decompositions introduced here are not specific to rebar detection. The separable-convolution
theorem applies to any 3D kernel expressible as a sum of axis-separable terms - a class that includes
cylindrical and tubular template kernels for pipes, cables, blood vessels, and similar targets. The integralstatistics PCA replaces point-wise KD-tree queries with a single-pass formulation suited to any pointcloud task requiring local neighborhood statistics (normal estimation, surface reconstruction), where
neighborhoods approximate rectangular cells and statistics are per-cell summable; higher-order
moments scale linearly while remaining loop-free. The rebar-detection pipeline serves as a demanding
19
validation case: dual-configuration 3D convolution with two non-trivial kernels and high-throughput PCA
over millions of points.
Experiments across 1, 3, 5, and 8 mm voxels identify 5 mm as best-balanced, reproducing baseline
detection (~96 nodes, ~152 mm) with 6-15x speedup. Finer 3 mm voxels remove only a few boundary
nodes via NMS while preserving geometry, whereas 1 mm triggers excessive suppression, implying subvoxel refinement and NMS parameters should adapt to voxel size.
Three limitations are noted. First, embedded runtime is extrapolated from VM results, not physical
hardware; memory bandwidth, not raw compute, dominates under external-SDRAM constraints, and
practical runtime may deviate due to DMA conflicts and interrupts. Second, the integral-statistics PCA
yields a 0.5 degree orientation bias, acceptable for rebar grids but problematic for high-precision tasks.
Third, validation uses one real-world scan; more layouts and occluded cases are needed.
Overall, this work demonstrates that point-cloud algorithms designed for workstations can be made
deployable on embedded-grade hardware through two general-purpose decompositions: exact
separable convolution (eight 1D passes; 36-fold MAC reduction, 10-20x practical) and loop-free integralstatistics PCA (280x Python, 30-50x compiled). Validated on rebar-mesh detection - cutting the
workstation's 19 s and 2-3 GB to 1-3 s and ~107 MB with nearly identical accuracy - VM tests (1.14-1.17
s, ~133 MB) and a C99 outline confirm deployment potential on UGV/UAV robots. The same techniques
extend to any axis-separable 3D kernel and neighborhood-statistics task, including pipe, cable, and
vessel point-cloud processing, though real-time performance depends on memory-subsystem tuning.
