# ABSTRACT

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have shown promise in novel view synthesis, but it still face challenges when applied to refractive objects. The presence of refraction disrupts multiview consistency, often resulting in renderings that are either blurred or distorted. Recent methods alleviate this challenge by introducing external supervision, such as mask images and Index of Refraction. However,acquiring such information is often impractical,limiting the application of NeRF-like models to complex scenes with refracting elementsand yielding unsatisfactory results. To address these limitations, we introduce RoseNeRF (R**efracting **o**nce i**s e**nough for **NeRF), a novel method that simplifies the complex interaction of rays within objects to a single refraction event. We design the refraction network that efficiently maps a ray in the 4D light field to its refracted counterpart, better modeling curved ray paths. Furthermore, we introduce a regularization strategy to ensure the reversibility of optical paths, which is anchored in physical world theorems. To help it easier for the network to learn the highly viewdependent appearance of refractive objects, we also propose novel density decoding strategies. Our method is designed for seamless integration into most NeRF-like frameworks and has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance without any additional information on both the Eikonal Fields' dataset and Shiny dataset.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Novel view synthesis from input images that are freely distributed around a scene is an important problem in 3D computer vision. Recently, Neural Radiation Field (NeRF) [36] has brought a great breakthrough in this topic by its ability to render novel views with high fidelity. Many follow-up works have extended NeRF in various ways, including high-quality rendering [2–4], accelerated training or rendering [9, 11, 38], dynamic scene rendering [8, 17] , editing NeRF [1, 21, 51], etc. A prevalent challenge in applying NeRF to real-world scenes is the presence of transparent objects, like glass containers. These objects cause ray to undergo refraction, complicating the rendering process, which leads to artifacts in rendered images, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Traditional methods [10, 23, 37, 44, 48, 55] for representing transparent objects typically rely on additional information acquired from controlled settings to model refractions. Following the proposal of NeRF [36], several studies have explored the integration of graphics and deep learning for the reconstruction of transparent objects. Eikonal Fields [5] and SampleNeRFRO [40] adjust the NeRF sampling by utilizing the laws of eikonal light transport [24] to calculate the way the ray bends. Other methods [30, 53, 60] apply Snell's law to determine the direction of refracted rays, which may be sensitive to the inaccurate estimated properties such as surface normal and Index of Refraction (IoR) [58]. While these methods reduce some restrictions, they still necessitate additional information such as image masks, refractive index, and surface normals. In the complexity of real-world scenes, obtaining precise masks and IoR for each scene is often impractical.

An alternative methods to modeling refraction is to eschew reliance on physical laws, instead employing purely implicit networks. Some NeRF-like methods [15, 18, 26, 33, 41, 43] design a deformation field to distort the path of ray. Notably, models such as those

They utilize a deformation network to predict the position offsets at each sampling point caused by refraction, thereby complementing NeRF's assumption of linear sampling. However, the lack of prior knowledge presents a significant challenge in accurately determining the bending of rays, thus hindering the network's capability to learn correct position offsets.

In this paper, we propose a novel neural representation framework, named RoseNeRF, to solve the challenge of novel view synthesis for refractive objects. The basic idea is illustrated in 2. We propose a method for simulating replicated ray bending through a single refraction event, leveraging physical theorems to guide the prediction of the path of refracted ray. Specifically, we describe a ray as a four-dimensional (4D) light field, employing an implicit function to represent the refraction process. This function is designed to map the 4D light field of the incoming ray to that of the refracted ray. Aligned with Snell's law, we incorporate the local normal into our function to aid in accurately predicting the refracted ray. This function is implemented as a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), with regularization guided by the principle of ray reversibility. To enhance the accuracy of predicting rays that retrace

their path, our method incorporates online distillation, enabling the simultaneous training of a smaller network. We employ online distillation for training a compact network that solely relies on a 4D light field as its input. In contrast to previous methods, we predicts a refracted ray and then employs NeRF's hierarchical sampling technique to identify the correct sampling locations. We do not require any additional information or the point-by-point deformation typically. Another advantage of the proposed method is that predicting the refractive ray before sampling provides a geometric prior (natural covariance of sampled points) to enable more efficient learning of implicit function.

Based on the proposed method, we develop a novel pipeline for rendering the refractive objects. Inspired by MS-NeRF [59], we design a Decoder to fuses the features of both the original and refracted rays, ensuring compatibility with NeRF's rendering approach. We put the decoder after the NeRF's positional MLP, and then feed the decoded density and direction into the directional MLP. Inputting the direction into the network last is more conducive to outputting the view-dependent RGB color, resulting in better performance than MS-NeRF's decoder strategy. Our experiments reveal that, in terms of new view synthesis quality on real datasets involving refraction, including Eikonal Fields' dataset [5] and Shiny dataset [57], our method outperforms the established benchmark method. Additionally, the design of our method allows for straightforward integration with any variant of NeRF, equipping most NeRF-like methods with the ability to model refraction. Our main contributions are as follows:

- We propose RoseNeRF, a framework developed for high-fidelity rendering of refractive objects, which achieve significant improvements over existing refraction-specific methods both quantitatively and qualitatively.
- We design a lightweight refraction network that simplifies multiple ray deformations into a single refraction event and introduce a reversibility regularization.
- We propose a decoding strategy that makes it easier for the network to learn the highly view-dependent appearance of refractive objects.

Novel View Synthesis. View synthesis is a task of using observed images of a scene to render images from novel unobserved camera viewpoints. Existing methods [7, 14, 22, 45] utilize meshes for geometry representation to render novel views through the reprojection and blending of multiple captured images. Other geometric representations such as voxel grids [25, 28, 32] and multiplane images [16, 35, 42, 62] are suitable for gradient optimization due to their parallelizability in computation. However, these discrete representation methods require significant computational resources and storage space to achieve high-resolution rendering. They are also less suitable for modeling large scenes.

Refractive Object Reconstruction. Past works have facilitated the modeling of refractive objects by implementing additional specialized setups such as gray-coded patterns [44], tomography [48] and polarization [23]. Wetzstein et al. [55] employs light field probes to observe distortions in the light field background illumination, which aids in reconstructing thin transparent surfaces. Han et al. [20] alter the paths of incident rays before they enter the object by immersing the object partially in a liquid to reconstruct transparent objects. In particular, our approach is heavily inspired by it, which simplifies modeling by not requiring detailed simulations of light interactions, assumptions about object shapes, or the precise count of refractions and reflections along ray paths. Qian et al. [44] describe a method for the simultaneous reconstruction of 3D positions and normals at both refraction points on an object's surface. This is achieved using a setup comprising two cameras and a monitor that serves as the light source. Some research [13, 56, 63] estimates the background distortion seen through refractive objects by employing environment matting techniques. The above methods require specialized equipment, which adds to the cost of reconstruction.

Recently, many efraction-specific NeRF-like methods have been proposed. NeRFrac [60] trains an MLP-based Refractive Field to estimate the distance from the origin to the refractive surface and uses Snell's law to calculate the direction of refracted rays. NeReF [53] leverages the correspondence and uniqueness of refracted rays to reconstruct a volumetric normal field along with its corresponding

density field. On the other hand, SampleNeRFRO [40] enhances NeRF's stratified sampling technique to simulate the bending of ray caused by refraction, employing the Eikonal equation for this purpose. SampleNeRFRO discretizes the refraction process into multiple steps, determining the direction of refracted rays based on the Index of Refraction (IoR) at two sampling points. Subsequent sampling is then conducted along the direction of these refracted rays. [5] construct and solve an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) to optimize the IoR field. Subsequently, the Eikonal equation [24] is employed to calculate how the rays bend. Additionally, some methods [15, 18] involve training a deformation network. This network computes the offset for each sampling point on a refracted ray, thereby determining the correct sampling positions. # 3.2 Refraction Modeling

NeRF utilizes MLPs to query the color and density of each particle, then computes the pixel color through alpha compositing with alpha values 𝛼𝑖 = 1 − exp(−𝜎𝑖𝛿𝑖). However, in a scene with refractive objects, the particles contributing to alpha compositing are not aligned in a straight line due to the bending of rays. As a result, NeRF may inaccurately estimate the density and color in wrong regions along the ray path to minimize the photometric loss. While these regions may account for some aspects of the input views, resluting in a satisfactory appearance from certain angles, they often suffer from floater artifacts when observed from alternate perspectives as shown in Fig. 5. To address this problem, modeling of the refraction process is crucial to determine the correct positions of the particles.

We aim to ensure that the sampling points precisely align with the path of ray propagation, but without adding any additional information. Inspired by Han et al. [20] who verified the feasibility of reconstructing objects but not modeling the complex interactions of rays, we aim to simplify the modeling of refraction. We focus on the final refracted ray and effectively ignore some of the ray bending, i.e., we assume that the density of particles potentially sampled during the intermediate process is zero. We naturally incorporate the distortion regularization from MipNeRF 360 [3] into our method as a "free lunch". Contrary to the SampleNeRFRO [40], our method does not require simulating multiple refractions for each ray to determine the correct sampling points. For transparent objects, we postulate that particles contributing significantly to the color of ray (i.e., with higher density) are predominantly located along two segments of the ray: the segment before the first point of refraction and the segment after the refraction, either exiting the transparent object or terminating inside it.

Choosing a single refraction over multiple refractions has several advantages. First, it allows for the use of a simpler neural network architecture to predict refracted rays which produces smoother results. Moreover, our method reduces the occurrence of jagged edges on objects as shown in Fig. 6, a common problem caused by insufficient sampling points in multiple refraction scene. In addition, our method strategically uses normal vectors predicted by NeRF, to match with Snell's law in predicting refracted rays. This

method ensures that our modeling process remains faithful to physical principles, requiring no additional information beyond what NeRF inherently provides, such as IoR fields or detailed surface reconstructions. By relying solely on NeRF's ability to estimate normal vectors, we maintain both simplicity and adherence to established optical laws. Our method can be easily integrated with any variant of NeRF, making it highly suitable for complex scenes.

Furthermore, in contrast to methods [15, 18] that rely on predicting offsets for each sampling point to model refraction, our method adopts a ray-to-ray mapping strategy. This has two significant advantages: First, by not requiring predictions for each point in space, our method significantly reduces computational load and degrees of freedom. A simple, shallow MLP is sufficient to predict refracted rays. Second, because sampling is performed directly on the predicted rays, these sampling points naturally fall on a straight line. This eliminates the need for additional constraints such as Collinearity Regularization [15].

# 3.3 4D Light Field Representation

Note that as the incident ray moves along the direction of the surface normal, the direction of refraction remains unchanged. Therefore, following the NeRF method which uses the ray's origin o and direction d as network inputs, is inappropriate for our context. This ray parameterization method may include data irrelevant to the refraction process, potentially affecting the accuracy of the model. We adopt the 4D light field representation technique [19, 29], which parameterizes lines based on their intersections with two arbitrary planes as shown in Fig. 3. In this representation, the intersection coordinates on each plane are denoted as (𝑢, 𝑣) and (𝑠, 𝑡) respectively, allowing us to represent each ray by a 4D coordinate (𝑢, 𝑣, 𝑠, 𝑡). For simplicity, we set 𝑍*0 = 0 and *𝑍*1 = 1, denoting the Z-coordinates of the two planes. Using this representation, we define a function *𝑓 : R4 → R4 to encapsulate the mapping relationship between incident and refracted rays. Since a single 4D coordinate can represent multiple rays with the same direction but different origins, the network is better equipped to learn the laws of refraction.

#### 3.4 Refraction Network

We formulate the mapping function 𝑓 as a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) to learn the refraction process from the input images. The refraction network (refnet) learns jointly with NeRF as part of the framework as shown in Fig. 2. A key advantage of the refnet is its ability to function effectively without the need for additional a priori knowledge, facilitating its effortless addition to any existing NeRF-like models.

First, according to the principle of ray reversibility, i.e., if the direction of a ray is reversed, despite the number of times the ray is reflected or refracted, it will follow the same path. # 4 EXPERIMENTS

#### 4.1 Setups

Datastes & Metrics. We selecred four real scenes from Eikonal Fields [5]: Ball, Glass, Pen and WineGlass. Each scene contains a distinct transparent object, and the sampling viewpoints are distributed across the upper hemisphere. To evaluate our model's performance in more complex scenes, we selected three scenes from the Shiny dataset [57]: Lab, CD, and Seasoning. Following NeRF [36], we hold out 1/8 of each dataset for the test set and report the mean of PSNR, structural similarity index (SSIM) [52], and the LPIPS perceptual metric [61]. #### 4.3 Ablation studies

In Table 3, we present an ablation study of our model on the two datasets. (A) We integrate our decoder strategy, originally designed for fusing two sets of densities, into MS-NeRF [59] and reduced the number of subspaces in MS-NeRF from 8 to 2. The modification led to a notable improvement in performance and served as a clear validation of the efficacy of our decoding strategy. (B) Removing L*���� ��* disrupts the fitting process of our network, since the absence of the correct refracted ray path directly leads to erroneous sampling points. This misalignment significantly impedes accurate model training. (C) Substituting our specialized decoder with a simple computed average leads to a significant decrease in performance. This again highlights the critical role of the decoder in the overall effectiveness of our model. (D) Using the origin and direction of the ray for prediction instead of a 4D light field parameterization worsens accuracy, especially for LPIPS. (E) We predict refracted rays without using normals, relying solely on the angular information of the incident ray. It does not significantly degrade performance and it even enhances it in some scenes (e.g., achieving a PSNR of 22.57 in Glass, higher than RoseNeRF's 22.45).

### 5 CONCLUSION

This paper addresses the challenge of refraction in complex realworld scenes for NeRF. We propose a simple yet effective method that models the refraction process, accurately predicting refracted rays without the need for additional information. Our method simplifies the complex refraction process by treating it as a single refraction event, predicted using a simple MLP. This simplification allows for easy adaptation to any NeRF variant. In addition, based on the principle of ray reversibility in the physical world, we have designed a reversible regularization strategy, enhancing the model's ability to learn the refraction process. Our method achieves high-quality novel view synthesis, demonstrating stateof-the-art results on various real-world scenes.

However,our results are not as sharp as the groundtruth at a fine-grained level, integrating supplementary techniques such as generative model [50] and super-resolution model [31] is expected to enhance the detail fidelity [15].
