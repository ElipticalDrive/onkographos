Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have shown promise in novel view
synthesis, but it still face challenges when applied to refractive objects. The presence of refraction disrupts multiview consistency,
often resulting in renderings that are either blurred or distorted.
Recent methods alleviate this challenge by introducing external
supervision, such as mask images and Index of Refraction. However,acquiring such information is often impractical,limiting the
application of NeRF-like models to complex scenes with refracting
elementsand yielding unsatisfactory results. To address these limitations, we introduce RoseNeRF (Refracting once is enough for
NeRF), a novel method that simplifies the complex interaction of
rays within objects to a single refraction event. We design the refraction network that efficiently maps a ray in the 4D light field
to its refracted counterpart, better modeling curved ray paths. Furthermore, we introduce a regularization strategy to ensure the reversibility of optical paths, which is anchored in physical world
theorems. To help it easier for the network to learn the highly viewdependent appearance of refractive objects, we also propose novel
density decoding strategies. Our method is designed for seamless
integration into most NeRF-like frameworks and has demonstrated
state-of-the-art performance without any additional information
on both the Eikonal Fields’ dataset and Shiny dataset.

INTRODUCTION
Novel view synthesis from input images that are freely distributed
around a scene is an important problem in 3D computer vision.
Recently, Neural Radiation Field (NeRF) [36] has brought a great
breakthrough in this topic by its ability to render novel views with
high fidelity. Many follow-up works have extended NeRF in various ways, including high-quality rendering [2–4], accelerated training or rendering [9, 11, 38], dynamic scene rendering [8, 17] , editing NeRF [1, 21, 51], etc. A prevalent challenge in applying NeRF to
real-world scenes is the presence of transparent objects, like glass
containers. These objects cause ray to undergo refraction, complicating the rendering process, which leads to artifacts in rendered
images, as illustrated in Fig. 5.
Traditional methods [10, 23, 37, 44, 48, 55] for representing transparent objects typically rely on additional information acquired
from controlled settings to model refractions. Following the proposal of NeRF [36], several studies have explored the integration
of graphics and deep learning for the reconstruction of transparent objects. Eikonal Fields [5] and SampleNeRFRO [40] adjust the
NeRF sampling by utilizing the laws of eikonal light transport [24]
to calculate the way the ray bends. Other methods [30, 53, 60] apply
Snell’s law to determine the direction of refracted rays, which may
be sensitive to the inaccurate estimated properties such as surface
normal and Index of Refraction (IoR) [58]. While these methods
reduce some restrictions, they still necessitate additional information such as image masks, refractive index, and surface normals. In
the complexity of real-world scenes, obtaining precise masks and
IoR for each scene is often impractical.
An alternative methods to modeling refraction is to eschew reliance on physical laws, instead employing purely implicit networks.

RELATED WORK
Novel View Synthesis. View synthesis is a task of using observed
images of a scene to render images from novel unobserved camera viewpoints. Existing methods [7, 14, 22, 45] utilize meshes for
geometry representation to render novel views through the reprojection and blending of multiple captured images. Other geometric
representations such as voxel grids [25, 28, 32] and multiplane images [16, 35, 42, 62] are suitable for gradient optimization due to
their parallelizability in computation. However, these discrete representation methods require significant computational resources
and storage space to achieve high-resolution rendering. They are
also less suitable for modeling large scenes.

MLP that maps from any continuous input 3D coordinate to the geometry and appearance of the scene at that location. NeRF shows

Refractive Object Reconstruction. Past works have facilitated
the modeling of refractive objects by implementing additional specialized setups such as gray-coded patterns [44], tomography [48]
and polarization [23]. Wetzstein et al. [55] employs light field probes
to observe distortions in the light field background illumination,
which aids in reconstructing thin transparent surfaces. Han et al. [20]
alter the paths of incident rays before they enter the object by immersing the object partially in a liquid to reconstruct transparent
objects. In particular, our approach is heavily inspired by it, which
simplifies modeling by not requiring detailed simulations of light
interactions, assumptions about object shapes, or the precise count
of refractions and reflections along ray paths. Qian et al. [44] describe a method for the simultaneous reconstruction of 3D positions and normals at both refraction points on an object’s surface.
This is achieved using a setup comprising two cameras and a monitor that serves as the light source. Some research [13, 56, 63] estimates the background distortion seen through refractive objects
by employing environment matting techniques. The above methods require specialized equipment, which adds to the cost of reconstruction.

uses Snell’s law to calculate the direction of refracted rays. NeReF [53]
leverages the correspondence and uniqueness of refracted rays to

Refraction Modeling
NeRF utilizes MLPs to query the color and density of each particle,
then computes the pixel color through alpha compositing with alpha values 𝛼𝑖 = 1 − exp(−𝜎𝑖 𝛿𝑖 ). However, in a scene with refractive objects, the particles contributing to alpha compositing are
not aligned in a straight line due to the bending of rays. As a result,
NeRF may inaccurately estimate the density and color in wrong regions along the ray path to minimize the photometric loss. While
these regions may account for some aspects of the input views,
resluting in a satisfactory appearance from certain angles, they often suffer from floater artifacts when observed from alternate perspectives as shown in Fig. 5. To address this problem, modeling of
the refraction process is crucial to determine the correct positions
of the particles.
We aim to ensure that the sampling points precisely align with
the path of ray propagation, but without adding any additional information. Inspired by Han et al. [20] who verified the feasibility of
reconstructing objects but not modeling the complex interactions
of rays, we aim to simplify the modeling of refraction. We focus
on the final refracted ray and effectively ignore some of the ray
bending, i.e., we assume that the density of particles potentially
sampled during the intermediate process is zero. We naturally incorporate the distortion regularization from MipNeRF 360 [3] into
our method as a “free lunch”. Contrary to the SampleNeRFRO [40],
our method does not require simulating multiple refractions for
each ray to determine the correct sampling points. For transparent objects, we postulate that particles contributing significantly
to the color of ray (i.e., with higher density) are predominantly located along two segments of the ray: the segment before the first
point of refraction and the segment after the refraction, either exiting the transparent object or terminating inside it.
Choosing a single refraction over multiple refractions has several advantages. First, it allows for the use of a simpler neural network architecture to predict refracted rays which produces smoother
results. Moreover, our method reduces the occurrence of jagged
edges on objects as shown in Fig. 6, a common problem caused
by insufficient sampling points in multiple refraction scene. In addition, our method strategically uses normal vectors predicted by
NeRF, to match with Snell’s law in predicting refracted rays. This

4D Light Field Representation
Note that as the incident ray moves along the direction of the surface normal, the direction of refraction remains unchanged. Therefore, following the NeRF method which uses the ray’s origin o
and direction d as network inputs, is inappropriate for our context. This ray parameterization method may include data irrelevant
to the refraction process, potentially affecting the accuracy of the
model. We adopt the 4D light field representation technique [19,
29], which parameterizes lines based on their intersections with
two arbitrary planes as shown in Fig. 3. In this representation, the
intersection coordinates on each plane are denoted as (𝑢, 𝑣) and
(𝑠, 𝑡) respectively, allowing us to represent each ray by a 4D coordinate (𝑢, 𝑣, 𝑠, 𝑡). For simplicity, we set 𝑍 0 = 0 and 𝑍 1 = 1, denoting
the Z-coordinates of the two planes. Using this representation, we
define a function 𝑓 : R4 → R4 to encapsulate the mapping relationship between incident and refracted rays. Since a single 4D
coordinate can represent multiple rays with the same direction but
different origins, the network is better equipped to learn the laws
of refraction.

Ablation studies
In Table 3, we present an ablation study of our model on the two
datasets. (A) We integrate our decoder strategy, originally designed
for fusing two sets of densities, into MS-NeRF [59] and reduced
the number of subspaces in MS-NeRF from 8 to 2. The modification led to a notable improvement in performance and served as a
clear validation of the efficacy of our decoding strategy. (B) Removing L𝑟𝑒 𝑓 disrupts the fitting process of our network, since
the absence of the correct refracted ray path directly leads to erroneous sampling points. This misalignment significantly impedes
accurate model training. (C) Substituting our specialized decoder
with a simple computed average leads to a significant decrease in
performance. This again highlights the critical role of the decoder
in the overall effectiveness of our model. (D) Using the origin and
direction of the ray for prediction instead of a 4D light field parameterization worsens accuracy, especially for LPIPS. (E) We predict
refracted rays without using normals, relying solely on the angular
information of the incident ray. It does not significantly degrade
performance and it even enhances it in some scenes (e.g., achieving
a PSNR of 22.57 in Glass, higher than RoseNeRF’s 22.45).

CONCLUSION
This paper addresses the challenge of refraction in complex realworld scenes for NeRF. We propose a simple yet effective method
that models the refraction process, accurately predicting refracted
rays without the need for additional information. Our method simplifies the complex refraction process by treating it as a single
refraction event, predicted using a simple MLP. This simplification allows for easy adaptation to any NeRF variant. In addition,
based on the principle of ray reversibility in the physical world,
we have designed a reversible regularization strategy, enhancing
the model’s ability to learn the refraction process. Our method
achieves high-quality novel view synthesis, demonstrating stateof-the-art results on various real-world scenes.
However,our results are not as sharp as the groundtruth at a
fine-grained level, integrating supplementary techniques such as
generative model [50] and super-resolution model [31] is expected
to enhance the detail fidelity [15].
