Currently whenever navigating to anywhere that is in nighttime, there is zero light and the entire scene is pitch black.
I am curious what the best way would be to make this less of an issue. On one hand the 3D tiles are generally made with photogrammetry and already have some daytime shadows baked in. This however hasn't in practice been very problematic even when those shadows and lighting are still present when we view a dusk/dawn scene because of the tour de force that the atmosphere rendering implementation is here.
I suspect maybe it could also work similarly under moonlight. it casts similar shadow shapes compared to the sun anyway.
So maybe if we make the moon a weak sun, we could maybe easily have very good looking moonlit lighting.
Then the shadows will be very wrong under a starlight only scenario. But again there is not much that can be done for that.
What do we think? I guess trying to expose for moonlight and starlight would force us to implement HDR tone mapping in a way that goes beyond how it is done currently? Is it feasible?

Sharing my workflow slightly here. I'm currently using Oh-My-Opencode v3.2.3 running Claude Opus 4.5. I've got a Prometheus work plan summary as follows. I manually massaged some of the markdown formatting.
Analysis & Proposed Approach
After investigating the codebase, here's what I found and a proposed path forward:
Current State
Already exists:
🌙 Moon disk rendering with physically-based lunar radiance (MoonNode.ts) — uses magnitude difference to calculate ~1/400,000th of solar irradiance
🌙 Accurate moon positioning via getMoonDirectionECEF()
☀️ SunDirectionalLight that samples transmittance texture for atmospheric attenuation
🎨 HDR pipeline with AgX tone mapping
Missing:
❌ MoonDirectionalLight — no equivalent light source to illuminate the ground
❌ Auto-exposure system — manual toneMappingExposure tweaking is required for night scenes
The Core Problem
The dynamic range between sunlight and moonlight is extreme (~6 orders of magnitude):
Condition
Illuminance
Relative to Sun
Direct sunlight
~100,000 lux
1
Full moonlight
~0.1-0.3 lux
0.000001-0.000003
Starlight only
~0.001 lux
0.00000001
Without automatic exposure adjustment, nighttime scenes will always appear pitch black at typical daytime exposure levels.
Proposed Solution (2-phase)
Phase 1: Practical Foundation
MoonDirectionalLight (+ R3F )
Modeled after SunDirectionalLight
Blue-shifted color for perceptual accuracy (matching the Purkinje effect (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purkinje_effect) — humans perceive moonlight as blue even though it's technically slightly warm)
Intensity: ~0.0000025 × solar radiance × atmospheric transmittance
Auto-Exposure / Eye Adaptation System
Analytical mode (default): Calculate target exposure from sun/moon elevation angles — handles twilight transitions smoothly with zero GPU overhead
Image-based mode (optional): Measure actual scene luminance with temporal smoothing for realistic eye adaptation
Configurable: adaptation speed, min/max bounds, mode selection
Starlight Ambient (simple)
Very dim ambient (~0.0001 × sun) when moon is below horizon
Could leverage existing SkyLightProbe with reduced intensity
Phase 2: Future Enhancement (Optional)
Moonlight atmospheric inscatter: Use the Bruneton scattering with moonDirection and scale by lunar radiance. This would add subtle moonlit sky glow, but isn't essential since auto-exposure already makes scenes visible.
On Baked Shadows
The concern about 3D tiles having baked daytime shadows is valid but likely acceptable — the same issue exists at dusk/dawn, and the atmosphere rendering still looks beautiful. The artistic tradeoff is worthwhile.
On Moonlight Color
Interesting physics note: Moonlight is actually slightly warmer than sunlight (~4100K vs ~5500K) since it's reflected sunlight filtered by the moon's surface. However, humans perceive it as blue due to scotopic vision (rod-dominant seeing in low light). For best visual results, we should go with perceptual accuracy (blue-shifted).
This approach prioritizes:
🎯 Immediate usability (visible nighttime scenes)
🚀 Performance (analytical exposure has zero overhead)
🧩 Incremental enhancement (atmospheric inscatter can come later)

Here are my findings so far. I've had good results without delving into much of the low level code, so please accept my apologies for any incorrect assumptions I make in the following overview. I also expect I will learn a lot more about the implementation as I write this out.
After a few attempts I was able to get the night glow working and a few more iterations to tune it so the scene is not torn down and sluggish upon adjusting the Leva sliders I've added. The nightglow should be what's described in #80 and your small suggested code change @shotamatsuda was surely instrumental in me getting that working. Below, I focus on looking at this in the atmosphere test pages, but everything largely works as expected even with clouds, which I am also delighted with.

I agree with what you said in the other thread that this has no basis in reality and just amounts to clamping "a wee bit of dawn/dusk level" for the atmosphere. That's certainly not a bad stylistic choice as last screenshot shows. However, it's clear something else would be needed for ambient ground lighting.
So I've taken a stab at that baseline ambient lighting, I haven't had reason to look into the implementation of this yet but it does the job adequately.

And 0.4 back in the earlier camera orientation:
Next steps when it comes to nighttime ambient would involve bringing in a set of nighttime luminance world tiles for showing city lighting, as that's what's most garishly missing. It seems a dim level of ambient light on everything will help a lot to actually see where you are, and I would definitely also make it bluer just to look more plausible, though it's probably worth noting here this should be largely a human biological property in the Purkinje effect due to a loss of color perception. At any rate, it is a definite compromise at this point between realism (night time non city lit areas should be very dark compared to sun-side) and usability (need to be able to get your bearings). Even very carefully adjusting that balance, it is overall not possible to get both strong realism and strong usability, since the former probably exists with my ambient parameter here <0.3 and the latter needs it >0.5...
That tension leads naturally into the introduction of exposure compensation as a way to raise the relative dynamic range between sunlit Earth and nighttime Earth. We already have exposure we can modulate, but I found that the limit for this is that the atmosphere colors on the terminator become quantized and a bit ugly. Banding (and with notable feathering between band lines, indicating some internal precomputed or pre-rendered lack of resolution at low intensities) becomes visible, here exposure is set to only 82 (default is 10, the high limit in Leva on main branch is 100)
Here is an Apollo 11 mission image from wikipedia of the solar terminator:
Notably I suppose it looks like geospatial is rendering a ten or more times thicker atmosphere compared to reality, and for a properly scaled atmosphere thickness the width of the terminator transition will be far smaller, but nevertheless I'd love to entirely banish the banding if it doesn't overly impact performance. I don't expect this will be too hard, we should be able to crank up resolution of some areas of the shader pipeline to eliminate this banding; if there are harder design limitations then I imagine that I shall hit them head on in short order and report back.
