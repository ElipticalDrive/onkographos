Planetary Terrain Laboratory
The voxel experiment has crossed an important threshold: the generated world is now large enough to feel like a world, but the current representation is still a voxel demonstration rather than the eventual open-world substrate.
Two separate performance axes
1. Detail density
How much terrain detail can the user see at interactive rates?
Investigate voxel/block surfaces, smoothed continuous surfaces, chunked surface meshes, distance-based LOD, and GPU-generated surface data where measurement justifies it.
2. World scale
How large can the traversable world become?
Investigate deterministic chunk coordinates, streaming/generation ahead of the player, distant buffered representations, coordinate/origin management, event-horizon update rates, memory residency, and eviction.
A huge world and a detailed local surface are independent capabilities.
Surface representation
The current voxel renderer creates triangles for every exposed voxel face. That is useful as a substrate experiment, but the next representation should investigate smooth terrain: marching cubes, surface nets, dual contouring-class approaches, chunk-local meshes, deterministic border ownership, crack-free LOD transitions, and cached/rebuilt chunks rather than one monolithic mesh.
Target boundary: C# Workshop authority/state -> thin browser GPU boundary -> GPU. The browser layer should not become the home of planetary rules.
Chunking
Conceptually: World -> Region -> Chunk -> Surface -> GPU.
The player moves through a continuously reassembled world. Chunks near the observer receive the finest representation; distant chunks can remain coarse or buffered.
Immersion
The open-world test should eventually support continuous walking, terrain generated before the player reaches it, no visible hard scene boundaries, local high-detail terrain, progressively cheaper distant terrain, and eventually vegetation, water, weather, geological events, and life occupying the same world.
The first immersive world contains terrain only. Life comes later.
Geological formation MicroBundles
Do not reduce geology to strings such as canyon.
A named terrain/formation concept should be representable as a MicroBundle containing, where appropriate: ontology identity, formation classification, deterministic generation parameters/rules, mathematical/process explanation, observational characteristics, factual geological information, visual manifestation providers, relationships to other formations/processes, and provenance/reference metadata.
Research examples: canyon, gorge, gulf, strait, lake, ocean, cave, underwater cave, karst formation, volcanic caldera, delta, alluvial fan, fjord, basin, plateau, dune field.
The distinction is important: a formation bundle describes what a formation is; a process bundle describes how processes such as erosion, sedimentation, volcanism, tectonics, and water flow create/change it.
Geological simulation table
The laboratory should not be constrained to a conventional screen-sized level.
Research floors can become large simulation spaces: wave/water-flow table, erosion/sedimentation table, tectonic interaction table, volcanic table, underground cave/karst volume, and river/delta formation table.
These are physical/spatial manifestations of the same underlying simulation laws.
A formation such as a canyon should eventually be something the user can observe forming in simulation, inspect semantically, and compare with real-world geological knowledge.
Civilization layer
Later, civilization MicroBundles can observe formations and assign authored names/classifications.
This gives us a distinction between physical structure, scientific classification, simulation process, and civilization naming/language.
Research questions
What is the visual/compute cost of smoothing the voxel substrate?
Which smooth-surface algorithm gives useful quality versus implementation/GPU cost?
Can chunks be generated faster than the observer can traverse them?
How much local detail can coexist with a vastly larger distant world?
Can deterministic chunk generation let us evict and regenerate terrain without storing the whole planet?
How should adjacent chunks share borders and transition between LODs?
Which terrain processes belong on CPU and which measurably benefit from GPU execution?
How do formation MicroBundles compose with process MicroBundles?
How large can a laboratory simulation table become before it needs the same chunk/event-horizon architecture as the open world?
Non-goals
Do not build a custom graphics engine.
Do not make Three.js the application's architectural center.
Do not move all computation to the GPU without measurement.
Do not hardcode a catalog of terrain names into the renderer.
Do not require foliage/life before the terrain substrate is proven.
Master remains untouched; development is the research branch.
