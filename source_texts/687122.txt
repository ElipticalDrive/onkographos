Abstract
Radio-frequency (RF) data synthesis predicts the received signal given transmitter and receiver positions, and is essential for wireless applications. Recent 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)-based methods achieve efficient synthesis at any transmitter location within a surveyed scene, but require a separate model for each
receiver. We therefore present RxGS, a shared 3DGS model that first learns
receiver-independent Gaussian geometry and then freezes it while learning directional radiance conditioned on receiver position. Its global conditioning branch
captures receiver-dependent effects shared across the scene, while a local branch
models per-scatterer variations from the receiver’s geometry and occlusion. The
shared geometry also supports batched rendering through a multi-receiver rasterizer.
Across RF datasets, RxGS predicts at receiver positions excluded from training
within the surveyed scene while significantly reducing training, inference, and
storage costs. Project page: https://kangyangg.com/projects/rxgs/.

Introduction
Radio-frequency (RF) data, such as received signal strength indicator (RSSI) and channel state
information (CSI), quantifies how RF signals propagate from a transmitter to a receiver in a scene [1].
Such data is essential for wireless networking and sensing applications [2–8]. For example, network
planning [3] requires coverage maps that evaluate signal strength across candidate transmitter and
receiver placements. Fingerprint-based localization [5, 8, 9] depends on databases of RF measurements collected across numerous transmitter positions at multiple receivers. However, acquiring
these measurements requires site surveys at thousands of transmitter–receiver positions, which is
prohibitively labor-intensive and time-consuming [10–13].
RF data synthesis reduces the need for exhaustive site surveys. Classical ray tracing simulations [14–
17] trace propagation paths through Computer-Aided Design (CAD) scene models. However, accurate
CAD models require precise scene geometry, material permittivity, and conductivity. These properties
are difficult to obtain in practice, making simulations infeasible.
Neural scene representations sidestep these requirements by learning RF scene responses from
measurements [5, 18–21]. This enables synthesis of novel RF data via differentiable rendering,
without precise CAD models or material priors. Neural Radiance Field (NeRF)-based RF methods [5,
21] pioneer this direction but incur prohibitive computational costs from dense volumetric sampling
and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) inference. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)-based RF methods [18–
20] replace volumetric sampling with 3D Gaussians for faster training and inference.
The rendering interface of 3DGS also shapes how these methods represent the two endpoints of an RF
link. Optical 3DGS [22–24] is parameterized by a single variable, the camera viewpoint, since the
scene is queried from one viewpoint at a time. RF data synthesis instead depends on two variables, the
transmitter position and the receiver position. Existing 3DGS-based RF methods expose the densely
surveyed moving endpoint as an input and absorb the other, fixed endpoint into model weights. In our
data, the moving transmitters are surveyed across thousands of positions, while the receivers occupy a

smaller set of fixed positions. Without an input pathway for the fixed receiver position, these models
learn receiver-specific radiance coefficients, as detailed in §2.
However, multi-receiver deployments in a scene are the norm in practice [11, 25, 26]. For example,
a Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) localization system deploys dozens of gateways distributed across a
facility [5]. With a separate checkpoint for each receiver, the costs of a multi-receiver deployment
grow in two ways. ❶ Scaling Cost: synthesizing RF data for N receivers within one scene requires
training N independent models. ❷ Adding New Receivers. For per-receiver 3DGS methods, a new
fixed receiver in the same scene requires a full transmitter survey and its own checkpoint.
We present RxGS, a 3DGS-based RF data synthesis framework that conditions one shared model on
fixed receiver locations within a surveyed scene. Our key insight is that scene geometry is receiverindependent while directional radiance is not: scatterers such as walls and furniture remain fixed
regardless of receiver position, but the angles and distances to the receiver change. This motivates
a two-stage training procedure. Stage I learns shared 3D Gaussian geometry of the scene. Stage II
freezes this geometry and jointly learns receiver-conditioned directional radiance.
When the receiver moves, both the global radiance pattern and each Gaussian’s individual contribution
change. To capture these effects, we propose two complementary conditioning modules. A global
module modulates harmonic coefficients based on receiver position, capturing the shared component
of receiver-dependent variation. A local module adjusts each Gaussian based on its direction,
distance, and occlusion relative to the receiver, capturing per-scatterer residuals. Both modules are
radiance-representation-agnostic and apply to existing 3DGS-based RF methods, including spherical
harmonics (SH), Fourier–Legendre expansions (FLE), and deformation networks [18–20]. For the SH
and FLE backbones, a batched rasterizer uses the Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) to
render N receiver outputs in parallel while sharing projection and sorting.
RxGS introduces explicit fixed-receiver conditioning to 3DGS-based RF synthesis, enabling predictions at receiver locations excluded from training within the surveyed region. On RSSI, it improves
the RF-3DGS [18] and GSRF [20] per-receiver baselines. With the WRF-GS+ [19] backbone, it
eliminates two high-error outliers with a 0.33 dBm increase in mean absolute error (MAE). On spatial
spectrum and CSI, it trails per-receiver baselines by 0.8 to 1.7 dB. Training is up to 45× faster,
batched FLE/SH inference up to 7.6× faster, and storage up to 24× smaller.

and a fixed receiver endpoint. In our datasets, the moving endpoint is the transmitter at t and the fixed

Related Work
NeRF-Based RF Data Synthesis. NeRF2 [5] pioneers neural RF data synthesis by representing the
scene as a continuous volumetric function parameterized by two large MLPs for attenuation and radiance. Several subsequent works study neural RF synthesis [21, 29–32]. For example, NeWRF [21]
leverages direction-of-arrival (DoA) priors to reduce the number of emitted rays, improving computational efficiency. GRaF [29] interpolates spatial spectra from geographically proximate transmitters,
but requires reference spectra at the query receiver at inference, preventing zero-shot prediction at
unseen receivers. NeRF-based methods such as NeRF2 represent receiver position through the ray
origin and sampled directions, allowing queries at previously unseen receivers. Dense volumetric
sampling and repeated MLP queries raise training and inference costs [33, 34].
3DGS-Based RF Data Synthesis. Existing 3DGS-based RF methods [18–20, 35, 36] fall into two
categories based on whether neural networks are adopted. WRF-GS+ [19] uses a deformation network
to predict offsets for Gaussian attributes from Gaussian and transmitter positions, and SwiftWRF [35]
follows a similar architecture with 2D Gaussians for faster rendering. RF-3DGS [18] and GSRF [20]
avoid neural networks. RF-3DGS learns geometry from camera images, then retrains SH coefficients
from RF measurements. GSRF optimizes per-Gaussian FLE radiance coefficients [28] as learnable
complex-valued parameters. These methods supply the moving endpoint as a spatial query and
encode the fixed endpoint in a checkpoint, as described in §2. A multi-receiver deployment therefore
trains one checkpoint per fixed endpoint. WRF-GS+ labels that fixed endpoint as the receiver,
while RF-3DGS and GSRF label it as the transmitter.
Radio Maps from Scene Layouts. RadioUNet [47] predicts scalar pathloss maps from 2D occupancy
maps and transmitter positions using simulated data. By contrast, RxGS synthesizes RSSI, spatial
spectra, and CSI from RF measurements, including floorplan-free BLE data.
Relighting in Neural Rendering. Generalizing across receiver positions in RF synthesis is structurally analogous to relighting in optical neural rendering, where both must generalize across a
variable baked into the learned representation. Several works address this by replacing the standard
rendering equation with a physically-based rendering (PBR) equation that takes lighting as an explicit input [37–41]. For instance, Relightable 3DGS [39] augments each Gaussian with surface
normals, bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) parameters, and incident light to
compute outgoing radiance under arbitrary illumination. Optical relighting uses BRDF-based priors,
whereas RF multipath calls for a receiver-dependent signal model tailored to propagation. RxGS
therefore introduces lightweight learned conditioning modules to capture these receiver-dependent
effects. Its global module plays a role analogous to a learned environment map, while the global-only
and additive variants in Table 3 isolate the effects of the two conditioning choices.

4.1 Geometry-Radiance Decomposition Workflow
The key insight behind RxGS is that the 3D Gaussian attributes partition into two groups with
distinct dependencies. The geometric attributes, position pk , covariance Ck , and transmittance τk ,
describe where scatterers are located, their spatial extent, and how they attenuate signals. These are
physical properties of the environment and are independent of the receiver position. The radiance
coefficients ϕk , in contrast, encode the directional signal response at each Gaussian and change with
receiver position. Appendix A provides a theoretical analysis showing that, under the rendering
abstraction adopted by 3DGS-based RF methods, receiver-dependent variation is naturally expressed
through the radiance term while the geometric attributes remain receiver-independent. Within this
rendering abstraction, receiver-dependent effects are assigned to radiance while shared geometry
supports all receivers. This motivates the two-stage training procedure illustrated in Figure 1.

4.3.2 Conditioning Architecture
We now instantiate the corollaries as architectural components. Given a receiver position r, the conditioning function gΘ proceeds in three steps: (i) r is mapped to a feature γ(r) via a learnable Fourier
encoding that captures the two-scale structure of ηeff . (ii) A global branch applies affine modulation
to all Gaussians based on γ(r), as required by Corollaries 1 and 2. (iii) A local branch refines
each Gaussian individually using per-scatterer geometric and propagation features, as required by
Corollary 1, with shared weights across all Gaussians by Corollary 3.

4.4
Single-Kernel Multi-Receiver Rasterization
Since the Stage I geometry is receiver-independent, per-Gaussian projection, depth sorting, and basis
evaluation at d̂k depend only on the transmitter and are shared across all N receivers. For the FLE
and SH backbones, receiver-specific radiance ϕk (d̂k , rj ) is evaluated in a batched operation after
shared geometry work. Their rasterizer launches one CUDA kernel that tiles the rendering sphere
along elevation θ and azimuth ϕ, with gridDim = (tilesθ , tilesϕ , N ). The receiver index maps to the
third grid dimension, with each block reading its signal slice and writing its output slot. The shared
projection and sorting work is amortized across receiver queries. The third grid dimension scales
with N to evaluate receiver-specific radiance and write N outputs, as detailed in Appendix D.

Implementation. We implement RxGS in PyTorch with custom CUDA kernels for RF signal
tracing and rasterization. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. Full
implementation and hyperparameter details are in Appendix E.

6
Conclusion and Future Work
RxGS learns shared 3DGS geometry and receiver-conditioned radiance for fixed receivers in a
surveyed scene. It predicts at receiver positions excluded from training using one model instead of N
receiver-specific checkpoints. On RSSI, it improves RF-3DGS and GSRF, while its WRF-GS+ variant
eliminates two high-error outliers with a 0.33 dBm increase in mean MAE. On spatial spectrum
and CSI, it trails per-receiver baselines by 0.8 to 1.7 dB. Training, inference, and storage costs
decrease by up to 45×, 7.6×, and 24×, respectively.
The method assumes a static surveyed scene, requires a reference-receiver survey in each new
environment, and models last-segment receiver effects within the 3DGS rendering abstraction.
Angular-spectrum experiments use simulated data, while measured CSI spans eight effective receivers
in one room. Future work will evaluate measured multi-receiver spectra, larger and dynamic scenes,
full-multipath variation, computational scaling, and cross-environment transfer.

We implement RxGS in PyTorch, with custom CUDA kernels for RF signal tracing and rasterization to support the differentiable RF rendering pipeline. All experiments are conducted on a
single NVIDIA H100 GPU. This section describes the implementation of the FLE-based scene
representation. Other representation variants (SH) and signal modalities (RSSI, CSI) follow the same
training pipeline and optimization strategy. Dataset-specific hyperparameters are reported in Table 5.

Both training stages use a modality-specific loss: L1 for scalar RSSI, L2 for complex-valued CSI,

E.4
Stage I: Geometry Learning
Stage I trains all Gaussian parameters using measurements from a single reference receiver. At each
iteration, a random training sample from the reference receiver is selected, and the rendered signal is
compared with the measurement using the modality-specific loss.
Optimizer. We use Adam with per-parameter learning rates listed in Table 5. The position LR
decays exponentially from its initial to final value with a delay multiplier of 0.01. Higher-degree FLE
coefficients are additionally scaled by 0.2 to prioritize low-frequency geometry early in training.
The FLE degree is progressively increased from 0, incrementing every Tramp iterations up to ℓmax .
Stage I runs for 30K iterations total.
Adaptive Densification. Gaussians are densified based on accumulated positional gradients every 100 iterations, from iteration 500 to the stage midpoint (iteration 15K). Gaussians exceeding the
densification threshold τg listed in Table 5 are cloned if their scale is below the scene extent threshold,
or split into two children with scales reduced by factor 0.8 if above. Gaussians with projected
screen-space radius above a pixel threshold, or world-space extent exceeding 0.1× the scene extent,
are pruned. To avoid saturation, transmittance resets to σ −1 (0.01) as specified in Table 5.
E.5
Stage II: Receiver-Conditioned Radiance
With geometry frozen, Stage II trains the FLE coefficients and the conditioning modules across all
receivers. At each iteration, a random training sample from an available receiver is selected. The
conditioning modules then modulate the base FLE coefficients using its position, and the modalityspecific loss compares the rendered signal with the corresponding measurement.
Global Branch. Table 5 specifies F frequency bands per spatial dimension for the learnable Fourier
encoding of receiver position. The bands start at 2k spacing and are optimized during training. For
each FLE component, a 3-layer ReLU MLP with hidden dimension d takes the concatenation of
the encoded RX position, per-component degree and order features, and a learnable per-component
embedding of dimension dc as input. The MLP produces per-component scale and shift parameters
that modulate the base FLE coefficients. This modulation is computed once per component and
broadcast to all K Gaussians, giving O (L) cost independent of Gaussian count.

each frozen Gaussian into a voxel density field using its position, transmittance, and scale with a 2σ
radius. For each Gaussian k, we cast a ray toward the receiver and probe the grid at S uniformly spaced

F.2
RFID Spatial Spectrum
Collecting real-world spatial spectrum data requires a calibrated antenna array and precise TX
positioning across hundreds of positions, making large-scale data collection prohibitively expensive.
Furthermore, no public multi-receiver spatial spectrum dataset currently exists. We therefore generate
this dataset via simulation using the NVIDIA Sionna ray tracer [42, 43]. Sionna is a physics-based,
differentiable ray tracing engine that models line-of-sight, specular reflection, diffuse scattering, and
refraction, with material properties calibrated to ITU-R standards. Its GPU acceleration enables
large-scale dataset generation at simulation speeds.
The scene is a cluttered conference room (8 m × 6 m × 3 m), as shown in Figure 5. Each measurement
is a 90 × 360 grayscale image capturing the angular power spectrum over elevation and azimuth
at 2.4 GHz. The dataset contains NTX = 5,089 TX positions and NRX = 21 distributed RX positions
at z = 2 m height, yielding NTX × NRX = 106,869 spectrum images in total. We use an 80/20
random split (4,071 train / 1,018 test TX positions).

Scene Construction. Each scene is modeled as a rectangular room with six surfaces (floor, ceiling,
four walls), built as Mitsuba triangle meshes. Furniture items including tables, chairs, bookshelves,
metal cabinets, and glass partitions are represented as axis-aligned box meshes. Each object is
assigned an ITU radio material (e.g., concrete, plasterboard, wood, metal, glass) with calibrated
relative permittivity, conductivity, and scattering coefficients following the ITU-R P.2040 standard.
Channel Simulation. For each TX–RX pair, we place a single isotropic TX antenna and, at
the RX, an 8 × 8 uniform planar array (UPA) with half-wavelength spacing at f = 2.4 GHz.
Sionna’s PathSolver performs ray tracing with up to 5 bounces, including line-of-sight, specular
reflection, diffuse reflection, and refraction. The solver returns H ∈ CNant ×Nsub with Nant = 64
antennas and Nsub = 512 subcarriers across 100 MHz.

phase and amplitude errors (±15◦ , ±1.5 dB) are introduced for realism. The spatial covariance matrix
is estimated as Rspec = HHH /Nsub . For each scan direction (φ, θ) on a 1◦ -resolution grid covering

F.3
WiFi CSI
Prior work [5, 19] uses the Argos dataset [46] for CSI evaluation, where all antennas are colocated at a single position. For multi-receiver evaluation, we use the KU Leuven Ultra-Dense Indoor MaMIMO CSI Dataset [44], collected using a 64-antenna massive MIMO testbed operating at a
center frequency of 2.61 GHz with 20 MHz bandwidth. The original measurements comprise 252,004
channel snapshots over a 1.25 m × 1.25 m transmitter grid at 5 mm spatial resolution, with 100
complex subcarriers per antenna. In the distributed line-of-sight scenario, eight groups of eight
antennas surround a ∼5 m × 5 m room in an octagonal layout at z = 1 m.
Preprocessing. We apply five preprocessing steps to the raw data. First, phase calibration removes
the random carrier phase offset per antenna, ensuring coherent combination across elements. Second,
the 8 antennas within each group are coherently averaged after calibration, yielding one effective RX
per group and NRX = 8 distributed RXs in total. Third, delay-domain denoising truncates the
channel impulse response to the first 10 of 100 taps via IFFT, truncation, and FFT, suppressing noisedominated late arrivals that carry no useful propagation information. Fourth, the 100 subcarriers are
averaged into Nsc = 26 frequency bins to reduce redundancy while preserving the frequency-selective
fading structure. Fifth, nearby TX positions are spatially averaged into NTX = 6,000 output positions
to reduce wavelength-scale phase variation and produce a spatially consistent dataset suitable for
scene-level learning.
23
Final Dataset. The resulting dataset contains NTX = 6,000 TX positions and NRX = 8 distributed RX positions, yielding NTX × NRX = 48,000 complex CSI vectors of dimension 52 (Nsc =
26 subcarriers × 2 for real and imaginary), normalized by the global maximum magnitude. We use
an 80/20 random split (4,800 train / 1,200 test TX positions).

G.3
Computational Cost: Detailed Breakdown
We expand on Table 2 and explain why the gains differ across backbones.
Implicit versus explicit scene representations. Table 2 shows different storage and compute profiles
for implicit and explicit models. NeRF2 stores only 2.6 MB but takes 14.3 h to train and 2436 ms to
render N = 21 spectra. GSRF + RxGS stores 26 MB yet trains in 0.6 h and renders in 31 ms. The
two paradigms swap their cost profiles:
• Implicit methods (NeRF2 , GRaF) encode the entire scene as a continuous function of position
and direction using a small MLP. Each transmitter-receiver sample casts one ray per pixel and
integrates by querying the MLP at O(64–256) depth points, totaling roughly 2 M MLP passes for
a single 90 × 360 spectrum, with the same number of backward passes during training. Storage
is small (only MLP weights), but compute scales as O(rays · depth_samples · MLP_FLOPs)
and grows linearly with output resolution.
• Explicit methods (RF-3DGS, GSRF, WRF-GS+) parameterize the scene as 10k to 40k Gaussian primitives, each carrying position, scale, rotation, opacity, and a directional coefficient
vector with L = 16 to 100 entries. Storage scales as O(K · L) and dominates total bytes, but
the CUDA rasterizer projects all Gaussians once, sorts them per tile, and runs a single perpixel alpha-blend pass with O(Gaussians overlapping the tile) work, typically tens of Gaussians
per tile. The pipeline is memory-bandwidth bound and parallelizes well on a GPU. For one
transmitter, the RxGS variants synthesize signals at all N = 21 receivers in 31 to 43 ms.
Implicit methods use less storage but require more computation per sample, whereas explicit methods
use more storage with lower per-sample computation.
Training. RxGS totals 90k iterations per backbone: 30k for Stage I geometry pre-training and 60k
for Stage II multi-receiver fitting. The per-receiver baselines run N independent fits of Tbase iterations
each, with Tbase = 200k for WRF-GS+ and 30k for GSRF and RF-3DGS. The raw iteration ratio
is N · Tbase /90k, with measured speedups also reflecting per-iteration cost differences.
26
• RF-3DGS: 2.8 → 0.4 h, a 7× speedup that matches the raw iteration ratio of 21·30k/90k = 7×
almost exactly, with similar per-iteration cost for Stage II and per-receiver training.
• GSRF: 5.5 → 0.6 h, a 9× speedup against the same 7× raw ratio. The extra factor comes
from Stage II running per-iteration slightly faster than per-receiver GSRF, which we attribute to
amortizing FLE-basis evaluation across the batched receiver dimension.
• WRF-GS+: 13.6 → 0.3 h, a 45× speedup against a raw iteration ratio of 21 · 200k/90k ≈ 47×.
Multi-receiver overhead explains the roughly 4% difference. The main gain comes from one 90kiteration run instead of 21 separate 200k-iteration fits.
NeRF2 and GRaF are already shared models without N -way training. Their 14 to 16 h costs reflect
roughly 600k iterations of volumetric sampling and backpropagation, consistent with the implicit
paradigm above.
Inference. For the GSRF and RF-3DGS backbones, the multi-receiver rasterizer detailed in Appendix D renders all N receivers in one CUDA call. The per-method speedup over the per-receiver
baseline depends on how much of the original cost was sharable versus per-pixel work:
• GSRF: 238 → 31 ms, a 7.6× speedup that is the largest of the three because FLE-degree 9
with L = 100 components per Gaussian makes basis evaluation the dominant per-receiver cost.
Our batched eval_fle now performs this evaluation once per transmitter and reuses it across
all N receivers, eliminating the most expensive sharable component.
• RF-3DGS: 156 → 35 ms, a 4.4× speedup. At SH degree 4 with L = 25, basis evaluation is
less costly, and most savings arise from shared preprocessing and tile sorting rather than the
per-pixel kernel.
• WRF-GS+: 52 → 43 ms, only a 1.2× speedup. In the measured implementation, the receiverindependent DeformModel step is shared, whereas Gaussian rasterization runs separately for
each receiver in Python. A native multi-receiver rasterizer offers a route to additional inference
savings for this backbone.
The cost breakdown also explains two ordering effects. First, GSRF + RxGS ends up faster than RF3DGS + RxGS at 31 versus 35 ms, despite per-receiver GSRF being slower than per-receiver RF3DGS at 11.3 versus 7.45 ms. Once basis evaluation is amortized, the rasterizer dominates, and rasterizer cost scales with the number of Gaussians, of which RF-3DGS has more at 37.7k versus 31.8k.
Second, NeRF2 and GRaF have no batchable structure across receivers. Their per-transmitter cost
remains N times the per-sample cost, yielding the 60 to 1900× differences reported in the table.
Storage. Per-receiver baselines store N independent Gaussian-state checkpoints, whereas RxGS
stores one. The deployable footprint decreases by roughly a factor of N :
• GSRF: 548 → 26 MB, a 21× reduction. The absolute RxGS size is the largest of the three
because GSRF carries K = 32k Gaussians at L = 100 FLE components, and storage scales
as K · L · C · 4 bytes plus roughly 10K bytes for geometry attributes.
• RF-3DGS: 182 → 8.8 MB, a 21× reduction. Although RF-3DGS has more Gaussians
than GSRF (K = 38k), its smaller L = 25 yields a smaller model.
• WRF-GS+: 75.6 → 3.2 MB, a 24× reduction. The smallest absolute size comes from K = 14k
at L = 16, plus a 2.2 MB DeformModel. The slightly higher reduction ratio reflects the same N
collapse at smaller absolute scale.
The 3.2 → 8.8 → 26 MB ordering of the RxGS rows therefore tracks K · L rather than the perreceiver baseline ranking. NeRF2 and GRaF are shared models that do not multiply with N but
pay this saving back in inference compute. Their sizes differ substantially (2.6 MB for NeRF2
versus 143.7 MB for GRaF), because GRaF stores training-receiver reference spectra.
Receiver-count scaling. Per-receiver baselines require N separately trained and stored models. The
shared approach stores one model and uses a batched rasterization call for the FLE and SH backbones.
Stage II training processes receiver-dependent samples, batched inference writes N outputs and
performs some receiver-dependent work, and the WRF-GS+ variant retains a render loop. Model
storage is shared, while training and inference costs vary with receiver count and backbone. Table 2
measures the resulting savings at N = 21.
27
Receiver-Conditioned Scene MLPs. NeRF2 [5] conditions a scene MLP on the receiver,
while GRaF [29] conditions on both endpoints. This approach incurs per-ray volumetric sampling and MLP evaluation at each query. At the measured N = 21 setting, Table 2 shows that NeRF2
and GRaF take roughly 60 to 1900× longer per transmitter than the shared 3DGS variants. RxGS
retains the explicit 3DGS scene representation and applies receiver-conditioned modulation around
frozen geometry. The global and local branches require O(L + K) network evaluations per receiver
query, consistent with §4.3. The rasterizer in Appendix D shares transmitter-side projection and
sorting across receivers. The two-stage decomposition trains geometry once and restricts receiver
dependence to radiance.

data at every possible position.

Sensitivity to Reference Receiver Choice
Setting. Stage I requires a reference receiver to learn the scene geometry. Deployment of RxGS
requires selecting a reference receiver for Stage I. We therefore evaluate the sensitivity of final
performance to this selection. We compare 21 single-receiver reference choices against an averagedsignal initialization that pools measurements across all receivers, training the full pipeline on the BLE
dataset under each choice. Stage II is trained across all 21 receivers with the same hyperparameters
in every run, and we report per-receiver MAE averaged across all 21 test receivers.
Results. Figure 11 reports the resulting per-receiver MAE. The single-reference variants yield 3.94 ±
0.13 dBm across a range of [3.67, 4.22] dBm, a 0.55 dBm spread or about 15% of the best singlereference MAE. The narrow spread across the 21 tested choices is consistent with the receiverindependent geometry representation derived in Appendix A. The averaged-signal initialization
performs slightly better at 3.57 dBm, since pooling across receivers averages out per-receiver measurement noise and yields a cleaner geometry-learning signal. We therefore adopt the averaged-signal
initialization as the default for all main-paper experiments. Each single-reference run learns Stage I
geometry using only one receiver without signal averaging, and its MAE is 0.10 to 0.65 dBm above
the 3.57 dBm averaged default. Averaging is a refinement rather than a requirement and uses only measurements already available to Stage II. In practice, however, either choice would yield comparable
performance, and a practitioner can use whichever is most convenient for their deployment.

G.7
Application Study: Network Planning
Setting. A common task for a building owner is to choose where to install a small set of access points
so that wireless coverage is maximized across all client positions. Without an accurate channel model,
the planner is restricted to deploying access points at positions already surveyed. With RxGS, the
planner can score unmeasured candidates and select higher-coverage sites.
As in §5.2, we use a 3-fold split of the 21-gateway BLE RSSI dataset into 14 trained gateways and 7
gateways excluded from training. For each fold we randomly sample K = 5 surveyed gateways
from the 14 trained ones and compare three deployment strategies. S URVEY-O NLY deploys at the 5
surveyed positions with no model and no choice. O URS runs greedy maximum-coverage selection
over all 21 candidate positions, using real RSSI for the 5 surveyed gateways and RxGS predictions
for the other 16. U PPER B OUND runs the same greedy selection but uses real RSSI at all 21 gateways,
giving a full-survey oracle. Once a deployment is chosen, we evaluate it on the test transmitters by
computing each transmitter’s strongest real RSSI among the 5 deployed access points, which is the
standard max-power association in wireless networks. Figure 12 renders one representative fold
as a 24 × 32 grid heatmap with cell size of about 1.6 × 1.6 m. Each cell is colored by the fraction
of transmitters whose strongest-AP RSSI exceeds −80 dBm, and cells with no test transmitters are
shown in gray.

We quantify similarity to the oracle by the average per-cell absolute coverage difference, denoted

the candidate set while retaining the same five-gateway survey budget.
Practical Survey Guidance. The measured-to-virtual balance depends on anchor diversity, synthesis
fidelity, learnability of receiver effects, and how well the model represents the scene. A practical
survey begins with spatially distributed gateways spanning distinct propagation conditions, then uses
virtual gateways to densify candidates within the surveyed region. Withholding some measured
gateways during model selection can reveal weakly supported areas and guide additional measurements until unseen-gateway accuracy begins to saturate. Figure 10 shows diminishing returns beyond
about 12 gateways in this scene. Jointly choosing real and virtual sites remains open.

Broader Impacts
RxGS reduces the cost of collecting RF data for wireless networking and sensing. Site surveys at
thousands of transmitter–receiver positions are labor-intensive and time-consuming. Synthesizing RF
data at unmeasured fixed receiver positions inside a static, surveyed environment could reduce
the measurement burden for wireless planning and indoor localization, as illustrated in §5.4 and
Appendix G.7. The coverage estimates may also guide infrastructure placement.
A future application is connectivity-aware planning for mobile agents within a static, previously
surveyed environment. A shared model could score candidate receiver positions inside the surveyed
region without fitting a new checkpoint at each location. Future work will evaluate continuous
receiver motion, changing environments, and robotic deployments.
The same capability could be misused for passive localization or tracking without consent if an
attacker combines synthesized fingerprints with captured signals. This risk is not unique to RxGS
and applies to any RF fingerprinting method, but practitioners deploying fingerprint databases should
follow standard privacy safeguards such as on-device inference, aggregation, and informed consent.
