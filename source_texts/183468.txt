ABSTRACT
This thesis develops an octree-based adaptive framework for efficient compressible flow simula­
tion within the Dendrite finite element environment. A stabilized continuous-Galerkin formulation
of the compressible Navier-Stokes equations is implemented using pressure-primitive variables with
a reduced internal energy representation and is coupled with BDF2 time integration, SUPG sta­
bilization, discontinuity-capturing, and octree-based adaptive mesh refinement. Four refinement
strategies are examined: a Pressure-Velocity-Temperature sensor indicator, a Verfürth-type resid­
ual indicator, a Hessian-type Mach feature-based indicator, and a Hybrid indicator. The framework
is assessed on supersonic flow over a flat plate, an oblique shock problem, and subsonic flow over a
NACA 0012 airfoil, and is further demonstrated on a torpedo-shaped body application in a super­
sonic flow regime. The results show that the developed formulation produces stable and physically
meaningful solutions across these different compressible flow regimes. Although the principal solu­
tion quantities remain similar across the AMR strategies in most cases, the mesh sizes, core-hour
costs, and compression ratios differ substantially. Among the indicators considered, the Hessiantype indicator provides the best overall balance of solution quality, selective refinement, mesh
compression, and computational efficiency. Overall, this work establishes a practical compressible
flow capability in Dendrite and shows that octree-based adaptive refinement is an effective strategy
for efficient high-speed finite element simulation.

CHAPTER 1.
INTRODUCTION
Compressible flow simulations play a vital role in aerospace, propulsion, and high-speed fluid
mechanics. Unlike incompressible flow, compressible flow has important flow features such as
shocks, expansion fans, contact discontinuities, shear layers, wakes, and thermal boundary layers
Feistauer et al. (2003). These features are not just mathematical complications, but they also
directly control quantities of engineering interest. A defining difficulty of compressible flow sim­
ulation is its multiscale character. In many practical configurations, only a small fraction of the
computational domain contains the strongest solution features, such as shocks, near-wall viscous
layers, separation regions, or wakes, while the rest of the field varies comparatively smoothly. If
the entire domain is discretized at the resolution required by the strongest gradients, the resulting
simulations become unnecessarily expensive in memory, runtime, and solver cost. What this really
means is that efficient compressible simulation is not only a question of having a stable governing
equation discretization, but also of placing computational resolution only where the physics requires
it.
Adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) provides a natural response to this challenge Berger and
Colella (1989). By refining the mesh locally in regions of strong solution variation and maintaining
coarser resolution elsewhere, AMR offers a route to improved accuracy at significantly lower cost
than uniform refinement. For large-scale simulations, octree-based AMR is especially attractive
because it combines hierarchical refinement, locality of mesh operations, and strong parallel scala­
bility Nikishkov (2004). These features are well-suited to time-dependent flow problems in which
the important solution structures move, strengthen, or weaken over time. However, the practical
success of AMR depends on more than the refinement criterion alone. The overall framework must
also provide robust intergrid transfer, balanced mesh operations, efficient parallel repartitioning,
stable finite element assembly on octree meshes, and reliable nonlinear and linear solvers.
2
Within this setting, the Dendrite framework has emerged as a promising platform for scalable
octree-based finite element simulation. The broader Dendrite ecosystem, together with Dendro-KT
Gamdha et al. (2023); Ishii et al. (2019) and PETSc Balay et al. (2001) based solver infrastruc­
ture, has already demonstrated several important capabilities on adaptive octree meshes, including
traversal-based intergrid transfer, dynamic repartitioning, weak boundary treatment through the
shifted boundary method (SBM) Main and Scovazzi (2018), and advances in handling hanging
nodes and conservative coarsening. Existing work in this ecosystem has shown strong capability
for incompressible flow, coupled thermal flow problems, and other multiphysics formulations on
complex geometries. These developments provide a solid computational foundation for extending
the framework to more demanding classes of problems.
At the same time, high-speed compressible flow places additional requirements on the discretiza­
tion that are not present, or are less severe, in incompressible formulations. Compressible solvers
must remain stable in the presence of strong advective transport, pressure waves, steep thermal
gradients, and potentially shock-dominated solution fields. They must also support physically
meaningful open-boundary treatment, robust nonlinear convergence, and refinement criteria that
respond to compressibility-driven features rather than only vortical structures. In stabilized finite
element settings, this typically requires careful choices of governing variables, temporal discretiza­
tion, weak-form construction, stabilization operators, discontinuity-capturing terms, and boundary
condition implementation. Although the Dendrite framework already contains many of the ingre­
dients needed for scalable adaptive simulation, a dedicated compressible flow capability must be
designed and assessed on its own terms.
The present thesis addresses that need by developing and studying an octree-based adaptive
framework for efficient compressible flow simulation within Dendrite. The implementation is built
around a pressure primitive finite element formulation of the compressible Navier-Stokes equations,
using a reduced internal energy representation together with stabilized space-time operators suitable
for high-speed flow. The resulting solver is coupled to an octree AMR workflow so that refinement
can be directed toward compressibility-relevant features such as sharp pressure gradients, localized
3
residual concentrations, and shock-associated curvature in the Mach number field. In this way, the
thesis is concerned not only with whether compressible flow can be solved on an adaptive octree
mesh, but with which adaptive strategy is most effective for this class of problems when accuracy,
mesh complexity, and computational cost are considered together.
1.1
Problem Statement
Although octree AMR has been widely recognized as an efficient strategy for multiscale sim­
ulation, its use in stabilized continuous-Galerkin compressible flow finite elements remains com­
paratively less mature than fixed grid or body-fitted approaches. In particular, the current Den­
drite literature has primarily established the framework for incompressible flow, thermal transport,
surrogate-boundary treatment, hanging-node management, and conservative field transfer on adap­
tive octree meshes Bielak et al. (2005); Sampath and Biros (2010); COIRIER (1994); Khokhlov
(1998). A comparable compressible flow capability must reconcile several additional challenges:
stable discretization of the compressible Navier-Stokes equations, robust treatment of advective
dominance and steep gradients, physically meaningful open boundaries, and refinement indicators
that remain effective across shocks, boundary layers, and smooth wave regions.
A second part of the problem is methodological rather than purely implementational. Even once
a compressible solver is available, it is not obvious which AMR indicator is most appropriate for
compressible flow on octree meshes. A sensor that works well for incompressible vortical structures
Yang et al. (2025) may under-resolve a shock or thermal layer. Likewise, a curvature-based indicator
may sharply identify shocks but miss broader residual-driven regions that still influence solution
quality. Because of this, the choice of AMR logic becomes part of the scientific question. An
efficient compressible octree framework therefore requires both a stable compressible formulation
and a clear assessment of how different refinement strategies perform for representative benchmark
problems.
4
1.2
Research Objectives
The main objective of this thesis is to develop an efficient and scalable compressible flow sim­
ulation capability within the Dendrite octree AMR framework and to evaluate its performance for
representative high-speed flow problems. To achieve this goal, the work pursues four connected
objectives.
First, a stabilized finite element formulation for the compressible Navier-Stokes equations is im­
plemented in Dendrite using pressure primitive variables and a reduced internal energy form. This
includes the construction of the transformation, advective, stress-power, and diffusive operators
required by the formulation, together with consistent residual and Jacobian assembly. Second, the
compressible formulation is coupled with appropriate temporal and stabilization machinery, includ­
ing BDF2 time integration, SUPG-type matrix stabilization, and discontinuity-capturing diffusion,
so that the solver remains robust in the presence of strong gradients and high-speed flow structures.
Third, the compressible solver is integrated with multiple octree-based AMR strategies, including
sensor-based, residual-based, feature-based, and hybrid indicators, in order to study how different
refinement logics affect accuracy and computational cost. Fourth, the resulting framework is as­
sessed on representative compressible flow test cases in order to determine which adaptive strategy
offers the most effective balance among solution quality, mesh economy, and runtime efficiency for
the class of flows considered in this thesis.
1.3 Organization of the Thesis
The subsequent sections of this thesis are structured as follows: Chapter 2 reviews prior work
on stabilized finite element formulations for compressible flow, adaptive mesh refinement, and the
development of the Dendrite framework and related octree-based advances. Chapter 3 presents the
methodology adopted in this work, including the pressure primitive compressible formulation, weak
form, stabilization, boundary treatment, code-level implementation, and AMR indicators. Chapter
4 presents the verification, validation, and adaptive mesh comparison studies used to assess the
5
framework. Finally, Chapter 5 summarizes the main conclusions of the work and outlines directions
for future development of octree-based compressible simulation in Dendrite.

CHAPTER 2.
2.1
LITERATURE REVIEW
Background on Compressible Flow Finite Elements
The numerical simulation of compressible flow has long required discretizations that remain
accurate and stable across a wide range of regimes, from smooth subsonic transport to shockdominated high-speed flow. In the finite element setting, this requirement has driven the devel­
opment of stabilized formulations that go beyond the standard Galerkin method Donea (1984).
Among the most influential contributions is the work of Shakib et al. (1991b), who developed a
stabilized finite-element formulation for the compressible Euler and Navier-Stokes equations. Their
work established a systematic framework for combining advective operators, stabilization matri­
ces, and discontinuity-capturing terms in a form suitable for high-speed flow computation. This
formulation also highlighted the importance of weakly consistent treatment of boundary contribu­
tions, particularly at open boundaries where strong Dirichlet imposition can generate nonphysical
reflections. A second important line of development is the unified treatment of compressible and
incompressible flows presented by Hauke and Hughes (1998). This work clarified how variable se­
lection, quasi-linear operator structure, and stabilization design can be written in a form that spans
both flow regimes. For compressible flow solvers, the choice of variables is especially important.
Primitive variables and pressure-based formulations can simplify boundary treatment and improve
interpretability, but they must be supported by a careful definition of transformation matrices and
constitutive couplings. These ideas are directly relevant to pressure-primitive formulations in which
density is reconstructed from the ideal-gas relation rather than carried as an independent variable.
Later developments extended stabilized compressible finite elements to more complicated settings.
For example, Xu et al. (2017) developed stabilized formulations for compressible flow on moving
domains, including weak imposition of essential boundary conditions and additional geometric com­
plexity. Taken together, these studies show that successful compressible finite element simulation
7
depends on a combination of ingredients: a suitable variable set, a consistent quasi-linear opera­
tor form, robust temporal integration, stabilization of advective transport, discontinuity-capturing
near steep gradients, and carefully designed weak boundary terms. Building on these stabilized
compressible formulations, Xu et al. (2019) further extended the methodology to immersogeometric
analysis for compressible flows around complex geometries. Their contribution is important because
it addresses a major practical limitation of body-fitted simulation, namely the difficulty of generat­
ing high-quality meshes for geometrically intricate configurations. In their framework, compressible
flow is solved directly on CAD boundary representations embedded in a non-body-fitted background
mesh, while essential velocity and temperature boundary conditions are imposed weakly through a
Nitsche-type Apostolatos et al. (2014) formulation. A key aspect of the work is the use of a nonsymmetric Nitsche method for immersed boundaries, which reduces sensitivity to penalty-parameter
estimation in cut elements and is therefore especially attractive for non-body-fitted discretizations.
The formulation is written for compressible flow in pressure primitive variables on moving domains
and is combined with SUPG stabilization, discontinuity-capturing, adaptive quadrature for cut ele­
ments, and direct surface integration over B-rep geometries. Extending these ideas further, Rajanna
et al. (2022) applied stabilized compressible finite element technology to fluid-structure interaction
with nonmatching interface discretizations. Their strongly coupled framework used weak interface
enforcement to transfer kinematic and traction conditions between the compressible fluid and struc­
tural shell model, showing that the same stabilized methodology can support robust multiphysics
simulation in moving-domain and interface-dominated settings. Another recent development has
focused on weak wall boundary conditions for compressible flows. Jaiswal et al. (2026) revisited the
weak imposition of wall velocity and thermal boundary conditions in pressure primitive stabilized
formulations and showed that earlier compressible weak-BC strategies could become unstable when
velocity and temperature conditions were both enforced weakly. To address this, they developed
a more systematic weak wall-boundary operator for isothermal and heat-flux conditions, designed
within the advective-diffusive compressible flow framework.
8
These classical formulations provide the main theoretical foundation for the present thesis.
In particular, they motivate the use of a pressure primitive compressible formulation, a reduced
internal-energy variable set, matrix-based SUPG stabilization, and a weak-form treatment in which
pressure and diffusive contributions are handled so that natural outflow terms are retained. Rather
than treating the compressible solver as an entirely separate numerical object, the present work
builds on this established stabilized finite element literature and adapts it to an octree-based AMR
setting.
2.2
Adaptive Mesh Refinement in Compressible Flow
Adaptive mesh refinement has become one of the most important strategies for resolving lo­
calized features in complex PDE simulations. The foundational work of Berger and Oliger (1984);
Berger and Colella (1989) established the basic AMR philosophy: dynamically increase resolution
only where the evolving solution requires it. In compressible flow, this is especially valuable because
shocks, contact surfaces, shear layers, and thermal gradients are often spatially localized and may
evolve significantly in time. The contributions of Almgren et al. (1998) substantially enhanced
Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) for turbulent and shock-driven flows. In related reactive-flow
settings, Day and Bell (2000) demonstrated that AMR could be combined with projection-based
formulations, hierarchical structured grids, and temporal subcycling to efficiently resolve laminar
reacting flows with complex chemistry. AMR therefore offers a path to high-fidelity resolution with­
out paying the cost of globally fine meshes. Recent advances have also shown that effective AMR
requires scalable mesh-management frameworks, especially where remeshing, data transfer, and
parallel load balancing must remain efficient at large scale Saurabh et al. (2023). Adjoint-based
AMR has further extended this idea by targeting refinement toward regions that most strongly
influence selected output quantities, rather than relying only on visible solution gradients Nemec
et al. (2008). This is particularly important for complex aerodynamic configurations, where the
features that matter most to forces, moments, or pressure signatures are not always obvious from
the flow field alone.
9
However, compressible-flow AMR is not only a question of refinement policy. In continuous
Galerkin finite element settings, especially on octree meshes, AMR introduces several additional
numerical issues. These include mesh balancing, data transfer between successive meshes, hangingnode treatment, and the interaction between the adaptive mesh and the nonlinear solver. In contrast
to many finite volume compressible AMR methods, finite element octree AMR must also preserve
continuity and operator consistency across elements of differing refinement levels. As a result, the
effectiveness of AMR in compressible FEM depends as much on the mesh and solver infrastructure
as on the refinement indicator itself.
2.2.1 Recent Octree-FEM Developments
Two recent developments in the Dendrite ecosystem are especially relevant to future compressibleflow AMR capability. The first is the work of Shadkhah et al. (2025), which studies the impact of
hanging-node removal on convergence and solver performance for linear PDEs on octree-based SBM
meshes. That study shows that transition regions between coarse and fine octants can negatively
affect conditioning, solver efficiency, and post-processing when hanging nodes are left untreated.
Their proposed local strategy eliminates or merges hanging nodes while preserving continuity, and
the reported results show improved conditioning, reduced Krylov iterations, and retained secondorder convergence. For compressible finite elements, where solution gradients and nonlinear cou­
plings are more severe, such mesh-quality and continuity improvements are likely to be even more
important. The second is the recent field-conserving coarsening method proposed by Saurabh et al.
(2026). That work addresses a fundamental issue in adaptive continuous-Galerkin octree sim­
ulations: although parent-to-child refinement is naturally conservative, standard injection-based
coarsening is generally not. Over many adaptation cycles, this can introduce systematic drift in
conserved quantities. Their method constructs coarse-element values at quadrature points and then
recovers nodal values through an 𝐿2 projection, thereby enforcing conservation across coarsening
events. Although the numerical demonstrations are given for phase-field systems, the underly­
10
ing message is broader: intergrid transfer is not a secondary implementation detail, but a central
ingredient in the accuracy of long-time adaptive simulation.
These advances also fit within a broader progression of octree-FEM development in the Den­
dro/Dendrite framework. An important step was the arbitrary-domain octree framework of Saurabh
et al. (2021b), which introduced scalable algorithms for carving arbitrarily shaped void regions out
of a parent octree and performing finite element computations directly on the resulting incomplete
octree. That work developed top-down and bottom-up traversal strategies, active-region partition­
ing, pruning algorithms, and careful handling of hanging nodes near carved boundaries. In practical
terms, it moved octree-FEM beyond complete box-shaped domains and showed how inactive re­
gions can be removed to reduce memory cost, improve conditioning, and improve scalability for
complex external-flow type geometries. For future compressible simulations around bodies, this is
especially important because it directly addresses the overhead associated with large void regions
in conventional complete-octree approaches. Another major contribution is the immersogeometric
analysis framework of Saurabh et al. Saurabh et al. (2021a), which integrated adaptive octree
meshes with weak boundary condition enforcement for industrial-scale large eddy simulation. In
that work, immersed geometries were represented independently of the background mesh, Dirich­
let conditions were imposed weakly through Nitsche’s method, and specialized algorithms were
developed for fast in–out tests, adaptive quadrature on intercepted elements, tensorized matrix
assembly, and weighted dynamic partitioning. The reported large-scale simulations demonstrate
that octree-based finite elements can be combined with robust immersed-geometry treatment in a
way that is both accurate and scalable. This is highly relevant to future compressible flow work,
where weak boundary treatment and efficient handling of complex geometries are often essential.
A further important step was the fully coupled Cahn-Hilliard Navier-Stokes framework of Khan­
wale et al. (2022). That work demonstrated that adaptive octree meshes in Dendro5 can support
a second-order, energy-stable, fully implicit multiphysics finite element formulation using conform­
ing continuous Galerkin elements together with residual-based variational multiscale stabilization.
Beyond the particular phase-field application, the broader significance is that the octree infrastruc­
11
ture was shown to support tightly coupled nonlinear systems, large-scale adaptive remeshing, and
modern stabilized finite element formulations in a massively parallel setting. This strengthens the
case that the same octree-FEM backbone can be extended toward more demanding compressible
formulations. Related ideas were extended further by Kim et al. (2024), who developed a direct
numerical simulation framework for the coupled Poisson-Nernst-Planck and Navier-Stokes system
using variational multiscale stabilization and octree-based adaptive mesh refinement. Their work
focused on resolving thin diffuse charge layers, strong near boundary gradients, and complex threedimensional device geometries, while leveraging the scalable DENDRO-KT and DENDRITE-KT
infrastructure. This contribution is important in the present context because it shows that the
octree-based framework is not limited to fluid-interface problems, but can also support strongly
coupled transport, electrostatics, and flow physics with localized refinement in challenging multi­
scale settings. At a broader algorithmic level, Saurabh et al. Saurabh et al. (2023) proposed scalable
adaptive algorithms for next-generation multiphase flow simulations on octree-based meshes. That
work emphasized automatic identification of localized flow features such as droplets and filaments,
more efficient multi-level refinement and coarsening strategies, and improved data movement and
assembly layouts for finite element operators. The demonstrated scaling of uniform grid points il­
lustrates that octree adaptivity is not merely a meshing convenience but a key enabling technology
for extreme-scale, high-fidelity simulation.
Taken together, these developments substantially strengthen the Dendrite framework. Hangingnode treatment improves adaptive mesh quality and solver behavior, conservative coarsening im­
proves the reliability of intergrid transfer, and arbitrary-domain octrees reduce overhead for complex
geometries, immersogeometric analysis strengthens weak-boundary and immersed-geometry capa­
bility, and fully coupled multiphysics solvers demonstrate that stabilized large-scale finite element
formulations can be deployed successfully on adaptive octree meshes. Collectively, these works
provide a strong foundation for extending octree-based FEM in Dendrite toward more demanding
compressible flow simulations.

2.2.2 The Dendrite Framework and Current Capabilities
Within this broader AMR landscape, the Dendrite framework represents a recent and important
development in octree-based finite-element simulation. Recent work has shown that Dendrite,
together with Dendro-KT and PETSc-based solvers, provides a massively parallel octree framework
with several capabilities that are directly relevant to large-scale adaptive simulation. Yang et al.
(2025) presented an octree-based AMR framework integrated with the shifted boundary method
(SBM) for incompressible flow and coupled thermal flow simulations. In that work, refinement
is driven by vorticity magnitude, intergrid transfer is handled by traversal-based algorithms on
single-level mesh changes, and distributed memory scalability is supported through repartitioning
and space-filling curve ordering. A key benefit of the framework is that complex geometries can be
handled without body-fitted meshing, since the SBM enforces boundary conditions on a surrogate
boundary that conforms to the computational grid. This contribution is important for two reasons.
First, it establishes Dendrite as more than a mesh library; it is a full simulation environment with
adaptive octree operations, field transfer, load balancing, and weak boundary treatment. Second,
it demonstrates that the framework is already capable of solving flow and thermal problems in
geometrically complex settings where conventional mesh generation can be a bottleneck. In other
words, the computational backbone needed for adaptive high-fidelity simulation is already present.
At the same time, the documented Dendrite applications have been concentrated mainly in in­
compressible flow, thermal transport, and other related multiphysics or linear PDE settings. This
is a strong starting point, but compressible flow introduces a different class of numerical demands.
Refinement indicators based on vorticity alone are often insufficient for compressible applications,
where shocks and strong pressure or temperature variations may dominate error distribution. Like­
wise, solver robustness must account for acoustic transport, strong advection, and thermodynamic
coupling. These differences motivate the need for a dedicated compressible extension of the frame­
work.
13
2.3
Summary of Research Gap
Although the literature has established two important foundations, namely stabilized finite ele­
ment formulations for compressible flow and scalable octree-based adaptive mesh refinement frame­
works, their integration remains incomplete in the context of the Dendrite ecosystem. Existing Den­
drite developments have demonstrated strong capability for incompressible flow, thermal transport,
weak boundary treatment, hanging-node management, conservative field transfer, and large-scale
adaptive octree simulation. However, a dedicated compressible flow capability within this frame­
work has not yet been systematically developed and assessed for high-speed flow applications. This
gap is significant because compressible flow introduces numerical and physical demands that are not
fully addressed by extensions of incompressible methodology alone. In particular, a compressible
octree-based finite element framework must support stable treatment of strong advective transport,
steep thermodynamic gradients, shock-dominated features, and physically meaningful open bound­
ary conditions, while also preserving robustness under adaptive remeshing. Moreover, refinement
criteria commonly used in incompressible applications, such as vorticity-based indicators, are not
sufficient for compressible flows in which pressure gradients, thermal layers, residual concentrations,
and shock-associated curvature may dominate the local error structure.
A second unresolved issue concerns adaptive strategy selection. Even when a compressible
solver is available, it is not yet clear which AMR indicator or combination of indicators provides
the most effective balance between solution accuracy, mesh economy, and computational efficiency
for representative compressible flow problems on octree meshes. Therefore, the central research
gap addressed in this thesis is the absence of a robust, stabilized, octree-based compressible fi­
nite element framework within Dendrite, together with the lack of a systematic comparison of
compressibility-aware AMR strategies for efficient high-speed flow simulation.

3.1
METHODOLOGY
Overview
Building on the stabilized compressible finite element literature and the octree infrastructure
already available in Dendrite, the present thesis develops a compressible flow implementation tai­
lored to this framework. The implementation uses a pressure primitive vector of unknowns together
with a reduced internal-energy conservative state Hauke and Hughes (1998); Xu et al. (2017). This
choice enables the governing equations to be written in a quasi-linear form involving the trans­
formation matrix, directional advective Jacobians, stress-power operators, and diffusive coefficient
matrices. Such a structure is well aligned with matrix-based stabilization and residual assembly.
The weak form adopted in this work separates the non-pressure advective contribution, the
pressure contribution, the stress-power term, and the diffusion term. In particular, the pressure
and diffusive parts are treated so that natural boundary contributions remain explicit, which is im­
portant for outflow treatment in compressible simulations Shakib et al. (1991a); Hauke and Hughes
(1998). Time integration is performed using BDF2 Liao and Zhang (2021), while stabilization is
introduced through a matrix SUPG Brooks and Hughes (1982) operator together with entropybased discontinuity-capturing Le Beau et al. (1993) diffusion. At the algebraic level, the resulting
nonlinear system is solved through a Newton–Krylov strategy using PETSc Balay et al. (2001)
based solvers.
An important feature of the present implementation is that the AMR strategy is designed
around compressible flow physics rather than borrowed unchanged from incompressible applica­
tions. The framework studied in this thesis includes multiple refinement indicators, including a
pressure-velocity-temperature sensor, a Verfürth-type Verfürth (1996) residual indicator, a Machbased Hessian Guo et al. (2017) indicator, and a hybrid indicator that combines residual and cur­
vature information. This is a meaningful departure from the currently documented vorticity-driven
15
Dendrite AMR logic and is one of the central contributions of the thesis. It allows the adaptive
mesh to respond to shocks, thermal layers, and other compressibility-driven structures that are
not necessarily well represented by vortical intensity or other methods used for incompressible flow
simulations.

CHAPTER 4.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
This chapter presents the numerical results used to assess the accuracy, robustness, and effi­
ciency of the compressible flow formulation and its adaptive mesh refinement capability. First, the
solver is verified and validated using three benchmark problems that represent different compressibleflow behaviors: a supersonic flat plate boundary layer problem, an oblique-shock problem, and a
NACA 0012 airfoil case. These examples are selected to test the primitive variable-based finite ele­
ment formulation for high-speed compressible flow in the Dendrite framework. Second, the adaptive
refinement strategies introduced in Chapter 3 are compared on the basis of accuracy, mesh distri­
bution, and computational cost. Finally, the overall implications of the results are discussed in the
context of octree-based adaptive simulation for compressible flows.

4.1.2 Oblique Shock
The second benchmark problem considered in this work is the oblique shock case. This exam­
ple is a classical verification problem for compressible flow formulations because it isolates one of
the most important numerical challenges in high-speed flow simulation, namely the accurate and
stable resolution of a sharp compressive wave. In contrast to the supersonic flat plate problem,
where the solution involves both a developing viscous boundary layer and a leading-edge compres­
sion region; the oblique shock benchmark focuses more directly on shock capturing capability. It
therefore provides a cleaner setting for assessing whether the stabilized pressure primitive finite
element formulation can reproduce a discontinuity-like feature in the correct location, with the
correct strength, and without introducing spurious oscillations. This role of the benchmark is fully
consistent with the broader stabilized compressible flow framework described by Hauke and Hughes
(1998), in which variable selection, quasi-linear structure, stabilization, and shock-related robust­
ness are central considerations, and with the specific numerical example reported by Hauke and
Hughes (1998); Xu et al. (2017).

consists of a two-dimensional uniform inviscid flow at Mach number 𝑀 = 2 impacting a wall at

an oblique shock at an angle of 29.3∘ relative to the wall, originating from the leading edge. The
reference non-dimensional inflow state is given by 𝑝 = 0.1786, 𝑢1 = cos 10∘ , 𝑢2 = − sin 10∘ , and
𝑇 = 6.1941 × 10−4 , as reported in Hauke and Hughes (1998); Xu et al. (2017). The computational
domain is taken as a bi-unit square. On the left and top boundaries, the primitive variables are
prescribed to the inflow state, while the lower boundary is treated as an impermeable wall through a
no-penetration condition. The right boundary is left open. The solution quality is assessed through
pressure and temperature profiles taken along a vertical line at 𝑥 = 0.9, where comparison with the

analytical solution showed that the shock was captured in the correct location and resolved within

4.3 Discussion
Taken together, the results from the flat plate, oblique shock, NACA 0012, and torpedo-shaped
body cases show that the present pressure primitive compressible finite element solver is able to
resolve a broad range of compressible flow behavior in a stable and physically meaningful way. The
validation set is important because it does not test only one type of response. Instead, it spans
viscous wall-bounded supersonic flow, a shock-dominated inviscid benchmark, a curved external
aerodynamic case with experimental surface pressure data, and a streamlined body application
with both compression and wake structures. Across these cases, the solver consistently captures
the main flow physics without obvious nonphysical oscillation, while the adaptive octree framework
provides large reductions in mesh cost whenever refinement is placed selectively. This overall
behavior indicates that the formulation, stabilization, and adaptive infrastructure are working
together in a robust way rather than succeeding only in a single specialized benchmark.
From the standpoint of solver behavior, the flat plate and oblique shock cases are especially re­
vealing. In the flat plate problem, the method resolves both the leading-edge compression structure
and the developing viscous layer, and all AMR strategies recover nearly identical 𝐶𝑝 and 𝐶𝑓 dis­
tributions relative to the reference solution. In the oblique shock case, the numerical pressure and
temperature profiles closely follow the analytical jump, the shock appears in the correct location,
and the contour fields remain sharp without visible spurious oscillation or excessive smearing. What
this really means is that the present compressible solver is not only able to capture smooth viscous
responses but can also preserve sharp compressive features within the same stabilized framework.
That is a strong result for a continuous Galerkin compressible formulation on adaptive octree
meshes, where both shock sharpness and numerical robustness are typically demanding require­
ments.
75
The NACA 0012 benchmark extends that in a different direction. Unlike the previous two cases,
the airfoil problem tests whether the solver can produce a realistic aerodynamic surface pressure
response on a curved body, which is closer to the type of quantity that matters in practical external
flow analysis. The computed adaptive solutions are all in close agreement with the experimental
𝐶𝑝 data of Harris (1981) over most of the chord, including the leading-edge suction peak and
downstream pressure recovery. This is crucial because it indicates that the formulation can do
more than only solve shocks or boundary layers in simple geometries. It can also make an accurate
prediction of aerodynamic loading in a configuration that is more relevant to the application. In
that sense, the combined validation cases suggest that the solver has good generality across both
supersonic and subsonic compressible regimes and across both rectilinear and curved geometries.
The torpedo-shaped body application then shows that the same solver and AMR framework can
be carried beyond formal benchmark cases and still produce a coherent compressible flow solution.
The results capture the front body compression region, the strong gradients extending away from
the body, the accelerated outer flow, and the elongated wake. Even though this case is not used
as a strict validation problem, it is still valuable because it demonstrates that the method remains
stable and interpretable in a more complex external flow setting. That gives additional confidence
that the framework is not merely tuned to canonical textbook tests.
Once the accuracy question is addressed, the more meaningful distinction among the AMR
indicators is efficiency rather than raw solution agreement. In the flat plate case, all four indicators
give essentially the same wall-coefficient distributions, so the deciding factors become node count,
core hours, and compression ratio. The same pattern appears in the oblique-shock case, where all
four strategies reproduce the shock jump with nearly the same quality, and in the NACA 0012
case, where the adaptive 𝐶𝑝 curves are nearly indistinguishable over most of the airfoil. Even in
the torpedo-shaped body application, the surface-pressure curves remain nearly identical among
the indicators. So the core conclusion is clear: for the class of problems studied here, the AMR
indicators are separated much more strongly by how economically they place refinement than by
whether they can reproduce the main solution features at all.
76
The Pressure-Velocity-Temperature sensor indicator performs reasonably well overall and can
be viewed as an efficient practical option, especially when a compact indicator is desired. In the
flat plate case, it ranks second behind the Hessian method, with a compression ratio of 6.21. In the
oblique shock case, it is again the second most economical strategy, requiring 83.0 core hours and
achieving a compression ratio of 3.71. In the NACA 0012 benchmark, it actually gives the smallest
adaptive mesh and the lowest computational cost, with 288,940 nodes, 1326.5 core hours, and the
highest compression ratio of 4.96. It also remains reasonably competitive in the torpedo-shaped
body case. These results show that the sensor-based method is not weak. Its main advantage is
that it reacts directly to strong primitive variable variations and can deliver substantial cost savings
while retaining good solution quality. Its limitation, however, is that its refinement is not always as
physically selective as the Hessian strategy. In the oblique shock case it also refines near the inlet in
addition to the main shock region, and in the airfoil and torpedo cases its refinement is broader and
less directionally aligned with the wake or downstream structures. So, while it is often economical,
it is not always the cleanest in terms of targeting only the most important compressibility-driven
features.
The Verfürth-type indicator is more difficult to recommend as the best choice, even though it
is not poor in the sense of solution quality. It is reliable, and it consistently captures the dominant
flow structures in every case. But across the chapter it tends to spread refinement more broadly
than necessary, especially around the airfoil and in the torpedo wake. That broader refinement does
not translate into meaningfully better 𝐶𝑝 , 𝐶𝑓 , shock profiles, or visible flow fields. Its compression
ratios are consistently below those of the Hessian method: 4.02 for the flat plate, 3.45 for the
oblique shock, 2.83 for NACA 0012, and 1.36 for the torpedo-shaped body. This suggests that the
residual-based logic is robust but somewhat diffusive from the standpoint of mesh economy. In
other words, it is a safe indicator, but not the most efficient one for this compressible solver.
The Hybrid indicator is the weakest AMR strategy in this study. The reason is not that it
fails to produce acceptable flow solutions; in fact, the resulting 𝐶𝑝 , 𝐶𝑓 , and shock comparisons
are similar to the other methods. The problem is that it over-refines. In the flat plate case, it
77
has the lowest compression ratio among the adaptive cases, 2.23. In the oblique shock benchmark
it performs similarly to the Verfürth method, but with no visible gain in shock resolution. The
most serious failures appear in the NACA 0012 and torpedo-shaped body cases, where the Hybrid
strategy becomes more expensive than the uniform reference mesh itself. For the airfoil, it generates
1,586,056 nodes and requires 11,299.4 core hours, compared with 1,433,056 nodes and 9748.2 core
hours for the uniform reference; its compression ratio drops below unity to 0.90. For the torpedoshaped body, the same pattern occurs again: 1,392,700 nodes and 600.1 core hours, compared with
1,176,040 nodes and 495.4 core hours for the uniform mesh, with a compression ratio of only 0.84.
That is the clearest evidence in the chapter that the Hybrid indicator is over-activating refinement
over broad regions and losing the fundamental economy that AMR is supposed to provide. For this
solver, that makes it the worst indicator overall.
The Hessian-type indicator is the best overall choice for the present compressible flow solver.
The reason is not just that it performs well in one case; it is the most consistent method across
all cases. In the flat plate benchmark, it gives the highest compression ratio, 8.78, together with
the smallest adaptive mesh and the lowest runtime among the AMR methods. In the oblique
shock case, it again gives the best performance, with 193,552 nodes, only 63.3 core hours, and the
highest compression ratio of 5.42, while preserving essentially the same shock resolution as the other
indicators. In the torpedo-shaped body application, it once more gives the smallest mesh, the lowest
cost, and the highest compression ratio, 2.00. The only case where it is not the absolute cheapest is
NACA 0012, where the Pressure-Velocity-Temperature sensor is slightly better numerically in node
count and runtime. Even there, however, the difference is small, and the Hessian pattern is more
physically targeted because it aligns more clearly with the wake and local downstream solution
variation. That is why the Hessian approach comes out ahead in a broader sense since it is not only
cost-effective but also selectively aligns with actual compressibility-driven features in the solution.
A useful way to interpret the Hessian indicator is that it refines where the solution curvature
is strongest rather than where the residual is broadly elevated or where multiple criteria overlap.
For the problems considered here, that appears to be the most suitable logic. Compressible flow
78
features such as shocks, compression bands, leading-edge responses, and narrow wakes are all
spatially localized and geometrically directional. The Hessian strategy responds well to precisely
that type of structure. By contrast, the Verfürth and Hybrid indicators tend to distribute refinement
more diffusely, and the Hybrid method in particular becomes too aggressive once multiple triggers
reinforce one another over a wider region. The Pressure-Velocity-Temperature sensor remains
competitive, but its sensitivity to primitive variable gradients can sometimes broaden the refined
zone beyond what is strictly necessary. This is why the Hessian method repeatedly gives the best
compromise between accuracy, mesh compression, and runtime efficiency.

CHAPTER 5.
CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
5.1
Conclusions
This thesis developed and assessed an octree-based adaptive framework for compressible flow
simulation within the Dendrite environment. The work was motivated by the need for a com­
pressible solver that is not only stable on adaptive octree meshes, but also able to use refinement
economically for the types of localized features that dominate high-speed flow problems. To address
this need, a stabilized continuous-Galerkin finite element formulation was implemented using pres­
sure primitive variables together with a reduced internal energy representation. The resulting solver
was coupled with BDF2 time integration, SUPG-type matrix stabilization, discontinuity-capturing
diffusion, and multiple octree-based AMR strategies.
The results show that the developed formulation provides a robust and consistent basis for
compressible simulation across several distinct flow regimes. The supersonic flat plate case demon­
strated that the method can resolve the coupled effects of leading-edge compression, boundary-layer
development, and wall-quantity prediction in a viscous high-speed setting. The oblique shock bench­
mark showed that the formulation can capture sharp compressive structures in the correct location
and with the correct strength while maintaining numerical stability. The NACA 0012 airfoil case
further showed that the same methodology is able to predict a realistic surface-pressure response
on a curved aerodynamic body, including the leading-edge suction peak and downstream pressure
recovery. Finally, the torpedo-shaped body application indicated that the solver and AMR frame­
work remain stable and interpretable beyond the formal benchmark cases, providing coherent flow
fields and surface-pressure behavior for a more application-oriented streamlined-body configuration.
A second major conclusion of the thesis concerns the role of adaptive refinement. Across the
cases considered here, the different AMR indicators generally produced very similar physically
meaningful solution quantities. The wall coefficients in the flat plate case, the shock profiles in
80
the oblique shock case, the airfoil pressure-coefficient distributions, and the torpedo-shaped body
pressure trends were all much closer to one another than the corresponding mesh statistics might
suggest. What this means is that, for the present solver, the main difference among the AMR
strategies is not whether they can reproduce the dominant flow features, but how economically
they place refinement to do so.
From that standpoint, the Hessian-type indicator emerges as the best overall AMR strategy
in this study. It was the most consistent method across the benchmark and application cases,
repeatedly producing the smallest or near-smallest adaptive meshes, the lowest or near-lowest
computational cost, and the highest or near-highest compression ratios. More importantly, its
refinement patterns were generally the most physically selective. The Hessian indicator aligned
well with shocks, compression bands, wakes, and directional solution curvature, which allowed
it to concentrate mesh resolution on the most important compressibility-driven features without
unnecessarily broadening the refined region. Although the Pressure-Velocity-Temperature sensor
indicator performed very well and was slightly cheaper in the NACA 0012 case, its refinement was
often somewhat broader and less selectively aligned with the dominant structures. The Verfürthtype indicator remained reliable in terms of solution quality, but tended to distribute refinement
more diffusely than necessary. The Hybrid indicator, while still capable of producing acceptable flow
solutions, was the weakest AMR strategy overall because it frequently over-refined broad regions.
And in some cases, it became more expensive than the uniform reference mesh itself. This was
the clearest indication that excessive refinement does not automatically lead to a better adaptive
solution.
Taken together, the thesis shows that octree-based AMR is a practical and effective approach
for compressible finite element simulation in Dendrite, provided that the refinement logic is chosen
carefully. The work therefore makes two connected contributions. First, it establishes a work­
ing compressible solver within the octree-based framework using a stabilized pressure primitive
formulation. Second, it shows through multiple representative cases that the success of adaptive
compressible simulation depends not only on accuracy, but on whether refinement is placed in a
81
way that preserves the economy that AMR is meant to provide. For the class of problems studied
here, the Hessian-type indicator offers the best overall compromise between solution quality, mesh
compression, and runtime efficiency.
5.2 Future Work
Although the present thesis establishes a useful compressible flow capability in Dendrite, several
important directions remain open for future development. The first concerns the governing variable
formulation itself. In the present work, the solver was developed in pressure primitive variables us­
ing a reduced internal energy representation. A natural next step is to extend the methodology to a
full energy formulation in primitive variables. This would provide a more complete thermodynamic
representation and broaden the range of compressible problems that can be studied. Especially
in settings where total energy transport plays a more direct role. In parallel with that extension,
it would also be valuable to develop an equivalent formulation in conservative variables. A con­
servative variable implementation would provide an additional basis for comparison and could be
particularly useful for problems in which strict conservation properties or alternative stabilization
designs are important.
A second major direction is the incorporation of the shifted boundary method (SBM) into
the compressible solver. At present, the results have demonstrated that the solver can handle
benchmarks and application cases on the available mesh setups, but a more general and accurate
treatment of curved and complex external bodies will require a boundary methodology that is more
naturally compatible with non-body-fitted octree discretizations. Integrating SBM with the present
compressible formulation would make it possible to treat curved geometries more flexibly, reduce
the burden of body-fitted meshing, and move the framework closer to the kinds of geometrically
complex engineering applications for which octree-based methods are especially attractive.
A third direction is extension to more complex compressible physics. In particular, reactive-flow
simulation represents an important long-term opportunity. Reacting compressible flows introduce
additional couplings among advection, diffusion, heat release, species transport, and often very
82
sharp localized structures. This makes them a natural but challenging next step for the present
framework. If the current compressible solver can be extended to support reactive transport and
chemistry, then the octree-AMR methodology developed here may become useful for a much broader
class of propulsion, combustion, and high-speed energy conversion problems.
Beyond these core directions, several methodological improvements are also worth pursuing.
Future work could include three-dimensional extensions of the present test cases, a stronger study
of solver robustness at higher Reynolds and Mach numbers, and refinement criteria tailored to
specific output quantities rather than only local solution features.
In summary, the present thesis should be viewed as a foundation rather than an endpoint.
It has shown that a stabilized compressible formulation can be implemented successfully in the
octree-based Dendrite framework, and that physically meaningful adaptive simulation is achievable
for representative high-speed flow problems. The next stage is to expand that foundation toward
full-energy formulations, conservative variable formulations, embedded curved-boundary treatment
through SBM, and ultimately more general compressible applications such as reactive flows.
