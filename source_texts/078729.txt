# Deep Learning Methods and Neural Representations for Scientific Volume Visualization

Manish Mathai\* University of Oregon

Hank Childs† University of Oregon

#### ABSTRACT

Scientific volume visualization faces growing data-scale challenges as simulations produce increasingly large and complex 3D fields. Deep learning and neural representation methods offer new approaches to compression, rendering, analysis, and interaction with volumetric data, yet the landscape of techniques is fragmented across application domains and pipeline stages. This work organizes the literature into a four-stage pipeline taxonomy — data-level enhancement (super-resolution and neural compression), visualization-level rendering, analysis-level detection and segmentation, and interactionlevel parameter design — threading four cross-cutting limitations (domain specificity, evaluation heterogeneity, absent uncertainty quantification, and insufficient scale) through per-section comparison tables with cross-domain evaluation columns. Across a literature that has accelerated sharply since 2024, three cross-cutting findings emerge: methodological innovation flows almost exclusively from computer graphics and mainstream AI into scientific visualization through a one-directional transfer pattern; the four cross-cutting limitations compound rather than operate independently, so that a method validated on a single domain at small scale with no uncertainty bounds contributes less cumulative evidence than its reported metrics suggest; and a maturity gradient separates data-level methods (which have reached production scale) from interaction-level methods (which lack evaluation standards entirely). Our analysis identifies open problems grounded in these gaps and outlines a research agenda centered on shared benchmarks, uncertainty-aware formulations, and adaptation of foundation models, generative surrogates, and neural operators for scientific volumes.

Index Terms: Scientific visualization, volume rendering, deep learning, neural representations, implicit neural representations, neural radiance fields, literature review.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Scientific simulations in climate modeling, combustion, astrophysics, and fusion energy produce 3D scalar fields that encode the spatial structure of physical phenomena. Volume visualization transforms these fields into images that reveal features invisible in raw numerical output: shocks, vortices, mixing interfaces, and turbulent cascades. The standard pipeline for this task (data acquisition, processing, transfer function mapping, and rendering) has served the scientific visualization community [26, 87], but the data volumes produced by modern simulations increasingly outpace what the pipeline can handle interactively. Storage, I/O, and rendering costs force practitioners to choose between spatial fidelity and temporal coverage and discard scientifically relevant detail at every stage.

Deep learning and neural representations offer a different approach. Rather than applying fixed algorithmic steps at each pipeline stage, learned models can compress volumetric data into compact

neural representations, synthesize high-resolution fields from coarse inputs, generate volume renderings without explicit ray marching, and automate parameter choices that previously required manual exploration. These techniques now address tasks across the full visualization pipeline: super-resolution, neural compression, differentiable volume rendering, feature segmentation, and language-guided interaction.

This work covers deep learning and neural representation methods applied to scientific volume visualization. The scope encompasses methods that operate on structured volumetric grids (regular, rectilinear, and curvilinear), including both scientific simulation data and medical imaging volumes (CT, MRI). These methods address visualization-relevant tasks across four pipeline stages: data-level enhancement and compression \(Secs. 3 to 4\), visualization-level rendering and synthesis \(Sec. 5\), analysis-level detection and segmentation \(Sec. 6\), and interaction-level parameter design and agentic systems \(Sec. 7\). Methods that operate exclusively on unstructured meshes, point clouds, or 2D data fall outside scope, as do traditional visualization algorithms without a learning component. 2D-only methods are cited where they inform the discussion but are not individually surveyed.

Four cross-cutting limitations emerge from the body sections and recur throughout this report:

L1: Domain-specific methods, not general tools. Most methods are designed for a single dataset or task and do not transfer across domains without retraining or architectural modification. L2: No standardized evaluation methodology. Comparison across methods is hampered by inconsistent datasets, metrics, and baselines, a problem that compounds when methods span different pipeline levels. L3: Insufficient error characterization. Few methods report uncertainty estimates or analyze failure modes, limiting confidence in scientific applications where quantitative accuracy is required. L4: Scalability to production-scale data. Evaluations rarely exceed 5123 resolution, leaving open the question of whether reported results hold at the 10243–40963 scales common in production simulations.

The most comprehensive prior survey of deep learning for scientific visualization [147] organized methods across five categories (data generation, visualization generation, prediction, object detection and segmentation, and feature learning and extraction), covering the literature through 2022. Our work builds on that foundation in four respects. First, it adopts a pipeline-level organization \(Secs. 3 to 7\) that maps methods to the visualization stage they address, a complement to DL4SciVis's task-based grouping that provides an operational framing closer to how practitioners select tools. Second, it moves beyond cataloguing toward assessment by threading an evaluative backbone through every comparison table via cross-cutting limitations L1–L4 and a cross-domain evaluation column. Third, it extends temporal coverage to capture the post-2022 wave of implicit

# 2.1 Volume Rendering and the Visualization Pipeline

Scientific simulations and measurement instruments produce 3D scalar fields sampled on regular grids. Direct volume rendering (DVR) visualizes these fields without extracting intermediate geometry, casting rays through the volume and accumulating color and opacity along each ray path. Max [107] formalized the optical models underlying DVR as a continuous integral: the intensity I(D) at the camera after traversing a path of length D through the volume is


$$I(D) = I_0 e^{-\int_0^D \kappa(t) dt} + \int_0^D q(t) e^{-\int_t^D \kappa(s) ds} dt, \quad (1)$$

where κ(t) is the absorption coefficient at position t along the ray, q(t) is the emission, and I*0 is the background intensity. In practice, ray marching discretizes Eq. \(1\) by sampling at regular intervals ∆*t and compositing front-to-back: each sample's color ci and opacity α*i* accumulate into a running result via C*out = *C*in + (1−αin)α*i ci . Drebin et al. [26] and Levoy [87] established the foundational techniques for this process, which remain the basis of production volume renderers.

A transfer function maps each scalar value in the volume to color and opacity: it controls which structures become visible and how they appear. Even a one-dimensional transfer function (mapping scalar value to RGBA) defines a high-dimensional design space that requires domain expertise. Multi-dimensional transfer functions that incorporate gradient magnitude or spatial context increase expressiveness but compound the design burden. This parameter-sensitivity makes transfer function design a recurring target for learning-based methods \(Sec. 7\).

As an alternative to DVR, isosurface extraction via Marching Cubes [100] generates explicit triangle meshes at a user-specified threshold but trades full volumetric information for geometric clarity. The standard visualization pipeline (acquisition, processing, mapping, and rendering) is targeted by deep learning methods at every stage \(Secs. 3 to 7\).

#### 2.2 Challenges Driving ML Adoption

Modern scientific simulations routinely produce volumetric data at resolutions of 10243–40963 and beyond, often across hundreds of timesteps in time-varying studies. A single 40963 floating-point field occupies approximately 256 GB; a time series of 100 such fields exceeds 25 TB. Storage and I/O constraints force practitioners to apply lossy reduction before data ever reaches a visualization system and discard spatial or temporal detail that may prove scientifically relevant. The in situ paradigm [19] addresses part of this gap by processing data as simulations produce it, but rendering and analysis at full resolution still exceeds what interactive frame rates allow on commodity hardware.

Beyond raw scale, the parameter space confronting a visualization user grows combinatorially. A volume rendering session involves choices of transfer function, camera viewpoint, clipping planes, and (for time-varying data) timestep. Exploring this space interactively requires rendering at 10–30 frames per second, yet a single DVR pass through a 10243 volume can take hundreds of milliseconds even on modern GPUs. Drawing on the challenges discussed by Wang and Han [147], this work identifies three primary drivers of deep learning adoption in scientific visualization: data scale, rendering cost, and parameter-space complexity, and extends this framing with a fourth driver: the absence of standardized evaluation methodology (L2, Sec. 9\).

#### 2.3 Neural Representations for Volumetric Data

Terms such as "neural representation," "implicit neural representation," "neural field," and "NeRF" appear frequently in the literature reviewed in Secs. 3 to 7, often with inconsistent usage across the computer graphics and scientific visualization communities. Tab. 1 pins down the definitions used throughout this review. The remainder of this subsection introduces each concept in turn. Readers familiar with these representations may proceed to Sec. 2.4.

Implicit neural representations. Standard volumetric data stores scalar values at discrete grid positions. Off-grid queries require interpolation, and memory cost is proportional to grid resolution. An implicit neural representation (INR) replaces this discrete grid with a continuous function f*θ : R 3 → R parameterized by network weights θ, mapping any coordinate (*x,y,z) in the domain to a predicted scalar value. Because the representation is a function rather than a lookup table, an INR decouples memory cost from spatial resolution: a network of fixed size can represent a volume at arbitrary query density. The tradeoff is inference cost: each query requires a full network forward pass, and fitting the network to a large volume can take minutes to hours depending on network capacity and target fidelity. Periodic activation functions (SIREN) enable INRs to capture high-frequency detail that standard ReLU networks smooth away [132]. Mapping input coordinates through random Fourier features before passing them to the network overcomes the spectral bias of multilayer perceptrons toward low frequencies [139]. These two techniques, periodic activations and positional encoding, underlie the INR architectures applied to scientific volumes in Secs. 3 to 4. Algorithm 1 shows the coordinate-based training loop that distinguishes INRs from conventional grid-based deep learning.

Neural radiance fields. A neural radiance field (NeRF) [108] extends the INR concept to view synthesis by training a network to predict both color and volume density at each spatial location. The network takes a 5D input, three spatial coordinates plus two viewing-direction angles, and outputs an RGB color and a density σ. Novel views are rendered by casting rays through the scene, querying the network at sampled points along each ray, and compositing the predictions using the volume rendering equation \(Eq. \(1\)\). Training requires only a set of posed images: the rendering is differentiable, so photometric loss between rendered and observed images provides the supervisory signal. NeRF originated in the computer vision and graphics community for photorealistic scene reconstruction, but its reliance on volume rendering creates a direct bridge to scientific visualization that Sec. 5 explores. Dellaert and Lin [20] provide an annotated bibliography of the rapidly growing NeRF literature.

3D Gaussian Splatting. Where NeRF represents scenes implicitly through a network queried per-sample, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [79] uses an explicit set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by position, covariance, opacity, and color (represented via spherical harmonics). Rendering proceeds by projecting ("splatting") these Gaussians onto the image plane and alpha compositing them in depth order, a rasterization process that avoids the per-sample network queries of NeRF and achieves real-time frame rates. This rendering speed comes at a storage cost: scenes typically require millions of Gaussians, and the total parameter count can rival the size of the original data. Optimization uses the same differentiable rendering strategy as NeRF: photometric loss from posed images drives gradient updates to the Gaussian parameters. The explicit representation makes 3DGS amenable to adaptive refinement, a property that several scientific visualization methods in Sec. 5 use.

Hash encodings. Multi-resolution hash encoding [109] replaces both positional encoding and learned feature grids with trainable feature vectors stored at multiple spatial resolutions in a hash table, mapping input coordinates to table entries via spatial hashing. Concatenating features from coarse to fine levels produces an input vector that captures both global structure and local detail. Training time drops by one to two orders of magnitude compared to positional-encoding INRs. Compression and surrogate methods in Sec. 4 exploit the resulting real-time training capability. Hash collisions between distant points that map to the same table entry can introduce artifacts when table size is reduced for higher compression.

Differentiable rendering. The NeRF and 3DGS methods above share a common principle: rendering is implemented as a differentiable operation so that gradients of an image-space loss propagate back through the rendering equation to update the scene representation. Kato et al. [77] survey differentiable rendering techniques broadly. In the context of this report, differentiable volume rendering is the mechanism that enables training neural representations from images alone, without direct supervision on the underlying 3D field. Visualization-level methods in Sec. 5 depend on this mechanism to learn volume renderings without access to ground truth volume data.

A broader survey of implicit neural representations across computer vision and graphics [32] covers the full scope of INR applications, but we focus specifically on the subset of these techniques that has been adopted or adapted for scientific volume data.

### 2.4 Deep Learning Techniques for Volume Tasks

This subsection introduces the deep learning architectures that recur across Secs. 3 to 7, framed by the volumetric tasks they address rather than as generic definitions. For comprehensive treatment of these architectures, see Goodfellow et al. [40].

Convolutional neural networks. 3D convolutions extend the 2D sliding window operation to volumetric grids [84], with encoderdecoder architectures stacking downsampling and upsampling stages to extract and reconstruct spatial features. The U-Net variant [118] adds skip connections between corresponding encoder and decoder stages. 3D CNNs and U-Net are the dominant architectures for volume SR \(Sec. 3\) and segmentation \(Sec. 6\), where their local receptive fields naturally match the spatial locality of volumetric features.

Generative adversarial networks. A GAN [41] trains a generator against a discriminator to produce perceptually realistic outputs rather than merely minimize pixelwise error. This perceptual advantage makes GANs well-suited for volume SR \(Sec. 3\), where MSE-optimized outputs tend toward oversmoothing, and several

Autoencoders and variational autoencoders. Autoencoders learn compressed latent codes by training encoder-decoder pairs to reconstruct their input. VAEs [81] impose a probabilistic latent structure enabling sampling and interpolation. In volume visualization, autoencoders serve two roles: lossy compression of large volumes \(Sec. 4\), where the latent code replaces the original grid, and feature learning \(Sec. 6\), where the latent space captures semantically meaningful structure.

Transformers and vision transformers. The Transformer [146] computes pairwise attention over input tokens, capturing long-range dependencies that convolutions miss. The Vision Transformer (ViT) [25] adapts this for images by treating fixed-size patches as tokens. Transformers appear in volume SR \(Sec. 3\) and denoising, where their ability to attend across the full spatial extent of a subvolume helps recover large-scale structures that local convolutions miss.

Diffusion models. DDPMs [65] generate data by learning to reverse a gradual noising process, iteratively denoising from pure noise to a data distribution sample. Diffusion models entered volume visualization after Wang and Han's survey [147]: their iterative refinement naturally handles the multimodal distributions common in scientific data, with applications spanning volume generation, TF design, and visualization synthesis \(Secs. 4, 5 and 7\).

# 3 SUPER-RESOLUTION FOR VOLUME DATA

Scientific simulations commonly produce volumetric data at resolutions constrained by computational budgets, storage capacity, and I/O bandwidth rather than the resolution required for accurate analysis and visualization. Super-resolution (SR) methods address this gap by learning mappings from coarse inputs to high-resolution outputs for enhanced visualization without the need to rerun simulations at finer grids. Deep learning approaches to volume SR have progressed from early CNN-based upscaling to GAN-driven frameworks, implicit neural representations, and diffusion models. Each stage has expanded achievable fidelity, temporal coherence, and scale generalization. This section organizes volume SR methods along four axes: spatial upscaling of scalar fields \(Sec. 3.1\), temporal and spatio-temporal upscaling \(Sec. 3.2\), vector field upscaling \(Sec. 3.3\), and physics-informed super-resolution \(Sec. 3.4\).

# 3.1 Spatial Upscaling of Scalar Fields

Spatial upscaling of scalar fields frames the SR task as recovering a full-resolution volume from a coarser version, without any temporal or directional information to exploit. Gains must come entirely from learned spatial priors. Zhou et al. [184] introduced the first CNN-based approach to volume super-resolution, adapting the threelayer SRCNN architecture to operate on 3D patches extracted from medical CT volumes. Trained with MSE loss at 2× upscaling, the method achieved 41.16 dB PSNR, approximately 4 dB above trilinear interpolation, but required separate models per imaging modality and was limited to small scale factors.

The first GAN-based spatial SR for time-varying data, SSR-TVD [48], paired a densely connected generator with dual discriminators: a spatial discriminator for per-volume realism and a temporal discriminator operating on triplets of consecutive volumes to enforce coherence across timesteps. At 4× upscaling (64× volumetric), SSR-TVD achieved 34–41 dB PSNR across combustion, ionization, and vortex datasets and outperformed interpolation baselines on both PSNR and isosurface similarity. The method required per-dataset training and incurred inference times of up to eight hours for large volumes due to sub-volume GPU processing. Ma et al. [104] refined this paradigm by incorporating self-attention into the generator encoder to capture global spatial dependencies beyond the local convolutional receptive field. The method achieved improved perceptual scores at scale factors up to 8× on ionization and hurricane data, though the evaluation reports SSIM improvement without quantifying absolute PSNR relative to SSR-TVD on matched splits, and the maximum tested volume (240×240×48) is smaller than prior GAN baselines.

A hierarchical framework for multi-scale SR [164] chains 2× upscaling networks across levels of an octree data structure. The method iteratively downscales all data to the coarsest uniform grid and then upscales one level at a time, overwriting stale voxels with higher-resolution data at each stage to eliminate seam artifacts. Tested with ESRGAN, SSR-TVD, and STNet backbones on volumes up to 10243 from turbulence and cosmological simulations, the hierarchical approach achieved approximately 5 dB higher PSNR than single-step 4× SR at equivalent data reduction.

The first diffusion-based approach to scientific volume SR, CD-TVD [11], combines contrastive pre-training on historical simulation data with fine-tuning from as few as one high-resolution timestep. A contrastive encoding module learns degradation-aware features in latent space, replacing the VGG-based perceptual losses typical

of image SR with domain-specific representations, while the SR module employs a Swin Transformer-based 3D denoising network. On four fluid dynamics datasets at 4× upscaling, CD-TVD achieved 43–48 dB PSNR and outperformed GAN-based methods: its key practical contribution is entropy-based key-timestep selection for adaptation to new simulation scenarios from a single exemplar.

A complementary study by Han et al. [56] systematically evaluated nine augmentation techniques for volume SR, finding that augmentation quantity matters more than method choice Collectively, spatial scalar SR has matured from single-modality CNNs to diffusion models that rival GAN quality with fewer training exemplars, yet all methods remain limited to ≤10243 volumes and per-dataset training regimes.

#### 3.2 Temporal and Spatio-Temporal Upscaling

Spatial SR applied independently to each timestep discards the temporal correlation structure of time-varying simulations, producing frame-to-frame inconsistency that distorts features in motion. Xie et al. [167] introduced tempoGAN, a conditional GAN for spatial SR of fluid flow density fields that established the temporal discriminator as a mechanism for enforcing coherence across timesteps. The temporal discriminator receives triplets of advection-aligned frames and passes gradients through a differentiable semi-Lagrangian advection layer. This mechanism enables the generator to produce temporally smooth 4× upscaled sequences. Originally developed for computer graphics smoke simulation at resolutions up to 2563 , tempoGAN demonstrated that adversarial training could recover finegrained structures absent from coarse simulations, though evaluation remained qualitative with no PSNR or SSIM reported.

The temporal discriminator concept was adapted to temporal interpolation with TSR-TVD [47], a recurrent generative network that synthesizes intermediate timesteps in time-varying volumetric data. The generator combines ConvLSTM for temporal modeling with a voxel shuffle layer for feature-space upscaling, and it makes both forward and backward predictions, blended with learned weights. TSR-TVD achieved 23–27 dB PSNR across combustion, supernova, and vortex datasets and outperformed linear interpolation on both PSNR and isosurface similarity, but could only synthesize intermediate volumes at integer time positions. Han and Wang [50] later extended temporal SR to vector field data with TSR-VFD, which uses component-wise processing and a learned mask to blend network predictions with linear interpolation in regions of extremely small values. TSR-VFD achieved 41–54 dB PSNR across hurricane, solar plume, supernova, and tornado datasets.

Two approaches extended SR to joint spatial and temporal dimensions. Han et al. [55] proposed STNet, an end-to-end GAN framework that performs feature-space temporal interpolation followed by voxel-shuffle spatial upscaling, with a ConvLSTM-based discriminator for spatiotemporal realism and a cycle-loss pre-training stage that uses unlabeled low-resolution data. An et al. [4] addressed the vector field counterpart with STSRNet, a two-stage pipeline combining U-Net-based motion estimation with deformable alignment. Whereas STNet optimizes PSNR-oriented losses, STSRNet introduces domain-motivated objectives including cosine direction loss and a Jacobian loss for capturing divergence and curl. These objectives reflect the additional fidelity constraints that vector data imposes.

An alternative line of work replaces CNNs with implicit neural representations (INRs), which offer resolution-independent inference at arbitrary scale factors. Han and Wang [52] proposed Coord-Net, a coordinate-based SIREN network with residual blocks that maps spatiotemporal coordinates directly to field values and unifies temporal SR, spatial SR, view synthesis, and ambient occlusion prediction within a single ∼5.7 MB architecture, an order of magnitude smaller than CNN-based alternatives. Tang and Wang [142] extended this approach with STSR-INR, adding variable-specific

latent embeddings via a variational auto-decoder and a modulated dual-network architecture to handle multivariate and ensemble data, with support for non-integer upscaling factors such as 1.75× spatial and 2.5× temporal.

A probabilistic strategy for spatial SR, PSRFlow [128], uses a conditional normalizing flow model that provides built-in uncertainty quantification. By sampling multiple times from the learned conditional distribution, PSRFlow produces both a reconstructed volume and a per-voxel standard deviation map. PSRFlow is the only SR method reviewed here that quantifies reconstruction uncertainty. At 2× upscaling, PSRFlow achieved 39–49 dB PSNR across vortex, cosmological, combustion, and plume datasets with a compact 15.7 MB model, though quality degrades at higher factors (8×) due to compounding errors from recursive application. The progression from GAN-based interpolation through coordinate networks to normalizing flows shows a clear trend toward smaller models and more flexible scale factors, though uncertainty quantification remains limited to a single method.

The methods above operate exclusively on scalar fields: extending SR to vector data introduces additional challenges, including directional fidelity and downstream integration accuracy, addressed by the following approaches.

#### 3.3 Vector Field Upscaling

Upscaling vector fields introduces challenges beyond scalar SR: directional fidelity and downstream integration accuracy must be preserved alongside standard reconstruction metrics, motivating loss functions that penalize angular error as well as magnitude. Guo et al. [43] introduced the first spatial SR method for 3D vector field data with SSR-VFD, using three separate CNNs (one per velocity component) to handle the dramatically different value ranges across u, v, and w. The loss function combines a magnitude term with an angular component that penalizes directional deviations via the arccos of the dot product between predicted and ground-truth vectors. At 4× upscaling, SSR-VFD achieved 48–55 dB PSNR across hurricane, solar plume, and tornado datasets with inference times of 0.2–7.6 s depending on volume size, and was further validated on hemodynamic vessel data for wall shear stress estimation. Sahoo and Berger [119] extended this architecture with an integration-aware streamline loss that backpropagates through differentiable Euler integration. This approach improved the fidelity of downstream streamline visualizations at the cost of slightly reduced PSNR.

An alternative formulation (recovering grid-based vector fields from representative streamlines) was pursued by Gu et al. [42] via gradient vector flow diffusion followed by CNN-based denoising with recurrent residual blocks. This two-stage approach achieved 31–36 dB PSNR with compression rates of 12–51× on supercurrent, supernova, and tornado data, a distinct input representation compared to grid-based upscaling.

Implicit neural representations were applied to flow field SR with FFEINR [74], using spatially and temporally decoupled SIREN networks enhanced by ConvLSTM-based feature extraction to enable arbitrary-scale upscaling, though the approach has only been demonstrated on 2D flow fields. Gadirov et al. [35] proposed Hyper-FLINT, which combines a hypernetwork conditioned on simulation parameters with a flow interpolation network for temporal SR of ensemble data. By generating parameter-specific network weights, HyperFLINT achieved 52.70 dB PSNR on cosmological Nyx data at 5× temporal interpolation, a substantial margin over CoordNet (44.02 dB) and STSR-INR (44.21 dB), with sub-second inference times. Vector field SR thus lags scalar SR in both method count and scale, with directional loss functions and integration-aware training emerging as the distinguishing design choices.

#### 3.4 Physics-Informed Super-Resolution

Physics-informed SR incorporates governing equation constraints directly into the training objective, seeking physically consistent reconstructions rather than minimizing purely statistical reconstruction error. Werhahn et al. [158] decomposed volumetric SR into orthogonal 2D slice passes via a multi-pass GAN, where a first generator upscales XY-plane slices and a second refines YZ-plane slices, without the memory cost of full 3D convolutions. This decomposition enabled 8× upscaling per dimension (512× volumetric) on smoke simulations through progressive growing with curriculum learning. The method produced detailed turbulent structures at 5123 from 643 inputs within 60 seconds, competitive with direct numerical simulation. The approach was evaluated only on synthetic buoyant smoke and reported no quantitative reconstruction metrics.

Outside the core SciVis community, at least ten studies have applied physics-informed constraints to flow field SR, primarily on 2D systems. Three categories of approach have emerged: hard-constraint methods that encode PDE residuals and boundary conditions directly into the network architecture [117]; lossbased approaches that embed time integrators or physics-consistent training objectives into GAN frameworks, requiring as little as 13% of the data needed by standard SRGAN [59]; and architectural specializations including self-attention discriminators for shock-capturing [130], linear-complexity transformers for 3D scaling [129], and residual diffusion models with PDE constraints [125]. Broader transfer work includes GAN-based SR for 2D turbulent flows [36, 177], diffusion-based reconstruction from sparse sensors [3], 3D medical volume SR [73, 186], astronomical imaging [138], and representation learning for flow field inpainting [150]. Jakob et al. [71] provide a benchmark 2D flow dataset. No study has validated these constraints on the 3D time-varying scientific volumes that characterize the SciVis pipeline. This gap represents a clear transfer opportunity from the computational physics community.

Tab. 2 reveals five patterns that characterize the current state of volume SR research. Domain specificity (L1): most methods are validated on one or two scientific domains, as the cross-domain column confirms, with only SSR-VFD [43] bridging the CFD–medical boundary. Evaluation (L2): seventeen of nineteen methods report PSNR and/or SSIM, yet no two groups use the same training or test splits. Results are comparable only within individual papers, not across them. The absence of a shared benchmark dataset or standardized evaluation protocol makes it impossible to rank methods reliably, a gap that limits cross-method comparison. Domain-specific metrics such as D-SSIM for floating-point fields [6] remain unused in the SR literature. Uncertainty (L3): of the nineteen methods reviewed here, only PSRFlow [128] provides per-voxel uncertainty estimates. All others produce point predictions with no mechanism for communicating reconstruction confidence to downstream analysis. Scale (L4): the majority of methods have been evaluated on volumes of 1283 , with only Wurster et al. [164] reaching 10243 via hierarchical decomposition and Werhahn et al. [158] generating 8003 output through progressive 2D passes, but production-scale scientific data at 20483–40963 remains untested. Fig. 2 provides visual evidence for these quality differences: the progression from trilinear interpolation through GAN-based methods (SRGAN, SSR-VFD) and normalizing flows (PSRFlow) to CD-TVD's diffusion-based approach shows increasing recovery of fine-scale flow structures, though even the highest-performing method (CD-TVD at 43–48 dB PSNR) has not been tested beyond 500×500×100 resolution. CG/AI transfer: the temporal discriminator, introduced by Xie et al. [167] for computer graphics smoke synthesis, has become a standard component adopted across SciVis SR methods [48, 55]. Likewise, INR architectures derived from SIREN now underpin coordinate-based SR [52, 74, 142]. Physics-informed constraints from the computational physics community \(Sec. 3.4\) represent a further transfer opportunity that has not yet been validated on the 3D time-varying volumes typical of the SciVis pipeline.

#### 4 NEURAL COMPRESSION AND SURROGATE MODELS

The methods in Sec. 3 generate high-resolution outputs from coarse inputs, requiring the original data at inference time. A complementary line of work replaces the stored volume entirely: an implicit neural representation (INR) or learned encoder maps spatial coordinates directly to field values and produces a compact, queryable model from which any transfer function, viewpoint, or downstream renderer can operate. This section surveys INR single-volume compression \(Sec. 4.1\), extensions to multi-volume and temporal data \(Sec. 4.2\), autoencoder and alternative compression architectures \(Sec. 4.3\), and surrogate models that predict unseen simulation states \(Sec. 4.4\). Tab. 3 summarizes the methods covered across all four subsections. Fig. 3 illustrates the INR compression pipeline that the majority of methods in this section instantiate.

#### 4.1 Single-Volume INR Compression

Storing and transmitting production-scale scientific volumes at full resolution strains both storage systems and I/O pipelines. Traditional lossy compressors such as SZ and ZFP provide error-bounded reduction but produce bitstreams that must be fully decoded before visualization. Implicit neural representations offer an alternative: a compact network that maps spatial coordinates to field values. This representation is simultaneously compressed storage and a queryable data source.

The first INR compression method for scientific volumes, neurcomp [102], uses a coordinate-based SIREN MLP with residual connections and 9-bit weight quantization via k-means clustering. On scalar fields up to 5123 , neurcomp outperformed tensor decomposition (TTHRESH) at high compression ratios: 47.9 dB PSNR at 545:1 compression on the Rayleigh-Taylor dataset. An optional gradient regularization term improved isosurface fidelity at the cost of slight PSNR reduction. Training required hours per volume on a V100 GPU, and the method could not render interactively from the

Interactive rendering directly from compressed INRs became feasible with fV-SRN [155], which pairs a volumetric latent grid (typically 323 resolution, 16 features per vertex) with a lightweight MLP (four layers, 32 channels). The key contribution is a custom GPU TensorCore kernel that fuses the entire network evaluation into 48 kB of on-chip shared memory. Feature grids with fixed, uniform placement waste parameters on homogeneous regions, an inefficiency that APMGSRN [166] addresses with multiple spatially adaptive feature grids. APMGSRN trains 16–32 adaptive grids with learnable transformation matrices, guided by a feature density loss that concentrates capacity in high-error regions. Hash grids combined with small fully fused MLPs can scale INR compression to tera-scale data on consumer hardware, as Wu et al. [162] demonstrated. The four methods above produce point estimates with no mechanism for communicating reconstruction confidence. Saklani et al. [121] first explored this gap by comparing Deep Ensemble (10 independent SIREN models) and Monte Carlo Dropout for INR volume visualization. Ensembles provide higher correlation between predicted uncertainty and actual reconstruction error but at 10× the storage cost. Saklani et al. [120] later proposed REV-INR, which replaces multiple models with a single SIREN whose final layer outputs four parameters of a Normal Inverse-Gamma distribution for closed-form estimation of both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty in a single forward pass. Two novel regularization losses align epistemic uncertainty with prediction error and aleatoric uncertainty with local gradient magnitude. On volumes up to 5123 , REV-INR achieved the highest uncertainty-error correlation among three uncertainty-aware INR variants (correlation coefficients of 0.37–0.85 across six datasets) at inference speeds comparable to a deterministic INR, though training takes 2–3× longer due to the evidential loss computation.

An orthogonal approach to uncertainty, RMDSRN [168], attaches multiple lightweight MLP decoders (five heads, two layers each) to a shared feature grid encoder to amortize the parameter cost of ensemble diversity. Variance regularization encourages the model's uncertainty to concentrate where reconstruction errors are largest. On volumes up to 10243 (4 GB), RMDSRN achieved the highest reconstruction PSNR among all uncertainty-aware methods and produced variance maps that identify high-error regions without access to ground truth.

Multivariate data requires compressing several correlated fields jointly rather than independently. MVNet [28] formulates this as multi-task learning. MVNet uses a Residual SIREN with a v*dimensional output layer, where *v is the number of physical variables, and shares all intermediate parameters across variables. This parameter sharing yields compression ratios that scale with variable count: on a 100-variable climate dataset (1568 MB), MVNet achieved 49.68 dB PSNR at 1298:1 compression, compared to 48.62 dB for TTHRESH at 343:1. The method also preserved inter-variable statistical dependencies (Pearson correlation and mutual information) better than traditional compressors, though it has only been tested on static datasets.

Several groups have extended INR compression to specialized domains. Sheibanifard and Yu [126] combined a SIREN trained on downsampled medical CT volumes (512 × 512 × 463) with a separate SRDenseNet super-resolution module to achieve 35.14 dB PSNR, though at compression ratios of only 1.3–3.7× (excluding the decoder model size). Son et al. [133] proposed MC-INR, which combines meta-learning initialization, k-means spatial clustering (20 clusters, each trained on a separate GPU), and a branched CoordNet architecture with per-variable output heads designed for multivariate data on unstructured tetrahedral meshes. On reactor simulation data, it achieves 54.10 dB PSNR at 110× compression.

# 4.2 Multi-Volume and Temporal INR

Single-volume methods \(Sec. 4.1\) train one network per volume. For time-varying simulations and ensemble datasets containing hundreds of volumes, repeating this optimization from scratch for each timestep or ensemble member incurs training costs that scale linearly with volume count. Several strategies reduce this cost: metalearned initializations, hypernetwork weight prediction, knowledge distillation across timesteps, mixture-of-experts architectures, and distributed training integrated into HPC pipelines.

Training a fresh INR for each timestep scales linearly with volume count. Meta-INR [172] reduces this cost by applying MAML to pretrain a shared parameter set on a spatiotemporally subsampled subset comprising less than 1% of the original data. Per-volume finetuning from this initialization converged 5.87× faster on average than training a SIREN from scratch, with higher reconstruction quality (55.92 dB vs. 48.89 dB on the half-cylinder dataset). Meta-INR has been validated only with MLP-based SIREN backbones. Whether the meta-learning strategy transfers to hash-grid or featuregrid encoders remains untested.

Per-volume optimization can be sidestepped entirely by predicting INR weights from scene parameters via a hypernetwork, as demonstrated by HyperINR [161]. An ensemble of multi-resolution hash encoders, organized in a KD-tree over the parameter space and interpolated via inverse-distance weighting, produces a compact INR for any query parameter in 0.07–0.28 ms. Knowledge distillation from a large CoordNet teacher enables the hypernetwork to match the teacher's reconstruction quality at 100× the inference bandwidth and reach 20–30 FPS with volumetric path tracing at 800×600 resolution. This speed enables real-time temporal superresolution, novel-view synthesis, and dynamic shadow computation from compressed representations, though quality is upper-bounded by the teacher model.

Rather than training each timestep from scratch, KD-INR [54] compresses time-varying data through a two-stage pipeline. In the first stage, each timestep is independently compressed by a small INR with bottleneck layers that reduce intermediate dimensionality, and entropy-based adaptive sampling concentrates training on high-information 4 3 blocks. In the second stage, offline knowledge distillation merges all per-timestep teacher models into a single student, an O(n) procedure compared to O(n 2 ) for continual learning approaches. On time-varying datasets up to 23.44 GB (argon bubble, 150 timesteps), KD-INR outperformed CoordNet, NeurComp, and SIREN at compression ratios from 733 to 10,000, with per-timestep

compression requiring 1–6 minutes on a single GPU.

A mixture-of-experts approach, MoE-INR [44], routes coordinates through a policy network to one of seven expert decoders, each specializing in a spatially distinct subdomain. A shared encoder extracts features used by all experts, maintaining boundary continuity that independent per-block INRs cannot guarantee. Pretraining the policy network with voxel-clustering labels proved necessary: without it, the router collapses to a single expert and degenerates to a conventional INR. MoE-INR achieved the highest PSNR among all compared methods across five time-varying datasets: 60.24 dB on combustion data at 1,951× compression and 50.73 dB on ionization data at 24,878× compression, at the cost of 8–50 hours total training time.

F-Hash [135] extends multi-resolution hash encoding to four dimensions with Tesseract grids and collision-free minimal perfect hashing. The result is 10–100× faster convergence than standard hash encoding (47.1 dB in 7.4 minutes vs. 23.7 dB in 45.7 minutes on combustion data), though the resulting compression ratios (9.5– 55.2×) position it as a fast-convergence encoding rather than a high-ratio compressor.

An improved version of APMGSRN [165] reduced model size by 80% through custom CUDA kernels and SZ3 post-compression of regularized feature grids, with less than 0.1 dB loss. On the asteroid dataset (111.71 GB, 100 timesteps), a 77 MB model achieved 49.04 dB PSNR at 1,529× compression.

No prior INR method had been validated at HPC scale. DVNR [163] fills this gap by training one independent INR per MPI rank on its local data partition. A weighted boundary loss using ghost cells and half-Gaussian sampling maintains cross-partition continuity without interprocess communication. Adaptive parameter tuning scales each rank's hash table size proportionally to its local voxel count, and weight caching from previous timesteps reduces compression time by up to 10× as simulations progress. Integrated with the Ascent in-situ infrastructure and tested on up to 512 MPI ranks on the Polaris supercomputer, DVNR demonstrated near-ideal weak scaling efficiency on domains up to 2,5803 , the only method in this section validated at HPC scale (L4).

Beyond the methods above, time-varying INR compression has also been approached through decomposition, partitioning, and caching. Tang and Wang [141] decomposed time-varying volumes via a Laplacian pyramid with per-block MLPs and achieved decoding speeds 13–30× faster than neurcomp on datasets up to 1,7283 . Han et al. [53] applied divide-and-conquer spatial partitioning with entropy-guided INR sizing to compress datasets up to 23.44 GB more than 10× faster than centralized INR methods, with PSNR that matches or exceeds theirs. Zavorotny et al. [181] addressed the rendering side with a multi-resolution GPU cache for INR-compressed volumes that achieves a 5× rendering speedup, sufficient for interactive exploration of a 0.96 TB DNS dataset at 92 FPS on a consumer GPU.

# 4.3 Autoencoder and Alternative Architectures

Unlike the coordinate-based INRs above, an alternative paradigm feeds volumetric data through an encoder that produces a compressed latent code, from which a decoder reconstructs the original field. This encoder-decoder family includes convolutional autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion models, each offering different tradeoffs between compression ratio, reconstruction fidelity, and decoding speed.

The first systematic integration of autoencoders into an errorbounded compression framework [95] produced AE-SZ. AE-SZ replaces the linear regression predictor in SZ2.1 with a pre-trained Sliced-Wasserstein autoencoder (SWAE), selected from eight autoencoder variants for its highest reconstruction PSNR. For each data block, AE-SZ selects whichever predictor (autoencoder or Lorenzo) achieves lower ℓ1 loss, then applies linear-scale quantization followed by Huffman and Zstd encoding. On volumes up to 5123 (NYX cosmology), AE-SZ achieved 100–800% higher compression ratios than SZ2.1 and ZFP at matched PSNR, though its compression throughput (12–68 MB/s) remains an order of magnitude below that of the unmodified SZ pipeline.

Compression can be reframed as super-resolution recovery, as ZipGAN [112] demonstrates. ZipGAN downsamples a DNS volume (compression ratios of 8 to 4,096 in 3D) and trains a super-resolution GAN to reconstruct the full-resolution field. On homogeneous isotropic turbulence at 10243 (Reλ ≈ 210), ZipGAN maintained high fidelity up to 512× compression and outperformed discrete wavelet transform (DWT) at all ratios above 8×. A discriminatorderived quality score provides a proxy for reconstruction confidence without access to the original data, though no formal error bound is guaranteed. Transfer learning from lower to higher compression ratios halved training time at each doubling of *n*δ .

Diffusion models entered scientific data compression with GCDTC [85]. A conditional diffusion model compresses tensor blocks, with post-processing that corrects blocks exceeding the userspecified error bound. On E3SM climate data, GCDTC achieved at least 2× larger compression ratios than SZ3 above NRMSE 10−4 . The iterative denoising process, however, imposes a severe decoding bottleneck: 978 seconds versus 12 seconds for SZ3 and 2 seconds for a standard autoencoder.

GCDTC's 978-second decoding time makes data-space diffusion impractical for interactive use. Li et al. [89] addressed this by moving diffusion into latent space. Rather than storing latent representations for every frame, the method compresses only selected keyframes via a VAE with hyperprior, then uses a conditional latent diffusion model with factorized space-time attention to interpolate latent representations for non-keyframes. This generative interpolation achieved up to 10× higher compression ratios than SZ3 on S3D combustion data and 4× on E3SM climate data. Fine-tuning from 1,000 to 32 denoising steps preserved compression quality while raising decoding throughput to 61 MB/s on an A100 GPU, 200× faster than GCDTC's data-space diffusion.

Several earlier works explored autoencoder and GAN architectures for scientific data compression before the frameworks above matured, including feature-aware in-situ autoencoders achieving 32× reduction on particle data [96], GAN in-situ compressors reaching 64:1 on CFD pressure fields [99], SR-enhanced SZ predictors with up to 75% ratio improvement on volumes up to 5123 [94], hierarchical vector-quantized autoencoders achieving 231× compression at 50 dB on climate data [83], and GAN volume completion synthesizing missing subvolumes at 34–45 dB with up to 50% data absent [51].

### 4.4 Surrogate Models and Extrapolation

The compression methods in Secs. 4.1 to 4.3 reconstruct volumes that were used during training. Surrogate models address a different task: predicting simulation outputs for parameter combinations that were never simulated. Scientists can then explore continuous parameter spaces without running new simulations. This extrapolation capability introduces evaluation criteria beyond reconstruction fidelity, including how gracefully quality degrades for out-of-distribution parameters and whether the model can quantify its own prediction confidence.

Continuous parameter-space exploration from a compact model is the goal of Explorable INR [16], which encodes both spatial coordinates and simulation parameters into a single queryable representation. A hybrid spatial encoding combines 3D and 2D feature grids with per-parameter embeddings, capturing spatial detail without the memory cost of full multi-dimensional grids. On the Nyx cosmological dataset (5123 , 3 parameters, 100 training members), Explorable INR achieved 45.31 dB PSNR with a 112 MB model, above both Instant-NGP (44.89 dB) and K-Planes (32.91 dB). Probabilistic affine forms propagate uncertainty through the network in a single pass (13.7 s on Nyx), and gradient-based inverse optimization identifies parameter combinations matching a target feature in 5.6 s. The method assumes Gaussian-distributed outputs, which limits uncertainty accuracy when the target data deviates from this assumption.

Explorable INR's fixed spatial encoding limits adaptation to complex spatial patterns. FA-INR [90] replaces it with a learnable cross-attention mechanism. FA-INR uses a learnable cross-attention mechanism over a memory bank and Mixture-of-Experts routing to concentrate capacity in spatially complex regions. On three datasets (MPAS-Ocean with 11.8 M unstructured grid points, Nyx at 5123 , and CloverLeaf3D at 1283 ), FA-INR outperformed Explorable INR by 5–10% in PSNR with 1.5–6.7× fewer parameters (1.10–9.65 M vs. 7.38–14.73 M) and 1.70 s inference per field on an A100 GPU. FA-INR provides no uncertainty estimates, a gap that limits its applicability in safety-critical scientific workflows.

SurroFlow [127] takes a different architectural approach by using normalizing flows, which learn an invertible transformation between a simple base distribution and the data distribution. SurroFlow trains a conditional normalizing flow in the latent space of a 3D convolutional autoencoder and supports bidirectional prediction: forward mapping from parameters to simulation outputs, and reverse mapping from observed data back to likely parameter values. On MPAS-Ocean (192 × 96 × 12, 128 training members), SurroFlow achieved 46.69 dB PSNR with a 48 MB model trained in 11.5 hours, compared to VDL-Surrogate's 45.82 dB at 0.63 GB and 139.8 hours. The constant-dimensionality constraint of normalizing flows limits SurroFlow to operating at lower resolution than the native simulation grid (Nyx at 1283 rather than 5123 ). On this dataset, PSNR drops to 30.93 dB, below VDL-Surrogate's 33.66 dB.

A complementary direction shifts attention from predicting outputs to characterizing which input parameters produce a given output feature [148]. Their framework trains a surrogate, then applies Bayesian inference to obtain the posterior distribution over parameters conditioned on a user-specified spatial feature. A Fisher Information Matrix prior accelerates prior probability computation by three orders of magnitude compared to deep ensembles. Posterior characterization then requires approximately 1 s of burn-in plus 100–200 sampling steps. The method has been validated only on 2D time-varying fields (up to 1024×256×200) with at most 4 parameters: extension to volumetric data and higher-dimensional parameter spaces remains an open challenge.

CNN surrogates provide no calibrated uncertainty bounds, a gap that ConfEviSurrogate [27] addresses. The model replaces the standard output layer with a four-parameter Normal Inverse-Gamma head, which separates aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in a single forward pass (0.03–0.36 s, compared to 0.83–25.17 s for Monte Carlo Dropout and 1.60–3.00 s for deep ensembles). A conformal prediction calibration step provides finite-sample coverage guarantees for prediction intervals, a property absent from all other surrogate methods in this section. On three datasets (MPAS-Ocean at 192×96×96, Nyx at 2563 , CloverLeaf3D at 643 ), ConfEviSurrogate achieved the highest voxel-level correlation between predicted uncertainty and true error, above both Monte Carlo Dropout and deep ensembles (L3). The CNN architecture generates full-volume predictions rather than point queries, and conformal calibration requires a held-out calibration set that may be unavailable for data-scarce simulations.

Rather than predicting simulation outputs from parameters, Neural Dependence Fields (NDFs) [33] pursue a complementary goal: learning the statistical dependencies between spatial locations across ensemble members. A bipartite encoder network with multiresolution hash grids maps pairs of 3D coordinates to Pearson correlation or mutual information values. On a 1000-member weather ensemble (250 × 352 × 20), NDFs achieved 87.63 dB PSNR for

self-correlation fields with 9 ms inference, 26× faster than GPUcomputed Pearson correlation and five orders of magnitude faster than CPU mutual information estimation, at over 6,000× compression relative to storing all pairwise correlations. The method operates on a single time step and tends to smooth fine-scale correlation structures due to hash table capacity constraints.

Surrogate modeling has also reached specialized domains, including streamline-based vector field reconstruction at up to 572× compression [46], Lagrangian flow map learning with 46× memory reduction over Delaunay triangulation [57], graph neural network surrogates for MPAS-Ocean on unstructured meshes (14.1 M cells, sub-2-second inference) [131], and deep learning surrogates reducing gyrokinetic plasma inference from hours to 0.88 ms [24].

Synthesis. Tab. 3 exposes four patterns across the 38 methods in this section. First, evaluation heterogeneity (L2) undermines cross-method comparison: INR methods report PSNR and compression ratio on self-selected volumes, autoencoder methods emphasize throughput and error-bound compliance, and surrogates measure prediction accuracy under parameter extrapolation. No shared benchmark exists: the closest to common ground is the Nyx cosmological dataset, used by nine groups, but at resolutions ranging from 1283 to 5123 with incompatible error metrics. Second, uncertainty quantification remains sparse (L3): of the 38 methods surveyed, only five provide confidence estimates (REV-INR, RMDSRN, SurroFlow, ConfEviSurrogate, and Explorable INR via probabilistic affine forms), despite the fact that compressed or surrogate data feeds directly into scientific analysis where undetected errors propagate into downstream conclusions. Third, scale limitations persist (L4): most INR methods validate at 2563–5123 , with only four reaching production scale. Wu et al. [162] compressed a 950 GB DNS volume via out-of-core hash encoding on a consumer GPU. Wurster et al. [166] processed a 450 GB channel flow via domain decomposition across 8 GPUs. Wu et al. [163] scaled to 2,5803 via distributed INR training on 512 MPI ranks. Zavorotny et al. [181] enabled interactive exploration of a 0.96 TB volume through a multiresolution rendering cache. Surrogates operate at smaller scales, with the largest evaluation at 5123 (Explorable INR on Nyx). Fourth, the CG/AI-to-SciVis transfer pipeline drives architectural innovation throughout this section: SIREN and multi-resolution hash encoding originated in neural rendering [109, 132], diffusion models migrated from image generation, normalizing flows from density estimation, and mixture-of-experts from large language model scaling.

### 5 VISUALIZATION-LEVEL METHODS

Methods in this section generate committed visual output from volumetric data rather than queryable representations. Unlike compression and surrogate models \(Sec. 4\), which produce data-level artifacts reusable with arbitrary transfer functions and renderers, visualization-level methods bake specific rendering decisions into their output. This D2 boundary separates queryable data representations from committed visual results.

#### 5.1 Learned Volume Rendering

Traditional direct volume rendering (DVR) requires manual design of transfer functions (TFs) mapping scalar values to color and opacity, a process demanding both technical expertise and domain knowledge. Neural networks offer an alternative by learning end-to-end mappings from volumetric data and parameters to rendered images, bypassing explicit TF specification.

The first learned volume rendering system [10] employed a twostage conditional GAN architecture. The approach encoded opacity transfer functions into an 8-dimensional latent space via 1D convolutions, then generated 64×64 opacity images conditioned on viewpoint and TF parameters. A second GAN translated these opacity images to 256×256 color renderings using color TF conditioning and skip connections. Trained on 200,000 procedurally generated

samples per volume, the model achieved 0.046–0.116 RMSE across six datasets spanning 170×160×140 to 1024×1024×750 resolution, with 7ms inference for interactive TF sensitivity analysis via backpropagation through the learned rendering function. However, the approach required per-volume training with no cross-volume generalization, and was limited to 256×256 output resolution with 1D transfer functions.

Unlike methods requiring explicit transfer function specification, DNN-VolVis [67] framed volume rendering as learned image-toimage translation that supports exploration from a single goal rendering. The system employed a U-Net-inspired generator accepting both a goal image and a pre-rendering from a trivial transfer function to synthesize high-resolution outputs up to 1024×1024 under new viewpoints through learned implicit TF and rendering mappings. Two discriminators enforced consistency of visual effects and viewing parameters. Trained on 10,000 rendering pairs with procedurally generated Gaussian TFs, the system achieved 24.5–38.0 dB PSNR with 0.3–0.4 second synthesis time on volumes up to 480×720×120 and demonstrated interactive 3D navigation and image-level editing of contrast and color curves. The approach was constrained by a 1024×1024 resolution ceiling due to GAN training instability, produced artifacts on unusual viewpoints absent from training data, and could not recover explicit transfer functions for further analysis.

A principled decomposition of the DVR pipeline into learnable stages emerged with DeepDVR [153], which explicitly modeled each rendering component within a neural architecture. Its generalized formulation replaced hand-crafted components with learnable networks: a volume encoder extracted discriminative features, learned color and opacity transfer functions (implemented as MLPs or direct mappings) operated in latent feature space, and an image decoder transformed n-dimensional latent renderings to RGB output. Training end-to-end from 2D image examples with a stepsize annealing strategy that reduced training time by 33%, DeepDVR achieved LPIPS 0.08 and FID 136.5 on hand-painted reference inversion tasks, and learned semantic colorization (kidney segmentation) and view-dependent shading effects from 27 clinical CT volumes at 192×192×192 resolution. Despite strong perceptual quality, the approach required long training times (hours to days on Tesla V100), achieved only 370–2008ms per frame at inference (non-interactive), and could not be fine-tuned manually once trained.

In-situ ensemble visualization typically stores only images rather than raw data, limiting post-hoc parameter space exploration. In-SituNet [61] overcame this constraint by learning an end-to-end mapping from simulation parameters, visual mapping parameters, and view parameters to visualization images using a convolutional regression architecture trained with combined feature reconstruction (VGG-19) and adversarial losses. Evaluated on combustion (Small-PoolFire), cosmology (Nyx), and ocean simulation (MPAS-Ocean) ensembles with 300–4000 members, the trained surrogate generated 256×256 images in approximately 30ms and enabled interactive sensitivity analysis via backward propagation, which achieved 24– 29 dB PSNR and substantially outperformed interpolation baselines. The approach traded flexibility for performance: visual mappings had to be predefined to limit training data requirements, and generated images remained approximations with artifacts rather than reconstructions that are pixel perfect.

Automatic optimization of rendering parameters requires analytic gradients through the volume rendering integral, a capability realized through differentiable DVR [157]. The implementation used an inversion trick that exploited the invertibility of front-to-back alpha blending to reduce memory from O(k ·n) to O(n) where k is samples per ray and n is pixel count, and it supports both forward and adjoint modes of automatic differentiation. Applications included best viewpoint selection via differentiable opacity entropy (21.3ms per iteration), transfer function reconstruction from reference images (42.6–47.8 dB PSNR), and tomographic density reconstruction from

64 projection views at 512×512 resolution with 49.70 dB PSNR for skull CT (vs. 34.40 dB for ASTRA algebraic reconstruction). For emission-absorption volumes with non-monotonic transfer functions, they introduced a two-stage approach reconstructing color volumes before density estimation, though non-convexity led to local minima and spurious noise in fine details.

Super-resolution methods enhance volume rendering quality at the visualization level rather than the data level. Devkota et al. [22] applied CNN super-resolution with supplementary features (depth, normals) and temporal reprojection for medical DVR and achieved up to 8×8 upsampling at 10 fps with 37–38 dB PSNR on clinical datasets. Weiss et al. [154] used frame-recurrent networks to upsample low-resolution depth and normal maps 4× for isosurface rendering. The approach inferred ambient occlusion without explicit ray tracing and achieved two orders of magnitude speedup (14.5s to 0.086s on 10243 volumes). Dyken et al. [30] combined progressive raycasting with DNN reconstruction of partial renders (85% completion) for isosurface visualization under memory constraints. Their method reduced memory overhead by 5.7× and data decompression by 8.4× and rendered 1280×720 frames at 85% completion in 339ms on 2048×2048×1920 volumes.

Neural networks also assist transfer function design workflows. Pan et al. [113] combined differentiable rendering with learned latent spaces to enable TF exploration in the image domain, where users query with exemplar renderings and apply semantic modifications rather than navigating TF parameter space directly. On five volumetric datasets, the method achieved 30.1–36.7 dB PSNR for reconstructed renderings. Jung et al. [75] employed content-based retrieval with CNN features and dynamic time warping to automatically generate TFs from labeled knowledge databases with 71.9% recall on medical CT structures. Nasim Saravi et al. [122] optimized continuous separable TFs via differentiable volume rendering. This approach enables automatic transfer across structurally similar volumes such as time-series simulations or multi-subject medical datasets.

Learning which pixels require expensive ray casting can substantially reduce rendering cost. Sun et al. [136] pursued this idea by synthesizing importance masks directly from view parameters using a DCGAN network, reducing rendering latency by 2–4× over reconstruction networks alone while maintaining quality within 1 dB PSNR by rendering only 10–40% of pixels.

These learned volume rendering methods establish end-to-end neural pipelines from data to images, but lack explicit 3D geometric scene representations. Neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting address this gap by learning volumetric scene representations with view synthesis capabilities.

### 5.2 Neural Scene Representations for Volumes

The learned volume rendering methods in Sec. 5.1 synthesize images from volumetric data but do not encode explicit 3D scene geometry that supports novel view synthesis or spatial reasoning. Neural radiance fields (NeRFs) and 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) from computer graphics provide continuous volumetric representations that encode both appearance and geometry, enabling photorealistic view synthesis from posed images. In computer graphics, NeRF [108] represents scenes as continuous 5D functions (position and view direction to density and color) optimized via differentiable volume rendering, while 3DGS [79] represents scenes as collections of 3D Gaussian primitives rasterized via splatting for real-time rendering. Extensions handle dynamic scenes [116] and accelerate training via hash-grid encoding [109]. Adapting these methods to scientific visualization requires addressing fundamental differences: scientific volumes contain scalar fields requiring arbitrary transfer function support rather than fixed RGB appearance, demand quantitative accuracy for analysis tasks rather than perceptual plausibility, and span multiple timesteps with physical meaning rather than static

photographic scenes.

Prior learned visualization methods lacked 3D geometric awareness, restricting viewpoints and requiring 100+ training views per scene frame. ViSNeRF [173] addressed this gap by extending NeRF to multidimensional scientific visualization. The approach factorized (3+K)-dimensional scenes into a 3D spatial tensor and K-dimensional parameter tensor and combined explicit feature grids with implicit MLP decoders to handle transfer functions, isovalues, timesteps, and simulation parameters within a single model. Trained on only 42 views per frame (95% data reduction versus InSituNet's 40,000 images for Nyx cosmology dataset), ViSNeRF achieved 27.51–37.26 dB PSNR and 0.945–0.995 SSIM in 1.06–1.66 hours, which included temporal interpolation from 11 training timesteps to 181 frames and TF exploration with continuous opacity variation. However, rendering remained slow at 1.86 fps for 256×256 images (1.60 minutes for 181 views), posed challenges for real-time interaction, and used volumetric isosurface representation rather than implicit surfaces, which yields less accurate geometry than dedicated surface methods.

A fundamental incompatibility limits standard 3DGS for scientific volumes: it bakes view-dependent color and opacity rather than supporting arbitrary transfer function exploration. Volume Encoding Gaussians (VEG) [29] resolved this limitation. VEG decoupled data representation from visual mapping: each Gaussian primitive encoded only scalar field values, and a training strategy guided by opacity with multiple TF opacity maps highlighted different scalar ranges to ensure full-range representation while maintaining TF-agnostic capability. The method achieved 6.76× better training PSNR than iVR-GS, trained 6.8× faster with 17× smaller models (0.7–4.9 MB), and rendered with arbitrary unseen transfer functions including broad opacity, narrow opacity, and random colormaps without retraining. Training coverage depended on carefully chosen opacity maps spanning the scalar range of interest, with incomplete coverage potentially leaving reconstruction quality gaps.

Non-photorealistic rendering of volume visualizations can benefit from neural style transfer, an idea that Tang and Wang [143] realized through a NeRF approach. The three-stage pipeline separated scene geometry from color appearance via a frozen density field and knowledge distillation for non-photorealistic transfer from reference images. The method achieved superior cross-view consistency (LPIPS 0.069 vs. 0.078–0.171 for baselines) at 145–153ms per frame, though NeRF cannot represent zero-opacity regions and style editing required optimization iterations rather than real-time interaction.

When raw volume data cannot be shared due to size, privacy, or security constraints, reconstructing density and color volumes from rendered images becomes necessary. ReVolVE [175] addressed this with a NeRF approach that combined vector-matrix tensor decomposition (reducing memory from O(N 3 ) to O(N )) with a compact MLP decoder that used only sampled features rather than point locations and view directions, and improved robustness with limited training images (6–18 views). Reconstructed volumes enabled visualization enhancements including depth ordering with lighting, opacity and color adjustments, isosurface extraction with ambient occlusion, and K-means color clustering for segmentation. The method outperformed five baselines (DiffDVR, Plenoxels, 3DGS, TensoRF, Instant-NGP) on datasets from 8 MB to 4.2 GB with 540× compression for large volumes. The PyTorch implementation rendered slower than CUDA methods due to batch processing without early ray termination, could not reconstruct zero-opacity regions, and required retraining if transfer functions changed to visualize previously hidden content.

Radiance caching for path-traced volume rendering can use 3DGS primitives to store attenuated radiance at multiple path lengths, as demonstrated by Bauer et al. [7], who optimized exponentially subsampled Gaussians in real time from noisy path samples. On six

scientific datasets at 720p, the cache achieved real-time frame rates (38–186ms total) with a 28 MB memory footprint versus 272 MB for neural radiance caching, though it introduced bias with no guaranteed error bounds and required re-initialization when transfer functions change.

Memory limits of a single GPU constrain 3DGS training to approximately 11.2M Gaussians on an A100, far below the requirements of large scientific volumes. Han et al. [58] overcame this barrier with distributed training at HPC scale that partitions datasets across nodes with ghost cells at boundaries to ensure smooth transitions and background masks to eliminate rendering artifacts. Training Gaussian splats independently per node then merging for global rendering, the system achieved 3.1× speedup scaling from 4 to 8 nodes on Richtmyer-Meshkov (106.7M Gaussians, 5.3 GB) at 2048×2048 resolution with PSNR 30.04, SSIM 0.97, LPIPS 0.019. Moderate-sized datasets showed limited scaling gains (Rayleigh-Taylor at 18.2M Gaussians: 1.4× speedup from 2 to 4 nodes) when insufficient to fully utilize additional nodes, with future challenges including load balancing for uneven distributions and integration with in situ simulation pipelines.

Real-time editing of transfer functions and lighting during neural volume rendering became feasible with iVR-GS [144], which uses editable and composable 3DGS primitives to overcome the limitation that existing methods bake visual parameters into fixed representations. The approach composed multiple basic models trained on disjoint transfer function ranges and extended Gaussian primitives with normals and shading coefficients to incorporate Blinn-Phong reflection. This design enables real-time editing of color, opacity, and lighting at inference and automatic optimization to approximate reference images. The composed model outperformed Plenoxels, CCNeRF, and base 3DGS on reconstruction quality (PSNR approximately 32–34 dB for novel view synthesis, 30–33 dB for relighting), where vector quantization achieved 7.2× compression (6.2 MB vs. 44.8 MB) and maintained accuracy within 0.06 dB. Model size scaled linearly with the number of basic scenes reaching gigabytelevel for hundreds of transfer functions, used uniform viewpoint sampling that may be suboptimal, and required redesigning the lighting representation for rendering models beyond Blinn-Phong.

Additional 3DGS adaptations include training Gaussians directly on point clouds extracted from isosurfaces for real-time rendering [123] and extending 2D Gaussian primitives with per-primitive texture maps for stylization consistent with geometry [140]. Neither has been validated beyond its target domain (L1).

Domain-specific NeRF and 3DGS adaptations include reconstructing internal anatomy from sparse MRI slices via factorized Gaussian density [93], NeRF emission tomography for turbulent flow reconstruction from multi-camera projections [78], and 3DGS compression of path-traced medical CT volumes for cinematic rendering on consumer mobile devices [111]. Each targets a single imaging modality, reinforcing the domain-specificity pattern (L1).

Several capabilities transferred directly from CG: continuous volumetric scene representation, differentiable rendering for optimization based on gradients, hash-grid encoding for training acceleration, and real-time Gaussian rasterization. However, the NeRF and 3DGS transfers required fundamental SciVis-specific adaptations: scalar field encoding for arbitrary transfer functions rather than baked appearance, quantitative accuracy demands rather than perceptual plausibility, and multi-timestep handling rather than static capture \(Sec. 8.1\). These adaptations reveal a tension between CG methods optimized for photorealism and the physical fidelity that scientific visualization demands.

Neural scene representations establish volumetric geometry awareness lacking in earlier learned rendering methods, but Monte Carlo path tracing introduces noise requiring specialized denoising approaches.

#### 5.3 Denoising and Quality Enhancement

Monte Carlo path tracing produces physically accurate volume renderings by sampling light transport paths, but requires high sample counts (hundreds to thousands of samples per pixel) to converge to noise-free images. At low sample counts enabling interactive frame rates (1–16 samples per pixel), the resulting images contain highvariance noise that obscures volumetric features. Neural denoising methods learn to reconstruct high-quality images from noisy lowsample renderings by exploiting spatial and volumetric correlations. These methods trade unbiased Monte Carlo convergence for biased neural reconstruction that achieves visually acceptable quality at a fraction of the computational cost.

Hofmann et al. [66] pioneered neural denoising for path-traced medical volumetric data. The key challenge is that existing surfaceonly denoisers fail on participating media where auxiliary features (depth, normals, albedo) are ill-defined or unavailable throughout scattering volumes. Their dual autoencoder architecture separated high-frequency direct lighting effects (processed via primary features from first scatter events including model-space position, world-space normal, gradient magnitude, optical thickness) from low-frequency indirect lighting (handled via secondary features from subsequent scatters), combined with a relativistic discriminator and a loss function that preserves specular detail that retained anatomical detail critical for medical interpretation. Trained on approximately 3000 frames of medical CT scans with 1024 spp ground truth, the method

achieved 30.50 dB PSNR and 0.915 SSIM on known scans with unknown transfer functions at 1 spp (1000× sample reduction, approximately 45× speedup for 300-frame video generation) and outperformed OpenImageDenoise by approximately 6 dB PSNR. However, the network exhibited limited generalization beyond medical CT anatomy (PSNR dropped from 32.98 to 24.75 dB on unknown scans and transfer functions), required approximately 131ms per frame unsuitable for interactive visualization, and temporal reprojection was limited to three frames to prevent noise accumulation artifacts.

Additional volumetric denoising methods demonstrate specialized architectural choices for path-traced volume rendering. Jabbireddy et al. [70] employed neural bilateral grid denoising with volumetric auxiliary features (depths, positions, normals from first surface-like interaction and first scattering event). The method matched 16 spp quality at 1 spp with 17ms denoising time for 1024×1024 resolution (PSNR 30.52, SSIM 0.966). Xu et al. [169] introduced dual-input feature fusion with radiance decomposition weighted by transmittance that separates low-noise SDF components from high-noise quotient components. The method achieves real-time 4 spp denoising in approximately 20ms at 2560×1440 resolution with superior temporal stability via learned weight map blending. Engel and Ropinski [31] predicted per-voxel ambient occlusion volumes at 128×128×128 resolution using 3D convolutional encoder-decoders with implicit transfer function representation. Their network computed volumetric AO in 411ms suitable for interactive DVR re-execution on transfer

function or volume changes (SSIM 0.875), though limited to coarse resolution due to 3D CNN computational cost.

Computer graphics denoising methods for surface rendering [15, 179] provide architectural foundations but require adaptation for volumetric media where scattering occurs throughout the volume rather than at discrete surfaces.

Beyond noise reduction, rendering optimization methods accelerate volume visualization through adaptive sampling, neural prefetching, and learned importance prediction.

#### 5.4 Rendering Optimization and Efficiency

Interactive volume visualization demands real-time frame rates, but direct volume rendering with high-quality sampling along each ray can require milliseconds to seconds per frame depending on volume size, transfer function complexity, and image resolution. Neural networks can accelerate rendering by learning where to sample densely (regions with high visual contribution) versus sparsely (empty space or homogeneous regions), predicting rendering outcomes to enable caching and prefetching, or optimizing computational bottlenecks in the rendering pipeline.

Uniform sampling allocates equal computational resources to all image regions regardless of visual importance, an inefficiency ripe for learned optimization. Weiss et al. [156] addressed this with adaptive sampling and reconstruction for volume visualization. The end-to-end pipeline trained three networks jointly: an importance network predicted where high sample density was needed from 128×128 input, a differentiable sampler converted importance values to adaptive sample positions via soft-max approximation (α ≈ 50), and a reconstruction network combined pull-push inpainting with residual CNN to reconstruct full-resolution images from sparse samples (5–10% for isosurfaces, 10% for DVR). Training with image losses (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) on final reconstruction quality, the method achieved PSNR greater than 30 dB and SSIM greater than 0.95 with only 5–10% of samples. It learned to place samples on silhouettes and high-curvature regions without explicit feature descriptors and demonstrated 1.5–2× speedup for large volumes (20483 ) at high resolutions (≥15362 ). However, reconstruction network overhead (approximately 90ms regardless of volume size) made the approach slower than optimized ray casting for volumes smaller than 10243 or images smaller than 10242 , quality degraded when test transfer functions used color ranges absent from training, and networks struggled with small-scale color variations from complex transfer functions, which led to uniform sampling and poor reconstruction in textured regions.

Additional rendering optimization methods demonstrate specialized acceleration strategies: foveated rendering using blue-noise sampling for interactive VR [8], acceleration of isosurface extraction from INRs guided by uncertainty [88], image synthesis without ray casting via coupled volume and TF feature branches [103], and RNN dual-space prefetching for multi-resolution volumes at large scale [137].

Across learned volume rendering, neural scene representations, denoising, and rendering optimization, visualization-level methods generate committed visual output optimized for specific viewing and styling parameters, in contrast to the queryable data representations of compression and surrogate models.

Visualization-level methods demonstrate four persistent limitations affecting scientific adoption. First, domain specificity (L1): examining cross-domain evaluation in Tab. 4 reveals that of 33 papers with sufficient evaluation detail, only 12 report testing on multiple scientific domains, with most methods trained and validated on single data types (medical CT, combustion simulation, cosmology) that exhibit degraded performance when applied to datasets outside training distributions. Hofmann's denoiser drops from 32.98 to 24.75 dB PSNR on unknown medical scans, Weiss's adaptive sampling fails on unseen transfer function color ranges, and RmdnCache prefetching degrades when user exploration patterns deviate from training trajectories. Second, evaluation heterogeneity (L2): no shared benchmarks exist for comparing neural rendering methods: papers report inconsistent metrics (PSNR for reconstruction quality, LPIPS for perceptual similarity, FID for distribution matching, SSIM for structural similarity, rendering time, sample count reduction, compression ratios) on different datasets with varying resolutions and sample counts, which prevents direct performance comparisons. ViSNeRF reports 27.51–37.26 dB PSNR on cosmology data while DeepDVR reports LPIPS 0.08 on medical CT, but these metrics and datasets are incomparable. Third, uncertainty quantification absence (L3): no NeRF or 3DGS adaptation papers report uncertainty estimates for synthesized novel views despite generating approximate reconstructions rather than ground truth, denoising methods trade unbiased Monte Carlo convergence for biased neural reconstruction without characterizing confidence intervals, and learned rendering methods provide no error bounds on generated images, which limits scientific trust in quantitative analysis from neural-rendered visualizations. Fourth, scale validation gaps (L4): most methods validate on volumes of 10243 or smaller (Berger GAN maximum 10243 , DeepDVR 1923 , ViSNeRF tested on 42 views rather than volume dimensions), with only distributed 3DGS explicitly addressing data at HPC scale (106.7M Gaussians, 3.1× speedup on 8 nodes), and it remains open whether learned visualization scales to production scientific datasets routinely exceeding 40963 or terabyte sizes common in contemporary simulations and experimental acquisitions. These limitations collectively indicate that while visualization-level methods achieve strong per-paper results on curated academic datasets (above 30 dB PSNR for denoising methods, 27–37 dB PSNR for novel view synthesis), substantial gaps remain before deployment in production scientific workflows that require cross-domain robustness, standardized evaluation, uncertainty-aware rendering, and extreme-scale performance.

# 6 ANALYSIS-LEVEL METHODS

The methods in Secs. 3 to 5 enhance, compress, or render volumetric data. Analysis-level methods instead extract semantic information: identifying structures within volumes and learning representations that capture meaningful variation across datasets or timesteps. This section surveys object detection and segmentation \(Sec. 6.1\), which locates specific features such as vortices, vessels, or material boundaries, and feature learning \(Sec. 6.2\), which produces latent representations suitable for clustering, selection, and exploratory analysis. Tab. 5 summarizes the methods covered in both subsections.

# 6.1 Object Detection and Segmentation

Identifying coherent structures in volumetric data is a prerequisite for scientific analysis, yet traditional detection criteria pose two persistent difficulties. Vortex identification methods such as the λ2 criterion, Q-criterion, and Instantaneous Vorticity Deviation (IVD) require users to select a threshold that separates vortex from background, a choice that varies across flow configurations and demands domain expertise. Biomedical segmentation faces analogous challenges: micro-level vessels in MRI volumes span only one to two voxels in diameter, and intensity-based extraction methods require manual parameter tuning per dataset. Deep learning reformulates both tasks as supervised classification and learns decision boundaries directly from labeled examples.

Automating vortex identification with learned classifiers was first explored by Deng et al. [21], who introduced Vortex-Net. Rather than designing a new vortex criterion, they trained a CNN on binary labels generated by the IVD method to replicate IVD's classification boundary from local velocity-field patches. On 3D shockwavevortex interaction volumes (81×81×81 and 161×81×81), Vortex-Net achieved 98–99% precision and 99.5–99.8% recall, with 7.4– 8.0× speedup over direct IVD computation. Because training relied

on IVD-generated labels, the learned classifier inherits that criterion's definition of vortex boundaries (L1).

Threshold selection remains a bottleneck even when the underlying criterion is fixed: the λ2 method identifies vortex cores at λ2 = 0 in theory, but practitioners often adjust the threshold to match visual expectations. Berenjkoub et al. [9] sidestepped this problem by training on parametrically generated synthetic cylinder flows whose vortex boundaries are known analytically. This approach eliminates threshold labeling entirely. Comparing CNN, ResNet, and U-Net [118] architectures on 64×64 patches of 2D flow, they found U-Net achieved the highest F-score (0.997) [9], an advantage they attributed to skip connections preserving boundary detail through the encoder-decoder path. The approach removes threshold tuning, though validation on a single 2D flow configuration leaves open the question of transfer to 3D turbulent fields.

Joint segmentation and classification in 3D requires methods that scale beyond the 2D patches used by Berenjkoub et al. Bai et al. [5] addressed this by operating directly on 3D grids. Their segmentation network, an MLP operating on local velocity vectors at each grid point, performs binary classification of vortices without the memory overhead of 3D convolutions. A separate classification network exploits the relationship between local vorticity magnitude and Reynolds number to identify flow regimes. On 3D shockwave volumes (81×81×81 to 161×81×81), segmentation accuracy reached 99.76% with greater than 50% time reduction, and classification accuracy exceeded 99% with 90% time reduction compared to traditional criteria. Both tasks were validated on canonical structured-grid configurations, including shockwave, cylinder, and turbulence flows (L1).

CNN detection transfers to physical systems beyond classical CFD (including 2D lid-driven cavity flow [76], atomic vortex cores in Bose-Einstein condensates [80], and point transformer detection in 2D unsteady flow [183]), though none addresses 3D volumetric data directly.

Detection in scientific visualization extends beyond fluid dynamics. Wang et al. [151] addressed microvascular segmentation in cerebrovascular micro-MRI, where vessels as narrow as one to two voxels are embedded in noisy, sparse 3D volumes. VC-Net combines a 3D U-Net operating on the raw volume with a 2D U-Net operating on maximum intensity projections (MIPs). A volume-composition module fuses the two streams by projecting 2D contextual predictions back into 3D space. On three cerebrovascular segmentation tasks, VC-Net achieved Dice scores of 82.98% (major arteries), 76.46% (major veins), and 74.40% (microvasculature). These scores outperformed both single-stream 3D U-Net and intensity-based baselines. The design guided by MIPs embeds a volume rendering step directly into the learning pipeline, which connects analysis-level segmentation to the differentiable rendering ideas in Sec. 5. Ghahremani et al. [38] pursued a related strategy for neurite segmentation in 3D brain microscopy, coupling CNN segmentation with hybrid volume rendering for interactive visual validation of the extracted structures.

Segmenting and tracking structures across timesteps in dynamic volumes compounds the difficulty of static segmentation: the method must maintain consistent labels as features deform, split, and merge. Yao et al. [176] proposed VolSegGS, which sidesteps grid memory constraints by representing dynamic volumetric scenes as deformable 3D Gaussians. Coarse-level segmentation clusters Gaussians by their view-independent colors. An affinity field network, guided by SAM 2D prompts, refines these clusters into fine-level segments that persist across timesteps through the deformation field. Across five time-varying datasets at resolutions up to 960 × 1440 × 240, VolSegGS achieved PSNR values of 25.76– 29.02 dB at 88–89 FPS \(Fig. 6\), the only method in this subsection validated at resolutions exceeding 5003 (L4).

Graph representations offer an alternative to grid-level classifi-

cation: He et al. [63] applied a graph convolutional network on super-voxel graphs for semi-supervised feature classification. In adjacent domains, the encoder-decoder paradigm extends to geospatial cloud masking [13] and cryo-electron tomography segmentation coupled with transfer function design [110].

#### 6.2 Feature Learning and Representation

Where detection and segmentation classify individual voxels or grid points, feature learning extracts compact representations of entire flow structures, volume regions, or timesteps. These learned representations enable downstream tasks that resist manual feature engineering: clustering thousands of streamlines by shape, selecting representative timesteps from long time series, or querying an ensemble for spatiotemporal patterns similar to a user-provided example. The methods surveyed below span autoencoder descriptors for flow structures, CNN-derived features for volume visualization, self-supervised similarity learning, and uncertainty-aware representations.

Clustering and selecting representative streamlines or stream surfaces from complex flow fields is tedious when performed manually and brittle when based on handcrafted geometric features. Han et al. [45] introduced FlowNet, the first deep learning framework for this task, which voxelizes each flow structure into a binary volume and trains a 3D CNN autoencoder to produce a 1024-dimensional feature descriptor per structure. Dimensionality reduction via t-SNE projects these descriptors into 2D space, where DBSCAN clustering identifies groups that an interactive interface exposes for filtering and representative selection. Across eleven flow field datasets, FlowNet achieved training F1 scores of 0.72–0.96 and test F1 scores of 0.57– 0.86. These results demonstrate that learned descriptors capture structural similarity well enough for automated grouping, though reconstruction fidelity varies across flow configurations (L1). Han et al. [49] later replaced the voxel representation with a graph convolutional network (SurfNet) that operates directly on surface meshes and learns both node-level and surface-level embeddings with less training time than FlowNet.

While FlowNet and SurfNet learn features from pre-extracted flow structures, transfer function design requires features computed directly from the volume grid. This problem demands a feature space that separates semantically distinct regions, a requirement that intensity and gradient features fail to meet when multiple materials share similar value ranges. Cheng et al. [18] addressed this by training a CNN on user-labeled voxel examples from electron microscopy and MRI volumes. The resulting high-dimensional deep features provide a new basis for transfer function design. Spectral reordering via the Fiedler vector of a feature similarity graph groups semantically related features together, and vector quantization produces a hierarchy of characteristic feature vectors. On FIB-SEM bacteria volumes (1,150 × 450 × 400), the fitted-transform postprocessing improved VOC segmentation scores across all four classes, though the approach requires per-volume user labeling and does not transfer learned features across datasets (L1).

Beyond spatial features, selecting representative timesteps from long time-varying simulations is a prerequisite for manageable visual analysis, yet selection methods from information theory typically handle only single scalar variables. Porter et al. [115] extended the autoencoder paradigm to this multivariate temporal problem, training an encoder-decoder network that accepts multichannel volumes (one channel per scalar variable) and produces a 1024-dimensional descriptor per timestep. Projecting these descriptors via t-SNE and selecting representatives by arc-length or angular criteria yields a compact temporal summary. Validation on climate (360×66×27, 60 timesteps), earthquake (256×256×96, 598 timesteps), and combustion (480 × 720 × 120, 122 timesteps) datasets demonstrated multivariate selection across three scientific domains, though the approach shares FlowNet's reliance on reconstruction quality as a

Most methods above require labeled data or paired reconstruction targets for training. Tkachev et al. S4 trains a Siamese neural network on a binary pretext task: given two spatiotemporal patches from an ensemble, predict whether they originate from nearby locations in space and time. #### 7 INTERACTION-LEVEL METHODS

The methods surveyed in Secs. 3 to 6 operate on volumetric data without requiring user input beyond initial configuration, yet scientific visualization tools expose parameter spaces that demand deep API knowledge and domain expertise. Large language models and vision-language models now target this gap: natural language can drive both rendering parameter optimization and end-to-end visualization workflows. The field is barely two years old. This section surveys parameter design guided by language \(Sec. 7.1\), which uses vision-language alignment to translate text descriptions into transfer function parameters, and agentic visualization systems \(Sec. 7.2\), which deploy LLM agents that autonomously generate, evaluate, and refine scientific visualizations. Fig. 8 illustrates the generalized workflow common to these methods, and Tab. 6 summarizes them.

#### 7.1 Language-Guided Parameter Design

Transfer function design remains one of the most persistent bottlenecks in volume rendering: users must manually map scalar data values to color and opacity, a process that demands exploration through trial and error of a high-dimensional parameter space. Huang et al. [68] explored an early approach to bridging the expertise gap with FlowNL, a grammar-based natural language interface for flow visualization that translated user queries into a declarative specification language, though its parser relied on a context-free grammar rather than learned representations and could not extract features beyond compositions of predefined flow primitives. The emergence of vision-language models such as CLIP shifted the paradigm from rule-based parsing to learned semantic alignment, and subsequent systems optimize rendering parameters directly from text descriptions.

Translating natural language descriptions into transfer function parameters poses an alignment problem of text and images that T2TF [72] was the first to address. Given a text prompt, T2TF optimizes piecewise-linear transfer function parameters via gradient descent on a contrastive CLIP loss: the rendered image should

be well-aligned with the user's description while remaining poorly aligned with randomly sampled negative prompts, all computed through a fully differentiable volume rendering pipeline. Regularization terms enforce density control via a beta prior on transmittance and promote TF simplicity via ℓ1 sparsity on opacity values, ensuring the optimized TFs remain editable. Across 15 datasets spanning medical CT, fluid simulations, and botanical specimens, T2TF produced semantically meaningful TFs from both generic prompts (yielding volume overviews) and detailed descriptions (isolating specific structures such as teeth or skin). The method requires approximately 300 gradient steps at 0.5–2 s per step depending on volume size, operates offline, and is camera-view dependent: features not visible from the sampled poses cannot be captured.

Two limitations of CLIP optimization (the lack of human-aligned quality assessment and the difficulty of escaping local optima in gradient-based search) motivated IntuiTF [149], which replaced gradient descent with an evolutionary algorithm that encodes transfer functions as Gaussian mixture model parameters and guides selection through pairwise comparisons evaluated by a multimodal LLM (GPT-4o or Gemini-2.0 Flash). This cycle of trial, insight, and replanning operates through an interactive system with three stages (initialization, customization, refinement) that accepts both text descriptions and reference images as input. On four medical CT datasets, the GPT-4o evaluator achieved agreement scores of 0.77– 0.85 with expert consensus derived from 28 annotators, outperforming a baseline for information entropy by 0.09–0.41 points, and four domain experts confirmed the system's usability in semi-structured interviews. Each optimization cycle required approximately 90 seconds for a population of 50 candidates, trading latency for broader exploration of the TF space compared to T2TF's gradient-based approach.

#### 7.2 Agentic Visualization Systems

While language-guided methods optimize individual rendering parameters, a more ambitious goal is to automate entire visualization workflows: translating a scientist's natural language intent into a complete, evaluated visualization without manual scripting. Dhanoa et al. The first visualization agent to close the perception loop, AVA [98], incorporated visual feedback directly into its decisionmaking. AVA operates through a cycle of perception and action powered by GPT-4V: the agent generates rendering parameters, observes the resulting image, and refines its choices based on visual assessment. Two operational modes address different use cases: a mode centered on heuristics that applies domain-specific rules (e.g., windowing presets for CT data) with LLM-guided refinement, and a mode centered on LLM that grants the agent full autonomy over parameter selection. Feedback from four domain experts (two senior AI researchers, a medical visualization professor, and a radiological diagnostics professor) confirmed the system's utility for transfer function optimization on medical datasets including MR angiography of the Circle of Willis. Unlike single-agent systems, NLI4VolVis [2] adopted a hierarchical multi-agent architecture whose agents follow a loop of visualization, perception, and action to collaboratively interpret user intents, generate declarative commands, and iteratively refine visualizations. NLI4VolVis is the most architecturally ambitious system in this section, bridging interaction-level and visualization-level methods by coupling multi-agent coordination with a neural scene representation.

Iterative self-refinement through domain-aware feedback defines VizGenie [12], a system with a single agent that takes a different approach from the multi-agent architectures above. VizGenie dynamically generates VTK visualization modules from natural language requests, retrieves contextual information via retrieval-augmented generation, and evaluates its own output using a domain-specific vision model fine-tuned from Llama-Vision (11B parameters) via LoRA. Across four scientific datasets including Hurricane Isabel (subsampled to 250 × 250 × 50) and deep water asteroid impact simulations, expanding the visual database from 150 to 300 reference images improved mean pairwise semantic similarity from 0.585 to 0.717, demonstrating the system's capacity for autonomous self-improvement. Response times ranged from 2 to 24 seconds depending on task complexity and model selection, with code generation tasks requiring substantially more time than operations using preexisting visualization tools.

Mallick et al. [106] and Peterka et al. [114] developed ChatVis, an LLM assistant for ParaView script generation that evolved across two publications. The initial system used GPT-4 with few-shot prompting and a loop for error detection and correction, succeeding on all five canonical visualization tasks where unassisted GPT-4 completed only one. The redesigned version incorporated retrieval-augmented generation from a vector database of ParaView documentation and source code, expanded the benchmark to 20 tasks (eight canonical, ten regression, and two science use cases), and achieved a 95% pass@1 rate with SSIM of 0.80 and PSNR of 40.1 dB using GPT-4o. Both versions evaluate correctness through image comparison at the pixel level rather than domain-specific accuracy measures. Whether visually similar outputs are scientifically equivalent remains an open question. The benchmark and RAG architecture make ChatVis the most reproducible system in this section, but its reliance on pixel similarity rather than scientific correctness leaves a gap that domainaware evaluation would need to fill.

This approach to code generation contrasts with architectures for tool use: Liu et al. [97] replaced script generation with real-time ParaView control via the Model Context Protocol (MCP). Their system uses Claude Sonnet 3.7 to issue API calls directly and incorporates visual understanding for iterative refinement. Additional systems extend the agentic pattern to flow visualization with finetuned LLMs [92] and mixed reality on Apple Vision Pro [124]. The agentic approach also extends to adjacent workflows including CFD setup [17, 171, 180] and scientific analysis [134]. Brossier et al. [14] survey LLM-enabled visualization broadly.

#### 8 CROSS-CUTTING ANALYSIS

Three patterns recur across the four pipeline stages reviewed in Secs. 3 to 7 but become visible only in aggregate: a directional transfer of techniques from computer graphics and mainstream AI into scientific visualization, a set of shared limitations that constrain practical adoption, and an uneven capability profile that shapes where future effort is most needed. This section synthesizes these patterns using evidence drawn from the comparison tables and synthesis paragraphs of the body sections.

#### 8.1 The CG/AI-to-SciVis Transfer Pattern

Techniques from computer graphics, computer vision, and mainstream AI appear in every pipeline level reviewed here. The transfer pattern is consistent: a proven method is re-engineered to meet the requirements for accuracy, interpretability, and data format of scientific applications.

Four transfers have reached production-ready maturity. Implicit neural representations, introduced by Sitzmann et al. [132] for continuous signal fitting, now underpin the majority of neural compression methods in Sec. 4: 19 of the 38 methods in Tab. 3 use coordinatebased architectures derived from SIREN or its variants. Multiresolution hash encoding, developed for real-time neural graphics primitives [109], enabled the compression results at the largest scale reviewed here. These span 950 GB to 0.96 TB across single-GPU, multi-GPU, and distributed configurations \(Sec. 8.2\). Neural radiance fields [108], designed for photorealistic novel view synthesis from posed photographs, were adapted for scientific volume rendering in Sec. 5.2, though most adaptations required replacing the fixed RGB appearance model with transfer function or emissionabsorption support for scalar field visualization. GAN temporal discriminators, introduced for computer graphics smoke synthesis by Xie et al. [167], became standard components across SciVis super-resolution methods \(Sec. 3\).

Each transfer required domain-specific modification. Scientific visualization demands quantitative accuracy rather than perceptual realism: compression methods must minimize reconstruction error measured by PSNR, not visual plausibility judged by FID. Structured volumetric grids replace the unstructured point clouds and posed image collections of CG pipelines. Transfer function support, absent from NeRF and 3DGS by design, must be added to enable the interactive parameter exploration that defines the scientific visualization workflow. Error bounds and uncertainty estimates, unnecessary for entertainment rendering, become requirements when neural outputs feed scientific analysis.

Three CG/AI techniques remain at early or untested stages of adaptation for scientific volume data. 3D Gaussian splatting [79] has been applied to volume visualization \(Sec. 5.2\), but the seven 3DGS methods analyzed here operate at volumes of 5123 or smaller. Only Han et al. [58] address distributed rendering, and temporal extensions such as 4D Gaussian splatting [159] have not been applied to time-varying scientific volumes. Diffusion models appear in compression \(Sec. 4.3\) and super-resolution \(Sec. 3.1\), but scientific volume applications remain limited to two compression methods [85, 89] and one SR method [11]. The broader diffusion-based 3D reconstruction literature [86] has not yet reached SciVis volumes. Foundation models for volumetric data, exemplified by RenderFM for CT rendering [37], represent the most recent transfer frontier: no equivalent exists for scientific simulation data.

A parallel transfer pathway runs from CG/AI methods not into visualization but into scientific measurement and tomographic reconstruction. Neural attenuation fields reconstruct 3D volumes from sparse X-ray projections [182], neural refractive index fields recover refractive-index distributions from background-oriented schlieren measurements [64], and hybrid neural velocity representations combine physics-informed neural fields with differentiable volume rendering to reconstruct fluid dynamics from sparse multiview video [178]. These measurement-to-volume methods share the INR and differentiable rendering machinery of the methods at the visualization level in Sec. 5 but target data acquisition rather than data presentation, and thereby extend the CG/AI transfer pattern beyond the visualization pipeline.

Two architectural directions are emerging that have not yet been adopted for scientific volume tasks. Self-supervised pretraining and foundation models for 3D volumes are active in medical imaging: Models Genesis [185] learned transferable representations from unlabeled CT scans, Swin UNETR [60] used self-supervised pretraining for segmentation, VoCo [160] learned contrastive features at the volume level, and NeRF-MAE [69] applied masked autoencoder pretraining to neural radiance field grids. None of these approaches have been applied to scientific simulation volumes \(Sec. 6\), despite the potential to address the labeled-data scarcity that constrains every supervised method reviewed here. Equivariant architectures that encode geometric symmetries by construction, rather than through data augmentation or loss-function penalties, represent a second emerging direction: 3D steerable CNNs [152] guarantee rotational equivariance for volumetric features, and equivariant graph neural operators [170] preserve SE(3) symmetry for 3D dynamics modeling. Scientific volume data possesses physical symmetries (rotational invariance of turbulence statistics, translational symmetry of periodic domains) that these architectures could exploit, but no SciVis application has tested this hypothesis.

The overall pattern is one-directional: CG and AI innovations flow into scientific visualization, but no SciVis-specific technique has influenced mainstream CG or AI practice. The adaptation gap narrows as methods achieve interactive rates on scientific data, yet the direction of transfer has not reversed.

#### 8.2 Limitation Synthesis

The four cross-cutting limitations introduced in Sec. 1 recur across every pipeline level, but their scale and distribution vary.

L1: Domain specificity. The cross-domain evaluation columns of the comparison tables reveal uneven generalization across pipeline levels. Super-resolution shows the highest rate of cross-domain testing: 13 of 19 methods were evaluated on two or more scientific domains, driven largely by one research group (Han and Wang) that systematically tested on both CFD and astrophysics data. In compression, 19 of 38 methods tested across domains, but crossdomain practice concentrates in hash-encoding groups, with the Nyx cosmological dataset serving as the closest approximation to a shared benchmark (used by nine groups, though at varying resolutions and with incompatible metrics). Visualization-level methods present the most ambiguous picture: of 33 methods, 17 reported insufficient evaluation detail to determine coverage across domains, and of the remaining 16, only 7 tested across domains. The analysis level is predominantly single domain: only 3 of 14 methods crossed domain boundaries. The coupling between detection architectures and the physical structures they target (vortices, cells, clouds) is strong enough to discourage evaluation across domains. Interaction-level methods fare better in absolute terms (7 of 11 multi-domain), but this reflects the domain-agnostic nature of LLM-based interfaces rather than demonstrated generalization of the underlying visualization parameter optimization.

The distribution reveals a structural problem: cross-domain evaluation correlates with research group practice more than with methodological maturity. Groups that maintain diverse data collections test broadly, but groups without such collections test narrowly. No external incentive (shared benchmark, community challenge, or standardized test suite) exists to change this pattern.

L2: Evaluation heterogeneity. Each pipeline level has developed its own evaluation conventions without coordination across levels. In super-resolution, 17 of 19 methods report PSNR and SSIM on self-selected volumes with no shared train/test splits, yet no two groups use identical evaluation protocols. Compression introduces a second axis of incomparability: tradeoff curves are dataset-specific, so a 40 dB result at 100× compression on Nyx at 2563 cannot be compared with a 35 dB result at 200× on combustion data at 5123 . Visualization-level methods split across rendering quality metrics (PSNR, LPIPS, FID, SSIM) and efficiency metrics (FPS, sample count reduction), with no consensus on which dimension matters more. At the analysis level, the metric space fragments further: precision, recall, F-score, Dice, IoU, and silhouette coefficients appear depending on the task, with no shared detection or segmentation benchmark for scientific volumes (unlike medical imaging, where challenges such as BraTS provide standardized comparisons). Interaction-level methods rely on ad-hoc evaluation (small-scale user studies, task completion rates, or qualitative expert feedback), with no reproducible benchmark for agentic visualization quality.

The closest precedent for standardized cross-method comparison is the work of Yao et al. [174], who benchmarked 10 neural surface reconstruction methods on scientific visualization datasets under controlled conditions. This remains the only such study in the surveyed literature.

L3: Uncertainty quantification. Of the methods reviewed across all five pipeline levels, seven provide uncertainty or confidence estimates: PSRFlow [128] in super-resolution, REV-INR [120], RMDSRN [168], SurroFlow [127], ConfEviSurrogate [27], and Explorable INR [16] in compression/surrogates, and Kumar et al. [82] in analysis. No visualization-level or interactionlevel method provides uncertainty estimates. This concentration (five of seven in a single section) reflects the relative maturity of compression research rather than a principled allocation of effort.

The gap is most consequential for visualization-level and interaction-level methods. Neural volume renderings approximate ground-truth images without communicating where the approximation is unreliable. LLM-generated transfer functions and visualization scripts offer no confidence measure, so users cannot distinguish a well-grounded parameter suggestion from a hallucinated one. Adjacent work in the CG/AI literature has begun addressing this gap: BayesRays [39] provides post-hoc Bayesian uncertainty quantification for any pre-trained NeRF model via spatial perturbation fields and Laplace approximation, and Maas et al. [105] developed visual analytics tools for presenting neural rendering uncertainty to users. Neither BayesRays nor NeRVis has been applied to scientific volume visualization, where uncharacterized error directly affects the scientific conclusions drawn from rendered images.

L4: Scalability. Most methods are evaluated at volumes of 1283–5123 , well below the 40963 or larger fields that contemporary simulations produce. Only four methods have reached production scale: Wu et al. [162] compressed a 950 GB DNS volume on a consumer GPU; Wurster et al. [166] processed 40963 via multi-GPU decomposition; Wu et al. [163] scaled to 25803 on 512 MPI ranks; and Zavorotny et al. [181] achieved interactive exploration of a 0.96 TB volume. All four are compression methods. No superresolution, visualization, analysis, or interaction method has been validated at comparable scales.

The gap between evaluated and production volumes is growing. Contemporary simulations produce 40963 or larger fields, yet the median evaluation volume across the literature reviewed here remains below 5123 . Interaction-level methods operate at the smallest scales (the largest tested volume is Hurricane Isabel at 250×250×50 after subsampling), a constraint imposed by the computational overhead of LLM inference loops layered on top of rendering pipelines.

Cross-level synthesis. These four limitations compound unevenly across pipeline levels. Data-level methods have reached production scale and begun addressing uncertainty quantification, while interaction-level methods lack evaluation standards, scalability testing, and cross-domain validation entirely. This gradient tracks the transfer pattern identified in Sec. 8.1: methods closest to established computer graphics practice matured first because they could import architectures with minimal adaptation, while methods requiring deeper domain integration remain nascent. Fig. 10 quantifies this gradient: the stacked bars show that compression and visualization output accelerated sharply after 2023, while interaction-level work remains confined to the last two years. Sec. 9 translates these compounding gaps into concrete research directions.

### 9 OPEN PROBLEMS AND RESEARCH AGENDA

The limitation synthesis in Sec. 8.2 identifies gaps that span multiple pipeline levels but resist solution within any single level. This section translates those gaps into research directions, organized in two groups: closing the four cross-cutting limitations that constrain current methods, and emerging frontiers where techniques from

adjacent fields have not yet been adapted for scientific volume visualization.

Evaluation infrastructure (L2). The absence of shared benchmarks is the most immediate barrier to measurable progress \(Sec. 8.2\), with no common evaluation protocol across the 38 compression methods or any other pipeline level. The only controlled cross-method comparison in the surveyed literature is Yao et al.'s benchmark of 10 neural surface reconstruction methods [174]. A multi-level benchmark suite would require prescribed train/test splits on publicly available datasets spanning at least three scientific domains, agreed-upon metrics at each pipeline level, and reproducible baseline implementations. BraTS and similar medical imaging challenges have established this model.

Cross-domain generalization (L1). Cross-domain evaluation rates vary from 3 of 14 at the analysis level to 13 of 19 at the super-resolution level \(Sec. 8.2\), and cross-domain practice correlates with research group data collections rather than methodological design. Two architectural directions could improve generalization. Equivariant architectures that encode physical symmetries by construction [152, 170] could exploit the rotational and translational invariances present in turbulence, combustion, and cosmological data without relying on augmentation. Self-supervised pretraining on diverse unlabeled simulation data, discussed below, offers a complementary path by learning transferable volumetric features that reduce dependence on labeled, single-domain datasets. Neither equivariant architectures nor self-supervised pretraining has been tested on scientific simulation volumes.

Uncertainty quantification (L3). Seven of the methods surveyed across all four pipeline stages provide uncertainty or confidence estimates: six are data-level methods and one is analysis level \(Sec. 8.2\). No visualization-level or interaction-level method reports confidence bounds. Post-hoc UQ methods such as BayesRays [39] and NeRVis [105] exist for CG radiance fields \(Sec. 8.2\) but have not been applied to scientific volumes. Adapting them would require replacing perceptual loss with physically grounded error metrics

tied to the scalar field values that scientists interpret. Extending UQ to interaction-level methods poses a distinct challenge: quantifying confidence in an LLM-generated transfer function or visualization script requires evaluation criteria that do not yet exist. Concrete approaches include calibrated confidence scores attached to each generated parameter set, ensemble-based parameter sampling that presents a distribution of plausible transfer functions rather than a single suggestion, and validation protocols with a human in the loop that measure whether scientists can distinguish neural outputs from ground truth. Across all levels, trustworthiness must become a first-class evaluation dimension alongside accuracy and efficiency: until learned representations report how wrong they might be, their adoption in scientific workflows that inform physical conclusions will remain limited.

Scaling to production data (L4). The four methods that have reached production-scale volumes are all compression methods. No super-resolution, visualization, analysis, or interaction method has been validated beyond 10243 \(Sec. 8.2\). Closing this gap requires three capabilities that current methods lack: distributed training that partitions volumes across nodes without boundary artifacts, streaming inference that renders sub-regions on demand without decompressing the full field, and integration with HPC I/O middleware that connects neural representations to existing simulation workflows. The gap between evaluated scales (below 5123 for most methods) and production scales (40963 and above) spans a factor of 512× in voxel count that architectural improvements alone cannot bridge.

Self-supervised and foundation models for scientific volumes. The medical-imaging pretraining successes catalogued in Sec. 8.1 have no counterpart for scientific simulation data, despite the many unlabeled volumes that would serve the same role. A foundation model pretrained on diverse simulation outputs could address both the labeled-data scarcity constraining analysis-level methods and the domain-specificity limitation affecting every pipeline level. RenderFM [37] demonstrates the concept for CT rendering, but no equivalent exists for scientific simulation data.

Generative surrogates for spatiotemporal data. Diffusion models have produced only three scientific volume applications in the surveyed literature [11, 85, 89], and autoregressive models for volumetric data remain unexplored in the SciVis context. Generative surrogates that sample spatiotemporal fields from parameter specifications could enable ensemble exploration without running additional simulations and bypass the data-scale bottleneck. Such models would also address the uncertainty gap (L3): sampling from a learned distribution provides a mechanism for communicating prediction confidence, unlike the point estimates that current deterministic surrogates produce. A central engineering challenge is conditioning: the generative model must be coupled to the simulation parameter space at training time, and validating fidelity against ground-truth simulations across that parameter space remains an open problem, especially when the training distribution covers only a sparse subset of the parameter domain.

Neural operators as visualization backends. No method in the surveyed literature generates volume fields directly from simulation parameters at interactive rates. Fourier neural operators [91] and DeepONet [101] learn function-space mappings that could be queryable visualization backends: they would produce fields on demand and replace the offline generate-store-visualize pipeline. Validating this concept would require demonstrating that operatorpredicted fields preserve the scalar statistics and spatial structures that scientists interpret, a benchmark that does not yet exist.

Immersive rendering and neural scene composition. Foveated neural rendering has been demonstrated for scientific volume data (FoVolNet [8]), and 3DGS methods achieve real-time rates

suited to head-mounted displays. Extending these capabilities to scientific volume visualization in immersive environments requires transfer function support and error characterization that current CGderived architectures lack. In particular, foveated quality reduction in the periphery must not introduce scientifically misleading artifacts such as false feature boundaries or suppressed gradients in the lowresolution regions. A related frontier is neural scene composition: independently trained neural representations of different physical fields (temperature, velocity, species concentration from the same simulation) could be composed into a unified multi-physics visualization. Implicit neural representations support this composition by design: each network can be queried independently at the same spatial coordinate. However, rendering cross-field interactions presents a distinct challenge. For example, jointly visualizing temperature and velocity requires coupled opacity and color mappings that independently trained networks cannot produce without an explicit composition layer that learns inter-field relationships. No system has demonstrated multi-field neural composition for scientific data.

Each open problem above traces a demonstrated gap to an adjacent field where the required technique already exists but remains untested on scientific volumes. The research agenda for neural volume visualization is less about inventing new methods than about adapting proven techniques to meet the accuracy, scalability, and trustworthiness requirements that distinguish scientific applications from the computer graphics and medical imaging contexts where most innovations originate.

### 10 CONCLUSIONS

This report has reviewed deep learning and neural representation methods applied to scientific volume visualization, organized across four pipeline stages: data-level enhancement \(Secs. 3 and 4\), visualization-level rendering \(Sec. 5\), analysis-level detection and segmentation \(Sec. 6\), and interaction-level parameter design \(Sec. 7\).

The per-level distribution reveals a clear capability gradient. Datalevel methods are the most mature: super-resolution and neural compression together constitute the largest body of work, four compression methods have reached production-scale volumes exceeding 40963 , yet no shared benchmark suite exists for cross-method comparison. Visualization-level methods have grown rapidly through NeRF and 3DGS adaptation but lack uncertainty quantification entirely. Analysis-level methods show limited deep learning adoption and no foundation model applications. Interaction-level methods (all published 2024–2025) are nascent, with no established evaluation standards.

Our work made four contributions. It provided a pipeline-level taxonomy covering four stages from data enhancement to interaction design. It threaded an evaluative backbone through per-section comparison tables via four cross-cutting limitations (L1–L4) and cross-domain evaluation columns. It offered the first review of interaction-level methods (language-guided transfer function design and agentic visualization workflows) for volume data. It identified open problems grounded in demonstrated gaps rather than speculative extrapolation. These contributions distinguish this report from prior work, which has either focused on a single pipeline level or omitted the evaluative and interaction-level dimensions.

Three findings emerge from the cross-cutting analysis \(Sec. 8\). First, the CG/AI-to-SciVis transfer pattern drives most methodological innovation: implicit neural representations, neural radiance fields, 3D Gaussian splatting, and large language models all originated outside scientific visualization and were adapted to its requirements \(Sec. 8.1\). Second, the four cross-cutting limitations compound rather than operate independently. Domain specificity (L1), evaluation heterogeneity (L2), absent uncertainty quantification (L3), and insufficient scale (L4) interact so that a method validated on one domain at small scale with no uncertainty bounds and no shared benchmark contributes less cumulative evidence than its reported metrics suggest \(Sec. 8.2\). Third, the capability gap between data-level methods (which have reached production scale and probabilistic formulations) and interaction-level methods (which lack evaluation standards entirely) defines where effort is most needed \(Sec. 8.2\).

In the near term, shared benchmarks and uncertainty-aware formulations would address the most actionable gaps: standardized evaluation would make progress measurable, and confidence bounds would make neural outputs trustworthy for scientific conclusions. Longer-term frontiers (foundation models pretrained on diverse simulation data, neural operators as interactive visualization backends, and generative surrogates for ensemble exploration) require adapting techniques that have matured in adjacent fields but remain untested on scientific volumes. The unifying pattern across this work is that innovation in neural volume visualization flows almost exclusively from computer graphics and mainstream AI into scientific visualization, and the field's central challenge is not generating new architectures but closing the gap between demonstrated capability and the accuracy, reproducibility, and scale that scientific practice demands.
