Polynomial Acceleration of Alternating Projections: Chebyshev
Acceleration, Convergence Rates, and Retractions
Dengyu Zheng∗

Abstract
Alternating projections (AP) near a clean intersection of smooth manifolds can converge
slowly when the Friedrichs angle is small. We develop the Fixed-Point Manifold Acceleration
(FPMA) framework for local convergence and polynomial acceleration of smooth maps that fix
a manifold pointwise. Under suitable regularity and uniform normal spectral stability, FPMA
establishes local R-linear convergence and a smooth limiting map inducing a local retraction.
At fixed points, consistent polynomial acceleration with p(1) = 1 preserves the selected limit’s
first-order response to normal initial perturbations whenever the transformed normal blocks
are uniformly spectrally stable. Under sufficient smoothness, we give a curvature criterion
for second-order induced retractions and show that stable consistent polynomial acceleration
preserves both the quadratic retraction expansion and this criterion. As a special case, we
establish new local R-linear convergence guarantees for fixed-degree Chebyshev-accelerated
exact AP and second-order induced retractions. For uniform Friedrichs angle θF ∈ (0, π/2),
acceleration strictly improves the local convergence rate per AP evaluation. The largedegree limiting spectral factor at fixed θF predicts O(θF−1 ) evaluations for fixed relative error
reduction as θF ↓ 0, versus O(θF−2 ) for ordinary AP. The framework also covers inexact,
relaxed, and generalized AP under their respective regularity and stability conditions.
Keywords. Alternating projections; Chebyshev acceleration; stationary polynomial transform;
limiting map; retraction; clean intersection; manifold optimization.

zero. AP can therefore converge slowly at small intersection angles.

This slow convergence motivates polynomial acceleration. For a linear iteration with operator
L, polynomial acceleration replaces its m-step operator Lm by pm (L), where the degree deg pm
is at most m. We call pm consistent if pm (1) = 1, so that pm (L) preserves vectors fixed by
L. On a real spectral interval excluding 1, the Chebyshev minimax polynomial minimizes the
maximum magnitude among consistent polynomials of degree at most m. Chebyshev polynomial
acceleration is classical in linear iterative methods [20, 33, 31, 32], and periodic Chebyshev
relaxation has also been studied for locally contractive fixed-point iterations [34]. For AP on two
subspaces, Knyazev et al. [23] developed the self-adjoint compression and optimal-polynomial
viewpoint. Bello-Cruz [9] gave an explicit full-interval Chebyshev semi-iteration. A related
benchmark is the optimally tuned generalized alternating projections (GAP) method, which
attains the linear convergence factor (1 − sin θF )/(1 + sin θF ), where θF is the Friedrichs angle.
Its corresponding local rate extends to smooth manifolds [17, 18]. Other acceleration mechanisms
include line search, extrapolation, supporting hyperplanes, and vector extrapolation [19, 8,
7, 30, 4]. To place the polynomial-operator viewpoint in a common local nonlinear setting,
we develop the Fixed-Point Manifold Acceleration (FPMA) framework for smooth maps that
fix a manifold pointwise and their consistent polynomial transformations. For exact AP near
clean intersections of smooth manifolds, we establish new local R-linear convergence results for
fixed-degree Chebyshev-accelerated maps and prove strict improvement of the normal spectral
bound per AP evaluation under the assumptions stated below. Iterations on subspaces with a
fixed polynomial are included as a linear special case, while inexact AP, relaxed AP, and full
GAP are also covered under their respective regularity and stability conditions.
A smooth AP limiting map induces a local retraction at x ∈ M by mapping each sufficiently
small tangent vector η ∈ Tx M to the iteration limit from x + η. This supports manifold
optimization when the intersection projection PM is difficult to compute but the component
projections PMi are tractable. Absil and Malick [2, Proposition 5 and Section 4] show that
(x, η) 7→ PM (x + η), η ∈ Tx M, is a local retraction and generalize this construction to projectionlike maps. Chen et al. [13] use tangent–normal decompositions of iteration errors and their
derivatives to establish smooth limiting retractions for AP-type maps and their second-order
property under the stated assumptions. Their NewtonSLRA analysis also gives fourth-order
agreement with metric projection for tangent perturbations in structured low-rank approximation
(SLRA). For Regularized Newton–SLRA with fixed regularization, Zheng and Chen [35, Appendix
B] establish a C 1 local limiting map and, under additional smoothness, a second-order induced
retraction with fourth-order agreement with metric projection for tangent perturbations. FPMA
recovers the corresponding local limiting-map and retraction results for smooth stationary maps
under its regularity and normal stability assumptions, while allowing more general tangent–
normal coupling and extending the analysis to polynomial acceleration. On a nonisolated solution
set, this extension must address both the rate of convergence and the dependence of the selected
limit on the initial point, since the latter can affect the accuracy of the induced retraction.

Throughout, E is a finite-dimensional Euclidean space with inner product ⟨·, ·⟩ and induced
norm ∥ · ∥, and r ∈ {1, 2}. All coordinate spaces carry the Euclidean norm; for linear and

corresponding orthogonal projectors. For a linear operator A, let spec(A) be its spectrum and
spr(A) := max{|λ| : λ ∈ spec(A)} its spectral radius, with spr(A) := 0 on a zero-dimensional
space. The symbols I and Id denote, respectively, a linear identity operator and an identity map
on the indicated space.

Retractions
In manifold optimization, retractions replace the often expensive exponential map with a tractable
map having the same first-order behavior at the zero section [1, 13].
The tangent bundle and its zero section are T M := {(x, η) : x ∈ M, η ∈ Tx M} and
0T M := {(x, 0) : x ∈ M}. Under the C r+1,αH assumptions used below, T M has the regularity
needed for the local C r maps used in the analysis.

Remark 4.12 (Scope of the stationary analysis). In the affine-combination map (4.2), the
coefficients are prescribed by a fixed polynomial. For linear problems, Krylov methods and
Anderson acceleration also admit a polynomial interpretation, but can select combinations
using evolving subspaces or stored residuals [32]. Such data-dependent coefficients generally
change with the iteration, so the fixed-map analysis above does not directly apply. In (4.19), the
relaxation parameters vary within a cycle, while the same composite map is repeated. For each
fixed degree and root ordering, local well-definedness follows by shrinking the neighborhood;
neither this neighborhood nor the nonlinear prefactor need be uniform in the degree.
Different root orderings give the same derivative at fixed points and the same spectral factor,
but can change intermediate growth, roundoff behavior, and the region of convergence. To moderate intermediate products, our implementation uses a greedy Leja ordering [3, Algorithm 3.1]:
start with the largest root, then select the remaining root that maximizes the product of its
distances from the selected roots. The theory does not compare the nonlinear or numerical
effects of these orderings.

Numerical experiments
We assess Chebyshev acceleration through three questions. Does the response to a small
normal perturbation agree with the predicted spectral factor? Do the accelerated limiting maps
27
preserve the retraction properties of AP? Can these methods reduce the computational cost of
a nonlinear feasibility problem? We answer the first two questions using controlled manifold
intersections with known geometry, and the third using finite unit-norm tight frame construction
and completion.
We count one application of the base AP map, comprising one projection onto each set, as
one base AP evaluation. A degree-m Chebyshev cycle costs m such evaluations, so comparisons
use evaluation counts rather than cycle counts. In the frame tests, each evaluation requires one
thin singular value decomposition (SVD); SVD count is therefore the primary cost measure,
with elapsed time as a secondary measure. Common warm-up steps and rejected proposals are
included. Additional protocol and implementation details are collected in Appendix D.

8
Conclusion
The FPMA framework separates normal convergence from limiting-map behavior through the
tangent–normal linearization of a map that fixes a manifold pointwise. Under uniform spectral
stability, polynomial acceleration changes the normal block from Ax to p(Ax ) while preserving
the normal derivative Bx (I − Ax )−1 of the limiting map. For exact AP on a clean manifold
intersection, the uniform normal spectral radius is ρAP = c̄2F , and Chebyshev acceleration gives
a smaller normal spectral factor per AP evaluation for every finite degree. In the large-degree
Chebyshev limit, the normal spectral factors predict an improvement in the Friedrichs-angle
dependence from O(θF−2 ) to O(θF−1 ). Under the stated smoothness conditions, the accelerated
limiting maps also induce local second-order retractions and agree with metric projection to
fourth order for tangent perturbations. The framework further applies to inexact maps that
match metric projections in value and derivative at the intersection and to relaxed and generalized
AP under appropriate stability conditions. The numerical experiments support the predicted
normal spectral factors, angle scaling, and retraction behavior, and show reduced SVD counts
on the tested FUNTF problems.

A sufficient condition for vanishing coupling in projectiontype compositions
We give a sufficient condition for the normal-to-tangential block to vanish. It does not require
the inexact maps to have the same first-order jet as the metric projections: instead, at points of
M, their derivatives preserve the individual normal spaces.
Proposition B.1 (Vanishing coupling for projection-type compositions). Suppose that each ϕi
fixes Mi and that, for x ∈ M,
Dϕi (x)Nx Mi ⊆ Nx Mi ,
i = 1, 2.
(B.1)
Then Dϕi (x) preserves Nx M, and the composition S = ϕ1 ◦ ϕ2 satisfies
BxiAP = 0.
(B.2)
Proof. Since ϕi |Mi = Id, its derivative is the identity on Tx Mi . Let n ∈ Nx M and write
n = ti + ni for its orthogonal decomposition relative to Tx Mi ⊕ Nx Mi . Because M ⊆ Mi , we
have Tx M ⊆ Tx Mi and hence Nx Mi ⊆ Nx M. Thus ni ∈ Nx M, and the assumed invariance
in (B.1) gives Dϕi (x)ni ∈ Nx Mi ⊆ Nx M. Also, ti = PTx Mi n is orthogonal to Tx M, so
ti ∈ Tx Mi ∩ Nx M. Both terms in Dϕi (x)n = ti + Dϕi (x)ni therefore belong to Nx M. By the
block definition (2.13), the composition’s invariance of Nx M gives (B.2).

FUNTF protocol
The projections and stopping residual used for (7.5) are defined in (7.6) and (7.7). Construction
uses (d, N ) = (16, 32), (32, 64), and (64, 128). Completion uses (d, N, p) = (32, 64, 16) and

All methods share each seed’s initial matrix.
After ten base AP evaluations included in the reported cost, the common heuristic estimator
is
ρb = min{0.9999, max{10−6 , 1.02 median(rk /rk−1 )}},
(D.1)
where rk := r(Xk ) is the residual defined in (7.7) after the kth counted base AP evaluation, and
the median uses at most five recent positive finite ratios. This residual-ratio estimate is not a
certified spectral bound; away from the local asymptotic regime it may under- or overestimate
the true uniform normal spectral radius. R-AP relaxes the full AP map SC as (1 − ω)Id + ωSC ,
with ω = 2/(2 − ρb) chosen by (4.14) for the interval estimated by (D.1); the degree-4 and degree-8
periodic schemes use Leja-ordered roots and restart. A nonfinite residual or a residual greater
than 1.2 times the previous residual rejects the proposed periodic scheme. The solver then
falls back to AP, and all attempted base AP evaluations are counted. The evaluation budget is
5 × 104 .
There are 20 seeds for each of five problems and four methods (400 runs). Timing is the
median of three deterministic repetitions; counts and times are aggregated by medians. Paired
speedup intervals are the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of 5000 bootstrap resamples (seed 20260912).
Each reported base AP evaluation for these frame methods performs one thin SVD, so the
base AP evaluation and SVD counts coincide. SVD count is the primary computational metric.
Timings depend on the implementation, and the fixed method order makes them secondary
descriptive evidence.
