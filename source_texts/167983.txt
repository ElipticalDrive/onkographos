Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) relies on multi-view images, creating a barrier for museum
artifacts archived only as colored point clouds without corresponding registered images. We propose
a 3DGS paradigm that operates directly on colored point clouds, enabling high-ﬁdelity reconstruction
independent of external image sets. Our approach is guided by dual priors. First, a feature-aware
sampling algorithm constructs a precise geometric prior with absolute scale from the input point cloud,
serving as the geometric foundation. Second, an ideal visual prior provides supervision by generating
synthetic views from the point cloud, which are then reﬁned using an enhancement chain that includes
anti-aliasing and super-resolution. Experiments on public and self-built datasets validate our
method’s superior rendering quality, achieving a signiﬁcant PSNR improvement of 3.25 dB over the
baseline method on our self-built dataset. The proposed framework thus offers an innovative and
robust pipeline for effectively leveraging vast archives of existing 3D scan data.
The digital preservation of museum artifacts is a critical mission in safeguarding human cultural treasures, predicated on creating high-ﬁdelity,
interactive digital surrogates to permanently archive their intricate forms
and historical context1–4. However, a vast amount of digital heritage is
archived solely as high-precision colored 3D point clouds across numerous
museums and research institutions worldwide5. While these valuable data
assets accurately record the geometry and color information of artifacts, the
corresponding sets of multi-view, registered images are often not provided
or retained due to proprietary system constraints, archival policies, or
simply being lost over time. This data landscape renders them directly
inapplicable to modern rendering techniques that mandate photographic
input. Concurrently, the emergence of 3DGS technology6, with its exceptional performance in rendering quality and efﬁciency, has positioned it as
an ideal pathway for achieving high-ﬁdelity digitization. Consequently, how
to effectively leverage the vast archives of existing colored point cloud data to
harness the powerful rendering capabilities of 3DGS has become a critical
and unresolved challenge in the ﬁeld of digital cultural heritage.
Existing 3D reconstruction approaches for high-ﬁdelity digitization
can be broadly categorized into two technical pathways: passive imagebased modeling and active scan-based modeling, both of which exhibit
fundamental bottlenecks. The passive pathway, represented by SfM7. and
MVS8,9, has seen widespread adoption due to its low cost and operational
convenience. However, its core deﬁciency lies in its performance being
entirely constrained by image texture10,11. Studies have demonstrated that
when processing the texture-less or poorly-textured surfaces common to
museum artifacts, this pathway fails to reconstruct reliable geometry, often
resulting in sparse, noisy, or even topologically incorrect data. Furthermore,
in the absence of external scaling references such as GPS, it is inherently
incapable of recovering the scene’s absolute scale12–14. Consequently, while it
can provide rich visual information, it suffers from a fundamental deﬁcit in
terms of geometric ﬁdelity15.
In contrast, the active pathway, epitomized by laser scanning, offers
unparalleled advantages in geometric ﬁdelity16. However, the primary
dilemma within this workﬂow lies in achieving visual ﬁdelity17. The issue is
not with laser scanning itself, but with the decoupled geometry-color
acquisition process it necessitates. This process, where color and texture
information is dependent on additionally registered RGB cameras, not only
frequently compromises color ﬁdelity due to registration errors but also
contends with challenges such as high equipment costs18. and difﬁculties in
capturing data from specular materials19. While early works attempted
shallow integration, such as projecting textures onto scanned point clouds20,
such methods generally fail to meet high-ﬁdelity requirements. These discrete geometric representations, while geometrically precise, are inherently
incapable of directly producing photorealistic renderings with complex
lighting and view-dependent effects. This creates a signiﬁcant technological
gap between high-precision data acquisition and high-ﬁdelity neural
rendering21,22. In summary, the demand for high-ﬁdelity digitization of
museum artifacts reveals a fundamental trade-off dilemma. This long-

The ability to synthesize novel views of a scene from a collection of
existing images has long been a central pursuit in computer graphics and
computer vision. Early Image-Based Rendering (IBR) techniques, such as
light ﬁeld rendering, established a foundational groundwork but were
typically constrained by dense image sampling requirements and a limited
understanding of scene geometry, hindering the generation of highly photorealistic results23,24. The advent of neural rendering, epitomized by NeRF25,
has brought about a paradigm shift, enabling the synthesis of unprecedentedly realistic views and opening new avenues for the virtual exhibition
of cultural heritage26–29. However, the success of NeRF is accompanied by
signiﬁcant limitations: its training and rendering processes are often slow,
and its implicit scene representation complicates the direct integration of
external geometric priors. Although a multitude of subsequent variants have
emerged, such as Plenoxels30, Instant-NGP31, Mip-NeRF 36032, NeRF + +33,
and Block-NeRF34, which have made signiﬁcant strides, the fundamental
nature of NeRF’s implicit representation continues to limit its potential in
high-ﬁdelity applications requiring precise geometric control.
Against this backdrop, 3DGS6. has rapidly emerged as a powerful
alternative. It employs explicit 3D Gaussian primitives and an efﬁcient,
differentiable rasterizer, achieving high-quality rendering at real-time
speeds. More importantly, the explicit nature of the 3DGS representation
and its adaptive density control mechanism offer a theoretical foundation
for integration with external geometric priors, positioning it as an ideal
candidate for cultural heritage digitization. However, underlying its
exceptional performance is a fragile link: its initialization process is entirely
dependent on the sparse point cloud generated by SfM. This strong
dependency means 3DGS directly inherits all of SfM’s intrinsic deﬁciencies.
The initial point cloud provided by SfM is not only sparse, noisy, and
geometrically inaccurate, but more critically, for object-centric scenarios
typical in cultural heritage digitization where external scaling cues like GPS
are unavailable, it fails to recover the absolute scale of the scene35. Utilizing a
prior that lacks both geometric precision and true physical dimensions as
the starting point for optimization inevitably steers the 3DGS process onto a
suboptimal trajectory. This critical initialization bottleneck severely
undermines the robustness and reliability of 3DGS in high-ﬁdelity reconstruction tasks and constitutes a pressing research gap.
To address the deﬁciencies in geometric accuracy inherent in purely
vision-based neural rendering methods, a signiﬁcant research direction has
been the integration of external geometric priors into the rendering pipeline.
Such methods seek to leverage geometric constraints, such as depth maps,
normal maps, or point clouds, to guide and regularize the network’s learning
process, thereby enhancing the geometric ﬁdelity of the reconstruction.
Early explorations focused primarily on incorporating geometric supervision into NeRF. For instance, both DS-NeRF36. and NerﬁngMVS37.
employ MVS-generated depth maps for supervision. Other studies have
utilized normal information, for example, Ref-NeRF38. introduces normals
to disentangle diffuse and specular reﬂections, while NeuS39. implicitly
learns high-quality normals. Furthermore, a series of works has attempted to
proceed directly from point clouds like Point-NeRF40. and Neuralangelo41.
However, due to the implicit volumetric nature of NeRF, these methods
often face challenges in fusing external priors.
With the rise of 3DGS, integrating geometric priors into its framework
to enhance reconstruction quality has emerged as a new research direction.
Some studies explore the use of dense point clouds from MVS to replace the
sparse SfM initialization, providing a richer starting point for 3DGS, as seen
in SUGaR42. and GeoSplatting43., the latter of which additionally utilizes
normal information. Some studies have begun exploring tighter integration
between LiDAR data and 3D GS, particularly in the domains of large-scale
outdoor scene reconstruction and real-time (Simultaneous Localization and
Mapping) SLAM. For instance, works such as Liv-GS44. and Li-GS45.
leverage the sparse yet precise geometric information from LiDAR point
clouds to enhance or initialize Gaussian primitives. By integrating images to

mapping performance in scenarios like autonomous driving and robotic

However, these approaches are fundamentally different from our work
in both their objectives and prerequisites. Firstly, their core is multi-sensor
fusion, meaning their pipelines still rely on the availability of a synchronized,
registered image stream to provide color and textural details. They do not
address the challenge of reconstruction from colored point clouds alone,
which is central to our research for scenarios where the original camera
images are unavailable. Secondly, their application scenarios (large-scale,
outdoor, dynamic) are distinct from our focus on the static, high-ﬁdelity,
and detail-rich reconstruction of museum artifacts. Consequently, their
methodologies prioritize real-time performance and the effective use of
sparse geometry, whereas our framework is designed to unlock the full
potential of complete, high-density ofﬂine point clouds to achieve the
utmost rendering ﬁdelity.For a detailed comparison of these approaches,

To overcome these limitations, this paper proposes a novel 3DGS
paradigm speciﬁcally designed for point cloud data and guided by dual
priors, offering an effective pathway to high-ﬁdelity rendering for vast
archives of legacy point cloud data. The core contribution lies in the construction of a fully self-consistent reconstruction pipeline, independent of
external image sets, which hinges on the synergistic application of two highquality priors that respectively address the two primary bottlenecks of the
standard pipeline. First, to resolve the fundamental issues of initialization
quality and scale ambiguity, a precise geometric prior is constructed. This is
achieved via a feature-aware point cloud prior sampling algorithm that
intelligently selects an optimized subset from the input high-precision point
cloud. This subset not only serves as the accurate geometric foundation for
initializing the gaussian primitives but also injects true physical scale into the
model. Second, to address the challenge of missing supervisory signals for
training, an ideal visual prior is established. This is accomplished through a
view enhancement chain that directly generates synthetic views via projection from the input point cloud itself. These views are then enhanced
using a combination of SMAA48. and super-resolution techniques49, thereby
providing high-quality and perfectly aligned visual supervision for the
3DGS optimization.
The efﬁcacy of this framework has been systematically validated
through extensive experiments on multiple public benchmarks and a selfcollected dataset comprising various museum artifact samples. Results
indicate that our method achieves signiﬁcant improvements across various
quantitative and qualitative evaluation metrics compared to a baseline
3DGS adapted for point cloud input and its variants, demonstrating
superior performance, particularly in restoring complex artifact details and
materials. This research thus provides an innovative and robust technical
pathway for unlocking the potential of vast archives of 3D scan data and
achieving high-ﬁdelity digitization of museum artifacts. The overall pipeline
of our proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.
Methods
The overall pipeline of our proposed dual-prior driven framework is illustrated in Fig. 2. The entire process is self-contained, operating directly on a
high-precision point cloud without any external imagery. It consists of three
main stages that will be detailed in the following subsections: (A) the construction of a feature-aware geometric prior, (B) the generation and
enhancement of a synthetic visual prior, and (C) the ﬁnal 3DGS training
guided by these two priors.

local spatial context for each point in the cloud by systematically processing a series of neighborhood radii that increase in a geometric progression. At each analysis scale, features from both the geometric and

Finally, to mitigate spatial biases associated with random sampling and
to ensure deterministic, reproducible results, a greedy point selection
algorithm that simultaneously satisﬁes both saliency-based prioritization
and spatial exclusion is employed. The selection process begins by sorting all
points in the dense point cloud in descending order based on their comprehensive saliency scores, SðpÞ. The point with the highest saliency score is
then unconditionally selected and added to the ﬁnal subset, Sf . Subsequently, all other points within the minimum exclusion distance, dexclusion , of
this newly selected point are marked as excluded and removed from further
consideration. This procedure is iteratively repeated, selecting the point with
the next highest saliency score from the remaining pool of valid candidates,
until the target number of points is reached. This deterministic, greedy
approach ensures that priority is always given to the most salient points,
while the spatial exclusion rule (Eq. 12) guarantees a uniform and representative distribution.

appearance modeling, the primitive’s color is represented by Spherical

intrinsic color and geometry, and then leverage multi-view consistency to

Projected view synthesis and enhancement
Synthesizing views via point cloud projection. Following the construction of the optimized point cloud prior, high-quality visual supervision signals are required for training the 3DGS model. To ensure that
geometric and visual information originates from a single, precise source,
thereby eliminating registration errors introduced by mismatched data in
traditional pipelines, real photographs are not directly used for training.
Instead, a series of synthetic views that are geometrically in perfect
alignment with the registered high-precision point cloud is generated.
This process not only yields an ideal input for the subsequent view
enhancement but also guarantees the intrinsic consistency of the entire
reconstruction pipeline.
npj Heritage Science | (2026)14:69
First, a diverse set of observation viewpoints is generated by deﬁning a
virtual camera trajectory. To achieve comprehensive, omnidirectional
coverage of the target artifact, a series of uniformly distributed virtual
camera poses is generated on a full bounding sphere centered at the point
cloud’s centroid. Each of these virtual cameras is precisely oriented towards
the object’s center. Diverging from conventional image-based reconstruction that simulates physical camera captures, our approach is designed to
fully leverage the completeness of the input 3D point cloud, which often
contains data from all surfaces, including the underside. The omnidirectional camera placement is therefore a deliberate design choice to ensure
that all geometric and textural information present in the source data is
converted into effective supervisory signals. This strategy facilitates the
reconstruction of a complete digital heritage model viewable from any angle,
rather than merely replicating a limited set of physical viewpoints. This step
yields a large number of virtual viewpoints with sufﬁcient parallax and
overlap, along with their precise extrinsic camera matrices (rotation R and
translation T), thereby establishing a foundation for the subsequent projection rendering.
Next, utilizing these virtual camera poses, the 3D point cloud is rendered into 2D images via a projection operation. The classic pinhole camera
model is employed to project the view-transformed 3D point coordinates
onto a virtual image plane. The color value of each projected point is directly
inherited from the RGB attributes of its corresponding 3D point. However,

when processing cultural heritage artifacts with complex topographies or
self-occlusions, a naive projection intermingles foreground and background
points. This results in penetration artifacts in the generated views, precluding the formation of a physically realistic visual target.
To resolve this critical issue, the Hidden Point Removal (HPR)
algorithm50. was integrated into our pipeline. The HPR algorithm effectively
simulates realistic visual occlusions by computing a hemispherical depth
map from each given virtual viewpoint. During the projection process, the
algorithm dynamically culls all background points occluded by the foreground. This ensures that the ﬁnal rendered pixels represent only the object
surfaces visible from the current viewpoint. Through this step, the foreground object integrity and physical realism of the generated projection
maps are effectively guaranteed. The ﬁnal output is a series of clean, 2D
synthetic views containing precise RGB color information. These selfgenerated views serve as the foundation for the subsequent anti-aliasing and
super-resolution processes, collectively constituting the sole, high-quality
visual supervisory signal that drives the 3DGS optimization. The conceptual
diagram of the HPR algorithm is shown in Fig. 6.

passed through a LayerNorm layer to stabilize its distribution. Subsequently, the features are split into X1 and X2 and are fed into three parallel
attention sub-modules. Two Mutual Attention (MMA) modules are
responsible for bidirectional feature alignment and fusion: one module
warps the features of X2 to align with X1 , while the other performs the
reverse operation. These modules respectively generate features Y1 and
Y2 , which contains fused information from the adjacent frame. Concurrently, a Self Attention (MSA) module operates on the concatenated
features of X1 and X2 , with the objective of extracting intra-frame contextual information to generate feature Y3 .

Following the attention computations, the three output streams, Y1 , Y2 ,
and Y3 , are aggregated via two sequential concatenation (Concat) operations,
forming a fused feature that encapsulates rich spatio-temporal information.

Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). To ensure effective information propagation
and mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, the module incorporates two
critical residual connections, which are implemented via element-wise
addition. The ﬁrst residual connection directly adds the original input X to the
output of the attention sub-module block. This result is then processed by a
standard Feed-Forward Network (FFN), consisting of a LayerNorm layer and
an MLP. The output is combined with a second residual connection, ultimately yielding a deeply reﬁned and enhanced feature clip.

3DGS training and optimization
Following the construction of the precise geometric prior and the ideal visual
prior, 3DGS is employed as the core reconstruction and rendering engine.
This section brieﬂy outlines how the 3DGS optimization process is conﬁgured and driven to transform these high-quality priors into a ﬁnal highﬁdelity 3D representation.
Prior-based initialization. Our pipeline begins with a high-quality
starting point. For each point in the feature-aware point cloud generated
in Sec. 3.1, a 3D Gaussian primitive is initialized. The core attributes of
each primitive include its 3D position μ (directly given by the point’s
coordinate), shape Σ (initialized as a small isotropic sphere), color
(represented by Spherical Harmonics (SH) coefﬁcients, with the DC term
initialized from the point’s RGB value), and opacity a (initialized to a high
value). This initialization, based directly on precise geometric and color
priors, provides an initial state that is extremely close to the ﬁnal solution,
fundamentally avoiding the convergence issues and scale ambiguity
caused by inaccurate SfM point clouds.
Optimization and supervision. The objective of the optimization is to
end-to-end adjust the attributes of all Gaussian primitives so that the
images rendered by the differentiable rasterizer are as consistent as
possible with the enhanced synthetic views generated in Sec. 3.2. The
rendering process involves projecting the 3D Gaussians onto the 2D view
plane, followed by alpha blending using an efﬁcient tile-based rasterizer
to synthesize the ﬁnal pixel colors.
A combination of an L1 loss and a D-SSIM loss is used to measure the
difference between the rendered views and our enhanced synthetic views.

Experimental results
The performance of our proposed method was compared against that of
several other approaches on the CHARM-3D, Tanks&Temples, and BlendedMVS datasets. For each dataset, a consistent test set was established by
selecting every eighth frame, ensuring a meaningful basis for comparison
and error metric generation.
Our method was evaluated against a series of 3DGS variants, including
the baseline 3DGS, LightGaussian55, AbsGS56, and Mip-Splatting57. Both
qualitative and quantitative comparisons are presented in the ﬁgures and
tables below. Despite variations in the information content and data distribution across the datasets, our method consistently achieved superior
results. While our method exhibits longer training durations, larger model
sizes, and in some cases, lower rendering frame rates compared to other
models, it demonstrates signiﬁcantly higher precision and ﬁdelity in detail
recovery. Furthermore, it substantially reduces the severe visual artifacts that
degrade the output quality of competing approaches.
To ensure a fair and meaningful comparison, a baseline 3DGS model,
referred to as “Baseline,” was speciﬁcally adapted to our point-cloud-driven
paradigm. This adaptation involved two key modiﬁcations to the standard
3DGS pipeline. First, for initialization, the sparse SfM point cloud was
replaced. To provide a fair starting point without the beneﬁts of our featureaware sampling, the Baseline was initialized using a uniformly sampled
subset of the high-precision point cloud, with the point count set to be
equivalent to that generated by our method. Second, for supervision, the real
photographs were replaced by the unenhanced synthetic views generated
directly from the point cloud projection. This setup ensures that both our
method and the Baseline start from the same high-quality geometric source
and operate under the same condition of having only a colored point cloud
without separate registered images, thereby isolating the performance gains
attributable to our proposed dual-prior framework. The performance
comparison across all datasets is visually summarized in Fig. 11, which plots
the key rendering quality metrics.

To clearly quantify the impact of each component, the independent
contributions and synergistic effects of the three core modules—morphological anti-aliasing (AA), Transformer-based super-resolution (SR), and
the feature-aware point cloud prior initialization (Li)—were systematically
validated. The incremental introduction of the AA, SR, and Li modules,
either individually or in combination, resulted in varying degrees of
improvement in the reconstruction quality metrics. When all three modules
were employed synergistically, our method achieved optimal performance
across all three test datasets, conﬁrming its strong generalization capabilities.
The ablation studies presented in Table 4 indicate that the performance
contributions of the core modules vary. The view enhancement modules (AA
and SR) consistently provide substantial performance gains across all datasets. This outcome is attributed to the fact that our pipeline is predicated on
synthesizing views via point cloud projection, a process where aliasing and a
loss of high-frequency detail are inherent artifacts. The AA and SR modules
directly address these fundamental bottlenecks, leading to signiﬁcant and
universal improvements in rendering quality. In contrast, the feature-aware
initialization (Li) module, while still beneﬁcial, shows a more modest impact.
It is hypothesized that this is because all evaluated initialization strategies
already begin with a high-quality laser scan point cloud, which is fundamentally superior to a sparse SfM point cloud. The variation in module
impact is also related to dataset characteristics; for instance, the point cloud
for the BlendedMVS dataset was generated from depth maps, which contain
different noise and surface properties than direct laser scans, thus affecting the
relative contributions of each module on that speciﬁc dataset.
The quality of the initial point cloud directly inﬂuences the convergence speed, geometric accuracy, and ﬁnal rendering quality of 3DGS. To
underscore the performance of our feature-aware point cloud prior, it was
compared against four common sampling strategies: uniform, random,
npj Heritage Science | (2026)14:69
voxel-based, and farthest point sampling. For this comparison, all other
modules of our framework were kept enabled, with only the source of the
initial point cloud being varied.
Prior to the formal comparison, a preliminary investigation was conducted into the performance of the uniform sampling strategy at four different density levels (100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% of the original point cloud).
The results indicated that the best rendering quality was achieved at a sampling density of 25%. Based on this ﬁnding, and to ensure a fair comparison
that highlights the performance differences between strategies at an equivalent scale, the target point count for all evaluated sampling methods was set to
be approximately equal to that of the optimal 25% uniform sampling. The
point clouds for initialization are obtained via different sampling methods.
Figure 14 provides a qualitative comparison of the initial Gaussian
primitives generated by different sampling strategies. The nosample
approach, which initializes a primitive for every point in the dense cloud,
suffers from severe over-densiﬁcation. This leads to visual aliasing and color
averaging during rendering, obscuring high-frequency details under blurred, high-intensity regions and demonstrating that a mere increase in primitive count does not guarantee a quality initialization. While traditional
sampling strategies (uniform, voxel, random, farthest_point) mitigate this
issue to some extent, their failure to perceive the underlying geometry results
in distributions that are either overly regular or chaotic, thus failing to
effectively represent complex surface details. In contrast, the distribution
from our method (ours) is visually more plausible. It adaptively preserves
density in geometrically complex regions while efﬁciently generalizing
smoother areas, thereby avoiding both detail loss and visual artifacts. This
saliency-driven layout provides a structurally superior and less redundant
starting point for optimization, which directly contributes to the superior
rendering quality reported in our quantitative results.

The quantitative evaluation results, presented in Table 5, conﬁrm that
the proposed feature-aware sampling algorithm provides a high-quality

point cloud as input. The behavior of reconstruction pipelines on sparse and
noisy geometric priors can be observed in our comparison with the SfMbased Baseline. As shown in our geometric evaluation, the sparse and noisy

instability, representing a practical failure case for high-ﬁdelity digitization.
Our framework is designed to leverage high-quality data, not to rectify
fundamentally ﬂawed geometric inputs. This reliance on high-precision scan
data constitutes a key limitation for scenarios where such data is unavailable.
Discussion
In summary, this paper addresses the initialization bottleneck of traditional
3DGS by proposing a novel reconstruction paradigm driven by high-quality
priors. Our framework fundamentally resolves the issues of detail loss,
geometric deviation, and scale ambiguity introduced by SfM. Extensive
experiments on public and self-built datasets validate our method’s superior
rendering quality, achieving a signiﬁcant PSNR improvement of 3.25 dB
over the baseline method on our self-built dataset, alongside up to a 67%
reduction in Hausdorff Distance on public benchmarks. Although our
framework incurs additional upfront computational requirements, this
trade-off is validated by the substantial improvements in reconstruction
quality, providing an effective and robust pathway for high-ﬁdelity cultural
heritage digitization. Practically, this work unlocks the potential of vast,
dormant archives of legacy data consisting solely of colored point clouds
where the original images are unavailable, enhancing public accessibility to
cultural heritage. Ethically, it underscores the importance of data provenance and transparently communicating the nature of algorithmic interpretation in the resulting digital surrogates.
Despite these promising results, we acknowledge the limitations of our
current framework. While our method demonstrates exceptional performance on high-density scan data, its primary limitation is the reliance on
high-quality geometric priors. This reliance is not only on density and low
noise but also on geometric completeness. As our experiments show, performance degrades when the initial point cloud is sparse or noisy, such as
those derived from SfM. Furthermore, for artifacts with incomplete data,
such as hollow bases, the proposed omnidirectional camera strategy is
npj Heritage Science | (2026)14:69
suboptimal, as viewpoints from beneath would cause rendering artifacts.
Addressing such cases would require adaptive viewpoint selection. This
reliance on high-precision scan data constitutes a key constraint for scenarios where such data is unavailable, highlighting an important avenue for
future improvement.
Future research could proceed along several concrete directions to
address this limitation and expand the framework’s capabilities. First,
exploring more lightweight and efﬁcient methods for prior acquisition is
crucial. This includes not only fusing sparse LiDAR with casually captured
images to handle sparse data but also developing geometry-aware viewpoint
selection strategies to accommodate incomplete point clouds. Adopting
more compact super-resolution architectures could also reduce computational overhead. Second, investigating an end-to-end optimization strategy
that jointly trains the view enhancement and rendering pipelines could
further improve both performance and efﬁciency. Finally, extending the
framework to model material properties for relightable and editable digital
artifacts remains an exciting avenue.
