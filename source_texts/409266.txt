1 INTRODUCTION
Three dimensional imaging of volume data gathered from scientiﬁc ﬁelds ranging from biologi-
cal science to earth science, has given researchers the opportunity to visualise internal structures
from new perspectives [Kau90]. Volume visualisation techniques are already common place in
medical applications. Research combining volume visualisation and virtual reality with 3D ul-
trasound, for the internal investigation of the human body, shows promising initial results but
presents other technical problems [BFO92]. Interactive high quality medical imaging require-
ments include improvements in ultrasound resolution, acquisition times for MRI, and processing
speeds of computing equipment. Many high speed computing solutions for interactive real time
imaging require custom designs at the hardware level. Unresolved problems in inter active medi-
cal imaging include the processing of information for viewing, and designing appropriate human
interfaces for three dimensional visualisation systems.
Video rate 3D medical imaging would be useful in cardiology where a sequence of movements
of the heart captured in three dimensions can be replayed from a variety of viewing perspec-
tives. This technology promises to assist with diagnosis and investigations of the dynamics of
the mechanical structures in the heart. The representation and rapid visualisation of internal or-
gans of the human body, such as the heart, as four dimensional objects, is one of the identiﬁed
applications of this technology.

2 VOLUME PROCESSING ALGORITHMS
Three key factors are useful in reducing the complexity of systems which generate video data:
the projection scheme used for the viewing of volume data; restrictions on the direction of inci-
dent light for the simple detection of light occlusions; and using table look up for surface shading.
2.1 Viewing calculations
In this design, limitations are placed on the viewing plane and coordinate system in order to re-
duce the complexity of coordinate system transformations. The viewing space is limited to being
a rotational transformation of the object space. This transformation is known as an axonometric
orthographic projection and offers the viewing of several faces of an object at once. The pro-
jection allows the transformation calculation to take advantage of each row of voxels having
identical object space coordinates in two out of the three dimensions and therefore reduces the
arithmetic calculations neededto transform objects to the viewing plane. Shown in Figure 1 is a
possible location of the viewing plane in relation to the original data set and the parallel nature
of the voxel projection onto the viewing plane.
2.2 Light Sources
Shadows provide an important visual cue when locating objects in computer generated images
[WFG92]. To add shadows and arbitrary light sources to a three dimensional model requires
complex lighting calculations[FvDFH89]. To reduce this complexity,light sources may be placed
at an inﬁnite distance from the volume data, with the direction of light being perpendicular to the
faces of the volume. This location of light sources means that the angle of incident light falling
on a surface voxel is parallel to other incident light rays from the same source in the data space.
Since the angle of incident light enters the volume data perpendicular to the faces, it also enters
the cubic window perpendicular to its faces. Once a surface is constructed in the cubic window,
the calculation for surface shading is only dependent on the normal located at the centre of the
surface, instead of both the angle of incident light and the surface normal. To represent the sur-
face surrounding each voxel in the data space an index value is created. This index value is used
by a look up table to return the light intensity for a perpendicular light ray falling on a surface
located in the current cubic window.
The use of light sources at an inﬁnite distance also affects the calculation of shadows, which may
be calculated as the volume data is processed in a scan-line manner, frame by frame. As each
voxel is processed to produce surface shading effects, it is also checked for potential shadowing
by other voxels which are blockinglight from the light sources. This is accomplished by check-
ing whether another surface voxel has already been found along the same scanline in the data
space. If so all other visible surface voxels in the current scanline will be marked as in shadow.
To keep track of the shadows in one scanline a single register is needed, for the second and third
light directions, larger arrays are needed. The second light direction runs along the surface of a
frame, similar to the ﬁrst, but at right angles to it. The second lighting direction array is reini-
tialised at the start of a new frame and requires a one dimensional array to keep track of shadows.
The third light direction is parallel to the direction in which the frames are stacked in the volume
data. The rays from this light source go through each frame and the position where a light ray
has been stopped by a voxel is kept. These locations are stored in a two dimensional array. In
Figure 1 the effect of shadowing of one voxel by another is shown.

transform volume data [CLL

Our architecture is designed as an intergrated processing element which generates screen pix-
els. The possibility exists for this element to be incorporated into a larger design with several
processing elements operating in parallel. The organisation for the processing element is shown
in Figure 2. In this ﬁgure, the split between the rotation and shading subsystems, which allows
these units to operate in parallel, is shown. The two subsystems realise the mechanisms for ro-
tation and surface shading described above, and the output of these two concurrently operating
units must be synchronised prior to display. The screen synchronisation is performed by a con-

5 CONCLUSION AND FURTHER WORK
This paper presents the organisation of a hardware system, that attempts to reduce complexity
and still produce good video rate images suitable for interactive volume visualisation. The LUT
design allows the changing of pixel values generatedfor various lighting situations, surface val-
ues and volume data processing applications. The processing method offers simple techniques
for mixing original data with rendered surfaces and adds shadowing effects to direct rendered
voxel systems. This system shows promising results for the generation of 3D medical images in
an interactive real time envirnoment.
Work is currently being carriedout on increasing the quality of surface shading and incorporating
more volume visualisation techniques into the system.

3D Volume Rotation Using Shear Transformations
Article
Jul 2000
Baoquan Chen
Arie Kaufman
We present a group of methods for decomposing an arbitrary 3D volume rotation into a sequence of simple shear (i.e., regular shift) operations. We explore different types of shear operations: 2Dbeam shear, a shear in one coordinate based on the other two coordinates; 2Dslice shear, a shear of a volume slice (in two coordinates) according to the third coordinate; and 2Dslice–beam shear, the combination of a beam shear and a slice shear. We show that an arbitrary 3D rotation can be decomposed into four 2D beam shears. We use this decomposition as a basis to obtain the sequence of 3D rotation decomposition into four 2D slice shears or three 2D slice–beam shears. Moreover, we observe that two consecutive slice shears can be achieved by shifting beams in 3D space, a transformation we call a 3D beam shear. Therefore, an arbitrary 3D rotation can be decomposed into only two 3D beam shears. Because of the regularity and simplicity of the shear operation, these decompositions are suitable for implementations on a multipipelined hardware or a massively parallel machine. In addition, we present a resampling scheme in which only a single-pass resampling is required for performing multiple-pass shears to achieve the 3D volume rotation.

Three-dimensional (3-D) surface reconstructions provide a method to view complex anatomy contained in a set of computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or single photon emission computed tomography tomograms. Existing methods of 3-D display generate images based on the distance from an imaginary observation point to a patch on the surface and on the surface normal of the patch. We believe that the normalized gradient of the original values in the CT or MRI tomograms provides a better estimate for the surface normal and hence results in higher quality 3-D images. Then two algorithms that generate 3-D surface models are presented. The new methods use polygon and point primitives to interface with computer-aided design equipment. Finally, several 3-D images of both bony and soft tissue show the skull, spine, internal air cavities of the head and abdomen, and the abdominal aorta in detail.

This paper examines seven computer architectures specifically designed to rapidly render 3D medical images from voxel data. The paper opens with a discussion of work on architectures for 3D medical image rendering and then specifies parameters for assessing the performance of a 3D medical image rendering architecture. We then describe and assess the 3DP4, the Cube, the INSIGHT, the PARCUM II, the PICAP II, the Voxel Flinger, and the Voxel Processor architectures. Real time discrete shading
Article
Jan 1990
Daniel Cohen
Arie Kaufman
Reuven Bakalash
Samuel Bergman
A shading technique for voxel-based images, termedcongradient shading, is presented. As the surface information is not available in voxel representation, the surface normal must be recovered from the 3D discrete voxel map itself. The technique defines the normal as one of a finite set of neighborhood-estimated gradients and can thus employ precalculated look-up tables. Furthermore, a table-driven mechanism permits changing the light source parameters by merely redefining the look-up table. The technique uses only simple arithmetic operations and is thus suitable for hardware implementation. Since it has been implemented not as a post-processor, but as part of the projection pipeline of the cube architecture, congradient shading can be executed in real time. Two versions of the technique in real time. Two versions of the technique have been conceived and implemented:unidirectional shading, in which the gradient is estimated only from neighborhoods along the scan-lines;bidirectional shading, in which both horizontal and vertical components of the gradient are considered. In spite of the simplicity of the technique, the results are practically indistinguishable from images generated by conventional techniques.

The sources of visual information that must be present to correctly interpret spatial relations in images, the relative importance of different visual information sources with regard to metric judgments of spatial relations in images, and the ways that the task in which the images are used affect the visual information's usefulness are discussed. Cue theory, which states that the visual system computes the distances of objects in the environment based on information from the posture of the eyes and from the patterns of light projected onto the retinas by the environment, is presented. Three experiments in which the influence of pictorial cues on perceived spatial relations in computer-generated images was assessed are discussed. Each experiment examined the accuracy with which subjects matched the position, orientation, and size of a test object with a standard by interactively translating, rotating, and scaling the test object
