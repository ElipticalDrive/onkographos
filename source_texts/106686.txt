SUMMARY
Extended reality (XR) applications demand significant computational power, often exceeding mobile device capabilities, making edge computing a compelling solution for offloading this burden to nearby servers and enabling high-quality user experiences. However,
enabling high-quality XR experiences to multiple users via edge offloading presents several
challenges. First, the limited resources of edge servers pose scalability issues for multi-user
serving, necessitating efficient workload optimization and resource allocation strategies.
Flexible XR workload distribution is crucial to improve end-to-end performance, adapting to varying edge resources, offloading overheads, network conditions, and application
demands. Furthermore, the stages of edge-offloaded XR pipelines, including data compression and transmission, require technologies to be developed with real-time and low-latency
requirements in mind, as XR applications are highly sensitive to delays and require immediate feedback to maintain immersion and interactivity.
This work explores the opportunities and challenges of edge-assisted XR around efficient workload distribution, adaptive XR workload optimization, and low-latency data
compression. FleXR is a framework that enables flexible distribution of XR workloads
via runtime instantiation of distributed XR pipelines, extending the edge benefits to diverse deployment environments. FLiCR is a fast and lightweight compression method for
light detection and ranging (LiDAR) point clouds, which substantially reduces compression latency and improves compression ratios for real-time LiDAR perception tasks for
XR applications. Then, Stimpack is a 3D graphics rendering optimization system that dynamically adjusts rendering quality based on available computing resources and user-side
visual quality, considering the data quality loss caused by compression. Finally, Morphis is
a context-aware adaptive perception framework for serving XR. It optimizes the perception
serving process by leveraging characteristics of XR perception and estimated effectiveness of perception results to adaptively schedule inference tasks. All these endeavors are
xxi
to deliver enhanced XR experiences to users and make the state-of-the-art XR closer to its
ideal shape, where users can enjoy high-quality XR experiences in their daily lives, without
being constrained by the limitations of their devices.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
XR, which encompasses augmented reality (AR), mixed reality (MR), and virtual reality
(VR), is the next frontier of interactive and immersive multimedia applications. VR provides users with a fully immersive and simulated experience, while AR enhances the real
world by overlaying digital content. MR combines elements of both VR and AR to create a
seamless integration of virtual and real-world environments, allowing users to interact with
both physical and digital objects in real-time. The rapid development and adoption of XR
technologies have opened up new possibilities for various industries such as entertainment,
gaming, healthcare, education, marketing, and manufacturing [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].
XR workloads, thus, involve complex and computationally intensive tasks to perceive
the real world and render the virtual world, which require significant processing power and
resources, including 3D graphics rendering, object detection and tracking, spatial mapping
and localization, and physics simulation, etc [6]. With this high computational demand,
XR applications are inherently interactive and operate in real-time, requiring low-latency
and high-throughput operations of such tasks for smooth and responsive user experiences.
These requirements of XR pose significant challenges in providing high-quality XR experiences to mobile users, who are with limited processing capabilities and battery life.
In this context, one promising approach to address these challenges and improve the
quality of XR experiences is to offload the computationally intensive tasks to the server at
edge sites in the access tier of the network. As the edge servers are located closer to the
end users, they can provide additional computational resources at lower latency and higher
bandwidth compared to the cloud, enabling the offloading of XR workloads and relieving
the burden on the user devices. Edge offloading of XR workloads is beneficial in reducing
the processing latency and extending the battery life of mobile devices, and in enabling
1
more advanced perception and high-quality rendering [7]. Edge computing, therefore, plays
a crucial role in enhancing the user experience and expanding the capabilities of mobile XR
devices.
There have been efforts for distributing XR workloads, and the current solutions provide
support for offloading XR workloads to servers. For graphics rendering, some studies make
use of the characteristics of linear perspective in 3D graphics and split the foreground and
background rendering to reuse a hardly changing background [8, 9], and others offload
the whole rendering pipeline to the server [10, 11, 12]. Then, there are also solutions for
perception offloading, such as object detection and tracking, to the server [13, 14, 15] while
some studies present fully offloading all the tasks with a thin client [16, 17]. These solutions
have shown their effectiveness of edge assistance in improving the performance of XR
applications for mobile users on the target deployment settings.
However, due to the popularity and wide adoption of XR, the deployment scenarios
have become increasingly diverse, presenting different distributed environments in terms
of the capabilities of user devices and edge servers, the connectivity among them, and XR
workloads. In such varied environments, the current solutions will be limited in their ability to provide effective edge assistance. Additionally, unlike cloud servers which can scale
up resources on-demand, edge servers are provisioned with limited computing resources
naturally, leading to scalability challenges when serving multiple users concurrently. As
XR devices are becoming more sophisticated with multi-modal functionality and enhanced
sensor capabilities, offloading perception tasks requires efficient data transfer with compression for emerging sensor data, such as LiDAR point clouds. This landscape poses various challenges in the serving system for XR workloads at the server and infrastructure
technologies for distributed XR.

1.1
Problem Statement
The diverse deployment scenarios of XR offloading make the existing solutions with
static workload distribution suboptimal. There have been efforts for systems for serving XR workloads in a distributed manner [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. A
common thread across them is that they make a priori decisions about the expensive functionalities whose execution should be offloaded to the server, based on assumptions about
the operating or environmental factors such as the client device capacities and network conditions. They provide support for offloading fixed functional components of perceptions or
graphics rendering (or both of them). In that sense, the resulting distributed platforms provide support for offloading of concrete functional components, and their benefits can be
realized only in specific deployment scenarios where the assumptions hold.
However, the optimal distribution of XR functionalities across end devices and edge
servers is contingent upon a complex interplay of environmental factors. These include
variations in processing capabilities, connectivity, and associated costs. Furthermore, the
process of offloading XR tasks introduces variable overheads related to data transfer and
compression, which are influenced by network conditions and device capabilities. Consequently, a dynamic approach to workload distribution is preferable to a predetermined
allocation, allowing for adaptive response to environmental factors, offloading overheads,
and server resource availability.
A compression method for emerging sensor data that ignores real-time XR constraints
undermines edge offloading benefits and degrades user experiences. Offloading XR
perception requires sensor data to be transferred to the server for processing, and compression is used for efficient data transfer. Modern XR devices are multi-modal, equipped with
advanced sensors such as LiDAR for 3D perception. While the compression methods for
traditional image and video data are well-studied and optimized [22, 23], the compression
for such emerging sensor data is not.
3
The compression method for XR applications on the edge is critical due to low-latency
and lightweight constraints. Inappropriate compression techniques can introduce high latency or high computational overheads, negating the benefits of offloading and disrupting
the seamless integration of virtual and real environments. To maintain the advantages of
reduced processing latency through offloading, compression methods for emerging sensor
data must be both lightweight and efficient, specifically tailored to meet the requirements
of online XR perception; the perception results are used immediately for rendering or interaction, and the delay in perception causes discrepancies between the real and virtual
worlds [24].
Edge computing infrastructures face significant challenges in accommodating concurrent workloads from multiple users due to inherent resource constraints.
The limited
computational capacities of edge servers are particularly strained when tasked with serving multiple users simultaneously. While prior work has explored perception serving for
multiple users in cloud environments [25, 26, 27, 28, 29], focusing on scheduling users’
perception tasks to meet specified service-level objective (SLO) such as task deadlines
or accuracy requirements, there remains potential for further optimization in the context
of XR applications by leveraging user-specific contextual information. In the domain of
graphics rendering, prior research has predominantly concentrated on bandwidth reduction for multi-user streaming scenarios [30, 31, 32, 33, 34], rather than addressing the
computational scalability challenges associated with real-time graphics rendering. While a
study [35] proposed reducing rendering load by adjusting frame quality from the rendering
pipeline, its benefits are hugely compromised as it does not consider the quality degradation
on the user side due to compression.
This current XR landscape presents unique opportunities for novel approaches to enhance the efficacy of resource allocation and task scheduling in edge computing environments. As state-of-the-art XR devices are multi-modal and equipped with various sensors,
including depth and motion sensors, the user context can be effectively captured and uti-
4
lized to optimize the scheduling of perception tasks on edge servers. Furthermore, the computational costs and qualities of rendering and perception tasks can be dynamically adjusted
on the server. The effectiveness of these server-side tasks can be realized differently depending on user-side contexts. The server, therefore, can judiciously allocate resources to
users based on its capacity and the user-side effectiveness of those tasks.
1.2
Thesis Statement
To deliver high-quality, multi-user mobile XR experiences via edge computing, the XR
serving system at the edge needs to be adaptive and coordinate the distribution and optimization of the XR workloads jointly with the data transfer, using quality metrics that
capture the end-user experience.
1.3
Contributions
In the context of edge-assisted XR, this thesis makes the following contributions.
Enhancing XR experiences via flexible workload distribution across diverse environments. A challenge in edge-assisted XR is to extend its benefits to diverse deployment
environments through workload distribution which is effective in each environment. This
necessitates dynamic decisions on which functionalities to offload and where to run them,
considering runtime environmental factors, offloading overheads, specific workloads, and
resource availability. To address this challenge and extend the edge benefits to diverse deployment environments, this thesis presents FleXR [36], a novel system enabling flexibly
distributed XR. FleXR provides a comprehensive framework that streamlines the development and deployment of XR pipelines, enabling dynamic instantiation of distributed XR
pipelines at runtime. In FleXR, XR functionalities are encapsulated as discrete, modular
compute kernels. Without modification, the pipeline kernels on the FleXR runtime system
can be flexibly configured for different connectivity, protocols to use, and dependency and
synchronization semantics. This flexibility is enabled by the separation of the development
5
and deployment phases, allowing developers to focus on the functional aspects of XR applications while the runtime system manages the distribution and execution. Evaluation
with three XR applications across four distinct distribution scenarios demonstrates that the
most effective distribution is highly dependent on the deployment environment, and FleXR
realizes it across different environments, presenting up to 50% less end-to-end latency and
3.9× higher throughput compared to alternative approaches.
Relieving the compression bottleneck of LiDAR point clouds for offloading online perception. A significant bottleneck in enabling edge-assisted real-time 3D perception arises
from the compression of LiDAR point clouds, which introduces prohibitive latency. To
directly address this challenge, this thesis proposes FLiCR, a fast and lightweight compression method for LiDAR point clouds [37]. FLiCR adopts a range image (RI) as its
core intermediate representation for unstructured LiDAR point clouds, further enhanced by
the application of dictionary coding for compact RI compression. The efficacy of FLiCR
stems from its innovative approach to leveraging lossy RIs and integrating quantization
and subsampling, which collectively deliver bytestream compression efficiency, resulting
in low latency, minimal computational overhead, and high compression ratios. Empirical
validation confirms that FLiCR significantly surpasses conventional LiDAR compression
techniques, such as Google Draco [38], for the context of edge offloading with low-latency
and lightweight requirements. Our evaluation results show that FLiCR hugely reduces compression latency by up to 80% and yields a 12× increase in compression ratio, establishing it as a highly advantageous solution for real-time perception trade-offs on resourceconstrained mobile devices. Then, in this work, we also demonstrate that the downstream
perception performance is differently affected by the data quality of the compressed point
clouds, highlighting the necessity of compression method’s adaptation to latency, computation complexity, and data quality for downstream tasks’ performance. In addition, as FLiCR
is a lossy compression method, an interpolation method is proposed to improve the quality
of the compressed point clouds at the receiver side [39].
6
Improving the scalability of resource-constrained edge in serving multi-user XR workloads. While offloading computationally intensive tasks to edge servers is crucial for enhancing XR experiences on mobile devices, these edge infrastructures are provisioned with
a fixed compute capacity and thus face a scalability challenge when serving multiple users
concurrently, leading to resource contention and degraded user experiences. To address this
scalability challenge, this thesis presents two systems that optimize 3D graphics rendering
and perception serving for XR at the edge. Stimpack [40] is an adaptive 3D graphics rendering optimization system that dynamically adjusts the rendering quality of users based on
their network conditions and the server’s resource capacity. By leveraging the insight that
end-to-end visual quality degrades more severely for frames with high rendering quality
under lossy network compression, Stimpack optimizes users’ rendering qualities to make
the limited server resources used more effectively. In our evaluations, Stimpack achieves up
to 24% higher service quality and 2× more users served with the same resource footprint
compared to other baselines.
For improving multi-user XR perception serving on the edge, this thesis presents Morphis [41], a system that adapts perception model selection and inference scheduling. Morphis leverages the characteristics of XR perception – its online nature, temporal coherence
of streaming sensory data, and impact of data quality on model performance – to optimize
the perception serving process. The model selection is based on the online performance of a
model, which is estimated from the model execution latency and performance degradation
with respect to different data qualities. The inference scheduling is guided by the estimated
effectiveness of perception results, which is derived from the user context, e.g., elapsed
time, inter-frame similarity, and object proximity. The effectiveness-aware scheduling enables flexible latency SLOs for multi-user perception, moving beyond the rigid deadlines
of conventional systems. Evaluation results show that Morphis can maintain comparable
user-perceived performance to a firm-deadline baseline, while significantly reducing computations and relaxing latency constraints.

2.1
2.1.1
Extended Reality
Characteristics and Requirements
Characteristics and Tasks.
XR is an interactive and real-time multimedia use case. As
XR applications provide immersive visual and auditory experiences, they require highquality graphics and audio rendering. To provide such experiences, XR applications leverage a user’s real-world context, e.g., the user’s movement, location, and surrounding objects. Thus, XR applications usually involve two main tasks: perception to understand the
user’s real-world context and interactions along with the application logic and graphics
rendering which provides the extended experiences on the perceived user context.
Latency and Performance Requirements. The success of XR applications hinges on
both low-latency and high-performance functionalities. As the virtual environment is based
on the ever-changing real-world environment, XR applications have stringent latency requirements; the latency requirement can differ based on the device form factors such as
head-mounted display (HMD) and mobile hand-held devices and applications, but it is
generally on the order of tens of milliseconds [42]. When the latency is high, the user
9
may experience motion sickness or discomfort due to the discrepancy between the real and
virtual environments.
In addition to latency requirement, the performances of XR functionalities are also
crucial. Perception systems must accurately capture and interpret the relevant real-world
context needed by applications. This ensures that the user’s environment is correctly understood and represented in the virtual world. At the same time, graphics rendering must
be of high quality to create immersive and realistic visual experiences for users. However,
these high-quality perception and rendering processes are computationally intensive. When
combined with the requirement for low latency, they present significant challenges for enabling high quality of XR user experiences on mobile devices, which typically have limited
processing power compared to stationary workstations or gaming consoles.
2.1.2
The State of the Art of XR
Devices. The state of the art in XR devices includes two main categories of form factors:
the hand-held and HMD devices. The XR experiences on hand-held devices are usually
provided by the prevalent mobile devices of smartphones and tablets as they are equipped
with diverse sensors such as global positioning system (GPS), cameras, and inertial measurement unit (IMU). It has a benefit of the wide accessibility to such devices, but the user
experiences are limited because they are not designed for XR applications and the form
factors are not optimized for XR experiences. On the other hand, HMD devices such as VR
headsets [43, 44] and AR glasses [45, 46, 47] are designed with multi-modal sensors and
operating system (OS) optimized for XR applications and have advantages in providing
more immersive experiences.
XR devices can be tethered or standalone, and some devices can be both as they can
be connected to a workstation or operate independently. When the devices are tethered,
they have advantages of more computing resources and power supply, enabling the usage
of higher quality rendering and perception functionalities. However, the tethered devices
10
have limitations in mobility and accessibility. The standalone devices are mobile and more
easily used in various environments, but they have limitations in computing resources and
battery life, which restrict the quality of the XR experiences; the state-of-the-art mobile
XR devices such as Oculus Quest [43] and Apple Vision Pro [44] have a battery life of 2-3
hours for moderate usage.
System and Processing Pipeline. The XR system serves as middleware between XR
applications and device OS, as illustrated in Figure 2.1a. This system provides essential
perception and rendering functionalities required by XR applications. These functionalities
might be integrated as a part of the device OS or offered as extensions by device vendors.
There is also a standardization effort to the functionalities and interfaces of the XR systems,
such as OpenXR [48], to address the fragmentation of the XR ecosystem and enable the
portability of XR applications. Regardless of whether the XR capabilities are provided by
the device OS or a separate middleware system, the fundamental processing pipeline for
XR remains similar across various devices and use cases.
Figure 2.1b shows the simplified processing steps of XR, which include the data capturing, perception, and rendering tasks. The perception algorithms run on the captured sensor
data, and they can be executed either on central processing unit (CPU) or graphics processing unit (GPU) depending on the tasks and the device capabilities. The simulation step
utilizes the perception results and is where the application logic is executed. When the
simulation is done, the rendering task is submitted to GPU, and the rendered frames are
displayed to the user. The end-to-end (or motion-to-photon) latency in XR usually refers to
the time from the sensor data capture to the display of the rendered frames, including the
processing times of perception and rendering functionalities.

Edge Computing
Edge computing is a distributed computing paradigm that brings the data processing and
storage closer to the data source, at the network’s edge, rather than relying solely on centralized cloud data centers. Compared to the cloud, edge computing offers benefits due to its
proximity. By processing data closer to the source, it reduces the physical distance and the
number of network hops that data need to travel, lowering network latency and bandwidth
usage. Moreover, processing sensitive data locally can enhance data privacy and security,
and edge devices can use local context information to make more informed decisions.
With its benefits, edge computing is increasingly being adopted in various domains
such as smart cities [49], Internet of Things (IoT) [50], and healthcare [51]. Along with the
studies on the application of edge computing to such use cases, there are research works

proximity benefits of edge computing in different workloads and scenarios. For machine
learning, federated learning and distributed collaborative training are studied to utilize the
data locality, enabling model training across decentralized edge devices and adapting to the
features of the local data [52, 53, 54]. The secure and privacy-preserving data processing
by using the edge is also studied, incorporating techniques such as differential privacy,
homomorphic encryption, and secure multi-party computation to protect user data while

2.2.2
Motivation and Challenges
In the context of XR, which demands substantial computing power for high-quality rendering and perception tasks on mobile devices, edge computing emerges as a promising
solution [59, 60, 61, 62, 12, 13, 63]. With edge assistance, the resource constraints of mobile devices can be alleviated, which might enable more lightweight XR devices with long
battery life while still providing adequate user experiences, or to allow the usage of more
advanced perception and photorealistic graphics rendering to enhance the XR experiences.
Resource-constrained Edge. However, enabling edge computing for XR workloads is
with its challenges. One of the primary challenges is the limited resources available on
edge servers. Unlike centralized cloud data centers, edge servers have constrained computing and storage capabilities. Moreover, these servers often need to serve workloads of
multiple users simultaneously, further straining their limited resources. Serving multiple
users’ XR workloads and maintaining the quality of service with limited resources on the
edge presents a significant challenge, necessitating the development of efficient serving
systems and workload optimization techniques.
13
Overheads of Edge Assistance. Additionally, offloading XR functionalities to the edge
presents the overheads of data transfer and compression as illustrated in Figure 2.2. Such
encoding and decoding steps, and the transmission over the network can increase the
motion-to-photon (end-to-end) latency of XR applications, lowering application responsiveness and quality of experience (QoE).
More specifically, in the case of remote graphics rendering on the edge, the frames
are rendered and encoded on GPU, and they are fetched to the main memory for the data
transfer via network interfaces. On the client side, the received packets should be moved
from the main memory to the GPU for decoding and display. Such overheads within the
device system on both the client and server sides also contribute to the latency.
These additional processing and transmission steps are not present in the local processing scenario, i.e., running the XR functionalities on the device. For effective edge assistance
via XR functionalities offloading, it is crucial to minimize these overheads and maintain
the QoS of the XR applications, and this requires efficient compression methods, workload
distribution and optimization strategies with consideration of the presented overheads and
QoS requirements.
Offloading Strategies and Scalability.
An important question in this context is which
XR tasks should be offloaded to the edge server. Since effectiveness of offloading depends
on multiple factors including user and server device capabilities, user network status, loads
on the server, data compression and transfer overheads. Thus, when making offloading decisions, it is essential to consider the distribution context and adapt the offloading strategies
accordingly to the dynamically changing factors.
While edge servers have proximity benefits, the limited resources pose scalability challenges for serving multiple users. The XR serving systems on the edge should be efficient in resource utilization to accommodate more users. This requires adaptive scheduling
and workload optimization techniques that dynamically allocate resources and manage the
loads on the server while maintaining the QoS of the XR applications.

2.2.3
End-to-end Metrics for XR
As discussed in Section 2.1, the success of XR applications hinges critically on providing a
seamless and immersive user experience, which is sensitive to the latency and performance
of XR functionalities. While traditional system-level metrics, e.g., packet latency and instruction throughput, are important for evaluating general computing systems, they often
fall short in fully capturing the complex interplay between system performance and QoE
for XR users.
XR systems inherently possess potential for intrinsic adaptation capabilities within their
application stack to react to dynamic environmental and network conditions and directly
impact the perceived user experience. For instance, rendering engines provide knobs to
adjust rendering quality and frame rates for resource constraints, and data compression and
transmission mechanisms balance content quality and size for user network conditions.
To achieve efficiency beyond methods solely based on application-agnostic, systemlevel metrics, it is crucial to effectively leverage these intrinsic adaptation capabilities with
application-level metrics that reflect the user experience. Relying solely on low-level metrics does not guarantee an enhanced perceived user experience, and application-level adaptation mechanisms based on user-centric metrics should be integrated for the system optimizations. This requires a holistic consideration of end-to-end and system-level metrics
(e.g., motion-to-photon latency, throughput, perceived visual quality, and interaction responsiveness) that are not only relevant to the system performance but also directly reflect
the user QoE. XR serving systems on the edge, that operate based on these metrics, ensure
that the benefits of edge assistance directly translate into an improved QoE for users, even
amidst the resource constraints and overheads associated with edge offloading.

CHAPTER 3
FLEXIBLE DISTRIBUTION OF XR WORKLOADS
This chapter explores the state-of-the-art distributed XR systems and their limitations in
realizing effective edge assistance due to diverse deployment contexts. To enhance the user
experiences in such contexts, this thesis contributes FleXR, a system1 that enables flexible distribution of XR workloads. The presented system is designed and built to bring the
runtime flexibility in workload distribution without requiring the modification of XR functionalities, which is essential for realizing the benefits of edge assistance across various
deployment contexts. We describe its design and implementation, and provide experimental results demonstrating the effectiveness of the system in improving the end-to-end performance of XR applications across different deployment environments through flexible
workload distribution.
3.1
Overview
Given the low-latency and high-throughput requirements of XR applications [64, 65], their
associated high computing costs limit the experiences that can be supported on resourceconstrained mobile devices. To address this, there have been a number of efforts for distributed XR systems [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 66]. A common thread
across them is to predetermine the functionalities that are offloaded to the server, based
on assumptions about environmental factors: the client device capacities, network conditions, and workloads. They provide support for offloading fixed functional components of
perceptions or rendering, and their benefits can be realized only in specific deployment
contexts.
However, we argue that the distribution contexts will differ vastly in terms of the capa1

bilities of the user devices and offload servers, the connectivity among them, and the XR
workloads. In such scenarios, current solutions will be limited in their ability to provide effective server assistance in various contexts. Applications would need to rely on combinations of existing techniques to leverage the server in assisting with different functionalities.
It will require significant additional development and configuration efforts.
Currently, flexibility in XR workload distribution is missing due to a lack of adequate
systems support. There are previous offloading systems for flexible function migration [67,
68, 69], but it is hard to extend their benefits to XR due to their design limitation of functionlevel offloading. Existing stream processing (SP) libraries can potentially enable flexible
workload distribution by creating a pipeline at runtime. While they are used in use cases
similar to XR, e.g., multimedia streaming [70] and perception pipelines [71, 72], the current SP frameworks lack some of necessary features to adapt distributed stream processing
(DSP) for use in XR. A DSP system for XR should support efficient local communication for collocated pipeline components and blocking and non-blocking communication
semantics to express the pipeline dependencies and synchronization. Moreover, it should
provide queue size management and multiple network protocol supports for data freshness
requirements of distributed XR pipelines, which are not available in existing solutions.
Simply adding the missing features to existing SP libraries is not sufficient to enable
the flexible distribution of XR pipelines. Even if the SP libraries are extended with those
DSP features, there are still issues about how to provide the features properly across the
development and deployment phases. Specifically, in existing SP libraries, a pipeline can
be created at runtime, and it requires a user to connect pipeline components (compute kernels) via the developer-specified communication ports. However, since the communication
attributes among compute kernels are determined under the user’s pipeline context, the
user (not the developer of the kernel) should configure the communication attributes of the
connection ports when creating a distributed pipeline.
In response, we present FleXR – an open-source, flexibly configurable, and high-
18
performance system for distributed XR. To bring flexibility, we design FleXR as a DSP
system specialized for XR. With FleXR, XR pipelines can be flexibly created for various distribution scenarios at runtime by a user, without requiring any code modification
in pipeline kernels. We identify the key issues in using DSP systems for XR and describe
our design decisions which provide the necessary DSP features and address them. FleXR
provides a framework to enable the flexible distribution of XR functionalities, streamlining the development and deployment phases. The developers write their kernels without
considering how and where each kernel runs in user pipelines. The user can configure the
communication attributes, e.g., connectivity (local or remote), network protocols to use, dependencies and synchronization semantics, of the given components without modifying the
kernels. This feature is realized by the FleXR kernel design with its port abstractions and
interfaces. Once the developer writes a FleXR compute kernel, it can be flexibly deployed
and executed in diverse distribution scenarios, per user configuration.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of FleXR through experimental evaluation with three
typical XR use cases and four distribution scenarios. Compared to the existing distributed
XR systems [14, 16, 8, 13, 11, 17, 12], FleXR is shown to support all distribution scenarios
by creating distributed pipelines with given kernels at runtime. Our evaluation results show
that the offloading effect of each scenario is different based on the workloads, offloading
overheads, and device capacity, which support the importance of flexibility in XR workload
distributions. Overall, this chapter makes the following contributions:
• We describe the limitations of existing distributed XR systems with respect to the need
for flexibility, and identify the required features for applying DSP to XR.
• We present FleXR, a DSP system specialized for distributed XR, which addresses the
design issues of DSP for XR and enables flexible distributions of XR pipelines. Our
evaluation in different distribution scenarios demonstrates that FleXR practically delivers
on the promise of flexibility and performance.
• We fully open-source FleXR, hoping that it would reduce the barriers for further research

3.3
Design Challenges in DSP for XR
There are intuitive advantages of building an XR system as an SP system. An XR system processes inputs as data streams from device sensors such as cameras, IMU, etc., and
provides output as data streams such as field of view content in VR and graphic overlay in AR. The SP design provides benefits of high throughput with pipeline parallelism
and distributed computation with its modularity, but the application of SP in XR presents
non-trivial issues. In this section, we present the design space exploration we conducted to
address those issues when building FleXR.
3.3.1
Issues with Stream Processing for XR
We use an example AR pipeline in Figure 3.2, to articulate the issues when applying SP to
an XR system. The application renderer overlays virtual objects on the camera frame based
on the result from the object detector. The objects can be manipulated using key inputs, and
the AR scene with the overlaid objects is displayed to the end user. Using this pipeline, we
discuss the main issues below.
I1: Communication Cost. As shown in Figure 3.2, there are a number of components in
the pipeline across which data transmissions must occur. Those data transfers need to be
performed with least latency because high latency lowers the application’s responsiveness
and causes discrepancies between the real and virtual worlds. However, the SP design increases the end-to-end latency as it requires data movement across the ports of the pipelined
kernels [77, 78]. Since XR functionalities process and produce large multimedia data, the
overhead of the cross-kernel communications is significant and must be addressed in order
to meet the latency constraints of XR.
I2: Communication Semantics. In Figure 3.2, the downstream kernels, i.e., the renderer,
has input dependencies with the upstream kernels. Some of those dependencies are hard,
i.e., an input must be received for the downstream kernel to execute, as is the case with the

Design Decisions for The Issues
FleXR, as a DSP system specialized for XR, incorporates solutions to address the issues
raised in the previous section.
D1: Efficient Local Communication. The remote communication cost is unavoidable
even with data compression, but the local communication should be efficient for low overheads. We evaluated the suitability of several existing SP libraries in terms of their communication costs: RaftLib [71], GStreamer [70], Python pipeline libraries [79, 80], and robot
operating system (ROS) [72].
We measure the communication costs with two locally connected kernels and raw RGB
frames of different resolutions. Table 3.2 shows the transmission latencies of the frames.
ROS and Python libraries provide process-level SP, where each kernel runs as a separate
process and the processes communicate via inter process communication (IPC) channels.
Based on our results, the local communication in process-level SP is hardly efficient for
large multimedia data even with shared memory channels [82, 81]. While the shared memory channel reduces the number of data copies, the data still needs to be copied between
the shared memory and process memory regions.
GStreamer and RaftLib provide thread-level SP with zero-copy communication ports.
As kernel functions are threads in the same address space, local communication can be
done without copy. A DSP system for XR should leverage a thread-level SP for efficient
local communication of collocated kernels, and extend its communication with support for
24
remote communication.
D2: Blocking and Non-blocking Semantics. To handle the hard and soft dependencies
and synchronize the kernel executions in a pipeline, providing the proper communication
semantics (blocking and non-blocking) for the local and remote communication primitives
(send and receive) is essential [83]. The design of FleXR handles this as a first-order concern when executing XR pipelines.
The send semantics of an output port is for synchronizing the kernel execution. A blocking send blocks the execution of an upstream kernel function when the downstream kernel’s
queue is full, and this backpressure leads to flow control and implicitly synchronizes the
upstream to downstream kernels. For non-blocking semantics, the upstream kernel continues when the downstream kernel cannot receive data on its input port. The output port
requires both blocking and non-blocking send semantics in XR pipelines. In the example
AR pipeline of Figure 3.2, if only blocking semantics are supported, the camera kernel is
synchronized to the longest path of object detection (blue line). Even if the app renderer
does not require the results from the object detector for every camera frame, the frame
stream (green line) is blocked by the object detector.
The receive semantics of an input port is for kernel dependencies. A blocking receive
waits for the message from a port, and a non-blocking continues when there is no message. So, when the kernel is written, the primary inputs on which the kernel depends (e.g.,
camera frame for a kernel performing frame processing) should be specified with blocking
semantics. For inputs generated by other sources (e.g., other sensors or user events), which
can impact or steer the kernel processing but are only optionally used, the semantics should
be with non-blocking to handle them.
When only a blocking receive is available, all input streams of a kernel are forced
as mandatory. The kernel execution and pipeline throughput are restricted by the lowest
frequency input. In Figure 3.2, the renderer is blocked until the key input arrives from the
user (red line). Even without the key input, the renderer execution is governed by the object
25
detector, and the pipeline throughput is limited by the path with the highest latency (blue
line).
Supporting both blocking and non-blocking primitives for the input and output ports
makes it possible to correctly describe stream dependencies and synchronize kernel executions in XR pipelines.
D3: Queuing Management and Network Protocols. Since poor data freshness causes
discrepancies between the real and virtual worlds, it is crucial to manage data recency in
XR pipelines. This can be achieved by minimizing the queuing delays of a pipeline [84].
For local communication, it is possible to bound the queuing delay by limiting the
number of outstanding data entries in the port buffer. For remote communication, recency
management becomes challenging because there is no way to control the queuing mechanisms of unknown middleboxes across the backend network. In this situation, recency
management can be enabled by compromising communication reliability. Reliable network
protocols such as TCP [85] guarantee in-order message delivery via retransmission and acknowledgment mechanisms. However, in cases where recent data is prioritized (e.g., the
object detection result on a live camera frame), the reliable protocols are inappropriate, and
should be replaced with protocols favoring data timeliness over reliability even with data
loss, e.g., RTP [86] and RTSP [87] over UDP.
Thus, the DSP system for XR should provide knobs for data recency management via
queue size management and support for multiple network protocols for local and remote
kernel communications.
3.4
System Design
3.4.1
Overview
To bring flexibility to XR workload distribution, we built FleXR as a DSP system specialized for XR, taking the design benefits of modularity and task parallelism. Driven by
our design decisions, FleXR is built on top of a thread-level SP library, RaftLib [71], and
26
provides the benefit of efficient local communication for the collocated kernels (D1). For
the communication semantics to enable kernel synchronization and dependencies of XR
pipelines, we extend the semantics of the RaftLib port with support for non-blocking and
for remote communication (D2). For recency management of local and remote communication, FleXR allows setting the maximum number of messages in the local port buffer and
specifying network protocols for remote ports at runtime (D3).
Even with the necessary DSP features for XR, there are still issues about providing
these features properly to the system stakeholders: developers writing kernels and users
requesting distributed pipelines with given kernels. FleXR enables flexibility in configuring XR pipelines via its kernel abstraction with interfaces separating development- and
deployment-time concerns. While the developer implements an XR kernel function and
knows its input dependencies, the user creates a distributed XR pipeline and configures the
connectivity of kernels (local or remote), output-port semantics, and recency management
mechanism of the pipeline context. In addition, there can be a case where the user needs to
connect an output of a kernel into multiple downstream kernels.
Our kernel abstraction provides the interfaces allowing the developer to register input
and output ports and use the registered ports in the kernel function regardless of how they
will be configured by a user. The behavior of the registered ports becomes different based
on the user-specified communication attributes at runtime. The user can also branch dynamically an output port with different communication attributes and flexibly create distributed
pipelines with various topologies without modifying the kernels.

3.4.2 Kernel Design
In SP, the compute kernel is a pipeline component, which includes an execution function
and communication ports. To provide the necessary DSP features for flexible configuration of the communication attributes, we design a FleXR kernel with two abstractions: the
FleXR port and port manager. They abstract the different communication channels for local
and remote operation of a FleXR kernel, allowing the developer to write kernels without
specifying the communication attributes and the user to configure the connection at runtime without modifying kernels. A developer registers the input and output ports of the
kernel with tags via the port manager interface, and the registered ports are instantiated and
configured by the port manager based on the user recipe.
Figure 3.4 shows our kernel design. Each kernel has its ID, logger, frequency manager,
execution function, and port manager. The ID is used for the recipe parser and pipeline
manager in Figure 3.3. The frequency manager adjusts the execution frequency when a
kernel should run at a stable frequency, and the logger is for the developer to log the kernel
events. The execution function processes data from the input ports and sends out the result
to the output port.
Port Manager. When kernels are instantiated, the port manager of each kernel activates
and dynamically branches the FleXR ports based on the pipeline metadata. In addition, it
provides developers with interfaces to use the FleXR ports without considering how the
29
ports will be configured by a user.
The port manager design is shown in Figure 3.4. The manager has input and output
port maps. These port maps have the mapping information of the port tags registered by
a developer and the FleXR ports activated with the communication attributes by a user. A
kernel function can get inputs and send outputs via the port manager interfaces with the tag.
The branched port map contains the ports branched from the registered output port. When a
registered output port needs to be connected into multiple downstream ports with different
communication attributes, the port manager activates the branched ports and keeps their
mapping information to the registered port. When a kernel function sends an output to the
registered port, it is also sent through the branched ports by using this mapping information.

game engines, which interface with the FleXR runtime via plugins. The FleXR plugins are
compatible with the shared memory port in Figure 3.4 and make it possible to run external applications with FleXR pipelines. The graphics rendering in our examples uses the
Mesa implementation [96] of OpenGL [97], EGL [98], and Vulkan [99]. For hardwareaccelerated encoding and decoding of H.264 [22], we use FFmpeg [100], NVIDIA Video

3.5.4
Design Benefit of FleXR
As shown in Table 3.2, GStreamer and RaftLib provide efficient local communication. Instead of using FleXR, a distributed pipeline can be supported by implementing auxiliary
kernels for remote communications, branching, and synchronization. However, supporting
a distributed pipeline with the auxiliary kernels introduces inefficiencies because each kernel, including the auxiliary ones, is a separate execution unit that is parallelly scheduled
and managed.
Figure 3.8 shows the overheads of the auxiliary kernels on our testbed. We create a
kernel sending output to multiple remote kernels, and measure the energy consumption for
the transmissions. GStreamer and RaftLib need the additional kernels for remote messaging and output branching; for sending output to 8 remote kernels, 9 auxiliary kernels are
required (1 for branching and 8 for remote messaging). The energy consumption with the
38
auxiliary kernels increases with their number. In contrast, with the FleXR kernel design
and interface, the output port registered by a developer can be branched and configured for
remote connections with different protocols as specified by a user, not requiring additional
kernels. In FleXR, the developers also don’t need to implement these auxiliary kernels to
make their kernels operate flexibly.
Table 3.5 shows the number of kernels for GStreamer, RaftLib, and FleXR to support
AR1 in the different scenarios. GStreamer and RaftLib need auxiliary kernels as local SP
libraries. GStreamer, as a multimedia framework, imposes strict synchronization across
streams based on their internal timestamps and requires all kernels to have a single synchronized stream for input and output [106]. Since RaftLib does not require such strict
synchronization, it requires fewer kernels. GStreamer requires two kernels for branching
the camera stream and synchronizing streams for the renderer, while RaftLib only needs
a branching kernel. For the distributed settings, both need additional messaging kernels: 4
for Perceptions, 8 for Rendering+App, and 6 for Full Offloading. In FleXR,
these auxiliary kernels are not required because each port can be configured for different
usage, which enables the various scenarios flexibly without the system overheads from the
auxiliary kernels.

Costs for XR Pipeline Distribution. Distributing XR pipelines incur additional costs: multimedia data compression [37], displaying the rendered scene from a server, and network

high bandwidth usage with backend network delays and consumes the battery of the user

server. Another cost is for the client to display the rendered scene from the server. When
the scene is rendered on the server, it should be fetched from GPU memory, sent to the
client, and displayed.

We describe the limitations of the existing distributed XR systems. We argue the need for
flexibility in XR workload distribution, and that the lack of flexibility in distributed XR is

of edge-assisted XR are gleaned through comprehensive evaluation of FleXR with various

CHAPTER 4
CONSTRAINT-AWARE COMPRESSION FOR XR OFFLOADING
In the previous chapter, we discussed how optimizing workload distribution improves edgeassisted XR performance, noting that data transfer overheads often undermine offloading
benefits [36]. While efficient transfer of image and video data can leverage standard codecs
and hardware accelerators which are widely available even on mobile devices [110, 111,
101], emerging sensor data like LiDAR point clouds lack such infrastructure, posing a challenge in real-time edge-assisted perception. To address this, this chapter introduces FLiCR1 ,
a fast and lightweight LiDAR point cloud compression method designed for real-time online perceptions. FLiCR utilizes RI as an intermediate representation (IR) and dictionary
coding, achieving efficiency through lossy RIs, quantization, and subsampling. We also
propose a new quality metric that reflects both point-wise and entropy-wise qualities for
lossy RIs, addressing limitations of current metrics. Our evaluation demonstrates FLiCR’s
suitability for edge-assisted online perceptions compared to existing methods. Additionally,
we show that downstream perception performance can be differently affected by the data
quality loss introduced by compression, highlighting the need for adaptive compression
strategies in XR applications.
4.1
Overview
LiDAR sensors have been used in robotics and autonomous vehicles for robust and accurate 3D sensing. The 3D point clouds from LiDAR sensors are used in perception tasks for
understanding real-world contexts, such as 3D object detection and tracking, and LiDAR
SLAM. A LiDAR sensor has advantages over image sensors as it can provide environmental information in 3D with higher accuracy and is more robust to challenging weather and
1
https://github.com/jheo4/FLiCR
45
light conditions. Previously, despite the benefits of LiDAR sensors, only a few device platforms were equipped with them because the early models of LiDAR were too expensive
and large in size [112]. However, as LiDAR technology advances, the sensors are becoming
smaller and affordable, while maintaining their sensing performance even at lower power
usage. Recently, Velodyne Lidar released a palm-size LiDAR sensor, Velabit [113], for
around $100, Intel RealSense camera [114] has a tiny LiDAR sensor, as do the latest Apple
iPhone and iPad [115] devices. As LiDAR becomes cost-effective and smaller in size, there
would be more opportunities for mobile devices to leverage perceptions of this new sense
in diverse use cases.
Although LiDAR technology is becoming available on mobile devices, there remain
challenges in leveraging LiDAR perceptions for real-time use cases. As discussed in prior
LiDAR perception research [116, 117, 118, 119, 120], the computational complexities of
LiDAR perceptions are very high because these algorithms process unstructured 3D data.
Even with these highly optimized algorithms, high-end processors and GPUs are required
to make them run in real-time. This poses limitations to enabling real-time LiDAR perception on resource-constrained (including by battery lifetime) mobile devices which lack
such high-end hardware.
Edge computing is a technology that can relieve such issues and enable computationally
intensive perceptions for mobile users with commodity hardware. The edge (or cloudlet)
is located at the edge of the network and close to the end users in the multi-tier cloud.
End users can utilize edge resources effectively for storing and processing data on nearby
edge servers accessible via low-latency and high-bandwidth networks such as 5G [121].
There has been research on offloading image-based real-time perceptions on the edge [15,
13, 122]. When remotely running the perceptions, a sequence of images is transmitted to
the edge server in real-time. In such settings, efficient and low-latency image compression
is essential because of the large size of the raw images. Fortunately, image compression
algorithms have been extensively studied by both industry and academia [123, 124, 23],
46
and the accelerators for such standard codecs are broadly available even on mobile devices,
given the popularity of video streaming [110, 111, 101]. These existing video codecs with
accelerators enable the online perceptions on the remote edge servers.
While offloading image-based perceptions takes advantage of the available codecs and
accelerators for real-time video streaming, there are challenges to enabling edge-assisted
online LiDAR perceptions due to the lack of such infrastructure for LiDAR point cloud
compression. LiDAR sensors generate unstructured 3D point clouds, and their volume
is too large to send them as raw data. As an example, the points per scan of the KITTI
dataset [125] are about 120,000 of 2 MB, and streaming the raw sensor data at 60 frames
per second (FPS) needs a bandwidth of 120 Mbps. In addition to the high bandwidth usage,
transmitting a large amount of data not only causes higher network loads on the backend
middleboxes, resulting in additional transmission delays, but also reduces the lifetime of a
mobile device by consuming its battery [108, 109, 126]. In this context, an effective point
cloud compression is crucial, but it is required to be low-latency and lightweight. Since the
responsiveness of online perceptions is determined by the end-to-end latency, high compression latency can introduce discrepancy between the real environment and the perception results [24]. The compression time should be sufficiently small not to compromise the
benefit of the reduced perception processing time on the server. Moreover, the compression
should be lightweight to run on mobile devices.
There are prior efforts to improve 3D point cloud compression, but their primary focus is on achieving higher compression ratio while preserving the original content qualities [127, 128, 129, 130, 131, 132, 133]. Even with real-time compression methods, the latency ranges of previous methods are too high to enable online perceptions, or they require
high-end processors with GPUs for low latency [134, 135, 136, 137, 138, 139]. In short,
the effectiveness of existing point cloud compression methods is limited when considering
mobile devices, which poses a challenge to enabling edge-assisted LiDAR perceptions.
For enabling edge-assisted LiDAR perceptions to real-time applications, we propose
47
FLiCR, a lightweight and low-latency point cloud compression method based on the RI
representation and a lossless compression algorithm. While previous research on RI compressions utilizes only the quantization of the point bit precision with lossless RI mapping [130, 134, 131, 128, 129, 135], we explore the optimization opportunities of lossy RIs
with subsampling for reducing the data size and improving the compression efficiency. The
idea is that compression algorithms such as dictionary coding, which use shorter references
to repetitive features, would become more effective since they operate with a more limited
data representation space. Subsampling of mapped points leads to point loss, and it is critical to understand how this translates to reduction in end-to-end perception quality. We
demonstrate the limitations of existing quality metrics to represent this total information
loss because their designs are only concerned with point-to-point distances. Then, we propose a unified metric, ePSNR, that captures both point-wise and entropy-wise point cloud
qualities, by extending the current peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) with a probability
function of entropy estimation.
We evaluate FLiCR and ePSNR with the current compression methods and metrics
and different LiDAR perception use cases. In our results, FLiCR achieves up to 5.3× improvement in end-to-end compression latency on mobile devices and 12.6× in compression

methods.

4.2
Emerging 3D Point Clouds and Compression
The multimedia data compression and transmission are the main offloading overheads
in edge-assisted XR. While there are efficient compression methods for 2D images and
videos, the compression for emerging 3D point clouds is still an open research area. Before we propose a fast and lightweight point cloud compression method in Chapter 4, this
section provides the background on 3D point clouds and their compression.
3D Point Clouds.
A 3D point cloud is a set of points in the 3D space. Point clouds
can be categorized into two categories by their characteristics: structured and unstructured.
The unstructured (raw) point cloud is a sequence of the coordinate values of 3D points
(usually x, y, z in a Cartesian coordinate system), optionally with other attributes such as
reflection intensities. The structured point cloud is a point set organized with geometric or
hierarchical structure contexts including meshes, octrees, etc. A LiDAR point cloud is an
unstructured point cloud directly captured from LiDAR sensors.

2. The compression performance effectiveness in reducing the data size is important

3. It should be lightweight to run on mobile devices of limited resources while satisfying

For the compression ratio and latency, Google Draco outperforms the other methods

4.5.1
Issues with Current Image Compressions
By representing LiDAR point clouds as images, it becomes possible to leverage the existing
image-processing infrastructures and techniques. With the popularity of video streaming,
56
modern processor platforms and GPUs are equipped with dedicated hardware modules for
the standard codecs such as H.264 and HEVC [110, 111, 101]. These codecs efficiently
encode and decode continuous images with spatial and temporal optimizations [123, 23],
and the pervasive accelerators enable the codecs in a low-latency and efficient way even
with commodity mobile devices.
In this context, it seems appropriate to rely on the existing codecs with hardware accelerators at first glance. However, we argue the existing codecs specialized for human
vision are hardly applicable to RI compression. The lossy image compression algorithms
for human vision fully utilize the characteristics of the human eyes to remove the data with
minimal impacts to visual quality as much as possible; one example is to convert an image
into the frequency domain via discrete cosine transform (DCT) or fast Fourier transform
(FFT) and the high frequency has more loss than the low-frequency data [123, 23, 158].
While the techniques that leverage the nature of human vision work well for normal images, the point cloud details are lost as a result of the frequency-domain loss in LiDAR
RIs.
Figure 4.5 shows the reconstructed point clouds from the RIs encoded and decoded
via H.264 with different QP. QP regulates how much spatial detail is retained and is set
from 0 for lossless to 51 for the most lossy compression. As QP increases, the spatial detail is aggregated so that the encoded bit rate drops at the expense of data loss, resulting
in lower quality [123]. The reconstructed point clouds become vague and noisy with high
QPs. Table 4.3 shows the averaged compression ratio and quality metrics of the reconstructed point cloud with different QPs. With the visual results of the reconstructed point
clouds, the PSNR and CD results become worse drastically while the compression ratio
increases moderately. Considering that the quantization parameter such as QP or constant
rate factor (CRF) of video streaming is usually set around 20 and 30 as a rule of thumb
(FFmpeg’s H.264 default CRF is 23 [159]), these results show the current human-vision
codecs are unsuitable for the RI compression. For preserving the quality of point clouds,
57
the codec quantization parameter should be set for lossless (QP 0), but it is at the cost of the
lower compression efficiency, as Google Draco achieves ∼33% higher compression ratio
in Table 4.1.

4.5.2
RI Quantization and Subsampling
RI has been used for losslessly mapping LiDAR point clouds to 2D frames, and previous
work only applies quantization of bpp [135, 129, 128, 130, 134, 131]. In these prior works,
the main objective is to maximize the compression efficiency while maintaining the point
cloud quality as high as possible. However, we argue that there are more optimization opportunities with lossy RI to decrease not only the data size but the downstream compression
tasks’ complexities. Specifically, the RI resolution is determined by the sensor’s precisions
as mentioned in section 4.4, and the subsampling of point clouds can be done by adjusting
the precision parameters; the 3D points are coarsely mapped to a 2D frame. Figure 4.6
shows the visualizations of reconstructed point clouds from the subsampled RIs. From the
58
raw point cloud of Figure 4.2a, we reduced the precision parameters to map it to the RIs of
four different lower resolutions. Even with the lowest subsampled RI of 16 KB with 8 bpp,
the shapes of scanned objects are recognizable.
While the subsampled and quantized RI has advantages for data reduction and compression with lower latencies, it would affect the performance of the perception tasks. So,
a quality metric for the point clouds from lossy RIs needs to reflect both the quantization
and subsampling errors. The currently used metrics, PSNR and CD, reflect the quantization
error well, but the sampling error of lossy RIs is not represented effectively as these metrics
are defined with the point-to-point distances between point clouds (see section 4.6 for more
details).
Table 4.4 shows PSNR, CD, and SE of the point clouds from four RI resolutions. In the
results, the changes of PSNR and CD exhibit different trends from SE because SE is about
the number of lost points from the original point cloud (the entropy-wise quality) while
PSNR and CD are with the distances of the closest point pairs between two point clouds
(the point-wise quality).
The current metrics’ issue is they only count the point-to-point distances, and each
point distance is calculated by finding the nearest point in the comparing point cloud. So,
when the point clouds have different numbers of points, they are limited to represent this
difference in the total number of points in the point clouds. To address the limitations of
the existing metrics, we propose a unified metric for both the point-wise quality and the
information amount to measure quantitatively the impacts on the downstream perceptions
from lossy RIs in section 4.6.

Based on the experiments, we demonstrate that FLiCR is suitable
for enabling edge-assisted online perceptions to mobile users. Compared to the existing
LiDAR point cloud compressions, it is fast in terms of the end-to-end compression/decompression latency, and lightweight and efficient in terms of energy usage and compression

the awareness of the impact on the downstream perceptions, and we leave this for future
work. Following this, FLiCR has currently performed an end-to-end evaluation of perception algorithms using sensor data from a single source. However, recently, there are cases
where perception is also being performed collaboratively based on sensor data from multiple sources [176]. In such cases, it’s necessary to study the impact on collaborative perception performance when users transmit data of varying qualities. This remains an important
area for future work.

CHAPTER 5
SCALABLE EDGE ASSISTANCE FOR 3D GRAPHICS RENDERING OF
REAL-TIME AND INTERACTIVE APPLICATIONS
In previous chapters, we discussed improving edge-assisted XR performance through efficient workload distribution and resolving emerging sensory data compression bottlenecks.
However, a fundamental challenge of scalability for multi-user XR workloads on resourceconstrained edges remains, especially for resource-intensive 3D graphics rendering. Edge
servers have fixed compute capacity, presenting a limitation when serving multiple users,
and resulting in users having to wait before they can play when the server is fully occupied.
In this chapter, given the shared requirements for low-latency and interactive experiences
between XR graphics rendering and cloud gaming, we tackle the scalability challenge by
examining it within the context of multi-user cloud gaming workloads. We identify a new
opportunity to improve the rendering resource efficiency of edge servers: the visual quality
loss due to compression is more significant for frames of higher rendering quality, wasting the server’s computing resources. This observation led to Stimpack1 , a new system
that adaptively optimizes frame rendering quality by considering both user-received frame
quality and server-side rendering cost. Stimpack’s optimization uses an efficiency score to
quantify the effectiveness of server resource usage on user-side gaming quality. Its opensource implementation demonstrates easy integration with modern game engines. Our evaluations show Stimpack achieves up to 24% higher service quality and serves 2× more users
with the same resource footprint compared to other baselines.

5.1
Overview
Cloud gaming services enable users to play video games on commodity devices by offloading games to remote servers and streaming the rendered frames. Unlike video streaming
services delivering pre-generated content, game frames are rendered based on a game state
and streamed on the fly, and the state changes in real time through user interactions. This
requires the server to have enough computing power with GPU and a low-latency network
connection for responsive and smooth gameplay experiences with high FPS and visual
quality.
While cloud gaming offers the benefit of playing video games without powerful user
devices, its network and computation requirements present challenges in providing satisfactory gaming experiences with an increasing number of users [177, 34, 30]. The servers
with GPU should be close to the users, at edge sites in the access tier of the network, in order to have a low-latency connection [178, 179, 180]. However, placing game servers close
to users poses a scalability challenge as the edge server has limited computing resources.
Cloud gaming service providers can rely on datacenter clouds for scalability, but this compromises service accessibility, as only users near the datacenters can have a low-latency
connection to the game servers [181, 182, 183].
The existing cloud gaming services [184, 185] deal with the scalability issue by limiting
the user playtime and queuing users when the nearby game servers are fully occupied. This
approach can guarantee the service quality for the served users, but it decreases the service
availability with long wait times during high-demand periods. Although there are previous
research efforts for cloud gaming to serve multiple users [30, 31, 32, 33, 34], their focus
is mainly on the network resources for effective bandwidth usage across users via network
resource allocation and adaptive compression quality control.
We identify an opportunity to increase the number of users that can be served with
each GPU on a server by utilizing the characteristics of cloud gaming, which has not been
76
leveraged by existing cloud gaming services and previous works. In cloud gaming, a game
runs on the server, and the rendered game frames are encoded and streamed with lossy
compression. When a user’s network condition is poor, game frames are more compressed
and transmitted with lower visual quality. Even with the highest rendering quality (RQ)
setting on the server, the user-side visual quality can hugely degrade, wasting the server’s
computing resources for high RQ.

With this observation, the following question arises: If a server can estimate the userside visual quality for given RQ and compression parameter corresponding to a user’s
network condition, could this be systematically utilized to optimize the users’ RQ to serve
more users with limited computing capacity while maintaining the gaming service quality?
To demonstrate its feasibility and benefits, we built Stimpack, an adaptive game-rendering
optimization system for scalable cloud gaming. Stimpack aims to increase the number of
users served with each GPU on a game server while maintaining their FPS threshold and
minimizing user-side visual quality loss. It runs on the server and adaptively optimizes
users’ RQs based on estimated user-side visual quality, considering compression and rendering qualities, current FPS, and FPS threshold.
Stimpack’s RQ optimization is based on three main features. (1) Stimpack estimates
the user-side visual quality with a given RQ and compression parameter on the server side.
To estimate the user-side visual quality, we train a regression model on a quality metric [186] and standard video codec [22], and Stimpack leverages the pretrained model for
its RQ optimization (subsection 5.4.2). (2) When serving multiple users with different network conditions and workloads, the optimization process prioritizes and coordinates the
RQ adaptation among them to maximize the aggregate gaming service quality and efficiency of the resource usage. To enable this, we propose a scoring mechanism that quantifies the efficiency of RQ settings with respect to the rendering cost and estimated visual
quality (subsection 5.4.3). (3) Changing RQ presents overheads to reconfigure and reload
the graphical game contents on the GPU. To reduce the negative impact of oscillatory and
frequent RQ adjustments on user experience, Stimpack has a backoff mechanism that prevents it and allows the user’s RQ to stabilize at a suitable level (subsection 5.4.4).
We demonstrate the effectiveness of Stimpack through experimental evaluations and a
78
user study, for two off-the-shelf games and a Stimpack plugin for UE [94].
Overall, this chapter makes the following contributions:
• We identify a new opportunity to improve the resource efficiency of edge game servers:
the visual quality loss due to compression varies based on the frame rendering quality.
We present a prediction method to estimate the user-side visual quality for given RQ and
compression parameters.
• We build Stimpack that performs multi-user RQ optimization based on a new scoring
mechanism that quantifies the resource efficiency of the RQ settings, and a backoff mechanism to stabilize the users’ RQs at suitable levels.
• We evaluate and show Stimpack’s effectiveness in improving scalability and maintaining
gaming service quality.
• We open-source Stimpack, hoping that would reduce the barriers for further research in
cloud gaming at scale.

5.4
An Adaptive Rendering Optimization System for Scalable Cloud Gaming
Based on our observation, we build Stimpack, a system that optimizes users’ RQs in
an online manner to improve scalability and resource-usage efficiency of a game server.
Stimpack adapts the application-level knob (RQ) to reduce the rendering workloads on the
server GPU as shown in Figure 5.3b, and resolves a key challenge: how to adapt the RQs
of users with different network conditions in a coordinated manner to improve the server
capability in serving more users while maintaining user-side gaming service quality.
We propose a novel approach that adaptively optimizes multi-user RQs using a prediction model for the user-side visual quality based on given RQs and QPs. This RQ opti83
mization relies on a new scoring mechanism that quantifies the efficiency of resource usage
based on the current rendering cost, FPS threshold, and estimated visual quality change
(gain/loss) based on the RQ adjustment. Our scoring mechanism enables multi-user optimization by comparing the users’ scores and prioritizing the RQ adjustments to maximize
the aggregate service quality and efficiency of the resource usage across users. Additionally, Stimpack has a stabilization mechanism based on exponential backoff to stabilize the
users’ RQs at suitable levels and prevent the overheads of unnecessary RQ adjustments
from degrading a user’s gaming experience.
5.4.1
System Overview
Stimpack runs on the server side as shown in Figure 5.5, not requiring any modification on
the client side. There are two main components: the Stimpack plugin and runtime system.
The Stimpack plugin integrates into each game application, monitoring rendering latency and reporting FPS to the Stimpack runtime (every second by default). Despite requiring game modifications, the plugin is easily attachable to existing games using the game engine’s extension interface. The Stimpack runtime determines efficient RQs for users based
on FPS thresholds, QP from the streaming module, and current FPS.

5.4.3
Rendering Quality Optimization
Objectives and Approach.
Stimpack aims to increase the number of users that can be
served by a server while maintaining their gaming service quality via RQ optimization. This
optimization process dynamically adjusts the users’ rendering settings to achieve three key
objectives: (O1) ensure their FPS is above the playable threshold despite resource limita-
87
tions, (O2) minimize the degradation of gaming quality while accommodating more users,
(O3) prioritize efficient RQ assignment based on varying network conditions to maximize
overall service quality and resource efficiency across users. To accomplish these goals,
Stimpack employs a round-based RQ optimization process which incorporates a scoring
mechanism that quantifies resource-usage efficiency by considering both FPS and estimated
user-side visual quality.

both cost and quality terms in a single score, Stimpack can prioritize the RQ adjustments
maximizing the resource efficiency by comparing the users’ scores (O3).

5.5.1
Implementation
Stimpack, currently implemented and tested on Ubuntu 22.04, uses Python for its runtime
and scikit-learn [195] for regression models. While it primarily supports UE games through
its plugin for UE, it can be easily adapted for other game engines like Unity 3D [95] and
Godot [189]. The evaluation games are C++ implementations on UE, and ZeroMQ [88] is
used IPC between the Stimpack runtime and plugin.
5.5.2
Objectives and Experiment Methodology
As the objective of Stimpack is to address the scalability challenge of cloud gaming services
by adapting the rendering workloads with RQ optimization, the evaluation answers the
following questions: (EQ1) As the number of users increases, does Stimpack effectively
adapt their RQs to maintain their FPS for playable gaming experience? (EQ2) Compared
to the case of the existing cloud gaming services where assigning a GPU to each user with
the highest RQ, how much can Stimpack improve the server scalability? (EQ3) Compared
to other baselines which reduce RQ settings, how well does Stimpack maintain gaming
service quality in terms of user-received visual quality and FPS?
To answer these questions, we conduct experiments by setting up three scenarios with
two sample games with different resource demands. We evaluate Stimpack’s impact on
scalability in terms of the number of users that can be served on a server with playable
FPS, compared to the case naively accommodating more users with the highest RQ setting
(EQ1 and EQ2). Then, we assess the gaming service quality of Stimpack against other
baselines with reduced RQ settings, using a score metric based on FPS and user-side visual
quality. We complement the evaluation on gaming service quality with a user study that
compares the serving state of Stimpack and the baselines, reporting Stimpack’s winning
rate (EQ3).

5.5.4
Impact on Gaming Service Quality
For scalability, Stimpack adjusts the users’ RQs, which may compromise the visual quality.
As a user’s RQ is an adjustable knob, there can be other cases that use this knob to reduce
the server load and accommodate more users. We evaluate and compare the gaming service
quality of Stimpack against such baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of Stimpack in
maintaining the gaming service quality (EQ3).
Baselines
Along with the highest-only case, we consider other baselines: the lowest-only case and
dJay [35]. The lowest-only case represents a naive scenario where all users are assigned
the lowest RQ. While the highest-only case maximizes visual quality, the lowest-only case
prioritizes user accommodation, offering contrasting perspectives on resource allocation
strategies.
dJay is a previous work that adjusts users’ RQs to reduce server load. Although it shares
similarities with Stimpack in terms of RQ adjustment, there are significant differences in
approach. Firstly, dJay adapts RQs to maintain FPS but does not consider the user-side visual quality affected by compression settings under varying network conditions. Secondly,
99
while dJay is also round-based, it simultaneously adjusts all users’ RQs every round without a stabilization mechanism.
In contrast, Stimpack selects the most efficient RQ adjustment among users based on
server load and estimated user-side visual quality, considering the QPs of their network conditions. This prioritization is enabled by Scoreu as described in subsubsection 5.4.3. Additionally, Stimpack incorporates a stabilization mechanism to mitigate the negative effects
caused by RQ oscillation overheads (subsection 5.4.4). The extreme cases (highest-only
and lowest-only) and dJay serve as reference points for evaluating Stimpack’s performance
in maintaining service quality.

Chapter Summary
In this chapter, we identify the opportunity to efficiently improve the edge server scalability
for multi-user 3D graphics rendering workloads by adapting the application-level knob
of RQ in a manner that is aware of users’ network conditions, compression parameters
(QPs), and user-received frame quality. We show that the user-side frame quality can be
predicted by a regression model based on RQ and QP. We build Stimpack – an adaptive
rendering optimization system. Stimpack adapts users’ RQs based on their serving states
and estimated quality changes due to RQs and QPs, and uses a new scoring mechanism
to prioritize among multiple users with different serving states. To prevent degradation of
service quality due to oscillatory and frequent RQ adjustments, Stimpack employs an RQ
stabilization mechanism with exponential backoff. The evaluation with two off-the-shelf
games powered by UE shows that Stimpack improves edge server scalability, serving more
users with higher FPS than the threshold, while effectively maintaining user-side visual
quality.

CHAPTER 6
MULTI-USER XR PERCEPTION SERVING ON RESOURCE-CONSTRAINED
EDGE
Along with 3D graphics rendering workloads, perception is another fundamental and computationally intensive functionality critical for immersive XR experiences. Modern perception methods heavily rely on deep neural network (DNN) models, which often demand
significant computational resources. To relieve the burden on mobile user devices and enable enhanced QoE, these perception tasks should be offloaded to edge servers. However,
this necessity introduces a scalability challenge when serving concurrent multi-user XR
perception workloads on resource-constrained edge servers. To address these multifaceted
challenges, we propose Morphis, a novel system designed to adaptively optimize multi-user
perception serving on resource-constrained edge environments. Morphis fundamentally rethinks perception serving by profoundly leveraging the unique characteristics inherent to
XR perception tasks. Unlike general-purpose inference serving systems, Morphis is tailored to exploit the dynamic nature of user context, the varying quality of input data, and
the temporal coherence of real-world environments. This allows Morphis to intelligently
manage computational resources and select efficient DNN models, thereby achieving improved scalability and efficiency in delivering perception services to multiple users.
6.1
Overview
XR inevitably requires the perception of the real-world environment to provide immersive
experiences. Technically, in XR, while 3D graphics rendering is to output the virtual environment to the user, perception is to input the user’s real-world context to applications.
Thus, the perception methods, i.e., object detection and tracking, should be low-latency,
high-accuracy, and frequently executed for QoE.
107
As the modern perception methods are DNN-based and computationally intensive, offloading DNN model inferences to edge servers is a common practice to enable DNN-based
perception on commodity mobile devices. However, unlike cloud servers, edge servers are
provisioned with limited computing resources and often face the scalability challenge due
to the resource contention when serving multiple users’ perception tasks simultaneously,
degrading the QoS and user experiences. In this context, the perception serving system on
the resource-constrained edge should be highly efficient and scalable to handle various user
requests while maintaining the QoS.
As DNN inference becomes a popular workload, researchers have focused on inference serving systems to improve the performance and efficiency of serving DNN models.
General inference serving systems [25, 198, 199, 33, 200, 201, 202, 203, 204, 205] have
shown promising results in large-scale cloud inference serving, achieving this through various methods like adaptive model selection and scheduling, horizontal and vertical resource
scaling, and batching. However, since these systems are designed for general cloud inference workloads, their effectiveness is limited when applied to resource-constrained edge
environments or XR perception tasks that demand ultra-low per-request latency.
Recognizing these limitations, systems have been specifically proposed for XR and
edge environments. Some of these [28, 206, 207, 208] aim to boost inference serving performance and efficiency by coordinating within edge clusters, demonstrating the effectiveness of optimized cross-node coordination for overall system performance. Others [26, 29,
27] focus on enhancing the quality of XR perception and improving end-user experiences
through edge server and device collaboration.
Despite the significant achievements of previous systems, we argue that existing XR
perception serving systems don’t fully leverage the unique characteristics of XR. They primarily focus on inference scheduling through coordinated task offloading. We contend that
the core functionalities of an inference serving system – model selection and execution
scheduling – can be further optimized by considering XR perception’s specific demands,
108
thereby improving the scalability of resource-constrained edge servers for multi-user XR
perception. Furthermore, current XR perception serving systems often explore collaboration between the edge server and user devices, implicitly assuming these devices possess
sufficient computational resources for perception sub-tasks. However, the XR device landscape is evolving, with increasingly resource-constrained devices like smart glasses (e.g.,
Ray-Ban Meta Glasses [46], Snap Spectacles [209]) becoming more prevalent. In these
cases, user devices may lack the resources for collaborative perception serving, necessitating the edge server to handle all perception tasks.
In this chapter, we propose Morphis, a system that adaptively optimizes perception
serving for multi-user XR workloads on edge servers by profoundly leveraging the unique
characteristics of XR perception, such as its real-time, interactive, and dynamic nature.
Morphis core functionalities include model selection and inference scheduling. For model
selection, Morphis dynamically adjusts to model performance changes based on data quality, specifically considering the degradation caused by compression loss influenced by a
user’s varying network condition. This allows Morphis to select effective DNN models in a
model zoo, which are best suited for the current serving users and their network conditions,
and this user-context-aware model selection helps to reduce the complexity of the real-time
scheduling. For inference scheduling, Morphis has a method to estimate the effectiveness
of the last served perception result based on the elapsed time since the last serving and the
user’s FoV change. This estimation quantifies how valuable and useful the prior result remains given environmental changes. As the estimated effectiveness of the last served result
is high enough, Morphis can skip or postpone processing the user request even with their
latency SLO, and this allows Morphis to optimize the inference scheduling and resource
utilization. We also identify further opportunities to enhance this effectiveness estimation
by incorporating multi-modal user context in XR, i.e., user movements and the spatial context of user environment, paving the way for future work to further improve efficiency of
XR perception serving.

Opportunity for Multi-user XR Perception Serving

challenges, yet also offers opportunities for optimization. Morphis aims to capitalize on
these by leveraging specific characteristics of XR perception in its model selection and
inference scheduling phases. This section delves into these characteristics and the corresponding optimization opportunities that motivate our approach.

on the estimated network condition. This lossy compression, however, inevitably degrades

powerful model do not translate into a proportional, or even noticeable, improvement in perception performance. Consequently, since a user’s network condition directly determines
the extent of lossy compression and thus the data quality, it effectively establishes a performance ceiling for perception models. Therefore, to ensure optimal resource utilization and

with low latency to maintain an immersive and responsive XR experience. While this challenge is conceptualized as a latency-performance trade-off [24, 231], we argue that latency
alone is not a complete proxy for perceived performance.

with a variety of sensors beyond just cameras, including depth sensors, LiDAR, and IMU.
This multi-modal sensory data can be leveraged to more accurately predict the effectiveness
of perception inference.
IMU data, for instance, provides crucial insights into a user’s motion state, indicating
whether they are stationary, walking, or actively rotating their head [232, 233, 234]. Un112
derstanding this motion allows for a more informed estimation of how rapidly the user’s
FoV is changing, and consequently, how fresh a perception result needs to be. Similarly,
depth sensors (or LiDAR) enable the system to perceive the spatial arrangement of objects
in the user’s environment, discerning whether there are numerous close-proximity objects
or distant ones. This spatial context is also useful because the perceived change within the
FoV differs based on the user’s movement and the proximity of surrounding objects. For
instance, a small head turn might cause rapid FoV changes with many close objects, but
minimal change with distant ones. By incorporating these rich insights from user motion
and spatial context, Morphis can refine its predictions of perception effectiveness, effectively enabling even more intelligent and resource-efficient XR perception serving.
6.3
An Adaptive Perception Serving System
Based on the identified opportunities, we propose Morphis, a system designed to adaptively
optimize DNN inference serving, i.e., model selection and scheduling, for multi-user XR
perception on resource-constrained edge servers. Morphis addresses the scalability challenge of serving multi-user workloads by dynamically adjusting its strategies based on
real-time insights from data quality (influenced by network conditions), temporal coherence
(considering staleness and inter-frame similarity), and multi-modal user context, which are
unique to XR perception. This holistic approach enables Morphis to maximize QoS and
user experience while efficiently utilizing the limited resources available at the edge.

6.4.1
Evaluation
Methodology
To evaluate Morphis, we employ a simulation-based methodology rather than direct deployment on an edge server and user environment. This approach allows us to isolate and
assess the core functionalities of Morphis without the confounding effects from external
variables, such as network fluctuations and hardware differences, which could otherwise
obscure the effectiveness of our system. In our simulation, the edge server’s timestamp
serves as the logical time base. In simulated scenarios, virtual users appear dynamically,
118
each with a pre-defined dataset that is replayed according to the progression of logical
time elapsing in the simulation. Morphis then processes the data from these users, and
the processing times are recorded alongside the sensor data timestamps. After running the
simulation, we analyze the perception results and their corresponding time logs, combined
with the sensor data timestamps, to precisely quantify how latency impacts performance.
The used metric is streaming average precision (sAP), which is a streaming version of AP
that measures the average precision of object detection results with respect to the elapsed
time from the sensor data timestamp [24]. This simulation-based evaluation framework
allows us to quantitatively measure how our proposed methods of Morphis influence XR
Perception performance, providing clear insights into its efficacy in optimizing multi-user
XR perception serving on the edge.
6.4.2
Implementation
Morphis is currently implemented and tested on Ubuntu 22.04. Its runtime and evaluation
simulation are written in Python with PyTorch [236] and Scikit-learn [195]. For the model
zoo, we use MMDetection [237], an open-source toolbox for vision tasks, which provides
a wide range of pre-trained models. We use MOT challenge dataset [230] for the evaluation, and our custom dataset for validating the object proximity and user movement for the
effectiveness estimation is collected using Intel RealSense L515 camera [114].

We established that QP and ∆T are crucial proxies for streaming perception performance,
and we demonstrated how incorporating ∆F alongside ∆T enhances the accuracy of effectiveness estimation, leading to more efficient multi-user serving. However, while ∆T
provides valuable insights into overall scene changes, it is not a complete proxy for the
actual change in user’s FoV regarding objects of interest.
Specifically, a small ∆F typically suggests minimal changes in the user’s environment,

small portion of the overall frame, and thus, ∆F on the entire frame may not accurately
reflect critical changes pertaining to these specific objects, leading to an overestimation of
effectiveness.

Chapter Summary
In this chapter, we introduced Morphis, a novel system designed to address the scalability
challenges of multi-user XR perception serving on resource-constrained edge servers. We
identified key optimization opportunities unique to XR perception, specifically data quality degradation due to lossy compression, the temporal dynamics of online perception, and
the rich insights available from multi-modal user context. Leveraging these characteristics,
Morphis proposes an adaptive approach to DNN model selection and inference scheduling.
For model selection, Morphis leverages the key characteristics of XR perception: its online
nature and its performance dependency on data quality. It profiles models by simulating
their online perception performance, which accounts for both the model’s execution latency
and performance degradation from network-dependent data compression. Based on this realistic profile, Morphis dynamically selects the most resource-efficient model that delivers
optimal performance under each user’s specific real-time data quality and latency requirements. Furthermore, Morphis employs a sophisticated effectiveness estimation model that
quantifies the real-time utility of perception results by considering user-context factors like
elapsed time, inter-frame similarity, and object proximity derived from depth data. This
estimation enables flexible latency SLOs for multi-user perception scheduling, moving beyond the rigid deadlines of conventional systems. Our evaluation demonstrated that by
adaptively and systematically skipping redundant inferences when the estimated effectiveness of prior results is high, Morphis can maintain user-perceived performance comparable
to a firm-deadline baseline, while simultaneously significantly reducing computations and
relaxing latency constraints. This intelligent resource management ultimately enhances the
edge server’s capacity to efficiently serve a larger number of concurrent XR users, making
immersive and interactive XR experiences more scalable.

7.1
Flexible Offloading for XR
There have been prior works on flexible offloading to a remote server, but they have limited applicability for XR. MAUI [67] proposed fine-grained function offloading with the
common language runtime (CLR) of the .NET framework. By having CLR on the client
and server and requiring developers to specify offloadable functions in application codes,
the functions are executed flexibly between the client and server. CloneCloud [68] and
ThinkAir [69] leveraged OS supports to migrate the execution context of threads running
application functions to the server’s virtual machine (VM); the server VM provides an environment identical to the client, and the thread execution becomes migratable.
Although these techniques offer some flexibility, their benefit for distributed XR is limited. In their design approach, devices interact with offloaded functions via client-server
interfaces, and the application execution flow is preserved. Since XR applications require
the processing of multimedia data across multiple functionalities, this can introduce multiple network round trips and compromise the benefits of reduced processing time due to
offloading. In addition, preserving the execution flow limits the opportunities to achieve
task parallelism. ThinkAir [69] provides parallelism by cloning VMs, but allows only for
data parallelism. Lastly, since these systems run the application codes on a server with the
same environment as the client, they cannot benefit from hardware resources only available
127
on the server.
Offloading XR functionalities requires additional operations such as data compression
and network transmissions. When distributing XR workloads, both overheads from those
auxiliary operations and the costs of the XR functionalities should be considered. Flexibility in reconfiguration thus requires not just techniques which provide transparent function
offload, but also systems support to properly configure and deploy the auxiliary functionality. In this regard, FleXR adopts a DSP design and addresses the issues that arise from the
application of DSP to distributed XR, enabling the flexible offloading of XR functionalities.
7.2
LiDAR Point Cloud Compression
Given the popularity of 3D point clouds, there are many point cloud compression methods.
Firstly, there are MPEG standard specifications: video-based point cloud compression (VPCC) and geometry-based PCC (G-PCC) [133]. V-PCC converts 3D point clouds into 2D
frames and compresses the frames with MPEG video codecs. G-PCC directly leverages the
octree structure as the IR, and compresses the octree of point clouds. Other than G-PCC,
Google Draco [38] and PCL compressors [140] utilize tree structures including k-d tree and
octree. After generating the tree structure from a point cloud, the occupancy information
with the leaf nodes is coded, and entropy or arithmetic coding is applied to compress the
coded information [241, 141].
For range image compression, Tu et al. present direct mapping of sensor data to 2D
frames by each laser ID with precision and compress these raw RIs using image compression methods [128]. Other RI-based compression methods convert the raw sensor data from
Cartesian coordinates into spherical coordinates by using the LiDAR sensor design [130,
134, 131]. Feng et al. propose spatial encoding in the plane granularity and temporal optimization with scene alignment and prediction by using IMU fusion. Even though these
existing compression methods show decent compression performance, it is hard to apply
them to our target use case of online remote perceptions because of their high latency mag128
nitudes.
Recently, there has been research to utilize ML for LiDAR point cloud compression.
One popular approach is with the octree because the high compression ratio can be achieved
by coding the tree into a more compact bytestream with well-predicted occupancy information of a given tree [241]. By fully utilizing the relationship of neighboring nodes in the
octree, the state-of-the-art works train the ML models to predict the distribution of the octree nodes [136, 143, 142, 132]. With the predicted distribution, the occupancy information
and nodes are effectively coded by assigning proper bits to each node of non-empty child
nodes. For RI-based ML approaches, the spatial optimization is done by using the encoder
and decoder networks trained with RIs of point clouds [129, 135]. Some of these ML algorithms achieve sufficiently low latency to run in real-time [136, 132]. However, they are
not practical for mobile users, because they rely on high-end processors and GPUs, which
are usually unavailable for mobile devices. Even if a mobile device has such computing
resources, there is another issue with its limited battery. In this context, FLiCR can be a
practical solution for mobile users offloading the LiDAR-based online perception for XR,
as it is low-latency and lightweight enough to run on a commodity mobile device.
7.3
Scalability of Multi-user 3D Graphics Rendering on Edge Cloud
Given the popularity of cloud gaming, there have been previous works to improve its service quality and efficiency. One popular approach is to provision cloud gaming servers
based on the postulated environment of the user demand and geographical distribution [179,
242, 243, 244, 32, 34, 245]. These works dealt with the server allocation problem, which
is to allocate the cloud gaming servers to minimize the network latency and the cost of the
service provider.
Along with the server-provisioning works, there have been other works that try to improve the servers’ resource efficiency by optimizing the GPU scheduling and resource allocation [246, 247, 248, 249, 250, 251]. In these works, the game server serves users with
129
VMs and its GPUs are virtualized and allocated to the VMs. As mentioned in section 5.3
with Figure 5.3a, when GPU resources are shared among multiple applications with time
and space multiplexing, there are opportunities to improve the efficiency of the GPU resource utilization by scheduling the tasks and assigning the GPU resources. These works
proposed various scheduling algorithms and resource allocation strategies to improve the
GPU resource utilization for multiple users.
From the perspective that Stimpack utilizes the application-level knob to adapt the user
workloads and improve each GPU scalability, it is orthogonal to the previous works focused
on the server allocation and GPU scheduling. Thus, there can be further opportunities of
joint optimizations with the previously proposed techniques to reduce the operational cost
of cloud gaming services while maintaining the service quality and availability.
There were previous works leveraging the rendering quality for scalability [31, 35].
Wang et al. presented a rendering adaptation system reducing the bandwidth usage of users
by reducing the streamed frame resolution, allowing a server to accommodate more users
with limited network bandwidth. Grizan et al. proposed a system, dJay, that adjusts the
rendering quality to reduce the GPU resource usage when serving multiple users [35].
As described in subsection 5.5.4, while dJay shares similarities with Stimpack in using
rendering quality to reduce GPU resource usage and accommodate more users on a server,
Stimpack differs in several key aspects: (1) Stimpack considers the user-side frame quality with compression loss and the RQ oscillation overheads, which are not considered in
dJay; (2) Stimpack has its scoring mechanism to coordinate the rendering quality adaptation across multiple users even when they are in different network conditions and serving
states. (3) Stimpack has the RQ stabilization mechanism to prevent the RQ oscillation. In
our evaluation, compared to dJay, we show the importance of these Stimpack features for
maintaining the service quality.
130
7.4
Multi-user Inference Serving for XR Perception on Edge Cloud
Research into DNN inference serving systems has largely focused on improving performance and efficiency. For general cloud environments, these systems employ techniques
like model selection, dynamic resource scaling, and batching to handle high throughput [25,
199, 200, 204, 205]. There are also works that focus on scheduling and resource allocation
across cloud servers to optimize the serving of DNN inference requests [198, 201, 203].
While effective for large-scale datacenter cloud, these designs often fall short when applied
to the resource-constrained nature of edge cloud and the ultra-low, per-request latency demands of interactive XR experiences.
Recognizing these limitations, a distinct body of work has emerged for edge- and XRspecific inference serving. As the target use cases on edge cloud are often interactive and
real-time, these systems focus on low-latency and high responsiveness. To meet these requirements, some works seek coordinated scheduling in edge clusters [28, 206, 207]. There
are also works that specifically target XR perception tasks, such as real-time object detection and tracking, by optimizing the serving of DNN models [15, 252, 13]. These systems
seek to collaborate between edge servers and user devices, distributing tasks and managing
load to achieve low-latency and high-throughput serving.
However, despite their advancements, these existing systems for XR do not fully leverage the unique characteristics of XR perception as discussed in section 6.2. This includes
neglecting the impact of varying user network conditions on data fidelity (e.g., quality
degradation from lossy compression) and how this influences the model performance. Additionally, existing works treat the latency SLO of users as a static threshold, without exploring how the dynamic effectiveness of a perception result changes based on a user context and affects the user-perceived quality. The serving systems for XR perception often
implicitly assume these devices possess sufficient computational resources for perception
sub-tasks. This assumption is not always valid, especially with the proliferation of resource-
131
constrained and lightweight XR devices like smart glasses, where user devices may lack
the necessary resources for collaborative perception, necessitating the edge server to handle
all perception tasks.
In contrast, Morphis presents a context-aware adaptive perception framework that directly addresses these limitations by leveraging the characteristics of XR perception. For
model selection, Morphis profiles the online performance of perception models based on
their execution latencies and varying data qualities, enabling the selection of the most suitable model for each user. For inference scheduling, Morphis has a novel approach of effectiveness estimation that quantifies the real-time utility of perception results, allowing
for flexible latency SLOs and systematically adapting the serving process. The effectiveness estimation is based on the user context including elapsed time since the last serving
and inter-frame similarity. With the effectiveness-aware scheduling, Morphis reduces resource usage by processing inference requests which would yield effective results for the
user-perceived quality, resulting in a more efficient serving process.

CHAPTER 8
CONCLUSION
This dissertation addresses the multifaceted challenges inherent in delivering high-quality
XR experiences from resource-constrained edge servers to multiple users. We contend that
realizing the full potential of edge-assisted XR necessitates a holistic and adaptive approach that spans efficient workload distribution, data compression for emerging sensor
modalities, and optimization of computationally intensive XR workloads considering the
user-perceived QoS. Throughout this work, we have explored the opportunities for enhancing scalability, responsiveness, and QoE under the context of edge-assisted XR, and we
have proposed novel systems and techniques that address the challenges in edge-assisted
XR and extend its benefits to a broader audience.
8.1
Contribution Summarized
This dissertation presents four key contributions to enhance XR user experiences through
edge offloading.
FleXR is introduced as a system enabling flexible distribution of XR workloads. It provides
runtime flexibility in creating XR pipelines across diverse deployment scenarios without
requiring modifications to underlying XR functionalities. This flexibility enables dynamic
adaptation to varying edge resources, network conditions, and application demands, improving end-to-end pipeline latency and throughput compared to static approaches.
FLiCR addresses the critical bottleneck of LiDAR point cloud compression for edgeassisted online perception. This fast and lightweight compression method leverages RI as
IR with dictionary coding, achieving high efficiency through lossy RIs, quantization, and
subsampling. FLiCR substantially reduces compression latency and improves compression
ratios for real-time LiDAR perception tasks, making it a practical solution for resource133
constrained mobile devices.
Stimpack tackles the scalability challenge of 3D graphics rendering on resource-constrained
edge servers for multiple users. It introduces a new insight: that visual quality loss due to
network compression is more significant for frames of higher rendering quality, leading
to inefficient resource usage. Based on the observation that high quality rendering is not
always effective for all users, Stimpack adaptively optimizes rendering qualities by considering user-side visual quality and server-side rendering cost, presenting an efficiency score
and RQ stabilization mechanism to prevent the overheads from oscillatory RQ adjustments.
This enables higher service quality and accommodates more users compared to the existing
approaches with the same resource footprint.
Morphis introduces a context-aware adaptive perception framework for multi-user XR
serving on resource-constrained edge servers. It optimizes the serving process by leveraging unique XR perception characteristics, including dynamic user context and varying
data quality. Morphis adaptively selects efficient perception models based on their profiled
online performance, which accounts for both execution latency and data quality degradation
due to compression. Furthermore, Morphis employs an effectiveness estimation model that
quantifies the real-time utility of prior perception results using user context (e.g., data quality, elapsed time, inter-frame similarity). This enables flexible latency SLOs and adaptive
inference scheduling, allowing Morphis to process only requests that meaningfully improve
user-perceived quality, thereby enhancing resource efficiency and multi-user scalability.
Collectively, these systems – FleXR, FLiCR, Stimpack, and Morphis – provide a holistic solution for robust edge-assisted XR. By enabling flexible workload distribution, efficient data transfer for emerging sensor modalities, and intelligent, adaptive optimization of
both rendering and perception tasks, they prevent inefficient resource usage, extend device
capabilities, and allow edge servers to serve more users while maintaining user-perceived
quality. This thesis enhances the scalability, responsiveness, and QoE for multiple users’
XR applications on resource-constrained edge infrastructures, thereby pushing the frontier
134
of immersive and interactive mobile XR experiences via effective edge assistance.
8.2
Lessons Learned
This dissertation’s comprehensive research, encompassing the design, implementation, and
evaluation of FleXR, FLiCR, Stimpack, and Morphis, yielded several fundamental lessons
and insights into edge-assisted XR.
8.2.1
The Imperative of Adaptive and Flexible Design in Edge XR Systems
Our work consistently demonstrated the limitations of static approaches in edge-assisted
XR. Given the dynamic nature of XR workloads and edge environments, which are influenced by varying network conditions, device capabilities, server loads, and application
demands, pre-determined offloading strategies and resource allocations often fail to sustain
their effectiveness. FleXR’s development highlighted that providing runtime flexibility in
pipeline reconfiguration and workload distribution is not merely beneficial but essential
for effectively adapting to environmental changes and improving end-to-end performance.
Stimpack and Morphis demonstrated that adaptive workload optimization is important for
relieving the scalability challenge of resource-constrained edge servers. Based on these insights, it is clear that future edge-assisted XR systems need to be designed with adaptability
and flexibility at their core.
8.2.2
Beyond Traditional Metrics: User-Perceived Quality is Key
A critical insight gained is that traditional system-level metrics, such as latency and throughput, can be insufficient to truly capture the QoE for XR users. Our work on Stimpack
vividly demonstrated this: even when 3D graphics were rendered at high quality on the
server, network compression losses often severely degraded the user-perceived visual quality, leading to inefficient resource usage. To address this, we introduced an approach that
directly quantifies resource efficiency based on the predicted user-side visual quality and
135
server-side rendering cost. By leveraging this application-level adaptation, we achieved optimized rendering quality, significantly enhancing both scalability and user experience in
ways traditional metrics alone would not have allowed us to do.
Similarly, with Morphis, we learned that simply reducing latency or adhering to rigid
deadlines for every request doesn’t consistently translate to a better user experience or optimal resource use. Through its effectiveness estimation method and user-context-aware
serving process, Morphis showcased how serving efficiency can be dramatically improved
by adaptively deciding when and how to process perception tasks for multiple users. These
findings underscore the importance of incorporating user-centric metrics that directly reflect perceived experience quality – such as visual fidelity, responsiveness, and overall immersion – into the core of system design and operation. This shift from traditional, lowlevel metrics to application-level adaptation mechanisms, driven by user-perceived quality,
makes it possible to unlock unprecedented efficiency and create a far more effective edgeassisted XR system.
8.2.3
Tailored Data Processing for Emerging Modalities on Constrained Devices
Our research exposed the challenges posed by emerging sensor data, such as LiDAR point
clouds, which lack the hardware acceleration and compression methods for low-latency use
cases. FLiCR’s development illustrated that general-purpose compression techniques often
introduce prohibitive latency or are too computationally intensive for resource-constrained
mobile XR devices. The key lesson here is the imperative for lightweight, low-latency compression methods tailored to the unique characteristics of new sensor modalities and edgeassisted XR. FLiCR demonstrates that purpose-built compression can effectively overcome
real-time constraints on mobile hardware. Additionally, it showed that downstream processing performance can be differently affected by the data quality degradation. As the motivation of data transfer is to offload computationally intensive processing on edge servers, it
is important to consider the performance degradation of the downstream processing tasks
136
when designing compression methods, and to provide a way to adapt the compression methods to the downstream processing tasks.
8.2.4
The Interconnectedness of XR Pipeline Components
Lastly, this thesis underscores that an XR pipeline is a tightly coupled system where components are not isolated but profoundly interdependent. In this case where multiple components interact and influence each other, holistic system-wide adaptability and optimization
are crucial for improving overall system responsiveness and user experiences. Optimizing
individual components in isolation is often insufficient to resolve system-wide bottlenecks
or enhance end-to-end performance. FleXR enables flexible workload distribution across
this pipeline, FLiCR addresses data transfer bottlenecks for specific sensors, Stimpack optimizes rendering resource allocation, and Morphis intelligently manages perception workloads. The collective impact of these systems demonstrates that holistic, cross-layer optimization is essential. True gains in edge-assisted XR scalability and QoE are achieved when
flexible distribution, efficient data processing, and intelligent, context-aware adaptation are
synergistically applied across the entire XR processing pipeline, preventing localized optimizations from simply shifting bottlenecks elsewhere. This highlights that future XR system research should prioritize integrated approaches that consider the complex interplay
between all pipeline stages.
8.3
Future Work
This section outlines promising avenues for future research and potential extensions of the
work presented in this dissertation, building upon the insights gained from them.
8.3.1
Holistic and Adaptive End-to-End XR System Optimization
While this dissertation has advanced flexible workload distribution (FleXR), efficient compression for emerging sensor modalities (FLiCR), and adaptive optimization of 3D graphics
137
rendering (Stimpack) and DNN-based perception serving (Morphis), a key future direction
involves their holistic and integrated end-to-end optimization across the entire XR processing pipeline. The individual contributions herein demonstrate significant performance
gains within their respective domains. However, in a complex, dynamic environment like
edge-assisted XR, optimizing one component in isolation might merely shift bottlenecks to
another part of the system or introduce unintended performance trade-offs.
Future research should therefore focus on the end-to-end optimization across the entire XR pipeline, which can simultaneously consider the interactions and dependencies between all components. This includes exploring sophisticated multi-objective optimization
techniques to model and balance competing goals, such as maximizing QoE, minimizing
latency, maximizing server utilization, and ensuring fairness across multiple users. As presented in this dissertation, the systems and compression techniques have hyperparameters
for optimizing their performance, and in our work, these hyperparameters were manually
tuned. As this manual tuning is not scalable to diverse and dynamic environmental conditions, future research could explore the ML-based optimization of these hyperparameters
with feedback loops that continuously learn from system performance and user interactions.
As ML techniques can learn complex relationships among the components and adapt system operation and parameters to given situation changes, they can enable adaptive strategies
by observing environmental changes, user behaviors, and resource availability.
Another promising direction is about multi-user XR systems, where the system considers the fairness and synchronization of multiple users’ experiences. In our systems, we
leverage the network dynamics as an opportunity to optimize the resource efficiency. In
multi-user XR experiences, however, the network dynamics can also be a challenge as it
can lead to unfairness among users [253, 254, 255, 256]. Thus, future research should explore how to ensure fairness and synchronization among multiple users while maintaining
the system’s overall efficiency.
138
8.3.2
Advancing User-Centric and Perceptual QoE Modeling
A critical insight gained from this work is that traditional, system-level metrics alone are
insufficient to fully capture the QoE for XR users. Systems and techniques should operate
by incorporating metrics that reflect user-perceived quality. In diverse domains, there have
been ongoing efforts to capture the user-perceived QoE for user interaction [257] and other
inference tasks such as serving large language models [258]. While Stimpack and Morphis
propose approaches to leverage the existing metrics for capturing end-to-end QoE, i.e., efficiency score and effectiveness score, there is still a limitation in the current understanding
of how users perceive XR experiences as these approaches are not thoroughly validated
through extensive user studies. In this regard with a lack of comprehensive user-centric
QoE models and metrics for XR user experiences, future research should focus on developing and validating such models, which has the potential to enable interesting system
designs and optimization opportunities.
This involves developing more sophisticated models that go beyond simple technical measurements to incorporate subjective user preferences and long-term experience
changes. Extensive user studies, potentially combined with physiological data (e.g., eyetracking, galvanic skin response [259, 260, 261]), could provide richer datasets for training
personalized QoE models. Research into integrating multi-modality QoE – encompassing
not only visual quality and latency but also haptic feedback, spatial audio quality [262], and
the overarching sense of immersion – will be crucial. Ultimately, the goal is to create adaptive systems that can dynamically tailor XR experiences not just for technical efficiency,
but for effective, personalized user satisfaction over extended periods.
8.3.3
Enhanced Data Handling for Emerging XR Sensor Modalities
While FLiCR has effectively addressed the compression bottleneck for LiDAR point clouds,
the continuous evolution and proliferation of diverse and novel sensors in modern XR devices present ongoing challenges for real-time data processing and transmission at the edge.
139
Beyond LiDAR, XR devices are integrating advanced depth cameras, high-fidelity hand and
eye-tracking sensors, and even physiological sensors, each generating unique data streams
with distinct characteristics and processing requirements.
As existing data compression and transmission techniques leverage the spatial and temporal redundancy inherent in each sensor modality, there can be an opportunity to explore
more advanced approaches that consider the fusion of multiple sensor data streams and
their interdependencies. This includes designing novel, integrated compression and streaming protocols that intelligently combine data from different modalities, considering their
unique characteristics, redundancy, and interdependencies. Furthermore, the development
of lightweight, on-device techniques for ultra-low latency pre-processing or lightweight
feature extraction can be explored. The effective initial handling of data at the device can
significantly reduce the amount of data that needs to be transmitted to the edge server or
provide a more efficient representation for compression and downstream processing.
8.3.4
Real-world Deployment and Open Research Platforms
The insights and systems presented in this dissertation were rigorously evaluated, primarily
through simulation and controlled testbeds. The vital next step is to deliver these research
findings to actual XR users, enabling them to benefit from a better experience. This requires real-world deployment and validation in diverse XR ecosystems, including live edge
environments and enterprise edge clouds, where systems can be tested under real-world
network variability, fluctuating workloads, and diverse user populations.
However, a significant impediment is the current landscape of closed and proprietary
XR platforms. The absence of end-to-end, open research platforms leads to the fragmentation of research results and achievements. Recently, there has been a growing recognition
of the need for open-source and standardized XR research platforms that can facilitate collaborative research and development [263, 6, 264, 48], but these initiatives often become
yet another silo due to a lack of compatibility with existing XR platforms.
140
To overcome these barriers and ensure that research advancements are effectively utilized by real users, it is crucial to facilitate their real-world deployment. This necessitates
the development of open research platforms that are compatible with current XR ecosystems. Such platforms are essential for enabling collaborative research, facilitating rigorous comparative studies across heterogeneous hardware, and accelerating innovation in
adaptive edge-assisted XR systems. Gathering extensive, long-term feedback from real XR
application developers and end-users will further refine system practicalities and uncover
unforeseen challenges and opportunities, thereby driving the next generation of robust and
scalable XR systems.
