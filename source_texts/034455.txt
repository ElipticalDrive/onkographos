Abstract
To achieve high-quality and high-precision surface extraction from industrial
CT data, this paper proposes an adaptive surface sampling algorithm based
on a particle system. The algorithm first constructs a multi-scale voxel pyramid and performs adaptive subdivision in a coarse-to-fine manner, incorporating
curvature information at each scale. The subdivided voxel units serve as the
fundamental units for particle behavior. Unlike traditional surface extraction
methods based on particle systems, this paper decouples the force mechanism
acting on particles in the time domain and adopts an adaptive weighted projection of grayscale and second-order gradient to replace the isosurface gravitational
force, driving particles to sample at precise edges. Within the 26-neighborhood
of the voxel unit containing the target particle, interacting particles are searched
and repulsive forces are calculated to improve computational efficiency. Meanwhile, the tangential component of the repulsive force is extracted to enhance
particle distribution while reducing the impact on projection accuracy. Furthermore, to increase sampling density under minimal system energy variation, this
paper proposes an incremental particle insertion mechanism based on the Voronoi
diagram.
Experimental results demonstrate that, compared to traditional surface extraction algorithms, the proposed method significantly improves the uniformity of
particle distribution and robustness against noise and artifacts while maintaining sampling accuracy. It enables adaptive sampling with fewer sampling
points, showing superior engineering practical value in industrial metrological
CT applications.

1 Introduction
1.1 Background
As product complexity and quality requirements continue to increase in engineering
applications, traditional contact-based inspection methods often struggle to meet the
demands of high-precision measurement when acquiring surface information of complex workpieces. This is due to the interference effects caused by physical contact and
the inherent limitations of the detection range. Moreover, these methods are unable to
capture the internal contour information of the workpiece. In addition, the inspection
process of such methods is relatively cumbersome and time-consuming, which severely
limits the overall efficiency of industrial production processes and makes it difficult to
meet the core requirements of modern manufacturing for efficient quality control.
Against this background, Industrial Computed Tomography (ICT)[1] has emerged as
an advanced non-contact and non-destructive testing technique. Its core advantage
lies in its ability to intuitively and accurately reflect the geometric information of
the inspected workpiece in the form of tomographic slice images, thereby providing
quantitative basis for processes such as product quality assessment, performance verification, and reliability analysis.
In industrial applications, surface extraction is often performed on CT scan data to
obtain a measurable 3D mesh model. Based on this model, internal details of complex
structures can be quantitatively observed, enabling rapid localization of issues such
as dimensional deviations and internal defects[2]. However, due to the influence of
artifact interference and limitations in the measurement accuracy of scanning equipment, the effectiveness of the extraction algorithm can be significantly reduced, and
reconstruction errors may even occur[3]. Therefore, developing high-precision surface
extraction algorithms is of great significance for the application value of industrial CT
in dimensional metrology.
1.2 Related Research
Surface extraction aims to recover a continuous geometric representation from discrete data. Currently, static surface extraction algorithms are widely used and can be
mainly categorized into methods based on spatial geometric decomposition and methods based on surface topology tracking.
The Marching Cubes (MC) algorithm is the most classic method based on spatial
geometric decomposition[4], proposed by Lorensen and Cline in 1987. This algorithm
generates triangular facets within voxel units using lookup tables and linear interpolation. Its surface extraction results are highly robust, but it suffers from issues such as
topological ambiguity, difficulty in preserving sharp features, and poor mesh quality.
In 2002, Ju proposed the Dual Contouring (DC) algorithm[5], which employs adaptive
octree subdivision[6] and quadratic error function minimization to generate optimal
vertices within voxel units for isosurface reconstruction. This algorithm can effectively
preserve sharp features, but it is relatively complex to implement and prone to topological ambiguities, mesh self-intersections, and vertex instability. In the same year,
2
Ohtake proposed a dual dual mesh optimization method[7], which projects mesh centroids onto an implicit surface and minimizes the quadratic distance error from vertices
to tangent planes, achieving high-precision reconstruction of sharp features and significantly improving mesh quality. However, it cannot change the initial topology and
incurs high computational costs. In 2004, Nielson proposed the Dual Marching Cubes
algorithm[8], which connects dual vertices generated inside four voxel units sharing a
common edge into quadrilateral faces to form a dual mesh, thereby reducing the lowquality triangles commonly found in MC isosurfaces.
Algorithms based on surface topology tracking generate triangles by continuously
expanding or advancing a front. A classic example is the Marching Triangles (MT)
algorithm proposed by Hilton and Illingworth in 1997[9]. This algorithm starts from an
initial seed triangle and progressively estimates the positions of new vertices through
boundary expansion, projecting them onto an implicit surface. At the same time, it
uses 3D Delaunay surface constraints to ensure that newly added triangles do not
self-intersect or overlap, ultimately completing the surface mesh extraction. However,
this algorithm can only handle relatively simple topological structures. Furthermore,
to avoid reconstruction errors, the advancing front must be capable of detecting and
avoiding self-intersections. Although some heuristic methods are built in, such behavior often leads to a degradation in the quality of triangles in the corresponding regions,
violating the original intent of the algorithm. Despite subsequent improvements made
by many researchers[10–13], the aforementioned issues remain unresolved.
In recent years, dynamic surface extraction algorithms have gradually gained
attention[14]. Unlike static methods, this class of approaches treats the surface as a
dynamic system capable of autonomously deforming, diffusing, and converging to the
target morphology under the drive of an energy field. Among these, surface reconstruction methods based on particle systems use a large collection of discrete particles
to represent the surface. Driven jointly by data constraint forces and inter-particle
interaction forces, the particles continuously move and eventually become uniformly
distributed on the isosurface, reaching mechanical equilibrium. This method was originally proposed by Witkin and Heckbert[15]. Due to the large number of interactions
among particles, the computational efficiency of the algorithm is low, and the final
sampling result is merely a surface point cloud lacking topological information. In 1997,
Heckbert introduced binning techniques[16], which limit neighbor searches in energy
and velocity calculations to small regions through spatial partitioning, thereby improving computational efficiency. In 2007, Miriah Meyer et al. combined dynamic particle
systems with Delaunay surface reconstruction theory[17], ensuring that particle distribution meets the geometric conditions for Delaunay sampling, thus guaranteeing
topological correctness of the generated mesh. In 2012, Mark Kim proposed using
GPUs to accelerate the iterative process of particle systems[18], significantly improving
the sampling efficiency of particle systems. Although surface reconstruction methods
based on particle systems have made progress in the aforementioned studies, they still
face challenges in metrological CT applications, such as difficulty in ensuring surface
accuracy, susceptibility to noise and artifacts, and insufficient adaptive sampling capability, making it difficult to achieve an effective balance between accuracy and storage
space. In summary, although static surface extraction methods are widely used, they
3
still have shortcomings in the field of industrial CT metrology. First, static methods
cannot ensure sampling quality while performing surface sampling, and are prone to
introducing issues such as long, thin triangles and even topological errors. The mesh
vertices in the surface extraction results are sampled non-uniformly and exhibit poor
distribution quality, causing many inconveniences for subsequent processing operations. Second, the extraction effectiveness of traditional algorithms is often highly
dependent on the choice of threshold values and is easily disturbed by inherent artifacts, severely affecting reconstruction accuracy[19, 20]. Moreover, these algorithms
lack sufficient adaptive sampling capability, maintaining high sampling rates even
in flat regions, which leads to a significant increase in data volume and severely
impacts the processing efficiency of subsequent workflows. In addition, traditional surface extraction algorithms based on particle systems suffer from low computational
efficiency due to the large number of inter-particle force interactions. The coupling
of repulsive and attractive forces during iteration can also affect the reconstruction
outcome. When increasing the sampling rate, the splitting mechanism of traditional
particle systems tends to cause a sharp decline in system equilibrium, further affecting
algorithmic efficiency and even leading to system imbalance and reconstruction errors.
To address the above issues, this paper improves the force mechanism of particles
based on traditional particle system surface extraction algorithms and inserts particles
in a manner that minimizes changes in system energy. Additionally, by incorporating
a multi-scale voxel pyramid and following a coarse-to-fine reconstruction order, the
correct contour is obtained at coarse scales, while details are gradually enriched during the scale refinement process, ultimately achieving adaptive sampling. Under the
premise of ensuring sampling accuracy, the proposed method is able to obtain adaptive
sampling results with good distribution quality.
1.3 Contributions of this Work
Building upon traditional particle system-based surface extraction algorithms, this
paper introduces multi-scale pyramid characteristics and proposes an adaptive surface
extraction method. First, the algorithm constructs a pyramid structure and the corresponding curvature field from CT slice data and performs adaptive region refinement
in a coarse-to-fine order based on curvature information. The refined voxel units serve
as the basic units for particle behavior. Voxel units at each level control parameters
such as the number of internal particles and their repulsion radius according to their
refinement degree, thereby achieving curvature-adaptive sampling.
To address the low computational efficiency caused by the calculation of repulsive
forces among a large number of particles in particle systems, this paper treats repulsion as a local short-range force, allowing only particles within the repulsion radius
to interact, thereby improving computational efficiency. Furthermore, the force mechanism is decoupled and adjusted. An adaptive weighted projection of grayscale and
second-order gradient is adopted to drive particles to precisely settle on the true isosurface position. Meanwhile, the tangential component of the repulsive force is extracted
to enhance the effectiveness of particle distribution optimization. To address the issue
that traditional particle systems tend to disrupt the system equilibrium state when
increasing the sampling rate through a splitting mechanism, this paper proposes an
4
incremental particle insertion mechanism based on Voronoi diagrams, which effectively
reduces the system energy fluctuation caused by particle insertion behavior.
In the experimental section, the effectiveness of the proposed method is validated
through comparison with classical surface extraction algorithms. The conclusion
section identifies the limitations of the method.
Compared with the previously proposed surface extraction methods based on surface
tracking[21], the method proposed in this paper introduces multi-scale pyramid characteristics and particle system. It not only can effectively reduce the sampling rate while
maintaining the sampling accuracy, but also significantly improves the uniformity of
particle distribution and the stability of size accuracy.
2 Method
2.1 Multi-scale Adaptive Refinement Strategy
Directly extracting surface meshes from raw CT volume data using threshold-based
methods is susceptible to artifact interference, which may cause the reconstruction
process to fall into local optima or produce topological errors. Although traditional
filtering methods can suppress artifacts to some extent, they often exacerbate the
partial volume effect of the data, thereby introducing significant errors. To address
these issues, this paper adopts a method that combines multi-scale characteristics with
edge-preserving filtering.
This section provides a detailed introduction to our multi-scale curvature-adaptive
nested refinement method. First, a voxel pyramid is constructed from the original
CT data through downsampling, and based on this, the curvature field and gradient
field at each scale are computed. Then, following a coarse-to-fine reconstruction order,
scale refinement is performed on high-curvature regions identified at the coarse scale,
serving as a necessary prerequisite for adaptive sampling.
2.1.1 Construction of Voxel Pyramid
The construction of the voxel pyramid is achieved through edge-preserving filtering
and downsampling of the CT data, in which guided filtering[22] is extended to three
dimensions as the filtering algorithm. After the voxel pyramid is constructed, the
gradient field and curvature field are further built using the voxel information at each
level.
The gradient field is used to describe the rate of change of voxel gray values in the
CT image within three-dimensional space. Its direction points toward the direction of
the fastest increase in gray value, and its magnitude reflects the strength of the edge.

2.1.2 Nested Multi-Scale Refinement
In the discrete space partitioning of a 3D scalar field, the cube formed by four adjacent
voxels on each of two adjacent slices is typically used as the basic processing unit,
referred to as a voxel unit, as shown in Figure 1, where the integer coordinates (i, j, k )
serve as the reference for the voxel unit. By comparing the grayscale values of the eight
vertices of a voxel unit with a given threshold, if a sign change exists, it indicates that
the isosurface passes through that voxel unit. For ease of explanation, such units are
referred to as ’response units’ in the remainder of this paper.
6
Fig. 1
Voxel unit
We assign a corresponding scale level l to each voxel unit to characterize its degree
of detail. Starting from the coarsest scale, the set of all response units is defined as the
’response region’, and the scale level l of these voxel units is marked as the maximum
number of levels L. Subsequently, the curvature statistical threshold θ of all voxel
units within the response region is calculated, from which the ’high-curvature region’
is identified to represent the parts with significant geometric features.
When calculating the curvature statistical threshold, the mean curvature at the center
of each voxel unit within the response region is sampled to construct a curvature
sample set, and its mean µ and standard deviation are σ calculated, which reflect the
overall level of curvature in the region and the degree of dispersion of its distribution,
respectively. The statistical threshold is determined by formula θ = µ + k · σ , where
k is an adjustable parameter. All voxel units with curvature values higher than θ are
marked as the ’high-curvature region’, serving as a search guide mask for subsequent
refinement reconstruction.
If the high-curvature region is directly mapped to the fine scale, important geometric
features may be missed, leading to inconsistent reconstruction results across scales and
making it difficult for particles to fully cover the surface, resulting in aliasing artifacts.
To address this, this paper applies morphological dilation to the high-curvature region,
with a dilation width of n voxels. The set of all response units in the processed result
is defined as the ’candidate region’ and mapped to the fine scale for refinement. In
experiments, n is typically set to 1 or 2, which yields good sampling results. For ease
of illustration, Figure 2 shows the scale refinement process in 2D form.

2.2 Extended Particle System
Based on traditional particle system-based surface extraction algorithms, this paper
adjusts the force mechanism during particle iteration by decoupling the repulsive and
attractive forces acting on particles in the time domain. In each iteration cycle, an
adaptive weighted projection of grayscale information and second-order gradient is first
used to drive particles to precisely settle on the true isosurface. Subsequently, repulsion is treated as a local short-range force, and its tangential component is extracted
to perform repulsive interactions among particles for distribution optimization. Furthermore, the average particle displacement is used as an approximation of the ’energy
gradient’ to evaluate the equilibrium state of the system. When the system approaches
equilibrium, incremental particle insertion is achieved by constructing a Voronoi diagram.
The overall workflow of this method mainly includes stages such as pyramid construction, particle system initialization, nested scale refinement, local decoupled iteration,
system equilibrium checking, and incremental particle insertion. Based on the constructed pyramid information, the particle system is first initialized to form a basic
coverage of the target surface. The initialization results are then iteratively optimized
by updating particle positions, and the overall equilibrium state of the system is
evaluated during the iteration process. If the equilibrium condition is not met, the iteration continues; when the system reaches equilibrium, new particles are incrementally
inserted into high-curvature regions according to the pyramid adaptive refinement
8
results, and the above process is repeated until the finest level of refinement is
completed.
2.2.1 Particle System Initialization
The particle system is initialized at the coarsest scale of the voxel pyramid. First, all
’response units’ are traversed, and one particle is created as an initial particle at the
approximate position corresponding to the mesh center generated by the cube in the
lookup table of the MC algorithm.
After initialization, the Mersenne Twister algorithm is used as the random number
generator to introduce small perturbations, thereby avoiding local extrema caused by
the symmetry of the initial particle distribution, while ensuring the periodicity and
uniformity of the random sequence, as shown in Figure 4. This mechanism provides a
diversified initial state for subsequent iterations, avoiding local minima traps caused
by distribution symmetry, thus enhancing the adaptability of the particle system to
geometric shapes.

2.2.5 Incremental Insertion
Particle insertion operations are performed within the high-curvature voxel units identified at each level of the voxel pyramid. This is the key for the proposed algorithm
to achieve adaptive progressive refinement and enrich geometric details. Furthermore,
to minimize the disturbance of the insertion operation to the system equilibrium
state, we propose an incremental particle insertion mechanism guided by Voronoi
diagrams[26, 27].
Considering the complexity of constructing a 3D Voronoi diagram, this paper constructs a 2D local Voronoi diagram to simplify the process. First, based on the currently
stabilized particle distribution, a local orthogonal basis is constructed according to
the gradient vector at the center of the target voxel unit, and the corresponding tangent plane is established. The activated particles within the local neighborhood are
projected onto this tangent plane, and a 2D Voronoi diagram is constructed using the
set of projected points. The area of each Voronoi cell is then calculated to quantify
the local influence range of each particle. By comparing the sizes of these ranges, the
largest uncovered region in the current particle distribution is identified. Finally, the
centroid of the identified region is mapped back to 3D space and projected onto the
isosurface, and the projected point is used as the insertion position for a new particle,
as shown in Figure 8.
Fig. 8 Principle of Incremental Insertion
Direct projection in high-curvature regions may introduce deviations from the
actual particle distribution due to normal-dimensionality reduction. To address this, in
the nearest neighbor determination stage of Voronoi diagram construction, this paper
introduces a correction term ϖ to the Euclidean distance of the 2D projected points,
as shown in Equation (18). The projection distance is corrected dω (p) = ϖ · d(p) based
on local curvature and the angle between normals, so that the 2D Voronoi analysis
results more accurately reflect the true void distribution on the 3D surface:
ω = (1 + α · κ(p)| cos θ|)

where α is the correction weight coefficient, κ(p) is the curvature at the position of the

its initial position is close to the equilibrium state, significantly reducing the number
of adjustments required in subsequent relaxation iterations. Furthermore, to ensure
that the algorithm can continue to execute under any circumstances, this method
incorporates a fallback mechanism. When the number of local particles is insufficient
or Voronoi construction fails, the original random offset scheme is used instead.
3 Experiment
In the experiment, the geometric data measured by a high-precision coordinate measuring machine (CMM) is used as the reference benchmark [28]. The absolute deviation
between the surface extraction results of the proposed algorithm and traditional algorithms and the CMM measurements is taken as the evaluation metric for assessing
the surface extraction effect. Considering the systematic errors in the measurement
process, statistical indicators including maximum deviation, standard deviation, and
residuals during geometric feature fitting are used for analysis to evaluate the surface
extraction effect of the algorithms.
In this study, a high-precision metrological CT platform independently developed by

3.2 Sampling Distribution Quality
High-quality surface sampling can faithfully reconstruct the geometric features of a
curved surface its quality essentially depends on whether the point set distribution
can approximate an isotropic uniform state at the local scale. From a geometric perspective, minimizing the potential energy of a particle system induces a hexagonal
structure, meaning that a high-quality sampling point distribution tends toward an
ideal regular hexagonal tiling.
To this end, when evaluating surface sampling quality, this paper constructs a tangent plane at the target point. The projection points of the six nearest neighboring
sampling points around the target point onto the tangent plane form a hexagon. The
degree of similarity between this hexagon and a regular hexagon is used to evaluate
the distribution quality at the target point. The normalized weighted result R of the
angular deviations and side length differences of the hexagon’s adjacent edges is calculated to quantify whether the local arrangement approximates an ideal hexagonal
tiling structure, as shown in Figure 13. A smaller weighted result R indicates that the
distribution is closer to a regular hexagonal tiling, and thus the sampling distribution
quality is higher.
18
Fig. 13 Sampling distribution quality assessment principle
We perform weighted color rendering for each point in the sampling result based on
the R value, intuitively presenting the overall sampling quality in the form of a colored
point cloud. A color closer to blue indicates higher sampling distribution quality, while
a color closer to red indicates lower quality. Figures 14 and 15 show the sampling
quality distribution maps for Sample 1 and Sample 2, respectively, and the statistical
results of the sampling distribution quality are presented in the form of histograms.
Fig. 14 The quality comparison chart of sampling distribution for Sample 1

Fig. 15 The quality comparison chart of sampling distribution for Sample 2
As can be seen from the figure, the sampling points of the MC algorithm exhibit a
noticeable clustering phenomenon in certain voxel units. The DMC algorithm generates sampling points inside voxel units with a uniformly arranged voxel grid, resulting
in a certain improvement in sampling distribution quality compared to MC. In contrast, the sampling points generated by the proposed method benefit from iterative
optimization of particle repulsion, achieving significantly better sampling distribution
quality. Further statistical analysis using histograms shows that the overall distribution quality of MC sampling results is generally poor. DMC shows improvement, with
sampling quality reaching a moderate level overall. The proposed method achieves the
highest overall sampling quality.

4 Conclusion
This paper proposes a surface extraction algorithm for industrial metrological CT
data, capable of generating high-quality adaptive sampling point clouds from CT
scan data and subsequently fitting mesh models that meet the requirements of highprecision metrological applications. Building upon traditional particle system-based
surface extraction methods, this paper makes improvements in the following aspects:
First, a multi-scale voxel pyramid is constructed through edge-preserving filtering
and downsampling. The pyramid is adaptively refined based on mean curvature,
and the refined voxel units serve as the basic containers for particle behavior. Second, a force mechanism with repulsive and attractive forces decoupled in the time
domain is adopted. A grayscale–second-order gradient weighted projection replaces
the traditional isosurface attraction to drive particles for precise sampling. Repulsion
is treated as a local short-range force, with interacting particles searched within a
26-neighborhood for iterative computation to improve algorithm efficiency. Simultaneously, the tangential component of the repulsive force is extracted to optimize
particle distribution uniformity while reducing the impact on projection accuracy.
Furthermore, an incremental particle insertion method based on a local Voronoi
diagram is proposed. After the iteration approaches equilibrium, regions with sparse
particle distribution are estimated, and new particles are inserted in a manner that
minimizes system energy changes. Experimental comparisons demonstrate that, compared to traditional surface extraction algorithms, the proposed algorithm exhibits
superior adaptive sampling quality, significantly reduces the overall sampling rate,
and achieves good reconstruction of most geometric features, showing clear comprehensive advantages.
However, the proposed algorithm still has certain limitations. First, the final output of
the algorithm is a point cloud object containing discrete spatial coordinates and normal vectors, lacking topological connectivity between vertices. This makes it difficult
to directly apply to subsequent geometric feature extraction and quantitative analysis
tasks that require a continuous surface representation, necessitating post-processing
steps such as surface fitting. Second, although the algorithm leverages multi-scale
characteristics to enhance robustness against noise and artifacts to some extent,
improper control of scale level settings can affect sampling correctness. When the
number of pyramid levels is too high, the coarse scales become blurred due to downsampling operations, potentially losing some detailed information. The subsequent
progressive refinement process is then constrained by the structure reconstructed at
the coarse scales, making it difficult to effectively express details. To address these
limitations, we hope to improve the algorithm in future work by constructing a voxel
pyramid that preserves topological structure. In the initialization stage, referencing
the topological construction approach of the DMC (Dual Marching Cubes) algorithm,
a rough mesh model with complete topological information could be built, with
details gradually enriched in subsequent processes. Additionally, since the algorithm
requires storing voxel, gradient, and curvature information at each level of the voxel
pyramid, it imposes high demands on runtime memory. Future work may consider
storing only valid regions rather than the entire region to reduce memory overhead.
