Dual laser holography for in situ measurement of plasma facing
component erosion (invited)
T. M. Biewer,1,a) J. C. Sawyer,2 C. D. Smith,2 and C. E. Thomas3
1 Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, Tennessee 37831, USA
2 Department of Mechanical, Aerospace and Biomedical Engineering, University of Tennessee, Knoxville,
Tennessee 37669, USA
3 Third Dimension Technologies, LLC., Knoxville, Tennessee 37931, USA
(Presented 16 April 2018; received 9 May 2018; accepted 19 June 2018;
published online XX XX XXXX)
A digital holography (DH) surface erosion/deposition diagnostic is being developed for 3D imaging
of plasma facing component surfaces in situ and in real time. Digital holography is a technique that
utilizes lasers reflected from a material surface to form an interferogram, which carries information
about the topology of the surface when reconstructed. As described in this paper, dual CO2 lasers
at 9.271 and 9.250 µm wavelengths illuminate the interrogated surface (at a distance of ∼1 m) in a
region of ∼1 cm × 1 cm. The surface feature resolution is ∼0.1 mm in the plane of the surface, and the
depth resolution ranges from ∼0.0001 to ∼2 mm perpendicular to the surface. The depth resolution
lower limit is set by single-laser and detector optical limitations, while the upper limit is determined
by 2π phase ambiguity of the dual-laser synthetic wavelength. Measurements have been made “on the
bench” to characterize the single-laser and dual-laser DH configurations utilizing standard resolution
targets and material targets that were previously exposed to high flux plasmas in either the Prototype
Material Plasma Exposure eXperiment (Proto-MPEX) or the electro-thermal (ET) arc source. Typical
DH measurements were made with 0.03 ms integration with an IR camera that can be framed at
rates approaching 1.5 kHz. The DH diagnostic system is progressing toward in situ measurements of
plasma erosion/deposition either on Proto-MPEX or the ET arc source. Published by AIP Publishing.

I. INTRODUCTION
27
The primary goal of research on magnetically confined
fusion devices is to develop the technology that will enable
the efficient generation of electrical power by a fusion demonstration reactor (DEMO).1 The approach to DEMO can be
thought of as progressing along two distinct pathways: (1)
understanding the physics behind the requirements of a thermonuclear fusion plasma core and (2) developing the engineered materials and structures that constitute the confinement device. In general terms, the confinement physics path
is routed through existing devices such as DIII-D and JET
and is progressing through planned devices such as ITER.
As the community approaches a viable nuclear fusion reactor, plasma discharges of increasing duration and intensity
have been made. These high heat and particle flux plasmas have revealed the importance of the problems associated with plasma-material interactions (PMI), especially
for the plasma facing components (PFCs) of fusion-relevant
devices. Research on PMI seeks to bridge the PFC “gap” and
develop engineered materials which are suitable for use in
fusion reactors.2 This pathway has been routed through dedicated plasma-surface interaction (PSI) devices like PISCES
and Magnum-PSI and could progress through proposed

A wide variety of PSI effects have been described, which
result in surface morphology changes. In the strongly coupled regime of PSI, high heat flux plasma traveling along a
magnetic field that intersects a material surface can implant
plasma particles into the material surface. The energy carried
by these particles can lead to sputtering of the surface, including chemical or physical erosion, as well as material ablation
and surface melting. The material that leaves the surface can
migrate over long and short distances, eventually redepositing somewhere in the fusion device, including onto the PFC
surface under measurement. The end result is that PFCs can
exhibit both erosion and re-deposition of material. Being able
to measure and monitor (in situ and in real time) this material
migration is an important concern for the health of the PFC,
especially in a fusion reactor where necessary cooling channels may be only a few millimeters below the surface, such as
in the ITER divertor. ITER baseline operational scenarios4 that
produce ∼10 MW/m2 heat flux on the divertor predict material erosion rates of ∼1-10 µm/h. For a 3000 s ITER discharge,
this is equivalent to ∼5 µm of tungsten erosion per discharge.
Off-normal events in ITER that result in ∼1 GW/m2 of plasma
flux are predicted4 to produce erosion of ∼500 µm per event.
Hence, diagnostic technologies are needed that can measure
in situ and in real time (for machine protection) the erosion
of material that spans the range of 5–500 µm. Dual laser
digital holography is a diagnostic technique that should be

II. BRIEF REVIEW OF DIGITAL HOLOGRAPHY
98
A holographic imaging system has three main components: an illumination source to provide coherent light, an
object to image, and a detector where the interferogram is
formed and measured. A detailed description and motivation
for the equipment utilized in this system have been given in
a previous publication.6 This system uses two Access Laser
AL30G grating tuned CO2 lasers for illumination, operating
at wavelengths of 9.250 and 9.271 µm. The detector is a FLIR
SC4000 infrared camera, operating with either 128 × 128 or
256 × 256 pixel arrays. (The term “digital” holography was
coined at a time when film cameras were more common than
digital cameras and was used to distinguish the unique aspect
of using a digital IR camera. The distinction is less useful now
since digital cameras have become pervasive.) The object to
be imaged can be any reasonably flat (metric to be described
later, see Sec. V), reflective item. For the results presented
here, a variety of items were used: an etched mirror with a
USAF resolution pattern available from Edmund Optics,7 a
terraced stack of metal shims of known thickness, and stainless steel target plates that had been exposed to plasma in
Proto-MPEX. When the diagnostic system is deployed “in the
field,” it is envisioned that the imaged object will be an in situ

For effective utilization of the available area of the detector, the illumination of the target (through the imaging optics)
should be matched to the detector. A photograph of the layout
for a single-laser setup is shown in a previous publication.6 The
beam emitted by the laser is enlarged using a beam expanding
telescope (BET), effected by matched off-axis parabolic mirrors, OAPs, to illuminate the area of interest of the object. Note
that mirrors are used to avoid second-surface reflections and
to allow the HeNe alignment laser to remain collinear with the
CO2 lasers. The same principle is true for a dual-laser system,
which is shown in Fig. 1. The two CO2 lasers are combined
using a beam splitter and made co-linear prior to entering the
BET; both OAP mirrors are enclosed by the green box shown in
Fig. 1. Either laser can be utilized to form single-laser interferograms. Differential, dual-laser interferograms can be formed
by comparing data between the two lasers, as will be described
below.
The laser beam(s) propagate from the BET into an optical layout that is essentially a Michelson-type interferometer.8
The laser light is divided into two paths using a 50:50 beam
splitter (BS). The two BSs used in this system have been manufactured by ULO Optics and II-VI Optics. The BSs are ZnSe
with a BS coating on one side and an antireflective (AR) coating on the opposite side. The two sides have a wedge angle
of one degree to direct spurious back reflections out of the
instrument. The “reference beam” is directed through imaging
optics onto the camera detector. The “object beam” is reflected
from the object of interest and combined on the camera detector through matching imaging optics. Since the illumination
source is provided by coherent laser wave-fronts, the object
beam and reference beam exhibit constructive and destructive
interference patterns on the detector. The path length difference of each beam is largely determined by surface feature
height variations on the object. A pixel-by-pixel fast Fourier
transform yields the complex amplitude and phase of the interfering light on the detector. The reconstituted amplitude image
shows the traditional contrast in the object due to light or dark
features. The reconstituted phase image shows a map of the
surface feature height variation. Hence the camera detector

The system in Fig. 1 is aligned by using Macken plates
(plates that display IR laser beams when illuminated with UV
light). Irises are aligned to the CO2 beam using the Macken
plates, and a HeNe laser is subsequently aligned to the irises
so that most of the alignment can be performed with the HeNe
laser. The system generally stays well-aligned on the optical
table for months at a time if components are not bumped or
moved. In general, it is necessary to optimize the number of
pixels-per-fringe slightly by minor adjustments to the reference beam. For in situ work, motorized elements can be used
for the final reference and object beam mirrors.
For a single laser, since the path length difference that
leads to interfering wave-fronts is due to surface height variation, a fundamental limitation of this technique becomes
apparent: If the surface feature height difference is greater than
λ/2, then there is a 2π phase ambiguity of the reflected light.
This fact, combined with the motivation that µm scale feature
(e.g., surface erosion in ITER) imaging is desired, drives the
hardware choices toward CO2 lasers operating at ∼10 µm and
infrared imaging.
As has been shown elsewhere,6 for a dual-laser system,
with two lasers operating at slightly different wavelengths, the
2π phase ambiguity can be moved to effectively larger surface feature height variation. Complex interferograms from
each laser, when divided by each other pixel by pixel (dividing the amplitudes and subtracting the phases), yield information based on the synthetic wavelength of the dual laser
system, which is given by λs = λ1 λ2 /(λ1 − λ2 ). For the
CO2 lasers of the system described here, operating at 9.250
and 9.271 µm, the synthetic wavelength is ∼4.1 mm, which
implies that the 2π phase ambiguity arises for surface features of ∼2 mm. In this way, the upper limit of surface feature
height variation spans ∼5 µm (for either single-laser hologram) to ∼2000 µm (for a dual-laser differential hologram).
This range is consistent with the range of erosion expected for
both baseline operation of ITER and single-event off-normal
occurrences.

IV. DUAL-LASER MEASUREMENTS
273
For dual-laser measurements, the timing of the acoustooptic modulators (AOMs) are staggered, allowing single-laser
interferograms to be captured on interleaved frames of the
camera data stream. (Note that as previously described,5 it is
possible to capture a hologram from both lasers on the same
frame. This complicates the reference beam optics but removes
differential vibrational noise from the two object beams.)
Differential measurements are then formed by dividing the
complex phase, pixel-by-pixel, from subsequent frames.
The resulting holograms have a z-dimension that is characterized by the synthetic wavelength of the two lasers, as
discussed in Sec. II. Since λs ∼ 4.1 mm for the system
described here, the ∼1 µm surface features of the USAF resolution standard have been replaced by forming a terraced
stack of 0.0150 in. thick (381 µm) stainless steel shims, as
shown in Fig. 4. The 3 shim stack has a total height change
of ∼1.1 mm.

laser beam(s) has been increased by using the BET, creating
a circular area of illumination. On the square pixel array of
the detector, the illumination circle does not fill the array completely; there are dark regions around the periphery. Within the

V. CONSIDERATIONS FOR IN SITU MEASUREMENTS
AT ORNL
The digital holograms presented in this paper have all been
measured “on the bench,” in that the objects/targets have been
placed on the same optical table with the illumination laser(s)
and the IR camera. The maximum depth of spatial scales for
the DH technique described cover from the micrometer scale to
the millimeter scale. Transitioning the DH technique to in situ
measurements necessitates a consideration of the time scale
capabilities of the hardware. The CO2 lasers operate continuous waves (CWs), with a power output of ∼7 W. The frame
rate of the measurement in this instance is determined by the
frame rate of the IR camera, which varies from ∼500 Hz
(256 × 256 pixels) to a maximum of 1500 Hz (128 × 128
pixels). The measurements shown here were framed on the
camera at 500 Hz, corresponding to a measurement interval of
2 ms. However, the AOM(s) which are used to limit the light
intensity on the detector are modulated for ∼30 µs exposures
for the data shown here. On the one hand, this implies that the
system has a large over-capacity (factor of ∼100) for delivering coherent CO2 laser light to enable an in situ measurement.
This light intensity will enable the discrimination of coherent laser light against any incoherent IR background light that
may be present during plasma exposures in situ. On the other
hand, analyzing the system in this configuration for measurement noise (perhaps) misrepresents the capability that could be
expected for in situ measurements. Nevertheless, on the bench,
by examining a 100-1000 frame sequence of DH images of a
static object, it has been experimentally determined that the
vibrational noise of the single laser system is equivalent to a
feature height of ∼10 nm. For the dual-laser system, this noise
equivalence scales with the ratio of the synthetic wavelength
to the laser wavelength, which is ∼450. This implies a vibrational noise floor of the dual-laser system of ∼4.5 µm. Taken
together, the typical measurement range (on the bench) of the
DH diagnostic system spans from 10 nm to 5 µm (single-laser)
at 500 Hz, and 5 µm to 2 mm (dual-laser) at 250 Hz.
An additional metric that is useful to consider is the surface “presentation flatness” (or tilt) that would produce a 2π
phase variation in the DH measured phase image. If the metric
is imposed that only one 2π phase jump is tolerable, then the
maximum z variation is set by either the laser wavelength for
single-laser DH or the synthetic wavelength for the dual-laser
DH. Comparing this dimensional scale to the (x, y) dimensional scale establishes a degree of surface tilt that is permitted.
For a single-laser system, the z-scale is ∼5 µm. When compared to the x,y-scale of 3.5 mm, this implies a tilt angle of
only ∼0.2◦ . For the dual-laser system, the x,y-scale does not
Rev. Sci. Instrum. 89, 000000 (2018)
change, but the z-scale is now ∼2 mm, which implies a tilt angle
of ∼20◦ . This relaxation in the tilt angle for a dual-laser system
implies that more realistic (less idealized) physical geometries
can be measured since the object reflected light need not be
specular (provided there is sufficient coherent intensity in the
diffuse reflection). For large tilt angles, other optical limitations also come into play, which will limit the surface tilt that
can practically be accommodated beyond which is allowed by
the 2π phase ambiguity.
The next step of the project is to implement the DH diagnostic for in situ measurements during plasma exposure. This
could be accomplished at ORNL utilizing either the ProtoMPEX device or an electrothermal (ET) arc source. As the
demonstration source concept for steady-state MPEX,3 ProtoMPEX has produced pulsed heat flux onto material targets up
to 14 MW/m2 . This should be compared to the 10 MW/m2 heat
flux that is estimated to be present in the standard operational
scenario of ITER. This heat flux is expected to produce erosion
of the divertor plates at ∼5 µm/h. This erosion rate is within
the single-laser measurement capability of the DH diagnostic,
provided that vibrational noise is not excessive. Proto-MPEX
pulse lengths are typically ∼0.5 s (and have been as long as 2 s),
which would contain ∼250 single-laser measurements. A DH
measurement every 2 ms, coupled to a piezo valve gas fueling
actuator with ∼10 ms time response, could constitute a realtime control system for effecting PFC erosion. Port access on
the central chamber of Proto-MPEX has been identified, which
would allow DH measurement from an engineered target that
is inclined at 45◦ to the magnetic field, allowing a normal
incidence of the DH object beam on the target. Though the
heat flux to the target would be commensurately reduced due
to the area increase in the inclined target, the erosion rate is
still expected to be within the capability of single-laser DH
measurements.
Off-normal events in ITER are expected to exhibit erosion
rates of ∼500 µm/event.4 The time scale of a large Edge Localized Mode (ELM) or Vertical Displacement Event (VDE)
could be ∼1 ms. To effect erosion/deposition rates of this
magnitude, the electrothermal (ET) arc source at ORNL could
be used. The ET arc is intended to be an “ELM simulator”
for Proto-MPEX or MPEX and has demonstrated transient
heat fluxes onto a target of ∼1-2 GW/m2 in a time scale of
1-2 ms by discharging a capacitor bank through an ablative
liner in the presence of magnetic field.10,11 These experiments were performed as “stand alone” experiments; i.e., the
ET arc source was not installed on Proto-MPEX at the time
the experiments were conducted. IR camera sequences have
been made of the discharge evolution through a ZnSe window, imaging the steel target plate. The same setup could
be appropriated for dual-laser digital holography, where the
plate surface is characterized before and after the ET arc
discharge.

dual-laser system has demonstrated proof-of-principle measurements “on the bench” with a depth scale that ranges from
5 µm to 2 mm at 250 Hz. The dual-laser system functions
by interleaving two single-laser systems operating at slightly
different wavelengths. The project is preparing to deploy “in
the field” and will make measurements in situ of plasma erosion/deposition from the Proto-MPEX device and/or the ET
arc source, which are both located at ORNL. For a 1 s pulse of
Proto-MPEX, the DH diagnostic can deliver ∼500 single-laser
(250 dual-laser) measurements of the plasma facing surface,
suggesting that real-time measurement of the surface evolution during plasma exposure could be possible. Real-time
control could then be subsequently enacted by coupling to
a fast piezo valve neutral gas nozzle (for example) as an
actuator.
