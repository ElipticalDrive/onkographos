

# MZEN: Multi-zoom Enhanced NeRF for 3-D Reconstruction with Unknown Camera Poses

Jong-Ik Park1 · Gary K. Fedder1 · Carlee Joe-Wong1

Received: 30 October 2025 / Revised: 30 June 2026 / Accepted: 30 June 2026 / Published online: 30 July 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

### Abstract

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) methods excel at 3D reconstruction from multiple 2D images, even those taken with unknown camera poses. However, they still miss the finedetailed structures that matter in industrial inspection, e.g., detecting sub-micron defects on a production line or analyzing chips with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). In these scenarios, the sensor resolution is fixed and compute budgets are tight, so the only way to expose fine structure is to add zoomed-in images; yet, this breaks the multi-view consistency that pose-free NeRF training relies on. We propose Multi-Zoom Enhanced NeRF (MZEN), the first NeRF framework that natively handles multi-zoom image sets. MZEN (i) augments the pin-hole camera model with an explicit, learnable zoom parameter that scales the focal length, and (ii) introduces a novel pose estimation strategy: wide-field (i.e., zoomed-out) images are used first to establish a global metric frame, and the poses of zoomed-in images are then initialized to the nearest wide-field counterpart via a zoom-consistent crop-and-match procedure before joint refinement of both poses and the NeRF model. Across eight forward-facing scenes—synthetic TCAD models, real SEM of micro-structures, and BLEFF objects—MZEN consistently outperforms pose-free baselines and even high-resolution variants, boosting PSNR by up to 32%, SSIM by 52%, and reducing LPIPS by up to 400%. MZEN, therefore, extends NeRF to real-world factory settings, preserving global accuracy while capturing the micron-level details essential for industrial inspection.

## 1 Introduction

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have significantly advanced 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis by learning continuous volumetric representations from multiple 2D images (Barron et al., 2022; Mildenhall et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2022; Yariv et al., 2021). By leveraging differentiable volume rendering, NeRF-based methods generate high-fidelity 3D representations from sparse image inputs, enabling broad applications like microscopy, aerial photography, medical imaging, and semiconductor inspection (Bouchard et al., 2015; Gray et al., 2018; Rade et al., 2024; Sakamoto et al., 2022). In microscopy, NeRF offers a way to reconstruct volumetric tissue structures and subcellular morphology from limited optical views, aiding disease diagnosis and biological research (Sakamoto et al., 2022). In semiconductor manufacturing, high-resolution Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) combined with NeRF can enable 3D geometric reconstruction of defects for more precise failure analysis (Fu et al., 2024; Lee & Lee, 2014).

Despite these successes, standard NeRF methods rely on two key assumptions: (i) accurate camera poses are known or can be reliably estimated by identifying shared image features (Mur-Artal et al., 2015; Schönberger & Frahm, 2016), and (ii) input images are captured at similar viewing scales with sufficient overlap to enforce multi-view consistency (Barron et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). However, both assumptions break down in many industrial settings. Real-world datasets often arrive with unknown or highly inaccurate camera poses, and the available imagery spans multiple zoom levels ranging from wide overviews (for locating targets) to extreme close-ups (for texture fidelity), so that overlapping content is either tiny or entirely absent (Jeong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). In microscopy, for example, pose inaccuracies arise from mechanical drift and stage repositioning, while imaging must capture both low-magnification tissue structures and high-magnification subcellular details (Bouchard et al., 2015). SEM imaging in semiconductor manufacturing is another example scenario where these challenges arise, as it demands precise alignment of micrometer-scale devices with sub-micrometer or nanometer-scale defect inspections across multiple layers (Al Rahman & Mousavi, 2020; Fu et al., 2024). A typical SEM workflow begins with a  $1 \times 10^5$  magnification survey image and then zooms to  $3 \times 10^5$  to inspect suspected defects (Nakamae, 2021). These high-zoom frames have almost no overlap with each other, and even small positional shifts can cause significantly greater pose inaccuracies compared to optical imaging since SEM imaging relies on electron interactions (Jin & Li, 2015).

Several NeRF variants have attempted to address camera pose inaccuracies by jointly optimizing pose estimation and scene reconstruction (Bian et al., 2023; Chng et al., 2024; Jeong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Complementary work enhances texture fidelity by incorporating anti-aliasing or multi-resolution features, but these methods still assume that all training images share the same zoom level, or at most have minor zoom variations (Barron et al., 2021, 2023; Park et al., 2023). In practice, however, images captured at high zoom levels often have minimal overlap with one another, since acquisition typically targets selective fine details rather than broad, shared context (Fig. 1), leading

to a breakdown of the conventional multi-view consistency assumption in existing NeRF methods (Han et al., 2025; Mildenhall et al., 2022) and thus instability in pose optimization. Despite these challenges, incorporating combinations of frames at different zoom levels is often necessary, since frames that capture wide overviews simultaneously with high resolution are often unavailable: cameras have a fixed pixel count, so arbitrarily high-resolution frames cannot be captured on demand (Albanese et al., 2022). Additionally, capturing or training on very large images is computationally prohibitive.

To address these challenges, we propose Multi-Zoom Enhanced NeRF (MZEN), illustrated in Fig. 2. Unlike existing NeRF variants that implicitly assume an identical viewing scale, MZEN introduces a zoom-consistent camera model that explicitly represents optical magnification as a learnable scalar in the intrinsic parameters. This reformulation allows MZEN to interpret image sets captured at different zoom levels under a physically

consistent model. To resolve the pose ambiguity caused by non-overlapping zoomed-in frames, MZEN further incorporates a cross-zoom pose registration mechanism. Specifically, MZEN aligns each zoomed-in image with its most compatible wide-field (zoomed-out) reference through a zoom-aware crop-and-match test, and then registers the poses of the zoomed-out views to those of the zoomed-in views — a process termed pose priming. Finally, the poses of the zoomed-in views are refined through optimization. Together, these components form the first framework that can natively integrate multi-zoom imagery into NeRF-based reconstruction.

In addition, MZEN adopts a structured three-phase training schedule designed to enable pose priming and stabilize optimization:

Phase A—*Global Pose Bootstrapping:** optimizes NeRF parameters and camera poses only for the most zoomed-out images, whose wide field of view (FoV) defines the global frame.

Phase B—*Zoom-in Pose Registration:** uses the learned intrinsics to initialize each zoomed-in image with the pose of its matched wide-field counterpart, refining only the local pose and zoom parameter while freezing the NeRF weights.

Phase C—*Joint Refinement:** jointly optimizes all poses, zoom parameters, and NeRF weights across every zoom level to recover fine-scale geometry and texture.

This staged design enables the proposed zoom-consistent formulation to converge reliably by avoiding direct, fully joint pose optimization across all magnifications. In particular, it is most effective when (i) the zoomed-out subset contains sufficient overlap to yield a stable global pose scaffold in Phase A, and (ii) each zoomed-in view retains enough shared content with at least one available wide-field surrogate for reliable pose priming in Phase B.

Our work makes the following contributions:

- • We introduce the first zoom-consistent camera model for NeRF, treating optical zoom as a learnable intrinsic parameter rather than an external crop or scale factor.
- • We develop a cross-zoom pose registration procedure that establishes geometric correspondence between wide-field and zoomed-in images via zoom-aware cropping, enabling pose-free NeRFs to operate on non-overlapping multi-zoom datasets.
- • We present a stable realization of this framework through a three-phase optimization routine that progressively aligns, registers, and refines all zoom levels.
- • We introduce the Forward-Facing Multi-Zoom NeRF (FF-MZN) benchmark, comprising (i) micro-scale SEM images of MEMS devices, (ii) four Synopsys Sentaurus TCAD test structures (Synopsys, Inc, 2025), and (iii) two scenes from the BLEFF dataset (Wang et al., 2021).
- • We evaluate MZEN on the FF-MZN dataset, achieving gains of up to 32.1 % in Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), 51.9% in Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) (Wang et al., 2004), 46.0% in Gradient Similarity Score (GSS), 57.8% in Laplacian Similarity Score (LSS), and a 400.0% reduction in Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) (Zhang et al., 2018) compared with pose-free NeRF baselines (NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021), BARF (Lin et al., 2021), NoPe-NeRF (Bian et al., 2023), Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021), and CamP (Park et al., 2023)).

MZEN's qualitative impact is illustrated in Fig. 3, and FF-MZN will be publicly released to foster further research on zoom-consistent NeRF reconstruction.

Section 5 discusses computational efficiency and practical constraints, and Sect. 6 concludes with future research directions.

# 2 Background and Related Work

Given a set of camera poses Π**1,* Π**2,...,* Π***N corresponding to N observed images of a given object, NeRF synthesizes new images of this object through a rendering function, optimizing the parameters Θ by minimizing a photometric loss:

$$\hat{\mathbf{I}}_i = F_{\Theta}(\Theta, \Pi_i), \quad \Theta^* = \arg \min_{\Theta} \sum_{i=1}^N \|\hat{\mathbf{I}}_i(\Theta, \Pi_i) - \mathbf{I}_i\|_2^2,$$

where F***Θ(Θ*,* Π***i) maps camera poses to rendered images ˆ**I***i*, and I***i represents the observed images in the training dataset.

NeRF's reliance on accurate camera poses makes it vulnerable when poses are unknown or inaccurate, leading to misalignment, incorrect ray sampling, and geometric distortions (Barron et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Traditional NeRF approaches assume known poses, often estimated using Structure-from-Motion (SfM) techniques like COLMAP (Schönberger & Frahm, 2016) and Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) (Mur-Artal et al., 2015). However, these methods struggle with low-texture regions, repetitive structures, and degenerate camera motions, resulting in incorrect or incomplete reconstructions (She et al., 2024; Van Vlasselaer et al., 2024). Several other works instead jointly optimize camera parameters and NeRF representations. In particular, NeRF −−,

BARF, SC-NeRF, and NoPe-NeRF refine pose estimation while learning NeRF representations (Bian et al., 2023; Jeong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021).

NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021) jointly optimizes camera parameters and NeRF representations. However, their approach remains stable only under small (limited to approximately 20%) translation and rotation perturbations, as stated in their work. Bundle-Adjusting NeRF (BARF) (Lin et al., 2021) refines camera poses using coarse-to-fine positional encoding scheduling, where high-frequency details are gradually introduced during training to stabilize optimization. This prevents misalignment in camera poses in the early stages and improves reconstruction accuracy. SC-NeRF (Jeong et al., 2021) further enhances pose estimation by modeling lens distortions, which standard NeRF models ignore. By optimizing all camera parameters, SC-NeRF improves ray alignment, ensuring that NeRF rays correctly correspond to real-world camera projections and reducing errors in datasets with optical distortions. NoPe-NeRF (Bian et al., 2023) tackles large pose errors by integrating monocular depth priors to stabilize training. It enforces depth consistency using a point cloud loss and improves alignment through a surface-based photometric loss, making it more robust in unconstrained camera trajectories.

While these methods improve pose estimation, none of them consider multi-zoom imaging. Zoomed-in images have minimal overlap with zoomed-out views, breaking NeRF's multi-view consistency assumption and causing instability, as shown in our experiments (Sect. 4). Therefore, a framework that explicitly models zoom consistency and optimizes pose estimation is required for stable training and accurate reconstruction across multiple zoom levels. Our MZEN provides such a framework.

Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021), Zip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2023), and CamP (Park et al., 2023) target high-resolution reconstruction by suppressing aliasing by training on a multi-resolution pyramid in which every image shares the same FoV. Additionally, Mip-NeRF combats aliasing by rendering cone trunks instead of rays. Zip-NeRF refines this idea for lower memory use. CamP sharpens details further through pose pre-conditioning. However, these approaches require large, high-resolution images for the image pyramid and rely on Structure-from-Motion (SfM)-supplied poses, which already fulfill multi-view consistency. Industrial microscopes, by contrast, have fixed sensors; high-resolution frames cannot be captured on demand, and training on gigapixel imagery is computationally prohibitive (Albanese et al., 2022). Therefore, real datasets often mix low-magnification context shots with tightly zoomed-in detail views whose poses are rarely consistent. In summary, prior work is most closely related to MZEN in two directions. Pose-free NeRF methods such as NeRF--, BARF, SC-NeRF, and NoPe-NeRF improve camera-pose optimization when poses are unknown or inaccurate, but they still assume that the input images share a compatible viewing scale. Anti-aliased and high-resolution NeRF variants such as Mip-NeRF, Zip-NeRF, and CamP improve rendering quality or camera conditioning, but they rely on consistent fields of view or reliable pose initialization.

MZEN sidesteps these issues by keeping every frame at native resolution and explicitly modeling optical zoom, eliminating the need for high-resolution images or external pose initialization.



### 3 Multi-zoom Enhanced NeRF

Industrial imaging systems-including optical microscopes, digital inspection cameras, and SEM-routinely capture the same specimen at multiple optical magnifications to get better details. Because the zoom mechanism is mechanical, exact camera calibration is unavailable, and the specimen stage moves only a few microns—conditions under which classical Structure-from-Motion (SfM) fails (Wang et al., 2021). Standard NeRF formulations assume fixed or small zoom-level perturbations across all images; when magnification changes, these assumptions break down, coupling translation, scale, and focal length in an unidentifiable way. As a result, pose-free NeRFs cannot maintain geometric consistency across zoom levels.

To cope with this setting, MZEN introduces a zoom-aware camera model that treats optical magnification as a learnable intrinsic parameter and a cross-zoom pose registration scheme to align non-overlapping zoomed-in and zoomed-out views. These components are optimized through three structured training phases which together enable stable, calibration-free multi-zoom reconstruction. Figure 2 gives an overview of the methodology developed below: MZEN uses a zoom-aware camera model, registers poses across zoom levels, and then refines the radiance field and camera parameters through the three-phase training schedule.

#### 3.1 Zoom-Consistent Camera Formulation

We introduce a zoom-consistent camera model that explicitly represents optical magnification as part of the intrinsic parameters. The training set  $\mathcal{I} = \{\mathbf{I}_k\}_{k=1}^N$  is split into the  $N_{\text{out}}$  most zoomed-out frames (minimal magnification;  $\xi_k = 1$ ) with indices  $i = 1, \dots, N_{\text{out}}$ , and  $N_{\text{in}}$  zoomed-in frames ( $\xi_k > 1$ ) with indices  $j = N_{\text{out}} + 1, \dots, N$ . Each image  $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_k$  is rendered as

$$\hat{\mathbf{I}}_k = F_{\Theta}(\Theta, \Pi_k), \quad \Pi_k = (\mathbf{R}_k, \mathbf{t}_k, \mathbf{f}, \mathbf{c}, \xi_k). \quad (1)$$

where NeRF parameters  $\Theta$  define the radiance field and  $\Pi_k$  is the camera pose. Here, each image comes with a rough estimate of its zoom parameter  $\xi_k$ : either read from the camera’s zoom dial or inferred by comparing the object’s apparent size across views taken from the same optical center.

Extrinsics, rotation  $\mathbf{R}_k \in \text{SO}(3)$  and translation  $\mathbf{t}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$ , locate the camera in world space. Intrinsics, focal length  $\mathbf{f} = (f_x, f_y)$  and principal point  $\mathbf{c} = (c_x, c_y)$ , complete the standard pin-hole intrinsics (Park et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). Finally, the zoom parameter  $\xi_k \geq 1$  scales the focal length, giving the effective intrinsic matrix  $\mathbf{K}_k^{\text{eff}} = \text{diag}(\xi_k f_x, \xi_k f_y, 1)$ . Unlike CamP (Park et al., 2023), which treats the focal length of every view as a perturbed parameter, we assume a single base focal length for the camera and model per-view magnification with a separate zoom parameter. Thus, all views share the same intrinsic focal length, while any variation is captured solely by the learnable zoom parameter.



### 3.2 Cross-Zoom Pose Registration

Assuming that the poses of the most zoomed-out images are reliably estimated from their larger FoV, the main challenge arises from zoomed-in views, which have little to no overlap with other zoomed-in images. This lack of shared features makes direct correspondence-based alignment infeasible. To bridge this gap, MZEN introduces a cross-zoom registration mechanism which establishes geometric correspondence between zoomed-in and the most zoomed-out images through a zoom-aware alignment process.

Every zoomed-in image  $\mathbf{I}_j$  is accompanied by a zoom parameter  $\xi_j > 1$ . To obtain a coarse yet physically plausible initial pose, we search the corresponding zoomed-out image set for the view that best matches  $\mathbf{I}_j$  after accounting for its narrower FoV. Both the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters are then pose-primed from this best wide-field surrogate.

To find the best match for each zoomed-in image  $j$ , for each of the most zoomed-out images with indices  $g \leq N_{\text{out}}$ , we extract the central  $1/\xi_j$  crop and bilinearly resize it to the native resolution, producing the surrogate view  $\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{g \rightarrow j}$ . We then find the zoomed-out image closest to image  $j$ :


$$g^*(j) = \arg \min_{g \leq N_{\text{out}}} \frac{1}{HW} \|\mathbf{I}_j - \tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{g \rightarrow j}\|_2^2, \quad (2)$$

where the mean-squared error is measured in RGB space. We copy the winning zoomed-out image’s rotation and translation,

Indeed, in Sect. 4's experiments, we empirically find that this method works well even when some zoomed-in images do not have an exact match to the center of a zoomed-out image.

### 3.3 Three-Phase Training Schedule

Simultaneous optimization of NeRF parameters and camera poses across all zoom levels is unstable. Moreover, executing the proposed pose priming requires optimized camera poses for the zoomed-out images. Therefore, MZEN adopts a structured optimization strategy composed of three phases. In the following subsections, we introduce the loss functions minimized in each phase; any suitable optimization method, e.g., variants of gradient descent, can be employed to minimize them.

### 3.3.1 Phase A - Global Pose Bootstrap

We start with the N*out most zoomed-out images whose dial magnification indicates *ξ ≈*1 . For these frames the zoom parameter is initialized at *ξi =1, while the NeRF parameters Θ(A), rotations R(A) i , translations t (A) i , and focal lengths f (A) are randomly initialized. With all zoomed-in images withheld, we minimize

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{coarse}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{out}}} \|F_{\Theta^{(A)}}(\Theta^{(A)}, \Pi_i^{(A)}) - \mathbf{I}_i\|_2^2,$$

where Π(A) i =(R***i, t***i,***f*,* c*,ξi*). Because every camera now shares the similar FoV, focal length and translation can no longer compensate for one another, and the optimizer converges to a single, metric reconstruction. The output is coarse NeRF parameters Θ(A) and globally consistent poses { Π(A) i }N*out *i=1 , obtained by the substantial overlap among the most zoomed-out views. These poses lock the world scale and act as references for Phase B.

## 3.3.2 Phase B - Zoom-in Pose Registration

After performing pose priming as described in Sect. 3.2, we refine the initialized camera poses while keeping the coarse NeRF parameters Θ(A) frozen. Each copied pose and its corresponding zoom parameter are optimized by minimizing


$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}} = \sum_{j=N_{\text{out}}+1}^N \left\| F_{\Theta^{(A)}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(A)}, \boldsymbol{\Pi}_j^{(B)}) - \mathbf{I}_j \right\|_2^2, \quad (3)$$

where Π(B) j =(R***j , t***j ,***f*,* c*,ξj* ). The optimization adjusts only (R***j , t***j ,ξj ); all intrinsics copied from the most zoomed-out counterpart surrogate remain fixed.

After convergence, Phase B produces the provisional full pose set

$$\Pi^{(B)} = \{\Pi_i^{(A)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{out}}} \cup \{\Pi_j^{(B)}\}_{j=N_{\text{out}}+1}^N$$

which seeds the joint fine optimization of Phase C.

### 3.3.3 Phase C - Joint Refinement

The coarse NeRF parameters Θ(A) and the provisional pose set Π(B) provide a good overall reconstruction of the large-scale global geometry, but still miss high-frequency texture. In this final stage, we unfreeze the NeRF parameters (initializing Θ(C) =Θ(A)) and optimize it together with every camera pose parameters, including the zoom parameters {ξk} . The loss is


$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fine}} = \sum_{k=1}^N \|F_{\Theta^{(C)}}(\Theta^{(C)}, \Pi_k^{(B)}) - \mathbf{I}_k\|_2^2. \quad (4)$$

Jointly minimizing (4) redistributes remaining discrepancies between geometry and camera parameters across zoom levels, sharpening fine details and yielding pixel-accurate poses for high-quality novel-view synthesis.

## 4 Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we first discuss the metrics used for evaluating MZEN's NeRF reconstructions, followed by the datasets, configurations, and training setups. We then present quantitative and qualitative results demonstrating MZEN's improvements over existing pose-free NeRF methods on both synthetic and real forward-facing datasets.

We also conduct experiments on two 360° scenes (Lego and Hotdog) from the NeRF-Synthetic dataset (Mildenhall et al., 2021\) to further verify MZEN's generalization beyond forward-facing setups. The corresponding results are presented in Appendix G.

### 4.1 Metrics for NeRF Reconstruction

We use the PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS to evaluate the quality of a given image's reconstruction. PSNR and SSIM focus on pixel-wise differences and structural similarity but struggle to evaluate fine textures and high-frequency details, particularly in blurry NeRF reconstructions (Fei et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2022; Pambrun & Noumeir, 2015; Setiadi, 2021; Zhang et al., 2018\). To complement these limitations, LPIPS is sometimes used to assess perceptual quality (Zhang et al., 2018\). However, LPIPS relies on pre-trained deep networks (we adopt

the author-released pre-trained AlexNet backbone (Zhang et al., 2018)), making it non-deterministic and less interpretable for evaluating NeRF reconstructions.

To complement the existing metrics while addressing these limitations, we employ two additional metrics: Gradient Similarity Score (GSS) and Laplacian Similarity Score (LSS). GSS directly compares gradient magnitudes, making it sensitive to edge sharpness and faint spatial smearing that standard metrics overlook. LSS measures second-order intensity variations, capturing the fidelity of high-frequency texture and surface curvature. Implementation details are given in Appendix B.

## 4.2 Forward-Facing Multi-zoom NeRF Dataset

To benchmark multi-zoom reconstruction, we introduce the FF-MZN dataset, which contains eight forward-facing scenes drawn from three sources:

- ● TCAD-SIM: four test structures rendered in Synopsys Sentaurus TCAD (Synopsys, Inc., 2025);
- ● SEM-MEMS: two real micro-electromechanical (MEMS) devices captured with a SEM; and
- ● BLEFF: two objects from the Blender Forward-Facing dataset (Wang et al., 2021).

All frames are RGB forward-facing views. TCAD and SEM images have a resolution of 400 × 400 pixels, while BLEFF images are 390 × 260 pixels.

TCAD and BLEFF scenes are rendered/photographed at three optical magnifications {1×, 2×, 4×}; SEM scenes use {1×, 2×, 3×}. For BLEFF, lower zooms (1×, 2×) are created by bicubic down-sampling the 4× render, while the 4× view is a center crop rescaled to the native resolution. This keeps the camera extrinsics identical across zoom levels while providing realistic multi-zoom pairs. After applying this procedure to all BLEFF scenes, we select the two whose surface texture vanishes in the 1× view but reappear in the zoomed-in images.

This layout allows us to test whether a method can recover both global structure (from the 1× images) and fine detail (from the > 1× images) while maintaining pose consistency. FF-MZN therefore offers the first structured benchmark for zoom-consistent NeRF training, as it stresses pose solvers with minimal overlap between high zoomed-in images and demands fidelity across three orders of zoom levels. Full acquisition details are provided in Appendix F.



## 4.3 Evaluation Configurations

We evaluate MZEN under four experimental configurations to isolate the contribution of each MZEN stage:

- ● Config 1: Training only with zoomed-out images using a baseline NeRF method without known camera poses. This serves as the primary baseline.
- ● Config 2: Training with all zoom levels using a baseline NeRF method without known camera poses. In this configuration, we independently optimize the camera poses of all images, without using any prior knowledge of the image zoom level. This serves as the

secondary baseline.

- ● Config 3: MZEN uses only zoomed-out images to perform the global-pose bootstrap. This corresponds to Phase A of MZEN and differs from Config 1 by introducing the zoom-aware camera formulation with a learnable per-view zoom scalar (initialized at  $\xi_i = 1$ ).
- ● Config 4: MZEN runs the full three-phase schedule, including pose priming and joint fine optimization, across all zoom levels. This corresponds to the complete MZEN framework, through Phase C.

These configurations illustrate the incremental performance improvements as each MZEN component is applied. For Configs 3 and 4, MZEN builds upon existing pose-free NeRF backbones (e.g., NeRF--, BARF, NoPe-NeRF, Mip-NeRF, and CamP) by (i) adopting the zoom-consistent camera formulation and (ii) applying a structured three-phase optimization schedule with cross-zoom pose priming and registration for the zoom-in views before joint refinement.

#### 4.4 Experiment Settings

For a fair comparison, we allocate an equal computational budget to each setting. Configs 1 and 3 are trained for the same number of epochs (identical forward and backward passes per image), and likewise for Configs 2 and 4. In Config 4, we count the Phase B pose-registration passes—although the NeRF weights are frozen—to match the total calculation cost of Config 2. Thus, each input image receives the same overall optimization effort in its respective pair (1 vs. 3, 2 vs. 4). Epoch numbers are chosen so that both camera poses and NeRF parameters have fully converged in all cases (empirically, training PSNR changes by less than 2 % when training is extended).

We treat the extrinsics and the focal length as unknowns that must be learned during training. The zoom value recorded by the camera is used only as an initial guess; it, too, is allowed to adjust during the optimization process.

We use NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021), BARF (Lin et al., 2021), and NoPe-NeRF (Bian et al., 2023) for pose estimation. When using BARF, the first phase leverages BARF’s coarse-to-fine encoding. When using NoPe-NeRF, depth supervision is utilized in every phase.

Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021) and CamP (Park et al., 2023) assume COLMAP-initialized poses (Schönberger & Frahm, 2016), which we could not obtain on the multi-zoom FF-MZN dataset. Accordingly, for Mip-NeRF, we keep the NeRF-- backbone but replace the standard positional encoding with Mip-NeRF’s Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE), and optimize camera poses and scene parameters jointly from scratch. For CamP, we begin with the same Mip-NeRF settings, then activate CamP’s pose-preconditioning module after a fixed number of epochs and continue training.

Each scene is uniformly randomly split into an 80 % training set and a 20 % test set. Because the split is performed at the image level, the training subset does not necessarily contain a perfectly paired wide-field counterpart for every zoomed-in view. This naturally produces unpaired zoomed-in instances, consistent with the scenario illustrated in Fig. 4. To

ensure a balanced evaluation, we maintain comparable numbers of training images across zoom levels within each scene.

Test poses are obtained by running MZEN's pose-registration step on the held-out frames once training is complete, mirroring the evaluation protocol of NeRF--. All metrics are then computed between the rendered test views and the ground-truth images for every configuration. Following the NeRF-- evaluation protocol, we obtain test poses by applying MZEN's pose-registration step to the held-out frames after training. To reduce stochasticity in the pose-registration/evaluation pipeline, we repeat testing with three random seeds and report mean ( $\pm$  std) in Tables 1 and 2.

All models are trained on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Additional details, including learning rates, training epochs, and network architectures, are provided in Appendix A.

#### 4.5 Quantitative results

Tables 1 and 2 report average scores over the three zoom levels for all eight scenes in FF-MZN. Across the 40 scene-metric combinations in Tables 1 and 2, Config 4 (full MZEN) achieves the best overall performance in 39/40 cases, with the only exception occurring on FinFET under Mip-NeRF. Figure 5 presents the same main-body results by backbone and metric, making the gap between full MZEN and the all-zoom non-MZEN baseline easier to inspect across the eight scenes.

Config 3 (Phase A) trains on the same zoomed-out images as Config 1. On average, it slightly improves performance, increasing PSNR by 0.60%, SSIM by 1.0%, GSS by 0.8%, and LSS by 1.6%, while reducing LPIPS by 2.0%. This indicates that introducing a learnable zoom scalar does not introduce noticeable noise when no zoomed-in views are present (the learned  $\xi$  deviates by  $< 5\%$  from its initial value).

After Phase C (Config 4), the learned zoom scalars differ from their dial readings by 5–15%. The deviation increases roughly in proportion to the original magnification–an effect similar to the intrinsic-drift reported for CamP (Park et al., 2023). Config 4 boosts image fidelity dramatically: PSNR rises by  $-0.3$ – $32.1\%$ , SSIM by  $-0.1$ – $51.9\%$ , GSS by  $1.1$ – $46.0\%$ , and LSS by  $0.6$ – $57.8\%$ , while LPIPS drops by  $1.1$ – $400\%$  relative to Config 1. On the real SEM scenes, the gains remain large (PSNR  $12.1$ – $32.1\%$ , SSIM  $7.5$ – $51.9\%$ , LPIPS  $21.7$ – $46.0\%$ , GSS  $32.4$ – $57.8\%$ , LSS  $55.1$ – $400.0\%$ ), confirming that MZEN can handle industrial imagery for the quality control process as well.

Config 2 (baseline trained on all zoom levels without any zoom prior) attains aggregate scores that are numerically close to Configs 1 and 3; however, it often fails qualitatively, producing severe geometric ghosting and color/texture bleeding (Fig. 6). This failure occurs because a single joint optimization can cause poses from different magnifications to collapse toward inconsistent (often overlapping) solutions: the pose solver attempts to reconcile wide-field and close-up observations without explicit cross-zoom registration, leading to misaligned camera estimates and duplicated structures. In contrast, MZEN mitigates this issue through pose priming and the staged three-phase schedule: it stabilizes the wide-field pose scaffold (Phase A), registers zoom-in views before full joint refinement (Phase B), and only then optimizes all views together (Phase C). This interpretation is further supported by the additional ablations in Appendix D, where removing Phase B or training only with zoom-in views consistently degrades performance. Per-zoom quantitative results are reported in Appendix C.

Our new metrics, GSS and LSS, act as edge- and texture-oriented complements to SSIM. Compared with SSIM, both metrics are  $2 \times$  to  $3 \times$  more sensitive to the high-frequency detail recovered by MZEN.

All experiments are conducted on the new FF-MZN benchmark. Its controlled magnifications, spanning  $1 \times$  to  $4 \times$ , expose the scale gap that breaks conventional NeRF training and therefore serve as a testbed for multi-zoom methods.



## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Computational Efficiency

Assume the application demands the detail seen in a  $4 \times$  optical zoom (the argument extends to any magnification). A naïve solution is to capture a single full-FoV image at  $4 \times$  the native linear resolution; the pixel count—and thus the ray count—grows quadratically, by  $4^2 = 16 \times$ . Training that frame, therefore, costs as much as processing sixteen native images, an impractical burden in industrial workflows.

MZEN instead records all frames at the sensor’s native resolution. To cover the same scene area as the high-resolution shot, we keep the  $1 \times$  view and take sixteen  $4 \times$  crops, each spanning  $1/16$  of the FoV. The total pixel budget becomes  $1 + 16 \times \frac{1}{16} = 2$  native frames—an 8  $\times$  reduction in memory and compute versus the naïve capture.

Large frames also exacerbate aliasing, motivating cone tracing in Mip-NeRF and Zip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021, 2023). Because each MZEN crop is already band-limited to the sensor grid, standard ray sampling is sufficient, and no cone integration is needed. The staged training schedule further reduces GPU memory: Phase B freezes the NeRF parameters and optimizes only the zoom-in poses, halving the gradient storage for that stage.

### 5.2 Constraints

In addition to the benefits of MZEN, we note several practical constraints. MZEN assumes that Phase A can bootstrap a globally consistent pose scaffold from the most zoomed-out views. This requires sufficiently many zoomed-out images with strong mutual overlap so that pose-free optimization converges to a stable metric reconstruction. If the zoomed-out subset is sparse or exhibits limited overlap, Phase A may produce inaccurate global poses, which in turn weakens Phase B because pose priming relies on a meaningful wide-field reference.

Cross-zoom registration, which we perform at the beginning of Phase B, is most reliable when the region observed in a zoomed-in image is covered by (or lies near the central area of) at least one available zoomed-out view. If many zoomed-in images focus on regions far from the centers of the captured zoomed-out views (e.g., due to stage drift or off-center targeting), the crop-and-resize surrogate in Eq. (2) can become a weaker approximation, which may degrade pose priming quality and make Phase B optimization in Eq. (3) ill-conditioned. Nevertheless, when Phase A yields a sufficiently accurate coarse radiance field, Phase B can often still recover usable zoomed-in poses by refining from the best available surrogate, even in the presence of unpaired views (Fig. 4).

Bottom: A baseline pose-free NeRF trained on all zoom levels jointly (Config 2) suffers from pose collapse: views captured at different magnifications are optimized toward inconsistent (and often overlapping) camera poses, producing severe geometric ghosting, double edges, and color/texture bleeding. Top: In contrast, MZEN (Config 4) prevents this failure by (i) bootstrapping a stable global pose scaffold from the most zoomed-out views (Phase A), (ii) priming and refining each zoom-in view from its best wide-field neighbor while freezing the radiance field (Phase B), and (iii) jointly fine-tuning all views after registration (Phase C). MZEN is not intended for extreme magnification gaps between zoom levels. Very large jumps (e.g.,  $1 \times$  to  $100 \times$ ) can significantly reduce cross-zoom visual correspondence, weakening the assumption that a zoomed-in image can be approximated by a wide-field view. In contrast, gradual zoom progressions (e.g.,  $1 \times \rightarrow 3 \times \rightarrow 5 \times \rightarrow 7 \times$ ) are more compatible with MZEN because adjacent zoom levels preserve more shared structure and enable more reliable registration. Handling larger zoom gaps may require intermediate zoom levels, hierarchical multi-stage registration, or more expressive cross-zoom matching (e.g., allowing shifted crops or learned alignment), which we leave for future work.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Directions

We introduced Multi-Zoom Enhanced NeRF (MZEN), the first framework that natively handles image sets captured at multiple optical magnifications. MZEN integrates a zoomaware camera model with cross-zoom pose registration and a three-phase training schedule that progressively stabilizes pose estimation before refining fine-scale details. This formulation enables consistent reconstruction across zoom levels without external calibration.

Experiments on industrial forward-facing datasets—including TCAD-simulated structures, real SEM images, and BLEFF objects—show that MZEN preserves global geometry while recovering sub-micron-level detail.

Promising directions for future work include relaxing the current cross-zoom matching assumption to better handle off-center zoom targets (e.g., using shifted crops or learned alignment to match zoomed-in and zoomed-out images), extending MZEN to more general capture protocols with partial overlap or larger zoom gaps via hierarchical multi-stage registration, and incorporating zoom-aware generative priors for denoising and robust reconstruction in challenging SEM settings.

We use a compact NeRF architecture, as shown in Table 3, which is designed for efficient scene reconstruction (Mildenhall et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021\). The network consists of two main input branches:

- Position Encoding Branch extracts spatial features from the 3D world coordinates of sampled points.

- View-Dependent Branch processes the camera viewing direction to predict realistic view-dependent colors.The final output consists of RGB color and density (σ), which are used for volume rendering to reconstruct the scene.

- fx,fy are the focal lengths in pixels.
- (cx,cy) is the principal point (optical center).For each pixel (i, j), the ray direction in camera space is:

$$d_{\text{cam}} = \left( \frac{i - c_x}{f_x}, \frac{j - c_y}{f_y}, 1 \right).$$

The ray direction in world coordinates is obtained using the camera rotation matrix R:

$$\mathbf{d}_{\text{world}} = R\mathbf{d}_{\text{cam}}$$

The ray origin in world space is given by the camera translation t:

$$\mathbf{O}_{\text{world}} = t$$

Thus, a ray is parameterized as:

$$\mathbf{r}(\lambda) = \mathbf{o}_{\text{world}} + \lambda \mathbf{d}_{\text{world}},$$

where λ determines the sampled depth along the ray.

### A.1.3 Extracting Inputs x and d

To generate 3D position samples along the ray, we use stratified sampling:

$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{o}_{\text{world}} + \lambda_k \mathbf{d}_{\text{world}}, \quad k = 1, \dots, N.$$

where λk are the sampled depths.

The viewing direction for each sample is:


$$d = \frac{d_{\text{world}}}{\|d_{\text{world}}\|}.$$

### A.1.4 Encoding Inputs for the Network

To enhance detail preservation, NeRF applies a positional encoding function γ before passing x and d into the network:

$$\gamma(p) = (\sin(2^0 \pi p), \cos(2^0 \pi p), \dots, \sin(2^L \pi p), \cos(2^L \pi p)), \quad (5)$$

## A.1.5 Network Processing Flow

The NeRF model processes these encoded features as follows:

- • The position encoding branch extracts scene structure from  $\mathbf{x}$ .
- • The view-dependent branch refines color based on  $\mathbf{d}$ .
- • The density branch estimates opacity  $\sigma$ .
- • The final RGB and density outputs are passed to the volume rendering module. This structured pipeline ensures efficient and accurate scene reconstruction by systematically leveraging camera pose parameters to derive NeRF inputs.

## A.2 Baseline Implementation Details

Unless stated otherwise, every baseline is trained with the same ray-sampling schedule so that performance differences stem from the learning algorithms and not from different render budgets. In particular, we use:

$$N_{\text{samples}} = 128, L_{\mathbf{x}} = 10, L_{\mathbf{d}} = 4,$$

- •  $N_{\text{samples}}$  is the number of stratified samples drawn per ray between the near and far planes  $[0, 1]$  in normalized device coordinates (NDC) space;
- •  $L_{\mathbf{x}}$  is the highest frequency of the positional encoding applied to the 3-D sample coordinates  $\mathbf{x}$  (resulting in  $3 \times (2L_{\mathbf{x}} + 1) = 63$  features in Eq. (5)); and
- •  $L_{\mathbf{d}}$  is the highest frequency of the encoding applied to the normalized viewing direction  $\mathbf{d}$  (yielding  $3 \times (2L_{\mathbf{d}} + 1) = 27$  features in Eq. (5)).

### A.2.1 NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021)

We follow the original photometric loss,  $\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}} = \|\hat{\mathbf{I}} - \mathbf{I}\|_2^2$ , where  $\mathbf{I} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W \times 3}$  is the ground-truth RGB image and  $\hat{\mathbf{I}}$  is the color rendered by the network along the camera rays.

### A.2.2 BARF (Lin et al., 2021)

We build BARF on top of the NeRF-- backbone, adding the coarse-to-fine positional-encoding schedule that BARF uses to stabilize early pose optimization. For training step  $t$  we compute a gating scalar

$$\alpha(t) = \begin{cases} 0, & t < T_s, \\ \frac{1}{2}[1 - \cos(\pi s)], & T_s \leq t \leq T_e, \\ \frac{1}{2}, & t > T_e, \end{cases} \quad s = \frac{t - T_s}{T_e - T_s},$$

where we set the start and end points to  $T_s = 0$  and specific  $T_e$  iterations (see Tables 4, 5, 6, 7). Level-0 of the positional encoding (the DC and first  $\sin / \cos$  pair) is always active;

each higher frequency band is multiplied by the current α(t). Formally, for a 1-D coordinate x, the gated encoding is

$$\gamma_{\text{BARF}}(x, t) = [x, \sin(2^0\pi x), \cos(2^0\pi x), \alpha(t) \sin(2^1\pi x), \alpha(t) \cos(2^1\pi x), \dots, \alpha(t) \sin(2^L\pi x), \alpha(t) \cos(2^L\pi x)].$$

The same gating is applied to the view-direction encoding (L***d =4). Ray sampling, learning rates, and loss weights match the NeRF-- configuration so that the only difference is the BARF-style α(t) schedule.

### A.2.3 NoPe-NeRF (Bian et al., 2023\)

We augment the NeRF-- baseline with the monocular-depth regularizer proposed in NoPe-NeRF. For each training image, a dense depth prior D*mono is extracted with the DPT-Large model (Ranftl et al., 2021). Because monocular depths are defined only up to an unknown scale and bias, we also use per-image learnable parameters *a and b and align the rendered NeRF depth *D*ˆ via an affine transform. The resulting loss is

$$\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\|\hat{\mathbf{I}} - \mathbf{I}\|_2^2}_{\text{photometric}} + \lambda \underbrace{\|\hat{D} - (aD_{\text{mono}} + b)\|_1}_{\text{depth consistency}}, \quad \lambda = 2 \times 10^{-5}.$$

The original NoPe-NeRF also includes a point-cloud term and a surface-based photometric term, both of which rely on substantial viewpoint overlap. Because the high-zoom images in FF-MZN share little or no overlap, we disable those two terms and keep only the monocular-depth regularizer. All other parameters match the NeRF-- configuration.

## A.2.4 Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021\)

Starting from the NeRF-- backbone, we replace the standard positional encoding with Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE). For each conical-frustum sample, the stratified sampler of NeRF-- returns a Gaussian footprint N (µ, diag *σ*2) in 3-D space. $$\text{IPE}_i(\mu, \sigma^2) = [e^{-2^{i-1}\sigma^2} \sin(2^i \pi \mu), e^{-2^{i-1}\sigma^2} \cos(2^i \pi \mu)], \quad i = 0, \dots, L-1,$$

### A.2.5 CamP (Park et al., 2023\)

After a pose-only warm-up of T*w iterations (see Tables 4, 5, 6, 7) using the NeRF-- loss, each camera pose **p***i =(r***i, t***i) ∈ R6 is pre-whitened by a 6*×6 zero-phase component analysis (ZCA) preconditioner **P***i:

$$\hat{\mathbf{P}}_i = \mathbf{P}_i^{\text{old}} - \eta \mathbf{P}_i \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\mathbf{p}_i^{\text{old}}),$$

with learning rate  $\eta$ . Each  $\mathbf{P}_i$  is computed once by sampling  $n_r = 256$  random 3-D points, projecting them with the current pose, and forming the covariance  $\Sigma_i = (J_i^\top J_i)/n_r + \lambda \text{diag}(J_i^\top J_i) + \mu \mathbf{I}$ , where  $J_i$  is the Jacobian of the projection function. The whitening matrix is  $\mathbf{P}_i = \Sigma_i^{-\frac{1}{2}} = \mathbf{U} \text{diag}(S^{-1/2}) \mathbf{U}^\top$  (SVD of  $\Sigma_i$ ). We use  $\lambda = 1 \times 10^{-3}$  and  $\mu = 1 \times 10^{-4}$ . All other parameters match the NeRF-- configuration.

## B Metrics—Gradient Similarity Score and Laplacian Similarity Score

We complement PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS with two detail-oriented, bounded similarity measures defined between a reference image  $\mathbf{I}$  and a reconstruction  $\hat{\mathbf{I}}$ . Both scores lie in  $[0, 1]$ , with higher values indicating better agreement.

### B. 1 Gradient Similarity Score (GSS)

GSS measures how well the edge structures in the reconstructed image align with those in the reference image by comparing image gradients, which highlight spatial intensity changes. A well-reconstructed image should preserve these gradients, ensuring sharp edges and consistent structural details.

NeRF-generated images often suffer from high-frequency noise and aliasing artifacts, leading to inconsistent texture rendering (see Fig. 3 for examples). Standard gradient-based approaches are highly noise-sensitive, potentially distorting edge assessments (He et al., 2012). To improve robustness, we apply a Gaussian-weighted gradient operator (Wang et al., 2014), reducing high-frequency noise while enhancing contour detection:

First, Gaussian smoothing is applied to suppress noise:

$$\mathbf{I}_s = \mathbf{I} * \mathbf{W}_\sigma,$$

where  $\mathbf{I}_s$  is the smoothed image,  $*$  denotes convolution, and  $\mathbf{W}_\sigma$  is the Gaussian kernel,  $W_\sigma(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2+y^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ , where  $x, y$  denote pixel coordinates in the filter window, and  $\sigma$  controls the degree of smoothing intensity. A  $5 \times 5$  Gaussian filter is applied to reduce noise artifacts and focus on true edge structures. Since convolution with a filter reduces the valid output size, we apply zero padding to maintain the original image dimensions.

The image gradients are then computed using a three-point finite difference scheme:

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_x I(m, n) &= I_s(m, n + 1) + I_s(m, n - 1) - 2I_s(m, n), \\ \nabla_y I(m, n) &= I_s(m + 1, n) + I_s(m - 1, n) - 2I_s(m, n).\end{aligned}$$

### B.2 Laplacian Similarity Score (LSS)

While GSS evaluates edge sharpness, it does not explicitly measure texture retention or curvature consistency. NeRF-generated images often lose high-frequency details due to volume rendering's averaging effects, leading to over-smoothed textures (see Fig. 3). LSS directly addresses this limitation by computing the Laplacian, which detects second-order intensity variations to quantify curvature consistency and fine texture preservation. However, the Laplacian operator focuses on pixel-wise intensity changes and is highly sensitive to local noise fluctuations. To improve the sensitivity of the Laplacian operator, we apply Gaussian smoothing, I***s = I ∗ W***σ, as done in GSS, suppressing high-frequency noise and allowing LSS to focus on meaningful texture rather than pixel-level fluctuations.

The Laplacian operator is then defined as:

$$\Delta I(m, n) = I_s(m + 1, n) + I_s(m - 1, n) + I_s(m, n + 1) + I_s(m, n - 1) - I_s(m, n).$$

Instead of using raw Laplacian values, which are highly sensitive to intensity variations, we normalize them by a scaling factor:

$$L(I(m, n)) = \frac{\Delta I(m, n)}{1 + I_S(m, n)}.$$

Here, 1+ Is(m, n) acts as an intensity-dependent normalization term. While the preceding Gaussian smoothing primarily reduces noise fluctuations, this normalization helps balance edge responses across varying intensity levels, ensuring texture-rich regions are emphasized without being dominated by extreme intensity values.

The Laplacian Similarity Score (LSS) is then defined as:

$$S_{\text{LSS}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{m,n} |L(I(m, n)) - L(\hat{I}(m, n))|}{\sum_{m,n} (|L(I(m, n))| + |L(\hat{I}(m, n))|)}.$$

This staged procedure stabilizes optimization under SEM conditions and yields sharper edges (GSS) and richer high-frequency texture (LSS) at all zoom levels compared to non-MZEN baseli nes.

# D Additional Ablation Studies

This appendix reports two additional ablations that isolate (i) the contribution of the pose priming/refinement stage (Phase B) and (ii) the benefit of multi-zoom supervision. We evaluate both ablations on the same eight datasets used in the main experimental evaluation and use BARF (Lin et al., 2021) as the backbone throughout. Unless stated otherwise, we follow the same data splits, total training budget, and hyperparameters as Config 4: MZEN in Sect. 4.3.

Skipping Phase B consistently degrades performance relative to full MZEN across all eight datasets (Table 16). Compared to this ablated variant, full MZEN (Config 4) achieves up to a PSNR increase of 9.1–33.4%, an SSIM increase of 4.0–52.0%, and an LPIPS reduction of 96.3–313.6% (lower is better). The structure-sensitive metrics also improve substantially, with GSS increasing by 16.4–85.6% and LSS increasing by 7.2–97.1%. The gains are consistent across datasets, indicating that the effect is robust to domain and capture variations.

The observed degradation is expected from both optimization and identifiability standpoints, especially for high-magnification views with a narrow field-of-view (FOV). Phase B is designed to decouple zoom-in pose estimation from radiance-field fitting by freezing the NeRF while refining zoom-in poses from a principled initialization (pose priming). When Phase B is removed, Phase C must simultaneously (i) estimate accurate zoom-in poses from an unprimed (unregistered) initialization and (ii) update the radiance field to satisfy multi-view photometric consistency across zoom levels. This coupling makes the objective more ill-conditioned: early pose errors for zoomed-in views induce large reprojection mismatches, and the radiance field can partially reduce photometric loss by absorbing these mismatches into view-dependent artifacts rather than correcting the underlying camera poses. As a result, optimization is more likely to converge to suboptimal local minima with compromised geometry and degraded texture consistency, as evidenced by drops in PSNR/SSIM and in GSS/LSS.
