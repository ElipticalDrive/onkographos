Abstract
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) methods excel at 3D reconstruction from multiple 2D
images, even those taken with unknown camera poses. However, they still miss the finedetailed structures that matter in industrial inspection, e.g., detecting sub-micron defects
on a production line or analyzing chips with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). In
these scenarios, the sensor resolution is fixed and compute budgets are tight, so the only
way to expose fine structure is to add zoomed-in images; yet, this breaks the multi-view
consistency that pose-free NeRF training relies on. We propose Multi-Zoom Enhanced
NeRF (MZEN), the first NeRF framework that natively handles multi-zoom image sets.
MZEN (i) augments the pin-hole camera model with an explicit, learnable zoom parameter that scales the focal length, and (ii) introduces a novel pose estimation strategy:
wide-field (i.e., zoomed-out) images are used first to establish a global metric frame, and
the poses of zoomed-in images are then initialized to the nearest wide-field counterpart
via a zoom-consistent crop-and-match procedure before joint refinement of both poses
and the NeRF model. Across eight forward-facing scenes—synthetic TCAD models, real
SEM of micro-structures, and BLEFF objects—MZEN consistently outperforms pose-free
baselines and even high-resolution variants, boosting PSNR by up to 32%, SSIM by 52%,
and reducing LPIPS by up to 400%. MZEN, therefore, extends NeRF to real-world factory
settings, preserving global accuracy while capturing the micron-level details essential for
industrial inspection.

1 Introduction
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have significantly advanced 3D reconstruction and novel
view synthesis by learning continuous volumetric representations from multiple 2D images
(Barron et al., 2022; Mildenhall et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2022; Yariv et al., 2021). By
leveraging differentiable volume rendering, NeRF-based methods generate high-fidelity
3D representations from sparse image inputs, enabling broad applications like microscopy,
aerial photography, medical imaging, and semiconductor inspection (Bouchard et al., 2015;
Gray et al., 2018; Rade et al., 2024; Sakamoto et al., 2022). In microscopy, NeRF offers a
way to reconstruct volumetric tissue structures and subcellular morphology from limited
optical views, aiding disease diagnosis and biological research (Sakamoto et al., 2022). In
semiconductor manufacturing, high-resolution Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) combined with NeRF can enable 3D geometric reconstruction of defects for more precise failure
analysis (Fu et al., 2024; Lee & Lee, 2014).
Despite these successes, standard NeRF methods rely on two key assumptions: (i) accurate camera poses are known or can be reliably estimated by identifying shared image
features (Mur-Artal et al., 2015; Schönberger & Frahm, 2016), and (ii) input images are
captured at similar viewing scales with sufficient overlap to enforce multi-view consistency
(Barron et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). However, both assumptions break down in many
industrial settings. Real-world datasets often arrive with unknown or highly inaccurate
camera poses, and the available imagery spans multiple zoom levels ranging from wide
overviews (for locating targets) to extreme close-ups (for texture fidelity), so that overlapping content is either tiny or entirely absent (Jeong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et
al., 2021). In microscopy, for example, pose inaccuracies arise from mechanical drift and
stage repositioning, while imaging must capture both low-magnification tissue structures
and high-magnification subcellular details (Bouchard et al., 2015). SEM imaging in semiconductor manufacturing is another example scenario where these challenges arise, as it
demands precise alignment of micrometer-scale devices with sub-micrometer or nanometer-scale defect inspections across multiple layers (Al Rahman & Mousavi, 2020; Fu et al.,
2024). A typical SEM workflow begins with a 1 × 105 magnification survey image and then
zooms to 3 × 105 to inspect suspected defects (Nakamae, 2021). These high-zoom frames
have almost no overlap with each other, and even small positional shifts can cause significantly greater pose inaccuracies compared to optical imaging since SEM imaging relies on
electron interactions (Jin & Li, 2015).
Several NeRF variants have attempted to address camera pose inaccuracies by jointly
optimizing pose estimation and scene reconstruction (Bian et al., 2023; Chng et al., 2024;
Jeong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Complementary work enhances
texture fidelity by incorporating anti-aliasing or multi-resolution features, but these methods still assume that all training images share the same zoom level, or at most have minor
zoom variations (Barron et al., 2021, 2023; Park et al., 2023). In practice, however, images
captured at high zoom levels often have minimal overlap with one another, since acquisition typically targets selective fine details rather than broad, shared context (Fig. 1), leading

Despite these challenges, incorporating combinations of frames at different zoom levels is
often necessary, since frames that capture wide overviews simultaneously with high resolution are often unavailable: cameras have a fixed pixel count, so arbitrarily high-resolution
frames cannot be captured on demand (Albanese et al., 2022). Additionally, capturing or
training on very large images is computationally prohibitive.
To address these challenges, we propose Multi-Zoom Enhanced NeRF (MZEN), illustrated in Fig. 2. Unlike existing NeRF variants that implicitly assume an identical viewing scale, MZEN introduces a zoom-consistent camera model that explicitly represents
optical magnification as a learnable scalar in the intrinsic parameters. This reformulation
allows MZEN to interpret image sets captured at different zoom levels under a physically

consistent model. To resolve the pose ambiguity caused by non-overlapping zoomed-in
frames, MZEN further incorporates a cross-zoom pose registration mechanism. Specifically, MZEN aligns each zoomed-in image with its most compatible wide-field (zoomedout) reference through a zoom-aware crop-and-match test, and then registers the poses of
the zoomed-out views to those of the zoomed-in views — a process termed pose priming.
Finally, the poses of the zoomed-in views are refined through optimization. Together, these
components form the first framework that can natively integrate multi-zoom imagery into
NeRF-based reconstruction.
In addition, MZEN adopts a structured three-phase training schedule designed to enable
pose priming and stabilize optimization:
Phase A—Global Pose Bootstrapping: optimizes NeRF parameters and camera poses
only for the most zoomed-out images, whose wide field of view (FoV) defines the global
frame.
Phase B—Zoom-in Pose Registration: uses the learned intrinsics to initialize each
zoomed-in image with the pose of its matched wide-field counterpart, refining only the local
pose and zoom parameter while freezing the NeRF weights.
Phase C—Joint Refinement: jointly optimizes all poses, zoom parameters, and NeRF
weights across every zoom level to recover fine-scale geometry and texture.
This staged design enables the proposed zoom-consistent formulation to converge reliably by avoiding direct, fully joint pose optimization across all magnifications. In particular,
it is most effective when (i) the zoomed-out subset contains sufficient overlap to yield a
stable global pose scaffold in Phase A, and (ii) each zoomed-in view retains enough shared
content with at least one available wide-field surrogate for reliable pose priming in Phase B.
Our work makes the following contributions:
● We introduce the first zoom-consistent camera model for NeRF, treating optical zoom
as a learnable intrinsic parameter rather than an external crop or scale factor.
● We develop a cross-zoom pose registration procedure that establishes geometric correspondence between wide-field and zoomed-in images via zoom-aware cropping, enabling pose-free NeRFs to operate on non-overlapping multi-zoom datasets.
● We present a stable realization of this framework through a three-phase optimization
routine that progressively aligns, registers, and refines all zoom levels.
● We introduce the Forward-Facing Multi-Zoom NeRF (FF-MZN) benchmark, comprising (i) micro-scale SEM images of MEMS devices, (ii) four Synopsys Sentaurus
TCAD test structures (Synopsys, Inc, 2025), and (iii) two scenes from the BLEFF dataset (Wang et al., 2021).
● We evaluate MZEN on the FF-MZN dataset, achieving gains of up to 32.1 % in Peak
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), 51.9% in Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)
(Wang et al., 2004), 46.0% in Gradient Similarity Score (GSS), 57.8% in Laplacian
Similarity Score (LSS), and a 400.0% reduction in Learned Perceptual Image Patch
Similarity (LPIPS) (Zhang et al., 2018) compared with pose-free NeRF baselines
(NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021), BARF (Lin et al., 2021), NoPe-NeRF (Bian et al., 2023),
Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021), and CamP (Park et al., 2023)).
MZEN’s qualitative impact is illustrated in Fig. 3, and FF-MZN will be publicly released to
foster further research on zoom-consistent NeRF reconstruction.

NeRF’s reliance on accurate camera poses makes it vulnerable when poses are unknown
or inaccurate, leading to misalignment, incorrect ray sampling, and geometric distortions
(Barron et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Traditional NeRF approaches
assume known poses, often estimated using Structure-from-Motion (SfM) techniques like
COLMAP (Schönberger & Frahm, 2016) and Simultaneous Localization and Mapping
(SLAM) (Mur-Artal et al., 2015). However, these methods struggle with low-texture regions,
repetitive structures, and degenerate camera motions, resulting in incorrect or incomplete
reconstructions (She et al., 2024; Van Vlasselaer et al., 2024). Several other works instead
jointly optimize camera parameters and NeRF representations. In particular, NeRF − −,

BARF, SC-NeRF, and NoPe-NeRF refine pose estimation while learning NeRF representations (Bian et al., 2023; Jeong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021).
NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021) jointly optimizes camera parameters and NeRF representations. However, their approach remains stable only under small (limited to approximately
20%) translation and rotation perturbations, as stated in their work. Bundle-Adjusting
NeRF (BARF) (Lin et al., 2021) refines camera poses using coarse-to-fine positional encoding scheduling, where high-frequency details are gradually introduced during training to
stabilize optimization. This prevents misalignment in camera poses in the early stages and
improves reconstruction accuracy. SC-NeRF (Jeong et al., 2021) further enhances pose
estimation by modeling lens distortions, which standard NeRF models ignore. By optimizing all camera parameters, SC-NeRF improves ray alignment, ensuring that NeRF rays
correctly correspond to real-world camera projections and reducing errors in datasets with
optical distortions. NoPe-NeRF (Bian et al., 2023) tackles large pose errors by integrating
monocular depth priors to stabilize training. It enforces depth consistency using a point
cloud loss and improves alignment through a surface-based photometric loss, making it
more robust in unconstrained camera trajectories.
While these methods improve pose estimation, none of them consider multi-zoom imaging. Zoomed-in images have minimal overlap with zoomed-out views, breaking NeRF’s
multi-view consistency assumption and causing instability, as shown in our experiments
(Sect. 4). Therefore, a framework that explicitly models zoom consistency and optimizes
pose estimation is required for stable training and accurate reconstruction across multiple
zoom levels. Our MZEN provides such a framework.
Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021), Zip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2023), and CamP (Park et
al., 2023) target high-resolution reconstruction by suppressing aliasing by training on a
multi-resolution pyramid in which every image shares the same FoV. Additionally, MipNeRF combats aliasing by rendering cone trunks instead of rays. Zip-NeRF refines this
idea for lower memory use. CamP sharpens details further through pose pre-conditioning.
However, these approaches require large, high-resolution images for the image pyramid
and rely on Structure-from-Motion (SfM)-supplied poses, which already fulfill multiview consistency. Industrial microscopes, by contrast, have fixed sensors; high-resolution
frames cannot be captured on demand, and training on gigapixel imagery is computationally
prohibitive (Albanese et al., 2022). Therefore, real datasets often mix low-magnification
context shots with tightly zoomed-in detail views whose poses are rarely consistent. In
summary, prior work is most closely related to MZEN in two directions. Pose-free NeRF
methods such as NeRF--, BARF, SC-NeRF, and NoPe-NeRF improve camera-pose optimization when poses are unknown or inaccurate, but they still assume that the input images
share a compatible viewing scale. Anti-aliased and high-resolution NeRF variants such as
Mip-NeRF, Zip-NeRF, and CamP improve rendering quality or camera conditioning, but
they rely on consistent fields of view or reliable pose initialization.
MZEN sidesteps these issues by keeping every frame at native resolution and explicitly
modeling optical zoom, eliminating the need for high-resolution images or external pose
initialization.

3 Multi-zoom Enhanced NeRF
Industrial imaging systems-including optical microscopes, digital inspection cameras, and
SEM-routinely capture the same specimen at multiple optical magnifications to get better
details. Because the zoom mechanism is mechanical, exact camera calibration is unavailable, and the specimen stage moves only a few microns—conditions under which classical Structure-from-Motion (SfM) fails (Wang et al., 2021). Standard NeRF formulations
assume fixed or small zoom-level perturbations across all images; when magnification
changes, these assumptions break down, coupling translation, scale, and focal length in an
unidentifiable way. As a result, pose-free NeRFs cannot maintain geometric consistency
across zoom levels.
To cope with this setting, MZEN introduces a zoom-aware camera model that treats
optical magnification as a learnable intrinsic parameter and a cross-zoom pose registration
scheme to align non-overlapping zoomed-in and zoomed-out views. These components are
optimized through three structured training phases which together enable stable, calibration-free multi-zoom reconstruction. Figure 2 gives an overview of the methodology
developed below: MZEN uses a zoom-aware camera model, registers poses across zoom
levels, and then refines the radiance field and camera parameters through the three-phase
training schedule.

3.2 Cross-Zoom Pose Registration
Assuming that the poses of the most zoomed-out images are reliably estimated from their
larger FoV, the main challenge arises from zoomed-in views, which have little to no overlap
with other zoomed-in images. This lack of shared features makes direct correspondencebased alignment infeasible. To bridge this gap, MZEN introduces a cross-zoom registration
mechanism which establishes geometric correspondence between zoomed-in and the most
zoomed-out images through a zoom-aware alignment process.

coarse yet physically plausible initial pose, we search the corresponding zoomed-out image
set for the view that best matches Ij after accounting for its narrower FoV. Both the intrinsic
and extrinsic parameters are then pose-primed from this best wide-field surrogate.

4 Experimental Evaluation
In this section, we first discuss the metrics used for evaluating MZEN’s NeRF reconstructions, followed by the datasets, configurations, and training setups. We then present quantitative and qualitative results demonstrating MZEN’s improvements over existing pose-free
NeRF methods on both synthetic and real forward-facing datasets.
We also conduct experiments on two 360° scenes (Lego and Hotdog) from the NeRFSynthetic dataset (Mildenhall et al., 2021) to further verify MZEN’s generalization beyond
forward-facing setups. The corresponding results are presented in Appendix G.
4.1 Metrics for NeRF Reconstruction
We use the PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS to evaluate the quality of a given image’s reconstruction. PSNR and SSIM focus on pixel-wise differences and structural similarity but struggle
to evaluate fine textures and high-frequency details, particularly in blurry NeRF reconstructions (Fei et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2022; Pambrun & Noumeir, 2015; Setiadi, 2021; Zhang et
al., 2018). To complement these limitations, LPIPS is sometimes used to assess perceptual
quality (Zhang et al., 2018). However, LPIPS relies on pre-trained deep networks (we adopt
13
Machine Learning (2026) 115:195
Page 11 of 94 195
the author-released pre-trained AlexNet backbone (Zhang et al., 2018)), making it nondeterministic and less interpretable for evaluating NeRF reconstructions.
To complement the existing metrics while addressing these limitations, we employ two
additional metrics: Gradient Similarity Score (GSS) and Laplacian Similarity Score
(LSS). GSS directly compares gradient magnitudes, making it sensitive to edge sharpness and faint spatial smearing that standard metrics overlook. LSS measures second-order
intensity variations, capturing the fidelity of high-frequency texture and surface curvature.
Implementation details are given in Appendix B.
4.2 Forward-Facing Multi-zoom NeRF Dataset
To benchmark multi-zoom reconstruction, we introduce the FF-MZN dataset, which contains eight forward-facing scenes drawn from three sources:
● TCAD-SIM: four test structures rendered in Synopsys Sentaurus TCAD (Synopsys,
Inc., 2025);
● SEM-MEMS: two real micro-electromechanical (MEMS) devices captured with a
SEM; and
● BLEFF: two objects from the Blender Forward-Facing dataset (Wang et al., 2021).
All frames are RGB forward-facing views. TCAD and SEM images have a resolution of
400 × 400 pixels, while BLEFF images are 390 × 260 pixels.
TCAD and BLEFF scenes are rendered/photographed at three optical magnifications
{1×, 2×, 4×}; SEM scenes use {1×, 2×, 3×}. For BLEFF, lower zooms (1×, 2×) are
created by bicubic down-sampling the 4× render, while the 4× view is a center crop rescaled to the native resolution. This keeps the camera extrinsics identical across zoom levels while providing realistic multi-zoom pairs. After applying this procedure to all BLEFF
scenes, we select the two whose surface texture vanishes in the 1× view but reappear in the
zoomed-in images.
This layout allows us to test whether a method can recover both global structure (from
the 1× images) and fine detail (from the > 1× images) while maintaining pose consistency.
FF-MZN therefore offers the first structured benchmark for zoom-consistent NeRF training, as it stresses pose solvers with minimal overlap between high zoomed-in images and
demands fidelity across three orders of zoom levels. Full acquisition details are provided in
Appendix F.
4.3 Evaluation Configurations
We evaluate MZEN under four experimental configurations to isolate the contribution of
each MZEN stage:
● Config 1: Training only with zoomed-out images using a baseline NeRF method without known camera poses. This serves as the primary baseline.
● Config 2: Training with all zoom levels using a baseline NeRF method without known
camera poses. In this configuration, we independently optimize the camera poses of all
images, without using any prior knowledge of the image zoom level. This serves as the
13

secondary baseline.
● Config 3: MZEN uses only zoomed-out images to perform the global-pose bootstrap.
This corresponds to Phase A of MZEN and differs from Config 1 by introducing the
zoom-aware camera formulation with a learnable per-view zoom scalar (initialized at
ξi = 1 ).
● Config 4: MZEN runs the full three-phase schedule, including pose priming and joint
fine optimization, across all zoom levels. This corresponds to the complete MZEN
framework, through Phase C.
These configurations illustrate the incremental performance improvements as each MZEN
component is applied. For Configs 3 and 4, MZEN builds upon existing pose-free NeRF
backbones (e.g., NeRF--, BARF, NoPe-NeRF, Mip-NeRF, and CamP) by (i) adopting the
zoom-consistent camera formulation and (ii) applying a structured three-phase optimization
schedule with cross-zoom pose priming and registration for the zoom-in views before joint
refinement.
4.4 Experiment Settings
For a fair comparison, we allocate an equal computational budget to each setting. Configs
1 and 3 are trained for the same number of epochs (identical forward and backward passes
per image), and likewise for Configs 2 and 4. In Config 4, we count the Phase B poseregistration passes—although the NeRF weights are frozen—to match the total calculation
cost of Config 2. Thus, each input image receives the same overall optimization effort in its
respective pair (1 vs. 3, 2 vs. 4). Epoch numbers are chosen so that both camera poses and
NeRF parameters have fully converged in all cases (empirically, training PSNR changes by
less than 2 % when training is extended).
We treat the extrinsics and the focal length as unknowns that must be learned during
training. The zoom value recorded by the camera is used only as an initial guess; it, too, is
allowed to adjust during the optimization process.
We use NeRF-- (Wang et al., 2021), BARF (Lin et al., 2021), and NoPe-NeRF (Bian et
al., 2023) for pose estimation. When using BARF, the first phase leverages BARF’s coarseto-fine encoding. When using NoPe-NeRF, depth supervision is utilized in every phase.
Mip-NeRF (Barron et al., 2021) and CamP (Park et al., 2023) assume COLMAP-initialized poses (Schönberger & Frahm, 2016), which we could not obtain on the multi-zoom
FF-MZN dataset. Accordingly, for Mip-NeRF, we keep the NeRF-- backbone but replace
the standard positional encoding with Mip-NeRF’s Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE),
and optimize camera poses and scene parameters jointly from scratch. For CamP, we begin
with the same Mip-NeRF settings, then activate CamP’s pose-preconditioning module after
a fixed number of epochs and continue training.
Each scene is uniformly randomly split into an 80 % training set and a 20 % test set.
Because the split is performed at the image level, the training subset does not necessarily
contain a perfectly paired wide-field counterpart for every zoomed-in view. This naturally
produces unpaired zoomed-in instances, consistent with the scenario illustrated in Fig. 4. To

ensure a balanced evaluation, we maintain comparable numbers of training images across
zoom levels within each scene.
Test poses are obtained by running MZEN’s pose-registration step on the held-out frames
once training is complete, mirroring the evaluation protocol of NeRF--. All metrics are then
computed between the rendered test views and the ground-truth images for every configuration. Following the NeRF-- evaluation protocol, we obtain test poses by applying MZEN’s
pose-registration step to the held-out frames after training. To reduce stochasticity in the
pose-registration/evaluation pipeline, we repeat testing with three random seeds and report
mean (± std) in Tables 1 and 2.
All models are trained on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Additional details, including
learning rates, training epochs, and network architectures, are provided in Appendix A.
4.5 Quantitative results
Tables 1 and 2 report average scores over the three zoom levels for all eight scenes in FFMZN. Across the 40 scene–metric combinations in Tables 1 and 2, Config 4 (full MZEN)
achieves the best overall performance in 39/40 cases, with the only exception occurring on
FinFET under Mip-NeRF. Figure 5 presents the same main-body results by backbone and
metric, making the gap between full MZEN and the all-zoom non-MZEN baseline easier to
inspect across the eight scenes.
Config 3 (Phase A) trains on the same zoomed-out images as Config 1. On average, it
slightly improves performance, increasing PSNR by 0.60%, SSIM by 1.0%, GSS by 0.8%,
and LSS by 1.6%, while reducing LPIPS by 2.0%. This indicates that introducing a learnable zoom scalar does not introduce noticeable noise when no zoomed-in views are present
(the learned ξ deviates by < 5% from its initial value).
After Phase C (Config 4), the learned zoom scalars differ from their dial readings by
5 − 15 %. The deviation increases roughly in proportion to the original magnification–an
effect similar to the intrinsic-drift reported for CamP (Park et al., 2023). Config 4 boosts
image fidelity dramatically: PSNR rises by −0.3–32.1 %, SSIM by −0.1–51.9 %, GSS by
1.1–46.0 %, and LSS by 0.6–57.8 %, while LPIPS drops by 1.1–400 % relative to Config
1. On the real SEM scenes, the gains remain large (PSNR 12.1–32.1 %, SSIM 7.5–51.9 %
, LPIPS 21.7–46.0 %, GSS 32.4–57.8 %, LSS 55.1–400.0 %), confirming that MZEN can
handle industrial imagery for the quality control process as well.
Config 2 (baseline trained on all zoom levels without any zoom prior) attains aggregate
scores that are numerically close to Configs 1 and 3; however, it often fails qualitatively,
producing severe geometric ghosting and color/texture bleeding (Fig. 6). This failure occurs
because a single joint optimization can cause poses from different magnifications to collapse toward inconsistent (often overlapping) solutions: the pose solver attempts to reconcile wide-field and close-up observations without explicit cross-zoom registration, leading
to misaligned camera estimates and duplicated structures. In contrast, MZEN mitigates this
issue through pose priming and the staged three-phase schedule: it stabilizes the wide-field
pose scaffold (Phase A), registers zoom-in views before full joint refinement (Phase B),
and only then optimizes all views together (Phase C). This interpretation is further supported by the additional ablations in Appendix D, where removing Phase B or training only
with zoom-in views consistently degrades performance. Per-zoom quantitative results are
reported in Appendix C.

5 Discussion
5.1 Computational Efficiency
Assume the application demands the detail seen in a 4× optical zoom (the argument extends
to any magnification). A naïve solution is to capture a single full-FoV image at 4× the
native linear resolution; the pixel count—and thus the ray count—grows quadratically,
by 42 = 16×. Training that frame, therefore, costs as much as processing sixteen native
images, an impractical burden in industrial workflows.
MZEN instead records all frames at the sensor’s native resolution. To cover the same
scene area as the high-resolution shot, we keep the 1× view and take sixteen 4× crops,
1
= 2 native
each spanning 1/16 of the FoV. The total pixel budget becomes 1 + 16 × 16
frames—an 8× reduction in memory and compute versus the naïve capture.
Large frames also exacerbate aliasing, motivating cone tracing in Mip-NeRF and ZipNeRF (Barron et al., 2021, 2023). Because each MZEN crop is already band-limited to
the sensor grid, standard ray sampling is sufficient, and no cone integration is needed. The
staged training schedule further reduces GPU memory: Phase B freezes the NeRF parameters and optimizes only the zoom-in poses, halving the gradient storage for that stage.
5.2 Constraints
In addition to the benefits of MZEN, we note several practical constraints. MZEN assumes
that Phase A can bootstrap a globally consistent pose scaffold from the most zoomed-out
views. This requires sufficiently many zoomed-out images with strong mutual overlap so
that pose-free optimization converges to a stable metric reconstruction. If the zoomed-out
subset is sparse or exhibits limited overlap, Phase A may produce inaccurate global poses,
which in turn weakens Phase B because pose priming relies on a meaningful wide-field
reference.
Cross-zoom registration, which we perform at the beginning of Phase B, is most reliable
when the region observed in a zoomed-in image is covered by (or lies near the central area
of) at least one available zoomed-out view. If many zoomed-in images focus on regions far
from the centers of the captured zoomed-out views (e.g., due to stage drift or off-center targeting), the crop-and-resize surrogate in Eq. (2) can become a weaker approximation, which
may degrade pose priming quality and make Phase B optimization in Eq. (3) ill-conditioned.
Nevertheless, when Phase A yields a sufficiently accurate coarse radiance field, Phase B can
often still recover usable zoomed-in poses by refining from the best available surrogate,
even in the presence of unpaired views (Fig. 4).

contrast, gradual zoom progressions (e.g., 1× → 3× → 5× → 7×) are more compatible
with MZEN because adjacent zoom levels preserve more shared structure and enable more
reliable registration. Handling larger zoom gaps may require intermediate zoom levels, hierarchical multi-stage registration, or more expressive cross-zoom matching (e.g., allowing
shifted crops or learned alignment), which we leave for future work.

6 Conclusion and Future Directions
We introduced Multi-Zoom Enhanced NeRF (MZEN), the first framework that natively
handles image sets captured at multiple optical magnifications. MZEN integrates a zoomaware camera model with cross-zoom pose registration and a three-phase training
schedule that progressively stabilizes pose estimation before refining fine-scale details.
This formulation enables consistent reconstruction across zoom levels without external
calibration.
Experiments on industrial forward-facing datasets—including TCAD-simulated structures, real SEM images, and BLEFF objects—show that MZEN preserves global geometry
while recovering sub-micron-level detail.
Promising directions for future work include relaxing the current cross-zoom matching
assumption to better handle off-center zoom targets (e.g., using shifted crops or learned
alignment to match zoomed-in and zoomed-out images), extending MZEN to more general
capture protocols with partial overlap or larger zoom gaps via hierarchical multi-stage registration, and incorporating zoom-aware generative priors for denoising and robust reconstruction in challenging SEM settings.

B Metrics—Gradient Similarity Score and Laplacian Similarity Score
We complement PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS with two detail-oriented, bounded similarity measures
defined between a reference image I and a reconstruction Î. Both scores lie in [0, 1], with
higher values indicating better agreement.
B. 1 Gradient Similarity Score (GSS)
GSS measures how well the edge structures in the reconstructed image align with those
in the reference image by comparing image gradients, which highlight spatial intensity
changes. A well-reconstructed image should preserve these gradients, ensuring sharp edges
and consistent structural details.
NeRF-generated images often suffer from high-frequency noise and aliasing artifacts,

B.2 Laplacian Similarity Score (LSS)
While GSS evaluates edge sharpness, it does not explicitly measure texture retention or curvature consistency. NeRF-generated images often lose high-frequency details due to volume
rendering’s averaging effects, leading to over-smoothed textures (see Fig. 3). LSS directly
addresses this limitation by computing the Laplacian, which detects second-order intensity
variations to quantify curvature consistency and fine texture preservation. However, the
Laplacian operator focuses on pixel-wise intensity changes and is highly sensitive to local
noise fluctuations. To improve the sensitivity of the Laplacian operator, we apply Gaussian
smoothing, Is = I ∗ Wσ , as done in GSS, suppressing high-frequency noise and allowing
LSS to focus on meaningful texture rather than pixel-level fluctuations.
The Laplacian operator is then defined as:
∆I(m, n) = Is (m + 1, n) + Is (m − 1, n) + Is (m, n + 1) + Is (m, n − 1) − 4Is (m, n).

This appendix reports two additional ablations that isolate (i) the contribution of the pose

D.1 Skipping Phase B: Full MZEN Without Pose Priming (Ablation 1)

we remove Phase B. Specifically, we run Phase A using only the most zoomed-out views

copying camera poses from the most similar wide-field view. Consequently, zoom-in poses

Skipping Phase B consistently degrades performance relative to full MZEN across all
eight datasets (Table 16). Compared to this ablated variant, full MZEN (Config 4) achieves
up to a PSNR increase of 9.1–33.4%, an SSIM increase of 4.0–52.0%, and an LPIPS reduction of 96.3–313.6% (lower is better). The structure-sensitive metrics also improve substantially, with GSS increasing by 16.4–85.6% and LSS increasing by 7.2–97.1%. The gains
are consistent across datasets, indicating that the effect is robust to domain and capture
variations.
The observed degradation is expected from both optimization and identifiability standpoints, especially for high-magnification views with a narrow field-of-view (FOV). Phase B
is designed to decouple zoom-in pose estimation from radiance-field fitting by freezing the
NeRF while refining zoom-in poses from a principled initialization (pose priming). When
Phase B is removed, Phase C must simultaneously (i) estimate accurate zoom-in poses
from an unprimed (unregistered) initialization and (ii) update the radiance field to satisfy
multi-view photometric consistency across zoom levels. This coupling makes the objective more ill-conditioned: early pose errors for zoomed-in views induce large reprojection
mismatches, and the radiance field can partially reduce photometric loss by absorbing these
mismatches into view-dependent artifacts rather than correcting the underlying camera
poses. As a result, optimization is more likely to converge to suboptimal local minima with
compromised geometry and degraded texture consistency, as evidenced by drops in PSNR/
SSIM and in GSS/LSS.

under the same training budget. Starting from Config 4: MZEN, we restrict training to

zoomed-in images. Concretely, we reuse the Phase C optimization settings from Config 4
(optimizer, ray sampling strategy, and iteration budget), but apply them exclusively to the
most zoomed-in views.

F Forward-Facing Multi-zoom NeRF (FF-MZN) Dataset
We introduce the Forward-Facing Multi-Zoom NeRF Dataset (FF-MZN), designed for
evaluating multi-zoom NeRF reconstructions. The dataset consists of eight distinct scene
categories, covering both real-world objects and Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)
imaging:
● SEM-MEMS (real): two micro-electromechanical-system (MEMS) devices imaged
with a scanning-electron microscope;
● TCAD-SIM (synthetic): four semiconductor test structures rendered from Synopsys
Sentaurus TCAD; and
● BLEFF (synthetic): two objects from the Blender Forward-Facing (BLEFF) dataset
(Wang et al., 2021).Every scene is provided at three optical magnifications, where
1× (survey), 2×, and 4× for TCAD/BLEFF (1×, 2×, and 3× for SEM). All frames
are RGB and forward-facing; TCAD and SEM images are 400×400 pixels and BLEFF
images are 390×260 pixels. For every scene, we sample camera extrinsics that remain
identical across zoom levels to isolate the effect of magnification.
F.1 SEM Data Acquisition
SEM images are captured on a FEI Quanta 600 FEG microscope. For two MEMS devices,
we record the triplet of each view. The zoom dial yields an approximate magnification that
serves as the initial zoom scalar for Phase A. Mechanical drift measured during the scan
remains within 1.2-degree rotation and 2.5 µm translation at 1×, providing a realistic poseerror budget.

G.1 Pose Estimation Challenges for Full Surrounding Views
Under 360° trajectories, conventional pose-free NeRF methods suffer from drift and pose
collapse due to rotational ambiguity and the absence of a fixed reference direction (Cheng et
al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). In such cases, translation and focal length parameters can easily
compensate for one another, leading to unstable optimization and degraded reconstructions.
Before recent advances, most NeRF frameworks relied on roughly known or pre-initialized camera poses, typically obtained through Structure-from-Motion (SfM) or similar
geometric preprocessing, to provide a stable starting point for optimization (Barron et al.,
2022; Lin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). LU-NeRF (Cheng et al., 2023) builds upon this
13
195 Page 84 of 94
Machine Learning (2026) 115:195

paradigm by jointly estimating scene geometry and camera poses directly from unposed
image collections. It adopts a local-to-global strategy: first training NeRFs on small “miniscenes” to recover local pose and geometry estimates, then synchronizing these into a consistent global coordinate frame before performing joint refinement.
To address the arbitrary-pose estimation problem, MZEN can be combined with existing pose-initialization frameworks to achieve stable, zoom-consistent reconstruction across
multiple magnifications. In our 360° experiments, we use the provided camera poses from
