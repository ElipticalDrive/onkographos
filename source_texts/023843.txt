Abstract
We propose a dual-path holographic laser rendering system capable of creating centimeter-scale volumetric graphics directly in physical space. The proposed system employs two synchronized optical paths, each consisting of a femtosecond laser combined with a liquid-crystal spatial light modulator, a three-dimensional beam scanning module composed of galvanometer mirrors and a varifocal lens, and an Xe-filled rendering volume. Our dual-path rendering approach, in which each optical path independently forms and cooperatively renders volumetric graphics, has been experimentally demonstrated to produce centimeter-scale volumetric graphics and animations with significantly improved brightness and voxel density. Furthermore, we developed a prototype system and successfully demonstrated it to the general public at SIGGRAPH 2024, providing interactive volumetric graphics with sufficient brightness under normal room lighting conditions, user safety, and intuitive user interaction. These comprehensive design considerations and demonstration outcomes represent a significant step toward the practical realization of laser-excited volumetric displays.
1 Introduction
Volumetric displays render graphics by generating volumetric pixels (voxels) through optical emission, scattering, or absorption [Blundell and Schwarz 2000]. They enable multiple viewers to perceive volumetric content naturally and simultaneously from 360-degree directions without the need for a head-mounted display, as these displays can directly represent the three-dimensional (3D) information of physical objects. Various methods have been proposed to address technical challenges in generating and arranging voxels with high-speed and high-density within arbitrary positions in physical space.
Among these methods, laser-based voxel activation is particularly promising for realizing volumetric displays, as it enables rendering spaces without physical wiring, high spatial resolution, and wide viewing angles. In particular, the use of lasers to render graphics in transparent gas gives users the impression that the graphics suddenly appear in an empty space without making them aware of the screen. This characteristic allows the visual content to blend into the surrounding physical environment.
Since the initial demonstration of voxel generation via ultrashort-pulse laser-excited plasma, the capabilities of laser-based volumetric displays in transparent gases have expanded. Subsequent advancements have enabled simultaneous multi-voxel rendering, physical interactivity, and color expression in approaches based on laser-excited voxels. Furthermore, the use of photophoretic trapped particles has also been explored to achieve color volumetric graphics.
Graphics example rendered by a dual-path holographic laser-excited volumetric display.
However, for these innovative laser-based volumetric displays to transition beyond laboratory demonstrations into systems suitable for interactive user experiences, several practical issues must be simultaneously addressed
—
It is necessary to display the graphics at a scale (in centimeters) that allows users to interact with it naturally, and to generate high-density voxels that can adequately fill it.
—
Graphics required to be sufficiently bright and clear even under typical indoor lighting conditions.
—
The design must guarantee safety due to the use of lasers and must allow ordinary users to approach the graphics and experience them interactively without anxiety.
In this article, we proposed a dual-path holographic laser rendering realized by two optical paths constructed with a liquid-crystal spatial light modulator (LCSLM) and a 3D laser scanner for laser-based volumetric displays. Our study aims at developing a laser-based volumetric display system explicitly designed for user-experience-oriented environments. We achieved centimeter-scale graphics with high voxel density by creating a single volume through the cooperative working of two optical paths in holographic laser rendering, as shown in Figure 1.

2 Related Works
Various approaches to 3D displays have been explored, including light-field displays that reconstruct light rays from objects [Yamaguchi and Higashida 2016; Martínez-Corral and Javidi 2018], holography that reproduce wavefronts [Yamaguchi 2016; Rabosh et al. 2023], and head-mounted displays [Solomashenko et al. 2025]. In contrast, volumetric displays render 3D graphics by generating emission or light-scattering points as voxels. By arranging these voxels to trace the shape of a target object within physical space, volumetric displays produce graphics that inherently satisfy depth perception cues and can be simultaneously viewed by multiple observers from 360-degree directions. In this section, we review related work on volumetric displays, categorized by the driving mechanism for voxel generation: electric drive, acoustic drive, and optical drive as shown in Figure 2.
2.1 Electric Drive
The electric drive type generates voxels using electrical energy, including electron-beam-based and LED-based system configurations. [Parker and Wallis 1948] introduced electron-beam systems using mechanically scanned fluorescent screens, which later evolved into rotational systems, such as the “Cathode Ray Sphere,” which utilizes multiple synchronized electron beams [Blundell et al. 1994].
In [Xie et al. 2009], a system was proposed using the rotation of a two-dimensional full-color LED array. This method rendered volumetric graphics by updating 512 display images per rotation of the LED array, without requiring synchronous operation with an external device such as an electron beam or laser. “Seelinder” utilized a cylindrical parallax barrier with multiple slits and a rotating one-dimensional LED array positioned along the slits within the barrier [Yendo et al. 2010]. By using light ray scanning when the slits and LED positions were changed, multiple viewpoints were reproduced in a 360° orientation. Another system by [Yamada et al. 2017] used the rotation of a drone equipped with eight 1D LEDs. This system features the ability to move the graphics rendering space itself, although the range of voxels that can be generated is limited to the surface of the sphere. In addition, drone shows [Hörtner et al. 2012; Sin and Jun 2023], in which large numbers of LED-equipped drones form 3D formations in open space, can also be regarded as an extension of the electric drive type of volumetric displays. Since LED-based systems require physical wiring within the rendering volume, undesired occlusion may occur in the displayed graphics. Drone-based displays are suitable for large-scale open spaces, but the image size and refresh rate are limited by the physical size and motion speed of individual drones.
2.2 Acoustic Drive
Acoustic approaches employ ultrasonic fields to levitate particles as scattering voxels in free space. [Ochiai et al. 2014] proposed “Pixie Dust,” which used an acoustic potential field generated by four speakers equipped with 285 ultrasonic transducers to supplement multiple particles with a plane, which was projected by a projector to render an image. [Hirayama et al. 2019] presented a multimodal acoustic trap display that can simultaneously present sound and tactile sensations in addition to graphics rendering using particle supplementation and scanning with ultrasonic standing waves. In acoustic drive displays, the number and arrangement of voxels are limited by the trapping force and scanning speed of the ultrasonic field.
2.3 Optical Drive
Optical methods generate voxels primarily using lasers and projectors. Unlike electron-beam methods, optical drive types do not require vacuum environments, and they avoid occlusion issues common in LED-based displays due to wiring or structural components. The optical voxel generation does not require continuous object acquisition, making graphics rendering robust to real-world disturbances.
2.3.1 Projector-based.
[Jones et al. 2007] proposed a system that combines a rotating holographic diffuser with a high-speed projector to form multiple viewpoints in 360° directions. Since the holographic diffuser can reflect light over a limited area of 1.25°, the graphics were updated by the rotational motion, enabling the generation of viewpoints according to the viewing direction. [Rakkolainen et al. 2005] developed an interactive fog screen that projects images onto a thin layer of fog. [Yagi et al. 2011] extended the method to a multi-view display that presents different graphics depending on the direction of observation based on the directional scattering properties of fog using a cylindrical fog screen and multiple projectors. Furthermore, [Barnum et al. 2010] presented a display system using high-speed projection onto water droplets falling in a straight line. These projector-based methods are particularly advantageous in their ability to render full-color graphics. The resolution and viewable range of the graphics are limited primarily by the optical characteristics and operation of the scattering medium that serves as the screen. The resolution and viewing characteristics of projector-based systems are limited by the optical and mechanical properties of the scattering screen.
2.3.2 Laser-based.
Laser-based volumetric displays enable the direct generation of voxels within a specified volume. This method offers high scalability due to its wide viewing angle, as it eliminates the need for physical wiring in the rendering volume, while also enabling the generation of high-resolution voxels with enhanced contrast. [Downing et al. 1996] initially demonstrated fluorescent voxel formation via intersecting laser beams within specially doped glasses. In this method, a glass doped with rare earth elements was used as the screen, and fluorescent voxels were generated by two-step excitation induced at the intersection of two laser beams, one at 800 nm and the other at 1550 nm, resulting in three-color volumetric graphics. In the method using fluorescent materials as a screen, it was demonstrated that a single focused laser can excite voxels [Honda et al. 1998]. Subsequently, volumetric graphics were rendered by introducing a scanning system [Miyazaki et al. 2005].
[Langhans et al. 1998] proposed a method of graphics rendering by laser irradiation on a rotating screen. [Hisatake et al. 2007] also used a rotating polymer sheet dissolved with Lanthanide. Voxels were generated by excitation on the polymer sheet with a laser of 410 nm wavelength, and the voxels were arranged in a volume using the rotational motion of the sheet and the scanning of the laser.
The generation of volumetric graphics via laser-excited plasma was initially demonstrated by [Kimura et al. 2006], successfully inducing emission voxels in air using focused ultrashort-pulse lasers. [Kumagai et al. 2015] were the first in the field of volumetric displays to employ computer-generated holograms (CGHs) with LCSLM. Phase modulation by CGH was achieved by arbitrarily designing the laser focusing pattern to improve the number of voxels per frame, and voxel intensity control [Kumagai et al. 2017] and color switching [Kumagai et al. 2018] were demonstrated. [Ochiai et al. 2016] integrated femtosecond laser-excited aerial emission voxels and CGHs to enable simultaneous multi-point creation of voxels in air and facilitate interactive volumetric content without physical barriers. To further expand the visual capabilities of these displays, [Kumagai et al. 2021] applied a drawing space separation method based on faced parabolic mirrors to the femtosecond-laser-excited aerial graphics to provide color expression. Furthermore, a recent study on the use of CGH for volumetric displays has proposed a method of constructing 3D graphics by projecting a 2D scene onto a plane perpendicular to the CGH and stacking multiple scenes on top of each other [Dorrah et al. 2023]. Besides laser-excitation approaches, [Smalley et al. 2018] achieved color volumetric graphics in air using photophoretic trapped particles illuminated by RGB lasers.
Emission voxels created by femtosecond laser excitation give users the impression that images suddenly appear in an empty space because the rendering space is a transparent gas free from physical wiring and supports. This characteristic is advantageous in that it enhances the blending of the graphics into the physical space. This study also utilizes femtosecond laser-excited emission voxels. Our system can activate voxels by remote optical addressing without physical screen structures, thereby eliminating undesired occlusion. The voxel size is on the order of the focal spot, enabling micrometer-scale rendering of fine structures. Since each voxel emits light, the generated graphics can be viewed from a wide range of directions.
2.4 Contributions of This Study
Laser-based volumetric displays have so far faced several challenges to transition from the laboratory and enable users to experience volumetric graphics, including increasing the size of graphics, higher voxel density, sufficient visibility under ambient room lighting, user safety, and interactivity. Our contributions are as follows:
—
We have developed a dual-path holographic laser rendering method and display hardware to achieve centimeter-scale and high-density volumetric graphics.
—
We achieved high-visibility graphics under ambient room lighting by selecting a transparent gaseous medium that enhances voxel emission intensity and conducting an experimental evaluation.
—
We constructed a display prototype that integrates interaction for safe system design and intuitive operation, allowing users to safely experience volumetric graphics.
The contributions of this study represent a significant step toward the practical deployment of laser-based volumetric displays, establishing foundational technology enabling volumetric content to coexist naturally with users in physical space.
3 Method
This section describes how to generate voxels using femtosecond lasers and their volumetric placement in physical space. In addition, the dual-path laser rendering approach is described, with a focus on the practical rendering method for the faced arrangement of two optical paths implemented in this study.
3.1 Femtosecond-laser-excited Voxel
In our volumetric display, localized emission points are generated as voxels by tightly focusing femtosecond laser pulses into a gaseous medium. When femtosecond pulses with sufficient peak intensity are focused into a gas, the molecules in the focusing volume are ionized based on nonlinear optical effects, and emission in the visible region is induced. The Keldysh parameter γ, which characterizes the dominant ionization mechanism [Keldysh 1965], varies depending on the peak intensity at the focal point, which is determined by both the pulse energy and the focal spot size of the optical system (see Supplementary Material S1–S3). At the pulse energies used in this study, γ ranges from approximately 2.3 at 10 µJ to 0.4 at 370 µJ, indicating a transition from multiphoton ionization at lower pulse energies to tunnel ionization at higher pulse energies.
The brightness and color of voxels depend strongly on the type of gas medium, particularly the ionization potential and emission spectrum. To achieve high brightness at low pulse energies, we employed Xe gas due to its advantageous optical properties, notably its low ionization potential energy Ip and strong emission within the entire visible spectral region. The probability of ionization in multiphoton absorption decreases rapidly as the number of photons required to reach the ionization potential increases. For a laser with a central wavelength of 1,030 nm, which has photon energy of 1.20 eV, about 12 photons are needed to ionize Xe (Ip = 12.13 eV), whereas oxygen (O) or nitrogen (N) in air may require 14 or more photons to reach their respective ionization thresholds (Ip = 13.61 eV for O2, I = 15.58 eV for N2). Gases with low ionization potential, such as Xe, ionize more easily at the same incident energy density. Therefore, Xe enables voxel generation with less energy than air and produces high-brightness emission that is visible even under ambient room lighting conditions. The quantitative evaluation of voxel luminance as a function of pulse energy is presented in Section 5.1.
The size of each voxel is determined by the focal spot size and the pulse energy. Since the laser beam is focused by a lens with a finite numerical aperture (NA), the focal volume is elongated along the optical axis relative to the lateral directions. The lateral voxel size is governed by the diffraction-limited spot diameter, while the axial extent is determined by the depth of focus, which is inversely proportional to the square of the NA. As a result, voxels exhibit an elongated shape along the optical axis. The detailed experimental characterization of the voxel shape is presented in Section 5.2.
3.2 Voxel Positioning
The voxel positioning within the rendering volume is achieved by dynamically scanning the focal point using a combination of a 3D laser scanner and CGHs. The 3D laser scanner, comprising a two-axis galvanometer scanner and a varifocal lens, provides voxel positioning at the millimeter-to-centimeter scale, primarily determining the overall shape of the rendered graphics. The CGHs enable micrometer-to-millimeter scale positioning and significantly increase the number of focal points that can be generated simultaneously. Angular positions of the two-axis galvanometer scanner mirrors (θx, θy) control the horizontal (x-axis) and vertical (y-axis) displacement of the focal spot within the rendering plane. The lateral displacement (x, y) can be described as
(x,y)=(zθx,zθy),
(1)
where z represents the axial voxel position calculated based on the combined focal lengths of the varifocal lens and the objective lens. The axial voxel position z is given by
z=fo(fv−d)fo+fv−d,
(2)
where fo and fv denote the focal lengths of the objective lens (which consists of an Fθ lens and a concave lens in our system) and the varifocal lens, respectively, and d is the fixed spacing between these two lenses. Therefore, axial voxel positioning can be controlled by adjusting the focal length fv of the varifocal lens. The actual dimensions of the rendering volume used in our system are described in the system implementation section.
The optimal design of CGH requires finding a phase distribution on the hologram plane that best approximates the target focal pattern. The weighted Fourier iterative algorithm [Leonardo et al. 2007], which is a well-established phase retrieval method, is employed to design optimized CGHs. The algorithm was chosen for its high uniformity among reconstructed focal points and its simplicity of implementation based on iterative fast Fourier transform operations. This algorithm has been used in applications requiring multi-point focusing, such as laser processing [Zhang et al. 2022]. In the algorithm, the hologram plane uh(x,y) and the reconstructed plane ur(νx,νy) are described as
ur(νx,νy)=F[uh(x,y)],uh(x,y)=ah(x,y)exp(iϕh(x,y)),
(3)
where ah(x,y) and ϕh(x,y) are the amplitude and phase distribution on the SLM, νx,νy are spatial frequencies, and F[⋅] denotes the Fourier transform, respectively. Initial ah is given as uniform values, and ϕh is formed by the inverse Fourier transform of the complex amplitude constructed with the target pattern. During each iteration step k, the reconstructed plane amplitude is constrained to the desired amplitude distribution at with the weighting function as follows:
ur(k+1)(νx,νy)=w(k+1)(νx,νy)at(νx,νy)exp(iϕr(νx,νy)),
(4)
where ϕr is given by arg(ur(k)), and the weighting function w(k+1) is iteratively updated as follows:
w(k+1)(νx,νy)=w(k)(νx,νy){at(νx,νy)|ur(k)(νx,νy)|}α,
(5)
where α is an adjustable parameter controlling convergence, in this study, α= 0.1 is used. Through several iterative calculations, an optimal ϕh is generated as a CGH by calculating arg(uh).
The physical dimensions of the local addressable volume provided by the CGH without mechanical scanning are limited by the optical system. Specifically, the maximum optically addressable range D in the horizontal direction is given by D=fλp, where f is the focal length of the lens, λ is the laser wavelength, and p is the pixel pitch of the SLM. Furthermore, considering the beam geometry with an aperture radius ra, the theoretical addressable range Z in the axial direction is given by Z=f2λpra [Kuroo and Hayasaki 2026] (see Supplementary Material S4).
The selection of the focal length f governs a tradeoff that affects both the scanner and the SLM. A longer focal length expands the overall reconstruction volume, but simultaneously increases the focal spot size and proportionally reduces the peak intensity (see Supplementary Materials S1 and S2). Therefore, the focal length must be carefully optimized based on the available laser pulse energy constraints to ensure reliable voxel excitation throughout the rendering volume.
The number of voxels per frame relative to refresh rate.
3.3 Dual-path Holographic Laser Rendering
3.3.1 The Number of Voxels.
A single rendering volume is created by combining two laser rendering optical paths with CGHs displayed on an LCSLM. In the holographic laser rendering, the maximum number of voxels per frame Nvoxel is expressed as follows:
Nvoxel=frepPfPlFr,
(6)
where frep is the laser repetition frequency, Pf is the number of simultaneously generated voxels per laser pulse, Pl is the number of parallel laser paths irradiating the rendering volume simultaneously, and the Fr is refresh rate of the display. However, voxel generation requires sufficient pulse energy E=W/frep, meaning that increasing frep also necessitates a higher average laser power W. frep is the number of laser pulses per second, and it directly contributes to increase Nvoxel. Note that voxel generation is required a high pulse energy E=W/frep, therefore an increase in average power W is also required when using high-repetition lasers. Pf is controlled through CGHs via an LCSLM. By displaying optimized CGHs on the LCSLM, multiple focal spots can be formed from each pulse, significantly increasing Pf. One of the key contributions of our method is represented by Pl, which is the number of parallel laser paths simultaneously irradiating the rendering volume. Increasing Pl effectively expands not only the graphics size but also the number of voxels that can be rendered.
We focused on implementing dual-path laser rendering (Pl=2) to demonstrate the advantages of parallel laser rendering. In the dual-path laser rendering approach, two separate laser beams independently and concurrently scan different sub-regions of the volumetric space. Figure 3 shows the relation between Nvoxel and the Fr as derived from the Equation (6). The frep was fixed at 10,000 Hz corresponding to a laser source in our system. Three different configurations, namely a single-path (Pl=1, Pf=1), single-path with holographic ten access points (Pl=1, Pf=10), and dual-path with the same access points (Pl=2, Pf=10), are indicated by the blue, green, and red lines, respectively. Compared to the single-path approach, which achieves Nvoxel = 666 at 15 Hz, introducing ten simultaneous access points per pulse substantially increases the number of voxels, and finally adopting dual-path laser rendering enhances it to Nvoxel = 13,333. The results show that the dual-path rendering approach significantly increases the achievable voxel number per frame, especially at practical refresh rates (around 15–30 Hz).
A single-path configuration with a CGH can allocate all laser energy to a single optical path. However, the input energy is bounded by the damage threshold of optical components, and increasing the number of reconstruction points raises the required spatial frequencies toward the Nyquist limit of the SLM, degrading voxel uniformity. The dual-path configuration relaxes both constraints by distributing the energy and CGH load across two paths, thereby contributing to a higher practical voxel capacity.
3.3.2 Stray Light Avoidance.
In our dual-path laser rendering system, two optical rendering paths (path 1 and path 2) are configured to face each other, sharing a common rendering volume. Since the two paths face each other, laser pulses from one path inevitably propagate into the opposing optical system after passing through the rendering volume, posing a risk of damaging optical components. This stray light, consisting of the residual laser energy that does not contribute to voxel generation, can be refocused onto optical elements such as the SLM in the opposing path, potentially exceeding their damage thresholds. To avoid this, we identified the spatial regions where stray light enters the opposing path through ray-tracing simulations, and designed the rendering trajectories of each path to avoid these regions during scanning.
Figure 4 shows the rendering method to avoid stray light. In this method, the spatial positions of points rendered by path 1 and path 2 are actively controlled to avoid overlapping regions during the rendering process. Each rendered voxel position defined by P1(x,y,z) for path 1 and P2(x,y,z) for path 2 has an associated spatial region (avoiding region) into which the opposing path must not irradiate to avoid stray light interference. If the rendering point P2(x,y,z) is positioned within a radius r from the rendering point P1(x,y,z), this point falls within the avoiding region, causing laser pulses from each path to enter the opposing optical system as harmful stray radiation. For instance, the point P2′(x,y,z) positioned at a radius r′, remains outside path 1’s avoiding region, thus eliminating the risk of stray-light-induced optical damage. Therefore, the rendering trajectory of each optical path is carefully designed to maintain positions outside each other’s avoiding regions. The pseudocode for the rendering algorithm is given in Supplementary Material S5.
The avoiding region was determined based on ray-tracing simulations using Zemax to minimize the stray laser light entering a virtual pupil at the detection plane of the opposing optical path. Figure 5(a) shows the optical setup used in the ray-tracing simulation, which includes a varifocal lens, a concave lens, a galvanometer scanner, an Fθ lens, and glass plates of the gas cell, arranged to match the actual setup. (Details of the setup are described in Section 4.) The detection plane is a virtual evaluation surface introduced for this simulation. The blue rays represent stray light propagating from path 1 toward path 2 after passing through the glass cell. The reverse case, in which stray light propagates from path 2 toward path 1, is equivalent by the principle of reversibility and is therefore omitted. Figure 5(b) shows the distribution of stray rays from path 1 arriving at the virtual pupil on the detection plane of path 2. The virtual pupil diameter was set to 10 mm, approximately matching the beam diameter of 9.6 mm used for voxel excitation. The ray distributions were simulated by varying the galvanometer scanner deflection angles of path 1 from 0 to 3 degrees along both the x and y axes while keeping the galvanometer scanner angles of path 2 fixed at 0 degrees. Due to the symmetry of the optical system, only positive deflection angles are shown in Figure 5(b). The black circle represents the virtual pupil of the detection plane, and the blue plots indicate the representative positions of incoming rays per unit area. The results demonstrated that no stray rays entered the pupil when the galvanometer scanner angles were both tilted by 3 degrees in the x and y directions. In a non-telecentric optical configuration such as our system, the size of the avoiding region shrinks as the rendered point moves further away from the origin point (x, y, z)=(0, 0, 0). Therefore, we defined the avoiding region in this study as the area within ±4 degrees along the x-axis and ±3 degrees along the y-axis of the galvanometer scanner deflection angle.

4 System Implementation
4.1 Overview
Figure 6 shows the overall optical configuration of a volumetric display with a dual-path holographic laser rendering system. The primary objective of the optical design is to generate and high-speed control multiple high-brightness voxels within a designated volumetric space, thereby enabling the rendering of centimeter-sized 3D graphics and animations. The optical path begins with the femtosecond laser, whose output beam is first split into two independent paths, designated as “path 1” and “path 2,” using a polarization beam splitter. A half-wave plate placed in front of the PBS can control the energy distribution between the two paths by rotating the linear polarization direction of the laser. Each optical path independently delivers femtosecond pulses to produce within a common volumetric rendering area filled with Xe gas. The pulses in each path pass through three main functional parts in turn: phase modulation, 3D scanning, and beam focusing and rendering volume.
An LCSLM performs phase modulation. The LCSLM modulates the incoming wavefront phase using Fourier CGHs. By spatially encoding a phase pattern into the laser beam, LCSLM enables the simultaneous generation of multiple focal points and facilitates the parallel generation of multiple voxels with each pulse.
After phase modulation, 2D focal point scanning is performed using galvanometer mirrors for two-axis (x–y) beam scanning, allowing for high-speed arrangement of the laser spots horizontally and vertically within the volumetric rendering space. Simultaneously, a varifocal lens dynamically shifts the focal point along the z-axis. The combined action of galvanometer scanning and varifocal focusing achieves 3D focal point scanning. A concave lens with a focal length of -568 mm was inserted immediately after the varifocal lens to prevent damage to the optical elements due to reflected light from the surface of the Xe-filled glass cell.
Finally, the laser pulses of each optical path are focused into an Xe gas-filled rendering volume by an Fθ lens, which achieves the formation of a voxel and precise spatial linearity over the scanning area. The two optical paths can operate independently and seamlessly overlap the rendering volume, effectively expanding the rendering volume. A detailed procedure for the optical alignment of the system is provided in Supplementary Material S6.
The implementation in this study encompasses not only an experimental optical system but also an interactive prototype system designed to safely demonstrate the display in front of a user in a typical room environment. In subsequent Sections (4.2–4.6), the specific hardware characteristics of each subsystem are described, including a light source, SLM, 3D laser scanner, rendering volume, and operating system. The design of the prototype system and the interface for interaction are also described.
4.2 Laser Source
The femtosecond laser with terawatt-class peak power is essential to generating voxels. In this research, two Yb:KGW femtosecond lasers were employed: one for experimentally evaluating the performance of the optical system, and the other for implementing the user-oriented prototype system.
For the experimental setup, we used a Yb:KGW femtosecond laser (Pharos series PH1SP-10-1M, Light Conversion), which has a central wavelength of 1030 nm and a pulse duration of less than 155 fs. The repetition frequency was set to 10 kHz, allowing for the theoretical maximum voxel generation of up to 10,000 voxels per second. The maximum pulse energy available from this laser is up to 1 mJ at an average power of 10 W. After passing through the entire optical system, each path delivered pulse energies of approximately 370 µJ after the Fθ lens, corresponding to an overall transmittance of approximately 74 %. The original beam diameter of 4.8 mm was expanded to 9.6 mm using a 2× beam expander (ZBE11, Thorlabs) to fully utilize the NA limited by the size of the galvanometer scanner mirror. The peak intensity after beam expansion is 4.4 × 1014W/cm2.
For the prototype system used in user demonstrations, we employed a higher-power Yb:KGW femtosecond laser (Pharos series PH2SP-20-1M, Light Conversion), capable of delivering maximum pulse energies up to 2 mJ. This laser proved robust enough for continuous, stable operation over a full day of interactive demonstrations under a standard air-conditioned room.
4.3 SLM
An LCSLM (IL-WU070, JVCKENWOOD) is used to increase the number of voxels generated simultaneously per laser pulse. The LCSLM modulates the phase of the incoming femtosecond laser beam, allowing for the simultaneous formation of multiple focal points within the rendering volume. This device features an active area of 1920 × 1200 pixels with a pixel pitch of 8 µm, resulting in a total active modulation area of approximately 15.4 mm × 9.6 mm. The refresh rate of the LCSLM is 60 Hz. The LCSLM achieves 0 – 2π radians phase modulation at the operational wavelength of 1,030 nm by displaying CGHs as 8-bit grayscale images. Each pixel independently modulates the local refractive index of the liquid crystal layer according to the corresponding CGH pattern, thus encoding the desired spatial phase distribution onto the reflected wavefront. To ensure efficient phase-only modulation, the input femtosecond laser beam is linearly polarized parallel to the alignment direction of the liquid crystal molecules. In our dual-path optical configuration, the LCSLM, placed in the reflective path of the PBS, is rotated by 90 degrees because of the orthogonal polarization directions between the two optical paths.
Data flow of the control system.
4.4 3D Laser Scanner
Precise positioning of femtosecond laser-excited voxels within the centimeter-scale volumetric rendering space is achieved through a combination of two optical scanning mechanisms: a galvanometer mirror pair for lateral (x-y axis) movement, and a varifocal lens for axial (z-axis) movement. A two-axis galvanometer scanner (GVS312, Thorlabs) is utilized to steer the beam horizontally (x-axis) and vertically (y-axis). Each galvanometer mirror provides angular deflection within a ±20° range, maintaining a mechanical positioning error of less than 14 µrad, which corresponds to approximately 8 µm spatial accuracy at the voxel location. For axial scanning, a varifocal lens (TL-16-40-TC, optotune) dynamically adjusts its focal length by deforming a thin polymer membrane, thus continuously translating the laser focus along the optical z-axis. The focal length of the varifocal lens varies between -555 mm and 333 mm, providing a total axial scanning depth of approximately 26 mm in the rendering volume. It is important to note that dynamic variation of the focal length for axial scanning alters the effective NA of the optical system. As the NA decreases, the focal waist becomes more elongated along the optical axis, which consequently stretches the voxel shape axially and affects its brightness. The relationship between the axial rendering positions and the corresponding voxel sizes is experimentally evaluated in Section 5.2.
An Fθ lens (FTH100-1064, Thorlabs), with a focal length of 100 mm, translates angular deflection from the galvanometer mirrors into lateral linear displacement, and converts the focal length variations of the varifocal lens into axial displacement within the rendering volume. Note that this Fθ lens is combined with a concave lens to form the objective lens, yielding a combined focal length of fo=115 mm. Ultimately, this 3D laser scanner configuration achieves a lateral scanning range of 50 mm × 50 mm and 60 mm × 60 mm at axial positions of 88.4 mm and 114.4 mm from the lens, respectively.
4.5 Rendering Volume
A 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm glass cell filled with Xe gas (750 Torr) was placed in the rendering volume to reduce the threshold energy for excitation to form voxels and increase the brightness intensity. The thickness of the side glass plates is 7 mm. In order to investigate the color change of the graphics depending on gas type, we also prepared a glass cell of the same size filled with Ne gas (750 Torr).
4.6 Control System
Synchronized control of the galvanometer scanner, varifocal lens, and finger tracking device was implemented by a dedicated control system. The CGHs displayed on the LCSLM were prepared in advance using a design software developed in Python and displayed as grayscale images.
Figure 7 shows the overview of the data flow in the control system. For controlling the galvanometer mirrors and the varifocal lens, an analog voltage-based control approach was adopted. The required analog signals were generated by a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) board (AIO-64/4/1B-USC, Y2) with 16-bit resolution. The galvanometer scanner and varifocal lens directly receive these analog signals, whose voltage values correspond precisely to spatial coordinates computed and calibrated by custom-developed software. The LCSLM, meanwhile, receives CGHs as 8-bit grayscale images via an HDMI interface from the computer, operating at a fixed refresh rate of 60 Hz. The control system operates on a standard laptop computer running Windows 11, equipped with 32 GB of RAM, to manage both pre-programmed and interactive volumetric content.
A C#-based software application was developed to render pre-programmed animations and static volumetric graphics. This software employs a calibrated mapping table that translates desired voxel positions into appropriate galvanometer deflection angles and varifocal lens focal distances, output as analog signals via the DAC board. The input data for rendering is derived from coordinate points contained within discretized two-dimensional cross-sectional layers of a 3D point cloud. The order of analog transformations within each cross-section follows the shortest scanning routes computed using Christofides’ algorithm [Christofides 1976], with additional fine-tuning performed experimentally by the operators. For interactive content, a real-time control software application was implemented in C++. This application processes real-time tracking data captured from finger interactions, converting detected finger positions into corresponding voxel coordinates within the rendering volume. The voxel positions are dynamically updated at a rate of 30 Hz, enabling users to intuitively manipulate and interactively render volumetric graphics directly within physical space.
4.7 System Prototype
Figure 8(a) shows the design illustration of our display prototype system. The prototype system dimensions, including the laser head, are 1,800 mm × 750 mm × 257 mm. The laser head and the rendering unit are placed on two connected optical tables (PBG7590A, Thorlabs). For safe operation, the rendering unit was enclosed with aluminum walls to prevent leakage of scattered laser light. Users observe the volumetric graphics through a window of a laser-cut filter (ACR-A5161R-D, KENTEK), as shown in Figure 8(b). This filter provides an optical density of 7 at 980 to 1,080 nm, attenuating the 1,030 nm laser light by a factor of 107 while transmitting visible voxel emission. This ensures that users are not directly exposed to the laser beam. Free-air operation involving close skin or eye exposure is outside the scope of this study.
Users can draw and manipulate graphics using finger movements. Finger movements are tracked by a square-shaped interface that has a motion sensor (AIRBAR133, Neonode) on the bottom side. The positions of the fingers in the interface frame are linked to the rendering positions of a path in the rendering volume.

5.1 Voxel Luminance
Figure 9 shows the voxel luminance as a function of the incident laser pulse energy. The red circles represent measured voxel luminance generated in Xe gas, whereas the blue squares indicate the luminance observed in ambient air. The voxel luminance was evaluated from images captured by a monochrome camera (CS1200-B, Shodensya) equipped with a 1× macro lens (VS-TCT1-65/S, VS Technology), positioned perpendicular to the laser propagation axis. The integrated pixel value within the voxel region was converted to luminance in cd/m2. The conversion factor was obtained by measuring a reference monitor with both a luminance meter (CS-2000, Konica Minolta) and the same monochrome camera used for voxel imaging, and comparing the corresponding readings. To maintain consistent experimental conditions, voxels generated in air were produced inside a glass cell identical in size and material to the cell containing Xe. The pulse energy was measured directly behind the Fθ lens using an optical power meter.
Voxel luminance increased with pulse energy for both Xe and air, with Xe exhibiting a significantly steeper dependence on pulse energy. The luminance in Xe surpassed that in air by approximately three orders of magnitude at pulse energies above 50 µJ. This large difference is attributed to the lower ionization potential of Xe compared to the major constituents of air (N2 and O2), which results in significantly higher ionization efficiency across both the multiphoton and tunnel ionization regimes. The slope of the Xe luminance curve changes around 50 µJ, corresponding to the Keldysh parameter γ≈1, which suggests a transition in the dominant ionization mechanism from multiphoton ionization to tunnel ionization as described in Section 3.1. Voxel luminance achieved in Xe was sufficiently intense for clear visualization under ambient room lighting. The illuminance at the experimental setup was measured to be 286 lux using a lux meter (KEW 5204, KYORITSU), which corresponds to typical indoor lighting conditions. The experimental results indicate Xe is a suitable gas medium for a rendering volume, providing sufficiently high brightness for viewing and user interaction.
5.2 Voxel Shape
Figure 10(a) shows magnified images of voxels generated in Xe as a function of pulse energy, observed from a direction perpendicular to the optical axis. The voxel shape is a crucial factor in determining the spatial resolution of the display. The red curves shown under each voxel image indicate the axial intensity profiles along the optical axis, passing through the pixel of maximum brightness. As the pulse energy increased, the axial voxel position shifted significantly toward the focusing lens. Comparing voxels at 19 µJ and 338 µJ, an axial displacement of 1.04 mm toward the lens side was observed. The voxel shape was compressed in the axial direction, resulting in an aspect ratio approaching unity. Such axial compression and positional shift are attributed to the optical Kerr effect, wherein the refractive index of the medium depends on the laser intensity I, expressed as n=n1+n2I, where n1 and n2 are the linear and nonlinear refractive indices, respectively. The nonlinear refractive index n2 of noble gases is positive and remains linearly proportional to intensity up to the ionization threshold [Wahlstrand et al. 2012]. This positive n2 further increases the wavefront curvature of the already focused beam, shifting the focal position toward the lens and compressing the voxel shape axially.
At pulse energies above 121 μJ, a secondary emission region appeared behind the main voxel, along the propagation axis. This observation suggests the occurrence of filamentation, which is based on self-focusing followed by plasma-induced defocusing. The refractive index of plasma regions is described as Np=1−ωp2/ω2, where the plasma frequency ωp is defined as ωp=e2Ne/meεo (e, electron charge; me, electron mass; εo, vacuum permittivity). The plasma-induced defocusing occurs due to the reduction in refractive index at the center of the laser focus, where the increase in electron density Ne is highest.
Figure 10(b) shows the voxel image in air and the axial intensity profiles as shown by the blue curve. Similar to Xe, the voxel positions shifted toward the lens with increasing energy. In contrast, the filamentation was significantly weaker compared to Xe. The voxel shape elongated along the optical axis, indicating weaker plasma-induced defocusing and lower multiphoton ionization efficiency in air.
Figure 11 shows the relationship between voxel size, brightness, and the axial position z of voxel generation. The z-position was defined as the relative axial displacement from the reference position of 0 mm, corresponding to a focal length of 115 mm achieved when the varifocal lens was set to infinity. Axial positions closer to the lens side are represented by negative values. The green and blue plots represent the measured axial and horizontal voxel sizes, respectively. The axial voxel size increased as the voxel generation position moved closer to the lens. The axial size ranged from a minimum of 56 µm to a maximum of 114 µm. In contrast, the horizontal voxel size remained relatively constant throughout the z-range investigated, varying only slightly between 20 µm and 26 µm. Consequently, the voxel aspect ratio (axial size divided by horizontal size) increased notably, from 9 at positions far from the lens to 27 near the lens side. The red plots indicate the corresponding voxel brightness measured at each z-position. It is observed that voxel brightness gradually decreased as the voxel position approached closer to the lens. These results are attributed primarily to a decrease in the effective NA of the optical system at positions closer to the lens. As the voxel position shifts toward the lens, the effective beam cross-section entering the Fθ lens becomes smaller, resulting in a reduced NA. The focal volume was expanded by lowering the NA, resulting in an enlarged voxel shape and reduced brightness. Furthermore, the variation in voxel shape and brightness may also be influenced by optical aberrations. In the present system, the varifocal lens is subject to gravitational deformation when oriented perpendicular to the optical table, which may introduce aberration. In addition, dynamic fluid motion within the liquid-based varifocal lens during focal length changes may introduce transient aberrations. Spherical aberration arises from laser propagation through media with different refractive indices, specifically air, the glass walls of the gas cell, and Xe gas. These aberrations not only affect the voxel shape but also reduce the photon density at the focal point, thereby decreasing the excitation efficiency of voxel generation. By controlling the phase pattern on the LCSLM to cancel the aberration-induced phase distortion at the focal point, it may be possible to suppress voxel deformation and achieve brighter and more uniform voxel luminance across the rendering volume.

5.3 Graphics Rendering
We have demonstrated applying our experimental display system to graphics rendering and user interactions. All examples of volumetric graphics were captured using a digital camera (α7 IV, Sony). Figure 12(a) shows two-dimensional images of two dogs rendered independently using path 1 and path 2, respectively. Each path successfully displayed its assigned content without damage to optical components, demonstrating the effectiveness of independent dual-path rendering. Figure 12(b) shows the capability of positioning graphics at different axial depths using the dual-path configuration. The SIGGRAPH logo and text were simultaneously displayed at different z-positions by assigning each graphic element separately to path 1 and path 2. By adjusting the camera’s focal plane, one graphics was observed in sharp focus while the other appeared blurred, confirming the separation along the depth direction. These images were captured with an exposure time of 50 ms. Figure 12(c) demonstrates volumetric rendering of a cat composed of 388 voxels. Images captured from four viewpoints clearly showed the natural change in the volumetric graphics corresponding to different viewing angles, providing depth perception. These images were extracted from a movie at 30 frames per second (fps). Figure 12(d) presents volumetric graphics simultaneously rendered by both paths. The xyz-axis markers (left side) were captured from a 30 fps movie sequence, while the double-sphere (middle) and Stanford bunny volumetric graphics (right side) were captured using an exposure time of 100 ms. These results demonstrated that dual-path rendering enables real-time, bright, and visible volumetric graphics within a centimeter-scale rendering volume. Figure 12(e) and (f) show animation examples captured as a 30 fps movie. These animations were created by sequentially rendering multiple pre-defined scanning patterns. This result indicates that our system provides dynamic visual content in real time for viewers. Overall, these experimental results demonstrated that our system successfully realized centimeter-scale volumetric graphics with sufficient brightness under ambient room lighting, accurate depth positioning, viewpoint-dependent perception, and smooth animation capability, all of which are critical for practical volumetric display applications.
We further evaluated the effectiveness of simultaneous multiple-voxel generation using CGH. Figure 13(a) shows a human-shaped volumetric graphics rendered using a single focal spot generated by path 1 without CGH. Figure 13(b) depicts the same graphics rendered using the CGH as shown in Figure 13(c). The CGH generated three simultaneous focal spots arranged within a spatial frequency range up to 1/8 the maximum spatial frequency of the LCSLM. The grayscale values in the CGH represent the phase modulation from 0 to 2π. The results demonstrated that holographic rendering using CGH increases the number of voxels in the graphics. However, Figure 13(b) exhibits more blur compared to Figure 13(a). This is because the scanning trajectory, originally optimized for single-point rendering, was used to render with three spatially separated focal points. Optimizing the scanning trajectory for multi-point rendering is expected to resolve this issue.

6 Discussion
6.1 Rendering Scalability
The scanning speed of voxel generation inherently limits the practical area for real-time rendering, as the entire volumetric frame must be completed within each refresh cycle. To further enlarge the rendering volume, improvement of the scanning speed, especially along the z direction, is crucial. Currently, the x–y scanning speed between voxels is approximately 100 µs, whereas the axial scanning speed is more than ten times slower. Due to this limitation, our current system utilizes sequential stacking of two-dimensional cross-sectional graphics to construct volumetric graphics, reducing the operational burden associated with z-scanning. Methods that convert high-speed x-y scans into z scans [Chakraborty et al. 2020] and varifocal MEMS mirrors [Pribošek et al. 2023] could dramatically enhance overall scanning speed. Improving the scanning speed, particularly along the axial direction, would enable more flexible optimization of 3D voxel positioning and scanning routes, facilitating larger and flicker-free real-time volumetric graphics.
Digital micromirror devices, including recently developed phase-modulation types, offer a promising approach for real-time multi-point beam shaping [Georgieva et al. 2022; Kilcullen et al. 2025]. However, their diffraction efficiency and damage threshold under high-intensity femtosecond laser irradiation require further investigation for application to laser-based volumetric displays.
Raster scanning using resonant scanners or polygon mirrors, which offer much higher scanning speeds than galvanometer scanners, could be one direction for increasing the number of voxels, as seen for example in laser-scanning ophthalmoscopy [Arathorn et al. 2007]. Combining such high-speed periodic scanning with rapid pulse picking using electro-optic modulators such as Pockels cells would enable efficient volumetric rendering without arbitrary point-by-point positioning.
Additionally, a further multi-path approach could substantially expand both the achievable volume size and the number of voxels. The 2 mJ femtosecond laser employed in our prototype system has the potential to extend the rendering paths from two to four, which appears feasible, thereby significantly scaling up the volumetric rendering capability while maintaining graphics quality comparable to that experimentally demonstrated.
The number of voxels per pulse achievable via CGH Pf can be expressed as Pf=ElaserTsysDCGH/Eth, where Elaser is the maximum laser pulse energy, Tsys is the optical system transmittance, DCGH is the CGH diffraction efficiency, and Eth is the threshold pulse energy required to generate viewable voxels. For example, assuming parameters of Elaser = 500 µJ, Tsys = 71 %, DCGH = 70 %, and a subjectively determined threshold of Eth = 27 µJ, 9 simultaneous voxels could be generated per optical path. Consequently, at a refresh rate Fr of 15 Hz, we estimate a maximum voxel number per frame Nvoxel of approximately 24,000 voxels according to Equation (6). Furthermore, dynamically updating CGHs to independently control each focal point during scanning is an important next step toward increasing voxel density. Recent advances in high-speed phase-only SLMs are making this approach increasingly feasible.

6.2 Display Performance
In the present system, voxel is generated by femtosecond-laser-excitation, and the emission lifetime is considered to be on the nanosecond order [Hao et al. 2005]. At this timescale, motion blur during beam scanning is not a limiting factor. However, if future approaches extend the emission lifetime to enhance voxel brightness, the effect of motion blur on graphics quality will need to be considered.
The display color in our system is limited by the inherent emission spectra of the gas medium, resulting in monochromatic white emission from Xe gas. As a preliminary investigation toward full-color volumetric display, we evaluated color rendering by substituting Ne for Xe gas in an identical glass cell. The heart-shaped graphics in Ne gas emitted a vivid red color corresponding to its characteristic emission spectrum, as shown in Figure 15. Toward extending the color gamut, femtosecond laser-excited Kr gas has been shown to exhibit distinct emission peaks at 550 nm and 580 nm, indicating the feasibility of green emission [Zhu and Fu 2002]. A blue shift of the spectral edge from 420 nm to 380 nm between the center and the axial end of the emission spot has also been observed, suggesting that CGH-based focal intensity control could potentially induce intentional spectral shifts. A comprehensive investigation of gas media, including combinations of multiple gases with different emission characteristics, remains as future work toward full-color volumetric displays.
Gas pressure is also an important parameter that can influence voxel luminance. As gas pressure increases, the molecular density within the focal volume increases, which is expected to enhance the emission intensity per voxel. In this study, experiments were conducted at a fixed pressure of 750 Torr. Optimization of gas pressure could further improve the energy efficiency and brightness of volumetric graphics
Evaluating how humans perceive laser-induced volumetric graphics is crucial for practical applications. Accordingly, developing measurement systems that account for human visual characteristics, as well as conducting comprehensive user studies to evaluate visibility, visual comfort, and interaction, constitutes another important direction.

7 Conclusion
We have proposed a dual-path holographic laser rendering technique that leverages femtosecond-laser-excited voxel emission combined with CGHs. The system was designed and developed with two synchronized optical paths, each comprising the femtosecond laser, the LCSLM, and the 3D beam scanner, enabling cooperative volumetric rendering within an Xe-filled gas cell. The relationship between pulse irradiation conditions, such as pulse energy and z-position, and voxel brightness and shapes was experimentally clarified, and display performance was evaluated. Our system successfully demonstrated bright, centimeter-scale volumetric graphics and animations visible under typical indoor lighting conditions. A prototype system was developed, integrating attributes such as centimeter-scale graphics, sufficient brightness for comfortable viewing, operational robustness, user safety, and intuitive interactive interfaces and contents. This prototype was publicly demonstrated for five consecutive days at the Emerging Technologies venue of SIGGRAPH 2024, successfully delivering volumetric graphics and interactive experiences to general users. Finally, our prototype achieved the specification as shown in Table 1. We believe this study contributes a step forward in the practical realization of laser-excited volumetric displays.
