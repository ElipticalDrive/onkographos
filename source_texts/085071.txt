Abstract
This thesis focuses on the repnsentation and volume rendering of data. With the
aid of biort hogonal wavelet transforms and multiresolution analysis, volume data are
decomposed in a set of subvolumes each of which corresponds to a different resolution
representation of the original data. Reconstruction is carried out by inverse transform
from selected wavelet coeScients. Using Frequency Domain Volume Rendering met h-
ods, the visualization of volume is carried out directly from a wavelet representation
of the data. Instead of the sinc function, this thesis adopts a scaling function based
on Coiflets to construct a 3-D interpolation filter when carrying out the frequency
domain reconstruction. Several experiments are designed to test the accuracy and
effectiveness of the proposed method. A comparison between our method and an
existing approach for frequency domain volume rendering is also presented.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Because of the on-going, rapid expansion of acquisition systems, more and more
information is obtained and stored. Text, pictures and sound are easy to understand
but readings &om instrumentation, mostly in the form of numbers, are not. How
should we interpret these numbers? What judgement should we make based on this
(often huge) amount of data?
An answer to this problem lies in the so-called scientific visualization approach a technique which has evolved rapidly and f i t f u l l y in the past decade. While there
is not a standard definition of scientific visualization, its birth a s a discipline is generally related to the 1987 report by the National Science Foundation's Advisory Panel
on Graphics, Image Processing and Workstations [l]. Although this report does not
give a definition, it introduces the terminology of Uvisualization in scientific cornputation" which can be considered a synonym for scientific visualization. In general,
scientific visualization is an effective approach to the interpretation of numeric data,
allowing researchers to observe the results of complex computations using graphical
representations.
Volume visualization is a subfield of scientific visualization. It is a method for
extracting meaningful information from data sets through the use of graphics and
imaging techniques. It comprises of the representation, manipulation and rendering of
volume data, and provides mechanisms for peering into structures and understanding
their cornplexity andfor dynamics.
Two categories of methods have been proposed: one is called indirect volume
rendering; the other direct volume rendering. Indirect volume rendering first generates
an intermediate geometrical representation of the three dimensional object and then
creates an image from that geometrical representation. Indirect volume rending is
simple, economic and easy to implement. However, no matter which geometry is
used, any representation under this category is only an approximation of the original
data. Thus inaccuracy is an inevitable drawback of indirect volume rendering.
With the development of powerful hardware, researchers soon realized that they
would be able to explore more sophisticated methods. Direct volume rendering is one
of these methods; it maps the essential content of the volumetric scalar data field onto
an intensity field displayable on the screen, without any intermediate representation.
Compared with the indirect methods, d i d volume rendering has these advantages:
0
It treats the volume as a continuous medium and separates different features
within it;
0
it highlights subtle variations throughout the volume;
0
it applies sophisticated mappings to bring out complex structures.
So, the visualization quality arising fiom direct volume rendering is much better.
Much research is going on in this field [2].
The thesis will concentrate on direct volume rendering: if no special explanation
is given, volume rendering will refer to direct volume rendering.
If we look at the literature on standard volume rendering algorithms, we realize
that the complexity of the related computations is a very important topic. Generally,
for each image, all subvolumes must be examined, and the creation of projections
from different viewing angles often requires massive recalculations.
Wavelet theory is a very useful mathematical tool developed primarily in connection with the techniques used in signal processing. It provides a unified frame for data
analysis. Wavelets allow efficient rnultiresolution data representat ion [3]; in addition,
the localized characteristics in both the spatial and the frequency domain make it
possible to reduce the storage needed in rendering. Additional merits, as explained
in [5] make wavelets an interesting approach to visualization.
This thesis applies wavelet theory to represent volume data in a multiresolution
framework. It uses a suitable way to reconstruct the volume in order to save storage,
and it adopts a freguency domain method to implement volume rendering directly
from the wavelet representation of data.
The outline of the chapters is as follows:

representations of volume data and Frequency Domain Volume Rendering.

Chapter 3 introduces wavelets and their classification; multiresolution analysis
using wavelet transforms; subband filtering and its relation to wavelet decomposition and reconstruction.

Domain Volume Rendering and Frequency Domain Volume Rendering. Frequency Domain Volume Rendering is introduced in great detail since it is used
extensively in the thesis.

volume data and for the implementation of volume rendering in the frequency
domain.

Frequency Domain Volume Rendering met hods.
0
Chapter 7 draws the conclusions and outlines future work.
Chapter 2
Previous Work
As this thesis focuses on finding a suitable wavelet representation for volume render-
ing,coupled with Multiresolution Analysis and Frequency Domain Volume Rendering,
we now outline the recent progress made in these areas and point out the problems
within each of the currentiy existing approaches.
Section 2.1 reviews the origin of wavelet theory and its application to signal processing. Section 2.2 introduces Multiresolution Analysis and its application to volume
rendering. Section 2.3 looks at the development of Frequency Domain Volume Rendering methods. Section 2.4 shows some results of the search for volume data and
software related to the wavelet description of volume data and the rendering in the

2.1
Wavelets in signal processing
Wavelet theory is a relatively recent development of applied mathematics. Proposed
by Morlet, Arens, Fourgeau and Giard in 1982, wavelets address the hierarchical de-
composition of functions. In the book "Ten Lectures on Wavelets" [13], Daubechies
gathers all aspects of wavelet theory introduced at the Computer Based Medical
Systems (CBMS) Conference in June 1990 by the Mathematics Department at the
University of Lowell. Among these topics we find: wavelet transforms as a framework to split functions into different Erequency components; wavelets as tools for
time-kequency localization; the use of continuous and discrete wavelet transforms;
orthogonal wavelets as the basis for Multiresolution Analysis; compactly supported
wavelets as a requirement for the effective aad accurate representation of fuoctions;
and, finally, how symmetry and compact support can be satisfied by extending the
basis to non-orthogonal wavelets. In addition, this book gives models of wavelet functions successfully used by several authors. We will give a self-contained presentation
of wavelets in the next chapter.
Applications of wavelets in signal processing have extensively appeared in the Literat ure for the last ten years. A survey in this area is given by Rioul and Vet terli [24]
who, toget her with the definition and properties of wavelet transforms, present applications in several fields such as image coding, non-stationary signal processing and
partial differential equations. They also mention the relation of multidimensional
subband filters to wavelet transforms.
2.2
Multiresolution Analysis
Mallat proposes the theory of Multiresolution Analysis (MRA) in [3]. He first studies the mathematical properties of an operator A= which transforms a given function
into an appropriate approximation at resolution 2j; then, based on this operator,
he shows that the difference of information contained in two approximations at adjacent resolutions 2jf1 and 2j can be extracted by decomposing the function in a
wavelet orthonomd basis. This decomposition defines a complete and orthogonal
multiresolution representation called the wavelet representation. Wavelets provide a
construction which can yield simple orthonomd bases of L2(R)whose elements have
good localization properties in both the spatial and Fourier domains. M d a t uses
the MRA theory in the context of 1-D signals and then extends it to 2-D images.
Because a physical measuring device can only measure a function at a a t e resolution, Mallat also proposes a method to implement the wavelet representation by
using a pyramidal algorithm called subband filtering. This algorithm is based on the
convolution of the original function with two filters H and G that correspond to the
high and low pass filtering respectively. He successfully applies his method to image
coding. He further applies MRA to texture discrimination and fkactal analysis. In
a successive paper [4], M d a t proves the correctness and applicability of wavelets in
the multiresolut ion description of discrete data.
Bhatia et al. [15]use H m and Daubechies compactly supported wavelets to decompose and reconstmct tomographic information. The wavelet-based represent at ion
makes it possible to reconstruct the data at multiple scales or resolutions. Bhatia et
al. obtain a complete reconstruction by combining the approximations at different
scales. This paper only deals with 2-D data.
The first description of volume data through wavelets is found in Musaki [25].
In this paper, after introducing the basic idea of wavelet transform, Muraki extends the 2-D case to 3-D by using tensor products of 1-D wavelet transform functions. Be uses a subband filtering (as Mallat does) to carry out the transforms
and adopts Battle - L e m a G orthogonal wavelets to represent volume data. Since
Battle - Lemarik wavelets have infinite support, Muraki truncates the original functions to some finite approximation and generates a corresponding finite impulse response filter. In this way Muraki obtains a unique and hierarchical volumetric shape
description of 3-D data. He applies this approach to Magnetic Resonance (MR)images. In volume reconstruction, Muraki selects the wavelet coefficients over a given
threshold and applies the inverse transform only to these coefficients. He finally
presents three different reconstructions for different thresholds. Compared with t raditional descriptions, which need a tremendous number of polygons or voxels, Muraki's
method is concise. Mwaki also indicates that the local properties of the wavelets are
useful in changing the data's resolution point by point. The disadvantage of Mwaki's
method is that the Battle - Lemarid wavelets generate a filter with infinite support;
and this does not allow the incorporation of symmetry or antisymmetry. The approximations are necessariy obtained through truncation and this may lead to the
introduction of errors in the final representat ion.
In order to overcome these drawbacks, later research by Muraki [26,271 uses
non-ort hogonal wavelets to describe volume data. Three-dimensional non-orthogonal
wavelets are constructed form a pair of spherically symmetric functions, based on
the so-called Difference of Gaussian Functions (DOG).The corresponding 3-D edge
information is manipulated by adjusting the weight parameters given to each resolution representation of the volume data, thus providing enhancements of the spatial
structures contained in the given volume data While the DOG representation simplifies the rendering process because of its symmetry, its lack of orthogonality causes
a serious problem - orthogonality being the premise of rnultiresolution analysis. The
components obtained horn the decomposition of volume data by a non-orthogonal
wavelet function are not independent of each other. The consequence of this fact
is that such decompositions are not accurate and the resulting reconstruction is not
complete.
Another application of wavelets for volume rendering is given by Guo [29]. He
uses a dyadic wavelet transform to convert the volume data into a set of subvolumes,
whose shapes provide a hierarchical description of the geometric structures of the
total volume- Reconstruction can be carried out from the information contained in
each of the subvolumes. Finally the total reconstructed volume is triangulated and
rendered. This technique does not lose much of the information contained in the
original data.
2.3
Frequency Domain Volume Rendering
We will introduce the general theory of Frequency Domain Volume Rendering (FDVR)
in Chapter 4. Although the idea behind FDVR is simple, it is always difficult to obtain
good visual results. For this reason, several optimization schemes have been devised
to improve the quality of rendered images.
The idea of FDVR comes from Dunne, Nape1 and Rutt [17]. In this paper, the
authors propose a new approach, called Fast Fourier Projection (FFP), for reproject-
ing the 3-D volume data- The FFP algorithm consists of three steps: (1) applying
the 3-D Fourier transform to the input data, yielding a 3-D array of samples in the
frequency domain; (2) extracting a 2-D slice plane through the centre of the array
data; (3) taking a 2-Dinverse Fourier transform of the slice to produce a reprojection
of the original 3-D data in a direction n o d to the plane. The theory behind FFP
is the Fourier Projection-Ske theorem which will be introduced later in this thesis.
The authors mention that the key step in FFP is the second step. In order to obtain
a slice in the kequency space, a proper interpolating function ought to be selected.
Resampling is carried out by convolving the 3-D array with a chosen interpolating
function. The authors emphasize that the shape of the function is very important:
it should be smooth and relatively flat in the middle to preserve the central image
and be near zero elsewhere to suppress the image replicas generated by the original
sampling process. Although they agree that the sinc function is a very good choice
for the interpolating function, Dunne, Napel and Rutt investigate a number of other
interpolating functions, each of which represents a compromise between computation
time and aliasing. Their final choice f d s on the trilinear function. The interpolation
of FFP, carried out on a S u n Microsystems 31260 workstation, adopts several schemes
to accelerate the rendering. The authors conclude that FFP is an efficient method
to produce 2-D density-sum reprojections from 3-D data. FFP can greatly reduce
the rendering time and can provide animation of volume data. The disadvantage of
this method is that the selected interpolating function is not a powerful filter - a fact
which may Lead to low-quality images.
Malzbender [16] implements FDVR by rendering a 2-D image taken along the line
of projection. According to the Fourier Projection-Slice theorem, the 2-D Fourier
tramform of a projection of an object at some angle 8 is a slice of the 3-D Fourier
transform of the object at the same angle 8. Thus Malzbender carries out rendering
by first applying the 3-D Fourier transform to the volume data; he then extracts
a slice which passes through the origin in the frequency domain and resamples it;
h d y , he applies the 2-D inverse Fourier transform to obtain the rendered image
from the same viewing direction in the spatial domain. By repeating this proce-
dure at different angles, different projections from arbitrary viewing directions can
be obtained. To carry out resampling, Malzbender uses the popular sinc lunction.
In addition, he proposes the following optimization steps: (1) the use of Hamming
Window function to construct a finiteextent filter; (2) the adoption of Projection on
Convex Set (POCS)techniques [20] to find functions which, while truly limited in the
Lequency domain, are of low amplitude outside the region of interest in the spatial
domain; (3) the use of some spatial preprocessing to make up for the overemphasis
of the reconstruction in the central part of the data; (4) the use of zero-padding to
control ahsing artifacts. Generally speaking, Malzbender's algorithm leads to good
visual results. By applying rendering in the frequency domain, the time complexity
is O(lV%gN), compared with 0(N3) of a spatial domain case. However, since interpolation is a time-consuming process, the actual run-time involving FDVR is still
high.
Totsuka and Levoy [30] improve upon M&bender7s algorithm by performing shading calculations in the frequency domain during slice extraction while simultaneously
introducing depth and shape cues. This latter modification has the important consequence of allowing the removd of hidden objects, thus generating more realistic
images, far away born the X-ray Like pictures of the early implementations. Totsuka
and Levoy also reduce the rendering time and save space through adaptiveiy selecting
the width of the reconstruction filter.
The fist application of wavelets in the frequency domain in the context of volume
rendering is due to Lippert and Gross [28], who first introduce the concept of 3-D separable wavelet bases. They employ orthonormal wavelets for calculating the volume
intensity integral along each ray. Instead of using the spatial form, they define and
use wavelets in frequency domain. Each local intensity integral is calculated through
a single wavelet by slicing the Fourier transform of the wavelet. The sliced wavelets
in Fourier space can be viewed as local intensity integrals of bundles of intersecting
rays. The overall volume intensity integral is obtained by superimposing the local
integrals over all wavelets intersected by the ray. Due to the similarity of the basis functions, each slice is derived from one another just by scaling and translating
one prototype associated with the mother wavelet. This can be accomplished for
each of the red, blue amd green hues. The back-projected slices are then treated as
self-similar, transparent, colored textures. By using texture maps, the rendering is
carried out by a weighted accumulation of wavelet textures. Although Lippert and
Gross combine some merits of both wavelet decompositions and Fourier domain descriptions for fast and accurate volume intensity integration, their met hod produces
only X-ray hke images and the rendering is slower than the previous FDVR rendering
met hods.

Chapter 3
The Application of Wavelets in
Multidimensional Analysis
Since its birth, wavelet theory has been regarded not only as an elegant mathematical
theory but also a useful tool which can be used in a variety of scientific fields. Gener-
ally speaking, wavelets provide a mathematical means for decomposing functions in
a hierarchical way. They allow a function to be described in terms of a coarse overall

just added everywhere.

Volume Rendering
lo order to visualize thee-dimensional data, many techniques have been developed
over the years. In contrast with the early rendering methods involving approximation of surfaces, volume rendering maps the entire three-dimensional data in twodimensional images. Volume rendering conveys more information than surface rendering in the projected images. Several volume rendering methods are described in
the literature.
Section 4.1 introduces the definition of volume rendering; Section 4.2 gives a
method to construct volume data; Section 4.3 covers two categories of volume rendering methods; Section 4.4 discusses the main theory of Frequency Domain Volume
Rendering.
4.1 Definition of volume rendering
Volume rendering is a process of creating a twu-dimensional image directly form
three-dimensional volumetric data. The data is typically a set of samples (I,y, r , v),
representing the value v of some property of the data, at location (I,y ,2). The value
v may simply be 0 or 1, where 0 indicates the background a d 1 indicates the object.
Such representation is called binary representation. The value v can also takes several
values, which represent some measurable property of the data, such as color, density.
temperature, speed or pressure.
In theory, the samples can be obtained at random locations in space. However,
data are often collected at regular spaced intervals along three orthogonal axes.
Creating volume data
4.2
Data are acquired as readings or measurements of some physical property. Sometimes,
we can only obtain 2D data out of the given objects. In order to realize volume
rendering, we must generate the volume data kom this 2-D information. One way of
doing this consists of:
obtaining a series of 2D images by means of an acquisition system;
enhancing the quality of images if necessary;
stacking up the 2D images to construct a volume data set, while p r e s e ~ n g
their alignment.
Such a volume contains the values at some discrete locations, To estimate the
value at any location in the volume, an interpolation method should be used, such as
Linear, bilinear or trilinear interpolat ion.
4.3
The methods of volume rendering
Two approaches exist in volume rendering: the Spatial Domain Volume Rendering
(SDVR) and the Frequency Domain Volume Rendering (FDVR). In this section, we
introduce the properties of these two met hods.
4.3.1
Spatial domain volume rendering
Spatid Domain Volume Rendering implements rendering direct 1y from the spatial
scalar data. All operations and calculations are in spatial space, i.e.
input and
output of the algorithm are the actual values of the properties to be showed in the
rendered results. Three schemes are used in SDVR:
Forwawd mapping schemes
These are also referred to as object-order schemes. Algorithms in this class project
voxels from object space onto screen pixels. This involves a viewing transformation
matrix to be applied to the coordinates of each voxel that is to be projected. A
famous example is back-to-front algorithm [9], which traverses the voxel array in a
sequence determined by the location of the viewpoint. The closer are the voxels to the
screen, the later they are projected. Binary classification is used to decide whet her a
voxel should be projected or not. The algorithm with a philosophy opposite to the
buck-to-front algorithm is the front-to-back approach, where the voxels are traversed
in the order of increasing distance from the image plane.
Back-to-bent method is easier to implemented, while the front-to-back method has
the advantage that once a voxel is projected onto a pixel, no other voxels projecting
on to the same pixel need to be processed, since it would be hidden by the fist voxel.
Backward mapping schemes
Backward mapping schemes are also called image-order schemes. Instead of projection a voxel &om the object space on to the image, backward mapping algorithms
operate the other way around: lor each screen pixel, they determine which voxels
project onto it. This is accomplished by some type of ray casting [lo] in which rays
are fired from a viewing point through the screen pixels towards the volume. A seach
is carried out along each ray and the values of the voxels encountered determine the
pixel color. Backward mapping schemes can be used to render any kind of object,
from opaque surfaces to transparent volumes.
Hybrid schemes
An hybrid scheme is a combination of forward and backward mapping. In [l11 such
a scheme is used to developed a technique for rendering volume that contain several
densities, such as those referring to hard and soft tissues for Computed Tomography
(CT) data.

SDVR vs FDVR
When we compare the Spatial Domain Volume Rendering (SDVR)with the Frequency
Domain Volume Rendering (FDVR), we find that the computational advantage of the
Fourier projection-slice theory in FDVR is, at least in theory, considerable. Given
N3 volume elements (voxets) in space, the cost of generating an image using SDVR
is 0 ( N 3 ) [21, 22, 231. The cost of using FDVR per image is only O(N?logN), corresponding to the complexity of the 2-D Discrete Fast Hartley Transform (DFHT)
or the Discrete Fast Fourier Transform (DFFT). For a volume of 128 x 128 x 128
voxels, volume rendering using SDVR requires 2Moperations, (1M = 1024 x 1024),
while only about 34K operations, (1K=1024),are needed in FDVR. Further merits
of FDVR include: we can limit the samples taken to the low-frequency region of the
slicing plane near the origin, reducing the complexity of both resampling and inverse
transformation stages. This, in turn, results in smaller images to be interpolated.
Also the spatial filtering operations are simple and computationdy inexpensive to
implement with a frequency domain representation [16].

Chapter 5
The Framework of WSVR
The new algorithm, called Wavelet Space Volume Rendering (WSVR), implements
frequency domain volume rendering horn a multiresolution representation of volume
data using wavelets. In this chapter, we describe WSVR in the following order: (1)
first, we propose a method to represent volume data in a multiresolution representation via biorthogonal wavelets; (2) second, we reconstruct the original data with
approximations at different resolutions; (3) third, we carry out frequency domain
rendering still using wavelets as the supporting structure.

In representing and rendering volume data, the biggest problem deals with storing the

description of the data is possible. After one pass of wavelet transform, the whole

smooth part of the volume data. So we can consider this subvolume as an approximation of the original data at a lower resolution. By applying this transform again, we
get a lower resolution representation of the volume data. Figure 5.5 show the result

N3/64 coefficients for the reconstruction.
The merit of this method lies with the fact that, usually, a volume data set
comprises mainly low fiequency components. So, after applying a wavelet transform,
most information of the volume data is represented by the coefficients corresponding
to the smooth components of the volume (such as the coefficients in S$/). We call
these coefficients "important coefficients". Most of the remaining coefficients are very
small and contribute little to the volume: we call them "trivial coefficientsn. So we
may only consider important coefficients, store this past of information only and
implement volume rendering on the basis of the smooth components of the volume
data. In this way we save a lot of storage; the reconstructed volume is based on a
reduced data set, representing a coarse description of the original data.
Using FDVR on such a reduced volume, we obtain images of acceptable quality
while saving on rendering time.
