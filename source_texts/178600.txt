# Abstract

Direct illumination from non-trivial emitters with occluding housing, high-polygon emissive meshes, spatially varying emission, or deformable assemblies is a persistent bottleneck in real-time rendering. Current real-time solutions either rely on simplified analytical representations or fall back to runtime sampling. Analytical methods are restricted to simple emitter geometry, pure sampling-based estimators need high sampling budgets to be noise-free, and proxy representations still require runtime integration over outgoing radiance around the emitter. We present Neural Emission Fields (NEF), a representation that eliminates runtime integration by precomputing the illumination in the volume around the emitter. This neural field is parameterized by position, normal, view direction, and material parameters. Using a two-headed diffuse/glossy architecture, it yields noise-free unoccluded direct illumination from a single network evaluation per shading point. Because training is performed in the emitter's local frame, a trained NEF acts as a portable lighting asset reusable across scenes under rigid transforms. Internal interreflections, self-occlusion, spatially varying emission, and deformations are absorbed into the learned representation at zero additional runtime cost.

# 1. Introduction

Direct illumination is a major cost in real-time rendering pipelines, especially when emitters are non-trivial—luminaires with housing geometry that occludes the internal filament, emissive meshes with thousands of polygons, light sources with spatially varying or textured emission profiles, or deformable emitters that change shape over time. While path tracing [Kaj86] handles all of these correctly, converging to noise-free direct illumination from non-trivial emitters is incompatible with real-time rendering budgets. Existing alternatives that do meet those rendering budgets typically compromise on generality, accuracy, or noise-free output.

Analytical methods like Linearly Transformed Cosines (LTC) [HDHN16] are extremely fast but assume 2D polygonal emitters and scale linearly with the emitter's vertex count. For more general emitters, Monte Carlo estimators of direct illumination, including reservoir methods [BWP∗ 20], support arbitrary emitters but produce noise that must be removed by a denoiser, which often introduces unwanted temporal and spatial artifacts. Proxy-based representations [HKSS98, VADWG15], including recent neural variants [ZBX∗ 21], sidestep both limitations by precomputing the outgoing radiance into a 4D near-field proxy, factoring out the cost of sampling paths within the emitter assembly. However, receiver points must still integrate over this proxy at runtime.

Our key insight is that this remaining runtime integration is avoidable. Rather than precomputing only the radiance leaving an emitter (which still needs to be integrated), we precompute the preintegrated outgoing direct illumination with respect to a receiver point (any shaded surface point in the volume around the emitter). We represent this as a neural field parameterized by the receiver's position, normal, view direction, and material parameters, learned by a neural network conditioned on a material model. By shifting the integration to a one-time precomputation phase, a single network evaluation replaces the entire integral over the (proxy) emitter.

Because the network is trained in the emitter's local coordinate frame, the resulting model functions as a portable lighting asset. World-space queries are mapped into the emitter's frame at shading time rather than baked into the network weights, so a trained NEF can be translated, rotated, and uniformly scaled at runtime without retraining. A single training run therefore amortizes across every scene and every instance in which the emitter is placed—much like a baked texture or precomputed BRDF.

This design facilitates several critical performance advantages over traditional methods. Inference cost is fundamentally decoupled from the geometric complexity of the emitter; the computational cost is determined by the network's size rather than the polygon count of the emitter. Furthermore, because the receiver's BRDF is incorporated into the learning objective, complex emissive effects are absorbed into the representation at no additional runtime cost. Finally, since the training phase relies solely on path-traced radiance estimates for training data, the same training procedure is used for simple planar polygons, complex emissive meshes, and deformable emitter assemblies.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

- A reformulation of direct illumination as a pre-integrated, receiver-aware quantity, eliminating the runtime integration retained by prior analytical, sampling-based, and proxy-based methods.
- A neural emission field employing a two-headed diffuse/glossy architecture and reflected-direction encoding, yielding noise-free direct illumination and accurate specular highlights from a single network evaluation per shading point.
- Scene-independent training in the emitter's local coordinate frame, so a single pre-trained NEF can be translated, rotated and scaled across different scenes without retraining.
- Extensions to complex emissive assemblies with internal interreflections, spatially varying emission, and deformable emitters which are non-trivial or unsupported in state-of-the-art real-time methods.

# 2. Related Work

We categorize previous work into three primary categories: analytical solutions, Monte Carlo estimators, and proxy-based representations. Finally, we discuss the broader context of using neural networks in the context of rendering.

Analytical Solutions. Analytical closed-form solutions for simple emitter geometries (e.g., planar polygons) exist for various receiver types, including diffuse [BRW89] or glossy receivers like the Phong distribution [Arv95], and more general glossy reflections [HDHN16, KYM22, LDSM16]. Analytical methods are extremely efficient for low-polygon emitters, but their cost scales linearly with the number of emitter vertices [Arv95], making them prohibitively expensive for high-polygon emitters in real-time applications. Furthermore, even on supported geometry they diverge from a path-traced reference when receivers are glossy or emission is non-uniform [HDHN16]. Our neural approach achieves more accurate shading with respect to a ground truth solution, natively supports non-convex and non-planar emitters, and decouples cost from the emitter geometry, drastically reducing shading time for complex emissive meshes.

Many-Light Sampling. Monte Carlo integration supports estimating direct illumination from any emitter but requires specialized sampling strategies to manage runtime noise. Early techniques addressed the many-lights problem by ranking lights according to their potential contribution [War94] or by classifying them as important or unimportant to guide sampling [SWZ96]. In complex scenes, hierarchical representations [WFA∗ 05, MPC19, Yuk20] can be used to enable emitter sampling at sublinear cost. More recently, ReSTIR [BWP∗ 20] introduced spatiotemporal reservoir resampling, achieving high-quality direct illumination under tight sampling budgets. Despite these advancements, the residual variance inherent to all Monte Carlo approaches typically necessitates a denoiser. In contrast, NEF avoids runtime sampling entirely, shifting the computational burden to a (one-time) per-emitter offline training phase.

Classical Pre-integration. Precomputation in rendering [Ram09] trades offline computations for runtime cost by tabulating light transport over a discretised parameter domain. Precomputed radiance transfer (PRT) [SKS02] projects the transfer function at each surface point onto a low-order spherical harmonics basis, reducing shading under distant lighting to a dot product. All-frequency variants require non-linear wavelet approximations to keep storage tractable [NRH03]. More fundamentally, PRT relies on the distant-lighting assumption, which breaks down for near-field emitters whose incident lighting varies with receiver position.

A complementary line of work stores lighting in a volume rather than on surfaces. Irradiance volumes [GSHG98] and their dynamic, ray-traced successors [MGNM19] cache irradiance at probes on a grid and interpolate between them at runtime. For strongly spatially varying signals, such as the near field of a complex emitter, these caches require careful probe placement [SL17, WKKN19] and fine discretisation to avoid interpolation and light-leaking artifacts.

These approaches share a common limitation: the storage of a tabulated representation grows exponentially with the dimensionality of the signal. Pre-integrated direct illumination around a nearfield emitter depends on receiver position (3D), normal (2D), and reflected direction (2D), which is already seven dimensions for a single glossy material. Conditioning on roughness to cover a continuous family of materials, as done in this paper, adds an eighth parameter. A regular grid with only 32 samples per dimension would already require 328 ≈ 1012 entries. Adding a time parameter for dynamic emitters worsens this further: a naive per-keyframe cache scales linearly with the number of keyframes, and complex temporal variation demands many of them. Like prior neural precomputation methods [RBRD22, CND∗ 26], NEF sidesteps this curse of dimensionality by fitting a compact, global representation that efficiently captures correlations in the high-dimensional signal.

Pre-integrated Emitters. Estimating the radiance emitted by complex light sources is non-trivial, particularly when light paths are obstructed by housing geometry or undergo multiple internal reflections. Early research tackled this by replacing the emitter with a 4D proxy geometry. Canned light sources represent the radiance leaving the emitter's bounding surface as a 4D light field slab so that scene points can be illuminated by ray-tracing against the proxy rather than the intricate original geometry [HKSS98]. Later work decoupled a luminaire's dual roles in rendering: a radiance volume captures its appearance when viewed directly, while virtual point lights approximate how it illuminates the surrounding scene [VADWG15]. Neural variants [ZBX∗ 21] reduced the storage requirements of these tabulated representations and combined it with a learned importance sampling distribution. Subsequent work learned radiance-field representations of the luminaire for faster evaluation [CJ22], or relied on wavelet-compressed light fields for fast per-ray reconstruction and hierarchical importance sampling [AK25].

Despite these advances, these methods still treat the proxy as an emitter that must be integrated at runtime. Each shading point integrates the product of the radiance sampled from the proxy's 4D near-field and the material evaluation. In contrast, NEF preintegrates this contribution offline, storing the full outgoing preintegrated direct illumination to be queried at runtime.

Rendering with Neural Networks. A common use of neural networks in rendering is capturing the radiance distribution during a training phase which amortizes over subsequent viewings of the scene. Early work anchored a learned radiance representation to scene geometry for interactive viewing from arbitrary directions [RWG∗ 13]. Modern variants extend this idea to high-quality static scenes [HCZ21], rotatable environment lighting [RBRD22], scene animations [CDD∗ 24], variable scene configurations [DPD22, SDR∗ 24], and volumetric irradiance caching [CND∗ 26]. These methods all rely on offline, scenespecific training. Alternatively, a small neural representation can be trained online as a radiance cache [MRNK21] with the additional runtime cost of ray tracing and denoising. NEF differs in that it is an offline method whose representation is anchored in the emitter's local 3D extent rather than on the 2D scene manifold, making it a portable, reusable asset across scenes.

### 3. Neural Pre-integrated Emitters

Prior proxy methods reduce the runtime cost of internal emitter transport but still leave the receiver-side integral to be evaluated per shading point. We close this remaining gap by learning the full pre-integrated direct illumination as a neural field around the emitter. Section 3.1 derives this formulation; Section 3.2 defines the emitter-local frame that makes the representation portable; Section 3.3 introduces the diffuse/glossy decomposition; Section 3.4 describes training; and Section 3.5 outlines rendering.

#### 3.1. Pre-integrated Emitter

Computing the direct illumination Lo with respect to a complex emitter involves two layers of integration: the recursive internal light transport inside the emitter, and the receiver's shading integral over the hemisphere. Proxy-emitter methods [HKSS98, VADWG15, ZBX∗ 21] already peel away the inner layer by precomputing internal transport into a function P defined on a bounding surface. The receiver, however, must still integrate against P at runtime. We take this idea one step further by absorbing the receiver's direct illumination integral into the learned field *D*θ itself:

where (x,n,ω*o*) defines the world-space position, normal and outgoing direction at an intersection point, (x˜,n˜,ω˜ o) are their counterparts expressed in the emitter's local frame (Section 3.2\), ω*i* is the incident direction, t(x,ω*i*) returns the point where the ray from x in direction ω*i* intersects the proxy surface, α are the material parameters for the BRDF fr, (ω*i* · n) is the cosine foreshortening term, and Ω denotes the hemispherical integration domain.

Crucially, a pre-integrated quantity is not a property of the emitter alone. Since the BRDF fr appears inside the direct illumination integral, different receivers will observe different values of D*θ at the same (*x,n). The receiver's shading model is therefore part of what is being learned. We instantiate this using the GGX microfacet model [WMLT07] over a roughness range of α ∈ [0.1,1]. By exposing α as input to the network, a single trained NEF represents the full roughness range for that material. While we restrict our scope to GGX in this work, our approach is generic and can be extended to other materials, such as Disney or layered BRDFs, by extending the conditioning inputs and the training distribution.

At runtime, evaluating *D*θ requires only a single network inference at the shading point. This eliminates the need for any sampling of the proxy P, importance sampling, and denoising.

Because *D*θ is agnostic to the source of the radiance field, all the complexity that would otherwise have to be handled at runtime is shifted to the offline solver and baked into the network weights. This includes complex effects such as self-occlusion, multi-bounce transport and non-uniform emission (Section 4.2\)—effects that often violate the assumptions made by analytical methods such as LTC [HDHN16]. As a result, *D*θ can stand in for a path-traced estimate of direct illumination from an emitter, provided the field can be trained to faithfully represent it.

Pre-integrated direct illumination is only physically meaningful within a bounded region around the emitter. Emitted radiance falls off with distance, and beyond a certain range an emitter's contribution becomes negligible. We therefore define the emitter-space as an axis-aligned bounding region in the local frame, sized to extend 20× the emitter's largest extent on each axis—well beyond the 5× threshold at which the inverse-square law becomes an acceptable approximation for emitters [Rea00]. Shading points inside this region are evaluated by a direct query of *D*θ, which is sufficient for all emitters in this work.

Analytical Fall-off. A simple heuristic to deal with points outside of the emitter-space, consists of clamping outside queries to the nearest boundary point and scaling the resulting shading value by (*d*boundary/*d*actual) 2 , where *d*boundary is the distance from the emitter center to the clamped boundary point and *d*actual is the true distance from the emitter center to the shading point. As shown in Figure 3, this fall-off approach generates a result that is hard to visually distinguish from the ground truth reference. Alternatively, the emitter-space can be enlarged at training time to avoid the heuristic, potentially requiring a larger representation to get the desired level of accuracy everywhere in the emitter-space.

Scene-independent placement. Anchoring the representation to the emitter's local frame yields two practical benefits: sceneindependence and free rigid placement at runtime. The trained NEF does not store scene-specific information, so the same parameters θ are reusable across scenes. At runtime, world-space shading configurations are mapped through M −1 before being evaluated by D*θ. Since *M is applied at shading time rather than baked into the network weights, translating or rotating the emitter is essentially free: it only changes the transformation applied to each query, not the representation itself. For emitters that are only expected to see diffuse surfaces at runtime, the glossy head f θ g can be omitted during training (and during shading).

All network inputs are normalized to [0,1] 3 , and a learned multiresolution hash encoding [MESK22] is applied to both the position x˜ and the reflected direction r(ω˜ o,n˜). Encoding the reflected direction is critical for capturing the sharp highlights and complex reflections characteristic of low-roughness receiver geometry (see supplemental material), consistent with findings in the field of novel view synthesis [VHM∗ 22]. Both the glossy and diffuse head use a 4-layer MLP of width 128 with ReLU activations. More details on the network architecture can be found in the supplemental material.

#### 3.4. Training

A schematic of the training procedure is shown in Figure 2. Training a NEF only requires a reliable estimate of the outgoing radiance Lo for any query within the network's input domain, making the procedure agnostic to the underlying emitter geometry.

Sample generation. For each training batch, we draw queries uniformly from the emitter-space (Section 3.2\). For the glossy head f θ g , we sample a position ˜x uniformly in the local bounding box, and a normal ˜n such that the emitter is visible in its hemisphere avoiding queries with zero radiance, an outgoing direction ω˜ o uniformly on the upper hemisphere defined by ˜n, and a roughness α uniformly in [0.1,1]. The diffuse head f θ d uses the same procedure restricted to (x˜,n˜). For deformable or animated emitters, we additionally sample a time parameter t ∈ [0,1] corresponding to the emitter's state, encoded with an 8-band Fourier encoding [MST∗ 20] and provided as an extra conditioning input.

Radiance targets. Target radiance is obtained via offline path tracing against the (possibly time-varying) emitter geometry. Bare emissive surfaces are handled with standard next-event estimation; emitters with non-emissive housing geometry that causes selfocclusion or internal interreflections (Figure 6\) are handled with full path tracing inside the emitter assembly, so that higher-order transport is naturally absorbed into the training signal. We use 64 samples per radiance estimate as default across all emitters in this paper as we saw diminishing returns beyond this (see supplemental material for an ablation). Targets are generated on-the-fly rather than cached, so all reported training timings include the cost of path tracing.

Optimization. We minimize a relative L*2 loss [MRNK21] between the network prediction and the path-traced target averaged over the batch, L(θ) = (*D*θ−⟨*Lo⟩) sg(*D*θ) 2+ϵ , where ϵ = 0.01 and sg denotes a stop gradient operation. The relative loss prevents bright regions of the emitter-space from dominating the gradient and helps the multiresolution hash encoding to allocate capacity uniformly across the field. We use Adam [KB14] with a learning rate of 10−3 , a batch size of 216, and use cosine annealing learning rate decay. The number of training iterations for each of our emitter types can be found in the supplemental material. Total wall-clock time on an RTX 4090 ranges from one minute for diffuse receiver with planar emitters to three hours for emitters with complex emissive effects, dominated by the cost of generating path-traced target values.

#### 3.5. Rendering

Because NEF stores a pre-integrated estimate of the outgoing radiance reflected by the receiver, no integration is required at runtime. Unlike methods that integrate with respect to the emitter geometry or to a proxy representation of the emitter [HKSS98, VADWG15, ZBX∗ 21], our approach reduces the shading process to a single network evaluation per pixel. At render time, we first perform a G-buffer pass to obtain the (x,n,ω*o*,α) channels at each shading point. These are transformed into the emitter's local frame via its current world transform M (Section 3.2\), encoded, and fed to *D*θ to obtain the outgoing radiance contribution pre-integrated with respect to the emitter.

Visibility. As with existing analytical methods [HDHN16, BRW89], NEF does not inherently capture occlusions of the emissive field caused by the surrounding scene. For simple emitters, a single shadow ray toward the emitter center is often sufficient (Figure 5\); for soft shadows, established techniques such as shadow mapping [Wil78] or a small ray-tracing budget combined with a denoiser [HHM18] apply directly, which we implemented in the supplemental material. Crucially, shadow rays are only required against non-emitter scene geometry: the NEF itself bakes in self-occlusion and internal interreflections within the emitter assembly (Figure 6\).

### 5. Discussion

Visibility and soft shadows. Similarly to analytical methods and proxy emitters, NEF only captures the unoccluded emission field. In practice, this is well-addressed by combining NEF shading with a small budget of path-traced visibility samples and a denoiser that are used as a ratio estimator [HHM18]. This approach yields plausible soft shadows while preserving the noise-free direct illumination produced by the NEF. Even 1 shadow ray per pixel yields perceptually accurate soft shadows (see Figure 10\), with further samples producing diminishing returns. This makes NEF compatible with standard real-time visibility pipelines at minimal additional cost.

Storage and training. All results in this paper were generated using a single NEF architecture totaling 15 MB (or 30 MB for the glossy variant) per emitter. For simpler emitters, this is often excessive and can be reduced (see supplemental). Training is a one-time offline cost, ranging from approximately one minute for simple planar emitters to three hours for emitters with internal interreflections, depending primarily on the cost of generating path-traced training samples. Crucially, this cost is incurred once per emitter and can be amortized across multiple scenes.

We see two avenues to mitigate this. First, a shared decoding MLP could learn to disentangle latents from multiple emitters; however a naive sumthen-decode scheme retains a linear cost for the latent fetching. Second, future work could combine NEF with lighting hierarchies [WFA∗ 05, MPC19] to achieve sub-linear scaling.

Sampling training data. We generate training targets by path tracing the emitter assembly with next-event estimation. For emitters with housing geometry more intricate than those presented in this work—heavily enclosing the emissive surface or admitting light only through narrow apertures—this strategy can produce a sparse training signal: most paths fail to reach the emitter and contribute zero, leaving the network to learn from a small fraction of informative samples. As shown in recent work [FLW∗ 26], light tracing [Arv86] can reduce the number of zero-radiance contributions. Orthogonally, when the emissive field around the emitter exhibits high-frequency variation, adaptive sampling the training loss [DPD22] could training data on the regions that need it most.

### 6. Conclusion

We presented Neural Emission Fields, a pre-integrated neural representation of direct illumination from non-trivial emitters. By moving the receiver-side integral into an offline training phase, NEF reduces shading to a single network evaluation per pixel, at a cost independent of the emitter's geometric complexity. The same formulation handles a wide range of lighting scenarios including spatially varying emission, internal interreflections, and deformable emitters—within a single, unified training procedure; trained models then act as portable lighting assets that can be placed in any scene under rigid transforms and uniform scaling. We hope NEF proves a useful step toward treating expensive direct illumination as a bakeable, reusable component of real-time rendering pipelines.

# Appendix D: Training Budget

Table 4 reports the training cost for each NEF in the paper, measured on a single RTX 4090 GPU. All timings include the cost of generating path-traced radiance targets on-the-fly, which dominates iteration time for the more complex emitters. Iterations and per-emitter training time scale with the difficulty of the underlying radiance field: planar emitters with uniform emission converge in minutes, bare emissive meshes and spatially varying emitters in under fifteen minutes, while configurations involving internal interreflections or temporal variation require several hours, both because the radiance field itself is more complex and because each path-traced target costs more to generate.

# Appendix F: Low-roughness Materials

While there are no theoretical constraints preventing NEF from being trained on low-roughness receivers, practical results indicate a bottleneck as α → 0, as illustrated in Figure 15. This degradation occurs because the specular highlight sharpens into a highfrequency signal that exceeds the network's representational capacity. Consequently, while training is technically feasible across the entire roughness spectrum, the model struggles to resolve the nearsingular gradients of mirror-like reflections, leading to the observed increase in approximation error in the highlight regions.

# Appendix G: Impact of Training Data Noise

The training targets used throughout this paper are generated via path tracing with Monte Carlo integration, leaving residual variance in the supervision signal. The magnitude of this variance depends on the emitter: emitters that are hard to sample yield noisier targets. The Pierced Sphere is a particularly challenging case, as its internal interreflections must also be captured during training, making it one of the noisiest emitters in our set. In Figure 16 we ablate the number of Monte Carlo samples used per training estimate and observe diminishing returns beyond 64 samples. We therefore adopt S = 64 for all emitters throughout the paper, noting that this budget could safely be reduced for simpler emitters.
