Abstract
Direct illumination from non-trivial emitters with occluding housing, high-polygon emissive meshes, spatially varying emission,
or deformable assemblies is a persistent bottleneck in real-time rendering. Current real-time solutions either rely on simplified
analytical representations or fall back to runtime sampling. Analytical methods are restricted to simple emitter geometry, pure
sampling-based estimators need high sampling budgets to be noise-free, and proxy representations still require runtime integration over outgoing radiance around the emitter. We present Neural Emission Fields (NEF), a representation that eliminates
runtime integration by precomputing the illumination in the volume around the emitter. This neural field is parameterized by
position, normal, view direction, and material parameters. Using a two-headed diffuse/glossy architecture, it yields noise-free
unoccluded direct illumination from a single network evaluation per shading point. Because training is performed in the emitter’s local frame, a trained NEF acts as a portable lighting asset reusable across scenes under rigid transforms. Internal
interreflections, self-occlusion, spatially varying emission, and deformations are absorbed into the learned representation at
zero additional runtime cost.

1. Introduction
Analytical methods like Linearly Transformed Cosines (LTC)
[HDHN16] are extremely fast but assume 2D polygonal emitters
and scale linearly with the emitter’s vertex count. For more general
emitters, Monte Carlo estimators of direct illumination, including
reservoir methods [BWP∗ 20], support arbitrary emitters but produce noise that must be removed by a denoiser, which often introduces unwanted temporal and spatial artifacts. Proxy-based representations [HKSS98, VADWG15], including recent neural variants [ZBX∗ 21], sidestep both limitations by precomputing the outgoing radiance into a 4D near-field proxy, factoring out the cost
of sampling paths within the emitter assembly. However, receiver
points must still integrate over this proxy at runtime.
Direct illumination is a major cost in real-time rendering pipelines,
especially when emitters are non-trivial—luminaires with housing
geometry that occludes the internal filament, emissive meshes with
thousands of polygons, light sources with spatially varying or textured emission profiles, or deformable emitters that change shape
over time. While path tracing [Kaj86] handles all of these correctly,
converging to noise-free direct illumination from non-trivial emitters is incompatible with real-time rendering budgets. Existing alternatives that do meet those rendering budgets typically compromise on generality, accuracy, or noise-free output.

Our key insight is that this remaining runtime integration is
avoidable. Rather than precomputing only the radiance leaving an
emitter (which still needs to be integrated), we precompute the preintegrated outgoing direct illumination with respect to a receiver
point (any shaded surface point in the volume around the emitter).
We represent this as a neural field parameterized by the receiver’s
position, normal, view direction, and material parameters, learned
by a neural network conditioned on a material model. By shifting
the integration to a one-time precomputation phase, a single network evaluation replaces the entire integral over the (proxy) emitter.
Because the network is trained in the emitter’s local coordinate
frame, the resulting model functions as a portable lighting asset.
World-space queries are mapped into the emitter’s frame at shading
time rather than baked into the network weights, so a trained NEF
can be translated, rotated, and uniformly scaled at runtime without
retraining. A single training run therefore amortizes across every
scene and every instance in which the emitter is placed—much like
a baked texture or precomputed BRDF.
Many-Light Sampling. Monte Carlo integration supports estimating direct illumination from any emitter but requires specialized sampling strategies to manage runtime noise. Early techniques
addressed the many-lights problem by ranking lights according to
their potential contribution [War94] or by classifying them as important or unimportant to guide sampling [SWZ96]. In complex
scenes, hierarchical representations [WFA∗ 05, MPC19, Yuk20]
can be used to enable emitter sampling at sublinear cost. More
recently, ReSTIR [BWP∗ 20] introduced spatiotemporal reservoir
resampling, achieving high-quality direct illumination under tight
sampling budgets. Despite these advancements, the residual variance inherent to all Monte Carlo approaches typically necessitates
a denoiser. In contrast, NEF avoids runtime sampling entirely, shifting the computational burden to a (one-time) per-emitter offline
training phase.
This design facilitates several critical performance advantages
over traditional methods. Inference cost is fundamentally decoupled from the geometric complexity of the emitter; the computational cost is determined by the network’s size rather than the polygon count of the emitter. Furthermore, because the receiver’s BRDF
is incorporated into the learning objective, complex emissive effects are absorbed into the representation at no additional runtime
cost. Finally, since the training phase relies solely on path-traced
radiance estimates for training data, the same training procedure
is used for simple planar polygons, complex emissive meshes, and
deformable emitter assemblies.
Classical Pre-integration. Precomputation in rendering [Ram09] trades offline computations for runtime cost by
tabulating light transport over a discretised parameter domain.
Precomputed radiance transfer (PRT) [SKS02] projects the transfer
function at each surface point onto a low-order spherical harmonics
basis, reducing shading under distant lighting to a dot product.
All-frequency variants require non-linear wavelet approximations
to keep storage tractable [NRH03]. More fundamentally, PRT
relies on the distant-lighting assumption, which breaks down for
near-field emitters whose incident lighting varies with receiver
position.
We summarize our contributions as follows:
• A reformulation of direct illumination as a pre-integrated,
receiver-aware quantity, eliminating the runtime integration retained by prior analytical, sampling-based, and proxy-based
methods.
• A neural emission field employing a two-headed diffuse/glossy
architecture and reflected-direction encoding, yielding noise-free
direct illumination and accurate specular highlights from a single
network evaluation per shading point.
• Scene-independent training in the emitter’s local coordinate
frame, so a single pre-trained NEF can be translated, rotated and
scaled across different scenes without retraining.
• Extensions to complex emissive assemblies with internal interreflections, spatially varying emission, and deformable emitters
which are non-trivial or unsupported in state-of-the-art real-time
methods.
A complementary line of work stores lighting in a volume rather
than on surfaces. Irradiance volumes [GSHG98] and their dynamic,
ray-traced successors [MGNM19] cache irradiance at probes on a
grid and interpolate between them at runtime. For strongly spatially
varying signals, such as the near field of a complex emitter, these
caches require careful probe placement [SL17, WKKN19] and fine
discretisation to avoid interpolation and light-leaking artifacts.
These approaches share a common limitation: the storage of a
tabulated representation grows exponentially with the dimensionality of the signal. Pre-integrated direct illumination around a nearfield emitter depends on receiver position (3D), normal (2D), and
reflected direction (2D), which is already seven dimensions for a
single glossy material. Conditioning on roughness to cover a continuous family of materials, as done in this paper, adds an eighth parameter. A regular grid with only 32 samples per dimension would
already require 328 ≈ 1012 entries. Adding a time parameter for
dynamic emitters worsens this further: a naive per-keyframe cache

Despite these advances, these methods still treat the proxy as

Crucially, a pre-integrated quantity is not a property of the emitter alone. Since the BRDF fr appears inside the direct illumination
integral, different receivers will observe different values of Dθ at
the same (x, n). The receiver’s shading model is therefore part of
what is being learned. We instantiate this using the GGX microfacet model [WMLT07] over a roughness range of α ∈ [0.1, 1]. By
exposing α as input to the network, a single trained NEF represents the full roughness range for that material. While we restrict
our scope to GGX in this work, our approach is generic and can be
extended to other materials, such as Disney or layered BRDFs, by
extending the conditioning inputs and the training distribution.
Rendering with Neural Networks. A common use of neural networks in rendering is capturing the radiance distribution during a training phase which amortizes over subsequent
viewings of the scene. Early work anchored a learned radiance representation to scene geometry for interactive viewing
from arbitrary directions [RWG∗ 13]. Modern variants extend this
idea to high-quality static scenes [HCZ21], rotatable environment lighting [RBRD22], scene animations [CDD∗ 24], variable
scene configurations [DPD22, SDR∗ 24], and volumetric irradiance caching [CND∗ 26]. These methods all rely on offline, scenespecific training. Alternatively, a small neural representation can be
trained online as a radiance cache [MRNK21] with the additional
runtime cost of ray tracing and denoising. NEF differs in that it is
an offline method whose representation is anchored in the emitter’s
local 3D extent rather than on the 2D scene manifold, making it a
portable, reusable asset across scenes.
At runtime, evaluating Dθ requires only a single network inference at the shading point. This eliminates the need for any sampling
of the proxy P, importance sampling, and denoising.
Because Dθ is agnostic to the source of the radiance field, all
the complexity that would otherwise have to be handled at runtime
is shifted to the offline solver and baked into the network weights.
This includes complex effects such as self-occlusion, multi-bounce
transport and non-uniform emission (Section 4.2)—effects that often violate the assumptions made by analytical methods such as
LTC [HDHN16]. As a result, Dθ can stand in for a path-traced estimate of direct illumination from an emitter, provided the field can
be trained to faithfully represent it.
3. Neural Pre-integrated Emitters
Prior proxy methods reduce the runtime cost of internal emitter
transport but still leave the receiver-side integral to be evaluated
per shading point. We close this remaining gap by learning the full
pre-integrated direct illumination as a neural field around the emitter. Section 3.1 derives this formulation; Section 3.2 defines the
emitter-local frame that makes the representation portable; Section 3.3 introduces the diffuse/glossy decomposition; Section 3.4
describes training; and Section 3.5 outlines rendering.

Pre-integrated direct illumination is only physically meaningful
within a bounded region around the emitter. Emitted radiance falls
off with distance, and beyond a certain range an emitter’s contribution becomes negligible. We therefore define the emitter-space as
an axis-aligned bounding region in the local frame, sized to extend
20× the emitter’s largest extent on each axis—well beyond the 5×
threshold at which the inverse-square law becomes an acceptable
approximation for emitters [Rea00]. Shading points inside this region are evaluated by a direct query of Dθ , which is sufficient for
all emitters in this work.
Analytical Fall-off. A simple heuristic to deal with points outside of the emitter-space, consists of clamping outside queries to
the nearest boundary point and scaling the resulting shading value
by (dboundary /dactual )2 , where dboundary is the distance from the
emitter center to the clamped boundary point and dactual is the true
distance from the emitter center to the shading point. As shown
in Figure 3, this fall-off approach generates a result that is hard
to visually distinguish from the ground truth reference. Alternatively, the emitter-space can be enlarged at training time to avoid
the heuristic, potentially requiring a larger representation to get the
desired level of accuracy everywhere in the emitter-space.

3.5. Rendering
Because NEF stores a pre-integrated estimate of the outgoing
radiance reflected by the receiver, no integration is required
at runtime. Unlike methods that integrate with respect to the
emitter geometry or to a proxy representation of the emitter [HKSS98, VADWG15, ZBX∗ 21], our approach reduces the
shading process to a single network evaluation per pixel. At render time, we first perform a G-buffer pass to obtain the (x, n, ωo , α)
channels at each shading point. These are transformed into the
emitter’s local frame via its current world transform M (Section 3.2), encoded, and fed to Dθ to obtain the outgoing radiance
contribution pre-integrated with respect to the emitter.

All network inputs are normalized to [0, 1] , and a learned multiresolution hash encoding [MESK22] is applied to both the position
x̃ and the reflected direction r(ω̃o , ñ). Encoding the reflected direction is critical for capturing the sharp highlights and complex reflections characteristic of low-roughness receiver geometry (see supplemental material), consistent with findings in the field of novel

3.4. Training
A schematic of the training procedure is shown in Figure 2. Training a NEF only requires a reliable estimate of the outgoing radiance Lo for any query within the network’s input domain, making
the procedure agnostic to the underlying emitter geometry.
Sample generation. For each training batch, we draw queries
uniformly from the emitter-space (Section 3.2). For the glossy head
fgθ , we sample a position x̃ uniformly in the local bounding box, and
a normal ñ such that the emitter is visible in its hemisphere avoiding
queries with zero radiance, an outgoing direction ω̃o uniformly on
the upper hemisphere defined by ñ, and a roughness α uniformly in
[0.1, 1]. The diffuse head fdθ uses the same procedure restricted to
(x̃, ñ). For deformable or animated emitters, we additionally sample a time parameter t ∈ [0, 1] corresponding to the emitter’s state,
encoded with an 8-band Fourier encoding [MST∗ 20] and provided
as an extra conditioning input.

denoised case exceeds the typical per-frame budget of real-time
pipelines. However, ReSTIR remains more general: it handles arbitrary per-light motion at runtime, whereas NEF requires the emitter
configuration to be fixed at training time. Rigid transforms of the
entire emitter group and pre-arranged relative motion of the emitters in the composition (via a time parameter, Section 4.2) are supported, but arbitrary independent light motion requires retraining.
Dynamic and Deformable Emitters. To support dynamic or deformable emitters, we extend NEF with a time-varying parameter
t. As with the higher-order emissive effects in the previous section, this only requires that training data collection account for the
time-varying state of the emitter. Figure 7 shows a textured fireball
whose time parameter indexes into frames of a source video. See
Figure 12 for an emitter with deformable geometry. We encourage
the reader to consult the supplemental video for a detailed demonstration of the results.
Proxy Emitters. Proxy representations—whether using 4D
light-field slabs [HKSS98], a radiance volume with virtual point
lights [VADWG15], or neural fields [ZBX∗ 21]—gather radiance
by sampling and integrating a proxy representation. We compare
NEF against an oracle proxy: an idealized proxy whose near-field
queries return a noise-free reference and whose evaluation cost is
treated as zero. Any sampling-based proxy method is bounded from
below by this oracle, so the comparison reveals the minimum number of proxy evaluations required to match NEF’s pre-integrated
estimate, regardless of how the proxy itself is represented.
4.3. Comparison to Sampling-based Methods

runtime. We compare against a representative of each category: ReSTIR [BWP∗ 20] and an oracle proxy formulation.

one-time training phase in exchange for a constant-time per-pixel

informative samples. As shown in recent work [FLW∗ 26], light

exhibits high-frequency variation, adaptive sampling the training

5. Discussion
Visibility and soft shadows. Similarly to analytical methods and
proxy emitters, NEF only captures the unoccluded emission field.
In practice, this is well-addressed by combining NEF shading with
a small budget of path-traced visibility samples and a denoiser that
are used as a ratio estimator [HHM18]. This approach yields plausible soft shadows while preserving the noise-free direct illumination
produced by the NEF. Even 1 shadow ray per pixel yields perceptually accurate soft shadows (see Figure 10), with further samples
producing diminishing returns. This makes NEF compatible with
standard real-time visibility pipelines at minimal additional cost.
6. Conclusion
We presented Neural Emission Fields, a pre-integrated neural representation of direct illumination from non-trivial emitters. By
moving the receiver-side integral into an offline training phase,
NEF reduces shading to a single network evaluation per pixel,
at a cost independent of the emitter’s geometric complexity. The
same formulation handles a wide range of lighting scenarios—
including spatially varying emission, internal interreflections, and
deformable emitters—within a single, unified training procedure;
trained models then act as portable lighting assets that can be placed
in any scene under rigid transforms and uniform scaling. We hope
NEF proves a useful step toward treating expensive direct illumination as a bakeable, reusable component of real-time rendering
pipelines.
Storage and training. All results in this paper were generated
using a single NEF architecture totaling 15 MB (or 30 MB for the
glossy variant) per emitter. For simpler emitters, this is often excessive and can be reduced (see supplemental). Training is a one-time
offline cost, ranging from approximately one minute for simple planar emitters to three hours for emitters with internal interreflections, depending primarily on the cost of generating path-traced
training samples. Crucially, this cost is incurred once per emitter
and can be amortized across multiple scenes.
