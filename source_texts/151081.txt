## ABSTRACT

Neural radiance representations in Gaussian Splatting (GS) deliver high-fidelity color detail but impose substantial rendering overhead from network evaluations. We present a learned sparse scene representation based on 2D surfels, which enables baking the neural component into a hardware-accelerated 2D texture atlas and eliminates runtime network inference. Our 2D surfels carry low-frequency geometry and view-dependent appearance, while view-independent, per-primitive high-frequency texture is encoded with a spatial hash grid and converted into the texture atlas. A novel sparsity objective that penalizes per-primitive kernel falloffs achieves a substantially sparser representation than prior methods. With a compute-optimized ray-surfel intersection shader our approach renders roughly 7 to 9× faster than 3DGS on common benchmarks, and over an order of magnitude faster on individual scenes, while surpassing the perceptual quality and speed of the fastest sparsification methods (FastGS and Speedy-Splat) at PSNR parity. A quality-optimized variant matches the perceptual quality of the strongest baseline while still rendering several times faster. Because inference is one texture fetch per fragment, frame rates can be pushed to 2,700–4,400 FPS with no loss in quality when using the GPU hardware rasterizer.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) \(Kerbl et al., 2023\) represents a scene as an explicit, unstructured set of anisotropic Gaussians that are rasterized and alpha-blended directly onto the image plane, and it has become one of the strongest tools for novel view synthesis (NVS). Its persistent limitation is texture. Each Gaussian contributes a spatially smooth basis function, so high-frequency appearance forces the optimization to place many small Gaussians densely across textured surfaces. Grid-based methods store such detail in voxel arrays or hash-encoded latent vectors \(Mildenhall et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2022\); 3DGS has no comparable spatially local feature encoding, and its primitive count is tied to the frequency content of the scene's appearance.

Textured Splatting approaches disentangle appearance from geometry with two families of methods. Explicit textured methods like BBSplat \(Svitov et al., 2025\), Textured Gaussians \(Chao et al., 2025\), Texture-GS \(Xu et al., 2024\), GSTex \(Rong et al., 2025a\), and Content-Aware Texturing \(Papantonakis et al., 2025\) all explicitly optimize per-primitive texture representations but are bound in memory by the primitive count and try to learn 3D textures with a 2D representation. Neural methods such as NeST Splatting \(Zhang et al., 2025a\), Nexels \(Rong et al., 2025b\), and Hybrid Latents Kelkar et al. \(2026\) query a global multi-resolution hashgrid at the ray–surfel intersection for appearance. The hash-grid provides stable convergence and caps the texture's memory overhead during training. However, since every fragment incurs a neural field query, rendering is significantly slowed down.

We combine 2D surfels with a radiance formulation that separates low-frequency geometry and view-dependent appearance, carried by the surfels, from view-independent appearance, carried by a hash grid as an unbounded residual. Because the residual is view-independent, it can be baked into a single explicit texture atlas of one rectangle per surfel. Using negative and positive color offsets for the residuals provides more representational power. The texels are then fine-tuned against the training images to overcome hash collisions from training.

A texture atlas alone does not make rendering fast, since frame time is bounded by sorting and blending the textured Gaussians and performance quickly suffers from overdraw. We therefore train the field jointly with the multi-view error criterion of FastGS \(Ren et al., 2025\) to limit densification. Furthermore, we introduce a falloff prior using Beta kernels \(Liu et al., 2025\), which progressively regularizes the kernels toward flat, hard-edged profiles. Flat kernels stop redundant overlaps between soft Gaussian tails and dampen positional gradients, reducing both primitive count and overdraw.

With our sparse surfel representation we accept 55–320 MB texture atlases for typical scenes to gain sparsity and speed. These are larger than memory-optimized 3DGS scene representations \(Niedermayr et al., 2024\), but run without issues even on low-to-mid-range GPUs. Using hardware texture mapping gives 5.4–6.9× the frame rate of 3DGS with the standard 2DGS ray–surfel shader \(Huang et al., 2024\). Since at these rates the shader becomes a performance bottleneck, we derive an exact rational reformulation of the intersection test, raising the speedup to 7.1–9.1× over 3DGS and 1.2– 1.8× over FastGS and Speedy-Splat at better perceptual quality, and to 14.5× over Beta-Splatting, the most accurate baseline. Using the GPU's hardware rasterizer further speeds up rendering of 1.8×. In summary, our contributions are:

- A neural view-independent appearance representation for unbounded texture residuals.
- A regularizer progressively flattening surfel kernels, yielding sparser geometry and less overdraw.
- A baking procedure from a neural representation to a compact RGB texture atlas, followed by direct finetuning of the texels.
- A closed-form ray-surfel intersection that removes the per-pixel intersection solve from the software rasterizer.

## 2 RELATED WORK

Primitives and kernels. 3DGS \(Kerbl et al., 2023\) models scenes as unstructured sets of volumetric Gaussians rendered by differentiable splatting. Soft, unbounded Gaussians bloat beyond surface boundaries, so 2DGS \(Huang et al., 2024\) flattens them into oriented planar surfels, Triangle Splatting \(Held et al., 2025a\) and 3D Convex Splatting \(Held et al., 2025b\) replace them with convex shapes, and Deformable Beta kernels \(Liu et al., 2025\) morph between soft blobs and hard-edged disks. For view-dependent color, Spherical Harmonics are band-limited and can miss sharp highlights. Spherical Gaussians \(Wang et al., 2026\) and Spherical Betas \(Liu et al., 2025\) fit specularities with localized lobes; Spherical Voronoi partitions \(Di Sario et al., 2025\) give an explicit parameterization that covers both diffuse shifts and sharp reflections, and score highest in our comparison on bonsai (Table 15\).



Figure 2: A multi-resolution 3D hash-grid learns view-independent appearance, and a spherical Voronoi model per surfel captures sharp, view-dependent effects. A falloff prior progressively flattens the kernels to reduce overdraw and let the hash grid drive densification. The hash-grid is sampled across surfels, decoded colors are optimized, and baked as residuals in a 2D texture atlas. We render with a hardware rasterizer with accelerated texturing.

Sparsification. Default density control relies on positional gradients and opacity thresholds and leaves many redundant primitives. MiniSplatting \(Fang & Wang, 2024\), GaussianSpa \(Zhang et al., 2025b\), FastGS \(Ren et al., 2025\), and Speedy-Splat \(Hanson et al., 2025\) reduce the count through intersection-preserving simplification, regularization, multi-view error scoring, and tight screenspace bounds with pruning. The surviving primitives must still encode high-frequency texture, which limits the attainable sparsity and the texture quality.

Textured primitives. GStex \(Rong et al., 2025a\), Textured Gaussians \(Chao et al., 2025\), BB-Splat \(Svitov et al., 2025\), and Content-Aware Texturing \(Papantonakis et al., 2025\) store textures on the primitives themselves. Texture memory then scales with primitive count and with resolutions fixed before training. Because the textures are allocated before densification, their parameters, gradients and optimizer state grow with every clone. Texture-GS \(Xu et al., 2024\) maps Gaussian centers to a global unit-sphere UV domain through an MLP, limiting to object-centric scenes. Pipelines using per-primitive textures start from a converged dense model with densification frozen \(Rong et al., 2025a; Chao et al., 2025\), which caps the attainable sparsity. Alpha textures increase overdraw on top of that. We allocate the atlas once, after sparsification, so its size follows the primitives that survive rather than limiting training.

The atlas requires about 55–320 MB for typical scenes, more than memory-optimized 3DGS scene representations \(Niedermayr et al., 2024\) need. Extreme compactness, however, comes at the cost of reduced rendering speed and quality, even with hardware rasterization (Sec. 4\). We compare against Compressed 3DGS in Table 3 and Sec. 4.4.

Neural and Hybrid representations. NeST-Splatting \(Zhang et al., 2025a\) offloads appearance to a global hash grid that is queried per fragment with an MLP decoder. Nexels \(Rong et al., 2025b\) and Hybrid Latents \(Kelkar et al., 2026\) combine neural texturing with per-primitive appearance. Due to the compact latent grid these methods require less memory than ours, but keep a slow neural query in the render loop. Like ours, NeRF-based approaches such as SNeRG \(Hedman et al., 2021\), MERF \(Reiser et al., 2023\), and SMERF \(Duckworth et al., 2024\) bake view-independent neural queries into lightweight 3D features for faster inference, but still require a costly screen-space decoder that limits rendering speed.

## 3 METHODOLOGY

We represent a scene as a sparse set of oriented 2D Beta kernels, regularized to reduce overdraw. We separate appearance into per-surfel view-dependent color and a per-fragment texture residual that is learned with a spatial multi-resolution hash-grid and a world-space MLP decoder (see Fig. 2\). For inference, we sample the hash-grid across the surface of each primitive, bake the output into a compressed RGB texture atlas, and finetune the atlas texels directly against the training images.

The residual carries the high-frequency detail the per-surfel term does not represent. Right: one textured surfel under three illumination angles. A signed residual reproduces the color variation with a single surfel (53–55 dB) where a positive additive texture cannot (14–20 dB); the last two columns show the negative and positive parts of each learned residual.

Rendering then reduces to fast, per-fragment texture fetches evaluated through an exact rational reformulation of the ray-surfel intersection.

#### 3.1 FUNDAMENTALS

3DGS \(Kerbl et al., 2023\) maps volumetric ellipsoids to the image plane with EWA splatting \(Zwicker et al., 2002\); 2DGS \(Huang et al., 2024\) flattens them into oriented planar surfels, which we build on. Each surfel i has a position µi , rotation qi , two-dimensional scale si , and opacity oi . A pixel's color is the front-to-back alpha composite of the depth-sorted surfels overlapping it, where surfel i contributes color ci(x) with effective alpha αi(x) = oi Gi(x) for kernel G at the ray–surfel intersection x, weighted by the transmittance accumulated in front of it. The color ci(x) may be constant per surfel or vary across its footprint, and the formulation is differentiable in every attribute. We replace the Gaussian kernel of 2DGS with the Deformable Beta kernel \(Liu et al., 2025\)



| $\mathcal{B}(x;b) = (1-x)^{\beta(b)}, \quad \beta(b) = 5\sigma(b), \quad x \in [0,1], b \in \mathbb{R}, \quad (1)$ |
|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

where σ is the sigmoid and x the squared radial distance from the kernel center, normalized to 1 at the edge of the support. While large b gives soft, Gaussian-like surfels, small b gives flat, hard-edged disks that fit sharp geometry with fewer primitives and less overdraw. We restrict b so that kernels lie between a flat disk and a soft Gaussian.

#### 3.2 NEURAL APPEARANCE DECOMPOSITION

We decompose each surfel's appearance into a per-primitive color fSV (d) depending on viewing direction d, and a global view-independent residual ρ(x) = fϕ(Eθ(x)) from a multiresolution hash grid Eθ and an MLP decoder fϕ. A fixed offset bSV = 0.5 inside the inner activation gives a neutral gray baseline on which the residual can build before fSV is trained. The fragment color is


$$\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) = \text{ReLU}\left(\text{ReLU}(f_{SV}(\mathbf{d}) + b_{SV}) + f_\theta(\mathbf{E}_\theta(\mathbf{x}))\right). \quad (2)$$

Gradient descent takes the pathway that lowers the photometric loss fastest. The hash grid is queried at every fragment along the surfel's surface, so it sees sub-surfel coordinate variation and absorbs fine spatial texture; per-surfel attributes are constant across a surfel, so they absorb what does not vary across it. View dependence is low-frequency and shared across a surfel, and it ends up in the per-surfel vector (Fig. 3\).

The residual is unbounded, so the texture can add to or subtract from the base color. This lets one surfel carry detail that a positive-only texture could reproduce only by splitting it (Fig. 3; Appendix D.1 quantifies the difference on real scenes). At each fragment x we trilinearly interpolate the features of every hash level, concatenate them, and decode with fϕ. The grid has four levels with 4-D features, over a base resolution that depends on the scene's extent (Appendix A\); fϕ has two hidden layers of width 16.

Texture baking. After training we evaluate the hash grid over every surfel and store the result in one RGB atlas, so inference needs no network. Each surfel g receives an anisotropic resolution rg = (rx, ry) matched to the Nyquist rate of the finest hash voxel δ along each tangent axis a ∈ {u, v}:


$$r_{g,a} = \text{clamp} \left( 2^{\lceil \log_2(4es_{g,a}/\delta) \rceil}, r_{\min}, r_{\max} \right), \quad (3)$$

where sg,a is the surfel scale on axis a, e is the UV half-extent in units of that scale, the factor 4 combines the 2e span with Nyquist sampling, and rmin = 4, rmax = 64. Anisotropic rectangles avoid square padding on thin surfels. The rectangles are packed into a fixed-width atlas by Shelf-First-Fit-Decreasing (Fig. 4\), and each texel center is lifted through the surfel's tangent frame to the world position at which ρ is evaluated. Appendix C.2 discusses issues related to mipmapping the texture atlas.

Atlas layout and texel evaluation. Once the resolutions of Eq. 3 are determined, the per-surfel grids are stored in an atlas of dimensions W × H using a Shelf-First-Fit-Decreasing algorithm. Surfels are sorted descending by ry, then rx, and placed into the first available shelf. We fix W = 4096 and grow H greedily, rounding to a multiple of 64, and automatically increase W if H approaches the 65,536-row cudaArray limit.



Figure 4: Atlas packing. Each surfel owns one rectangular tile with sides chosen by the rate– distortion probe (4 to 64 texels); tiles are sorted by height and placed first-fit into full-width shelves, whose height is fixed by their first tile, hence the white gaps. The size mix is chosen for legibility; the full atlas is dominated by 64-texel tiles and packs tighter.

At the center of each texel, the hash grid and MLP are evaluated. For a texel (i, j) assigned to surfel g, local coordinates are computed using the texel-center convention:


$$u = \frac{i + 0.5}{r_x} 2e - e, \quad v = \frac{j + 0.5}{r_y} 2e - e. \quad (4)$$

This mapping prevents boundary seams by aligning the baked evaluation with the hardware bilinear sampler. The local coordinates are lifted to world space via the surfel tangent frame R:


$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p}_g + u s_{g,u} \mathbf{R}_{\cdot,0} + v s_{g,v} \mathbf{R}_{\cdot,1}. \quad (5)$$

The evaluated RGB vectors ρ(x) are written to the atlas in a batched pass that evaluates the hash grid and the fused MLP decoder over chunks of surfels, bounding peak memory during the bake.

Support-aware extent. The atlas covers a square of half-extent e in surfel-scale units around each surfel. At e = 4 this patch reaches well beyond the kernel: the compact Beta kernel is identically zero past r = 3σ, so on a representative indoor bake 50.5% of all texels lie outside the support even after a one-texel guard band for bilinear filtering, and are never fetched. Baking over e = 3 removes them. Because two thirds of the rectangles are at the rmax cap, most of this saving is returned as a 33% finer texel pitch rather than as bytes.

Rate–distortion allocation. Nyquist sizing follows the hash grid, not the contribution each surfel makes to the rendered image. We instead measure per surfel what each smaller size of its rectangle destroys, as the kernel- and opacity-weighted error of a down- and up-sampled patch, weight it by the surfel's accumulated αT visibility across the training views, and let a single Lagrange multiplier fix every rectangle's size; the multiplier is bisected to a distortion target. We set that target to the error the BC7 codec already introduces, so the allocation costs no more than the quantizer we use. The probe runs on the raw bake, which is band-limited by the hash grid, so halving it loses little; it keeps 45% of the texels, and a single finetune of the smaller atlas recovers the quality. Probing the finetuned atlas instead keeps 60%, because the finetune adds detail beyond the grid's band and every fitted texel then looks necessary; that variant also costs a second finetune and over the 13 scenes gives 0.05 dB less PSNR and 0.002 more LPIPS for 23% more bytes. Table 1 compares the sized atlas with the full-extent shelf atlas under the same protocol: 0.44× the bytes, higher PSNR and SSIM on every benchmark, lower LPIPS on two of three. The deepest cuts, playroom at −52% bytes and room at −42%, are where LPIPS moves up by 0.002–0.010, since the distortion target is MSE.

Quantization. Instead of storing a dense FP16 RGB atlas, we apply Block Compression (BC7) to reduce the memory footprint. The residual range is estimated as the empirical [min, max] with a 2% margin over roughly 1.3 million samples (64 random UV positions on each of 20,000 surfels), and the atlas is quantized uniformly with a single global (offset,scale) pair, written to the bake metadata and applied as one multiply-add per read by the rasterizer. Two details affect fidelity. First, range estimates of the form µ ± kσ clip the heavy tails of the residual distribution. Clamping the bright peaks dims the render uniformly, so we use the full empirical range instead. Second, the scale is snapped so that a residual of zero coincides with an integer quantization level; otherwise the halfstep rounding bias propagates through the outer ReLU of Eq. 2 and systematically dims pixels near the activation threshold. The data is padded to RGBA to match the 4×4 block layout of BC7, which stores each block in 16 bytes, or 1 byte per texel. As Table 2 shows, quantization introduces no perceivable quality loss at a large memory reduction.

Atlas finetuning. Baking is a lossy projection of the neural residual: quantization, the Nyquist clamp on large surfels, and bake-time pruning all discard information, so a baked atlas can only lose quality relative to the hash grid that produced it. We recover this loss, and more, by treating the bake as an initialization. After baking, the atlas is dequantized into a single trainable FP16 texture and optimized for 3,000 iterations against the training images with the standard L1 + D-SSIM photometric loss. Geometry and view-dependent color stay frozen: each surfel's texels were evaluated at its bake-time world-space positions, so moving a surfel would invalidate the binding between its texels and the scene, and jointly training the view-dependent term is ill-posed because Eq. 2 lets it cancel against the residual. A freeze ablation of our method (garden, room, bicycle) confirms both effects: unfreezing geometry costs 0.18 dB and 0.009 LPIPS even at a tenth of the stock learning rate, joint training at the full rate costs 2.7 dB, and unfreezing the view-dependent color alone is a tie (0.01 dB).

The finetune renders through a differentiable variant of the inference rasterizer whose forward pass is the exact atlas fetch (rect transform, clamp, bilinear); the backward pass scatters the residual gradient into the four texels of each bilinear read, and the hash grid and MLP are never evaluated. Because only 5–7% of texel rows receive gradient in any iteration, we apply a lazily allocated sparse Adam step over the touched rows with bf16 moments. This halves optimizer memory and, unlike a dense step, leaves untouched texels unchanged, avoiding the slow random walk that otherwise degrades the atlas once the coherent photometric signal is absorbed. The full stage costs a few minutes per scene, and primitive count, atlas bytes, and frame rate are unchanged. As Table 5 shows, the finetuned atlas surpasses the neural model it was baked from, which we call the teacher, on PSNR and LPIPS on all three benchmarks. Two properties explain this. First, the atlas is free of hash collisions: the hash grid's fixed-size feature table forces distant scene locations to share table entries at its finest levels, so their gradients average into the same feature and high-frequency detail is lost, whereas every atlas texel is an independent parameter owned by a single surfel location. Second, the explicit texture holds 5–29× (13× on average) the parameters of the network that produced it. Optimizing the texels directly therefore recovers detail the hash grid cannot represent.

#### 3.2.2 VIEW-DEPENDENT SURFEL COLOR

Each surfel carries a soft spherical Voronoi model \(Di Sario et al., 2025\): K sites sk on the unit sphere with colors ck and temperatures τk, blended as

$$f_{SV}(\mathbf{d}) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(\mathbf{d}) c_k, \quad w_k(\mathbf{d}) = \frac{\exp(-\tau_k \|\mathbf{s}_k - \mathbf{d}\|)}{\sum_{k'=1}^K \exp(-\tau_{k'} \|\mathbf{s}_{k'} - \mathbf{d}\|)}. \quad (6)$$

Smooth, learnable cell boundaries give a stable explicit parameterization, and τk sets the angular sharpness, so one surfel can hold both broad diffuse shifts and sharp highlights. The mixture is offset by bSV = 0.5 and gated by the inner ReLU of Eq. 2.

#### 3.3 RENDERING

For novel view synthesis, we replace the neural renderer with a 2D rasterizer using hardware accelerated texture fetches. For each fragment in a surfel's footprint, the texture unit samples the BC7 block at the fragment's UV with bilinear filtering, and the dequantized residual ρˆ enters the outer ReLU of Eq. 2 before α-compositing. When using hardware rasterization, our Vulkan port follows the vertex- and fragment-shader pipelines built for 3D Gaussians \(Niedermayr et al., 2024; Rota Bulò et al., 2025\): it precomputes per-surfel rendering parameters and generates depth keys in a compute pass to GPU sort visible surfels \(Niedermayr et al., 2024\) instead of per tile surfel sorting. Each surfel is then hardware-rasterized as a convex polygon circumscribing its exact screen support.

Exact rational ray–surfel evaluation. A surfel is a flat rectangle in space. For each pixel, the renderer needs the point where the pixel's viewing ray hits that rectangle, in the surfel's own coordinates (u, v). 2DGS solves this intersection from scratch at every pixel, with a cross product and two divisions. 3DGS works directly in image space and has no such step, so surfels pay a per-pixel cost that Gaussians do not. Most of that cost can leave the pixel loop.

A flat surface seen in perspective maps to the image by a homography, so (u, v)is a ratio of two linear functions of the pixel position, the same fact rasterizers exploit for perspective-correct texturing. Around the center of the surfel's screen-space bounding box,



| $u = u_0 + \frac{(\mathbf{J}\Delta)_u}{1 + d^{\top}\Delta}, \quad v = v_0 + \frac{(\mathbf{J}\Delta)_v}{1 + d^{\top}\Delta},$ | (7) |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|

where ∆ is the pixel offset from that center, (u0, v0) the intersection there, J its rate of change per pixel, and d that of the perspective denominator. These eight numbers depend only on the surfel and the camera, so we compute them once per surfel and frame; per pixel, about 15 fused multiply– adds and two divisions become 10 and one, and a surfel then costs roughly what a Gaussian costs in 3DGS. The form is algebraically exact, and the renders match the 2DGS formulation to within 0.03 dB PSNR and 0.0002 LPIPS on all 13 scenes (individual pixels differ where a floating-point difference near the alpha cutoff flips whether a fragment is drawn), at 32% higher mean frame rate (Table 4\). Pixels with a non-positive denominator look at the plane from behind the camera and are skipped. We derive this formulation below. Training keeps the 2DGS formulation, where the backward pass and hash-grid evaluation dominate.

Derivation. A planar surfel seen through a pinhole camera projects to the screen by a homography: a 3 × 3 matrix T with rows Tu, Tv, Tw maps the surfel point q = (u, v, 1) to the screen point (Tu· q, Tv · q)/(Tw · q). 2DGS inverts this per pixel: the pixel (x, y) is hit by the surfel point that satisfies Tu · q = x Tw · q and Tv · q = y Tw · q, two linear constraints k · q = 0 and l · q = 0 with k = x Tw − Tu and l = y Tw − Tv. Their common solution is the cross product p = k × l, and dividing out the homogeneous scale gives u = px/pz and v = py/pz. Per fragment this is two vector constructions, a cross product, and two divides: roughly 15 fused multiply–adds, two divides, and 56 bytes of shared-memory traffic.

The cross product does not have to be evaluated per fragment. Both k and l are affine in (x, y), and their only bilinear term is xy (Tw × Tw), which vanishes because the cross product of a vector with itself is zero, so p is itself affine in the pixel. Each of u and v is therefore a ratio of two affine functions of the pixel, u(x, y) = (a0 + a1x + a2y)/(b0 + b1x + b2y), the familiar form of perspective-correct interpolation. Writing that ratio around the center of the surfel's screen-space bounding box gives Eq. 7: (u0, v0) is the intersection at the center, J its Jacobian there, and d the gradient of the shared denominator, so the eight coefficients follow from one evaluation of p and its two partial derivatives per surfel. The form is the exact closed form, not a linearization. Where the denominator 1 + d ⊤∆ reaches zero the surfel plane is parallel to the ray, and beyond it the intersection lies behind the camera; the CUDA renderer discards those fragments per pixel, and the hardware port clips the polygon it draws by that line so they are never generated.

Precision of the stored coefficients. Precision determines whether the rational reformulation of Sec. 3.3 stays exact. The coefficients (u0, v0), J, opacity, and shape have small dynamic range and are stored as FP16, while d spans several orders of magnitude and stays FP32, since any error there scales the correction across the whole surfel. With this split, the closed-form intersection reproduces the reference formulation's quality to within 0.03 dB PSNR and 0.0002 LPIPS on all 13 benchmark scenes (10 of 13 within 0.001 dB). The images are not byte-identical: rendering the same bake twice through one build is deterministic (PSNR between runs at the metric floor), while the two formulations differ by 59–66 dB PSNR from each other, because floating-point reassociation near the alpha cutoff flips whether a fragment is drawn. The staging changes no arithmetic, only the shared-memory layout, and any rasterizer of this form could adopt it. It is also a different trade-off from packing the plane rows themselves at reduced precision, which routes FP16 inputs through the error-amplifying cross product and costs 0.05–0.7 dB; the closed-form intersection has no cross product, so the staging is safe here.

#### 3.4 OPTIMIZATION

Our hybrid representation already converges to a sparse set of primitives under standard density control. To further improve sparsity, we adopt the multi-view error criterion of FastGS \(Ren et al., 2025\). We further propose a novel falloff regularizer on the Beta kernels, and we freeze the hash grid periodically to bound training time.

Multi-view error criterion. FastGS scores each primitive i by the average number of high-error pixels in its footprint Ωi over K sampled training views,

$$s_d^i = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{j=1}^K \sum_{p \in \Omega_i} \mathbb{I}(\mathcal{M}_{\max}^j(p) = 1), \quad (8)$$

where Mj mask marks pixels with high photometric error in view j. The score gates densification: only surfels whose footprint covers high-error pixels are eligible, and surfels are pruned when their

contribution to photometric accuracy is negligible. Which eligible surfel is cloned is then decided by the accumulated positional-gradient proxy ∂L/∂µ2D. Because the hash grid is queried at the ray–surfel intersection, its backward pass feeds that proxy alongside the kernel-footprint term, and in a controlled test with a randomized grid and decoder the hash term accounts for 99.5% of it. Flattening the kernel shrinks the footprint term further, so the signal that drives splitting is almost entirely the texture's.

Falloff reduction. A surviving Beta surfel can still spread influence beyond its geometric core through the soft tail of its kernel. We add a prior that pulls every shape parameter of Eq. 1 toward the flat end, Lβ = N P i βi , using the objective L = Lphoto + λLβ with λ = 0.005. Balanced against the photometric term, the prior flattens those surfels whose soft tails the images do not require, from a Gaussian-like profile toward a hard-edged disk. This bounds the surfels' influence to their geometric extent and reduces pixel overdraw at inference (Fig. 5\). Appendix D.2 varies λ on all nine Mip-NeRF 360 scenes; 0.005 is where the quality–speed trade-off bends.

Training time. In early training iterations, many fragments are rasterized and backpropagated through the hash grid and MLP. Thus, after 5,000 iterations we freeze both for nine of every ten iterations. The residual is close to converged by then, and freezing it gives the surfels a stable target to fit against at a fraction of the cost. Training averages 45 minutes per Mip-NeRF 360 scene, baking about 28 seconds at 12 GB peak VRAM, and finetuning the atlas requires about four minutes.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS

We evaluate on all scenes of Mip-NeRF 360 \(Barron et al., 2022\) and two scenes each from Tanks and Temples \(Knapitsch et al., 2017\) and Deep Blending \(Hedman et al., 2018\), against dense primitive-only methods (3DGS, 2DGS, Beta-Splatting), textured and hybrid methods (BBSplat, Nexels, Content-Aware Texturing, Textured Gaussians, NeST-Splatting), speed-optimized methods (Speedy-Splat, FastGS), and primitive-budget variants of Beta-Splatting and Nexels. We report PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, primitive count, storage, and frame rate. Every baseline is trained and rendered per scene with its authors' released code, configuration, and evaluation scripts; quality is recomputed by one shared metric script, which reproduces the authors' own numbers where reported. Only the frame rate is measured with our protocol: one harness calls each method's released render function on the held-out cameras, on the same idle NVIDIA RTX 5090 with torch.cuda.Event (50 warm-up, 400 timed frames, sort and preprocess included), with no method-specific optimization applied to any baseline. For timing the hardware rasterizer, we use Vulkan timestamp queries around the same three stages, with warm-up and timed frames recorded into a single submission. We report GPU time only, and measure inference on the quantized BC7 atlas rather than the unquantized texture optimized during atlas fine-tuning. Quantization costs 0.001–0.002 LPIPS and is included in every number we report. Speedy-Splat and FastGS are timed with the screen-space bounding and tile-intersection optimizations in Appendix B, so the software rasterizer stack is matched where that comparison is made. Mip-NeRF 360 uses the standard downsampled images (outdoor at quarter, indoor at half resolution); the other two benchmarks use native resolution. "Ours" denotes the full pipeline: baked BC7 atlas, atlas finetune, and the closed-form intersection of Sec. 3.3.

## 5 CONCLUSION, LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

We present a textured 2D surfel representation using a neural field that learns view-independent appearance during training and is finally baked into a compressed and finetuned texture atlas. A closed-form ray–surfel intersection accelerates the software rasterizer by a third at unchanged quality, and hardware rasterization adds a further 1.4–2.2×. Our results demonstrate high speed and quality on common benchmarks, with the best perceptual quality among methods above 1000 FPS.

Compared to untextured methods our training is slower, due to hash-grid evaluations at high overdraw, and periodic freezing only partly hides that cost. To reduce the additional memory required by the texture atlas, a learned mapping from the trained hash grid to a fixed-size atlas is a natural next step. With the software rasterizer, sorting and binning bound additional fragment-level gains, and an overdraw loss driven by the per-pixel error is a natural extension of the falloff regularizer. The texture residual is view-independent, so view-dependent color is constant across a surfel, as it is for a Gaussian with spherical harmonics; finer view dependence falls to densification, and viewdependent texture residuals are a direction for future work.

### A.2 STAGE 2: BAKE AND TEXEL ALLOCATION

No training happens in this stage.

- 1. Bake. Evaluate the trained hash grid and MLP on each surfel's tile over the kernel support, surfel UV in [−3, 3]2 at texel centres, up to 64 × 64 texels per surfel, in FP16.
- 2. Rate–distortion probe. For every surfel and every size on its halving ladder (rx × ry → rx/2 × ry/2 → . . . , each side clamped at 4, then no texture), measure the importanceweighted distortion of the downsampled tile against the full-resolution bake.
- 3. Allocation. One global Lagrange multiplier, bisected so that the total importance-weighted distortion equals the BC7 floor; each surfel takes the size minimizing D + λ texels. Surfels assigned no texture render from their spherical Voronoi color alone.
- 4. Re-bake at the chosen sizes, shelf-packed into one atlas and BC7 compressed.

## B RASTERIZATION OPTIMIZATIONS

We incorporate the rasterization optimizations introduced by Speedy-Splat \(Hanson et al., 2025\) and adopted in FastGS \(Ren et al., 2025\) to maximize rendering speed. We use SnugBox, a tight screenspace bounding box derived directly from the conic equation, which provides numerical stability under edge-on tilts. We also implement AccuTile, an analytic per-row ellipse intersection during the duplicate-keys phase that emits keys only for tiles the ellipse strictly overlaps.

## C ATLAS COMPRESSION AND MIPMAPPING

### C.1 BLOCK-DICTIONARY COMPRESSION FOR TRANSMISSION

For distribution, the BC7 stream is optionally compressed with a block dictionary. The atlas is cut into 4 × 4 texel blocks, K-means clustering builds a codebook of K = 65,536 block centroids, each centroid is encoded as one 16-byte BC7 block, and every atlas block stores a 16-bit codebook index. The codebook occupies 1 MB and the indices 2 bytes per block, a 7.0× reduction of the atlas payload (173 MB to 24.7 MB on average for Mip-NeRF 360), and the complete web bundle, dictionary atlas with 8-bit surfels (FP16 positions) and cameras, is 38.4 MB. At load time, the indices are gathered back into a dense BC7 stream, so the renderer is unchanged and keeps a single hardware bilinear fetch per fragment. The dictionary therefore reduces download size, while GPU memory and frame time are those of the raw BC7 atlas.

## C.2 MIPMAPPING THE ATLAS

A distant surfel covers few pixels but keeps its full tile, so one pixel can span several texels and the single bilinear fetch undersamples the atlas. Our viewer can load a mip chain for this case: each level is a box filter of level 0, computed in the quantized domain and compressed like level 0, and four extra levels add 33% to the atlas bytes. The level is chosen once per surfel from the texel footprint of a one-pixel step, which the closed-form intersection of Sec. 3.3 already provides, so rendering keeps one bilinear fetch per fragment. Because the finetune optimizes texels as they are read at level 0, filtering to the full footprint costs 0.32 dB and 0.045 LPIPS on garden; choosing one level sharper leaves quality at parity (27.20 / 0.8417 / 0.1270 against 27.06 / 0.8405 / 0.1247 without mips) and the frame rate unchanged on the RTX 5090, where texture reads are a small share of the frame. Mipmapping is therefore off by default, and every number in this paper is rendered at level 0.

## D ADDITIONAL ABLATIONS

These studies support design choices that are not among the main claims: the sign of the texture residual, the strength of the falloff prior, and the per-primitive view-dependent color representation.

Two controls are absent by construction. A primitive-matched comparison between kernels is not available in this regime: the multi-view criterion of Sec. 3.4 sets the primitive count from the error maps and exposes no budget, so forcing the Gaussian-kernel model down to the count of the regularized one means changing the densification thresholds, which changes where the scene is densified and therefore more than the kernel. We report each configuration at the count it converges to, next to that count. Training explicit per-surfel textures from the start, without the hash grid, is not a usable baseline either: textures would have to be allocated for every surfel alive during densification, before pruning has removed the redundant ones, and their texels, gradients and optimizer state would grow with every clone at the overdraw of the unpruned model. The hash grid caps that cost during training, and the atlas is allocated once, for the surfels that survive.

The residual enters inside the outer ReLU of Eq. 2, so it may subtract from the per-primitive base color as well as add to it. Restricting it to add only, ReLU(base + bSV ) + ReLU(ρ), gives a strictly smaller function class: any non-negative correction is reproduced by the signed model, while the converse fails wherever the texture must darken the base. A non-negative texture would be simpler to store and to read in a viewer, so Table 12 measures the cost of the restriction through the full pipeline, training, bake, and atlas finetune, on the nine Mip-NeRF 360 scenes and the two Deep Blending scenes, with identical commands apart from the activation.

The signed residual is better in LPIPS on 10 of the 11 scenes, the exception being garden, where positive-only is lower by 0.0002 and in PSNR on 10 of 11, by 0.31 dB and 0.019 LPIPS on Mip-NeRF 360 and 0.19 dB and 0.011 LPIPS on Deep Blending. Indoor scenes lose about 0.5 dB and 0.01 LPIPS consistently; on stump and treehill the positive-only model loses 0.065 LPIPS, and the loss is already present in the neural model before baking, so it comes from training rather than from the atlas. The activation adds no evaluation cost: the positive-only model renders 5–7% faster only because it converges to about 2.5% fewer primitives, since with a non-negative residual about half its channels are gated closed and the hash gradient that drives densification is correspondingly weaker.

## E.2 SEED VARIANCE OF THE SIGNED RESIDUAL

Table 12 reports one seed per scene, and its mean gap is carried by stump and treehill. Table 17 repeats both arms on those two scenes with three seeds each.
