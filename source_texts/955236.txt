This is a photo of a painted bunting, taken by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. At this size, it's about two million pixels. Stored as raw numbers, it takes six point two million bytes. Saved as a JPEG, it takes two hundred and sixty-two thousand. That's about twenty-four times smaller, and side by side, you'd struggle to tell them apart. So where did ninety-six percent of the data go? To find out, we'll zoom in, all the way down, and follow one tiny piece of this bird through every step. We measured everything on our own computer, and we even wrote our own JPEG encoder to check that we really understood it. Zoom in on the eye and the smooth picture breaks into squares. Each square is a pixel, and this patch is a hundred and twenty-eight pixels wide. Closer still, here is one block of eight by eight pixels, on the red ring around the eye. And here is a single pixel from that block. A pixel is just three numbers: how much red, green and blue. Each number is one byte, eight bits, so every pixel costs twenty-four bits. Two million pixels, three bytes each: that's where the six point two million bytes come from. JPEG's job is to store fewer numbers, without you noticing. The name comes from the committee that wrote it: the Joint Photographic Experts Group, formally created in November nineteen eighty-six. Blind tests of picture quality whittled twelve competing methods down to one, built on a tool called the discrete cosine transform. Nasir Ahmed had proposed that transform in nineteen seventy-two, and the funding agency turned him down. One reviewer said the idea seemed too simple. The standard was approved in September nineteen ninety-two. But a free library had shipped a year earlier. Tom Lane's Independent JPEG Group built it from a paper copy of the draft, and by nineteen ninety-three, JPEG was in one of the first web browsers. The first trick exploits your eyes. We notice small changes in brightness far more than small changes in colour. So JPEG turns each pixel's red, green and blue into one brightness value and two colour-difference values. Here is our block, split that way. Then it keeps the colour at lower resolution: one colour sample for every two-by-two square of pixels. Look at the eye in each channel. Brightness keeps every feather. The colour channels are blurry, and you'd barely notice. That alone halves the numbers. On our photo, keeping colour at full resolution would make the file sixteen percent bigger. Next, each channel is cut into blocks of eight by eight: sixty-four numbers at a time. Then comes the transform. Any eight-by-eight block can be rebuilt exactly, as a mix of these sixty-four patterns, from a flat average in the corner to the finest checkerboard. The transform measures how much of each pattern our block contains. Now watch it rebuild the block: the average first, then broad shapes, then finer and finer detail. In a photo, neighbouring pixels are usually similar, so most of a block lives in the first few patterns. And so far, nothing has been thrown away. Sixty-four numbers went in, and sixty-four came out. This is where the loss happens. Each of the sixty-four numbers is divided by a step size from a table, and rounded to a whole number. Fine patterns, which your eye barely sees, get the biggest steps, so most of them round to zero. In our block on the eye, thirty-three of the sixty-four numbers survive. Across the whole photo, eighty-eight percent of all the numbers become zero. The quality slider in your software is just a knob that scales those tables. At the usual default, seventy-five, they're halved. Turn it down, and the file shrinks. Turn it up, and it grows. Even quality one hundred still rounds, so it's never lossless. Now the packing. The numbers are read in a zigzag, so the zeros bunch up at the end. Each value is paired with the count of zeros before it, an end-of-block code says the rest are all zero, and Huffman coding, from a nineteen fifty-two paper, gives the most common symbols the shortest bit patterns. The brightness of our block on the eye ends up as a hundred and eighty-four bits: twenty-three bytes, for sixty-four pixels. A block of blurry background, colour included, needs just fifteen bits. So here is where the whole file goes: almost nothing on the soft background, and most of it on feathers and bark. To check that we'd understood it, we wrote our own JPEG encoder, about a hundred lines of Python, following exactly these steps. Its file came out at two hundred and sixty-one thousand, four hundred and sixty-one bytes. The library most software uses, libjpeg turbo, made two hundred and sixty-one thousand, five hundred and eighty-one. That's a hundred and twenty bytes apart, and both files open in any image viewer. Push the quality down far enough, and the trick shows. At quality five, the eight-by-eight grid appears as flat squares, and sharp edges grow ripples, called ringing. Re-saving is gentler than its reputation. We saved our photo a hundred times with the same settings, and after about ten saves, it simply stopped changing. But then we moved it by one pixel before each save, the way a small edit might, so the blocks never lined up. After a hundred saves, it had fallen apart. JPEG is thirty-four years old, and newer formats beat it. On our own computer, we tuned WebP, HEIC and AVIF to the same error as JPEG at quality seventy-five. Their files came out thirty-three, thirty-eight and forty-three percent smaller. iPhones have saved photos as HEIC since twenty seventeen, and this month, Chrome and Firefox both started showing JPEG XL. Yet JPEG is still on sixty-nine percent of websites, according to W3Techs. The committee's own chair has said that every effort to replace it, by the committee and by big companies, has failed. So, zoom back out. Brightness kept sharp, colour kept soft, every block turned into patterns, the finest ones rounded away, and the rest packed tight. Twenty-four times smaller, and the bird still looks like a bird.
