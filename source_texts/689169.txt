What / Why / How
What
The raymarch shader's gradient-shading path samples the volume texture
6 times per step (a central-difference gradient: +/-x, +/-y, +/-z) to
compute the pseudo-normal used for Lambert shading and gradient-opacity
modulation. A real-device GPU-timing investigation found this is by far
the most expensive part of the raymarch pass -- with shading enabled,
this pass costs roughly 3.7x what it does with shading off, on the same
hardware, same view, same quality tier.
Why
Real-device testing (an iPhone 14 Pro) already surfaced mobile render
cost as a real problem, and a follow-up investigation swept the raymarch
pass's cost across quality tiers and view angles to find where the time
actually goes: quality tier (max step count) turned out to barely matter
for a typical view, since early-ray-termination already keeps actual
step counts well under even the lowest tier's budget for this content --
but shading, when enabled, multiplies the cost of every step actually
taken, regardless of tier. Directional Occlusion Shading (a separate,
optional toggle that does its own extra sampling) was assumed to be the
pricier of the shading-related toggles, but measured cheap by
comparison -- gradient computation for the Lambert shading term is the
real cost driver.
How (proposed approach)
Reduce the gradient computation from a central-difference (6 samples)
to a forward-difference (3 samples: +x/+y/+z only), reusing the current
step's own already-sampled center density value instead of also
re-sampling it and the -x/-y/-z offsets. This trades a small amount of
directional accuracy (forward-difference is a first-order approximation
versus central-difference's second-order one -- more sensitive to local
asymmetry in the density field) for roughly half the sampling cost.
Gradient magnitude would need rescaling to keep the existing
gradient-opacity-strength slider's sensitivity unchanged, since forward-
and central-difference produce different magnitude conventions for the
same underlying gradient.
