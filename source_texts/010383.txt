Rendering
• Rendering or image synthesis is the automatic process of
generating a photorealistic or non-photorealistic image from a
2D or 3D model (or models in what collectively could be called
a scene file) by means of computer programs. Also, the results
of displaying such a model can be called a render. A scene file
contains objects in a strictly defined language or data structure;
it would contain geometry, viewpoint, texture, lighting, and
shading information as a description of the virtual scene
•
shadding
. Shading
• Definition (short): How the surface’s color/brightness is computed from light,
viewing direction, and material properties.
• Physical / math basis: Shading computes outgoing radiance Lo(x,ωo)L_o(\

reflectance model — typically a BRDF fr(ωi,ωo)f_r(\omega_i,\omega_o)fr​(ωi​,ωo​):
• Lo(x,ωo)=∫Ωfr(ωi,ωo) Li(ωi) (n⋅ωi) dωiL_o(\mathbf{x},\omega_o)=\int_{\Omega}
f_r(\omega_i,\omega_o)\,L_i(\omega_i)\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\omega_i)\,d\omega_iLo​
(x,ωo​)=∫Ω​fr​(ωi​,ωo​)Li​(ωi​)(n⋅ωi​)dωi​Common models: Lambertian diffuse (constant
diffuse), Phong/Blinn-Phong (empirical specular), Cook–Torrance microfacet
(physically-based: D, F, G terms).
• Implementation
• Offline / Ray tracing: evaluate BRDF integrals (Monte Carlo path tracing) for correct
global energy transfer.
• Real-time: use analytic BRDF approximations and light loops per pixel in a fragment
shader; modern engines use PBR (metalness/roughness + energy-conserving Cook–
Torrance).
• Gouraud vs Phong shading: vertex vs per-pixel; per-pixel gives better highlights.
• Artifacts & notes
• Incorrect normals cause wrong shading.
• Non-energy-conserving BRDFs produce unrealistic bright surfaces.
• Normal interpolation can cause specular artifacts if not per-pixel.
Texture Mapping
• ..
• Definition: Projecting a 2D image (texture) onto a 3D surface using UV coordinates to add fine
detail without extra geometry.
• Physical / math basis: A texture is a 2D function T(u,v)T(u,v)T(u,v). The shader samples TTT at
interpolated UVs. Issues: minification/magnification, aliasing.
• Implementation
• UV mapping: unwrap 3D model to 2D domain; seams and islands.
• Filtering: nearest, bilinear, trilinear, MIP-mapping to reduce aliasing (sample from lower-
resolution mip levels when surface is far).
• Anisotropic filtering for grazing angles.
• Atlases, arrays & arrays of textures to reduce draw calls.
• Artifacts & notes
• Texture seams: seams in UVs visible if normal/tangent not blended.
• Aliasing (moiré) without mipmaps/aniso.
• High-res textures cost memory; streaming and compression (BCn/ASTC) used in games.
Bump mapping
• Definition: Simulate small bumps/wrinkles by altering surface normals at shading time — without adding
geometry.
• Physical / math basis: Instead of changing vertex positions, perturb the surface normal n\mathbf{n}n per-
pixel. Normal maps store a perturbed normal in tangent space as RGB.
• Implementation
• Bump map (height map): single-channel height used to perturb normals (via gradient).
• Normal map: direct normal vectors in texture (RGB → XYZ).
• Parallax mapping / parallax occlusion mapping: give illusion of depth by offsetting UVs; POM traces steps
through height field for occlusion effect.
• Displacement mapping: actually move vertices (tessellation) — true geometry change.
• Artifacts & notes
• Normal maps break silhouette (no geometric change).
• Incorrect tangent basis yields lighting errors.
• Parallax is image-space and can cause popping if LOD not smoothed.
Fogging / Participating Medium
• Definition: Light interacting with a medium (air, fog, smoke) — scattering and absorption reduce
or change light before it reaches the camera.
• Physical / math basis
• Beer–Lambert law for attenuation: T=e−∫σt(s)dsT = e^{-\int \sigma_t(s) ds}T=e−∫σt​(s)ds where
σt\sigma_tσt​is extinction coefficient.
• Scattering: light redirected by particles — modeled with phase functions (e.g., Henyey–
Greenstein).
• Single vs multiple scattering: multiple scattering requires integrating contributions from light
after many scatters.
• Implementation
• Ray marching / volumetric rendering: sample medium along camera ray and integrate
extinction & in-scattered light.
• Light shafts / volumetric shadows: compute light visibility inside volume (shadow maps +
marching).
• Precomputed or sliced volumes (3D textures) for performance.
• Artifacts & notes
• Volumetric sampling is costly; use lower resolution or temporal accumulation.
• Banding if too few samples; noise if Monte Carlo scattering is used.
shadow
• Definition: Regions receiving less or no direct light because geometry blocks a light source.
• Physical / math basis: For a point on a surface, determine if the path to the light is occluded. With
ray tracing that's a visibility test; with rasterization you use shadow maps/volumes.
• Implementation
• Shadow mapping: render scene from light’s viewpoint into depth map; compare depth in camera
pass. Simple and versatile.
• Shadow volumes: extrude occluder silhouette into space and use stencil buffer; produces hard,
correct shadows.
• Ray traced shadows: accurate soft/hard shadows with ray casts.
• Artifacts & notes
• Shadow map aliasing (blocky or shimmering) — mitigated with higher resolution or cascaded
shadow maps (CSM) for large scenes.
• Peter Panning and self-shadow acne due to precision — use bias carefully.
• Shadow volumes are expensive on complex geometry.
Soft Shadows
• Definition: Shadows with gradual transition from light to dark (penumbra) caused by lights with
finite size (area lights).
• Physical / math basis: For area lights, parts of the light are occluded (umbra) and parts remain
visible (penumbra). Penumbra width depends on distance between occluder, receiver, and light
extent.
• Implementation
• Analytic / area-light integration: sample many points on the area light (multi-sample shadow
maps or Monte Carlo integration).
• PCF (percentage-closer filtering): blur shadow map samples for softer edges (cheap
approximation).
• PCSS (percentage-closer soft shadows): estimate blocker depth to scale filter kernel by
penumbra size (realistic-looking with cheap cost).
• Variance shadow maps (VSM) and Exponential shadow maps: enable faster filtered softening.
• Artifacts & notes
• Too few samples → noisy shadows.
• PCF blurs but does not reproduce correct geometric penumbra scale — PCSS better.
• Realistic area lights are expensive in rasterization; often baked or approximated.
Reflection
• Definition: Light bouncing off surfaces producing mirror-like or glossy returns.
• Physical / math basis: Reflection is a component of BRDF/BSDF. Specular reflection usually
modelled with microfacet BRDFs where microfacets reflect light according to Fresnel and
distribution DDD.
• Implementation
• Planar reflection: render scene mirrored across plane (perfect mirror).
• Environment mapping (cube maps): approximate distant reflections by sampling a
precomputed environment texture.
• Screen-space reflections (SSR): trace reflected rays in screen buffer for dynamic reflections,
but limited to visible pixels.
• Glossy reflection: importance sampling microfacet BRDF in path tracing; in real-time use
prefiltered environment maps (split-sum approximation) + BRDF LUT for specular IBL.
• Artifacts & notes
• SSR misses off-screen geometry → temporal artifacts.
• Cube maps cannot reflect dynamic objects unless updated.
• Glossy reflections require roughness parameter; high roughness = blurred reflection.
Transparency / Opacity
• Definition: Degree to which light passes straight through an object (transmission without
scattering).
• Physical / math basis: Transmission modeled in BSDF/BTDF terms. For simple
transparency you combine surface color and alpha. For volumetric transmission you
integrate through medium.
• Implementation
• Alpha blending: classic over operator: Cout=αsCs+(1−αs)CdC_{out} = \alpha_s C_s + (1-\
alpha_s) C_dCout​=αs​Cs​+(1−αs​)Cd​. Requires correct draw order (back-to-front).
• Order-independent transparency (OIT): depth peeling (exact, multi-pass), weighted
blended OIT (approx), stochastic transparency.
• Refraction: use Snell’s law to refract rays (see refraction section) for realistic effect.
• Artifacts & notes
• Sorting is expensive; translucent objects often need careful handling.
• Alpha-tested (cutout) vs blended transparency.
• Refraction combined with transparency must account for absorption and Fresnel.
Translucency / Subsurface
Scattering
• Definition: Light enters an object, scatters within, and exits at different points — gives soft
appearance (skin, wax, marble).
• Physical / math basis
• Modeled with BSSRDF S(xi,ωi,xo,ωo)S(\mathbf{x}_i,\omega_i,\mathbf{x}_o,\omega_o)S(xi​,ωi​,xo​
,ωo​) (Jensen’s dipole approximation is common).
• Diffusion approximation for highly scattering materials: light quickly becomes diffuse within
medium.
• Implementation
• Offline: full BSSRDF integration via path tracing or photon mapping.
• Real-time:
• Screen-space subsurface scattering (SSSS): blur lighting in screen-space using kernels
per color channel to mimic scattering (fast).
• Separable blur + depth & normal awareness to avoid bleeding across surfaces.
• Precomputed radiance transfer for static objects.
• Artifacts & notes
• SSS is view/lighting-dependent; screen-space methods can’t handle off-screen lighting well.
• Too much blur merges features; tune scattering radius per channel (red scatters more than blue).
Refraction
• Definition: Bending of light at an interface between materials with different indices of refraction (glass, water,
lenses).
• Physical / math basis: Snell’s law:
• n1sin⁡θ1=n2sin⁡θ2n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​Refracted ray direction computed from
incoming direction and normal; can create total internal reflection when sin⁡θ2>1\sin\theta_2>1sinθ2​>1.
• Implementation
• Ray tracing: compute refracted ray and continue path; handle dispersion (wavelength-dependent index) for
chromatic effects.
• Rasterization: approximate via environment mapping or thickness-based refraction (screen-space offsets using
depth).
• Fresnel: combine reflection and refraction depending on angle.
• Artifacts & notes
• Rasterized approximations can’t refract background that’s off-screen.
• Chromatic dispersion requires sampling wavelengths or RGB approximation.
Diffraction
• Definition: Wave-optics phenomenon: bending, spreading, and interference of light when it
encounters apertures/edges or periodic microstructures (gratings); produces fringes and spectral
effects.
• Physical / math basis: Requires wave optics — Huygens–Fresnel principle, Fraunhofer and
Fresnel diffraction integrals. Interference depends on wavelength λ\lambdaλ.
• Implementation
• Exact: evaluate diffraction integrals (Fourier transforms) — expensive, used in scientific
simulation and specialized rendering.
• Approximations:
• Spectral shading for gratings and iridescence (simulate microstructure BRDF).
• Use precomputed diffraction patterns or analytic models for thin-film interference (soap
bubbles, oil).
• Microfacet diffraction models for structural color (some advanced BRDFs).
• Artifacts & notes
• Diffraction is wavelength-sensitive — must render spectrally for full accuracy (costly).
• Often approximated purely artistically for visuals (e.g., iridescence maps).
Indirect Illumination (Global
Illumination)
• Definition: Light that reaches surfaces after bouncing off other surfaces rather than directly from
a light source.
• Physical / math basis: The rendering equation accounts for both direct and indirect light.
Indirect illumination is crucial for color bleeding and realistic contact lighting.
• Implementation
• Offline: path tracing, photon mapping, bidirectional path tracing (accurate, physically-based).
• Real-time: approximations:
• Light probes / reflection probes (baked environment captures).
• Indirect lightmaps (bake static GI into textures).
• Voxel cone tracing / grid-based GI: voxelize scene and perform approximate cone
tracing for dynamic GI.
• Screen-space GI (SSGI): cheap local approximation using depth buffer.
• Precomputed Radiance Transfer (PRT) for low-frequency GI on deformable objects.
• Artifacts & notes
• Baked GI is great for static scenes but fails for dynamic changes.
• Voxel-based and screen-space methods are approximate and can miss far-field bounces.
Depth of Field (DoF)
Definition: Some parts of the scene in focus while nearer/further areas are blurred, determined by lens
aperture and focus distance.
Physical / math basis: Thin-lens model: each pixel integrates rays through lens aperture; out-of-focus
points result in circles-of-confusion (blur kernels). Blur depends on aperture size and distance to focus
plane
Implementation
•Ray tracing / path tracing: jitter camera rays across lens aperture per sample — physically correct
DOF.
•Post-process DoF: generate a CoC (circle-of-confusion) map from depth, perform layered blurs or
gather filters; use bokeh shapes and depth-aware blending.
•Tile-based DoF: separate into near/in-focus/far layers and blur with different radii.
Artifacts & notes
•Post-process DoF can cause background leak into foreground if depth not handled correctly.
•Quality vs performance: real ray-based DoF is expensive; post-processing is cheaper but approximate .
Motion Blur
Definition: Blur produced by object motion or camera motion during exposure time, giving a sense of
speed.
Physical / math basis: Integrate radiance over time for each pixel: C=1T∫t0t0+TL(t)dtC = \frac{1}{T}\
int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} L(t) dtC=T1​∫t0​t0​+T​L(t)dt. Movement of objects over exposure time yields smeared
images.
Implementation
•Object-space / camera-space: jitter rays in time with velocity; path tracing handles transparently.
•Screen-space velocity buffer: store per-pixel motion vectors (object motion + camera motion) and
apply blur post-process using motion vectors to smear pixels in direction & magnitude of motion.
•Accumulation buffer: temporal accumulation across frames (works with careful weighting).
Artifacts & notes
•Motion vector precision / disocclusion handling crucial (artifacts where objects move into previously
occluded areas).
•Screen-space methods fail for large motions and off-screen content.

Non-Photorealistic
Rendering (NPR)
Definition: Rendering styles that intentionally deviate from photorealism to achieve artistic looks
(cartoon, ink/watercolor, technical illustrations).
Approaches / algorithms
•Cel/Toon shading: quantize lighting into discrete bands + edge outlines (silhouette and feature
edges).
•Stroke-based / painterly: simulate brush strokes placed adaptively based on image features.
•Hatching / cross-hatching: procedural strokes driven by tone/curvature.
•Image-space filters: bilateral filter + stylization, edge detection (Sobel/Laplacian) combined with
color quantization.
•Technical illustration: combine contours, hidden-line removal, and simplified shading.
Applications
•Animation (cartoons), stylized games, scientific visualization, architectural illustration.
Artifacts & notes
•NPR can require artist control (stroke placement, stylization parameters).
•Combine geometry-based edges with screen-space edges for robust outlines.
