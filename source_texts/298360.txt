Model-based rigid and nonrigid volumetric image registration for
image-guided interventions

Abstract
Purpose Accurate image registration is essential for aligning preoperative and intraoperative scans in image-guided neuro
interventions, where even small misalignments can compromise navigation. This work introduces a model-based framework
for rigid and nonrigid volumetric registration using shape-constrained deformable brain segmentation to establish anatomical
point-based correspondence.
Methods Unlike traditional methods, the registration relies solely on anatomical geometry. Rigid registration aligns centroids
and estimates transformations between segmented meshes. Nonrigid registration fits B-spline surfaces to corresponding mesh
vertices, generating smooth deformation fields that capture local anatomical changes.
Results Quantitative validation was performed using synthetically transformed MR scans. Additional structure-specific
analysis confirmed accuracy across multiple brain regions. The rigid registration performed noninferiorly to an FDA-cleared
approach, while the nonrigid registration effectively captured realistic deformations such as those resulting from brain shift.
Both methods offer high accuracy and computational efficiency.
Conclusion The proposed model-based registration framework offers a robust, anatomically driven alternative to conventional
image-based registration methods that eliminates reliance on image intensity. It results in improved registration accuracy and
demonstrates strong clinical potential for integration into interventional workflows, with the promise of enhancing procedural
precision, safety, and patient outcomes.

Introduction
Image-guided neurointerventions have significantly advanced
neurosurgery by enabling precise, minimally invasive procedures such as targeted drug delivery [1, 2], biopsy [3,
4], laser ablation [5, 6], and deep brain stimulation [7, 8].
These procedures demand submillimeter targeting accuracy,
yet achieving this level of precision is often challenged
by the limited spatial resolution and soft tissue contrast of
intraoperative imaging modalities. To mitigate these limitations, image registration, a computational method that

Image registration approaches are commonly categorized
as rigid or nonrigid. Rigid registration is restricted to rotations
and translations, preserving structural distances and angles,
and is therefore well suited for aligning images of rigid
anatomy such as the skull. In contrast, nonrigid registration
permits local deformations and is essential when anatomical changes occur between preoperative and intraoperative
scans. Such changes are common during neurosurgical procedures, where brain shift may arise from cerebrospinal fluid
leakage [12, 13], alterations in intracranial pressure [14], or
gravitational effects [15]. Nonrigid registration is typically
performed after an initial rigid alignment step [9] and aims

to improve soft tissue correspondence by explicitly modeling these deformations. Biomechanical modeling approaches
updated with sparse intraoperative measurements offer an
alternative strategy [16, 17], but their reliance on limited and
often difficult-to-acquire intraoperative data restricts their
widespread clinical adoption.
Historically, image-based registration methods have been
computationally demanding [18]. Achieving clinically acceptable accuracy at full image resolution still requires substantial
computational resources [19, 20]. These demands are particularly problematic in time-sensitive intraoperative settings,
where computational delays can disrupt surgical workflows.
The performance of image-based similarity metrics is highly
sensitive to the quality of intraoperative imaging [21, 22].
Factors such as reduced spatial resolution, limited field of
view, poor tissue contrast, imaging artifacts, and navigational
frames, as illustrated in Fig. 1, can substantially degrade
registration accuracy. Surgical tools that appear in only one
image further complicate alignment by corrupting similarity
metrics and misleading machine learning-based approaches
trained on limited or idealized datasets. Achieving accurate
and robust registration under realistic intraoperative conditions still remains a challenge. While advances in hardware
and machine learning have alleviated some computational
constraints [23], point-based registration methods continue
to play a critical role and are often more robust under
degraded imaging conditions [24]. More importantly, when
conventional image-based registration fails due to poor image
quality, anatomical distortion, or other factors, the entire
image-guided intervention can be compromised. By offering
a point-based approach with noninferior accuracy, clinicians
have a dependable fallback that can improve the overall
robustness and safety of surgical navigation.
The proposed rigid and nonrigid registration framework
builds on point-based correspondence derived from shapeconstrained brain segmentation [25]. As MR or CT scans
become available within the neuronavigation platform, they
are automatically segmented to enable registration. Although
segmentation is required as an initial enabling step, the registration framework is designed to support rapid updates once
segmentation outputs are available. The GPU-accelerated
segmentation enforces anatomical shape constraints and
produces consistent vertex correspondence across surface
meshes. Rigid registration is performed by aligning mesh
centroids and estimating the optimal rotation and translation parameters. Nonrigid registration is achieved by fitting
B-spline surfaces to the corresponding mesh vertices, resulting in a smooth, continuous three-dimensional deformation
field. Both registration stages are computationally efficient
and are designed to support single and multimodal (MR/CT)
alignment. The validation compares the proposed rigid registration against a clinically approved image-based rigid
registration and assesses the ability of the nonrigid regis-

tration to accurately recover local deformations. Synthetic
datasets generated by applying known transformations to a
reference scan are well suited for this evaluation, as they
enable controlled, quantitative assessment of deformation
accuracy.
The contribution of this work lies in the task-specific integration of well established shape-constrained deformable
brain segmentation, point-based rigid, and B-spline nonrigid registration. The proposed framework leverages shapeconstrained segmentation to provide anatomically derived
correspondence that directly influences the stability, convergence, and accuracy of both rigid and nonrigid registration.
The mean shape representation reduces errors in landmark
correspondence and deformation estimation, in contrast with
traditional point-based registration methods that rely on
sparse or noisy point sets. The performance of the pointbased rigid registration is evaluated against a clinically
deployed image-based registration. Furthermore, the nonrigid B-spline deformation is guided by dense anatomical
correspondences instead of intensity-based similarity measures or sparse point matches alone, resulting in deformation
fields that are anatomically consistent and better suited for
interventional image-guided navigation.

Methods
Shape-constrained deformable brain segmentation
The shape-constrained deformable brain segmentation is a
fully automated process originally designed for T1-weighted
MR scans and then extended to CT head segmentation. It
integrates a shape-constrained deformable brain model with
voxel-wise tissue classification within the cerebral hemispheres and cerebellum [25]. The segmentation consists
of two main steps. In the first step, brain structures are
segmented using closed surface meshes through a deterministic, shape-constrained approach. This is formulated as
an iterative energy minimization problem, where the model
penalizes deviations from a mean mesh derived from training
data while adapting it to patient-specific anatomical boundaries. The model’s total energy combines internal energy,
which enforces similarity to the mean mesh, and external energy, which guides deformation toward image-derived
boundaries using boundary detector functions. These boundary detectors are deterministic, single-valued functions based
on image intensity gradients along mesh normals. A convolutional neural network (CNN) is used to identify the most
probable boundary point for each mesh triangle during each
iteration. Since the CNN operates as a fixed convolution
filter, it produces consistent outputs for identical inputs. Segmentation concludes when the total model energy reaches
its minimum, defining the final surface mesh representation
of brain structures. In the second step, the regions enclosed
by the segmented surfaces are parcellated into white matter (WM), gray matter (GM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)
using a CNN based on the F-Net architecture [26]. This step
is also deterministic: once the F-Net is trained, its learned
weights are applied without modification, ensuring consistent voxel-wise labeling across scans. Figure 2 illustrates
two views of the current mesh geometry in the shapeconstrained deformable brain segmentation, which includes
the amygdala, brainstem, caudate, cerebellum, hemispheres,
hippocampus, globus pallidus, lateral ventricles, putamen,
thalamus, and third ventricle. Although not shown in the figure, the segmentation also includes cortical and cerebellar
tissue parcellation into WM, GM, and CSF.
The segmentation provides a robust, anatomically grounded
correspondence that supports subsequent rigid and nonrigid
registration. A shape-constrained segmentation approach
was chosen over alternative methods because it leverages
a mean shape to establish accurate point-based correspondence, thereby reducing the sensitivity of registration to
local minima and initialization errors. The shape-constrained
deformable brain segmentation has been previously validated and demonstrated to perform reliably across a wide
range of brain anatomies, making it a stable and suitable first
stage of the overall pipeline. The resulting segmentations are
smooth and anatomically correct, and they effectively guide
both landmark correspondence and deformation modeling.
Importantly, the segmentation is used as an enabling component of the proposed registration workflow rather than as
a novel methodological contribution. The primary novelty
of the present work lies in how the segmentation output is
integrated into and used by the registration, not in the segmentation method itself.
Image registration
Image registration aligns images into a common coordinate
space by transforming a moving image to match a fixed reference image. Depending on the transformation, registration
methods are broadly classified as rigid or nonrigid [9, 27].
Rigid registration is limited to rotation and translation and
preserves distances between anatomical structures. In this
work, the performance of the rigid registration is compared
against an image-based registration method that is cleared for
clinical use on ClearPoint Neuro navigation platforms and
routinely used for image-guided interventions. This method
employs mutual information as its similarity metric, a widely

Quantitative validation of model-based nonrigid
registration
To evaluate the accuracy of the model-based nonrigid registration, a known thin-plate spline deformation [32] was
applied to an MR scan. To simulate realistic brain shift,
the deformation targeted anatomically relevant structures,
specifically the ventricles and corpus callosum. An illustration of this process is shown in Fig. 4. Deformation
magnitudes were restricted to 0-10 mm, reflecting brain shift
expected in minimally invasive image-guided interventions
[33]. Using this method, a validation dataset of (n=64)
deformed scans was generated. Each scan was segmented
using the shape-constrained deformable brain model, and
the resulting segmentation meshes were used to align the
deformed scans with the original MR images using the
model-based nonrigid registration. In addition to measuring surface distances between corresponding vertices on the
original and registered meshes, two additional quantitative
metrics were computed to assess registration accuracy: Dice
coefficient and volume difference. These metrics were also
instrumental in the regulatory clearance of the ClearPoint
Maestro system [25].
Results
The model-based rigid registration computed the transformation in approximately 0.76 ms on average. The model-based
nonrigid registration method required an average of approximately 2.9 s to compute the dense deformation field using a
7-level B-spline pyramid. Reducing the pyramid to 6 levels
significantly decreased the computation time to the millisecond range, but introduced a slight reduction in registration
accuracy. This illustrates the inherent tradeoff between computational efficiency and registration accuracy. Nevertheless,
the ability to compute dense deformation fields within milliseconds to a few seconds underlines the practicality of
the proposed nonrigid registration method for time-sensitive
applications, including potential integration into near-realtime clinical workflows where preprocessing with the shapeconstrained segmentation is performed beforehand when the

Quantitative validation of model-based nonrigid
registration
To evaluate the accuracy of the proposed model-based nonrigid registration, a series of controlled deformations were
applied to MR scans using thin-plate spline transformations.
These deformations were designed to mimic realistic brain
shift, targeting anatomically significant structures such as
the ventricles and corpus callosum, with displacement magnitudes ranging from 0 to 10 mm. A validation dataset of
64 synthetically deformed scans was generated, and each
was segmented using the shape-constrained deformable brain
model. The resulting segmentation meshes were used to align
the deformed scans back to their original configuration using
the model-based nonrigid registration. Figure 6 illustrates
the outcome of the nonrigid registration process. This visual
comparison highlights the accuracy of the deformation and
the preservation of anatomical correspondence across structures.
Registration accuracy was assessed using three quantitative metrics: mean surface distance, Dice coefficient, and
volume difference. The model-based method achieved a
mean surface distance of 0.13 ± 0.01 mm, a Dice coefficient of 95.86% ± 0.64%, and volume difference of 1.78%
± 0.56% across all structures. Figures 7, 8, and 9 illustrate
nonrigid registration accuracy for individual brain structures. These metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of
anatomical alignment across the different brain structures
following nonrigid surface-based registration. The obtained
results demonstrate that the model-based approach provides
accurate and anatomically consistent nonrigid alignment,
supporting its potential for intraoperative use where rapid
alignment of brain structures is critical.
Discussion
The results demonstrate that model-based registration, grounded in anatomical geometry rather than image intensity,
can achieve accuracy comparable to established imagebased methods. Leveraging shape-constrained segmentation
and consistent anatomical correspondences, the model-based
rigid registration provides a robust, computationally efficient
alternative well suited for neurointerventions. The nonrigid
registration extends this capability by accounting for local
geometric deformations, such as brain shift. These findings
are supported by quantitative evaluation using synthetically
generated MR deformations, where controlled transformations allow precise measurement of registration accuracy.
Synthetic deformations were used to provide ground-truth
evaluation of nonrigid registration, which is otherwise difficult to obtain with clinical datasets. This approach allows
exact calculation of surface distances, Dice overlap, and
volume differences, providing unambiguous quantitative validation. The focus of this study is on assessing the integrated
framework, which tightly couples shape-constrained segmentation with rigid and nonrigid registration. Because most
existing nonrigid registration methods do not incorporate
comparable anatomical point-based correspondence, direct
comparison would require extensive reimplementation and
could be misleading. Within this context, the reported metrics
demonstrate that the deformation fields are smooth, stable, and robust with no self-intersections. Comparisons with
external nonrigid registration methods remain an important
future direction; the present validation emphasizes internal
reliability rather than superiority.

Model-based registration accuracy depends on the quality of the underlying segmentation. Submillimeter-accurate
segmentations enable highly precise rigid and nonrigid
alignment, whereas lower segmentation accuracy directly
limits registration performance. Triangular surface meshes
representing anatomical regions closely approximate true
anatomy. Quantitative evaluation using Dice coefficient and
volume difference demonstrates that dense deformation
fields derived from sparse point-based correspondence can
be generalized to unmodeled regions, such as cortical and
cerebellar gray and white matter. While further evaluation
is needed for brain tumor alignment, the proposed modelbased registration framework, leveraging shape-constrained
deformable brain models [25], can be extended to tumor surfaces, highlighting its versatility and potential for broader
neurointerventional applications.
Beyond the controlled validation experiments presented
here, the model-based registration framework may support
multimodal and intraoperative imaging workflows. MRto-CT registration is particularly critical for image-guided
neurointerventions, where accurate localization of anatomical structures directly impacts surgical safety. Combining
preoperative MR with intraoperative CT merges the soft tissue contrast of MR with the bony anatomy of CT, supporting
precise navigation. Low-dose intraoperative CBCT, however, often exhibits artifacts and limited soft tissue contrast,
which can degrade conventional MR-to-CBCT registration
accuracy. A common workaround involves aligning MR
to a diagnostic CT before registering to CBCT, but this
introduces additional radiation exposure. Model-based registration, leveraging shape-constrained segmentation in both
MR and CBCT, provides a robust alternative that establishes

Conclusion
This manuscript presents an anatomical geometry driven
framework for both rigid and nonrigid volumetric image registration, developed to support image-guided neuro interventions. By leveraging shape-constrained brain segmentation
and its inherent point-based correspondence, the proposed
model-based methods achieve high registration accuracy
without relying on image intensity values. Quantitative validation using synthetic image-deformations demonstrated
that the model-based rigid registration is noninferior to an
FDA-cleared image-based method across a number of different subcortical brain regions. Similarly, the nonrigid registration approach effectively corrected anatomically realistic
deformations, achieving strong performance across surface
distance, Dice coefficient, and volume difference metrics.
The ability to perform rapid and accurate registration using
only anatomical geometry offers significant advantages in
intraoperative settings, where image quality may be an issue
and computational efficiency is critical. These findings highlight the clinical promise of the proposed methods and their
potential integration into interventional workflows, potentially enhancing precision, safety, and patient outcomes.
