Abstract
Model-based image reconstruction algorithms are known to produce high-accuracy images but are
still rarely used in cone beam computed tomography. One of the reasons for this is the computational
requirements of model-based iterative algorithms, as it can take hundreds of iterations to obtain
converged images. In this work, we present a measurement space-based preconditioner applied to the
primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) algorithm. The method is compared with the regular PDHG,
FISTA, and OS-SART algorithms, as well as to a PDHG algorithm where the step-size parameters are
adaptively computed. All tested algorithms utilize subsets for acceleration. The presented filteringbased preconditioner can obtain convergence in 10 iterations with 20 subsets, compared to a hundred
or more iterations required by the other tested methods. The presented method is also computationally
fast and has only a 15% increase in computation time per iteration compared to PDHG without the
preconditioner.

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) is a
CT imaging technique with more specialized applications than the typical helical CT used in
hospital settings [1–4]. The benefits of CBCT
when compared to helical CT are the lower dose
and the easier capability for specific region of interest (ROI) scans. Disadvantages include the loss
of contrast due to increased amount of scatter [5]
The image reconstruction process is similar
for helical CT and CBCT. However, CBCT has
larger detector panels, leading to a larger number of voxels in the image volumes, and usually
1
or increase in noise. Secondly, FDK can fail if
non-standard scanning geometry is used. Thirdly,
iterative reconstruction methods allow modeling
of physical phenomena during image reconstruction, such as scatter, unlike FDK, leading to
improved reconstruction accuracy. While iterative
methods have many advantages when compared
to FDK, they are slow to compute for large-scale
3D problems which is commonly the challenge in
CBCT.
To solve these computational issues, one approach is to reduce the number of iterations required for an optimal image. The fewer iterations
we need, the faster the final image is computed.
The issue with early iterations is that the image
tends to be blurry [8]. This is also problematic if
a quick initial image is required to assess whether
the examination must be redone owing to errors
such as incorrect positioning. While an FDK reconstruction can be done almost in real-time, it
can be poor in low-dose or limited-angle cases due
to artifacts and/or noise. If an iterative algorithm
has slow convergence, and a new scan is required,
the time needed for the final image increases as
the initial image also takes more time to compute. A sufficiently high-quality image in the early
iterations would also be beneficial.
Another significant issue is to compute the
iterations as fast as possible. Even with fewer iterations, the iterative reconstruction framework can
be too slow if a single iteration takes too long.
In the past decade, this issue has been solved by
using graphics processing units (GPUs) computing. Since in CT imaging, we deal with millions or
billions of independent rays and voxels, the process can be very effectively parallelized. While a
single GPU thread is slower than a single CPU
thread, the thousands of additional threads give
a significant advantage over standard CPU-based
computation.
Reducing the number of iterations needed for
reconstruction can generally be done by using, for
example, some or a combination of the following
methods: use a more efficient algorithm that converges faster, use a more optimal step-size value,
use a preconditioner or multiple preconditioners,
or dividing the measurement data into subsets
that can be computed in smaller subiterations. In
this work, we focus on the last two aspects, i.e.
preconditioning and subsets but we also compare
the presented methods to the use of optimized
step-size values. Preconditioners are additional
matrices used as weights to move the solution
towards the desired direction [9, 10]. Commonly
preconditioners operate in the image space, i.e. on
the image estimate [11–16], but it is also possible to utilize measurement space preconditioners
[8, 17–19] as we demonstrate in this work. Subsets,
on the other hand, operate on the measurement
space by dividing the measurements into S subsets
[20, 21]. While only a subset of the measurements
is used, the entire image volume is updated with
every subset.
In this work we present a measurement spacebased preconditioner, derived for the primal-dual
hybrid gradient (PDHG) algorithm [22, 23]. The
preconditioner uses frequency space filtering in the
measurement space and is similar to the methods
presented in [8, 18]. In [8] a similar preconditioner is used with magnetic resonance imaging
(MRI) data which is already in the frequency
space and in [18] a similar filtering preconditioner is used with a conjugate gradient algorithm.
[24] is a similar approach to ours but uses a
different algorithm, utilizes fan-beam data, does
not use subsets for acceleration, does not employ
GPU computing, and does not consider non-linear
regularizers. [25] uses proximal forward-backward
splitting with Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) operator for combined analytical and iterative update
and a separate denoising step. [26, 27] use similar
filtering in an iterative FDK [7] type reconstruction. This work can be considered as a further
extension of these previous works. The benefits of
this work compared to the previous work is the
implementation, and derivation, of the filtering
straight into the PDHG algorithm. Furthermore,
since the filtering is applied to the dual estimate,
it does not affect the primal estimate or any regularizer, whether linear or non-linear, included.
The filtering can thus also be used with, for example, three-operator methods [28, 29] allowing for a
broader scale of convex regularizers.
We also utilize subsets with all the algorithms
to further accelerate the convergence [30–33].
However, unlike the original articles using subsets with PDHG, we employ fixed subsets rather
than randomly chosen ones. This was done purely
to simplify the implementation though further acceleration to convergence might be achieved with
the original random permutation. The presented
2
acceleration methods are compared with subsetonly PDHG [30, 31], an adaptive method for
the primal, and dual, step-size computation [34]
as well as to fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding
algorithm (FISTA) with subsets [32, 35] and
ordered-subset simultaneous algebraic reconstruction techniques (OS-SART) [33, 36] algorithms.
The focus of this work is purely on the convergence
speed and thus we mainly focus on minimization of non-regularized least-squares data misfit.
However, we also present comparison of the filtered and adaptive step-size PDHG algorithms
with total variation (TV) [37] regularization as
an example that the presented filtering is applicable with regularization and can provide faster
convergence with regularization as well. In addition to the presented FISTA and OS-SART, we
also tested Krylov-based methods CGLS [38] and
LSQR [39]. However, these are not included here
as the algorithms did not offer any computational
advantages in terms of convergence speed and are
applicable to linear quadratic regularization only,
limiting their practical use.
PDHG has many advantages over other algorithms, such as the tested FISTA or OS-SART, as
well as other methods, such as Krylov-based algorithms. One of the main advantages of PDHG, and
its variants, is their flexibility with different regularization methods. PDHG allows the use of nondifferentiable regularization methods such as TV
but also enables the use of gradient-based regularization with three-operator algorithms. PDHGtype algorithms also allow for the easy inclusion of
constraints to the optimization and are also very
beneficial for the presented filtering-based preconditioner since the algorithm contains a separate
dual estimate. Due to the dual estimate, the preconditioner can be mathematically derived for the
algorithm.
This paper is an extension of our previously
presented conference paper [40]. Here we present
the full derivation of the filtering preconditioner.
We also utilize an additional dataset in the results,
the adaptive step-size method, comparisons with
FISTA and OS-SART, computation times of each
tested method, and expanded discussion on the
subject.
The paper is organized as follows. Section II
presents the mathematical background of PDHG,
PDHG with subsets as well as the filtering-based
preconditioner. We also present the algorithms for
OS-SART and subset-based FISTA. Section III
includes the measurement setup and the reconstruction properties. In section IV we present the
comparisons with two different datasets. Finally,
section V contains the discussion and conclusions.

All reconstructions were computed with a fixed

the subset. This means the first subset included
projection numbers 1, 21, 41, etc., the second

a single subset are considered subiterations. A
full-convergence ground truth image was reconstructed using 500 iterations and 20 subsets with

The measurement space preconditioner based
on high-pass filtering has a significant improvement in the convergence speed. It is also computationally simple and only increases the computation time by around 15% per iteration when
compared with the unmodified PDHG, yet the
reduction of the number of iterations is significantly greater. The same level of convergence as
500 iterations of regular PDHG can be obtained
with only 10 iterations and over 38 times faster.
The method based on adaptive step-sizes is an alternative to increase the convergence speed. The
adaptive method could also be combined with the
filtering, but in our tests, this did not have a
noticeable impact on the convergence. The downside of the adaptive method is that it is generally
computationally more intensive and requires more
memory than the filtering-based preconditioner.
When using subsets, the filtering also has the
advantage that it only needs to operate on the
current subset, while the adaptive method operates on the image volume. Another potential
downside of the adaptive method is the possible inclusion of multi-resolution reconstruction, as
outlined in our other paper [54]. With the multiresolution reconstruction, it is beneficial to have
one dual step-size variable although each multiresolution volume would have its own individual
primal step-size values. A mismatch between the
dual and primal step-sizes, however, can lead to
divergence. While it may be possible to scale the
primal step-size such that the dual step-size remains at 1, this can have a negative impact on
the convergence speed and reduce the beneficial
effect of the adaptive method. It is also possible
to improve the convergence speed of the adaptive method by finding more optimal parameters,
but this requires manual tuning and might be required for each measurement setup. One benefit of
the presented method is that no tuning is needed
between different measurement setups.

Compared to OS-SART or FISTA, the adaptive PDHG and the filtering-based PDHG provide faster convergence speeds. When comparing
the regular PDHG with OS-SART or FISTA,
OS-SART is the slowest method. The difference between PDHG and FISTA is small, but
PDHG, based on these results, can be considered
slightly faster. The filtering-based preconditioner
has several benefits when combined with PDHG.
Not only is the derivation of the preconditioner
straightforward for PDHG, but PDHG additionally allows the use of a broad range of convex
regularizers.
The presented preconditioner also works well
with regularization methods. In this work, we
showcased TV regularization with the filtering
preconditioner and compared it with the adaptive
step-size method as these two provided the most
converged image. The fast convergence of the algorithm is also present when using the regularization
and the filtering is also more reliable in convergence than the adaptive step-sizes. The downside
of the adaptive step-sizes is that they can have
a negative impact on the convergence speed of
the regularization. Using heuristic methods, this
can be mitigated. However, since the filtering preconditioner already achieves faster convergence, it
also provides a sharp regularized image and does
not need any heuristic modifications.
A potential downside of the filtering-based
preconditioner is that it can emphasize highfrequency components, meaning that the convergence of the contrast might be slower, or edges
might be emphasized too much. However, in all
our tests, the convergence was very good also for
contrast. It is possible, however, to only use the
filtering at the early iterations and after that use
regular iterations. This would, in a sense, serve
as an initial value for the basic PDHG algorithm.
The Hamming windowing also reduced the emphasis on the high-frequency components and thus
helped with the convergence of the contrast as the
HU values tend to be smaller when no windowing is used. The use of other windowing methods
has some effect on the convergence rate, but there
do not seem to be big differences between different windowing methods. Hamming, Hann, Parzen,
cosine, Gaussian, and Shepp-Logan windowing
methods were tested. Using filtered steps and then
adaptive steps was also tested, but the extra computational cost of the adaptive method removes
any benefit given by the slightly faster convergence
speed.
We remark that the filtering can be combined
with convex regularization functionals, whether
linear or non-linear. Any variation of the PDHG,
such as the Condat-Vũ (CV) algorithm [28, 29]
or primal-dual Davis-Yin algorithm [55], can utilize the presented filtering-based preconditioner.
The extension to regularized and non-linear formulations will be the direction of our future
work for accelerated model-based CBCT reconstruction with limited or low-dose CBCT data.
Compared to OS-SART and FISTA, the PDHGtype algorithms allow for much higher flexibility
in the choice of the regularizer. FISTA and OSSART would require separate sub-problems for
the regularizers, while CV or PDHG do not.
One potential future topic for improvement
to the convergence rate of the contrast could be
extensions of the presented filtering-based preconditioner. One possibility could be to use relaxed
weighting for the filtering, where the strength of
the filter reduces over time. The challenge here
would be, however, to find the proper reduction
rate. Another possibility would be to study twostep filtering, i.e. first use the presented high-pass
filter and later switch to a low-pass filter.
All the algorithms presented in this paper,
as well as the image space preconditioners mentioned in the introduction, are part of our opensource multi-dimensional tomographic reconstruction software (OMEGA) package, available for
MATLAB, GNU Octave, and Python. The algorithms can be applied to any tomographic data
and not just CBCT. OMEGA is available from
GitHub: https://github.com/villekf/OMEGA/.
The high-pass filter preconditioner presented
in this paper provided a significant improvement
in convergence speed compared to the regular
PDHG, PDHG using adaptive step-sizes, FISTA,
or OS-SART. The preconditioner is also computationally fast and easy to implement. Combined
with subsets an optimal image estimate can be
achieved in as little as 10 iterations and under a
minute. The method is also fully compatible with
any convex regularization technique.
