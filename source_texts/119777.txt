A swept-volume display is a display that creates a three-dimensional image by moving a two-dimensional surface, mirror, diffuser, or light array through a physical volume while changing the image shown on it.[1] Each rapidly updated slice contributes visible points, or voxels, at different locations in space. When the sweep repeats quickly enough, visual persistence and neural integration fuse the slices into one apparent 3D object.
Unlike stereoscopic screens, most Light-field displays, and head-mounted displays, a swept-volume system places light inside a bounded volume rather than only simulating depth from a flat viewing plane. Multiple viewers can walk around many swept-volume displays without glasses and see different sides of the same object. That strength comes with a defining cost: the image depends on high-speed mechanical motion, tight synchronization, protective enclosures, and content pipelines that can slice 3D data fast enough for display.
Definition and Scope
Swept-volume displays are a subtype of true volumetric display, often described as mechanically scanned volumetric displays. Their defining feature is that a physical image-bearing element is swept through the display volume. The element may be a spinning diffuser, a reciprocating translucent screen, a rotating LED blade, a mirror, a phosphor plate, or another surface that becomes visible only when illuminated or energized at the right position.
The scope is narrower than the broad cultural word “hologram.” A swept-volume display is not an optical holographic display, because it does not reconstruct a coherent wavefront by interference. It is not a digital holographic display, because it does not compute phase patterns for a spatial light modulator. It also differs from fog and mist volumetric displays, which project onto airborne particles, and from Laser-induced plasma displays, which create luminous points in air or gas. The closest sibling is the solid-state volumetric display, which tries to create voxels inside a fixed medium without sweeping a macroscopic surface.

Historical Context and Evolution
The idea of building floating 3D images by rapid scanning predates modern flat panels. Early 1960s and 1970s laboratory systems included varifocal-mirror displays, in which a thin mirror or film vibrated so a projected image appeared at changing depths. Sher's 1972 patent is a classic example: depth planes were projected onto a moving mirror quickly enough for the viewer to perceive a 3D reconstruction.[2] These systems established the central pattern—synchronized slices plus motion—but were limited by projection speed, analog control, and the difficulty of preparing many depth planes.
During the 1980s and 1990s, researchers explored rotating screens, spinning phosphor plates, and volumetric CRT variants. Barry Blundell's color volumetric CRT sphere is one representative lineage: an electron-beam system projected color-coded light onto a spinning phosphor plate.[3] Such experiments showed that true volumetric images were possible, but they remained specialized, mechanically complex, and hard to scale.
A major commercial milestone was Actuality Systems' Perspecta display in the early 2000s. Gregg Favalora and collaborators described a 100-million-voxel swept-volume display using high-speed DLP projection and a rotating diffuser.[4] Contemporary industry accounts described roughly 198–200 projected slices and volume update rates in the tens of hertz; reported prototype and product figures vary, but the important distinction is between projector slice rate (thousands of 2D images per second) and volumetric refresh (complete 3D volumes per second). Perspecta made medical scans, engineering models, and molecular structures visible as walk-around objects, but its price, size, power draw, and mechanical enclosure kept it in professional and research niches.

Large-format research continued after Perspecta. Sawalha and collaborators' 2012 Large 3D Swept-Volume Display used several rotating LED-panel assemblies arranged as cylindrical columns, with FPGA control and image-partitioning algorithms for real-time video.[5] This work matters because it treated scale as both an optical and computational problem: adjacent swept volumes had to overlap, partition a scene, and remain synchronized.
The 2010s brought smaller commercial and semi-commercial examples. Voxon Photonics' VX1 used a rapidly moving translucent screen with rear projection to render a tabletop volume, while cheap LED persistence-of-vision “hologram fans” popularized a much simpler rotating-light version for signage.[6] The result is a split field: research systems pursue higher voxel density, occlusion, and content pipelines, while commercial devices often emphasize demonstrations, education, advertising, and visual impact.
Core Mechanisms and Processes
Every swept-volume display coordinates three things: the geometry of the sweep, the timing of the light, and the representation of the 3D content.
Rotational scanning spins a screen, disk, helical surface, paddle, LED arm, or mirror around an axis. A complete rotation fills a cylindrical, spherical, or toroidal region depending on the moving element's shape. LED fan displays are the simplest public example: LEDs on rotating blades flash at controlled angles to paint an image.
Reciprocating scanning moves a surface back and forth along a line, usually vertically or horizontally. The Voxon VX1 family uses this logic: a translucent projection surface moves through a column while a projector shows the correct cross-section for each height.
Orbital, helical, and multi-panel scanning use non-planar paths or multiple offset surfaces. These designs can improve fill factor or scale but make calibration, balancing, and slice mapping harder.
A graphics pipeline slices a 3D scene into many 2D cross-sections. A motor encoder or position sensor reports where the moving element is; a controller or GPU then displays the slice assigned to that position. If the sweep repeats at roughly cinema/video rates, usually tens of complete volumes per second, the viewer perceives a stable object rather than a flickering sequence of planes. The required 2D update rate is much higher than the volume rate: a 24-volume/s display with 200 slices per volume needs about 4,800 slice images per second before accounting for color channels, interleaving, or blanking.
Several specifications should be kept separate:
Volumetric refresh rate counts complete 3D volumes per second. It determines flicker and motion smoothness.
Slice rate counts how many 2D patterns the projector, LED array, or light engine can change per second.
Addressable voxel locations estimate the grid of possible 3D points, such as in-plane pixels multiplied by slice positions.
Voxel throughput counts point updates per second and is often a marketing-friendly number. It should not be confused with simultaneously visible independent voxels.
Effective resolution is lower than the ideal grid when blur, diffuser thickness, mechanical wobble, angular sampling, and viewer distance are included.
The visual advantages are real but bounded. Since light is emitted or scattered from physical positions, viewers get motion parallax and natural accommodation over the display volume. However, most swept-volume voxels emit broadly rather than directionally. Back surfaces can shine through front surfaces, making objects look translucent or ghostly unless content is designed around that limitation. Occlusion-capable variants have been researched, but ordinary swept-volume displays should not be described as equivalent to opaque real objects.
Mechanical engineering is therefore as important as optics. A useful display needs balanced moving parts, low vibration, repeatable position sensing, enough brightness during very short dwell times, cooling, noise control, and a safety enclosure. Larger swept surfaces increase stored kinetic energy and tolerances; smaller systems are safer and cheaper but offer less volume and resolution.

Methods of Study and Evaluation
Research on swept-volume displays combines optical engineering, mechanical design, electronics, computer graphics, and human perception.
Scan-geometry modeling determines what volume a moving surface covers, how many slices are needed, and where sampling density becomes uneven. Multi-column displays also need overlap and partitioning calculations so the same voxel is not duplicated or missed.
Encoder and timing analysis verifies that slice images arrive at the correct physical position. Oscilloscopes, logic analyzers, motor encoders, and high-speed cameras reveal jitter, phase drift, dropped slices, and rotational wobble.
Photometric and temporal measurement uses cameras, photodiodes, and calibrated test patterns to measure brightness, flicker, voxel persistence, color uniformity, contrast, and angular visibility. Because dwell time is short, peak brightness and duty cycle must be measured together.
Mechanical balancing and safety testing measures vibration, acoustic noise, enclosure integrity, bearing wear, thermal behavior, and fault modes. These tests are not secondary: a high-speed diffuser or LED arm can be dangerous if a motor, mount, or guard fails.
Content-pipeline evaluation measures how 3D models, CT/MRI volumes, point clouds, or animation data are sliced and rendered. Real-time interaction adds latency constraints, while medical or engineering use adds accuracy and annotation requirements.
Perceptual and task studies compare depth judgments, collaboration, fatigue, and task performance against flat screens, stereoscopic displays, light-field displays, AR/VR, and physical models. The best use cases are usually those where shared walk-around depth matters more than photorealistic opacity.
Limitations and Open Questions
Resolution and scale remain the central engineering limits. Increasing volume size, slice count, color depth, and refresh rate multiplies demands on motors, projectors, LEDs, computation, and heat management. A desktop display can use a small enclosed moving surface; a room-scale display would have much larger moving masses and stricter safety requirements.
Occlusion and realism are also unresolved. Standard volumetric voxels are additive and broadly visible, so overlapping structures can look transparent. Medical scans, molecules, vector fields, and wireframe engineering models often tolerate this; solid characters, opaque interiors, and cinematic scenes expose the weakness. Directional emission, multi-view modulation, and hybrid light-field/volumetric approaches can help, but they make hardware and rendering more complex.
Brightness, color, and ambient light constrain deployment. Because each voxel is lit only briefly, the source must be bright enough for the integrated image to remain visible. Full color multiplies timing and calibration burdens. Public signage can use bright LEDs and simple content; scientific visualization may need subtler color fidelity and spatial accuracy.
Content and standards lag behind flat video. Conventional media standards encode 2D frames, while swept-volume displays need device-specific slices, voxel grids, point clouds, or volume textures. SDKs can import common 3D formats, but there is no universal volumetric-display equivalent of a normal video file that preserves geometry, color, interaction, and occlusion behavior across devices.
Market viability is therefore niche rather than absent. Swept-volume displays compete with cheaper flat screens, maturing VR/AR headsets, light-field monitors, projection mapping, and physical 3D printing. Their strongest case is not generic “3D wow,” but shared spatial inspection without headgear.
Significance and Applications
Swept-volume displays matter because they make digital 3D data physically inspectable. A group can stand around the same volume, point at features, and maintain social eye contact without wearing headsets. That is a different collaboration pattern from both flat monitors and personal VR.
Common and plausible applications include:
Medical imaging: CT, MRI, ultrasound, and surgical-planning datasets are naturally volumetric. A swept-volume display can show a tumor, vessel tree, heart, or bone structure as a shared 3D object, though it still needs clinical validation and careful scale/annotation tools.
Engineering and scientific visualization: CAD assemblies, molecules, fluid simulations, radar/weather volumes, geological structures, and vector fields can benefit from walk-around depth cues and interactive slicing.
Education and museums: Anatomy, astronomy, crystallography, architecture, and mathematics can be demonstrated as luminous models that students or visitors inspect from several angles.
Art, exhibits, and advertising: The same ghostly transparency that limits photorealism can be visually powerful for logos, abstract sculpture, data art, and trade-show demonstrations.
Human-computer interaction research: Swept-volume systems test how people point, gesture, annotate, and collaborate around a real 3D image rather than a private headset view.
For everyday consumer computing, swept-volume displays remain less practical than flat panels: they are bulkier, mechanically active, usually enclosed, and harder to author content for. Their long-term significance is therefore twofold. They are a specialized display family with real niche uses, and they are a continuing research pressure that pushes fast projection, LED control, volumetric graphics, safety engineering, and human perception studies.
