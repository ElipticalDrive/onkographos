BTW My confusion on fᵉ(0,0,0) and efforts to resolve it led to a conclusion about the documentation of this class.
The documentation is heavily skewed to the pure math. We use the phrase "a point Q in element E" (and its variants) multiple times. That language, combined with the notation is slightly divorced from the reality of the API:

That there is value in evaluating for a Q outside the element E, etc.

Thoughts on testing.
While I'm typically an advocate of testing private methods that public APIs depend on, I'd say for this PR it isn't necessary. Both CalcValueAtMeshOriginForAllElements and CalcValueAtMeshOrigin are so simple, that poking at them individually isn't worth it. The ability to get good answers from EvaluateCartesian is ample proof for their correctness.
So, for this PR, I'd propose a simple strategy.
Have a single test: GTEST_TEST(MeshFieldLinearTest, EvaluateCartesian).
Create a single mesh.
Define a piecewise, C0, linear field such that for at least two tets,i and j, fⁱ(Q) ≠ fʲ(Q) (see comments below for recommendation).
Create two instances of MeshFieldLinear - one with gradients one without.
Confirm that the former successfully invokes EvaluateGradient and the latter throws.
Select some interesting points (at origin, inside a tet, outside a tet, outside a mesh) and evaluate both mesh fields on those Qs on a reasonable selection of elements. (Showing the values to match.)
I'd expect that they require different tolerances compared to the ground truth and it would be nice if that were captured in the test.
What is being tested is
1.EvaluateCartesian always works whether gradients are calculated or not.
2. The answer it provides is reasonably similar either way. (And a measure of that similarity.)

Reading this and the new documentation makes me think that merely extending the documentation of a couple methods doesn't go far enough.
As I see it, one can construct a MeshFieldLinear in one of two modes. It is controlled by whether or notcalc_gradient is true or false. That choice has a number of implications. They are big enough that it should be part of the class documentation.
Constructing mesh field linear with gradient:
Longer construction time (as the gradients will be calculated)
May throw if the mesh is of "poor" quality -- and some definition of "poor quality" should be given so that the caller can be empowered to make that decision.

That's what I suspected. And this discussion needs to be clarified.
We use the MeshFieldLinear to model two quantities. The pressure field on the soft volume mesh and the pressure field on the contact surface mesh. In this current implementation, it is, essentially the same class.
For the soft volume mesh, we pre-compute the gradients of the volume mesh's pressure field.
For the contact surface mesh, we don't. The primary reason is that the gradient of the surface mesh gradient is not the value of the soft volume mesh field's gradient sampled on the surface mesh (which is what you want). It is, in fact, the gradient of a different (yet related) pressure field. The gradient of the surface mesh pressure field is a bunch of vectors that lie parallel to the triangles of the surface mesh. Currently, we have no use for this gradient. Paying the cost to compute it would be wasted and even the endeavor is highly likely to throw an exception because degenerate triangles in the contact surface will be very likely.
So, if we want to sample the soft volume mesh on the contact surface manifold, that is fine. We'll just have to include an additional piece of data that represents that quantity in the ContactSurface.
