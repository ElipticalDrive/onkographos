Notes on 2D FFT for Images
Dan Vassilev
·
November, 2026
Abstract
These notes revisit the two-dimensional Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of an image
through the group-theoretic lens emphasised by Maslen and Rockmore, tracing it from the
theory to the libraries. The DFT of an image is the Fourier transform on the group ZN ×ZM
and a few facts about that group do most of the work: its direct-product form is why the
2D transform separates into one-dimensional transforms, and the way a transform length
factors into smaller pieces is what the Cooley–Tukey algorithm exploits. When a length
resists such factoring, libraries fall back on Bluestein’s algorithm. These are the choices that
cuFFT, FFTW (and hence MATLAB’s fft2) and OpenCV make in practice.

excerpts make them concrete (Section 4), and a comparison and conclusion close the notes.

2.1
The Cooley–Tukey Algorithm
Separability reduces the 2D problem to a one-dimensional transform on ZN . The Cooley–Tukey
algorithm speeds that up by applying, inside a single ZN , the very idea separability used across
the two factors of ZN × ZM : when the group factors, the transform factors.

Schematically,
FN = (Fr ⊗ Is ) D (Ir ⊗ Fs ) P,

representation-theoretic origin of the Cooley–Tukey factorisation: a large transform is broken into smaller transforms on a subgroup and itsQquotient, glued together by twiddle factors.
Applied recursively along a subgroup chain N = i pi , it gives the O(N log N ) algorithm. In
short, the FFT decomposes one large representation problem into many small ones.
The coset picture and the butterfly. Concretely, if N = N1 N2 then ZN contains a subgroup H ∼
= ZN1 (the multiples of N2 ), splitting the indices into N2 cosets. Decomposing each
index through its coset representative turns the length-N sum into N2 inner transforms of length
N1 , recombined by an outer transform of length N2 ; the twiddle factors W k that couple the two
stages are exactly the values the characters take on the coset representatives — the entries of D
above. The smallest non-trivial case, N2 = 2, is the radix-2 butterfly (Figure 1), the two-point
transform from which radix-2 FFTs are built.

2.2
Bluestein’s Algorithm
The Cooley–Tukey recursion helps only to the extent that N factors into small primes —
equivalently, only when ZN possesses a chain of small-index subgroups to recurse through.
When N is prime, or carries a large prime factor, no such useful chain exists and the recursion
degenerates. Libraries therefore fall back to Bluestein’s algorithm [2], which side-steps the
missing subgroup structure by re-expressing the DFT as a convolution. Starting from the chirp
identity 2kn = k 2 + n2 − (k − n)2 , the kernel factorises as

The sum is the convolution of the modulated signal f (n) e−πin /N with the chirp eπim /N . That

positive integer N . Group-theoretically, the trick is to stop recursing inside ZN and instead
embed the computation into a larger, highly factorable cyclic group where Cooley–Tukey does
apply.
3
Implementation
Each library below is a concrete realisation of the same two ideas: separable 1D transforms (Section 2) accelerated by Cooley–Tukey (Section 2.1), with a Bluestein-type fallback (Section 2.2).
They differ mainly in how aggressively they tune the factorisation, how they cope with awkward
lengths and which hardware they target.
3.1
cuFFT (NVIDIA)
cuFFT [3] implements a highly optimised mixed-radix Cooley–Tukey algorithm with automatic
fallback to Bluestein’s algorithm for difficult sizes. It excels at batched 2D/3D transforms
and real-to-complex operations on NVIDIA GPUs, and is typically the fastest choice for large
batched image transforms, because the data stays on the GPU and plans are reused across
transforms.
3.2
MATLAB fft2 (FFTW-based)
MATLAB’s fft2 [5] is powered by the FFTW library [4], which pairs a planner with machinetuned codelets and performs the transform separably (rows then columns) with excellent numerical accuracy. It is mentioned here for completeness as a widely used reference implementation;
it is not benchmarked in these notes.
3
3.3
OpenCV: cv::dft and cv::cuda::dft
OpenCV [6] exposes two entry points relevant to images. The CPU routine cv::dft is always
available and, on x86, frequently dispatches to Intel IPP kernels; it is used in one of the accompanying examples. The GPU routine cv::cuda::dft lives in the cudaarithm module of the
opencv contrib repository and is, under the hood, a thin wrapper around cuFFT: the transform itself is the very cuFFT kernel of Section 3, with OpenCV managing the GpuMat operands
and the cuFFT plan.
3.3.1
Enabling the CUDA path
Because cv::cuda::dft wraps cuFFT, it is present only when OpenCV is compiled against the
CUDA toolkit. Concretely, OpenCV must be built from source with CUDA enabled and with the
contrib module cudaarithm included (e.g. -DWITH CUDA=ON, -DBUILD opencv cudaarithm=ON).
The CUDA toolkit, which supplies cuFFT, must be installed at build time. Crucially, the
pre-built distributions do not include CUDA: neither the Python wheels (opencv-python,
opencv-contrib-python) nor the Linux system-package-manager builds — for example the
Debian/Ubuntu packages libopencv-contrib-dev and libopencv-contrib4.6 — are compiled with WITH CUDA. The -contrib packages add the contrib modules’ CPU code, but not
GPU support. The build configuration — including the line reporting NVIDIA CUDA support —
can be inspected at run time via cv::getBuildInformation().
3.3.2
Practical limitations of cv::cuda::dft
Even when present, the wrapper is more constrained than cuFFT itself. It operates only in single
precision (CV 32F / CV 32FC2); it incurs host-to-device and device-to-host transfer costs for the
GpuMat operands; and it manages the cuFFT plan per call rather than exposing the fine-grained
plan reuse and batched real-to-complex control of the native API. The arithmetic is identical to
cuFFT — the overhead lies in this surrounding management, not in the transform kernel. For
heavy batched image workloads, calling cuFFT directly therefore remains preferable, whereas
cv::cuda::dft is convenient when one is already inside an OpenCV pipeline and working in
single precision.
Section 4 shows the core of three small programs that compute the same centred logmagnitude spectrum of an image, routing only the transform and magnitude through three different engines: cv::dft on the CPU, cv::cuda::dft (OpenCV’s cuFFT wrapper) and cuFFT
called directly.

4.2
Libraries
The following excerpts show the core of three small programs that all produce the same result —
the centred log-magnitude spectrum of a greyscale image — but route the transform through
three different engines. In every case the image has already been zero-padded to an FFTfriendly size with getOptimalDFTSize, keeping the length a product of small primes so the
Cooley–Tukey factorisation of Section 2.1 applies directly; cuFFT additionally falls back to
Bluestein (Section 2.2) for awkward sizes. Only the transform and magnitude differ between
the three; the quadrant shift, normalisation and display are shared.
4.2.1
CPU — cv::dft
The baseline performs the whole computation on the host. A real image is widened to a twochannel complex array so the result is a full complex spectrum.
// padded: greyscale image already zero-padded to getOptimalDFTSize()
Mat planes[] = { Mat_<float>(padded),
Mat::zeros(padded.size(), CV_32F) };
Mat complexI;
merge(planes, 2, complexI); // real + imaginary (= 0)
dft(complexI, complexI); // in-place 2D DFT on the CPU
split(complexI, planes); // planes[0] = Re, planes[1] = Im
magnitude(planes[0], planes[1], planes[0]); // |F| = sqrt(Re^2 + Im^2)
// ... log(1 + |F|), fftshift, normalise, imshow (shared) ...

4.2.3
Raw cuFFT
Calling cuFFT directly is a few lines longer, but each line exposes a control point. The plan is
created once and reused, the data stays resident on the device and the magnitude is fused into
a single kernel so only the finished result returns to the host.
__global__ void logMagnitude(const cufftComplex* in, float* out, int n) {
for (int i = blockIdx.x*blockDim.x + threadIdx.x; i < n;
i += blockDim.x*gridDim.x)
out[i] = logf(1.f + sqrtf(in[i].x*in[i].x + in[i].y*in[i].y));
}
// ---cufftHandle plan;
cufftPlan2d(&plan, rows, cols, CUFFT_C2C); // create the plan ONCE ...
cufftExecC2C(plan, dData, dData, CUFFT_FORWARD);// ... reuse across images/batches
logMagnitude<<<blocks, threads>>>(dData, dMag, count); // post-process ON device
// data stays resident; precision, plan reuse and cufftPlanMany batching are yours
Listing 3: Direct cuFFT transform with an on-device magnitude kernel.
The trade-off is now concrete. The CPU and cv::cuda::dft versions are the shortest,
because a single dft call hides everything — and that same hiding is the limitation: the wrapper
transfers data around every call, rebuilds the cuFFT plan each time, is fixed to single precision
and offers no handle on batching or residency. The raw-cuFFT version trades brevity for those
handles. The plan is created once (or replaced by cufftPlanMany for a batch); the data stays
on the device between transform and post-processing; and the magnitude is computed in a
fused kernel. These are precisely the levers that convert into throughput once images are large
or numerous. The wrapper is the right tool inside an existing OpenCV–CUDA pipeline; raw
cuFFT is the right tool for a deployable image-FFT component — and, as Section 3 noted, it
needs only the stock OpenCV plus the CUDA toolkit, rather than a custom contrib build.
4.3
Reading a Spectrum: Orientation
A practical use of the transform is reading geometry straight off the magnitude. A straight edge
at orientation θ is a one-dimensional structure in the plane, and its energy concentrates on the
line through the origin perpendicular to it, at θ + 90◦ . Figure 2 demonstrates this on a binary
edge map: line segments recovered by a Hough transform are coloured by orientation (left),
and each family’s predicted spoke — rotated 90◦ — is drawn in the matching colour on the
log-magnitude spectrum (right). The near-horizontal courses (red) map to the vertical ridge;

the diagonal families map to the arms of the fan, whose angular spread reflects the perspective
convergence of the lines.

6
Conclusion
The group-theoretic perspective emphasised by Maslen and Rockmore [1] connects these pieces:
the direct-product structure of ZN × ZM gives separability, the subgroup and coset structure
of ZN gives Cooley–Tukey and the breakdown of that structure for awkward sizes gives the
Bluestein fallback. The production libraries implement these same choices, differing mainly in
tuning, size handling and target hardware. Section 4 showed the same image transform three
ways: cv::dft on the CPU, cv::cuda::dft as a convenient but constrained GPU wrapper
and cuFFT called directly. The wrapper requires a custom OpenCV build (WITH CUDA plus the
cudaarithm contrib module) yet exposes none of the plan-reuse, residency or batching control
that turns the GPU into a throughput win; for a deployable image-FFT component, calling
cuFFT directly — with stock OpenCV reserved for input and display — is the more practical
and more portable choice.
