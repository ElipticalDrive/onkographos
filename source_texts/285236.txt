Abstract. In this paper three algorithms are developed for the streaming
compression of scientific data. The algorithms presented are reliant on the
theory of vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and operator valued
kernel. The scientific data is modeled as a snapshot of time dependent vectorfield F (x, t) over a manifold M and the recovery of the data is framed as a
learning problem. These processes are then appropriately modified and analyzed for the streaming scenario in which data is generated without the ability
to revisit past entries.

1. Introduction. There has been a substantial increase in data generated by experiments and simulations. As an example, climate models often produce peta-bytes
of data making storage and analysis of the data difficult [10]. Data is generated
without the ability to store and revisit past entries spurring the need for online
techniques. Hence our goal is to develop an online compression technique.
For scientific data there is often an underlying ordinary differential equation (ode)
or partial differential equation (pde) that generates the data. In turn, scientific data
often comes with some additional smoothness that may not be present in normal
data. If we further assume that our data, represented by a function F (x, t), lies
in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (to be defined later), representer theorems
show that the most accurate approximations are given in terms of the native kernels (see [11]). Here, we assume that our scientific data takes the form of a time
dependent vector-field over a manifold M , i.e. F (t, x) : [0, T ] × M → Rd . Our goal
is to then process a sequence of snapshots F (t, x) online and return a compressed
representation of the data.
Our main tool for this will be vector valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
(vvRKHS)with operator valued kernels. We will outline three algorithms which

Remark 4.1. In our experiments we found that using a Halton sequence [7] over
the time domain [0, T ] was effective in picking a subcollection of points. Halton
sequences are deterministically generated but offer low discrepancy and are thus
“quasi-random” sequences. They are typical for use in interpolation problems.

interval [0, T ]. The use of a Halton sequence has an additional benefit of making
it unnecessary to store the location of interpolation points since Halton sequences
are deterministically generated. This reduces the cost of storage to dJC. We will
ignore the cost of information necessary to generate the same Halton sequence as
it is minimal.

Remark 4.2. In order to avoid recording the value hFxn (t), φj (t)i for all discretization points xn of the manifold M one could instead use an interpolation approach.

is necessary to fully recover the data. However, as all points of the discretization
of the manifold M are available for the entire streaming process we can sub-select
at the beginning of the streaming process or adaptively add interpolation points
during the streaming process.

4.3. Using kernel interpolation for compression. In this section we discuss
the advantages of the specific choices of kernels made in Section 4.3. Specifically,
given our choice of kernel we can calculate our surrogate as a sum of separable
functions, i.e. we can independently solve the interpolation problems over t and x.
Moreover, our operator valued kernel function is a scalar valued kernel multiplied by
an identity matrix. As in the previous sections this allows us to solve the problem
component-wise.

4.4. Shape-parameters, robustness to noise, and regularization. Often kernel functions have an adjustable shape parameter, such as the width parameter in
the Gaussian kernel K(x, y) = exp(−µ2 kx − yk2 ). While most of the theory of
reproducing kernels does not place an emphasis on the shape parameter, in practice
it can have a dramatic effect. See [14] and [5] for details on the effect of the shape
parameter. In our algorithms, the shape parameter can be chosen in the offline
process where the interpolation takes place. In Algorithm 1, the shape parameter
can be chosen by comparison to the original time data. In Algorithm 2 the shape
parameter can be chosen via cross validation techniques using the interpolation
points. Both of these methods are use in Algorithm 3.
In general, our algorithms already exhibit some inherent robustness to noise in
the signal. Foremost, numerical integration is naturally robust to noise in the sense
that integration can act as an averaging procedure. As Algorithms 1 and 2 are

Finally, we can replace our interpolation problems with a kernel ridge regression
problem. In a kernel interpolation scheme, the Gram matrix G is inverted to fit the
pointwise data. However, we can add in a regularization term, given by λkwk2 , to
tamp down over-fitting as seen in kernel ridge regression. Additionally, the parameter λ can be seen as a smoothing parameter which prevents poor conditioning. On
a practical level, G is modified to G̃ = G + λId where λ is a positive regularization
parameter.
It is also well known that adding an `1 penalty, given by λkwk1 , enforces sparsity.
In the interpolation steps of our algorithms, first a subset of sample points are
chosen and then the data is fit against a Gram matrix. This subset of sample
points correspond to a subset of kernel functions. Hence, a possible alternative
is to replace the offline interpolation steps with a multi-task Lasso fit to find a
sparse collection of kernel functions use to recreate the data. In practice, this is
computationally expensive.

6. Discussion and conclusion. Our numerical experiments show the effectiveness of our algorithms as a form of lossy compression. Moreover, the parameter selection process outlined in the numerical sections are reasonably effective at
choosing appropriate parameters for both the kernel widths and smoothing. In the
numerical experiments, a variety of bases and kernels were used showing that the
method is flexible to user choices. However, performance may be gained by choosing
more appropriate kernels or bases. Moreover, the numerical experiment in Section
5.3 shows the ridge-regression process is effective in providing a small amount of
robustness to noise.

The biggest disadvantage of the given algorithms is picking the locations and
amount of interpolation points. In Algorithm 1 the Halton sequence points are selected in an offline process and the amount chosen depends on the level of wanted
accuracy in recovering all time signals gj (t) = hF (x, t), νj (x)i. However, in Algorithms 2 and 3 the spatial interpolation points are chosen ahead of time and there
is no guarantee of accuracy. Of course, as stated in Section 4.5 there do exist estimates on the accuracy of the interpolation given a density of interpolation points
(in terms of the fill-distance). Hence, in Algorithms 2 and 3 where the points are
pre-selected a sufficient density of points must be used. The same is true in Algorithm 1 but in all cases it can lead to using too many interpolation points which is
why interpolation points are chosen in an offline process in Algorithm 1. This spurs
future work in which interpolation points are chosen in an adaptive online process
and/or domain decomposition techniques in which areas of low activity have less
points. This is particularly relevant for very large domains (i.e. domains with a
large number of discretization points).
