A BSTRACT
Anti-aliasing is essential for Virtual Reality (VR) applications, as
the pixels of current VR displays subtend a large field of view. This
makes various undersampling artifacts particularly noticeable. Understanding state-of-the-art anti-aliasing techniques and their tradeoffs is therefore crucial for optimizing VR experiences and developing high-quality VR applications. This paper investigates multiple
anti-aliasing techniques through a user study with pairwise comparisons to determine the best method for image quality in VR, considering both static and moving objects in four different plausible
environments. Results indicate that the ranking of methods does
not differ significantly between moving and static scenes. While

1 I NTRODUCTION
In virtual reality (VR), users are fully immersed in a digital world,
surrounded by 3D objects and environments. This proximity amplifies the impact of visual imperfections, making even minor flaws
significantly detrimental to the overall experience. Anti-aliasing
techniques play a critical role in the rectification of undersampling
artifacts, enhancing the visual quality of objects and environments
alike, and reducing the likelihood of visual discomfort or even simulation sickness.
Given the large fields of view of current VR displays, as well as
their low resolutions, aliasing artifacts become particularly noticeable, making anti-aliasing essential to maintain an immersive and
seamless user experience [21]. Various anti-aliasing methods are
employed in VR, including super-sampling, multi-sampling, fastapproximate, and temporal anti-aliasing, each with its benefits and
drawbacks. The choice of method often hinges on factors such as
computational resources, image quality requirements but also the
structure of the existing rendering pipeline. Effective anti-aliasing
reduces the visibility of geometry and shading induced artifacts,
such as jagged edges or temporal flickering, which can otherwise
lead to visual discomfort, eye strain, and headaches for VR users.
Even though previous work evaluated image quality with artifacts like aliasing or 3D mesh-related flaws in VR [19, 16, 15] none
of the work focused on anti-aliasing techniques in VR in a pairwise
comparison with realistic and plausible scenes [13].
Therefore, this work aims to bridge the gap by evaluating several
commonly used anti-aliasing methods in realistic VR scenes. We
seek to provide a comprehensive evaluation and ranking of these
methods within the context of plausible scenes in VR. Understanding the interplay between anti-aliasing and movement in VR is crucial, as real-world motion necessitates high frame rates and smooth
transitions to maintain immersion and comfort.
Our contribution can be summarized as follows:
• Investigating the impact of scene dynamics on various antialiasing techniques by a perceptual user study with pairwise
comparison.
• The results reveal differences between temporal anti-aliasing
(TAA) and multisample anti-aliasing (MSAA) under different conditions, while supersampling (SSAA) was rated significantly better across various VR scenes and variants.
• Our findings helps to the design and development of more
effective and immersive VR technologies, with applications
spanning gaming, education, and healthcare.
This article is structured as followed: The section 2 gives an
overview over the state-of-the-art in terms of anti-aliasing methods,
image quality in VR, as well as the perceptual pairwise comparison. In section 3 we describe the experiment in detail and the used
technology and conditions, followed by their results in section 4.
Finally, section 5 further discusses the findings in the given context and elaborate the potential benefits and drawbacks. The last
section 6 summarize all results and concludes the paper.
2 R ELATED W ORK
2.1 Anti-Aliasing Methods
Achieving high-quality visual output is a critical objective in computer graphics. One of the primary challenges in this domain is the
mitigation of aliasing, an artifact class that arises due to the discrete
sampling of continuous signals. To mitigate the resulting visual artifacts, various anti-aliasing techniques have been developed.
One such technique is Supersampling Anti-Aliasing (SSAA),
which significantly improves image quality by rendering scenes at
resolutions higher than the display’s requirement. This process captures more samples per pixel, resulting in smoother and more detailed images. The high-resolution images are then downsampled,
averaging neighbouring pixel colors into a single lower resolution
pixel. When it comes to anti-aliasing, SSAA is often considered
the brute force reference method. Despite its high computational
cost, the achievable visual fidelity makes this method a very popular choice for VR applications targeting high-end hardware.
Another very effective technique that became popular in the recent years is Temporal Anti-Aliasing (TAA). In contrast to SSAA,
TAA amortizes its costs by gathering samples over the course of
multiple subsequent frames. In order to account for viewer or scene
motion, the technique employs reprojection and various sophisticated blending heuristics. This results in high performance, while
reaching a quality [6, 29] that can rival or even surpass SSAA in
some instances. The main problem of the technique is maintaining a consistent level of quality, as it is highly dependent on scene
composition and employed movement.
Multisample Anti-Aliasing (MSAA) offers an optimized approach by selectively generating higher-resolution samples. It focuses on critical areas such as polygon edges while leaving less
important areas at standard resolution. This selective sampling significantly reduces computational load compared to SSAA. During
the downsampling process, the final pixel color is determined by
averaging the sampled values, making MSAA an efficient solution
that balances image quality and processing power requirements [1].
For applications where performance is critical, Fast Approximate Anti-Aliasing (FXAA) provides a practical solution. FXAA
applies a post-processing step to smooth jagged edges after rendering. It detects high-contrast edges and uses a selective low-pass
filter to smooth them out. Designed to be fast and efficient, FXAA
requires minimal processing power, making it ideal for real-time
applications where maintaining high performance is essential [9].
These anti-aliasing techniques, each with its strengths, collectively contribute to the enhancement of visual quality in virtual reality and gaming, addressing the challenges posed by jagged edges
and visual artifacts.
2.2
Image Quality in VR
In the past, several studies have addressed image quality in general
and virtual reality [30]. For instance, Kim et al. [5] proposed a deep
learning-based virtual reality image quality assessment method that
automatically predicts the visual quality of omnidirectional images.
Madhusudana et al. [11] introduced a Gaussian mixture model to
capture the bivariate statistics of neighboring coefficients of steerable pyramid decompositions, effectively modeling the increased
spatial correlation due to ghosting artifacts. Other previous work
used machine learning and developed a human perception guider
that criticizes the predicted quality score of the predictor using adversarial learning based on human perceptual scores [7] and convolutional neural networks [24]. Modeling the temporal aspect of
visual perception in the periphery was the focus of Tursun et al. in
2022 [25] that measure the sensitivity of human observers to different stimuli across a wide field of view. Multiple studies have also
explored anti-aliasing in VR based on image quality. Various image
artifacts, such as aliasing, which significantly reduce image quality [21]. Another study examined the visual quality of 3D meshes
with diffuse colors in virtual reality [16]. Additionally, a perceptual
study compared the MSAA method with the FXAA method in a
pairwise comparison in VR [13]. However, this experiment compared different colored patterns rather than realistic scenes.
2.3
Pairwise Comparison Scheme
In pairwise comparison, each option is systematically compared
with every other option based on certain criteria. The decision
maker assigns a relative weight to each criterion and decides which
option is better in each comparison. This process continues until all
possible pairs have been evaluated. The results are then aggregated,
often by counting how many times each option was considered superior, to determine the overall ranking or preference of the options.
In recent years, various innovative approaches have been developed
to enhance the effectiveness of pairwise comparison methods in different fields. Luisa et al. [10] introduced a novel method based on
binary logistic regression to improve content-based image retrieval
(CBIR) and computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) techniques applied to
bronchoscopies, particularly for different pathologies. This method
has shown significant promise in scenarios where a large number of
features must be analyzed despite having only a limited number of
images. Expanding on the potential of pairwise comparison, Xu et
al. [28] proposed its use over conventional numerical rating systems
for image quality assessment (IQA). They developed a new optimization objective to rank image quality more effectively, using image quality ratings as weights in the rank model, thereby making the
ranking process sensitive to these ratings. Some researcher intro-
duced a perceptually weighted rank correlation indicator designed
to prioritize the correct ranking of high-quality images while minimizing the impact of ranking errors for lower-quality images [27].
Their method introduce a pairwise comparison only for image pairs
with quality differences exceeding a specified sensory threshold,
ensuring more reliable rankings. In another significant advancement, an architecture based on deep-learning models that leverages
visual information such as spatial color attributes and visual attention mechanisms. This architecture is adept at accurately ranking
contrast-enhanced images, showcasing the power of deep learning
in refining pairwise comparison techniques [3].
In general, the results of pairwise comparison experiments are
often measured in Just-Noticeable-Differences (JNDs) units. Two
conditions are considered to be 1 JND apart if 75% of the observers
can detect a difference between them. The design of such experiments does not evaluate the explicit difference between procedures,
but rather determines which procedure yields better quality and is
closer to a perfect reference. Such experiments involve a forced
choice between two presented images, where the user must decide
which image is of better quality.
Lastly, Pérez-Ortiz and Mantiuk [18] introduced a new unit specification; the Just-Objectionable-Differences (JODs) units. For example, images can be affected by jagged edges and strong blooms
which appear, which are similarly degraded when compared to the
reference image (indicating the same JOD), but they are noticeably
different and thus several JNDs apart. The mapping between JODs
and JNDs can be very complex. Therefore, the measure of JOD
is more related to visual equivalence or to quality expressed as a
difference-mean-opinion score rather than JNDs [20]. Therefore,
higher values of JOD indicate a greater perceived difference in quality between the two images being compared.
3
M ETHOD
To compare various anti-aliasing techniques, we designed an experiment as a between-subject design. This approach allows each
participant to experience all variants, thereby controlling for intersubject variability and increasing the reliability of our findings.
We utilized an HP Reverb G2 virtual reality headset with a native resolution of 2160 x 2160 pixels. To account for lens distortion, we adjusted the internal per-eye resolution to 2700 x 2630
pixels. The headset was powered by a computer equipped with an
Nvidia RTX 3090 graphics card and running the Unity3D engine
(v.2023.1.0a25), ensuring high performance and fidelity in rendering virtual environments.
3.1
Scenes
Our evaluation focused on four different scenes with three viewpoints, each presented in both static and moving variants, to assess
the performance of different anti-aliasing techniques. These scenes
were carefully chosen to cover a wide range of possible scenarios
and image parameters, including color gamut, depth distribution,
brightness, and contrast (see Fig. 2).
The Sibenik Cathedral scene is characterized by its large area
and predominantly indirect lighting. The checkerboard floor with
repeating textures introduces sharp, rapidly changing contrasts,
making it an ideal testbed for evaluating the handling of highfrequency details. Overall, this scene also provides a clean aesthetic look. The scene of the Fireplace Room offers a indoor environment with warm tones, designed to induce a more natural and
warm environment. This scene includes transparent objects such
as windows and glass bottles, which pose a significant challenge
for anti-aliasing techniques due to their complex interactions with
light and reflections. The Crytek Sponza model is a widely used
reference in video games and scientific research due to its complex
geometry and rich detail. The scene features architectural elements
such as arches, columns, and intricate wall designs, combined with
a variety of surfaces and materials including stone, cloth, and vegetation. Finally, the Viking Village scene is a ready-to-use asset
known for its handcrafted appearance, featuring natural lighting and
physically-based textures. It is the most coherent scene among the
four, offering a seamless blend of various elements to create a realistic environment.
Each scene contained three key objects: a vase, a table with a
marble bust, and a torch, as shown in Fig. 2b. These objects were
consistently positioned within the subject’s field of view to maintain
visual continuity. In the moving scene variants, the vase was scaled
frequently, the table was translated, and the bust was continuously
rotated, accompanied by a particle system that simulates fire.
3.2
Procedure
The experiment commenced with a visual acuity test to identify
any severe visual impairment. Participants were instructed to view
a test chart placed at a distance of precisely three meters in front
of them (see Fig. 3). A participant was deemed to have sufficient
visual acuity if they were able to recognize at least half of the letters on the chart. Following a brief introduction, the participants
completed a demographic questionnaire. Subsequently, each participant’s variant type was selected and they were placed in the virtual
environment. Subsequently, the various scenes and anti-aliasing
techniques were presented in a randomized order to prevent order
effects and bias. To enhance the validity of our comparisons, each
pairing was presented multiple times. The participant is positioned
in one of the designated locations and then permitted to observe the
method for a period of 10 seconds. Thereafter, the image briefly
fades to black and then returns to its original state. The participant
is situated in the same location and position and now observes the
same scene with a different method. After another 10 seconds, the
image fades to black once more, forcing the user to decide which
image was superior. To mitigate fatigue and maintain engagement,
varying viewpoints were employed to continuously provide new visual stimuli. Two scenes were presented for a fixed duration, followed by a prompt for the participant to choose which option they
preferred. This process was repeated until every possible pairwise
comparison was recorded.
3.3
Conditions
We chose to remain within the Unity3D engine due to its
widespread use in the development of virtual reality applications.
All four anti-aliasing methods described in subsection 2.1 are included in the Unity engine. For the purposes of our evaluation,
we have identified two independent variables: the different antialiasing methods (Supersampling Anti-Aliasing, Temporal AntiAliasing, Multisample Anti-Aliasing, and Fast Approximate AntiAliasing) (see Fig. 1) and the movement within the scene. The
subjective assessments of image quality are set as dependent variables in our experiment. We used mostly Unity default settings for
the different anti-aliasing methods: SSAA used 2x supersampling,
MSAA was set to 4x multi sampling, TAA used the internal High
profile and Contrast Adaptive Sharpening of 0.85 and scenes with
FXAA usage was set to default. No post-processing effects are used
for all methods except for FXAA which is it mandatory.

5
D ISCUSSION
Most of the participants preferred the supersampling (SSAA)
method. The lack of significance for MSAA and TAA indicates that
participants’ preferences were not consistent, resulting in varied decisions regarding these methods. When comparing these findings
with those in Fig. 5a and Fig. 5d, it becomes clear that it is challenging to identify unique differences based solely on the general
frequency of a chosen method.
The analysis reveals two key findings. Firstly, it is evident that
there is a clear distinction between the FXAA method and the other
three methods. Supersampling AA consistently demonstrates the
highest quality in both comparison groups, with the maximum number of JOD units and highest mean probability of higher quality.
Based on the SSAA implementation, it is clear, why it outperforms
all other methods. As it uses existing higher resolution pixel information are correctly aggregated and combined as in TAA or FXAA
which uses only data visible in the frames. However, this comes
with large performance cost when using SSAA x4 or x8 [26, 13].
In the comparison group with the static scene, the significance
between the TAA and MSAA methods increases slightly, with a
noticeable tendency towards better quality with TAA. A possible
explanation could be the more temporal stability, and it’s effectiveness to reduce artifacts such as jagged edges and shimmering that
might be more pronounced with MSAA. These results are strong
related to its environments and scene complexity.
In General, the measured divergences in JODs between the four
anti-aliasing methods across the various scenes are of considerable interest (see Fig. 2). These results demonstrate that even
within scenes featuring different viewpoints, the optimal method
to achieve the highest quality varies considerably. This indicates
that different anti-aliasing techniques are more or less effective in
specific scenarios. This aligns with previous work where the image
quality varies in different environments [2].
We would argue for choosing SSAA over the other methods
in Virtual Reality, while TAA and MSAA perceptually performs
slightly worse and is highly dependent in regard to scene complexity with a performance cost. This aligns with previous evaluations
[2].
5.1
Limitations
Besides the ranking, there are still some limitations. We only focused on integrated Unity AA methods. However, it would be interesting to extend the evaluation of methods with deep learningbased methods such as Deep Learning Anti-Aliasing (DLAA) [8].
This could be an important area for future research, as deep learning methods improve anti-aliasing performance, potentially offering better image quality and better handling of complex scenes
compared to traditional methods. It would also be interestingly
to further analyze foveated [17] or saliency map-based [21] approaches.
Using an HMD with limited resolution may affect the study’s
findings in several ways. First, lower resolution can mask the
differences between anti-aliasing methods. Fine details and subtle variations in image quality that are critical for evaluating the
performance of these techniques might not be as discernible on
lower-resolution displays. Consequently, participants might find it
challenging to distinguish between methods like SSAA, TAA, and
MSAA, leading to less pronounced preference trends and statistical
differences. Second, the limited resolution may exacerbate aliasing
artifacts, making them more prominent than they would be on modern higher-resolution displays like Apple Vision Pro or Varjo XR-4
Series. This could skew participant preferences towards methods
that are particularly effective at reducing visible aliasing on lowerresolution screens, such as SSAA, potentially overestimating their
effectiveness compared to methods that might perform better on
higher-resolution HMDs.
Although the scenes were selected to encompass a broad range of
imaging variables, even minor differences appear sufficient to influence the results. To reduce variability, all tests could theoretically
be conducted in a single scene with one viewpoint. However, this
approach might exceed the tolerance to frustration of individual participants, potentially leading to less reliable responses. Moreover,
this would only determine the optimal method for that specific scenario. To make more general conclusions, it is better to include a
variety of scenarios.
For the evaluation part, it should be noted that the statistically
significant differences observed in both experimental groups are
not present for all techniques. This is likely due to the relatively
small sample size of the experimental groups. The JOD values (see
Fig. 6b) also indicated that the TAA technique exhibited slight superiority over the MSAA technique. However, as illustrated in Fig. 6a,
this finding did not meet the threshold for sufficient statistical confidence. To overcome this lack of confidence, the number of subjects
would likely need to be significantly increased.
6 C ONCLUSION
In conclusion, the statistical evaluation indicates that the presence
of moving objects in the scene does not significantly impact the
overall ranking of the anti-aliasing methods. In both static and
moving scenarios, the FXAA method consistently demonstrated the
poorest performance. No statistically significant differences were
observed between the TAA and MSAA techniques when evaluated
within individual groups. However, the distance in JOD units between these techniques increases when examining the static group
results, suggesting that TAA may perform worse in moving scenes
where frame-to-frame differences are pronounced. The supersampling method (SSAA) consistently achieves the highest JOD values,
distinguishing itself statistically from MSAA but not from TAA.
When the results from both groups are combined, it can be seen
that SSAA emerges as the most effective method overall. The discrepancy between TAA and MSAA becomes more pronounced,
although it remains insufficient for a robust statistical conclusion.
Therefore, a perceptual classification of these methods is only partially achievable based on the findings of this study. SSAA demonstrated the greatest performance, followed by TAA and MSAA,
with FXAA exhibiting the lowest performance.
It is recommended that future studies increase the number of participants and consolidate them into a single experimental group.
This approach would provide a stronger statistical foundation, allowing for more precise conclusions about the performance differences among anti-aliasing techniques. Furthermore, exploring
a wider range of scene types and motion dynamics could elucidate
the conditions under which each method excels or fails. We also
suggest comparing user scores with established metrics, as mentioned in [12, 25].
