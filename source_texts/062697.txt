Abstract
CCS Concepts
Recent advances in Gaussian splatting, 3D foundation models, and
video-passthrough mixed reality offer unprecedented fidelity for
capturing and blending physical and virtual worlds. However, established XR methods rely on polygon-based pipelines that treat
physical and digital environments as separate domains, making
seamless integration across recent novel representations difficult.
Because splats lack explicit surface geometry, they impede standard
graphics techniques necessary for cross-reality interactions, such
as shadow or collision handling. Furthermore, while 3D foundation models can reconstruct physical objects on-the-fly into digital
splats, current XR systems fall short of leveraging this capability for
user experiences that transition seamlessly between physicality and
virtuality. To address this, we organize XR as a multi-layered reality
of interacting primitives, letting pixels and objects move across
physical and digital boundaries. We contribute SplatXR, a Unity
toolkit that operationalizes this structure as building blocks for interactive, immersive apps leveraging 3D Gaussian splatting: a standalone renderer, splat-to-mesh reconstruction, splat physics, splat
blending, and offloaded splat object reconstruction via on-headset
capture. SplatXR unites vision and graphics techniques to make
splat-based XR development broadly available, and we validate it
through demonstrative applications and a small developer study.

Introduction
In recent years, three fundamentally new ways of reconstructing,
representing, and rendering a scene have emerged. First, vision foundation models such as SAM 3 [10] and SAM 3D [11] have made it
possible to gain a detailed understanding of 3D scenes and their
semantics. Second, differentiable rendering techniques such as neural radiance fields [49] and 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) [34] have
enabled capturing complex 3D scenes with photorealism. Third,
splat- or video-based world modeling (e.g., World Labs [87]) has
the ability to simulate visual 3D worlds directly from the physical world, enabling the generation of interactive 3D environments
from minimal visual input. All of these novel techniques enable
the creation of 3D worlds at unprecedented fidelity grounded in
physical reality by leveraging implicit representations.

At the same time, a new generation of video-passthrough headworn displays, such as the Meta Quest 3, Apple Vision Pro, and Samsung Galaxy XR, has pushed the limits of displaying a scene and the
achievable visual integration of physical-digital scene compositions.
These extended reality (XR) devices leverage perspective reprojection and high-resolution displays to obtain scenes that seamlessly
blend virtual elements with physical reality. However, despite the
technical innovation underlying these new XR displays, their applications are still fundamentally based on and designed for displaying
explicit polygon mesh representations, used for decades in computer graphics, as they provide visual information for rendering
and geometric information for collisions, shadows, and occlusions.
This mismatch between the explicit representations in traditional polygon meshes and the implicit representations driving
today’s computer vision and graphics innovations has hindered
the adoption of the latter for interactive purposes in XR. With
this work, we introduce a toolkit that is organized around a multilayered composition that accounts for the complementary strengths
and shortcomings of explicit and implicit scene representations,
all while ensuring ease-of-use for XR application developers. In
particular, we focus on 3DGS as it is at the core of many graphics
innovations, also serving as a common interface to vision foundation models and world models (e.g., SAM 3D [11] can export its
reconstruction as a splat, and Marble by World Labs [87] can also
export its generation as a splat).
We organize these physical and digital layers into a manageable
compositional structure, given that perceived reality is a composite
of various structural, visual, and semantic components. By treating
reality as a set of distinct but interacting layers across different
representations (physical reality, Gaussian splats, environmental
meshes, scene semantics), our framework allows developers to design cross-reality interactions where pixels and objects can move
between these layers. To operationalize this, we lay out several
example configurations that recur across different layers of reality.
For instance, structural layers can influence visual layers through
proxy phantom meshes, i.e., invisible geometries reconstructed
from the physical environment which let virtual splats cast shadows and appear to collide with real-world surfaces. We showcase
configurations for semantic linking across layers, demonstrating
how inputs, such as hand tracking, can be used to anchor and manipulate digital splat objects. This provides developers with the
mental model and technical foundation necessary to build experiences that bidirectionally interact with the real environment and
captured or generated 3D scenes.
We contribute SplatXR, an end-to-end Unity toolkit for building XR applications with Gaussian splats on standalone, untethered headsets, integrating real-time splat rendering, physics, and
developer-facing abstractions. SplatXR thus facilitates an integrated developer workflow that brings together and adapts computer vision and graphics techniques in one toolkit so that building XR applications with Gaussian splatting becomes available to
the wider XR community, from research to development. Through
building blocks, SplatXR automatically handles proxy geometry
computation, Gaussian splat materials, and render queue ordering,
maintaining visual and physical consistency across splats, meshes,
and proxy geometry without additional developer input. Unlike
prior splatting work confined to VR, SplatXR also enables AR by

simultaneously running wire- and server-free for unbounded outdoor use and providing AR-specific support, including passthrough
compositing, shader-based alpha blending, color correction, and
occlusion from reconstructed environment geometry. The toolkit

preliminary developer evaluation (n=3) provides initial insights
into the toolkit’s usability. SplatXR and our technical evaluation

Related Work
SplatXR is a toolkit that enables developers to leverage Gaussian
splats of captured physical objects and spaces for interactive XR
applications. Its contributions therefore relate to cross-reality interaction, capture-based XR experiences, neural scene representations
in XR, XR toolkits, and the emerging space of Gaussian splatting
software tools and libraries.
2.1
Cross-Reality Interactions
Cross-reality interactions have been conceptualized in a variety of
ways to support different research or development purposes. Cools
et al. [14] examine the compositional structure of cross-reality interactions for design patterns. Auda et al. [4] survey cross-reality systems along the reality-virtuality continuum. Blending Spaces [13]
transitions between physical and virtual layers on an object level,
and RealityCheck [24] does so on a pixel level. GradualReality [69]
further incorporates interaction state awareness. All of these works
assume—even if implicitly—that the virtual world consists of polygon meshes and thus forms a single coherent layer. Therefore, they
do not account for the challenges that arise in using Gaussian splatting for interactive and situated applications, e.g., ensuring lighting
coherence or supporting physics simulations between explicitly
and implicitly represented content. We instead implement a Unity
toolkit that accounts for both.
2.2
Capture-Based XR Experiences
Several smartphone-based apps scan physical objects into 3D representations for downstream XR applications. While meshes have
predominated, recent products from Luma [44], Scaniverse [67],
and Polycam [59] export splats; SplatXR expects developers to
provide a splat created with such an app.
Captured 3D models underpin a multitude of XR experiences:
remote robot operation in VRoxy [65], environmental design prototyping in PointShopAR [83], redirected walking in SwitchAR [86],
collaboration in VirtualNexus [28], interface personalization in
MineXR [12], educational experiences in Museum in the Classroom [46], memorable item interaction in InteRecon [42], and animating the physical environment in Scene Responsiveness [33].

2.3
Implicit Scene Representations in XR
The use of splats and other implicit scene representations in XR
has ranged from rendering to interaction. VRSplat [78] and VRPipe [38] tackle the challenges of rendering splats under headset
constraints, MetalSplatter [48] enables splat rendering on Apple
Vision Pro, and Snap’s Lens Studio [72] does the same on Snap
Spectacles. VR-GS [30] deforms otherwise static splats for interaction purposes, and GS-Verse [56] similarly enables single-object
soft-body deformation by building on the mesh-based GaMeS representation [82]. However, both are tethered and VR-only, targeting
neither passthrough AR nor an easily importable toolkit. Reality
Promises [32] renders untethered scene and object splats, but does
not offer a modular toolkit or mesh-splat interactions. Li et al. [41]
render neural radiance fields (NeRFs) on a headset with simple
transform manipulations, Instant-NGP [51] allows VR viewing of
NeRFs and cleaning of floaters, and Splatshop [68], VR-Doh [45],
and GaussianShopVR [70] facilitate editing splats in VR. GaussianNexus [27], CoCreateAR [53], Reality-Linked Gaussian-Splatted
Proxies [89], Thing2Reality [26], and SharedNeRF [66] implicitly
represent objects of interest across spatially distributed users for
telepresence and remote collaboration. MiXR [19] similarly applies
scanned-object interactions to a specific domain (generative-aided
design) and, like GaussianNexus, targets a particular use case rather
than a general-purpose XR toolkit. Additional applications of splatting and NeRFs in XR are discussed by Qiu et al. [62] and Li et
al. [40], who survey various use cases for radiance fields.
Like prior work, SplatXR builds on several existing splatting
techniques for on-headset rendering, and, to this end, unifies and
packages established algorithms into a single, coherent XR toolkit
for developers, adapting techniques as needed for rendering splats
in an HMD. Table 1 provides a summary of existing systems. SplatXR
differs by (1) lowering the barrier to splat-based XR development by
uniting computer vision and graphics techniques, (2) enabling ARspecific support, unlike prior work, which largely remains VR-only,
and (3) providing reusable components for recurring XR tasks.

2.5
Gaussian Splatting Tools
Since the inception of splatting [34], a variety of tools have emerged
that seek to satisfy different purposes.
Training and rendering splats is at the core of Kerbl et al.’s original implementation [34], Brush [8], and Nerfstudio’s gsplat library [76, 90]; these low-level implementations require substantial
domain expertise and are incompatible with XR engines such as
Unity, Unreal, or WebXR. Postshot [60], Luma [44], Kiri Engine [18],
and Polycam [59] simplify training for non-expert users through
a higher-level user interface, but are self-contained applications
instead of developer dependencies.
In-browser viewing and editing of splats motivates tools such
as SuperSplat [57, 74], web renderers [2, 50], Nerfstudio’s viewer
[76], Gracia [23], Niantic’s Scaniverse [67], Spline [73], Sparkjs [88],
and Arrival.Space [3]. Built on the low-level WebGL API [84], on
general-purpose 3D engines such as Three.js [77], or on specialpurpose visualizers such as Viser [91], these tools support viewing
and, in some cases, pre-defined editing; Gracia and SuperSplat go
further with stereoscopic viewing, and other web-based experiences
reach the headset via the WebXR API [85]. Their design nevertheless
remains centered on viewing, and their browser-based architectures
are incompatible with native XR development in engines such as
Unity and Unreal.
Integrating splats into a 3D engine is facilitated by platformspecific engine plugins: Luma [44] and Volinga [81] for Unreal,
NVIDIA Omniverse [54] for robotics simulations, the GSOPs plugin [64] for SideFX Houdini, and ReshotAI’s plugin [63] for Blender.

All of these focus on editing and compositing splats but lack proper
tooling for building interactive experiences with them. Pranckevičius’ splat renderer for Unity [61] follows a PC-VR architecture
requiring a tethered connection and it offers no XR-specific features,
making it unsuitable for standalone deployment. Ninjamode [35]
likewise provides a Unity splat renderer. Like the authoring tools
GaussianShopVR [70] and Splatshop [68], it offers no splat physics,
shadows, or AR blending, and several of these tools remain tethered.
In summary, while these tools are useful for different purposes,
none addresses the need for building interactive XR applications.
To this end, SplatXR introduces a Unity-based toolkit, designed for
standalone, untethered headsets including Meta Quest 3 and Apple
Vision Pro, that provides real-time performance while leveraging
game engine features to enable immersive experiences, simplifies
recurring tasks such as preprocessing and importing splats, and
provides building blocks for rendering and user-splat interactions.
3
Multi-Layered Reality Framework
Enabling inter-object and human-object interactions between splats,
classical meshes, and the physical scene requires systematically
managing these entities on both a technical and a conceptual level.
To this end, we describe a multi-layered framework that conceptualizes reality as a set of logically isolated, yet operationally intertwined layers that align visible and invisible splat and mesh
geometry with associated semantics, appearance, and physical context. In the following, we outline the proposed set of layers and the
layer-bridging operations defined on them.
3.1
Reality Layers
Our framework uses five primitive layers:
3.1.1 Physical layer. The physical layer comprises the user’s environment, visually captured and displayed through the passthrough
feed of the XR headset.
3.1.2 Photometric layer. The photometric layer refers to appearancerelated components of a scene, such as splats or a rendered frame,
which may lack explicit structure. Appearance can be obtained from
captures of real spaces or generated by a 3D foundation model. Its
content comes from the rendering engine, which provides the rendered view defined by the headset’s intrinsics and extrinsics.
3.1.3 Geometric layer. The geometric layer includes traditional
3D polygon objects which possess explicit geometry that can be
rendered visually while interacting with the scene’s physics.
3.1.4 Phantom structure layer. The phantom structure layer contains invisible explicit geometries, meshes in our case, that are
isometrically anchored or aligned to visual layers, letting a visual
splat cast a shadow or a physical table support a virtual bouncing
ball. Such invisible proxy geometry is a standard graphics technique; we name it as a layer here to make its role in splat-based
composition explicit.
3.1.5 Semantic layer. The semantic layer encompasses a higherlevel understanding of element function and identity in reality via
passthrough (e.g., hand tracking or object recognition) to enable
cross-layer interactions like linkage and scene understanding.

3.2
Operations
While layers define the static compositional structure of a mixedreality environment, operations enable the dynamic transitions
and interactions between them. Fig. 2 depicts possible configurations and operations, which we categorize into three primary types:
transformation, blending, and linkage.
3.2.1 Cross-layer transformation. Elements can be transferred across
layers (Fig. 2f). To move an element from the physical environment
to a digital representation, a region of the passthrough feed (physical layer) is processed by a generative model such as SAM 3 or SAM
3D Objects, which lifts 2D visual data into an implicit 3D structure
and decodes it into an explicit 3D Gaussian splat (photometric layer).
To let particle-based splats interact meaningfully, we rely on a splatto-mesh operation which derives an invisible phantom mesh (phantom structure layer) from the splat file and aligns the visual splat to
it, yielding a mesh-augmented splat that can physically collide, cast
shadows, and interact with classical meshes and other scene layers.
3.2.2 Layer blending. Operations also control how layers are composited within the user’s view. Additive operations selectively stack
and reveal layers using spatial masks and custom splat blending
shaders, as shown in Fig. 2e. This enables volumetric portals: the
user sees their raw physical surroundings everywhere except within
a defined boundary, through which an imported splat environment becomes visible, blending smoothly with the surrounding
passthrough as the viewpoint shifts. Fig. 2a shows an example of
diminished reality using a reality blending volume covering a real
object, and Fig. 2b shows an instance of mediated reality, which
inserts a modified version of that object into the physical space as
a photometric layer.
3.2.3 Linkage. Whereas the operations above are transitions, linkage defines persistent connections that maintain spatial and contextual coherence in a mixed-reality scene by binding digital elements
to the physical environment. We categorize these into two types.
Geometric Linkage. This establishes a spatial relationship between layers, anchoring virtual elements to specific coordinates or
distinct physical features. A possible implementation is AR image
target tracking, where a virtual splat or phantom mesh is persistently locked to the position and orientation of a physical marker,
ensuring static spatial alignment across the physical, geometric,
and photometric layers.
Semantic Linkage. Beyond rigid coordinates, semantic linkage
dynamically binds a layer to contextual elements in physical reality,
requiring a real-time understanding of the user or environment. In
Fig. 2d, hand-tracking data from the physical reality layer links a
virtual watch splat to the user’s wrist, ensuring continuous positioning and enabling behaviors like dynamic occlusion from the
geometric layer.

4.1
Architecture and Developer Experience
SplatXR is implemented as a Unity package, thus making it easily
installable through Unity’s Package Manager. It features a developerfacing graphical interface, integrated into the Unity Editor, that
provides access to a set of well-defined building blocks through
panels, scripts, materials, and shaders. Building blocks can be implemented as needed, and previewed interactively in the Unity
Editor scene view. Notably, this makes SplatXR’s splat renderer
compatible with non-XR applications as well.
4.2
Building Blocks
SplatXR comprises four main building blocks:
B1: a Splat Renderer building block enabling splat rendering on
XR headsets in passthrough AR or full VR with computational constraints
B2: a Splat Physics building block based on our mesh augmentation procedures, enabling both shadows, cast from splats
onto the environment, and rigid-body collisions between
splats, classical meshes, and the environment
B3: a Splat Effects building block based on customizable shader
programs, enabling compelling visual experiences such as
object dis- and re-integration
B4: a Splat Blending building block enabling blending between
the physical scene and the virtual scene in AR
Building blocks can be accessed by developers via the developer
interface, which is implemented as a dedicated panel in the Unity
Editor. Actions performed in the developer interface add or modify
assets in the project folder as well as game objects and attached
components in the scene hierarchy. We show our editor interface
in more detail in Appendix A.1.
4.2.1 B1: Splat Renderer. The Splat Renderer building block comprises a 3D Gaussian splat rendering pipeline designed for compatibility with standalone XR devices, including Meta Quest and
Apple Vision Pro. On both Quest and Vision Pro, it can render
fully immersive splat-based VR scenes, and on Quest, it can render
splats and blend them with the passthrough layer for situated AR
experiences.2 It can be executed untethered on standalone headsets
or tethered to a PC. In our multi-layered reality framework, this
represents the photometric layer.
Developer Workflow. To leverage the renderer, developers simply
import a splat into a Unity scene by making use of the SplatXR
Importer panel. Within the panel, they choose a standard splat ply
file, which they either create themselves or download from a third
party. Upon import, the plugin automatically adds a game object to
the scene hierarchy, applies a predefined splat material associated
with our splat shader, and adds a splat rendering script to the game
object. The renderer not only works properly on-headset but is also
aware of when it renders in-editor, switching to the correct editor
camera and rendering parameters accordingly. This ensures faithful

Implementation. We implemented the renderer as a shader program in Unity’s high-level shader language (HLSL). The shader
program is based on the functionality presented in the original
splatting paper [34]. At design time, once the developer specifies
a splat ply file in the import panel, SplatXR converts it into a
custom CSV format by performing preprocessing steps that include
computing the covariance matrix needed by our renderer, and denormalizing color and transparency information. At run time, our
splat rendering script reads the custom splat file, which contains the
attributes of each 3D Gaussian (covariance, color, alpha), and then
assembles an arrangement of triangles. The triangles are passed
into the shader memory and sorted from the perspective of a given
camera (either in-editor or on-headset) at an adjustable rate (1 Hz
by default) in a background thread on the CPU in the rendering
script. The shader takes care of rendering each splat-associated
triangle and alpha-blending it for the rendering result.
Level-of-Detail Representation. High-resolution splats lead to noticeable frame drops, and very high-resolution splats, e.g., with 1
million Gaussians or more, become infeasible on current mobile
GPUs, such as the Qualcomm Snapdragon XR2 Gen 2 found in
the Quest 3, especially because the computational budget needs
to be shared with many other tasks that are performed on the device. To ensure that end-users can still benefit from high-resolution
splats, we propose and implement a level-of-detail (LoD) rendering approach. It displays the high-resolution splat only in close
proximity to the user while rendering lower-resolution splats with
increasing distance. In our implementation, we use three levels
of detail. To make use of the level-of-detail representation, developers merely need to provide the three splats with varying splat

renderings, their representation lacks surface information. This lack

models for physics simulations, e.g., for shadow map computations
or collision detection. We therefore augment the splat with an
underlying surface mesh representation that can be accessed by
the 3D engine for physics simulations, while we still rely solely on
the splat to write to the visual framebuffer, thus obtaining the best
of both worlds. In our multi-layered reality framework, this allows

mesh colliders as needed. This ensures only the splat is visible,

SplatXR implements two mesh generation algorithms. First, we
implemented a custom marching-cubes-based algorithm that produces fine detail but hinges on the assumption of convexity of the
splat to be reconstructed. While this assumption is sufficiently satisfied for objects, it does not hold for spaces, and therefore, we also
implemented a Poisson-based reconstruction algorithm for spaces.
Developer Workflow. To enable physics simulations for an imported splat object, developers can check the generate mesh checkbox in SplatXR’s import panel, select an appropriate mesh generation algorithm, and override required parameters as needed. To
maintain simplicity in the developer interface, we allow developers to choose between an object-optimized reconstruction based
on our marching cubes implementation (ideal for close interaction, physics simulations, casting shadows, and manipulation) and
a space-optimized approach for indoor or outdoor spaces based
on the Poisson-based implementation (apt for providing coarser
collision meshes for larger environmental scenes).
Implementation: Object-Optimized Phantom Mesh Generation. To
generate a mesh from an object splat, we first create a mesh for
each Gaussian ellipsoid from the input PLY file, arranged in a mesh
grid. The intermediate representation is a mesh reconstruction of
each Gaussian, which together represent the entire object. Next, a
splat grid and an isosurface are constructed based on the densities
from the Gaussian ellipsoids. We then perform marching cubes
over this result to create a mesh with a smoothed surface before
decimating it for better performance. Object reconstructions can
vary in accuracy depending on the structural characteristics of the
input splat, and SplatXR allows developers to tune parameters
for the splat scale and meshing grid threshold. Problematically,
Shadows and Occlusions. Splat objects that have been augmented
with the generated and co-aligned mesh are registered with Unity’s
native shadow mapping procedure, enabling them to cast shadows onto other objects in the scene. On Quest devices, SplatXR
leverages the Meta Scene API to obtain an environment mesh, automatically configures it as a phantom shadow catcher [7, 31], and
thus enables the visual impression of casting splat shadows onto
the physical world. Practically, SplatXR automatically attaches
a custom shadow-casting material to the augmenting mesh. The
shadow-casting material uses an HLSL program that writes to neither the framebuffer nor the depth buffer—leaving the augmenting
mesh invisible and non-interfering with the splat renderer—while
still contributing a shadow-casting pass to Unity’s shadow mapping.
Fig. 5 shows a shadow of a splat on a physical environment. Occlusions where splats cover parts of an otherwise visible mesh are
implemented by culling occluded mesh geometry in the splat shader.
Collisions. SplatXR further makes use of the augmenting mesh
for collisions. To this end, it automatically attaches a mesh collider to the augmenting mesh, thus computing a tightly fitting
convex hull over the vertices within that mesh. This in turn allows
attaching Unity’s rigid-body behavior to the object for collision
simulation. Fig. 2 shows an example of a splat object colliding with
other splats (coke cans), other classical mesh objects (green ball),
and the physical environment (table). Based on the rigid-body behavior, SplatXR can automatically enable grab interactions with
the splat. Furthermore, when used with Unity’s navigation mesh
component on a space splat, this approach can turn the space splat
into a walkable surface for end-users or virtual characters.

block, we leverage this property of splats to our advantage for vivid

provide real-world awareness [24], diminish or animate physical

Implementation: 3D View-Dependent Portal. We demonstrate the

two eye-aligned cameras, in image space. By allowing the developer
to customize the rendering layers, SplatXR can ensure that only
the desired virtual splat geometry is rendered.

Headset-Guided Object Reconstruction
SplatXR’s building blocks provide a fully self-contained library
of reusable components for on-device splatting. To bring the advances of compute-intensive vision-foundation models for on-thefly splat reconstruction to interactive XR applications on computeconstrained headsets, we also provide an edge-computing-based
system for lifting physical objects into splat replicas using imageto-3D foundation models.
To reconstruct an object, users select a single point or multiple
points in their physical space through their headset. The system

Applications
To provide an overview of SplatXR’s use cases, we developed
several applications demonstrating multi-layered reality configurations built from SplatXR’s building blocks, as shown in Fig. 2.
Three capabilities that no prior tool provides together recur across
all of them: untethered on-headset splat rendering, which lets them
run outside without a rendering server or PC-VR cable; automatic
splat-to-mesh proxy generation, which supplies the collision and
shadow geometry that splats lack; and AR passthrough compositing
with occlusion from environment geometry.
Virtual Try-On. An application showcasing semantic linkage
where a splat of a watch (photometric layer) is dynamically overlaid
onto the user’s wrist (physical layer) as identified by the semantic
layer. By exposing the splat as a Unity GameObject, SplatXR’s
Splat Renderer allows developers to parent it directly to the Meta
SDK’s hand-tracking anchor. This demonstrates how splats can
be driven by physical reality and leverage mesh augmentation in
Splat Physics for occlusion, sparing developers from implementing
complex rendering and mesh-occlusion logic.
Outdoor Tour. A full overlay application that places the user
entirely within a captured splat environment, replacing the physical layer with a comprehensive splat photometric layer enabled
by the Splat Renderer. The user’s physical movement is restricted
by a phantom structure mapped to the splat using Splat Physics.

Its splat-to-mesh proxy generation yields a mesh collider that prevents users from walking or clipping through surfaces, keeping

Library Focus Mode. A reality blending application where the
user replaces their busy peripheral surroundings with a quiet,
static splat of an empty library (photometric layer) rendered by the
Splat Renderer, keeping only their immediate desk visible through

Ball Toss Game. An AR game in which a user tosses a virtual
ball at an arrangement of splat soda cans resting on a physical
table. Mapping each component to its respective layer (Fig. 2c)
defines how they interact across different representations of reality: the mixed-reality passthrough forms the underlying base in
the physical layer, the splat cans are situated in the photometric
layer using the Splat Renderer, and the virtual ball operates within
the geometric layer, which encapsulates both its explicit geometry
and its visual rendering. Cross-layer dynamics, such as the ball
bouncing off the table and colliding with the cans, rely on phantom
structures operationalized by Splat Physics. These act as invisible,
anchored geometries related to both the physical environment
mesh (via the Meta SDK scene mesh) and the splat cans, which
underpin the visible elements and allow physics interactions across
layers. This decomposition provides a mental model for how distinct visual, structural, and physical elements converge in a unified
mixed-reality environment.
Botanical Garden Experience. A volumetric portal, created with
additive layering and spatial masking from Splat Blending, reveals
a botanical garden (photometric layer via the Splat Renderer). Users
can reach through the portal to extract individual plant splats and
place them into their physical room (physical layer). Phantom structures via Splat Physics, attached to the garden, plants, and physical
environment, allow the splat plants to cast accurate shadows over
real-world surfaces and enable collisions across layers.
Situated Interior Design. Users place high-fidelity furniture splats
(photometric layer via the Splat Renderer) into their physical room
(physical layer). To ensure plausible interaction, Splat Physics generates phantom structures for both the imported furniture and surrounding surfaces. This enables physical collisions and realistic
shadow casting. SplatXR handles proxy generation, environment
occlusion, and passthrough color correction so captured furniture
matches the room’s lighting—an AR-specific challenge unaddressed
by VR-only systems.

We evaluated our renderer and toolkit to investigate different components of the system and their support for a range of splat types
for standalone experiences. We tested 27 configurations: 3 objects
(plant, car, bonsai), 3 indoor scenes (office, botanical, library), and 3
outdoor scenes (sundial, lighthouse, plaza), each at 3 splat counts

(50k, 100k, and 200k). We include additional details and data in
Appendix A.2 and supplementary materials.
6.1
Renderer
Because visible splat density affects performance, indoor and outdoor scenes were measured at both their densest and sparsest viewpoints. Frame rate was capped at 72 fps. CPU frame time was
negligible across all conditions (M=2.3 ms, SD=0.3 ms), making the
results GPU-bound. Metrics were averaged over 3 repetitions on a
standalone Quest 3, measured with the OVR Metrics Tool, under
Unity default quality, on a full battery, from a fixed position.
For objects, the renderer sustained a full 72 fps for both 50k
and 100k splats (GPU time 7.6 and 9.7 ms, with negligible frame
drops). At 200k, GPU time averaged 15.4 ms and frame rate averaged
59 fps, with frame drops averaging under 6%. For outdoor scenes
at the sparsest viewpoint, the renderer held 72 fps through 100k
and averaged 64 fps at 200k (GPU time 3–11 ms); at the densest
viewpoint it averaged 72 fps at 50k, 53 fps at 100k, and 38 fps at 200k
(GPU time 12–26 ms), with frame drops staying low. For indoor
scenes at the sparsest viewpoint, the renderer averaged 63 fps at
50k, 61 fps at 100k, and 58 fps at 200k; at the densest viewpoint
it averaged 58 fps at 50k, 46 fps at 100k, and 36 fps at 200k with
GPU time ranging 13–32 ms, with GPU utilization at 92% and frame
drops averaging 2%. On launch, memory was 1.3 GB at the first
splat, increasing by 60–140 MB per additional 50k splats. Repeating
the full suite reproduced nearly identical results. We observed no
performance drop when splat objects were moved or augmented
with meshes. Internal tests mixing objects, scenes, and passthrough
showed that performance depends on the total number of visible
splats. We also tested our system with combined splats at different
scales and with XR scene interactions.
The average scene import and CSV conversion times for the 9
splats increase with the number of splats, measuring 0.73 ± 0.14 s
for 50k, 1.38 ± 0.12 s for 100k, and 2.5 ± 0.23 s for 200k splats.
6.2
Splat-to-Mesh
We ran splat-to-mesh conversion on the same 27 splat files used
in the rendering evaluation. The object algorithm was applied to
the 3 objects and the space algorithm to the 6 scenes. Object meshing averaged 25.7 s for 50k splats, 29.9 s for 100k, and 41.4 s for
200k, scaling gradually with splat count. Space meshing was faster,
averaging 18.8 s for 50k splats, 22.7 s for 100k, and 31.6 s for 200k.
6.3
Object Reconstruction
This pipeline, which offloads SAM 3D to a PC, reconstructed an
object in an average of 2.38 min (SD = 0.16 min) across four trials.
6.4
Failure Cases
With our renderer, performance degraded with increased camera
proximity and the associated rise in visible splat density. Large
high-density splats close to the camera caused stuttering. For our
splat-to-mesh operation, the coarse space meshing may struggle
with scenes containing many detailed objects or intricate structures,
as mesh simplification loses this detail.

Preliminary Developer Study
We evaluate SplatXR following Ledo et al.’s [37] framework, pairing a demonstration of applications with a usage evaluation in the
form of a first-use study structured around Olsen’s UI systems criteria [55]: importance and STU context (Situations, Tasks, and Users),
expressive leverage and match, power in combination, generality,
and scale. We conducted four expert interviews during the development process of the building blocks and a small first-use study
(n=3) after creating an initial version of the toolkit.
7.1
Formative Expert Interviews
We conducted four semi-structured interviews with experts from
the XR domain and the computer vision/graphics domain to understand their potential needs. The first participant (E1) was a
filmmaker of Unity-based VR films who uses Nerfstudio, Postshot,
Blender, and Houdini to train, edit, and render splats as part of
their artistic workflow. The second participant (E2) was a computer
vision researcher working on splatting algorithms. The third participant (E3) was an HCI researcher who works with splats and
XR. The fourth participant (E4) was a computer vision researcher
with experience in neural rendering. We first asked a set of basic
questions regarding their use of splatting, and then aimed to elicit
thoughts on pain points for the problem of bringing splats into XR.
E1 stressed integration challenges, noting that “the biggest pain
point is that splats are just not playing nicely with everything else
in the Unity project” and wishing that features spread across many
tools could be combined into “one mega tool.” E2 emphasized the
need for a real-time preview in any splatting system to “get a sense
of the [splat] density.” E2 further highlighted the computational
efficiency of splats over neural radiance fields, but felt unsure about
the feasibility of implementing splat rendering on a mobile GPU. E1
pointed out that splats really “shine” compared to other methods in
terms of photorealism of lighting; however, this advantage would
be invalidated if even basic lighting phenomena such as shadows
were not properly rendered in XR. E1 and E3 mentioned the issue of blending and compositing multiple splats in a scene with
current tools. E4 described the need for a “lightweight” real-time
renderer. E3 described a limitation of splats compared to meshes:
“[if] I want some physics... just throwing this object... mesh will be
better.” E3 found current tools difficult to use and expressed interest
in a toolkit that can “accelerate that process or make it simpler for
people to use.” E4 mentioned that, despite splats’ lack of accurate
“3D structure,” meshing would enable “physics interaction... that is
probably good enough.”
Using Olsen’s strategy [55], these interviews establish the importance of the problem and its STU context: the users are XR
developers, the task is assembling XR experiences that combine
captured splats with virtual meshes, and the situation is on-headset
development for standalone XR where compute is constrained and
workflows must integrate with existing Unity/Meta SDK pipelines.
The interviews also surface a problem not previously solved: splats
are incompatible with Unity projects and difficult to build XR applications around. From this, we derive two criteria the toolkit
must satisfy: a match with existing XR development practices, and
splat-mesh augmentation to enable physics and shadows.

Limitations and Future Work
While SplatXR was designed to help developers solve recurring
challenges in AR/VR, future research is needed to improve it.
Improving the Renderer. With our LoD representation, we can render scenes across an order of magnitude (e.g., 100K–1M Gaussians).
However, to enable splats with even higher counts, research into
potential computational improvements of the renderer is needed,
e.g., by adapting techniques for foveated rendering. Furthermore,
SplatXR supports static splats, but a sequence of static splats is
less expressive and computationally less efficient than a dedicated
representation for dynamic splats. Therefore, it would be worth
investigating options to bring dynamic splats to the headset, such as
4D video or splat-based avatars. Additionally, while our framework
supports shadows, the splats themselves remain unresponsive to
environmental lighting. Exploring real-time relighting techniques
on splats would enable more realistic compositing.
Analyzing Splats for Interactive Purposes. SplatXR abstracts away
from the content of a splat. It would be interesting to add methods
based on either standard geometric processing or machine learning
to semantically analyze an imported splat and set it up for common
uses in XR. For example, the walkable space of a splat could be
derived to navigate a game character on it, to facilitate VR illusions,
or to automatically align the splat with the physical walkable area.
Providing Training Capabilities. We implemented SplatXR to
support developers in integrating existing splats in their XR applications. We believe that it could be worth exploring interactions
and system components needed to capture a splat from the XR
device’s cameras, potentially using generative models.
Outlook. We believe that a fundamental mismatch has emerged:
while established XR pipelines are explicitly geometric, emerging
scene representations are increasingly implicit and lack structure.
Because 3DGS lacks the explicit properties of traditional polygonal
meshes, it remains isolated from standard graphics techniques like
shadows and collisions. Furthermore, unlike 2D pixel-based generation or generative video, XR is defined by 6DOF movement through
3D physical space. This spatial nature demands explicit grounding;
we cannot yet operate solely in pixel space when user perspectives and hand interactions are tied to physical 3D coordinates.
While recent action-conditioned generative models allow world
exploration via keyboard inputs (WASD) [15], such paradigms are
fundamentally incompatible with the fluid spatial input of XR.
UIST ’26, November 02–05, 2026, Detroit, MI, USA
We designed our framework to bridge this divide by treating
3DGS strictly as a visual rendering layer, an interface that maps
camera poses to pixel outputs. In this capacity, 3DGS serves as a performant proxy for the long-term vision of personalized generative
situated world models, world models situated in reality which will
eventually model immersive 3D scenes and dynamics based on an
implicit understanding of the physical environment and the user. By
decoupling the visual output from the underlying scene structure,
our architecture remains representation-agnostic. Whether the underlying representation is 3DGS today or structured fully implicit
world models in the future, our system provides a vision for an
architecture to integrate these unstructured, implicit 3D representations with the explicit structure required for physical interaction.

Conclusion
We presented SplatXR, a Unity toolkit that brings Gaussian splatting to standalone XR headsets through modular building blocks:
a real-time on-device renderer, splat-to-mesh reconstruction, splat
physics, splat blending, and offloaded object reconstruction from
headset capture. Together, these components integrate computer
vision and graphics techniques into a single developer workflow,
making splat-based XR development accessible to the wider XR
community. By organizing its components around a multi-layered
composition of physical and digital primitives, SplatXR allows
pixels and objects to move across the boundaries between physical
and virtual representations, so that virtual splats cast shadows on
real surfaces, physical geometry constrains splat physics, and captured objects re-enter the scene as interactive digital twins. Unlike
prior splatting work confined to VR, SplatXR equally supports AR,
running wire- and server-free with passthrough compositing, alpha
blending, and occlusion from reconstructed geometry. By exposing these capabilities through a familiar Unity interface, SplatXR
provides the technical foundation and mental model to treat the
physical and digital as a cohesive, multi-layered reality, where the
transition between physicality and virtuality is architecturally supported rather than technically disjointed. We release SplatXR as
open source to support continued community development.
