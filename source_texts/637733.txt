Abstract
Multi-view 3D human pose estimation underpins applications in virtual reality,
motion capture, and sports analytics, yet most methods critically depend on
pre-calibrated cameras, requiring controlled environments and laborious setup
that hinder real-world deployment. We propose PACT (Joint Pose and Camera Transformer), an end-to-end multi-task framework that jointly recovers 3D
human poses and camera parameters from only multi-view 2D keypoints, eliminating the need for any prior calibration. Inspired by the Vision Geometry
Grounded Transformer (VGGT), PACT integrates view-aware feature encoding
to inject joint identity and view-specific spatial context, an alternating attention
mechanism that interleaves frame-level and cross-view attention to balance local
detail with global geometric consistency, and multi-task prediction heads that
iteratively refine both 3D pose and a compact 9-DoF camera parameterization,
optimized through a composite loss combining adaptive MPJPE, multi-stage
camera supervision, and skeleton constraints. Beyond achieving competitive pose
accuracy, PACT distinguishes itself from prior uncalibrated methods in two key
aspects: it jointly estimates camera parameters alongside 3D pose, treating the
human body as an implicit calibration target, and it exhibits strong generalization across both variable view counts and unseen real-world domains, owing to the
view-invariant alternating attention design and geometric normalization scheme.
2
Extensive experiments on Human3.6M and CMU Panoptic demonstrate that
PACT achieves 28.1 mm MPJPE on Human3.6M among uncalibrated methods
while predicting camera parameters with 0.9◦ rotation and 32.2 mm translation
errors, remains robust across 2–24 views, and generalizes to handheld real-world
captures without retraining or fine-tuning. Ablation studies further verify the
necessity of each architectural component for both accuracy and generalization.
Our code is available at https://github.com/PACT-3D/PACT.

1 Introduction
Multi-view 3D human pose estimation and camera parameter estimation are core
research topics in computer vision, with significant theoretical value and broad practical application prospects in virtual reality, motion analysis, film production, sports
analytics, and human-robot interaction [1]. The development of these application
domains requires increasingly accurate, convenient, and robust 3D human pose estimation algorithms that can operate without restrictive hardware setups. In recent years,
deep learning-based methods have achieved remarkable progress, and the end-to-end
learning paradigm has demonstrated great potential for simultaneously addressing
pose estimation and camera estimation [2, 3]. However, how to effectively fuse multiview information and establish geometric consistency across views remains a critical
open challenge in this field.
Current 3D human pose estimation methods can be broadly categorized into singleview and multi-view approaches [4]. Single-view methods, by virtue of requiring only
a monocular camera, are widely applicable and easy to deploy. However, they fundamentally suffer from the inherent depth ambiguity of monocular 3D reconstruction,
which makes 3D human pose estimation an ill-posed problem—especially in challenging scenarios involving severe occlusion, multi-person interactions, and unusual camera
viewpoints [5, 6]. In contrast, multi-view methods can recover depth information and
occluded body parts by exploiting complementary observations from different viewpoints, achieving substantially higher accuracy than single-view methods [7, 8]. This
accuracy advantage has made multi-view systems the preferred choice for applications
demanding precise 3D reconstruction.
Nevertheless, traditional multi-view methods treat pose estimation and camera
calibration as two separate tasks, relying heavily on pre-calibrated camera information [9–11]. Obtaining such calibration requires either a controlled environment
or resource-intensive structure-from-motion (SfM) procedures, severely restricting
flexible deployment in real-world scenarios.
The Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer (VGGT) [12] offers a new paradigm
for addressing this bottleneck. VGGT is a feed-forward neural network capable of
directly inferring camera intrinsics and extrinsics, point maps, depth maps, and
3D point trajectories from multi-view images. Its core architecture encodes image
3
patches into tokens and alternately performs frame-level attention and global selfattention—frame attention preserves single-view local details while global attention
establishes cross-view geometric consistency—with dedicated prediction heads simultaneously outputting camera parameters and 3D structure. This design enables VGGT
to perform end-to-end multi-view 3D reconstruction and camera estimation without
pre-calibration, without epipolar matching, and without explicit geometric constraints.
3D human pose estimation is particularly well-suited to the VGGT paradigm for
several reasons. First, the human skeleton possesses strong structured priors: 17 joints
form a tree-structured kinematic chain through known anatomical connections, and
relative positions between joints are constrained by fixed bone lengths—making predictions significantly more tractable than reconstructing arbitrarily distributed point
clouds in general scenes. Second, human joints maintain unambiguous semantic correspondences across views: regardless of the camera angle, “left wrist” remains the
same semantic entity, eliminating the feature matching ambiguity that plagues general
scene reconstruction. Third, the human body itself can serve as an implicit calibration target: the known skeletal structure and approximate bilateral symmetry provide
natural geometric references for inferring the relative poses between multi-view cameras. These properties imply that by migrating VGGT’s joint prediction capability
from image patches to scene point clouds to 2D keypoints to 3D skeleton and camera
parameters, the structured priors of the human body substantially reduce the degrees
of freedom (DoF), making joint prediction even more feasible than in general scene
reconstruction.
Conversely, four characteristics of the VGGT architecture are particularly wellmatched to these requirements. First, the alternating attention mechanism is naturally
suited to multi-view human pose estimation: frame attention focuses on joint localization within each view, while global attention establishes correspondences between
the same joints across views without requiring explicit epipolar matching—avoiding
the error accumulation inherent in traditional triangulation-based pipelines; moreover,
since global attention treats all views as an unordered set, a single model can accommodate an arbitrary number of cameras, inherently supporting view-count generalization.
Second, the multi-task prediction head design enables a unified Transformer backbone
to simultaneously output 3D structure and camera parameters, avoiding error propagation in decoupled pipelines while allowing the two predictions to constrain and refine
each other through geometric consistency. Third, VGGT does not rely on explicit 3D
inductive biases such as epipolar constraints or triangulation formulas—all geometric
knowledge is learned purely from data, which potentially allows the model to learn
more robust multi-view reasoning beyond handcrafted geometric rules. Fourth, and of
particular relevance to this work, VGGT exhibits strong cross-domain generalization,
performing effectively on datasets and real-world scenes unseen during training. This
generalization is not incidental: it stems from the view-invariant alternating attention
design, first-camera coordinate normalization, and large-scale multi-dataset training.
A central concern of this paper is to verify whether such generalization persists under
sparse keypoint inputs and to analyze its underlying mechanisms.

Inspired by VGGT, we aim to transfer this uncalibrated joint prediction paradigm
to the human pose estimation domain. However, adapting VGGT to human pose estimation still faces targeted challenges: (1) at the input level, 2D keypoint coordinates
lack the rich texture and appearance information present in image patches, causing
the loss of joint semantic identity and view-specific spatial context when directly used
as tokens; (2) at the output level, human joint prediction must capture skeletal kinematic constraints and biomechanical plausibility, rather than generic scene point cloud
reconstruction; (3) at the optimization level, carefully designed multi-task strategies
are required to ensure that pose and camera parameter predictions mutually reinforce
rather than interfere with each other. Fig. 1 contrasts conventional methods with our
unified framework.
To this end, we propose PACT (Joint Pose and Camera Transformer), a framework
built upon VGGT’s core backbone with three key adaptations for the human pose
domain: (1) view-aware feature encoding—projecting 2D keypoints into a highdimensional embedding space and injecting learnable joint identity embeddings and
view-specific spatial embeddings to compensate for the absence of texture information; (2) alternating attention fusion—retaining VGGT’s mechanism of alternating
frame-level and global attention to balance single-view fine-grained localization
with cross-view geometric consistency; (3) multi-task prediction heads—cascaded
Transformer decoders iteratively refine 3D human joint coordinates while concurrently
predicting compact 9-DoF camera extrinsics, optimized through a composite loss
combining adaptive MPJPE, multi-stage camera supervision, and skeleton constraints.
Building upon this foundation, our framework PACT integrates the three core
components detailed in Section 3: view-aware feature encoding, alternating attention fusion, and multi-task prediction heads with iterative refinement. Thanks to the
random view count sampling strategy during training and the design where global
attention in the alternating mechanism treats all views as an unordered set, PACT
5
can be directly deployed with any number of views from 2 to 24 without retraining or
parameter modification.
We conduct extensive experiments on CMU Panoptic and Human3.6M [13, 14],
demonstrating that PACT achieves competitive 3D pose accuracy among uncalibrated
methods while accurately predicting camera parameters. We further provide ablation
studies, robustness analysis across 2–24 input views, qualitative visualizations, and
real-world generalization experiments.
In summary, the primary contributions of this work are as follows:
• We propose PACT, an end-to-end framework that jointly predicts 3D human keypoints and camera parameters from uncalibrated multi-view 2D inputs, eliminating
the need for pre-calibration.
• We design an alternating attention mechanism and dynamic view-count-adaptive
spatial embedding that together make PACT inherently invariant to the number
and ordering of input views, enabling a single trained model to operate on 2–24
views without retraining.
• We demonstrate that PACT learns transferable multi-view geometric priors: it not
only achieves competitive uncalibrated 3D pose accuracy on standard benchmarks
but also generalizes to real-world handheld captures with unknown cameras and
uncontrolled environments, without any fine-tuning.
• We provide comprehensive ablation studies, per-joint error analysis, and robustness
evaluation across view counts, together with a methodological analysis explaining how alternating attention, geometric normalization, and shared joint identity
embeddings structurally enable PACT’s generalization.
2 Related Work
Multi-view 3D human pose estimation has emerged as a fundamental task in computer vision, enabling applications ranging from motion capture to augmented reality.
Over the past decade, significant progress has been made, driven by advances in deep
learning and geometric modeling. In this section, we review the key developments in
this field, focusing on calibrated methods, uncalibrated approaches, camera parameter
estimation, and multi-view feature fusion strategies, highlighting the limitations that
motivate our approach.
2.1 Calibrated Multi-view 3D Human Pose Estimation
Calibrated methods represent the traditional approach to multi-view pose estimation,
where camera intrinsic and extrinsic parameters are assumed to be known a priori.
Early work in this area was dominated by geometric methods based on triangulation.
Ionescu et al. [14] introduced the Human3.6M dataset and demonstrated the effectiveness of model-based triangulation using 2D joint detections across four synchronized
cameras. Joo et al. [13] extended this paradigm to large-scale multi-view systems with
the CMU Panoptic dataset, enabling whole-body pose estimation in complex scenes
with hundreds of cameras.
6
With the advent of deep learning, researchers began exploring end-to-end learning
approaches that move beyond explicit geometric formulations. Pavlakos et al. [4] proposed a coarse-to-fine volumetric method that aggregates 2D heatmaps across views
into a 3D occupancy grid, representing one of the first fully differentiable multi-view
pose estimation pipelines. This work laid the foundation for subsequent volumetric
approaches, including Tu et al. [15] who extended the voxel-based framework to multiperson scenarios, and Ye et al. [16] who improved computational efficiency. Iskakov
et al. [17] introduced learnable triangulation that jointly refines 2D heatmaps and
3D pose through differentiable algebraic triangulation, achieving strong results on
standard benchmarks.
Transformer architectures have also made significant contributions to the calibrated setting. Zheng et al. [18] introduced Spatial-Temporal Transformers that
model long-range dependencies across views and time steps through dedicated attention mechanisms. Similarly, Dong et al. [19] employed graph convolutional networks
to explicitly model inter-view relationships with anatomical constraints, achieving
state-of-the-art results on standard benchmarks. He et al. [20] incorporated epipolar geometry as an inductive bias within the learning framework, further improving
robustness to viewpoint variation. More recently, Ma et al. [21] proposed a progressive
pose transfer method that incrementally refines 3D pose estimates across views, and
Qiu et al. [22] introduced cross-view fusion for occluded joint recovery.
Despite these advances, all calibrated methods critically depend on precalibrated cameras, which requires controlled environments or resource-intensive
SfM procedures—often infeasible in dynamic real-world deployments. This fundamental dependency limits their practical applicability beyond laboratory settings.
2.2 Uncalibrated Multi-view 3D Human Pose Estimation
Uncalibrated methods aim to reconstruct 3D poses without prior camera calibration, making them more adaptable to unconstrained environments. Early attempts
focused on self-calibration techniques. Rhodin et al. [23] introduced a multi-view
geometry-aware approach that learns to factorize viewpoint and pose information from
uncalibrated footage.
Recent years have seen significant progress in this direction. FLEX [24] proposed
FLEX, a neural network approach that jointly estimates camera parameters and 3D
poses from multi-view 2D keypoints using a lightweight architecture, demonstrating
promising results on challenging in-the-wild datasets. This work is among the first to
explicitly target joint estimation of both outputs in an uncalibrated setting.
More recently, Chen et al. [25] introduced the UniCodebook framework, which uses
unified 2D-3D discrete priors to address error propagation across views by quantizing
pose representations into a shared codebook. Jiang et al. [26] extended probabilistic methods with learnable geometric constraints, improving robustness to extreme
viewpoint variations. Zhang et al. [27] proposed a deep semantic graph transformer
that captures both local joint-level and global body-level dependencies for multi-view
fusion. Zhang et al. [28] developed a progressive refinement pipeline that incrementally updates 3D pose estimates through successive rounds of cross-view triangulation
and refinement. In a different direction, Chen et al. [29] proposed a 3D space attention
7
mechanism that reasons over spatial relationships across views for multi-view fusion,
and Bragagnolo et al. [30] explored the use of 3D Gaussian splatting for differentiable
rendering-based multi-view fusion, representing emerging trends that integrate explicit
3D representations.
While these methods eliminate the need for explicit calibration, most focus solely
on 3D pose reconstruction and do not predict camera parameters, limiting their
utility in downstream tasks such as free-viewpoint rendering and scene reconstruction.
Moreover, a persistent trade-off between single-view detail preservation and cross-view
consistency creates an accuracy gap relative to calibrated methods. Our framework
addresses both issues through alternating attention and unified multi-task prediction
heads.
2.3 Camera Parameter Estimation
Camera parameter estimation is a foundational problem in computer vision, with
applications spanning SLAM, SfM, augmented reality, and robotics. Traditional methods rely on calibration patterns such as chessboards or fiducial markers [9], or SfM
techniques that recover camera parameters from multi-view image correspondences
[10]. While highly accurate, these approaches require either specialized calibration
equipment or extensive scene information with sufficient texture and viewpoint
variation, severely limiting their practicality in dynamic, uncontrolled environments.
The emergence of deep learning has enabled data-driven camera parameter estimation directly from visual inputs. Kendall et al. [11] pioneered the direct regression of
camera pose from single images using a convolutional neural network, demonstrating
that learned features can encode sufficient geometric information for pose estimation
without explicit feature matching. This work sparked a line of research on learningbased camera localization. DeTone et al. [31] introduced SuperPoint, a self-supervised
feature detector and descriptor that can be used as a frontend for both camera pose
estimation and mapping tasks. Wang et al. [32] integrated scene geometric constraints
into their deep learning framework through an SfM pipeline built on Transformer
features, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on challenging benchmarks.
For human-centric applications, several works have explored the relationship
between human pose and camera geometry. FLEX [24] demonstrated that camera
parameters can be estimated jointly with 3D pose in a multi-view setup, treating the
human body itself as an implicit calibration target, though their approach is limited
to a fixed parameterization and does not scale effectively to variable view counts.
A critical limitation of most existing methods is that camera estimation and 3D
reconstruction are treated as separate, sequential tasks. This inherently introduces error accumulation: inaccuracies in camera prediction propagate irreversibly
into 3D reconstruction, with no corrective feedback loop. In contrast, our framework
jointly optimizes camera parameters and 3D pose in a single end-to-end differentiable
pipeline, treating the human body as the calibration target and enabling mutual
cross-task correction through iterative refinement.
8
2.4 Multi-view Feature Fusion Strategies
Effective feature fusion across views is crucial for accurate multi-view pose estimation, as it determines how complementary information from different viewpoints is
aggregated and reconciled. Early methods used simple averaging or concatenation of
features from different views, which, while computationally efficient, fail to model the
complex geometric relationships between viewpoints and treat all views as equally
informative regardless of their quality or relevance to specific joints.
More sophisticated fusion approaches have been proposed in recent years.
A particularly influential development is the VGGT framework proposed by Wang
et al. [12], which leverages a pure Transformer backbone to jointly process all-view
tokens in a unified attention space. Unlike previous methods that require explicit
epipolar matching, feature correspondence, or geometric cost volumes, VGGT demonstrates that a Transformer can implicitly learn multi-view geometric constraints
through self-attention alone. This approach naturally captures both local image details
and global geometric relationships without hand-crafted geometric priors, providing
a strong and principled foundation for multi-view reasoning that is both elegant and
effective.
Our work is directly inspired by this paradigm and extends it to the human
pose domain: (1) we redesign the input encoding for 2D keypoints with joint identity and view-specific spatial embeddings instead of generic image patch tokens; (2)
we introduce dedicated prediction heads for 3D pose and camera parameters that
exploit human articulation structure through cascaded Transformers; and (3) most
critically, we introduce the alternating attention mechanism—interleaving frame-level
and global attention—specifically designed to address the tension between singleview detail preservation and cross-view consistency. This alternating pattern is shown
through ablation to be the most impactful architectural design choice.
3 Method
3.1 Overview
We propose PACT (Joint Pose and Camera Transformer), an end-to-end multi-task
learning framework that jointly predicts 3D human pose and camera parameters solely
from multi-view 2D keypoints, without any pre-calibrated camera information. PACT
treats the human body itself as an implicit calibration target, enabling pose estimation
and camera parameter estimation to mutually benefit each other within a single unified
pipeline. The overall architecture of PACT is shown in Fig. 2, which consists of three
core modules: (1) view-aware feature encoding, (2) alternating attention fusion, and
(3) multi-task prediction heads.

L layers of alternating attention, the aggregated features are obtained for subsequent
3D pose and camera parameter prediction.

3.5 Prediction Heads
Before the alternating attention blocks, PACT prepends two types of specialized learnable tokens to the joint token sequence: a camera token per view that accumulates
view-level geometric information, and a small set of register tokens that provide
additional capacity for the attention mechanism. The camera token uses two parameter templates—one for the first camera (fixed at the origin after geometric alignment)
and one shared across all other cameras—to encode the distinct roles of the reference
view and the remaining views. All tokens are processed jointly through the alternating attention backbone; afterward, the camera tokens and joint tokens are separated
and fed into PACT’s dual prediction heads.
12
3D Pose Head. To leverage hierarchical representations, PACT extracts joint
tokens from shallow, intermediate, and deep layers of the aggregator, where each layer’s
output concatenates frame and global attention features. Independent MLPs project
these features to a common dimension, and a learned gating mechanism fuses them
via softmax-weighted summation, yielding F ∈ RB×S×J×C .
Subsequent refinement proceeds in three stages. First, a cross-view Transformer
gathers per-joint features across views into a sequence with a learnable fusion token,
whose output serves as the canonical joint representation Jbase . Second, an interjoint Transformer processes all joints jointly to capture kinematic dependencies
between anatomically connected joints. Third, iterative refinement progressively
improves the pose estimate: the refinement begins from a learnable empty-pose parameter initialized near zero. At each step, the current pose estimate is embedded via an
MLP and mapped to modulation parameters (α, β, γ ) that condition Jbase through
adaptive layer normalization, followed by a residual update. The modulated features
pass through the inter-joint Transformer and a lightweight Conv1d regression head
to predict a residual ∆P̂(k) . On the first iteration, this delta directly forms the initial estimate P̂(0) ; on subsequent iterations, it is added to the previous estimate:
P̂(k) = P̂(k−1) + ∆P̂(k) .
Camera Parameter Head. The camera tokens are fed into a dedicated head
that predicts a compact 9D camera parameterization, defined as θ = [t, q, f ], where

and f ∈ R2 represents the horizontal and vertical field-of-view angles. PACT refines
the prediction over multiple stages, where each stage takes the previous estimate as
input and predicts a residual update:
θ (k) = θ (k−1) + ∆θ (k) ,

3.7 Generalization Mechanism Analysis
This section analyzes, from a design perspective, why PACT generalizes across both
variable view counts and unseen real-world domains.
View-Count Invariance through Alternating Attention. The alternating
attention mechanism (Sec. 3.3) is the architectural foundation of PACT’s view-count
generalization. Frame attention operates independently within each view, while global
attention treats tokens from all views as an unordered set for self-attention. Crucially,
neither attention type depends on the total number of views : frame attention scales linearly with view count, and global attention is invariant to the permutation of tokens.
As a result, the model cannot infer “how many cameras there are” from the architecture alone—it is forced to learn relative geometric relationships across views rather
than memorizing absolute camera-specific patterns. Coupled with random view count
sampling during training (Sec. 3.6), PACT never develops a fixed expectation of view
count, enabling natural generalization to 2–24 views at inference time.
14
Geometric Normalization for Cross-Domain Consistency. The geometric
alignment scheme (Sec. 3.4) serves a dual purpose: stabilizing training and enabling
cross-domain generalization. By baselining all coordinates to the first camera and normalizing by the average joint-to-origin distance, every training sample is projected into
a consistent normalized space—regardless of the absolute camera positions, subject
sizes, or capture volumes in the original scene. When PACT encounters a real-world
scene with substantially different camera baselines (e.g., handheld phones spread
across a room vs. fixed lab cameras in a controlled volume), the normalized coordinates remain within the distribution seen during training. Geometric alignment thus
acts as an implicit domain normalizer.
Shared Joint Identity Embeddings Prevent Overfitting. The joint identity
embedding (Sec. 3.2) is shared across all views—the same embedding vector represents “left wrist” regardless of which camera observes it. This design forces the model
to identify joints by their anatomical semantics rather than by their 2D positions in a
specific camera view, preventing the model from overfitting to dataset-specific 2D keypoint distributions. When tested on real-world scenes with different camera angles and
body orientations, the identity embedding provides a view-invariant anchor, enabling
correct cross-view joint association even when 2D positions differ significantly.
Design Synergy. These three components are not independent: alternating attention provides invariance to the number of views, geometric normalization provides
invariance to the capture domain, and shared joint identity embeddings prevent the
model from degenerating into dataset memorization. Their collective necessity is verified through ablation studies in Sec. 4.7, where removing each component individually
leads to substantial performance degradation. The generalization observed in PACT
is thus not an incidental byproduct of large-scale training, but a property structurally
enforced by these architectural choices.
4 Experiments
4.1 Datasets and Evaluation Protocol
4.1.1 Datasets
We conduct experiments on two standard multi-view human pose estimation benchmarks: Human3.6M [14] and CMU Panoptic [13].
Human3.6M is one of the most widely used benchmarks for 3D human pose estimation. It contains 3.6 million video frames captured by 4 synchronized and calibrated
cameras at 50 Hz, recording 11 professional actors performing 15 distinct actions in
a controlled indoor laboratory environment. Each frame is annotated with accurate
3D joint positions from a marker-based motion capture system, providing 32 joints
per subject. We follow the standard protocol: using subjects S1, S5, S6, S7, and S8
for training, and subjects S9 and S11 for testing. The 17-joint skeleton subset is used
following prior work [4, 17].
CMU Panoptic provides multi-view recordings captured in a geodesic dome
equipped with 480 VGA cameras and 31 HD cameras, offering dense spatial coverage
15
with rich viewpoint diversity. We focus on single-person scenarios and use the standard 17-joint COCO skeleton format. During training, we randomly sample 2 to 24
camera views per pose to expose the model to diverse viewpoint configurations.
4.1.2 Evaluation Protocol
Both datasets follow a unified evaluation protocol. MPJPE in millimeters measures the
mean Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth 3D joint positions after
aligning the root joint (pelvis). Procrustes-aligned MPJPE (PA-MPJPE) measures
the error after applying Procrustes alignment (rigid rotation and translation) between
the predicted and ground-truth 3D poses. For CMU Panoptic, we additionally report
Average Precision (AP). For camera parameter evaluation, we report rotation error,
translation error, and Area Under the Curve (AUC), following prior work [24].
4.2 Implementation Details
4.2.1 Model Configuration
Our model employs a Transformer backbone with L alternating attention layers (L/2
frame attention blocks and L/2 global attention blocks), a hidden dimension of C ,
and H attention heads per multi-head self-attention block. By default we use L = 8,
C = 512, and H = 8. The 3D pose head performs K = 4 iterative refinement steps,
and the camera head uses 6 refinement steps. The multi-scale feature fusion in the
pose head extracts joint tokens from the 0th , 4th , and 7th aggregator layers.
4.2.2 Training Configuration
All models are implemented in PyTorch and trained using the AdamW optimizer
with a base learning rate of 5 × 10−5 , a linear warmup schedule (5 epochs, starting
at 10% of base LR), and weight decay of 10−3 . The batch size is set to 32, and
we apply gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1.0. Training runs for 20 epochs
on Human3.6M and 50 epochs on CMU Panoptic. The multi-task loss weights use
a 3-epoch warmup: w3D = 12, wcam = 2.4, wskel = 2.5 during warmup, increasing
to w3D = 30, wcam = 3.0, wskel = 5.0 thereafter. The camera sub-loss weights are
wT = 1.5, wR = 2.0, and wFL = 0.3. We use an exponential moving average (EMA)
of model weights with decay 0.999 for validation and final evaluation. Training uses a
single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.

4.4 Cross-Dataset Analysis
Observing the results across datasets reveals a notable asymmetry: our method
achieves better 3D pose accuracy on CMU Panoptic (MPJPE 25.1 mm) than on
Human3.6M (28.1 mm), despite CMU Panoptic featuring more diverse camera configurations. We attribute this to the following: CMU Panoptic’s 500+ cameras distributed
across a geodesic dome provide rich viewpoint diversity during training, enabling
stronger cross-view correspondence learning, whereas Human3.6M’s 4 fixed cameras
provide limited viewpoint variation.
In terms of camera parameter estimation, Human3.6M performs better: AUC@30
of 96.7% vs. 94.4% on CMU Panoptic, with lower rotation error (0.9° vs. 1.2°). This is
largely because Human3.6M’s 4 fixed cameras provide stable and consistent geometric
constraints, which are easier to learn compared to the greater variability introduced
by random subsets drawn from CMU Panoptic’s 500+ cameras. This highlights an
inherent trade-off between pose accuracy (favored by viewpoint diversity) and camera estimation (favored by simpler, consistent configurations), which our framework
navigates effectively without calibration-dependent components.

4.7 Ablation Studies
We conduct ablation experiments on CMU Panoptic (4-view evaluation) to systematically verify each component. Table 6 reports the results, with each row removing a
single component while keeping others enabled.
Coordinate Alignment. Removing coordinate alignment (i.e., disabling
rebase to first camera ) causes the most severe degradation: MPJPE surges from
11.8mm to 69.6mm (+57.8mm) and PA-MPJPE from 10.2mm to 28.3mm. Camera
translation error rises sharply from 29.0mm to 144.0mm and rotation error from 1.2°
to 5.1° (AUC@30 87.1%). Without a unified coordinate frame, each camera operates
in its own local reference system, producing conflicting 3D position signals that the
model cannot reconcile. This confirms that baselining all cameras to a single consistent
coordinate system is a prerequisite for multi-view pose estimation.
Alternating Attention. Replacing frame attention with global attention
throughout all layers causes catastrophic failure across all metrics: MPJPE surges
to 25.5mm (+13.7mm), rotation error to 40.7°, and translation error to 541.6mm
(AUC@30 6.2%). Pure global attention averages view-specific details, causing the
model to lose fine-grained spatial cues critical for precise joint localization. The
22
absence of per-view processing prevents establishing reliable view-to-view correspondences, leading to near-complete camera estimation failure. This confirms that the
interleaved frame-global attention pattern is the most critical architectural design.
This result directly supports the analysis in Sec. 3.7: without frame attention to
preserve per-view spatial cues, the model loses the ability to establish reliable crossview correspondences, which is the prerequisite for generalization to arbitrary view
configurations.
View-Aware Encoding. Removing joint identity and view-specific spatial
embeddings raises MPJPE to 18.9mm (+7.1mm) with rotation error rising to 6.2°
and translation error to 97.1mm (AUC@30 75.7%). Without explicit identity encoding, the model confuses joints with similar local appearance (e.g., left vs. right limbs)
across views. The absence of view-specific spatial information additionally hinders the
model from associating 2D observations with their source cameras, which is essential for recovering viewpoint geometry. The combined degradation demonstrates that
view-aware encoding injects essential structural and geometric priors.
Scale Normalization. Removing scale normalization increases MPJPE to
18.5mm (+6.7mm), translation error to 102.6mm, and rotation error to 1.9° (AUC@30
90.7%). Absolute coordinate magnitudes vary across sequences depending on subject
size and camera placement, and without normalization the model must simultaneously handle scale variations and pose variations, which harms convergence stability
and keypoint localization accuracy.
Iterative Refinement. Disabling iterative refinement in both the 3D pose and
camera prediction branches increases MPJPE to 14.4mm (+2.6mm) and translation
error to 31.2mm (+2.2mm), with rotation error unchanged at 1.2° (AUC@30 94.3%).
The adaLN-modulated updates incrementally correct residual errors at each iteration
by conditioning features on the previous prediction, yielding clear improvements in
pose accuracy and a modest benefit for camera translation estimation.

error gradient reflects error accumulation along the kinematic chain.

wrists: +7.3–7.4 mm), where single-view ambiguity is most severe due to self-occlusion
and limb symmetry. Proximal joints benefit the least (hips: +3.8–4.0 mm), as their 3D

4.9 Generalization to Real-World Scenes
To further evaluate the practical applicability of our method beyond controlled laboratory settings, we conduct qualitative experiments on real-world images captured by
handheld mobile phones in unconstrained environments. Specifically, we collect multiview images of two distinct actions in everyday scenes with varying backgrounds,
lighting conditions, and camera placements. We apply an off-the-shelf 2D keypoint
detector to obtain the input keypoints, which are then fed into our pre-trained model
for 3D pose and camera estimation without any fine-tuning or adaptation.
As shown in Figure 7, our method successfully reconstructs plausible 3D human
poses and estimates the relative camera positions despite the significant domain gap
between the training datasets (indoor motion capture) and the testing conditions (outdoor and indoor real-world scenes with handheld captures). These results demonstrate
the strong generalization capability of our approach: the model learns fundamental geometric priors of multi-view human articulation that transfer effectively across
different environments and camera configurations.

4.10 Generalization Analysis
Taken together, the robustness experiment across view counts (Sec. 4.6) and the
real-world evaluation (Sec. 4.9) reveal a consistent pattern: PACT’s performance
degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically under distribution shift, and both
generalization behaviors can be traced back to the architectural design analyzed in
Sec. 3.7.
View-Count Generalization. When the number of views is reduced from 24
to 2, MPJPE increases from 5.8 mm to 18.6 mm—a factor of 3.2, yet still far better than the single-view baseline of 46.3 mm. Camera AUC@30 drops only marginally
from 96.4% to 93.0%. This smooth rather than catastrophic degradation pattern indicates that the alternating attention mechanism effectively extracts basic binocular
geometric constraints from as few as two views, and progressively refines them as more
views become available. The accuracy gain diminishes beyond 8–12 views, suggesting
a practical sweet spot between precision and computational efficiency.
Scene-Domain Generalization. When deploying the model on real-world scenes
captured by handheld phones—covering both indoor and outdoor environments, with
two different actions captured at varying camera distances—the predicted 3D poses
and camera layouts remain plausible despite the significant domain gap from the
indoor motion capture training data. This transferability stems from two factors identified in Sec. 3.7: (1) geometric normalization projects all scenes into a consistent
coordinate frame, so that normalized inputs from real-world captures fall within the
same numerical distribution as the training data; (2) the combination of alternating
attention and shared joint identity embeddings forces the model to attend to cross-view
structural relationships rather than dataset-specific surface patterns.
25
Connection to the Generalization Mechanism Analysis. It is worth noting that PACT operates on only 17 sparse 2D keypoints—a far lower information
density than the dense image patches used in general scene reconstruction. That the
model not only jointly predicts 3D pose and camera parameters but also generalizes
across domains under such constrained input suggests that the three design pillars
identified in Sec. 3.7—alternating attention, geometric normalization, and shared
joint identity embeddings—collectively constitute a strong geometric prior. Under
extreme information constraints, the model is forced to derive 3D reasoning from crossview 2D correspondences rather than relying on texture or appearance cues. These
results position generalization—alongside joint pose-camera prediction and competitive accuracy—as a core strength of PACT, distinguishing it from prior uncalibrated
methods that either assume fixed view counts or have not been evaluated under
cross-domain transfer.
5 Conclusion
We presented PACT, an end-to-end framework for uncalibrated multi-view 3D human
pose and camera estimation. PACT consists of three core modules—view-aware feature
encoding, alternating attention fusion, and multi-task prediction heads—and jointly
predicts 3D human pose and camera extrinsics from only multi-view 2D keypoints,
without any pre-calibration. Experiments on Human3.6M and CMU Panoptic demonstrate that PACT achieves competitive uncalibrated pose accuracy while accurately
estimating camera extrinsics, generalizes robustly across 2–24 views, and adapts to
real-world handheld capture scenarios.
Limitations include reliance on 2D keypoint detections (whose errors propagate
to 3D predictions) and degraded performance under heavy occlusion (e.g., the Photo
action). Future work may explore end-to-end pipelines with learned image features,
extension to multi-person scenes, and temporal integration for video-level estimation.
