#### Abstract

3D Gaussian Splatting has recently emerged as a novel 3D representation method, gaining recognition for its exceptional rendering speed and visual fidelity. However, these advancements come at a cost: this technique exhibits substantial memory demands, with optimized models often containing millions of Gaussian primitives that can exceed 1 GB of storage. We attribute this inefficiency to inherent redundancy in primitive allocation, where overlapping or non-essential elements remain unoptimized in current implementations. In this paper, we propose a novel memory-efficient Gaussian compression method named MiniGS, featuring Full Factors Weighted (FFW) Importance Score Pruning and Variational Densify Threshold (VDT) components. On one hand, we construct several importance score to selectively combine pixel coordinates, Gaussian distances and opacity on the set of Gaussian primitives, and utilize them to prune out redundancy while preserving a small number of highly contributive primitives, achieving an impressive 90% compression ratio. On the other hand, to compensate for the quality loss of pruning Gaussians, we utilize the plug-and-play Variational Densify Threshold (VDT) Component to recover fine details of the 3D scenes, effectively compensating for quality losses with primitives at a

low scale. We demonstrate the performance of MiniGS with extensive experimental results on classic dataset and our self-constructed synthetic dataset. For example, our proposed method MiniGS can achieve 29.63 PSNR with 303.271 thousand Gaussian primitives, while the vanilla Gaussian splatting algorithm achieves same-level PSNR(29.73) with one order of magnitude higher of Gaussian primitives on the DeepBlending dataset.

Keywords: Gaussian Splatting, Pointcloud, Pruning

## 1 Introduction

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) Mildenhall et al. \(2021\),Barron et al. \(2021\),Barron et al. \(2022\),Barron et al. \(2023\) have transformed photorealistic 3D reconstruction, opening new possibilities for boosting visual fidelity across applications like human avatar reconstruction Hong et al. \(2022\); Gafni et al. \(2021\); Zheng et al. \(2022a,b\); Yang et al. \(2024a\), SLAM Rosinol et al. \(2023\); Zhang et al. \(2023\), and extended reality (XR) Yang and Zhang \(2022\); Yang et al. \(2023\). NeRF's power comes from its capacity to encode complex 3D scenes—including view-dependent effects—within a network of multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and to generate new views via alpha-compositing using a sophisticated volumetric rendering strategy. However, its dependence on deep MLP architectures and the high computational cost of volumetric rendering impose significant overheads, thereby limiting real-time usability. This challenge has spurred research into alternative data structures Chen et al. \(2022\); Fridovich-Keil et al. \(2022\); M¨uller et al. \(2022\) aimed at improving rendering efficiency.

In contrast, the recent development of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) Kerbl et al. \(2023\) has made significant strides in achieving high-quality, real-time 3D scene reconstruction. Departing from traditional NeRF techniques—which use implicit scene representations via MLPs and specialized data structures Mildenhall et al. \(2021\); M¨uller et al. \(2022\); Yu et al. \(2021\)—3DGS models scenes explicitly with parameterized 3D Gaussian primitives. These primitives are quickly projected and rasterized onto the image plane through a process known as Splatting. By leveraging a tile-based, differentiable renderer with alpha-blending, 3DGS not only delivers photorealistic results but also efficiently optimizes the Gaussian primitives. This approach marks a notable advance in balancing rendering quality with computational speed, and it has been successfully applied to domains such as SLAM Keetha et al. \(2024\), XR Dongye et al. \(2024a\), and human modeling Dongye et al. \(2024b\).

Yet, despite its efficiency gains, 3DGS encounters issues related to primitive overgrowth. During the densification stage—when primitives are cloned and subdivided to address reconstruction inaccuracies—their numbers can surge, leading to increased memory usage and slower rendering, especially on lower-end devices. As Fan et al. Fan et al. \(2023\) have pointed out, many of these additional primitives contribute little to the final rendered output and can be pruned without compromising visual quality.

To address this, several recent studies Fang and Wang \(2024\); Fan et al. \(2023\); Niemeyer et al. \(2024\); Navaneet et al. \(2023\); Lee et al. \(2024\); Liu et al. \(2024\); Yang et al. \(2024b\); Niedermayr et al. \(2023\) have proposed various Gaussian primitive pruning techniques that assess the significance of each primitive and remove those deemed unimportant. However, these strategies have not been exhaustively validated, and improper tuning of pruning parameters can result in substantial quality loss. This underlines the need for a more robust, thoroughly investigated approach to balance efficiency with rendering quality.

In this paper, we present MiniGS, a novel method that inherently enforces a natural cap on pruning parameters to prevent severe scene degradation. Our approach demonstrates superior performance under extreme pruning conditions compared to existing methods. Specifically, our experiments show that the Importance Score combining all factors as view-independent color, opacity and pixel coordinates with Variational Densify Threshold is highly effective for discerning and preserving critical primitives, thereby recovering fine-grained details and significantly enhancing the final rendering quality in post-pruning reconstruction.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

- We introduce a novel and efficient 3D Gaussian primitives pruning method MiniGS. In this method we procedurally construct the full factors importance score with pixel coordinates, Gaussian position distances, opacity and transparency to effectively prune out redundancy while preserving a small number of highly contributive primitives.
- We present Variational Densify Threshold (VDT) for postprocessing quality compensation, a gradient optimization mechanism applied during the backward process. VDT enhances the preservation of fine-grained details and significantly improves rendering quality in pruned Gaussian representation.
- We specifically constructed a novel synthetic dataset to rigorously evaluate the performance of our proposed method, MiniGS, across various compression ratio configurations. The experimental results conclusively proved its stability and efficiency.
- We perform comprehensive qualitative evaluations and quantitative analyses to benchmark MiniGS (MG) against state-of-the-art methods. Extensive experimental results highlight the superiority of our approach, particularly under extreme pruning conditions.

## 2 Related Works

## 2.1 Efficient Neural Radiance Fields

Over the past several decades, a diverse array of methods for 3D scene reconstruction and representation has captured the attention of both the computer vision and graphics communities. Traditional techniques—such as structure-from-motion (SfM) Snavely et al. \(2006\) and multi-view stereo (MVS) Goesele et al. \(2007\)—have long been used to estimate camera parameters from image collections and to generate dense 3D point clouds of object shapes. The integrated framework of SfM and MVS, exemplified by COLMAP Schonberger and Frahm \(2016\), has become the de facto standard for producing virtual 3D scenes.

At the same time, the advent of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) Mildenhall et al. \(2021\) has pushed rendering quality toward photorealism. NeRF encodes scene colors and geometry via features learned by multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and synthesizes new views through volumetric rendering. This breakthrough has led to applications spanning SLAM, generative modeling, and human modeling.

Despite these advances, NeRF faces challenges such as reconstructing unbounded scenes Zhang et al. \(2020\), managing noisy or inconsistent data Martin-Brualla et al. \(2021\), and handling temporal variations Gao et al. \(2021\); Li et al. \(2021\). Of these, improving rendering efficiency and memory usage has emerged as a critical concern. Notably, Sun et al. Sun et al. \(2022\) identified MLPs as a major bottleneck and introduced a hybrid approach that combines feature voxel grids with lightweight MLPs to mitigate this issue.

Additional grid-based methods have pursued reduced memory demands by employing more efficient data structures—such as tensor decomposition Chen et al. \(2022\), hash tables M¨uller et al. \(2022\), and octrees Yu et al. \(2021\). While these approaches have achieved faster scene convergence and enhanced image quality compared to conventional MLP-based NeRFs, they still face limitations. In particular, the choice of grid data structure can constrain image quality, and the intrinsic multi-sampling nature of volumetric rendering can slow down forward-pass performance, preventing these methods from serving as perfect solutions.

## 2.2 Efficient 3D Gaussian Splats

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) Kerbl et al. \(2023\) has set a new standard by striking an impressive balance between rendering quality and speed. Its success is primarily due to an MLP-free 3D representation that leverages parameterized 3D Gaussian primitives for real-time, high-fidelity rendering. Additionally, its explicit scene representation dovetails seamlessly with conventional graphics pipelines, taking full advantage of GPU parallelism for efficient processing.

However, the densification algorithm in 3DGS Kerbl et al. \(2023\) presents its own challenges. By populating new Gaussian primitives in under-reconstructed regions, the algorithm can generate millions of primitives, significantly increasing memory usage. To combat this, the original work Kerbl et al. \(2023\) proposed periodically discarding primitives with opacities below a preset threshold. Yet, research such as LightGaussian Fan et al. \(2023\) has shown that this approach is insufficient, introducing a Gaussian importance evaluation metric that indicates up to an additional 60% of primitives can be pruned without degrading rendering quality.

Similarly, various studies Liu et al. \(2024\); Fan et al. \(2023\); Niemeyer et al. \(2024\); Fang and Wang \(2024\); Lee et al. \(2024\); Yang et al. \(2024b\) have suggested different pruning strategies based on distinct importance metrics to remove less critical primitives. However, the fundamental principles and key design factors for such metrics remain underexplored, underscoring the need for further research to optimize 3DGS for memory efficiency while maintaining its outstanding rendering capabilities.

#### 3 Method

#### 3.1 Background

3D Gaussian Splatting 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a point-based method for representing 3D scenes. It starts by initializing from sparse point clouds generated using Structure-from-Motion (SfM) Snavely et al. \(2006\). Each 3D Gaussian, denoted as g, is characterized by several parameters: its mean (position), covariance (shape and scale), opacity, and view-dependent color, which is typically represented using spherical harmonics (SH). A scene in 3DGS is modeled as a collection of these Gaussians. Mathematically, each Gaussian is defined by a covariance matrix Σ, centered at a point µ (the mean):

$$C(x) = e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^T \Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}, \quad (1)$$

Here, x represents an arbitrary position within the scene. To ensure stability during the optimization process and to guarantee that the covariance matrix Σ remains positive semi-definite, it is decomposed into two components: rotation and scaling.

$$\Sigma' = RS\Sigma S^T R^T, \quad (2)$$

During rendering, the 3D Gaussians are projected onto the 2D image plane and blended together using alpha blending. The projection of the covariance matrix from 3D space to the 2D image plane is achieved using the projective transform matrix W (the world-to-camera matrix) and the Jacobian J (which approximates the projective transform). This process is mathematically described as:

$$\Sigma' = JW\Sigma^T J^T, \quad (3)$$

The color C of a pixel p is calculated by blending the contributions of all 3D Gaussians that overlap the pixel. These Gaussians are sorted based on their depth before blending.


$$C = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} c_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad (4)$$

Here, Np represents the ordered set of Gaussians that overlap the pixel p, and c denotes the view-dependent color, which is computed from the spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients.

In the original 3DGS, contribution is assessed using Gaussian opacity as Eq. 5, with low-opacity Gaussians being discarded. However, this opacity-based method can inadvertently remove important Gaussians while keeping high-opacity outliers. The original importance score in vanilla 3DGS is as Eq. 5,



| $Score_{vanilla}(G_i, r) = \alpha_i * T_i$ | (5) |

#### 3.4 Variational Densify Threshold

To better restore lost details in rendered images from heavily pruned Gaussian splats, we incorporate the plug-and-play homodirectional gradient accumulation feature from AbsGS Ye et al. \(2024\) and Gaussian Opacity Fields Yu et al. \(2024\). Through indepth analysis, we refine this approach by introducing a dynamic threshold version, further enhancing its effectiveness. The optimization process begins with a sparse point cloud, necessitating an increase in the number of 3D Gaussians to achieve a more accurate reconstruction of the scene. We adopt the densification strategy outlined in 3DGS Kerbl et al. \(2023\). Specifically, the densification of a Gaussian—whether through cloning or splitting—is guided by the magnitude of the view-space position gradient dL dx , where x represents the center of the projected Gaussian. Mathematically, dL dx aggregates contributions over all pixels pi that the Gaussian influences.

$$\frac{dL}{dx} = \sum_i \frac{dL}{dp_i} \frac{dp_i}{dx}. \quad (10)$$

If the gradient norm dL dx 2 exceeds a predefined threshold τx, the corresponding Gaussian is selected as a candidate for densification. However, we observed that this criterion is ineffective in detecting overly blurred areas, as it fails to differentiate between well-reconstructed regions and areas where gradient signals from different pixels cancel out, resulting in low overall gradient magnitudes. To address this, we propose a straightforward modification to the metric: instead of using a single gradient norm, we accumulate the norms of individual pixel gradients as Sgrad in Eq 11.


$$S_{grad} = \sum_i \left\| \frac{dL}{dp_i} \frac{dp_i}{dx} \right\| \quad (11)$$

i In Eq 12, accumulated gradients Sgrad are compared with the gradient threshold τ .


$$Operation = \begin{cases} \text{continue}, & S_{grad} \leq \lambda_\alpha \\ \text{densify}, & S_{grad} > \lambda_\alpha. \end{cases} \quad (12)$$

In Eq. 12, λα is the gradient threshold for triggering densification. In the vanilla 3DGS Kerbl et al. \(2023\), this value is fixed at 0.0002. Inspired by optimal threshoulding method in traditional pruning method Ye et al. \(2020\), we hypothesize that the size of this gradient norm threshold impacts the final image quality, measured by metrics, such as PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. To verify this hypothesis, we designed and conducted a series of experiments. Several scenes, including flowers, garden, treehill, counter, and drjohnson, are selected for quantitative evaluation. The results of our experiments are illustrated in Fig. 3.4. The vertical axis is PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS in subplot a,b,c, respectively. The horizontal axis stands for different densify grad threshold value. The minimum is 0.0002 and the maxmium is 0.0006, at intervals of 0.00005. From our observation, it is evident that an extreme value exists around 0.00045 in horizontal axis, which we have highlighted with a red circle. Based on this finding, we adopted this value for all subsequent benchmarks in our proposed method, MiniGS. The per scene result are presented in Table 5, 4.6, 7, respectively corresponding to SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS among various λα, and data analysis in Section 4.6.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Implementation Details

Source Code Leveraging the open-source code of 3DGS Kerbl et al. \(2023\), we established our experimental platform with ease. We implemented the primitive pruning algorithm on top of vanilla 3DGS's Python framework and extended the CUDA-based differentiable renderer to incorporate various score functions. To understand the implications of different pruning techniques, we integrated pruning implementations (i.e., pruning settings and score functions) from prior works. The pruning techniques evaluated include LG, MS, RS, and EG, derived respectively from LightGaussian Fan et al. \(2023\), Mini-Splatting Fang and Wang \(2024\), RadSplat Niemeyer et al. \(2024\), EfficientGS Liu et al. \(2024\) and our proposed method. As of this paper's submission, only MiniGS, LightGaussian and Mini-Splatting have open-sourced their code. Therefore, we manually implemented the pruning techniques for RadSplat and EfficientGS based on the descriptions provided in their respective papers.

Training Each technique was trained for 30,000 iterations on 8-card Nvidia H100 server. To ensure a fair comparison, pruning was applied once at the same time point, which is 15,000 iterations during training. For other hyperparameters, such as learning rate scheduling, spherical harmonics (SH) degree, and the opacity reset point, densification operation, we adhered to the vanilla 3DGS configuration to maintain consistency across all pruning techniques and datasets. For other comparison works, some amount controlling parameters were added to balance the final primitive amount into similar scale as close as possible.

Rendering The inference and rendering of all methods for benchmark was executed on one single NVIDIA H100 GPU with 80 GB HBM respectively. It's ensured no any additional CPU/GPU resources occupied meanwhile.

#### 4.2 Datasets

To comprehensively assess the effectiveness of pruning techniques and importance score functions, we trained each method on a diverse set of datasets, including Mip-NeRF360 Barron et al. \(2022\), Tanks&Temples Knapitsch et al. \(2017\), and Deep Blending Hedman et al. \(2018\). For dataset usage, we adhered to standard practices. For the unbounded, real-world scenes in the MipNeRF360 dataset (comprising four indoor and five outdoor scenes), Tanks&Temples (two outdoor scenes), and Deep Blending (two indoor scenes), we used every 8th image for testing and the remaining images for training, all at their original resolutions. Additionally, we constructed a Synthetic dataset named gym scene, it's a basketball gym scene, with rich textures on the walls and floor. The dataset contains multiple different views from 101 cameras.

#### 4.4 Quantitative Comparison of Compression Methods

We conducted systematic quantitative evaluations to compare our proposed MG method with existing state-of-the-art approaches, including vanilla 3DGS Kerbl et al. \(2023\), LG Fan et al. \(2023\), EG Liu et al. \(2024\), and MS Fang and Wang \(2024\). All methods were configured to represent scenes using a range of [200, 500] thousand primitives, representing a pruning of approximately 90% compared to vanilla 3DGS. Note that RS Niemeyer et al. \(2024\) was excluded from the comparison due to its lack of a controllable mechanism to achieve a specified number of primitives.

Evaluation Metrics Rendering quality was assessed using PSNR, SSIM Wang et al. \(2003\), and LPIPS Zhang et al. \(2018\) metrics. Additionally, we measured the number of primitives (in thousands) to ensure a fair comparison across methods with equivalent levels of information density.

As shown in Table 3, the experimental results demonstrate that our proposed MG method consistently outperforms other state-of-the-art pruning techniques across various scenarios. Specifically, MG achieves the best performance in 78% of cases and secures second place in 22% of instances across all datasets. A particularly notable result is observed on the DeepBlending dataset Hedman et al. \(2018\), where MG achieves a PSNR score of 29.63, significantly surpassing the results of the other three methods, which range between 24.05 and 25.28. This substantial performance gap can be attributed to the complex textural characteristics of the drjohnson scene. The MG method excels in handling such complexity, accurately reconstructing intricate details with exceptional fidelity.

Furthermore, the results on the Tanks&Temples dataset Knapitsch et al. \(2017\) exceed expectations. MG achieves a PSNR score of 24.16, outperforming vanilla 3DGS (23.77) while using only 14.1% of the primitives. Similarly, MG achieves results that are very close to vanilla 3DGS across all three evaluation metrics and significantly higher than other state-of-the-art pruning methods.

Extensive experiments confirm the superiority of our proposed MG method over current state-of-the-art approaches, as demonstrated by quantitative benchmarks. This advantage is both stable and generalizable across diverse datasets and scenarios.

Effect of Components Table 4 presents an ablation study evaluating the effectiveness of various components of the proposed method MiniGS on the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset. The first row of modification, '+FFW' (Full Factors Weighted Importance Score Pruning), reduces the number of primitives dramatically by 86%(down to 403.5k) but

slightly lowers SSIM and PSNR, and increases LPIPS. The addition of homodirectional gradient accumulation from AbsGS (+HG(AbsGS)) improves PSNR by 0.06 and marginally improves LPIPS while maintaining a compact representation (409.2k primitives). Adding Variational Densify Threshold ('+VDT') shows a further improvement in SSIM and LPIPS with 408.7k primitives. The final version, labeled Ours(MG), integrates all components and achieves a good balance: SSIM of 0.800, PSNR of 27.17, and LPIPS of 0.234 with 408.7k primitives. These results indicate that the proposed modifications significantly reduce the Gaussian primitive redundancy to improving efficiency - while preserving, and in some aspects improving, quality compared to the original 3DGS baseline.

## 5 Limitation

The densification and pruning processes in 3D Gaussian Splatting constitute a complex systematic engineering task, with multiple factors influencing the accuracy of the final results. This paper aims to analyze the impact of each element and identify the optimal solution. However, automating the selection of densification and pruning is also essential to enhance efficiency and consistency. By systematically evaluating different parameters and strategies, we can better understand how each factor affects the final rendering quality. Automated methods not only reduce human intervention but also enable more precise control in complex scenarios. Future research could explore intelligent algorithms and machine learning techniques to further optimize this process, leading to more efficient and accurate 3D reconstruction and rendering. This advancement will contribute significantly to the fields of computer graphics and vision.

## 6 Conclusion

This paper proposes MiniGS, a memory-efficient Gaussian compression method featuring Full Factors Weighted (FFW) Importance Score Pruning and Variational Densify Threshold (VDT). FFW selectively combines pixel coordinates, Gaussian distances, and opacity to prune redundancy, achieving a 90% compression ratio while preserving critical primitives. Comprehensive experiments were conducted to determine the optimal importance score option. VDT compensates for quality loss by recovering fine details with low-scale primitives. We analysis the distribution of the different backward gradient threshold and give the best value. For the overall method. Finally, Systematic experiments on classic and synthetic datasets show MiniGS achieves the best result in all evaluation metrics comparing with existing state-of-the-arts. In future research, we will further explore the key factors influencing pruning efficiency and refine the Gaussian pruning methodology for enhanced performance.
