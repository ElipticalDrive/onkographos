Abstract
2 4D RENDERING TECHNIQUES
A novel approach for rendering time-varying data based on the
Shear-Warp factorisation is presented. Reduction in storage space
is achieved by detecting the changed areas within each volume and
compressing them. Time-coherence is exploited by detecting and
rendering the changes in every volume while spatial-coherence is
exploited by utilising a data structure that allows easy volume update and stores information about the empty space within each volume.
The most prominent characteristic of time-varying volume data is
that there is a substantial coherence in time. A great percentage of
voxel values do not change, or change very slowly over time. This
fact can be exploited in time rendering. Dobashi [2] proposed a rendering method for time-varying data by using orthonormal wavelets
to encode time coherency. Ma [15] described a method for 4D rendering that uses quantisation of scalar values to reduce the space
required to store the volume, octrees to encode spatial coherence
and differencing to exploit time coherence. Shen [21] used differencing in order to detect changes between successive volumes.
He used ray-casting to fully render the first volume in the series
and subsequently updated the final image by casting rays only from
pixels that correspond to changed areas in the volume. Silver [22]
identifies features of interest and tracks them in time.
All the above approaches utilise ray-casting for rendering. Our
novel approach is based on the Shear-Warp factorisation [11] [9] of
the viewing transformation. The Shear-Warp based rendering is an
object order method that eliminates the arbitrary mapping between
object and image space by making all rays intersecting the volume
parallel to a coordinate axis. This can be achieved by shearing the
viewing transformation. Each slice of the volume is translated by
a shearing coefficient, resampled and composed to an intermediate
image. Once this is done for all the slices in the volume, a 2D
warping of the intermediate image must take place to give the final
image.
The Shear-Warp approach is simple, fast, easy to implement and
produces images of quality comparable to ray-casting. Furthermore
the data are stored in physical memory order thus making data access efficient due to memory and cache coherency. On the other
hand, the use of a 2D resampling filter might have an impact on image quality, and the two resampling stages (one for the compositing
and one for the warping) might introduce aliasing effects to the final
image, although [11] demonstrates that such filter differences have
little impact on image quality.

1 INTRODUCTION
The increased capabilities of modern imaging instruments have
made it possible to sample 3D information over time. Samples
can range from microscopic bacteria to human parts to gas or
liquid flows. Four-dimensional imaging is a term used to describe the study of three-dimensional samples as they change over
time. For example, state of the art, digital microscopy can produce 10 1024x1024x256 volumes per second, which amounts to
2.56 GBytes per second at 8 bits per voxel. This vast amount of
data can provide scientists with a new view of how things develop
and change over time, and this can be achieved only by using interactive and efficient imaging techniques. The task of rendering 4D
samples is very time consuming by its nature, because multiple volumes must be acquired, processed and rendered at interactive rates.
There are two strands in 4D visualisation: one deals with rendering of stored, preprosessed data and the other with rendering of live
data as they originate directly from the sampling equipment. In this
paper we consider rendering of stored, preprocessed data.
3 RENDERING 4D DATA
Storing of the volume data is a necessity fir later analysis and viewing. The amount of data that has to be stored is vast, so compression
techniques assume a special importance. Various change detection
techniques can be used during preprocessing of the volume data
(prior to storing) to eliminate the temporal coherence and transform the data into a form that allows encoding of change, efficient
storage and fast rendering.

For lossy compression, Ning [18] used vector quantisation to
compress the volume, Muraki [17] and Gross [6] discussed the use
of wavelet transforms to obtain a hierarchical, multiresolution, description of the volume suitable for progressive coding, transmission and reconstruction of volumes, while Yeo [23] compressed
volume data by using a Discrete Cosine Transform. Various methods for Fourier domain compression and rendering are discussed
in [13] [16] [3]. On the other hand, Fowler [5] utilised a combination of differential pulse-code modulation and Huffman coding to achieve lossless compression as well as Lacroute [11] and
Rhodes [20] which used run-length encoding to eliminate blank
space in the volume.
Lossy algorithms, although they perform better than lossless
ones in terms of compression efficiency also degrade the volume
data, a fact that may not always be acceptable in biomedical applications. The run-length encoding approach performs well in this
case because it only stores the non-transparent voxels of the volume, along with some information needed for the reconstruction.
Furthermore the run-length encoding is best suited to the scanlineoriented processing performed by the Shear Warp algorithm. We
utilise this method to compress and then store the changed areas to
disk, dramatically decreasing the required storage space.
Although the run-length encoding data structure performs well
in the case of rendering a single volume, its use becomes inefficient
when it comes to rendering time-varying volumes. Due to its nature (a 1-D array) it is difficult to insert new data or update, because
this would amount to moving data around which is an expensive
operation. Other data structures could be used that support updating, such as a linked list. The linked list has drawbacks though:
first there is substantial memory overhead to store the pointers, and
second in general the volume data will not be stored in successive
memory locations, breaking the memory and cache coherence.
During rendering, the changed areas must be loaded from the
hard disk and updated in the memory buffer that contains the volume. The way we accomplish this is by using a data structure that
is easy to update and maintains memory coherence, without losing the essential property of the run-length encoding, blank space
elimination, that enables us to efficiently leap over blank space, accelerating the rendering phase significantly.

To estimate the noise variance over the entire volume, we can

be the average of only a fraction of them (the first N). The largest

can plot the histogram of number of blocks over block variance
and since we assume that the largest part of the volume remains

quite accurately estimate the noise variance of the whole volume.

The described rendering algorithm requires the accurate detection
of the changed areas in the volume in order to produce the correct
result. Change detection takes place in the preprocessing stage. In
this stage apart from detecting the changed areas, we run-length
encode them and save them to disk as well.
There are a variety of change detection algorithms in the literature, such as Simple Differencing [8], the Likelihood ratio
method [7], Adaptive Threshold method [1] and others [4]. Of all
the change detection techniques, differencing is the simplest and
fastest but is also the most prone to error due to noise. Both the
Likelihood ratio and the Adaptive Threshold methods require an accurate definition of a threshold that will determine whether change
has taken place or not. It is generally difficult to estimate a global
threshold that will work with all datasets. Also many change detection techniques take into consideration various phenomena that
happen in the real world such as shadows, occlusion or changes in
illumination which do not take place in volume sequences and thus
are not always suitable for change detection in such areas.

rendering but will not have an impact in the final image quality. In
the examined cases the 2 criterion performed adequately.
In figure 17 we present the number of changed blocks detected
by the 2 criterion with the median estimation of noise variance
and, for comparison, the real number of changed blocks. The noise
variance was set to 6,12 and 20 and the confidence level to 99.5 .
The criterion generally overestimates the number of changed
blocks due to the noise present in the volume. In the one case where
the estimated number of changed blocks is less than the real number, due to not being able to tell apart noise from small changes,
some loss of detail will appear in the final rendered image. When
the estimated number is greater than the real number, the rendering
speed will decrease by a small amount but the final image quality
will not be affected.
The first volume in the sequence was compressed as a whole and
occupied about 3Mbytes of disk space. The original uncompressed
volume would occupy approximately 17Mbytes so a compression
ratio of 5.6:1 was achieved for the given dataset. In the subsequent
volumes only the changed area was compressed, occupying less that
0.5Mbyte per volume achieving a 34:1 compression ratio. We noted
that the difference in size for noise variance 6 and 20 is not significant. Also we have to stress that the compression performance of
the algorithm is completely data and opacity transfer function dependent.

[11] P. Lacroute and M. Levoy. Fast Volume Rendering Using a
Shear-Warp Factorisation of The Viewing Transformation. In
Computer Graphics Proceedings, Annual Conference Series
(SIGGRAPH ’94), 1994.
A novel approach for rendering time varying data based on the
Shear-Warp factorisation was presented. Reduction in storage
space was achieved by detecting the changed areas within each volume and compressing them. Time-coherence was exploited by using ”slabs” to render only the changed areas, and spatial-coherence
was exploited by using a data structure that allowed easy volume
updates but also maintained crucial space leaping information.
The presented method was developed and tested on synthetic
data. During the next few months we expect to apply and test this
method on real 4D volume data samples originating from optical
microscopes.
