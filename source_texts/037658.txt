Abstract
Achieving lifelike atmospheric effects, such as fog, is essential in creating immersive environments and poses a formidable challenge in real-time rendering. Highly realistic rendering of complex lighting interacting with dynamic fog can be very resource-intensive, due to light bouncing through a complex participating media multiple times. We propose an approach that uses a multi-layered spherical harmonics probe grid to share computations temporarily. In addition, this world-space storage enables the sharing of radiance data between multiple viewers. In the context of cloud rendering this means faster rendering and a significant enhancement in overall rendering quality with efficient resource utilization.
1. Introduction
Rendering atmospheric effects is essential to enhance realism and provide the scene with a sense of depth and scale, making it more immersive and visually engaging. These effects are especially challenging when dealing with participating media such as visible fog, clouds, smoke, or dust plumes, where the interaction of light with tiny particles or droplets must be simulated accurately. The complexity arises from the intricate behaviors of photons as they traverse these media, undergoing absorption, scattering, and potential emission at each step. Properly simulating these interactions, particularly in dynamic and heterogeneous media, leads to computational challenges that often prevent achieving real-time performance [KF12], even on state-of-the-art hardware. Consequently, real-time techniques often rely on significant simplifications, which reduce accuracy and may lead to noticeable artifacts, especially noticeable in heterogeneous media.
A common simplification in rendering participating media is to limit the simulation to single scattering events during the ray marching process. By excluding multiple scattering events, this approach can lead to a reduction in the overall brightness of the media. In this approach, only the probability of a photon being scattered once towards the camera from a light source is considered. To minimize additional computational overhead from secondary ray marching, absorption is only accounted for along the path between the camera and the scattering point, while the absorption between the light source and the scattering point is ignored. However, this approximation can lead to artificially increased brightness in the volume, as certain light interactions are not fully represented [Hil15].
In real-time applications such as games, a froxel-based approach has become the standard method. This technique uses a frustum-aligned voxel grid to store intermediate calculations, like incoming radiance, which can then be accumulated over multiple frames to reduce computational load. To exploit spatial coherence, the voxel grid is often sized to represent blocks of 8 × 8 screen pixels. However, this strategy introduces challenges: reprojection during camera movement can lead to diffusion artifacts, and the limited spatial resolution of the grid restricts the ability to accurately capture fine, high-resolution effects [Wro14; Hil15]. See Figure 1 for an overview of the various simplifications.

With the increasing power of graphics processors and the exploration of alternative structures for storing global illumination, we challenge the assumption that froxel-based representations are the optimal solution for representing participating media in realtime rendering applications such as games. Instead, we investigate the use of a world-space-aligned hierarchical grid of omnidirectional probes to store incoming radiance. This structure enables us to completely avoid reprojection artifacts while allowing for adjustable resolution based on both display detail and frequency content within the volume and illumination. Furthermore, we extend the ray marching process to account for volume self-shadowing of scattered rays and incorporate multiple scattering, which enhances realism. The world-space alignment of the grid also makes our approach well-suited for stereo rendering and cloud rendering scenarios involving multiple viewers within the same scene. Overall, we envision a rendering system for heterogeneous media that fulfills the following criteria:
High-quality. We aim to generate highly realistic renderings of participating media, with support for heterogeneity and anisotropy, that are indistinguishable from the ground truth.
Real-Time. We target real-time and stutter-free frame rates for interactive and dynamic applications.
Shareability. In order to reduce the total workload and increase the performance and resource efficiency, we want to share as many computations as possible when rendering multiple views.
Our system incorporates several key optimizations to achieve real-time rendering of participating media. We improve upon the basic single scattering model by incorporating ray marching to light sources and integrating transmittance, rather than relying solely on direct light sampling. To cache radiance, we employ an adaptive world-space hierarchical probe grid based on spherical harmonics. Storing data in world space, instead of screen space, avoids reprojection errors and enables efficient spatio-temporal reuse. This structure also facilitates sharing computations across multiple viewpoints, making it ideal for cloud-based rendering scenarios involving multiple users.
Our system dynamically adjusts both the resolution and update frequency of the probes. The initial resolution is determined by the required level of detail, which is influenced by factors such as the viewer's distance, camera parameters, and screen resolution. We reduce the resolution in regions with low radiance variability to improve runtime. Additionally, our framework is highly extensible and adaptive: it can enhance existing froxel-based methods by applying ray marching for transmittance to light sources, thus improving visual quality. Multiple scattering is another supported feature, which we will show with a prototype implementation with preliminary results.
In summary, we make the following contributions:
Real-time evaluation of transmittance from scattering points to light sources, significantly enhancing visual quality in heterogeneous media.
An adaptive, level-of-detail-based hierarchical radiance storage system that adjusts to both the distance to multiple viewer cameras and radiance variations, optimizing performance and resource usage.
Demonstration that world-space allocation, rather than screen-space, enables more efficient spatio-temporal reuse of data while avoiding reprojection errors.
A scalable rendering system for participating media that efficiently scales with multiple viewers by sharing computations and is well-suited for cloud rendering scenarios.
2. Related Work
Cerezo et al. [CPP*05] and Novak et al. [NGH*18] published excellent surveys of techniques to render participating media and Monte Carlo methods for physically-based volume rendering. We distinguish the most relevant ones and newer ones published after the survey based on (1) how they store data to speed up computations, (2) whether they are usable for real-time rendering, (3) if they utilize only single scattering techniques in contrast to multiple scattering ones, and (4) whether they work for homogeneous/heterogeneous and isotropic/anisotropic participating media.
Jensen and Christensen [JC98] extending the photon mapping technique to participating media by storing photon data when light interacts with the participating media. Many other deterministic approaches have been proposed as well, including discrete ordinate methods and methods based on spherical harmonics. Considering stochastic methods, Mueller et al. [MRNK21] presented a method for real-time neural radiance caching for global illumination and discussed its applicability to volumetric rendering with preliminary results. Mueller et al. [MESK22] also mention the applicability of their multigrid hash encoding to heterogeneous volumentric density fields and show preliminary results as well, by training NeRF-like radiance fields from the noisy output of a volumetric path tracer.
Spherical Harmonics allow for efficient storage of directional radiance information and have been used in rendering to speed up computations [RH01; SKS23] and were consequently used extensively in rendering participating media. In an effort to achieve higher quality, various techniques utilize multiple scattering [JDZJ08; JZJ08], emphasizing the importance of interpolation by storing a gradient. Later work improved on that by also combining it with a Hessian-based metric [MJJG18]. While these methods aim to improve the quality and speed, they far exceed real-time frame times.
In the area of real-time participating media rendering we have approaches that store data in either world-space lattices or screen-space aligned data structures. Kaplanyan and Dachsbacher [KD10] use a lattice with spherical harmonics to compute indirect illumination which can also be used for simple participating media effects. Their cascaded light volume propagation was improved later to even support multiple scattering, but restricted to homogeneous isotropic participating media [BSA12]. Radiance from point light sources is propagated in a full octree by Olovsson [OD13]. They propose an index volume to determine which level to use in which part of the scene. Elek et al. [ERDS14] proposed propagating radiance in multiple grids aligned with the principal light directions to calculate light transport in clouds. They represent the local anisotropy in each cell using a single scalar value, which parametrizes the Henyey-Greenstein distribution.
Most other real-time methods use computation and acceleration structures that are screen-space aligned. Wyman and Ramsey [WR08] proposed a technique for shadow volumes for homogeneous media storing visibility information for light sources and later improved it to (imperfect) voxelized shadow volumes [Wym11; WD13]. Hofmann et al. [HHCM21] proposed a neural temporal adaptive sampling technique for sparse participating media. However, this method does not yet support light interactions between volumes and triangle-based surfaces.
Most modern fog rendering approaches used in practice use some form of frustum-aligned voxels which are referred to as froxels based on the work of Hillaire [Hil15] and Wronski [Wro14]. Each froxel computes and stores incoming radiance information, which can be accumulated along a view ray to render the final image. For fog rendering in Red Dead Redemption 2, Bauer [Bau19] stores the single scattered light and transmittance in a clip-space volume and marches through the depth slices to render the results. In The Last of Us: Part Two, Kovalovs [Kov20] improves the approach with more fine-tuning and a reduction of artifacts introduced through the view space data structure. Based on the froxel rendering approach, Stojanovic et al. [SWT*23] show how to speed up the volume rendering by sharing the shadow computations between users, however, their method does not work for non-analytical light sources. Overall, froxel-based methods mostly only work for isotropic media and lack visual quality. Furthermore, they do not lend themselves to being shared by multiple viewers and re-use over multiple frames requires remapping data from previous frames, which accumulates errors over time.
In order to efficiently share computations between viewers, world-space data structures prove to be a much better fit. Previous work has shown how a hierarchical probe grid can be efficiently used for shared computations [WTS*23] and dynamically adapted as needed [CNS*11]. Based on their caching approach, Stadlbauer et al. [SWTS23] showed how to share direct illumination and Weinrauch et al. [WLT*23] proposed caching far away clouds with on-surface caches.
Our method uses a hierarchical probe grid similar to Weinrauch et al. [WTS*23], which adapts to the required level of detail based on the distance to the viewer(s). Our method additionally adjusts the probe density based on the inherent detail in the participating media and lighting effects. This allows us to achieve real-time performance, supporting both heterogeneous and anisotropic participating media, with enhanced single- and multiple scattering for multiple viewers.

However, the computational cost can be increasingly high for complex scenes as an inordinate amount of samples is required to converge to the solution. In our cases, multiple scattering does not add much to the final image quality, as seen in Figure 1. Multiple scattering is important when the scattering albedo is close to one, which indicates that most of the incident light is scattered rather than absorbed. This is commonly observed in environments like clouds and can in general be very important. However, for our test scenes, the scattering albedo is not close to one, meaning that scattering is less dominant compared to absorption. Thus, we will first focus on single scattering, however, considering Eq. (10), we will later discuss how our data structure can also be exploited for multiple scattering. To reduce the sample count per frame, our goal is to reuse as much data as possible to reduce overall costs as much as possible while maintaining the same perceived visual quality.
Optimal reuse should reuse not only data temporally but also between viewers. Clearly, the computed radiance is view-dependent, making it not easily shareable between multiple viewers. We could store the radiance scattered towards the viewer Ls(xt, ω) at a specific world-space position. Looking at the evaluation of Ls reveals a view-dependency caused by the phase function (Equation 3). When g approaches zero, the results become view-independent. Limiting our approach to only isotropic cases is not a trade-off we are willing to make. Therefore, we aim to cache Ls with a directional component to fulfill the goal of shareability.
4. Method
We propose caching the scattered radiance Ls using probes that store the single scattered radiance Lss through spherical harmonics (SH). SH are frequently used to store directional information and build the foundation of our approach to cache scattered radiance. The amount of detail stored by SH is defined by its order which defines its number of coefficients. We performed a number of experiments to determine the order of SH which satisfies our quality criteria. We found that, for most participating media, the radiance scattered inside can be captured well by SH of order two, which can be expressed by nine coefficients.
To ensure simple indexing and shareability, we place radiance probes in a world-space-aligned grid, as can be seen in Figure 3 (left). The grid is divided into bins, where each bin contains 4 × 4 × 4 probes. Packing 64 probes into a bin optimizes the memory layout and forms the basis for our dynamic memory management. This was most efficient in our tests, striking a good balance between memory requirements and data structure overhead. Additionally, a multiple of 32 fits the current GPU SIMD widths well. We use a hierarchical grid setup to produce multiple levels of detail (LOD), as can be seen in Figure 3 (right) with adaptive non-uniform bin resolutions. Coarser levels have a lower computational cost, while finer levels are able to capture more details.

In order to avoid reserving memory for every possible probe at every LOD, we opt for a sparse representation of the grid, requesting memory on demand. To locate bins efficiently, a mapping from the bin's position and LOD to its memory location is needed. For this purpose, we employ a hash table, similar to world-space caches used by Weinrauch et al. [WTS*23]. Including the LOD in the hash calculation allows us to implicitly build our hierarchical structure. Hashing, in combination with a free list, serves as a means to dynamically assign memory.
4.1. Pipeline
Our pipeline (Figure 4) is split into an update and a rendering part, each consisting of a separate multi-stage pipeline. The first multistage pipeline determines the set of visible bins for each viewer and issues allocation requests for them. Based on these requests, bins are allocated during the following allocation stage. If bins remain unused, they can be deallocated. To increase re-use, we delay deallocation by a few frames to profit from potential re-use of bins which are not visible only for short amounts of time or might switch back and forth between LODs. In the final part of the update stage, we update the radiance data of the active probes.

Once the update pipeline is finished, the rendering pipeline can start consuming this data by ray marching the participating media. It calculates the radiance reaching the viewer along view rays with the help of the previously calculated world-space radiance data.
4.2. Probe Placement
In order to achieve high visual quality and ensure efficient sharing and updating of probes, optimized probe placement and LOD selection are vital. Highly dynamic scenes require the adaption of the probe data structure in a fast and efficient manner. To this end, we first determine the set of visible probes at the beginning of an update cycle and mark new probes for allocation. Considering multi-viewer and stereo rendering scenarios, we iterate over all camera frusta, each of which is sectioned into LOD frusta using a quadratic function, based on the distance to the respective camera position, as shown in Figure 3 (right).
In the next step, we place probes at world-space-aligned grid positions inside the respective LOD frusta. Before placing a new probe, we first check on the GPU for each potential position if participating media is present and that no probes are already assigned to this region. To get smoother interpolations and better results while moving through the scene, we consider an enlarged view frustum and allocate probes that are likely needed soon. While our LOD strategy is a good starting point, it is often beneficial to adjust LOD selection based on the cached data.
We start adjusting the probe LODs when reliable radiance estimates become available. Typically, ten update iterations are sufficient when using four light update samples. Due to the enlarged frustum allocation, estimates usually become reliable before they are in view. In cases where the radiance inside a bin does not vary significantly, or more specifically the differences between individual SH coefficients in a bin are below a threshold (we use 20%), we reduce the LOD in this area, reducing the computations spent on homogeneous areas, implicitly considering both high frequencies due to the participating media and lighting effects, like shadow boundaries. An indication for increasing the resolution of a bin is given by the opposite condition: If the radiance estimates inside a bin vary strongly. However, we only increase resolution if any viewer has originally requested increased resolution to avoid adding detail that cannot be captured in any view. In other words: we may increase LOD back to the original levels if the effect frequency inside a bin is increased.
4.3. Probe Updating
For each active probe, we update the stored scattered radiance every tick. To calculate this outgoing data, we first approximate incoming radiance. We employ random light sampling to estimate incoming radiance data for single scattering, which is expressed as Ll in Equation 8. The number of samples required is determined by the number of lights as well as the surface area of emissive lights. In our system, four or eight samples were sufficient to achieve fast convergence. Ray tracing is used to determine whether the light sample is blocked by geometry or if it reaches the scattering point. In addition to geometric occluders the radiance can also be reduced by the participating media itself. To account for this reduction, we accumulate the transmittance by ray marching from the light source, starting at the border of the participating media, to the scattering point, as in Equation 5. These self-shadowing calculations for heterogeneous participating media improve quality considerably compared to other real-time techniques, while keeping costs per-viewer low due to shared computations.
After determining incoming radiance, we can update the SH coefficients. We use multiple pre-generated random sample sets on the sphere for outgoing directions. To this end, we employ a separate pass before the update pass to generate and preprocess samples for efficiency reasons. Each outgoing directional sample is used in combination with every incoming radiance sample to multiplex data as much as possible. Additionally, to avoid discretization artifacts, we apply a jitter to the probe locations.
We then evaluate Equation 8 with each of these combinations to calculate outgoing radiance, which is then encoded to SH coefficients. Storing outgoing radiance data during updates, along with phase function evaluation, reduces the computational load at a specific point in space to a single evaluation. This significantly decreases noise and the number of samples needed during evaluation. In contrast to froxel-based approaches, our probe locations remain static in world space. Thus, temporal accumulation is straightforward and we use simple exponential smoothing over time.
4.4. Per-Viewer Rendering
After the relevant probes have been updated, we use them during shading to render the desired volumetric effects via per-viewer ray marching. We bound ray marching based on the first geometry intersection in the scene, as well as the bounding volume of the participating media. To avoid banding, we apply a random blue noise offset to each ray starting location.
Choosing the right step size is important to achieve good quality and low computation costs. As our probe data structure is adapted to the level of detail of the radiance, the allocated LOD provides a very good approximation of step size needed in this specific area. While selecting the step size based on the probe spacing can be helpful, setting the step size equal to the probe spacing may lead to poor capture of radiance and density details. Using linear interpolation improves radiance capture and further reducing the step size by multiplying it by 0.6 helps to preserve finer details. This leads to larger step sizes than usual, yet still yields good results. Different choices for step sizes are shown in Figure 5. For each point, we evaluate Equation 7, where for Lss the cached single scattered radiance is used. We ignore points with zero density to avoid unnecessary memory accesses and computations. When the transmittance goes to zero during ray marching, we stop marching.

Assessing a single SH sample is efficient. However, during ray marching, we trilinearly interpolate across multiple probes for all SH coefficients—where the same probes and direction are used for interpolation in multiple successive samples. To increase the efficiency of ray marching, we separate SH evaluation from interpolation. We run a pre-evaluation step for each viewer to evaluate the SH coefficients for all probes that will be needed during rendering and store the results inside a temporary buffer. During rendering, we sample the temporary buffer instead of the probes, which saves around 60% in rendering costs. To further speed up rendering, we choose to render in half resolution and up-sample during compositing. A bilateral filter helps smooth the image while taking into account depth discontinuities. The final color of the image is calculated by rewriting Equation 6 as L = Tr (xs, x)L0,ω + Lvol, with Tr being the transmittance, L0 the reflected radiance of the geometry hit and Lvol the radiance computed by the ray marching.
5. Evaluation
We evaluate our approach with various test scenes containing dynamic participating media and multiple viewers, example views are shown in Figure 6: Our variant of the Intel Sponza scene features animated ground fog, two dynamic lights, and viewers walking along arbitrary paths. Explosion places viewers in a circular arrangement, all directed towards the center where a highly detailed rapidly changing explosion occurs. In Dungeon a prominent smoke pillar rising at the center of the scene. We also show two open environments. In Swamp multiple lamps are modeled as emissive surfaces and animated fog moves over the ground. The last scene features Viking Village (VV) with emissive torches illuminating a fog stretching all over the scene. The output resolution for all our tests was fixed at 1920 × 1080 and the experiments were evaluated using a system that is equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 graphics card, an AMD Ryzen 7 3700X CPU, and 32GB of system memory. We implemented all approaches using the NVIDIA Falcor [KCK*21] rendering framework to ensure a fair comparison.
We compare all methods against a GroundTruth generated through volumetric raymarching, as detailed in Section 3. Step size, number of light samples and the number of raymarching steps from the scattering point to the light source were carefully selected to achieve the highest quality. Additionally, multiple frames were accumulated to enhance the results.

5.2. Scene Evaluation
In the previous section we showed how individual design decisions of our system were made. We now want to show how our system performs for different test scenes.
To establish a baseline for common real-time approaches, we implemented a froxel-based approach as described by Hillaire [Hil15], called Hillaire henceforth. We use a froxel grid with the typically used dimensions of 240 × 135 × 128 (each froxel spanning 8 pixels). For each froxel, light sources are sampled, samples are ray traced to determine a possible occlusion and radiance data is stored in the froxel. Froxels are also temporally accumulated through remapping current froxel world positions. This works well for isotropic media, but produces artifacts for anisotropic media. For rendering, each pixel's corresponding froxels are marched, transmittance and radiance is calculated via linear interpolation and pixel color is composed. By default, self-shadowing of participating media is not calculated due to performance constraints, resulting in sub-par quality. We also added a version (called Hillairemod) with added transmittance calculations for the incoming radiance, moving the method closer to our visual results.
While Ours-Raymarched results in the overall best quality, we want to emphasise that our probe sharing system can also be used with different rendering techniques. Therefore, we show a version of froxel-based rendering (called Ours-Froxel) that samples the probe structure instead of generating new data per froxel.
Single Viewer Use Case We first demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in a single-viewer scenario. As shown in Tables 8 and 10, due to our adaptive probe structure, our approach is capable of capturing the radiance in participating media in high quality, achieving consistently high quality metrics. As expected, Ours-Raymarched at quarter resolution runs faster than at half resolution, with the performance gap largely depending on the amount of visible participating media. For instance, scenes with minimal media, such as ground fog in Swamp, show little difference in timing, while scenes with extensive fog coverage, like Viking Village, exhibit more significant differences. Our hybrid approach, Ours-Froxel, closely matches the timing of our quarter-resolution raymarched solution but delivers lower quality results.

Memory In terms of memory consumption, we account for both the probes themselves and the additional data structures required for level-of-detail (LOD) management and dynamic allocation. Each probe consists of nine spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients per RGB channel, along with an accumulation counter and an average light intensity value for determining LOD changes. By using half-precision floats, we pack each probe into just 64 bytes. To minimize the number of memory allocations, dynamic allocation occurs on a per-bin basis rather than per-probe, with each bin containing 4 × 4 × 4 probes. For the bins, 18 integers are needed to store allocation, deallocation, status, and index data.
Each viewer also requires memory for rendering. Since SH evaluations are performed prior to rendering, we store these values between passes, using an RGB value for each evaluated probe. We optimize our approach for faster rendering at the cost of increased memory usage in multi-viewer scenarios. To reduce memory consumption, rendering can be done serially by reusing intermediate buffers, though this may lead to longer runtimes. In practice, memory consumption depends on scene size, the distribution of participating media, and the lighting setup. Figure 8 shows the memory usage of our approach and competing methods. Even in single-viewer setups, our approach uses less memory than Hillaire, and for twelve viewers, it uses less than one fifth of the memory. In contrast, Ours-Froxel would require duplicating memory structures from both methods, leading to the highest memory usage.
Multiple Scattering Our system can be extended to efficiently compute multiple scattering, in addition to single scattering, due to it only effecting the update step and therefore amortizing well with an increasing number of viewer. As described in section 3, multiple scattering paths can be constructed using single scattering computations. Similarly, multiple scattering information can be recursively retrieved. By storing radiance due to multiple scattering rather than single scattering in our probes and querying a single probe for each scattering event, we can simulate multiple scattering over time, akin to dynamic diffuse global illumination via probes [MGNM19]. In our early experiments, this method adds multiple scattering with an overhead of approximately 40% compared to single scattering alone. An example is shown in Figure 10 for the Sponza scene. However, for the types of media we focused on, this overhead does not provide enough of a quality improvement to justify the cost. For media where multiple scattering is more critical, such as clouds, this overhead may be more acceptable. It is also important to note that recursive accumulation over time introduces some temporal lag, which we have yet to fully explore.

6. Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work
Rendering participating media in real-time remains a significant computational challenge, requiring careful simplification of physical models and optimization of computations. We introduced a hierarchical spherical harmonics-based radiance caching grid, specifically designed to handle heterogeneous and anisotropic participating media efficiently. This grid enables temporal reuse without introducing reprojection artifacts and supports data sharing between multiple viewers in a scene. Our evaluation demonstrates that we can achieve high-quality, real-time rendering, even for multi-viewer scenarios, thanks to the shared data structure.
Despite these advances, ray marching remains the primary bottleneck in our approach. Scenes requiring high detail throughout the entire volume still demand a high number of steps, which can prevent real-time performance, even with our optimizations. Ongoing research into accelerating ray marching could further enhance the efficiency of our system, providing opportunities for retroactive improvements. Future work could reduce costs by decreasing the required light samples and thus the number of rays to be marched through alternative light sampling techniques, similar to ReSTIR. Another somewhat under explored avenue of our work is handling highly dynamic media and lighting changes. Similarly to other caching approaches, we rely on the fact that the temporal changes remain low such that we can accumulate radiance over sufficient amounts of time. Handling temporal changes will become even more critical when further exploring caching multiple scattering data. We use spherical harmonics with 3 band to balance memory, speed and quality. However, this choice limits the angular resolution of the representation, leading to reduced accuracy in cases of highly forward or backward scattering, which can be effectively mitigated by using more than 3 bands. Finally, our level-of-detail adaptation strategy is sampling-based, which can lead to errors such as mistakenly reducing resolution or failing to restore resolution where it is necessary. In addition to these points, future work may explore distributed rendering systems, where probe data could be computed on one node and streamed between nodes to boost both quality and performance.
