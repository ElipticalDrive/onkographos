Instant Neural Graphics Primitives with a Multiresolution Hash Encoding | alphaXiv

Abstract
Neural graphics primitives, parameterized by fully connected neural networks,
can be costly to train and evaluate. We reduce this cost with a versatile new
input encoding that permits the use of a smaller network without sacrificing
quality, thus significantly reducing the number of floating point and memory
access operations: a small neural network is augmented by a multiresolution
hash table of trainable feature vectors whose values are optimized through
stochastic gradient descent. The multiresolution structure allows the network
to disambiguate hash collisions, making for a simple architecture that is
trivial to parallelize on modern GPUs. We leverage this parallelism by
implementing the whole system using fully-fused CUDA kernels with a focus on
minimizing wasted bandwidth and compute operations. We achieve a combined
speedup of several orders of magnitude, enabling training of high-quality
neural graphics primitives in a matter of seconds, and rendering in tens of
milliseconds at a resolution of 1920 ⁣× ⁣1080{1920\!\times\!1080}1920×1080.

Reconstructing a navigable 3D scene from a set of photographs is only useful if you can look at the result without waiting hours for the model to train. Neural graphics representations such as NeRF produce impressive results, but slow training makes experimentation painful. This paper's move is to stop asking a large network to learn every fine detail on its own. Instead, a small network consults trainable lookup tables at several spatial scales and combines what it finds. The authors pair this encoding with fully fused CUDA kernels, so the speedups come from the system as a whole, not the encoding alone. [p2] [p1]
The result is that recognizable outputs appear within seconds across several different graphics tasks.
The same encoding is applied to four tasks: a gigapixel image, a signed distance function (SDF), neural radiance caching (NRC), and NeRF. Each row shows results at increasing training times alongside a reference.
A lookup at every scale
How can a small network keep fine detail without an explicit, task-specific tree? For each input coordinate, the method consults a set of grids whose resolutions grow geometrically from coarse to fine. Each grid level has its own array of trainable feature vectors. At coarse levels, grid vertices map directly to entries. At fine levels, the integer grid coordinates are spatially hashed into a fixed-size table, so distinct locations can end up sharing an entry. The method retrieves the features at the corners surrounding the query point, linearly interpolates them, concatenates the results across levels (along with any auxiliary inputs), and feeds this encoded input to a small MLP. Training backpropagates through the network and the interpolation, updating the looked-up features. In the reported default configuration there are 16 levels with two feature dimensions per entry; table size and finest resolution are among the choices tuned per task. [p4]
The encoding in order: hash the voxel vertices, look up their feature vectors, interpolate linearly, concatenate across levels together with the auxiliary input, then pass the result to the neural network. Gradients flow back from the MLP into the looked-up features. The drawing is a 2D illustration with two resolution levels.
Sharing table entries sounds risky, and the authors offer an account of why it works, though not a guarantee. Coarse levels are collision-free but capture only broad structure; fine levels capture smaller detail but can alias distinct points. Collisions land in different places at different levels, and the authors reason that during training the gradients from more consequential samples can dominate the averaged update to a shared entry. [p5] The paper also reports collision-related artifacts, discussed below.
Does the encoding explain the speed?
NeRF learns a scene's radiance and density from images with known camera poses, then renders new views by sampling along rays. For background, see NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis. [p9]
Averaged over eight scenes (Mic, Ficus, Chair, Hotdog, Materials, Drums, Ship, Lego), test-view PSNR, peak signal-to-noise ratio in decibels, is 31.407 dB for the hash method after 15 seconds of training, versus 31.005 dB for the original NeRF after approximately hours. The prior-work numbers come from their own papers, so this is not a matched-hardware, identical-protocol benchmark. [p10]
The more informative control for the encoding claim is internal: the authors replace the hash encoding with frequency encoding, enlarge the MLP, and keep a nearly identical implementation. The hash version beats it at much shorter training times. There is a caveat: the frequency version costs about 30 ms per step against about 6 ms for the hash model, so at five minutes it has completed about 10,000 steps versus about 50,000, and it could keep improving with longer training. The authors also say they did not fully quantify the independent effect of advanced ray marching. [p10] [p10]
The method does not win everywhere. mip-NeRF and NSVF score better on the Materials scene, which has complex view-dependent reflections; the authors associate this weakness with their necessarily smaller MLP. [p10]
A second ablation tests the role of the learned MLP rather than the encoding itself. Feeding the encoded features through the MLP, instead of a linear transformation, gives noticeably finer specular detail and less background noise after one minute, for a layer that is 15% more expensive than the linear one. The paper reads this as better handling of specular effects and of hash-collision artifacts. [p9] [p10]
After one minute of training, red and blue boxes enlarge two details so the MLP result, the linear-transformation result, and the reference can be compared. The MLP version shows finer specular detail and less background noise.
An adaptive-image comparison
The closest adaptive-image comparison is ACORN, which allocates capacity by adapting a multiscale block partition during training (ACORN: Adaptive Coordinate Networks for Neural Scene Representation). On the Tokyo panorama, the hash method reports matching ACORN's 38.59 dB PSNR in 2.5 minutes with a similar parameter count, and reaching 41.9 dB after 4 minutes. The authors say direct comparison is difficult: roughly a factor of ten comes from their fully fused CUDA kernels, and the smaller MLP enabled by the encoding accounts for much of the remaining advantage. So this is illustrative, not a clean estimate of what the encoding alone buys. [p7] [p7]
Where the design travels, and where it frays
On signed distance functions (SDFs), whose zero level-set represents a surface, the hash method approaches NGLOD's numerical IoU at similar reported performance and memory cost. Unlike NGLOD's close-fitting octree, it can be evaluated throughout the training volume. The authors note visible roughness in the hash result and attribute it to collisions. [p7] [p8]
For neural radiance caching, a model learns online from sparse path-tracer samples while the camera moves. The hash encoding yields sharper detail than the prior triangle-wave encoding, at 133 versus 147 FPS at 1920×1080. The 0.7 ms overhead includes both training and runtime costs. This is an online task where the model continually adapts to what the camera currently sees. [p9]
Taken together, multiscale learned lookups give a small network a way to recover detail without first building and maintaining a task-specific adaptive structure, which shortens the wait between changing something and seeing the result. [p12]
