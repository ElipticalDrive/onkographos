Abstract
Extracting high-fidelity isosurfaces from implicit data is an important step in additive manufacturing (AM)of
topology optimized shapes. Topology optimization determines material placement for AM in order to build
optimal shapes based on underlying physics. An optimized shape is generally represented implicitly using a
scalar field on a discrete model(e.g. a density field on a hex mesh). An explicit representation is then generated
by constructing an isosurface of the implicit data using a popular algorithm such as marching cubes (MC).
Isosurfaces constructed using MC generally contain slivers and abrupt changes in surface normal that cause
high-frequency noise. Also, the underlying scalar field itself might have waviness which contributes to lowfrequency noise. This high and low frequency noise, in combination with the stair-step artifact of layer-by-layer
AM, results in a poor-quality surface finish. Therefore, we propose a method to extract high-fidelity smooth
isosurfaces that are suitable for AM. The proposed algorithm uses refinement, projection, and smoothing in
generating a suitable high-fidelity smooth isosurface. The effectiveness of the proposed method has been
visually inspected using zebra stripes and curvature color plots before 3D printing the parts.
1. Introduction
In the last decade, advances in additive manufacturing(AM)[1,2]. have evolved significantly to build complex
shapes. Unlike Rapid Prototyping(RP)[3,4,5], which is mainly used to build replicas of existing parts, AM can
produce fully functional parts with a variety of materials. Hence, AM can be considered as a primary
manufacturing process provided the economics are met. The advantages of AM include minimal to no tooling
even with complex features, minimal material waste, reduced setup time, and design freedom. The required
material, mechanical properties, part accuracy, surface finish and level of functionality dictates, which AM
process should be chosen.
One ofthe greatest advantages in using AM is in the area of"design freedom." AM processes are not restricted
by rules which dictate that certain features must be included or modified to ensure that a part can be physically
manufactured. For example, draft angles need to be taken into account and overhangs should be eliminated
when injection molding parts. Access for cutting tools in downstream machining operations also leads to design
compromises. Eliminating all these external constraints, allows completely new structures to be realized which
are only possible using AM techniques. Unlike traditional subtractive processes, AM approaches "prine parts
by fusing the material together with thermal or other processes. In this way, AM can build parts that are nearly
impossible to realize with traditional subtractive processes due to geometric and material complexities
accessible by these methods.
The geometric and material flexibility afforded by additive manufacturing can be fully leveraged by using the
Topology Optimization(TO)design paradigm. Topology optimization is a mathematical approach to optimize a
performance objective (e.g., maximum stiffness, minimum peak stress, etc.) given a set of requirements (e.g.,
applied loads, boundary conditions, and mass and volume budget). Topology optimization gives answers to
fundamental engineering questions such as how to place material within a prescribed design domain in order to
meet the best performance objective. Since the seminal paper by Bendsoe and Kikuchi in 1988 [6], topology
optimization has spread from mechanical design problems to a wide range of other physical disciplines[7].

The coupling of AM with TO, and the need to produce advanced design tools capable of leveraging AM, has
begun to garner significant attention [8]. Designs realized with TO are typically quite freeform in their
geometric and material attributes. The components are completely redesigned to do the same job, but look
radically different. This paper addresses one of the challenges in this design workflow, i.e., creating printable
model representation (typically a faceted model in STL format) from the TO shape (typically a scalar field on a
discretized domain such as fmite element mesh). In both level set-based and density-based TO methods, the
actual surface of the design is the isosurface of a scalar field, and this isosurface must be extracted into a
printable, water-tight faceted representation with smooth transition in facet normals.
The remainder of this paper focuses on extracting high-fidelity isosurfaces from the implicit data output from
the topology optimization. An overview of the algorithm is given in Section 2, followed by a detailed
description in Section 3. The Results section discusses volume shrinkage and computing time with different
parameters and displays the 3D printed parts manufactured using the proposed method.

3. Details of the Proposed Method
3.1.Input Hex Mesh with a Scalar Field
Unlike traditional design which involves a manual step of generating a complex CAD model using commercial
CAD software packages, the workflow for generating topology optimized shapes is automatic and guided by the
functional requirements and underlying physics. First, a simple cuboid which bounds the target shape is
created and a Cartesian hex mesh is generated. Next the boundary conditions and material properties are
specified. The topology optimization step iteratively runs the desired physics such as structural analysis,
thermal analysis, etc. to determine the material placement using the density field at the nodes to optimize the
performance objective such as structural stiffness, or minimal displacement. The final topology optimized
shape is therefore implicitly represented via a scalar filed such as density field on the hex mesh nodes. The
following sections describe how to extract explicit representations ofthese shapes.
3.2.Extract Coarse Isosurface
Marching cubes is a simple algorithm for creating a triangle mesh from an implicit function such as 0(x, y, z)=
O. It works by marching over a uniform grid of cubes (hexes) superimposed over a region of the function. If
the values of 0 at the 8 vertices of the cube are all positive or all negative, then the cube is entirely above or
entirely below the isosurface and no triangles are generated. Otherwise, the cube intersects the function and
some triangles and vertices are generated. Since the value of0 at each vertex can either be positive or negative,
there are technically 28 possible configurations, but many ofthese are equivalent to one another. There are only
15 unique cases, which are shown Figure 3 [9]. As we march over all of the cubes, the generated triangles are
added to a list, and the final mesh is the union of all these triangles. The main drawback of this method is that it
produces meshes with many small and badly shaped triangles. Also, the triangles' normal can change
significantly depending on the resolution (mesh size) of the hex mesh. To improve the performance of MC,
several algorithms have been proposed [10].

Desbrun et al. [16] used a special cotangent weight operator in place of A. The resulting smoothing method is
referred to as curvatureflow smoothing. Using this new operator reduces the effect of the mesh parametrization
on the resultant smoothing. In particular, high triangle densities correspond to slower convergence in the
Gaussian approach, whereas coarser triangulations suffer from faster diffusion. This is true of entire meshes and
also of local regions. Thus, curvature flow is useful when a single mesh contains significantly different triangle
sizes, so as to accomplish the same qualitative level of smoothing throughout the surface. In our application,
the hex elements are generally uniform in size. The Curvature flow paper also claims exact volume
conservation, but this is not due to the curvature flow operator. Instead, the method accomplishes volume
conservation via a post-processing step in which the mesh is uniformly scaled in all directions. Any
constriction of features is still present; the constricted features are simply scaled.
The Laplace-tangential [11] method takes a simpler approach to volume conservation. It functions similarly to
Gaussian smoothing, but only the tangential component of the Laplacian is used. In contrast, curvature flow
smoothing only moves vertices in the direction normal to the surface. By construction, tangential smoothing
fails to remove any sharp variations in mesh geometry that occur normal to the surface.

Low frequency smoothing:
Low frequency ripples present in the density field are reflected in the extracted isosurface. In our experience,
trying to remove the low-frequency noise using the local smoothing methods described so far is not effective.
The reason is that local smoothing accomplishes low-pass filtering to remove high-frequency noise [9, 14].
Here we discuss a direct method which identifies ripples or bumps based on curvature and then flattens these
regions with aggressive Gaussian smoothing. As the nodes surrounding the ripples are kept fixed, the large
number of smoothing iterations does not cause widespread volume shrinkage. An example ofthis workflow is
shown in Figure 10. First, Gaussian curvature is calculated at every vertex as shown in Figure 10(a).
Rusinkiewicz [16] investigates several ways to compute the curvature of a mesh. We calculate the second
fundamental tensor of each triangle, compute the Gaussian curvature as the product of the tensor's eigenvalues,
then compute an average of neighboring triangles to assign a curvature to each vertex. The regions to be
flattened are then identified based on a positive cutoff curvature, which are shown in red. These red regions
represent hills and valleys of the surface mesh. Blue regions indicate negative Gaussian curvature, which
represent saddle points. The green regions correspond to zero Gaussian curvature, where one of the principal
curvatures is zero (e.g. a planar or cylindrical surface).
The red regions with positive Gaussian curvature are flattened in order to get rid oflow frequency ripples. This
flattening step fills the valleys and removes the hills by fixing the nodes at the boundary of each hill and valley.
However, this step increases surface roughness as shown in Figure 10(b). Figure 10(c) shows the final mesh
after smoothing the rough surface using one of the smoothing schemes to effectively remove low-frequency
noise. If we do not perform flattening, then local smoothing methods discussed in the above section will
remove only the high-frequency noise and low-frequency noise will be retained as shown in Figure 10(d). This
illustrates how low frequency noise can be removed using this method.

3.7.Visualize the High-Fidelity Isosurface
Zebra Stripes:
Zebra stripes are commonly used in the automobile industry to evaluate the continuity ofsurface patches. In the
real world, a car model covered in a reflective material is viewed under a bank of strip lights, and the resulting
reflection lines are analyzed. The most important part of the analysis is to move your viewing position and
observe how the reflection lines change as you move the eye point.
This real-life visualization method has been simulated by using a bounding sphere with black and white stripes
instead of using a planar black and white board. User controls are provided to specify the width of the stripes.
The black and white stripes on the sphere act as an environment, which the topology optimized smooth surfaces
reflect. The law of reflection (i.e., angle of incidence is equal to angle of reflection) is used to color code the
surface mesh nodes. This means the color at the nodes will vary as the model is rotated within the zebra stripe
environment or the eye point is changed. The triangles of the mesh are then rendered using the colors at the
nodes. These zebra stripes can identify the continuity in the surface normals as illustrated in Figure 11.

Gaussian Curvature:
Another way to visualize the variation in the surface normal is to make a color plot ofthe nodes based on their
Gaussian curvature. The Gaussian curvature is a product of the maximum and minimal principal curvatures at a
given point. The hills and valleys will have positive Gaussian curvature, the planar surfaces such as optimized
blocks will have zero Gaussian curvature, and the saddle points will have negative Gaussian curvature. These
Gaussian curvature values are used to set the color at each node using a color plot. In our implementation,
green refers to zero Gaussian curvature in the regions such as planar or cylinder regions where one of the
principal curvatures is zero. Red refers to hills and valleys with positive Gaussian curvature. And blue refers to
saddle point with negative Gaussian curvature. Figure 13 shows the Gaussian color plots on rough and smooth

a thermoplastic filament is metered into a print head, which melts it and extrudes a 0.25 mm thick bead. The
thermoplastic used in this process is acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS). The main advantages of this process

are that no chemical post-processing is required, the material does not need to cure, the machine is relatively
inexpensive, and the materials are affordable. These advantages result in a more cost effective process. The
disadvantages are that the resolution is low compared to other additive manufacturing processes, so finishing is
required for a smooth surface, and it is a slow process, taking days to build large or complex parts. To save time
some 3D printers permit both a fully dense and a partially dense print, at the cost of reduced mechanical
properties.

5. Conclusion
This paper proposes an algorithm for extracting high-fidelity smooth isosurfaces from implicit data such as a
scalar field defined on the nodes of a hex mesh for additive manufacturing. The scalar field representing the
topology optimized shape implicitly contains low-frequency noise and the marching cubes method of building
coarse triangles at each hex results in high-frequency noise with abrupt changes in surface facet normals and at
element boundaries. The proposed method uses different local smoothing algorithms to smooth high-frequency
noise. Also, it proposes a new curvature flattening method to remove low frequency noise. Work is underway
to use a frequency filtering-based method to remove low-frequency noise and address volume shrinkage. The
paper uses zebra stripes and Gaussian curvature to visualize the smoothness of the final high-fidelity surface
mesh. The final smooth surface mesh in STL format is 3D printed to build AM parts with high-quality surface
finish.
