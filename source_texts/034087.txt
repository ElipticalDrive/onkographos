ABSTRACT
360° images have attracted increasing attention due to their
wide field of view. However, spherical 360° images need to
be converted into 2D equi-rectangular projection (ERP) images for compression. This conversion often leads to pixel
overstretching in the ERP image, which results in a lot of redundancy in the texture. Performing a direct prediction without considering the stretching will inevitably make it difficult
to achieve optimal results. To tackle this problem, we propose a multi-layer adaptive scale-block scheme for ERP image compression. In particular, we introduce a multi-layer
structure based on the overstretching rate and use multi-scale
convolution kernels to better match each layer and extract features more effectively. Subsequently, we employ an adaptive
scale-block method to effectively reduce bitrate redundancy
in overstretched and less important regions. Finally, we propose a new end-to-end model that is efficient for 360° image compression. Experimental results demonstrate that our
scheme outperforms other image compression methods and
reduces bitrate by nearly 16% compared to the latest learned

1. INTRODUCTION
With the advancement of virtual reality (VR) technology, an
increasing number of wearable VR devices are emerging.
These devices allow users to experience 360° images and
videos, providing a highly immersive experience. This indicates that 360° images and videos will have immense growth
potential in the future. Notably, 360° images are known for
their expansive perspectives, often requiring higher resolutions to represent. Consequently, the data size of 360° images
can be up to ten times greater than that of regular images.
Therefore, it is crucial to effectively compress 360° images.
Since 2D image compression techniques have already
been well-developed, 360° images are often projected into
2D formats to facilitate more effective compression. The
equi-rectangular projection (ERP) is the most commonly
used projection method. However, a significant drawback
of ERP is the overstretching issue, which increases storage
requirements without corresponding visual benefits. To improve compression efficiency, it is crucial to reduce the bitrate
redundancy introduced by this overstretching. In order to address this issue, this paper presents a multi-layer end-to-end
360° image compression scheme. The novelty and main
contributions are summarized as follows:
To adapt to varying overstretching rates across regions,
we introduce multi-scale convolution kernels to capture rectangular receptive fields and reduce overfitting based on overstretching rates.
We present an adaptive scale-block method that considers
both overstretching rates and texture complexities of 360° images. This method effectively reduces bitrate redundancy in
both overstretched and less significant areas.
By integrating the above two methods, we propose a novel
end-to-end compression scheme built on a multi-layer structure specifically designed for 360° images. Experimental results demonstrate that our method outperforms existing approaches.
2. RELATED WORK
In recent years, end-to-end neural networks have been widely
used for image compression [1, 2, 3]. Ballé et al. [1] utilized
autoencoder-like architectures to learn nonlinear transformations, and integrated these with quantization to optimize the
entire process in an end-to-end manner, thus achieving higher
compression efficiency compared to traditional handcrafted
methods [4, 5]. Minnen et al. [2] revealed that combining
autoregressive and hierarchical priors could better exploit the
probabilistic structure of latent representations. While these
end-to-end models perform well for 2D images, they are less
effective for 360° images, as they can not adequately address
the issue of overstretching in ERP images.
Recent efforts in 360° image compression have focused
on deep neural network models. Yu et al. [6] introduced
an adaptive tile representation for 360° images, optimizing
the size of each tile to address the problem of overstretching. Bidgoli et al. [7] proposed a novel convolution operation designed specifically for the sphere that maintains the
expressiveness and low complexity of traditional 2D convolutions. Li et al. [8] introduced the first end-to-end optimized 360° image compression model, which uses adaptive
latitude maps to mask feature thus reducing rate loss. Furthermore, they introduced a parametric pseudocylindrical representation and corresponding convolution operation to match
significantly varying contents within 360° images [9]. Although their work achieved state-of-the-art performance in
learned 360° image compression, they did not consider varying overstretching rates across different regions, which should
be addressed separately. In order to address this issue, this
paper proposes a multi-layer adaptive scale-block 360° image
compression scheme. Experimental results demonstrate that
this approach delivers superior performance.
3. PROPOSED SCHEME
3.1. Multi-layer Structure
360° images are typically represented in a spherical surface
indexed by longitude and latitude. To facilitate compression,
the spherical projection first needs to be mapped to a 2D image. As shown in Fig. 1(a), regions A and B on the sphere
have different sizes, but they are mapped to equal-sized areas in the ERP image. This results in horizontal pixel overstretching between the spherical surface and the ERP image
content, a phenomenon commonly known as horizontal overstretching.In particular, the closer a region is to the poles, the
smaller its area on the sphere, and the more significantly it
is stretched in the corresponding ERP image. It is evident
that the overstretching rates vary significantly across different
latitudes, leading to substantial differences in texture redundancy. Therefore, prediction should be conducted in conjunction with the stretching rates.
To quantify the degree of this distortion, we employ an
overstretching factor, Lat(i) introduced in [8], as depicted in
Fig. 1(b). Since the overstretching rates may vary across different latitudes, predicting each latitude separately would undoubtedly achieve optimal encoding results. However, due to
the large number of latitudes, this approach would make the
prediction process extremely complex. To address this, we
can group latitudes with similar stretching rates into the same
category. This way, prediction only needs to be performed for
a few categories, significantly reducing the complexity of the
prediction process.

3.2. The Selection of Multi-scale Convolution Kernels
As noted earlier, ERP images suffer from significant overstretching in the horizontal direction, which distorts the image texture. Moreover, within the ERP image, different regions are stretched to different degrees, and convolution kernels of fixed size can not adapt to all stretched regions. This
overstretched image texture, especially in regions with severe distortion, poses a challenge for square convolution kernels, which are unable to effectively extract features of overstretched areas and often lead to overfitting [10].

areas of the image where textures are highly stretched, leading to significant redundancy in pixel values. In these overstretched regions, the texture features tend to be highly similar, creating opportunities for bitrate reduction through pixel

We introduced multi-scale convolution kernels into our
model. Multi-scale kernels are designed with greater horizontal dimensions relative to their height to adapt to the overstretching problem, each tailored to the specific stretching degree of image at different layers. At the same time, we select
the optimal convolution kernel scale for each layer through
experiments. For Layer 1 as an example, where the overstretching is most pronounced, we designed a set of rectangular convolution kernels of varying sizes to determine the
optimal configuration. Most end-to-end networks [3] use 5×5
convolution kernels to balance computational complexity and
receptive field size. As mentioned above, we selected convolution kernels of sizes 5×7, 5×9, 5×11, and 5×13 as candidates. Distortion quality is evaluated using the WS-PSNR
metric [11], and the implementation details are provided in
Sections 3.4 and 4.1. As shown in Fig. 2, the 5×7 kernel
performed the worst in terms of overall image quality. On
the other hand, the 5×9, 5×11, and 5×13 kernels performed
very similarly, effectively mitigating the overstretching problem and capturing the horizontal texture elongation. However,
larger kernels, while providing better receptive field coverage,
also come with an increased computational cost, which results
in longer training and inference times.
Considering both the performance of kernels and their
computational efficiency, we opted for the 5×9 kernel for
Layer 1. We applied the same testing method for the remaining layers. smaller kernels are more suitable for regions
where the overstretching effect is less severe. In Layer 2, we
selected a 5×7 kernel, and for Layer 3, where the stretching
ratio approaches 1 and the overstretching is minimal, a 5×5
kernel is used.

Given the varying degrees of overstretching across different layers, we designed multi-scale average pooling blocks of
three distinct sizes to address these differences. As illustrated
in Fig. 1(b), the average overstretching ratios vary across layers: approximately 1:7 for Layer 1, 1:2 for Layer 2, and 1:1
for Layer 3. These ratios influence the block sizes designed
for each layer. As shown in Fig. 3(a), for Layer 1, with
the most significant stretching (1:7), we use a 1×8 horizontal block to closely match this ratio. In Layer 2, we employ
a 2×4 block, and for Layer 3, with a 1:1 ratio, we use a 4×4
square block [12, 13]. This multi-scale block design allows
us to adapt the compression mechanism to the varying degrees of overstretching, enabling more efficient compression
of regions with less significant distortion.

3.4. Multi-layer End-to-end 360 Image Compression
Scheme
In this section, we first present the network structure of the
proposed 360° image compression model, and then describe
the flow of the entire compression process. As shown in
Fig. 4, the network structure of our model comprises four
key components: the encoder, the adaptive scale-block network, the entropy estimation model, and the decoder. As
shown in Fig. 5, for the encoder, we adopt the design from a
current mainstream end-to-end image compression model[3].
We first use the proposed multi-kernel convolution to improve
feature extraction, followed by residual blocks (RBs) and an
attention module to enhance the network’s performance. The
proposed adaptive scale-block network incorporates a multikernel convolution and a Gaussian gambling layer [14] to generate an optimal scale-block map. In the decoder, we utilize a
structure similar to the encoder, but replace the convolutional
(Conv) layer with a transposed convolutional (Tconv) layer.
The ERP input image X is first split into three layers.
Each layer is processed by the encoder to convert it into a feature representation M (i) of the i th layer. Subsequently, M (i)
is passed through the adaptive scale-block network to generate the optimal block mode map ρ . The feature M (i) uses

of 18,160 high-quality ERP images, each with a resolution of 512×1024 pixels. The images in the dataset include
panoramic natural images, as well as panoramic indoor images, featuring rich textures and natural structures that closely
reflect real-world photographic environments.

Testing Datasets. For the test set, we select 200 highquality ERP images from the training set. To evaluate the

Implementation Details. We train our model in two
stages. In the first stage, we train our model for 24 epochs using a learning rate of ξ=0.001. In the second stage, we reduce
the learning rate to ξ=0.0001 and train for an additional 12
epochs until the model stabilizes. Both the encoder and decoder, as well as the entropy estimation network, are trained
together using the same learning rate. Our batch size is set
to 4 in the training process. The ADAM solver [16] is used
to optimize the whole model. We adopt WS-MSE as the distortion loss for WS-PSNR. For fine-tuning the model during
the second stage, we employ both WS-MSE and WS-SSIM
as the distortion loss for an additional 12 epochs.

In our ablation study, we evaluated the effectiveness of multiscale convolution kernels and adaptive scale-block in 360°
image compression. We tested three configurations: using

each individual component, with a significant reduction in
