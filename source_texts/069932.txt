A BSTRACT
Sparse-view cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is severely under-constrained by limited
X-ray projections. Learned reconstruction can provide complementary anatomical information, but
using it as a prior in patient-specific Gaussian reconstruction introduces two challenges. First, the
discrepancy between the learned prior and current reconstruction is spatially non-uniform: some
local differences contain useful missing 3D information, whereas others reflect prior bias. Second,
even a volumetrically beneficial correction can conflict with the observed X-ray measurements.
Since X-ray measurements are line integrals, spatially separated corrections may affect overlapping
rays, with their signed projection responses reinforcing or partially canceling in the measurement
residual, so their feasibility cannot always be assessed independently. In this paper, we introduce
RePrior, a multi-stage progressive prior-correction framework that addresses correction actionability,
measurement feasibility, and representation expressivity. Selective Correction Proposal (SCP) first
identifies actionable signed local corrections from prior–reconstruction discrepancies. MeasurementConstrained Reconciliation (MCR) then jointly enforces their consistency with patient-specific X-ray
measurements, while Hybrid Residual Enhancement (HRE) recovers additional correction capacity
through complementary voxel- and Gaussian-domain degrees of freedom around the reconciled
anchor. RePrior requires neither test-time ground truth nor case-specific network fine-tuning. RePrior
gains approximately 2.6 dB in 3D PSNR over R2 -Gaussian across LUNA16, PANORAMA, and
PENGWIN. Beyond prior-assisted reconstruction, it delivers additional gains in volumetric quality
and observed-view projection fidelity, with consistent gains in held-out-view fidelity in the backbonematched evaluation.

Introduction
Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) reconstructs a three-dimensional attenuation field from multi-view X-ray
projections. Reducing the number of acquired views is a direct way to lower radiation exposure and acquisition
burden (Sidky et al., 2006; Bian et al., 2010). This is particularly attractive in settings where CBCT is acquired
repeatedly or under tight acquisition constraints. However, reduced angular sampling can produce prominent streak
artifacts and degrade the recovery of structural details in reconstructed CT images (Bian et al., 2010). Preserving
these local anatomical details is important for anatomically faithful 3D reconstruction and interpretation (Lin et al.,
2025). Early learning-based approaches mitigated these sparse-view artifacts through image-domain or dual-domain
reconstruction networks (Jin et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2021). More recently, neural
fields and explicit Gaussian representations have enabled patient-specific 3D reconstruction directly from measured
X-rays through differentiable projection (Zha et al., 2022; Cai et al., 2024b; Zha et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026).
Complementary directions include reconstruction-friendly projection interpolation (Wang et al., 2022), meta-learned
neural attenuation fields (Shin et al., 2025), learned 3D Gaussian feature representations (Lin et al., 2024), and diffusion

For sparse-view CBCT, the central challenge is not merely to recover plausible anatomy, but to recover anatomy that
is supported by the measurements of the current patient. To complement the weakly constrained 3D information,
recent feed-forward approaches such as X-GRM (Liu et al., 2025), X-LRM (Zhang et al., 2026), and DeepSparse (Lin
et al., 2026) learn mappings from sparse X-rays to volumetric or explicit 3D representations, providing strong
anatomical information learned from training data. Here, we focus on a complementary post-reconstruction question:
whether individual local prior-induced changes are supported by the current patient’s measured X-ray projections.
Complementarily, patient-specific neural-field and Gaussian reconstruction methods directly optimize against the
current measurements and therefore provide strong data fidelity, yet sparse acquisition still leaves substantial 3D degrees
of freedom weakly constrained. This raises a key question: how can complementary 3D information from a learned
reconstruction be converted into patient-specific corrections that are supported by the measured X-rays, rather than
simply choosing between a learned prior and projection fitting?

Based on these observations, we propose RePrior, a multi-stage progressive prior-correction framework for sparse-view

patient-specific X-ray responses, thereby establishing a measurement-feasible reconstruction. Finally, Hybrid Residual
Enhancement (HRE) uses this feasible solution as an anchor and jointly exploits voxel- and Gaussian-domain residual
freedom to recover useful 3D corrections that remain unexpressed after reconciliation.

In this paper, our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose RePrior, a progressive prior-correction framework for sparse-view CBCT that transfers learned
volumetric priors into patient-specific Gaussian reconstruction through a three-stage progression from correction actionability to measurement feasibility and representation expressivity, rather than direct global prior
fusion.
• We conduct a systematic analysis of prior correction in sparse-view CBCT and identify two key failure modes:
local prior discrepancies have spatially non-uniform utility, and volumetrically beneficial corrections do not
necessarily improve patient-specific X-ray fitting. These findings motivate separating selective correction
proposal from measurement-constrained reconciliation.
• Across three anatomies and 230 evaluation cases, RePrior improves 3D PSNR by approximately 2.6 dB
over R2 -Gaussian. Beyond the matched prior-assisted reconstruction, its correction stages further improve
volumetric quality and observed- and held-out-view projection fidelity.

Related Work
2.1
Sparse-View Tomographic Reconstruction
Sparse-view reconstruction has attracted considerable attention in medical CT imaging. Early deep-learning methods (Zhang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2021) refined analytical reconstructions to suppress undersampling artifacts, while
neural fields enabled direct optimization against measured projections. NAF (Zha et al., 2022) models attenuation
with a coordinate-based network through differentiable X-ray rendering, and X-Gaussian (Cai et al., 2024a) adapts 3D
Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) for efficient X-ray optimization and rendering.
More recently, feed-forward models such as X-GRM (Liu et al., 2025), X-LRM (Zhang et al., 2026), and
DeepSparse (Lin et al., 2026) learn transferable 3D reconstruction priors from large-scale data. Generative approaches such as CvG-Diff (Chen et al., 2025b) and CT-SDM (Yang et al., 2025) further exploit diffusion-based priors
to recover anatomical structures under severe angular undersampling. These learned reconstruction models provide
strong structural information. RePrior focuses on a complementary question: which remaining local prior-induced
changes should be retained under the measured projections of the current patient.
2.2
Tomographic Reconstruction with 3DGS Representation
Although early NeRF-based approaches (Zha et al., 2022; Cai et al., 2024b) enable high-fidelity tomographic reconstruction, their implicit representations require per-scene optimization. R2 -Gaussian (Zha et al., 2024) introduced
3DGS-based sparse-view CT reconstruction with X-ray-specific rasterization for efficient reconstruction.
Subsequent works advance Gaussian tomography from complementary perspectives. 3DGR-CT (Li et al., 2025)
combines FBP-guided Gaussian initialization with a differentiable CT projector, while Exact-GS (Yang et al., 2026)
derives an exact splatting formulation for X-ray reconstruction. PGE (Wu et al., 2026) further explores uncertaintyguided active view selection.
These advances establish 3D Gaussians as an efficient explicit representation for projection-driven tomographic
reconstruction. RePrior studies a complementary problem: selectively transferring learned volumetric prior corrections
into a patient-specific Gaussian reconstruction.
2.3
Learned Priors with Measurement Constraints
Another line of work combines learned generative priors with explicit physical measurements during reconstruction (Chung et al., 2023). DOLCE (Liu et al., 2023) couples a conditional diffusion prior with an explicit CT
measurement model for limited-angle reconstruction. Recent work has also studied discrepancies introduced by
data-consistency operations in diffusion-based sparse-view CT reconstruction (Chen et al., 2025a), while diffusion
generative priors have been incorporated into iterative reconstruction through physics-informed initialization and latent
optimization (Evangelista et al., 2026). DiffNR (Su et al., 2026) instead uses diffusion-generated pseudo-reference
volumes to provide auxiliary 3D supervision during neural representation optimization for sparse-view tomography.
These studies demonstrate the value of combining learned structural priors with physical measurements for tomographic
reconstruction. RePrior builds on this general direction and focuses on selectively utilizing the residual prior discrepancies that remain after prior-assisted Gaussian reconstruction, with subsequent correction constrained by patient-specific
X-ray measurements.

Problem Analysis: Prior Utility and Measurement Compatibility
Learned-prior utilization in sparse-view CBCT faces two related but distinct challenges. Let VP denote the learned
prior and VR the corresponding prior-assisted Gaussian reconstruction, as illustrated in Fig. 2; their formal construction
is introduced in Sec. 4.1.
First, the remaining prior discrepancy does not have uniformly reliable local utility. Sparse-view measurements may
admit multiple attenuation volumes consistent with the same data (Sidky et al., 2006; Bian et al., 2010). This motivates
our use of a learned prior as a source of complementary structural information. However, this does not imply that the
remaining discrepancy VP − VR should be transferred unconditionally. As shown in Fig. 2(a), within the same patient,
some regions of the learned prior are closer to the reference volume, whereas others exhibit larger prior bias. The
remaining discrepancy should therefore be regarded as candidate correction information with unknown local reliability.
Reliable prior correction must first determine which local differences correspond to useful structures still missing from
the current reconstruction and how strongly they should be retained.
Second, volumetric utility does not imply measurement compatibility. As shown in Fig. 2(b), even a local change that
improves the volumetric reconstruction can worsen the fit to the patient’s observed X-ray measurements. Our post-hoc
diagnostic shows that this conflict is systematic: 43.3% of the 3D-beneficial recovery candidates increase the projection
error over the observed X-ray views, and such conflicting corrections occur in all 230 analyzed cases. Thus, potential
volumetric benefit alone is insufficient for deciding whether a correction should be retained.
Moreover, under the calibrated log-domain line-integral model, the responses of multiple local corrections add in
projection space. Let r = P(VR ) − Y denote the current measurement residual and hi = P(δVi ) the projection
response of correction δVi . For two corrections, the change in squared projection error is

Reliable prior correction therefore requires answering two distinct questions in sequence: which local prior differences
should be adopted, and how should the resulting candidate changes remain compatible with the current patient’s X-ray
measurements when acting jointly? The former concerns the local reliability and utility of prior information, whereas
the latter requires reasoning about the joint ray effects of multiple corrections in patient-specific measurement space.

Method
4.1
Overview
RePrior addresses the requirements identified in Sec. 3 by selecting useful prior differences and reconciling their
joint X-ray effects. Given sparse-view X-ray measurements Y, a pretrained reconstructor FP first produces a learned
volumetric prior VP . We additionally construct a patient-specific prior-assisted Gaussian reconstruction by optimizing
R2 -GS against the measured projections and subsequently introducing fixed spatial supervision from VP . This yields
the Gaussian representation GR and its voxelized attenuation volume VR :

As illustrated in Fig. 3, SCP converts the remaining prior discrepancy into an actionable signed correction DA . MCR
jointly reconciles its patient-specific X-ray responses, yielding DB and the measurement-feasible anchor VB . Because
MCR uses a restricted structured correction space, HRE introduces complementary voxel- and Gaussian-domain
residual freedom around VB to recover unexpressed SCP corrections while preserving measurement consistency.
4.2
Selective Correction Proposal
The discrepancy VP − VR should be regarded as candidate prior information rather than a direct correction target. As
analyzed in Sec. 3, such local disagreement may correspond either to anatomical structure genuinely missed under
sparse acquisition or to prediction bias introduced by the learned reconstructor. Its magnitude alone does not indicate
local prior reliability.

4.3
Measurement-Constrained Reconciliation
SCP identifies locally beneficial corrections in the volumetric domain, but volumetric utility does not guarantee
consistency with the measured X-rays. As shown in Fig. 2(b), some corrections that improve the volume can still
degrade the X-ray fitting of the current patient. Since each X-ray measurement integrates attenuation along a source–
detector path, spatially separated corrections may affect the same measurements. As illustrated in Fig. 4, a correction
that is beneficial in 3D can worsen projection fitting when considered alone, while another signed mode can partially
compensate for its ray response. This motivates reconciling multiple corrections jointly in measurement space.
MCR organizes DA into a low-dimensional structured correction space. We first establish a coarse measurement-aware
correction and then parameterize the remaining correction using regional recovery modes, which control how much
of the desirable correction is retained across spatial regions. However, resolving measurement conflicts through
suppression alone may discard useful 3D changes. We therefore introduce signed compensation modes that provide
additional attenuation-redistribution freedom across regions, allowing their measurement responses to reinforce or
partially cancel rather than simply suppressing the original correction.1
We then map the variable correction modes into the X-ray measurement space of the current patient. Rather than
reasoning about candidate corrections independently in voxel space, their patient-specific ray responses directly
characterize how each 3D change perturbs the measured projections. The corresponding coefficients are jointly
optimized to preserve as much of the SCP proposal as possible while satisfying patient-specific measurement and
physical constraints:

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setting
Datasets and evaluation. We evaluate on LUNA16 (Setio et al., 2017), PANORAMA (Alves et al., 2026), and
PENGWIN (Sang et al., 2026). Unless otherwise specified, matched experiments use 25 observed projections and
40 held-out views reserved for evaluation. We report volumetric PSNR/SSIM and reproject each reconstruction with
the same physics-based projector to compute observed- and held-out-view PSNR/SSIM under a common per-view
normalization protocol. Reconstruction outputs are fixed before ground-truth and held-out evaluation.
Baselines and implementation. We compare with SAX-NeRF, X-Gaussian, R2 -GS, Exact-GS, and DeepSparse.
DeepSparse-10 is the official 10-view checkpoint, reported as an additional reference; DeepSparse-25 is our continuation
trained under the matched 25-view setting. RePrior uses R2 -GS as its primary backbone and a frozen SCP predictor at
test time. Further training and implementation details are provided in the Appendix.

5.4
Stage-wise Ablation and Analysis
The three stages of RePrior serve complementary rather than redundant roles. SCP first proposes local corrections
with potential volumetric utility, MCR reconciles these corrections under patient-specific X-ray measurements, and
HRE restores residual expressivity around the resulting measurement-feasible anchor. Accordingly, these stages should
not be interpreted as repeatedly optimizing the same 3D objective, and their addition need not produce a monotonic
increase in 3D PSNR.
Table 3 summarizes the stage-wise contribution of each component in RePrior. For this ablation, we use 20 randomly
selected cases from each dataset and evaluate all configurations on the same cases. Results are reported as datasetbalanced averages across the three datasets, and the 2D metrics are computed on the 25 observed projections.
SCP recovers useful volumetric information from the prior–reconstruction discrepancy, without explicitly enforcing
measurement compatibility. Compared with Prior-R2 -GS, adding SCP increases 3D PSNR from 32.03 to 32.58 dB
(+0.55 dB) and 3D SSIM from 0.8966 to 0.9033. This gain comes with a slight decrease in observed-view projection
PSNR from 46.46 to 46.30 dB and SSIM from 0.9956 to 0.9952, confirming that volumetric improvement does not
necessarily imply measurement compatibility.
MCR then reconciles the candidate corrections under the patient-specific measurements. From SCP to MCR, 3D
PSNR decreases from 32.58 to 32.24 dB and 3D SSIM from 0.9033 to 0.8989, while observed-view projection PSNR
increases from 46.30 to 46.63 dB (+0.33 dB) and SSIM from 0.9952 to 0.9957. Importantly, MCR still improves
over Prior-R2 -GS in both 3D PSNR (32.24 vs. 32.03 dB) and projection PSNR (46.63 vs. 46.46 dB), indicating that
measurement reconciliation preserves useful prior corrections rather than simply removing them.

Conclusion
We propose RePrior, a multi-stage select–reconcile–enhance framework for transferring learned priors into patientspecific Gaussian reconstruction for sparse-view CBCT. RePrior identifies useful local prior corrections, reconciles
them under patient-specific X-ray measurements, and recovers residual information beyond the restricted reconciliation
space. This design separates correction selection from measurement enforcement while preserving additional freedom
for reconstruction refinement. Across LUNA16, PANORAMA, and PENGWIN, RePrior improves 3D PSNR by
approximately 2.6 dB over R2 -Gaussian and provides further volumetric and projection-fidelity gains beyond matched
prior-assisted reconstruction, with consistent improvements in held-out-view fidelity. These results support using
learned priors as sources of selective, measurement-compatible corrections rather than transferring them unconditionally
as reconstruction targets.

Algorithmic summary.
HRE can be summarized as follows:
1. Compute RB = VA − VB .
2. Determine the largest measurement-certified scalar voxel recovery and obtain V⋆ .
3. Generate a small set of gradient-derived Gaussian residual directions.
4. Build a local voxel–Gaussian response model and solve the bounded QCQP.
5. Apply the proposed update using backtracking.
6. Re-voxelize and reproject every real candidate.
7. Accept only candidates that satisfy all measurement, support, numerical, and Gaussian trust constraints.
8. Repeat until the stopping criterion is reached.
9. Select the final source-first certified endpoint and output V ∗ .
