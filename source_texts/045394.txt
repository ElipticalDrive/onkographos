Abstract
Purpose:
To develop and assess the feasibility of integrating model-based CBCT reconstruction with rigid motion estimation, using only a projection-domain data fidelity objective coupled with a differentiable forward projector.
Methods:
We propose a joint image reconstruction and motion estimation framework that integrates motion compensation directly into a model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) scheme. Rigid motion with six degrees of freedom (DoF) is modeled as geometric perturbations that alter the projection geometry and thereby affect the estimated projections. To enable gradient-based optimization, a differentiable forward projector with an analytical gradient formulation is employed, allowing efficient backpropagation of the loss between the estimated and measured projections. Within this differentiable framework, the motion parameters and attenuation volume are optimized simultaneously. To evaluate the proposed method, we conducted both simulation and physical phantom studies. In the simulation study, we assessed performance under jerk motion with progressively increasing motion amplitudes, ranging from 1 mm to 10 mm in translation and from 0.02 rad to 0.2 rad in rotation. Reconstruction accuracy was quantified using structural similarity index (SSIM) relative to motion-free ground truth. In the physical phantom study, a jerk motion of 5 mm translation and 0.1 rad rotation was applied during scanning. Motion-free, motion-corrupted, and motion-compensated reconstructions were displayed side-by-side for qualitative comparison, enabling visual assessment of motion artifact reduction.
Results:
The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed motion compensation method substantially enhances image quality across a range of motion amplitudes. Qualitative evaluation reveal that even under significant translational and rotational motion, the method effectively recovers anatomical structures with minimal residual errors. In simulation studies, SSIM values improved by 18%, 37%, and 48% for motion amplitudes (3 mm, 0.06 rad), (6 mm, 0.12 rad), and (9 mm, 0.18 rad), respectively. Reconstructions from physical bench data further confirm efficacy, showing restored sharpness and anatomical continuity across coronal and sagittal planes.
Conclusion:
The discrepancy between the estimated and acquired projections serves as a sufficient objective for jointly estimating both the image volume and motion parameters, potentially avoiding the need for image-based sharpness criteria.
1. INTRODUCTION
Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) is widely used in image-guided radiation therapy, dental imaging, and intraoperative navigation, owing to its ability to provide high-resolution 3D volumetric information with relatively low radiation dose. However, the patient movement during the relatively long acquisition time often make CBCT susceptible to motion artifacts.1 Conventional motion mitigation methods include patient immobilization2 or external tracking with fiducial markers.3 However, these approaches face limitations such as patient discomfort, increased procedural complexity, and registration errors. 2D–3D registration methods4 estimate motion by aligning reconstructed volumes or prior CT scans with 2D CBCT projections. While effective in certain cases, their performance heavily depends on the availability and quality of prior images. Unlike registration-based methods, autofocus-based motion compensation approaches5–7 require no prior scans or additional acquisitions. They formulate motion estimation as an optimization problem, iteratively searching for motion trajectories that optimize specific image quality metrics derived from the reconstructed volume. Recently, data-driven metrics parameterized by neural networks have gained increasing interest for their ability to guide motion correction adaptively.8–10 However, these methods often favor general image sharpness or consistency over anatomically accurate, motion-free reconstructions. An alternative strategy is joint reconstruction and motion estimation, which integrates motion estimation directly into the reconstruction process. These methods aim to simultaneously estimate motion from the measured projections and reconstruct a motion-compensated volume by minimizing the discrepancy between the forward projections of the estimated image and the acquired projection data.11–13
A critical step in the joint estimation problem is the computation of the iterative updates for the image volume and the motion parameters. The loss function measures the discrepancy between the estimated and acquired projections. Existing update methods typically rely on gradient-free algorithms,14 finite-difference approximations,11 manually derived analytical gradients,12 or differentiable interpolation operators.13 For example, Ouadah et al.14 employed the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES),15 a stochastic and gradient-free optimization method that needs to evaluate multiple candidate solutions per iteration, which leads to high computational cost. Sun et al.11 estimated gradients via finite differences, which are sensitive to perturbation size and can introduce numerical instability. Gradient-based optimization has the potential for improved convergence rates; however, this requires functional forms for computation of those gradients. Bousse et al.12 used Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (L-BFGS) optimization algorithm using analytically derived gradients, which can be computationally expensive for very large-scale problems due to the storage and the manipulation of gradient history. De Paepe et al.13 performed view-specific rigid transformations on image volume prior to forward projection. Since the transformation is implemented using a differentiable interpolation operator, the gradient of motion parameters can be automatically propagated through the entire computational graph via automatic differentiation. However, warping large volumes repeatedly can be computationally expensive and memory-intensive, which limits scalability to high-resolution reconstructions.
In this work, we propose a unified framework for the simultaneous optimization of image volume and rigid motion parameters. To address the limitations of existing optimization strategies, we adopt a gradient-based approach that balances both computational efficiency and gradient accuracy. Building on our previous work, which introduced a general framework for differentiable forward and back projectors, we leverage a differentiable forward projector to analytically compute the gradient of projections with respect to rigid motion in cone-beam geometries. We further demonstrate that minimizing the discrepancy between estimated and acquired projections serves as a sufficient objective for jointly recovering both image and motion parameters, without relying on image-based sharpness criteria. Simulation and phantom experiments validate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm in compensating for motion-induced artifacts.
2. METHODS
2.1. Joint Estimation of Motion and Volume
In the following, the 6 Degrees of Freedom (DoFs) of rigid motion are parameterized by θ=tx,ty,tz,γx,γy,γz, where tx,ty, and tz denote translations along the x-, y-, and z-axes, respectively, and γx,γy, and γz represent rotations about the corresponding axes. 2.2. Experimental Design
2.2.1. Simulation and phantom Study
Simulation Study:
A head phantom from the CQ500 dataset18 was used for the experiments. The phantom was resampled to a 160 × 160 × 128 3D volume with voxel spacing (1.444,1.444,1.172) mm. Motion-corrupted projections were simulated by forward-projecting the volume along a circular trajectory with 360 projections, using the CTorch toolbox.19 A flat-panel detector with 384 × 256 isotropic pixels of 1.0 mm was emulated. For each view, the six degrees of freedom (DoFs) of motion were added to the system geometry as perturbations. For comparison, motion-free projections were also simulated and reconstructed to serve as the ground truth.
Physical Phantom Study:
An anthropomorphic head phantom (The Phantom Laboratory, Greenwich, NY) was scanned on a CBCT test bench20 (Fig. 1) equipped with a flat-panel detector (Varex PaxScan 4343CB) and a six-DoF hexapod robot (PI H-900K Series). The X-ray tube operated with a tube voltage of 100 kVp, a tube current of 10 mA, and a pulse width of 10 ms. The raw projections form a 1536 × 1536 × 360 matrix, where 360 denotes the number of views, and the detector pixel spacing is 0.276 mm. These projections were subsequently downsampled to 384 × 384 × 360 with a pixel spacing of 1.114 mm. The reconstructed volume had dimensions of 256 × 256 × 290 with a voxel spacing of 1 mm.

2.2.2. Motion simulation and parameterization
Motion Simulation:
To better reflect real patient motion, we simulated motion with non-smooth, jerk-like characteristics in our experiments. The jerk motion was modeled using a combination of sine and sigmoid functions: J(i)=cos(ωi+ψ)+2⋅σ(τ⋅(i-i‾)). Here, i=n360×6 (s) denotes the continuous time index, ranging from 0 to 6 (second) over 360 equally spaced samples (n is the frame index). The parameter ω controls the frequency of the low-frequency motion and was set to 0.3π (rad/second) for about one cycle of motion per scan. ψ was set to 1.6π (rad), shifting the trajectory to ensure that the motion reaches its maximum speed at the midpoint of the scan. The sigmoid function σ(⋅) introduces a sharp jerk, with the steepness of the transition governed by τ, which was randomly sampled from the range [2, 11] (second−1) for each of the six DoFs. A temporal offset i‾=3 (seconds) was applied to center the steepest part of the sigmoid at the scan’s midpoint. Each motion trajectory for the six DoFs was subsequently normalized and scaled by a specified amplitude.
For simulation studies, the amplitude of translation is varied from 1 mm to 10 mm in increments of 1 mm, paired with a corresponding rotation amplitude varied from 0.02 rad to 0.2 rad in increments of 0.02 rad. This results in a total of 10 distinct amplitude settings. For the phantom study, a jerk motion with an amplitude of 5 mm for translation and 0.1 rad for rotation was applied to perturb the motion of the phantom.
Motion Parameterization:
To reduce the complexity of motion estimation, we represent each motion trajectory with a B-spline model21 defined by S control points, where S≪N. Instead of directly optimizing all 6N parameters across N frames, the optimization is carried out over the much smaller set of 6S parameters, substantially lowering the optimization burden while still allowing smooth and realistic motion trajectories to be captured. In our experiment, S is set to 20.
2.2.3. Evaluation metrics and experimental details
Evaluation Metrics:
In addition to a qualitative visual comparison between the motion-compensated images and the ground-truth images, we computed the structural similarity index (SSIM) and plotted the metrics as functions of the motion amplitude. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of in-plane motion tx,ty,γz and out-of-plane motion tz,γx,γy separately. To further validate the accuracy of the proposed method, the estimated motion parameters were visualized and compared with the corresponding ground-truth motion.
Experimental Details:
Experiments used 400 iterations of the AdamW optimizer16 with motion parameters initialized to zero. Learning rates were set to 1 × 10−3 for the volume u, 1 × 10−1 for the translation parameters tx,ty,tz, and 1 × 10−3 for the rotation parameters γx,γy,γz. Experiments were performed on a single NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU.
3. RESULTS
Fig. 2 displays the simulation results under varying motion amplitudes. The first row shows that increasing rotational and translational motion amplitudes lead to progressively more severe artifacts and structural distortions in the reconstructed images. The second row illustrates that the proposed method successfully restores anatomical details even under strong motion conditions, producing images that closely resemble the ground truth. The error maps in the third row further confirm the high fidelity of the compensation, with minimal residual errors for small to moderate motions and well-localized differences for larger motion amplitudes.

Fig. 3 compares ground-truth and estimated six-DoF motion trajectories across 360 views for two amplitude settings—mild (blue; 1 mm, 0.02 rad) and severe (orange; 10 mm, 0.2 rad). For both settings, estimates closely follow the ground truth in most parameters; however, larger deviations are observed for tx and ty. This behavior arises partly because an in-plane shift mostly causes a lateral detector shift that the model can mimic with small detector reparameterizations or slight yaw/roll. As a result, many different (tx and ty) trajectories produce almost the same projections, so the optimizer can remove motion artifacts while the parameter estimates remain biased.

Fig. 4 plots SSIM as a function of motion amplitude for three motion conditions (full, in-plane, out-of-plane), with and without compensation. Without correction, image quality degrades markedly with amplitude: for full motion, SSIM drops from 0.84 at (1 mm, 0.02 rad) to 0.49 at (10 mm, 0.20 rad), and the in-plane curve approaches the full-motion degradation at larger amplitudes. With the proposed compensation, in-plane motion is effectively neutralized, maintaining SSIM ≈ 0.84 across all amplitudes. For out-of-plane and full motion, the method recovers most of the loss, raising SSIM by 12%, 28%, and 36% for out-of-plane motion and 18%, 37%, and 48% for full motion at (3 mm, 0.06 rad), (6 mm, 0.12 rad), and (9 mm, 0.18 rad), respectively, with a small residual gap attributable to depth-dependent effects.

After applying the proposed motion compensation method, the corrected images recover sharp edges and anatomical continuity, closely matching the ground-truth references. The enlarged regions further highlight that small structures, such as bone boundaries, fine soft-tissue interfaces, as well as the markers embedded in the phantom, are effectively restored by the compensation process.

4. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented a unified framework for joint image reconstruction and motion estimation in cone-beam computed tomography. By leveraging a differentiable forward projector and an analytically derived gradient with respect to rigid motion, the proposed method enables efficient and accurate optimization of both image volume and motion parameters. Simulation and phantom experiments demonstrate that the method significantly mitigates motion-induced artifacts and restores anatomical structures, even under challenging motion scenarios. Quantitative evaluations show substantial improvements in SSIM, confirming the high fidelity of the reconstructed images. Physical bench data further demonstrate the ability to mitigate motion artifacts without the need for hand-crafted measures of image sharpness.
