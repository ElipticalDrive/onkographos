How Do Rendering Optimization Techniques Balance Visual Fidelity,
Computational Performance, and Educational Usability in Virtual Reality

Thom luis
Czech University of Life Sciences Prague
Abstract
Background: Immersive virtual reality (VR) demands sustained stereoscopic rendering at high frame rates with low motion-to-photon latency,
yet modern content exceeds the GPU, CPU, memory, and thermal budgets of standalone headsets and classroom deployments. Objective: This
systematic review examines how rendering optimization techniques—level-of-detail (LOD), adaptive and dynamic LOD, dynamic resolution,
foveated and gaze-contingent rendering, visibility culling, workload adaptation, and hardware-aware policies—trade visual fidelity against
computational performance and practical educational usability. Methods: Following PRISMA 2020 [1], English-language records were
retrieved on 18 August 2026 from OpenAlex and Crossref (7822 database records; 6257 unique after deduplication). Title/abstract screening,
full-text eligibility assessment, Crossref bibliographic verification, and citation snowballing yielded 146 primary studies and six background
surveys. Findings: Foveated rendering consistently reduces pixel shading cost when eye tracking latency remains below approximately 50–70
ms [2], but aggressive peripheral degradation remains visible for high-contrast geometry unless combined with dynamic resolution or LOD
policies [3] [4]. Standalone and cloud-edge systems shift optimization toward split rendering, predictive pre-rendering, and adaptive bitrate
streaming rather than raw resolution [5] [6]. Educational VR seldom reports frame-time stability or GPU headroom as outcomes; where
reported, stable ≥90 Hz operation and moderate LOD correlate with usable instruction more strongly than maximal visual realism [7] [8]. Gaps:
Cross-device benchmarks, standardized latency–fidelity reporting, reproducible adaptive policies, and education-specific validation remain
sparse. Contribution: A literature-derived taxonomy, comparative synthesis, temporal analysis (2022–2026 emphasis), and evidence-based
design guidance for VR rendering in learning environments.

I. INTRODUCTION
Stereoscopic head-mounted displays (HMDs) impose a
coupled constraint: each frame must be rendered twice (or via
multiview extensions), projected with low distortion, and
displayed at refresh rates commonly targeting 72–120 Hz to
limit vection and discomfort [9]. As scene complexity,
material fidelity, and sensor pipelines (eye tracking,
passthrough, hand tracking) expand, unoptimized pipelines
exhaust GPU fill rate, CPU submission time, and memory
bandwidth—particularly on standalone System-on-Chip
(SoC) platforms used in classrooms and training labs.
LOD techniques reduce geometric complexity using
discrete mesh resolutions, progressive meshes, or impostors,
selected by distance, screen-space error, or saliency.
Adaptive/dynamic LOD adjusts thresholds at runtime based
on frame budget or gaze [3]. Dynamic resolution (render
scale) modulates eye-buffer resolution per frame; on
standalone platforms it interacts with fixed foveated rendering
(FFR) and eye-tracked foveated rendering (ETFR). Foveated
rendering reduces shading rate in the periphery via fixed
radial masks (FFR) or gaze-aligned ETFR [13] [15]. A
standardized classification of foveated displays distinguishes
these operating modes from hardware-level foveation and
from gaze-contingent optics [16]. Kernel foveated rendering
maps the eccentricity falloff into a continuous kernel rather
than a small set of resolution islands, which reduces boundary
artifacts when the gaze moves [17]. The same kernel
formulation was later specialized to eye-dominance-guided
stereo foveation [18] and to 3D kernels for light-field HMDs
[19]. Foundational gaze-contingent work had already coupled
peripheral LOD to measured visual sensitivity [20] [21].
Visibility and occlusion culling limit submitted geometry;
hybrid pipelines combine foveated path tracing, denoising,
and radiance-field culling [22] [23].
Rendering optimization in VR is not a single algorithm but
a portfolio of techniques that reallocate detail where it matters
perceptually or operationally. Foundational work on level-ofdetail (LOD) in VR identified early that multiresolution
models and switching thresholds must account for head
motion and stereopsis [10] [11]. Foveated rendering exploits
the falloff of acuity in the periphery [12] [13], while dynamic
resolution scales render targets to preserve frame time under
load. Recent surveys synthesize foveated, stereo, cloud, and
low-power strategies as complementary rather than
competing axes [3].
Educational and training applications add a third objective
beyond fidelity and throughput: educational usability—the
practical suitability of a VR system for sustained instruction,
including readable text, stable interaction, predictable update
latency, and deployability on institutional hardware. Prior
educational VR reviews emphasize learning outcomes and
presence [7] but rarely isolate rendering choices as
independent variables. Concurrently, graphics systems
literature reports performance gains without classroom
validation. This disconnect constitutes the literature gap
addressed here.
Hardware tiers structure feasible strategies: PC-tethered
VR permits higher baseline resolution and multiview
extensions; standalone/mobile VR prioritizes thermal-stable
frame time; cloud/edge VR introduces network latency and
encoding constraints [5] [6]. On tightly budgeted HMDs, nonrendering subsystems further reduce the compute available
for shading: a minimal-hardware VR measurement platform
showed that real-time behavioral sensing can be sustained
only when the remainder of the pipeline is kept deliberately
lean [24]. Educational usability requires that optimization
preserves text legibility, stable interaction metaphors, and
sufficient visual cues for learning tasks—not merely
acceptable presence [8] [25].
This review contributes: (1) a reproducible PRISMAcompliant search and screening protocol focused on technical
rendering trade-offs; (2) a taxonomy derived from 146
primary studies; (3) structured comparative analysis across
hardware tiers and metrics; (4) a temporal synthesis
contrasting foundational foveation/LOD work with 2022–
2026 standalone, eye-tracked, and cloud-edge research; (5) a
technical analysis of educational VR deployments where
rendering performance is measured or implied; and (6) an
evidence-based research agenda linking rendering policy to
deployability. Applied VR systems evidence is used only
where it illuminates real-time resource constraints, sensing–
compute contention, or adaptive control; it is not treated as
direct evidence of foveation, LOD, or dynamic resolution
efficacy.

explicitly label LOD relative to foveation, geometry
simplification remains essential for authored educational

independent knobs [62].
Standalone and mobile constraints: Neural light-field
acceleration with voxel-grid optimization targets standalone
XR [63]; CAD-in-standalone-VR studies emphasize CPUside modeling limits separate from GPU shading [64].
Minimalistic display comfort interventions highlight that
optimization must preserve comfort constraints, not only

VI. EVOLUTION OF THE FIELD
Fig. 3 plots publication counts by year. Foundational
period (1995–2016): LOD surveys and gaze-contingent
multiresolution displays established that VR requires
different switching rules than desktop fly-throughs [10] [67].
Guenter et al. [12] and Patney et al. [13] demonstrated
practical foveated pipelines for HMDs, while Albert et al. [2]
quantified latency tolerance. Kernel foveated rendering [17]
and time-warped foveation [14] then closed two remaining
gaps: spatially smooth eccentricity falloff and compensation
for head motion between tracking and photon. Earlier gazecontingent display work measured how late a foveal update
can occur without detection [68], and perceptually based
foveation was later examined in relation to motion sickness
[69]. Gaze-prediction models such as FixationNet [70] and
high-frequency pupil tracking [71] subsequently reduced the
sensing burden of ETFR.

VII. EDUCATIONAL VR ANALYSIS
Among educational studies, rendering optimization is
rarely the independent variable. Xie et al. [7] review IVR
science education outcomes without isolating frame-time
policies. Earlier controlled studies did, however, vary display
type and interaction fidelity and then measure learning and
presence [79] [80], which is closer to a rendering-relevant
manipulation than most subsequent classroom deployments.
Cognitive-complexity gaming work [8] manipulates task
demand and game level—a proxy for scene complexity—
showing performance–load interactions relevant to rendering
budgets. Assembly serious games [25] assume real-time
interaction but seldom publish GPU headroom or adaptive
policies.

milestones.
Where visual fidelity is discussed, metaverse memory
studies suggest fidelity affects recall [81], implying
educational consequences of aggressive foveation if text or
fine cues degrade. Intelligent tutoring architectures [82] and
collaborative design-review environments [83] likewise treat
real-time interaction as a prerequisite, yet they rarely publish
frame-time distributions or GPU headroom. The practical
implication is that educational usability is often assumed from
successful task completion rather than measured as a
rendering-constrained property of the system.
Transition period (2017–2021): integrative reviews
connected eye tracking, foveation, and comfort [72] [73]
without yet standardizing GPU metrics for educational
deployments. Recent period (2022–2026): standalone SoCs,
ETFR, neural denoising, cloud split rendering, and adaptive
streaming dominate [3] [5] [74] [63]. Applied VR
instrumentation over the same interval has also become more
resource-aware: temperature-adjusted electrodermal sensing
was designed specifically to operate inside an HMD session
without assuming a dedicated high-end laboratory stack [75].

Cross-cutting observation: studies that report only mean
FPS without frame-time variance understate VR risk; the
balancing FPS/latency/comfort analysis [4] and multi-user
latency QoE work [26] motivate reporting percentile frame
times (p95/p99) and motion-to-photon latency as standard
supplementary metrics.
Reproducibility threat: few included papers publish scene
assets, foveation maps, or eye-tracking calibration
parameters; surveys [3] [15] call for open benchmarks, but
implementation diversity (Unity, Unreal, custom engines)
remains a barrier to direct numerical comparison.
Technical implication: educational usability should be
operationalized as measurable constraints—minimum
sustained frame rate, maximum frame-time variance, text
height in pixels, and foveation level—reported alongside
learning outcomes, mirroring how latency studies treat QoE
[26].
IX. DISCUSSION
Evidence supports a three-way trade-off among fidelity,
computational cost, and educational usability rather than a
single Pareto surface. High-fidelity PC pipelines [32]
maximize visual quality but are misaligned with institutional
standalone labs. Aggressive foveation [13] [35] preserves
FPS yet can degrade instructional text and peripheral cues
needed for spatial learning tasks [81]. Cloud-edge rendering
[5] decouples client GPU from scene complexity but
introduces latency that educational interactions may not
tolerate [33]. The resulting design rule is conservative on
standalone class hardware: keep auxiliary sensing and
interaction code paths within a documented CPU budget so
that foveation and render-scale controllers retain headroom
[24] [4].

queries), OpenAlex query caps, English-only restriction, and
heterogeneous metrics limit quantitative meta-analysis. Fulltext availability constraints required abstract-based eligibility
for some records. Author classification of educational
usability relies on explicit reporting and may undercount
implicit assumptions.
XI. CONCLUSION
Rendering optimization in VR balances visual fidelity,
computational performance, and educational usability
through composable techniques—foveation, LOD, dynamic
resolution, culling, and adaptive workload policies—whose
effectiveness is hardware- and latency-dependent. The 2022–
2026 literature emphasizes gaze-aware and cloud-assisted
pipelines, yet educational VR rarely validates optimization
choices against learning tasks. Future work should prioritize
standardized, reproducible benchmarks linking frame-time
stability, perceptual quality, and instructional effectiveness
across standalone and institutional deployments.
