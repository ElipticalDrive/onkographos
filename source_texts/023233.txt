Abstract
Abstract
Traditional 3D representation models, such as 3D meshes and 3D point clouds, provide accurate geometric measurements but lack the visual fidelity of modern novel view synthesis approaches. In contrast, recent differentiable rendering methods, such as Plenoxels and Gaussian Splatting, enable photorealistic real-time visualization but do not support common geometric measurement operations. This work builds on Sparse Voxel Rasterization (SVR), a differentiable rendering method that combines the visual fidelity of Gaussian Splatting with an adaptive voxel representation. Our first contribution is VolSVR, a volume-preserving modification of SVR training that retains voxels inside objects while maintaining accurate scene representations. Our second contribution is WebSVR, a browser-based SVR viewer for interactive visualization and volume measurement. Together, these contributions form a semi-automated workflow for volume measurement in real-time rendered scenes with high visual fidelity. The proposed method requires only 100–200 input images, does not increase SVR runtime, and achieves volume estimates with an approximately 17% mean absolute error. The approach is robust to object occlusion and is scale-agnostic, supporting measurements ranging from hundreds of milliliters to thousands of cubic meters.

1 Introduction
High-fidelity 3D scene representations generated from images have recently become practical through novel view synthesis (NVS) techniques such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [Mildenhall et al. 2021] and Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [Kerbl et al. 2023]. These approaches can reconstruct realistic, real-time renderable scenes from image data alone, making them attractive for visualization, inspection, and analysis tasks. However, when using these models for geometric measurement tasks such as volume measurement, a separate model is needed. While high-quality visualizations can be generated using NVS methods, volume estimation is currently performed using independently computed point cloud, mesh, or voxel representations, which as of now require additional processing.
In this paper, we present WebSVR, a unified workflow that combines real-time visualization and volume measurement in a single 3D representation (fig. 1, fig. 2). It builds on Sparse Voxel Rasterization (SVR) [Sun et al. 2025], a 3DGS-adjacent NVS technique using voxels as display primitives. WebSVR implements an improved SVR loading and visualization process in a web-based environment. To enable volume measurement, SVR is replaced by an extended version named VolSVR, which extends the SVR training process to preserve occluded voxels throughout the Adaptive Density Control (ADC) step while maintaining visual quality, interactive rendering performance, and full file compatibility with the original method. The WebSVR browser-based workspace enables exploration of realistically rendered scenes, comparison of temporal captures, and interactive volume estimation directly within the reconstructed environment. By integrating visualization and measurement into a single training pipeline, the proposed approach eliminates the need for separate reconstruction workflows. This enables volume estimation of solid materials, e. g., found at stockpiles or excavation sites, as well as remote monitoring and change detection, while requiring only image data and a reference scale.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. section 2 reviews related work in novel view synthesis and volume measurement. section 3 introduces the proposed VolSVR training modification and volume estimation approach. section 4 presents the WebSVR visualization system and browser-based interaction workflow. section 5 evaluates visual fidelity, measurement accuracy, and performance across multiple datasets. section 6 concludes the paper and discusses future work. Our main contributions are:
•
VolSVR, a drop-in replacement for SVR that preserves occluded voxels, thereby providing a consistent reconstruction workflow that simultaneously produces a visualization-ready and measurement-ready scene representation.
•
WebSVR, a browser-based system for real-time visualization, change detection, and volume measurement of SVR scenes.
•
An efficient machine learning (ML) model for inferring voxel fill level from its 3D density field representation.
2 Related Work
Our work combines concepts and ideas from NVS and optical volume measurement, which have largely been investigated independently in the literature. To identify relevant work across both domains, a systematic literature review was conducted. The reviewed literature reveals extensive advances in NVS representations and reconstruction methods, numerous optical approaches for volume estimation, but only very limited efforts to jointly address visualization and measurement within a unified representation.
2.1 Novel View Synthesis
Recent NVS research is dominated by three representation paradigms: Neural Radiance Fields, voxel-based methods, and Gaussian Splatting. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [Mildenhall et al. 2021] introduced implicit neural scene representations capable of producing highly realistic novel views. Subsequent work improved training and rendering performance [Han et al. 2023; Kato and Tarashima 2024; Kurz et al. 2022; Müller et al. 2022; Reiser et al. 2021], enabled sparse-view reconstruction [González et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024], reduced dependence on camera calibration [Wang et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024], and extended NeRF with additional capabilities such as anti-aliasing, dynamic scenes, and inverse rendering [Barron et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2022]. Despite their visual quality, NeRF representations remain implicit and are therefore less suitable for direct geometric analysis. Voxel-based approaches provide explicit scene representations. Plenoxels [Sara Fridovich-Keil and Alex Yu et al. 2022] demonstrated that high-quality differentiable rendering can be achieved without neural networks, motivating a wide range of follow-up work [Aroudj et al. 2022; Bao et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2020; Momeni et al. 2024; Schwarz et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2022; 2023; Toussaint et al. 2025]. Explicit voxel structures naturally support geometric queries and volume analysis while maintaining competitive rendering quality. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [Kerbl et al. 2023] represents scenes as collections of anisotropic Gaussians and achieves high rendering quality at interactive frame rates. Subsequent research improved geometric consistency [Guédon and Lepetit 2023; Huang et al. 2024b; Paliwal et al. 2024; Song et al. 2024], rendering performance [Chen et al. 2024; Niemeyer et al. 2025; Yu et al. 2024], compression [Huang et al. 2024a; Lee et al. 2023], and camera self-calibration [Fan et al. 2025; Foroutan et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2023; Jang and Pérez-Pellitero 2025; Ji and Yao 2025; Sun et al. 2024]. While Gaussian splatting offers excellent visualization performance, extracting volumetric information from unordered Gaussians typically requires additional processing or conversion into alternative geometric representations. However, the adaptive density control (ADC) method developed for Gaussian splatting and its tile-based software rendering naturally lend themselves to supporting alternative display primitives apart from Gaussians. Among these methods, Sparse Voxel Rasterization (SVR) [Sun et al. 2025] achieves real-time rendering performance and state-of-the-art image quality, all while being the first technique to employ grid-aligned voxels as display primitives requiring a novel Morton-order sorting strategy in its software rasterization. Overall, voxel-based representations are particularly attractive for applications that require both realistic rendering and direct access to scene geometry, motivating the use of SVR as the foundation of the proposed approach.
2.2 Optical Volume Measurement
Optical volume measurement methods can generally be divided into laser-based, depth-based, and photogrammetric approaches. Laser and LiDAR systems enable volume estimation from dense depth measurements and are widely used for stockpiles, terrain analysis, and object reconstruction [Alsayed and Nabawy 2023; Ding et al. 2023; Hou et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2022; Yao 2021; Zhongyi et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2018]. Related approaches employ structured light or projected laser patterns to recover object geometry [Luan et al. 2024; Peng et al. 2019]. These methods provide highly accurate depth information but typically require specialized hardware and do not directly support photorealistic scene visualization. Photogrammetric approaches estimate geometry from multiple images and are therefore closely aligned with NVS pipelines. Existing work reconstructs objects using voxel models [Cao and Neilsen 2020], geometric primitives [Omid et al. 2010], or triangle meshes [Brzeziński et al. 2022; Farina et al. 2024; Yusoff et al. 2020]. Because both photogrammetry and NVS operate on image collections, they offer the potential to share acquisition data and reconstruction workflows.
2.3 Joint Visualization and Volume Measurement
Only limited work addresses visualization and volume measurement simultaneously. Most existing approaches treat volume estimation as a downstream task performed on reconstructed meshes, point clouds, or other geometry generated for separate purposes [Cao and Neilsen 2020; Yao 2021; Zhu et al. 2024]. Consequently, visualization and measurement are typically optimized independently and often require different processing pipelines. A first work in the field of combining NVS and measurement into the same workflow is the work of Baar et al. [Baar et al. 2024], which combines 3D Gaussian Splatting with geometric measurements for tomato leaf size estimation. However, their focus is on deriving distance measurements and not volume measurements. Despite this, their approach clearly shows the additional accuracy achievable from using a high-fidelity NVS representation rather than a LiDAR-generated point cloud. Joint visualization and volume measurement within the same NVS workflow seems a promising strategy, which is why in this work the proposed VolSVR framework extends SVR such that a single training process produces a representation that is simultaneously visualization-ready and volume measurement-ready. This enables real-time NVS and direct volume estimation without requiring separate reconstruction, conversion, or post-processing stages.
3 Volume Measurement Approach
By providing a grid-aligned voxel data structure, SVR enables volume measurement using the following formula, which only requires selecting a set of voxels and then adding their weighted volumes together.
volume=∑#voxelsvoxel extent3⋅voxel fill level
(1)
Here voxel extent is an arbitrary multiple of the smallest voxel grid size and the fill level is a factor between zero and one. However, measuring volumes inside a SVR scene representation like this poses two challenges: occluded voxel preservation and fill-level computation.
3.1 Object Filling
VolSVR extends SVR with the ability to preserve the interior volume of opaque objects and occluded area behind solid surfaces. This requires augmenting the ADC algorithm step within, which controls the voxel pruning and splitting every few training iterations. The three modifications are: transmittance-based interior voxel detection, propagation of interior voxel masks through ADC, and voxel filling.
Interior Voxel Detection.
Pruning and keeping decisions for a randomly generated 2D grid of leaf node squares.
SVR determines the relevance of voxels, i.e., how likely they are to be pruned, using the maximum observed importance weight during training. The importance weight is computed as the product of a voxel’s opacity contribution and the accumulated transmittance along the viewing ray. This leads to very transparent and very occluded voxels being pruned every few training iterations. We are only interested in preserving very occluded voxels. To identify such voxels, VolSVR additionally tracks the maximum transmittance value Tmax observed for each voxel over all training views. Surface voxels typically retain high Tmax values because at least one viewing ray intersects them before encountering significant occlusion. In contrast, voxels within opaque geometry remain heavily occluded from all viewpoints and therefore exhibit low Tmax values. Voxels whose maximum transmittance falls below a predefined keep threshold are classified as interior voxels and excluded from subsequent ADC operations. A 2D example of this algorithm step is depicted in fig. 3, where an marks the default SVR pruning behavior and a shows low Tmax targets which need to be preserved.
Keep-Mask Propagation.
Keep mask propagation through the pruning and subdivision process. Both pruning and subdivision are prevented by performing a NAND operation and then updating the keep mask.
The keep threshold classification produces a binary keep mask that identifies voxels which should be retained regardless of their visual contribution. This mask must remain consistent throughout ADC operations as shown in fig. 4. During pruning, the keep mask overrides pruning decisions and prevents interior voxels from being removed. This is achieved by performing a NAND operation with the existing prune mask. After pruning, the mask is updated to reflect the reduced voxel set. During voxel subdivision, kept voxels are excluded from splitting since additional subdivision of interior regions would increase memory consumption and rendering cost without improving reconstruction or volume measurement quality. This step now uses the pruned keep mask for the NAND operation. For each subdivided voxel, its eight sub-voxels inherit the non-kept state, ensuring that the keep mask remains valid throughout training. This modification and propagation mechanism preserves interior voxels while maintaining the original ADC behavior for visually relevant surface regions.
Voxel Filling. To satisfy eq. (1), the correct fill level value must be assigned to all occluded voxels obtained in the previous step. Since VolSVR assumes that opaque objects are solid, which is consistent with other optical volume measurement approaches [Alsayed and Nabawy 2023; Brzeziński et al. 2022; Cao and Neilsen 2020], these inner voxels need to have a fill level of 100%. The fill level is evaluated directly from voxel opacity. Thus, a fully filled voxel is completely opaque. In SVR, opacity is represented through voxel corner densities that define a continuous volumetric density field. Therefore, all preserved interior voxels are assigned large density values every few ADC iterations starting in the middle of the total training process. These large density values result in fully opaque and consequently fully filled voxels, ready for volume measurement. The resulting representation preserves the surface quality of SVR while additionally retaining the interior structure required to enable volume estimation. Because the modification operates directly within the SVR training workflow, the same reconstructed voxel representation can be used both for real-time visualization and for quantitative volume measurement.
3.2 Fill-Level Computation
Density field of an exemplary SVR voxel and its fill level value of 68.3 %.
While the geometric volume of a voxel is directly available from its dimensions, determining its fill level is more challenging. In the SVR model format, each voxel contains eight corner density values that define a continuous volumetric field through trilinear interpolation. A rendered view of an exemplary density field can be seen in fig. 5. During rendering, this field is evaluated using one to four ray-marching steps to compute opacity values. To compute a fill level, the entire density within the voxel domain needs to be taken into account. Formally, this can be expressed as a volume integral over the unit cube,

Together, negexp and explin result in a sigmoid-like function that maps high corner values v→geo to one and small or even negative corner values to zero. Direct numerical evaluation of this integral is computationally expensive because it must be performed for potentially millions of voxels during interactive measurements. Instead, in VolSVR the mapping from voxel corner densities to voxel fill level is modeled using a compact feed-forward neural network that can be evaluated efficiently on the GPU.
Volume MLP. The voxel fill estimation problem can be formulated as a regression task. Given the eight corner density values of a voxel, the objective is to predict its fill level as defined by the volume integral above (eq. (2)). Because the relationship is continuous and deterministic, a compact multilayer perceptron (MLP) can approximate this mapping. The proposed network consists of an eight-dimensional input layer, two hidden layers with 128 neurons each, and a single output neuron. ReLU activations are used between hidden layers, while a sigmoid activation constrains the final prediction to the valid range of [0,1]. The resulting model contains only a small number of parameters and can be evaluated efficiently during interactive volume measurements. Training data is generated offline by computing reference fill levels for randomly sampled corner configurations. These are then processed by a GPU-accelerated numeric integration solver, which accumulates 5123 ≈ 134 × 106 samples. This process is performed two million times to create a large dataset of ground-truth examples. To ensure robust behavior for voxels generated by the filling procedure described in section 3.1, the dataset contains two million additional examples with large corner values. The network is trained using mean squared error loss and the Adam optimizer [Ansel et al. 2024]. Once trained, the model provides an efficient approximation of the voxel fill level that can be evaluated directly on the GPU. This enables interactive volume measurements while maintaining the continuous occupancy representation used for rendering. Together, this workflow enables volume measurement in the same training system that already provides data for real-time high-fidelity NVS, without altering the visual results.
4 Web Visualization
Screenshot of the WebSVR workspace. The user interface consists of three panels: the viewer (top left), the controls (top right), and the timeline (bottom).
Visual comparison between the VolSVR rendered output and the ground truth on test views, including its PSNR score for real and synthetic models.
To make VolSVR models accessible for interactive analysis, we present WebSVR, a browser-based visualization and volume measurement system (fig. 6). WebSVR serves as the final stage of the workflow, loading trained SVR or VolSVR models and providing a unified environment for visualization, volume measurement, change detection, and animation. The system follows a client-server architecture consisting of a lightweight Flask 3.0 backend and a JavaScript frontend. The backend is responsible for loading trained model checkpoints and exposing model metadata through a web interface, while all rendering and interaction are performed directly in the browser using WebGPU. Model-specific information, including timeline data and visualization parameters, is stored in accompanying configuration files, allowing multiple reconstructions to be organized and analyzed within a common workspace. The user interface combines a 3D-viewport with controls for real-time visualization, volume measurement, and temporal comparison. Users can inspect voxel models from arbitrary viewpoints, define measurement regions, adjust the scene scale, and compare volume measurements across multiple time steps. Since WebSVR operates directly on the voxel representation produced during training, no additional reconstruction or mesh generation stage is required.
4.1 Voxel Rendering
WebSVR renders the voxel model directly in the browser using hardware rasterization. This is in contrast to SVR, which uses differentiable software rasterization to generate the images. Without the need for derivability, graphics performance can be regained by utilizing the graphics hardware for vertex and fragment processing. Two rendering modes are supported. The sorted rendering mode matches SVR’s approach, where visible voxels are partially duplicated and then sorted according to Morton ordering before rasterization. To efficiently sort the 64 bit keys, we implemented radix sort in a WebGPU compute shader. This is called before rasterization but after frustum culling in order to avoid sorting out-of-frame voxels. While this approach produces high-quality results, sorting introduces a computational overhead that becomes increasingly significant for large voxel sets. To improve scalability, WebSVR additionally implements an order-independent transparency (OIT) render mode. Instead of explicitly sorting voxels, visibility and transparency contributions are accumulated in screen space using screen-door blending. This approach preserves the volumetric appearance of the scene, enabling interactive rendering of larger reconstructions (60+ FPS for 1.5 million voxels). The availability of both rendering modes allows users to balance visual fidelity and rendering performance depending on the application. Rasterization itself is done analogously to SVR, where opacity and colors are computed using ray marching within the density field and spherical harmonics evaluation, respectively. For the sorted rendering mode, the opacity value is used for back-to-front hardware alpha blending. The OIT mode instead uses the alpha value to choose the appropriate Bayer dither pattern [Mulder et al. 1998] for the fragment, either using it at full opacity or discarding it entirely.
4.2 Bounding Box Selection
With WebSVR, volume measurements are performed within user-defined oriented bounding boxes. This mode of selection is comparably straight forward to implement, compared to object or natural language selection [Sun et al. 2025], and is versatile enough to select more complex structures successfully. WebSVR provides multiple independently transformable bounding boxes that can be translated, rotated, and scaled within the scene. The number of bounding boxes is currently limited to three for design reasons alone. When the volume measurement is started, all bounding boxes are separately evaluated. All voxels are processed in parallel, each checking if its center position intersects the bounding box within the bounding box reference frame. If this check succeeds, the corner value and extent information are collected atomically for further processing. Otherwise, the voxel is ignored. As a result, the user can measure three different scene objects simultaneously and across all loaded scenes, which enables temporal volume comparison.
Bounding boxes are rendered as standard wireframe objects, including writing the depth buffer. For better visibility, a post-processing grow kernel is applied, increasing the single-pixel lines to three pixels in width. To highlight the currently selected voxels more clearly, all voxels within the chosen bounding box are marked and highlighted in the same color as the bounding box.
4.3 Volume Measurement
With all voxels selected and their corner value information collected, the data can be passed to the volume MLP model to compute the fill levels as required by eq. (1). The machine learning model is powered by the ONNX Runtime [ONNX Runtime developers 2021], a language agnostic hardware accelerated machine learning library. It uses WebGPU to process all selected voxels in parallel and infer all necessary fill level factors. In a final sequential pass, the CPU computes the volume sum (eq. (1)), returning the object’s volume as a unitless measurement.
The photogrammetric process employed in creating the voxel model does at no point require, estimate, or store the real metric scale of the scene. To convert the unitless volume estimate computed in the last step into a metric measurement, a scene scale and unit are required. This can be configured by scaling a bounding box to the size of a known distance within the scene, which can then be entered in the scale and unit form fields in the control panel. With this setup, all subsequent volume measurements will have the correct scaling factor and unit applied.
In addition, any measurement can be designated as a reference volume, allowing measurements earlier and later in the timeline to be expressed relative to this common baseline. This functionality supports common scenarios such as material accumulation, excavation progress, or container filling, while preserving the interactive workflow of the visualization system.
5 Results and Evaluation
In this section, we evaluate the proposed VolSVR-to-WebSVR workflow. We first assess the accuracy of the fill level prediction model used for volume estimation, followed by an ablation study of the keep-threshold parameter introduced by VolSVR. Finally, we evaluate end-to-end volume measurement performance on synthetic and real-world datasets. All reported runtimes are for an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2070 Super graphics card.
5.1 Volume Integration Approaches
Before selecting the MLP-based fill level estimation, we explored several more direct approaches to solving the three-dimensional integral in eq. (2). The goals for the solution are high runtime performance, accuracy, space efficiency, and parallel processing capabilities. Solving the integral accurately is possible using numerical integration by evaluating the function to integrate at many sample points within each voxel. This approach also requires little memory and is embarrassingly parallel but is not efficient enough (6 ms per voxel) to be used for more than an individual voxel with sufficient accuracy. As a second direct approach, we investigated analytic integration. For the one-dimensional version of the problem a closed-form solution was derived. However, extending this derivation to the full three-dimensional integral proved intractable due to the nonlinear transformations involved. Thus, no closed-form solution was found for the three-dimensional case, nor did interpolations of one-dimensional solutions lead to accurate results. A third direct method for computing the volume integral was approximating it using a model. The simplest model is an interpolated lookup table (LUT). Such tables provide extremely fast evaluation by interpolating precomputed reference values. However, the dimensionality of the problem scales with the dimensionality of the input, which consists of eight voxel corner values, causing memory requirements to grow exponentially (16 floating point samples per dimension requires around 13 GB of LUT). The resulting storage costs become prohibitive for web-based applications and even exceed the size of the reconstruction models themselves. The final solution was modeling the volume integral using machine learning, which provides a compromise between the stated goals. Although it introduces a small approximation error, it remains compact (90 kB), GPU-friendly, and sufficiently fast for interactive volume measurement (20 μs per voxel). As a result, it was selected as the final fill level estimation method used in WebSVR.

Error for a fully filled voxel for different MLP setup choices.
For fill level inference, we chose a small four-layer MLP model. The eight-parameter input layer, two 128-parameter hidden layers, and single-parameter output layer are each connected via ReLU activation operations. To improve numerical stability for extrapolation, the output layer result is fed through a sigmoid operation, which reduces the output value range to the desired zero-to-one interval. This model sits in the middle of a larger tested model pool we evaluated using our numerically generated training dataset. An important model feature is its ability to predict the fill level of fully opaque voxels, like the ones generated by voxel filling (section 3.1). An overview of the tested layer configurations is shown in fig. 8. The highlighted model is the one used for all volume measurement evaluations, and its error of 3% is one of the reasons the presented volume measurements are mostly underestimations. We still chose this model over its competitors since it is fast to train and evaluate even for a batch size of one million items.
5.3 VolSVR Evaluation
We propose VolSVR as a drop-in replacement for SVR, adding volume measurement capabilities to the resulting voxel models, without altering the input types, parameters, training workflow and output file format. Nevertheless, a noticeable difference in visual quality can be observed after training for the SVR default of 10,000 iterations with a single voxel filling operation just before saving. This difference is reduced by training longer with additional voxel filling steps in between. The results in fig. 7 were computed with 20,000 training iterations with two voxel filling steps at 15,000 and 20,000 steps. This yields comparable visual quality, with a PSNR score of 30.4 dB on average for real scenes and 44.6 dB on average for synthetic objects. From our testing, fully equivalent results to SVR can be achieved with 30.000 training iterations and three voxel filling steps. Storage-wise, the model files produced by VolSVR are about 20% larger than SVR models of the same scene because the additional occluded voxels have to store fill spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients for direction-dependent colors.
5.4 Synthetic Volume Measurement
To test the volume measurement accuracy, a dataset of synthetic models was created using virtual cameras in Blender [Community 2025]. The captured objects were chosen for their distinct topological and surface properties (e.g., concave, convex, holes) as well as for their challenging properties (e.g., cutouts, occlusion, thin geometry) for the reconstruction algorithm. Every model was rendered from 200 views, storing both camera intrinsics and extrinsics to provide exact ground truth camera position and orientation data. Ground-truth volumes were computed analytically, except for the Stanford Bunny, for which the volume was approximated using the 3D-Print-Toolbox [Barton 2025] – a Blender plugin for 3D printing that provides filament volume estimates. Within WebSVR, the entire object was always selected and measured without configuring a specific scene scale or unit, as SVR and VolSVR both retain the scene scale provided by the input camera positions.

Additionally, the cube-with-cutout example highlights a limitation of this photogrammetry-based volume measurement approach (red highlight in table 1). While the overall volume estimate is accurate, the individual volumes of the cube and the cutout are not. The cube volume is underestimated, whereas the cutout volume is nonzero. Together, these errors partially cancel each other out, resulting in an improved overall score. This effect originates from incomplete visual evidence inside the cutout and shows how reconstruction ambiguity can propagate into volume measurements.

To evaluate the workflow under practical conditions, we captured a collection of real-world objects using a Samsung S23 smartphone camera and reconstructed them using our presented method. The dataset spans several orders of magnitude in scale, ranging from small household objects to a large outdoor coal stockpile. The latter was filmed using a UAV and uploaded to YouTube [haller concepts st louis video production 2024].

Results for real-world scenes are summarized in table 2. The average error of 17% is higher than for synthetic datasets, reflecting the additional challenges introduced by imperfect registration, reconstruction ambiguities, inaccurate scene scale, insufficient scene coverage, and uncertainty in the ground-truth volume measurements. A ground-truth volume for all scenes was determined using three different means: geometry, submersion, and a beaker volume readout. The geometry method combines several distance measurements in the scene to build a model from geometric primitives that capture the shape of the object and provide an analytical volume estimate. For example, the styrofoam sphere was modeled as a sphere with a 5 cm diameter and the Lego® torus as a collection of 18 bricks. The submersion method uses the displacement of water within a beaker after an object is submerged to measure its volume. This is used for the lemon and banana, which are both hard to model accurately from geometric primitives. Finally, the beaker method uses the beaker directly to portion a quantity of sand of a target volume.
To improve image registration and scene scale estimation, a contrast-rich DIN A6 postcard is included in most captures. This provides additional stable image features for registration and a well-defined distance measurement along the edge of the card. Furthermore, it provides a flat ground plane for software like RealityScan (formerly RealityCapture) [Games 2025] to align its up vector. This measure noticeably improved reconstruction quality and allows for consistent volume measurement across captures, as can be seen by the two scenes showcasing the same lemon. This consistency is important for temporal monitoring applications. Overall, the evaluation demonstrates that VolSVR preserves sufficient volumetric information to enable practical volume measurements while simultaneously supporting real-time visualization through the WebSVR system.
6 Conclusion
This paper presents WebSVR, a browser-based workspace that provides a unified workflow for joint novel view synthesis and volume measurement from image data. The approach builds on SVR, a state-of-the-art NVS technique that applies ADC to a hierarchical voxel grid. VolSVR serves as a drop-in replacement for SVR and extends it by preserving occluded voxels during training while maintaining compatibility with the original SVR models. This volume-preserving representation retains visual quality with additional required input images, while the preservation of interior voxels requires only moderate storage overhead.
Building on this representation, WebSVR provides a browser-based environment for interactive visualization and volume measurement. The system combines voxel selection, scene scale configuration, and MLP-based voxel fill level estimation to measure volumes directly from the trained voxel model without requiring additional reconstruction stages. The proposed fill-level model achieves high approximation accuracy while remaining efficient enough for real-time web-based analysis. Evaluation on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrates that the workflow produces consistent volume estimates across different scene scales while maintaining interactive rendering performance.
While the achieved measurement accuracy remains below that of specialized surveying solutions, our results demonstrate that a single voxel-based representation can support both visualization and volume measurement within a unified pipeline. Future work includes improving voxel selection, handling semi-transparent materials, integrating recent advances in NVS, and further automating preprocessing and scale calibration to enable more efficient end-to-end workflows.
