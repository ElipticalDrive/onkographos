I NTRODUCTION
Motivations et context
Une explosion des données visuelles - L’ampleur à laquelle les données visuelles sont produites,
transmises et stockées continue de croître à un rythme exponentiel. En 2024, la vidéo représentait
déjà plus de 80% du trafic Internet mondial [Git25]. Les photographies contribuent également
de manière massive à ce flux : plus de 1,9 billions d’images sont prises chaque année, soit
en moyenne environ 5,3 milliards de photos par jour. Ce chiffre devrait encore augmenter
avec la diffusion croissante des smartphones et l’essor des images générées par l’IA. Une
plateforme comme YouTube reçoit plus de 720 000 heures de vidéos mises en ligne chaque jour
[Git25]. Dans le même temps, plus d’un milliard d’heures de contenus vidéo y sont visionnées
quotidiennement. TikTok, Instagram Reels et d’autres plateformes de vidéos courtes accentuent
cette dynamique en favorisant une production de contenus rapide et à grande échelle.
Malgré les volumes considérables de données supprimées chaque jour, le volume net de
données mondiales poursuit sa progression. Selon les modèles prédictifs, la quantité totale des
données atteint 175 zettaoctets d’ici 2025, contre seulement 33 zettaoctets en 2018 [IDC18]. Les
données visuelles, comprenant images, vidéos et contenus multimédias, sont à l’origine d’une
partie majeure de cette croissance. Par ailleurs, d’autres rapports estiment que les données liées
à la vidéo pourraient bientôt dépasser 90% du trafic Internet total [Cis18]. Cette tendance ne
montre aucun signe de ralentissement : il est estimé que 90% des données existantes dans le
monde ont été produites au cours des deux dernières années.
Une grande proportion de données inutilisées - La croissance exponentielle des données
visuelles a des conséquences directes sur les infrastructures de stockage. En 2012, on comptait
environ 500 000 centres de données dans le monde. Aujourd’hui, ce nombre a grimpé à plus
de 8 millions. Cette expansion du stockage a un coût considérable (en termes de consommation d’énergie et de maintenance du matériel). Les centres de données nécessitent d’énormes
quantités d’électricité pour fonctionner en continu, ce qui contribue de manière significative à la
consommation énergétique mondiale. De plus, les dispositifs de stockage tels que les disques
durs et les disques à semi-conducteurs ont une durée de vie limitée, généralement comprise entre
3 et 30 ans selon le support [DA22]. Par conséquent, maintenir et renouveler l’infrastructure de

En effet, bien que le volume de données visuelles mises en ligne et stockées est colossal,
seule une petite fraction est réellement consommée de manière significative. Par exemple,
parmi toutes les vidéos disponibles, seules 5 milliards occupent le milliard d’heures visionnées
chaque jour. Plus frappant encore, seulement 3,67% des vidéos YouTube génèrent 93,61% de
l’ensemble des vues [Glo25]. Pendant ce temps, 38% des vidéos comptent moins de cinq vues
et 93% en reçoivent moins de 1 000 [Int23]. Cette distribution très déséquilibrée reflète un
principe de Pareto numérique, où une petite minorité de contenus capte l’essentiel de l’attention
des utilisateurs. Les médias archivés qui restent stockés mais qui sont rarement consultés ou
visionnés sont couramment appelés données froides (par opposition aux données dites chaudes,
qui sont populaires et largement utilisées).
Données froides et chaudes : des défis différents - La réduction de la taille de ces données
visuelles pour le stockage est devenue un enjeu de plus en plus critique. Comme les données
chaudes représentent la plus grande part de la consommation, la plupart des techniques de
compression existantes leur sont spécifiques. Elles se concentrent alors sur la préservation de la
fidélité des pixels et de la qualité perceptuelle sous des contraintes de débit relativement élevé,
afin d’optimiser la consommation humaine. En revanche, les données froides ne suivent pas les
mêmes contraintes. Elles ouvrent une autre possibilité : réduire de manière agressive la taille de
stockage en utilisant des stratégies de compression avec pertes atteignant des débits extrêmement
faibles. En effet, la très faible fréquence d’accès supprime le besoin de haute fidélité ou de rendu
en temps réel. Selon le type de données, la fidélité des pixels devient un aspect plus flexible
de l’image. Dans ce contexte, la complexité de l’encodage mais aussi du décodage est moins
problématique en raison de l’accès et de l’utilisation rares, ce qui permet d’adopter des méthodes
complexes telles que les approches génératives. Cela permet même de viser des débits souvent
inaccessibles avec des approches plus traditionnelles.
Nouveaux défis à des débits extrêmement faibles - La plupart des méthodes traditionnelles
de compression d’images se concentrent sur la préservation de la fidélité du signal. Mais à des
débits extrêmement faibles, ces méthodes montrent leurs limites. Elles produisent souvent des
résultats inutilisables ou fortement dégradés, comme le montre la Figure 1. Blau et al. [BM18]
ont souligné l’existence d’un compromis entre débit, distorsion et perception. À mesure que le
débit diminue, il devient impossible de préserver à la fois la fidélité des pixels et le réalisme
de l’image. Mais à des débits extrêmement faibles, ce compromis explique aussi la perte de
sémantique, c’est-à-dire du contenu ou de l’information réellement présents dans l’image. En

Compression sémantique : un autre paradigme - En revanche, la compression sémantique adopte une approche fondamentalement différente. Dans ce contexte, si nous sommes
capables de préserver ce que représente l’image, même si les valeurs réelles des pixels changent
complètement, la compression peut toujours être considérée comme réussie. Cela nous amène
à cibler des objectifs alternatifs pour la compression d’images : (i) préserver les descripteurs
sémantiques (par exemple, « un arbre », « une personne assise », « une montagne enneigée
») ; et (ii) permettre une reconstruction réaliste et cohérente, sans nécessairement être identique
pixel par pixel. Par exemple, une image d’un arbre au bord d’un lac pourrait être reconstruite
comme un arbre différent au bord d’un lac similaire, à condition que le contenu sémantique et la
plausibilité visuelle soient respectés, comme vu en Figure 1. Cette approche implique de repenser
à la fois l’encodage et le décodage. L’encodeur doit extraire des représentations sémantiques
compactes de l’image, tandis que le décodeur doit reconstruire des images plausibles à partir
de ces représentations, ce qui nécessite souvent des priors génératifs. Le cadre typique de la
compression sémantique est présenté dans la Figure 2.

Q1 : Comment construire une représentation sémantique à la fois compacte et
expressive d’une image ?
Comme illustré dans la Figure 2, le cadre de la compression sémantique repose sur la manière
dont l’espace sémantique Ψ d’une image est défini. Contrairement à la compression traditionnelle,
qui se base sur des objectifs clairs au niveau des pixels, la notion de ce qui doit être préservé est
intrinsèquement ambiguë et subjective. Une image n’a pas d’interprétation universelle unique :
une photo de famille dans les montagnes peut attirer l’attention d’un membre de la famille sur
les visages et expressions, celle d’un botaniste sur les espèces d’arbres en arrière-plan, ou celle
d’un artiste sur le jeu de lumière et de couleurs. Bien qu’il soit impossible pour un codec de
capturer un tel sens personnel, cet exemple souligne la difficulté de définir la sémantique de
manière à la fois significative et compressible. De plus, la compacité ajoute une couche de
difficulté supplémentaire : même si la sémantique peut bien décrire une image, atteindre des
débits extrêmement faibles tout en ne préservant que l’information la plus essentielle reste un
défi majeur.
Fixer la définition de l’espace sémantique impacte également le décodeur. Le rôle du
décodeur devient de pouvoir reconstruire une image correspondant à la description sémantique
afin d’obtenir une image la plus proche possible de l’original, ce qui nous amène à notre deuxième
axe de recherche.
Q2 : Comment concevoir un décodeur capable de reconstruire une image
réaliste et fidèle sémantiquement à partir de la représentation, à des débits
extrêmement faibles ?
Cette question découle directement de la première : le décodeur et l’encodeur dépendent tous
deux de la définition de l’espace sémantique. Concevoir un décodeur qui préserve la sémantique choisie tout en décodant des images visuellement plausibles à partir d’une représentation
compacte constitue un véritable défi.
Une contrainte de non-entraînement - Nous ajoutons une contrainte clé à notre approche :
aucun réseau ne doit être entraîné ni ajusté. Cette décision est motivée par plusieurs raisons.
L’entraînement de grands modèles est coûteux et a un fort impact environnemental. De plus,
il existe déjà un écosystème riche de modèles pré-entraînés puissants tels que CLIP, DINO,

Exploration de la représentation sémantique des images
Dans le Chapitre 3, nous présentons notre exploration des représentations sémantiques pour la
compression d’images, qui a conduit aux travaux présentés dans [1, 2]. Nous avons commencé
avec une hypothèse de base : le contenu d’une image peut être décrit comme un ensemble
d’objets, disposés selon une organisation spatiale, chacun associé à une apparence (par exemple,
texture, couleur, éclairage). Un premier candidat naturel pour définir cet espace sémantique est
les cartes de segmentation, offrant une représentation structurée de l’image avec des régions
étiquetées. La segmentation sémantique fournit une abstraction grossière mais interprétable,
préservant à la fois l’identité des objets et la disposition spatiale. Cependant, nous avons rapidement constaté ses limites pour une reconstruction fidèle. Par exemple, elle ne permet pas
de distinguer un arbre en plein jour du même arbre la nuit, puisque les deux sont associés à
la même étiquette « arbre ». Elle élimine également les indices visuels de bas niveau que les
humains associent au contexte, au style ou à l’ambiance. Pour y remédier, nous avons enrichi
les cartes de segmentation avec une carte de couleur, une miniature basse résolution de l’image,
qui réintroduit des indices globaux d’apparence tels que l’éclairage et les couleurs dominantes.
Cette représentation hybride capture à la fois la sémantique structurelle et l’apparence, tout en
restant compacte, permettant un schéma de codage complet qui atteint des débits extrêmement
faibles tout en préservant une grande partie du contenu de l’image. En nous appuyant sur
ces observations, nous nous sommes tournés vers une approche fondamentalement différente :
exploiter des modèles de base (foundation models) entraînés sur des tâches vision–langage à
grande échelle. En particulier, nous nous sommes concentrés sur le modèle CLIP [Rad+21],
une architecture à double encodeurs qui aligne images et textes dans un espace d’embedding
partagé. CLIP présente plusieurs propriétés intéressantes pour la compression sémantique : il
encode des informations sémantiques de haut niveau comparables à des descriptions textuelles
dans des vecteurs compacts, permet un alignement texte-image pour une reconstruction guidée
par des entrées textuelles ou visuelles, et est un modèle pré-entraîné, largement accessible et
déjà populaire dans les tâches génératives. Pour compléter les embeddings CLIP, nous avons de
nouveau introduit une carte de couleur, qui fournit des informations de mise en page via une
représentation miniature. Nous avons ensuite conçu un pipeline complet d’encodage–décodage
pour la compression d’images basé sur cette représentation combinée, atteignant des débits
encore plus faibles avec une description sémantique plus compacte et complète.

Adapter le décodeur à la sémantique
Alors que le rôle de l’encodeur est d’extraire une représentation sémantique à partir d’une
image, un problème largement étudié dans de nombreuses définitions d’espaces sémantiques,
le décodeur fait face au défi inverse : reconstruire une image à partir d’informations purement
sémantiques. Cela conduit naturellement à des approches génératives. Dans nos explorations
initiales, le décodeur était traité comme une boîte noire, où son choix et sa qualité pré-entraînée
importaient plus que ses mécanismes internes. Cependant, conditionner un modèle génératif sur
une nouvelle modalité sémantique sans réentraînement s’avère être un problème particulièrement
difficile.
La plupart des générateurs disponibles sont entraînés pour produire des images à partir
de seulement quelques modalités : cartes de segmentation (via des réseaux image-à-image),
invites textuelles (via diffusion texte-à-image), ou entrées basse résolution (via des modèles de
super-résolution). Aucun de ces modèles ne répond directement à notre exigence de décodage
à partir d’une petite carte de couleur combinée à une représentation sémantique. Les modèles
de super-résolution échouent lorsque la carte de couleur est trop grossière, et les générateurs
basés sur la segmentation ou le texte suppriment des informations essentielles d’apparence. Dans
le Chapitre 3, nous avons abordé cette lacune grâce aux travaux publiés dans [3]. Nous avons
« ouvert la boîte » des modèles génératifs modernes, en nous concentrant spécifiquement sur
les modèles de diffusion, et nous nous sommes demandé : comment guider l’échantillonnage
pour que le générateur respecte à la fois notre code sémantique et la carte de couleur basse
résolution, sans aucun réentraînement ? Cela correspond à notre deuxième question de recherche,
Q2, tout en respectant strictement la contrainte de non-entraînement. Les modèles de diffusion
sont particulièrement adaptés à cette tâche car ils permettent le classifier-free guidance [HS22]
lors de l’inférence. Grâce à des modifications basées sur les gradients appliquées pendant le
débruitage, la génération peut être orientée vers des signaux de conditionnement arbitraires.
Nous avons formulé la guidance de la carte de couleur comme une contrainte linéaire sur le
latent de diffusion, assurant la cohérence même avec des entrées fortement sous-échantillonnées.
Cette formulation offre un contrôle précis du processus de diffusion tout en respectant le principe
du zéro-réentraînement, ouvrant ainsi une nouvelle modalité de génération conditionnelle.

Entre distorsion et sémantique
Comme discuté précédemment, la pertinence du contenu visuel est souvent subjective : ce qui
importe à un spectateur peut être secondaire pour un autre. Au-delà de cette subjectivité,
les scènes sont fréquemment dominées par un objet sémantiquement ou perceptuellement
important—comme une personne dans un portrait, une voiture dans une vue de rue ou un
animal dans une image animalière. Cela souligne une limitation cruciale des méthodes explorées
jusqu’ici : elles fournissent une description sémantique uniforme de la scène, répartissant le débit
de manière égale sur toutes les régions ou objets. Les approches basées sur les embeddings CLIP
capturent des abstractions de haut niveau mais ne disposent pas de mécanismes pour allouer le
débit de manière non uniforme, surtout sans réentraînement. De plus, les embeddings ne peuvent
pas être facilement manipulés pour préserver ou affiner les détails pixel par pixel des objets clés.
Dans le Chapitre 4, correspondant à notre préprint soumis à l’OJSP [4], nous introduisons la
compression sémantique sélective à des débits extrêmement faibles. Ce travail aborde à la fois
Q1 et Q2, nécessitant une gestion soigneuse du débit pour transmettre l’information de distorsion
uniquement là où c’est nécessaire, tout en permettant une représentation sémantique qui supporte
cette séparation côté décodeur. Pour y parvenir, nous proposons une stratégie de représentation
hybride : les sémantiques globales et la disposition de la scène pour les régions de fond ou moins
importantes sont encodées avec des codes compacts, tandis que des bits supplémentaires sont
alloués aux objets sémantiquement significatifs, permettant leur reconstruction avec des détails
fins, jusqu’à la précision pixel. Cela permet une allocation adaptative du débit, où les objets
clés—comme les visages humains—sont stockés à une précision plus élevée, tandis que les
régions de fond environnantes sont compressées beaucoup plus agressivement.
En nous appuyant sur le cadre de conditionnement du Chapitre 3, nous étendons le décodeur
de diffusion pour gérer cet objectif de reconstruction sélective. Notre méthode de compression
sémantique sélective permet :
• Un contrôle au niveau des objets sur la fidélité de reconstruction.
• Une intégration flexible de patches pixel par pixel avec les cartes de disposition sémantique.
• Une incorporation transparente dans un pipeline génératif complet sans réentraînement.
Cette formulation ouvre la voie à une compression adaptée aux tâches et à l’utilisateur, où
la fidélité est allouée de manière sélective selon la pertinence des objets dans la scène ou les
exigences des applications en aval.

visuelles humaines, les estimer uniquement à partir de la sémantique reste une tâche difficile.

cartes d’attention fournissent de forts priors sur le placement des objets et la composition de la
scène. Pour affiner la reconstruction et réduire les problèmes de désalignement ou d’ambiguïté,
nous transmettons en complément un signal résiduel compact de disposition.

et visuellement plausibles à des débits extrêmement faibles, sans réentraînement du modèle
génératif ni supervision basée sur les pixels.

An overview of the thesis
The core of this thesis addresses the two main challenges outlined above in Q1 and Q2 while
making sure to keep the no-training constraint. These challenges are tackled over several chapters,
progressively refining the method and framework for semantic compression. All along this thesis,
we will use the image presented in Figure 3 to illustrate and observe the evolution of our works.
Before presenting our contributions, we revisit the broader context of image compression.
In Chapter 1, we review how the objectives of compression vary across different targeted rates.
These objectives range from high-fidelity preservation at higher bitrates to extreme compression
aimed at maximizing storage efficiency. We categorize existing approaches accordingly, tracing
their evolution from high-bitrate, distortion-oriented methods to ultra-low-bitrate approaches
driven primarily by perceptual quality and semantic preservation.
Exploring image semantic representation
In Chapter 2, we present our exploration of semantic representations for image compression,
which led to the works in [1, 2]. We began with a basic assumption: the content of an image can be
described as a set of objects, arranged in a spatial layout, each associated with an appearance (e.g.,
texture, color, lighting). A natural first candidate to define this semantic was segmentation maps,
offering a structured representation of the image with labeled regions. Semantic segmentation
provides a coarse but interpretable abstraction, preserving both object identity and spatial layout.
However, we quickly observed its limitations for faithful reconstruction. For instance, it cannot
distinguish between a tree in daylight and the same tree at night, since both are mapped to the
same label “tree.” It also discards low-level visual cues that humans associate with context,
style, or mood. To address this, we extended segmentation maps with a color map, a lowresolution thumbnail of the image, which reintroduces global appearance cues such as lighting
and dominant colors in a first codec for semantic compression. The hybrid representation
captures both structural and appearance-level semantics while remaining compact, enabling a full
coding scheme that reaches extremely low bitrates while preserving much of the image content.
Building on these insights, we turned to a fundamentally different approach: leveraging
foundation models trained on large scale vision and language tasks. In particular, we focused on
the CLIP model [Rad+21], a dual-encoder architecture that aligns images and text in a shared
embedding space. CLIP offers several attractive properties for semantic compression: it encodes
high-level semantics comparable to textual descriptions into compact vectors, supports textimage alignment for reconstruction guided by either textual or visual inputs, and is a pretrained,

Adapting the decoder to the semantic

a particularly difficult problem. Most off-the-shelf generators are trained to produce images
from only a few modalities: segmentation maps (via image-to-image networks), text prompts
(via text-to-image diffusion), or lower-resolution inputs (via super-resolution models). None
of these directly address our requirement of decoding from a tiny color map combined with a
semantic representation. Super-resolution models fail when the color map is too coarse, and
segmentation-based or text-based generators discard essential appearance information.

Q2, while strictly adhering to the no-training constraint. Diffusion models are particularly

formulation gives precise control over the diffusion process while preserving the zero-retraining
principle, effectively unlocking a new modality of conditional generation. We show, at the same
rates, that the improvement on the generator gives us much better performance on the previous
coding scheme.

In between distortion and semantic
The importance of visual content is often subjective, what matters to one viewer may be irrelevant
to another. In many cases, however, a single object dominates the scene: a face in a portrait, a
car in a street view, or an animal in a wildlife shot. This exposes a key limitation of existing
methods: they treat the scene uniformly, spreading bitrate evenly across all regions. While
embeddings such as CLIP capture high-level semantics, they lack mechanisms for non-uniform
bitrate allocation without retraining. Even more, they cannot easily be adjusted to preserve or
refine the pixel-level details of crucial objects.
In Chapter 4, based on our OJSP preprint [4], we introduce selective semantic compression
at extremely low bitrates. This work tackles both Q1 and Q2, balancing bitrate usage by
transmitting distortion information only where it matters, while ensuring the decoder can separate
and reconstruct content accordingly. Our solution is a hybrid representation. Global semantics
and scene layout (for background or less relevant regions) are encoded with compact codes,
while extra bits are dedicated to semantically important objects, allowing reconstruction with
fine-grained, even pixel-level, detail. This adaptive allocation ensures that key elements, such as
human faces, are preserved with high precision, while the background is compressed far more
aggressively.
Building on the conditioning framework from Chapter 3, we extend the diffusion decoder to
support selective reconstruction. Our method enables:
• Object-level control of reconstruction fidelity.
• Flexible fusion of pixel-level patches with semantic layout maps.
• Direct integration into a generative pipeline, without retraining.
This approach paves the way for task-aware and user-adaptive compression, where fidelity is
allocated according to object relevance or the needs of downstream applications.

So far, existing approaches have relied on partial pixel cues, like low-resolution color maps,
to complement semantic descriptors. While helpful, these cues often duplicate information
already implied by semantics. A label such as tree suggests green tones and vertical structure;
sky implies blue near the top; sand points to beige near the bottom. Large-scale vision models

1.1
Introduction
The goal of image compression is to represent visual data with as few bits as possible while
preserving essential content. This trade-off between bitrate and reconstruction quality is formalized by rate-distortion (RD) theory, illustrated in Figure 1.1, which minimizes distortion
D(x, x̂) between the original image x and its reconstruction x̂ under a bitrate constraint. At
high or medium rates, this distortion-oriented compression frameword is effective, typically
using distortion measures such as mean squared error (MSE) or structural similarity (SSIM).
Both traditional codecs like JPEG, JPEG2000, HEVC and modern learned compressors focus
on signal fidelity, producing reconstructions that remain close to the original in both visual
appearance and numerical accuracy. Yet as bitrate decreases, these metrics break down: images
may score well in PSNR but look unnatural, overly smooth, or visually implausible.

This limitation led to a new framework. Blau and Michaeli (2019) [BM19] introduced the
rate-distortion-perception (RDP) trade-off, showing that minimizing distortion and bitrate alone
cannot ensure perceptual quality and can even harm it. In this extended view, perception is
defined as the statistical closeness between the distributions of real and reconstructed images.
This shift gave rise to perceptual compression, a class of methods that optimize perceptual quality
using adversarial losses, perceptual similarity metrics such as IQA and FID, and generative
decoders. These models are especially effective in the low-bitrate regime, showed as green in
Figure 1.1, where signal fidelity is harder to maintain and perceptual quality becomes the main
objective, offering a balance between rate, distortion, and perception.
As compression rates drop to the extremely low-bitrate regime, yellow in Figure 1.1, pixel
information alone is no longer enough to preserve content. At this stage, the goal of compression
shifts from how an image looks to what it contains. Semantic compression focuses on preserving
the image’s meaning: objects, relationships, scenes, structure, and concepts. It introduces a new
notion of fidelity, semantic fidelity, where reconstructions may differ visually from the original
but remain interpretable by humans or machines for tasks like classification, captioning, or
retrieval. This shift requires new models, representations, and evaluation metrics that go beyond
pixels, perceptual quality, or traditional distortion measures.
In this chapter, we trace the evolution of image compression across these three regimes. We
illustrate in Figure. 1.2 the different regimes and we identify the paradigms that they categorize.

1.2.3
Handcrafted Codecs
Handcrafted image compression methods, notably JPEG and JPEG2000, have long been the
backbone of digital media distribution. Both rely on transform coding, quantization, and
entropy coding, but differ significantly in design choices that impact their rate-distortion (RD)
performance and scalability. Some are image coders and other are intra coding method from
video codecs.
JPEG [Wal92] is based on block-based discrete cosine transform (DCT) coding. The image
is divided into blocks, each transformed via DCT. The resulting coefficients are quantized using
a perceptually tuned quantization matrix, then entropy coded (typically using Huffman coding).
This block-based processing introduces visible artefacts, especially blocking and ringing, at low
bitrates. Its lack of support for scalability and its performances limits its adaptability in modern
scenarios. However, it is still massively employed for day to day image compression.
JPEG2000 [TM02] introduces a wavelet-based alternative to JPEG. Instead of block-based
transforms, it applies a multi-scale discrete wavelet transform (DWT), producing a globally
decorrelated representation. This enables higher coding efficiency, better visual quality at low
bitrates, and inherent support for scalable and progressive decoding. This architecture eliminates
blocking artefacts and provides a much smoother degradation under high compression. Despite
its technical superiority, JPEG2000 adoption was hindered by computational complexity and
licensing concerns.
Most advanced hand crafted methods can be found in video codecs and can also be applied
to images. Advanced Video Coding (AVC/H.264) [Wie+03], High Efficiency Video Coding
(HEVC) [Sul+12], and Versatile Video Coding (VVC) [Bro+21] form three generations of handcrafted video compression standards. AVC, standardized in 2003, brought major improvements
over MPEG-2 and became the dominant format for video distribution. HEVC further improved
rate-distortion efficiency, especially in intra-frame coding, and is the basis of still-image codecs
such as BPG. VVC, the most recent standard, enhances intra coding and reduces bitrate by
30–50% at similar perceptual quality, representing the current state of the art in traditional codec
design.
1.2.4
Learned Image Compression
With the progress of machine learning, and more particularly deep learning, the field of image
compression has seen an increase in learning-based approaches. Initially, partially learned
codecs emerged, where specific modules (e.g., prediction blocks) were replaced with learned

End-to-end autoencoder-based codecs
Learned image compression methods model the entire compression pipeline, analysis, quantization, and reconstruction, using deep neural networks, typically variational autoencoders [KW+13]

1.3
Perceptual Image Compression
As the bitrate decreases, the rate distortion tradeoff fails to capture what human actually perceive,
limited by traditional quality metrics like PSNR. Perceptual compression focuses on making
images look good to the eye, even if it means sacrificing part of the pixel values. This section
covers how compression objectives are rethought, with different quality metrics, and the existing
models, especially generative approaches using GANs and diffusion methods.
1.3.1
Problem formulation
Rate-Distortion-Perception tradeoff
Traditional image compression focuses on balancing bitrate (R) and distortion (D), aiming
at minimizing both. However, optimizing solely for distortion often produces images that
appear unnatural or blurry, especially at low bitrates. Introducing the perception Ψ as a third
axis to better reflect human visual quality, Blau and Michaeli [BM18; BM19] formalized this
rate-distortion-perception (RDP) trade-off, showing that enhancing perceptual quality generally
requires increasing the bitrate or accepting higher distortion. This trade-off challenges traditional
compression goals and motivates perceptual compression methods that explicitly incorporate
human-aligned quality measures.
Formulation of perceptual image compression
Building on the RDP trade-off, perceptual compression aims at optimizing a combined objective
that balances bitrate and perceptual quality. Given an image x, an encoder f , and a decoder g, the
goal is to find a compressed representation y = f (x) and reconstruction x̂ = g(y) minimizing:
min R(y) + λD(x, x̂) + γΨ(x̂) ,

No reference Image Quality Assessment (IQA) metrics
No-reference image quality assessment (IQA), also called blind IQA (BIQA), estimates perceived
image quality without a reference. This is especially useful in semantic compression, where
the original image may not be available or relevant for evaluating quality and realism. Modern
BIQA methods use deep learning and pre-trained models to predict human-aligned quality

using learned semantic and perceptual priors. CLIP-IQA generalizes well across distortion types
and datasets without human supervision. Traditional BIQA methods, such as NIQE [MSB12]

perceptual compression systems, in order to align reconstructions with human preferences.
GAN-Based Perceptual Metrics
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [Goo+20] introduce discriminators trained to distinguish real from compressed images, acting as learned perceptual evaluator. Incorporating
adversarial losses in training encourages outputs that compare with natural images, enhancing
visual realism beyond pixel fidelity.
GAN-based perceptual metrics evaluate how well reconstructions fool discriminators, indirectly measuring the perceptual quality of compressed images. This approach is effective but
requires careful balancing to avoid artefacts.
1.3.3
Perceptual compression methods
Perceptual compression methods improve the visual quality by using generative models to
produce visually realistic or semantically meaningful reconstructions. Some approaches focus on
image realism, others on semantic preservation, and many target low to really low compression
by leveraging strong priors. The following works illustrate these trends across different bitrate
objectives.

Leveraging perception for quality
Starting at low rates along the rate-distortion tradeoff, enough pixel information is still transmitted
to preserve much of the image structure. In this regime, perceptual models focus on recovering
fine textures, high-frequency details, and visual sharpness, that are often lost early in compression.
HiFiC (High-Fidelity Generative Compression) [Men+20] is one of the first methods to
use GANs to enhance the perceptual quality of compressed images. Built on the hyperprior
architecture from [Bal+18], HiFiC introduces a discriminator to optimize a rate-distortionperception (RDP) tradeoff. Through subjective evaluations, they show that humans often prefer
perceptually enhanced images, even when they are encoded at half the bitrate of distortionoptimized ones. However, HiFiC requires retraining for each point on the RDP curve, limiting its
flexibility. Another instance of GAN based compression is presented in [Yao+22], compressing
face images using a pretrained GAN, transmitting both a latent representation and a feature tensor
from the original image to improve reconstruction. This methods also proves useful when applied
to video compression. In [YM23], the authors use a conditional diffusion model that reconstructs
an image from a noised version of its latent. The latent itself is produced by a VAE trained
for distortion, while the diffusion model denoises and completes the image from its learned
generative prior. Similarly, [Rel+24] introduces a system where a quantized latent is used for
conditioning, and the degree of denoising is controlled via a predicted parameter. Both methods
allow for some modulation of distortion by adjusting how much information is transmitted in
the latent. However, the first requires manual tuning at each rate, whereas the second automates
this process. Hoogeboom et al. [Hoo+23] also leverage diffusion, but rather than denoising
a noisy latent, they condition the diffusion model on an already decoded image produced by
a standard VAE. The diffusion model refines this reconstruction, effectively restoring missing
high-frequency content. This setup enhances perceptual quality while preserving core image
structure from the RD-optimized baseline. In contrast to these fixed operating points on the RDP
curve, Agustsson et al. [Agu+23] propose a codec capable of dynamically trading off between
rate, realism and distortion. Their model is able to produce multiple outputs from a single latent
code, ranging from relatively low-MSE reconstructions to highly realistic images. This enables
flexible navigation of the distortion-realism space, achieving strong perceptual results without
sacrificing distortion too much. Jia et al. [Jia+24] compresses images in the latent space of a
vector-quantized VAE [VV+17]. They argue that the VQ-VAE’s latent space is more sparse and
semantically aligned than pixel space, which helps at low rates. They use a trained diffusion
model on the latent space for generation but do not transmit specific explicit semantic information.
Similarly, DiffEIC [Li+24b] propose a diffusion decoder conditioned on latent features to help

constraining the generation. In [Spa+24], the authors introduce GABIC, a graph-based attention

Overall, these high-fidelity methods focus on restoring perceptual quality when most of the

A first step toward semantic
Some perceptual methods take a first step to what we call semantic compression. They explicitly
encode semantic information, such as segmentation maps, captions or learned embeddings
alongside the compressed visual content. However, we still consider them as perceptual approach
as they transmit a low resolution or degraded version of the image and rely on generative models
to synthesize more detailed outputs. The semantic information is not at the center of the approach,
and condition the generation to improve performance. Those methods often serve a different
objective than visual reconstruction, aiming at preserving the semantic for an application in a
coding for machine scheme. Because those methods target a too high range of rate, they are more
closely related to super-resolution rather than semantic compression. They could be categorized
as semantic compression if they were to target lower rates, the information on the pixels would
then have less impact on the reconstruction and more focused on preservation of the content
rather than having a focus on an MSE minimization.
A notable example of this scheme is the work of Pezone et al. [Pez+24], who propose a dualstream encoding strategy that transmits both a low-resolution image and a semantic segmentation
map. The decoder uses a conditional diffusion model that is conditioned jointly on these inputs,
producing reconstructions that retain semantic structure and visual realism. Similarly, Choi
et al. [CB18] presents a bit allocation and rate control strategy tailored to object detection.
Using the initial convolutional layers of a state-of-the-art object detector, the authors create
an importance map that guides bit allocation to areas important for object detection. Building
on the use of external knowledge, the MISC method [Li+24a] leverages a Large Multimodal
Model (LMM) to extract high-level semantics directly from the input image. These semantics are
encoded along with a low-resolution image, allowing the decoder to balance perceptual quality
and consistency even at ultra-low bitrates. Wu et al. [WCB25] extend this design by introducing
content-adaptive diffusion and segmentation-based guidance at the decoder, effectively adjusting
the reconstruction process based on spatial complexity and improving structural consistency.

1.4
Semantic Image Compression
At extreme compression levels, the bitrate is too low to preserve a correct pixel-level. This
comes as an issue for perceptual compression when no semantic is involved. In that regime,
focusing only on the perception, we can not recover enough information to reliably reconstruct
an image consistent with the input. Moreover, MSE or PSNR no longer guarantees visual quality
or semantic consistency, as captured on the MNIST dataset by Blau et al.[BM19], see Figure 1.3.
Preserving the meaningful information becomes a more viable way to preserve the image. In
such cases, bitrate and quality are not sufficient descriptor of performance, it must be coupled
with semantic fidelity metrics.

Evaluation and metrics
Evaluating semantic compression remains a challenging and open problem, primarily because
“semantic quality” is subjective and task-dependent. Thus, no consensus has been made on

Embedding-based similarity
By projecting both original and reconstructed images into a shared feature space, such as
using a foundation model like CLIP [Rad+21], we can compute cosine similarity between their
embeddings. With a high similarity indicating that the key semantic concepts (objects, scenes,
actions) are well preserved. Those types of metric, although difficult to understand deeply, are
becoming a consensus among semantic compression, as they evaluate the compression on a
variety of aspects, mostly related to language.
Quality control
Because semantic compression operates under perceptual constraints, it also requires perceptualquality evaluation protocols. Traditional perceptual metrics as described in the previous section
can be applied to ensure that reconstructions not only encode semantics but also satisfy visual
realism requirements. Usually, methods are evaluated using distribution based metrics such as
the FID or KID or no reference IQA metrics. Those methods are used to ensure that the quality
of the reconstructed image satisfy a given standard but do not compare the result with the input.
Layout consistency
If the value of the pixels becomes almost irrelevant at low bitrates, the position of objects in
the scene remains an important aspect of semantic preservation. To assess how well the spatial
layout is preserved after compression, we can use structural information such as segmentation
masks or edge maps, before and after compression, using metrics like boundary F1-score or
region overlap. This evaluation in perceptual or traditional compression was not necessary as
indirectly measured by the MSE.

When semantic compression targets a machine task (e.g., object detection, segmentation, etc...),
evaluation becomes more straightforward: simply measuring task performance on the decoded
images. For instance, one can compare mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) between segmentation maps and labels extracted from the original versus the reconstruction, or track the

1.4.3
Semantic compression methods
As illustrated in Figure 4, semantic compression depends heavily on the defined semantic
representation, or what we call the semantic space Φ. Indeed, not only the semantic space defines
what type of content we want to preserve during the compression, but it also defines constraints
on the encoder and decoder. The question of ideal semantic representation is particularly difficult
to answer as not only highly subjective but also be image dependent.
Language: LLM, BLIP and CLIP
As observed in perceptual compression, language plays a central role in semantic compression.
One of the earliest approaches was proposed by Weissman et al. [Bho+18], who simulated
the encoding and decoding process through human interaction: individuals described images
to each other using natural language, with feedback loops guiding the reconstruction, they
called this process human compression, spending few bits in human interaction to reconstruct a
realistic image. This led to the concept of textual transform coding [Wei23], drawing an analogy
between language-based representations and Fourier transforms, to explore what compression
through language might look like. More recently, Arıkan et al. [AW24] further investigated this
direction by comparing various language-based representations for semantic compression. These
include human-written captions, captions generated by ChatGPT-4, automatic captions from
the BLIP model [Li+22], and CLIP embeddings [Rad+21], which are jointly trained on vision
and language. The decoding step is performed using a diffusion model that generates images
from the language representations. They showed that a language representation for compression
scored highly on a satisfaction scale with human rating the preferences at low bitrates. A similar
approach as been adopted in [Dot+24], mimicking a human feedback loop using an LLM to
iterate over the image caption and correct the layout. By emulating the generative decoder several
times at the encoder side, they are able to improve the semantic description.
Pushing the generative aspect further, Pan et al. [PZT22] propose compressing an image into
a compact textual embedding via text inversion. A diffusion model is then used to reconstruct

text vector, relying on the diffusion model’s priors to restore structure and fine detail, closely
resembling a guided super-resolution pipeline. Along similar lines, ResULIC [Ke+25] combines
generative modeling with semantic correction. It transmits a coarse latent representation with
a semantic residual, the residual acts as a correction signal to better align the generated image
with the original. Using textual inversion on the synthesized image to retrieve missing semantic,
while keeping the overall bitrate extremely low. Careil et al.’s PerCo [Car+23] methods allows
much lower rates as low as 0.003 bpp. Their decoder operates as an iterative diffusion process
conditioned on a highly compressed VQ-VAE latent and a global image descriptor. The representation is essentially a text-like description of the image, supported by highly compressed
encoding. The method produces visually convincing outputs by leveraging strong priors and
high-level descriptors for reconstruction, prioritizing perception over distortion.
Except for human captioning, which does not integrate well into a practical coding framework, the other language-based representations are widely used in semantic-aware perceptual
compression methods. While language excels at transmitting content, it often lacks information
about spatial structure and object positioning within the image. In many approaches, the distortion optimization implicitly fills this gap by providing a coarse structural prior, often in the form
of a low-resolution image that is later upscaled during decoding.
Other modalities
Fixed semantic descriptors inevitably miss aspects of an image content. A single modality
can only capture one aspect: while language excels at describing objects and actions it lacks
precision on color information and precise spatial layout. Segmentation maps encode class
labels and positions but cannot convey texture or illumination. Consequently, most semantic
compression schemes supplement their principal descriptor with pixel-based cues, either lowresolution previews or quantized latents, to fill in missing details. One of the earliest examples
of such an approach is proposed by Agustsson et al. [Agu+19], who develop a GAN-based
decoder that reconstructs visually plausible images from extremely compressed representations.
At bitrates where standard codecs produce heavy artifacts, their model generates fine details
that are irrecoverable through traditional decoding. Through a user study, they show strong
subjective preference for their method, even at half the bitrate of competing codecs. Although
the core model does not transmit explicit semantics, they demonstrate that conditioning on
semantic maps allows the system to synthesize unimportant regions entirely, suggesting early
45
Part , Chapter 1 – State of the art
forms of content-aware prioritization. Similarly, Song et al. [SYF25] fuse segmentation masks,
natural-language captions, and an aggressively encoded latent stream, leveraging each modality’s
strengths to improve reconstruction at ultra-low bitrates.
Yet this kind of approach still relies on non-semantic, pixel-derived data. An alternative is to
seek a single, maximally compact semantic representation that fully encapsulates all necessary
information. In perhaps the first attempt in this vein, Lei et al. [Lei+23] propose compressing
solely with semantic content: they use textual inversion to generate a descriptive prompt and
transmit a simple sketch of object edges. During decoding, a diffusion model interprets the
text-plus-sketch pair to synthesize the final image. Although this method sometimes falls short
of perfectly matching the original’s overall appearance, it marks an important step toward pure
semantic compression—demonstrating that, with a powerful generative decoder, even minimal
structured cues can reconstruct coherent, semantically faithful images without resorting to pixel
data.

In this chapter, we reviewed state-of-the-art image compression models and examined semantic

This discussion lays the foundation for our work by clarifying the context and challenges of
semantic representation. In the following chapters, we will present our approach and relate it to
existing methods for encoding and decoding images from semantic representations.

Problem setting and formulation
2.1.1
Setting
Recent neural compression methods address distortion issues by incorporating perceptual metrics
during training. Our focus is on much lower bitrates, where MSE-driven generative methods
struggle to preserve semantic content. We choose to discard the MSE criterion entirely and
instead aim to optimize both realism and semantic fidelity. We want to encode images by
extracting a compact semantic description. The decoding step then relies on a generative model
to reconstruct the image.

Generative decoding with LDM: D
We use Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) [Rom+22] as our generative decoder. Diffusion models [DN21; HJA20] are trained to generate samples matching the distribution of a target dataset by
gradually denoising a random vector until it becomes a realistic image. Unlike standard diffusion
in pixel space, LDM operates in the latent space of a pretrained Variational Autoencoder (VAE),
denoted (E ′ , D′ ) [ERO21]. The diffusion model ϵθ generates embeddings in this latent space.
LDM provides models trained with conditional inputs, such as text, segmentation maps, or layout
information.
Following the standard diffusion process, sample generation with an LDM conditioned
on an input is illustrated in Figure 2.3(a). A latent noise vector zT is first drawn from a
Gaussian distribution and iteratively guided toward an image embedding z0 over T timesteps
using zt−1 = ϵθ (zt , t, σs ). The segmentation map σs conditions ϵθ to guide semantic content
generation. The VAE decoder D′ is then used to reconstruct x̂, with further details available
in the original LDM publications. When generating samples using only the segmentation map,
output variability is high, motivating the addition of the color map to the semantic representation.
In LDMs, ϵθ does not natively accept a color map as input. Rather than retraining an already
powerful and large network, we propose an alternative method to integrate color guidance,
illustrated in Figure2.3(b). Inspired by [Men+21], we initialize the diffusion process using the
color map encoded into the latent space via the VAE encoder, E ′ (σc ), corresponding to z0 in
Figure2.3(b). Noise is then added, and the diffusion process is intercepted at timestep t < T , as
if denoising has already begun. The remainder of the diffusion proceeds normally for t timesteps.
This method is feasible because the VAE in LDM was trained with an MSE loss, ensuring that
the latent space encodes color information from the input map.
The choice of interception timestep t significantly affects generation. If t is too small, zt
is close to a realistic sample, making it difficult to redirect generation. Conversely, if t is too
close to T , zt contains insufficient color information for the output to respect the color map due
to excessive noise. Empirically, we find that intercepting at 70% of T provides the best results.

the segmentation model is not able to predict correctly the classes for the images encoded using
VVC. Our method has a better preservation of the semantic when measured on the 182 possible
labels. For the second criteria, we use a CLIP alignment metric. CLIP is a model originally used
to measure alignment between text and images, however it can also be used to compare two
images. Projecting the input and decoded images into CLIP latent using the image encoder, the
angle between the two normalized vectors gives an alignment score. We present the measures as
the scalar product between the two vectors, with values ranging from 0 for no alignment to 1 for
a perfect match. We use the ViT-L/14 version of CLIP. Note that this metric is different from
CLIP-IQA which relies on text and image alignment to assess the quality. We notice that even
though our method was not trained to optimize these criteria, we still score really close to the
input and higher than the VVC encoded image. SGC thus propose an alternative to the distortion
optimization, offering quality and semantic preservation at extremely low bitrates.

Generating the decoded image
The decoder is based on a generative approach. In this work, we employ a conditional Latent
Diffusion Model (LDM) trained on conditional CLIP embeddings. Diffusion models iteratively
remove noise from a random Gaussian vector, gradually converging to realistic images. This
process effectively optimizes the perceptual quality of generated outputs.
Formally, the conditional diffusion model ϵθ generates the image, computing at each step
zt−1 = ϵθ (zt , t, σclip ), with the semantic description σclip conditioning the generation. The VAE
decoder Dldm is then applied to decode the generated latent vector z0 into the reconstructed
image x̃.
Our generative decoder D, illustrated on the right-hand side of Figure 2.7, differs slightly
from the standard LDM to incorporate color map information. The quantized color map σ̂color is
first upscaled to the original image dimensions and passed through the LDM encoder to obtain
its latent vector zcolor . The diffusion process is then initialized at a later timestep tstart , skipping
the first tstart denoising steps, with zcolor , combined with the corresponding noise level, used
as ztstart . The choice of tstart controls the amount of initial noise and the number of remaining
denoising steps, as discussed in [Men+21].
The conditional diffusion model is applied to this noised latent, with the quantized CLIP
vector σ̂clip provided as side information at each step. The resulting z0 is decoded by the VAE
to produce x̃. This approach integrates color information without requiring any retraining or
fine-tuning of the diffusion model.

2.4
Conclusion
In this chapter, we investigated two complementary approaches for semantic representation in
compression: segmentation maps and CLIP embeddings. Each provides distinct advantages and
limitations. Segmentation maps offer a structured description of the scene, clearly outlining
object locations and spatial relationships, but they fail to capture finer details such as color
or subtle appearance cues. CLIP embeddings, on the other hand, encode a more abstract and
semantically rich description, aligned with textual concepts and free from the constraints of
predefined class labels. However, this flexibility comes at the cost of reduced spatial precision.

In this chapter, we propose an improvement to the decoder design, focusing on the question raised
in Q2, specifically addressing the challenge of controlling the global color appearance of images
generated by a diffusion model, without requiring any training or fine-tuning. We introduce a
new formulation of the guidance equations used in diffusion models to better align the generated
outputs with a given color map, while maintaining high visual quality. Our approach leads to
modified guidance equations tailored for color control. In the context of this task, we show that
the guidance scaling should increase, rather than decrease, throughout the diffusion process to
achieve more effective results. We then integrate this guidance into a compression framework,
where both semantic and color information are jointly used for decoding. This enables image
reconstruction at extremely low bitrates (on the order of 10−2 bpp). We demonstrate that our
method significantly improves the fidelity and realism of the reconstructed images, outperforming
both traditional codecs and more recent semantically-driven compression schemes under similar
constraints.
3.1
Motivations
Image generation has made significant progress, mainly due to the emergence of diffusion models
[SE19]. These generative methods rely heavily on computation and data to produce the highest
quality images. Therefore, when using the latest networks for other applications, it may be
interesting to avoid retraining or fine-tuning them, but rather to adapt the models. The currently
popular and widely used text or image conditioning is often not sufficient for all applications
that require finer control. Getting control of the outputs without retraining becomes a challenge.
One of the difficulties in controlling image generation lies in the fact that most recent models
are trained to work in the latent space of a fixed VAE, as proposed in [Rom+22], these models
are called Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs). By reducing the dimension considered, the model

can be shrunk and the generation process accelerated. However, VAE coding in LDMs loses the
structure of the signal during denoising, so assumptions we can make about an image cannot
easily be applied to the decoded latent. This is particularly the case for frequency decomposition,
where the organization of the signal in latent space is unknown. Thus, most methods in the
literature that add control to diffusion models have been designed for pixel space. The solutions
for controlling outputs in pixel space are not always relevant for LDMs, and unfortunately most
current state-of-the-art models are trained in this configuration.

Control through color maps can be a useful tool for image generation, especially in the context of

are already some ways to control color for diffusion in the pixel space, such as generating only
the high frequencies using a decomposition of the image proposed by the authors of [YFD23],
or using a specific scheduler for the diffusion model [Kaw+22]. But a good control in LDM

diffusion models, by learning the directions in the latent space associated with semantic meaning
[KJU22] or by adding a condition as input [ZRA23]. However, we do not want to rely on training
or fine-tuning a new model for the specific task of color map conditioning, as this would not

training-free control that is adaptable to any diffusion model, and in particular to LDMs. In

3.3. Existing solutions
3.3.1
Conditional diffusion models
A conditional diffusion model follows the same principle as a standard diffusion model, the main
difference being that the prediction of the noise is done over (zt , t, c). The condition, c is then
given as input of the diffusion model at each timestep, with the parameters θ trained to take c
into account. Conditional models have become widely popular for text-to-image generation or
image-to-image generation applications, however, they require either retraining a whole network
or fine-tuning an already trained network for the desired condition. This is something we want to
avoid in our framework.
3.3.2
Enforcing colors in diffusion model
Given a diffusion model ϵθ , it is sometimes possible to enforce a condition on the output of the
model, ensuring that the output exactly follows our condition. For color map conditioning, this
is the case, but only if the diffusion process is done in the pixel space, denoising over images
rather than their latents. Then, for a given image, z0 can be uniquely separated into lower and
higher frequencies z0 = l(z0 ) + h(z0 ) for specified functions l and h. For example, the authors
in [YFD23] use a wavelet decomposition. It is then possible to enforce the value of the low (or
high frequencies) of the generated image by replacing at each step the generated low frequencies
by our target c. With ẑ0 (zt , t) the predicted z0 in (3.3) is replaced by its value at each step by:
ẑ0 (zt , t, c) = c + h(ẑ0 (zt , t))
(3.5)
At each step, the low frequencies of the sample are enforced, and only the higher frequencies are
generated. Enforced diffusion is illustrated in Figure 3.3b, where the vertical movement in the
arrow indicates the correction of the low frequencies. This method is similar to the inpainting
process described in [Lug+22], where a mask is used to do the partitioning of z0 rather than a
frequency decomposition. This method can be used when the constraint on c needs to be strong
and when the diffusion is done in pixel space. However, with the popularity of diffusion models,
the speed of their training and sampling time has become a major concern, and most models are
now processed in the latent space of the VAE to reduce the dimension. This method does not
extend to latents where the decomposition of the signal is not defined.

Initializing the diffusion is a simple method that does not require a computation of gradients,
however, this is a method that loses both in realism of generation and fidelity to the color map
because of the presence of a tradeoff between both. Our formulation of guidance is similar to the
formulation of universal guidance, as both coincidentally requires computing similar gradients.
The main difference between the two methods lies in the scaling of the loss. While in universal,
the importance of the guide decreases after each step, we found that to optimally guide the

Fine guidance for compression
We apply fine color guidance in the compression framework previously described in Section

information sent within the color map. We compare our method with state-of-the-art compression
methods, especially VVC. But we also compare it to other generative approaches that aim at

usage, it might be preferable to invest the time needed in training the model. But for a research
application, where the number of generated images is lower, allowing to add conditioning
on any diffusion model. Moreover, when comparing with universal guidance [Ban+23], the

4.1
Introduction
In this section, we shift focus from decoder design to the information transmitted, aiming to
preserve distortion only where it matters. This defines a selective semantic compression problem:
determining what to compress and what to preserve. We maintain a perception-first approach,
prioritizing semantic fidelity and perceptual quality to decode semantically realistic images. For
a given semantic-perception target, controlled distortion is used to manage bitrate. At extremely
low bitrates, the transmitted data should capture only the most important aspects of the image,
which may vary depending on the application or viewer. For example, facial accuracy may be
crucial in a family photo but irrelevant in a football crowd. This chapter addresses both Q1, by
enabling finer control over semantic representation, and Q2, by exploring how to integrate such
representations at the decoder. In a new coding pipeline, we bridge semantic and pixel fidelity.
Our goal is to provide flexibility: focusing on distortion where it matters, while still being able to
drop to extremely low bitrates and rely purely on semantics when pixel fidelity is less critical. The
problem of selective compression at low rates was first explored in [Agu+19], where GANs and
segmentation maps were used to transmit pixel information only for selected regions, with the
rest generated from semantics. We extend this idea by enabling partial generation of each region,
with adjustable precision across objects, allowing us to reach much lower rates while keeping
control over what is preserved. We formulate this task as Selective Semantic Compression
(SeSeCo). We reuse the semantic representation from our first work, based on segmentation
maps improving their coding. To avoid retraining, we adopt a graph signal processing approach
that leverages the image segmentation to transmit object-specific information depending on
both spatial location and assigned importance. As illustrated in Figure 4.1, when the label tree
is selected as important, the corresponding region is better preserved by transmitting targeted
information, while the rest of the image is reconstructed more loosely.
At extremely low bitrates, pixel-level precision deteriorates, and image representations
become coarse, ranging from ultra-low-resolution color maps, as previously discussed, to sparse

4.2
Framework and problem formulation
4.2.1
The selective compression problem
In the context of image compression at extremely low bitrates, our objective is to preserve both the
semantic content and a high level of perceptual realism in the reconstructed image. By semantic
content, we mean the information represented by a segmentation map, typically produced by a
100
4.2. Framework and problem formulation
network trained to detect semantically meaningful objects. This map encodes object categories,
boundaries, and their spatial relationships within the scene. Beyond object identity, we aim
to selectively preserve the appearance of certain objects, not just their categorical labels. The
selection is guided by the semantic map, enabling the encoder to adapt the reconstruction fidelity
across regions. As a result, objects can be decoded with different levels of pixel accuracy, while
the overall image remains visually coherent and free of perceptual artifacts across regions. This
chapter builds on our first work using segmentation maps, SGC. We use the precise object
delimitations of the segmentation to provide much finer and precise color maps that depends on
the object rather than a downsampling.
We would like to encode and decode the selected objects. The selection should be done using
a vector of preference p, assigning to each object a value of importance. Thus, associating with
each type of object i, an importance pi in [0, 1] indicating how well the corresponding image
should be preserved. Then, we can have a granularity over the precision of the reconstruction

Automated weights preferences
While object importance can be manually adjusted, the importance often varies depending on the
context. In such scenarios, a fully automated weighting mechanism becomes preferable. For
image compression, whether intended for human viewing or machine interpretation [PMR23],
it is generally beneficial to allocate more bitrate to objects that are likely to attract the most
attention. In the case of human visualization, this attention can be effectively modeled using
saliency prediction.

saliency map is computed from the input image using a model such as SUM [Hos+25]. This
saliency map is then combined with the segmentation map to identify which semantic objects
warrant higher importance. In practice, we compute the average normalized saliency score for
each segmented object to determine its preference weight. These weights are then transmitted to
the decoder, encoded with 4-bit precision per object.

ATTENTION SEMANTIC IN THE DECODER
This final chapter focuses on reducing the bitrate of the representation by exploiting prior
information contained in the data. Once the semantics of an image are known, much of its
structure becomes implicit, and only minor adjustments are needed to place objects correctly and
give them the proper shape. We found that by leveraging attention maps in diffusion models, we
can not only correct positioning but also estimate a prior location based on the semantics. In this
context, we address both key research questions. First, which semantic representation to choose
(Q1), and second, how to adapt the decoder to effectively use this semantic information (Q2).
By estimating the image layout and adapting the decoder accordingly, our approach provides a
clearer and more efficient way to integrate semantic priors into image representations.
5.1
Introduction
We propose to improve on the framework proposed in Chapter 3, going back to the sem. We saw
through different state of the art method and previous proposed method that the semantic content
of an image can be represented in various forms, including textual descriptions, segmentation
maps, or embeddings derived from foundation models. However, each of these semantic
representations presents inherent limitations. To enhance the overall description of an image,
the semantic representation are complemented with additional information, which may be either
semantic or more closely related to pixel-level details. For instance, image captions can provide
a general semantic description, but they often lack spatial or positional information. To address
this, additional modalities such as edge maps or color maps can be incorporated to produce a
more comprehensive and structured representation of the image content. We can see different
examples of such frameworks in various works. The preservation of semantics and layout thus
takes various forms; however, they tend to be highly redundant with textual information, such
as color maps or latent representation. We seek an alternative that is more compact and less
correlated with semantics.

5.2.1
Attention in diffusion models
In text-to-image diffusion models, the standard U-Net architecture is augmented with attention
layers to integrate textual information into the image generation process. As illustrated in
Figure 5.2, adapted from the work introducing latent diffusion models [Rom+22], these attention
mechanisms play a central role during the diffusion process. Specifically, the image x is first
encoded, and the iterative denoising procedure is performed within the latent space of a separately
trained Variational Autoencoder (VAE). The conditioning on text is introduced through crossattention layers, which are integrated at multiple levels of the U-Net, allowing the semantic
information from the text to influence the generation across the entire network hierarchy. The
computation of cross-attention between text and image enables the network to interpret the
textual input as a conditioning signal for image generation. The outputs of this mechanism,
referred to as attention maps, retain the same spatial dimensions as the corresponding inputs,
and their number matches the number of tokens used to encode the text or semantic description.

5.2.3
Guiding the generation using attention
Semantic in attention
The authors of [Eps+23] showed how the attention maps produced during the generation process
of diffusion models contain some positional information on semantics. Specifically, they observed
that the network’s attention response to a given token from the generation prompt reflects both the
location and shape of the corresponding object within the scene. This behavior can be observed
in the attention maps, where regions with high activation values are spatially concentrated
around the object’s position. We illustrate this phenomenon in Figure 5.3, which reproduces
an observation from the original study. This property generally holds for most latent diffusion
models, particularly at the higher layers of the U-Net within the diffusion network. However,
the extent to which positional information is preserved may vary depending on the training
characteristics of the corresponding Variational Autoencoder (VAE).

diffusion trajectory. To address these limitations and reduce the volume of information necessary

5.3.2
Residual attention
We know that the semantics contained in text or used to describe an image can already carry
implicit positional information. For example, a sky is rarely at the bottom of an image, and a nose
in a face is rarely upside down. While this information appears intuitive to humans, exploiting
this bias in the data is not straightforward. However, by examining positional information as a
result of attention, we can estimate this bias by analyzing images generated without guidance.
By predicting the attention distribution from a few generated images, it becomes possible to
encode only the residual attention not already captured by the semantics. Since prediction is
required on both the encoder and decoder sides, the process must be deterministic. To achieve
this, the random Gaussian noises used to initialize the generation are drawn using fixed seeds.
In practice, after estimating and quantizing the attention matrices of the source, As,p , we
generate n images based solely on the semantics to be transmitted, stopping at timestep p. We

5.3.3
Compressed representation
The image is represented through the semantics captured by the CLIP foundation model and
the positional information contained in the attention layers. The CLIP embedding of the image
is projected to a dimension of 32 and encoded over 16 bits before being transmitted to the
transformer layer. Therefore, before entropy coding, the information contained in the CLIP
embedding is at most 512 bits.
The attention layers are encoded after computing the residual. Specifically, we use three
prediction methods to estimate the attention. Each attention map is encoded using one of the
following approaches: intra, encoding the entire map; average, using an average attention map
that captures consistently attended regions; and best, retaining only the closest matching attention
map among the n images generated from the fixed seeds. Accordingly, for each attention layer,
we also transmit the method used to compute the residual. Overall, the residuals and the attention
are largely independent, as the CLIP embedding is used to predict attention. Therefore, we
choose to encode them separately. The total bitrate of an image is simply the sum of the encoded
CLIP embedding and the encoded attention.

Subjective evaluation
Protocol of evaluation - We evaluated in collaboration with [AMI25] the perceptual quality of
three generative-AI image-compression methods using a controlled, pairwise comparison study
designed to capture both global preferences and detailed quality judgments. The set comprised
seven source images selected to span a range of content (animals, buildings, centered objects,

images were selected without cherry picking.
Sixteen observers completed the experiment. Each trial began with a brief presentation
of the original image as a reference, followed, after a short neutral screen, by a simultaneous
side-by-side display of two compressed images generated by different methods. Observers first
indicated which of the two they preferred overall, then rated each image independently on three
5-point attributes: (i) the perceived visual quality, (ii) the presence of AI-induced artifacts, and
(iii) the content preservation relative to the original. To mitigate positional and order biases,
both the trial sequence and the placement of images were randomized so that no method was
consistently shown in the same position.

interpret the results. Using pairwise preferences, we computed a ranking with the Bradley-Terry

consistently ranked first in this experiment. Figure 5.9b presents the winning distribution for

in perceived fidelity, artifacts, and semantic content preservation. Overall, the three methods

Conclusion
In this last chapter, we present a novel approach for integrating layout information into semantic
compression. Rather than transmitting the full layout, we estimate it directly at the decoder
using the attention layers, sending only a residual layout when necessary. Beyond the standard
evaluation metrics commonly used in compression research, we introduce an additional assessment based on subjective testing. This evaluation not only measures the performance of our
method but also positions it within the broader framework introduced in Chapter 3. Our results
demonstrate that it is possible to achieve strong subjective quality and excellent preservation
of both semantic content and layout without sending any direct pixel information, highlighting
the potential of semantic-driven compression approaches. We showed in this last chapter the
last and most efficient framework for image compression. Using attention, we are not only able
to transmit a residual layout of the image, but we are also transmitting a layout adapting to
the semantic, giving position on objects that only a highly versatile and thus costly to transmit
segmentation map could do.
This framework improves on the previous approaches, rather than transmitting color information through low resolution images, we notice that CLIP embeddings already capture this
information well enough and that by correcting the layout of the scene we are able at the same
rates to improve significantly the compression scheme.

This thesis explored semantic image compression as a new frontier beyond traditional
and perceptual codecs. While classical approaches optimize signal fidelity and perceptual
realism, semantic compression aims to preserve meaning at extremely low bitrates, relying
on powerful generative priors for reconstruction. Across the chapters, we investigated how
to build compact yet expressive semantic representations, how to adapt decoders to exploit
them, and how to balance semantics and pixels in a flexible way. The following answers to our

Q1: How to construct a compact yet expressive semantic representation of an
image ?
Through our work, we explored semantic space and reached several conclusions. First,
foundation-model embeddings emerged as particularly effective. These models, trained on

billions of image and text pairs, provide representations that are both compact and expressive.
Their strength lies in capturing the shared structure of vision and language, embedding semantic
similarity into a space where related concepts cluster naturally. Unlike handcrafted descriptors
or supervised features tailored to specific tasks, these embeddings generalize across a wide
range of content, which makes them a natural fit for compression where versatility is crucial.
We noticed through our work that those embeddings contain information on the objects in the
scene, the global organization and context, but also quite precise information on the general
colors in the image. A second key factor is their robustness. Such embeddings withstand
aggressive quantization without catastrophic degradation, which is essential when operating
under bitrate constraints. This robustness opens the door for embeddings not just to measure
semantic similarity, as they are already widely used in the literature, but to serve as a base for
representing the image itself in compression pipelines. Their growing acceptance in evaluation
tasks further strengthens their role, since a representation aligned with human perception and
used for benchmarking can also form the basis of coding schemes.
At the same time, the exploration also highlighted their limitations. Semantics encoded purely
through language, whether in the form of captions, CLIP features, or embeddings extracted
from LLMs, proved too abstract when considered in isolation. While they capture what an
image depicts, they lose much of the how: spatial arrangements, textures, and stylistic aspects
that make reconstructions feel faithful to the source. Reconstructions based solely on such
representations often preserve meaning but fail in subjective quality, leading to oversimplified or
distorted visuals.
Our contributions addressed this gap in two ways. First, we paired semantic embeddings with
ultra-low-resolution image signals, transmitting just enough pixel information to preserve global
layout and colors. This combination allowed semantics to anchor content while pixels provided
a scaffold for spatial fidelity. Later, we advanced this approach by replacing redundant pixel cues
with more structured priors extracted from attention layers. By leveraging the ability of attention
to encode layout directly, we achieved a more compact representation without sacrificing fidelity
to the source.
Finally, we observed that semantic descriptions are often complemented by additional
information more closely correlated with the signal, in order to improve reconstruction. In our
work, as well as in others’, a clear duality emerges between semantic and positional information,
with several contributions converging on this representation of an image.

6.1. Conclusion
Q2: How to design a decoder that reconstructs a realistic and semantically
faithful image from such a representation, at extremely low bitrates ?
.
The semantic description goes hand in hand with the design of the decoder. In semantic
compression, the decoder is fundamentally generative: without access to dense pixel-level
information, it must rely on powerful priors to reconstruct an image that is both semantically
faithful and visually realistic. This means that every step forward in representation also requires
a rethinking of the decoder. In our contributions, the evolution of semantic descriptions naturally
led to a parallel evolution in how the decoder was designed and controlled.
Our first contribution on this topic introduced a way to guide diffusion models without
retraining. We proposed conditioning latent diffusion models on linear filter signals, in particular
extremely low-resolution color maps. This provided the decoder with additional structural
anchors at negligible bitrate cost, improving semantic alignment while preserving a sense of
similarity to the original image. Building on the same principle of external guidance, we then
developed a selective compression scheme. Here, semantic and pixel information were combined
in a more controlled manner: the decoder could balance both sources of information dynamically,
emphasizing fidelity where necessary and relying on semantic generation elsewhere. This
selective strategy opened the door to a more versatile framework, adaptable not only to different
rates but also to different user or content priorities.
Finally, we leveraged priors already present within diffusion models. By analyzing attention
maps, we found that diffusion implicitly encodes layout information as it reconstructs an image.
We showed how this layout “leakage” could be harnessed and modified to control the generation
process itself. This approach reduced bitrate even further while improving consistency and
providing an additional handle on the reconstruction, effectively turning an internal prior into a
tool for compression.
Taken together, these contributions highlight a central insight: semantic compression is
not only about choosing the right representation but also about controlling the decoder. By
learning how to steer diffusion models externally, selectively, and through their internal priors,
we demonstrated a pathway toward realistic, semantically faithful reconstructions at extremely
low bitrates. Furthermore, given the availability of already powerful models trained under diverse
conditions, it is promising to explore how these existing resources can be leveraged effectively,
rather than systematically requiring retraining for each new case.

Part , Chapter 6 – Conclusion and perspectives
General conclusion on semantic compression
With hindsight, our work suggests a shift in how semantic compression should be understood.
Traditional compression has long been anchored in the pixel domain, where distortion is measured
against a reference signal. Perceptual compression took the first step, integrating realism and
human perception into the tradeoff. Semantic compression pushes this boundary further: the
goal is no longer to reproduce the original signal, but to preserve its meaning, while relying on
generative priors to reconstruct plausible images at incredibly small rates.
Our contributions frame semantic compression as a joint design of representation and decoder.
On the one hand, compact semantic embeddings, especially those from foundation models,
provide expressive and quantization-resilient descriptors of image content. On the other, decoders
powered by diffusion models can be guided, selectively balanced, or even steered through their
own internal attention priors to turn sparse descriptions into full-resolution reconstructions.
Together, these design creates a compression paradigm where semantics anchor the content,
while generative models supply detail, realism, and controllability.
In this sense, semantic compression is not simply an incremental step after learned or
perceptual codecs. It is a new framework, where the information to be transmitted is not the
image itself but a minimal set of signals, semantic, structural, or both at the same time, that allow
a powerful decoder to regenerate the rest. By progressively separating semantics from pixels,
and showing how generative priors can be exploited without retraining, we tried to lay some
novel approaches for this next stage in compression research.
6.2
Perspectives
Our work has explored only a small portion of the broad field of semantic compression. We focused on two key aspects to provide meaningful insights, but many questions remain unanswered.
In this section, we highlight several areas that require further investigation.
Semantic compression for videos- While our study focused on images, we also considered
extending semantic compression to video data. At the time of our work, the available generators
for video were not sufficiently convincing in terms of quality and temporal coherence. Nevertheless, this direction remains highly promising and opens several questions. The addition of a
temporal dimension fundamentally changes the problem: unlike animating a single image, video
generation requires consistent appearance and motion across consecutive frames. Maintaining
temporal consistency is crucial to avoid flickering or semantic drift. Furthermore, while language
140
6.2. Perspectives
descriptors work effectively for still images, they are less suited to capture dynamics, subtle
motion patterns, or temporal dependencies. This suggests that the choice of semantic descriptors
may need to evolve, potentially requiring new foundation models specifically trained to align
visual and temporal semantics, or hybrid representations combining object-level descriptors with
motion vectors. Exploring such models could unlock powerful video compression schemes at
ultra-low rates.
Evaluation- Although some consensus is beginning to emerge regarding evaluation strategies
for semantic compression, no universally accepted metric has been established. Each existing
measure brings advantages and limitations. While traditional metrics such as MSE or FID are
clearly insufficient to capture semantic or perceptual fidelity, they can still provide partial insights,
particularly for benchmarking against conventional codecs. CLIP similarity has emerged as
a more promising direction, as it correlates better with high-level semantics, but it remains
incomplete as an evaluation tool. Subjective evaluation continues to be the most reliable method,
especially when compression is intended for human consumption, since it directly reflects
perceptual preferences. However, subjective studies are resource-intensive, hard to scale, and
not always practical for incremental work. Future progress in evaluation might require hybrid
protocols that combine automatic semantic-perceptual measures with lightweight human studies,
or even the design of new machine learning models explicitly trained to predict human judgments
of semantic compression quality.
A semantic tradeoff- In this work, we emphasized the extremely low bitrate regime, where
semantic information is indispensable for preserving meaningful image content. However, as the
bitrate increases, the importance of semantics diminishes, and they primarily serve to preserve
fine-grained details such as textures and stylistic cues. This suggests a potential tradeoff to be explored: how to balance semantic fidelity with pixel-level accuracy across different bitrate regimes.
A possible line of investigation would be to frame this problem within the information bottleneck
principle, identifying the optimal semantic-perceptual balance for a given rate. Another direction
would be to study lossy compression of high-rate semantic descriptors, potentially allowing
alternative representations that adaptively allocate bits depending on perceptual importance.
Such work could lead to compression schemes that remain effective across a broader range of
operating points, seamlessly transitioning from semantic-driven to detail-driven reconstructions.
Semantic coding for machines- Our study has focused primarily on semantic compression
for human perception, where the goal is to produce visually plausible and semantically faithful
reconstructions. However, images can also be coded for machine-oriented tasks, such as classification, detection, or scene understanding. In these contexts, fidelity to human perception
141
Part , Chapter 6 – Conclusion and perspectives
may be secondary to task-specific accuracy. While our method is likely to perform reasonably
well across a range of tasks, the optimal design for machine coding may differ significantly.
For example, representations that preserve object boundaries or structural cues might be more
useful than those optimized for perceptual quality. Furthermore, balancing multiple tasks without degrading performance across them remains an open challenge. Exploring frameworks
specifically designed for machine-oriented coding,or hybrid ones that balance both human and
machine needs—could open new avenues for compression research, particularly in scenarios
like autonomous driving, medical imaging, or large-scale data storage for training.
Ethical discussion- While this work on semantic compression has been stimulating, several
ethical considerations remain. The training of generative networks designed to continually
improve quality leads to increasingly large models. This also applies to the foundation models
used to derive semantic representations. Although our goal is to enhance compression at
extremely low bitrates to improve storage efficiency and reduce data transfer, it is important to
ensure that the energy required for encoding and decoding does not exceed the savings achieved
through compression.
Another significant ethical concern relates to the generation of images themselves. When
producing images of people or specific locations, questions arise regarding ownership and
intellectual property of the resulting content. Moreover, generative models can inherit and
amplify biases from their training data, raising ethical issues when reconstructions misrepresent
individuals, contexts, or cultural elements. Transparency and safeguards are thus essential.
Finally, it is important for users to be aware when they are viewing a reconstructed or generated
image rather than the original. Techniques such as watermarking within the generative model
could help address this issue and represent a valuable avenue for future development.
Many questions remain unanswered, and several of these could serve as the basis for entirely
new research directions. This work illustrates that semantic compression is a field still in its early
stages, with many open challenges related to representation, evaluation, and decoder control.
Progress will likely depend on advances in foundation models, the development of more reliable
evaluation metrics.
I will conclude by noting that working on this topic has been both a rewarding and instructive
experience. It has offered the opportunity to investigate a rapidly evolving area of research and
to contribute to the understanding of how semantics can be leveraged for compression. While
this thesis addresses some of the central questions, it also leaves many avenues open for future
exploration.

achieve this, we introduce a new framework
based on semantic representations of images,
shifting the focus from pixel-level fidelity to the
preservation of meaningful content. The objective becomes clear: retain what matters
to human perception while reconstructing images that are realistic. We address mainly two
research questions: How can we construct
a compact yet expressive semantic representation of an image? and How can we de-
sign a decoder capable of reconstructing realistic and semantically faithful images from
such a representation, even at extremely low
bitrates? Throughout the thesis, we show
that foundation models embed images into
rich semantic spaces that can be exploited
for compression. We further adapt the decoder to these representations by harnessing
the power of diffusion models, proposing techniques to guide them effectively without retraining. We propose a state-of-the-art framework that achieves drastically low compression rates while preserving semantic fidelity,
onfirmed through subjective evaluation.
