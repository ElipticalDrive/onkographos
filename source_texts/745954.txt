# ABSTRACT

Existing neural 3D representations separately address continuous surface modeling, efficient image synthesis, or object-specific compression. They do not jointly provide the explicit surface queries required by ray tracing and a structured output space that can be predicted across objects. We introduce NeuOctree, a neural explicit representation that directly predicts whether and where a ray intersects a surface, together with its normal and albedo. NeuOctree stores shared features at the corners of sparse octree voxels and interpolates them into a continuous local field. A lightweight decoder with recurrent intersection-guided sampling refines each query near the predicted surface, enabling direct surface rendering without mesh extraction or texture baking and accommodating open, non-watertight geometry. The same octree structure supports both compact per-object optimization and a shared octree CNN that constructs renderable assets from colored point clouds without per-object fitting. Across seven meshes and four storage budgets, NeuOctree achieves the lowest positional error in 24 of 28 comparisons while retaining competitive normal accuracy, and substantially improves joint geometry–appearance reconstruction over N-BVH and VQAD. The feedforward model constructs a representation in 0.05 seconds and transfers from Objaverse to ShapeNet without retraining. We further demonstrate ray-traced rendering and image-conditioned generation, showing that NeuOctree provides a common output space for neural inference and direct surface rendering. # 1 INTRODUCTION

Learning compact 3D assets requires a representation that connects neural prediction with geometric computation. A ray-tracing renderer needs to determine where a ray intersects an object and recover the normal and material attributes at that point. A network trained across objects benefits from a



∗Corresponding author.

structured output space in which to predict geometry and appearance. Bringing these requirements together would allow learned assets to pass directly from a neural network to a ray-tracing renderer.

Existing representations address different parts of this problem. Signed distance fields \(Park et al., 2019; Chabra et al., 2020\) and occupancy fields \(Mescheder et al., 2019\) encode surfaces as level sets of learned scalar functions. NeRF \(Mildenhall et al., 2020\) jointly models density and radiance, with hash encodings \(Muller et al., 2022\) and sparse voxel structures \(Yu et al., 2021; Fridovich-Keil et al., ¨ 2022; Liu et al., 2020b\) reducing the cost of field evaluation and rendering. SDF-based volume rendering \(Wang et al., 2021\) improves surface reconstruction, while Gaussian splatting and sparse voxel rasterization \(Huang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025\) enable efficient image synthesis. These advances illustrate the value of compact, spatially organized representations. However, density or occupancy values do not directly specify the intersection and material attributes required by a surface ray tracer: these quantities must be recovered through additional surface localization or geometry extraction.

Neural compression methods bring learned representations closer to conventional surface rendering. Neural Geometry Fields \(Sivaram et al., 2024\) encode detailed geometry through displacements of coarse mesh patches, while N-BVH \(Weier et al., 2024\) directly predicts ray intersections and appearance within a bounding volume hierarchy. Both construct their representations through objectspecific optimization. Building on the direct ray-query interface, we seek a representation whose geometry and appearance can also be predicted by a shared network across objects.

We introduce NeuOctree, a neural explicit shape representation that couples direct ray–surface intersection prediction with an octree-based sparse voxel structure. The octree organizes local geometry, skips empty space during traversal, and provides a structured domain for 3D neural networks. Adjacent voxels share corner features, defining a continuous feature field through trilinear interpolation. A lightweight, shared MLP decodes the ray and interpolated features into a hit probability, intersection position, surface normal, and albedo. These outputs supply the surface queries needed for ray tracing without mesh extraction or texture baking. Because the representation does not require an inside–outside labeling, it also accommodates open, non-watertight surfaces.

Accurate intersection prediction requires querying the right part of the feature field. Features sampled only at a voxel's boundary may be far from the surface, and rays observing the same surface point can enter the voxel at different locations. We therefore introduce a recurrent sampling strategy that uses predicted intersections to guide subsequent feature queries. This refinement brings queries closer to the surface and encourages rays from different views to access features near the same location. The ablation shows improved intersection and normal accuracy over endpoint-only and uniform sampling.

The same structure enables us to amortize representation construction across objects. We build a feedforward 3D U-Net on octree-based CNNs \(Wang et al., 2017\) to predict voxel-corner features from colored point clouds. Averaging predictions at shared vertices produces a consistent feature field, which is decoded by the shared intersection MLP. Training the feature extractor and decoder jointly with rendering losses allows a single checkpoint to construct geometry and appearance for new inputs without per-object optimization. NeuOctree thus supports both per-object compression and feedforward construction within the same representation and renderer.

We evaluate per-object compression on seven meshes from Thingi10K and the Stanford 3D Scanning Repository at four storage budgets. NeuOctree achieves the lowest positional error in 24 of 28 model–budget comparisons and competitive normal accuracy compared with geometry-only methods, and significantly overperforms N-BVH and VQAD which jointly represent geometry and appearance. On the separate feedforward task, a shared checkpoint constructs a representation in 0.05 seconds with lower intersection error, compared with approximately 3.5 minutes for N-BVH fitting under the reported settings. The same checkpoint, trained on Objaverse, transfers to ShapeNet without retraining. We further demonstrate direct ray-traced rendering with reshading, reflection, refraction, and fisheye projection, and show image-conditioned generation of NeuOctree assets as a downstream application. Our contributions are:

- A structured neural surface representation that jointly encodes geometry and appearance in shared voxel-corner features and supports direct ray-traced rendering.
- Recurrent intersection refinement that adaptively queries the continuous feature field to improve the accuracy of predicted surface intersections and normals.

# 2 RELATED WORK

Neural Surface and Radiance Representations. Neural implicit fields provide a compact and differentiable alternative to explicit geometry. Early methods represented shapes with coordinatebased multilayer perceptrons, using signed distance functions (SDFs) \(Park et al., 2019; Chabra et al., 2020\) or occupancy fields \(Mescheder et al., 2019\). Subsequent approaches introduced octrees and adaptive spatial partitions \(Takikawa et al., 2021; Martel et al., 2021\), while neural radiance fields jointly modeled density and appearance through volume rendering \(Mildenhall et al., 2020\). Octree and hash-grid variants \(Yu et al., 2021; Fridovich-Keil et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020b; Muller et al., ¨ 2022\), SDF–density hybrids \(Wang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023\), and explicit rasterization-based representations \(Huang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025\) have substantially improved memory use or image-rendering speed.

These methods nevertheless expose a mismatch with surface ray tracing. A density or occupancy value does not directly specify a ray–surface intersection, normal, or material; obtaining these quantities typically requires repeated field evaluations, surface localization, or mesh extraction. The resulting geometric ambiguity can compromise fine detail, and level-set formulations often rely on an inside–outside convention that is awkward for open or non-watertight surfaces. NeuOctree instead makes the ray–surface query the representation's primary interface: a shared decoder predicts the hit, position, normal, and albedo from a ray and a continuous octree feature field. This design preserves the compactness of neural representations while enabling direct ray-traced rendering without an intermediate mesh or texture-baking stage.

Neural Mesh Compression. Traditional mesh compression combines connectivity coding, coordinate quantization, and simplification to reduce storage while preserving surface fidelity \(Deering, 1995; Chow, 1997; Diaz-Gutierrez et al., 2005; Touma & Gotsman, 1998; Heckbert & Garland, 1999\). Learning-based methods replace hand-designed operators with graph networks or lightweight MLPs that upsample a coarse mesh and predict vertex displacements \(Liu et al., 2020a; Chen et al., 2023; Sivaram et al., 2024; Pentapati et al., 2025\). Although effective for individual assets, these approaches are generally optimized per mesh, focus primarily on geometry, and require a fixed coarse topology; appearance is commonly handled by a separate texture-baking step. N-BVH \(Weier et al., 2024\) is closer to our setting because it predicts ray intersections and appearance within a hierarchical structure, but it also relies on object-specific optimization.

NeuOctree addresses these limitations with a sparse octree that supplies a global spatial prior and shared voxel-corner features that provide local context. Geometry and appearance are decoded through the same ray-query interface, so the representation can be optimized as a compact per-object asset or predicted by a shared network across objects. The distinction is therefore not only a different compression structure, but also a shift from per-mesh fitting to a renderable representation that supports feedforward construction.

Structured 3D Learning and Feedforward Reconstruction. Point-, voxel-, and sparse-voxel networks have become standard tools for 3D perception and generation \(Qi et al., 2017a;b; Li et al., 2018; Maturana & Scherer, 2015; Wu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2017; Graham et al., 2018; Choy et al., 2019; Wang, 2023; Wu et al., 2024\). Their structured spatial domains support scalable feature extraction, but their outputs are typically occupancies, semantic features, or task-specific predictions rather than a representation that can be queried directly by a surface renderer. NeuOctree uses the same structured prior for a different purpose: an octree U-Net predicts features at voxel corners from colored point clouds, and averaging shared vertices yields a consistent continuous field for the ray-intersection decoder. This establishes a direct bridge between feedforward 3D learning and surface rendering: one shared checkpoint constructs geometry and appearance without per-object optimization, while the resulting representation remains suitable for ray tracing and can serve as a native output space for 3D generation.

## 3 METHOD

Overview. NeuOctree is built around a direct surface query. An asset is represented by an occupied sparse octree and a continuous feature field defined on its voxel corners. Given a ray segment inside an occupied voxel, a shared decoder predicts whether the ray hits the surface and, if so, the intersection position, normal, and albedo. The same decoder is used for every voxel and every ray; only the octree topology and its feature values vary across assets. This separation lets us use the representation in two regimes: a feature table can be optimized for one asset, or an octree network can predict the feature field in a single feedforward pass. In both cases the output is directly consumable by a surface ray tracer and does not require mesh extraction or texture baking.

#### 3.1 STRUCTURED NEURAL SURFACE REPRESENTATION

Sparse octree and continuous feature field. We construct an octree from the input point cloud and retain its occupied leaf voxels. The octree provides a structured support for spatial queries and lets the renderer skip empty space. For an occupied voxel v, let {fv,ijk}i,j,k∈{0,1} denote its eight corner features. At a local coordinate q ∈ [0, 1]3 , the decoder reads the continuous feature field by trilinear interpolation,

$$\phi_v(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{i,j,k \in \{0,1\}} w_{ijk}(\mathbf{q}) \mathbf{f}_{v,ijk},$$

where wijk are the standard trilinear weights. Corner features shared by adjacent voxels are identified as one vertex feature, so interpolation remains continuous across voxel boundaries. In per-object optimization, the unique vertex features are learned directly. In the feedforward model, the network first predicts eight corner slots for each occupied voxel; slots that refer to the same geometric vertex are averaged before rendering. This deterministic aggregation converts voxel outputs into the same shared feature field used by the optimized representation.

For the colored feedforward setting, the interpolated field can be augmented with a voxel-local residual feature. We optionally use a pooled input-color prior in the material branch. These terms provide local appearance context without changing the surface-query interface or shared vertex field.

Ray-query decoder. For a ray traversing voxel v, we denote the local entry and exit points by pin and pout, and use d = norm(pout − pin) as the local ray direction. At a query position q, a geometry branch receives the interpolated feature, the local position, the ray direction, and the current refinement state. A separate material branch uses the same spatial feature field and ray information together with the current position and color state. Both branches are lightweight residual MLPs whose weights are shared across all voxels and rays. Their outputs are

| $(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{z}, s_h) = G_\theta(\mathbf{p}_v(\mathbf{q}), \mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}, \text{state}),$ | $\mathbf{c} = \sigma(M_\theta(\mathbf{p}_v(\mathbf{q}), \mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}, \text{state})),$ |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

where a ∈ R 3 parameterizes the position, z ∈ R 3 is normalized to produce the surface normal, sh is the hit logit, and σ is the sigmoid function. The predicted hit probability is h = σ(sh) and the predicted albedo is c ∈ [0, 1]3 .

The position parameterization is ray-local. In the segment-bounded variant used by the feedforward rendering configuration, the first component of σ(a) gives α ∈ [0, 1] and

$$\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{in}} + \alpha(\mathbf{p}_{\text{out}} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{in}}).$$

The per-object geometry compression protocol uses the equivalent ray-projection parameterization, which projects the predicted local point onto the current ray line. When the segment-bounded option is enabled, this projection is restricted to the current segment. Both forms preserve the key property that the decoder predicts a surface intersection rather than a density level set. Fourier position features are not used in the reported configurations.

#### 3.2 RECURRENT INTERSECTION REFINEMENT

Samples taken only at a voxel boundary can be far from the surface, and the boundary point depends on viewing direction even when rays observe the same surface point. A single boundary query therefore makes the decoder learn an unnecessarily view-dependent mapping. We use the predicted intersection to place later queries in the continuous feature field.

Let qk be the k-th adaptive query. The decoder produces an intersection estimate pbk and the next query is obtained by projecting this estimate back onto the current ray parameterization,

$$\mathbf{q}_{k+1} = \Pi_{\text{ray}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_k),$$

where Πray denotes ray-line projection or its segment-bounded variant, depending on the chosen position parameterization. The recurrent decoder can maintain a chained hidden state across iterations. Our pooled variant, used for the reported compression and sampling experiments, encodes the entry and exit points as ordinary members of the feature set, initializes the first interior query at the segment midpoint, and then adds further adaptively placed queries from the preceding readout. The per-query hidden increments are averaged before a shared output head predicts the final attributes. With three adaptive iterations this gives five feature reads per voxel segment (entry, exit, and three adaptive queries). The fixed-sampling ablations use the same decoder modules and replace only the adaptive positions with predetermined positions.

This construction couples all queries to the same local feature field while moving later queries toward the surface. It therefore improves localization and normal prediction without introducing a large per-voxel network or requiring volume samples along the entire ray. When the chained recurrent variant is used, intermediate predictions are also supervised; at inference we use the final stage.

#### 3.3 OCTREE TRAVERSAL AND SURFACE RENDERING

Given a camera ray, we enumerate its intersections with occupied leaf voxels in front-to-back order. Each candidate is represented by its local entry and exit points and the index of its voxel feature block. The decoder is evaluated on these candidates in chunks. A candidate is accepted when its hit probability exceeds a threshold; the first accepted candidate for each ray is selected. Its local position is converted back to octree coordinates by adding the voxel origin, while the predicted normal and albedo are retained as surface attributes. Rays for which no candidate is accepted return the background value. Thus octree traversal replaces the broad volume sampling used by neural radiance fields, and the selected attributes can be passed directly to a standard ray-tracing integrator for reshading, reflection, refraction, or other camera effects.

### 3.4 FEEDFORWARD REPRESENTATION CONSTRUCTION

The structured representation makes feature construction amortizable across objects. We build an input octree from a colored point cloud and attach point color, local point offset, and normalized global coordinates to occupied cells. An octree-based U-Net \(Wang et al., 2017\) processes this sparse hierarchy with octree convolutions, downsampling and upsampling stages, residual blocks, and skip connections. At the query depth, it predicts a tensor with eight feature slots per occupied voxel. Reshaping these slots and averaging all slots that share a geometric vertex yields the continuous field used by the decoder.

The feature extractor and the ray-query decoder are trained jointly with the same rendering losses used for per-object optimization. Consequently, a single checkpoint maps a new colored point cloud to a renderable NeuOctree without optimizing the object-specific feature table. The resulting field is also a native output space for the image-conditioned generation pipeline in Section 4.3.

# 3.5 TRAINING OBJECTIVE

Training supervision is obtained by unprojecting RGB–D images into camera rays. For every occupied ray–voxel segment before the ground-truth surface, we store a hit label, the local intersection position, the oriented normal, and the albedo. Let m denote the hit label and let P be the positive surface pairs. The pointwise objective is

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PNCH}} = \mathcal{L}_p(\mathcal{P}) + \mathcal{L}_n(\mathcal{P}) + \lambda_h \mathcal{L}_{\text{hit}}(h, m) + \lambda_c \mathcal{L}_{\text{color}}(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{c}^*; \mathcal{P}),$$

where Lp and Ln are position and normal reconstruction losses, Lhit is BCE (or its class-balanced variant), and Lcolor is a relative color reconstruction loss. The geometry terms are masked by the ground-truth hit labels; in the feedforward configuration their normalization is restricted to positive surface pairs so that empty ray–voxel segments do not dilute the surface signal.

For colored reconstruction, we supplement the pointwise terms with image-level normal and albedo SSIM losses, together with optional smoothness, color constancy, detail-gradient, and hit-topology regularizers. In compact form,

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_k w_k \left( \mathcal{L}_{\text{PNCH}}^{(k)} + \lambda_{\text{img}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{img}}^{(k)} + \lambda_{\text{topo}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{topo}}^{(k)} \right), \quad \sum_k w_k = 1,$$

where k indexes recurrent stages. Image terms are masked by valid surface pixels when configured, while hit-topology terms penalize holes, floaters, and boundary inconsistencies. Geometry-only experiments use the corresponding subset to supervise the renderer.

## 4.1 RESULTS OF PER-MESH COMPRESSION

We compare NeuOctree with the per-object optimization methods NGF \(Sivaram et al., 2024\), QNDF \(Pentapati et al., 2025\), VQAD \(Takikawa et al., 2022\), and N-BVH \(Weier et al., 2024\) over a broad range of storage budgets. VQAD is adapted to predict surface normals and intersection positions under the same geometry supervision.

Dataset and Metrics. We evaluate the seven meshes in Table 1 from the Stanford 3D Scanning Repository \(Stanford University, 1994\) and Thingi10K \(Zhou & Jacobson, 2016\), rendering 16 fixed 512 × 512 views. We report mean ray–surface position error dpm in normalized object coordinates, scaled by 103 , and oriented normal error dnorm in degrees.

Geometry Compression. As shown in Table 1, NeuOctree obtains the lowest dpm in 24 of 28 model–budget comparisons. It wins all 21 at 81–244 KiB and three of seven at 823 KiB, where QNDF takes the other four. NeuOctree also wins 12 normal-error comparisons; NGF leads mainly at the smallest payload, while QNDF is competitive only at the largest. Thus, the strongest baseline changes with payload, whereas NeuOctree maintains the most consistent positional accuracy. The curves in Fig. 4 and qualitative results in Fig. 3 corroborate this trend, showing cleaner silhouettes and better preservation of fine structures.

# 4.2 RESULTS OF FEEDFORWARD METHODS

Dataset and Metrics. Our feedforward model is trained on a randomly sampled subset of Objaverse with 32k assets. We evaluate on 30 unseen Objaverse assets and 30 ShapeNet assets to compare with per-object methods. The same metrics dpm, dnorm as in Section 4.1 are used, along with PSNR for appearance reconstruction.

Evaluation. We evaluate a shared feedforward checkpoint under the same rendering and scoring protocol used for the per-object methods. Ours-F retains the direct ray-query interface without per-object fitting. On Objaverse, its reconstruction is comparable to VQAD: it has substantially lower positional error, while VQAD remains stronger on normal accuracy and PSNR. On ShapeNet, Ours-F improves all three reported metrics over VQAD, showing that the same structured output space remains effective after the dataset shift.

Efficiency. Table 3 reports practical fitting times of each method. N-BVH requires about 3 minutes and VQAD requires tens of minutes of optimization for each object. In contrast, our feedforward model constructs a renderable representation from a colored point cloud in 50 ms with 19.5 hours for training paid once across all objects. As shown in Table 2, this removes per-object optimization while retaining competitive reconstruction quality.



Generalization. The shared model is trained on Objaverse and evaluated on ShapeNet without retraining. Despite this distribution shift, our feedforward model outperforms both VQAD and N-BVH on ShapeNet, although both baselines optimize each object separately. This result shows that the octree feature extractor and intersection decoder transfer across datasets while preserving the directly renderable output space.

# 5 CONCLUSION

We presented NeuOctree, a neural explicit surface representation for ray tracing. Shared corner features in a sparse octree and a recurrent decoder jointly predict ray–surface hits, positions, normals, and albedo, supporting compact geometry–appearance modeling, direct rendering, and open surfaces. Experiments show accurate reconstruction across meshes and budgets; the shared feedforward model constructs a renderable representation in about 50 ms, rather than minutes of per-object optimization, and transfers from Objaverse to ShapeNet without retraining. Ray-traced rendering and image-conditioned generation further demonstrate the versatility of this structured output space.

NeuOctree still requires costly high-fidelity per-object fitting and models albedo rather than viewdependent materials; generation remains a proof of concept. Future work will accelerate octree construction, extend surface queries to richer materials, lighting, and dynamics, and develop NeuOctree as a native output space for 3D generative models.

#### A.2 FEEDFORWARD NEUOCTREE

Input representation and architecture. The reported feedforward model is the depth-9 union variant. For each object, its input octree is the union of a conservatively rasterized depth-9 mesh occupancy (with colors sampled from eight rendered albedo views) and a 200k point cloud. The union is capped at 900,000 depth-9 cells; this cap limits the encoder memory without changing the depth-7 coverage used by the renderer. The octree uses full depth=4 and query depth 7. Its nine LPF input channels encode position, within-cell offset, and local color. A sparse octree U-Net maps the depth-9 input to 128 channels at query depth 7. These channels are reshaped into eight 16-dimensional corner features per voxel and averaged at shared corners, yielding the continuous vertex feature field used by the renderer. The renderer has hidden width 128, four recurrent refinements, 8-dimensional voxel features, RMSNorm, and segment-position encoding. Its hit threshold is 0.4, and the forward pass is chunked at 65,536 candidates (with a maximum render batch of 262,144 queries).

Figure 9 details the 3D U-Net used by the feedforward encoder. The sparse octree input and pointcloud features enter a multiscale encoder, are fused at the bottleneck, and are decoded with skip connections to produce the output octree and point features. Each octree residual block uses two 1 × 1 convolutions with RMSNorm and ReLU, followed by a residual addition and a final ReLU, as shown in the lower panel.

Losses. The hit term uses balanced binary cross-entropy with positive weight 1.5, Tversky weight 0.2 and (α, β, γ) = (0.3, 0.7, 1.0). Hole, floater, and boundary penalties have weights 0.1, 0.1, and 0.2. Color uses weight 2.0, a Charbonnier penalty, gradient weight 0.2, and masked SSIM weight 0.5.

## A.5 NATIVE 3D GENERATION DETAILS

The generation demonstration uses NeuOctree itself as the output domain. A sparse-octree VAE encodes the occupancy and feature tensors of a renderable NeuOctree into an 8 3 latent grid. An image-conditioned flow-matching DiT predicts both latent features and occupancy; the VAE decoder then reconstructs the octree feature field, which can be queried by the same surface renderer as the reconstructed assets. No mesh extraction or post-hoc texture baking is required, and the paper does not use this experiment for a quantitative comparison with external generation systems.

The generation setup uses 5,000 ShapeNet objects from ten categories. The VAE receives a 1283 representation, downsamples it four times to 8 3 , and uses a latent dimension of 32. It is trained with AdamW (learning rate 10−4 , weight decay 0.01), batch size 32, on four RTX 5090 GPUs. For the image-conditioned model, 16 random Blender views are rendered per asset, with one retained for testing. The DiT has approximately 0.7B parameters: width 1,536, 16 transformer blocks, 12 attention heads, and MLP width 8,192. It uses the same optimizer settings, batch size 16, and four RTX 5090 GPUs.
