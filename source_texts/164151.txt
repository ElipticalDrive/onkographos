High-density storage of big data in the information age calls for higher data transmission rates.
Holographic data storage technology, based on the principles of three-dimensional volume
holographic recording and two-dimensional planar transmission, delivers an extremely high data
transmission rate. Given the need for encoding two-dimensional data pages, spatial light modulators
(SLMs) are employed for information encoding, and the modulation region of SLMs has become a
key limiting factor for the data transmission rate of holographic data storage systems. However, to
overcome the limitation that the limited imaging area of SLMs in holographic data storage fails to
fully exploit the potential of this technology, this paper proposes an ultra-high-speed holographic
data storage system based on extending data page size. The signal beams modulated by two digital
micromirror devices (DMDs) are stitched and aligned through an optical system to achieve an
expanded data page size. The reference beam is modulated using a mask to conserve the limited pixel
resources of DMDs. Coupled with the 5:16 amplitude data encoding rule, the proposed system achieves
an ultra-high data transmission rate of 20.06 Gb/s.
Holography was first proposed by British physicist Dennis Gabor to improve the resolution of electron
microscopes1. Endowed with inherent characteristics including three-dimensional stereoscopic properties
and high information capacity, it has been widely applied across diverse fields over decades of development,
such as information display2, biomedical engineering3, and holographic data storage4. As the widespread
application of artificial intelligence technology in human society has triggered explosive information growth,
it has exerted enormous pressure on efficient data storage5. Current societal data applications have unleashed
an increasing volume of cold data, which has become “hotter” in the data-driven AI era and imposed higher
requirements on the transmission rate of data storage systems. Unlike traditional bit-by-bit storage methods,
holographic data storage leverages volume holography principles to record two-dimensional encoded data pages
in three-dimensional holographic storage media6–8. It achieves higher data transmission rates by virtue of twodimensional data page transmission for data transfer. As stored data must be encoded into two-dimensional
encoded data pages9,10, electro-optical conversion via light field modulation devices is essential. Common spatial
light modulators (SLMs) currently include digital micromirror devices (DMDs)11 and liquid crystal on silicon
(LCOS) spatial light modulators12.
The LCOS-SLM achieves multi-level signal modulation by applying different voltages to the liquid crystal,
thus realizing high information modulation for each pixel13–16. However, limited by the response rate of liquid
crystal molecules, which is usually on the millisecond order, it is not suitable for ultra-high-speed data page
encoding. In contrast, DMDs—based on semiconductor technology—enable high-speed, high-efficiency, and
reliable spatial light modulation. Boasting advantages of high resolution, high contrast, fast response time, and
high reliability, they can easily achieve high refresh rates of tens of kilohertz, finding wide applications in digital
projection17–19, optical communication20,21, and various imaging fields22–24. They further offer reliable hardware
support for high-speed data modulation in holographic data storage, making DMDs the preferred choice for
light field modulation in most current high-speed modulated holographic data storage systems25–27.
However, constrained by the fabrication process of DMDs, the size of individual micromirrors cannot be
further scaled down. Further miniaturization will face inherent challenges associated with physical resolution,
imaging quality and fabrication complexity. Consequently, the number of addressable micromirrors is inherently

DMD area allocated for signal modulation. This limitation curtails the volume of data modifiable per frame
and thereby impedes the data writing rate of the system. Furthermore, governed by its intrinsic modulation
mechanism, a DMD attains its maximum modulation frame rate exclusively when encoding binary information.
This necessitates the implementation of binary ON–OFF modulation in holographic data storage configurations,
which in turn imposes an upper limit on the data capacity of a single DMD-modulated data page. Meanwhile,
the finite imaging area of the DMD must be shared for the concurrent modulation of the reference beam. This
further compresses the spatial resources dedicated to signal beam modulation. Collectively, these constraints
form a critical bottleneck that hinders the enhancement of data writing speed in holographic data storage
systems.
This paper proposes an ultra-high-speed holographic data storage system based on extending data page size.
Without considering the response of the holographic storage media, two DMDs are used to modulate the signal
beam. The upper and lower parts of the modulated signal light are processed by an optical system to stitch
the modulated light fields, achieving a larger data modulation area. Modulation of the reference beam adopts
mask modulation, which does not occupy the limited modulation area of the DMDs. It is modulated with the
signal light part into collinear holography via an optical system. Meanwhile, in data encoding, the 3:16 encoding
scheme9 is upgraded to a 5:16 encoding scheme. The amount of information encoded per encoding unit is
increased from 8-bit to 12-bit, further enhancing the information modulation capacity per pixel. Experimental
results show that the ultra-high-speed collinear holographic data storage system based on extending data
page size can achieve ultra-high-speed data transmission rate at 20.06 Gb/s. This provides reliable support for
holographic data storage systems in the current era of “hotter” data.
Methods
Without considering the response of the holographic storage media, the data writing speed of a holographic
data storage system depends on two factors: the amount of modifiable information per frame of the SLM and
the refresh rate of the SLM. The refresh rate of SLM is a fixed value limited by the device’s modulation principle,
and it cannot be exceeded in practical storage applications. Thus, it is determined that the only way to improve
the system’s data writing speed is to increase the amount of data modulated per frame. In a collinear holographic
data storage system, the advantage of the high refresh rate of the DMD is fully leveraged. Therefore, the amount
of modifiable information per frame of the DMD determines the data writing speed of the system.
According to the two-beam interference principle of holography, an encoded data page can be divided into
two parts: the signal beam and the reference beam. Traditional collinear holographic data storage uses the
same DMD for loading both beams. To ensure equal energy between the signal beam and the reference beam,
it is required that the number of pixels used for reference beam modulation is equal to that for signal beam
modulation. However, the reference beam is arranged in a radial pattern in the outer ring, with radial lines
maintained at a 3° interval28,29. This results in a large number of pixels being required for reference beam loading,
leaving only a small portion of the already limited DMD pixels for signal beam modulation. This greatly restricts
the quantity of modulable information per frame. Therefore, this paper proposes a data encoding scheme as
shown in Fig. 1.

To make full use of the limited imaging area, the reference beam is loaded using a mask and no longer
occupies the limited modulable pixels of the DMD. The signal light part is split into two DMDs for loading. Each
DMD has a resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels, so the area available for signal light modulation is 1024 × 1536 pixels.
Considering that the effective aperture of optical devices is usually circular, the signal light area is thus encoded
as an approximately circular region as much as possible. At this point, each DMD imaging area loads 1002 data
subpages, so each encoded data page contains 2004 data subpages.
In terms of data encoding rules, a 5:16 amplitude encoding format is adopted. That is, in each encoding
unit composed of 4 × 4 pixels, only 5 pixels are in the “ON” bright state, while the remaining pixels are in the
“OFF” dark state. In this encoding mode, 4096 of these cases are selected to represent 12-bit data, meaning
each encoding unit can carry 12-bit data. After user data is converted into a bitstream, it can be encoded into
encoding units according to the encoding rules. Each data subpage contains 32 encoding units and includes a
4 × 4-pixel positioning mark at its center, which is used for positioning the data subpage and pattern correction.

In the encoding rules proposed in this paper, each encoded data page contains 2004 data sub-pages, each data
sub-page includes 32 encoding units, and each encoding unit can encode 12 bits of data. Through calculation,
it can be obtained that the single-page data capacity of the extended encoded data page can reach 769,536 bits.
In contrast, the traditional collinear holographic data storage system adopts a single DMD for modulation. It
employs the 3:16 amplitude encoding scheme and requires the same DMD to modulate both the signal beam and
the reference beam simultaneously8. Therefore, each encoded data page contains 51 data sub-pages, each data
sub-page consists of 32 encoding units, and each encoding unit encodes 8 bits of data, with the single-page data
capacity of each encoded data page thus reaching 13,056 bits.
In contrast, compared with the traditional collinear holographic data storage system using single-DMD
modulation, the collinear holographic data storage system based on dual-DMD modulation adopts 5:16
amplitude encoding. It not only retains the advantage of the high refresh rate of DMD but also gives full play to
the advantages of holographic data storage, thereby improving the data storage transfer rate. The combination of
dual-DMD modulation enables ultra-high-speed data transmission. The amount of data that can be modulated
per frame by the proposed system in this paper has increased by approximately 59 times compared with the
traditional system.
Signal beam modulation results
Based on the above-mentioned data page encoding scheme, a dual-DMD modulated holographic data storage
optical path diagram as shown in Fig. 2 is designed in the experiment. First, after beam expansion, the light
beam is split into two beams: one is used for modulating the signal beam, and the other for modulating the
reference beam. The modulation of the signal beam is split into two parts, which are incident on the two DMDs
respectively and loaded with the corresponding encoded data pages. After modulation, the upper and lower
parts of the encoded data pages are transmitted through the 4F system and combined via beam splitter (BS) 2 to
achieve imaging plane stitching of the dual DMDs. For the modulation of the reference beam, the custom-made
mask is directly transmitted through the 4F system and combined with the stitched signal beam via BS3, thus
realizing modulation of the entire data page. Finally, the light beam is scaled by the 4F system composed of lenses
L11 and L12, ensuring that the entire modulated data page can pass through the entrance pupil of the objective
lens for recording and reading in the storage media.
Typically, DMD display chips have two mounting methods: 0° and inclined 45°. As shown in Fig. 3 are the
different mounting angles of DMD chips and light incidence angles. In the experiment, to facilitate observing the
stitching of information pages between the two DMDs during optical path adjustment, a DMD chip mounted at
0° is selected. However, as shown in Fig. 3b, when the chip is mounted at 0°, there is a height difference between
the incident light direction and the emergent light direction. Thus, the optical axis of the incident beam and
that of the emergent beam are not at the same height. Moreover, affected by the manufacturing process, the tilt
angles of micromirrors on the DMD are not all the specific ± 12°. This means that after determining the height of
the emergent optical axis, the angle of the mirror and the height of the incident light cannot be directly inferred
based on the principle of optical path reversibility. This makes the collimation of each DMD require extremely
high precision, and the stitching alignment of the two DMDs poses great challenges.

Figure 5 shows the diffraction spots under different patterns. It can be seen from the figure that three DMDs
are tested respectively, distinguished by three dashed boxes in blue, orange and green. When loading the all-on
pattern, only the diffraction spots in the blue box are four light spots with uniform brightness. Similarly, when
loading the checkerboard pattern, the bright spots displayed by the detector become five bright points. Except
for the large central spot, the four surrounding bright spots have uniform brightness and are symmetrically
distributed. Thus, it can be considered that only the deflection angles of micromirrors on the DMD in the blue
box meet the requirements of holographic data storage. However, it can be seen that the remaining two DMDs
exhibit uneven bright spots when loading the two types of images. This situation will lead to uneven brightness
during the modulation of encoded patterns in holographic data storage. Some encoded “ON” pixels will diffuse
to surrounding “OFF” pixels or be submerged by noise, making it impossible to distinguish between noise and
information, which affects the accuracy of data recording and reading.
After repeating the above steps to collimate the DMDs, select those with suitable micromirror angles,
and choose two such DMDs for the construction of the overall optical path. The key consideration in system
construction is the alignment and stitching of imaging regions. According to the encoding scheme shown in
Fig. 1, the imaging regions of both DMDs are 1008 × 600 pixels, with each DMD pixel size being 13.68 μm. Thus,
the size of each DMD’s imaging region is 13,789.44 × 8208 μm. The stitching direction is vertical, so the height
difference between the emergent optical axes of the two DMDs should be greater than 8208 μm. Considering
that it is difficult to control micron-precision positioning during system construction, the height difference
between the two optical axes is set to 8.3 mm, allowing a certain gap at the stitching position, which does not
affect subsequent calculations. For the two DMD channels, 4F imaging systems are constructed using lenses (L)
L5, L6 and L7, L8 respectively for object-image transmission, and BS2 is used for beam combining and stitching.
Figure 6 shows the schematic diagram of signal beam stitching effect detection. After BS2, the combined
signal beam is detected using a charge coupled device (CCD) to observe the confocal status and stitching
alignment status of the two signal beams.

Reference beam modulation results
In holographic data storage, in addition to the modulation of the signal beam, modulation of the reference beam
is also required. Figure 9 shows the schematic diagram of the structural parameters of the custom reference beam
mask. According to Texas Instrument’s product manual, as shown in Fig. 9a, the size of each micromirror is
13.68 μm. This size includes the reflective areas of the micromirror and the gaps between the two reflective areas.
The DMD surface has hundreds of thousands of micromirrors, so it can be regarded as a grating in holographic
data storage. At this point, the grating constant can be considered as P = 13.68 μm. Therefore, to keep the grating
structures of the signal beam and the reference beam consistent, the influence of this structure is also considered
in the custom reference beam mask.
In practice, when customizing the mask, parameters of the coating process need to be considered. The
reference beam pattern consists of “bright pixels”, so a negative mask is adopted in mask design. The modulated
pattern area is transmissive, while the remaining area is chromium-plated and opaque. Under this condition,

Considering that the pixel size of the CCD in the actual optical path cannot detect a precision of 0.1 μm,
the custom-fabricated mask was inspected using a Nikon-E100 microscope. Figure 10 shows the microscopic
inspection images of the custom mask. Figure 10a shows the transmissive region of the reference beam. It can
be seen that the width of a single transmissive region marked in the figure is 12.635 μm, and the total with the

the design requirements and can satisfy the experimental requirements. In the experiment, according to the
dual-DMD modulated holographic data storage optical path diagram shown in Fig. 2, the reference beam mask
performs object-image transmission through the 4F system constructed by lenses L3 and L4. The reference beam
modulated by the reference beam mask and the signal beam modulated by the dual-DMD are combined via
beam splitter BS3, and the optical axes of the two beams are adjusted to coincide, thus realizing the ultra-highspeed collinear holographic data storage system based on dual-DMD modulation.

System test results
To test the data read–write rate of the system, the CCD in Fig. 2 is replaced with a high-speed camera. As shown
in Table 1 are the relevant parameters of the high-speed camera used in the experiment. The Revealer -XJ1200
high-speed camera is used in the experiment, which has a resolution of 1280 × 1024, a pixel size of 14.6 μm, and
a frame rate of 13,500 FPS at full frame. Since the frame rate of the DMD is 27,995 Hz, a resolution of 128 × 128
pixels are selected for the test, and the frame rate of the high-speed camera in this case is 100,000 FPS.
To avoid the interference of the holographic storage media response on the data transmission rate test of the
optical system, a mirror was used instead of the holographic storage media in the test experiment, so as to obtain
the data transmission rate of the collinear holographic data storage system during the data writing process. The
DMD-modulated signal beam is directly returned to the high-speed camera via the mirror for detection to test
the data writing rate of the system. The test images loaded on the DMD are as shown in Fig. 11. The image size
is 1024 × 768 pixels, and the central 128 × 128 pixels are marked with numbers 1–30,000 respectively, which are
used to test the actual frame rate of the system.
In the test, the signal output of the DMD is connected to the external trigger input of the high-speed camera.
Each time the DMD switches one image, the high-speed camera receives a trigger signal and captures one frame
of image. Figure 12 shows the experimental results of the tested system frame rate. This interface is the operation
interface of the high-speed camera. As can be seen from the exported data, the operating frame rate of the
high-speed camera is 30,303 FPS, and the actual detection duration is 923.8 ms. Within the 923.8 ms detection
duration, the total number of frames captured by the detector is 27,995. This means that the DMD of the system
can work accurately at 27,995 Hz, which is consistent with the original intention of the overall system design.
Based on the test results of the system frame rate and the aforementioned 5:16 amplitude encoding rule, the
single-frame data encoding capacity of the dual-DMD modulated collinear holographic data storage system is
769,536 bits. The refresh rate of the DMD is 27,995 Hz, that is, the DMD can display 27,995 frames of data per
second. This indicates that the ultra-high-speed collinear holographic data storage system based on data page
extension proposed in this paper can achieve a data transmission rate of 20.06 Gb/s during data writing.

Discussion
Holographic data storage stores two-dimensional encoded data pages in three-dimensional holographic storage
media, featuring advantages of high transfer rate and high storage density. However, limited by the size of a
single DMD pixel, the imaging area available for data modulation is limited. Meanwhile, traditional collinear
holographic data storage uses the same DMD to load both the signal beam and reference beam. As a result, a
large number of pixels cannot be used for information modulation, leading to a significant waste of limited
imaging pixels and preventing the high-rate advantage of holographic data storage from being fully utilized.
This paper proposes an ultra-high-speed holographic data storage system based on extending data page size.
In signal beam modulation, precise stitching of the dual-DMD imaging planes is achieved optically, expanding
the imaging area for signal beam modulation. Additionally, a custom-made reference beam mask is used to
realize the loading of the reference beam, avoiding the waste of limited imaging area. On this basis, combined

those of common technologies currently available.
Currently, the advantages of holographic data storage technology are constrained by the performance of
photoelectric conversion devices. With the continuous advancement of photoelectric conversion devices in the
future, the performance of holographic data storage is expected to be further improved. While maintaining highdensity storage, it will be capable of achieving ultra-high-speed data read and write rates, thereby meeting the
demands of the big data era where cold data is gradually “turning hot.” Although the ultra-high-speed collinear
holographic data storage system based on data page extension is proposed in this paper, realizing ultra-highspeed data storage in holographic storage media still requires considering the response of holographic storage
media, as well as the influence of multiple factors such as laser power for data recording, recording exposure
methods, servo spatial addressing, crosstalk, and shift multiplexing. Further in-depth research is needed in the
future to achieve ultra-high-speed data storage in holographic storage media.
