Voxel Terrain
Intro to Computer Graphics Final Project:
Voxel-Based Terrain
By: Robert Kavert, JD Stockford, and Logan Ver Hoef
The program generates a voxel-based terrain environment, using a series
of Perlin Noise functions and some crawlers for erosion. Each environment
is randomly generated on the fly, and lit with three-point lighting. The
user is given a free-flying camera to move around, and can place or remove
voxels themselves. They can also save and load environments that they like
to be reloaded later. Given the massive number of polygons this creates,
the challenge became optimizing the drawing to take such a large environment.
User's Guide:
W,A,S,D move the camera forward, left, back, and right respectively.
Q,E move the camera down and up respectively.
Hold Left-Mouse Button and move the mouse to look around.
Space Bar generates a new random environment.
C key creates a new voxel under the mouse.
X key deletes the voxel under the mouse.
1-9 Save an environment to the respective slot.
Hold 0 and press 1-9 to load the saved environment from that slot.
Hold R, G, or B, and press Up or Down arrow to increase or decrease the red, green ,or blue value of the voxel under the mouse.
To implement this, we created a three-dimensional array of voxels. The voxels are objects that can construct information on how to draw themselves based on their neighbors. The optimizations come primarily from culling out backfaces and faces that are touching each other.
Several different optimizations were tried, including oct-trees, leveraging OpenGL functionality to avoid repeated glVertex calls, frustum culling, occluded voxel elimination, occluded face elimination, and stitching voxels together. However, the biggest performance boost was observed simply by maintaining a very fast and lean tight loop (the repeated calls to Draw for each voxel). Because the previous optimizations were slowing down the tight loop, even though they were vastly eliminating the number of polygons that had to be checked and drawn, removing them resulted in a speed up.
Currently, the only major optimizations (besides a highly optimized tight loop) remaining in the project are backface culling and occluded face elimination. Backface culling is achieved though OpenGL's standard backface culling methods. Occluded face elimination is the elimination of the faces between two adjacent voxels, since they will not be visible. This is computed at generation and on the placement and removal of voxels, and is used to create a data set that can be very quickly accessed for drawing.
Picking via the normal OpenGL method was problematic, as we needed information on which face of a voxel was selected, and the data type used for the names of rendered items is a 16 bit integer. This was obviously too small to contain a unique name for every face of every voxel, so a multi-level sweep approach was taken for selection.
The original idea of smoothing the edges of voxels was exchanged in favor of the faster and more visually appealing color gradient. The elevation-dependent color gradient achieves the same goal of smoothing transitions between levels, and is most visible when lighting is disabled.
Terrain generation is achieved through repeated overlays of a perlin noise function. Caves are generated by randomly placed walkers that traverse through the active voxels and carve them out around them.
