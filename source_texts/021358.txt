ABSTRACT
We present Ignis, a GPU Eulerian fluid solver capable of simulating
and rendering at VR resolutions and refresh rates. Core to our approach are an approximate shadowing technique and an adaptive
dithering technique, which allow us to cheaply render fully lit volumes at high resolutions and under a range of visibility conditions.
We discuss the design decisions which enable our solver to run at
interactive rates.

Our requirements for runtime fidelity are much higher than
a flipbook. While a flipbook may run at a reduced framerate, an
interactive simulation must run at the refresh rate of the headset to
avoid input lag. Second, while flipbooks are typically rendered as
effects in the distance, in VR our user is immersed in the simulation
volume, with single voxels occupying an unbounded amount of
screenspace. So, we must be able to perform expensive raymarch
operations on every pixel in each of the HMD’s high resolution
displays. These challenges, and others, motivate the design of Ignis’
volumetric solver and renderer, which we discuss in the remainder
of this paper.

SIMULATION
Ignis utilises a dense Eulerian fluid solver using operator splitting
[Stam 1999] to approximately solve the Navier-Stokes equations.
We track, at each grid cell centre, a velocity, density, and temperature which are each stored in 3D textures with 16-bit float formats.
Using half-precision storage has negligible impact on the visual
quality of our simulations, while effectively doubling their performance on bandwidth constrained portions of the solver.
Each simulation presented is specified using a custom injection
kernel which hooks into the rest of our solver. These kernels receive
as input the voxel position, and timestep, and add density and
temperature to the simulation, as well as applying arbitrary forces,
such as buoyancy.

Advection
We use semi-lagrangian advection with a MacCormack backtrace
[Selle et al. 2008]. Though we use linear sampling for velocity during
the backtrace, cubic interpolation is used for our final resample of
all advected properties, and we also optionally apply a vorticity
confinement force as in [Fedkiw et al. 2001].
2.2
Projection
Pressure projection is solved using geometric multigrid, using a
single V-cycle with 1 pre and 2 post-smooths, and the fullweight
and trilinear transfer operators described in [Trottenberg et al.
2000]. Jacobi iteration is used for smoothing, and we approximately
solve on our 3rd, coarsest, grid using red-black Gauss-Seidel with
successive over-relaxation (SOR).

RENDERING
Our volumetric renderer supports up to two shadowed directional
lights, anisotropic scattering using the Henyey-Greenstein phase
function, and light emission due to blackbody radiation. In VR, pixel
counts can easily exceed voxel counts, so we opt to move as much
of the rendering process onto the simulation grid as we can.
3.1
Approximate Shadowing
Rather than performing a full shadow raycast per voxel (or worse,
per primary raymarch step), we utilise an approximate shadowing
algorithm. Baking axis-aligned light extinction into two RGB textures is commonly used to relight VFX flipbooks in real-time use
cases, a technique typically referred to as 6-point, or 6-way lighting.
Extinction in arbitrary light directions is then approximated by
linear combination of the extinction along the appropriate axes.
We extend this technique to work on an interactive simulation
by observing that axis-aligned extinction can be computed via
cumulative products of the extinction along each axis. In practice
since each axis writes to a different texture, we can schedule these
compute kernels to run concurrently.
Since our primary raycast is mainly bottlenecked on GPU bandwidth, we also opt to collapse the axis aligned extinctions down
to two interpolated directional extinction values. This allows us to
compute approximate shadows along our raymarch without the
full bandwidth cost of accessing all 6 directional shadow textures.
Even with this additional packing step, precomputation of shadows costs <1ms on all of our experiments.

We instead opt to use dithering without any temporal accumulation. In practice, due to our high target resolutions, differing

the resulting image is crisp, temporally stable, and renders quickly.
We can even adapt our step size to varying performance conditions,
which we refer to as adaptive dithering. Adaptive dithering is critical to meeting our frame targets, particularly as shading costs vary.
For the scenarios in our supplementary video, adapted step counts
vary by a factor of 4. Without dithering, as shown in the video, this
results in juddering of the image. With dithering we have a stable
image free of artifacts except for a small amount of noise.

RESULTS
As shown in Table 1, we tested our solver on a range of scenarios
designed to test quality and performance under varying densities,
velocities, and light levels. All simulations were run with a 1.5cm
voxel scale, and rendered at a resolution of 2016 x 2240 x 2. The
solver is implemented in DirectX 12 and OpenVR, using a NVIDIA
RTX 3080 and a Valve Index HMD. In all scenarios our frametimes
are well within the 11.11ms frame budget for a 90 Hz HMD.
5
LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Currently, Ignis is primarily limited by the low simulation resolution
necessary to run in VR on current hardware. However, many games
and training use cases may only require full interactivity on a
portion of a volumetric scene. Future work will investigate the
composition of interactive and pre-simulated volumetrics in order
to enable VR volumetrics with larger bounding volumes and voxel
resolutions. In addition, Ignis will be extended to simulate thin
flame combustion, and respond to collisions with obstacles in the
environment.
