1. Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [21] represents a scene with
millions of anisotropic Gaussians. A renderer projects each
Gaussian to a screen-space ellipse [50], sorts the resulting
splats by the depth of their centers, and blends them with
the over operator [37]. The depth order changes with the
camera. 3DGS recomputes the sort from scratch in every
1
frame.
The cost of the sort depends on the platform. On desktop
GPUs, a radix sort in CUDA or WebGPU orders millions of
splats in well under a millisecond [13, 36]. Web and mobile
viewers draw one instanced quad per splat with the hardware rasterizer and sort the splats in a CPU thread [25]. Web
pages and phone browsers show captured scenes with these
viewers and download each scene before its first frame. Visionary [13] reports 173 ms for a CPU sort of 6 million
splats. In our OpenGL viewer, the CPU sort takes 24.1 ms
of a 26.8 ms frame. Each new order also uploads an index
buffer with one entry per splat.
Existing work reduces this cost in three ways. The first
way keeps a per-splat order and computes it less often.
Neo [34] and Lumina [10] reuse the sorted lists of previous frames and update them per splat. The second way
removes the order. Sort-free methods replace the over operator with weighted sums [17], order-independent transparency [6, 32, 46], or stochastic transparency [18, 23]. The
new operators differ from the operator that the scene was
optimized with, and stochastic estimators add noise at realtime sample counts. The third way orders groups of splats.
Duplex-GS [28] sorts cells and blends the Gaussians inside
a cell with a weighted sum. StreamingGS [49] sorts voxels and still sorts the Gaussians inside each voxel in every
frame. Outside Gaussian splatting, Voxlines [35] sorts the
voxels of a streamline rendering and stores line orders along
the coordinate axes inside each voxel.
AbacusGS also orders groups of splats and keeps the
over operator inside each group. On an abacus, the position of a bead stores a digit and also holds a partial result
of the computation. AbacusGS uses the order of its storage
in the same way. The splats are grouped into small octree
boxes, and the splats of one box usually cover a small patch
of surface. The depth order inside a box changes little with
the camera, apart from a reversal when the camera moves to
the other side of the patch. AbacusGS sorts each box once
at write time along an axis that it derives from the training
cameras. At render time, AbacusGS sorts only the visible
boxes, and one dot product per box decides whether the box
is read forward or backward. A short fine-tuning adapts the
model to the remaining ordering errors. On a hardware rasterizer, each visible box becomes one command of a multidraw indirect call, and the frame needs no per-splat index
buffer.
Keeping the over operator has practical value. Existing
3DGS models, training code, and viewers assume this operator. AbacusGS converts an existing model with a short
fine-tuning, and a viewer changes only how it orders and
draws the splats. Sort-free methods retrain or fine-tune the
scene for a new operator.
Our paper has three contributions. The first is the task
of converting an existing 3DGS model to a blending order
stored in the scene layout, together with an evaluation that
compares each converted model with a control fine-tuned
for the same number of steps and times all methods in one
hardware rasterizer with ordering on the CPU. The second
is the method, which stores one order per octree box along
an axis derived from the training cameras, chooses only
the reading direction in every frame, fine-tunes the model
with exactly this order, and draws each visible box with one
multi-draw command. The third is a set of findings on 13
standard scenes. Fine-tuning reduces the average loss of the
two-level order from 1.27 to 0.13 dB PSNR. An octree BSP
order, which is exact for the cells, loses more PSNR than
the centroid-depth order for overlapping Gaussians. The
speedup is largest when the CPU orders the splats, where
frames render 4.5 times faster than with per-splat sorting,
and it shrinks when the GPU sorts or the resolution grows.
A compact format of 17 bytes per splat and a CUDA tile
renderer use the same order, and we will release the code.
2. Related Work
We divide the related work by four smaller problems behind
our goal. The four problems are computing the per-splat order quickly, compositing without an order, ordering groups
of splats, and storing the scene compactly.
Computing the per-splat order. 3DGS [21] sorts one
key per pair of tile and splat with a GPU radix sort.
OneSweep [1] is a faster least-significant-digit radix sort
for GPUs. FlashGS [9] reduces the number of pairs
with a tighter intersection test between tiles and ellipses.
HiGS [36] reports sort times below 0.1 ms at 4K with a
hierarchical renderer. GS-TG [20] shares one sort among
groups of screen tiles in a hardware accelerator. WebSplatter [15] sorts with a hierarchical radix sort in WebGPU, and
Texture3dgs [40] lays out a bitonic sort for the texture cache
of mobile GPUs. StopThePop [38] sorts hierarchically per
pixel and removes popping at extra cost, and VRSplat [44]
fine-tunes models with the sort of StopThePop for virtual reality. Neo [34] and Lumina [10] reuse the sorted lists of previous frames on mobile hardware, and the WebGL viewer
splat [25] skips the sort while the view direction barely
changes. CoherentRaster [42] shares per-splat sorts among
the views of a light-field display. Each of these methods
keeps one entry per splat in the order of every frame, and
AbacusGS keeps one entry per visible box.
Compositing without an order. WSR [17] replaces the
over operator with a learned weighted sum. MobileGS [6] combines order-independent rendering with distillation and pruning for mobile devices. Moment-based transmittance [32] and an active-set method [46] improve orderindependent transparency [29]. Hybrid transparency [14]

Ordering groups of splats. Duplex-GS [28] sorts cells
of 5 to 10 neural Gaussians per tile and blends inside each
cell with a weighted sum. StreamingGS [49] orders voxels, corrects the errors between voxels with fine-tuning,
and sorts the Gaussians inside each voxel in every frame
on dedicated hardware. Hierarchical 3DGS [22], OctreeGS [39], and V3DG [47] group splats into trees or clusters
to select a level of detail and then sort the selected splats.
Voxlines [35] is the closest prior design. Voxlines renders transparent streamlines, sorts uniform voxels in every
frame, stores line orders along the coordinate axes inside
each voxel, and reverses a stored order for the opposite direction. Depth-presorted triangle lists [4] let each viewpoint
select an exactly sorted subsequence of one stored triangle
sequence, and presorted index buffers draw hair without a
runtime sort [41]. A VRChat viewer [30] stores orders of a
whole Gaussian scene for a set of view directions. Front-toback traversal of presorted voxel grids is a classic technique
of volume rendering [11], BSP trees [12] give an exact visibility order for disjoint cells, and SVRaster [43] renders
sparse voxels in a view-dependent Morton order. AbacusGS
differs from these methods in three ways. AbacusGS stores
one order per adaptive box along an axis derived from the
training cameras, fine-tunes the model with exactly this order, and draws each box with one indirect command.

3DGS sorts the splats by the depth zi = f · (µi − o) of their
centers. The center-depth order approximates the per-pixel
order [38]. A CUDA tile renderer sorts one key per pair of
tile and splat. A hardware rasterizer sorts the N depths and
uploads the permutation as an index buffer. Both renderers
repeat the sort in every frame.
We seek an order whose per-frame cost grows with the
number of visible groups of splats, together with model
parameters for which Eq. (1) under this order stays close
to the images of 3DGS. The groups are B boxes. Box b
holds nb ≤ K splats in a contiguous range that starts at
startb . The centroid of these splats is µ̄b , and its depth is
z̄b = f · (µ̄b − o). Each box also stores a unit axis ab for
the order of its splats.
3.2. Boxes
AbacusGS sorts the splats by the 48-bit Morton code [31] of
their centers and builds an adaptive octree on these codes.
The leaves are the largest octree nodes that hold at most K
splats, and K=128 unless stated otherwise. A node that
holds more than K splats at depth 16 is split into chunks of
K splats. Each leaf is a box. The splats of a box occupy
one contiguous range in the same way as a row of a CSR
matrix. A box holds 31 splats on average. The header of
a box stores its start offset, its octree cell, a culling radius,
and its axis ab .
Storing the scene compactly. Niedermayr et al. [33]
cluster colors and shapes into codebooks with sensitivityaware k-means and render directly from the compressed
data. Lee et al. [26] remove splats with learned masks and
store geometry in learned codebooks. LightGaussian [8]
prunes splats, distills spherical harmonics, and quantizes
the remaining attributes. HAC [5] compresses anchor-based
scenes with a hash-grid context model. A recent survey [2]
compares these and later methods. The compressed PLY
format of PlayCanvas [19] quantizes each splat to 16 bytes
relative to the bounds of its chunk of 256 splats. The compact format of AbacusGS quantizes positions relative to octree cells, follows Niedermayr et al. for the codebooks, and
also stores the blending order in its layout.

3.4. Order-aware fine-tuning
AbacusGS fine-tunes a converged 3DGS model with the order of Eq. (2) in both the forward and the backward pass.
Our implementation replaces the depth key of each splat in
a differentiable tile rasterizer [48] with κi = 212 r(bi ) + ℓi .
Here r(b) is the rank of box b in the box order, and ℓi is the
position of splat i in the traversal of its box. The list of every tile then follows π, and the gradients use the same order.
The box partition and the stored orders stay fixed during
fine-tuning because they are part of the stored format. Densification is disabled. The position learning rate is the final
value of the 3DGS schedule, and the other learning rates are
the constant values of gsplat [48]. The float model trains for
3,000 steps and the compact model for 10,000 steps.
Figure 3 compares the two-level order with the per-splat

fine-tuning, the two-level order leaves visible blotches, for

Box order. Octree cells admit an exact visibility order. A
BSP traversal visits first, at every node, the child on the
camera side of each split plane [12]. The traversal needs no
sort and changes only when the camera crosses a split plane.
AbacusGS sorts the boxes by centroid depth instead. Splats
extend across the faces of their cells. When a split plane is
nearly parallel to the viewing rays, the BSP order can draw
a far splat of one box before a near splat of the neighboring
box. The centroid-depth order stays closer to the per-splat
depth order that the model was trained with (Sec. 4.3). The
centroid-depth order costs one sort of the visible boxes and
one dot product per box in every frame.

3.5. Compact format
cusGS issues one indirect draw command per visible box
with glMultiDrawElementsIndirect. The vertex shader reads the box index from gl_DrawID. The
shader maps instance k to splat startb +k, or to splat
startb +nb −1−k when box b is read in reverse (Fig. 2c). No
per-splat index buffer is built or uploaded. Platforms without multi-draw expand the box ranges into an index buffer.
The hardware blends from back to front, and the renderer
traverses the order of Eq. (2) in reverse.
The position of a splat is stored relative to the octree cell
of its box with 10 bits per axis. Opacity, the three DC
color coefficients, and the mean log-scale take 8 bits each.
One 16-bit index selects the scale anisotropy and the rotation from a shape codebook. A second 16-bit index selects the spherical-harmonic bands 1 to 3 from a color codebook. Each codebook has 65,536 entries. The shape codebook clusters log-Euclidean features of the normalized covariance, and isotropic splats share one shape code regardless of their rotation. Both codebooks come from k-means
weighted by the sensitivity of each splat. Niedermayr et
al. [33] measure this sensitivity with the sum of all pixel values, and we measure it with the training loss. Fine-tuning
keeps the codebook assignments fixed and refines the codebook entries and the remaining per-splat values. The scalar
values are quantized to 8 or 10 bits after fine-tuning. A box
header takes 16 bytes, and a splat takes 13 bytes. Codebooks, box headers, and box centroids computed at load
time raise the average to 17.3 bytes per splat in memory.
Our CUDA renderer decodes a variant of the format with
larger box headers inside its projection kernel without a decompression pass.
CUDA tile renderer. The CUDA renderer replaces the
global sort of pairs of tile and splat with a two-level index.
Pairs of tile and box are sorted by tile and by box rank. Each
tile streams the splats of its boxes in traversal order, skips
the splats whose screen rectangle misses the tile, and blends
the remaining splats in order. On a synthetic test scene with
300,000 Gaussians, the CUDA renderer matches the differentiable reference renderer at 108 dB PSNR or higher with
the same order and the same data. The OpenGL renderer
matches the reference renderer at 54.4 dB PSNR in the median over 26 test views.

4.1. Image quality
The first question is how much image quality the two-level
order costs. Table 1 reports the average metrics of each
dataset, and Fig. 4 shows the PSNR change of every scene
against its control. Without fine-tuning, the two-level order
lowers PSNR on all three datasets. Order-aware fine-tuning
brings the float model close to its control in PSNR, SSIM,
and LPIPS. The compact model loses slightly more against
a control that trains for the same 10,000 steps. With the
compact format, the four indoor scenes of Mip-NeRF 360
lose the most, and the two Deep Blending scenes and Treehill lose almost nothing. Figure 5 shows two test views with
a large loss of the compact model. The crops of the compact model differ from the control mainly in smooth regions
such as the sky and the ceiling. On the gsplat base models,
a second fine-tuning seed gives a similar loss (Tab. 5).
We fine-tune StochasticSplats [23] from the same base
models with the protocol of its authors. StochasticSplats
approaches its base model only at 1,024 samples per pixel.

5. Conclusion
AbacusGS stores the blending order of 3DGS in the layout
of the scene. In every frame, AbacusGS sorts the visible
boxes and reads each box in a stored order or in its reverse,
and a short fine-tuning adapts an existing model to this order. The over operator of 3DGS stays unchanged, and the
image quality stays close to that of an equally fine-tuned
3DGS model. On a hardware rasterizer, every visible box
becomes one draw command, and the frame needs neither
a per-splat sort nor an index upload. The same layout also
yields a compact format and a faster CUDA tile renderer.
4.5. Limitations
The two-level order approximates the per-splat depth order,
which itself approximates the per-pixel order. Every model
needs order-aware fine-tuning, and the box order still sorts
the visible boxes in every frame. Splats larger than their box
receive no special treatment. On desktop GPUs with fast
radix sorts, the CUDA renderer gains less than the OpenGL
renderer. Our OpenGL renderer reads splats decoded to
floating point, and decoding the compact format in the vertex shader is not implemented. The browser results come
