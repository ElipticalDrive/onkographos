Abstract
The manifold hypothesis proposes that high-dimensional data concentrates near
low-dimensional curved surfaces in the ambient space. While extensively studied in static
image and text domains, its applicability to spatiotemporal data — where spatial and
temporal structure are jointly encoded — remains less explored. We present a minimal
experiment probing whether a simple autoencoder trained on a 2D+time reaction-diffusion
system (Gray-Scott) spontaneously discovers low-dimensional manifold structure in its
latent representations. We simulate five qualitatively distinct pattern regimes (spots, stripes,
waves, labyrinth, holes) and train a fully-connected autoencoder with a 16-dimensional
bottleneck on spatiotemporal patches. Analyzing the latent space via PCA, t-SNE, intrinsic
dimensionality estimation (TwoNN), and latent interpolation, we find that the network
compresses the spatiotemporal data onto an approximately 3-dimensional submanifold
(TwoNN estimate: 3.19) despite the 16-dimensional bottleneck. Qualitatively distinct pattern
regimes partially separate in latent space, though linear classification accuracy (33%)
indicates that the learned geometry is not fully discriminative at the chosen parameter
settings. We discuss implications for the broader question of how artificial and biological
neural networks represent spatiotemporal structure, and situate the results within the
literature on the manifold hypothesis and neural representations of dynamical systems. ---
1. Introduction
The manifold hypothesis has become a foundational intuition in modern deep learning: the claim that
natural data, despite living nominally in a very high-dimensional space, occupies a much
lower-dimensional geometric structure — a manifold embedded in that ambient space [1, 2]. In the
context of neural network representations, the hypothesis becomes: trained networks learn maps that
"unroll" or "flatten" this latent manifold into a representation more amenable to downstream tasks.
Existing investigations of this hypothesis have focused predominantly on static data — images, text
embeddings, protein structure — where the manifold is a structure in a fixed feature space. The
spatiotemporal case is qualitatively different. Here, the data is not a snapshot but a trajectory: a field
evolving over time, where the spatial organization and the temporal dynamics are jointly structured. The

relevant manifold, if one exists, must encode not where the system is but how it moves through state
space over time.
This paper addresses a minimal version of the question: does a simple autoencoder trained on
spatiotemporal reaction-diffusion patterns learn representations that respect the manifold structure of
the underlying dynamical system? We choose the Gray-Scott reaction-diffusion model [3] as our test
system for three reasons. First, it produces a rich diversity of qualitatively distinct patterns (spots,
stripes, labyrinthine structures, traveling waves, and holes) by varying just two parameters (F and k),
creating a two-dimensional parameter manifold whose qualitative geometry is well understood. Second,
it is computationally cheap to simulate, making the experiment fully reproducible on a CPU in under 20
minutes. Third, the ground truth dimensionality of the system is known — the qualitative behavior is
parameterized by essentially two dimensions — providing a reference against which learned
representations can be compared.
We are motivated by a broader question rooted in discussions of biological neural computation: do the
"tangled manifolds" observed in neural population recordings [4, 5, 6] arise because the brain actively
learns manifold-structured representations of a manifold-structured world, or are they an architectural
consequence of how neural populations represent information? The present experiment is not designed
to answer this question directly, but to establish the minimal conditions under which an artificial network
spontaneously discovers manifold structure in spatiotemporal data.
2. Background
2.1 The Manifold Hypothesis
The manifold hypothesis [1] posits that the support of natural data distributions, while nominally
high-dimensional, concentrates near a low-dimensional manifold M ⊂ Rn. Under this hypothesis, the
effective dimension of the data is the intrinsic dimension of M, which may be far smaller than n.
Evidence for this view comes from the success of dimensionality reduction methods (PCA,
autoencoders, manifold learning), from intrinsic dimensionality estimates of image datasets, and from
the observation that generative models trained in low-dimensional latent spaces can produce coherent
high-dimensional outputs [7].
2.2 Neural Manifolds in Biological Systems
Neuroscience has provided some of the most direct experimental evidence for manifold-structured
representations. Recordings from place cells and grid cells in the rodent hippocampal-entorhinal
system reveal that population activity during spatial navigation traces a toroidal manifold — a finding
confirmed by applying topological data analysis to neural recordings without presupposing the topology
[4]. Head direction cells encode orientation on a ring (S¹) [5]. Motor cortex activity during reaching tasks
occupies a low-dimensional curved surface in neural state space [6].

These findings suggest that the brain does not represent information uniformly across its full
high-dimensional state space, but organizes representations around low-dimensional geometric
structures matched to the structure of the represented variable.
2.3 Spatiotemporal Representations
Less is known about the geometry of neural representations of spatiotemporal processes — patterns
that unfold continuously in both space and time. Early convolutional approaches to video understanding
treat time as a sequence index rather than a geometric dimension. More recent work on neural PDE
solvers [8] and neural operators [9] encodes spatiotemporal fields as functional objects, but their
internal representations have been less studied from a manifold geometry perspective.
The Gray-Scott system provides an ideal test case because its macroscopic state space (the space of
qualitatively distinct pattern types) has known low-dimensional structure, while the microscopic state
(the U and V concentration fields on the full grid) is high-dimensional. A network that learned a good
representation should recover something close to the macroscopic structure.
2.4 Intrinsic Dimensionality Estimation
Several methods exist for estimating the intrinsic dimensionality of a dataset without knowing the true
underlying manifold. We use the TwoNN estimator [10], which exploits the ratio of distances to the first
and second nearest neighbors of each point. Under the assumption that the data lies on a smooth
d-dimensional manifold, this ratio follows a Pareto distribution with shape parameter d, whose
maximum likelihood estimate gives the intrinsic dimensionality. The method is parameter-free and
computationally efficient.
3. Methods
3.1 Gray-Scott Simulation
The Gray-Scott model [3] describes the interaction of two chemical species U and V via the equations:
∂U/∂t = D_U ∇²U − UV² + F(1 − U)
∂V/∂t = D_V ∇²V + UV² − (F + k)V
where D_U = 0.16, D_V = 0.08, F is the feed rate, and k is the kill rate. We discretize on a 48×48 grid
with periodic boundary conditions, using Euler integration with dt = 1.0 over 1200 timesteps.
Simulations are initialized with U = 1 everywhere and V = 0 everywhere, except a central 12×12 region
perturbed by Gaussian noise.

For each regime, we generate 20 simulations with small Gaussian perturbations to the parameters (σ =

3.2 Feature Extraction
From each simulation, we extract a spatiotemporal patch by cropping a 24×24 region centered on the
grid across all frames and both channels (U and V). The patch is flattened to a vector of dimension 15 ×
2 × 24 × 24 = 17,280. This preserves the full spatiotemporal structure of the patch in a form amenable
to a fully-connected autoencoder.
For comparison, we also compute eight hand-crafted statistics per simulation: mean and standard
deviation of U and V (averaged over frames), temporal variation in U and V (standard deviation of
frame-to-frame differences), and spatial gradient magnitude in both x and y directions.
3.3 Autoencoder Architecture
We train a fully-connected autoencoder with the following architecture:
Encoder: Input (17,280) → Dense (128, ReLU) → Dense (128, ReLU) → Bottleneck (16, linear)
Decoder: Bottleneck (16) → Dense (128, ReLU) → Dense (128, ReLU) → Output (17,280, sigmoid)
The network is implemented in pure NumPy without any deep learning framework, making it fully
transparent and reproducible. We train with MSE reconstruction loss, SGD with learning rate 5×10■■,
batch size 16, for 60 epochs. The entire experiment runs on a CPU in approximately 15–20 minutes.
3.4 Latent Space Analysis
We analyze the 16-dimensional latent representations via:
1. PCA — principal components analysis to visualize the first two dimensions and estimate
variance explained.
2. t-SNE — non-linear dimensionality reduction with perplexity 20 for visualization.
3. TwoNN intrinsic dimensionality — maximum likelihood estimation of the manifold dimension.
4. Linear classification — 5-fold cross-validated logistic regression to quantify regime

4.3 Intrinsic Dimensionality
The central quantitative result is the intrinsic dimensionality of the learned latent representations. The
TwoNN estimator gives an intrinsic dimension of d ≈ 3.19, far below the 16-dimensional bottleneck and

5. Discussion
5.1 Spontaneous Emergence of Low-Dimensional Representations
The main finding — that a 16-dimensional bottleneck produces representations with intrinsic dimension
approximately 3 — confirms that the autoencoder spontaneously discovers the low effective
dimensionality of the spatiotemporal data. This is consistent with the manifold hypothesis applied to
Ritschel (2026) — Spatiotemporal Manifold Geometry — Page 9
spatiotemporal fields: the space of "interesting" Gray-Scott patterns is not 17,280-dimensional but
approximately 3-dimensional, and the network exploits this structure.
The discrepancy between the PCA estimate (2–4 dimensions for 90–95% variance) and the TwoNN
estimate (3.2) is expected: PCA measures linear dimensionality while TwoNN measures the local
intrinsic dimensionality of the manifold, which may be curved in ways that require more linear
dimensions to approximate.
5.2 Reconstruction vs. Discrimination
The finding that hand-crafted features outperform learned features on linear classification is instructive.
The autoencoder is not trained to discriminate regimes — it is trained to reconstruct. As a result, its
latent space encodes variation from all sources: regime identity, random initialization, parameter
perturbation, and spatiotemporal transients. The hand-crafted features, by contrast, explicitly capture
mean statistics that happen to be regime-discriminative (mean U and V values differ systematically
across regimes).
This points to a more general principle: the geometry of a learned representation reflects the training
objective, not just the structure of the data. A reconstruction-trained autoencoder and a
classification-trained encoder will discover different manifold structures even from the same data,
emphasizing the same dimensions that are relevant to their respective objectives.
5.3 Continuity of the Gray-Scott Parameter Space
The interpolation results suggest that the autoencoder has learned a representation that respects the
continuity of the Gray-Scott parameter space. The five regimes we labeled are not discretely separated
in (F, k) space — they form a continuous map with smooth transitions between qualitative behaviors.
The fact that cross-class interpolations in latent space produce coherent intermediate patterns (Figure
7) suggests the network has captured something of this continuous structure rather than treating the
regimes as isolated clusters.
This connects to the broader question of how neural representations handle continuously
parameterized families of behaviors — a question relevant to motor control, sensory processing, and
language understanding in both artificial and biological systems.
5.4 Limitations
Several limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn from this experiment:
Scale. The dataset (100 simulations) and network (two hidden layers of 128 units) are minimal.
Larger-scale experiments might reveal richer geometric structure or different intrinsic dimensionality
estimates.
Architecture. A fully-connected architecture treats the spatiotemporal patch as an unstructured vector,
discarding translational symmetry and the continuous nature of space and time. A convolutional or
equivariant architecture would be better matched to the structure of the data.
Ritschel (2026) — Spatiotemporal Manifold Geometry — Page 10
Single system. The Gray-Scott model is a specific choice. Whether similar results hold for other
spatiotemporal systems — particularly those with less regular pattern structure, such as turbulence or
neural dynamics — is an open question.
Patch-based representation. By using a center crop, we discard boundary effects and long-range
spatial correlations. A full-field representation would capture more of the system's macroscopic
structure.
5.5 Implications for Neural Computation
The results are consistent with, though do not prove, the hypothesis that biological neural circuits
representing spatiotemporal processes organize their representations on low-dimensional manifolds.
The approximate 3-dimensionality of the learned representation (corresponding roughly to two
parameters plus initialization noise) mirrors the structure of neural manifolds observed in motor cortex
[6] and hippocampal spatial representations [4], where the manifold dimensionality matches the
dimensionality of the represented variable.
The key open question is whether biological systems actively learn to compress spatiotemporal
representations onto manifolds (as the autoencoder does here by minimizing reconstruction error), or
whether manifold structure arises from architectural constraints (e.g., the bottleneck structure of cortical
hierarchies) without explicit optimization pressure. The present experiment cannot distinguish between
these possibilities, but provides a minimal demonstration that learning-based manifold compression of
spatiotemporal data is feasible at small scale.
6. Conclusion
We have presented a minimal experiment showing that a simple autoencoder trained on Gray-Scott
reaction-diffusion patterns spontaneously discovers low-dimensional manifold structure in its latent
representations. The intrinsic dimensionality of the learned space (≈3.2) is far smaller than the
bottleneck dimension (16) and closely matches the effective number of degrees of freedom in the
Gray-Scott parameter space. Latent interpolations produce coherent intermediate patterns, and partial
regime separation is visible in both linear and non-linear projections.
The experiment is intentionally small — designed to be fully reproducible on a laptop CPU in under 20
minutes, with all code publicly available. We hope it serves as a useful minimal baseline for future work
on the geometry of spatiotemporal neural representations, and as an accessible entry point for
researchers interested in connecting the mathematics of manifolds to the empirical study of neural
network representations.
