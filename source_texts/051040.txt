Abstract
Objective. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) often has a truncated acquired field of
view (FOV) due to the limited detector size, leading to image reconstruction from truncated projection data. CBCT reconstructions using an image volume that just encloses the acquired FOV
exhibit reconstruction artifacts due to attenuation in the tissues outside the image volume. On
the other hand, extending the high-resolution voxel volume far enough beyond the FOV to fully
enclose the imaged body often leads to a significant increase of the computational complexity in
model based iterative reconstruction techniques. We propose a multi-resolution reconstruction
model that eliminates the out-of-FOV reconstruction artifacts and enables accurate recovery of
Hounsfield unit (HU) values within the FOV. Approach. We propose a multi-resolution extended
reconstruction volume (MR-ERV) approach that extends the image volume beyond the FOV using
separate extension volumes with coarser voxel representation, leading to appropriate modeling of
the observed rays outside the FOV without significant increase of the computational complexity.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that by augmenting the model with a simple projection extrapolation yields a further reduction of the out-of-FOV artifacts. In this study, the model is evaluated
with model based iterative reconstruction minimization using high-resolution 3D CBCT data. The
optimization problems considered are non-negativity constrained least-squares estimation, with
and without regularization. The optimization is performed using a primal-dual hybrid gradient
algorithm. Results. The proposed MR-ERV model effectively removes out-of-FOV reconstruction
artifacts and it also achieves accurate HU values within the FOV when the volume extension fully
encloses the imaged body in the transaxial direction. Significance. The MR-ERV model provides a
platform for computationally efficient and accurate model based iterative reconstruction of CBCT
data.
1. Introduction
One of the most common medical imaging techniques is computed tomography (CT), which provides
high-resolution cross-sectional images of the human body and is widely used in research, diagnosis, and
treatment planning (Khadivi and Kalender 2006). Clinical helical CT uses typically a fan-shaped x-ray
beam and a thin two-dimensional detector array, which requires multiple rotations and table movement
to cover the entire imaging volume. Cone beam CT (CBCT), on the other hand, is a three-dimensional
(3D) CT technique which provides volumetric reconstruction from a single rotation by emitting a coneshaped x-ray beam onto a larger 2D flat-panel detector (Mozzo et al 1998). CBCT can produce highresolution 3D images of small fields of view (FOV) using a low radiation dose, making it a well-suited
technique for applications such as dental imaging, maxillofacial treatment planning, extremity imaging,
and image-guided radiotherapy applications (Scarfe and Farman 2008, Carrino et al 2014). However,

compared to helical CT, CBCT is more sensitive to imaging artifacts, such as beam hardening, scatter,
and artifacts arising from limited-FOV (Hsieh 2003, Yang 2016, Samei and Pelc 2020).
In this article, we focus on artifacts arising from a limited FOV, known as out-of-FOV artifacts,
which occur when the imaged object is larger than the FOV. In CT, the FOV is the region covered by
the detector through x-ray projections at all projection angles, illustrated as the circled area in figure 1.
When the object extends beyond the FOV, as shown in figure 1, the image is reconstructed from truncated and incomplete projection data, a problem known in the mathematical literature as local tomography. Local tomography is typically caused by one of the following conditions: 1) the imaged object
exceeds the available FOV, or 2) when only a specific region of the body is of interest, collimators can
be used to focus the x-ray beam to a localized region of interest (ROI) to minimize radiation exposure
outside the ROI (Huang et al 2021).
CBCT reconstruction can produce accurate high-resolution reconstruction only in the FOV, while
resolution is much poorer outside the FOV because tissues outside the FOV contribute only to some
of the projection images. From a computational point of view, it would be desirable to utilize in the
reconstruction an image volume that only encloses the FOV. However, reconstruction using an image
volume that only encloses the FOV, when imaging a larger object than the FOV, is known to lead to
severe reconstruction artifacts, which are essentially caused by incorrect modeling of the x-rays, by omitting the path they traverse in tissues that are outside the image volume. The artifacts typically appear
as bright peripheral bands that cover structures near the boundary of the FOV, making them appear
obscured or distorted (Ohnesorge et al 2000).
There are several methods to overcome out-of-FOV artifacts, one of which is to extend the image
volume beyond the FOV and also estimate the attenuation values outside the FOV. However, extending
the image volume with uniform high-resolution voxels outside the FOV has limitations. In particular,
the use of large image volume with uniform high-resolution voxels can lead to an excessive increase in
computational cost, memory requirements, and reconstruction time, especially in iterative reconstruction
methods (Cao et al 2015). To overcome these constraints, applying a multi-resolution approach is beneficial, where the volume outside the FOV is modeled using a coarser representation, as in the method
proposed in Dang et al (2017) for reducing truncation artifacts in head data. However, a limitation of
that method is that it was developed only for head data, where the truncation artifacts arise from the
head holder. Figure 1 illustrates the multi-resolution extended volume in CBCT imaging with an object
larger than the FOV. The rectangle enclosing the FOV, called the main volume, uses a higher resolution,
and the extended volumes employ a lower resolution.
Multi-resolution techniques have been studied for local tomography reconstruction in order to enable
artifact-free reconstruction of the FOV with high computational efficiency. For example, Hamelin et al
(2007) proposed an irregular image sampling, a smaller sampling for the ROI, and a larger sampling
for the background in 2D tomography reconstruction. Based on this idea, Cao et al (2016) combined
a multi-resolution strategy with a penalized weighted least squares algorithm, in which the 3D volume
was divided into separate high- and low-resolution regions to reduce the computational burden of iterative reconstruction. Additionally, a penalty function was introduced to handle the boundary conditions between the two regions. Multi-resolution can also be implemented by sampling the data in polar
coordinates, where the ROI is expanded using circular harmonics, and the voxel resolution decreases
exponentially with increasing distance from the center of the ROI (Sahiner and Yagle 1995).
Another approach for multi-resolution is to use wavelet decomposition for multi-resolution representation of the unknown attenuation image. Bhatia et al (1996) and Delaney and Bresler (1995) proposed a multi-resolution filtered backward projection method in which wavelet coefficients are calculated at different resolution levels; more densely within the ROI and more coarsely outside it. Another
example of wavelet-based multi-resolution can be found in Niinimäki et al (2007), which focused
on dental x-ray imaging with a Bayesian approach to estimate the wavelet coefficients of the multiresolution image in local tomography. A limitation of this approach is that it was formulated only for
2D imaging rather than for 3D reconstruction.
One class of approaches proposed for mitigating out-of-FOV artifacts is projection extrapolation
techniques, which aim to reduce these artifacts by extrapolating the missing projection data. Projection
extrapolation can be implemented using different methods, such as water-cylinder-based sinogram extension (Hsieh et al 2004), sinogram decomposition techniques (Zamyatin and Nakanishi 2007), adaptive
mathematical extrapolation techniques (Ohnesorge et al 2000), and deep learning algorithms (Fonseca
et al 2021, Fu et al 2024). Although these extrapolation-based methods have been shown to reduce outof-FOV artifacts, they are often not sufficient for iterative reconstruction methods. Better results can be
achieved by combining extrapolation with reconstruction volume extension methods.

In this article, we propose a 3D multi-resolution extended reconstruction volume (MR-ERV) model
for the model-based iterative CBCT reconstruction method. The MR-ERV model allows independent
volume extension in each of the six directions of the FOV, depending on the object size and the orientation of boundary-truncation-induced out-of-FOV artifacts. For computational efficiency, the volume
extension uses six separate extended volumes at each side of the FOV using a lower resolution (larger
voxel size) than is used in the FOV. GPU based forward and backward projections are computed independently for each volume.
Among these six directions, extensions in the transaxial directions are the most critical because when
the object is larger than the transaxial FOV, the whole volume is affected by truncation. This results in
intensity inaccuracies and out-of-FOV artifacts throughout the entire reconstructed volume. To solve
this, we extend the image volume beyond the FOV in the transaxial direction to cover the entire object.
In contrast, axial truncation affects only the top and bottom slices, since it occurs where the cone-beam
coverage narrows at the beginning and end of the scan. Moreover, axial truncation is not unique to
CBCT and the same issue is often also present in helical CT. For axial direction, the volume should be
extended to cover the entire cross-section of the cone beam projected onto the detector. The proposed
model is demonstrated to effectively mitigate out-of-FOV artifacts, leading to high quality reconstruction
with accurate Hounsfield unit (HU) values.
In this study, we consider a non-negativity constrained least-squares (LS) formulation and solve
the minimization problem with the primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) algorithm (Chambolle and
Pock 2011). However, the proposed model is also compatible with other reconstruction approaches.
PDHG is an optimization method designed for convex optimization problems, providing rapid convergence and flexibility in adapting to different types of optimization formulations relevant to CT imaging (Sidky et al 2011). In particular, it supports a wide range of regularization models, including nondifferentiable sparsity promoting functionals (Chambolle et al 2018). Moreover, the dual formulation of
the PDHG algorithm allows straightforward inclusion of preconditioners to improve computational efficiency. One such preconditioner is the filtering-based preconditioner derived for the 3D CBCT problem
in Wettenhovi et al (2025). To further accelerate the computation, we apply a stochastic variant of PDHG
using ordered subsets (Chambolle et al 2023).
To test the feasibility of the presented multi-resolution method with regularization, we also employ
a regularized reconstruction with the multi-resolution approach. To incorporate regularization into the
PDHG algorithm, we employ the Condat-Vũ (CV) algorithm (Condat 2013, Vũ 2013), which is a modification of PDHG that can handle problems involving three operators instead of two in the primal-dual
method. The third operator can represent a convex and differentiable regularizer (Wettenhovi et al 2025),
allowing the use of various types of regularization terms. In this work, we augment the least squares data
misfit with a non-local relative difference prior (NLRDP) regularization functional (Buades et al 2005,
Wettenhovi et al 2026).

In this work, we use a simple projection extrapolation method to extend the projection data in
either, or both, the vertical and horizontal directions. The extrapolation technique helps gain a small
additional reduction of reconstruction artifacts. The MR-ERV model can be combined with any projection extrapolation method, for example, those in Hsieh et al (2004), Kolditz et al (2011), Fonseca et al
(2021), Fu et al (2024), to achieve the best results.
The structure of the paper is as follows. Section 2 outlines the concept of the multi-resolution and
separated extension of the FOV, the GPU projector types, and the separated MR-ERV model algorithm
based on the PDHG and LS techniques, incorporating extrapolation and regularization. Section 3
presents the CBCT experiments that are used to validate the model and the reconstruction parameters. Section 4 reports the reconstruction results and section 5 provides the discussion and concluding
remarks.
2. Theory
2.1. Multi-resolution extended reconstruction volume
To mitigate artifacts arising from a limited field of view (FOV) in CBCT, we propose a separated MRERV model in which the rectangular reconstruction volume enclosing the FOV is extended independently in each direction to cover unmeasured and truncated regions; see figure 2. These extended volumes
can have different sizes that are selected based on the size of the imaged object. The overall image
volume consists of the high-resolution volume enclosing the main volume and the six extended volumes,
which use lower resolution (larger voxel size) than the main volume. In figure 2 purple indicates the
high-resolution main volume. The top and bottom (cyan) volumes are the extended volumes in the
axial direction, and the front, back, left, and right (white and gray) volumes correspond to the transaxial
extensions. The transaxial direction lies in the xy-plane, perpendicular to the CBCT rotation axis (zaxis), about which the x-ray source and flat-panel detector rotate around the object. By contrast, the
axial direction is aligned with the z-axis and is parallel to the patient’s longitudinal axis. Ideally, the
transaxial extensions should be selected large enough to cover the whole (cross-section) of the imaged
body, in order to cover the whole support of the attenuating material in the computational model of the
measured x-rays. Similarly, in the axial direction, we extend the reconstruction volume to incorporate
the cross-section of the cone beam on the detector. The combination of low-resolution volumes and the
high-resolution volume is referred to as the multi-resolution volumes.
The multi-resolution scheme has some similarity to the model presented in Dang et al (2017), where
the lower resolution region is a single volume. We also tested a single-volume approach for the extended volume, but it was observed to be computationally slower than the extension with six separate
volumes without any benefit in accuracy. Further, the use of separate extended volumes gives more
flexibility for the use of different extensions in different directions based on body size and scanning
geometry.

For the computation of forward Af and backward projections AT p, various types of projectors
have been developed. Among these, a voxel-based projector is generally preferable for backward projection, whereas a ray-based projector is more suitable for forward projection (Wettenhovi et al 2022).
Accordingly, In this work we employ a ray-based projector (Wettenhovi et al 2025) for forward projection and a voxel-based projector (Peters 1982, Jia et al 2011, Wettenhovi et al 2025) for backward
projection.
The ray-based forward projector we use in this work is an interpolation-based projector where the
value in each voxel is linearly interpolated at fixed intervals and multiplied by the fixed interval length.
The projections through the sub-volumes are computed separately, formally as Au fu , and then summed
to produce the forward projection through the entire volume, as illustrated in figure 3. Because the
extended volumes are processed separately, it is not necessary to track when, or whether, a ray enters
a next volume. This separation significantly improves computational efficiency and makes the process
faster compared to using a single multi-resolution volume for the extended region.
In the voxel-based backward projection, each voxel is processed separately. For each voxel, a ray is
drawn from the x-ray source through the center of the voxel and extended to intersect the detector. The
projection value recorded at this detector position is then backprojected to the voxel. This procedure is
repeated for all projection views and the backward projection values from different projection views are
summed up to give the backward projection for that voxel.
Using a ray-based forward projector and a voxel-based backprojector causes the two projectors to not
be adjoint. However, in this case, the difference between these two is not significant. The adjointness of

3. Methods
3.1. Datasets and computational models
We used three different experimental CBCT datasets to test the MR-ERV model. The first dataset is
a scan of a plastic cylinder container filled with water (shown in figure 4), scanned using a Planmeca
Viso G5 CBCT head and neck scanner. The distance from the source to the detector was approximately
700 mm (with small variations between projections), while the distance from the source to the center of
rotation was 410 mm. The tube voltage was 100 kV, and the current was 100 mAs. A set of 500 projection
images was taken on a full 360◦ rotation. The size of a single projection image is 404 × 404 pixels with
pixel size 0.254 mm × 0.254 mm. When utilizing projection extrapolation, the images were extrapolated
equally in all directions, resulting in a projection size of 564 × 564 pixels per projection, corresponding
to an increase of approximately 40% in each dimension (about 20% per side).
In the discretization of the image volume, the main volume has a size of 401 × 401 × 401 cubic
voxels with voxel size (edge length) 0.15 mm, leading to a FOV size of approximately 60 × 60 mm. This
FOV is collimated from the maximum of 200 × 170 mm, which is common in dental imaging studies. In
the MR-ERV model, different sizes of transaxial extended volumes were tested. The transaxial extensions
were set to 20%, 40%, and 80% of the main volume length in each direction, with the 80% extension
fully covering the entire object. In the axial direction, the extension was 33% of the main volume length
when extrapolation was applied and approximately 9.5% when extrapolation was not used, which was
sufficient to cover the cone beam cross section on the detector.
In the extended volumes, different resolutions were tested such that the voxel sizes (scales) were 1 ×,
2 ×, and 4 × of the voxel size in the main volume. This means that voxel sizes of 0.15 mm, 0.3 mm, and
0.6 mm were used. The number of voxels in the extended volumes depends on the sizes of the volumes
and voxels. For example, when the extended volumes use a voxel size of 0.6 mm and the extensions are
80% of the main volume in the transaxial directions and 33% in the axial direction the resulting multiresolution volume (main volume and extended volumes) has an image matrix size of 561 × 561 × 467,
corresponding to a total of approximately 147 million voxels. If the same volume were uniformly discretized using the voxel size of the main volume, the resulting image would have an image matrix size of
1041 × 1041 × 665, corresponding to a total of approximately 720 million voxels, indicating a significant
reduction in the number of voxels achieved by utilizing the MR-ERV model.

Viso G7 CBCT head and neck scanner. The distance from the source to the detector was approximately 700 mm, and the distance from the source to the center of rotation was 500 mm. In this case,
the tube voltage was 100 kV and the current was 100 mAs, and a set of 500 projections was taken
over a full 360◦ rotation. The projection images are of size 656 × 152 pixels with detector pixel size

3.2. Reconstruction parameters
In the PDHG algorithm, all reconstructions were performed using K = 10 iterations with S = 20 subsets.
The subsets were selected such that the first subset contained projections 1, 21, 41, and so on, while the
second subset contained projections 2, 22, 42, and so forth. With a total of 500 projection views, each
subset consisted of 25 projections. The PDHG dual step-size was σ = 1 for all multi-resolution volumes,
while the primal step-sizes τ u were determined using the power method. The step-size parameter was set
1
θ = 1 for all cases, and the subset probability was ϕs = 1S = 20
.
We employed an interpolation-based projector (ray- and voxel-based) for forward and backward
projections with an interpolation length of 0.5, multiplied by the voxel size, meaning that the interpolation length corresponded to half of the voxel size. A fixed interpolation length was used for all multiresolution volumes (main and extended volumes).
In the tests with regularization, we used the NLRDP regularization with the regularization parameter
β = 4 for the dental head phantom and β = 10 for the cadaver lumbar spine dataset. We applied the
regularization algorithm only to the main volume and not to the extended volumes, because the image
quality inside the FOV is of primary importance to us.
The MR-ERV implementation was performed using OMEGA software (Wettenhovi et al 2021),
and all reconstructions were computed with code written in OpenCL, utilizing the ArrayFire library
(Yalamanchili et al 2015). All reconstructions were performed with Nvidia Titan RTX GPU. For further
technical details on the out-of-FOV correction in OMEGA, see the online documentation: https://omegadoc.readthedocs.io/en/latest/corrections.html#out-of-fov-correction.

on reducing out-of-FOV artifacts and achieving accurate HU values, as well as the effect of projection
extrapolation using data from the homogeneous water phantom. In this case, we investigate the influence
of different extended volume lengths in transaxial direction and different extended volume voxel sizes.

5. Discussion
In this paper, we presented an MR-ERV model to overcome out-of-FOV artifacts caused by projection
truncation and the limited FOV in CBCT image reconstruction. The proposed model extends the image
volume beyond the FOV by using six separate low-resolution extended volumes with independent GPU
based forward and backward projection computations in all volumes. The results presented demonstrate
that the MR-ERV model effectively suppresses out-of-FOV artifacts, as long as the extended volumes are
large enough, preferably covering the support of the attenuating body in the transaxial plane and the
cone beam cross section in the axial direction. In addition to efficient out-of-FOV artifact suppression,
the model was demonstrated to enable accurate recovery of the HU values. Furthermore, augmenting the
MR-ERV model with a simple projection extrapolation technique further enhanced the artifact removal
with the cost of slightly increasing computation time.
The water cylinder dataset, due to its uniform composition and homogeneous texture, was utilized
to study different extended volume lengths and resolution settings in our study, since the assessment
of HU fidelity in this dataset is natural. Based on the results, achieving accurate HU values requires an
extended volume that approximately fully covers the actual object size in the transaxial direction. This
finding aligns with the fact that the measured x-rays will be appropriately modeled by line integrals
of the attenuation density when the whole support of the attenuation density is enclosed by the image
volume. A smaller extension may already reduce boundary artifacts efficiently, but it is insufficient for
accurate recovery of HU values. Using larger extensions leads to reduced HU-values. For example, using
an extension of 120% for the water phantom, reduces the HU-values by around 80 HU. For the most
accurate HU-values, one needs to be careful with the extension length as larger extensions reduce the
HU-values while smaller ones increase it. Larger extensions also do somewhat increase the computational
time, although that is significantly reduced due to the MR-ERV model.
Based on the results of using different voxel sizes for the extended volumes, employing a voxel size
that is four times longer than that of the main volume was found to be an appropriate choice for accuracy and computational efficiency. Extending the reconstructed volume without using a multi-resolution
approach (voxel scale of 1 for the extended region) increases the computational time and memory
requirements, which can even render iterative image reconstruction computationally infeasible. Moreover,
utilizing a larger voxel size for the extended volumes introduces a regularization effect by model dimension reduction, which was found to be beneficial for reduction of the out-of-FOV artifacts. However, if
the voxel size in the extension becomes too large, it may have too much averaging effect, leading to some
decrease in reconstruction quality. Further increasing the voxel size leads to slightly lower HU-values
18

and can also cause instabilities in the reconstructions in certain cases. For example, with the dental head
phantom, the region on the left of figure 10 has much fewer rays than the right side due to the measurement setup. With a scale of 8, the accuracy reduces so that the region no longer correctly models the
rays, leading to zero values and issues with the power method computations in that specific volume.
However, in certain high-dimensional cases, using the larger voxel scale may still be beneficial. The
model was also tested with NLRDP regularization, with results demonstrating that the MR-ERV model
is compatible with the regularized reconstruction problem.
We tested different variations of the MR-ERV model. For example, instead of using separated
volumes in the extended area, we implemented a single-volume MR-ERV model that extends the outof-FOV region with one continuous volume. However, not only did this approach show no improvement
compared to the separated model, it was also computationally slower. We also tested a single-volume
extension model with uniform resolution. This model was slower, did not offer clear advantages, and
had slightly larger HU values in regions affected by artifacts as well as overall slightly larger HU values.
Additionally, we tested using multiple resolutions for the extended volumes, for instance, higher resolution near the main volume boundary and lower resolution farther away, but this configuration did not
provide benefits over using a single resolution for the out-of-FOV extended volumes.
In this study, we tested the combination of the MR-ERV model with a simple projection extrapolation approach that performed well; however, more advanced methods, such as those proposed in Hsieh
et al (2004), Kolditz et al (2011), could potentially lead to even better performance. Furthermore, the
proposed model can be integrated with various optimization algorithms. Although the PDHG algorithm
was used in this work, the model is applicable with other optimization methods as well. The model can
also be used with the FDK algorithm.
From the results obtained in this work, the following conclusions can be drawn. Extending the FOV
using the MR-ERV model effectively removes out-of-FOV artifacts arising from limited FOV in CBCT
image reconstruction. Using a low-resolution volumes for the extended volumes produced high quality
iterative CBCT reconstructions in a computationally efficient form. In the considered test cases, a voxel
size four times larger than that of the main volume was found to be a well performing choice for the
out-of-FOV regions. In addition, an optimal extension length for accurate HU values and elimination
of out-of-FOV artifacts is to select the extended volumes to cover the entire object in the transaxial direction and the full cone-beam cross section on the detector in the axial direction. The MR-ERV model
is compatible with projection extrapolation techniques, and the tested extrapolation provided a slight
improvement over the MR-ERV model alone. Further, the model was demonstrated to be compatible for
regularized reconstruction.
