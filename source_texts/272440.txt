Ambient occlusion (AO) makes tight corners and contact points look grounded; FSR renders at a lower internal resolution and reconstructs a higher-resolution image; ray tracing traces light paths to produce more physically believable lighting, shadows, reflections, and specular highlights. Use them as separate layers in a rendering pipeline: AO for cheap contact shading, FSR for performance, and ray-traced effects where visual accuracy matters. [help.maxon](https://help.maxon.net/c4d/2024/en-us/Subsystems/Default/Content/html/Ambient+Occlusion.html)

## Ambient occlusion

AO estimates how much of the surrounding environment light is blocked at a surface point. Corners, crevices, under objects, and areas where geometry nearly touches get darker; open surfaces stay bright. [help.maxon](https://help.maxon.net/c4d/2024/en-us/Subsystems/Default/Content/html/Ambient+Occlusion.html)

### How to use it

1. **Choose the type**
   - **SSAO (screen-space AO):** Fast, uses the depth/normal buffers, but misses occluders outside the camera view. [docs.godotengine](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/environment_and_post_processing.html)
   - **GTAO:** Higher-quality screen-space approximation, usually better for broad soft occlusion. [foxrenderfarm](https://www.foxrenderfarm.com/share/what-is-ambient-occlusion/)
   - **RTAO (ray-traced AO):** Casts rays around the surface normal to measure actual occlusion; best for accurate contact shadows, especially in dense geometry. [switchbladegaming](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/explained/)

2. **Set the radius**
   - Small radius: tight contact shadows around feet, props, seams.
   - Medium radius: general grounding for characters and objects.
   - Large radius: broad environmental darkening, but it can look muddy.

3. **Control intensity**
   - Keep AO subtle: it should darken *ambient* light, not replace direct shadows.
   - A good starting point is roughly 0.3–0.7 intensity, then adjust per scene.
   - Avoid applying AO to emissive materials, fire, neon signs, or bright glass unless you want them to feel physically enclosed.

4. **Composite it correctly**
   - Multiply AO into the ambient/indirect-light contribution, not into direct sun or lamp highlights.
   - Example:  
     \[
     finalColor = directLight + ambientLight \times AO
     \]
   - This preserves crisp direct shadows while making recessed areas feel naturally darker. [help.maxon](https://help.maxon.net/c4d/2024/en-us/Subsystems/Default/Content/html/Ambient+Occlusion.html)

## FSR upscaling

FSR renders the game or 3D scene at a lower internal resolution, then reconstructs detail to your target output resolution. It is mainly a performance tool: use it when ray tracing, high shadow resolution, or dense geometry drops your frame rate. [switchbladegaming](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/explained/)

### How to use it

1. **Pick a quality mode**
   - **Quality:** Best image stability; use when you have decent GPU headroom.
   - **Balanced:** Good default for demanding scenes.
   - **Performance / Ultra Performance:** Use only when frame rate is critical; expect softer detail and more artifacts.

2. **Set the render scale indirectly**
   - Don’t manually lower resolution if the engine has an FSR mode; select the FSR preset and let it calculate the internal render resolution.
   - For example, at 1440p output, Quality mode renders internally below 1440p and reconstructs to 1440p.

3. **Enable sharpening carefully**
   - FSR often includes a sharpening pass.
   - Use low-to-medium sharpening for realistic scenes; high values can create halos around bright edges, text, thin wires, and specular highlights.

4. **Combine it with ray tracing**
   - Enable FSR **after** setting your target resolution and before pushing ray-traced settings too high.
   - A practical setup: FSR Quality + ray-traced reflections + ray-traced shadows + medium RTAO, then raise settings only if performance remains stable. Ray tracing is expensive, so upscaling is commonly paired with it to recover performance. [switchbladegaming](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/explained/)

## Ray-traced lighting

Ray tracing follows rays from the camera into the scene, finds intersections, and evaluates how light reaches those points. Full path tracing extends this by simulating multiple light bounces, which produces indirect lighting, color bleeding, reflections, shadows, and occlusion in one unified model. [switchbladegaming](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/explained/)

### Lighting workflow

1. **Start with physically based materials**
   - Use albedo/base color, roughness, metallic, normal, and emissive maps.
   - Keep albedo values realistic; overly bright textures cause blown-out bounce lighting.

2. **Use real light sources**
   - Add area lights, spotlights, sun/sky systems, and emissive meshes.
   - Ray tracing responds strongly to light size: larger lights create softer shadows and broader specular highlights.

3. **Balance direct and indirect light**
   - Direct light: the immediate illumination from a lamp, sun, or emissive object.
   - Indirect light: light that bounces from walls, floors, and objects.
   - In a hybrid pipeline, direct lighting may remain rasterized while ray tracing handles indirect diffuse lighting. [advances.realtimerendering](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2019/Benyoub-DXR%20Ray%20tracing-%20SIGGRAPH2019-final.pdf)

## Ray-traced shadows

A shadow ray is sent from a surface point toward a light. If something intersects that ray, the point is shadowed; if not, it receives direct illumination. [cs.auckland.ac](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci373s1c/PatricesLectures/2011/CS373-Part1-Lecture10-RayTracing2.pdf)

### How to use it

- **Hard shadows:** Small or distant light source; good for sunlight, lasers, and stylized drama.
- **Soft shadows:** Larger area light; produces a gradual penumbra from shadow core to lit surface. [switchbladegaming](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/explained/)
- **Contact shadows:** Keep AO enabled alongside ray-traced shadows; AO handles tiny crevice darkening, while ray-traced shadows handle object-to-ground occlusion.
- **Avoid double-darkening:** If using RTAO and ray-traced shadows, reduce AO intensity so corners do not become pure black.

## Glows and bloom

Glow is usually a post-processing effect: bright pixels are extracted, blurred, and added back over the image. It is not inherently ray traced, though ray-traced emissive lighting can make the source of the glow feel more integrated into the scene. [docs.godotengine](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/environment_and_post_processing.html)

### How to use it

1. Mark light-emitting objects as **emissive**: neon signs, screens, lava, magic effects, headlights.
2. Set a **bloom threshold** so only genuinely bright pixels glow.
3. Use a small bloom radius for crisp neon and a larger radius for hazy atmosphere.
4. Keep intensity low; glow should imply brightness, not wash out the frame.
5. For physical accuracy, let emissive surfaces also illuminate nearby geometry through ray-traced indirect lighting or path tracing. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00292v1)

## Reflections

Ray-traced reflections cast a ray from the camera through a reflective pixel, find what it hits, and shade that hit point. This allows accurate off-screen reflections, unlike screen-space reflections, which can only reflect what is already visible in the frame. [docs.godotengine](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/environment_and_post_processing.html)

### How to use it

- **Mirrors and polished metal:** Low roughness, high reflectivity, ray-traced reflections enabled.
- **Wet pavement:** Moderate roughness, normal-map ripples, and a reflection intensity around 0.2–0.6.
- **Glass:** Use refraction plus reflection; control roughness for frosted versus clear glass.
- **Rough surfaces:** Increase roughness so reflections blur naturally instead of looking like a mirror.
- **Fallback:** If a reflected ray misses geometry, sample the sky/environment map; this is a standard way to avoid black reflections. [deepwiki](https://deepwiki.com/sixthsurge/photon/4.3-deferred-shading-pass)

## Specular data

Specular data controls how light reflects sharply off a surface. It is separate from diffuse color: diffuse describes scattered surface color, while specular describes the glossy highlight and mirror-like response. [cs.auckland.ac](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci373s1c/PatricesLectures/2011/CS373-Part1-Lecture10-RayTracing2.pdf)

### Key maps and values

| Data | What it controls | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| Roughness / glossiness | Blur and spread of reflections and highlights | Low roughness = mirror; high roughness = matte |
| Metallic | Whether reflections take on the base color | Metals reflect tinted light; non-metals mostly reflect neutral light |
| Specular intensity | Strength of the highlight/reflection | Use sparingly on cloth, skin, concrete, and wood |
| Normal map | Surface detail that breaks up reflections | Adds micro-detail to metal, wet ground, and plastic |
| Fresnel | Reflection strength based on viewing angle | Makes surfaces more reflective at grazing angles |

### How to use it

1. Keep non-metal materials mostly non-metallic.
2. Use roughness variation rather than making everything perfectly glossy.
3. Let specular highlights come from the actual light setup; do not paint fake highlights into the albedo map.
4. For ray tracing, give reflective materials a proper environment map or real surrounding geometry so reflections have something meaningful to sample. [cs.auckland.ac](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci373s1c/PatricesLectures/2011/CS373-Part1-Lecture10-RayTracing2.pdf)

## Recommended setup

For a realistic, performant scene:

- **AO:** RTAO if available; otherwise GTAO/SSAO at subtle intensity.
- **Upscaling:** FSR Quality or Balanced.
- **Lighting:** Physically based materials, area lights, and emissive sources.
- **Shadows:** Ray-traced shadows with soft area lights.
- **Glow:** Low-intensity bloom driven by emissive materials.
- **Reflections:** Ray-traced reflections on metal, glass, water, and polished floors; environment-map fallback elsewhere.
- **Specular:** Correct roughness/metallic maps, with realistic Fresnel behavior.

The core rule: **AO darkens ambient occlusion, FSR restores performance, ray tracing simulates actual light transport, and specular data defines how each surface reacts to that light.** [help.maxon](https://help.maxon.net/c4d/2024/en-us/Subsystems/Default/Content/html/Ambient+Occlusion.html)

Main shader types
Shader type	What it controls	Examples
Vertex shader	Moves and transforms mesh vertices	Character animation, wind-blown cloth, waves, displacement
Fragment / pixel shader	Calculates the final color of each pixel	Textures, lighting, metal, glass, cel shading, fire
Geometry shader	Can create or modify primitives between vertex and pixel stages	Grass blades, particle expansion, silhouette effects
Compute shader	General-purpose GPU computation	Particles, image processing, physics, denoising, upscaling
Ray-generation / intersection / miss shaders	Parts of a ray-tracing pipeline	Reflections, shadows, global illumination
Post-process shader	Operates on the finished image	Bloom, chromatic aberration, color grading, motion blur
Vertex shaders run once per mesh vertex and determine its screen position; fragment shaders run once per pixel covered by the mesh and determine its final color.

How a basic render works
A simplified pipeline looks like this:

Vertex stage: The GPU takes a 3D model’s vertices and transforms them into camera/screen space.

Rasterization: The GPU figures out which pixels each triangle covers.

Fragment stage: For every covered pixel, the shader samples textures and calculates color, lighting, transparency, and effects.

Lighting: The shader combines base color, normals, roughness, metallic values, and scene lights.

Post-processing: Separate shaders apply bloom, tone mapping, grain, blur, color grading, and similar full-screen effects.

Shader inputs
Shaders commonly receive:

Attributes: Per-vertex data such as position, normal, UV coordinates, tangent, and vertex color.

Textures: Albedo/base color, normal maps, roughness, metallic, emission, masks.

Uniforms: Values shared across an entire draw call, such as time, camera position, light color, or a slider value.

Buffers: Larger data arrays, often used by compute shaders.

Scene data: Lights, shadows, environment maps, and camera matrices.

A shader’s inputs and outputs connect it to the next stage in the graphics pipeline; for example, a vertex shader may output UVs and world normals that the fragment shader later uses.

Lit vs. unlit shaders
Lit shader: Responds to scene lighting. It calculates diffuse light, specular highlights, shadows, ambient light, and reflections.

Unlit shader: Ignores scene lighting and outputs a color directly. It is useful for UI, holograms, stylized effects, skyboxes, and emissive signs.

For example, a neon sign might use an unlit emissive shader for the sign itself, while the wall beside it uses a lit PBR shader so it receives the neon’s colored bounce light.

PBR shaders
Physically based rendering (PBR) is the standard realistic material model in modern engines. It separates surface properties from light properties, so the same material can react correctly under sunlight, neon, firelight, or a flashlight.

Typical PBR inputs:

Albedo / base color: The surface’s inherent color.

Metallic: Whether the surface behaves like metal.

Roughness: How sharp or blurry reflections and highlights are.

Normal map: Small-scale surface detail that affects lighting.

Emission: Light the surface produces itself.

Ambient occlusion: Darkening in crevices and contact areas.

This is where your earlier topics fit: AO is usually multiplied into ambient lighting, ray tracing can replace or improve shadow/reflection calculations, and specular data controls the sharpness and strength of highlights.

Simple fragment-shader example
This GLSL-style example makes a surface pulse red over time:

text
uniform float time;

void fragment() {
    float pulse = 0.5 + 0.5 * sin(time * 3.0);
    ALBEDO = vec3(pulse, 0.0, 0.0);
    EMISSION = vec3(pulse, 0.0, 0.0) * 2.0;
}
time is a uniform supplied by the engine.

sin(time * 3.0) creates a repeating pulse.

ALBEDO sets the base surface color.

EMISSION makes it glow.

Where shaders connect to FSR and ray tracing
FSR: Uses compute and post-process shaders to reconstruct a lower-resolution render into a higher-resolution output, often adding sharpening.

Ray tracing: Uses specialized shaders to generate rays, test intersections, evaluate hits, and handle rays that miss geometry.

AO: Can be implemented with screen-space shaders or ray-traced shaders.

Bloom/glow: Usually a post-process shader that finds bright pixels, blurs them, and adds them back to the image.

Reflections: Can be screen-space shader effects or ray-traced shader effects, depending on quality and performance needs

The game engine renders a normal frame first—using rasterization, ray tracing, or both. DLSS 5 then receives that output plus motion data from the engine.

Its main inputs are:

Rendered color: The finished 2D image from the game.

Motion vectors: Per-pixel movement information, telling the model how objects moved between frames.

Temporal history: Information carried over from the prior frame for stability.

Artistic-direction controls: Developer-set intensity, color grading, masking, and structure/tone settings.

Renderer-derived guidance: During training, scene attributes help the model stay anchored to what the engine actually authored.

What it changes
DLSS 5 does not replace the game engine’s geometry or invent new objects. It analyzes the existing frame and regenerates its appearance—especially lighting and material response.

It can enhance:

Skin: Subsurface scattering, so light appears to pass through skin rather than sitting flat on it.

Hair and fabric: Fine sheen, light transmission, and material-specific highlights.

Contact shadows and AO: Stronger sense that objects are grounded.

Reflections and light-material interaction: More convincing response on metal, foliage, water, and other surfaces.

Overall lighting mood: Color, tone, and broad illumination can be adjusted without changing the underlying scene.

How the frame is processed
The game renders normally. The engine produces its native or upscaled frame using its existing lighting pipeline.

DLSS Super Resolution may run first. NVIDIA describes DLSS 5 as operating after conventional rendering and after techniques such as DLSS Super Resolution.

The neural model analyzes the frame. It identifies scene semantics such as characters, hair, fabric, translucent skin, lighting direction, and environmental conditions.

It generates an enhanced appearance. A one-step pixel-space diffusion model produces the final displayed image, conditioned on the current frame, motion vectors, temporal state, and artistic controls.

The result is composited and displayed. NVIDIA says it runs locally in real time at up to 4K on GeForce RTX 50-series GPUs.

Why it stays stable
Earlier generative AI images often change slightly every time they are generated. DLSS 5 is designed to be causal and deterministic: the same input frame produces the same output, and each frame uses prior-frame information to avoid flicker or shifting details.

It is also trained with renderer-derived consistency supervision, meaning the model learns to preserve character identity, object shape, composition, lighting direction, and developer-authored design rather than freely reimagining the image.

Developer controls
NVIDIA gives developers controls so DLSS 5 does not impose one universal “AI look”:

Structure intensity: Affects effects such as ambient occlusion, contact shadows, reflections, and subsurface scattering.

Tone intensity: Affects color and broader lighting.

Masking: Lets developers choose where the neural enhancement applies.

Color grading and intensity settings: Help preserve the game’s intended visual style.
