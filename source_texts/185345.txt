Abstract
P989
Introduction: Monte Carlo (MC) is the gold standard for accurate voxel-level radiation dosimetry because it simulates particle transport throughout the patient’s anatomy, eliminating the need for simplifying assumptions inherent in kernel-based or local energy deposition methods. This is especially important in regions where the tissue density or composition changes abruptly, such as tissue/lung or bone/marrow interfaces. Due to large computational demands and long simulation times, MC methods have not yet been feasible for clinical radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) dosimetry. The aim of this work is to accelerate MC simulation for RPT dosimetry so that accurate patient-specific voxel-level dosimetry can be performed within clinically relevant timeframes.
Methods: We have modified a version of the Monte Carlo dose calculation code Dose Planning Method to be optimized for operation on GPUs. Our Torch® Dose Assessment software application utilizes GPU technology to accelerate coupled photon/electron radiation transport simulation to provide extremely accurate modelling of energy deposition in each voxel of the patient in clinically acceptable execution times. Using off-the-shelf GPUs in a Windows PC environment, Torch samples decays in each voxel; all particle types emitted by a particular isotope—alphas, betas, gammas, Augers and bremsstrahlung X-rays—are sampled. The particles are then transported throughout the entire patient volume and the absorbed dose is quantified at the voxel level according to the tissue density as determined by the HU value of each CT voxel, allowing for the creation of dose cloud images with isodose lines for visualization and dose volume histograms (DVHs) for statistical analysis of the results.
Results: Two experiments were performed to demonstrate the accuracy of the Torch dose calculation engine. First, a set of simulations were performed using the ICRP 110 male phantom with I-131, Y-90 and Lu-177 activity in each of six organs (liver, right kidney, left kidney, spleen, left lung, and lumbar spongiosa). The resulting dose maps were processed to produce organ-level S-values describing the dose delivered to each of those six organs as targets from activity in each of the six organs as sources. Those S-values were compared to results calculated by using OpenDose (which represents the average results from six Monte Carlo codes: EGSnrc/EGS++, FLUKA, GATE, Geant4, MCNP/MCNPX, and PENELOPE) and from OLINDA 2.0. The results indicate close agreement between Torch and the other calculation methods, with the S-values over all 36 source-target combinations agreeing within ±2% for each isotope (both Torch vs. OpenDose and Torch vs. OLINDA 2.0), and all individual source-target S-values agreeing within ±6% (Figure 1). In the second experiment, physical dose measurements for Y-90 were performed using a custom-designed film phantom. The Torch-simulated doses agreed both with the measurements and with EGS-simulated doses to within 5% for each of the five depths in the phantom. Figure 2 shows the experimental setup and the close agreement between the measured and calculated does results. The speed of the Torch dose calculation was tested using 128x128x128 clinical data acquired on a GE Discovery NM/CT 570 SPECT/CT scanner. Dose maps were computed to a statistical uncertainty of 2% in 7, 18, and 281 seconds for Lu-177, Y-90, and I-131, respectively using a laptop computer with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU.
Conclusions: Torch dose assessment software successfully demonstrates the accuracy of Monte Carlo RPT dosimetry calculations with patient-specific voxel-level precision in clinical RPT dose assessment. The dose estimates generated by Torch are comparably equivalent to well-established Monte Carlo codes in clinically feasible times using off-the-shelf moderate-level GPU technology.
