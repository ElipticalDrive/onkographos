Atomic electron tomography (AET) is a powerful technique for determining the three-dimensional atomic structure of matter in real space. However, conventional AET requires numerous projections across a wide angular range. The high dose and prolonged acquisition severely limit its application. Here, we propose an interpretable and universal algorithm for low-dose, fast and tilt-constrained AET: null-space iterative reconstruction (NSIRE), which uses an unsupervised diffusion model to iteratively compute null-space solutions of projection equations, thereby generating tomograms consistent with both projection constraints and atomic potential prior. NSIRE can resolve a wide range of complex materials under 6–12° sparse projection or within ±29° small tilt-range without retraining. Using the NSIRE, we determine the three-dimensional atomic structures of a 4-nm Pt nanoparticle with grain boundaries and a 3-nm PtCo nanoalloy (the smallest one resolved so far), achieving a root-mean-square displacement of <20 pm and high projection consistency, overcoming the scale, dose and time limitations of AET.
The authors present NSIRE, a generative-AI-based algorithm that reconstructs 3D atomic structures from sparse data, enabling low-dose, fast, and tilt-constrained atomic electron tomography while preserving high accuracy across complex materials.

Atomic electron tomography (AET) is an advanced technique for characterizing the three-dimensional (3D) internal structure and chemical composition of materials at the atomic scale1. AET typically employs an incoherent annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscope (ADF-STEM) to acquire two-dimensional (2D) projections of a sample at different tilt-angles, where the image intensity is increases with both thickness and atomic number (Z)2,3. This enables reconstruction of the 3D structure using an iterative algorithm based on the Radon transform, producing a 3D image whose computed projections match the experimental projections4,5. Mathematically, this process corresponds to solving an inverse problem. Iterative reconstruction techniques (IRTs), widely used in AET, include equally sloped tomography (EST)6–9, generalized Fourier iterative reconstruction (GENFIRE)10–15, and real space iterative reconstruction (RESIRE)16–23. These methods enable projection-consistent 3D reconstruction of the tomogram, but typically require numerous 2D projections acquired at small angular intervals (1–2°) over a wide angular range (±70°) to resolve reliable atomic structures. The lengthy acquisition process (often several hours) places stringent demands on sample stability, resulting in a low success rate of AET experiments and hindering dynamic or time-sensitive studies25,26. Increasing angular intervals and reducing the number of projections (known as under-sampling25,27) can lower the irradiation dose and accelerate data acquisition, but also render the inverse problem ill-posed, resulting in severe artifacts in the reconstructed tomograms and ultimately degrading the accuracy of atomic localization.
Incorporating prior information to constrain the solution space of the inverse problem helps mitigate the effects of data sparsity and enables high-quality 3D reconstructions. According to physical principle, atomic tomograms should contain only the 3D atomic potential convolved with the point spread function (PSF) of the microscope28–31 (hereafter referred to as the atomic potential); namely, the tomograms should conform to the atomic potential data distribution. This provides a precise and stringent constraint; however, conventional regularization methods cannot incorporate such complex priors32–34. Artificial intelligence has led to a paradigm shift in solving tomographic inverse problems. The most popular approach employs neural networks to construct an end-to-end mapping between the inputs (e.g., under-sampled tomograms27,29,35 or projections36) and the target outputs (e.g., fully-sampled tomograms), yielding high-quality reconstructions that respect the tomogram data priors. In the field of AET, Yang et al. used an end-to-end 3D U-Net for tomograms post-processing to remove artifacts according to atomicity and resolve the 3D structure of Pt nanoparticles29,37 at 6.5° angular intervals. However, neural network outputs do not guarantee projection consistency and may produce artifact-free tomograms that deviate from experimental observations (as we will show later). More importantly, in practice, these methods often encounter generalization problems when dealing with materials of different compositions, structures, or projection angles, reducing accuracy and requiring retraining for each new scenario.
Advances in generative artificial intelligence37–41 provide a novel approach to AET reconstruction. In this work, we propose a Null-Space Iterative REconstruction (NSIRE) algorithm based on diffusion model that leverages the mathematical properties of the null-space to constrain 3D reconstructions to the atomic manifold. This ensures strict projection consistency while incorporating atomic priors. The NSIRE efficiently explores the solution space by integrating stochasticity and determinism, enables accurate 3D reconstruction of single-elemental samples from sparse projection with ~12° intervals, maintaining stable performance for the multi-elemental samples outside the training distribution at ~6° intervals. Using NSIRE, we determine the 3D stacking fault structure of a 4-nm Pt nanoparticle and the 3D atomic structure of a 3-nm PtCo alloy nanoparticle (the smallest nanoalloy resolved by AET so far) under sparse projection conditions, with root-mean-square displacements of atomic coordinates reaching 13 and 17 pm, respectively. The electron dose and acquisition time are reduced by approximately 70% compared to traditional AET10,11,22–24, thereby significantly reducing sample damage and improving the success rate. Last, we determine the atomic structure of an irregular-shaped Pt nanoparticle within a small tilt range of ±29°, demonstrating NSIRE’s potential for AET under tilt-constrained in situ conditions.

Fast AET experiment of Pt nanoparticles
To compare the NSIRE and the traditional IRT’s performance on real data, we acquired experimental tilt-series of a 4-nm single-crystal Pt nanoparticle (Fig. 3a) at an average interval of 3.5° (38 projections in total) to form a full dataset, from which a sparse projection subset (at ~6° or 12° intervals) was selected. As the ground-truth is unavailable, we used the previously-reported R1-factor error10,11,29,42 between the experimental projection and the computed projection using the reconstructed atomic structure to assess the reliability of the two algorithms. Details of data processing are shown in Methods and Table 1. Owing to the small particle size, the RMSD of IRTs using the full dataset was only 17.7 pm, comparable to that of previously-reported AET at an average interval of 1–2° (Supplementary Fig. 21)10,11,18. Due to the inherent missing-wedge, the influence of the null-space always persists. Even using the full dataset, the R1-factor error of IRTs remained higher than that of NSIRE (Fig. 3b). As the angular interval increased to ~6° and 12° (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Figs. 22, 23), the R1-factor error of NSIRE remained consistently low, even lower than that of the IRTs using the full dataset. Limited-angle AET experiment of Pt nanoparticle
In addition to accelerating imaging and reducing dose, NSIRE can also extend AET to extreme limited-angle conditions of approximately ±30° (see Supplementary Figs. 15, 19, 20). We further demonstrate this using experimental tilt-series of an irregular-shaped Pt nanoparticle collected at intervals of 2° within a ± 29° tilt range (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b–d, Supplementary Figs. 26, 27, when the null-space components are correctly computed using the atomic potential prior, the reconstruction quality of NSIRE is significantly improved. Regularization methods such as self-supervised neural field (CryoNeFEN50), and IRTs with weak prior (positivity constraints) or imprecise priors (total variance), which are widely applied to reconstruction without clear priors such as single-particle and nanoscale electron tomography, can resolve the morphology but fail to effectively recover meaningful 3D atomic structure information (Supplementary Fig. 27).Fig. 4Limited-angle three-dimension reconstruction of a Pt particle within ±29° tilt-range.

Theoretically, under the same number of projections, when the dimension of unknown variables is reduced by the atomic potential prior (Fig. 1c), the limited-angle tilt-series provides the same constraints as the conventional wide tilt range. However, when the tilt range is very small, the condition number of the projection operator A will be very large, amplifying the perturbations of non-ideal factors, leading to unstable solutions of the inverse problem. To evaluate the uncertainty of atomic coordinates, in addition to the multi-slice simulation of surrogate atomic model, we also perform cross-validation using two independent experimental tilt-series (Methods). The results show that the end-to-end networks perform similar to NSIRE on simulated tilt-series, but rapidly degrade under perturbations from real noise and potential measurement errors, while NSIRE consistently exhibits the highest consistency (Table 3, Fig. 4e, f). This stems from the fact that the end-to-end networks lack the interactive iterative correction process like NSIRE, and ultimately only generate distorted reconstruction with poor consistency (Fig. 4g). During the iterative sampling, NSIRE dynamically updates both the atomic intensities and atomic coordinates of Pt and Co, guided by the conditional constraint imposed by the projection equations. This enables the accurate reconstruction of PtCo atomic tomograms that are strictly consistent with the projection constraints (Fig. 5b–e). The atomic priors ensure that the atomic profile in the NSIRE tomogram remains isotropic and robust to projection sparsity, thereby enabling more accurate coordinate fitting than IRTs (Fig. 5f). The relative intensity of the 3D atomic potential for averaged Pt and Co atoms closely matches the Hartree-Fock theoretical calculations2 (Fig. 5g–i). These results demonstrate that the diffusion model, guided by experimental projection constraints, can adaptively generate atomic potentials that conform to physical principles for unknown atoms.
The atomic structure of the PtCo nanoparticle reconstructed by NSIRE exhibits an RMSD of 17.2 pm, with the CAR exceeding 98% (Table 4), and an average R1-factor error of 0.121 (Supplementary Fig. 28b). To assess the benefit of computing the null-space solution, we compared the reconstruction errors of NSIRE for different elements with those of traditional IRTs using multi-slice simulations. In IRTs tomograms relying solely on projection constraints, a large number of Co atoms are either missing or dislocated (Supplementary Fig. 29). This is because the weaker Co atoms exhibit higher positional error. Additionally, in under-sampled IRTs tomogram, high-contrast Pt atoms generate numerous ray-like artifacts with intensities comparable to the Co atomic peaks (Fig. 5f), which obscure the Co signal and lead to significant atom loss (the atomic recovery ratio is only 41.2%). The tomogram reconstructed by the end-to-end method29, without retraining for the Pt-Co system, exhibits a similar issue: the neural network fails to distinguish Co atomic peaks from Pt-induced artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 29). NSIRE iteratively samples the atomic manifold, effectively suppressing inappropriate null-space solutions, such as ray-like artifacts, while adaptively recovering the Co atomic potential. This significantly improves the reconstruction accuracy of Co atoms. NSIRE performance on complex materials
In addition to ultra-small nanoparticles, NSIRE also demonstrates excellent performance for fast AET of thicker samples. We extensively validate NSIRE using high-quality, publicly available experimental datasets. These datasets include a fivefold-twinned core–shell Pt@Pd nanoparticle20 (~5 nm, Fig. 6a–e, Supplementary Figs. 30–32), representing a class of material systems whose atomic numbers differ by one period; three CoNiRuRhPdAgIrPt high-entropy nanoalloys17,23 (5–9 nm, Fig. 6f–j, Supplementary Figs. 33–35), representing materials with complex 3D chemical distributions; an icosahedral Pd@Au nanoparticle19 (~5 nm, Fig. 6k–o, Supplementary Fig. 36) with amorphization and dislocations, representing non-platinum materials outside the training distribution; an amorphous ZrO2 nanoparticle21 (~10 nm, Fig. 6p–t, Supplementary Fig. 37), representing oxide materials. NSIRE can still achieve high-precision reconstruction comparable to traditional AET, while using as few as ~20 images. This reduces the experimental time by 50–70%. Discussion
In summary, we developed a NSIRE algorithm based on generative artificial intelligence for the AET with sparse data. During iterative reconstruction of under-sampled AET, the null-space component of the tomogram is constrained to the atomic manifold, ensuring that the reconstruction results remain consistent with both experimental projection constraints and atomic priors. Recent studies have clarified that the accuracy of AET is constrained by the information contained in the measured projections and the fidelity with which the reconstruction workflow preserves and transfers that information into an atomic model52,53. By embedding a sparse reconstruction kernel with a precise prior information into the current AET workflow52, NSIRE suppresses artifact formation at its origin and excludes a large number of erroneous solutions, thereby effectively extending the information limit of AET.
The NSIRE enables high-precision reconstruction of 3D atomic structures using as few as 10–20 images (angular interval: 6°–12°), as demonstrated by a twinned Pt nanoparticle, where the RMSD is only 13.2 pm and the common atomic ratio exceeds 99%. Even for samples outside the training set, such as PtCo or high-entropy nanoalloys, the NSIRE also significantly improves the reconstruction accuracy of the lighter atoms. Without the need of retraining, the NSIRE offers a flexible and fast approach to determine the 3D atomic structures of a wide range of materials of various structures and compositions, demonstrating strong generalizability. Due to the universality of atomicity in the microscopic world, this strategy of introducing the complex atomic potential priors through generative models can also be applied to other inverse problems in high-resolution electron microscopic imaging, enabling the precise characterization of materials.
It should be emphasized that this work mainly focuses on improving the ADF-based AET. NSIRE relies on RESIRE as linear generalized inverse for inversion and cannot be directly applied to nonlinear data such as HRTEM54, nor can it effectively reconstruct light-elemental atoms or large-sized samples whose thickness exceeds the depth-of-field. Moreover, when non-null-space artifacts such as sub-volume boundaries of thin film are present, the atomic prior is disturbed and leads to sub-optimal reconstruction. Finally, although we introduce an accelerated sampling technique, it still has higher computational consumption than IRTs and end-to-end networks, especially when the number of projections or the image size is large. Introducing more accurate physical models and more advanced generative models to address these issues remains the primary focus of future work.

IRTs that minimize the difference between the observed and computed projections using the least squares method can satisfy the definition of generalized inverse under sparse projection conditions. Among them, simultaneous algebraic reconstruction technique (SART) and simultaneous iterative reconstruction technique (SIRT) are the fastest. However, their forward and backward projection steps rely on mathematical approximation, which is not precise enough for accurate 3D atomic structure reconstruction. Fourier-based iteration techniques, such as EST and GENFIRE12, use Fourier transforms for projection and back-projection and introduce oversampling57 in their implementation to achieve high accuracy, but require a large amount of memory to assemble the 3D Fourier array. This further reduces the efficiency of iterative sampling. We recommend using the RESIRE algorithm as A−. As the DDPM generation process (from noise to data) is approximately the inverse of the forward diffusion process (from data to noise), at least 1000 iterations are required to obtain high-quality samples, with each iteration involves 50 RESIRE calculations, making the process highly inefficient. Therefore, we use an accelerated sampling algorithm denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM)39, which enables multi-step sampling and reliably accelerates atomic tomogram generation by a factor of 10. The NSIRE calculation was performed on a RTX 4090 GPU, with a memory consumption of 12 G. Depending on the number of projections, it took ~5 min for twinned Pt (14 tilt-angles, 256 × 256 × 190 voxels) and 6 min for PtCo (23 tilt-angles, 256 × 256 × 134 voxels). Since typical AET experiments usually require several hours for projection collection, we believe that this calculation time is acceptable in practical applications.
It is worth noting that while many diffusion-model-based methods exist to deal with inverse problems involving noise, most of them rely on singular value decomposition (SVD) to cope with noise in the observed constraint62,63. However, due to the unbounded nature of the Radon operator A, SVD is difficult. Therefore, our iterative algorithm is mainly based on noise-free method41, and Poisson-Gaussian noise should be completely removed during tilt-series pre-processing (Supplementary Figs. 39), which causes a slight resolution degradation that can be counted into the B-factor.
