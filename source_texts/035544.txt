Neural Radiance Fields have become a leading approach to 3D reconstruction, but they were built for natural scenes captured from many angles. Real-world datasets often violate that requirement. Fluorescence microscopy is one such case, and it breaks almost every assumption the setting rests on. A widefield microscope does not view a specimen from different directions, instead it captures a stack of 2D slices by moving the focal plane along a single axis, and the images are photon-limited and noisy. How standard NeRF implementations behave when their input assumptions are violated this way, and whether a constrained single-axis z-stack can still yield a reasonable 3D reconstruc- tion, had not been studied. This thesis works through that question in stages. Several standard implementations are compared, Instant-NGP, Nerfacto and TensoRF among them, and TensoRF is adapted to the requirements of the data and then tested on a fluorescence z-stack of an embryo and an independent tissue dataset. On a per-slice basis, measured by PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS against ground truth, the results are strong, but the problem appears in three dimensions. As the axial sampling is thinned the learned representation is overtaken by simple trilinear interpolation, and the gap grows as the data become sparser. Querying the density field directly shows why. The model satisfies its training slices while leaving the surrounding volume degenerate, with density collapsing onto the coordinate axes rather than the specimen. The failure traces to a shape-radiance ambi- guity in which the single-axis geometry leaves the axial dimension underdetermined. This locates the limitation in the training objective rather than the representation. A z-stack is not a set of photographs but a set of intensity measurements on a known 3D grid, and supervising the density field directly against those measurements recovers the coherent volume that ray-based rendering could not. To our knowledge this is the first study to evaluate standard ray-based NeRF on fluo- rescence z-stacks and to diagnose its failure directly by interrogating the density field. The result is offered as a proof of concept and motivates radiance-field methods shaped around the physics of fluorescence imaging.
