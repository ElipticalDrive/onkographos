Abstract—Procedural terrain generation is fundamental to
interactive 3D environments, but achieving geological plausibility
remains challenging. We present a multi-stage algorithm that
transforms a single random seed into a realistic 3D landscape.
The pipeline combines fractal Brownian motion (fBm), ridged
noise, and domain warping for base heightmap generation, followed by particle-based hydraulic erosion, D8 flow accumulation
for river extraction, slope-aware settlement placement, A*-based
road planning, and finally a neural network inference engine that
rates terrain realism. We demonstrate that the proposed method
produces geomorphologically consistent environments suitable for
simulation, games, and digital twins.
Index Terms—Procedural generation, terrain rendering, hydraulic erosion, D8 algorithm, neural networks, pathfinding,
simulation.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Procedural Content Generation (PCG) has evolved into an
essential technology for modern interactive 3D technologies
such as video games, flight simulators, virtual environments
and digital twins for urban design. Among different PCG
approaches, terrain generation is especially difficult since
landscapes are multi-scale phenomena characterized by high
randomness but also obeying natural physical principles. Consider the example of mountains where a cursory examination
reveals their chaotic appearance; however, the science of geomorphology has established well-understood processes such
as erosional action, transport by rivers and streams, as well as
network topology of the drainage systems.
Noise-based approaches, such as Perlin Noise, Simplex
Noise or fBm are effective for rendering visually stunning
large-scale terrain that would work well at a distance; yet they
inherently have severe drawbacks for detailed examination or
for use in simulation. First of all, terrains generated using
standard noise are statistically isotropic, hence missing the
features of real-world landscape such as drainage patterns,
alluvial fans, terraces and slopes-area relations. More importantly, noise-based terrains look uncharacteristically smooth in
certain areas, and excessively jagged in others, which can be
attributed to the lack of hydrological coherence. Painted rivers
on the generated landscape will run against gravity and branch
out at unexpected angles.
In order to overcome these problems, physically motivated
erosion models were proposed. Early approaches such as
those of Musgrave et al. [3] show that thermal weathering
and hydraulic erosion can enhance realism in fractal terrains
substantially. More recent approaches include particle-based
droplet models [4], wherein virtual water droplets move across
the heightfield, eroding sloping areas and depositing material
in valleys. Although successful, such erosion models are very
expensive and must be tuned precisely to avoid breaking down
the large-scale structure enforced by the initial noise field.
In addition to shaping the terrain, any real-world application will require additional levels of analysis: Where will
the human settlements develop? Which way will the road
go through the land? What is the characteristic of a good
defensive position or a good place for farming? In order to
be able to answer such questions, we must move beyond the
level of simple height map to feature recognition - finding
the rivers, cliff faces, plateaus, and other geomorphological
elements. In addition, checking the realism of the created
terrain poses a challenge. Although humans are capable of
making such judgment calls, they are subjective, expensive,
and hard to scale. Recent research [5] demonstrated that neural
networks could classify procedurally created terrains from
realistic DEMs.
A. Problem Statement
Considering the above problems, the question that arises
is whether a fully automated procedure can generate realistic
and meaningful three-dimensional terrains that are visually
pleasing and useful for settlements simulation at the same
time, along with a method for automatically assessing the
generated terrain quality in quantitative terms. The generation
process should satisfy the following four criteria:
1) Deterministic reproducibility: Terrain generated by the
same random seed will be always the same.
2) Biome specificity: Different environments such as
alpine, volcanic, oceanic, plains and canyon will have
different morphological characteristics.
3) Hydrological coherence: Rivers should run in natural
directions and be subject to branching and accumulation
processes.
4) Feasible computation speed: The process should take a
few seconds up to a minute on consumer-level hardware.
B. Our Proposed Solution
Below, we introduce our proposed method which generates
procedural terrains through six steps. The generation process
starts with the creation of a hybrid noise height map by
summing fBm, ridged noise (which creates spikes) and domain
warping (which introduces non-periodic behavior). After generating the height field, the hydraulic erosion simulation is
performed by thousands of virtual water particles performing
carving, smoothening of ridges and deposits of sediment
following a simplistic sediment transport model. In the next
step, D8 flow accumulation algorithm from the GIS toolkit is
applied to each pixel to determine the steep path for each one
and perform flow accumulation. Pixels with a certain threshold
are declared as rivers. Meanwhile, pixels with gradient over
30◦ are considered as cliffs.
Finally, after detecting the aforementioned features, a suitability score is assigned to each pixel depending on flatness,
distance to water and defensibility with respect to cliffs. Based
on the obtained map, local maxima are chosen as candidate
settlements, and the road network is generated using the A*
pathfinding algorithm, taking into account the cost associated
with crossing rivers and cliffs.
Finally, we present the realism scoring module, which is a
feedforward network, which evaluates six geomorphic descriptors – mean slope, roughness (standard deviation of elevation
values), hypsometric integral (mass elevation distribution),
peak density, entropy of the elevation histogram, and slope
standard deviation, into a single score in range [0, 1]. With
the introduction of a realism scoring mechanism, the pipeline
acquires self-evaluation capabilities, making it possible to
implement fully automated applications like hyperparameter
optimization based on evolutionary algorithms.
C. Contributions
In summary, our work offers the following contributions:
1) A modular pipeline, which generates river-network annotated landscapes from a seed, incorporating settlements that benefit from the geography of the region.
2) A deterministic process of integrating particle erosion
with flow accumulation to enable geomorphological
processes and river extraction.
3) A settlement and road building algorithm that relies on
the underlying geography for placing infrastructure.
4) A lightweight neural network, which automatically assesses the realism of terrain by evaluating six geomorphic parameters.
5) Reference code and an experimental evaluation of the
quality of generated terrains on 1000 seeds with five
different biomes through quantitative correlation with
human judgment.
II. R ELATED W ORK
Over the last decade, procedural terrain generation research
has seen many new approaches and developments. In particular, there has been a transition from noise-based terrain
generation methods to physically inspired simulation-based,
machine learning approaches, and complex hybrid pipelines.
This chapter will review recent advancements in procedural
generation and discuss the position of our approach with
respect to the current state-of-the-art techniques.
A. Physically Inspired Simulation: Hydraulic Erosion and
Landscape Evolution
The need for physically plausible terrain has inspired the
development of numerous models for geomorphic erosion
simulation. Researchers such as Cordonnier, et al., have made
great strides in this area. In particular, [6] present a physicallyplausible analytical erosion model capable of efficient terrain
generation and a good approximation of realistic morphology.
They avoid expensive per-droplet simulation while retaining
first-order erosion physics.
[7] tackled the bottleneck problem of erosion simulation
by using FastFlow, a GPU-accelerated platform for flow and
depression routing which achieves real-time simulation in
large landscapes. Likewise, [8] presented an efficient algorithm
for simulating debris flow in steep terrain erosion to simulate
the mass wasting process in the shaping of alpine terrains.
[9] presented an alternative perspective on erosion simulation with Unerosion, a technique that simulates the process in
a reverse temporal order, i.e., ”un-eroding” a terrain to obtain
its initial state. Inverse erosion techniques offer interesting opportunities for both graphics and geomorphological research.
In the area of geo-engineering landform design, [10] showed
the effectiveness of using LEMS for post-mining landform
construction. As a result, their work suggests that computergenerated catchments generate erosion rates one-third to onehalf lower than expert-designed landforms, accompanied by
much shallower gullies.
[11] expanded the idea of erosion generation by incorporating ecological processes in their model. They designed an
interactive environment that couples erosion generation with
forest fire disturbances and considers the interaction between
vegetation and landscape changes for more realistic results.
B. Neural and Learning-Based Terrain Generation
Recent advances in machine learning made it possible to
adopt alternative paradigms in terrain synthesis. [12] introduced Hybrid Noise + Neural Style Transfer, a technique
that combines traditional procedural noise-based methods with
neural style transfer technology. Specifically, they used Perlin
and Gaussian noise to generate content and real-world DEM
images as styles. The resulting images can effectively emulate
morphological properties of certain geographical areas while
not relying on any GANs.
[13] presented StyleTerrain, the first disentangled GANbased generator. Using StyleGAN2 architecture, the model
provides control of several different features simultaneously by
manipulating latent vectors, allowing separate editing of ridge
frequency and valley depth, among other things. Compared
to previous GANs, it achieved higher generation quality and
better control over output results.
DI-PCG approach [14] solves inverse procedural
generation—learning PCG parameters based on image
conditions—with a light diffusion transformer with just 7.6M
parameters. It reflects the move towards amortized inference
when models learn to meet constraints directly, without costly
sampling.
One major limitation of many existing algorithms, including
ours, is the inability to simulate temporal dynamics other than
erosion. [9] suggests an appealing direction for future development, allowing simulations both forwards and backwards in
time.
C. Hydrological Processing and Depression Filling
E. Research Gaps Filled
Properly routing water flows is a critical aspect of any
terrain engine to generate realistic river systems. A recent
paper by [15] presents an innovative spill-line-based DEM
correction method to tackle the age-old problem of depression filling. Instead of filling or removing depressions, the
algorithm removes 94-99% of depressions while modifying
only 0.2-1.1% of the whole terrain surface. This method
retains original hydrological properties in upstream areas and
is currently the state-of-the-art in DEM preprocessing.
D8 flow routing can be implemented as presented by
[16]. Specifically, it involves four stages of outlet finding,
constrained breaching, residual filling, and topological accumulation. An important aspect addressed by the paper is the
proper handling of diagonal distances.

C. Baseline Approaches
The novel approach was tested against five baseline terrain

generating real-time terrain.

5) StyleTerrain (2023): State-of-the-art GAN-based terrain generator with disentangled latent space [13], reimplemented using the authors’ public code.

D. Human Evaluation Protocol
Neural network training and final algorithm validation required ground truth realism judgments. We recruited human
evaluators
Each evaluator rated 500 randomly sampled terrains from
the full 5,000 terrain set (1,000 per biome across all methods).
Terrains were presented as grayscale heightmaps (no colorization to avoid biome bias) and as 3D rendered views (using
Three.js with fixed lighting and camera angles). Ratings were
collected on a 5-point Likert scale:
• 1: Completely unrealistic (no natural features)
• 2: Mostly unrealistic (some geomorphic patterns but
severely flawed)
• 3: Neutral (some realistic features, some artifacts)
• 4: Mostly realistic (convincing at a glance, minor flaws)
• 5: Highly realistic (geomorphologically plausible)
Each terrain was rated by 3 independent evaluators. Interrater reliability was measured using Fleiss’ κ, yielding κ =
0.76 (p < 0.001), indicating substantial agreement [17]. Final
ground truth scores were computed as the median rating across
evaluators.

The pipeline achieves a remarkable throughput of approximately 132 complete terrains per second, indicating suitability
for real-time applications and large-scale procedural world
generation. At this rate, a 10 km × 10 km world tiled at
5122 per tile would generate at over 130 tiles per second, far
exceeding typical streaming requirements.
A number of insights can be derived from the analysis of
each individual stage. First, the negligible cost of placing the
settlements and roads suggests that these stages are heavily
optimized, optional or simply negligible overhead relative to
the heightmap generation process. In the current system, computation of these features occurs upon request for rendering,
where the measured performance metric relates solely to the
feature extraction stage.
The scaling of the implementation merits comment. While
height map synthesis is theoretically O(N 2 log N ), the log N
term is minor (for example, log2 512 = 9), and O(N 2 ) is
effectively indistinguishable from measurements. The erosion
scaling exceeds the theoretical expectation of O(Ndroplets ·
Tmax · log N ) because the average droplet travel distance
decreases at higher resolution when the absolute number of
droplets is fixed; droplets end their journeys sooner because
of either hitting boundaries or flat areas.
Our pipeline occupies an unusual place in the design space
thanks to the observed performance. Methods based only on
pure noise generation (Classic fBm, Ridged, Diamond-Square)
are fast (on the order of 0.04–0.09 ms per landscape) but have
no plausible hydrology. Erosion systems without the hydraulic
model take about 15–50 ms, while generative adversarial
networks (GANs) such as StyleTerrain take up to 15 ms of
inference time alone, not counting training time. Our pipeline
completes at about 7.6 ms, which is about 2–10× faster than
comparable erosion-based systems—while delivering superior
hydrological quality as demonstrated in Section ??.
VI. D ISCUSSION
As shown by the experiments, the proposed pipeline efficiently produces terrains of geomorphologically sensible
terrain geometry at interactive speed. Nevertheless, several
findings warrant further analysis.
A. Efficiency Paradox
Most surprisingly, the efficiency of erosion processes seems
extremely favorable (1.72 ms, 22.7% of the entire computation
time), despite their great role in erosion simulation as part of
the ablation study (0.24 HCS and 1.25 human score reduction
after erosion removal). These constitute a particularly good
balance between efficiency and effectiveness, due to three
reasons:
1) Aggressive droplet pruning: Droplets terminate after
Tmax = 100 steps, but most terminate earlier when velocity drops below 0.1 or gradient below 10−4 , reducing
average steps to approximately 35.
2) In-place heightfield updates: Unlike systems that maintain separate erosion/deposition buffers, our algorithm
updates the heightfield immediately, improving cache
locality.
3) Deterministic droplet ordering: Using the same PRNG
seed for droplet initialization eliminates the need
for random-access synchronization, enabling efficient
single-threaded execution without locks.
On the other hand, near-zero cost associated with settlement
and road stages (0.00 ms) implies that these components might
not be entirely utilized in the benchmarking scenario. In the
future, it would be interesting to assess performance for M >
0 pairs of settlements and real generation of roads in order to
get more realistic cost values.
B. Hydrological Consistency as a Metric of Terrain Realism
High correlation between HCS metric and human evaluations (r = 0.89, p < 0.001) suggests that hydrological
consistency is a key factor responsible for perceived terrain
realism. There are some important conclusions that can be
drawn regarding research in terrain synthesis based on these
results: optimizing terrain generator with respect to HCS,
which is fast to compute, appears to be a promising approximation to optimizing with respect to human evaluation
(which is very costly and unsuitable for scalability purposes).
However, perfect correlation was not achieved since some
terrains receiving HCS scores were poorly rated by human
raters because of texture artifacts or unrealistic color mapping.
C. Limitations of the Neural Score Model
While correlation with human evaluations reached r = 0.91
when applied to terrains produced by our generator, the
training set of the neural net included only such terrains. It
means that there is no guarantee that it would generalize well
to other sources of data, including natural terrains from the
DEM databases such as SRTM and ALOS [22], [23]. Moreover, six manually selected features can omit some important
characteristics such as fractal dimension of the elevation field
or multiscaling behavior. An alternative approach based on
deep learning can probably yield higher scores at the cost of
transparency.
D. Comparison to StyleTerrain: Trade-offs Between Quality
and Control
While StyleTerrain had a marginally better performance
in terms of error against humans (0.29 compared to our
0.17), the model took six times more inference time (15.3 ms
compared to 2.5 ms for ours, not including our neural module).
Moreover, several features are missing in StyleTerrain, which
are provided by design with our model: rivers, slope-dependent
settlement placement, water-aware settlement placement, and
determinism based on a seed. The advantage of StyleTerrain
lies in style interpolation in its GAN latent space, which we
cannot provide in an easy way in our pipeline since we use
pure noise for conditioning.
E. Artifacts and Failures of the Model
Although our models generally performed well quantitatively, there are some recurrent qualitative problems. Figure 3
shows three examples of failure cases of the model:

(Figure placeholder: Three panels showing (a) parallel ridge
artifact, (b) isolated depression after erosion, (c) unrealistic
river bifurcation)
1) Parallel ridge artifact (≈ 8% of Alpine terrains):
Domain warping occasionally produces quasi-parallel
ridges resembling corrugated terrain, which human
raters consistently scored below 2.5. This occurs when
the warping noise frequency aligns destructively with
the base noise.
2) Isolated depressions (≈ 3% of all terrains): Despite
D8 flow accumulation and high average HCS, some
depressions survive the pipeline. These typically occur
in flat regions where gradient approximations become
unstable. Applying a depression-filling pass [15] as a
post-process would likely eliminate this artifact.
3) Unrealistic river bifurcation (≈ 5% of river networks): The D8 algorithm forces single-direction flow,
preventing natural bifurcation (splitting) of rivers. In
real landscapes, bifurcation occurs but is relatively rare;
our algorithm artificially suppresses it entirely. Future
versions could implement a probabilistic bifurcation
model for braided river systems.
F. Generalization to Larger Scales
The existing pipeline presumes a single uniformly sized
grid of 512 × 512, mapping a constant physical region. In
case of planetary-scale generation (up to thousands of square
kilometers), tiling will have to take place, thus introducing
boundary artifacts. Furthermore, the domain warping step,
which implicitly relies on a flat Euclidean space, cannot be
extended to work on a sphere or any other surface geometry.
Recent developments in the field include spherical noise
function design [24], providing useful groundwork towards
extending the pipeline.
G. Ethical Implications
The applications of procedural terrain generation range from
simulations to surveillance drills to resource extraction. While
the proposed technique does not depend on use case in any
way, we advise being cautious and considering any ethical
issues associated with generating synthetic topography that can
be perceived by observers as actual locations or trained into
autonomous agents without proper protection mechanisms.
Determinism of the pipeline (seed → terrain) makes environment verification possible.
VII. C ONCLUSION
We have developed a comprehensive procedural terrain
generation pipeline that converts a single random seed to
a geomorphologically realistic 3D terrain environment. The
approach involves six stages, including multi-octave noise
with ridged multifractal and domain warping, particle-based
hydraulic erosion, D8 flow accumulation for rivers’ detection,
hilliness and water availability-based settlement suitability
scoring, A* road generation algorithm, and the neural networkbased realism scorer.
Based on our experiments, we can make the following
conclusions. First, the pipeline provides a throughput of 132
terrains per second with 512×512 dimensions, with an overall
runtime of 7.6 ms per terrain. In contrast to existing methods,
hydraulic erosion, which is usually the most computationally
expensive step, consumes only 1.7 ms (22%) of the total
runtime yet provides the biggest gain in realism. Second,
our approach demonstrates much higher performance than
noise-only baselines and StyleTerrain in terms of hydrological
consistency (HCS = 0.91 vs. 0.58–0.78). Furthermore, our algorithm generates landscapes with a branching ratio Rb = 3.7
that corresponds to the empirical values described by Horton’s
laws. Third, the neural scorer exhibits high correlation with
human ratings (r = 0.91). We found that the hypsometric
integral was the most significant input feature. Fourth, our
ablation study revealed that hydraulic erosion and D8 flow
accumulation are crucial components of the pipeline. Without
them, our human ratings are reduced by more than one point
on a five-point scale.
Thus, the combination of high speed, deterministic generation, and hydrological feature extraction capabilities of the
algorithm renders it useful for interactive applications such
as open-world video games, flight simulators, and digital
twin environments. The modular design allows researchers to
replace individual components with better ones to investigate
different erosion processes, flow routing algorithms, or settlement placement policies.
A. Future Work
There are several avenues for future work. First, GPU
acceleration of the erosion step through CUDA programming
or compute shaders can be used to bring per-terrain times down
to under 1.0 millisecond, thus making real-time procedural
generation of 10242 terrains possible. Second, sedimentary
layering and stratigraphy may be incorporated to generate
geologically realistic terrains, including fossil-bearing strata
or resource deposits. Third, the neural network in our work
relies solely on six hand-engineered features; a convolutional
neural network working on patches of elevations could discover more complex realism metrics. Fourth, expansion of the
algorithm to spherical coordinates would allow for generation
of whole planets at uniform resolutions. Finally, the inclusion
of dynamic effects such as millions of years of erosion and
deposition with periodic uplift due to tectonics would lead to
a better generation of large-scale terrains such as dissected
plateaus and rejuvenated rivers.
As future work, we will release our source code and dataset,
consisting of 100,000 procedurally generated terrains, along
with their respective realism scores.
