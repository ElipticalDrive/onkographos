Abstract
Surface Reconstruction based on posed RGB images is a difficult and important task to allow 3D meshes to be generated without expensive scanning tools. The generation of accurate meshes is also important for robots trying to manipulate real-world objects. Constructing such meshes from camera images is a promising, cost effective direction. Recently Volume Rendering based solutions such as Neural Radiance Fields and Neural Implicit Surfaces reached better performance than non-ML solutions such as COLMAP’s Structure-from-Motion pipeline. Most methods extract a 3D mesh directly from the density field or train an SDF instead of a density field which can be trivially converted to other representations. DepthMeshNeRF follows the former but utilizes depth maps generated from the input poses to create a 3D point cloud. This point cloud is filtered and converted to a 3D mesh. The underlying model is trained utilizing recent advancements in NeRF model training like InstantNGP and loss functions that reduce artifacts. DepthMesh-NeRF also applies a new loss function named Ray Total Variation loss and ways to alter Volume Rendering weights for better depth extraction on occluded and thin geometry. The solution demonstrates great performance in diffuse lighting conditions and presents improvements on ambiguous surfaces against previous NeRF based solutions.
1 Introduction
Surface reconstruction from posed (rgb images including a camera to world transformation matrix) and unposed images has gained popularity for use in generating 3D meshes. Such meshes are used for 3D modeling and printing as well as creating representations of real objects in the virtual world. The generation of accurate meshes is also important for robots trying to manipulate real-world objects. Constructing such meshes from camera images is a promising, cost effective direction.
While reconstructions can be highly accurate when using 3D scans from LiDAR sensors [31] or structured-light systems [23], posed image-based methods generally do not achieve the same level of geometric precision. Their main advantage is that image capture is a cost-effective way to represent an object without the specialized hardware, strict calibration requirements, and acquisition fragility associated with projector-based scanning. The challenge is to reconstruct surface geometry from images alone, since geometric information can be lost due to lighting variation, occlusions, and suboptimal camera placement.
While prior work has explored NeRF-based surface reconstruction pipelines, DepthMeshNeRF does not build upon a single existing pipeline, but instead proposes a method utilizing the depth estimates provided by a NeRF (Neural Radiance Field) [19] model to reconstruct 3D surface point clouds of objects. Such point clouds can then be converted to meshes using approaches like screened Poisson surface reconstruction [10]. While most approaches directly train SDFs (Signed Distance Field) to represent a scene, DepthMeshNeRF utilizes the density field directly. This design choice is deliberate: existing methods that train SDFs sacrifice compatibility with the broader NeRF ecosystem, whereas operating on the density field allows the method to benefit from ongoing NeRF research. The contributions listed below are then layered on top of this foundation to address the specific failure modes that arise in density-based reconstruction.
The contributions are
(i)
a new loss function (RayTV) to encourage flat surfaces inside the density field representation,
(ii)
re-weighing rays to get more accurate depth estimations in scenarios where the rendering weights don’t sum to 1 or dual surfaces are present along rays and
(iii)
visibility and backface filtering of the point clouds to ensure consistent representations and filtering of noise without loss of quality.
The proposed DepthMeshNeRF pipeline is shown on Figure 1, where the novel contributions are indicated by a green, bold font at the stages they are applied.
The DepthMeshNeRF pipeline. The training of the model is adjusted by applying the novel RayTV loss (i) to encourage flat surface representations. The point cloud extraction is done by depth estimations along the ray extended by re-weighing the rays (ii) where the rendered depths are incorrect. Point clouds are further filtered (iii) by visibility and removal of backface points before the mesh reconstruction.
2 Related works
COLMAP’s [26] structure-from-motion pipeline is a fast way to create point cloud reconstructions based on images. It estimates extrinsic and intrinsic camera parameters and provides an accurate sparse 3D reconstruction while having the ability for full reconstructions, albeit at lower accuracy. Noise to noise mapping [16, 40] is an approach with which the reconstructed point clouds of COLMAP can be denoised and SDFs can be trained.
Representing scenes via NeRF, [19]. Images are synthesized by sampling 5D coordinates along camera rays (a). An MLP or other density field representation produces color and density at the locations (b). These values are then composed using volume rendering into an image (c). This final representation allows training to depend on the pixel-wise color of the ground truth image (d). (Recreation of a Figure in [19])
NeRFs [19] prove to be a powerful method for view synthesis. By applying volume rendering [18] to posed images, it is able to synthesize new images from arbitrary viewing directions. Figure 2 illustrates the process from scene representation to training loss. The original MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) based representation is improved upon by the authors of IstantNGP [20] via multi-resolution hash encoding, decreasing the time required to train from hours to minutes.
The structure of NeRFs and volume rendering don’t enforce the generated field to be geometrically correct. The most common artifacts of this are “floaters” and “background collapse”, a phenomenon in which distant surfaces are incorrectly modeled as semi-transparent clouds of dense content close to the camera. Mip-NeRF 360 [1] proposed distortion loss which is minimized when the weights occupy as little space along the rays as possible. DVGOv2 [28] further improves the loss function to linear time complexity in relation to the sampled point count. DS-NeRF [3] allows the sparse estimations provided by COLMAP to further regularize the density field.
Density fields can be converted to meshes using marching cubes [14] [20]. NeRF2Mesh [30] further improves the mesh by backpropagating image-space loss gradients similar to nvdiffrec[21].
SDF based approaches represent the objects directly as the zero level set of an SDF. IDR [37] represents a scene without utilizing volume rendering while UNISURF [24] combines volume and surface rendering into one. NeuS [32, 34] and VolSDF [36] directly train the SDF via volume rendering, utilizing the regularization [8] advantages of SDF based representations to ensure smooth surfaces. Methods like Voxurf [35], MonoSDF [38] and NeuS2 [33] utilize InstantNGP’s feature grid encoding for quicker training. [13, 17].
As for converting a point cloud to a mesh, several non-ML algorithms are in use, like alpha shapes [5], ball-pivoting [2, 4] and screened Poisson surface reconstruction [10]. A recent survey [39] categorizes point cloud based mesh reconstruction methods with fitting geometric primitives, encoder-decoder based architectures, iterative deformation and SDF based reconstructions. Noise to noise mapping [16, 40] could also be utilized. DepthMeshNeRF uses Screened Poisson surface reconstruction due to its simplicity and watertight mesh creating properties.
2.1 Volume Rendering for scene representation
NeRF [19] represents the space as a continuous field of density values. A point in space can be queried for its density value σ and RGB color value c with its 3D coordinates and viewing direction {x,y,z,θ,ϕ}∈R5. With the described field a pixel in image space can be rendered using volume rendering. Considering a r(t) = o + td ray with o∈R3 origin and d∈R3 view direction in world space, while t ∈ [tn, tf] is a parameter inside the near and far bounds of the camera. The volume rendering integral of the pixel color is estimated using quadrature, resulting in N evenly spaced sample points in the range [tn, tf]∋ti. Thus, predicted color C^(r) is
C^(r)=∑i=1NTi(1−exp⁡(−σiδi))ci, where Ti=exp⁡(−∑j=1i−1σjδj)
(1)
where δi = ti + 1 − ti is the distance between adjacent samples.
The function of Equation 1 is differentiable, thus the underlying model can be trained using backpropagation. The MSE (Mean Squared Error) loss is
Lcolor=Er∈R[||C^(r)−C(r)||22]
(2)
where C(r) is the ground truth color and R is a set of rays.
2.2 Difficulties of NeRF point cloud based mesh reconstruction
NeRF training does not enforce geometric correctness in the density field, which leads to several difficulties in using depth estimations for mesh reconstruction. When using depth estimation to extract a surface point cloud, this results in holes and inconsistencies. The naive depth estimation equation creates floating points in cases where the ray weights don’t sum to 1 or aren’t packed into a single contiguous section along the ray. Point cloud filters are required to eliminate floaters and noise occurring even after applying TV loss and depth supervision loss. After using Screened Poisson surface reconstruction, the final meshes require additional filtering to create a consistent and accurate mesh.
3 Methodology
3.1 The DepthMeshNeRF Pipeline
The following pipeline is proposed to solve the difficulties discussed in Section 2.2:
(1)
Alpha re-weighing along rays, normalizing the ray weights to sum to 1. (See Section 3.3.)
(2)
Dual surface re-weighing along rays, modifying ray weights to render the first surface they encounter. (Applied when the object occludes itself in a way that the first encountered surface has a less than 1 alpha value. See Section 3.3.)
(3)
RayTV loss, regularizing the density field by applying TV loss to the ray with 2 parallel rays’ density values. (See Section 3.2.)
(4)
Viewpoint based point cloud filtering, eliminating points occluded from the rendered view points due to viewing direction based rendering. Points with normal vectors facing away from the camera are also filtered. See Section 3.5.
(1) helps correctly reconstructing thin surfaces, while non-conti-guous packing of weights along the ray is eliminated by (2). Inconsistencies in surfaces, especially flat ones, are reduced by applying (3). (4) reduces artifacts and noise inside the extracted point clouds, while a triangle count based mesh filter can eliminate any remaining floating artifacts.
DepthMeshNeRF utilizes the backbone introduced by InstantNGP [20] while adjusting the bounding box to [-1,1] instead of [0,1]. All parameters where distance is taken into account, such as occupancy, bit thresholds are adjusted accordingly.

3.2 Ray Total Variation (RayTV) loss
Visualizing effects of RayTV loss on DTU scan24. The covered pose and image is visible on (a). When LRayTV is not used, the point cloud (and later the generated mesh) includes large holes, inconsistently placed on farthest left wall from the viewpoint (b). When including LRayTV the point cloud (and thus the generated mesh) is more consistent with one’s expectations and the ground truth’s flat wall (c). While the approach can reduce high-frequency details, much like a low-pass filter, unlike simple smoothing LRayTV actively drives wall surfaces toward the true plane rather than leaving residual undulations.
Ray TV (Ray Total Variation) loss regularizes ambiguous flat surfaces. It differs from TV loss applied by Control-NeRF [11] by applying it along parallel rays rather than grid coordinates of the density field. For ray r(t), TV loss is computed for the σ values compared to 2 parallel rays offset by ray sample step size. A random perpendicular vector is picked to the viewing direction oa⊥d and another vector perpendicular to both ob⊥oa∧ob⊥d. This defines 2 more rays, ra and rb, by shifting the origin o of r(t) by a factor of the step size along the rays. An illustration of the parallel rays partaking in the calculations is shown in Figure 4. Ray TV loss computation is defined by Equation 4. A small ϵ is added inside the square root to avoid exploding gradients near zero. To increase performance, a Region of Interest of size N/4 is defined around the largest rendering weight of the primary ray. An example of LRayTV regularizing a flat surface is shown on Figure 3.

The depth estimation D^(r) won’t always return feasible depths on/near the surface of a model. Two distinct scenarios are pointed out where the estimated depth is inherently incorrect based on the density field alone.
•
By the definition of D^(r), non-opaque surfaces are estimated to be closer to the camera than the density field suggests. Low alpha re-weighing is applied when α(r) ∈ [αt, 1.0), meaning the rendered surface is semi-transparent. Due to the nature of Volume Rendering, thin surfaces are often represented by these areas as training only affects a couple sample points near these surfaces. This also occurs when we reach an object’s edge as the probability of a point existing in those regions (above 0 density) decreases continuously rather than stepwise. DepthMeshNeRF proposes the re-weighing formula of Equation 5 and achieves consistent estimation of depths for thin surfaces. The effects on ray weights and point clouds are shown in Figure 5. Since α = 1.0 is desirable for depth estimation, this is named low alpha re-weighing where the αt threshold acts as a way to filter noise.

3.5 Constructing a point cloud from the trained NeRF
Constructing a point cloud from the trained model is done by applying depth estimation to K poses, utilizing re-weighing of rendering weights indicated by Equations 5 and 6. Poses are chosen from the training views using farthest point sampling. DepthMeshNeRF utilizes Open3D [41] to work with the point clouds and meshes. Voxel downsampling and a DBSCAN [6] clustering based filter is applied to point cloud.
Effects of point cloud artifact filtering on scan24. The before (a) includes artifacts mentioned in Section 3.5 like floaters, incorrect normal vectors and below surface noise. Once these are filtered (b), the resulting point cloud is much cleaner to allow for mesh generation.
Normal vectors are estimated using the negative gradient of the density field at the estimated points’ positions. The dense point clouds are down sampled to increase the performance of filters and algorithms. Noise caused by artifacts such as floaters are filtered by applying the DBSCAN algorithm [6], merging low distance clusters and removing any cluster that corresponds to less than 1% of the total point cloud. Noise caused by depth estimation and lighting conditions while training are filtered by visibility and backface filters.
Point cloud visibility and backface filter example on DTU scan114 with Chamfer Distances displayed. The effects of the point cloud filters on the final mesh are shown throughout (a)-(d). For each, a section of scan114 is shown from the front (left) and the same area from the back of the mesh (right). The visibility filter helps cuts down the noise present behind the surface of the mesh while the backface filter reduces some noise above the surface. While the backface filter doesn’t have a significant effect on the Chamfer Distance metric, it improves visual quality.
To filter the incorrect normal vectors and cavities of the surface, DepthMeshNeRF creates a novel visibility and backface mask for the point cloud. For this, the chosen views that generated the point cloud are taken, and the points are reprojected onto them. The reprojection uses a reduced resolution by a factor of 2 or 4 along x and y axes. A point is visible from an angle if it is inside the image space after reprojection and is the closest point to the camera or is within a distance of 0.002. The algorithm filters out all points which aren’t visible from anywhere. This works well because extreme differences in lightning conditions between angles are learned as different colored cavities in the surface, which this filter smooths out. Backface filtering works alongside the calculation of visible points, marking every point for filtering which faces the same way as the viewing direction n · d > 0. This filters noise that exist above the surface generally with perpendicular normal vectors to the surrounding points’ normal vectors. The effects of visibility and backface point cloud filters are demonstrated in Figure 8. Combined effects of the filters discussed are shown in Figure 7.
3.6 Converting the point cloud to a mesh
As the filtered point cloud contains normal vectors, the screened Poisson surface reconstruction algorithm [10, 41] can be utilized to create a watertight mesh with a maximum octree depth of 9. Because K poses are chosen from the training poses we run the risk of not covering the entire space and generating backs/bottoms of objects. For certain datasets (mentioned later) additional angles are added to cover such areas. However, this sometimes isn’t feasible as only a small section of the space is covered by the training poses. Additionally, when only a small section is covered, the watertight reconstruction can extend the edges of the object to the sides of the [ − 1, 1] bounding box. This is filtered by removing the lower 3rd percentile densities returned by the screened Poisson surface reconstruction algorithm. Disconnected mesh sections with less than 1000 triangles are removed to eliminate any remaining artifacts above the surface of the object.
3.7 Experiments and Datasets
Both quantitative and qualitative evaluation is carried out on 2 datasets. DTU [9] is selected to evaluate against SoTA SDF based and non-Volume Rendering based surface reconstruction algorithms. (The 15 standard scenes are used.) Generally, the poses and images of the scenes only partially represent the objects, usually consisting of 49 or 64 poses with 1600x1200 images. The scenes contain challenging lightning conditions and surfaces varying in thickness, texture and curvature. For these scenes, the alpha masks provided by IDR [37] are utilized to decrease training time. The Chamfer Distance metrics are calculated using the evaluation script provided by the dataset.
The NeRF Synthetic [19] dataset is used to compare against Nerf2Mesh [30]. It contains 8 scenes, each with 100 training poses in 800x800 images. From this dataset K = 32 views sampled from training poses using farthest point sampling for point cloud generation, and an additional 4 poses are sampled from the sphere of the training poses to recreate the undersides of objects as mentioned. The dataset also provides alpha masks for the objects which are utilized to increase training speed. This dataset is less challenging than DTU due to the synthetic nature of its images, but the scenes still include specular reflections and highlights as well as thin surfaces. The Chamfer Distance metric is calculated following Nerf2Mesh’s [30] evaluation method where 2.5 million rays are cast from validation views at the original and reconstructed mesh to recover a point cloud.

The performance of DepthMeshNeRF to Nerf2Mesh on NeRF Synthetic’s “lego”, “mic”, “drum” and “ship” scenes are visually compared in Figure 10. The “ship” scene illustrates the difference in thin surface performance where Nerf2Mesh has the advantage. The net like structure between the deck and the mast of the ship is much better represented than with DepthMeshNeRF. The dissimilarity between the water representations is also striking as NeRF2Mesh has straighter edges while DepthMeshNeRF produces a smoother result.
5 Discussions
DepthMeshNeRF presents a novel way to generate mesh reconstructions based on density fields trained via volume rendering. Rather than utilizing a set density threshold for marching cubes, which results in a lot of noise and inaccuracies both on the inside and surface of the model, the depth map based reconstructions creates far less noise. The main advantage of the method is utilizing training poses to generate the depth maps and point clouds as this negates the effects of incorrectly set density values in parts of the scene which were never visible while training. The novel RayTV loss helps represent ambiguous, consistently lit areas as flat surfaces. The re-weighing of rays when rendering depth maps helps represent edges and dual surfaces consistently regardless of viewing direction. DepthMeshNeRF can reach and even surpass the performance of SoTA methods using the more popular implicit surface/SDF based learning approach.
Highlights of DepthMeshNeRF are the representation of the cable in the “mic” scene (Figure 10), where the cable is round with well positioned normal vectors. The “lego” scene (Figure 10) presents each individual lego piece in high detail with the expected even spacing, demonstrating how good the reconstruction resolution of DepthMeshNeRF is. The flat surfaces of scan40 (Figure 9) are well represented with minimal noise while facing the camera. This is also the result of the introduced RayTV loss’s regularizing capabilities. Scan65 (Figure 9) is also a highlight, as even in diverse lighting conditions DepthMeshNeRF is capable of representing the skull well. This is due to the diffuse behavior of light on the skull, where the simple appearance network is suitable for view synthesis.
DepthMeshNeRF can utilize advancements made in NeRF based training such as depth supervision and distortion loss with minimal to no changes required. In contrast, such functions have to be carefully adapted to work with SDF based approaches. In real world applications where a NeRF pipeline is already established, it is easier to adapt the proposals of DepthMeshNeRF rather than re-implement and test an entirely new approach. This idea goes both ways, as advancements made in volume rendering based learning can easily be adapted to work with DepthMeshNeRF, testing how they affect point cloud and mesh reconstruction.
The evaluation datasets do not provide scenes, apart from “drums” in NeRF Synthetic, where reconstruction of semi-transparent surfaces can be evaluated. DepthMeshNeRF chooses to reconstruct the first encountered surface, the outer shell of the object. While other methods may attempt to reconstruct both the inner and the outer surface, their performance is not clear with the standard datasets. For the application of, for example, 3D printing, always reconstructing the outer shell provides reliability which the compared solutions lack for such scenes.
In Tables 3 and 4 the performance advantage of DepthMeshNeRF in contrast to marching cubes based extraction was noted. This enables an interesting use case of the generated mesh by using it as an input to the second, refinement stage of Nerf2Mesh.
While the volume rendering re-weighing and the visibility filter contributions improve surface reconstruction quality, they eliminate the possibility of rendering behind transparent surfaces. For example, in the case of a pane of stained glass with objects behind it, the current method would only reconstruct the mesh of the glass without anything behind it. This may be undesirable for certain objects and scenes. A future direction could be dual-surface rendering based on dual-surface re-weighing to solve this. When weights are non-contiguous along the ray, instead of only rendering the first one, it should be possible to render a point for each contiguous section individually as long as the total alpha isn’t over 1 along the ray.
Reconstruction of ambiguous surfaces, especially under difficult lighting conditions remains a challenge. This originates from how NeRF represents directionality based colors. When the color is evaluated for a point in space only the 5D coordinates are used without taking a normal vector into consideration. Normal-NeRF [27] promises highly accurate normal vector estimation compared to previous methods [7, 12] inside the density field. This can also act to regularize the field in later stages. Adding this to DepthMeshNeRF could prove to regularize the surfaces the model currently has difficulties with.
Observing the meshes generated by DepthMeshNeRF it is noticeable that depending on the scene there is a varying amount of noise on the surfaces caused by lighting and texture representation. Apart from the initial LTV and LRayTV losses, smoothness for the encoding grid is not enforced. It is difficult to define a loss function or any form of regularization that can smooth surfaces when purely extracting them from the density field. Control-NeRF [11] utilizes an MLP based density field and points out that a form of smoothness over neighboring coordinates implicitly originates from representing the samples as 3D coordinates via some positional encoding function. While total variation loss encourages similar behavior, the usage of the hash encoding grid, when creating a smooth representation, is an open problem. In this case the downside of the introduced noise is usually outweighed by the training time and geometric accuracy it provides. When using the the Eikonal term, like in the training of SDFs like NeuS [32] [8], such issues are less common.
6 Conclusions
This paper presented DepthMeshNeRF, a depth map based point cloud and mesh reconstruction pipeline. It is is based on InstantNGP extended with loss functions like depth supervision, distortion, total variation loss. It introduced the novel RayTV loss for regularizing NeRF based geometry, encouraging locally flat surfaces. The depth extraction was modified by re-weighing rays where the alpha value doesn’t reach a threshold and where 2 distinct surfaces take part in the rendering of a ray (called dual surface re-weighing). These modifications allow the pipeline to generate very accurate point clouds.
The method was quantitatively and qualitatively evaluated on both the DTU and NeRF Synthetic datasets. Computational experiments confirmed that the Chamfer Distance performance of DepthMeshNeRF is close to or on par with previous works. It also demonstrated highly accurate, lower noise meshes compared to previous NeRF based methods, namely Nerf2Mesh. However, DepthMeshNeRF is currently outperformed by the best SDF based neural surface reconstruction approaches such as Voxurf or FastN2N.
Future improvement possibilities for the pipeline include better appearance representation based approaches (e.g., Normal-NeRF) to handle reflections, highlights and ambiguous surfaces. Another possibility is the application of some form of adaptive smoothing for the final point cloud, mesh or both. For transparent surfaces, the idea of dual surface rendering instead of dual surface re-weighing can also be explored.
Potential applications include 3D modeling, 3D printing and mesh reconstruction for graphical applications. Adapting to real world use requires only a couple improvements such as NeRF based bundle adjustment.
The results presented in this paper demonstrate that density field based neural reconstruction can be accurate when utilizing the estimated depth maps of the model.
