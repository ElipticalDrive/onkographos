Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) enables efficient, high-quality novel-view synthesis, but its reconstruction quality remains sensitive to how Gaussian primitives
are densified during optimization. Existing densification strategies commonly
rely on gradients, reconstruction errors, or isolated regional cues, which may
not capture the complementary perceptual and structural characteristics of
a scene. We propose PSCD-GS, a role-aware perception–structure collaborative densification framework that integrates image-space perceptual responses
with multi-view structural evidence. These cues are projected onto individual
Gaussian primitives through rendering-contribution-weighted aggregation, producing continuous memberships for perception-dominant, structure-dominant,
co-dominant, and low-response behaviors. This primitive-level representation is
used to prioritize densification candidates, select between cloning and splitting,
and guide the placement of newly generated Gaussians. Experiments on MipNeRF 360, Deep Blending, and Tanks & Temples show that PSCD-GS achieves
competitive dataset-level rendering quality while maintaining a controlled Gaussian representation. Qualitative comparisons suggest better preservation of fine
textures, object boundaries, and thin structures. These results indicate that
jointly modeling perceptual and structural evidence can provide more informative
guidance for selective Gaussian densification.

1 Introduction
Novel-view synthesis seeks to render photorealistic images from previously unobserved
viewpoints using a set of posed input images. Neural radiance fields (NeRFs) represent scenes as continuous implicit functions and have demonstrated high rendering
quality [1]. However, their reliance on dense ray sampling and repeated network evaluation often entails substantial computational cost during training and rendering.
Three-dimensional Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) provides an explicit alternative by representing a scene with anisotropic Gaussian primitives and rendering them through
differentiable splatting and alpha compositing [2]. This representation supports efficient optimization and real-time rendering, but its reconstruction quality depends
strongly on how Gaussian primitives are allocated and refined during training.
Densification is a central component of this refinement process. In the original
3DGS framework, primitives with sufficiently large accumulated view-space positional
gradients are selected for densification, after which their spatial scales determine
whether they are cloned or split [2]. Gradient averaging may nevertheless attenuate strong optimization signals arising from different pixels or views, resulting in
insufficient refinement of fine textures, object boundaries, and thin structures. Subsequent methods have addressed this limitation through absolute-gradient accumulation,
pixel-aware gradient estimation, budget-constrained allocation, resource-aware optimization, stochastic density control, and perceptual evidence modeling [3–10]. These
developments improve the selection or allocation of Gaussian primitives, but the
individual stages of densification are often guided by different criteria or treated
separately.
Effective densification involves more than identifying primitives that require additional capacity. Once a Gaussian has been selected, the method must determine
whether cloning or splitting is more appropriate and, when splitting is performed, how
the child primitives should be positioned. These decisions depend on scene attributes
that are not fully described by a single optimization signal. Perceptual responses
identify image regions whose appearance variations strongly affect visual similarity, whereas multi-view structural evidence characterizes boundaries, thin structures,
occlusion transitions, and local geometric changes. The two types of evidence are
complementary but not interchangeable: a texture-rich region may exhibit a strong
perceptual response without a pronounced structural discontinuity, while a low-texture
contour may remain structurally important despite a weaker perceptual response.
A unified primitive-level representation of these cues may therefore provide more
informative guidance for successive densification decisions.
Based on this observation, we propose PSCD-GS, a role-aware perception–
structure collaborative densification framework for 3DGS. Image-space perceptual
responses and multi-view structural cues are first mapped to individual Gaussian primitives through rendering-contribution-weighted aggregation. The resulting
Gaussian-level perceptual and structural evidence scores are converted into continuous
memberships in four roles: perception-dominant, structure-dominant, co-dominant,
and low-response. In contrast to a hard assignment, these memberships retain the
gradual transitions between regional characteristics and allow a primitive to respond to
2
both types of evidence. The derived role representation is then used to coordinate candidate prioritization and to construct competing cloning and splitting utilities, while
the associated response directions guide the placement of newly generated Gaussians.
Perceptually important primitives receive additional representation capacity,
whereas structurally important primitives are refined with reference to local structural evidence. Low-response primitives are treated conservatively to limit unnecessary
growth of the representation.
We evaluate PSCD-GS on Mip-NeRF 360 [11], Deep Blending [12], and Tanks
& Temples [13]. All baseline methods are re-evaluated using their official implementations and recommended configurations on the same scene sets and train/test
splits, with all experiments conducted on the same hardware platform. Method-specific
image-resolution policies and training settings follow the corresponding official implementations, while evaluation metrics are computed using a common protocol. The
reported results show that PSCD-GS achieves competitive or leading dataset-level
image-quality averages while maintaining a controlled number of Gaussian primitives.
Qualitative comparisons further indicate clearer texture details, more coherent object
boundaries, and better preservation of thin structures.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• We formulate Gaussian densification in a joint perception–structure space and
derive continuous primitive-level memberships for perception-dominant, structuredominant, co-dominant, and low-response roles. This formulation preserves the complementary and potentially overlapping characteristics of perceptual and structural
evidence.
• We construct a rendering-contribution-weighted aggregation mechanism that maps
image-space perceptual responses and multi-view structural cues to Gaussian primitives. The resulting role memberships support collaborative candidate prioritization
and define competing cloning and splitting utilities, while the associated response
directions guide the structure-aware placement of child Gaussians.
• We evaluate the proposed framework on three real-world novel-view-synthesis
benchmarks. The quantitative and qualitative results show that PSCD-GS provides
a favorable balance between rendering quality and representation size, with visible
improvements in texture-rich and structurally challenging regions.
2 Related work
2.1 3D Gaussian Splatting and density control
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) represents a scene using anisotropic Gaussian primitives and renders novel views through differentiable splatting and alpha compositing
[2]. Its explicit representation enables efficient optimization and real-time rendering,
but reconstruction quality depends on the spatial distribution, scale, and number
of Gaussian primitives. Several subsequent studies have improved other components
of the 3DGS pipeline. Mip-Splatting introduces three-dimensional smoothing and
two-dimensional filtering to reduce aliasing [14]; GaussianPro progressively propagates Gaussian primitives into poorly initialized regions using geometric priors [15];
3
and Scaffold-GS organizes local neural Gaussians around anchor points to support
view-adaptive rendering [16]. These approaches improve representation quality or
organization, but they do not primarily investigate how multiple regional cues should
coordinate the densification decisions considered in this work.
The original 3DGS adaptive density control selects primitives according to accumulated positional gradients in the view-space and applies cloning or splitting according
to their spatial scales [2]. Subsequent methods have revised different parts of
this procedure. AbsGS accumulates absolute gradients to prevent opposing pixellevel signals from canceling each other [3], while Pixel-GS introduces a pixel-aware
gradient that accounts for the number of pixels covered by each primitive [4]. MiniSplatting constrains the number of Gaussians through densification, simplification, and
importance-based sampling [5], while Taming 3DGS regulates primitive growth under
limited computational resources [6]. The Monte Carlo Markov chain formulation of
3DGS replaces heuristic density control with stochastic relocation and growth operations [7]. SteepGS instead interprets density control from an optimization perspective
and seeks a compact distribution through steepest-descent updates [8].
More recent work has extended densification beyond candidate scoring. Deng et
al. [10] combine an edge-aware candidate score with long-axis splitting, recoveryaware pruning, growth control, and multi-view updates, thereby addressing candidate
selection, primitive transformation, and overfitting within a broader density-control
pipeline. These developments show that effective densification requires coordinated
treatment of several stages rather than an isolated selection criterion. PSCD-GS
shares this general motivation, but focuses on deriving a common primitive-level
representation from complementary perceptual and structural evidence.
2.2 Perceptual- and structure-aware densification
Image-domain priors have increasingly been used to identify regions that require additional Gaussian capacity. The learned perceptual features provide a measure of visual
similarity that is more closely related to human judgments than conventional pixel-wise
distances [17]. Perceptual-GS incorporates scene-adaptive perceptual sensitivity into
Gaussian allocation and assigns finer granularity to visually important regions while
constraining the representation size [9]. CDC-GS introduces a loss-agnostic visualcomplexity prior derived from high-frequency wavelet components and encourages
consistency between image complexity and local primitive density; this consistency
is then used to identify primitives for densification and pruning [18]. These methods
demonstrate that appearance complexity and perceptual sensitivity can provide useful
information beyond rendering gradients or pixel-wise residuals.
Structural evidence offers a related but distinct source of guidance. Surface-oriented
representations, including SuGaR [19], 2D Gaussian Splatting [20], and Gaussian
Opacity Fields [21], improve geometric alignment or surface reconstruction by imposing stronger structural constraints on the Gaussian representation. Although their
objectives differ from density control, they demonstrate the importance of primitive
orientation and spatial organization for representing scene geometry. More directly,
SAD-GS estimates multi-scale image structure using structure tensors and Laplacian
4
scale-space analysis. It compares projected Gaussian extents with local image frequencies and performs multi-view-consistent, axis-dependent anisotropic splitting when a
primitive under-resolves the observed structure [22]. Deng et al. [10] likewise use
edge information to identify densification candidates and employ long-axis splitting to
reduce geometric distortion.
Perceptual and structural evidence nevertheless describe different properties of
a scene. Perceptual or frequency-based scores emphasize appearance-sensitive and
texture-rich regions, whereas structural cues can highlight boundaries, occlusion transitions, thin objects, and local geometric changes. A region may exhibit a strong
response in one dimension but a weak response in the other, and the two cues may
also be simultaneously important. PSCD-GS therefore maps both types of evidence to
individual Gaussian primitives and represents their relationship through continuous
perception-dominant, structure-dominant, co-dominant, and low-response memberships. Unlike methods based on a single complexity, perceptual, edge, or frequency
score, the proposed framework retains the complementary and overlapping responses
of the two cue types. The resulting memberships are used to compute a collaborative
densification-priority score and to construct competing role-conditioned cloning and
splitting utilities, while the corresponding evidence directions guide the placement of
child Gaussians.

This rule provides two useful signals: the gradient measures the current optimization pressure, and the scale prevents an oversized primitive from being duplicated
without spatial decomposition. However, neither signal describes the image-space characteristics associated with the primitive. A large positional gradient does not indicate
whether the unresolved variation arises from appearance detail, a structural boundary, or both. Likewise, the scale-based operation rule does not determine whether a
small primitive requires additional appearance capacity or an explicit spatial separation. The baseline split initialization also does not use the direction of the image
evidence. Consequently, Gaussians with similar gradients and scales receive the same
densification treatment even when they represent different regional conditions.
PSCD-GS extends adaptive density control by introducing a continuous
perception–structure role representation. At a densification event k , the method takes
the training views and the current Gaussian set Gk as input. It first constructs perceptual and structural evidence in the training views and transfers this evidence to the
visible Gaussian primitives. The resulting Gaussian-level scores describe the strength
6
of the two evidence types and are converted into continuous co-dominant, perceptiondominant, structure-dominant, and low-response roles. These roles then condition
three successive decisions: which Gaussians should be densified, whether each selected
Gaussian should be cloned or split, and how reliable directional evidence should be
used to initialize split children.
The proposed framework does not replace the Gaussian representation, differentiable renderer, reconstruction objective, or the general optimization and pruning
schedule of 3DGS. The original positional gradient remains an explicit measure of
optimization demand, and the original scale criterion is retained as a safeguard
for oversized primitives. PSCD-GS instead augments these baseline signals with
Gaussian-level perception–structure evidence and uses different role-conditioned readouts for candidate selection, operation selection, and split placement. This separation
is important because the three decisions answer different questions: whether additional capacity is needed, whether that capacity should preserve or redistribute spatial
support, and where the redistributed support should be initialized.
3.2 Perceptual and structural evidence
The first stage constructs two complementary response fields from the training views.
Its input is a posed training image I v and, for structural cues, the current depth rendered from Gk at densification event k . Its output is a perceptual magnitude map Av ,

variation, whereas structural evidence describes boundaries and geometric discontinuities that are supported by the current multi-view reconstruction. The two branches

textured structural boundary can require different forms of refinement. Throughout
this section, RNormΩv (·) denotes robust image-space normalization: the response is

3.4 Role-conditioned densification
The role representation is consumed through three distinct readouts. Candidate
selection treats perceptual and structural evidence symmetrically because either can
indicate a need for additional capacity. Operation selection is deliberately asymmetric

because structural evidence more directly supports spatial decomposition. Split placement additionally depends on whether the image-plane response axes can be lifted
and fused reliably. Separating the readouts prevents one score from being forced to
answer three different questions.

a boundary or geometric change across which a single support may be inadequate.

3.5 Integration with 3DGS optimization
PSCD-GS is inserted into the densification stage of the original training loop. Before
optimization, the perceptual maps, perceptual tensors, image-boundary responses,
and camera-neighbor graph are precomputed. During ordinary training iterations,
the renderer and reconstruction objective are unchanged, and the original view-space
positional gradients are accumulated over the current densification interval. No role
decision is made at these iterations.
At each densification event, the current Gaussian parameters are treated as a
synchronized snapshot. Depth is rendered at the evidence resolution for the training views, after which the depth-discontinuity maps, cross-view reliability gates, and
structural tensors are refreshed. An auxiliary compositing pass accumulates Civ , the
two contribution-weighted response sums, and the compact symmetric-tensor statistics required by Eq. (11). The accumulators are reduced across views to obtain Pi and
Qi , followed by the role weights in Eq. (8).
The event then follows the same causal order as the method description.
Equation (9) selects at most Kk candidates. Equation (10) assigns clone or split
while retaining the scale safeguard. Direction lifting and confidence evaluation are
performed only for the selected split candidates. The resulting primitives are inserted
into the Gaussian set, the selected split parents are removed, and the original opacity, screen-size-, and world-size-based pruning rules are applied. Densification statistics
and evidence accumulators are then reset, and optimization resumes with the updated
Gaussian set. The original opacity-reset schedule is retained.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental setup
4.1.1 Datasets
We evaluated PSCD-GS on three widely used real-world novel-view synthesis benchmarks: Mip-NeRF 360 [11], Tanks & Temples [13], and Deep Blending [12]. For
Mip-NeRF 360, we used the five outdoor scenes bicycle, flowers, garden, stump, and
treehill, together with the four indoor scenes room, counter, kitchen, and bonsai. For
Tanks & Temples, we evaluated the train and truck scenes, while for Deep Blending,
we used Dr Johnson and Playroom.
16
PSCD-GS followed the official 3DGS evaluation protocol. The Mip-NeRF 360
outdoor scenes used the images 4 image directory, whereas the indoor scenes used
images 2. Tanks & Temples and Deep Blending were evaluated using their default
image directories. The official evaluation splits were used for all scenes, and no
scene was excluded. All compared methods were evaluated on the same scene set
and train/test partitions, while method-specific image-resolution policies and training
configurations followed the corresponding official implementations.
4.1.2 Baselines
We compare PSCD-GS with the original 3DGS [2] and representative 3DGS variants,
including AbsGS [3], Pixel-GS [4], Mini-Splatting [5], Taming 3DGS [6], 3DGSMCMC [7], SteepGS [8], Perceptual-GS [9], and Deng et al. [10]. These methods cover
different directions in recent 3DGS research, including gradient-based densification,
pixel-aware response modeling, compact representation, sampling-based optimization,
perceptual guidance, and Gaussian distribution refinement. This comparison evaluates whether the proposed perception–structure role modeling improves Gaussian
allocation beyond existing density-control strategies.
4.1.3 Implementation details
PSCD-GS is implemented on top of the original 3DGS framework, with modifications restricted to the densification stage. Its rendering formulation, reconstruction
losses, and general optimization pipeline follow the original 3DGS implementation.
All baseline methods were reproduced from their official public repositories using the
method-specific training configurations and recommended settings provided by the
respective authors. This includes the official resolution policies, optimization schedules,
Gaussian-budget settings, and other method-dependent parameters where applicable.
All methods were evaluated using the same dataset splits and metric implementations, and all experiments were conducted on the same workstation equipped with
a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory, 16 virtual CPU cores,
and 120 GB of RAM. This setup ensures that the reported comparisons are obtained
under a consistent hardware and evaluation environment while preserving the officially
recommended configuration of each method.
The perceptual response maps of PSCD-GS were precomputed from the training
images using the fixed VGG feature extractor employed by LPIPS. The structural
response maps were updated during densification using image-boundary responses,
geometric discontinuities, and multi-view structural-consistency cues. The default
hyperparameter settings of PSCD-GS are summarized in Table 1 and were kept fixed
across all datasets and scenes.

similarity index measure (SSIM), and learned perceptual image patch similarity
(LPIPS). PSNR measures pixel-level reconstruction fidelity, SSIM evaluates structural

4.2 Quantitative comparison
Table 2 summarizes the quantitative comparison on Mip-NeRF 360, Deep Blending,
and Tanks & Temples. Compared with the original 3DGS, PSCD-GS improves SSIM
and PSNR and reduces LPIPS on all three benchmarks. These gains are consistent
across pixel-level fidelity, structural similarity, and perceptual quality in the reported
dataset-level averages.
The results on Mip-NeRF 360 are particularly strong. PSCD-GS ranks first in
both SSIM and PSNR and second in LPIPS, with Mini-Splatting achieving the lowest
LPIPS. This benchmark combines indoor and outdoor environments with dense vegetation, fine textures, irregular boundaries, and substantial viewpoint changes, making
it difficult to preserve appearance details and local structures at the same time. PSCDGS performs well under these conditions and provides a more balanced result than
methods that mainly rely on gradients or pixel responses, such as AbsGS and Pixel-GS.
It also achieves these gains with 2.8 million Gaussians, fewer than the 3.3 million used
by the original 3DGS and substantially fewer than those used by several aggressive
densification methods.
A similar balance is observed on Deep Blending. PSCD-GS obtains the highest SSIM and PSNR, while its LPIPS is second only to Perceptual-GS. Although
Perceptual-GS has a slight advantage in perceptual distance, PSCD-GS reports higher
SSIM and PSNR in this dataset-level comparison. Deep Blending contains indoor
scenes with planar structures, door and wall boundaries, detailed textures, and viewdependent appearance effects. The results suggest that PSCD-GS can handle these
different visual characteristics without favoring one aspect of reconstruction quality
at the expense of the others.
Tanks & Temples presents a somewhat different pattern. Here, PSCD-GS achieves
the best SSIM and LPIPS and the second-best PSNR, closely following PerceptualGS. The scenes in this benchmark contain prominent object silhouettes, occlusion
boundaries, geometric transitions, and thin structures. The improvements in SSIM
and LPIPS are consistent with the intended effects of the proposed structural and
perceptual cues, while the small PSNR gap indicates that these improvements are not
accompanied by a substantial loss in pixel-level fidelity.
The gains of PSCD-GS are unlikely to be attributable solely to using a larger
representation. The method does not aim to minimize the number of Gaussians at
all costs, nor does it rely on unrestricted primitive growth. Its Gaussian count is
lower than that of the original 3DGS on Mip-NeRF 360 and remains comparable

4.3 Qualitative comparison
Following the quantitative comparison, we further examine the visual differences
among the evaluated densification strategies. Figure 3 presents representative examples selected from different datasets and challenging scene regions, including bicycle
and garden from Mip-NeRF 360, Dr Johnson from Deep Blending, and truck from
Tanks & Temples. These scenes contain thin bicycle spokes, dense vegetation, indoor
door frames, wall boundaries, vehicle contours, and window edges, providing representative cases for evaluating fine-detail recovery, boundary continuity, and structural
fidelity.

enlarged regions. The remaining columns show the corresponding enlarged results produced by the

As shown in Figure 3, the original 3DGS tends to produce softened boundaries
and blurred fine structures in challenging regions. Its gradient-driven densification
21
strategy increases the representation capacity of under-optimized regions, but it does
not explicitly distinguish texture-dominant regions from structure-dominant regions.
Consequently, thin structures and regular boundaries may remain incomplete or oversmoothed.
AbsGS and Pixel-GS recover additional details in some high-frequency regions by
improving gradient statistics or introducing pixel-aware density control. However, local
noise, broken contours, and unstable boundaries can still occur when dense textures
and complex structures appear simultaneously. Perceptual-GS provides clearer results
in visually salient regions, but thin structures and regular boundaries may remain
insufficiently reconstructed because structural cues are not explicitly incorporated into
the complete densification process.
PSCD-GS produces more consistent visual results across the selected examples. In
the bicycle scene, the wheel spokes and surrounding contours are reconstructed with
fewer merged or interrupted structures. In garden, vegetation textures are retained
more clearly, with less noticeable over-smoothing. In Dr Johnson, the door frames and
wall boundaries appear sharper and more continuous. In truck, the vehicle contours,
window edges, and transitions between adjacent body regions are reconstructed with
fewer local artifacts.
These qualitative results further support the quantitative improvements reported
in Table 2. In particular, the visual comparisons indicate that the gains in SSIM,
PSNR, and LPIPS are accompanied by improved preservation of local textures, thin
structures, and object boundaries. Meanwhile, PSCD-GS maintains a controlled representation size rather than relying on unrestricted growth in the number of Gaussian
primitives.
4.4 Ablation experiments
To analyze the contribution of each component in PSCD-GS, we performed ablation
experiments on Mip-NeRF 360, Deep Blending, and Tanks & Temples. In addition to
removing the two evidence branches, we further evaluated the continuous role representation and the subsequent densification decisions. This analysis is necessary because
the proposed perception–structure role is used not only to characterize Gaussian-level
evidence, but also to determine where and how densification is performed.
Specifically, we constructed six ablated variants. The first two variants removed
the perceptual evidence branch and the structural evidence branch, respectively. The
third variant removed the soft role assignment and replaced the continuous perception–
structure role representation with the equal-weight fusion Sei = (Pi + Qi )/2. The
remaining three variants independently disabled joint candidate selection, regionadaptive clone/split selection, and role-guided split placement. These components
correspond to three successive decisions in the densification process: which Gaussians
should be densified, whether clone or split should be applied, and where the split
children should be placed.
For all ablations, only the specified component was changed, while the remaining
training settings and hyperparameters were kept identical to those of the full model.
In the variant without perceptual evidence, the perceptual response and its associated directional contribution were disabled, while the structural branch remained
22
unchanged. Conversely, the variant without structural evidence retained only the perceptual branch. In the variant without soft role assignment, the equal-weight fused
evidence Sei was used in place of the continuous role memberships in the subsequent
densification decisions. The variant without joint candidate selection reverted to the
original gradient-based candidate criterion of 3DGS. The variant without regionadaptive clone/split selection used the original scale-based operation rule, whereas
the variant without role-guided split placement restored the original 3DGS split
initialization. No other component was modified in any ablated variant.

In Mip-NeRF 360, the removal of joint candidate selection results in the largest
degradation in SSIM and PSNR, indicating that the identification of reliable and
informative densification candidates is particularly important in complex unbounded
scenes. In contrast, removing the perceptual evidence branch causes the most pronounced increase in LPIPS. This result suggests that perceptual responses are
especially useful for locating visually sensitive regions, such as textured surfaces and
23
high-frequency appearance variations, which may not be sufficiently characterized by
gradient magnitude or structural cues alone. The structural branch also contributes
consistently, although its influence is smaller than that of the perceptual branch and
the selection of joint candidates on this benchmark.
A similar tendency can be observed on Deep Blending. The perceptual evidence
branch and joint candidate selection produce the two largest overall contributions.
In particular, removing the perceptual branch leads to a clear reduction in PSNR
and a noticeable deterioration in LPIPS, whereas removing joint candidate selection
causes the largest decrease in SSIM. These results indicate that appearance-sensitive
evidence construction and reliable candidate screening play complementary roles: the
former improves the representation of perceptually important details, while the latter
prevents densification from being allocated to redundant or low-value primitives.
The behavior on Tanks & Temples is different. Removing the structural evidence
branch produces the largest degradation in SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS. This benchmark contains prominent object boundaries, depth discontinuities, geometric edges,
and thin structures, making structural information more influential than on the other
two datasets. Joint candidate selection produces the second-largest overall degradation, further confirming that reliable candidate screening remains important even
when structural cues dominate. The dataset-dependent variation also indicates that
perceptual and structural evidence should not be treated as interchangeable signals.
Removing soft role assignment consistently reduces the quality of reconstruction
on all three benchmarks. Although its effect is generally smaller than that of the
dominant importance branch or joint candidate selection, the degradation confirms
that a continuous perception–structure role is more effective than directly combining the two evidence scores with a fixed fusion rule. The proposed role representation
allows each Gaussian to occupy a continuous position in the joint perception–structure
space, thereby distinguishing perception-dominant, structure-dominant, and jointly
important regions. This provides a more adaptive basis for subsequent densification
decisions. The region-adaptive clone/split strategy and role-guided split placement
also improve performance, although their contributions are relatively smaller and vary
across metrics. This behavior is reasonable because these components operate after
the important regions and candidate Gaussians have already been identified. Regionadaptive operation selection determines whether a Gaussian should be cloned or split
according to its regional role, while role-guided placement controls the spatial distribution of the generated child Gaussians. Their removal does not eliminate the upstream
evidence construction and candidate-selection mechanisms, but it reduces the precision
with which new primitives are allocated within local regions.
In general, the ablation results support the contribution of collaboration in the
estimation of perceptual evidence, the estimation of structural evidence, the modeling
of soft roles, and the role-aware densification decisions. The two evidence branches
provide complementary cues, the soft role assignment organizes these cues into a continuous regional representation, and the three densification components progressively
determine where to densify, how to densify, and where to place the generated Gaussians. Together, these components extend the original gradient-driven densification
mechanism of 3DGS into a perception–structure role-aware decision process.

4.5 Discussion
The experimental results suggest that the improvement of PSCD-GS is associated with
explicit perception–structure role modeling rather than with unrestricted primitive
growth. The original 3DGS densification strategy uses view-space gradients to identify
under-optimized regions, which is effective but does not describe the regional attributes
of different Gaussians. In contrast, PSCD-GS assigns each Gaussian a continuous role
in the joint perception–structure space and uses this role to guide candidate selection,
clone/split decisions, and split placement. This design allows the densification process
to respond differently to texture-rich regions, structural boundaries, thin structures,
and low-response areas.
The results also show that perceptual and structural signals are complementary.
Perceptual evidence primarily reflects texture details, local appearance variations, and
visually sensitive regions, whereas structural evidence provides complementary cues
for boundaries, contours, thin structures, and geometric transitions. A single cue is
therefore insufficient for complex real-world scenes. The proposed soft role assignment
avoids treating these cues as fixed-weight scores and instead adapts their contributions
according to the regional role of each Gaussian.
PSCD-GS should also be distinguished from compact or efficiency-oriented 3DGS
methods. Some compact methods reduce the number of Gaussians through pruning, sampling control, or compression, whereas PSCD-GS focuses on quality-oriented
Gaussian allocation during densification. Its goal is not to obtain the minimum representation scale, but to assign representation capacity to regions where additional
Gaussians are most beneficial. Therefore, PSCD-GS is complementary to compact
Gaussian representations and can be further combined with pruning or compression
strategies to improve the trade-off between quality, scale, and efficiency.
There are still several limitations. First, estimating perceptual and structural
responses introduces additional training-time computation. Although the perceptual maps are precomputed, the structural responses, multi-view reliability, and
contribution-weighted tensor statistics are updated during densification events. These
operations do not alter the standard Gaussian representation or introduce an additional neural inference module at rendering time, but their training overhead remains
an aspect that could be examined more systematically in future work.
Second, the reliability of structural evidence depends on view coverage, depth quality, and reprojection consistency. Sparse views, reflective surfaces, motion, or noisy
boundaries may weaken the cross-view gate and reduce the benefit of role-aware placement. Third, the current framework is evaluated mainly on static real-world scenes;
dynamic scenes, online reconstruction, and large-scale environments remain untested.
Finally, the role assignment and densification policy still rely on fixed hyperparameters. Adaptive or learning-based policies may improve robustness across scenes and
training stages, but such extensions require separate validation.
25
5 Conclusion
We presented PSCD-GS, a perception–structure collaborative densification framework
for 3D Gaussian Splatting. The method maps image-space perceptual responses and
multi-view structural evidence to Gaussian primitives, represents their joint state
through continuous role memberships, and uses this representation to coordinate candidate selection, clone-or-split decisions, and split placement. In this way, densification
is treated as a sequence of related decisions rather than only as gradient-thresholded
primitive growth.
Experiments on Mip-NeRF 360, Deep Blending, and Tanks & Temples show that
PSCD-GS achieves competitive dataset-level rendering quality with a controlled number of Gaussian primitives. The ablation results support the contributions of the
perceptual evidence branch, the structural evidence branch, continuous role assignment, and role-aware densification decisions. Qualitative comparisons further suggest
that the method better preserves local textures, object boundaries, and thin structures
in challenging scenes. Future work will focus on reducing the additional evidenceconstruction overhead, extending the method to dynamic and large-scale scenes, and
replacing fixed densification hyperparameters with more adaptive policies.
