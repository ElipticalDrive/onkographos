Abstract
Abstract
We present a novel framework that enhances 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) with improved computational efficiency and relighting capabilities through probabilistic optimization. While 3DGS enables real-time, high-quality scene reconstruction and rendering, it remains limited to static lighting environments and suffers from redundancy due to millions of Gaussians. To address these limitations, we introduce a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)-based approach that adaptively prunes redundant Gaussians while preserving critical details. Our contributions include MCMC-based adaptive thresholding, surface normal learning for realistic shading under dynamic lighting, and gradient-guided light sampling via the Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA). Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed method achieves significant memory reduction and faster convergence, enabling real-time relighting with fewer Gaussian primitives.

1 Introduction
Real-time, relightable 3D scene reconstruction from multi-view imagery remains a challenging problem in immersive content creation, virtual production, digital humans, and AR/VR. While neural implicit representations such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [Mildenhall et al. 2021] have demonstrated impressive performance in novel view synthesis, their computational overhead poses a major limitation for real-time applications. Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [Kerbl et al. 2023] has emerged as a powerful explicit alternative, representing scenes with millions of anisotropic 3D Gaussians. Leveraging tile-based rasterization and efficient differentiable optimization, 3DGS achieves real-time rendering at over 100 FPS with NeRF-level fidelity. However, the original framework supports only static lighting and lacks support for relightable rendering.
In this work, we reinterpret 3DGS from a probabilistic perspective and introduce an MCMC-based inference framework for adaptive relighting and interpretable Gaussian pruning. By leveraging posterior sampling through Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods, our approach enables physically grounded filtering and lighting-aware scene adaptation. We integrate this sampling-based optimization into a deferred shading pipeline for efficient and scalable real-time rendering under dynamic illumination. Our key contributions are:
•
Adaptive Gaussian Pruning via MCMC: We propose a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling strategy to adaptively prune Gaussians based on their posterior contribution, preserving essential scene details while reducing redundancy.
•
Gradient-Guided Light Sampling with MALA: We employ the Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) to efficiently sample plausible incident lighting directions, guided by the gradient of scene response.
•
Real-Time Relighting Integration: Our framework integrates these probabilistic components into the deferred shading pipeline, enabling dynamic relighting with minimal overhead and high visual fidelity.
Our method enables dynamic relighting at real-time frame rates, maintaining visual fidelity while reducing Gaussian count by up to 86% without noticeable quality loss.

2 Related Work
3D Gaussian Splatting. 3DGS [Kerbl et al. 2023] represents scenes as sets of anisotropic Gaussians defined by position, covariance, color, and opacity. Rendering involves projecting these Gaussians onto the image plane and compositing pixel colors using alpha blending. However, the explicit representation with numerous Gaussians leads to rapid growth in computational and memory requirements.
Gaussian Simplification. Prior work reduces memory usage by pruning [Zhang et al. 2025], saves training time and memory cost [Fang and Wang 2024b], and adaptive densification [Mallick et al. 2024]. Especially, Mini-Splatting [Fang and Wang 2024a] further refines Gaussian placement to improve efficiency. Nevertheless, these methods often lead to a degradation in rendering quality due to loss of important geometric details during simplification.
Relighting. Relighting methods for 3DGS separate texture and illumination, enabling flexible control [Gao et al. 2024]. GS3 [Zoubin et al. 2024] proposes normal-free relighting but requires additional interpolation. However, they still rely on dense Gaussian sets and heuristic pruning strategies, limiting efficiency and interpretability.
Markov Chain Monte Carlo Methods. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods generate samples from complex distributions by constructing Markov chains that converge to the target stationary distribution. Metropolis-Hastings [Metropolis et al. 1953] proposes candidate states accepted based on probability ratios. Langevin Monte Carlo [Roberts and Tweedie 1996] incorporates gradients to guide sampling. The Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) [Grenander and Miller 1994] combines both approaches for improved sampling accuracy.
MCMC in Rendering and 3DGS. MCMC techniques such as Metropolis-Hastings [Metropolis et al. 1953] and Langevin Monte Carlo [Roberts and Tweedie 1996] have long been used in rendering for efficient path sampling. Recently, MCMC-based Gaussian relocation has been explored for 3DGS initialization [Kheradmand et al. 2024]. However, this approach primarily focuses on optimizing the placement and distribution of Gaussians, rather than explicitly aiming to reduce their overall number. In contrast, our method targets memory and computational efficiency by effectively reducing the number of Gaussians without compromising rendering quality.
3 Method
Fig. 2 presents an overview of the proposed method. Our framework employs the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) and lighting model to enable physically-based rendering. Each Gaussian primitive is augmented with trainable shading attributes including albedo, roughness, and surface normals for relighting. The existing Gaussian Splatting methods rely on fixed heuristics for both geometry refinement and light sampling, often leading to excessive computation in redundant regions or insufficient fidelity in critical areas as they ignore local scene complexity or illumination sensitivity. To overcome this, we employ MCMC techniques that allow our system to adaptively focus computational effort where it matters most. By leveraging gradient information—either from geometry (normal gradients) or lighting (importance gradients)—we guide densification and sampling in a data-driven, principled probabilistic manner. This makes our framework capable of balancing efficiency and accuracy in a principled and scene-aware way.
3.1 MCMC-Guided Adaptive Densification
We enhance 3DGS by explicitly learning surface normals and employing an adaptive densification strategy based on MCMC thresholding. To ensure accurate representation of complex geometry, we focus densification efforts on regions where surface orientation changes rapidly. Regions with large normal variation often correspond to geometrically complex structures such as sharp edges, corners, or high-curvature surfaces. These areas require denser Gaussian placement to capture fine details, preserve visual fidelity, and avoid smoothing artifacts that degrade reconstruction quality.
Normal Learning. While prior methods optimize color and depth, they lack explicit surface orientation information, limiting illumination accuracy. We address this by directly learning per-Gaussian normals, which improves geometry representation and supports accurate shading and light sampling.
Adaptive Gaussian Densification. To balance representation accuracy and computational cost, we adaptively split or prune Gaussians based on the magnitude of their normal gradients, which indicate regions with significant geometric detail. Unlike fixed-threshold approaches [Kerbl et al. 2023], our threshold ϵ dynamically adapts throughout training via a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. This ensures the fraction of Gaussians selected for splitting matches a target ratio δsplit, preventing over- or under-sampling.
MCMC-Based Adaptation. At each step, given the current threshold ϵcurr, we compute the fraction of Gaussians exceeding it. A new threshold proposal ϵprop is generated by perturbing ϵcurr with Gaussian noise. We evaluate losses based on the deviation from the target ratio and accept or reject the proposal using the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance criterion. The noise scale is adaptively adjusted to maintain an optimal acceptance rate near 0.44.
Gaussian Selection and Splitting. Gaussians are selected for splitting if either position or normal gradients exceed respective thresholds and their scale is above a scene-dependent minimum:
Mselect=(|∇x|≥τp∨|∇n|≥ϵcurr)∧(max(sx,sy,sz)>τs).
Selected Gaussians are subdivided by offsetting their positions, scaling down their sizes, and perturbing rotations, while preserving and normalizing normals.
Pruning. To control computational overhead, we prune Gaussians with low importance using a stochastic method inspired by Mini-Splatting [Fang and Wang 2024a]. This complements the adaptive densification to maintain a compact yet detailed representation. This probabilistic thresholding approach enables the model to dynamically adjust the level of detail according to the scene’s geometric complexity. In contrast to fixed heuristics, which may overfit to either high-detail or low-detail regions, our MCMC-guided densification maintains a consistent trade-off between representation fidelity and computational budget across diverse scenes. This adaptivity is especially crucial for scalable deployment in real-world settings where scene structures vary significantly.
3.2 Gradient-Guided Incident Light Sampling Based on MALA
Efficient sampling of incident light directions is critical for approximating the rendering equation. Uniform sampling wastes resources on directions with minimal contribution. Importance sampling concentrates samples but requires well-defined distributions that are challenging in complex scenes. We adopt MALA, which uses the gradient information to sample from the target distribution P(x) favoring directions near the surface normal n. Starting from Fibonacci-distributed samples on the hemisphere, MALA iteratively proposes new directions guided by the gradient of log P(x), and accepts moves probabilistically, improving the concentration of the samples near relevant light directions. Fig. 3 compares uniform Fibonacci sampling with our MALA-based approach, demonstrating improved sample distribution aligned with surface normals (drawn with a red arrow). The target distribution P(x) favors directions near the surface normal n, prioritizing the light contributions relevant to shading.
Visualization of (a) Fibonacci Sphere Sampling and (b) gradient-guided incident light sampling using MALA.
The necessity of MCMC in this context stems from the limitations of traditional sampling techniques. Uniform and cosine-weighted sampling cast rays indiscriminately, leading to wasted compute in directions with negligible contribution. In contrast, our MALA-based sampling leverages local surface orientation to preferentially explore light directions that are more likely to influence the final radiance. This gradient-aware mechanism significantly improves shading accuracy, particularly in regions with subtle or complex lighting effects, while reducing the number of required samples.
3.3 Deferred Shading Pipeline
To efficiently support dynamic relighting with reduced Gaussian complexity, we adopt a lightweight deferred shading pipeline [Deering et al. 1988]. This pipeline decouples geometry and lighting, enabling scalable and memory-efficient rendering. In the geometry pass, 3D Gaussians are projected into screen space, and per-pixel attributes such as depth, albedo, roughness, and surface normal are stored in a G-buffer. The lighting pass performs physically-based shading using the G-buffer. Incident light directions are sampled using MALA, and the corresponding BRDFs are evaluated to compute the final pixel colors. By separating geometry and lighting, this deferred shading pipeline avoids redundant lighting computations, improving efficiency, and seamlessly integrates with our MCMC-based relighting framework for dynamic, real-time applications.

4 Experiments
We evaluate our method on synthetic and real-world datasets with varying geometry and materials, including NeRF-Synthetic [Mildenhall et al. 2021], Syn4Relight [Zhang et al. 2022], and Tanks and Temples [Knapitsch et al. 2017]. We compare against two categories of Gaussian-based methods: relightable models such as R3DG [Gao et al. 2024], GS3 [Zoubin et al. 2024], and GShader [Jiang et al. 2024]; and compact non-relightable methods including 3DGS [Kerbl et al. 2023], GaussianSpa [Zhang et al. 2025], Taming-3DGS [Mallick et al. 2024], Mini-Splatting [Fang and Wang 2024a], and MSv2 [Fang and Wang 2024b]. All experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4070 Ti SUPER (16 GB VRAM) to ensure fair comparison on consumer hardware. We report quantitative metrics including Gaussian count, model size, training time, PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.
Gaussian Count Reduction. Our method significantly reduces the number of Gaussians needed for high-quality rendering. Table 1 shows that we achieve the highest quality, using only 272k Gaussians. This is enabled by our MCMC-based Gaussian filtering, without sacrificing structural fidelity. Qualitative results in Fig. 4 confirm that this aggressive pruning maintains perceptual quality.
Visualization of Gaussian distributions before (a, c), and after MCMC-based filtering (b, d).
Training Time Reduction. As shown in Table 2, our method achieves substantially faster training times. For instance, on the Drums scene, training time decreases from 50 hours with R3DG to just 30 minutes. On average, our method is 5× faster than GS3 and 3× faster than GShader across all tested scenes. Moreover, unlike R3DG, which requires 24 GB of memory, our approach operates efficiently on a single consumer-grade GPU with less than 16 GB of memory.
Relighting Evaluation and Representation Visualization. Our method supports environment map-based relighting with high fidelity and efficiency. 5 Conclusion and Future Work
We present a compact and efficient Gaussian Splatting pipeline for relightable 3D scene representation. The method integrates adaptive Gaussian filtering based on MCMC and gradient-aware light sampling to reduce computational cost while preserving rendering fidelity. It achieves up to 86% Gaussian reduction and over 60× faster training on benchmark datasets, without significant quality loss. It runs efficiently on consumer-grade GPUs with 16 GB VRAM, making it suitable for interactive and resource-constrained applications. Future work includes extending the approach to dynamic scenes and optimizing it for mobile or edge devices.
