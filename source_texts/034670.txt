Abstract
With the continuous improvements of imaging technology, astronomy images
are getting more and more detailed and fascinating. Images of nebulae and galaxies
by space satellites such as the Hubble Space Telescope or the James Webb Space
Telescope have had an impact on the entire world, from the scientific community
to the general public. However, when it comes to computer graphics, creating realtime images of such complex structures for education and entertainment purposes
proves to be much of a challenge. In this paper, we present a way to efficiently
generate details to improve low-resolution data of dust and gas space clouds.

Introduction
1.1
Background and motivation
In the field of Computer Graphics, the ability to model and render realistic scenes in real-time is
often an essential component of the production of visualization applications. In this study, we
will be looking at the case of real-time rendering of astronomical objects such as galaxies and
nebulae, which span across a wide number of scales, for example for use in digital planetariums.
The real-time aspect of the rendering is required by our desire to create an interactive system,
with the ability to "walk through" the objects, and switch from an exterior point of view to an
inside view in a continuous motion.
This particular case poses a few interesting challenges. We want to achieve real-time rendering performances, which restricts the maximum computing time allowed per frame (approximately 41 milliseconds for the cinema standard of 24 images per seconds, and down to
approximately 16 milliseconds for 60 images per seconds, which is the usual standard for video
games). We also want to impose a limit on the required hardware. We want to achieve real-time
rendering performances on commercially available Graphics Processing Units (GPUs).
This necessity to reconcile the high computational and memory requirements of large-scale
Figure 1.1: Real-life examples of possible procedural generation applications
volumetric rendering and the real-time rendering performances on restricted hardware comes
with a number of great challenges.
1.2
Problem statement
Many of the issues related to real-time rendering of large-scale space scenes have to do with
volumetric rendering. While light is mostly emitted by stars, most of the visible interactions
of light with matter are due to gasses and dust clouds, mostly as part of specific objects like
galaxies and nebulae.
In graphics, gasses and dust clouds are often referred to as volumes, in opposition to the
surface-based objects modelled using meshes. To use them in a scene, these volumes require
specific data structures for the modelling aspect, and specific algorithms for the rendering aspect.
The main objective for the modelling aspect of volumetric rendering of space volumetric
density fields is to design a way to specify, for each point in space, the parameters of the volume,
such as the density. Existing data obtained from observation or simulation can be used, but is
often lacking in details and resolution, mostly because of the limits of observation devices.
Our goal is to improve the low-resolution density data for space dust clouds by generating
additional data to fill in the gaps with new details.
While the naive brute-force method would be to use physics-driven simulations, this solution comes with it’s own challenges. First of all, the physics models used are not complete. In
particular the 3D/spatial distribution of gasses and dust clouds is an ongoing field of research
in astrophysics. Furthermore, fluids and particles simulations at this scale are extremely computation intensive, and therefore are not compatible with our real-time generation goal. Since
the objects we want to render span across several scales, and since we do not restrict the view
point, it is simply impossible to compute and store enough data to have a good representation
of these objects from any view points. Even if we restricted ourselves to rendering a single
galaxy, there are so many different layers of details that storing all of them in a high enough
resolution to fly a camera through it would hardly be possible on a single computer, let alone
in a Graphics Processing Unit.
Instead, we will be using procedural noise generation to add details to the low resolution
data. Our goal is then to design a noise model for dust cloud generation which will be used
to modulate the original data with a multiplication. To do so, our model must satisfy some
quantitative and qualitative properties.
The quantitative properties we want are the following:
• Expected value of 1: The expected value of a random distribution is the mean of all the
values in the distribution, weighted by their respective probability. The reason we need to
ensure this property is that, since we will be multiplying our noise values with the data,
we want to maintain the density of the low resolution real-life data to simply change its
spatial distribution and add details. The expected value of 1 will ensure that the quantity
of matter of the original data stays untouched in the final result.
• An open value range, with values between 0 and N: both the noise values and original
data will correspond to a density value of the dust cloud, which cannot be negative. This
2
enforces a lower bound of 0 for our model. We will see later that keeping the lower bound
as close as possible to 0 is going to be part of our goal. Density fields can contain values
greater than 1, and there are no limits on the upper bound. However, the requirement for
the expected value means that N has to be greater than 1.
This requirements will ensure that the model will work for our use case. In addition, we
can define several qualitative guidelines we can use to evaluate the usability and visual aspect
quality of the noise model:
• High expressive range: We want to find a model that can be shaped in as many ways as
possible while still meeting the requirements stated above.
• "Empty" zones: Dust and gas clouds in galaxies and nebulae tend to present holes and
very low density zones. We want our model to provide these zones of very low density.
Finally, we discuss some guidelines and notes on the visual aspect of the objects our noise
generator attempts to recreate. One of the discussed visual elements shared by many instances
of space volumes are filaments. Figure 1.2 shows an example of these structures in the M51
galaxy, where different scales of lines are visible along the arms of the spiral. In Chapter 4, we
discuss a start of a possible solutions to obtain filaments-like patterns in our noise model.
1.3
Outline
In Chapter 2, We will give the necessary background knowledge and previous works on the

Finally, Chapter 5 will give the conclusion and talk about future possible work on this subject.

2.1
Preliminary notions
Before we begin, we introduce some preliminary notions used for this work, and present in the

2.1.1
Procedural noise
Noise definition
In graphics, a noise can be intuitively defined as a random, continuous, unstructured pattern
generator. Noise functions are usually defined in 2 or 3 dimensional spaces, but can be define
for any dimension count. Noise functions are mainly used to add randomness and additional
details to objects or textures. Noise functions can be used in a lot of situations, for example for
cloud shape [10], terrain generation or procedural texture creation. Figure 2.1 shows examples
of noise used in procedural generation.
Figure 2.1: Examples of noise used for procedural generation. Left: Texture example from
Perlin [9]. Middle: Procedural terrain generation from Minecraft [14]. Right: Procedural cloud
generation in Horizon: Zero Dawn [10]
Noise patterns can be described in two different ways. First, in the spatial domain, a noise
function will assign a value to every location in space. Given a N-dimensional space, a noise
function assign a value from RN to R. To analyse statistical properties, noise is more often described as a signal, and treated as such. This means that the noise is described using its power
spectrum, which specify the magnitude of each frequency (since the pattern is unstructured,
the phase is random and therefore not useful information). Particularly, a more specific definition of noise was given by Perlin and Hoffert [9]: noise is an approximation to white noise
band-limited to a single octave. White noise is a signal containing all frequencies with equal
amplitudes and random phases. This definition of noise as a signal described by a power spectrum is the most used across literature to analyse and compare different noise implementations,
and justify the creation of newer noise models [3][2].
Procedural Noise
While Section 2.1.1.1 presented a general definition of noise, we will now define procedural
noise. The procedural adjective is used in computer sciences to specify objects defined by
a program or a procedure rather than by data structures. In computer graphics, procedural
generation often refer to the creation of objects, volumes and textures by a program, as opposed
to storing and loading them from a file. A procedural noise function is then a program used to
evaluate noise at possibly any point in space, on the fly at render time.
The advantages of procedural noise functions (and procedural generation in general) compared to stored data are the following:
• In computer graphics, programs are usually much more compact in memory than objects,
texture or volumes stored in a 3-dimensional grid. This can help in managing V-RAM
usage in GPUs.
• Procedural noise functions are non-periodic. They can cover a large area of the 2D or 3D
space without seams or visible repetitions. On the contrary, using textures can introduce
tiling or visible seams when the texture is repeated.
• Procedural functions are parametric, meaning they can be controlled using input parameters, and therefore can represent a class of similar noise patterns, which gives more
control on the final look.
• Procedural noise functions are randomly accessible, meaning the function can be evaluated at any point in space regardless of neighbor evaluation and in constant time.
However, these advantages are not always guaranteed, and depend on the implementation [2].
Perlin Noise
Perlin noise is a noise function introduced by Perlin [7] in 1985. It is one of the most commonly used noise function in graphics, for its simplicity of use and implementation, and for its
low computational cost. 2-dimensional Perlin noise is evaluated by creating a pseudo-random
gradient at each vertex of a square lattice. The noise value for a given point is then obtained by
the spline interpolation of of the dot product of gradient at the 4 nearest vertices with the vector
from the vertex to the evaluated point. Figure 2.2 shows an example of Perlin noise.
Since its introduction, efforts have been made to try and improve this model, mainly with a
better interpolation function and gradient computation [8] and with the introduction of Simplex
noise.

Fractional Brownian Motion
While Perlin noise can be used on its own, it is usually lacking in details for most applications.
One common way to solve this problem is referred to as Fractional Brownian Motion (fBm).
Fractional Brownian Motion consists of layering multiple Perlin noises with different frequencies, called octaves. Each additional octave will usually have twice the frequency and half
the amplitude of the previous octave. The phase is considered random, which is obtained in
practical implementations by adding a random offset to the sampled position for each octave.

2.1.2
Volumetric Rendering
Volumetric rendering is the process of creating an image from volumetric data. The main way
of achieving this is through an algorithm know as Ray-Marching. Since the amount of light
transmitted by a volume along a ray depends on the density of the volume along the ray, we
sample the volume several times, moving along the ray with a fixed step size. This allows us to
compute an approximation of the integral of the density of the volume along the ray, which we
can use to compute the quantity of light transmitted along the ray.
Several extensions of this algorithm are possible to include more complex lighting formulas,
and these extension are usually specific to a particular type of volumetric rendering such as the
rendering of clouds viewed from the ground.

3.1
Basic Approach
3.1.1
First approach with Fractional Brownian Motion
In procedural generation, the use of Perlin noise usually refers to the use of fBm. This is
because most applications of procedural generation (terrain, ocean surface, clouds) usually
consists of multiple layers of details with different frequencies. For example, 3-dimensional
fBm is used as a base for the cloud shapes (in combination with another noise model called
Worley noise) in the generation of clouds for the game Horizon: Zero Dawn [10] (see Figure 3.1
for an example). But in our case, the fBm method has some drawbacks that prevent it’s use.

First, the range of values is restricted. The first octave has a range of [−1; 1], and each
following octave divide this range by 2. Since we add all the octaves together, we can determine
that the maximum value of the noise, as we increase the number of octaves, is the following:

The fBm method will maintain the symmetry of the Perlin noise distribution, so the maximum range of fBm is [−2; 2]. Since we need to shift the values to fit in our [0; N] range
requirements, while keeping an expected value of 1, this means that the maximum possible

Furthermore, another drawback of the fBm method is the lack of correlation between detail
levels. Each octave’s contribution to the final result is not impacted by the other octaves. This
means that "empty" zones of the low frequencies will still contain the same amplitude of high
frequency variations. Thus, this method does not provide real empty zones.

To find a solution to these drawbacks, we choose the Multiplicative Perlin noise instead.
3.1.2
Basic Multiplicative noise
The idea behind Multiplicative Perlin noise is similar to the fBm method, but instead of adding
the octaves with a diminishing amplitude, the octaves are shifted into a positive range of values
and multiplied together. Here is the basic process:
Multiplicative noise description:
• Generate each octave’s Perlin noise, with a range of [−1; 1] and a mean of 0.
• Add 1 to each octave, to make the range positive only. The range is now [0; 2] and the
mean is 1.
• Multiply all the octaves together to form the result.
Using this method, we expect the following results
• A range of [0; 2n ], with n being the number of octaves used.
• An expected value of 1, as the expected value product of two statically independent
random variables is the product of the expected values of the two variables.

Drawbacks
While the results obtained with Multiplicative Perlin noise are better than what we obtained
with the fBm method, they still fall slightly short of our expectations in terms of the upper
bound of the range, which is lower than the theoretical limit of 2n of the multiplicative noise
model.
These shortcomings can be explained by considering that, while Perlin noise is theoretically
in the range [−1, 1], the noise rarely generates extreme values. Figure3.4 shows the histogram
of a Perlin noise instance (on the left) with red and green markings for the minimum and
maximum values. It is very visible then that these bounds are far from the expected bounds of
the Perlin noise.
We remind the reader that one of our goals for the final look of the model is to include
empty zones. Since our model will describe the volume in terms of the density of the clouds
in the spatial domain, we know that during the rendering process, the computation of the transmittance of the volume will involve summing a lot of density values along a ray. Given the
scales of the objects involved in this study, this ray can be very long, and therefore even areas
of very low density can have an important impact on the transmittance. The lack of extreme
values in the Perlin noise results in the lack of empty zones and high upper bound observed in
the Multiplicative Perlin noise results. We will look in Section 3.2 at a solution to this issue.

3.2
Applying transformations on the octaves before
the multiplication
Since we want to obtain empty zones in our model, we need to find a way to "force" the values
of each octaves to contain enough values close to the bounds. One way to achieve this is to
apply transfer functions to the noise before multiplying the octaves together. Since these functions will modify the final look of the model, they also are a source of control on the final look
of the model. Passing a noise through functions to modify its look is a common occurrence in
procedural generation, but we still want our model to abide to specific requirements of expected
values and range, so we need to take some precautions when performing transformations to the
noise. For example, applying the 1 − |x| function on a Perlin noise shifts the mean to the right
(see Figure 3.5 for a visual example).
Figure 3.5: Mean value shift when applying 1 − |x| to a Perlin noise. Left: Perlin noise (Scaled
to [0; 1] for visualization). Center: Perlin noise after the 1 − |x| function. Right: Histogram of
the Perlin noise after transformation. The vertical red line marks the mean value of the image.
In this section, we look at three different ways to approach this issue, each of them with
different advantages and drawbacks.

3.2.2
Compensating for the shift of expected value
As stated previously, an ideal model would allow us to use any function to modify the noise,
giving the user full control on how to shape the look of each octaves prior to blending them
together in the multiplication. This requires us to find a solution to the shift of expected value,
to ensure that every octave has a mean of 1. In this section, we look at to partial solutions to
this problem.
Perlin noise as a normal distribution
As we can observe from the histogram in Figure 3.4, Perlin noise is similar to a normal distribution. If we can find the parameters of this normal distribution, we can use an interesting
property of the normal distribution: If X is a random variable following a normal distribution of
mean µ and standard deviation σ , and F(x) is the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of
this normal distribution, then F(X) is a random variable following a continuous uniform distribution of range [0; 1] with a mean of 0.5. Applying transformations to this uniform distribution
while maintaining the mean is much easier, as we can determine analytically the modification
of the mean. Let X be the random variable following the uniform distribution of range [0; 1],
f (x) a function we want to apply to the distribution, then mean of the new distribution f (X)
will be:

Estimating the shift of the expected value through sampling
Another solution to compensate the shift of expected value is to estimate it through sampling.
The process is the following:
• Choose the function to apply to the noise. If the function requires extra parameters, they
need to be set to a specific value before hand.
• Sample a Perlin noise and apply the function.
16
Figure 3.9: Transformation of Perlin noise into a uniform distribution. a) Perlin noise. b) Perlin
noise after applying the normal distribution’s CDF. 1) Noise. 2) Value Histogram.
• Compute the mean of the obtained sample.
• Use the new mean to normalize any further use of the function on a Perlin noise
This solution is very straightforward and doesn’t have any requirements, other than for
the applied function to be positive. However, this method requires pre-computation, which
makes its use in a procedural settings more complicated. For function that do not require any
external parameters, such as 1 − |x| for example, this pre-computation only needs to be done
once. However, the situation is more complicated with more complex functions, which need
to perform the estimation again every time a parameter is changed. Figure 3.10 illustrates this
method with f (x) = 1 − |x|. The noise on the left is passed through f (x) and then divided by a
mean estimated previously on another sample. The resulting histogram on the right shows that
the mean shift is properly compensated.
In this Chapter, we looked at three approaches to create a noise generation model conforming
to the requirements and guidelines stated in Chapter 1. The first approach focused on working
with transformations of the original Perlin noise while keeping the mean. The next two approaches attempted to find a way to compensate for the impact of the transformations on the
mean, either by approximating the Perlin noise as a normal distribution to turn it into a uniform
distribution, or by pre-computing the effect of the transformation through sampling. The next
Chapter will provide details on the implementations used and discussions on the results.

Implementation and results
In this chapter, we give a more in-depth description of the implementations of the model used
to test the different strategies and obtain the results used to illustrate the previous chapter. In
addition, we will also give more examples and results from the different strategies.
Most of the tests and research was done using a 2-dimensional implementation of the models. It is worth noting that the goal is to extend the implementation to a 3-dimensional model
more suited for the problem we want to solve. All of the strategies and results from Chapter 3
are independent of the dimension. However, the 2-dimensional implementation is easier to
setup. Therefore, a 3-dimensional implementation was started but we lacked the time to get
enough results.
4.0.1
2D implementation and analysis
Implementation details
The 2-dimensional implementation was done using GNU Octave, Jupyter notebooks and Vulkan.
The GNU Octave is a free and open-source programming language specifically designed for
mathematical and scientific computing. It was chosen for its ease of use and for its efficient
matrix manipulation tools and mathematical tools. Octave was used inside a Jupyter notebook,
using Xeus-octave [16], an Octave kernel for Jupyter notebooks allowing the execution of Octave code in Jupyter notebooks code cells.
Since the model is intended to be used as part of a computer graphics rendering pipeline,
and to leverage the computing capacities of a Graphics Processing Unit (GPU), the Perlin noise
generation function was implemented using a Vulkan compute shader. The function was then
exposed to the Octave code using the Octave’s C++ extension API using an Oct file. This
allowed the noise generation function to be called from the Octave code like any other Octave
function. Appendix A contains a print of the notebook used as an example.
Note on the Perlin noise implementation
There are many different implementations of Perlin noise available, with minor differences.
Most implementations differ in the way the pseudo-random gradient of vectors is computed,
with varying levels of correctness. The implementation used in our tests was provided by
Mathéo Moinet, PhD student in the team. This implementation uses a pseudo-random gradient
generation method from a scratchpixel.com course [11], and a hash function from a Journal
of Computer Graphics Techniques publication by Jarzynski et al. [1]. Furthermore, it uses a
normalization factor as presented in this digitalfreepen.com post [12].

example. We can observe from the results that such a transformation has a tendency to bring
a filament-like pattern from the Perlin noise. This type of transformation could be useful for
obtaining the type of patterns observed in Figure 1.2. However, while this pattern appears
correct in 2 dimensions, the first tests seem to indicate that the intuition doesn’t transfer as
easily to 3 dimensions, and more work is needed to obtain a correct visual representation in a
3-dimensional context.

Conclusion
In this work, we tasked ourselves with designing a new procedural noise generation model to
find a solution to the issues involved with mimicking spatial dust and gas clouds when it comes
to real-time rendering of space objects such as galaxies and nebulae. Based on this goal, we
defined several requirements that our model needed to meet, both in terms of mathematical and
qualitative properties, and used these requirements to create a model based on the Perlin noise.
During this work, we discussed three methods to design a model following the expected
requirements, all based on the Multiplicative Perlin noise. The first approach used the symmetric property of Perlin noise to justify the use of functions following a specific rule to apply
transformations to the individual layers of the Multiplicative Perlin noise. We observed that
despite its advantages brought by its strict correctness, this method lacked expression range
and fine control on the resulting look of the noise model. The second approach used an approximation of Perlin noise as a normal distribution to create a uniform distribution based on the
Perlin noise. We showed that, while this method allowed for a greater expressive range than the
previous approach, the approximation of Perlin noise by a normal distribution isn’t as accurate
as it seems. Further work into determining the true quality of the approximation, and possibly
even defining a stricter Cumulative Distribution Function of Perlin noise could improve this
approach. The last approach used estimation through sampling to compensate for the impact
on the mean value of applying any function to the noise. This method also showed promising
results but restrict the procedural capabilities of the model.
There are several ways our work could be extended in the future. First of all, we already
discussed the possible improvements of the second method. In addition, there is still work to
be done to extend and test those approaches in a 3-dimensional context. Even if the results
obtained in this paper are mostly dimension-independent, there could still be a noticeable variation of the visual aspect of the models between 2-dimensions and 3-dimensions. On a more
general note for the future, when it comes to the rendering aspect and integration into existing rendering models, there are still a lot of questions to be answered. First of all, no matter
the approach, our current model is isotropic, meaning that the properties of the noise do not
depend of the direction in which we observe it. This is usually not the case in the real dust
and gas clouds we want to represent, so the model still needs to be modified. For example,
work by Moinet [5] presents a way to sculpt isotropic noises into anisotropic noises. Another
way would be to experiment with anisotropic noise generation models such as Gabor noise [3],
but these models are usually much more computation-heavy and will come in conflict with the
real-time requirements of our model. Furthermore, there is still work to be done when it comes
to the lighting aspect of rendering, which is a hard topic in our particular case. The clouds are
lit from the inside by thousands of different light sources, which makes computing an accurate
light model a real challenge for a real-time rendering application.
