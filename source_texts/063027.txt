Abstract
The proliferation of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) has significantly advanced
large-scale 3D data acquisition, paving the way for Digital Twin applications. While
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) now enable
exceptional photorealism, editing these vast neural representations remains a
significant challenge. This survey systematically analyzes large-scale scene editing
from UAV imagery, specifically focusing on the architectural dichotomy between
implicit (NeRF) and explicit (3DGS) representations. We introduce novel taxonomies
to deconstruct their core mechanisms and critically evaluate why explicit
representations are currently emerging as the more viable path for interactive, cityscale manipulation. Furthermore, we identify key bottlenecks—such as handling
oblique views and variable atmospheric conditions—and outline future research
directions.A comprehensive and regularly updated collection of the surveyed papers

wealth of data serves as the foundation for critical applications, ranging from Digital
Twin cities [3] and smart city management [4] to disaster assessment [5] and environmental monitoring [6, 7].

This technological gap motivates the transition from explicit meshes to neural representations. The emergence of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [18] and, more recently, 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [19] offers a significant evolution [20]. By learning a continuous volumetric function or an explicit point-based radiance field directly from images,
these methods synthesize novel views with unparalleled photorealism. They excel at capturing fine-grained details and view-dependent effects often lost in traditional pipelines

23].
However, these powerful new representations introduce their own set of challenges
[24]. Applying them to large-scale UAV aerial data involves handling vast geographic
coverage [25], inconsistent illumination [26], and the presence of dynamic elements [27].
Beyond these general issues, UAV imagery introduces specific acquisition constraints
that critically impact neural representation choices. Varying flight altitudes result in
inconsistent Ground Sampling Distances (GSD), which challenges the multi-scale consistency required by implicit NeRF encoders. Oblique viewing angles, while essential
for capturing building facades, often lead to occlusion-induced sparsity that destabilizes
the initialization of explicit 3DGS primitives. Furthermore, high-speed flight frequently
introduces motion blur, creating high-frequency noise that explicit representations may
erroneously reconstruct as ‘floaters,’ whereas implicit fields tend to smooth over these
artifacts at the cost of detail.
Most foundational research in this field was conducted in controlled, object-centric,
or indoor environments. These “laboratory” settings assume dense viewpoint coverage
that rarely holds in expansive aerial surveys. Therefore, this survey focuses exclusively
on large-scale aerial cityscapes. We deliberately exclude indoor datasets and hybrid sensor-fusion methods (e.g., LiDAR-NeRF) from our scope. Indoor data lacks the extreme
nadir and oblique viewpoint variations characteristic of UAV imagery. Furthermore, traditional fusion methods often prioritize geometric reconstruction over the vision-driven
editability that defines the current NeRF and 3DGS paradigms.
This shift in domain exposes a fundamental tension between a representation’s fidelity and its editability. While NeRF-based methods offer unparalleled realism through
a continuous, implicit function, this very nature makes them akin to a “black box.”
Consequently, editing becomes an indirect, optimization-based process. In contrast,
the discrete, explicit structure of 3DGS provides a more direct and intuitive path for
manipulation. The primary thesis of this survey is to critically examine the journey of
these neural editing paradigms as they migrate from laboratory settings to the complex
domain of aerial cityscapes. We will systematically dissect how the core architectural

1.2 Related work
Our work is situated at the intersection of large-scale 3D reconstruction, neural rendering, and interactive scene editing. While numerous surveys cover these topics individually, a comprehensive review connecting them within the context of UAV aerial imagery
is currently lacking. Recent bibliometric analyses, for instance, have mapped the broader
landscape of AI-driven video generation, identifying key technological trends and
research hotspots [28], yet a focused analysis specifically on the editability of large-scale
3D aerial representations remains a critical gap. Similarly, while AI-powered tools are
emerging in related creative fields, such as using virtual reality to enhance the efficiency
of 3D animation storyboarding [29], a systematic survey on editing city-scale neural representations is still needed.We structure our review of related surveys into three main
categories.
Surveys on Large-Scale 3D Reconstruction. The reconstruction of large-scale environments from imagery has been a long-standing research topic [30]. Early comprehensive
reviews, such as the one by Remondino et al. [31], provided a thorough overview of traditional photogrammetric pipelines, from SfM to MVS, detailing their mathematical
foundations and practical applications in cultural heritage and urban modeling. Another
notable survey by Nex and Remondino [32] specifically focused on the challenges and
opportunities of using UAV platforms for 3D mapping, highlighting issues like flight
planning, georeferencing, and data processing scalability. More recent surveys have also
covered the integration of deep learning with traditional MVS pipelines [33]. However,
these surveys offer an excellent foundation but primarily focus on generating static,
mesh-based geometric models. They do not address the modern challenges of achieving
photorealism or enabling dynamic, semantic editing, which are central to the new paradigm of Digital Twins.
Surveys on Neural Radiance Fields. The advent of NeRF initiated a new wave of
research [34], leading to several dedicated surveys. The foundational survey by Tewari
et al. [35], offers a broad and systematic overview of neural rendering techniques, with
a significant focus on NeRF and its theoretical underpinnings. It categorizes methods
based on their representation (implicit vs. explicit) and application. A more recent and
focused survey by Gao et al. [36] reviews NeRF and its variants, specifically analyzing
advancements in addressing NeRF’s limitations, such as slow rendering speed [37], generalization [38], handling of dynamic scenes [39] and achieving ultra-high-definition
rendering quality [40].A critical limitation of early NeRF methods is their heavy reliance
on dense viewpoint coverage. The challenge of reconstructing compelling 3D scenes
from sparse or even single-view inputs is a fundamental and long-standing problem in
the field. This very challenge has spurred significant research into developing robust
methods that can infer 3D geometry and appearance from limited 2D observations,
highlighting a shared goal with the NeRF community.While these surveys are invaluable
for understanding the rapid evolution of NeRF for representation, they only briefly touch
upon editing as a downstream task. Their scope does not deeply investigate the unique
set of challenges that arise when applying these methods to vast, gigapixel-scale aerial

datasets [25], nor do they provide a structured taxonomy for the diverse approaches to

Furthermore, a critical observation is that the foundational methods discussed in these
surveys, and indeed the field of neural editing at large, were predominantly developed
and benchmarked on object-centric or indoor datasets. While invaluable for establishing
core principles, these works do not directly address the unique set of challenges posed

Surveys on 3D Scene Editing and Manipulation. The topic of 3D editing is broad, with
most reviews focusing on traditional representations [42]. For instance, the survey by
Yumer and Chaudhuri [43] provides a comprehensive look at semantic and functional
editing of 3D shapes, but is confined to object-level manipulation of explicit meshes.
This focus on mesh-based editing remains an active area of research, with recent work
exploring text-driven modifications [44]. Similarly, editing other explicit formats like
point clouds often relies on precise, interactive labeling systems [45, 46]. However,
there is a noticeable scarcity of surveys dedicated to the editing of neural representations. Most knowledge in this area is fragmented across individual research papers, such
as those introducing CLIP-NeRF [47] or EditNeRF [48]. While some works have demonstrated impressive editing capabilities on object-centric [49] or indoor scenes, a systematic framework for understanding how to perform complex, multimodal edits (e.g.,
“replace this industrial zone with a park based on this blueprint”) on large-scale neural
scenes is missing. The recent emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting [50], with its explicit
and real-time nature, has opened up new possibilities for editing that are entirely absent
from existing NeRF-centric literature reviews. Our survey aims to fill this critical void.
We provide the first systematic review that synthesizes the state of the art in editing neural representations (both NeRF and 3DGS), framed specifically within the challenging
and high-impact domain of large-scale UAV aerial scenes.

This paper provides a structured and critical analysis of the landscape of large-scale aerial scene editing. While prior work primarily focuses on 3D reconstruction, our survey
evaluates neural representations through the lens of editability for UAV-captured data.

Domain-specific taxonomies for aerial scene editing We introduce two novel taxonomies
tailored for large-scale aerial data. Unlike rendering-centric surveys [35, 36], our framework deconstructs the editing process from multimodal intent extraction to localized
manipulation. These taxonomies specifically address the unique constraints of UAV datasets, such as vast geographic scales and extreme nadir-to-oblique viewpoint variations.
Analysis of the implicit-explicit representation dichotomy We conduct a systematic
comparison between implicit (NeRF) and explicit (3DGS) representations. Our analysis
identifies NeRF’s continuous nature as a “black box” that necessitates indirect, optimi-

2 Editing with NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting
The evolution from traditional photogrammetry to neural rendering has unlocked
exceptional photorealism, yet the critical challenge remains in making these complex
scenes editable. This chapter examines the two leading methodologies at the forefront of
this challenge: NeRF and 3DGS. These two approaches represent a fundamental dichotomy in scene representation—the implicit versus the explicit—which dictates their
inherent suitability for interactive editing. While NeRF-based methods offer unparalleled realism through a continuous, implicit function, this very nature makes them
akin to a “black box,” turning editing into an indirect, optimization-based process. In
contrast, the discrete, explicit structure of 3DGS provides a more direct and intuitive
path for manipulation. This chapter will first deconstruct the complex editing pipeline
required for NeRF models and then explore the emergent, interactive workflows enabled
by 3DGS, critically analyzing their respective strengths and limitations in the context of
large-scale aerial scenes.
To effectively deconstruct these paradigms, it is instructive to examine the foundational contributions through the figures presented in their original publications. Consequently, many of the illustrations in this chapter will feature object-level or indoor
scenes. However, our accompanying critical analysis will consistently serve as a bridge,
rigorously evaluating the specific implications, vulnerabilities, and necessary adaptations
that arise when these techniques are scaled to the complex and expansive context of
UAV aerial scenes. This approach allows us to dissect the core mechanism of a method
before assessing its real-world viability for our target domain [51].
2.1 The NeRF editing pipeline: an optimization-based approach
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) represent a scene as a continuous volumetric function learned directly from input images. Typically modeled by a Multilayer Perceptron
(MLP), this implicit representation maps 3D coordinates (x, y, z) and viewing directions
(θ, φ) to volumetric density (σ ) and emitted radiance (c). Through differentiable volume
rendering, NeRF synthesizes photorealistic novel views with exceptional quality. This
capability has made it a cornerstone for high-fidelity digital reconstruction, particularly
in UAV aerial mapping where precision is paramount.
However, the architectural design that powers NeRF’s reconstruction also hinders
editing. The scene’s geometry and appearance are not explicitly defined. Instead, they
are entangled within millions of neural network weights. Consequently, there is no
direct analogue to manipulating vertices on a mesh or moving objects in a traditional
Page 5 of 30
Gong et al. Discover Computing

3D editor. Editing a NeRF is fundamentally an indirect, optimization-based process. To
“change” a part of the scene, one must formulate the edit as a loss function and backpropagate gradients to adjust the network weights.
This optimization process introduces significant challenges, which are amplified in
large-scale aerial contexts. First is the Semantic-Geometric Disconnect. Translating
abstract user commands, such as “make this building taller,” into a differentiable loss is
non-trivial. It is difficult to modify implicit geometry without distorting textures or creating artifacts. Second is the Lack of Locality. Due to the global nature of the MLP, a
local edit intended for a single object can unintentionally propagate changes across the
entire scene, disrupting structural integrity. Third, the process suffers from Computational Intractability. Re-optimizing a massive NeRF model for every edit is computationally prohibitive, rendering real-time workflows virtually impossible for city-scale models.
Finally, Multimodal Ambiguity complicates the optimization. When edits are guided by
text or images, challenges like viewpoint bias and feature misalignment arise. In aerial
data, terms like “front of the building” become ill-defined due to nadir viewpoints.
Faced with these limitations, the research community has developed specialized techniques to facilitate editing. We distill these into a generalized Multimodal-driven NeRF
Editing Pipeline, as shown in Fig. 1. This framework breaks the complex task into four
canonical stages. The process begins with (1) Feature Extraction and Mapping, where
abstract user inputs—such as text prompts or reference images—are parsed into highlevel semantic vectors using pre-trained models like CLIP. These vectors are then
utilized in the (2) Conditional NeRF Architecture Design stage. Here, they serve as conditioning signals that modulate the MLP, effectively translating semantic intent into specific structural or textural changes within the implicit field. Subsequently, (3) Targeted
Optimization Strategies define how these changes are applied. This involves utilizing
carefully designed loss functions to update network weights, ensuring the edit is realized
without disrupting the global consistency of the scene. Finally, (4) Inverse Optimization
handles the integration of new data, aligning the pre-trained NeRF with updated aerial
imagery to reflect real-world changes.

2.1.2 Conditional NeRF architecture design
Once semantic features are extracted and mapped, the next critical stage involves
designing a NeRF architecture that can effectively integrate these conditions to modulate
the scene’s geometry and appearance. This is where user intent is translated into specific
structural and textural changes within the implicit representation. The core challenge
lies in achieving precise, disentangled control over shape and appearance without compromising the integrity and continuity of the radiance field.
Existing methods showcase a variety of architectural innovations to achieve this
conditioning:
CLIP-NeRF [47] stands as a pioneering work that enables multimodal control by leveraging CLIP embeddings to guide the optimization of a NeRF model. Its fundamental
principle is to translate the semantic distance in the CLIP feature space into a differentiable loss function, thereby “nudging” the NeRF’s weights towards a desired semantic concept. For large-scale aerial scenes, this provides exceptional flexibility in editing objects
like buildings or vegetation using simple text prompts. The architecture achieves this
by introducing an explicit volumetric deformation network that operates on positional
encodings to modulate geometry, while a separate mapper conditions the appearance.
As illustrated in Fig. 2 with object-level edits, the method’s core strength is its semantic
flexibility. However, this very reliance on gradient-based “nudging” of a monolithic MLP
becomes a significant liability for large-scale aerial scenes, where geometric accuracy is
paramount. The lack of explicit geometric handles makes it exceedingly difficult to perform precise, constrained edits, such as cleanly modifying a building’s roofline, extending a road with sharp boundaries, or ensuring new structures align perfectly with the
ground plane. The resulting “floaty” artifacts are not just visual blemishes but represent a
fundamental failure to maintain structural integrity in an architectural context.
However, its limitations stem from two root causes inherent to the implicit paradigm.
First, the MLP’s nature as a monolithic function means there is no explicit geometric
handle; edits are performed via gradient descent on the entire network. This fundamentally leads to “floaty” artifacts and a lack of sharp, precise control, a significant issue
when editing man-made structures in UAV data that demand geometric accuracy. Second, its reliance on 2D-based CLIP priors creates an inherent view-consistency problem.
A concept like “modern building” might be understood by CLIP from a frontal view, but
applying this consistently to the nadir (top-down) and oblique views common in UAV
datasets is a non-trivial challenge, often resulting in inconsistent or distorted geometry
across different perspectives. The alternating optimization strategy between foreground
and background is a practical workaround but does not solve this underlying issue, often
creating visible seams at edit boundaries.
Text2NeRF [52] introduces a progressive scene inpainting strategy, allowing for the
incremental expansion of a scene from textual commands. Its core architectural innovation is the ability to add new objects while freezing the parameters of the existing scene,
thus preventing catastrophic forgetting. This makes it particularly suitable for large-scale
urban scene editing, where dynamic growth is a common requirement.

2.1.3 Targeted optimization and scene update
With a conditional architecture in place, the final stage is the optimization process
itself. Here, the network’s weights are adjusted to realize the desired edit, guided by the
extracted features. This process is not a simple one-off training; it requires sophisticated
strategies to ensure that local edits are applied accurately while global scene consistency
is maintained. This stage encompasses both “forward” optimization (applying the edit)
and “reverse” optimization (aligning the model with new, real-world data).
Forward optimization strategies: The primary goal is to refine the scene progressively.
Various methods employ distinct optimization schemes:
Progressive refinement (CLIP-NeRF [47]): This strategy alternates between optimizing
the foreground region and the full image. It offers flexible control suitable for interactive
editing but relies on hard boundary segmentation, which can create color jumps or geometric seams at edit boundaries.

Inverse optimization for real-world data integration:
To enable the editing of scenes based on new real-world imagery (e.g., updating a Digital Twin with recent photos), methods often employ inverse optimization. This involves
projecting the new input image into the latent space of a pre-trained NeRF model by
minimizing reconstruction error.
Customize your NeRF [18] directly employs this technique. However, it faces challenges with feature compatibility, as stylized features from diffusion models used for
editing can conflict with the physical rendering characteristics of the NeRF latent space.
Furthermore, its local-global strategy can induce contradictory gradient directions during inverse optimization, significantly slowing down convergence.
SinNeRF [53], while not using direct inverse optimization, performs an implicit inverse
process by propagating pseudo-labels from limited data. This is data-efficient but risky,
as the confidence of pseudo-labels is not considered, potentially amplifying geometric

2.2 The 3DGS editing workflow: a direct manipulation approach
In stark contrast to the implicit nature of NeRF, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [19] represents a significant evolution toward explicit scene representation. Instead of relying
on a monolithic neural network, a 3DGS scene is composed of a vast collection of discrete, 3D Gaussian primitives. Each Gaussian is explicitly defined by a set of optimizable
parameters: its 3D position (mean), shape (covariance matrix), color (spherical harmonics coefficients), and opacity. These millions of primitives are projected onto an image
plane via a highly efficient, differentiable splatting process. This architecture enables the
real-time rendering of photorealistic scenes, a capability often absent in high-fidelity
NeRFs.
This explicit, point-based structure fundamentally alters the editing landscape.
Because each primitive acts as an independent, addressable entity, 3DGS sidesteps the
core “black box” limitations of implicit models. Unlike NeRF, where geometry is entangled within network weights, 3DGS allows for direct manipulation workflows. This
makes it inherently more suitable for interactive tasks where precise control is required.
However, applying this explicit paradigm to large-scale aerial scenes introduces unique
complexities.
The primary challenge is the Semantic Abstraction Gap. While individual points are
accessible, the raw representation is essentially a “sea of points” without high-level structural understanding. Grouping millions of Gaussians into meaningful entities—such as
“the roof of this skyscraper”—requires sophisticated selection algorithms or semantic
segmentation, which are often unavailable in raw aerial data. Furthermore, direct manipulation does not inherently respect Physical Plausibility. For instance, moving a building
requires a real-time recalculation of its shadow on neighboring structures to avoid violating lighting coherence. Naive editing can easily lead to unnatural geometric distortions. Finally, Structural and Topological Coherence remains a constant battle. Unlike
meshes with defined connectivity, 3DGS primitives are disconnected. Consequently,
operations like deleting a building section often leave behind hollow voids rather than
plausible interior structures.
To navigate this terrain, the research community has rapidly developed specialized
techniques for structuring and controlling 3DGS edits. We synthesize these into a generalized Interactive 3DGS Editing Workflow. As illustrated in Fig. 6, this workflow typically consists of five canonical stages: (1) interpreting user commands via Initial Layout
Generation; (2) defining the primitives in 3D Gaussian Representation Construction;
(3) refining their shape and placement through Geometric Optimization; (4) enhancing
realism via Surface Detail Enhancement; and (5) extending capabilities to time-varying
scenes via Dynamic Scene Support and interactive tooling. The following subsections
will explore each stage of this workflow, detailing the current approaches and the open
research questions they present.

2.2.2 3D Gaussian representation construction
In the 3DGS representation construction phase, the primary task is to define the position, color, opacity, and covariance matrix for each splat. This process is divided into
two steps: initialization and parameter definition. For UAV aerial data, the initialization
step can efficiently use the sparse point cloud generated by traditional photogrammetry
(e.g., SfM) as the initial positions for the Gaussians. This provides a solid foundation for
subsequent editing. The techniques in GaussianEditor support initialization of dynamic
scenes and user-friendly interaction through natural language commands.
In the parameter definition stage, precise settings for the 3DGS parameters are
required. LangSplat [60] efficiently learns a scene-specific latent space, reducing memory
consumption and improving the efficiency of parameter definition, making it suitable
for large-scale datasets. Additionally, “Fast Dynamic 3D“ [62] proposes a fast dynamic
adjustment algorithm that enhances system interactivity.
GaussianEditor [55] epitomizes the direct manipulation workflow enabled by 3DGS.
Its core principle is to leverage the explicit nature of Gaussian primitives by first using
a Large Language Model (LLM) to parse user commands (e.g., “select the roof ”) into a
spatial selection, and then applying transformations directly to the parameters (position,
rotation, scale, color) of the selected Gaussians. This approach provides an exceptional
level of real-time, fine-grained control.
The primary limitation arises from the semantic abstraction gap between language
and the raw “sea of points” representation. The method’s success is heavily reliant on
the LLM’s ability to accurately ground abstract concepts onto 3D geometry. For vast and
complex UAV aerial scenes, LLMs may struggle with ambiguous spatial relations (“the
building behind the park”) or fail to understand domain-specific architectural terminology. This can lead to incorrect selections and unpredictable edits. Furthermore, while
editing the parameters is direct, ensuring physical plausibility remains a challenge. For
example, naively changing the color of Gaussians representing a building facade ignores
lighting and material properties, resulting in a flat, unrealistic appearance. The method
lacks an intrinsic understanding of physics, placing the burden of maintaining coherence
entirely on the user or subsequent optimization steps.

correct relative positions and proportions. The GIR [61] inverse rendering method is
crucial here, estimating material properties, lighting, and geometry for high-fidelity
scene decomposition. For large-scale aerial scenes, the combined use of GIR and LangSplat can significantly enhance realism.

2.2.6 Interactive editing
The development of interactive 3D scene editing tools integrates advanced technologies to meet user demands for precise and efficient editing of large-scale aerial scenes.
However, existing methods still face challenges with real-time interactivity, user intent
understanding, and physical plausibility. The ambiguity of natural language can lead to
directional confusion in parameter optimization, and a lack of causality in dynamic editing can trigger unforeseen chain reactions.
GaussianEditor [55] provides a highly precise editing environment using 3DGS representation, allowing for detailed operations on specific regions. However, its explicit editing mechanism risks decoupling from natural language commands.
GIR [61] expands editing capabilities to material properties and lighting conditions.
However, its joint optimization framework requires specialized user knowledge.
SC-GS [64] focuses on dynamic content management by combining sparse control
points with 3DGS. However, its predefined control point layout conflicts with free-form
editing demands.
Efficient4D [62] introduces an efficient 4D Gaussian representation scheme for interactive environments. However, its real-time optimization comes at the cost of editing
granularity.
Magic3D [67] provides an end-to-end solution from text to high-resolution 3D content, excelling in creating complex indoor scenes. However, its end-to-end black-box
architecture lacks editing interpretability.
These deficiencies reveal the core contradiction of interactive editing tools: the chasm
between the ambiguity of user intent and the deterministic requirements of computational models. Based on a comprehensive evaluation, Efficient4D strikes an optimal balance between speed, quality, and methodology, making it suitable for most interactive
editing tasks. However, the most appropriate method still depends on the user’s specific
needs. For visual quality, GIR might be more suitable. For frequent interaction, GaussianEditor excels. For dynamic scenes, SC-GS is a strong tool. For complex indoor scenes,
Magic3D is the ideal choice.

[70] provide the necessary scale and visual complexity, but they are static and unannotated. They cannot validate structural changes or dynamic updates. The absence of such

3.1.3 Quantitative analysis and computational infrastructure
While qualitative metrics assess visual fidelity, the deployment of editing pipelines for
large-scale aerial digital twins is ultimately governed by quantitative constraints: processing latency, rendering frame rates, and hardware infrastructure requirements. As
detailed in Table 4, empirical evidence highlights a fundamental dichotomy between
implicit and explicit paradigms.
1. Quantitative benchmarking: latency and speed
There is an order-of-magnitude performance gap between the two representations.
Training and Editing Latency: NeRF-based pipelines are inherently compute-bound
due to the requirements of volumetric ray-marching. Generative methods like DreamFusion [50] require 1.5 to 6 h per asset on high-end hardware (TPUv4 or NVIDIA A100).
Similarly, propagating edits through a scene with Instruct-NeRF2NeRF [8] typically
takes 45 min to 2 h. In contrast, explicit 3DGS-based methods utilize fast rasterization.

2. Infrastructure for large-scale deployment: compute vs. memory
Applying these methods to city-scale aerial scenes introduces specific infrastructure
bottlenecks.
NeRF (Compute-Bound): Implicit methods are memory-efficient for storage (compact weights) but require massive computational power for training gradients. Training a city-scale NeRF typically demands Multi-GPU clusters (e.g., 4–8 A100s) to handle
the batch size of rays. However, once trained, the model size is small, facilitating easier transmission over networks, though client-side rendering remains computationally
heavy.
3DGS (Memory-Bound): Explicit methods shift the bottleneck to Video RAM
(VRAM). A high-fidelity aerial scene can consist of millions of Gaussians. For example,
methods like GSGEN [65] demonstrate superior capture of high-frequency geometry,
but this detail comes at the cost of VRAM usage that can easily exceed the 24GB limit of
consumer GPUs (e.g., RTX 3090/4090).
Streaming Pipelines: To enable large-scale editing, future infrastructure must move
beyond single-GPU optimization. For 3DGS, this implies implementing out-of-core
streaming pipelines and dynamic Level-of-Detail strategies, where Gaussian blocks are
dynamically loaded into VRAM based on the camera frustum, decoupling the scene’s
geographic scale from local hardware limits.
3.2 Work summary and limitations
In this survey, we have presented a structured overview of the large-scale aerial scene
editing landscape. By introducing specific taxonomies for NeRF-based and 3DGS-based
pipelines, we analyzed the transferability of neural editing from laboratory settings to
complex UAV-captured environments.
Our analysis highlights a clear architectural trade-off. NeRF’s continuous representation is powerful for capturing subtle, view-dependent effects and atmospheric haze.
However, its implicit nature makes localized, structural manipulation computationally

(2026) 29:142
expensive and prone to artifacts. Nevertheless, implicit representations remain indispensable for specific non-geometric editing tasks. NeRF variants excel in scenarios
involving volumetric environmental editing, such as modifying fog density, simulating
cloud formations, or altering global lighting atmospheres—tasks where discrete Gaussians often struggle to maintain continuum consistency. Additionally, for artistic style
transfer where global coherence outweighs local structural precision, implicit fields
often provide superior generalization in the far-field background.
In contrast, the explicit, discrete structure of 3DGS enables real-time interaction and
granular geometric control. These advantages position 3DGS as a more viable candidate for practical, interactive Digital Twin applications. Despite these strengths, explicit
methods still face challenges in ensuring physical plausibility and managing memory
consumption at city scales.
The future of scene editing likely lies in the convergence of these two paradigms.
Emerging hybrid models aim to fuse the geometric interactivity of explicit primitives
with the high-fidelity appearance modeling of implicit functions. Such a “best-of-bothworlds” approach would allow for sharp, direct manipulation of buildings while preserving the complex reflections and lighting characteristics of urban scenes. Moving
forward, the field must also prioritize the development of standardized benchmarks to
move from qualitative assessment to rigorous, quantitative validation.
3.3 Future research directions
Based on the challenges and contradictions identified through our systematic analysis,
we propose several promising directions for future research. Crucially, these directions
can be effectively framed and guided by the NeRF and 3DGS editing taxonomies we
have introduced. Our frameworks not only structure past work but also illuminate the
path forward by highlighting the key stages ripe for innovation.
1. Unified and hybrid representations: moving beyond the implicit-explicit dichotomy.
The fundamental tension between implicit (NeRF) and explicit (3DGS) representations
is a recurring theme in our analysis. The future lies not in choosing one over the other,
but in unifying them.
Insights from Our Comparative Analysis: Our taxonomies reveal that NeRFs excel at
high-fidelity appearance representation while 3DGS excels at geometric manipulation.
Future research should focus on creating neuro-explicit or hybrid structures—such as
object-centric neural primitives or Gaussian-based radiance field parameterizations—
that combine the best of both worlds. By doing so, we can retain NeRF’s superior capability in modeling atmospheric scattering and sky appearance—critical for realistic aerial
twins—while leveraging 3DGS for efficient structural editing.This would allow for direct,
editable control over geometry (like in the 3DGS workflow) while preserving the continuous, view-dependent appearance detail captured by implicit functions (a key part of
the NeRF pipeline).
2. Scalability and efficiency for true city-scale models.
While current methods can handle large scenes, true city-scale, interactive editing
remains a significant hurdle.

Implications for Pipeline Scalability: Both the NeRF and 3DGS pipelines involve stages
that are not yet scalable to billions of primitives or city-wide neural fields. Research
should focus on out-of-core algorithms, distributed frameworks, and advanced data
structures. For example, developing AI-driven optimization strategies for explicit representations, such as utilizing cross-entropy loss to intelligently refine large-scale point
clouds, can drastically improve rendering performance and data handling efficiency for
complex virtual city models [73]. This line of inquiry, focusing on advanced data structures like neural scene graphs or hierarchical Gaussian structures, directly addresses
the “Optimization” stage in our NeRF taxonomy and the “Representation Construction”
stage in our 3DGS taxonomy, aiming to make them efficient at an exceptional scale.
3. Physics-informed and physically plausible editing.
A major limitation identified across most methods is the lack of physical realism. Edits
often look visually plausible but are physically incorrect.
Integration with Editing Stages: This challenge touches upon the “Conditional Architecture Design” (NeRF) and “Geometric Optimization” (3DGS) stages. Future frameworks must integrate physical laws and constraints directly into the optimization
process. This includes developing differentiable physics simulators that can enforce
structural integrity, ensure correct lighting interactions (e.g., real-time shadow casting
upon moving an object), and simulate material properties, moving beyond simple color
changes to physically-based material editing.
4. Robust multimodal data fusion for living digital twins.
The true power of a Digital Twin lies in its ability to integrate and synchronize with
diverse, real-world data sources.
Enhancing Input Modalities: The “Feature Extraction and Mapping” stage of our NeRF
pipeline and the “Initial Layout Generation” of our 3DGS pipeline currently rely heavily
on text and images. A critical future direction is the seamless fusion of neural representations with traditional geospatial data, such as GIS vector layers and LiDAR point
clouds. This requires developing methods for bidirectional updates, where an edit in the
neural scene (e.g., adding a building) automatically updates the GIS database, and vice
versa.
5. Intuitive human–computer interaction paradigms.
To empower domain experts like urban planners and architects, research must move
beyond simple text prompts to more sophisticated HCI paradigms.
Refining User Interaction: This directly relates to the user-input stages of both taxonomies. Future work should explore sketch-based or gesture-based modeling within
the 3D scene, immersive VR/AR editing experiences that allow users to “walk through”
and modify the scene from within, and advanced conversational AI that can understand
complex, context-aware, and sequential editing commands (e.g., “Add a two-story building here, then place a park next to it with a walking path connecting to the main road”).
In this survey, we have presented a comprehensive and structured overview of the rapidly evolving landscape of large-scale aerial scene editing. The novel NeRF and 3DGS
editing taxonomies introduced in this work serve as more than just a means of categorization; they provide a standardized analytical framework for the entire field. By

Ultimately, these taxonomies offer a clear roadmap for future innovation. They
empower researchers to target specific bottlenecks within the editing pipeline—be it
improving the semantic understanding in the initial input stages, designing more controllable neural architectures, or developing more scalable optimization strategies. By
