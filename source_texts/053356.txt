ABSTRACT
Keywords
Data sets acquired from complex scientific simulation, high
precision engineering experiment and high-speed computer
network have been exponentially increased, and visualization and analysis of such large-scale of data sets have been
identified as a significant challenge to the visualization community. Over the past years many scientists have made attempt to address this problem by proposing various data
reduction techniques. Consequently the size of data can be
reduced and issues associated to the visualization can be
improved (e.g. real-time interaction and visual overload).
However, during the process of data reduction, the information of original data sets was approximated and potential
errors were introduced. It leads to a new problem with regard to the integrity of the data and might mislead users for
incorrect decision making. Therefore in this paper we aim to
solve the problem by introducing three novel uncertainty visualization methods, which depict both the multi-resolution
(MR) approximations of the original data set and the errors
associated with each of its low resolution representations. As
a result we faithfully represent the MR data sets and allow
users to make suitable decisions from the visual output. We
applied our techniques on a data set from medical domain
to demonstrate their effectiveness and usability.

1.
INTRODUCTION
Visualization and analysis of large-scale of data sets remains a significant challenge to the visualization community
[3]. Although memory and disk storage capacity continues
to increase and the cost to purchase them is cheaper, the
data obtained from complex scientific simulation, engineering experiment and high-speed computer network continues
to outpace hardware advantages. It leads to many difficulties regarding visualization e.g., visual overload and non
real-time interaction. Over the past years, many research
works have been proposed and carried out to address the
issue. One of the growing interests is to generate the multiresolution approximations of the original data. However,
these techniques lead to a new problem with regard to the
integrity of data. Errors were introduced into the low resolution approximations of original data set and they are often
ignored to be depicted by traditional visualization methods
[7]. As a result, users might be mistrustful or unconscious of
the processed data, and thus make their incorrect decisions
or inferences. Therefore the motivation of our research is
to provide users with effective and meaningful methods to
present both the low resolution approximations of original
data set and the errors associated with them. Consequently
we faithfully preserve the integrity of the data and allow
users to identify and realize the reliability of data, and therefore make their suitable decisions.
The structure of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the related research and their significance
to our work. Section 3.1 introduces the characteristics of
volumetric data with regular grids. Section 3.2 introduces
the Haar wavelet transformation in order to obtain the MR
data sets. Section 3.3 describes the error model we used to
quantify the errors that was introduced into the low resolution approximations of original data. Section 4 introduces
the extended marching cubes algorithm that was exploited
to include the error information. In Section 5 we present
three novel marching cubes based uncertainty visualization
methods for the MR data. Section 6 discusses the experimental results after applying these methods on the data set
from medical domain. Section 7 presents our conclusions
and we discuss the future direction and possible work to be
carried out.
2.
RELATED WORK
2.1
MR Data Model
With the growing interest of visualization and analysis
of large-scale of data set, MR techniques have been developed rapidly to reduce the size of original data over the
past years. There are a variety of different MR approaches
available, each one with its preferred application. Here we
only discuss two methods that we feel interested in our research. The first method is called wavelet-based algorithms.
You can consider this process as two steps. The first step
is to apply a low pass filter to the original data to create
its coarser approximations. Secondly, a high pass filter is
used to capture the details. Due to its firm mathematical
basis and nice theoretical properties, many papers have been
proposed to use it for data compression, computer graphics
and visualization [15][4][16]. The second method is called
decimation. The method iteratively removes elements (vertices, edges or triangles) from the mesh and the information
decimated from the mesh is simply discarded. A number
of papers have also been published to use the method to
decrease the size of data [8][10]. In this research, we only
used wavelet-based algorithm, in particular, Haar wavelet
transformation method to obtain the MR data set. Section
3.2 gives a detailed description of such a method. However,
it is also possible to test our methods using the decimation
based method.
2.2
Marching Cubes
Marching cubes, also known as “3D contouring”, “iso-surface
rendering” or “surface reconstruction”, is an effective technique to create a polygonal surface representation of an isosurface through a 3D scalar field. Compared to the direct
volume rendering (DVR), although marching cubes algorithm is not able to represent the entire volumetric data
directly, it can be used to clearly extract the features that
are interested to the users. Therefore, marching cubes is one
of the most popular techniques existing to visualize the volumetric data. Many domains have applied or extended the
technique to visualize their data. In [14], P. J. Rhodes et al.
have pointed out that the nature of marching cubes makes
it amenable to an effective solution to uncertainty visualization. In our research, we extended their work and present
more methods to visualize both MR data and its associated
errors.

3.
MR DATA MODEL
3.1
Volumetric Data with Regular Grids
This type of data is distributed on the three-dimensional
grid points and composed of plenty of cuboids. The position of each grid point can be specified by x, y and z coordinates and in each dimension, the distance between two
sampling points is equidistant. However, it is not necessary to be equidistant in all three dimensions. On each grid
point, there is one or multiple scalar values associated with
it. The coordinates of the grid points together with their
scalar values form both the topology and attributes of the
data set. Figure 1 illustrates an example of the structure of
volume data with regular grids.
In this research, we utilize the volumetric data with regular grids from medical domain to demonstrate the usability
of our methods. However, it is also possible to utilize the
regular grids-based volumetric data from other domains to
test out methods.
3.2
Haar Wavelet Transformation
Haar wavelet transformation is one of the simplest waveletbased algorithms. It can be used effectively to generate MR
data model. Applying the Haar wavelet on discrete data will
yield both summary as well as detail information [3]. The
size of summary and detail information will depend on the
concrete dimensions of the data set. For a one dimensional
data set with s values, the 1D Haar wavelet will be used to
transform it into a s/2 summary and a s/2 detail coefficient.
For a two dimensional data set with m × n values, the 1D
Haar wavelet algorithm will be applied firstly along its each
row, and then along its each column. This process is normally called 2D Haar wavelet transformation which results
in a summary with 1/4 size of original data (m/2×n/2), and
3 quadrants of detail data. Similarly, for a three dimensional
data set with m × n × l, the 1D Haar wavelet algorithm will
be applied on it firstly along each row, and then along each
column, finally along each slice. This process is normally
called 3D Haar wavelet transformation and a summary with
1/8 size of original data (m/2 × n/2 × l/2), and 7 octants of

5.
UNCERTAINTY VISUALIZATION USING
MARCHING CUBES
The local errors measurement and the extended marching
cubes algorithm allow us to map the errors of the volumetric
data into various forms. In [14], P. J. Rhodes et al. have
introduced two useful techniques. They mapped the error
information into the hue and opaque texture, respectively.
In this section, we introduce three more mapping strategies
to map the error information.
5.1
Uncertainty Visualization Using Blurring
Texture
Blurring is a natural cue to the eye that something is
amiss [7]. Pang et al. have summarized a variety of techniques suited for uncertainty visualization including blurring
[12]. However, they only explored the particle tracking using blurring to show the uncertainty in the path. In our
research, we explore to apply the blurring texture into the
iso-surface rendering for MR volume data.
The process to apply blurring texture for uncertainty visualization can be divided into three steps and it is performed
iteratively on every triangular facet. The first step is to calculate the error for each triangular facet. In our case we
only calculate the weighted average of the errors from the
three vertices of the triangular facet, as described in Section
4. The second step is to process the image which will be
used as the texture of the triangular facet into corresponding
blurring effect, based on the average value of error acquired
from step 1. The bigger the error value is, the more blurring the image is and vice versa. In other words the error
values of these triangular facets are mapped into the different blurring levels of the texture. The third step is to map
the blurring texture to the corresponding triangular facet.
As a result after rendering, users can now distinguish and
perceive the authenticity of MR data by means of blurring
effects. Figure 5 shows the result of the technique.

Uncertainty Visualization Using Glyphs
This technique is quite similar to the blurring texture technique. The same three steps can be applied to the technique.
However, this time instead of mapping the error values to
the blurring effects of texture, we map them to different
glyphs of texture. We design two sets of different glyphs
to present the error values, as show in figure 6 and figure
7. The first set of glyphs is designed with different shapes,
which were expected to assist users to distinguish the different levels of errors apparently. The second set of glyphs
is designed with different shapes as well as different sizes
(in our case they are the gradient shapes of the moon). It
was expected to allow users to perceive the different levels
of errors more easily, compared to the first one. In addition,
for the purpose of clarity, in both designs we use the solid
color texture to express the error-free or little errors’ data.
Consequently, uses can distinguish the authenticity of data
through different glyphs.
5.3
Uncertainty Visualization Using Transparency
In this technique we map the errors into the transparency
of the triangular facets. The more opaque the triangular
facet is, the more reliable the data is and vice versa. Three
steps are required to perform such a mapping. Firstly, we
obtain the value of error for every vertex of the triangular
facet, as described in Section 4. Secondly, we normalize the
error value for every vertex of the triangular facet to the
range between 0 and 1. Thirdly, we map the normalized
error value to the alpha component of RGBA (RGBA refers
to red, green, blue and alpha) based on every vertex of the
triangular facet. Figure 8 shows the result.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Our program is implemented in C++ language base on
openGL and Glut packages. The reason we do not use highlevel APIs like VTK (The Visualization Toolkit) is because
we need the low level APIs for extending the marching cubes
algorithm and controlling the rendering. We ran the program on a dual processor 2.4GHz Window XP OS laptop
using an AIT 3450 graphics card that has 256MB of memory.
The test data set we used is MRI (magnetic resonance
imaging) human brain data set, available at [1]. The original data consists of regular grids of 200 × 160 × 160 with 1
byte per voxel. We apply the 3D Haar wavelet transformation twice on the data set and then get two low resolution
approximations of the original data set. Figure 4 illustrates
the MR data model hierarchy of the data set. The value
we used for all iso-surface extraction in our research is 128.
Figure 4 (a) presents the original data set with dimensions
of 200 × 160 × 160. Figure 4 (b) presents the low resolution
approximation of the original data set after one pass of 3D
Haar wavelet transformation (100 × 80 × 80). From the
figure we can see that the coarse representation preserves a
good impression of original data. Figure 4 (c) presents the
further approximation after two passes of 3D Haar wavelet
transformation (50 × 40 × 40). It is clear that the coarse
representation has lost a great detail, compared to the original data shown in figure 4 (a).

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presents three uncertainty visualization using
marching cubes techniques for MR volumetric data. We
describe and discuss the specific detail of the Haar wavelet
transformation, extended marching cubes algorithm that includes error information as well as the advantages or disadvantages of these techniques. In general, for the iso-surface
rendering based uncertainty visualization it is more effective
and straightforward to map the values of errors to the blurring effects of texture, or the transparency of the triangular
facets, rather than to the glyphs.
There are three directions we are planning to do in the future. Firstly we intend to design an experiment to validate
and compare these iso-surface rendering based uncertainty
visualization techniques with techniques described in [14].
Secondly we want to extend and apply these techniques on
volume data with irregular grids. Thirdly we intend to explore more complicated methods such direct volume rendering (DVR) or hybrid rendering for the uncertainty visualization of MR volume data.
