Volume Rendering with Metric–Phase Fields for Thin Structures and
Sign-Ambiguous Geometry
arXiv:2610.13088v1 [cs.CV] 9 Oct 2026
Junkai Deng, Jiayi Kong, Xuhui Chen, Guanli Hou, Fei Hou, Junhui Hou, Wenping Wang, Ying He
Abstract—Neural implicit surface methods that couple volume
rendering with signed distance functions (SDFs), such as VolSDF
and NeuS, achieve high-quality multi-view reconstruction for
watertight objects. However, these approaches rely on a globally
consistent inside–outside partition of space. This assumption
becomes fragile for thin structures (e.g., sheets, tubes, and thin
walls) and can break down for sign-ambiguous geometry (e.g.,
surfaces with boundary, non-manifold junctions, and closely
spaced surface components), often resulting in spurious closures
or loss of fine structures. Unsigned distance field (UDF) formulations relax the global sign requirement and better support non-watertight geometry, but are substantially harder to
optimize: near the surface, unsigned distances exhibit cusplike non-differentiability, which complicates stable, occlusionaware rendering and surface extraction. We propose PhaseSurf,
a neural volume rendering framework built on Metric–Phase
Fields (MPFs). MPFs decompose geometry into a nonnegative
metric field capturing unsigned proximity and a continuous phase
field providing a soft sign cue. By coupling the two through
residual phase injection, MPFs yield a signed composite implicit
function with stable near-surface gradients while retaining UDFlike robustness in sign-ambiguous regions. This design enables
direct iso-surface extraction using standard Marching Cubes and
avoids the failure modes of both pure SDF- and UDF-based
formulations. We further introduce MPF-specific training losses
that enforce metric consistency, stabilize phase transitions near
the interface, and suppress spurious geometry in free space.
Experiments on multi-view datasets with thin structures and signambiguous geometry show that PhaseSurf produces more stable
reconstructions and higher-quality geometry than UDF-based
methods and recent 3DGS pipelines, while avoiding artifacts
typical of SDF-based approaches.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Recovering high-quality 3D geometry from posed multiview images is a central problem in vision and graphics.
Neural volume rendering has made this task practical by optimizing 3D representations directly from photometric supervision. However, standard volume rendering formulations that
represent geometry implicitly through volumetric density fields
often produce blurred geometry, and the extracted surfaces
can be noisy or poorly defined. To obtain sharper and more

and NeuS [2], along with many follow-up works, convert
signed distances into rendering weights and achieve strong
reconstruction and view synthesis performance. Despite their
success, SDF-based formulations implicitly assume a globally

structures, and non-manifold attachments. On such shapes,

sures, thickness inflation, or missing sheets.
Unsigned distance fields (UDFs) offer an appealing alternative by removing the need for a global sign and naturally
supporting open surfaces. However, learning UDFs within
a volume rendering framework is substantially more challenging. Near the interface, a UDF behaves like an absolute
value function and exhibits a cusp, complicating gradientbased optimization. More importantly, volume rendering requires converting distances into unbiased and occlusion-aware
rendering weights; without sign information, designing stable
weight functions becomes delicate. As a result, recent methods
often rely on two-stage optimization or specialized training
strategies to achieve robust convergence [5].
In point-cloud reconstruction, S2 DF [6] addresses this nondifferentiability by learning a scaled squared distance field
that remains differentiable at the zero level set. However,
this idea does not directly translate to posed multi-view
reconstruction: S2 DF remains an unsigned, nonnegative field
and therefore does not provide an inherent sign change for
occlusion reasoning, while the squared-distance formulation
yields vanishing gradients on the surface, weakening normal
cues and near-surface regularization under purely photometric supervision. Moreover, S2 DF relies on explicit surface
anchoring and second-order (Hessian-based) regularization,
which are expensive to evaluate densely along camera rays
and difficult to constrain from images alone.
Extracting the zero level set from a learned UDF or S2 DF
is considerably more difficult than from sign-based representations, where standard Marching Cubes can be applied
directly. In the absence of sign information, surface extraction
typically requires additional cues, such as using gradient
directions to detect zero crossings [7], which can be unreliable
due to unstable UDF gradients, or resorting to optimizationbased extraction methods [8], [9], which are computationally
expensive and significantly slower.
In this work, we adopt Metric–Phase Fields (MPFs) [10]
as a geometric backbone for neural volume rendering. MPFs
decouple geometry into (i) a metric field r(x) ≥ 0 that
captures unsigned proximity in a UDF-like manner, and (ii) a
continuous phase field θ(x) that provides a soft sign/side cue
via P (x) = tanh(β θ(x)). The metric and phase components
are coupled through residual phase injection to form a signed
composite implicit function ϕ(x) = r(x)P (x) + θ(x) with
stable near-surface gradients. The metric branch retains the
robustness of unsigned representations for open and thin structures, while the phase branch supplies signed behavior where
meaningful, enabling standard iso-surface extraction and SDFstyle regularization near the interface. In sign-ambiguous
regions such as thin sheets or tubes, boundaries, and nonmanifold junctions, MPFs tend to form a double cover—a
narrow two-sided shell—that supports a local sign change even
when the enclosed volume is arbitrarily thin (Fig. 1).
MPFs have demonstrated advantages in reconstructing thin
and open geometry from unoriented point clouds [10]. Our
goal is to bring these advantages to posed multi-view reconstruction by integrating MPFs into a differentiable volume rendering pipeline. Since the composite field φ is signed, we can
directly adopt the NeuS [2] signed-field-to-opacity mapping

to obtain occlusion-aware rendering weights concentrated near
{φ = 0}. Nevertheless, integrating MPFs into NeuS/VolSDFstyle frameworks is not a simple drop-in replacement. Unlike
an SDF, φ is not inherently distance-calibrated; under purely
photometric supervision, its scale and near-surface gradients
can become poorly conditioned. Moreover, the MPF decomposition introduces additional degrees of freedom: many different
pairs (r, θ) can induce similar rendered colors, so photometric
supervision alone does not uniquely determine how the two
branches should share responsibility. Finally, sign-ambiguous
regimes such as open boundaries and thin structures are
especially challenging under multi-view supervision, where
only 2D photometric cues mediated by visibility are available.
Preserving thin and open geometry without spurious closures
or floaters therefore requires MPF-tailored regularization.
Building on these insights, we introduce PhaseSurf, a neural
volume rendering framework that addresses the above challenges by rendering with the signed composite field φ while
enforcing MPF-specific geometric constraints. Residual phase
injection ensures non-vanishing gradients near the interface,
and additional coupling and regularization terms stabilize optimization in sign-ambiguous regions. This design preserves the
advantages of SDF-based surface rendering, including standard
Marching Cubes extraction, while substantially improving
modeling capacity for open surfaces and thin structures. Evaluation on datasets with sign-ambiguous geometry and thin
structures shows consistent improvements in reconstruction
fidelity and training stability over strong SDF- and UDF-based
baselines, as well as recent 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)
pipelines.
II. R ELATED W ORK
We briefly review the most relevant work on SDF-, UDFand 3DGS-based 3D reconstruction from posed multi-view
images.
a) SDF Methods: A main line of work couples differentiable rendering with signed distance functions to obtain
sharp geometry from multi-view supervision. Early approaches
such as DVR [11] and IDR [12] learn implicit surfaces
through differentiable surface rendering. Building on NeRFstyle volume rendering, later methods integrate SDFs into
volumetric rendering to jointly optimize geometry and appearance, notably UNISURF [13], VolSDF [1], and NeuS [2],
which achieve high-quality reconstruction and view synthesis.
Follow-up work improves fidelity and/or efficiency, including
HF-NeuS [14], Neuralangelo [15], and NeuS2 [16].
b) UDF Methods: To relax the watertight assumption
implicit in SDF-based formulations, several methods learn
unsigned distance functions from multi-view photometric supervision. Representative approaches include NeUDF [17]
and NeuralUDF [18], which introduce UDF-specific mappings from distances to rendering weights or densities, as
well as 2S-UDF [5], which improves robustness via a twostage optimization strategy. NeAT [19] also targets arbitrarytopology reconstruction by augmenting SDF modeling with
a validity field. Recent work explores learned or data-driven
rendering priors to stabilize UDF learning and improve surface
extraction.
JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 18, NO. 9, SEPTEMBER 2020
c) 3DGS Methods: 3D Gaussian Splatting represents
scenes using explicit Gaussian primitives and enables efficient optimization and real-time rendering [20]. Because
Gaussians do not directly define an explicit surface, extracting high-quality geometry typically requires additional constraints or dedicated meshing pipelines, such as SuGaR [21],
2DGS [5], Gaussian surfels [22], GOF [23], PGSR [24],
and GS2Mesh [25]. Hybrid approaches combine Gaussians
with implicit surfaces to improve geometric quality, including
3DGSR [26], NeuSG [27], and GSDF [28], while additional
regularization schemes aim to improve multi-view geometric
consistency.
d) Challenges: Despite significant progress, reconstructing high-quality surfaces for sign-ambiguous geometries (e.g.,
open boundaries, thin sheets, and non-manifold junctions)
from posed images remains challenging. SDF-based renderers
couple geometry and sign in a single field and assume a globally consistent inside–outside partition; when this assumption
is violated, they often favor watertight explanations, leading
to spurious closures, thickness inflation, or missing thin parts.
UDF-based formulations remove the global sign requirement
but introduce their own difficulties under image-only supervision: cusp-like behavior near the interface makes optimization fragile, and converting unsigned distances into stable,
occlusion-aware rendering weights is non-trivial; reliable surface extraction often requires additional cues or costly postprocessing. 3DGS methods excel at efficient view synthesis,
but they represent geometry indirectly through opacity distributions over Gaussians, making meshing sensitive to heuristics
and regularization; thin or open structures may therefore be
over-smoothed, fragmented, or affected by floaters. These
challenges are amplified in sign-ambiguous regions, where
purely 2D supervision provides weak geometric constraints.
Metric–Phase Fields have recently shown promising results
for reconstructing thin sheets and other sign-ambiguous geometries from unoriented point clouds. However, extending
MPFs from point-cloud reconstruction to posed multi-view
images introduces additional challenges. In point-cloud settings, surface samples are directly available and provide strong
geometric supervision for learning the metric and phase fields.
In contrast, multi-view reconstruction relies only on indirect
photometric cues mediated through visibility and rendering. As
a result, the additional degrees of freedom introduced by the
metric–phase decomposition must be carefully regularized to
avoid degenerate solutions. This motivates integrating MPFs
into a volume rendering framework with MPF-specific constraints that stabilize optimization while preserving flexibility
for open and thin structures.

IV. P ROPOSED M ETHOD
Given posed multi-view images, our goal is to reconstruct
a surface that may contain sign-ambiguous structures such as
thin sheets, open boundaries, and non-manifold junctions. We
build on the NeuS pipeline [2] and replace the SDF geometry
backbone by an MPF. Concretely, we represent geometry using
two coupled fields: an unsigned metric field r(x) ≥ 0 and a
phase field θ(x), and form the signed composite implicit function φ(x) = r(x) P (x) + θ(x), whose zero level set {φ = 0}
defines the reconstructed surface. Since φ is signed, we can
directly adopt NeuS-style rendering to obtain occlusion-aware
weights concentrated around the interface. The key difference
from NeuS is our MPF-specific regularization: under purely
photometric supervision, the metric and phase branches can
otherwise drift or collapse. We therefore augment the RGB
loss with a set of losses tailored to MPFs to stabilize optimization near the interface and preserve UDF-like robustness
in sign-ambiguous regions.
A. MPF Geometry and Appearance Networks
As illustrated in Fig. 2, our model contains two neural
components: (i) an MPF geometry network that predicts the
metric–phase decomposition and a geometry feature, and (ii)
a radiance network that predicts view-dependent color. The
composite field φ is used for NeuS-style signed surface
rendering, while MPF-specific regularizers are applied to the
predicted fields to stabilize image-only training.
a) Geometry network (MPF): We parameterize MPF
geometry with an MLP that maps a 3D query point x to
an unsigned metric field r(x) ≥ 0, a phase field θ(x) ∈ R,
and a learned feature vector f (x) for appearance. Following
NeuS [2], we adopt a shared trunk with lightweight heads.
Given x, we apply positional encoding γ(x) and compute a
shared representation h(x) = Fψ (γ(x)). Two scalar heads
predict r̃(x) = gr (h(x)) and θ(x) = gθ (h(x)), while an additional head (or intermediate activation) outputs the appearance
feature f (x). Non-negativity of the metric branch is enforced
by construction via r(x) = softplus(r̃(x)). From the phase
field we derive a bounded indicator P (x) = tanh(β θ(x)),
where β > 0 controls the sharpness of the phase transition and is learned with a positive parameterization (e.g.,
β = softplus(β̃)). The signed composite implicit function
is then formed as φ(x) = r(x) P (x) + θ(x), and geometry
is extracted from the zero level set {φ = 0}. Surface
normals are computed from the composite field via automatic
differentiation as n(x) = ∇x φ(x)/(∥∇x φ(x)∥2 + ε). A
key practical benefit of residual phase injection is improved
conditioning near the interface: around {φ = 0}, the metric
term becomes small, while the additive phase term provides a

We follow the standard NeuS training pipeline for optimization
and ray sampling, and normalize each scene to a fixed bounding volume. For initialization, we adopt NeuS-style sphere
initialization to obtain a well-conditioned signed field at the
start of optimization: we initialize the phase branch θ (and
thus the composite field φ) to approximate the signed distance
of a coarse sphere, and initialize the metric branch r as
a nonnegative, distance-like field (e.g., roughly proportional
to |θ|) so that training begins with stable zero crossings
and meaningful gradients. When coarse geometric priors are
available (e.g., a visual hull, sparse SfM points, or depthbased initialization), we can initialize θ from the corresponding
coarse implicit surface instead of a sphere. During training,
we jointly optimize the network parameters together with the
NeuS sharpness parameter s (controlling the transition width)
and the phase scale β. To balance the loss terms and avoid
the early collapse of the metric–phase decomposition, we use
a warm-up schedule: in the first Twarm = 50, 000 iterations,
we optimize primarily with Lrgb and near-surface terms and
Leik that directly stabilize the rendered interface (e.g., Lanchor
and Lsurf , and Lmask when masks are available). We then
progressively enable the coupling and free-space regularizers
by ramping λalign and λfar from 0 to their target values. In
practice, we choose λ such that the four groups in Eq. (13)
contribute comparable gradient magnitudes. Optionally, we
treat rendering weights as soft selectors and stop gradients
through wi when used inside regularization losses, preventing
trivial solutions that reduce the penalty by reshaping the
weight distribution.
e) Remarks: In our work, the regularization losses look
similar to the one used by the original MPF literature [10].
However, MPF is designed for surface reconstruction from
known point clouds, while our work focuses on reconstruction
from multi-view images.
In the point cloud reconstruction task, a subset of ground
truth location are known as priors in the form of input point
clouds. This information not only makes the separation of
near surface and far-away points easy, but also makes the
definition of various losses (Lanchor , Lsurface , Lalign , Lfar )
straightforward. In this case, Lanchor and Lsurface act as
supervision losses.
In the multi-view images reconstruction task however, such
ground truth is not available. The location of the surface is
dynamic and can change anytime. This poses a fundamental
distinction between our work and the original MPF. In our
work, we use a simple hypothesis that the surface location in
every iteration of training can be roughly determined from the
rendering weights of each sample along the ray. In our case,
the only supervision losses are Lrgb and the optional Lmask ,
while the other loss terms Lanchor , Lsurface , Lalign , Lfar are
regularization terms.
The two tasks – reconstruction from point clouds and
reconstruction from multi-view images – are related. But in
our case, they are fundamentally different and the adaptation
is therefore nontrivial.

C. Ablation studies
In our method, the regularizers are categorized into two
categories: near-surface regularization and free-space regularization. They reflect the interactions between the metric and
phase branches. We provide ablation studies on these two
types of regularizations. As shown in Figure 10, without nearsurface regularization, the model has difficulties learning thin
structures. Without free-surface regularization, the extracted
mesh is noisy and contains floaters.
1) The effect of near surface regularizers: Near-surface
regularization ensures SDF-like behavior near the surface of
the object which is required for representing the intricate
details of the object. Figure 11 on the left shows the results
of removing individual near-surface regularizers. Without the
regularizations, the metric field and the phase field cannot be
adequately separated and constrained, often leading to suboptimal learning results and consequently cannot accurately
reconstruct the intricate details of the object. We specially
observed that Lsurf , which enforces the Eikonal constraint of
the composite field ϕ and the phase field θ, is critical for the
stability of the surface. As shown in Figure 12, removing Lsurf
completely deteriorates the reconstruction quality.
2) The effect of free space regularizers: The Free-space
regularizers aims to stabilize the neural fields and prevent
floaters. Figure 11 on the right demonstrates the effect of
removing the free-space regularizers Leik and Lfar . We observe
that removing Leik , which encourages the metric field r to
exhibit UDF behavior and abide to the Eikonal constraint
away from the surface, will make the implicit geometry
unstable, introducing floating geometry into the extracted mesh
in Figure 11, even making training completely collapse in the
Lego and Shoe test case. Lfar suppresses large regions of nearzero values. This is important for the implicit geometry field
to accurately represent the geometry as well as for the mesh
extraction method to successfully extract the mesh surface. We

reconstruction of sign-ambiguous geometry. By decoupling
geometry into an unsigned metric field and a continuous
phase field, MPFs maintain the robustness of UDF-like representations for open and thin structures while providing a
stable signed signal for occlusion-aware rendering and wellbehaved gradients near the zero level set, enabling iso-surface

a) Limitations and future directions.: A current limitation of PhaseSurf is its training efficiency. In its present
form, training a single scene typically takes on the order of
10+ hours, which is comparable to NeuS-style SDF-based
volume rendering methods. This cost mainly stems from dense
geometry queries and gradient evaluations along camera rays
using an implicit MLP representation. Improving training efficiency is therefore an important direction for future work. One
promising approach is to incorporate explicit spatial data structures, such as voxel grids or octrees, following frameworks
like Voxurf [39], to accelerate geometry queries and reduce
unnecessary evaluations in empty space. Because a majority
of time is used to calculate the gradients of the implicit field
and calculate the computational graph to the gradient to enable
optimization with respect to the losses, another promising
approach is to adopt numerical gradients instead of analytical
gradients as pioneered by Neuralangelo [15]. We observe that
using numerical gradients can reduce the training time by half
without significantly impacting the results. Another direction
is to adopt multi-resolution hash encodings, as in InstantNGP [40], which have been shown to significantly improve
convergence speed and scalability for neural fields.
As an implicit-field based reconstruction method, our
method performs comparable to NeuS but inferior than Gaussian Splatting methods in novel view synthesis. Table V reports
the quantitative comparison on novel view synthesis using
PSNR and SSIM. This result is expected: our method is
comparable performance to NeuS because both approaches
adopt a similar differentiable volume rendering architecture;
while 3DGS methods explicitly optimize radiance fields by
fitting dense Gaussian primitives to the observed images,
which naturally favors photometric fidelity. In contrast, our
method focuses on learning a well-conditioned geometric field.
Although this design may sacrifice a small amount of rendering accuracy, it leads to significantly more reliable surface
reconstruction. Finally, hybrid representations that combine the
efficient rendering and optimization properties of 3D Gaussian
Splatting [20] with the robust geometric modeling capabilities
of MPFs represent an exciting avenue for future research,
potentially combining the strengths of both paradigms.
