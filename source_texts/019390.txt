Intraoperative optical coherence tomography (iOCT) has emerged as a promising modality for real-time three-dimensional image guidance in retinal surgery, enabling visualization of different retinal layers and tool-tissue interactions with micrometer-scale precision. Indirect viewing systems, which employ additional optical elements to increase the surgical field of view (FOV) of the retina, are widely adopted in vitreoretinal procedures. However, the optical design of these indirect viewing systems introduces nonlinear optical distortions that compromise the spatial accuracy required for absolute positioning, such as might be required for robotic end effectors operating in the surgical FOV. In this paper, we present a computationally efficient method to achieve accurate distortion correction between the distorted optical coherence tomography (OCT) image space and the physical surgical workspace through a combination of optical ray tracing and parametric spline fitting. Our approach leverages physics-based ray tracing to characterize the complex optical transformations introduced by the indirect viewing system, then employs numerical spline interpolation to create a fast mapping function suitable for robotic control applications. The spline-based transformation achieves approximately 14 × faster computation than direct commercial software based optical ray tracing. Additionally, we developed a simplified porcine eye model encompassing the cornea through the crystalline lens for use in ray tracing simulations. Validation on ex vivo porcine eyes demonstrated that the proposed method reduced distortion errors along simulated surgical tool insertion paths. Across 30 OCT volumes from three eyes, the reconstructed length of a 7 mm straight tool path deviated from the open-path ground truth by 498 μm before correction and by 19 μm after correction. Over the same volumes, the correction reduced the residual registration error against the ground truth from 150 μm to 90 μm, and the straightness deviation of the reconstructed path from 226 μm to 137 μm, compared with 109 μm for the ground truth itself.

Optical coherence
tomography (OCT) has been widely applied in retinal surgery - including robotically assisted procedures [1], providing three-dimensional, micron-level resolution images of biological tissues and surgical instruments [2]. Robotic surgical systems offer sub-millimeter positioning accuracy that surpasses the capabilities of human surgeons [3]. The integration of OCT-based image guidance with robotic operation holds significant potential for improving outcomes in ophthalmic surgery. In particular, vitreoretinal surgery encompasses a broad range of procedures, including retinal detachment repair, epiretinal membrane peeling, macular hole repair, and subretinal therapeutic injections. The human retina is a thin, multi-layered sensory tissue lining the posterior inner surface of the eye, with an average thickness of approximately
300μm
[4]. It absorbs photons, initiates photochemical transduction, and relays electrical signals to the brain [5]. Consequently, retinal surgery demands both high-resolution three-dimensional imaging and high-precision surgical manipulation. Although recent studies have demonstrated promising results in autonomous robotic subretinal injection [6–8], a significant challenge remains: the OCT image distortion introduced by indirect viewing systems. Moreover, calibration of robotic surgical instruments relies on low-distortion OCT images and volumes [9–11]. An indirect viewing system, such as the Oculus BIOM or Zeiss Resight, is an optical assembly that enables surgeons to visualize the posterior segment of the eye. While it provides a larger field of view (FOV), it also introduces nonlinear distortions into the acquired images. Compared with the two-dimensional microscope view, the distortion in OCT volumes is more complex, as it extends into three spatial dimensions. This distortion is particularly challenging for robotic systems, which typically rely on absolute spatial linear kinematic transformations, unlike human surgeons who can intuitively compensate for nonlinear spatial distortions. For example, as shown in the right half of Fig. 1, the surgical tool trajectory is straight in the physical workspace but appears curved in the OCT image owing to the optical distortion.

This distortion must similarly be corrected to recover the true surgical tool trajectory.
2.Related work and contributions
Several approaches have been proposed to account for the distortion introduced by indirect viewing systems. One approach adopted in research settings is to perform “open-sky” surgery on ex vivo eyes or phantoms (Fig. 1, left), in which the anterior segment is removed to directly expose the retina to the imaging system [12–15]. However, open-sky surgery eliminates the refractive surfaces and optical components that are present in clinical vitreoretinal procedures and therefore does not account for the optical distortions encountered in intact-eye surgery. To avoid the distortions introduced by the optical apparatus of the eye while maintaining a closed-globe configuration, several researchers have developed instrument-integrated OCT probes that are inserted directly into the eye [6,16], some of which can be maneuvered intraocularly to map the retina [17]. However, compared with galvanometer-based OCT systems, instrument-integrated OCT performs one-dimensional intensity scans, which provides direct positional feedback but limits the retinal mapping rate to the robot’s maneuver speed. Zhang et al. demonstrated OCT-guided robotic subretinal injection with an intact eye and an indirect viewing system. They employed Jacobian matrices to approximate the distortion as a local linear mapping [18]. However, this approach requires recalibration whenever the FOV changes due to adjustments in the OCT reference arm or wide-field lens position.
Several methods have been proposed to correct distortion in OCT images. Bayhaqi et al. developed a pivot-point scanning model for their handheld panretinal OCT system to correct retinal curvature distortion [19]. Kuo et al. presented a spectral-domain OCT correction method for retinal curvature distortion [20]. McNabb et al. employed a whole-eye OCT system to image both the anterior and posterior segments and applied ray tracing for distortion correction [21]. Repka et al. proposed calculating subretinal injection volume without distortion by referencing the physical size of the injection needle within the OCT FOV [22]. Li et al. utilized the voxel pitch to quantitatively measure surgical tool width in the 2D en face view [23]. However, none of these studies addressed surgical tool path correction, which would be required for retinal surgery. Moreover, none of them reported the computation time of the ray tracing or voxel pitch calculation procedures.
To the best of our knowledge, no existing work directly addresses computationally efficient optical distortion correction of surgical tool trajectories via ray tracing for OCT-guided retinal surgery with an indirect viewing system and an intact eye (Fig. 1, right). The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
•
We present a method to correct surgical tool path distortion in OCT volumes by leveraging physics-based ray tracing to model the complex optical transformations introduced by the indirect viewing system.
•
Our method accounts for intraoperative variations in the wide-field lens position and image field position by constructing a precomputed spline model library that eliminates the need for repeated time-consuming ray tracing across different surgical configurations.
•
We develop a simplified porcine eye model for ray tracing and validate it through wet-lab experiments.
•
We validate the proposed approach on ex vivo porcine eyes by inserting custom flat calibration targets with distance markers to simulate surgical tool insertion paths. The correction performance is evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA) of straightness, iterative closest point (ICP) of overall fitness, marker sphere fitting errors, and inter-marker distance measurements with and without distortion correction.
3.Methods3.1.System setup
The system employed a 400 kHz swept-source OCT laser with an adjustable reference arm path length via a retro-reflector on a linear motorized stage (Fig. 2(a)). The sample arm was modified from a Leica Enfocus microscope integrated OCT scanner (Leica Microsystems Inc) for 1050 nm operation with upgraded galvanometers (Saturn 5, ScannerMax Inc), attached to a Leica M844 surgical microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc) equipped with an Oculus BIOM 5 indirect viewing system (Oculus Surgical Inc). The scan pattern was a 15 mm × 15 mm × 7.77 mm volume with 750 × 750 × 950 pixels along the C-scan, B-scan, and A-scan directions in telecentric scanning mode. Ray tracing was performed in Zemax OpticStudio (Ansys Inc) using ZOSpy [24] with the optical model of the indirect viewing system and a custom-developed porcine eye model. Zemax computations were executed on a Windows Server 2016 machine (Intel Core i9-7920X, 128 GB RAM). Other processing was performed on an Ubuntu 20.04 laptop (Intel Core i7-10750H, 15.4 GB RAM). Neither the Zemax ray tracing nor the spline model evaluation used GPU acceleration, and all reported computation times are single-threaded CPU times.

3.2.Distortion analysis for retinal surgeries
In open-sky retinal surgery, the telecentric scanner’s galvanometer pivot point is located at the focal point of the objective lens, and the OCT beam directly reaches the retina, producing a nearly flat image plane with minimal distortion. However, for intact-eye surgery, much of the telecentric beam is blocked by the iris, resulting in a very limited FOV. The indirect viewing system addresses this by introducing a correction lens and a wide-field lens (Fig. 2(b)) that enable the beam to pass through the pupil and reach the retina. As shown in Fig. 2(c), this configuration creates a scanning geometry in which the beam pivots anterior to the crystalline lens, introducing optical path deviations that produce distorted OCT images of surgical instruments.

3.4.OCT bottom image field optimization
The OCT volume is bounded along the A-scan (depth) direction by two surfaces: the bottom image field and the top image field (Fig. 3), which correspond to the lower and upper boundaries of the volume, respectively. Throughout this work, a geometric path length (GPL) denotes a physical distance measured along the beam path, without weighting by the refractive index of the medium, in contrast to an optical path length (OPL), which is the corresponding distance weighted by the refractive index. As shown in Fig. 2(a), (b), and (c), both the adjustable reference arm geometric path length
(GPLr)
and the geometric path length between the wide-field lens and the correction lens
(GPLivs)
can change during surgery. Although the distance between the wide-field lens and the cornea is a free parameter that can also vary, this distance is difficult to measure in real time during surgery. To constrain this distance, we placed a spacer lens (central thickness: 2.740 mm) in contact with both the wide-field lens and the cornea. The spacer lens was subsequently removed while preserving the established spacing during OCT data acquisition. The value of
GPLivs
was read directly from the graduated scale on the focusing mechanism of the indirect viewing system (labeled in Fig. 2(b)), which requires no additional instrumentation in the surgical field. The two quantities were established in a fixed order: the spacer lens was placed first to set the wide-field lens to cornea spacing, and
GPLivs
was then read from the scale and recorded; whenever
GPLivs
was subsequently adjusted, the spacer lens was reinserted to re-establish the same spacing before the new value of
GPLivs
was read and recorded. A similar measurement approach has been applied to in vivo human imaging in the operating room [23], indicating that it is practical and sufficiently stable in a surgical setting. Changes in
GPLivs
shift the focal position within the posterior segment of the eye. Changes in
GPLr

3.5.Ray tracing
With the optimized bottom image field position and curvature, we sampled 50×50 galvo configurations uniformly across the 15 mm × 15 mm OCT volume FOV along the C-scan and B-scan directions. For each galvo configuration, the Zemax ray tracing engine computed the ray refraction angle and propagation speed at each optical surface in the OCT sample arm and the porcine eye model. The Zemax Merit Function Editor RAGX, RAGY, and RAGZ operands were used to compute the ray positions on the posterior lens surface and the bottom image field. The ray direction was calculated as the vector from the posterior lens ray position to the bottom image field ray position. Since the OCT A-scan image depth is known
(7.7/1.344≈5.729mm)
, the top image field was determined by extending the reversed ray direction unit vector, scaled by the image depth, from the bottom image field ray position. A ray tracing result
(GPLivs=6.266mm,GPLpos=13.102mm)
is shown in Fig. 3. Based on these ray tracing results, the mapping between the OCT image space and the physical surgical workspace was established to correct distortion in OCT images and volumes.
3.6.Spline fitting for fast distortion correction
Directly performing ray tracing in Zemax OpticStudio is computationally expensive and cannot meet the real-time demands of dynamic intraoperative retinal surgery. We adopt OpticStudio as the timing baseline because it is the package used to build and validate the sequential optical model of the indirect viewing system and the porcine eye, and therefore represents the practical status quo for this workflow rather than the fastest achievable ray tracer. To address these limitations, we proposed a spline fitting method to rapidly compute the OCT bottom and top image fields. Using the 50×50 galvo configurations and the corresponding ray tracing results from Sec. 3.5, we fit third-order bivariate splines in the x, y, and z directions for the bottom and top image fields separately, yielding a total of six spline models. A degree-k spline is
Ck−1
continuous [30], so third order is the lowest order for which the fitted image field has continuous curvature. Continuous curvature is required here because the bottom image field is modeled as a curved surface of radius
Ri
, and because the ray direction is recovered from the difference between the fitted top and bottom image fields. Lower orders are only
C0
or
C1
and cannot represent that curvature continuously, whereas higher orders reduce the approximation error, which scales as
O(hk+1)
for grid spacing h, only to levels below the imaging resolution while increasing the per-point evaluation cost, which scales as
(k+1)2
. Third order is therefore the lowest-cost order satisfying the continuity requirement, and is also the default interpolating spline degree in the fitting library used. Based on the wet-lab data collected, we determined the range of
GPLivs
and
GPLpos
values. We constructed a spline model library covering different
GPLivs
and
GPLpos
values within the observed range at 0.1 mm increments for each variable. A spacing of 0.1 mm was selected as a practical balance between interpolation accuracy and library size. This precomputed spline model library can be used across different surgical configurations without repeating time-consuming ray tracing. To evaluate the spline interpolation accuracy, we randomly sampled 100 galvo configurations and computed the interpolation error of each spline model against the OpticStudio ray-tracing ground truth. We also compared the OpticStudio ray-tracing time with the spline interpolation time per A-scan.
4.Experiments and data processing4.1.Wet lab experiments
We used three ex vivo porcine eyes within 3 hours of procurement from an abattoir. For each eye, a simulated human vitrectomy was performed by a vitreoretinal surgeon to replicate standard surgical technique. A three-port pars plana vitrectomy was performed using a 25-gauge vitrectomy system (Alcon Inc). A 3D-printed (Form 3, Formlabs Inc) flat calibration target with hemispherical distance markers was used to simulate a surgical tool insertion path in the eye (Fig. 4). The distance markers were 0.49 mm radius hemispheres with 1.0 mm center-to-center spacing along one direction. Prior to target insertion for each eye, we captured one OCT volume of the target in telecentric scanning mode to obtain the ground truth point cloud. This ground truth volume was acquired with the target held in free space in front of the objective lens, with neither the indirect viewing system nor the porcine eye present in the beam path. The ground truth measurement is therefore a completely open-path scan of the calibration target that traverses no ocular tissue and no element of the indirect viewing system, and is consequently free of both sources of distortion addressed in this work.

Microscope camera view of an ex vivo porcine eye with the calibration target simulating a tool insertion path. The target insertion video is provided in

We used a measured open-path scan of each individual target, rather than the nominal computer-aided design (CAD) values of the 3D-printed part, as the ground truth reference. Although the design geometry is known a priori, the hemispherical markers are small relative to normal 3D printing, and each printed target is post-processed by isopropyl alcohol washing followed by thermal and ultraviolet curing, which introduces part-to-part dimensional variation. The CAD values therefore describe the design intent rather than the as-built geometry of the specific target being imaged. Scanning each target in the open path recovers its true dimensions and simultaneously serves as a quality-control check that no gross printing error is present. Referencing the corrected point clouds to a measurement of the same physical object thus removes 3D-printing tolerance as a confounding source of error.
The target was then inserted into the porcine eye through a small scleral incision. After insertion, we captured OCT volumes with the indirect viewing system (Fig. 4). The value of
GPLivs
was recorded at the beginning of the first OCT volume acquisition for each eye. After each volume acquisition, the target was repositioned and reoriented randomly within the microscope FOV using forceps. The value of
ΔGPLr
was recorded for each volume acquisition. A total of 8–11 OCT volumes were acquired per eye, yielding 30 volumes across all three eyes.
4.2.Point cloud processing
All OCT data were saved in the spatial domain. For each OCT volume, only the maximum intensity pixel along each A-scan was retained to generate a 3D point cloud. Because the OCT scan center may not be precisely aligned with the center of the eye and the wide-field lens, a point cloud re-centering procedure was implemented to register the acquired OCT volume with the optical model scan coordinates in Zemax OpticStudio. In OCT summed voxel projection (SVP), the boundary of the iris or the wide-field lens was visible. We manually selected 3 boundary points and fitted a circle to these points. The center of the fitted circle defined the central galvo scan position. The contribution of this step to the correction performance is reported in Sec. 5.
After re-centering, we manually cropped the point cloud to retain only the points on the target using the Open3D library point cloud editing tools [31]. We selected 8 consecutive hemispherical markers closest to the edge of the C-scan and B-scan FOV to evaluate distortion correction performance. The edges of the C-scan and B-scan FOV exhibit the most significant distortion, making the correction performance at these locations more critical for robotic surgical instrument calibration and control. In current microscope-integrated OCT systems [32] used for human retinal surgery, a common scan protocol is 7 mm × 7 mm along the C-scan and B-scan directions. The distance between the centers of the first and last hemispheres was 7.0 mm in the physical world, which is comparable to the FOV in [32] along one direction.
4.3.Distortion correction and performance evaluation
For the uncorrected point cloud, we directly rescaled the point cloud to millimeter coordinates based on the telecentric scanning mode scan lengths. The A-scan direction length was divided by the balanced saline solution group refractive index (1.344). For the ground truth point cloud, the target was scanned in the open path with no intervening medium, so the A-scan direction length was scaled using the refractive index of air instead. For each OCT volume, using the recorded
GPLivs
and computed
GPLpos
, we selected the nearest spline model from the library. For every point in the uncorrected point cloud (Fig. 5(a)), the ray direction and bottom image field position were computed from the spline model based on the corresponding galvo configuration. The spline model fitting error compared with Zemax OpticStudio ray tracing is reported in Sec. 5. We also recorded the spline model search and load times compared with the Zemax OpticStudio ray tracing time in Sec. 5. Based on each point’s location along the A-scan direction, we calculated its physical-world position using the ray direction, top image field, and bottom image field positions. The corrected point cloud is shown in Fig. 5(b).

Three metrics were used to evaluate distortion correction performance.
4.3.1.Straightness evaluation
PCA was applied to the uncorrected, corrected, and ground truth point clouds. The standard deviation along the third principal direction was then used to compare straightness among the three types of point clouds. Example PCA results are shown as arrows in Fig. 5(a)(b)(c). The third principal direction corresponds to the blue arrow.
4.3.2.Overall fitness evaluation
The ICP algorithm [33] was used to evaluate the overall geometric similarity between each point cloud and the ground truth. Either the uncorrected or corrected point cloud was designated as the source, and the ground truth point cloud served as the target. All point clouds were first zero-centered by subtracting their respective means. The initial alignment was established by matching the first and second principal directions of the two point clouds. To resolve PCA sign ambiguity, all 4 sign combinations of the first two principal directions were evaluated. The third principal direction was computed via the cross product to enforce the right-hand rule. We used the Open3D [31] implementation of the ICP algorithm with point-to-point distance and a maximum of 200 iterations. The maximum correspondence distance was set to 1.5 mm to exclude outlier points. The relative fitness and relative root mean square error (RMSE) convergence thresholds were both set to
10−7
. The lowest RMSE among the 4 sign combinations was recorded as the final ICP result for each point cloud. Example ICP registration results are shown in Fig. 5(d)(e).
4.3.3.Sphere fitting and sphere center distance evaluation
We computed the distance between the centers of the first and last hemispheres for the uncorrected point cloud, corrected point cloud, and ground truth point cloud, and compared the distances across all three. The first and last hemispheres span the longest baseline among the eight selected markers, so that any residual error accumulates over the full tool path and the distance error reported here is the largest obtainable from this target. Using Open3D [31], we further manually cropped the point cloud to isolate the first and last hemisphere points. Because this crop retains a single region containing both hemispheres, k-means clustering [34] with a known cluster count of two was then applied to separate the first and last hemisphere points. Within each cluster, points whose distance to the cluster centroid exceeded the mean distance by more than three standard deviations were discarded, which removes the residual stray points that the manual crop, being a coarse region selection, could not exclude. Since the ground truth sphere radius was known (0.49 mm), we used the least-squares method to determine the centers of the two hemispheres in the point cloud, and then computed the distance between the two centers. 4.3.4.Statistical testing
To statistically evaluate the distortion correction performance, one-sample t-tests were used to compare the before- and after-correction results with the mean of the ground truth measurements. The one-sample formulation was adopted because the ground truth sample size (n = 3) was insufficient to support a two-sample test. Each test was performed at two levels of analysis. At the pooled level, each OCT volume contributes one observation (n = 30; for the sphere fitting error each volume contributes its two hemisphere clusters, giving n = 60). At the eye level, the values are first averaged within each eye, yielding three independent observations (n = 3). Because the 30 volumes were acquired from only three eyes, volumes from the same eye share that eye’s anatomy and imaging configuration, so pooled observations are not fully independent; averaging within each eye removes this dependence. It also allows the variability between eyes to be compared with that within an eye, which we quantify by decomposing each metric into between-eye and within-eye standard deviations using a one-way analysis of variance across the three eyes. To additionally assess practical equivalence between the corrected results and the ground truth, two one-sided t-tests (TOST) were performed. TOST was applied only to those after-correction results for which the one-sample t-test indicated a significant difference from the ground truth, since an equivalence test is informative only when a difference has been detected. The equivalence margin was set to the diagonal of the posterior imaging resolution cell, computed as the Euclidean combination of the lateral Airy disk diameter (

5.2.Distortion correction performance
Visual inspection of Fig. 5 provides a qualitative assessment of the distortion correction performance. For the straightness evaluation, the corrected point cloud (Fig. 5(b)) appeared straighter than the uncorrected point cloud (Fig. 5(a)) and more closely resembled the ground truth (Fig. 5(c)). For the overall fitness evaluation, the corrected point cloud (Fig. 5(e)) exhibited better ICP registration than the uncorrected point cloud (Fig. 5(d)), as it was straighter and more closely matched the ground truth geometry. The corresponding quantitative results are summarized in the box plots of Fig. 6. Figure 6(a) shows that the third principal direction standard deviation for the corrected point cloud was lower than that of the uncorrected point cloud and close to the ground truth in both mean and median, indicating improved straightness after correction. Figure 6(b) confirms that the ICP root mean squared error (RMSE) between the corrected point cloud and the ground truth was lower than that between the uncorrected point cloud and the ground truth. For the sphere fitting and center distance evaluation, the corrected point cloud exhibited lower sphere fitting error (Fig. 6(c)), as defined in Eq. (6). The distance between the first and last hemisphere centers for the corrected point cloud (Fig. 6(d)) was closer to the ground truth value than that of the uncorrected point cloud.

The PC3 standard deviation is thus the one metric for which equivalence could not be established, and its residual difference can be attributed to differences in the data acquisition conditions between the correction and ground truth experiments. Specifically, the correction data were acquired in an intact eye configuration (Fig. 1, right), in which the imaging beam must traverse the correction lens, the wide-field lens, the porcine cornea, and the porcine crystalline lens. Reflection and scattering at these optical interfaces attenuate the signal both on its path toward the simulated surgical tool target and on its return to the detector. In contrast, the ground truth data were acquired in the open-path telecentric scanning mode described in Section 4.1, in which the target is scanned in free space and neither the indirect viewing system nor any ocular tissue lies in the beam path. The attenuation described above is therefore eliminated in the ground truth acquisition rather than merely reduced. Consequently, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) achieved in the telecentric mode inherently exceeded that achieved in the indirect viewing mode. The post-correction point clouds acquired in the indirect viewing mode were therefore noisier, and the third principal direction standard deviation, being a direct measure of the scatter of the points about the fitted tool path, absorbs that noise most directly of the four metrics. The sphere center distance, by contrast, remained robust to this noise because the least-squares estimation of the sphere center is insensitive to zero-mean noise distributed symmetrically across the fitted surface.

The mismatch between the individual eye anatomy and the population-averaged model introduces an eye-specific systematic bias, which the variance decomposition above measures directly as the growth of the between-eye component after correction.

6.Discussion6.1.Clinical significance of the residual post-correction error
We interpret the residual distortion correction errors relative to two clinically meaningful benchmarks: the average retinal thickness
(∼300μm)
[4] and the diagonal of the posterior imaging resolution cell
(33.38μm)
. For the straightness evaluation, the corrected third principal direction standard deviation was 0.137 mm, compared with 0.109 mm for the ground truth, a difference of only 0.028 mm
(28μm)
– smaller than one posterior imaging resolution cell
(33.38μm)
. Clinically, when a robotic or human surgeon moves a tool along a straight path in the posterior segment, the residual straightness deviation after correction is smaller than a single posterior resolution cell, providing reliable spatial image feedback during surgery. For the overall fitness evaluation, the residual post-correction ICP RMSE
(∼90μm)
along the 7 mm straight tool path is well below the average retinal thickness
(∼300μm)
, indicating that the overall geometric deviation after correction is smaller than the targeted tissue thickness. This benchmark alone, however, does not demonstrate the benefit of the correction for this particular metric, because the uncorrected ICP RMSE
(0.150±0.020mm)
also falls below the retinal thickness across all 30 volumes; the correction reduces this residual by
40%
, but both point clouds sit on the same side of that threshold. For the sphere fitting and sphere center distance evaluation, the corrected mean distance between the first and last hemisphere centers was 6.992 mm, compared with the 7.010 mm ground truth over the 7 mm tool path – a residual error of approximately
19μm
, far below the average retinal thickness
(∼300μm)
. In contrast, the uncorrected mean distance (6.513 mm) deviated from the ground truth by approximately
498μm
, exceeding the retinal thickness. The absolute distance along the tool path is therefore the metric on which the retinal thickness benchmark does discriminate: distortion correction reduces absolute positioning error from above to well below a clinically relevant scale.
Retinal thickness sets the scale of the targeted tissue, but not the positioning tolerance of a robotic maneuver. A more demanding reference is the accuracy reported for OCT-guided robotic navigation performed in the same setting, namely an intact ex vivo porcine eye viewed through an indirect wide-field lens. For autonomous needle navigation toward a user-defined subretinal target, Zhang et al. reported mean errors of
55.2μm
,
72.0μm
, and
23.8μm
along the X, Y, and Z axes of their 6 mm × 6 mm × 3.38 mm scan volume, corresponding to a mean error vector of magnitude
93.8μm
[18]. The residual error of the corrected tool path measured here,
19μm
over a 7 mm path, is smaller than that value, whereas the uncorrected error,
498μm
, exceeds it by a factor of five. Left uncorrected, the distortion introduced by the indirect viewing system would therefore dominate the error budget of such a robotic system, whereas after correction it contributes less than the reported navigation error itself. The two quantities are not measured identically: Zhang et al. report the error of a robot reaching a target defined within the OCT image space and convert it to metric units using the nominal scan dimensions, whereas the present residual is an absolute physical length referred to an independent open-path ground truth. That distinction reinforces rather than weakens the comparison, since the metric scale of the image space is itself undetermined without an optical model of the indirect viewing system [18]; ray traced correction supplies that mapping, which is a prerequisite for absolute positioning in OCT-guided robotic retinal surgery.
6.2.Porcine eye model
A simplified porcine eye model is one of the contributions of this work. The previous distortion-correction study [23] had employed human eye models even when their wet-lab experiments were performed on ex vivo porcine eyes. Because the porcine crystalline lens is substantially thicker than the human lens, applying a human eye model to porcine data would introduce larger errors in the estimated posterior image plane position and in the ray tracing, degrading correction accuracy. To quantify this, we re-corrected the same 30 volumes with a human eye model [28]. The porcine eye model outperformed the human eye model in all four evaluation tests; the largest difference was in the sphere center distance, where the residual error against the ground truth was
19μm
for the porcine model and
285μm
for the human model. The complete comparison, including box plots for all four evaluation metrics, is reported in
Supplement 1 (Fig. S1 and Table S4). A sensitivity check in which the same volumes were re-corrected with porcine eye models whose anterior segment was set to the thin and the thick extreme of the literature-reported range [25,26,35,36] further suggests that the anterior segment thickness – the cornea, the anterior chamber and the crystalline lens taken together, rather than the lens thickness alone – might be the parameter that most changes the reconstructed sphere center distance between individual eyes: the residual error against the ground truth grew from
19μm
for the proposed model to
351μm
at the thin extreme and
444μm
at the thick extreme, while the straightness and sphere fitting metrics moved by only a few micrometers. The complete sensitivity analysis, including the parameters of the two bracketing eye models, box plots for all four evaluation metrics and the corresponding statistics, is reported in
Supplement 1 (Fig. S3 and Tables S6–S9). A further check in which the meridional term of the gradient index profile was restored, so that the modeled index varies laterally rather than only with depth as in the θ = 0 simplification of Eq. (2), left the straightness, registration and sphere fitting residuals essentially unchanged while moving the reconstructed sphere center distance further from the ground truth, again suggesting that this metric is governed by the mismatch between the population-averaged model and the individual eyes rather than by the gradient index description itself.
6.3.Toward automated intraoperative implementation
Several processing steps, in particular point cloud cropping, were performed manually. These manual steps were used only for proof-of-concept performance evaluation and are not required components of the distortion correction itself. The correction maps any input point from image space to physical space without manual intervention. In a real surgical setting, we will utilize automatic surgical tool detection [11] to extract the tool point cloud in place of manual cropping, enabling automated intraoperative implementation.
6.4.Toward real-time robotic surgery
Real-time robotic control was not demonstrated in this study. Nevertheless, the correction cost can be placed in the context of a current microscope-integrated OCT system, which acquires a 7 mm × 7 mm posterior volume at 340×80 A-scans at 7.7 Hz, that is, 129.6 ms of acquisition per volume [32]. Because a robot cannot be supplied with a more recent tool tip pose than the volume stream provides, the correction is applied once each volume has been acquired, so its cost is added to the 129.6 ms acquisition time rather than absorbed by it. For robotic guidance, the correction is applied to the segmented surgical tool tip point cloud, whose physical coordinates are the quantity the robot consumes, and recovering a rigid tool tip pose requires only three non-collinear points. Correcting three points typically costs 2.825 ms on a single CPU thread without GPU acceleration, comprising the spline model search (1169.767 μs), the model load (1653.567 μs), and three per-point corrections
(3×0.433μs)
, which adds only
2.2%
to the OCT acquisition time. Correcting the same three points by direct Zemax ray tracing would instead cost 119.782 ms, adding
92.4%
and thus nearly doubling the interval between the start of a volume acquisition and the delivery of a corrected tool tip pose. Expressed as an effective update rate, the precomputed spline model library reduces the 7.7 Hz volume rate only to 7.5 Hz, whereas direct ray tracing would reduce it to 4.0 Hz. The memory required is likewise small: while the optical configuration is unchanged, a single spline model suffices, occupying 256.97 kiB on disk and at most 352.34 kiB of RAM during loading, which is negligible relative to the 15.4 GB available on the processing laptop. A fully distortion-corrected volume, by contrast, serves human visualization rather than robotic guidance; it is a substantially larger per-voxel workload that we would address with GPU acceleration, and it lies outside the scope of the present work.
6.5.Future work
This study was validated on ex vivo porcine eyes, and its applicability to human eyes and common clinical scenarios remains to be established. Future studies will evaluate human-specific eye models and account for clinical conditions that alter the eye’s optics, such as pseudophakia (an eye in which the natural crystalline lens has been replaced by an artificial intraocular lens), aphakia (an eye from which the crystalline lens has been removed without replacement), silicone oil or gas tamponade (materials used to fill the vitreous cavity during retinal surgery), and patient-specific anatomical variation. Each factor changes the optical model of the eye – the number of refractive surfaces and layers, the surface curvatures, the thicknesses, and the refractive indices – and therefore affects distortion correction performance. In addition, the OCT scan center was selected manually in this study. Future work will automate scan-center localization by detecting the iris in the OCT summed voxel projection.
7.Conclusions
In this paper, we presented a fast ray-tracing-based distortion correction method for OCT volumes acquired through an indirect viewing system during retinal surgery. The proposed precomputed spline model library achieved approximately
14.14×
faster computation than direct Zemax ray tracing, laying the foundation for potential real-time distortion correction suitable for surgical applications, particularly robotic retinal surgery. We also developed a simplified porcine eye model encompassing the cornea through the crystalline lens for use in the ray tracing framework. Experimental validation on ex vivo porcine eyes demonstrated that the proposed method significantly reduced distortion along simulated surgical tool insertion paths and improved the overall geometric correspondence between OCT point clouds and ground truth measurements. The proposed approach has the potential to enhance spatial accuracy in OCT-guided retinal surgery performed with an indirect viewing system on intact eyes.
