ABSTRACT
This thesis reviews several key concepts and equations which are vital to the
creation of a physically credible and realistic smoke animation. The main
contributions of this thesis include the reorganization of the scene by distance to
permit the proper interaction between a translucent gas and its surrounding
environment, the incorporation of a hashtable to minimize the storage needed for
the smoke’s density, and an API for future developers looking to create a smoke
simulation.

CHAPTER I
INTRODUCTION
Creating natural, controllable, and efficient computer generated smoke has
become a challenging goal in the area of computer graphics. Such effects are of
great use in diverse areas that include video games, computer animation, and
special effects in live action cinema. Smoke can provide a location with a sense of
mystery and ambiance or it can be used as an effect to draw the audience into a
narrative such as the scene from “The Fellowship of the Rings” where Gandalf and
Bilbo practice their smoke rings.
Unfortunately, creating such effects can often become complicated and time
consuming. This is due to the smoke’s very natural yet complex movements and its
dynamic interactions with the surrounding environment. In this thesis, I create a
method that will produce believable and compelling smoke formations in an efficient
and intuitive manner.
The following pages describe the history of computer generated smoke and the
related works that have influenced its development over time. The methods and
techniques used to produce smoke effects have evolved greatly over the past decade.
The first smoke created by a computer was a simple cloud of simulated gas that the
animator had little control over. Over time, smoke producing methods created much
more believable effects and have been able to produce intricate sculptural
formations composed entirely of smoke.
The underlying principles and concepts behind the formation and actions of
smoke are also discussed within this thesis. Several key concepts and equations are
reviewed and explained. These concepts are vital in producing realistic, physically
credible smoke animations.
The rest of this paper will describe the outcome of the thesis. These results are
outlined as follows: 1) The creation of a smoke flow simulation using proved stable
methods which employs an efficient data structure. This data structure preserves
1
storage space within the simulation. 2) The organizion of the smoke flow simulation
to ensure that the program is rendered in realtime. 3) The construction of an API
which will allow the user to quickly create any desired smoke flow.

CHAPTER II
RELATED WORK
There have been many methods proposed in the production of smoke effects.
These methods have evolved as technologies and abilities increased over time.
Previous contributions have been influenced through both the scientific and
engineering literature which represents smoke’s realistic capabilities as well as the
sometimes fantastic desires of those producing the effects. Each new smoke effect
has built off of the successes of its predecessors and such is the case with this thesis.
The best results toward creating realistic smoke have come from those efforts
which sought to replicate the physical nature of smoke. Among the very first to
begin doing this were Kajiya and Von Herzen [10]. Their work involved the ray
tracing of clouds but it was their physically accurate attempts to model the
dynamics of clouds that allowed their work to influence other models.
Due to smoke’s properties as a fluid, advances in other areas of elemental
reproduction such as fire or water have proven indirectly beneficial. Such is the case
with the early works of Foster and Metaxas who sought to render the effects of
liquid flow.
2.1 Realistic Animation of Liquids
In their paper [6], the authors, Foster and Metaxas, sought to model the motion
and effects of a liquid animation realistically in both two and three dimensions. To
do this, the authors solve the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe the motion of
a fluid at any point within a flow. The method that the authors provide was able to
accomplish behavior that was more complex than anything else available at the
time. The simulation is able to create waves, spray, foam, ripple effects and
droplets. The authors are also able to model foreign objects such as floating coke
cans within the water simulation.
The simulation is set up on a rectangular grid which is aligned with the
3
Cartesian grid. The cells are then labeled i, j, k which are in exact relation to the x,
y, and z directions. Velocity is measured at the centers of every cell face on the
rectangular grid and is marked as u, v, w. Each cell is categorized by its contents.
A cell may be Solid if it contains an obstacle or object that is not fluid, it may be
Full of fluid, it may be a Surface cell which indicates the outline or top of the fluid,
or it could be Empty.
One of the ways that the authors simplified the simulation was through reducing
the environment’s complexity and using boundary conditions. Boundaries are areas
where the fluid borders an obstacle or the atmosphere and where the fluid can enter
or exit the system. When the contents of each cell have been determined, the
boundary conditions are set automatically. Once they have been set, they
approximate the locations of surfaces or obstacles and the Navier-Stokes equations
can be applied easily. They are also checked once at the beginning of an iteration
and provide for cheap computation but without compromising realism or accuracy.
In order to produce a smooth and realistic fluid flow from the grid mesh, the
surface of the fluid must be tracked. This will allow the simulation to update the
grid cell’s status (Empty, Full, etc.) as well as produce an accurate depiction of a
fluid flow. Surface tracking is accomplished using three methods: Marker Particles,
Free Surface Particles, and the Height Field.
Marker particles are massless particles that posses the same velocity as the fluid
around them. Marker particles aren’t actually a part of the fluid, they are just the
guidelines to indicate the location of a fluid at a certain point during the simulation.
Using marker particles is the easiest way to track fluid position in 2D simulations.
It is also an ideal way to animate splashy or frothy waves because the particles
define the exact position of the fluid. Because marker particles are influenced by the
velocity of the fluid around them, they can flow freely and can enter or exit a
simulation where the fluid enters or exits.
A marker particle’s position is determined by taking the velocities of the four
nearest cells and applying an area weighting interpolation to them. The marker
4
particle will be affected the most by the greatest and closest velocity of its
neighbors. The resulting velocity is then multiplied by the timestep. The grid mesh
is then labeled as either being Empty, Surface or Full. These labels are meant to
indicate the areas which are empty of fluid, the surface of a fluid, and the body of
the fluid. The labels are determined in the following way: a cell with no marker
particles is labeled Empty, a cell with a particle that is next to an Empty cell is
labeled as Surface, and finally, a cell which contains a particle and is surrounded by
other cells with marker particles is Full.
Free surface particles are similar to marker particles except that they indicate
the border of the atmosphere with the fluid. These particles also use the local
velocity, but they are different in that the number, the spacing and connectivity of
the particles can change as the surface changes. This means that the free surface
particles have to be removed or introduced to the system. This happens when two
particles become too close together or too far apart. When cells get too close
together they are both deleted and their neighbors become connected to each other.
The cells of the grid mesh are then labeled by an algorithm that connects regions
together. For example, all the cells that contain a surface marker are set to Full and
the other cells are all labeled as Unknown. Then the algorithm picks an area that is
and will be empty of particles no matter what. This cell is labeled Empty and all of
its neighbors that are marked Unknown are labeled Empty as well. Then the
Unknown cells that are neighboring Full cells are labeled Full. Finally, the cells
containing the original surface markers are labeled Surface. In other words, the
algorithm finds the surface first, fills in the atmosphere next and then fills in the
body of the fluid contained by the Surface.
2.2 Controlling Fluid Animation
A different method can be used in situations where detailed surface tracking, as
described above, is not necessary for creating a fluid flow. This is possible when no
dramatic waves or violent splashes occur in the fluid flow. In instances such as this,
5
the surface is instead approximated by taking the height of a body of water. In this
method, cells crossing the height field are considered Surface cells, the cells above
are Empty and the ones below are labeled as Full. Using these methods to produce
fluid flows, Foster and Metaxas released a follow up paper [7] which set out to
produce a simple, intuitive method for allowing animators to control a fluid
simulation. This means that the animator no longer needs to know the underlying
methods or ideas of the fluid simulator but will still be able to produce beautiful,
physically accurate fluid flows. In previous methods, the fluid flows were simulations
and the animator had to settle for what the simulation happened to produce, but
now the animator has some control over the desired results.
A controller is a procedure that takes a function from the animator and then
correctly uses that function on the Navier-Stokes calculations to influence the
outcome of an animation. It bridges the gap between the underlying mechanisms of
the simulation that are concerned with the logistics of the fluid flow and the
animator who may or may not care about the logistics of the simulation but instead
is worried about the aesthetic properties of the animation. A control mechanism is
made up of the set of controllers and a set of user-defined functions. The functions
are simply ways of implementing a controller in the overall program. The animator
must write the function him or herself, specifying which controller must be
implemented and what that controller should do during the animation.
There are six different controllers that the authors break up into three different
categories. Each category describes the level of detail each controller can provide.
These categories are labeled initial value, boundary level controllers, and higher
level controllers. The initial value controllers are those that are very basic low level
controllers and they produce very natural looking flows. This level of control is not
much greater than the previous method proposed by the authors. A controller at
this level sets up the fluid’s basic properties. The next level of control is the
boundary level. This level still produces very realistic and natural looking waves,
but the influence of the animator over the flow is greater. The controllers at this
6
level are the external pressure controller and the surface tension controller. The
next level of control is the higher level in which the controllers are ideal for creating
explosions or animating objects moving within the fluid. Examples of controllers at
this level are the internal pressure controller, the moving boundary property
controller and the velocity controller.
The basic property controller can set density, viscosity, body forces and obstacle
friction. This controller can produce effects like hardening of the fluid, melting and
mixing of different fluids with different properties.
The external pressure controller can control the direction that the fluid’s surface
flows. This is done by controlling the pressure of the Surface cells in the grid mesh.
Fluid will flow from regions of high pressure to regions of lower pressure. By using
this rule the surface can be moved or influenced accordingly. For example, if an
animator wanted to adjust the spray of a geyser, he can get a tighter flume by using
more atmospheric pressure.
The surface tension controller affects how cohesive the surface of the liquid is to
its neighboring particles. This can cause small amounts of water to come together
to form droplets or globs depending on the fluid being modeled. The surface tension
controller allows liquids to form a sheeting effect when flowing around or off of
edges, creating a cascading waterfall effect. The surface tension controller works by
creating a small negative pressure in each cell labeled Surface on the grid/mesh.
This creates a bonding between the Surface cells and more or less surface tension
depending on the specifications of the animator.
The internal pressure controller is a higher level controller. It is capable of
creating interesting effects such as explosions, splashes and air bubbles. This
controller is similar to the external pressure controller in that it can direct fluid flow.
However, it is different in that it adds internal pressure to already existing pressures.
The moving boundaries controller is used when there is an object floating or
moving through the fluid. It prevents the simulated fluid from flowing through the
object and creates a realistic interaction between the object and the fluid. This is
7
accomplished by adjusting the pressure of cells that are part of the boundary and
marking cells that represent the floating object as an Obstacle.
The velocity field controller is the controller that can have the greatest affect
over the fluid. The controller takes a list of different areas where the fluid’s velocity
will be affected and the specified velocities of those areas. It then sets those areas to
the prescribed velocity.
2.3 Modeling the Motion of a Hot, Turbulent Gas
Having contributed to the field of liquid simulation, Foster and Metaxas then
translated their work toward gaseous flow [8]. Their work was among the first in
terms of proposing a physics-based smoke or gas animation. Many other methods at
that time required the animator to control every single element of the smoke to the
point that even wind field parameters had to be set up by the animator. This was
tedious and unreliable when it came to recreating the natural motion of smoke.
Instead, the method proposed was able to give smoke a believable churning and
floating quality by taking into account the physics of realistic smoke. This motion is
influenced by such physical properties as vorticity, thermal buoyancy, turbulence,
convection and drag.
There are several guiding principles that will determine the motion and actions
of smoke and gas. For example, smoke will always experience some kind of motion
due to diffusion, which is caused by the motion of its molecules. The way smoke
acts is also partially dependent on its velocity as it moves through the air around it.
Smoke particles move through the process of convection and are pushed along by
their neighbors. Smoke is usually drawn into areas where there is low pressure or, in
other words, the smoke flow will follow the areas around it with the greatest
velocity. This is called vorticity or drag. As it moves through the surrounding
atmosphere, the smoke will begin to mix itself with the air, however, the air can
sometimes be much slower than the smoke. This causes a drag effect to act on the
smoke and will produce a swirling quality.
8
The temperature of the smoke also has a big effect on how it will act. Heat rises,
and an effect called thermal buoyancy takes place. The hotter parts of the smoke
will rise faster than the parts that have already begun mixing with the cooler air.
This also causes drag as well as rotation. In addition to reacting to the atmosphere,
smoke will also be affected by solid objects within its environment. The smoke will
flow smoothly around the surface of an object, but will churn as it mixes with the
air behind the surface.
The authors explain that the type of equations being used are based on the
assumption that the gas comes from normal, everyday sources such as fires or steam
effects. Due to this aspect, the authors assume the gas is incompressible and are
able to use a simplified version of the Navier-Stokes equations. Most of the other
methods proposed also assume that the fluid they are modeling is
incompressible.The form of the Navier-Stokes equations as given by the authors is
presented below:
∂u
= v∇ · (∇u) − (u · ∇)u − ∇p
∂t
(2.1)
This equation is a representation of the velocity of the gas, u, over time t, where v is
the viscosity, ∇ is the gradient operator, · is the inner product operator, and p is
the pressure of the gas. The velocity of the gas over time is described through
convection, ((u · ∇)u), its pressure gradient, (∇p), and drag(v∇ · (∇u)).
The authors break down the motion of the gas they are modeling into two basic
components. The first is concerned with the simple velocity and convection of the
gas. The second has to do with elements such as thermal buoyancy that cause the
gas to swirl, rotate and rise. These two steps are combined together in a reduced
form of the Navier-Stokes equations.
The thermal buoyancy of a gas is modeled with the following equation, which
defines a buoyant force on a gas, Fbv , as:
Fbv = −βgv (T0 − Tk )
(2.2)
In this equation, β stands for the coefficient of thermal expansion, gv represents

gravity in a vertical direction, T0 is an initial reference temperature, and Tk
accounts for the average temperature on the boundary between a gaseous cell and
the one above it.
The Thermal Buoyancy of the gas must take into account the change in
temperature within the gas. The differential equation that provides this change in
temperature is similar to the equation that models the gas itself. This is because
the temperature also moves through convection and dissipates in the same way that
a gas will diffuse. By solving the equation for temperature change given below, the
equation for Thermal Buoyancy can be solved. Temperature change of a gas over

, is defined as follows:

= λ∇ · (∇T ) − ∇ · T u

Where λ represents a diffusion process, T is the temperature, and u is the velocity
of the gas. Furthermore, because the Thermal Buoyancy affects velocity, it can be

∂u
= v∇ · (∇u) − (u · ∇)u − ∇p + Fbv
∂t
(2.4)
Both equations (2.3) and (2.4) produce a model for the rotational and turbulent
motion of a gas.
The authors use these equations to model the gas in a three dimensional
environment which is broken up into cubic cells. These cells are aligned to a x, y, z
coordinate system like the ones used in previous methods. The gas is also divided
up into cells and aligned on this grid.
This method generally produced realistic smoke effects; however, the downside of
this approach was that the equations used were only stable for a certain period of
time. The smoke was also forced to be of a low resolution to help with efficiency.
These problems were addressed in the works of Jos Stam [22, 23, 24],who introduced
a model that is unconditionally stable and can be run at any speed.

2.4 Stable Fluids
Stam [22] states that his priority is based on the aesthetic quality of computer
graphics rather than the practical. Instead of creating engineering simulations he
wanted to contribute towards movies or video games. It was because of this visual
rather than practical focus that the Navier-Stokes Equations were used. These
equations are used to create just about any physically natural fluid flow and are
generally complex and time consuming. However, because our interests were limited
to the visual element of a fluid flow, the algorithms could become much more stable.
Whereas previous methods used an unstable finite differencing method, Stam used a
stable Semi-Lagrangian method to solve for the Navier-Stokes method.
The Navier Stokes Equations are involved in two aspects of fluid flow, these are
the velocity of the fluid and the density of a substance such as smoke within the
fluid. The velocity of a fluid is mapped out into a velocity vector field which assigns
a velocity vector to every point in the space that the fluid is contained. It is this
velocity field that is used to animate the smoke’s movement realistically. The
velocity field is influenced by outside forces whose sources can be wind fans, a
moving object, a source of heat, or anything else that would affect the velocity of a
fluid. The density of the substance within the fluid is affected by the velocity and its
sources while simultaneously diffusing over time due to the velocity field’s disruptive
properties. The density can also be increased by some outside source, for example
the density of smoke can increase as a result of a fire or the tip of a cigarette.
The equations for both the velocity and the density are listed below:
V elocity :
∂u
= −(u · ∇)u + v∇2 u + f
∂t
(2.5)
Density :
∂p
= −(u · ∇)p + k∇2 p + s
∂t
(2.6)
Where velocity, u, and density d are described over time t. Both of these equations
are very similar. This is because both the velocity and density have three elements
in common with each other. Both are expected to move from one area to another,
11
they decrease over time, and they are both affected or created by outside sources.
These elements are referred to as
1. the advection stage:
−(u · ∇)u
−(u · ∇)p
2. the diffusion stage:
v∇2 u
k∇2 p
(where v stands for viscosity and k stands for diffusocity)
3. and the add forces/sources stage:
f or s
It is these stages that when added together create the velocity or the density of the
simulation over a specific time step.
Finding the source element is straight forward, simply multiply the source
location by the time step and add it to the density’s location grid. It is the diffusion
step that is slightly more complicated. The most intuitive way to create the
diffusion step is to exchange density among the cells of the density grid. Thus a cell
receives a portion of its neighbors’ density while simultaneously transferring a
percentage of its own density. However, integrated diffusion such as this is unstable.
This method runs into problems when the cell size is too small or when the time
step or the diffusion rate is too high. The densities stored in the grid can become
negative or grow towards infinity. This is because the density exchange needs to be
done over a larger area of cells instead of the explicit neighbors.
Stam introduces an idea he calls “implicit time stepping”. This is when a
diffusion effect is found by moving backwards in time to produce the initial set of
12
densities. Finding a new density that will yield the original density requires the
solution of several linear equations at every step. Stam finds that solving these
linear equations with a long time step is more economic than resorting to the
explicit integration and taking many small time steps in order to avoid instability.
The final stage in the density equation, the advection step, is the most
challenging. It is similar to the diffusion step in that density is transferred among
cells. This density transfer is greatest in the direction of the velocity. Instead of
using the same linear equations as the diffusion step and adjusting them for velocity,
Stam decides to use a different approach. Stam envisions the density as particles
that are affected by a velocity field. He uses a method that looks backward in time
over a single time step, to find the particles which end up producing a cell with a
particle that is exactly at a grid cell’s center. The method then finds the density of
the previous particle’s neighbors and sets the new, centered particle’s cell to contain
that density. This method is done automatically for each grid cell and is stable
because the new density values are never allowed to be larger than the maximum
density of the previous time step. This means that the density values will remain
under control no matter how large the time step.
The velocity field is handled in a similar fashion as the density field. This is due
to the similarities between the two qualities. To add sources to a velocity, you
simply multiply the source grid by the time step and add it to the velocity. The
diffusion process is exactly similar to the density equation except that it must now
take into account the viscosity of a fluid. The diffusion rate of a velocity field is
dependant on the viscosity of the fluid that is being modeled. Allowing a velocity
field to diffuse quickly creates a high viscosity glue-like fluid. The advection step for
the velocity field is also similar to the density’s advection step. The difference is
that instead of using the velocity to move the density field, the velocity field is now
moving along itself. Stam traces a velocity particle backwards in time through the
velocity field and then interpolates the velocity at that location and transfers that
velocity value to the grid cell. Stam also is able to show that although this equation
13
was used to solve the linear density advection term, it can also be used on the
nonlinear velocity advection term.
One of the physical properties of fluid is that it must conserve mass. There must
be an equal amount of flow entering the cell as there is leaving the cell. Forcing the
velocity step to conserve mass produces realistic swirls and vortices. The steps that
have been used to find the velocity however, do not take mass conservation into
account. To do this a vector field must be subtracted from the velocity. This is
because every velocity field is the sum of a mass conserving field and a gradient
field. It is this gradient field that we want to subtract from our velocity field in
order to get a mass conserving field. Stam uses a Poisson equation to find this
gradient field that must be subtracted.
Stam’s proposed method was a great success in that it created very stable and
realistic computer generated smoke. However, there were some downsides as well.
The work of Foster and Fedkiw [5] addressed some of these concerns.
2.5 Practical Animation of Liquids
Foster and Fedkiw [5] built off of the advances of previous works to build a
liquid animation that is both realistic and more stable than previous methods. The
procedures outlined in this paper use the minimal amount of computations to
achieve visually realistic, easily controllable liquids. The authors continue to use the
Navier-Stokes equations and use Stam’s Semi-Lagrangian method [22] for solving
the equations. However, one of the side-effects of using the Semi-Lagrangian method
that Stam proposed is gradual mass dissipation.
Stam had previously set out to model gas and so dissipation was not necessarily
an unwanted feature. Unfortunately, the authors of this paper sought to model
liquids, and consequently, any dissipation became unacceptable. To fix this
problem, the authors tracked the motion of the liquid’s surface through two
elements, particles and a level set. The authors also came up with a new way to
handle objects that move within the liquid. This new technique can also be used to
14
control the direction of the liquid.
The environment is set up on a rectangular voxel grid with side length ∆τ . Each
cell has a pressure variable at its center and a velocity variable on each side that it
shares with its adjoining neighbors. Each cell is either labeled empty or full. The
empty cells are available to be filled with liquids while the full cells are filled with
an impermeable object.
The surface of the liquid is represented in two ways, through marker particles
and through a dynamic level set. The first method of tracking the liquid’s surface,
through particles, is similar to using marker particles. These particles are massless
and are moved over the surface of the water through convection. Particles can do a
great job of tracking the surface of a liquid and are easy to calculate. However, one
of the drawbacks of using particles, is that they make it difficult to produce a
smooth liquid surface. Therefore, the authors recommend using a technique which
involves taking the isocontour of the particles. The isocontour is simply an outline
of the top part of all the surface particles. This isocontour is then smoothed out by
using an algorithm that flattens out the extreme curves of the isocontour by setting
a value to everything outside the liquid to positive and everything inside the liquid
as negative. The values of the particle outlines are then simplified to get them closer
to zeros. This yields a surface that is smoother but still might have some awkward
folds or kinks in it. Therefore, the surface is smoothed out again using a special
smoothing equation that yields a much better representation of the surface of a
liquid.
Using the isocontour is much better than relying on particles alone, however,
there are still drawbacks to this method. There has to be a large accumulation of
particles near the isocontour before you can get a believably smooth surface, there
also must be particles throughout the liquid despite the fact that those particles will
not affect the surface. One way to get around this is to use particles that are
directly attached to the liquid surface and then simply add or remove them as
needed. A level set uses a similar idea to this but instead of using particles, it uses
15
the isocontour outline instead. This yields a very smooth and realistic surface, but
unfortunately, suffers from dissipation. The level set, because it is based off of a
curve that is a simplification of the surface and because it changes over time due to
convection, faces the problem of volume loss near detailed surface features.
The authors make up for the drawbacks of each method by simply combining
the two methods. The particle system is strong at representing splashy, turbulent
surfaces, and the level set approach creates smooth realistic water surfaces. The
strengths of the two methods counteract the weaknesses that they both possess.
The authors use the level set to influence the number and density of the particles, if
the particle is not affecting the surface it is deleted, if there are too few particles
they are added. There is also an algorithm that determines whether or not the
surface is calm or turbulent. The particles are then allowed to influence, or update
the level set depending on the results of this algorithm. If the surface is calm, the
level set is used and the particles are ignored, otherwise, the particles are influential
in modifying the local values of the isocontour.
The authors also provide a method that will allow for solid moving objects to
interact with the simulated liquid. To do this the authors move the liquid particles
so that they are always on the outside of the object. This is easily accomplished
because the location and velocity of the object’s surface polygons are usually
already determined. In order to prevent the liquid from continuing to flow into the
object the velocity of the liquid is changed so that it flows in the direction of the
object’s surface normal. In other words, the direction of the fluid flow is set to flow
away from the surface of the object. This allows the liquid to be pushed out of the
way of an object, while simultaneously producing a realistic free flowing effect. This
method has been adapted to control the direction and flow of liquids. This is done
by using a “fake” object to influence the surrounding liquid. By adjusting the
surface of the liquid as if a moving object was there, the animator can change and
control the velocity of the liquid. This work was a successful step towards the
creation of sophisticated liquids, however, the dissipation in gas was still a problem.
16
The work of Fedkiw, Stam and Jensen [4] builds on Stam’s original proposals.
2.6 Visual Simulation of Smoke
The authors of this paper [4], found that there was too much “numerical
dissipation” which caused the smoke to fade too quickly when it came to the smaller
whirls and vortices that are usually found in realistic smoke. What they proposed
was a way of replacing the lost energy back into the fluid using a forcing term that
would increase the vorticity of the smoke flow and keep the smoke churning for
longer periods of time. This “vorticity confinement” is a technique used in the
engineering field of computational fluid dynamics.
One of the problems that the authors ran into was that the mesh/grid that is
used to produce the smoke and its environment is too simple or coarse. In order to
get really fine details, the grid would need to have many more grid points. At the
time that the authors wrote this paper, a method used to engineer the very
turbulent flows around helicopters had just been invented. It was this vorticity
confinement method that the authors used as a basis for their graphics based
simulation. The authors came to the conclusion that the best way to add detail to
the plumes of smoke was to determine where the vorticity of the smoke is highest.
Once the concentrations of vorticity are found, the size and direction of the vorticity
are calculated and the details are added. This helped to create a more sophisticated
and intriguing smoke effect.
2.7 Structural Modeling of Flames for a Production Environment
Fire is also an element that is addressed by computational fluid dynamics and as
such, the challenge of creating complex and unpredictable flames can be similar to
the creation of intricate and turbulent smoke plumes. The authors Lamorlette and
Foster [13] set out to create CG flames for the specific purpose of creating special
effects for an animated movie. This situation meant that the flame effects had to be
easy to control and would quickly produce the results that animators needed.
17
The authors state that producing fire effects can be much more complicated
than producing liquid or smoke effects. One of the reasons for this is the inherent
chaotic nature of flame. Slight changes in the environment around the flames or the
conditions in which the flame is started can lead to unpredictable results. In
addition to this, the type of simulations used to model gas and liquid do not require
as much resolution as that of a flame effect. Before the authors released their paper,
computer generated flame was created using two different techniques. The first
technique is a numerical simulation, this is similar to the methods used to produce
gas or liquid effects in that it seeks to use physics derived algorithms such as those
found in computational fluid dynamics to produce flames. This method is capable of
producing both a convincing rotational flame-like motion and the resulting smoke
which are caused by the fire’s heat. The second technique available for modeling
flame is through the use of particles. Particles are easy to produce regardless of
scale or behavior and are easy to control. Unfortunately, they do not produce the
characteristics and behaviors of fire.
The authors mention that in many aspects of flame there are few resources in
terms of engineering or scientific measurement to assist them in the creation of the
fire. The fire was eventually produced through statistics based on the physical and
aesthetic behavior of flames. The authors categorized different characteristics of the
flames into separate problems and developed procedures to handle each of these
characteristics. The basic body of the flame was created by using an interpolating
B-Spline curve that is acted upon by wind and atmospheric parameters. This curve
makes up the center of the flame and the wind fields act upon it to distort and bend
the shape of the flame. The actual visual flame is built around the curve using a
cylindrical profile. The area around the flame’s edge is built up with particles. As
the flame grows and evolves it will reach a certain height, at which point the top of
the flame will either separate from the main body or the flame will flicker and begin
to grow again. The part of the flame that separates will eventually burn itself out as
it rises away from the main point of the flame.
18
The flames can be controlled in almost every aspect that the user may need.
The animator has direct control over the basic shape, scale, color, flickering and
separation behavior of the fire. The motion of the flame can be indirectly controlled
by managing the wind fields that affect the fire. This method is a very simple and
scenario specific way of producing fire effects yet the results produced are very
lifelike and compelling flame effects.
2.8 Animation and Rendering of Complex Water Surfaces
Another paper by Fedkiw, Enright, and Marschner [2] builds on the detail and
control of previous work done in the area of simulated liquids. The main
contributions are in the areas of surface tracking and a new velocity extrapolation
method.
The authors propose a new “thickened” front tracking system which they dub
the “particle level set method”. This method allows for a previously unattainable
level of detail and control when applied towards modeling the surface of the liquid.
Earlier methods that were used to model liquids focused only on the volume and
surface of the liquid rather than the liquid’s interaction with the atmosphere. The
opposite approach is taken with this method. Instead of modeling the volume of the
liquid to achieve the surface, the authors model the surface as a result of the
atmosphere and the liquid. They continue to use the same sort of particle and
isocontour methods proposed earlier, however, they add symmetrical particles on
the opposite side of the liquid, in essence modeling the atmosphere as well as the
liquid. This gives the liquid surface a fuller, easier to manipulate, and more detailed
appearance.
The authors also propose a new way of controlling the surface by again taking
the atmosphere into account. The authors take the extrapolation of the velocity
across the liquid’s surface into the atmosphere. By doing this they can find the
velocity of the atmosphere and control the particles near the surface on the dry side
of the liquid. This can then be used as a control to produce effects such as a
19
wind-blown liquid surface.
2.9 Physically Based Modeling and Animation of Fire
Fire simulations gained a greater depth and sophistication through the work of
Nguyen, Fedkiw, and Jensen [17].The method they used to create fire was based
solely on the physical properties of flame and does not depend on the need to
quickly produce easy to control flames for an animation environment. Instead,
attention was placed on concepts such as the source and cause of flames and how
they react to the environment. The flame is seen as a chemical reaction that occurs
when the flame’s fuel is converted into hot gaseous products.
The authors break up their physically based model of flames into three separate
components, the core, what they call the blackbody radiation, and the smoke or
soot produced by the fire. The core is the bright bluish base of the flame where the
reaction that produces the flame takes place. The size of the core depends on the
amount of fuel that is being introduced to the reaction. The fuel can be either a
solid fuel such as a log, or a gaseous fuel. When a solid is lit on fire, a reaction must
take place that converts the materials of the solid into a gaseous fuel which is then
ignited. With greater amounts of fuel comes a larger blue core. The blackbody
radiation is the part of the flame that is emitted by hot gaseous products such as
the soot that is produced by the core. This part of the flame is the familiar
yellowish-orange body of fire. The smoke is then produced from this blackbody
radiation segment.
The flame is created by tracking the temperatures of the fire’s elements. The
elements begin at the core and pass upwards and outwards as the fire expands. By
modeling the elements as they expand and go through their temperature variations,
the simulation can achieve uniquely flame-like characteristics. The expansion is
represented as a ratio of the density of the flame’s fuel over the density of the hot
gaseous products. By increasing the expansion, flames can look fuller and more
turbulent. Because of the different properties of the flame, the fuel and the gaseous
20
products are modeled using two different equations. The velocities of the elements
as they cross over from the fuel to the hot gaseous products will differ and this is
represented by using separate Euler equations. To relate the two equations, a “ghost
fluid” method is used, in which a possible ghost value is calculated to allow the
simulation to know what the velocity or pressure would be in the other part of the
flame.
The temperature of the fluid elements of the flames is important in that it
governs the movement and shape of the flame. By modeling the temperature change
of the flame’s fluid elements as they travel from the core into the larger body of the
flame, the simulation can produce physically faithful effects. This is done by
determining how much time has elapsed since an element has passed through the
blue core. The temperature will increase slightly over time and then begin to cool
once it has reached an ignition temperature. The animator has control over setting
specific temperatures and densities.
2.10 Keyframe Control of Smoke Simulation
The next major stepping stone in the area of smoke animation came within the
area of highly unnatural special effects. Smoke was now being used to create
intricate formations such as words, designs, and in some cases characters. One of
the more influential of these proposals was done by Treuille, McNamara, Popovic
and Stam [25]. Their work sought to control the motion and formation of smoke
using keyframes.
Keyframes are a common technique in computer animation in which the
animator sets the basic poses or states of an entire animation sequence at specific
frames over the course of an animation. The computer will then connect the dots
between these dramatic poses allowing for a fluid animation. The method that this
paper suggests is to set keyframes with the smoke in different formations. The
program will then attempt to create an animation using these keyframes by
adjusting the velocity field and thus creating “wind” that will blow the smoke into
21
position.
However, while using a force such as wind can generate realistic smoke effects, it
is also very difficult to control and is a very blunt tool in creating complex smoke
formations. To combat this lack of control, the authors proposed running several
simulations that would approximate the desired animation. The best of these
simulations would then go on to become the finished animation.
Meanwhile, the authors also sought to use the smallest amount of force possible
in producing the finished animations. In order to make this possible, the authors
had to calculate the derivatives of these properties at every time-step of the
animation. This was very time consuming and as a result, animations with multiple
key frames were very difficult to produce. The authors then suggested a method
they called “layered multiple shooting” to break up the problem of a long complex
animation into sets of subproblems. The entire animation is broken into segments
and each segment is solved as if it were the finished product.
However, when it comes time to reattach each segment, the animation does not
match up well because each segment was accomplished in a way that is indifferent
to the needs of the upcoming portion of animation. To blend the segments together
into one fluid animation, the simulation must create “pseudo-keyframes”. This is
done by creating an alternate schedule of subproblems that are worked on
simultaneously with the original segments. These pseudo-keyframes are meant to
occur between the original keyframes so that when taken together, all of the
segments and subsegments add up into the overall animation.
The methods proposed in this paper did produce realistic and interesting smoke
animations, but unfortunately there were several drawbacks. Although it works well
for simple and short animations, this method is far too complex and
computationally expensive to achieve longer and more sophisticated smoke
animations. While this paper offered a very limited approach to producing smoke
animations it was an important step in producing complex smoke animations.
22
2.11 Target-Driven Smoke Animation
The work of Fattal and Lischinski [3], was a major step in setting up a viable
method for creating realistic and complex smoke animations. Instead of optimally
approximating a set of keyframes that the smoke is driven to, this method uses
target smoke states to attract the smoke into. It introduced the use of a driving
force to herd the smoke particles into a larger, complex formation and a gathering
term that kept the smoke from dissipating while it was in the target formation. This
paper also allowed animators to control smoke fields that shared the same fluid.
The driving force is generally the most important term in this Target-Driven
method. The purpose of the driving force is to cause the smoke, at any point in the
simulation, to advect towards the next target. The driving force must also take into
account the fact that the fluid comes to a state of rest once the target has been
reached. It is also important to note that, unlike keyframe animation, this method
does not require the smoke to exactly match a target at an exact time. Rather, the
driving force depends only on the state of an animation at an instant by instant
basis. As the smoke approaches its target, the momentum of the driving force is
decreased and the values of the smoke at the target are briefly “blurred” allowing
the smoke to come to rest at the target.
It isn’t always possible to match a target density field with advection. This is
because smoke is likely to diffuse and some target states require a higher density
than is currently available. This is where the method proposed introduces the
gathering force. The difference between the density values of the initial density and
the target density is referred to as the density error. The gathering term works by
taking this density error and diffusing it. Gathering is only put into effect in areas
where smoke is already at and near the vicinity of the target density. This prevents
diffusion in the early stages of the simulation and allows the smoke to converge
correctly into the target area.
The authors also allow the animation to affect different parts of the density field
at the same time. For example, smoke is usually herded towards the closest peak of
23
a target, however, with “multiple field flow” the animator can split up the smoke
into separate fields that have their own paths toward a target state. This allows
more control over how the smoke reaches its formation. The authors allow this
differentiation among the smoke field itself, but are careful to note that the overall
velocity field is not affected to allow for control of multiple smoke fields.
Despite the number of improvements made by this method, it unfortunately is
also subject to some drawbacks. First, the method proposed does not provide direct
quality control of the animation. An animator must increase or decrease control
parameters to tweak animation, rather than use a more intuitive hands-on
approach. These parameters allow the animator to: smooth the driving force and
allowing the smoke to form in a more organized manner, change the speed of the
simulation, change the speed of the smoke, and to adjust the “wispy-ness” of the
smoke’s final formation. The second drawback of this method is due to the fact that
these control forces are not regulated, allowing for an animation that is not
physically true. This means that the smoke is not controlled in a realistic fashion so
the results are not always realistic.
2.12 Taming Liquids for Rapidly Changing Targets
The authors Lin Shi and Yizhou Yu [20], hoped to achieve very detailed and
unnatural formations of liquids to be used as unique special effects in either movies
or video games. They cite movies such as “The Abyss” and the morphing character
from “Terminator 2: Judgment Day”. These effects are both natural and unusual in
that a realistic element, in this case the liquid, is used to achieve an effect that
could otherwise never be accomplished in reality. This is accomplished by using
target states which the liquid should form. The liquid is then formed into these
target states through the use of two separate force fields. The force fields guide the
liquid into the shape of the target. The first force field is the feedback force field
which manages the shape and velocity of the liquid so that it is congruent with the
target. Both the velocity as well as the shape must be kept in check as the target
24
changes over the course of the animation. The second force field is used as a sort of
driving force.
2.13 Controllable Smoke Animation with Guiding Objects
Another influential piece of work was done by Lin Shi and Yizhou Yu [19]. In
their paper, the authors use target objects as a sort of container that matches the
shape the animator wants. The smoke can be moved from one shape or position to
another by being carried along in this container or guiding object. The authors set
their goals toward creating smoke effects that looked more natural when achieving
their shapes or formations. They wanted to create smoke that could last in a moving
or stationary formation for any given amount of time without changing the motion
and structure of the smoke itself. They also wanted the smoke to be responsive to
changes in the environment. For example, if a gust of wind or some other object
acted upon the smoke, it would destroy the formation that the smoke had created.
The authors make sure that the smoke is being contained by its target through
an equation that is referred to as a guiding implicit function. This function
measures whether or not the smoke is matching up with its guiding object. It
returns zero at the object’s boundary and positive when inside the object.
Consequently, the function can also then provide the shortest distance from any
point on the smoke’s edge to the guiding object’s edge. By using the feedback from
the guiding implicit function, the simulation can adjust the boundary of the smoke
and guide it so that it fits within the outline of the target. This is done by altering
the velocity field within the guiding object so that the smoke is continuously guided
into the exact form of the target.
The authors also experimented with another way of creating the smoke effect
itself. They found that in applying their guiding object methods, the equations that
were used before them by Stam and Fedkiw [4], did not work. This is because the
velocity of the method that is proposed would have to be constrained in a way that
would affect the overall quality of the simulation. What the authors suggest instead
25
is an equation for compressible gas. The previous methods have all used the
incompressible flow equations. This is because the compressible equations are
expensive and need a very specific time step value to work.
2.14 Smoke Simulation For Large Scale Phenomena
The authors of this paper [18] set out to create a method for creating expansive,
high detailed smoke simulations that would result from a powerful explosion such as
a nuclear blast. Unfortunately, creating the level of detail desired by the authors,
was not feasable due to a lack of computational resources. This was because the
previous methods used [8, 22, 4] require unavailable amounts of memory when
implemented on the authors’ large scale velocity grids. Instead, the authors devised
a new method for producing velocity fields.
The authors use the previously created Computational Fluid Dynamics methods
to create a pair of similar two dimensional velocity grids. These flat grids are then
positioned in three dimensional space and used to interpolate their values into the
three dimensional environment. This interpolated velocity field is combined with
another three dimensional component that is created using a Kolmogorov energy
spectrum. The Kolmogorov spectrum was used as a technique in [13]. It is this
energy spectrum that determines the structure of the velocity field in space.
2.15 Simulating Water and Smoke with an Octree Data Structure
The use of interpolation for large scale grids can produce amazing results,
however, the authors Losasso, Gibou, and Fedkiw claim that they can faithfully
produce three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations using a new method. In their
paper [14], the authors use an octree data structure and some mesh refinement
techniques to create three-dimensional fluid effects such as smoke and water.
In creating their fluid effects, the authors use an adaptive meshing strategy.
This means that more grid cells are placed in locations where the fluid needs a
greater amount of detail. Unfortunately, using this strategy can produce unevenness
26
in the mesh and can be problematic when solving the equations needed to produce
the flow effect. One possible method to fix this problem is through using a multigrid
approach. However, this approach has limited appeal as it is problematic in
simulations where there is a lot of detail and where interactions between surfaces
such as water and air take place. Instead, the authors use an unrestricted octree
which allows the user to bypass the problems of the multigrid approach.
2.16 Animating Large Bodies of Water
The paper “Creating Large Bodies of Water with Two and Three Dimensional
Methods” [9] created a method for producing large bodies of water by using three
dimensional Navier-Stokes methods for the complex surface of the water and a two
dimensional height field approach. This method allows for efficiency because most of
the water’s volume can be represented by condensed, “tall” grid cells. These tall
cells are used because they produce good resolution horizontally which allows for
greater detail at a further depth. They are meant as a sort of abbreviation for a
long column of regular cells which can be simplified. The simulation requires a
specified optical depth which determines the depth of the surface or interface. This
variable is actually greater than the visible optical depth of a liquid as it is meant to
capture the depth where turbulent behavior in the water can still affect the surface.
This turbulent motion in the water can affect the larger layer of the water beneath
it and thereby give an increased optical depth. Below the optical depth boundary,
however, two dimensional methods take place and the grid is coarsened to reduce
computational costs. This is done with both traditional square uniform cells and
tall cells which are a replacement of several uniform cells in a column. The tall and
uniform cells can both occupy the same column in any combination of formations.
The uniform cells contain velocity components at their faces and pressure values at
their centers. Tall cells have two pressure values that refer to the top and bottom
most cells that the tall cell has replaced.
27
2.17 Multiple Interacting Liquids
This paper [15] uses the particle level set method to create multiple fluids
interacting with each other. Previously, the level set method was used to represent
the interaction of a liquid with atmosphere or fire with its fuel, this method allows
several different interactions to take place in the same simulation. This is done by
using a seperate particle level set for each of the different regions in the simulation.
Each level set does its own work and when one contradicts another the authors use
a dictionary they created. This dictionary is used to translate between the
discrepancies in the various level sets to create accurate surface location.

Upon reviewing the previous work, it is evident that attention must be paid to
the scientific fundamentals of the smoke flow in order to produce a realistic and
natural effect. As a fluid, much of the behavior and qualities of smoke can be

Computational Fluid Dynamics is a way of expressing the flow of any fluid through
space and/or time in a numerical form. It is because CFD techniques apply to all
fluids, that the innovations made in other areas such as water simulations can then

3.2 Solving the Equations
Having already described the fundamental governing equations of Computational
Fluid Dynamics, attention must now be placed on their solutions. There has been
much written on these solutions, particularly with those involving the Navier-Stokes
equations which are so vital to smoke flow. In fact, Jos Stam goes so far as to state:
“The problem of simulating realistic fluids is equivalent to writing good solvers
for the Navier-Stokes equations.”[23]
3.2.1 Initial Conditions
The smoke that will be used within the program will be an incompressible,
inviscid gas. This means that the smoke modeled will have no major changes in
density or internal friction within the flow. The smoke flow will have to be initiated
by the user who will designate a source for the smoke to originate from as well as
the original density of the smoke flow.

3.2.3 Difficulties with the Finite Differences Methods
Using the finite difference approach, unfortunately, does not yield the best
results when used in the fluid simulations of the past. For example, Foster and
Metaxes use finite difference approximation [6, 7] but are forced to keep their
simulations within a specific time step. If that time step exceeds a certain duration
the simulation will “blow-up” as Stam notes [22].
This difficulty is due to the nature of using finite differences to approximate the
gas flow. By using finite differences, the program suffers from the high
computational cost of detail within the smoke flow. High detail requires that the
finite steps needed to approximate the gas flow must be minute and therefore must
be numerous inorder to describe the entire flow. Which is to say that there must be
36
many densely packed points of data that must be computed. This problem can be
resolved by reducing the number of these points, however, the simulation’s detail
will suffer as a result of this solution.
Over the course of the work done in computer generated smoke flow, the use of
finite difference approximation was looked over in favor of Stam’s
“Semi-Lagrangian” methods [22, 23, 24]. These methods suffered only from
numerical dissipation which was then compensated for [4] through the idea of
“vorticity confinement”.

HASHTABLES
In order to further the conservation of resources as well as ensure a high degree
of detail, the density fields of the smoke flow are placed within a space saving data
structure. This structure is a hashtable which contains the density field. The
hashtable stores only the nonempty density cells, making it possible to render
arbitrarily high resolution scenes.
4.1 Hashtables
Hashing is a good way to store and retrieve data quickly. A hashtable is simply
a long array in which elements are stored according to an individual key. This key
determines where the information will be stored in the hashtable. To set this
location, the key is used in a hash function which then yields an address in the
hashtable. If this address is already taken, then several solutions are available. The
hashtable may allow chaining, or the creation of a list, at the key’s address. In this
way, two or more pieces of information can be stored at the same address. If
chaining is not an available solution, then the information can be pushed down the
hashtable to the next available address. This problem of keys indicating the same
hashtable address is referred to as a collision. Depending on how a collision is
handled, the search time for a hashtable can range from O(1) to O(1 + a) where a is
the length of the hashtable
4.2 Creation of the Hashtable
The hashtable created to contain the smokeflow program’s data was
implemented with a vector full of objects called hash entries. These objects contain
three variables that consist of an integer that acts as a key to the hashtable, a float
containing the information which needed storing, and a boolean variable which
indicated whether or not a position in the hashtable was occupied. These variables
38
could all be retrieved individually and could be set to specific values through the
use of a hash entry constructor.
The two most important functions that pertain to the hashtable revolve around
storing and retrieving information. The placement of information within the
hashtable necessitates a hash function and a specific key number, k, for each piece
of information stored in the table. The hash function, h(k), which was used in the
program, multiplied the hashtable’s size, M, with the result of some number
between zero and 1, A, multiplied by the key and modulated by 1. This hash
function was adapted from [12]and is listed below.
h(k) = M ((Ak) − bAkc)
(4.1)

five minus one multiplied by M and M must be some power of 2. The equations for

In order to place data within the hashtable, two parameters must be supplied,
the data and its hash key. It is important to note that each piece of data has a
unique key value which will be acted upon by the hash function. If two different
data entries have the same key, the hashtable will place the data at the same spot at
the same location. This means that a previous value stored in the hashtable will be
overwritten by a newer value if the two different data entries have the same key.
However, sometimes the hash function can produce an already occupied index

and the data is stored in the hashtable at the next available index number. To
determine if an index number is available, each hash entry from the point of the
collision is checked to see if it is active. This is done by returning the hash entry’s
Boolean information which is set to true when the index has a value placed in it. If
the remainder of the indexes in the hashtable vector contains true Booleans
indicating filled entries, the function will loop around to the very first index in the
array and won’t stop until it gets to the original index that it started searching
from. Thus, if a collision takes place, the function will continue to loop through the
hashtable until a hash entry object supplies a false Boolean.
4.3 Hashtable Retrieval
Retrieving data from a hashtable is slightly complicated in that there are three
scenarios that may occur upon a retrieval attempt. The first scenario is
straightforward and the data is found immediately. The second scenario involves the
searching of the hashtable due to a collision that may have occurred upon
placement of the data into the hashtable. The third scenario occurs when the data
searched for has not been entered into the table. When this scenario occurs the
number zero is returned.
Retrieval of information from the hashtable requires the hash key of the data
being retrieved. The retrieval function will then begin searching for the data at the
index derived from the supplied key. If this index provides a false Boolean, then the
empty entry value of zero is returned because this means the data was never entered
into the hashtable. If, on the other hand, the boolean value reported is true, then
the key passed to the retrieval function is compared to that of the hash entry’s
stored key value. If these two keys are the same then the data has been found
without searching and the hash entry’s stored data value is returned. However, if
the two keys do not match up, then that means a collision may have occurred when
the data was placed in the table. If this is the case, the function loops through the
40
hashtable until it either finds the data with the matching key or finds a Boolean
that presents as false. If the function finds this false Boolean after progressing
through the indexes of the hashtable, it means that a collision did not occur. If a
collision never occurred, then the data was never entered into the hashtable and the
number zero is returned.
4.4 Implementation of the Hashtable
Two placement and retrieval functions where written for the smoke flow
program. Each did the same thing but used different parameters or return types.
The first placement function used a key and a data value as parameters while the
retrieval function only returned the value of the sought after data. In addition, two
more placement and retrieval functions were written due to the fact that a hash
entry contains both the key and the data value needed in a hashtable. This hash
entry can be used as a parameter or a return type when creating a placement or
retrieval function. This overloading of functions proved useful when dealing with the
large remainder of code present within the smoke flow program.
Cleaning the hashtable is necessary at certain points during the smoke flow
program so that data can be updated. This is done by looping through the
hashtable and replacing the hash entries at each vector index with new, empty hash
entries. This results in a clean and unused hashtable.
The hashtable was used in two areas of the smoke flow program. The first was
the data that was used to represent the density field of the gas. This data was
moved back and forth between two hashtables as the density field was updated
throughout the simulation. This swapping of values occurred before the diffusion
process and again before the advection process where one of the hashtables is then
cleaned out.The second area in the program where hashtables were used dealt with
the creation of boundaries and the information containing the simulation grids that
had been visited by the density.

DEPTH SORTING
The simulation was reorganized so that it is capable of running in realtime. In
order to render the simulation efficiently, the scene was sorted according to depth.
This is due to the fact that OpenGL can only handle transparency correctly if the
scene is drawn in back-to-front order. Transparency is a necessary element in
coloring the smoke and therefore must be included in the program.
The portion of code used to color the smoke uses an RGBA color scheme, which
stands for Red, Green, Blue, and Alpha. By combining the color components red,
green and blue, any color necessary can be displayed. Alpha is a fourth color
component which is never actually seen, but is instead used to manipulate the color
that is being displayed. Alpha can be used for color blending and represents opacity.
An alpha value of 1.0 is completely opaque, while a value of 0.0 is completely
transparent.
As each new element is rendered in the scene, its color value is stored so that
you can blend the color of any later object which will be drawn in the foreground.
This is controlled by the alpha value and produces a transparent effect. In order to
let the background colors show through, the scene must be sorted so that the
background colors can be drawn first and stored so that they can be blended with
the foreground objects which will be drawn later.
5.1 Depth Sorting
In order to render the simulation environment properly, depth sorting must be
established for all elements in the scene. Without this process, smoke will remain
visible as it passes behind objects in the foreground. Not only is this effect
incorrect, but it is also confusing and can distract the viewer from properly
observing the reaction of the smoke flow to surrounding obstacles.
To sort the scene by depth, I used the bin sorting or Radix sort algorithm. This
42
Figure 5.1: Depth sorted scene using bin sort
algorithm divides the scene into separate bins based upon the location in the
environment. Then, over multiple passes, the algorithm sorts the bins gradually
until the information that they contain are in order. This is done by comparing the
digits of each key and placing them in the according bin. The keys are sorted in
passes based on the least significant digit first and then gradually building up to the
most significant, at which point all of the elements will be sorted. The bin/radix
sort algorithm is quick and has a complexity of O(N).
5.2 Depth Bins
In order to achieve depth sorting, bins were created for the smoke and each type
of obstacle element in the simulation. These bins are essentially a vector full of lists
that help to determine the order a scene should be rendered based on the distance
an element is from the camera. Each list in the vector represents a bin or a range of
distance from the camera viewpoint. The following equation was used to assign an
element’s data to a bin.

min and max distance,D1 . The element’s data is then placed in the corresponding

environment contained smoke flowing around a box, then not only did the smoke
element need its’ own set of depth bins, but the box also needed its individual set of
bins as well.
Sorting boxes using this method was somewhat more complicated than that of
the smoke. Each box consisted of six separate faces which needed to be sorted
individually. The need for this individual sorting is based on the fact that each quad
face exists in separate areas of space and will produce separate distance values. For
example, the front face of a box has the potential to exist in a very different range
of space then the cube’s back face. Therefore each face needed to be drawn as a
quad which consisted of three coordinates for each of the quad’s four vertices. This
was done by breaking up the box coordinates that were either given by the user or
assigned by the program. Each box has its location stored in a vector while another
vector is used to store all of the quad coordinates. A simple function is called which
can strip the box into quads and store the coordinates for sorting. This method of
conversion from box to quad makes programming easier for the user as there are
fewer coordinates to specify.
It is the average distance of these vertex coordinates that allow the quad to be
depth sorted. So, for example, all four of the quad’s x coordinates are added
together and divided by four in order to be averaged. This is done for all twelve x, y
and z coordinates of the quad so that there are three seperate axis averages. Then
the x average, y average and z average are compared to the x, y and z coordinates of
the camera inorder to find the distance. When this distance is found, all twelve of
the quad’s coordinates are stored in the box element’s distance bin. This, in
essence, produces a depth sorted cube when six correctly placed quads are in proper
formation.

5.3 Rendering Elements
After determining distance and filling each element’s bins with correctly sorted
data, the program then displays the sorted contents one bin at a time. This is done
with a simple for loop which extracts the data for each element. The bin list for
each element is run through on every iteration of the for loop so that if two different
element types(ie: gas and a quad face) existed in the same distance range, they
could be displayed simultaneously. The for loop begins at the greatest distance
value represented by the bins for all elements and counts down until a value with no
distance remains. This allows the simulation to be drawn in Back-to-Front order.
Now elements in the foreground are allowed to overlap separate elements in the
background because the foreground is being painted over the background.

7.2 Depth Sorting
Depth sorting is necessary inorder to make a scene visibly correct(fig 7.4). To do
this, the information from the obstacles and the gas were stored in individual bins
that organized the data by what point in space it pertained to. At render time, the
gas and obstacle bins where emptied and rendered simultaneously, whereas the
previous simulation rendered all the obstacle data before any of the gas information
was considered. The bins were emptied backwards inorder to ensure that the
foreground would be rendered over the background.

CHAPTER VIII
CONCLUSION
This thesis has showcased software capable of creating a three dimensional
smoke flow which responds to obstacles within the simulation environment. In
addition, this software has been enhanced through the use of depth sorting and a
hashtable data structure. These enhancements were made to improve both the
image rendered and the potential size of the created environment. The methods
used and created for this thesis have been composited together into a library which
can be used quickly in future projects.
The depth sorting methods outlined by this thesis proved to be generally
effective. There were no discernable errors in foreground and background interaction
as had occurred in the methods used previously. The depth sorting created a
believable interaction between the smoke and its environment despite changes in
either camera angle or distance.
The use of hashtables within the smoke flow program provided a dramatic
decrease in initialization time.There was, however, a general increase in processing
time, but this was merely due to the computation of the hash key and was not
introduced through the data structure itself. Possible improvements on simulation
time can be made through adjusting the hash function.
This thesis can be expanded upon by using octrees as a data structure to hold
the velocity component of the smoke. This process would increase the detail of the
smoke as it moved throughout the simulation environment.
