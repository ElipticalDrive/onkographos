Programme of colloquia for the Autumn 2004 semester
5 October 2004
Dr Martin Rajman, Swiss Federal Institute of Technology, Lausanne
Thematic Annotation: extracting concepts from documents
Abstract: Topic annotation – i.e. the annotation of document segments with tags identifying the topics discussed in those segments – can be used in many tasks, and, in particular, to improve information retrieval. Unlike standard approaches to topic annotation, the technique presented here does not rely primarily on some form of – possibly statistical – keyword extraction. Instead, the proposed annotation algorithm utilises a large-scale semantic database – the EDR Electronic Dictionary – which provides a concept hierarchy based on hyponym and hypernym relations.This concept hierarchy is used to generate a synthetic representation of the document by aggregating the words present in topically homogeneous document segments into a set of concepts that best preserve the document’s content. The proposed approach to topic extraction was experimentally tested and evaluated on a database of 238 documents corresponding to bibliographic descriptions extracted from the INSPEC database. A novel evaluation metric was designed to take into account the fact that the topics associated with the INSPEC descriptions – taken as the ground truth for the evaluation – were not generated on the basis of the EDR dictionary, and therefore needed to be approximated using the available EDR entries.
12 October 2004
Dr Ing. Otto Fučík, FIT, Brno University of Technology
HW/SW Co-design
Abstract: This paper deals with the methodology for the design of complex digital systems in which individual subsystems are implemented in both software and hardware. The aim of concurrent hardware and software design (Hardware/Software Co-design) is to maximise the performance-to-cost ratio when implementing a given application – that is, to find the optimal division of the task between ‘slow but cheap’ software and ‘fast but expensive’ hardware implementation of the application, and to ensure effective interaction between the individual parts. The use of this approach in the implementation of practical applications – an IPv6 packet router and a real-time image processing system – will also be presented.
19 October 2004
Prof. Philippe Jorrand, Leibniz Laboratory, Grenoble, France
Quantum: from Physics to Programming
Abstract: Information is physical: the laws governing its encoding, processing and communication are bound by those of its inevitably physical manifestation. In today’s informatics, information obeys the rules of classical Newtonian physics: this holds true all the way from commercial computers down to (or even up to?) Turing machines and lambda-calculus.
The driving force behind research into quantum computation is the quest to understand the consequences of basing the encoding, processing and communication of information on a different kind of physics – namely quantum physics – that is, the most advanced understanding we currently possess of the alien world of elementary particles, as described by quantum mechanics.
A brief overview of the principles of quantum information processing and communication shows that algorithms and protocols utilising quantum resources always comprise both quantum and classical components, assembled in such a way that they communicate and cooperate. Indeed, for the results of quantum information processes to be of any practical use, there is an intrinsic need for organised cooperation and communication between quantum and classical devices, controlled by the classical side. This simple, obvious fact has profound consequences for a number of central issues in computer science—such as machine architectures, language design, semantics, type theories and abstract models of computation—as soon as quantum primitives and resources are utilised by information processes. These issues cannot be ignored if the aim is to achieve a satisfactory understanding of the meaning, power and limitations of computing and communicating with the aid of quantum resources.
After laying these foundations, this seminar will present a recent contribution in this field, concerning the design of a quantum communication process calculus, in which the quantum and classical parts of information processes can be described and combined consistently, whilst ensuring that the distinctive properties of the quantum world are guaranteed by the semantics. Communication protocols utilising quantum resources will be provided as examples, illustrating this approach to the rigorous description of quantum-classical information processing.

2 November 2004
JUDr. František Cvrček, CSc., Institute of State and Law, Czech Academy of Sciences, Prague
Legal Information Systems and Legal Language
Abstract: The aim of this lecture is to familiarise the audience with the results of current research carried out by the Legal Informatics Laboratory (hereinafter LPI) of the Institute of State and Law of the Czech Academy of Sciences. The LPI has been creating legal databases since 1985. These databases, containing legislation dating from 1790, case law from 1918 and selected literature from 1990, form the basis for the analyses carried out. The LPI focuses on the regularly updated description of the legal system, the development of legal information systems and the analysis of legal language at the level of legal documents. The results of the LPI’s work have led to the debunking of certain myths prevalent in legal scholarship, such as the existence of so-called ‘legislative inflation’, the consistency of the legal system, the unambiguity of legal terminology, the role of rationality in legal reasoning, and the explanatory power of traditional legal classifications, amongst others. Since 1990, LPI staff have successfully completed 10 projects funded by the General Programme of the Czech Academy of Sciences (GA AV ČR) and the General Programme of the Czech Science Foundation (GAČR), and have participated in international projects supported by the CRN in Italy and the European Commission (hereinafter the EC). They are the founders of the discipline of Legal Informatics, which has been taught at all law faculties in the Czech Republic since 1993.
9 November 2004
Prof. Eduard Groeller, TU Wien
Importance-Driven Image Generation in Scientific Visualisation
Abstract: Visualisation is the use of computer-supported, interactive, visual representations of data to enhance cognition. Following a brief introduction to scientific visualisation, several previous projects in medical visualisation at the Vienna University of Technology will be reviewed.
Importance-driven volume rendering, a novel technique for the automatic display of focus and context in volumetric data, is presented in detail. The technique is a generalisation of cut-away views, which – depending on the viewpoint – remove or suppress less important parts of a scene to reveal more important underlying information. For each object, several representations – i.e. levels of sparseness – are specified. The display of an individual object may incorporate different levels of sparseness. The aim is to emphasise important structures and to maximise the information content in the final image.
The final part of the talk discusses VesselGlyph as an importance-driven display of CT angiography data. The VesselGlyph covers several regions where different rendering methods are used. The region type, the visualisation method used and the region parameters depend on the distance from the blood vessel centreline and on viewing parameters as well. Typically, direct volume rendering (DVR) and curved planar reformation (CPR) are combined within a single image.
Further information on the research projects is available at http://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/vis/
16 November 2004
Dr Martin Leucker, Technical University of Munich
Message-Passing Automata are expressively equivalent to EMSO Logic
Abstract: We study the expressiveness of finite message-passing automata with a priori unbounded channels and show that they capture exactly the class of MSC languages definable in existential monadic second-order logic interpreted over MSCs. Moreover, we prove that the monadic quantifier-alternation hierarchy over MSCs is infinite and conclude that the class of MSC languages accepted by message-passing automata is not closed under complement. Furthermore, we show that satisfiability for (existential) monadic second-order logic over MSCs is undecidable.
23 November 2004
Dr Joern Mueller-Quade, PhD, University of Karlsruhe
What does cryptographic security mean?
Abstract: Whether a protocol is secure depends heavily on how security is defined. Starting with an intuitive and very informal definition of security, we present several problems and drawbacks of an ad hoc approach to cryptographic security.
Next, we provide an overview of a more modern definition of security, which is so strict that it even guarantees that the property of security is preserved under composition. With such a strict notion of security, a modular design of larger protocols becomes possible.
Unfortunately, even the new definitions of security do not cover all aspects of security, and we offer a critical assessment of the models underlying the definition of security. In particular, we shall show that the notion of security depends on the quality of the underlying network (modelled by the influence the adversary has on message delivery) and that, paradoxically, some cryptographic tasks cannot be realised if the quality of the network is too high.
30 November 2004
Doc. Petr Hliněný, PhD., Technical University of Ostrava
On Matroids, MSO Logic, and Computation
Abstract: Matroids represent a powerful generalisation of graphs, and some recent profound results in structural matroid theory have found (somewhat surprising) applications back in graph theory. Similarly, we may mention the approximation algorithm for graph clique-width by Oum and Seymour, and the related WQO results.
We provide a brief introduction to structural matroid theory and demonstrate its close connection to Robertson and Seymour’s Graph Minor Project. We define matroid branch-width (and tree-width), outlining their fundamental properties. We also discuss matroidal analogues to the results of Courcelle and others on the computability of MSO-definable graph properties on graphs of bounded tree-width.
7 December 2004
Ing. Jan Žižka, CSc., Faculty of Informatics, Masaryk University, Brno
Discovering Complex Patterns by Machine Learning
Abstract: Humans are generally able to solve difficult problems simply by learning from specific and generalised examples that provide knowledge — especially when there is no suitable, analytical and exact way to define and complete complex tasks. Ideally, machines should be able to learn in a similar way to humans. Chess is an excellent and well-established model for testing artificial intelligence tasks.
This talk deals with a particular form of pattern recognition using machine learning. The patterns are specific chess positions in which the white player can sacrifice the bishop (placed on the diagonal b1–h7) on the square h7: Bxh7+. A computer learns whether the sacrifice leads to a win or a loss, i.e. a classification task based on the outcomes of examples from real games. An entropy-based decision tree is used as the learning algorithm. The algorithm does not utilise any chess rules or positional calculations; it simply learns from a selected set of 219 positive and 231 negative training examples, featuring eight different representations of real positions originally played by expert human players between 1601 and 1999. The accuracy ranges from 87 per cent to 93 per cent, depending on the specific representation of the game positions.
The aim is to demonstrate that machines can learn to quickly recognise typical situations and then decide which possibility should be analysed first.
14 December 2004
Dr Josef Pojsl, Trusted Network Solutions
Trends in firewall development
Abstract: The two fundamental technologies for firewalls – devices that separate networks with different levels of trust – are stateful IP filters and application proxy gateways. Their operating principles have remained virtually unchanged for several years.
The problem with both is that, in their basic form, they are often very ineffective against modern threats. Attacks are concealed within seemingly legitimate traffic, so, for example, identifying a URL that will cause a client to be infected by a worm – which will then spread throughout the entire internal network and effectively overwhelm it – is generally a very difficult problem.
Firewalls are therefore beginning to be combined with other technological tools so that they can fulfil their role more effectively. In this presentation, we will outline the current possibilities for integrating firewalls with antivirus programmes, intrusion detectors and other tools that analyse communication content, and we will consider the future potential of this approach.
