Abstract. Walker’s Alias Method, made linear by Vose, is the gold
standard for sampling from a fixed discrete distribution in constant time,
but its reliance on floating-point arithmetic makes it unreliable once
weights exceed double-precision resolution, a regime already reached by
applications such as combinatorial chemistry. Existing exact remedies
either rely on arbitrary-precision rational fractions, at a substantial cost
in time and memory, or, like the Rust weighted_rand crate, on rescaled
integer weights, without any published correctness proof. We introduce
the Integer-Alias Sampler, a construction and sampling scheme that
operates natively and exclusively on arbitrary-precision integer weights.
We prove its correctness, give tight bounds on the resulting table size,
and analyze the random-bit consumption of the associated generator.
Experiments, including a large-scale combinatorial-chemistry case study,
show that once weights genuinely require arbitrary precision, our sampler
is faster than the other exact Alias variants at comparable memory cost,
while the entropy-optimal samplers, which consume fewer random bits,
require more memory and are faster only on low-entropy distributions.
Keywords: Alias method · Exact random sampling · Arbitrary-precision
arithmetic · Discrete random variate generation.

Introduction
Sampling a discrete object from a prescribed, possibly non-uniform, distribution is
a recurring primitive in computational science, underlying Monte Carlo simulation,
agent-based modeling, and the stochastic components of modern machine learning
pipelines, where billions of independent draws must be produced under strict
time and memory budgets. The related question of generating or sampling from
a large, implicitly described universe of discrete structures has likewise been
studied extensively, from the classical treatises of Nijenhuis and Wilf [9] and
Kreher and Stinson [4] to Ruskey’s survey [10], more recent developments [2],
and Skiena’s algorithmic toolbox [13]. In practice, the objects to be generated
are rarely equally likely: they carry integer or rational weights, and an unbiased
sampler must reproduce the corresponding distribution exactly, however skewed
it is.
The reference solution to this weighted sampling problem is Walker’s Alias
Method [17,18]: it reorganizes a set of n unequal probabilities into n buckets

of equal capacity, each holding at most two outcomes, a primary value and its
designated alias, so that a single bucket, drawn uniformly at random, together
with one biased coin flip, suffices to recover the target distribution. Vose later
showed how to build this table in linear time [16], and Smith and Jacobson
provided a thorough analysis of the resulting sampler [14]. By front-loading the
whole complexity of the distribution into a one-time linear-time preprocessing
step, the Alias Method achieves O(1) sampling independently of n, making it
the gold standard whenever a fixed distribution must be queried a very large
number of times. As for every sampler considered here, a one-time preprocessing
is followed by a sampling step repeated as often as needed, each with its own
cost.
This efficiency, however, is traditionally obtained at the price of floating-point
arithmetic, and modern computational-science workloads increasingly expose
the fragility of this choice. Both the construction of the table and the sampling
step rely on repeatedly comparing and renormalizing probabilities represented as
IEEE 754 doubles, so that rounding drift can silently corrupt the classification of
outcomes during preprocessing, and bit-creep can corrupt the coin-flip threshold
during sampling. As soon as the total weight of a distribution exceeds 253 ,
the granularity of double-precision floats becomes coarser than the weight of
individual items, which then acquire a structurally zero probability of ever being
drawn. Such regimes are no longer isolated cases: in combinatorial chemistry,
the space of chemically plausible molecules grows so quickly with the number of
atoms that it must be sampled rather than enumerated, with reference libraries
already ranging from about 108 molecules for the smallest structures to several
trillion compounds for modern make-on-demand libraries [15], well beyond 264 for
molecules of about thirty heavy atoms. Monterrubio-Chanca and von Rudorff [7],
whose open-source nablachem package [8] we use in our own experiments, address
this regime with a pipeline whose final step is precisely a weighted random draw
among combinatorial groups, over weights of several hundred bits, well beyond
the reach of a double-precision mantissa.
Several directions have been explored to make the Alias Method robust
to this kind of scale. The most direct remedy is to abandon floating-point
numbers altogether and represent probabilities as arbitrary-precision rational
fractions (when it is possible), which restores mathematical exactness but incurs
a substantial overhead in both running time and memory, since every comparison
and table entry now manipulates a pair of big integers. An alternative, adopted
by the Rust ecosystem’s weighted_rand crate [11], is to restrict the input to nonnegative integer weights, instead of probabilities, so that the whole construction
can be carried out with exact integer arithmetic; this avoids fractions entirely,
but its construction departs from Vose’s and its correctness had, to the best
of our knowledge, never been established in the literature, and, as we show in
Section 3, it also produces alias tables with systematically fewer alias-free cells
than Vose’s construction, which slows down the sampling step. Orthogonally,
entropy-optimal samplers such as Fldr [12] and its refinement Aldr [1] build
on the discrete-distribution-generating trees of Knuth and Yao [3] to approach

the information-theoretic minimum number of random bits per sample, at the
cost of a preprocessing and sampling cost that grows with the entropy of the
distribution, again without native support for arbitrary-precision weights.
In this paper we introduce the Integer-Alias Sampler, a construction and
sampling scheme that operates natively and exclusively on arbitrary-precision
integer weights, without ever resorting to floating-point numbers or rational
fractions. We revisit Walker and Vose’s construction in this exact integer setting,
present the key-ideas for proving the correctness of the Rust integer-weight
variant [11], and prove that our own construction, built around a natural integer
cell size, yields alias tables with more alias-free cells, and hence a faster sampling
step, together with matching tight bounds on the size of the resulting table
(Sections 2 and 3). We design an efficient random generation procedure on this
table, together with a virtual-object rejection mechanism, and bound its average
random-bit consumption, which we then compare experimentally with the nearoptimal consumption of the entropy-optimal samplers of [12,1]. We implement
all of the above under arbitrary-precision arithmetic, coupled with a background
thread that continuously refills a cryptographically secure random-bit stream,
overlapping bit generation with sampling, supporting distributions whose number
of objects and individual weights can both grow without any fixed-width bound.
Finally, we experimentally compare our approach against the Fraction-Alias
sampler, the Rust-Alias sampler [11], and arbitrary-precision adaptations of
Fldr [12] and Aldr [1], including a large-scale combinatorial-chemistry case
study derived from [7,15], showing that once weights genuinely require arbitrary
precision, our sampler offers a favorable trade-off between sampling time, memory
footprint, and random-bit consumption: it is faster than the other exact Alias
variants at comparable memory cost, while Fldr and Aldr consume fewer
random bits at a higher memory cost (Section 4).

Alias algorithm beyond floating numbers rounding
errors
The Alias Method, designed by Walker in consecutive papers [17,18], represents
a fundamental shift in how we sample from discrete probability distributions.
Walker’s geometric approach transforms a set of n unequal probabilities into
n uniform buckets of equal capacity, each containing at most two outcomes, a
primary value and its designated alias, by reallocating the surplus from highprobability outcomes to fill the deficits of low-probability ones. Sampling then
operates in constant time, regardless of the number of possible outcomes, requiring
only a uniformly drawn bucket and a single biased coin flip to pick between its
two outcomes.
While Walker’s approach is elegant, its efficiency is largely attributed to
Vose’s 1991 refinement [16], which introduced a linear-time construction of the
bucket table (a phase we call preprocessing in what follows). Vose achieves this
by maintaining two worklists (e.g., stacks) classifying outcomes as small or large
relative to the mean, and systematically pairing underfilled with overfilled buckets,

improving on Walker’s original super-linear construction. The combination of
Walker’s O(1) sampling and Vose’s O(n) preprocessing makes the Alias Method
the reference method whenever a fixed distribution must be sampled a very large
number of times, with a decisive advantage over binary-search-based methods as
the number of objects grows.
The practical implementation of the Alias Method, however, must contend
with finite-precision floating-point arithmetic, which introduces subtle vulnerabilities in both phases. During preprocessing, the iterative normalization and
reallocation of probabilities can lead to precision drift, causing the total probability mass to deviate from unity or outcomes to be misclassified between worklists.
During sampling, the same imprecision can corrupt the coin-flip threshold: even
a microscopic deviation, such as a probability intended to be exactly 1.0 stored
as an infinitesimally smaller fraction, can erroneously trigger the alias outcome,
while the discrete granularity of the underlying pseudo-random number generator
can further distort the intended distribution over the course of massive Monte
Carlo trials.
To address rounding errors in both the preprocessing and simulation phases,
the most effective approach is to replace standard floating-point arithmetic
with arbitrary-precision fractions. In practice, since the floating number have
finite precision, the probabilities can be represented as rational numbers, i.e.
as fractions of arbitrary size: to that order, the distribution of the objects is
based on some non-negative integer weights. This restriction is directly reflected
in the probability distribution of the objets, because the probability of any
given object is then a rational number. This technique effectively treats the
input as a mathematically perfect probability distribution (without any consideration on approximations in the inputs), eliminating the precision drift and
bit-creep that typically plague massive Monte Carlo trials. By maintaining exact
rational representations, the software ensures that the statistical integrity of
the simulation remains uncompromised, regardless of the number of iterations
performed. Adapting Vose’s preprocessing to exact fractions follows the same
two-stack classification scheme as the general integer construction we present
in Algorithm 1 (there parametrized by an integer cell size cs rather than by
fractional probabilities); since no rounding error occurs, the resulting code is
in fact simpler than Vose’s original implementation. However, relying on exact
rational arithmetic introduces significant computational overhead and increased
memory usage compared to native floating-point types, which impact overall
performance in large-scale simulations.

Proposition 1. Rust algorithm is correct for the building of the Alias Table.
The proof key-ideas are similar than those we present for our algorithm in Theorem 1.
Both tables produced by Vose and Rust have the same number of buckets,
but the number of buckets without aliases may differ. Let us provide an extreme
case to compare Vose’s and Rust’s approaches. Consider a set of n objects where
all objects have weight 2, except for the first two objects which have respectively
weights 1 and 3. The total weight is w = 2n, i.e., the average weight is 2. In
Vose’s approach, the first n − 2 objects (all with weight 2) are directly placed
into the result table without aliases. In Rust’s approach, due to the LIFO policy
(Last-In, First-Out for stacks), and the classification of objects of average weight
as large, the processing order is different. The largest object is processed after
other large objects and each object with weight 2 is used to complete a small
object. This lead to a situation where only a single object ends up in the return
table without an alias.
Proposition 2. The number of cells without aliases in the table produced by Rust
is smaller than or equal to the one in the table by Vose. Hence, for the sampling
step, the approach by Rust is less efficient than the one of Vose sampling.
Indeed, the more buckets without aliases, the more efficient the sampling step,
since an alias-free bucket returns its object directly, while an aliased one requires
an extra biased coin flip.
Rust’s integer-weight approach thus remains an interesting way to avoid
floating-point arithmetic, but it suffers from two drawbacks: the integer weights
may grow very large, and the resulting table has fewer alias-free buckets, which
impacts both memory and sampling throughput at scale. Note that deciding
whether a table with the maximum number of alias-free entries can be constructed
is itself N P -complete; we refer the reader to [14] and references therein for this
research direction.

Large-scale sampling experiments
This section is built on our code repository, which is available at GitHub. All the
competitive methods are implemented in C, using arbitrary precision arithmetic
and a flow of cryptographic random bits. A part of the implementation is devoted
to reproduce our experimental results. Experiments were conducted on a MacBook
Pro (Apple M4 Pro chip, 14-core CPU) with 48GB of unified memory.
4.1
Overview of the experimental approach
This section evaluates the practical performance of the algorithms introduced in
Section 3 with a focus on measurable scalability limits, targeting five quantities:
preprocessing time, sampling throughput, memory footprint, random-bit consumption, and savings. All computations use exact integer arithmetic, so results
are free of floating-point rounding artifacts, and rely on compact data structures
that make explicit the core trade-off between entropic efficiency (in bits) and
time efficiency.
A distinctive feature of our implementation is native support for arbitraryprecision arithmetic (to the best of our knowledge, the only one in our comparison
to provide exact sampling at arbitrary precision). Sampling draws from a background thread that continuously refills a bitstream, enabling the use of expensive
cryptographic random bits with a limited impact on performance, since bit production is decoupled from and overlapped with sampling (reported times include
its CPU time).

Case Study: Combinatorial Chemistry
What is following illustrates how exact sampling becomes essential when the
underlying combinatorial space is too large, as in several nowadays applications.
The chemical space, i.e. the set of all combinatorially plausible stable molecules,
is far too vast to be enumerated. Reference databases report sizes ranging from
9.7 · 107 molecules for the smallest structures (e.g., GDB-17, up to 17 heavy
atoms) to several trillion compounds for modern make-on-demand combinatorial
libraries [15], and beyond roughly thirty heavy atoms the number of possible
structures far exceeds 264 ≈ 1.8 · 1019 . Consequently, any statistical analysis based
on a subset of this space is structurally biased unless the sample is representative
of the true underlying distribution.
To address this context, Monterrubio-Chanca and von Rudorff [7] proposed a
method for producing unbiased, representative samples of chemical space without
ever enumerating it, applicable to molecules containing on the order of 30 heavy
atoms. Obviously, this is a regime in which exhaustive enumeration is impossible.
The method proceeds in five steps:
1. Generation of all theoretically possible chemical formulas by integer partitioning;
2. Grouping of these formulas by degree sequence;
3. Distinction between “pure” and “non-pure” sequences, depending on whether
several elements of the same valence appear;
4. Estimation of the size of each group, either through exact counting or through
a heuristic based on the graph’s average path length when exact counting
proves too costly;
5. Weighted random sampling among the groups, proportional to their estimated
size, followed by the sampling of a representative molecule within the chosen
group.
We note that the two steps 1. and 5. are classical in the context of combinatorics
and combinatorial algorithm design. Especially step 5. is a classical application
of the Alias method. All their results are reproducible through their open-source
nablachem Python package [8].
Using their library, we derive the distribution of chemical molecules containing
32 heavy atoms, among Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen, Nitrogen, and Fluorine. The
total number of molecules is n = 20 484, and the sum of their weights is about
1.25 · 2168 ≈ 4.7 · 1050 . Thus in the following benchmarks for the sampling method,
we used these parameters, which seem representative of the actual combinatorial
chemistry research. Remark that step 5. cannot be performed without bias in
their Python library based on floating-point arithmetic samplers that are not
precise enough in this regime. Note that exactness is understood with respect
to the supplied weights: when some group sizes are only estimated heuristically
(step 4.), our sampler reproduces this estimated distribution exactly, but does
not correct the estimation itself. More details about this case study case be found
in our code repository.

Algorithms with arbitrary precision integers
Following the design of our Integer-Alias Sampler, the next phase focuses on an
experimental evaluation of our algorithm against existing methods (adapted with
arbitrary precision). We start from the code repository of Saad et al. [12] in order
to add our implementation in their comparison: we thus are able to run all their
benchmarks. We have adapted the original memory consumption analysis to the
precise size used by the data structures built by the preprocessing step.
While Saad et al. compared many literature approaches to emphasize random
bit consumption, we retain the three most relevant ones for our constraints
(time, memory, and random bits) at arbitrary precision, all exact (floating-point
samplers are left out: on 179 of our 201 distributions, they would never draw
some objects, whose bucket share nwi /w is below 2−53 ):
– Fldr Sampler: Saad et al. [12] that we adapted for arbitrary-precision
integers;
– Aldr Sampler: Draper and Saad [1], an improvement of FLDR in the context
of random bit consumption, that we also adapted for arbitrary-precision
integers;
– Rust-Alias Sampler: Rust Developers, a variant of Walker version coded
in Rust with integer arithmetic [11], where we also have modified types to
support arbitrary-precision integers;
– Fraction-Alias Sampler: our new exact version of Walker-Alias using exact
rational fractions (with arbitrary precision) instead of floating-point numbers;
– our Integer-Alias Sampler: our new algorithmic approach, in two implementations of the same table: v1 saves random bits with Lumbroso’s optimal
Uniform and Bernoulli [6]; v2 saves time with Lemire’s Uniform [5] and
a Bernoulli test on the same 64-bit word (64 bits per draw).
The experimental protocol starts by fixing the distribution size (the number
of objects), that we take to be n = 20 484 and the total weight distribution to
be the one computed in the context of combinatorial chemistry (see Section 4.2)
w ≈ 4.7 · 1050 . The measurements were collected on a set of random distributions
following a Dirichlet distribution. Using a Dirichlet distribution allows varying the
entropy while maintaining fixed values for parameters n and w. The distribution
itself is not of major importance, once we can choose for the average weight of an
object or the entropy of the resulting random distribution (e.g. a gaussian distribution of weights would have given the same kind of experimental simulations).
The observed entropy corresponds to Shannon entropy. The latter is a central
theoretical measure in the context of random sampling. We refer the reader to
the seminal paper of Knuth and Yao [3] for a general overview. Essentially, we
note that the closer a distribution is to a uniform distribution, the higher its
entropy (bounded by log2 (n)). Conversely, a strongly biased distribution (e.g.,
one element with a very large weight and many elements with a weight of 1)
results in lower entropy. For each distribution, among the 200 random ones, 10
millions of objects have been sampled to measure the average sampling time and
the average number of random bits consumed.

Figure 1 (left) plots the runtime of the sampling step against the memory
footprint of the internal data structures, one dot per random distribution (averaged over 10 million samples; 2−20 s = 0.95 µs and 220 B = 1MB). The tight
horizontal clustering of Alias-based methods reflects their O(1) sampling step,
structurally independent of the distribution, whereas Fldr and Aldr spread
much more widely, exposing their sensitivity to its shape. The picture traces
an implicit Pareto frontier: Fldr and Aldr are fast but memory-intensive on
favorable distributions, while Alias-based approaches remain compact and predictable. Within this family, our Integer-Alias Sampler consistently runs faster
than Rust-Alias at comparable memory cost, since normalizing by cs rather than
by the full cumulative weight w speeds up the sampling step regardless of the
distribution; v2 is the fastest of all, 2.3 times faster than v1.
Figure 1 (right) confirms that Alias-based runtimes stay near-constant across
the entropy spectrum: the preprocessing step encodes the whole distribution into
the table, so each draw only needs a uniform bucket selection and a single Bernoulli
test. Fldr and Aldr, built on the DDG-tree framework of Knuth and Yao [3],
instead consume random bits one at a time to navigate a binary decision tree whose
expected depth equals the distribution’s Shannon entropy; they are therefore
faster at low entropy (skewed distributions yield short, predictable tree paths)
and slower as entropy approaches log2 (n) (near-uniform distributions require
paths of maximal depth). The crossover, at roughly half the maximum entropy,
marks the point where Aldr’s entropy-proportional cost exceeds the constant
cost of our sampler. The combinatorial-chemistry case study of Section 4.2, being
strongly skewed, falls in the low-entropy regime most favorable to Fldr and
Aldr; even there, our Integer-Alias Sampler stays within the same order of
magnitude in runtime while using strictly less memory.

compact than Aldr across all entropies, and than Fldr except at low entropy,
where its tree is small (we report the exact footprint of the preprocessed data
structure). Within the Alias family, although Rust-Alias’s integer weights are n
times larger than ours (it normalizes by the total weight w rather than by cs),
our table needs at most one extra cell (since cs ≤ n − 1), so both approaches

Among the remaining algorithms, Fldr and Aldr consume fewer random
bits than our sampler, with Fldr reaching the information-theoretic optimum
of Knuth and Yao [3]; however this advantage narrows as entropy grows, since
the dominant cost then becomes the uniform bucket selection (log2 (n) bits for
every algorithm). Near the uniform distribution, this fixed cost dominates and
our overhead relative to the optimum becomes negligible: in that regime, our
sampler simultaneously achieves O(1) sampling time, a memory footprint among
the smallest of the compared methods, and near-optimal bit consumption.
