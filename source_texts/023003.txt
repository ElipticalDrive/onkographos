Abstract
Volume rendering methods enable the user to explore interactively scalar data on regularly spaced three-dimensional grids. This article discusses how to use this method to explore and analyse three-dimensional tensor fields. The proposed visualization makes use of the programmability of modern graphics hardware and of “Line Integral Convolution”, a texture-based technique for the visualization of vector fields. While an example from geomechanics is used for presentation purposes, the rendering methods introduced are generic and would suit other application areas that involve volumetric data with several attributes equally well.
Introduction
Several quantities in three-dimensional geoscientific simulations are described by symmetric tensors of the second order. Examples of such quantities are the anisotropic permeability in fluid flow calculations or the stress tensor as used in geomechanics. Tensors of the second order in 3D space are represented by a 3×3 matrix which is symmetric about its diagonal. The tensors describe three magnitudes (the principal components) along three mutual perpendicular axes in space, as well as the orientations of these axes relative to the superordinated coordinate system. For the permeability tensor of an anisotropic medium, they represent the minimum, intermediate and maximum permeability. For the stress tensor, the principal components are the major, intermediate and minor principal stresses σ1, σ2, σ3, which together define the shape of the stress ellipsoid. This paper uses examples which involve the stress tensor that is used in geomechanics to describe the stress state in the subsurface. The stress state at a certain point on a well trajectory, together with the pore pressure and the mechanical properties of the rock at this point, determine the borehole's stability (Fjaer et al., 1992, e.g.). Therefore, stress is used as part of the mechanical earth model during the well planning and drilling process (Plumb et al., 2000, e.g.). The 3D stress field required can, for example, be computed with the help of a reservoir simulator (Stone et al., 2000).
Visualizing all the tensor's attributes of scalar and vector type is very difficult, if not impossible. This is especially true if the structure of the tensor field is complex because the resulting image becomes quickly cluttered. The visualization of a large number of attributes can be substituted to a certain extent by the interactive exploration of a subset, provided that the user can switch rapidly between the different attributes. A further reduction in visual complexity can be achieved by concentrating on features which are important for the problem under review. For example, while planning a well, a drilling engineer might wish to identify regions with high differential stress to avoid drilling into them. If such a region cannot be avoided, the engineer would at least try not to drill parallel to the intermediate stress direction, as this would mean the exertion of maximum shear stress on the borehole. A good visualization for this use-case would show the regions of high differential stress, the magnitude of the major stress and simultaneously would make the orientation of the intermediate stress visible within this region. Two of the three attributes which need to be explored in this use-case are scalar data (magnitude of differential and major stress) and one is represented by a vector (the orientation of intermediate stress).
To illustrate the meaning of the tensor's components it is worth looking at the coherence between the magnitude and orientation of the principle components (λ1, λ2, λ3). If the magnitudes and their orientation as normalized vectors (n⇒1,n⇒2,n⇒3) in the superordinated coordinate system is known, the stress tensor for this coordinate system can be expressed according to Jaeger (1966):σxxτxyτxzτyxσyyτyzτzxτzyσzz=n⇒1xn⇒1yn⇒1zn⇒2xn⇒2yn⇒2zn⇒3xn⇒3yn⇒3z*λ1000λ2000λ3*n⇒1xn⇒2xn⇒3xn⇒1yn⇒2yn⇒3yn⇒1zn⇒2zn⇒3z.Conversely, if the tensor is given for the superordinate coordinate system, the eigensystem can be solved and the orientation of the principle components and their magnitude obtained as eigenvectors and eigenvalues. Solving the eigensystem transforms the problem of visualizing a 3D tensor field into the problem of visualizing three vector fields that are each represented by a vector type component (the eigenvector) and a scalar component (the eigenvalue).
3D scalar fields are often visualized with the help of isosurfaces. This can be done interactively by using volume rendering techniques, as is now common for data sets from medical tomography and is described in Van Gelder and Kim (1996) or Westermann and Ertl (1998). For the visualization of vector fields, several methods have been developed, mainly for flow visualization. Most of them are based on visualizing the properties with the help of geometric objects, such as arrow glyphs, streamlines and streamsurfaces. This only permits the visualization of a low amount of data, and relatively uncomplicated structures, since the objects in the foreground obscure those behind them. Streamlines are a common concept for looking at the changes of direction in a tensor field. If the vector field is a steady state flow field, they represent the way a particle would move from a to b and hence the flowline can be evaluated by computing x⇒(t)=∫0tv⇒dt. The magnitude of the vector field can be shown by colour coding the streamlines.
Hultquist (1992) introduces the use of streamsurfaces to examine fluid velocity fields. He describes the streamsurface as the surface which is formed by an infinite set of streamlines that are rooted along a continuous line segment. In tensor visualization this line segment could be a streamline along one of the tensor's principal directions. A set of streamlines is calculated, starting from the line segment, and thereafter connected as polygons. The resulting surface is always tangential to one of the principal components. Delmarcelle and Hesselink (1993) extend the concept of streamlines to hyperstreamlines for the visualization of tensor fields. The streamline follows one direction of the tensor field while the two remaining transverse directions are encoded into its cross sections. Jeremic et al. (2002) show how hyperstreamlines and hyperstreamsurfaces could be applied in the visualization of 3D stress simulations from geomechanics. Similar visualization methods were developed that are texture based. They have in common that they use certain features of a vector or tensor field to generate an image. Cabral and Leedom (1993) introduce line integral convolution (LIC), a texture-based technique that is explained in more detail in Section 3 of this report, in order to visualize vector fields as a dense set of streamlines. Zheng and Pang (2003) extend the LIC algorithm to render tensor fields in a manner that is related to hyperstreamlines. Hotz et al. (2004) use the eigenvalues of the tensor field to influence the LIC and the input noise texture in a way that results in a dense texture (showing densely spaced streamlines) in regions of compression and a sparse texture (showing widely spaced streamlines) in regions of extension. Kindlmann et al. (2000) visualize tensor fields with the help of reaction diffusion textures or of a classification of the tensor at each point.
Since it is usually not possible to show all attributes simultaneously for the 3D field as a whole, it is often an advantage if several attributes can be interlinked or an attribute can be shown in dependence on another one interactively. This work investigates the possibility of using volume rendering techniques that provide the necessary degree of interactivity, in order to explore 3D tensor fields for features in which the user is interested. Volume rendering for the scalar components of the tensor is combined with texture-based methods to show the vector components. As a proof of this concept, a Windows-based software prototype was developed using Open Inventor (Wernecke, 1994) and OpenGL (Woo et al., 1999).
Newer commodity graphics boards permit the use of multiple volumes and for programming the graphics pipe at the fragment processing stage. This makes it possible to operate directly on several precomputed volumetric images simultaneously and facilitates the development of software that allows for the interactive visualization of several attributes at the same time. For the above-mentioned use-case this would mean that we can use the data volume for differential stress to render an isosurface that shows in which region its magnitude is above a certain threshold. With the help of a second volumetric data set that contains the magnitude of the major stress, the colour on the isosurface can be defined accordingly. As a third item, the visualization of the streamlines that indicates the direction of the intermediate stress (σ2) can be bound to these volumes. This results in an image where the streamlines are only shown in regions where the differential stress is above a certain threshold and are coloured according to the magnitude of the major stress. For the drilling engineer this means: “Avoid regions where the streamlines are rendered. If you cannot avoid them, do not drill parallel to their orientation as this would expose the borehole to maximum shear stress”.
Fig. 1 shows a simulated mechanics data set that has been rendered using the software prototype. The input data are the result of a finite element simulation that was generated using the structural mechanics module of the finite element package FemlabTM, which runs within the MatlabTM environment. The geometry is a 2.0×0.8×1.0m large granite block with two holes through it. The stress field is simulated for compression along the block's long axis. The top of the figure shows the geometry on the left side and the resulting FEM mesh on the right. The bottom illustrates how the simulation results are presented, on the left as it can be done with MatlabTM (for the magnitude of one of the stress tensor's components) and on the right as proposed in this paper.
This report will first introduce the concept of volume rendering with shading for the interactive exploration of the scalar components of the data. It will then illustrate how LIC can be used within a volume rendering environment to visualize vector fields and show how new graphics hardware with a graphics programming unit (GPU) can be used to explore interactively two scalar and one vector components of a tensor at the same time.

Results
This article describes a method that can interactively show two independent scalar and one directional components of a 3D tensor field simultaneously. As the method is based on hardware accelerated volume rendering, it enables users to query interactively the 3D field for certain features they are interested in. The implemented prototype that performs the visualization proposed here runs under the windows operating system on a standard Dell workstation with an ATI FireGL X1-256p graphics board.

Computer rendering of high-frequency stacking patterns in cross-sectional stratigraphic simulations using the line integral convolution
2022, Marine and Petroleum Geology
Show abstract
Here, we show a novel use of a mathematical technique in stratigraphic visualization, which has been effectively used in other fields, to generate high-resolution strata texture maps to improve the rendering of two-dimensional forward stratigraphic simulations during post-processing. The texture maps are obtained by convolving stratigraphic timelines with a random dotted pattern that produces realistic layerings similar to those observed in seismic cross sections. The texture mappings are scale-independent and are not affected by artifacts like moiré patterns. Thus, the stratigraphy in poorly resolved areas is rendered at the same level of detail as those zones with high numerical resolution. The texture maps can also be combined with other attributes generated by numerical simulations like sedimentation rate or grain size to bring into the foreground subtle details that otherwise could be swamped by large amplitude or high frequency stratigraphic signals.

Three-dimensional colour functions were designed for visually agreeable and computationally efficient stress state visualisation in failure analysis. The colours they generate depend on three aspects of the stress state: the distance to failure, the stress character (compressive or tensile), and the relative magnitude of the intermediate principal stress. Thus, the colour of a single pixel carries information about all three scalar components of the stress tensor at a point. By discarding the intermediate principal stress information, a three-dimensional colour function degenerates to a family of two-dimensional colour functions with free palette choice parameter. We demonstrate the performance of our colouring technique in the application example – visualisation of the stress field in the Brazilian test.
