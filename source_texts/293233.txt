Abstract
We present the classical coordinate-free formalism for forward and backward mode ad in the
real and complex setting. We show how to formally derive the forward and backward formulae for a
number of matrix functions starting from basic principles.
This document is aimed at a reader who has some experience with auto-differentiation (ad) frameworks (PyTorch, Tensorflow, JAX. . . ) and would want to get a better understanding of the mathematical
ideas behind their forward and backward ad engines. We expect that the reader will be able to derive
from scratch forward and backward formulae for virtually any function in PyTorch, Tensorflow, or JAX
after reading these notes.
We do not assume any previous mathematical knowledge, but some mathematical maturity, as we will
present the necessary results from different areas of mathematics in the text. That said, some familiarity
with linear algebra and multivariate calculus would be helpful.
There will be a plethora of examples throughout the text to make the abstract definitions easier to
digest. We will omit some computations in the examples, but these will always be simple enough so that
the reader should be able to deduce them themselves after some thinking.
Sections 1 and 2 contain a review of a number of definitions and results from linear algebra and
multivariate calculus. We encourage the reader that already feels comfortable with this material to have
a quick read through these, as we will use them to set the notation.
Sections 3 and 4 show how to use the definitions from the first two sections to derive the forward and
backward (ad) formulae for most functions used in machine learning or statistics.
Section 5 treats the more advance topic of differentiation of functions of complex variable. It may be
skipped on a first read.

Coordinate-free Linear Algebra
In this section we answer the question: What is a coordinate-free approach and why do we care? With
this objective in mind, we define abstract vector spaces, bases, and linear maps. A secondary objective
of this section is to show that thinking of a linear map as something that preserves the operations of a
vector space is easier than thinking about it as a matrix.
1.1
Vector spaces
Definition 1.1. A vector space over a field K (think K = R or K = C) is a set V with two operations
+ : V × V → V and · : K × V → V such that for every u, v, w ∈ V and a, b ∈ K
• Associativity: (u + v) + w = u + (v + w)
• Compatibility: a · (b · v) = (ab) · v
• Identity: There exists a 0 ∈ V such that
∀v ∈ V , v + 0 = v
• Scalar identity: 1 · v = v where 1 ∈ K
is the multiplicative identity
• Inverse: For every v ∈ V there is a −v ∈ V
such that v + (−v) = 0
• Distributivity 1: a · (u + v) = a · u + a · v
• Distributivity 2: (a + b) · u = a · u + b · u
• Commutativity: u + v = v + u
As with any mathematical multiplication, we often omit the symbol and simply write av = a · v.
The elements of V are called vectors and the elements of K are called scalars.
Vector spaces over the real (resp. complex) numbers are called real (resp. complex) vector spaces.
The first time one encounters these axioms, they can be quite overwhelming. They become a bit easier
to digest once one realises that all they are doing is to model Rn abstractly. Once one sits down and
checks that Rn fulfils all these axioms, we find the first example of a vector space.
Example 1.2. Rn is a vector space over R.
Rn is the most important example of a vector space. In fact, virtually all the vector spaces over the
real numbers that we will work with will have an Rn lurking behind one way or another. Whenever we
think about an abstract vector space, it is good to picture in mind something that looks like Rn to fix
the ideas. That being said, there are other vector spaces.
Example 1.3. Cn is a vector space over C.
Cn is also a real vector space, as addition of vectors is performed on the real and complex part
separately. In fact, Cn is pretty much the same as R2n = Rn × Rn as a real vector space.
We finish with some slightly less conventional examples of vector spaces.
Example 1.4. The set of polynomials in one variable of degree less or equal to n is a real vector space.
The set of polynomials in one variable of degree exactly equal to n is not a real vector space.
The set of matrices Rm×n is a real vector space.
The set of tensors with real entries of a fixed shape is a real vector space.
The set of infinite sequences of real numbers (ai )∞
i=1 = (a1 , a2 , . . . ) is a real vector space.
It is a good exercise to convince oneself that all these examples are what they claim to be. In general,
to check that an object is indeed a vector space, it tends to be enough to check that multiplying a vector
by a scalar gives a vector in the set and adding two vectors in the set gives a vector in the set (the second
example above is a counterexample of this).

1.3
Linear maps
The other object that linear algebra studies are linear maps. These are maps between vector spaces
that respect the vector space structure.
Definition 1.10. A map T : V → W between vector spaces over a field K is said to be K-linear—or
simply linear—if
T (u + v) = T (u) + T (v)
T (av) = aT (v)
∀u, v ∈ V, a ∈ K.

T (B) is a basis of W for one basis B of V , then T (B ′ ) is a basis of W for any basis B ′ of V , that is, T

1.4
Differences between a real vector space and Rn
For now, we have set up the basic ideas of linear algebra: We have a vector space, which is an abstract
space in which we can add vectors and multiply vectors by scalars, we can encode most of the information
of a vector space into a basis, and we can map vector spaces to other vector spaces via linear maps. In
particular, if we have a linear isomorphism between vector spaces, this allows us to translate operations
on one to operations on the other and back, rendering them somewhat equivalent.
On the other hand, while doing all this, we have barely talked about Rn . This is a bit odd given that
it is the main space that we want to study. The only thing that we have mentioned is that “real vector
spaces model Rn ” and that, as one could expect, Rn is a real vector space. But why would we care about
real vector spaces in the abstract? Why not simply work on Rn ?
There are two main differences between real vector spaces and Rn . One huge and one subtler.

The point here is that, given a finite-dimensional abstract vector space, there is no canonical choice of a
basis. It is for this reason that the approach to linear algebra that simply talks about vector spaces and
linear maps and not about matrices and Rn is often referred to as coordinate-free. There are times, as
it happened in Example 1.15, that there exists a clear choice, but others there is not distinguished basis.

Backward Mode ad
In this section, we go over the most popular method of automatic differentiation: Backward ad. This
method has the advantage that, in order to compute the backward pass for a model, one does not have
to deal with matrices—the Jacobians associated to the differential of the model—but just with vectors
of the same size of the parameters.

Remark (Real differentiable vs. complex differentiable). Here we have defined the real differential
as an R-linear map, that is, a map such that (df )x (av) = a(df )x (v) for a ∈ R. If we require the differential
to be C-linear—that is, (df )x (av) = a(df )x (v) for a ∈ C—we get the definition of a complex differentiable
map, often called holomorphic map.
It should be clear that, if a complex map is complex differentiable, it is also real differentiable, but
the opposite is not true. Consider for example f (z) = z for z ∈ C. We have that f (az) = af (z) for
a ∈ C, so it is not C-linear, but it is R-linear as f (az) = af (z) for a ∈ R. Luckily, we will not need to use
holomorphic maps, as real differentiable maps will be enough to compute differentials and gradients.
