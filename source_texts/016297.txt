Abstract

low and the rendering performance high. Recent works like efficient
Sparse Voxel Octrees (SVOs) [Laine and Karras 2010], Gigavoxels
[Crassin et al. 2009] or Sparse Voxel DAGs [Kämpe et al. 2013],
prove the benefit of using voxels in rendering systems. They all
have in common that the sparsity of a scene is exploited. Empty
and filled spaces can be efficiently encoded in a hierarchical manner to store more detailed information of the scene geometry. The
voxelization of triangle meshes is still the main method to generate
such an SVO structure.
In this paper, we present the first grid-free, out-of-core GPU voxelization method. Our method combines efficient parallel triangle
voxelization on GPU with out-of-core technologies in order to allow the processing of scenes with large triangle counts at a high
resolution. We directly generate the voxelized data in a sparse
voxel octree (SVO) representation, without any intermediate grid
structure (“grid-free”). We apply triangle preprocessing and avoid
atomic operations, thus leading to an optimized balanced GPU
workload and efficient parallel triangle processing. Compared to existing out-of-core CPU approaches, we manage a proper handling
of voxel attributes, i.e. all triangle attributes contributing to a voxel
are accessible when calculating the voxel attribute.
In-core GPU methods, like the approach by Schwarz and Seidel [2010], perform very well. However, in-core GPU-based methods for voxelization rapidly reach their limit in terms of available
memory if highly detailed models with high voxel resolution are to
be voxelized. On the other hand, generating SVOs with in-core CPU
voxelization techniques, as presented by Laine and Karras [2011],
allows the processing of much larger scenes at higher resolution.
However, these methods are far less performance efficient, as specific hardware functionalities of the GPU are not exploited. Both,
in-core GPU and CPU methods can handle triangle attributes such
as color and normals properly on a per-voxel level, as all triangle
attribute contributions to a voxel can be held in-core.
We test and compare our approach to state-of-the-art methods and
demonstrate its viability in terms of speed, input triangle count, resolution and output quality.

The out-of-core CPU method presented by
Baert et al. [2013] uses an efficient streaming approach for voxelization and SVO creation. First, the final grid is partitioned into
smaller grids that fit into memory. Then, all subgrids are sequentially processed by voxelizing the triangle stream and creating the
corresponding part of the SVO. An optimized SVO creation that
removes the need for processing a large number of empty subtrees
is proposed. In a subsequent work, Baert et al. [2014] suggest the
direct use of a voxel list, which improves the performance, but does
not remove the need of maintaining the full grid because there is
no estimation how large the voxel list can get for the subgrid. The
attributes of the voxels are created independently of the triangle
position, i.e. of the voxel’s barycentric coordinates with respect to
the triangle. Furthermore, the voxel attribute is generated by the
first triangle intersecting the voxel. In general, extending this approach to allow arbitrary attribute computation would require maintaining all triangle contributions per voxel and functionalities like
texture lookups re-implemented on CPU, leading to significant performance loss.

Related Work
Commonly, voxelization can be categorized into surface and solid
voxelization. While solid voxelization is meant to fill all voxels inside a watertight geometry, surface voxelization only fills voxels on
the boundary of the object. Our proposed method creates a surface
voxelization and uses the conservative voxelization of Schwarz and
Seidel [2010] to create a similar SVO structure to Laine and Karras [2010], but without contour information. Therefore, we focus
on methods that create an SVO from a surface voxelization. For a
recent in-depth discussion about voxelization, we refer the reader
to Laine [2013].
Schwarz and Seidel [2010] present a surface and
solid voxelization on the full grid, as well as a solid voxelization
method to an SVO representation. In all cases, they do not utilize
the GPU rasterizer. They exploit the sparsity in the voxelization by
determining and processing lists of active nodes in the octree construction. The octree structure is determined in a bottom-up fashion
starting from the parent nodes of the finest level to fill the leaf nodes
with the voxel data afterwards which means that the octree structure
and triangle data need to be stored completely in memory. The attributes of the generated voxels only contain binary information or
a scalar representing the coverage factor, while we store voxel color
and normal.
In-Core GPU
Our grid-free out-of-core GPU approach is similar to
Baert et al. [2013; 2014], as we also use triangle and voxel
streaming. However, in our approach all triangle attributes
per voxel are accessible while computing the voxel attributes.
Furthermore, we do not use subgrid-partitioning but triangle
sorting in order to be more flexible and grid-free. In contrast to
Baert et al. [2013; 2014], we use the conservative voxelization
instead of the surface voxelization, both proposed by Schwarz and
Seidel [2010], and we perform the voxelization on GPU rather than
CPU. In contrast to Schwarz and Seidel [2010], we create the leaf
voxels directly and create parts of the SVO bottom-up in a single
pass, making out-of-core processing possible.
A sparse voxelization on GPU that uses the hardware rasterizer is
proposed by Crassin and Green [2012]. The voxel generation is
done with a conservative rasterization. The tree generation is done
top-down by determining the octree-nodes that contain the generated voxels of the finest level. The resulting leaf nodes are filled
with the attributes from the voxel fragment list and averaged to the
coarser levels bottom-up. In terms of memory, this approach is limited to the available GPU memory for storing the complete voxel
fragment list and the resulting octree-structure.

Triangle Stream Processing
The main goal of the triangle processing is to prepare the given triangle set {Ti } in such a way, that parallelized batch processing can
efficiently be performed on GPU. Therefore, we apply a subdivision
scheme on the triangles (Sec. 4.1), sort the triangles appropriately

4.1
Subdivision of Triangles
The first step of our algorithm consists of pre-processing that sequentially consumes triangles to apply a subdivision scheme. The
main goal is to create triangles which are as homogeneous as possible with respect to the following criteria:
• The number of voxels generated per triangle Nvox/tri (Ti )
should be as equal as possible among all triangles
• The range of Morton indices m generated for the voxels of a
triangle determines how long a triangle stays active in the outof-core voxelization scheme. Thus, the Morton indices should
be as compact as possible, i.e.
(mmax (Ti ) − mmin (Ti ))/Nvox/tri (Ti ) → min!,
where mmin (Ti ) and mmax (Ti ) denote the minimum and maximal Morton index generated by triangle Ti , respectively.

Of course, we have to prevent the trivial solution of 1 voxel per
triangle, i.e. Nvox/tri (Ti ) should not be too small, because the I/O
time for transferring a higher triangle count would increase while
the computation time for processing less voxels per thread would
decrease. Therefore, the voxelization would not benefit from GPU
processing.
From the voxelization of a batch of triangles, we obtain a voxel attribute set, which is a list of voxel-attribute pairs containing nonunique Morton indices and attribute values of triangles at voxel positions. The Morton index [Morton 1966] is used to represent the
voxel indices because it allows to map multidimensional data to a
linear index that preserves locality, which is needed for the creation
of octree structures like the SVO.
Both criteria mainly depend on the size and shape of the triangles.
Additionally, the Morton-range criterion depends strongly on the
specific spatial location of the triangle and a determination of compacted Morton ranges would basically require voxelization already.
Therefore, we try to approximate both criteria by applying purely
geometric subdivision rules. While these rules can not avoid triangles that span a large Morton range, they limit the number of resulting voxels that need to be maintained during batch processing.

Morton Queueing
The Morton indices of the voxel grid directly determine the order
of node creation of the SVO. When the triangles are voxelized, we
can not ensure that all generated voxels can be directly consumed
for SVO creation, because the Morton index range covered by a
triangle may be large. Thus, these voxel-attribute pairs need to be
processed in the SVO creation much later. As voxel-attribute pairs
can not be skipped, we use the Morton queue in order to maintain all
generated voxel-attribute pairs from previous batches which have
not been processed yet. Therefore, the size of the Morton queue
varies according to the number of remaining voxel-attribute pairs
in each iteration.
Since our intention is to provide arbitrary, even order-dependent or
multi-pass operations in the attribute computation of an SVO node,
a node can only be finalized, if all child nodes have themselves been
finalized. Therefore, we use a stitch queue buffer that always stores
those SVO nodes and their attributes in a ”stitch queue” which are
required for processing the last immediate parent node on the current hierarchy level. Fig. 4 shows an example for the processing of a
voxel attribute set. A quadtree is used for simplicity. The parent indices of the bottom level are determined by an integer division of 4
(quadtree nodes). The child nodes 32 and 33 belong to the last parent node 8 and need to be stored in the stitch queue buffer, because
future triangle batches may create further child nodes, e.g node 34.
All other child nodes, e.g. 3, 17, 25, 26 and 29, are processed and
pushed to the next higher level. Again, the parent indices are determined. The nodes 4, 6 and 7 are moved to the stitch queue buffer,
because they belong to the largest parent index 1.
After adding the voxel attribute set of a batch to the Morton queue
and sorting the resulting queue, we determine the new voxel attribute set, which is usable for SVO creation, by splitting the Morton queue at the stop index (see Sec. 4.2 and Fig. 2) and an extraction of all voxels with a smaller Morton index. Using this method,
we ensure that no further triangle will intersect any voxel of the
extracted voxel attribute set, i.e. all future Morton indices will be
equal to or greater than the stop index.
Since the remaining voxel-attribute pairs block memory in the Morton queue, the number of elements in the Morton queue should be
kept small. The subdivision of triangles in Sec. 4.1 tries to limit
the Morton index range of the triangles to minimize the number of
voxels remaining in the Morton queue over a longer time. However,
this does not guarantee the prevention of overflow in extreme cases,
e.g. when a huge number of triangles contributes to a single voxel
or a large number of triangles spans very large Morton ranges.

Implementation Details
The proposed voxelization method is implemented in CUDA. For
the Morton queue handling and the triangle sorting, we use the
Thrust library1 . We sort the triangles on GPU, but if the sorting
data does not fit to GPU, Thrust allows us to easily switch to the
fallback of CPU sorting.
To achieve a more memory efficient storage of the voxel attributes
(Sec. 5.2), we encode color to the 16 bit RGB565 format, and
the normals to 16 bit, with the fast octahedron compression of
Cigolle et al. [2014], directly after the completed attribute creation
of one hierarchy level.
The Hairball scene leads to the longest processing time with a resolution of 8192, despite the comparably low triangle count. Apparently, the scene complexity in terms of sparsity has a much
larger influence on performance than the triangle count, because
more voxels contribute to the final tree. In comparison to Laine
and Karras [2011] we process the Hairball at resolution 8192 in
roughly half the time while we are nearly four times faster than
Baert et al. [2014].
For rendering the SVO, we use the method proposed in Laine and
Karras [2010]. They use specific bitmasks for an optimized access
of non-empty child nodes, preventing the storage of empty child
nodes. The traversal approach does not process empty nodes at all.
The linear ordering of voxel values that is needed for storing only
one pointer to find all children linearly in memory is implicitly assured by the sorted Morton order of the child nodes. The pointers,
represented as linear indices in an array, are created after the batch
processing just by counting and exclusively scanning the set bits in
the created bitmasks for the finished tree in a breadth-first order (see
”Create SVO Structure” of Alg. 4 and Alg. 5 in the supplemental
material).

In the Lucy scene, the triangle availability on all levels of Laine
and Karras [2011] gets disadvantageous. Baert et al. [2014] and our
approach voxelize the scene between one and two orders of magnitude faster, because only the leaf nodes use the triangles directly.
Furthermore, our approach is voxelizing this scene at a resolution
of 8192 faster than all other scenes, which is due to the high regularity of the input mesh leading to a good load-balance for our GPU
processing.

The Atlas dataset has the largest triangle count of the test scenes.
We could not process the scene with the framework of Laine and
Karras [2011]. This dataset is the only one for which our triangle
sorting cannot be done on GPU, i.e. CPU sorting was applied here.
We encountered large variation in the timings, thus we give average timings over three runs for each resolution. The minimum timings for the resolutions 2048, 4096 and 8192 are 254.8 s, 231.7 s
and 300.5 s, respectively. Additionally, the sorting of triangles for
the resolution of 2048 were around 40 seconds slower than for the
resolution of 4096. We assume that the used radix sort needs larger
buckets due to less Morton indices for the same triangle count in the
limited available memory, leading to memory fragmentation. The
voxelization of the Atlas with resolution of 2048 is the only case
where we perform slower than Baert et al. [2014]. In this scene, the
triangle partitioning to a voxel grid with low resolution is beneficial
in comparison to triangle sorting of a high triangle count.

Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work
We presented an out-of-core voxelization method that processes
batches of triangles and creates sophisticated voxel attributes in
parallel on the GPU, and maintains the currently relevant part of
the SVO in GPU memory. In Triangle Stream Processing, triangles are subdivided in order to achieve better load balancing on the
GPU. The voxel processing on the GPU is steered by grouping a
sorted triangle list into batches and determining upper bounds for
created voxels. In Voxel Stream Processing, the intermediate voxel
and SVO data is maintained in a Morton queue and a stitch queue
buffer on GPU, respectively, which is the core concept for out-ofcore processing. Our approach allows for sophisticated computation of voxel attributes, as all contributing triangles are accessible
at leaf node level. It performs very well for complex scenes with
various surface and material attributes and scales well in terms of
resolution for these kinds of scenes (see Tab. 1).

The presented approach is currently one to two orders of magnitude
slower than a pure in-core GPU technique, like [Crassin and Green
2012]. This is mainly due to the maintenance of created but unprocessable voxels for order-dependent attribute creation and out-ofcore handling of triangles. Future research may improve on this.
For example, applying an in-core GPU approach in a divide-andconquer manner based on partitioning the voxel grid, voxelizing the
subgrids and merging the subtrees could lead to performance gains.
Since streaming of the full triangle set for voxelization on each subgrid would be inefficient, a Bounding Volume Hierarchy could be
used to reduce the number of triangles streamed to the GPU for
the voxelization of each partition. However, it is difficult to estimate the computational overhead for allowing an order-dependent
attribute generation and out-of-core voxelization in the subgrids,
i.e. the selection and streaming of the contributing triangles for each
partition.
