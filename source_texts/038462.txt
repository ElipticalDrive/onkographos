Abstract
1
Web-based large-scale terrain visualization is often limited by traditional graphics APIs in computation and data transfer. This paper
presents an efficient parsing and rendering method using WebGPU,
covering data organization, compute pipeline scheduling, and rendering optimization. A block-based quadtree index is designed for
GPU batch reading, with elevation and texture data rearranged
by spatial proximity. Compute shaders handle frustum culling,
continuous LOD calculation, and dynamic vertex generation, offloading geometry processing from CPU to GPU. Multi-level index
buffers and instanced drawing reduce draw calls. Experiments on
SRTM30m and NASADEM datasets show that, against a WebGL
2.0 baseline, our method maintains 58–62 FPS at 10 million triangles, cuts initial loading time by ∼62%, and reduces memory usage
by ∼41%. This work offers a feasible path for high-performance
web-based geospatial visualization.
Web-based geospatial visualization underpins digital twins, smart
cities, and online GIS. With remote sensing resolution improving
from 90 m to 30 m or finer, elevation datasets have grown from
hundreds of megabytes to gigabytes, making smooth 3D interaction
in browsers challenging.
WebGL, the de facto standard over the past decade, was designed
for earlier GPU hardware. It does not fully embrace programmable
pipelines, strictly separating compute and rendering. Dynamic vertex updates require repeated CPU–GPU memory copies. For massive terrain, geometry processing cannot be effectively offloaded,
and data paths contain redundant copying. Studies indicate that
when rendering over one million triangles, CPU-side vertex processing and state switching may consume over 60% of the frame
budget in WebGL.
WebGPU, the next-generation W3C Web graphics standard, natively maps to Vulkan, Metal, and DirectX 12. Unlike WebGL, it
exposes general-purpose compute shaders in the browser, enabling
geometry generation, culling, and LOD decisions entirely on the
GPU, while the CPU handles logical scheduling and resource management.
Previous works have explored WebGPU for terrain visualization.
Liu et al. proposed occlusion culling and multi-threaded scheduling
[1]; Komon et al. developed weBIGeo for geosimulation, achieving
large speedups [2]; Shu et al. applied WebGPU to underwater terrain with LOD and multi-pass rendering [3]; ISPRS comparative
studies confirmed WebGPU’s superior rendering throughput over
WebGL [4]; Tong et al. extended it to marine TIN data [5]. However, most existing studies pay less attention to raw terrain parsing
and organization, often assuming preprocessed formats. In practice, reading, decoding, and reorganizing elevation data consume
significant loading time, especially over networks.
This paper proposes a full-chain terrain processing and rendering method tailored for WebGPU, addressing three issues: GPUfriendly storage organization, compute-shader-based dynamic LOD,
and copy-free rendering pipeline scheduling. The following sections detail data organization, algorithm design, and experimental
validation.

GPU-FRIENDLY ORGANIZATION OF
TERRAIN DATA
2.1 Data Tiling and Spatial Indexing
The core contradiction of terrain elevation data lies in the fact that
the total data volume far exceeds the geometric scale required for
a single frame, yet real-time interaction demands that data access
latency from any viewpoint must be controlled within milliseconds. Traditional solutions rely on CPU-side quadtree traversal
to select visible tiles, then assemble the vertex data corresponding
to the selected results and upload to the GPU. This path incurs
non-negligible traversal and assembly overhead at the CPU when
data scales grow large.
This paper adopts a block-based quadtree index structure [6],
dividing the terrain region into fixed-size tile blocks, each containing N×N elevation samples. The difference from conventional
schemes lies in the storage of index information: bounding boxes,
center coordinates, hierarchy levels, and offsets within the data
buffer of quadtree nodes are packed into a compact structure array that resides entirely in GPU video memory. Frustum culling
and occlusion determination are directly executed on the GPU via
compute shaders in parallel, with each thread processing one node
and outputting the list of visible nodes and their LOD levels. The
constraints of data tiling and dynamic update strategies can refer to
related research on dynamic terrain [7]. The efficiency advantage
of parallel processing on the GPU has been verified in relevant performance comparison studies [8]. In addition, tile data are written
into the buffer in the order of a space-filling curve (Z-order curve),
so that spatially adjacent tiles also remain adjacent in storage addresses; space-filling curves have been shown to offer good cache
friendliness in large-area terrain data [9].

2.2
Data Layout and Contiguous Storage
The memory access pattern of elevation data has a direct impact
on rendering performance. When terrain is rendered, adjacent tiles
are spatially adjacent, and their vertex data are read contiguously.
If the data are discretely distributed in storage, GPU cache hit
rates will drop significantly. In this paper, tile data are written
into the buffer according to the Z-order curve, and within each
tile, elevation values are stored contiguously in row-major order,
facilitating linear address access by the vertex shader.
103
Texture data are organized following the same principle. For
satellite imagery or terrain color maps containing RGB threechannel data, texture atlases are aligned one-to-one with elevation
data blocks, ensuring that elevation and texture for the same tile
have a deterministic mapping relationship in buffer offsets. The construction method of 3D terrain models based on big data provides
reference for the corresponding organization of texture atlases and
elevation data blocks [10]. This design avoids secondary lookup
tables for texture coordinates during rendering.

3.1 GPU-Based LOD Parameter Computation
In traditional terrain LOD methods, the subdivision level of each
tile is calculated by the CPU based on viewpoint distance, screenspace error, and other parameters. When the number of visible
tiles reaches several thousand, the CPU-side computation is not
negligible. This paper fully migrates LOD decisions to compute
shaders. The parallelization strategy for frustum culling and occlusion judgment draws on related achievements based on the
WebGPU compute pipeline [11]. In addition, big data processing
techniques for large-scale terrain mapping also provide references
for efficient data scheduling and parallel computation [12].

3.2
Dynamic Assembly of Vertex Data
After determining the LOD level, the next step is to extract the vertex grid at the corresponding resolution from the raw elevation data.
For a tile at LOD level ll, its grid resolution is (N/2l + 1)×(N/2l + 1)
vertices. Traditional methods complete vertex data extraction and
assembly on the CPU, then upload to the GPU via buffer updates.
This process involves substantial memory allocation and data copying, which is a performance bottleneck.
This paper leverages WebGPU’s storage buffers and the random
write capability of compute shaders to accomplish dynamic vertex
assembly on the GPU. The compute shader computes the sampling
step s = 2l for each vertex based on the LOD level, then reads the
corresponding z-values from the elevation buffer and calculates
world coordinates in combination with the tile’s spatial position.
The assembled vertex data are written directly to the target vertex
buffer without passing through CPU memory.
The core advantage of this scheme is that vertex buffer updates
are completed entirely within the GPU, eliminating per-frame CPUGPU transfers of dynamic vertex data. The elevation data itself is
uploaded from CPU to GPU storage buffers during initialization,
and subsequent per-frame vertex assembly is performed entirely
on the GPU. This difference is particularly significant for terrain
meshes that need updating every frame. Taking 10 million vertices as an example, the traditional scheme would need to transfer
about 120 MB of vertex data per frame (positions + normals + texture coordinates), whereas the GPU-side assembly scheme reduces
this transfer volume to zero (except for the one-time initialization
transfer).

4.1 Multi-Level Index Buffer Design
The triangle topology of terrain meshes is highly regular—the vertex
connectivity pattern within each tile depends only on the grid
resolution, not on the elevation values. Based on this observation,
this paper pre-generates corresponding index buffers for all possible
LOD levels and stores them in GPU memory. During rendering,
the index buffer corresponding to the tile’s LOD level is directly
bound, eliminating per-frame regeneration or upload.

Instanced Drawing and Batch Merging
When the number of terrain tiles is large, issuing draw calls one
by one incurs non-negligible CPU overhead. This paper adopts
WebGPU’s instanced drawing mechanism, merging tiles with the
same LOD level into the same draw call. Each instance passes
the tile’s world transformation matrix and texture atlas offset as
instance data.
Let the total number of visible tiles in the current frame be M,
with LOD level distribution c0 , c1 , … , cmax . The number of draw
calls is reduced from M to the number of distinct LOD levels (typically no more than 10). In our experimental scenario with a 2°×2°
region, M is typically between 50 and 200, reducing draw call counts
by one to two orders of magnitude.
4.3
Overlapped Compute-Render Scheduling
WebGPU’s API is built around the concept of a device queue, to
which applications submit command buffers. Within a single command buffer, compute and render passes are encoded using GPUComputePassEncoder and GPURenderPassEncoder, respectively.
Although the WebGPU runtime may internally overlap the execution of these passes on the GPU, this behavior is not directly
controlled by application fences. To systematically overlap computation and rendering, this paper implements a double-buffering
strategy.
In this design, two sets of resources (vertex buffers and indirect
draw buffers) are allocated, functioning as a front buffer and a back
buffer. For each frame:
1. The CPU submits a command buffer to the device queue that
encodes compute passes for LOD calculation and vertex assembly,
writing the results into the back buffer.
2. The CPU then submits a second command buffer that encodes
a render pass, which draws the terrain mesh using the front buffer
(prepared in the previous frame).
Synchronization between these two operations is implicitly handled by the WebGPU queue, which guarantees that commands are
executed in the order they are submitted. Crucially, the results of
the compute work from frame N (written to the back buffer) are consumed by the render pass of frame N+1 (after swapping the buffer
roles). This pipelining scheme ensures that the GPU can continue
rendering previously prepared data while simultaneously computing data for the next frame, effectively overlapping compute and
render workloads. This method partially removes compute latency
from the critical path of the frame budget, effectively improving
overall throughput. It should be noted that this mechanism introduces a one-frame latency (about 16.7 ms), which is negligible for
interactive experience in most terrain navigation scenarios. Based
on our experimental measurements, this compute-render overlap
scheduling improves the effective frame rate by approximately 11%
compared to a serial execution model.

5.3
Scalability at Different Data Scales
To evaluate the scalability of the method, we tested frame rates at
different data scales, covering scenes from 1 million to 50 million
triangles. When the triangle count grows from 1 million to 10
million, the frame rate drops slowly from 64.2 FPS to 59.7 FPS, a
decrease of about 7%; when the triangle count further increases to
50 million, the frame rate drops to 41.3 FPS, with a larger decline.
Bottleneck analysis shows that at the 50-million scale, vertex shader
105
execution time becomes the main limiting factor, directly related
to GPU floating-point throughput.
5.4
Discussion
Results confirm that offloading parsing and assembly to GPU is feasible and beneficial in Web environments. Compute shaders eliminate
CPU–GPU transfers and enable high parallelism. The method’s effectiveness depends on WebGPU runtime; major browsers (Chrome,
Edge) support it natively, and Firefox/Safari are progressing, ensuring portability.
6
CONCLUSION
This paper addresses performance bottlenecks in large-scale terrain rendering on the Web with a WebGPU-based solution. Data
organization uses block quadtree and Z-order for GPU-friendly
layout. Compute shaders handle LOD and vertex assembly entirely
on the GPU, avoiding per-frame data transfers. Multi-level index
buffers and instanced drawing reduce draw calls by two orders of
magnitude. Experiments confirm significant gains in FPS, loading
time, and memory usage.
Future work includes combining WebGPU ray tracing with terrain rendering for enhanced realism, and extending to dynamic
terrain data (e.g., hydrological or geological simulation) leveraging
per-frame compute-shader updates.
