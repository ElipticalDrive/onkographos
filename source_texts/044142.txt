Abstract
Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has gained attention for its high
fidelity and real-time rendering. However, adapting 3DGS to different camera
models, particularly fisheye lenses, poses challenges due to the unique 3D
to 2D projection calculation. Additionally, there are inefficiencies in the tilebased splatting, especially for the extreme curvature and wide field of view of
fisheye lenses, which are crucial for its broader real-life applications. To address
these challenges, we introduce Fisheye-GS, a novel method that recalculates the
projection transformation for fisheye cameras, and then optimizes the rendering
process with the reduced number of overlapped tiles. Our method reduces
computational redundancy and memory usage, significantly improving rendering
speed and enabling 3DGS to be effectively used with a wider range of camera
perspectives. With our optimization, we achieved a maximum rendering efficiency
of 570 FPS at a high-definition resolution of 1752 × 1168.

Introduction
The advent of high-resolution fisheye cameras has transformed how we capture and interact with the
world, providing a 180° or greater field of view that offers unique perspectives. These perspectives
are essential for various real-world applications. In immersive virtual reality (VR), fisheye cameras
enable the creation of panoramic environments that enhance the user experience by providing a
more comprehensive and realistic field of vision [1]. This is crucial for applications such as VR
gaming [2], virtual tourism [3], and training simulations [4], where a wide and uninterrupted view
can significantly improve immersion and usability. In surveillance systems, fisheye cameras allow
for broader area coverage with fewer cameras, reducing costs and complexity. They are particularly
valuable in security and monitoring scenarios, such as in large public spaces[5], retail stores [6, 7],
and traffic monitoring [8], where capturing every angle with high detail is vital for effective oversight
and incident management.
However, integrating fisheye imagery into novel view synthesis poses several challenges due to the
inherent curvature and distortion. While Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [10] handles the distortions
of fisheye lenses through its ray sampling approach, which can easily accommodate various camera
models, the state-of-the-art 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [9] faces difficulties. The original 3DGS
approach relies on perspective projections and is less adept at managing the extreme curvature and
wide field of view of fisheye imagery, leading to inaccuracies and inefficiencies in rendering.
In this work, we aim to integrate fisheye cameras with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) to fully
unleash the potential of fisheye cameras in real-world applications that require real-time rendering
performance. By combining the wide field of view provided by fisheye lenses with the efficient
rendering capabilities of 3DGS, we seek to overcome the challenges associated with the curvature and
distortion inherent in fisheye imagery. We identify the key challenge lies in accurately representing

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). NeRF[10] represents a breakthrough in the field of novel view
synthesis, which aims to generate realistic images of a 3D scene from any viewpoint. NeRF employs
a fully connected deep neural network to encode a continuous volumetric scene function. This
function maps a 3D point and a viewing direction to the corresponding RGB color and volume
density. To enhance rendering quality, some methods directly refine the point sampling strategy in
ray marching, resulting in more accurate modeling of the volume rendering process [11]. Others
enhance rendering by reparameterizing the scene, which creates a more compact scene representation
and simplifies the learning process [12, 13]. Despite many optimizations, NeRF still consumes a
lot of time and computational power during the rendering process. NeRFs are typically represented
as global MLPs encoding the entire scene space. However, this approach can be inefficient and
costly when reconstructing complex and large-scale scenes. Some works focus on innovations in
scene representations. They propose voxel grids [14] and octree-based [15]. Although these methods

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). 3D Gaussian Splatting [9] models the scene with a set of Gaussian
ellipsoids, allowing efficient rendering through rasterizing these Gaussian ellipsoids into images.
While 3DGS produces high-quality reconstruction results, there is room for improvement in its
rendering capabilities. [16, 17, 18, 19, 20] are designed to improve rendering performance under
sparse views and challenging inputs. These methods enhance the robustness and accuracy of the
rendered images in difficult scenarios.For anti-aliasing and improving both efficiency and quality,
approaches like [21, 22, 23] are utilized. These techniques help in reducing aliasing artifacts
and ensuring smooth transitions in the rendered images, providing a more visually appealing
output.Anchor-based methods, such as [24, 25] focus on improving efficiency and quality by using
hierarchical structures and anchor points. These methods enhance the computational efficiency and
scalability of the rendering process, allowing for faster and more accurate scene reconstructions.

NeRF-based approaches. NeRF often assumes a pinhole camera model for simplicity, as it directly

the reconstruction of a wide range of scenes from different types of input images.

3DGS-based approaches. 3D Gaussian Splatting primarily works with perspective projections.
Adapting it to work with fisheye and panoramic camera models, instead, involves additional
considerations to handle specific distortions and projections associated with these types of images
involving various approximations in the rasterization process. [32] suggests pre-processing fisheye
images to correct for radial distortion, effectively converting them to a more standard perspective view
before applying Gaussian Splatting. Alternatively, one can modify the projection process within the
Gaussian Splatting pipeline to approximate the non-linear mapping, ensuring correct positioning and
scaling of splats to fix the distortion. For example, [33] projects 3D Gaussians onto the tangent plane
of the unit sphere, addressing challenges in spherical projections and enhancing rendering quality by
leveraging structural priors from panoramic layouts. [34] introduces an optimal projection strategy
for Gaussian splatting by analyzing projection errors and minimizing artifacts through function
optimization, refining the projection of Gaussians to improve the overall accuracy and visual quality
of 3D scene representations.

Implementation Details
In 3DGS, we need to determine the tiles covered by the Gaussian ellipsoid projection for subsequent
rendering. The original 3DGS implement calculates the bounding box of the ellipse based on
the major axis after the Gaussian ellipsoid projection, and then all tiles within the bounding box
participate in the key-value binding and alpha blending of this Gaussian ellipsoid. However, many
tiles within the bounding box are not actually covered by the projected ellipse, which introduces a lot
of computational redundancy for subsequent key-value binding and alpha blending.
In the original 3DGS, the size of the ellipse is often calculated according to the 3σ principle (σ is
the standard deviation), without considering the opacity of the Gaussian ellipsoid. In statistics, it is
generally believed that in a Gaussian distribution, data points with an absolute difference from the
mean greater than 3σ are extremely rare. Considering that the contribution to the final result is related
to the opacity of the Gaussian ellipsoids, we use Eq. (13) as a substitute to narrow the definition of
the ellipse size:

During the key-value binding process, the original 3DGS will perform key-value binding for all tiles

As shown in Fig. 2(c), we carefully determine whether there is an intersection between the tile and
the ellipse during the key-value binding traversal of the bounding box tiles. If there is no intersection,
then do not perform key-value binding for this tile. For each tile, if one of the following conditions is
met, it can be said that there is an intersection between the ellipse and the tile, and key-value binding
should be performed for this tile:
• The center of the ellipse is inside the tile.
• There is an edge e of the tile that either intersects with the ellipse or is inside the ellipse.

Although eliminating redundant key-value pairs is the most effective method for optimizing compute
workload and memory usage, we have also implemented additional optimizations to further reduce
memory consumption and save memory bandwidth.
Due to the significant size differences among the Gaussian ellipsoids, the original implementation
of the second function exhibited severe load imbalance issues. We modified the thread organization
in this section so that each warp is responsible for the computation of one Gaussian ellipsoid.
Additionally, since the output order of key-value pairs is not important because they will be sorted,
we replaced the original prefix sum with dynamic scheduling implemented by atomic addition,
eliminating the array point_offsets and the CUDA library call for computing the prefix sum.
223
Finally, we fused the two CUDA kernels preprocessCUDA and duplicateWithKeys, eliminating
the array radii, which stores the temporary information of bounding box size. Another significant
advantage of kernel fusion is that it prevents all accesses to the array shs from being concentrated in
the first CUDA kernel preprocessCUDA, thereby avoiding the reduction in computational efficiency
caused by the bottleneck of memory bandwidth.

Dataset. We have adopted 6 indoor scenes from Scannet++[32] dataset as our real world dataset,

model for the Lego scene, and 100 frames for other scenes. For each scene, we render the dataset
separately for the field of view (FOV) settings of 120◦ and 180◦ . We use 7/8 of each scene as the
training set for optimization and the rest as the test set for evaluation.

Visual Quality. We use visual PSNR, SSIM[37] and LPIPS[38] to test our method and the baseline
on 6 real indoor scenes captured by fisheye cameras. As is shown in table 1, our method outperforms
the baseline on each metric and scene on average. Fig. 3 shows that the baseline performs well on the
central area of the image. However, artifacts frequently appear on the edge. Besides the interpolation
of the undistortion process can lead to the loss of information, the difference between the pinhole and
fisheye camera models plays a key role. The fisheye camera has a larger field of view (FOV) than
the pinhole camera, so the undistortion process will clip the scenes on the edge of the image plane.
Then the 3DGS struggles to learn the edge area of the scene. In other words, the corners of the scene
can be more frequently viewed by our Fisheye-GS, add more geometry constraints, and show fewer
artifacts in novel views.

Limitaion
Conclusion
This paper introduces a new approach, Fisheye-GS, aimed at addressing the adaptability issues of 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) across different camera models, particularly fisheye lenses. Fisheye-GS
recalculates the projection transformation for fisheye cameras and optimizes the rendering process
by reducing the number of overlapped tiles, effectively minimizing computational redundancy and
memory usage. These optimizations significantly enhance rendering speed, achieving a maximum
efficiency of 570 frames per second at a high-definition resolution of 1752 x 1168. This paper
demonstrates that Fisheye-GS can effectively expand the application range of 3DGS technology to
various camera perspectives, which is crucial for its broader real-life applications. We anticipate
that the outcomes of this study will provide robust support and new perspectives for research and
development in related fields.
