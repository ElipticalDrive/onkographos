Motion Blur Explained: Velocity Buffers and Smear
Per-object and camera motion blur are often implemented as separate, unrelated features even though both come from the exact same velocity-buffer idea, causing confusion about which technique to reach for.
RenderingBeginner12 min
Quick answer: A real camera’s shutter stays open for a small but nonzero amount of time, so anything that moved during that window smears across the frame instead of freezing at one instant. Real-time motion blur fakes this with a velocity buffer — each pixel’s on-screen displacement since the previous frame — and a post-process that samples several points along that vector and averages them. Per-object motion blur and camera motion blur are the same technique; they only differ in whose movement produces the velocity: an object moving through a static camera, or a static scene seen through a moving camera.

In per-object motion mode, only the ball carries a velocity — raise the shutter angle and its trail lengthens while the background grid stays perfectly sharp, because the background genuinely didn’t move relative to the camera. Switch to camera pan motion and the entire scene smears together at the same shutter angle, while the small reticle in the center stays crisp — that reticle represents UI or HUD elements, which are deliberately excluded from camera motion blur because they aren’t part of the 3D world the camera is panning through.
Shutter angle: the actual control that matters
Shutter angle is borrowed directly from film cameras, where a rotating shutter disc’s open angle (out of a full 360°) determines what fraction of each frame’s time the sensor is actually exposed. A 180° shutter angle — a common cinematic default — means the sensor is exposed for exactly half the time between frames, which sets how far anything moving travels during that exposure, and therefore exactly how long its blur trail should be:
```
blurLength = speed × (shutterAngle / 360)
```
A 0° shutter angle means no exposure time at all — no blur, ever, regardless of speed. A 360° shutter angle means the sensor is “open” for the entire frame interval, producing the maximum possible blur length for a given speed.
Where the velocity comes from
Where a per-pixel velocity comes fromEach pixel's current clip-space position minus its reprojected previous-frame position gives a screen-space velocity vector, stored in a velocity buffer that the motion blur pass samples along.CURRENT positionthis frame's clip positionPREVIOUS positionlast frame, reprojected− (subtract)per pixelVELOCITY BUFFERscreen-space vector per pixelMOTION BLURsample along itCamera motion blur is the exact same buffer — every pixel just shares one dominant camera-motion vector.
Reprojection matters here: the "previous position" isn't just where the object was — it has to be transformed by last frame's view-projection matrix to know where that same point *would have appeared* on last frame's screen, so the subtraction produces a true screen-space displacement rather than nonsense from comparing two unrelated coordinate spaces.
For every pixel, the renderer needs to know “how far did whatever’s here move across the screen since last frame?” That requires two positions for the same point: where it is now, and where it would have appeared last frame — which means reprojecting using last frame’s view-projection matrix, not this frame’s. Subtracting those two screen positions gives a 2D velocity vector per pixel, stored in a velocity buffer alongside the color and depth buffers. The motion blur pass then samples the color buffer several times along that vector, per pixel, and averages the results — mechanically similar to how depth of field samples along a different, depth-driven pattern instead.

Both ultimately populate the exact same velocity buffer — a renderer usually computes camera motion for the whole scene and then adds each object’s own motion on top, so a moving object seen from a panning camera correctly gets the combined velocity of both.
Common bugs
A fast-moving object leaves a hard-edged, doubled-looking trail instead of a smooth blur
Too few samples along the velocity vector produces visible “ghost” copies rather than a continuous smear — increasing the sample count (or, in the demo above, the accumulated sample density) smooths this at a direct performance cost.
Motion blur appears on objects that shouldn’t have any — like a character standing still while only rotating in place
Any change to an object’s transform between frames — including pure rotation, not just translation — produces nonzero screen-space velocity for its vertices (a spinning object’s edges genuinely do move across the screen), so this is usually correct behavior, not a bug, unless the object visually reads as “not moving” in a way that makes the blur look wrong.
Skinned/animated characters show broken or exaggerated motion blur at the joints
The velocity buffer needs each vertex’s previous-frame skinned position, not just the previous-frame bone transform — see skeletal animation and skinning for the deformation step that has to be replayed for last frame’s pose specifically to get this right.
UI elements or the player’s own weapon/hands blur along with a camera pan
This needs to be explicitly excluded, typically by rendering those elements in a separate pass that doesn’t contribute to (or read from) the velocity buffer used by the motion blur post-process — it doesn’t happen automatically just because something is close to the camera.
Motion blur looks fine at 60 fps but excessive or “swimmy” at 30 fps
At a lower frame rate, more real-world time passes between frames, so objects move farther per frame — if blur length is computed from raw pixel displacement without accounting for the actual elapsed time, it can scale incorrectly across different frame rates or frame-time spikes.
FAQ
Does motion blur require temporal anti-aliasing (TAA) or vice versa?
No, they’re independent techniques that happen to both use a velocity buffer and reprojection — many engines compute one shared velocity buffer and use it for both TAA’s history reprojection and motion blur’s directional sampling, but either can exist without the other.
Why do some games let you disable motion blur separately from other post-processing?
Motion blur is one of the more polarizing settings — some players find any smearing disorienting or motion-sickness-inducing, especially for camera motion blur during fast turns, which is why it’s commonly exposed as its own toggle rather than bundled into a general “post-processing” setting.
Is object motion blur only for translation, or does it capture rotation and scale too?
Any transform change that moves a vertex’s screen position captures correctly, since the velocity is computed from actual before/after screen positions, not from a simplified “linear speed” number — rotation, scale, and skinning-driven deformation all naturally contribute.
Can motion blur be baked instead of computed per frame?
For pre-rendered content (film, cutscenes) with known motion in advance, blur is often accumulated by rendering and averaging multiple sub-frame time samples directly (true temporal supersampling) rather than approximated from a velocity buffer — this is more accurate but isn’t feasible for real-time, unpredictable player-driven motion.
