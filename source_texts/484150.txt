Dynamic Foveated Rendering for Real-Time Ray Tracing via
Screen-Space BVH Selection
Guy Barel
18 August 2024
Abstract
Human visual acuity is sharply concentrated in the fovea, covering only a few degrees
around the gaze direction, while sensitivity in the periphery decays rapidly. Foveated rendering exploits this property by allocating computational resources non-uniformly across the
image plane. In this paper we propose a mathematically explicit model for dynamic foveated
rendering in a hybrid rasterization / ray-tracing pipeline. Our method assumes a gaze point
estimated from an eye-tracking system and uses a perspective projection of a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) into screen space to decide which objects fall inside the foveal region.
Objects whose projected bounding volumes intersect the foveal disc are rendered using ray
tracing, while the remainder are shaded via rasterization. We derive the full screen-space
geometry required for this decision, specify a continuous resolution falloff based on retinal
acuity, and provide a simple performance model for the expected cost. Although our evaluation is synthetic, the framework is implementation-ready and shows how mathematical
modeling can link human vision, projective geometry, and real-time rendering.
1
Introduction
Modern real-time rendering pipelines increasingly rely on ray tracing to achieve high levels of
visual realism [1]. However, full-resolution ray tracing across the entire screen remains prohibitively expensive on commodity hardware. At the same time, human visual perception is
highly non-uniform: the fovea, a small region near the center of the retina, supports much
higher spatial resolution than the periphery [12]. Dynamic foveated rendering aims to exploit
this by concentrating computation around the observer’s gaze point while aggressively reducing
quality elsewhere.
Most practical systems for foveated rendering are engineered from a systems perspective and
described qualitatively. In contrast, this work emphasizes a mathematically explicit formulation
of a dynamic foveated ray-tracing scheme built on three components:
• A simple geometric model for mapping gaze direction to a point in screen space.
• A continuous resolution falloff function derived from a coarse approximation to retinal
acuity decay.
• A screen-space selection rule for a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) that decides which
objects are eligible for ray tracing.
We do not claim algorithmic novelty in ray tracing or foveated rendering themselves. Instead,
our contribution is the explicit coupling of projective geometry, BVH projection, and foveation
into a single coherent model that can be implemented in a modern rendering engine. The
emphasis is on transparency of the mathematics: every decision in the pipeline is derived from
equations that can be inspected, modified, or extended [1].
1
Problem statement
Let the image plane be a rectangle Ω ⊂ R2 with pixel coordinates (x, y), and let g ∈ Ω denote
the gaze point. We assume a BVH over the scene, where each node is a bounding box in world
space. Our goal is to determine, for each BVH node, whether any part of its projected image
falls within a foveal disc
D(g, rf ) = {p ∈ Ω : ∥p − g∥2 ≤ rf },
where rf is the foveal radius in pixels. Nodes intersecting D(g, rf ) are rendered using ray
tracing; those outside are rendered via rasterization and approximate shading.
We further wish to define a scalar resolution factor R(p) for each pixel p ∈ Ω, satisfying
• R(p) ≈ 1 near the gaze point (full quality);
• R(p) decays monotonically with distance ∥p − g∥2 ;
• R(p) is bounded below by a positive minimum Rmin to avoid complete loss of information.

Background
2.1
Human visual acuity and foveation
The fovea occupies approximately 1◦ –2◦ of visual angle around the gaze direction and exhibits
significantly higher cone density than the periphery [7, 12]. A common engineering approximation is to treat the foveal region as a small disc of radius θf degrees around the gaze direction
and to assume that perceived acuity decays roughly inversely with eccentricity (angle from the
gaze axis) [12, 7]. If the viewing distance is d and the physical size of a pixel on the screen is s,

2.2
Rasterization and ray tracing
Rasterization maps scene geometry to screen space via a perspective projection and then shades
the resulting fragments using approximate lighting models such as Phong or Blinn–Phong. Ray
tracing, in contrast, traces rays from the camera (or screen pixels) into the scene, computing intersections with geometry and evaluating light transport more accurately [1, 2]. Hybrid
pipelines often use rasterization for primary visibility and ray tracing for selected effects (e.g.,
reflections, global illumination, or shadows).
2.3
Bounding volume hierarchies
A bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) is a tree in which each node represents a volume enclosing
a subset of scene primitives [4]. BVHs accelerate ray tracing by enabling early rejection of large
parts of the scene: if a ray does not intersect a node’s bounding volume, none of its children need
be considered. In our setting, we use the BVH differently: we project the bounding volumes of
nodes into screen space and intersect them with the foveal disc. Only nodes whose projections
overlap the disc are given high-quality ray-tracing treatment.

7
Hybrid rendering pipeline
We now place the BVH selection into a hybrid pipeline that combines rasterization and ray
tracing.
7.1
Overview
The pipeline for one frame proceeds as follows:
1. Obtain the gaze point g from the eye tracker.
2. Render the entire scene once using rasterization to obtain primary visibility and a G-buffer
containing per-pixel positions, normals, and material indices.
3. Traverse the BVH, marking nodes whose screen-space projection intersects D(g, rf ) as
ray-traced.
4. For pixels inside D(g, rf ), spawn high-quality ray-tracing passes (e.g., reflections, shadows,
global illumination) restricted to geometry within ray-traced nodes.
5. For pixels outside D(g, rf ), use cheaper approximations or lower ray budgets as determined
by R(p).

10
Discussion
The proposed model is intentionally explicit and modular. Each component—gaze mapping,
foveal disc definition, BVH projection, and intersection test—can be modified independently.
For example, more sophisticated eye-tracking algorithms could provide not only a gaze
point but also an uncertainty ellipse; this could be incorporated by replacing the disc D(g, rf )
with an anisotropic region. Similarly, the reciprocal resolution falloff function φ(r) could be
replaced with a model fitted to psychophysical measurements of acuity, or constrained by display
hardware capabilities.
The screen-space BVH selection is conservative: if a bounding rectangle overlaps the foveal
disc, the entire node is treated as foveal. This guarantees that no object partially inside the
fovea is rendered at low quality, at the expense of potentially ray tracing some primitives that
are only marginally relevant. More aggressive schemes could intersect the disc with refined
sub-boxes or use per-primitive selection within a node.
11
Limitations
Several limitations of this work should be emphasized.
First, we have not implemented the full pipeline inside a production renderer; our evaluation
is based on an abstract performance model for ray counts. Actual frame times will depend on
GPU architecture, driver optimizations, and interactions with other parts of the engine.
Second, the eye-tracking component is treated as a black box that returns a gaze point in
pixel coordinates. In reality, mapping from raw camera observations to gaze requires careful
calibration and error modeling. Latency in gaze estimation may require predicting future gaze
positions or expanding the foveal region to hide lag.
Third, we approximate the foveal region with a simple disc, whereas the true sensitivity of
the human visual system is anisotropic and frequency-dependent. A more faithful model could
incorporate contrast sensitivity functions and task-dependent sampling patterns.
Finally, the BVH projection assumes that the entire bounding box is within the camera frustum and that the projection does not suffer from numerical instability. Robust implementation
must handle degenerate cases and clipping.

Conclusion
We presented a mathematically explicit framework for dynamic foveated rendering in a hybrid
rasterization / ray-tracing pipeline. Given a gaze point in screen space, our method projects
BVH nodes into the image, tests their intersection with a foveal disc, and allocates ray-tracing
effort accordingly. We derived the necessary projective geometry, defined a continuous resolution
falloff model, and sketched a simple performance analysis.
While conceptually simple, the explicit formulation exposes all assumptions and parameters,
making the method a useful pedagogical and prototyping tool. Future work includes integrating
this model into a real-time engine, validating perceptual quality with user studies, and extending
the mathematical model to more faithful representations of human vision and eye-tracking noise.
