EnvMap-GS: two-stage outdoor Gaussian reconstruction with
background-to-environment map baking

Abstract
Reconstructing outdoor environments from “inside-out” captures, where a camera moves within a restricted area but looks
outward, remains challenging due to the presence of both well-textured nearby regions and low-detail distant backgrounds.
We introduce a two-stage Gaussian Splatting framework that explicitly separates and optimizes these regions, yielding higherfidelity novel view synthesis and allowing the replacement of the distant part with a high-quality, inpainted environment map
to speedup the rendering process. In stage one, background primitives are initialized within a spherical shell and optimized
using a loss that combines a background-only photometric term with two geometric regularizers: one constraining Gaussians
to remain inside the shell, and another one aligning them with local tangential planes. In stage two, foreground Gaussians
are initialized from a Structure-from-Motion reconstruction, added and refined using the standard rendering loss, while
the background set remains fixed but contributes to the final image formation. Background Gaussians can be rendered to
an object-free environment map that is inpainted to fill missing parts and can replace the Gaussian-based background for
faster rendering. Experiments on diverse outdoor datasets show that our method reduces background artifacts and improves
perceptual quality of novel view renderings compared to state-of-the-art baselines, including the removal of floaters in the
navigation region.
Keywords Outdoor scene reconstruction · 3D Gaussian Spatting · Volume rendering · Environment mapping · View synthesis
1 Introduction
Gaussian Splatting (GS) [7] is a popular approach to encode
light-field information reconstructed from a collection of
digital images of real-world scenes, to enable high-fidelity,
real-time rendering of novel views. In this paper, we focus
on interactive navigation in outdoor environments using
“inside-out” captures. Unlike traditional “outside-in” captures (which focus on a central object and often ignore the

the broader environment. For such scenes with both nearby
objects and a far-field background, Gaussian Splatting still
has some critical issues. In particular, the reconstruction of
the far field is problematic, manifesting in the presence of
“floaters”, i.e., large Gaussians distant from scene geometry.
Many variations of the original approach have been proposed
to reduce floaters, that work well for nearby objects, but fail to
improve far-field reconstruction. In our experiments, floaters
appear in the far field for two main reasons: incorrect initialization and densification choices in the GS algorithm, and
the limited range of views used during scene optimization.
In this paper, we address these issues and present a solution
for the reconstruction of floater-free outdoor environments
targeting real-time applications. We assume that the user
can only navigate in a navigation area, which is usually the
region where training images have been captured. We segment the scene into a foreground area which encapsulates
the navigation area, where the scene has been recorded from
multiple directions and it is thus possible to reconstruct fairly

improves the quality of novel view synthesis for outdoor environments, especially for views far from training views. We
also show experimentally that the method is more effective
than other strategies in reducing floaters in the navigation

replacing the far-field by an environment map. The code
of our pipeline is publicly available at the following URL:

While our approach is general, it is particularly relevant
for applications like Virtual Reality (VR) exploration. In VR,
users expect a seamless sense of presence, which is often broken in traditional Gaussian Splatting by “floaters” or popping
artifacts when navigating outside the training camera path.
In conclusion, our main contributions are:
– a novel variant of Gaussian Splatting that works well
for outdoor scene reconstruction by explicitly separating
foreground and background regions in two concentric
shells of Gaussian primitives optimized sequentially;
– two geometric loss functions, L shell and L planarity , designed to constrain background Gaussians to a spherical
shell and encourage tangential alignment;
– a modified pruning strategy that preserves large, stable
Gaussians necessary for representing distant scenery;
– a procedure to convert the external shell into a standard
environment map, that can be used to speedup rendering.
2 Related works
Gaussian Splatting (GS) [7] uses optimized anisotropic
Gaussian primitives to provide high-fidelity scene reconstructions for real-time novel view synthesis. Beyond objectcentric scenes, recent works have extended GS to handle
outdoor environments using different approaches to the prob-
123

lem. Kulhanek et al. identify a relevant issue for outdoor
scenes, as the Gaussians cannot be created in the sky with
Structure-from-Motion (SfM) initialization [10]. To overcome this problem, the authors sample points on a sphere
around the scene and add them to the rest of the points used
to initialize the Gaussians. Wang et al. adopt another strategy and implement a pipeline with different neural modules
[21]. They separate the sky from more detailed elements
in the scene (e.g., buildings) and generate a cubemap to
render the sky. However, while the resulting sky reconstruction looks visually realistic, in some instances (i.e., sky with
clouds), it struggles to reproduce images close to the ground
truth. Cheng et al. introduced GaussianPro, using a different,
progressive densification strategy to improve robustness in
texture-poor and large outdoor regions [5]. Liu et al. incorporate homogeneous coordinates into the 3DGS framework,
providing a unified representation that enhances near and
distant objects [12].
Other authors tried to combine points with neural rendering to overcome the limitations of GS, at the cost of not
being compatible with the standard renderers. Franke et al.
use a trilinear point splatting scheme blending GS and neural
rendering, showing effective large-scale landscape rendering
at real-time rates [6]. Lu et al. use anchor points to distribute local 3D Gaussians and use a network to predict their
attributes on-the-fly based on viewing direction and distance
within the view frustum [13].
Scalability with scene extent has also been the focus of
much attention. Lin et al. partition large scenes into paralleloptimized volumes, allowing real-time splatting across large
environments [11]. Ren et al. employ hierarchical levels of
detail (LoD) via octree structures to adaptively manage rendering fidelity in large landscapes [16]. Kerbl et al. introduce
a hierarchy of 3D Gaussians to preserve the visual quality
for extensive scenarios [8]. Zhang et al. reduce the number of Gaussians with optimization-based sparsification[24].
Pateux et al. enhance fidelity under real-world capture variability with confidence-aware updates and rate-distortion
densification [14].
Early NeRF-based methods such as NeRF++ [23] and
Mip-NeRF 360 [1] pioneered the representation of unbounded
scenes using inner and outer volumes. We bring a similar philosophy to the 3DGS framework. Despite these
advances, current GS approaches often rely on dense viewpoint coverage, structured capture, or point priors. Modeling
distant background regions (e.g., sky, distant mountains,
etc.) remains challenging. Our method addresses the gaps
between nearby and distant elements, including but not limited to the sky, by combining “standard” Gaussians within
a defined foreground depth for detailed local fidelity, with
a spherical shell of Gaussians to model the background
elements.

Using the estimated camera poses, we unproject each pixel
of the camera-specific depth maps into 3D space. For every
resulting 3D point, we compute its distance to the scene center O. These distances are stored in image-aligned maps,
which we refer to as Distance-from-Center (DFC) maps. The
DFC representation expresses depth information in a common global reference frame, so we can use a single distance
threshold to separate foreground and background regions
across all views.
3.1.2 Foreground - background segmentation
The separation between foreground and background is based
on a threshold Ri applied to the DFC maps. While the optimal
threshold is scene dependent, we found a simple heuristic
that worked well for all scenes. We estimate the histogram of
the DFC of the original point cloud, with a binning interval
of ten meters. We then set the threshold Ri after the first
interval with a point count less than 2.5% of the reconstructed
points. Although this heuristic works most often, the user can
override the result by manually selecting a point. Changing
the threshold within reasonable ranges does not affect the
novel view synthesis metrics too much, as we will show in
Section 4.3 and detail in the Supplementary Materials, but it
might affect the reconstruction detail of the regions moved
to the foreground.
Once the threshold is defined, we generate two datasets
for the following steps. The first one is a set of Masked Background Images, where the input images are masked to remove
all foreground pixels, leaving only the background content
for the first optimization stage. The second is a set of Filtered Foreground Points, where the initial sparse point cloud
of COLMAP is filtered to keep only the points positioned
within the distance threshold Ri .

3.2 Stage 1: background reconstruction
3.2.1 Initialization of the outer spherical shell
We represent the background as a spherical shell that surrounds the entire scene. We define it as the area between two
concentric spheres centered at O (Figure 1). The smaller one
has radius Ri , which corresponds to the threshold used to separate background from foreground. The radius of the outer
one, Ro , denotes the maximum limit of the scene, and we
set its value to guarantee a bounded parallax when viewed
from different cameras placed in the navigation area. We
empirically found that a value of 10 times the diameter of
the navigation area, approximated with the maximum distance between estimated camera positions, is a reasonable
trade-off.

We base this reconstruction on the results of the background
pass. The background Gaussians are included in the rendering computation to contribute to the final image, but are kept
fixed during optimization. The foreground is initialized using
the filtered point cloud generated during the segmentation
step (Section 3.1.2). During optimization, the foreground
Gaussians are confined to the spherical volume defined by
the inner radius Ri . This radius defines the maximum spatial
extent for foreground elements.
3.3.2 Optimization
The foreground Gaussians are optimized using the standard
3D Gaussian Splatting loss function on the original unmasked
images. To enforce the boundary defined in the setup, we perform a spatial pruning: any foreground Gaussian that moves
beyond the inner radius Ri of the background shell is deleted.
This constraint ensures a clean separation between the two
layers and prevents the foreground from spreading into the
background region.
The final output is a complete scene representation that
combines the optimized foreground Gaussians with the separately modeled background.
3.4 Environment map conversion
3.2.3 Custom pruning
In the first optimization stage, we also modify the pruning
logic. The original GS implementation includes a heuristic
mechanism aimed at deleting large Gaussians, both in screen
space and world space. However, in our case, this strategy
can be overly aggressive, particularly for Gaussians representing large and distant background regions. We observed
that large Gaussians often model these elements well and are
incorrectly removed by this strategy. To preserve the integrity
and, more importantly, the numerical stability of the background representation, we disable this size-based pruning
step. At the same time, we introduce a new pruning strategy
that deletes Gaussians that are never seen from any camera
viewpoint. To this end, we count the times each Gaussian
is rendered, using the indices returned from the rendering
step. Any Gaussian with a visibility count equal to zero is
removed. This logic is executed in the same pruning stage as
in the original code.
3.3 Stage 2: foreground reconstruction
The second stage of our pipeline reconstructs the foreground
on top of the already optimized background reconstructed in
Section 3.2.
To extract a high-fidelity environment map from the trained
background Gaussians, we developed a rendering pipeline
based on the slit-scan technique. Since the Gaussian Splatting rasterizer is designed for planar perspective projection,
it cannot natively render a spherical or equirectangular image
in a single pass without introducing significant geometric distortion at the edges. To overcome this limitation and ensure
continuity on the horizontal axis, we first generate an environment map sampled on a cylindrical projection and then
further project it in the commonly-used equirectangular projection, for easy integration in existing renderers.
3.4.1 Cylindrical slit-scan rendering
First, we align the scene’s vertical axis to an up-vector of
[0, 1, 0]. We define a virtual camera positioned in the center
of the scene. To generate a w × h cylindrical panorama, we
perform w different rendering passes. For each image column
i ∈ [0, w−1], the view direction is set to [sin(θi ), 0, cos(θi )],
with θi = 2π · i/w + π , and the up-vector to [0, 1, 0] to
maintain a consistent horizontal horizon. The camera has
a vertical field of view (FoVY ) of 120◦ . To accommodate
the rasterizer’s internal tile processing, we render a narrow
vertical strip of 16 pixels width for each angle, and extract the
central column of pixels. These columns are concatenated

To obtain an equirectangular map, we apply a geometric
warping transformation. For each pixel (x  , y  ) in the target
equirectangular image, we map the coordinates back to the

Since the vertical FoV was limited to 120◦ , the polar
regions are empty.
To complete the environment map, we optionally perform
generative inpainting using Stable Diffusion [17] via the
Krita Generative AI plugin. Missing sky regions are manually selected and described with the prompt “realistic sky”.
To avoid seams at the horizontal boundaries, we apply an
offset and perform a second inpainting pass. To fill the bottom part of the map, which is covered by the foreground
Gaussians, we paint the regions containing extra elongated
Gaussians in black. The result is a seamless panoramic image
suitable for spherical visualization (Figure 8). This inpainting can also fill holes caused by occlusions, though it was
not needed in our examples.
4 Experimental results
We evaluated our framework on four outdoor datasets,
selected according to our task requirements: (a) captured
in a relatively constrained camera navigation area, and (b)
including a foreground region with objects of interest and a
distant, open background containing a significant portion of
elements like sky, faraway buildings, or trees.
It is worth noting that public datasets fitting these requirements are surprisingly scarce. Most outdoor data consist of
forward facing trajectories (e.g., driving datasets), unconstrained drone flights, or massive large-scale environments.
These do not reflect the “inside-out”, semi-bounded capture
style needed for our target. Because scenes with these characteristics are not easily searchable in public repositories, we
carefully curated three existing ones and captured a custom
one, which is publicly available for future research at the
following URL: https://github.com/DebbyX3/EnvMap-GS.
The chosen datasets are Person and Plane from NerfStudio [19], Francis from Tanks and Temples [9], and Fields,
specifically captured by us. The latter was recorded with a
smartphone camera, from which we extracted frames at 2
FPS, for a total of 602 frames.

For our novel view synthesis benchmarking task, each
sequence was randomly divided into a training set, with 80%
of the frames, and a test set, with the remaining 20%. For each
dataset, we optimized the Gaussian environments using our
pipeline, the official GS code [7], the official Hierarchical GS

[12] and GaussianPro (GauPro) [5]. All methods were initialized with the same point cloud for the training of the
foreground area. We ran all experiments on a machine with an
NVIDIA RTX 5090 (32 GB), an AMD Ryzen 9 7900X CPU,
and 96 GB of RAM. On this machine, our method’s training
time was approximately 40–50 minutes for both stages.

Artifacts do not always significantly affect standard quality metrics. For instance, in the Francis example, HiGS has
the lowest LPIPS score, even though it is heavily affected by
visual artifacts, because it aggressively optimizes fine details
in the foreground grass. However, this comes at the cost of
temporal artifacts like “pop-up” effects and a blurred horizon. In contrast, we prioritize structural stability. While our
foreground might appear slightly softer than HiGS in static
snapshots, we believe that for VR navigation, visual stability
and a clean background are more critical than local highfrequency details that can cause flickering during movement.
This fact suggests that the evaluation of virtual environments
reconstructed with light-field representations should not rely
solely on these metrics, and that different methods could be
designed for specific applications.

4.1.1 Rendering performance evaluation
To evaluate the rendering performance of our Gaussian Splatting method, we measured the average frame time and Frames
per Second (FPS) under two different background settings:
(i) using Gaussians from the first stage as background, and
(ii) replacing these Gaussians with an environment map representation.
All tests were performed on a single camera view. Before
measuring, the GPU was warmed with 30 preliminary
frames, and the average metrics were computed over 500
consecutive frames, disabling gradients to measure inference
performance only.
The results, summarized in Table 2, show the total rendering time over 500 frames, FPS, and the average frame
time for each background configuration. Replacing background Gaussians with an environment map reduces the
average frame time and increases the throughput, indicating
that background Gaussians introduce a significant rendering
overhead even when visually static.
4.1.2 Unity demo
To verify the suitability of our method for interactive VR
navigation, we developed a demo using the Unity Gaussian
Splatting plugin viewer [15]. We recorded video sequences
comparing Gaussian environments created with the different methods and also using our Gaussian + Env. map hybrid
representations. The sequences, available as a Supplemen-

Regarding deployment, we validated our application using
a Meta Quest 3 connected to a PC (with the same hardware

link. Currently, the available Unity Gaussian Splatting plugin relies on compute shaders that are not natively supported
by the mobile VR standalone pipeline (which runs on ARM
architectures). Therefore, real-time performance is achieved
by offloading the rendering computation to the PC while
streaming the output to the VR headset. FPS and metrics
are shown in the Supplementary Video. This setup ensures
a consistent frame rate, preventing the latency that typically
causes motion sickness in VR environments.
4.2 Quality/performance trade-off
While replacing background Gaussians with an environment
map significantly boosts rendering throughput, it introduces
an inherent trade-off. A static 2D environment map cannot
reproduce the motion parallax of mid-distance background
elements when the camera translates. Consequently, standard novel view synthesis metrics (such as PSNR or SSIM)
computed against ground-truth images inevitably drop. However, during VR navigation, this lack of parallax is barely
noticeable for objects like the sky or distant mountains, and
is largely compensated by the benefits of maintaining a stable
frame rate and the elimination of floaters.

Our method is designed for semi-bounded environments
where the navigation area is well-defined. While the distancebased segmentation is effective for this scenario, its reliability
in cluttered environments, such as crowded scenes with many
objects scattered at varying depths, depends on the accuracy
of the input depth estimation. In these cases, a simple distance
threshold might be insufficient to distinguish foreground
from background. We acknowledge this, and suggest that
future research could address this by incorporating semantic
segmentation.
We also plan to create datasets, potentially synthetic,
with uniformly sampled test views to fairly evaluate immersive reconstruction. Our current implementation uses LDR
images, but it can be extended to HDR Gaussian frameworks
[3] or HDR environment maps using learning-based methods
[2, 20].
5 Limitations
Our method shows considerable improvements over prior
work, but has some limitations. First, it assumes static scenes
and cannot handle dynamic background objects. Second,
the threshold-based separation works well for most distant
scenes, but for mid-distance structures that require parallax
(e.g., long bridges) a hard threshold might unfit the geometry
and introduce distortion. A way to mitigate this might be to
use a multiple spheres approach.
Dense depth map initialization produces spherical backgrounds that may still contain holes due to missing input
data. We optionally remove them via image inpainting, but
we plan to integrate inpainting directly into the Gaussian
backgrounds.
Standard metrics for novel view synthesis do not fully
capture artifacts or their visual impact, and full-image metrics
dilute the effect of our improvements. In future work, we
plan to design specific methods to better assess the light-field

6 Conclusions
We presented a two-stage/two-shell framework for reconstructing Gaussian environments, particularly suited for outdoor environments with distant objects and sky. Our method
leverages a segmentation of the image spaces based on the
remapping of metric depth maps onto a global reference system centered in the “navigation” area, where we want to be
able to create realistic and novel synthetic views. By optimizing a first set of Gaussians with properly designed losses to
represent the background and the outer shell, we can avoid the
creation of floaters, while also improving the reconstruction
