Abstract and Figures
In the holographic three-dimensional (3D) display, the numerical synthesis of the computer-generated holograms needs tremendous calculation. To solve the problem, a fast polygon-based method based on two-dimensional Fourier analysis of 3D affine transformation is proposed. From one primitive polygon, the proposed method calculates the diffracted optical field of each arbitrary polygon in the 3D model, where the pseudo-inverse matrix, the interpolation, and the compensation of the power spectral density are employed. The proposed method could save the computation time in the hologram synthesis since it does not need the fast Fourier transform for each polygonal surface and the additional diffusion computation. The numerical simulation and the optical experimental results are presented to demonstrate the effectiveness of the method. The results reveal the proposed method could reconstruct the 3D scene with the solid effect and without the depth limitation. The factors that influence the image quality are discussed, and the thresholds are proposed to ensure the reconstruction quality.

1. Introduction
Computer-generated holography (CGH) is a powerful
technology suitable for a wide range of display types,
including two-dimensional (2D), stereoscopic, auto-
stereoscopic, volumetric, and true three-dimensional
(3D) imaging [1]. The 3D display generated by the
CGH has been intensively researched because it
could precisely provide the parallax and depth infor-
mation of a 3D scene without any special eyewear. At
present, to realize 3D holographic display, one of the
most important tasks is to obtain the CGH by the fast
numerical calculation. There are two main ap-
proaches for sampling the 3D object and calculating
the CGH: the point-based method [2–4] and the poly-
gon-based method [5–21]. The point-based method
needs a great amount of computation for achieving
the solid effect since the 3D object is sampled by a
large number of points. To reduce the computation,
scientists developed the polygon-based method,
where a 3D object is represented by many planar seg-
ments. It is believed the polygon-based method is a
promising approach, which could compute the CGH
at a high speed.
The essence of the polygon-based method is the
calculation of the tilted planar aperture diffracted
optical field in the hologram plane based on the sca-
lar diffraction theory. Compared with the Rayleigh–
Sommerfield integration approach, the plane-wave
decomposition (PWD) method has received attention
for more than 40 years since the angular spectrum
and its propagation are convenient for numerical
computation [22]. Under the PWD frame, the
polygon-based method has three branches: the tradi-
tional method [5–17], the full analytical method

[18,19], and the spatial approximate method [20]. In
the traditional method, Ganci used Fraunhofer ap-
proximation to obtain the diffraction pattern of the
tilted slit [5]. Rabal extended this approach to the
2D aperture [6]. Leseberg and Frére further ex-
tended the calculation to the Fresnel domain by mul-
tiplying a quadric phase factor to the angular
spectrum of the tilted aperture. They analyzed the
distortion caused by nonlinear coordinate transfor-
mation, investigated the interpolation approach,
and considered the tilted hologram plane [7,8].
Tommasi and Bianco first evaluated the relation
for the angular spectrum of the rotated planes. In
the spectral domain, they used the rotational trans-
formation matrix to compute the angular spectrum of
the rotated aperture without any approximation and
analyzed the issue caused by the numerical calcula-
tion [9,10]. Matsushima summarized the whole for-
mulation of the traditional method, analyzed the
influence of different interpolation approaches, and
investigated the surface rendering with texture,
shade, and reflection. He used the method to recon-
struct extremely high-definition full-parallax photo-
realistic scenes [11–16]. Onural utilized the impulse
function over a 3D surface and converted the 2D
diffraction problem to a general 3D problem, which
makes the spectral rotation and translation be more
easily understandable [17]. In the implementation,
the traditional method rotates an arbitrary polygon
in the global coordinates to the tilt local coordinates,
represents its property function, and calculates its
global spectrum by the rotational transformation
in the spectral domain. For each polygon surface,
it must explicitly conduct angular-dependent rota-
tional transformations, one surface representation,
and one fast Fourier transform (FFT).
Full analytical methods were introduced to elimi-
nate those extra computations, such as FFT, in the
traditional method [18,19]. Its basic idea is to estab-
lish a connection between the analytical expression
of the spectrum of the primitive aperture and that
of the arbitrary aperture. Using the 2D Fourier
analysis of the 2D affine theorem [21], this method
first computes the local angular spectrum of an
arbitrary polygon from the spectrum of a unique
primitive polygon. Then it calculates the global
spectrum of the arbitrary polygon by the use of
the rotational transformation of the traditional
method and propagates the wave by the angular
spectrum approach. All the computation is realized
by implicit variable substitution. Although the full
analytical method avoids the FFT and the property
representation, it still needs angular-dependent ro-
tational transformations, a further texture-adding
algorithm, and the diffusion computation for the
reconstruction on the phase-only spatial light
modulator (SLM).
The spatial approximate method is developed to
reconstruct the 3D scene that has limited depth
[20]. An optical field in an intermediate plane that
is diffracted by the primitive polygonal aperture is
precomputed. This method uses the precomputed op-
tical field to approximately calculate the field dif-
fracted by other apertures. Those apertures have
translational, rotational, and scaling relation with
the primitive polygonal aperture. This method saves
the FFT and could add diffusive information without
extra computation, but it has depth constrains. In
brief, although all the three previous methods could
compute the diffraction, the traditional method
needs the FFT and the surface representation, the
full analytical method needs additional texture add-
ing and diffusion computation, and the spatial ap-
proximate method is not suitable for a 3D scene
with large depth.
In this paper, we propose a fast polygon-based
method under the PWD frame, where the FFT for
each surface is not needed. In the local coordinate
system, a unique primitive polygon with an ampli-
tude and a phase function is defined. The 2D Fourier
analysis of the 3D affine transformation is used to
connect the angular spectrum of the arbitrary poly-
gon and that of the primitive polygon without any ap-
proximation, where the parameters of the affine
transformation between the global coordinates and
the local coordinates are calculated from the vertices
of the arbitrary polygon and that of the primitive
polygon. The proposed method can calculate the
CGH in one step since the affine transformation con-
tains the rotational, translational, and scaling trans-
formation. The advantage of the proposed method is
it does not have FFT for each surface, extra diffusion
computation, and the limitation of the depth. It re-
duces the computation time in the hologram
synthesis.
In the following sections, we will introduce the
theory and the implementation, show the numerical
and the optical experimental results, analyze the
advantages, and discuss the limitations. The paper
is organized as follows: in Section 2, the basic
formulas and theories of the proposed method and
the implementation approach are presented. In
Section 3we describe the numerical simulation
and the optical experimental results, where the ac-
celeration performance is presented as well. Section 4
gives the analysis of the acceleration performance
and the factors that influence the reconstruction
quality. In Section 5the concluding remarks
are given.

4. Discussion
In this section, we will first discuss the mechanism
behind the acceleration performance. In order to
make a comparison between the proposed method
and the traditional method clearly, we reproduced
the traditional method in the same simulation envir-
onment. In the reproduction, the FFT and the prop-
erty representation occupy around 23% of the total
computation time, and the other subordinate calcu-
lation, such as the angle solving and matrix multipli-
cation, occupies around 2% of the total time. The
proposed method uses the unique angular spectrum
of the primitive triangle to calculate the CGH, where
it discards the FFT and property representation
of each arbitrary polygon and saves the corres-
ponding subordinate computation. Therefore, it
saves the computation time. From Fig. 6, the 25% re-
duction of the computation time agrees with the
conclusion.

5. Conclusion
A fast polygon-based method is proposed to synthe-
size the CGH of a 3D model, where a unique isosceles
right primitive triangle assigned with the unit ampli-
tude and a random phase with flat PSD is employed
to compute the angular spectrum of each arbitrary
triangle in the 3D model. The numerical simulation
and the optical experimental results showed the pro-
posed method could reconstruct the 3D scene with
the solid effect at a high speed. This method does
not have the representation and the FFT for each
surface, additional diffusion calculation, and depth
limitation. It is worth noting that, compared with
the traditional polygon-based method [16], the pro-
posed method cannot directly add texture and
smooth shade to the 3D scene at present. To imple-
ment the texturing and smooth shading, one solution
is conducting 2D spectrum convolution, though it is
time consuming. In the future, we would like to in-
vestigate a simple algorithm in the spectrum domain
to solve the issue. Another limitation of the proposed
method is it cannot reconstruct the 3D scene that has
a large range of variation of the size of the arbitrary
triangle since it is hard to choose an appropriate pri-
mitive triangle to reconstruct the scene without un-
expected noise and energy loss. In the following
research, we will improve the PSD-compensation al-
gorithm, which allows the new frequency to exceed
the original frequency domain and make the pro-
posed method more adaptive. The parallel computa-
tion could further improve the speed performance of
the method. We believe, in the future, the proposed
method with the GPU parallel computation will
make the real-time 3D holographic display into
reality.

Conventional fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based algorithms suffer from computational redundancy and fidelity loss due to the decoupled rasterization pipeline and the spatial-domain interpolation required to resolve the geometric shape mismatch in texture mapping. Computer graphics aim to create visual representations for screens, where depth is simulated. In contrast, Computed Generated Holograms (CGH) focus on encoding and recreating light patterns to generate a true 3D holographic image that appears as a physical object in space. Therefore, although both use digital models, the computation of CGHs necessitates additional phase‐related calculations, which in turn escalate computational demands. These calculations often result in excessively long development times or, at worst, render the process unfeasible. In order to reduce computational time, Partial Monte–Carlo Sampling (PMCS) techniques for CGH generation are presented, integrating them into the whole process of generating a CGH for a synthetic 3D scene, from design to rendering. PMCS is based on the random choice of a subset of rays used to compute the CGH and relates the computation time spent to the quality of the reconstructed scene. Quantitative analysis shows that PMCS does not significantly compromise image quality. Both simulated and in‐laboratory image reconstruction from holograms demonstrates consistent trends, showcasing improved quality with higher numbers of rays and increased resolution. Furthermore, we establish a direct relationship between image quality and computational time, which effectively addresses specific requirements.

Effective computer-generated hologram method combining deep learning with angular spectrum method
Article
Apr 2025
Meilan Piao
Chaoyu Liang
Tianyi Zhu
Jian Wei
We introduce an approach to generating good-quality computer-generated holograms (CGHs) by integrating deep learning with the angular spectrum method (ASM). The approach incorporates an optimized U-Net network, which is enhanced with attention mechanisms and residual modules, aimed at improving hologram generation. The dataset comprises a variety of input intensity distributions along with their corresponding holograms, propagated via the ASM. The training encompasses more than just random spot images and their holographic propagation; it extends to the random adjustment of spot density and size. These randomized blob images facilitate the generation of both real and imaginary holograms through ASM. Two separate datasets, one featuring real holograms and the other imaginary, are utilized to train two unique models. Subsequently, the network-generated holograms are integrated with those generated by ASM, yielding a total of four distinct hologram types. The effectiveness of the method was validated through both numerical and optical reconstructions. The results demonstrate that our method simplifies and expedites the generation of good-quality CGHs, outperforming traditional approaches.

Computer-generated holograms of tilted planes by a spatial frequency approach
Article
Full-text available
Feb 1993
J OPT SOC AM A
Tullio Tommasi
Bruno Bianco
Computer-generated holograms of plane surfaces tilted and shifted with respect to the hologram plane are considered. The analysis is made in the spatial frequency domain by using the translation and rotation transformations of angular spectra. The frequency approach permits the use of the fast-Fourier-transform algorithm, which decreases the computation time and makes it possible to consider any position of the planes in space. Various configurations of tilted and shifted planes have been investigated, and computer-generated holograms of off-axis planes have been obtained. Computer and optical reconstructions, both of which show the feasibility of the proposed approach, have been carried out.

Simple wave-field rendering for photorealistic reconstruction in polygon-based high-definition computer holography
Article
Apr 2012
Kyoji Matsushima
Hirohito Nishi
Sumio Nakahara
A simple and practical technique is presented for creating fine
three-dimensional (3D) images with polygon-based computer-generated
holograms. The polygon-based method is a technique for computing the
optical wave-field of virtual 3D scenes given by a numerical model. The
presented method takes less computation time than common point-source
methods and produces fine spatial 3D images of deep 3D scenes that
convey a strong sensation of depth, unlike conventional 3D systems
providing only binocular disparity. However, smooth surfaces cannot be
reconstructed using the presented method because the surfaces are
approximated by planar polygons. This problem is resolved by introducing
a simple rendering technique that is almost the same as that in common
computer graphics, since the polygon-based method has similarity to
rendering techniques in computer graphics. Two actual computer holograms
are presented to verify and demonstrate the proposed technique. One is a
hologram of a live face whose shape is measured using a 3D laser scanner
that outputs polygon-mesh data. The other is for a scene including the
moon. Both are created employing the proposed rendering techniques of
the texture mapping of real photographs and smooth shading.

The field amplitude diffracted by a tilted aperture, under the Fraunhofer approximation, is investigated. A new coordinate system is found in which the amplitude of the diffracted field is still expressed as the Fourier transform of the aperture. The complex degree of coherence (CDC) originated by a tilted planar source is analysed in a similar way.

Exact hidden-surface removal in digitally synthetic full-parallax holograms
Conference Paper
Apr 2005
Kyoji Matsushima
A new algorithm for removing hidden surfaces from reconstruction of computer-generated holograms is presented. The object used in the algorithm is defined by surface model and each polygon composing the object provides a mask for blocking the incident field into the backside of the polygon. The computational cost of the proposed algorithm is 2 FFT/polygon by handling field transmission in Fourier space and integrating the surface diffraction method for generating fields. Reconstruction of a hologram synthesized by using the presented algorithm is demonstrated.

Fourier diffraction through a tilted slit
Article
Dec 2000
EUR J PHYS
Salvatore Ganci
Describes a simplified treatment of the diffraction from a single slit, in a particular case of oblique incidence. Using the methods of Fourier optics, one can see that the angular spectrum consists of two factors: the first is the same as that obtained by normal incidence and the second represents a law of 'reflection' or 'refraction' for the diffracted wave. This property substantially constitutes the 'law of edge diffraction' and from it, as a limiting case, one can deduce the laws of geometrical optics.
