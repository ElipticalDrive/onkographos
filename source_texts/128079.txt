INTRODUCTION (HEADING A)
Monte Carlo (MC) particle simulation is a technique in
which large numbers of individual particles are simulated in
random walks through a structure to estimate a radiological
quantity. These techniques are critical for applications
ranging from designing particle detectors to analyzing
radiation safety. They are particularly useful for simulating
systems with complex geometry, where designing numerical
or analytical solutions may be highly fraught, but a
stochastic simulation only needs to simulate enough
individual particles to confidently assert the quantity of
interest. These simulations are critical in simulating neutron
behavior in both fission and fusion reactors alike.
In order to trust such a physics simulation, the geometry
of the simulated device needs to be confidently and
efficiently represented. The GPU-based Oak Ridge
Advanced Nested Geometry Engine (ORANGE),³ used by
the Celeritas MC code to model high-energy particle
physics and particle detectors, and by Shift to model
radiation transport, represents complex geometries through
constructive solid geometry (CSG). ORANGE tracks
particles through a series of basic “primitive surfaces” like
spheres and planes, each having an ‘inside’ and an ‘outside’,
which are combined to form 3D volumes.
To robustly represent a given structure this way,
enough kinds of surfaces are required to model the shapes of
that structure. ORANGE currently supports surfaces which
are sufficient for most nuclear reactors and many detectors,
and is additionally planned to support shapes like toroids,
particularly useful for fusion reactors.
However, toroids are a quartic surface, more
complicated than typical quadric surfaces like cylinders or
spheres. As a result, to find the intersection between a ray
and a toroid, finding the minimum positive root of a fourthdegree polynomial is required, rather than that of a seconddegree polynomial for a quadratic, or a first-degree
polynomial for a linear (planar) surface.
Understanding the options for, and limits of, simulating
such a quartic surface will be valuable to integrating this
feature into Celeritas, to ensure it is implemented as
effectively as possible. It may also potentially be relevant to
end users of the software who may wish to design models
containing toroidal shapes.
In this work, two techniques for solving quartic
polynomials are compared for polynomials of ray-toroid
intersection: the Ferrari-Cardano method¹ and Algorithm
1010.² The comparison of these algorithms across multiple
levels of floating point precision serves to determine
feasibility of the algorithms for this application, precisionbased limitations on the use of toroidal geometry, and the
necessity of additional modifications like bounding boxes,
or a higher level of floating point precision.
Background
While quartic equations can be solved analytically,
analytic solutions such as the Ferrari-Cardano method can
easily suffer dramatic precision loss, having many
opportunities for cancellation errors and precision loss
through evaluating radical or trigonometric functions. Using
numerical solutions for higher accuracy also becomes
problematic because of the computational time and initial
estimates required.
For a MC code simulating a vast number of particles,
and particularly one like Celeritas which uses the GPU
(where more complex or iterative algorithms can severely
hinder performance), this introduces new potential precision
limitations and raises a number of concerns.
For example, as shown in Figure 1, a typical routine in
Celeritas is for a particle to move along a path, keeping
track of when it crosses from one side of a surface to
another, or changes direction inside a volume to mimic a
scattering event. Should a particle be located on one side of
the surface, and generate a scattering event very close to that
surface but pointing away from it, the code may, due to
limits of precision, decide the particle has passed through
the surface, behind it, even though it was already on that
side of the surface.
This is one mechanism that can result in a highly
undesirable “lost particle,” as the random walk can no
longer proceed. Other undesirable results of precision loss
might include inaccurate boundary intersection distances, or
missed boundary intersections entirely.

There exist many methods to solve a quartic equation
such as this. This analysis focuses on the traditional Ferrari
method, and the recently developed Algorithm 1010, which
is currently implemented in the OpenMC MC radiation
transport code for CPU simulation of toroidal surfaces. The
Ferrari-Cardano method uses a substitution to factor the
quartic equation into two quadratic equations, by solving a
“resolvent cubic” equation for that substitution. Likewise,
Algorithm 1010 solves for two quadratic sub-polynomials,
however, in the process of solving for these, the algorithm
introduces several branches at key steps where precision
may be lost. At each branch multiple equivalent formulae
are considered, and one chosen which loses the least
precision. The pairs of quadratic coefficients are then further
refined using the Newton-Raphson iterative root-finding
method, before solving for the final roots. Algorithm 1010
is thus designed generally to maintain precision, but given
the increased complexity of the algorithm it is desired to
more specifically understand cases of precision loss in the
application of toroidal ray tracing, in order to optimize for
GPU performance.

Experiment 1: Minimum Escape Distance
The minimum escape distance ‘epsilon’ was compared
at different levels of precision between Ferrari and
Algorithm 1010 solvers. Figures 6 and 7 compare the largest
such required epsilon across several ray directions across
different levels of precision, between the two solution

rays by an order of magnitude, a subtle difference compared
to that of changing precision level.

Experiment 2: Precision Loss by Distance Scale
Figures 8, 9, and 10 compare the average deviation of
calculated distances from a high-precision reference
solution, for normal rays, vertically oriented and laterally
oriented grazing rays, respectively. In the grazing ray cases,
and especially lateral grazing rays, a substantial (5 orders of
magnitude) difference in error was found when using
Algorithm 1010 over the Ferrari method at close distances.
Distances for which a solver failed to find a distance for at
least 1 reflection (including laterally grazing rays at any
distance at single precision) were not included.
Discussion and Implications
For both rays in normal and tangential directions, the
Algorithm 1010 implementation could consistently resolve
closer rays to the toroid than the Ferrari-Cardano method
implementation without false intersection, though this was
dwarfed by the effect of different precision levels.
Likewise, while the deviation of results using the
Ferrari-Cardano method were consistently somewhat higher
than those using Algorithm 1010, and particularly for nearly
grazing rays, the much greater effect was whether single,
double, or quad precision floating points were used.
Notably, unlike with experiment 1, the orientation of
grazing rays also seemed to introduce differences between
solver accuracy, with laterally-oriented grazing rays (as seen
in Figure 12) having 7 orders of magnitude more error when
resolved using the Ferrari-Cardano method than with
Algorithm 1010 (compared to th 4 of vertically grazing
rays). Also notable were the two regimes of deviation: for
distances lesser than 100 cm (for the JET-scale toroid, of
radii 300 cm, 125 cm, and 200 cm), deviation seemed not to
vary with distance, whereas above this distance deviation
was logarithmically proportional to the distance from
surface and did not seem to depend on the quartic solver
used. This suggests that other factors than distance, like the
aspect ratios of the toroid, also play a substantial role in loss
of precision.
Another such factor to consider is the role of
polynomial calculation in precision loss. Whatever precision
is lost while calculating the polynomial coefficients
themselves is not precision that can then be recovered using
more elaborate root-finding techniques. In particular,
consider the mistaken backwards intersections of
experiment 1: since this involves finding a negative root
extremely close to 0, that means finding a polynomial of the
nature shown in Equation 2:
4
3
2
ax + bx + cx +dx+ e=0 , e≈0
(2)
When the coefficient e is, as a result of cancellation
errors, flipped, this necessarily flips the sign of that small
root (Descarte’s Rule of Signs), resulting in a mistakenly
counted or ignored intersection. Better resolution of the
initial polynomial, in these types of cases, may be even
more beneficial to precision than using a more sophisticated
solver. In the case of ORANGE, particle tracking typically
stores a priori if a particle is approximately on a surface
(and thus one root will be close to zero), allowing this
problem to be appropriately avoided.
The data comparing deviation across distance scales
also present a means to assess the necessity of a bounding
box technique (Where calculations are first performed on a
simpler shape encapsulating the complex one) to improve
precision. If, for any distance a ray might reasonably
approach a toroidal surface from, the expected numerical
error is too great to handle, a bounding box would then be
necessary to avoid incurring that error.
There are many other factors that this work has yet to
fully explore. For example, one would expect the
dimensions of the toroid to also play a crucial role in these
precision losses, especially as this directly changes how
closely the roots are spaced to each other, a noted source of
quartic solver error. Extending this analysis over various
dimensions of toroids would make its results more broadly
usable towards implementing or using toroidal shapes in a
variety of applications
In addition, further analysis of how toroid intersection
cases correspond to the polynomial would also guide
investigations into cases where solvers like the FerrariCardano method may be irredeemably error-prone, and if
any cases would then fully necessitate the more robust
Algorithm 1010. Understanding where these cases
correspond to the control flow of Algorithm 1010 would
allow a reduced implementation of the algorithm to
optimize GPU performance while avoiding critical error
cases.
