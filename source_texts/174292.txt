Abstract
This paper presents a GPU-accelerated visualization framework for interactive exploration of fusion-plasma simulation data represented as scalar fields and particle trajectories. The primary technical contribution is a capacity-safe isosurface-extraction pipeline that combines inclusive slab partitioning with a two-pass counting and exact allocation strategy. This design prevents slab-boundary omissions and output-buffer overflow while supporting large scalar grids. The generated positions and normals are written directly to CUDA–OpenGL interoperable buffers, enabling the standalone renderer to display explicit isosurfaces without an intermediate CPU-side copy. The extracted geometry and scalar-volume data are additionally integrated into Unreal Engine through a separate staging interface. This integration provides a unified environment for volume rendering, explicit-isosurface inspection, particle-trajectory visualization, clipping, and VR-based spatial exploration. These engine-side functions are used as interaction and visualization mechanisms rather than being presented as new rendering algorithms. Experiments on electromagnetic-potential fields with resolutions up to 5123 demonstrate stable isosurface extraction across different isovalues. In the most geometrically complex tested case, the method generates approximately 42.81 million vertices in 44.7 ms, uses less than 11 GB of GPU memory, and renders the resulting geometry at 28.6 FPS. The results indicate that the proposed framework provides a practical explicit-surface visualization path for large plasma datasets while complementing direct volume rendering and established scientific-visualization toolkits.
Introduction
Fusion plasma simulations involve extremely large-scale interactions among electromagnetic fields, fluids, and particles, and both grid and temporal resolutions continue to grow rapidly. As a result, the data volume frequently reaches hundreds of gigabytes to several terabytes, comprising mixtures of isosurfaces, scalar fields (e.g., potential, pressure, charge density), and trajectory/streamline-based particle datasets. Visualizing such complex and massive data in 3D is essential for understanding morphological phenomena–-such as boundary structures, instabilities, and transport paths–-yet the workflow in many research environments remains fragmented into offline preprocessing

offline viewer loading, which inherently limits interactive analysis.
In particular, examples of real-time (interactive-rate) 3D visualization for plasma data are rare. This is primarily due to the following factors: (1) for high-resolution grids (e.g., 5123 or above), isosurface extraction methods such as Marching Cubes or Flying Edges incur substantial memory and bandwidth bottlenecks; (2) the extracted meshes often contain tens of millions of vertices, creating additional bottlenecks at the rendering stage; and (3) when particle-based visualization (trajectory/streamline tracing) is incorporated, host-to-device (H2D) and device-to-host (D2H) data transfers between the computation and visualization stages can introduce substantial overhead. Consequently, a common pattern has been “simulation on the GPU, visualization via CPU/files/external viewers,” forcing users to rely on batch-style, offline results instead of interactive exploration.
To address this gap, we propose a GPU-centered plasma visualization pipeline and game-engine integration framework that minimizes CPU-side transfers in the standalone rendering path while supporting engine-based visualization through a separate staging interface. Specifically, we integrate isosurface extraction–implemented via a capacity-safe Marching Cubes algorithm–with particle-based rendering (particle/trajectory tracing) into a single lightweight GPU workflow. The key idea is to employ slab tiling and inclusive slab indexing to eliminate boundary cracks, and to adopt a two-pass pipeline (classification/scan → exact allocation) that guarantees crack-free isosurfaces and prevents buffer overruns even at high grid resolutions. The generated mesh and normals are streamed zero-copy into VBOs through CUDA–OpenGL interop, allowing them to be rendered immediately without any CPU round-trip.
Furthermore, the proposed pipeline is integrated with the commercial game engine Unreal Engine, enabling an end-to-end workflow that unifies volume → isosurface mesh → particle tracing within a single editing and execution environment. Engine-side compute-based particle tracing (streamlines/pathlines) combined with color, size, and lifetime mappings allows rapid identification and emphasis of plasma trajectories. Parameter sweeps (ISO threshold, seed/step), LOD control, and snapshot/export (OBJ) functions streamline the entire analysis workflow, from data loading to reporting.
In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:
A slab-based, two-pass GPU isosurface-extraction pipeline that combines exact output allocation with boundary-consistent indexing for large scalar grids.
A tightly coupled extraction and rendering workflow that writes the generated geometry directly to an interoperable graphics buffer.
An Unreal Engine visualization environment that integrates scalar-volume rendering, explicit isosurfaces, particle trajectories, clipping, parameter control, and VR-based spatial inspection.
Related work
This section reviews representative research across key axes of large-scale 3D scientific visualization–-isosurface extraction, GPU acceleration and memory/bandwidth optimization, particle (trajectory/streamline) visualization, screen-space enhancement techniques, and in-situ/engine integration.
Explicit isosurface extraction
The classical Marching Cubes (MC) algorithm by Lorensen and Cline introduced a simple procedure in which the signs of the eight cell corners are classified to generate triangles using a case table [1]. However, the standard MC formulation can produce topological discontinuities and holes due to ambiguities in cell-internal interpolation. The Asymptotic Decider addresses such cases by resolving ambiguous configurations using the asymmetry of the internal contour [2]. Chernyaev’s MC33 systematically enumerates 33 disambiguated cases, ensuring improved topological consistency [3], while Dual Contouring preserves sharp features by utilizing edge Hermite data (intersection points and normals) [4]. Recent approaches such as SurfaceNets and Flying Edges emphasize boundary locality and parallelism. Unlike topology-oriented extensions such as MC33 and Dual Contouring, the present work focuses on boundary-consistent slab processing and exact GPU output allocation for engine-integrated visualization.
Direct volume rendering and implicit isosurfaces
Direct volume rendering maps scalar values to optical properties through transfer functions and accumulates color and opacity along viewing rays without explicitly constructing surface geometry. GPU-based ray casting has become a standard approach for interactive volume visualization because it supports flexible transfer-function control, clipping, gradient-based shading, and inspection of internal structures. Implicit isosurfaces can also be detected during ray traversal by locating scalar-field crossings at selected isovalues. This approach avoids explicit triangle storage and allows multiple isosurfaces to be rendered within the same ray-casting framework.
In the present system, direct volume rendering and explicit isosurface extraction serve different purposes. Volume ray casting is used in the Unreal Engine environment for interactive inspection of the complete scalar field, including transfer-function-based emphasis and clipping-plane exploration. In contrast, the Marching Cubes pipeline produces explicit geometry that can be rendered through conventional rasterization, combined with reactor meshes, manipulated as a geometric object, and exported for subsequent use. The two approaches are therefore treated as complementary rather than competing visualization modes.
GPU-based large-scale isosurface extraction
For large grids, the main computational challenges arise from memory-bandwidth requirements, branching during cell classification and table lookup, and management of output geometry whose size is not known in advance.
HistoPyramids-based Marching Cubes determines output locations through hierarchical counting and stream compaction, thereby avoiding uncontrolled dynamic output. Prefix-sum-based GPU implementations similarly compute per-cell primitive counts before geometry generation. Flying Edges reorganizes the extraction process around grid edges to improve memory locality and reduce redundant computation. VTK-m provides data-parallel abstractions and manages intermediate and output storage through its execution framework, supporting portable isocontouring across CPUs and GPUs.
These approaches demonstrate that safe output-buffer management is already well established in GPU-based isosurface extraction. The present work therefore does not claim that existing methods fail to handle memory safely. Instead, our contribution is a lightweight CUDA implementation in which slab-local counts are accumulated into an exact global allocation, inclusive slab boundaries maintain consistent cell coverage, and the generated vertices and normals are written directly to an interoperable rendering buffer. This design specializes existing counting and compaction principles for tightly coupled extraction, rendering, and engine-oriented visualization within a compact single-GPU workflow.
GPU interoperability for visualization
Graphics–compute interoperability enables GPU-generated data to be consumed directly by a rendering API without an intermediate CPU copy. CUDA–OpenGL interoperability has been widely used for GPU-based geometry generation, particle systems, and scientific visualization. In this work, interoperability is used as an implementation mechanism for transferring the output of isosurface extraction directly to the OpenGL rendering stage. Detailed buffer-registration and synchronization procedures are described in the Proposed Framework section.
Shading and screen-space techniques
In scientific visualization, conventional Lambertian shading may provide insufficient contrast when surface orientation varies substantially across the view. Wrapped diffuse shading extends the illuminated region by shifting and rescaling the Lambertian term, while camera-aligned lighting places the light direction near the viewing direction to provide relatively consistent illumination over view-facing structures. Hemisphere lighting and screen-space ambient occlusion can further improve the perception of surface orientation, concavity, and local depth. In this work, we use camera-aligned two-sided diffuse shading as a conventional visualization configuration, together with optional SSAO for enhanced local contrast. These components are used only to improve surface readability and are not presented as novel shading techniques.
Particle (advection) and streamline visualization
In plasma and fluid analysis, particle trajectories and streamlines play a complementary role to scalar isosurfaces. Laramee et al. established the classical taxonomy of texture-based, geometry-based, and feature-based flow visualization techniques [5,6], and McLoughlin et al. surveyed two decades of integration-based and geometric flow visualization methods [7]. More recently, particle advection using VTK-m has been employed in large-scale simulations due to its performance portability [8]. Our pipeline is designed so that particle visualization can be seamlessly combined within the same frame (with GUI-controlled seeds and step size, and color/size/lifetime mappings), and when integrated with Unreal, it is executed through the engine’s compute pathway.
In-situ and commercial engine (unreal engine) integration
In-situ visualization performs analysis and rendering concurrently with the simulation (in memory), reducing disk I/O bottlenecks and enabling interactive feedback. ParaView Catalyst provides a lightweight API/adapter for in-situ coupling [9], and VisIt’s Libsim offers a similar interface for simulation-driven visualization [10–13]. SENSEI proposes a generic in-situ interface enabling compatibility with various backends such as Catalyst and Libsim [14,15], while Ascent demonstrates an interactive in-situ pipeline through a lightweight visualization library [16]. This paper explores how such in-situ workflows can be coupled with a commercial engine (Unreal), which offers high-quality rendering, extensive editing workflows, and an established VR ecosystem. However, bridging scientific data with the engine requires addressing technical challenges such as large-volume streaming, precise vertex/normal management, and analysis-oriented shading. By unifying volume extraction, isosurface generation, and particle tracing inside the engine, our framework accelerates the exploration of plasma trajectories.
Proposed framework
This section describes the proposed pipeline in the following order: problem setup and notation, capacity-safe slab-based Marching Cubes, zero-copy rendering, Unreal Engine integration, and implementation parameters and limitations.
Rationale for Explicit Isosurface Extraction. Implicit isosurface raycasting is generally more memory-efficient when the objective is limited to direct visualization of one or more isovalues from a resident scalar volume. It avoids explicit triangle generation and can compute smooth normals directly from volume gradients. We therefore do not claim that Marching Cubes is universally more efficient than implicit raycasting.
In this work, however, the extracted surface is used as an explicit geometric asset rather than only as an intermediate rendering representation. The generated geometry is streamed to an OpenGL vertex buffer, imported into the Unreal Engine rendering pipeline, combined spatially with reactor geometry, manipulated using standard mesh-based interaction tools, and optionally exported as an OBJ file. These operations require accessible vertex and normal data and are difficult to support through a purely implicit raycasting representation without an additional surface-extraction stage.
Accordingly, the purpose of the proposed capacity-safe Marching Cubes pipeline is not to replace implicit volume raycasting, but to provide a robust and interactive explicit surface-extraction path for workflows that require reusable geometric output. For visualization tasks that require only direct inspection of scalar volumes or simultaneous display of multiple isovalues, implicit raycasting remains a more appropriate alternative.

Correctness and computational cost. The inclusive slab ranges ensure that every valid cell is processed exactly once, including cells adjacent to slab boundaries. Because shared edges use the same scalar samples and isovalue, their interpolated intersection positions remain consistent across neighboring slabs. For M=(Nx−1)(Ny−1)(Nz−1) cells, both passes require O(M) time. The additional storage consists of the slab-local count and offset arrays, while the output buffers are allocated according to the exact number of generated vertices.
Zero-copy streaming and visualization-oriented shading
Interop Rendering. After CUDA finishes writing the generated positions and normals, the interoperable buffers are unmapped and consumed directly by the OpenGL rendering pipeline. Standard vertex-attribute binding and triangle rasterization are used to render the extracted geometry. Two-sided normal handling, camera-aligned diffuse shading, and optional SSAO are applied as established visualization components. Because these operations follow standard CUDA–OpenGL and GLSL practice, low-level API calls are omitted for brevity.
The current implementation assigns one face normal to each generated triangle, resulting in flat-shaded visualization rather than gradient-based smooth shading. In contrast, implicit raycasting can evaluate smooth normals directly from the scalar-field gradient. Gradient-derived vertex normals could also be incorporated into the explicit pipeline by sampling the volume gradient at each interpolated surface vertex.
Parameters and Limitations. The recommended slab thickness is S∈[48,96] for a 5123 grid. If Nz−1 is not divisible by S, the final slab is processed as a partial slab without padding, with its upper cell index clamped to Nz−2. The method targets explicit isosurface extraction and is therefore less suitable for sharp-feature preservation than methods such as Dual Contouring or MC33. In addition, the current implementation is limited to volumes that fit within the memory of a single GPU.
Unreal Engine Integration Architecture. The CUDA–OpenGL interoperability path and the Unreal Engine visualization path are implemented as two distinct rendering backends that share the same extracted geometry and scalar-volume representation. In the standalone renderer, CUDA writes vertex positions and normals directly into OpenGL vertex buffers registered through cudaGraphicsGLRegisterBuffer. After the buffers are unmapped, OpenGL renders the generated geometry without a CPU-side copy.
For the Unreal Engine backend, the generated geometry is transferred through a host-side staging interface rather than by directly sharing the OpenGL VBO with Unreal Engine’s rendering hardware interface. Vertex positions, triangle indices, and normals are submitted using a ProceduralMeshComponent, while scalar fields are converted into volume-texture-compatible data for ray-marching visualization. Thus, the zero-copy property applies to the standalone CUDA–OpenGL backend, whereas the Unreal Engine implementation uses an explicit staging transfer.
Experimental results
GPU-based isosurface extraction
Plasma Simulation Dataset. The input data used in this study are three-dimensional scalar fields generated by a fusion-plasma simulation. Each grid sample stores an electromagnetic potential value defined over a uniform Cartesian grid, and the grid resolutions used in the experiments are 2563 and 5123 samples.
The scalar values describe the spatial variation of the electromagnetic potential within the computational domain. Isosurfaces are extracted at selected threshold values to reveal regions having the same potential magnitude. Of particular interest are the shape of the plasma boundary, internal high-gradient structures, localized unstable patterns, and the spatial continuity of the extracted surfaces. These features are useful for visually examining morphological changes and identifying regions of interest in the simulated plasma field.
Before visualization, the scalar values are normalized to [0,1] using the minimum and maximum values of each dataset. The isovalue τ is therefore specified in the normalized scalar range. Multiple isovalues are used to examine how the extracted surface topology, geometric complexity, and generated vertex count vary with the selected threshold.

Quantitative Performance Evaluation. To quantitatively evaluate the scalability and interactive performance of the proposed pipeline, we measured the isosurface extraction time, rendering frame rate, and peak GPU memory usage at grid resolutions of 1283, 2563, and 5123. For each resolution, three normalized isovalues, τ=0.35, τ=0.50, and τ=0.65, were evaluated to account for variations in surface complexity and the number of generated vertices.
All experiments were performed on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 3955WX CPU, 256 GB of system memory, and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of VRAM. Each configuration was executed 100 times after 20 warm-up iterations. The extraction time includes cell classification, prefix-sum computation, exact VBO allocation, and triangle generation. The rendering frame rate was measured after the generated geometry had been transferred to the CUDA–OpenGL interoperable VBO. Peak VRAM usage includes the input scalar volume, intermediate count and offset buffers, and the generated position and normal buffers.
As summarized in Table 2, increasing the grid resolution increases both the number of processed cells and the amount of generated geometry. At 1283 and 2563, all configurations achieve extraction times below 20 ms and rendering rates above 90 FPS. At 5123, the most geometrically complex configuration generates approximately 42.81 million vertices and requires 44.7 ms for isosurface extraction. Nevertheless, the resulting geometry can still be rendered at 28.6 FPS, while the configurations with moderate and high isovalues maintain rendering rates of 36.9 FPS and 49.1 FPS, respectively.

The results also show that performance depends not only on grid resolution but also on the selected isovalue. Lower isovalues generally produce larger and more complex isosurfaces, increasing the number of generated vertices, extraction time, and memory consumption. In contrast, higher isovalues reduce the geometric output and therefore improve both extraction and rendering performance. Even in the most demanding 5123 case, peak VRAM usage remains below 11 GB, demonstrating that the two-pass exact-allocation strategy avoids excessive static buffer reservation and operates within the memory capacity of a single commodity GPU.

Although complete isosurface regeneration at 5123 is slower than the subsequent rendering stage, interactive viewpoint navigation and visual analysis remain possible at approximately 29–49 FPS once the VBO has been generated. Moreover, parameter changes such as the isovalue can be processed within tens of milliseconds, allowing responsive exploration of large-scale plasma volumes.
Visual Analysis.
Fig 1 shows spatially continuous isosurfaces without visible discontinuities at slab boundaries. Fine-scale structures remain identifiable across different viewpoints, while the outer and internal surface regions retain geometric continuity. The jagged appearance of some thin structures primarily originates from the discretization of the input scalar field and the polygonal approximation produced by Marching Cubes. Because the current implementation uses one face normal per triangle, flat shading further emphasizes triangle boundaries and rapid changes in surface orientation. Screen-space aliasing may additionally affect a small number of silhouette pixels, but it is not the primary source of the visible internal patterns. The corresponding extraction time, rendering performance, generated vertex count, and peak GPU memory usage are reported in Table 2.
The method is designed for explicit isosurface extraction and is therefore less suitable for preserving sharp features than approaches such as Dual Contouring or MC33. In addition, the current implementation is limited to volumes that fit within the memory of a single GPU. Future work will investigate out-of-core streaming, tile-based caching, and hybrid extraction methods for improved scalability and feature preservation.

Input Data and Smoothing Scope. The input to the smoothing stage is the same electromagnetic-potential scalar field used for isosurface extraction in the preceding experiments. The term “uniform scalar field” refers to the fact that the simulation values are sampled on a regular Cartesian grid with constant spacing along each spatial axis; it does not refer to a separate or artificially generated volume.
The smoothing filter is applied to the scalar-field samples before Marching Cubes isosurface extraction. Thus, the method does not directly average or displace the vertices of an already extracted mesh. Instead, the filtered scalar field is used to recompute the edge-intersection positions during isosurface extraction.

The triangle connectivity and vertex count may change if the filtered field changes the local Marching Cubes case. Face normals are recomputed from the resulting triangle geometry after extraction, and the choice of normal representation does not affect the scalar-field smoothing operation.
The smoothing example is presented at 2563 to allow repeated filtering and side-by-side visual comparison with lower computational and memory cost. Visual Result. As shown in Fig 2, filtering suppresses high-frequency variations in the scalar field and produces a more spatially coherent isosurface. Compared with the unfiltered result, local irregularities in the elongated structures are reduced while the overall plasma morphology is retained. Among these, the grid-based dataset consists of 3D spatial coordinates (x,y,z) and the electromagnetic potential measured at each position. Particle-based fusion plasma analysis
The particle trajectory data consist of each particle’s unique identifier, the total number of frames, and its 3D position at each frame. In this study, 100 particle trajectories were used to visualize toroidal particle distributions combined with the Tokamak reactor geometry.
Scalability with Larger Trajectory Sets. The practical upper limit of trajectory visualization depends on both the number of trajectories and the number of sampled points along each trajectory. For a tube mesh with Nt trajectories, Np sampled points per trajectory, and Ns radial segments, the number of generated vertices is approximately NtNpNs, while the number of triangles is approximately 2Nt(Np−1)Ns. For example, 1,000 trajectories with 200 sampled points and eight radial segments generate approximately 1.6 million vertices and 3.18 million triangles.
The current ProceduralMeshComponent-based implementation is suitable for moderate trajectory sets, including configurations containing hundreds of trajectories. Extension to several thousand trajectories is feasible when the geometry is generated once and subsequently rendered as static geometry. However, performance increasingly depends on trajectory length, tube resolution, and update frequency.
For larger datasets, computational and memory costs can be reduced through trajectory decimation, fewer radial segments, distance-based level-of-detail control, and spatial or temporal batching. For continuously updated trajectory sets containing several thousand paths, a GPU-based implementation using compute shaders, structured buffers, or Niagara-based rendering would be more appropriate than repeatedly extracting procedural meshes on the CPU. Thus, the current implementation is intended primarily for interactive analysis of moderate trajectory sets, whereas larger dynamic datasets would benefit from GPU-driven trajectory generation and rendering.
Trajectory visualization was implemented in Unreal Engine using the Procedural Mesh technique. Procedural Mesh enables dynamic generation of mesh data at runtime, offering high flexibility for diverse visualization requirements. By defining vertex positions, triangle indices, normals, UV coordinates, and tangents directly in code, only the necessary geometry is generated, minimizing memory usage while maintaining real-time rendering performance.
To visualize each trajectory, the system generates tube-shaped meshes of constant thickness along the particle paths, creating the appearance of continuous lines.
The spiraling and toroidal patterns visible in Fig 10 originate from the centerline trajectories contained in the underlying particle-simulation data. The tube representation does not alter the sampled particle positions or introduce additional curvature; it only assigns a finite radius around each trajectory to improve visibility. Consequently, the tube radius and radial tessellation affect the apparent thickness and smoothness of the rendered paths, while the overall spiral geometry is determined by the simulated trajectories. In densely overlapping regions, the tube representation may visually emphasize the bundled structure, but it does not create the observed trajectory pattern.

Discussion
This work proposes a unified framework comprising capacity-safe Marching Cubes, a CUDA–OpenGL zero-copy pipeline, and an Unreal Engine integration to provide real-time visualization of large-scale fusion plasma data. The framework addresses structural limitations found in traditional CPU-based or offline visualization workflows. Below, we discuss the design implications, computational efficiency, advantages over existing approaches, limitations, and avenues for future research.
Comparison with Scientific Visualization Toolkits. ParaView, VTK, and VisIt provide mature scientific-visualization capabilities, including scalable data processing, distributed rendering, advanced filtering, quantitative probing, and support for established scientific data formats. These frameworks are more suitable than the proposed system for large-scale distributed analysis, general-purpose data processing, and production-level scientific workflows.
The purpose of the proposed Unreal Engine integration is therefore not to replace ParaView, VTK, or VisIt. Instead, it provides a complementary visualization environment emphasizing interactive scene composition, real-time rendering, immersive VR exploration, and integration with complex engineering geometry. In particular, Unreal Engine enables plasma volumes, extracted isosurfaces, particle trajectories, reactor geometry, lighting, clipping controls, and interactive user interfaces to be combined within a single spatial environment.
Compared with conventional scientific visualization software, the proposed system offers greater flexibility for constructing immersive digital-twin scenarios and spatially examining the relationship between plasma structures and surrounding reactor components. Conversely, it currently provides fewer quantitative analysis functions, such as statistical probing, distributed-memory processing, and general scientific-filter pipelines. Accordingly, the proposed framework should be regarded as a complementary interactive and immersive front end rather than a replacement for established scientific visualization toolkits.
Scientific Utility and Role of VR. The scientific value of the proposed system lies primarily in interactive morphological inspection rather than in replacing numerical analysis. Transfer-function adjustment, clipping planes, isovalue control, and trajectory visualization allow researchers to identify regions of interest, inspect internal plasma structures, and examine spatial relationships between plasma behavior and reactor geometry.
VR further supports spatial understanding by providing stereoscopic depth perception and direct manipulation of three-dimensional structures. This can be useful when examining overlapping surfaces, toroidal trajectories, and internal regions that are difficult to interpret from a fixed two-dimensional display. However, the current VR implementation remains a proof-of-concept visualization interface, and its advantages over desktop-based scientific visualization have not yet been validated through a formal comparative user study. Therefore, we limit our claims to technical feasibility and interactive spatial exploration.
Explicit Surface Extraction versus Implicit Isosurface Raycasting. Implicit isosurface raycasting provides several advantages when an isosurface is used only for visualization. It avoids mesh storage and preprocessing, supports multiple isovalues within a single ray-marching pass, and enables smooth normals to be evaluated on the fly from scalar-field gradients, for example using central differences. These properties can make raycasting more efficient than explicit Marching Cubes for direct volume inspection.
The proposed method instead targets workflows in which the isosurface must remain available as explicit geometry for subsequent processing, export, integration with engineering meshes, or use by conventional rasterization and interaction systems. The cost of this flexibility is the additional memory required for vertex and normal buffers and the surface-extraction time incurred whenever the isovalue changes. Therefore, the proposed method should be regarded as complementary to implicit raycasting rather than as a general replacement. A hybrid implementation that switches between direct raycasting and explicit extraction according to the analysis task would be a useful direction for future work.
Design implications and benefits
The proposed inclusive slab indexing and two-pass capacity-safe allocation eliminate boundary omissions, cracks, and buffer overflows that frequently occur in grids of size 5123 or larger. By computing the exact vertex count in Pass 1 and allocating VBOs accordingly in Pass 2, the method avoids GPU memory contention and atomic increment overheads present in conventional Marching Cubes implementations, ensuring stable performance even at high resolutions.
The CUDA–OpenGL interop–based zero-copy pipeline is another key strength of the proposed method. Because vertex and normal data remain entirely on the GPU, H2D/D2H transfers are eliminated, enabling interactive frame rates even for tens of millions of vertices. This design is particularly beneficial in scientific visualization, digital-twin systems, and XR-based interaction environments where low-latency feedback is essential.
Integration with game engines and scientific utility
Integration with Unreal Engine provides an additional major contribution. Isosurface extraction, volume rendering, and particle-flow visualization are executed cohesively within a single engine environment. Ray-marching volume rendering, clipping planes, transfer functions, and Procedural Mesh–based trajectory rendering enable simultaneous exploration of plasma boundaries, internal instabilities, and transport phenomena within a unified scene.
Interactive experiments in VR further highlight the utility of the system. Users can directly grab, rotate, and slice the plasma volume to examine structural instabilities and boundary transitions. Such capabilities extend naturally to real-time digital-twin analytics, operational support tools, and immersive educational platforms.
Limitations
Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. First, the Marching Cubes algorithm used in this work follows the classical MC family, and is therefore less suitable for sharp-feature preservation and handling complex topological ambiguities compared to Dual Contouring or MC33-based schemes. Second, the framework operates entirely within a single GPU’s memory, so extremely large (out-of-core) datasets beyond 5123 require integration with tile-caching and streaming techniques. Third, particle tracing currently relies on compute-based processing within Unreal Engine, and additional optimization is needed to support very large particle counts (on the order of millions) efficiently.
Future work
Several directions can be pursued in future work:
Out-of-core Marching Cubes: Coupling tile caching with the capacity-safe MC pipeline to support very large plasma datasets (e.g., 10243 and beyond).
Sharp-feature preserving schemes: Designing hybrid approaches that combine Dual Contouring/MC33 methods with the proposed pipeline.
NTF/AI-assisted extraction: Leveraging Neural Transportation Fields or diffusion-based correction to further improve iso-surface quality.
GPU-based large-scale particle advection: Offloading particle advection to an external CUDA path (outside Unreal) to support real-time tracing of hundreds of thousands to millions of particles.
Full digital-twin integration: Developing an operational interface that couples experimental control parameters with visualization in real time.
Conclusion
This paper presented a GPU-accelerated framework for interactive visualization of fusion-plasma data. The framework combines capacity-safe GPU isosurface extraction, CUDA–OpenGL interoperable rendering, and an Unreal Engine environment supporting volume rendering, particle trajectories, clipping, and VR-based spatial inspection. The quantitative results demonstrate stable extraction at resolutions up to 5123, with rendering rates of approximately 29–49 FPS for the tested high-resolution cases.
The proposed system is intended as a complementary immersive visualization front end rather than a replacement for established scientific-visualization toolkits. Future work will focus on out-of-core data processing, gradient-derived smooth normals, GPU-driven large-scale trajectory rendering, and tighter integration with simulation and digital-twin workflows.
