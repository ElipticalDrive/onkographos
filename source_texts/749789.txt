Abstract
Recent advances in learned image compression (LIC) have demonstrated superior
performance over traditional methods but often require training and storage of multiple models to handle different bitrate settings. In this paper, we propose the Uniform
Spatial-Frequency Residual Bottleneck Modulation Adapter (U-SFRB), a plug-andplay, adapter-based framework for variable rate image compression that significantly
reduces training and storage overhead. Our method freezes the backbone network
and only trains lightweight adapters—Spatial-Frequency Residual Bottleneck Adapters (SFRBs)—to achieve rate adaptability. By inserting multiple SFRBs in parallel,
our approach enables a single model to support a wide range of bitrates. Unlike
prompt-based methods restricted to transformer architectures, our approach is compatible with both CNN- and transformer-based compression models. Experimental
results on the Kodak and CLIC datasets show that our method achieves competitive
rate-distortion performance compared to state-of-the-art variable rate compression approaches, with the advantage of lower training complexity and better model
flexibility.
Keywords: Variable rate compression, Spatial-frequency residual bottleneck adapter
1 Introduction
Image compression is essential for reducing the storage and transmission costs of visual
data, which is increasingly critical for applications such as multimedia sharing, cloud
storage, and real-time communication. As high-resolution images become increasingly
common, efficient compression techniques are more crucial than ever to manage bandwidth and storage. Traditional methods, such as JPEG [1] and JPEG2000 [2], rely on
hand-designed transforms (e.g., DCT or wavelet) followed by quantization and entropy
coding. While efficient, their performance is fundamentally limited by the fixed nature
of these handcrafted designs.
Learned image compression methods have recently demonstrated strong performance across many computer vision tasks [3–9]. These methods typically employ endto-end trainable autoencoders with convolutional or transformer-based architectures
to learn compact latent representations that are optimized for the rate-distortion

quantized and encoded, and the decoder reconstructs the image. By jointly optimizing the trade-off between rate and distortion, learned models can surpass traditional
codecs in both objective and perceptual quality. This optimization is typically formulated as L = R + · D , where R denotes the bitrate, D the distortion, and controls
the trade-off. Larger values favor higher-quality reconstructions, whereas smaller
values favor stronger compression. In practice, models are often trained with multiple
values to cover a range of trade-offs, but this approach increases computational and
storage costs.
To address this, variable rate compression methods have been proposed [10–14].
As shown in Fig. 1, they can be divided into two groups. The first enables variable
rate within a single model, e.g., gain units [10], quantization regulator vectors [13], or
conditional autoencoders with adjustable quantization bins [12]. The second group
requires training multiple models for different values, e.g., vision prompt tuning
[14] for transformers. Existing methods still face limitations, such as performance
drops at high bitrates [10, 13] or restriction to transformer architectures [14].
In this work, we propose a plug-and-play adapter-based approach, the Spatial-Frequency Residual Bottleneck Modulation Adapter, designed to balance computational
complexity and compression performance. Inspired by traditional coding and recent
learned compression techniques, the adapter modulates intermediate features in spatial and frequency domains. Similar to prompt-based methods, the backbone network
is frozen, and only the adapter parameters are fine-tuned, which reduces training
costs and storage requirements. Unlike prompt-based methods, our adapter is flexible and can be integrated into both CNN and transformer architectures. To enable
variable rate compression efficiently within a single model, we introduce multiple
adapters as the Uniform Spatial-Frequency Residual Bottleneck Modulation Adapter
(U-SFRB).

2 Related work
2.1 Single rate image compression
In the domain of image compression, Ballé et al. [15] first proposed an end-to-end
approach, which was subsequently extended by incorporating a hyper-prior model [3].
The proposed hyper-prior model effectively reduces the redundancy of the latent representation and facilitates a more precise estimation of its distribution, allowing the
latent to be compressed with fewer bits. Addressing the challenges of estimating more
accurate distributions for the latent, [16] introduced a context model that explores the
relationship between pixels. However, the context model introduced by [16] can only
be processed in a sequential manner, resulting in a longer inference time. To balance
computational complexity and inference time, [17] introduced a parallel checkerboard
context model, and [18] proposed a channel-wise context model. Subsequently, [19] proposed an uneven grouping model combined with their previous work [17] to improve
coding performance without affecting inference speed. In addition, [7] proposed the
Multi-Reference Entropy Model (MEM), which can capture channel-wise, local spatial, and global spatial correlations, thereby enhancing the performance of the context
model. Since [20] introduced the transformer into computer vision, various transformerbased methods have demonstrated their effectiveness in the field of image compression.
[21] proposed a novel Symmetrical Transformer (STF) framework and a plug-and-play
window-based attention to fully utilize both global structure and local texture for image
compression. Similarly, [tic] also proposed units that combine the Swin Transformer [22]
and convolutional layers to analyze and aggregate local and non-local information for a
more compact representation of the input image. [6] presented a hybrid approach consisting of transformer and CNN blocks, utilizing the strengths of the transformer’s nonlocal capabilities and the local processing abilities of CNN. Furthermore, [23] proposed
an architecture capable of achieving lossy and near-lossless compression, while [24],
based on invertible neural networks, introduced a lossy image compression method.
2.2 Variable rate image compression
Single rate compression methods suffer from retraining models for different target
rates and computational resource consumption, making them hard to implement to
real-world applications. For the sake of practicality, various methods have emerged to
achieve variable rate compression methods with a single model or with a frozen backbone network. [10] revealed that the importance to reconstruct the image is varied from
different channels of the latent. Hence, [10] proposed a pair of gain units to scale the

channels of the latent to enable variable rate compression, and [10] also achieved continuous rate adaption by interpolating between gain units. [12] utilized two rate control
parameters for variable rate adaption, the Lagrange multiplier is a response to coarse
adjust the model, and the fine-tuned is achieved by adjustable quantization bin sizes.
Song et al. [11] employed a quality map that can provide spatially varying quality to enable the model to obtain arbitrary compression rates. Chen et al. [25] designed a pair of
scaling factors that are trained at a variety of bit rates with a fixed high bitrate model to
achieve variable rate compression. In [13], Tong et al. introduced a vector of quantization regulators coupled with predefined Lagrange multipliers to control the quantization error and achieve variable-rate compression. Specifically, their method adjusts the
quantization step-size for different values, enabling the model to operate at multiple
rate-distortion trade-offs. However, the selection of appropriate quantization regulators
is challenging, as their optimal values vary across different , and the model still suffers
from performance degradation at high bitrates. Building upon this, [26] further incorporated quantization-reconstruction offsets into the quantization process. Since quantization converts latent features into integers, decoding inevitably introduces errors. By
adding a quantization-reconstruction offset to the decoded latent representations during dequantization, the method aims to make the reconstructed latent features as close
as possible to the original pre-quantized latent features, thereby reducing reconstruction
error.
As transformer-based compression methods gained traction, several variable-rate
approaches tailored to transformer architectures have also been proposed. Qin et al.
[14] designed a Layer-adaptive Prompt Module (LPM) that generates prompts conditioned on the input and injects them into the Swin Transformer layers of a pre-trained
compression model. This prompt-based method enables variable-rate compression by
modulating intermediate encoder and decoder representations. Although the model
must be fine-tuned separately for different rates, only a small number of prompt parameters need to be updated, while the backbone remains frozen, making the method lightweight. Similarly, Kao et al. [27] proposed a prompt-based approach with a uniform
map, where the trade-off between rate and distortion is controlled by adjusting the value
of . In line with these parameter-efficient strategies, Alice et al. introduced a LoRAbased variable-rate compression method. By freezing the backbone and injecting lowrank adapters into the MLP layers of the transformer, their method achieves flexible rate
adaptation while training only the LoRA modules, offering another efficient solution for
transformer-based image compression.

3.2 Spatial‑frequency residual bottleneck modulation adapter
Adapters were originally introduced to facilitate efficient transfer learning by freezing
the parameters of the backbone network while inserting small trainable modules. This
strategy enables the fine-tuning of large pre-trained models with significantly reduced
computational overhead. Inspired by the success of adapters in natural language processing (NLP), we adopt a similar idea to enable variable-rate image compression. Specifically, we propose to incorporate domain-specific adapters, one for the spatial domain
and another for the frequency domain. These adapters are inserted into a fixed rate
backbone model and fine-tuned to achieve variable rate compression with minimal overhead. The proposed Spatial-Frequency Residual Bottleneck (SFRB) module consists of
two parallel components: the Spatial Residual Bottleneck (SRB) and the Frequency Modulation Adapter (FMA).
Typically, both the encoder and the decoder are composed of four stages, formulated
as ga = ga1 ◦ ga2 ◦ ga3 ◦ ga4 and gs = gs1 ◦ gs2 ◦ gs3 ◦ gs4 , respectively. Let xj denote the
output of the j-th encoder stage and x̃j the corresponding output of the j-th decoder
stage. The SFRB-enhanced feature at stage j is computed as:
x̂j = SFRB(xj )
= xj + FMA(xj ) + SRB(xj )

where FMA(·) and SRB(·) represent the frequency and spatial modulation adapters,
respectively. The decoding process mirrors the encoding process, with the corresponding SFRB modules applied at each decoder stage.

4.1 Experimental setup
Training Details. We randomly sampled 300,000 high-resolution images from ImageNet [29] to construct our training set. The proposed model is optimized using the
Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the distortion metric. For comparison, we selected two
representative baseline methods: the CNN-based model by Ballé [3] and the Transformer-based model TIC [5].
For the Ballé2018 model [3], we adopt the Lagrange multiplier set
{0.0018, 0.0035, 0.0067, 0.0130, 0.0250, 0.0483, 0.0932, 0.1800}. Following the standard
configurations in CompressAI [30], the convolutional channel dimensions are set to
N = 128 and M = 192 for the first five values, and N = M = 192 for the last three
values. All models are trained using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 8, and
the learning rate is fixed at 10−4 throughout the entire training process. Training
is performed on the RGB images. Specifically, the model trained with = 0.0067 is
used as the backbone for the first four values, and each is trained for 40 epochs. For
the remaining three values, we adopt the backbone of = 0.0932 and train for 20
epochs, also using the same constant learning rate of 10−4.
For the TIC model [5], we use the set {0.0018, 0.0035, 0.0067, 0.0130, 0.0250, 0.0483},
with N = 128 and M = 192 . The model is trained for 40 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 8. The learning rate is initially set to 10−4 and decayed to
10−5 for the final 20 epochs. Training is also performed on RGB images.
All experiments are conducted on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU, using
image patches of size 256 × 256 . The details of the adapters are shown in Table 1.

4.3 Ablation studies
To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of different design components and the
influence of model complexity, we conducted a series of detailed ablation experiments.
Comparison of model complexity and parameter count:
We compare the proposed method with other variable-rate image compression frameworks in terms of the number of trainable parameters and training iterations. The corresponding results are reported in Table 2 and Table 3. It is observed that our method
requires the fewest trainable parameters among all evaluated approaches. This advantage is primarily attributed to our strategy of freezing the parameters of the original
compression model while training only a lightweight adapter module. As a result, GPU
memory usage is significantly reduced during training. Furthermore, the proposed
framework exhibits strong adaptability and can be seamlessly integrated into various
existing compression backbones. When pre-trained model checkpoints are available, our
approach eliminates the need to train the entire model from scratch, thereby yielding
substantial computational savings. Despite the reduced number of trainable parameters,
our method consistently delivers competitive rate-distortion performance. Specifically,
for the Ballé2018 backbone [3], our method achieves a performance comparable to that
of single-rate compression models across low, middle, and high bitrate regimes. In the
case of the TIC framework [5], the proposed method performs similarly to the original
model at low bitrates, while only exhibiting a marginal degradation at higher bitrates, yet
still surpassing several competing approaches. Moreover, we report the inference time
and FLOPs in Table 5. Although both inference time and FLOPs increase slightly, our
method eliminates the need for retraining at each target rate, which is more time-efficient overall.

convergence during training, configurations involving only Stage 2 and Stage 3 are not
reported. The results demonstrate that placing U-SFRBs in both Stage 1 and Stage 2 provides nearly equivalent performance to that of using three U-SFRBs. These observations
suggest that early stage adaptation plays a critical role in maintaining overall compression quality.

5 Conclusion
In this paper, we present Uniform Spatial-Frequency Residual Blocks (U-SFRBs), an adapterbased framework for variable-rate image compression. By introducing lightweight spatial and
frequency domain adapters while keeping the backbone network fixed, our method reduces
the number of trainable parameters and memory usage during training. This allows the model
to support different rate-distortion trade-offs without retraining the entire network. We evaluated our approach on both CNN-based (Ballé2018) and Transformer-based (TIC) models.
We also performed ablation studies to analyze the impact of model complexity, adapter location, and backbone choice. In general, U-SFRB provides an efficient and flexible solution for
learned image compression.
