Abstract
This paper proposes improved local search heuristics based on collision
detection for solving volume maximization problems, with a particular focus on
single item volume maximization. The objective is to find the biggest item of a
predefined shape that can be extracted from a larger container. Both the item and
the container are three-dimensional objects and can have irregular shapes. Our
goal is to find high-quality solutions for these problems within a reasonable
amount of time, even for complex instances where the object and container are
represented by thousands of triangles. We consider an approach where the position
and orientation of an item are optimized heuristically, while the scale of the item is
maximized using a fast inflation procedure. This inflation procedure uses bisection
search and collision detection to determine the largest possible scale that satisfies
all geometric constraints for a given position and orientation of the item within the
container. We introduce improvements to this approach to reduce the required
amount of geometric computations required. Finally, we compare our results
against a matheuristic method from the literature on an expanded data set, which
shows the improved collision detection approach is more than 100 times faster and
highlights the impact of our improvements.
Keywords. cutting, maximum volume extraction, 3D irregular cutting and
packing, collision detection

Introduction
In volume maximization problems (VMPs) the aim is to find the biggest given
three-dimensional items that can be extracted from a larger three-dimensional object,
the container. Applications of VMPs appear in various real-world contexts, such as gem
cutting and circuit manufacturing, where finding better solutions is typically highly
valuable. Still, the number of publications on this topic is rather limited and most do
not include the experimental data required to benchmark algorithms. We consider the
general case where both the items and the container have irregular shapes and are not
necessarily convex. Given this degree of generality, we use triangle meshes as inputs,
one of the most frequently used methods to represent three-dimensional objects in
computer-aided design and computer graphics.
The scope of this paper is limited to problems where only a single item has to be placed
within a given container, which we will refer to as the single volume maximization
problem or SVMP. While these problems might appear easy to solve, they quickly
become challenging as the detail of the objects, and thus the number of vertices that
represent them, increases. For example, the rock shape in Figure 1 has close to 4000
vertices. However, in applications like gem cutting an even higher level of precision is
often desired, leading to instances with tens or hundreds of thousands of vertices.

The widely used typology for cutting and packing problems, introduced by Wäscher
et al. (2007), does not account for the assignment of items with a variable scale.
However, we still consider this to be a cutting and packing problem as it concerns the
placement of smaller items within a larger container subject to certain geometric
constraints. Furthermore, in the case of a single item, we can formulate an equivalent
open dimension problem by reversing the objective and minimizing the size of the
container that can fit the smaller item. From this point of view, the SVMP could fit the
typology. This also further legitimizes our decision to categorize this work under
cutting and packing.
The geometric constraints are a result of the physical context from which cutting and
packing problems originate. The containment constraint indicates that an item must be
fully embedded in the container and therefore cannot intersect its boundaries. Next, the
non-overlapping constraint states that if multiple items are present, they are not allowed
to overlap with each other. A third common constraint is that items cannot overlap with
any impurities that the container material may have. Such defects are not considered
in this work. Evaluating geometric constraints for 3D irregular instances is typically
expensive in terms of required computations, especially when the shapes have a high
degree of detail and therefore require a large number of vertices to represent them.
In this research, we aim to design algorithms that tackle SVMPs efficiently. One of our
goals is to be able to solve instances which feature real-world objects without having
to significantly reduce the complexity of their shapes to achieve reasonable computation
times. As the literature review in Section 3 will show, this has often not been the case
in earlier works. This will lead us to an approach based on collision detection, which has
been an active area of research in the field of computational geometry for a long time
given its relevance in disciplines like physics simulations and game engines.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section will formally
introduce the problem and define the input and output variables. Section 3 will discuss
approaches from several related works, focusing on their limitations where applicable.
Section 4 will elaborate on our own heuristic methodology. Improvements to the
heuristics that greatly reduce the required computational effort will be introduced in
Section 5. In Section 6, we introduce our data set and results. To analyse the impact of
the improvements, we will benchmark different versions of the proposed algorithm.
These results will also be compared against a matheuristic approach. Finally, Section 7
will conclude this work and highlight some future research opportunities.

Problem formulation
The objective of the SVMP is to maximize the volume of a given item I that can be
extracted from a larger container C subject to the containment constraint. The threedimensional objects I and C are stored as triangular meshes. This means each object is
defined as a set of triangles that make up its surface. We denote the set of triangles of
an object m with ∇m , where m is either a container or an item. Each triangle is in turn
defined by three vertices and the collection of all vertices in an object m is described
with V m . This triangle mesh representation enables us to accurately approximate any
shape, where the number of triangles can be adapted to the desired precision. Table 1

Related work
3.1
Early work
Nguyen and Strodiot (1992) consider cutting the largest possible diamond of prescribed
shape and orientation from a polyhedral rough stone which is not necessarily convex. The
stone and diamond are assumed to be a compact set and a convex compact polytope in R3 ,
respectively. These properties enable the development of an efficient global optimization
algorithm. However, the problem addressed by Nguyen and Strodiot (1992) is a special
case of the SVMP. Solving the general SVMP would require algorithmic procedures
which can additionally determine the best orientation of the item and can also handle
non-convex items.
The SVMP can also be viewed as a design centering problem (DCP) which was formally
described by Stein (2006) as follows. Given a container C ⊂ Rm and a parametrized
design B(x) ⊂ Rm where x ∈ Rn , the DCP maximizes some function f (x) such that
B(x) is contained within C.

The SVMP is in fact a DCP with m = 3, n = 7 and f (x) = V1 x31 or simply f (x) = x1
where x1 denotes the scale. Stein (2006) formulates the DCP as a general semi-infinite
programming problem (GSIP) and investigates the optimality conditions for the
corresponding GSIP. Winterfeld (2008) models a gemstone cutting problem which
considers only convex items (designs) as a GSIP and solves the corresponding problem
using an interior-point method. However, in order to fulfill the required convexity and
smoothness assumptions, both the gem designs and the container are approximated.
More specifically, the gem designs are limited to convex shapes and are represented by
a smooth enclosing hull. The container from which the gems are cut is replaced with its
convex hull and elliptic paraboloids as concave cutouts. These approximations also help
reduce the size of the GSIP, which would otherwise be too large to solve for practical
applications, by decreasing the number of triangles. This requirement for items to be
convex together with approximations of the container limit the achievable solution
quality, which makes this methodology unappealing for the context of this paper.
For recent advances in semi-infinite programming, we refer interested readers to the
review by Djelassi et al. (2021) and continue this section by reviewing other studies
focusing on the SVMP and its special cases.
3.2
Two-level strategies
A recurring observation with other methods for the SVMP is that they solve the problem
using a two-leveled strategy. At the lower level, the largest possible scale is determined
for an item with a given position and orientation. We refer to this level as the inflation
procedure. This information can be then used by the second level, the placement level,
which searches for the maximal scale over all positions and orientations. A variety of
techniques can be employed in both the first and the second levels, as the following
examples will show.
In da Silva et al. (2012), the maximum scale is determined for a simplified hull of the item
by checking if all of the container mesh’s vertices are outside this body. If a vertex lies
inside, the scaling factor is reduced to situate it on the surface of the calibration body.
Because only the vertices of the container are tested, and not the complete facets that
make up the surface, the precision is limited by the resolution of the mesh. A genetic
algorithm is used as the overarching strategy to explore the solution space. Sadani et al.
(2021) expand on this method to maximize the volume of two items simultaneously.
7
Maag (2015) uses bisection search to approximate the largest possible scale with the
desired level of precision in a limited number of steps. They rely on collision detection to
determine if a solution is feasible at a certain scale. Bounding volume hierarchies (BVHs)
accelerate the collision detection queries to enable the use of this approach without
simplifying the objects (Ericson, 2005). The optimization of position and orientation is
achieved using a generating set search method (Kolda et al. (2003)). This method is in
turn part of a larger multi-start approach, where the best starting solutions are selected
by optimizing the promising candidates in the convex hull of the container first, before
moving on to the actual container. The algorithm is compared against a semi-infinite
programming approach and also several hybrids that combine the collision method with
semi-infinite programming. This comparison showed that their new approach produces
better results while requiring comparable computation times.
Silva et al. (2021) introduce a matheuristic approach to the SVMP. A local search
heuristic optimizes the orientation, while a linear programming (LP) model is utilized
to find the maximum scale for a given orientation. This model also determines the
optimal translation for that orientation. The main drawback of the LP model is that it
can only deal with convex materials. In case of non-convex materials, this problem is
addressed by solving the LP for the convex hull of the material and iteratively resolving
it with additional cutting planes which eliminate solutions violating the containment
constraints for the original material. Their experiments show that the required
computation time for this approach drastically increases as the detail level of the
shapes increases. The data set for these experiments contains simple objects with no
more than 1400 vertices.
The performances of the aforementioned two-level approaches largely depend on the speed
and precision of the inflation procedure, as typically most of the computation time is spent
here. We observe that most of these strategies do not scale well with increasing object
complexity and approximations must be made to deal with real-world instances. Only
Maag (2015) seems to achieve good scaling, with the BVHs yielding collision detection
that has near-logarithmic runtime complexity with respect to the number of vertices.

3.3
Cutting and packing
There are many additional publications that focus on the cutting and packing of irregular
items, especially for two-dimensional problems. The number of publications that tackle
three-dimensional irregular geometry is, however, much more limited. And even for those
publications which do, most is largely irrelevant for this research as they typically do not
solve problems with variable item scaling, the feature that distinguishes VMPs. Indeed,
the geometric constraints are often addressed with data structures that would have to be
recomputed for every possible scaling factor. Examples of these data structures are the
no-fit polygon (NFP), phi-functions, pixelation/voxelization methods and recently the
no-fit voxel (NFV) introduced in Lamas-Fernandez et al. (2022). An overview of these
techniques can be found in Bennell and Oliveira (2008) and Leao et al. (2020).
3.4
Other related areas of research
Many other areas of research also deal with collision detection and similar geometric
challenges. Examples of these are physics and game engines, computer-aided design,
robotics and ray tracing applications.
In robot motion planning, for example, collision detection techniques are used to
guarantee collision free paths (Laumond et al. (1998), Elbanhawi and Simic (2014)).
Robot motion planning problems and the VMPs discussed in this paper can appear
quite similar, as they both concern the positioning of objects in a three-dimensional
space. However, their decision variables and objective functions are typically different.
VMPs are concerned with finding a single placement with optimal scaling, while robot
motion planning usually concerns determining an efficient sequence of valid placements
to connect desired start and end positions. As a result, the techniques used to solve
these two problem categories are typically incompatible with each other.

4
Methodology
As mentioned in the previous section, our methodology belongs in the category of twolevel strategies. Analogous with the inflation procedure used in Maag (2015), we will
use bisection search combined with collision detection between the triangle meshes of
the item and the container to determine the maximum scale for a fixed position and
orientation. A hill climbing heuristic searches for the best position and orientation .
This section describes these components and their implementation in further detail.
4.1
Feasibility check
An important component of our algorithm requires determining if a solution is feasible,
that is, whether a solution satisfies or violates the containment constraint (T × I ⊆ C,
see Equation 2). In order to verify this constraint, we need to check that the item’s
transformed triangle mesh does not intersect with the container mesh. If we denote an
arbitrary triangle of the transformed item as △i ∈ ∇T ×I and a triangle of the container
as △c ∈ ∇C , the constraint implies that no intersecting triangle pair (△i , △c ) exists.
However, this non-intersecting condition is not sufficient because an item can also lie
completely outside of the container without intersecting its mesh, as illustrated in Figure
2c. Thus, we can break the containment constraint down into two conditions:
1. The triangle meshes of the item and the container do not intersect each other.
2. The item is not located completely outside the container

Checking these two conditions is computationally expensive, especially determining
whether the meshes intersect or not. To avoid testing all triangle pairs between the
container and the item, we use BVHs, similar to Maag (2015). These data structures
store the triangles of an object in a hierarchical, spatially subdivided fashion and also
store their bounding volume at each level. When searching for intersecting triangles,
only nodes whose parents’ bounding volume intersect have to be traversed. As a result,
intersection tests will only be executed for triangles that are near each other. This is a
well-established method to significantly speed up collision detection [Ericson (2005)].

In our implementation, the bounding volumes are Axis-Aligned Bounding Boxes
(AABBs). These are subdivided using a simple surface area heuristic (SAH), which
strives to minimize the expected cost of intersection tests based on the total surface
area of the children in each node. This heuristic is commonly used in ray-tracing
applications (Aila et al. (2013)) and is also effective in the context of this research. The
most important property is that these BVHs enable collision detection with
linearithmic time complexity regarding the number of triangles in the meshes. While
further fine-tuning of these data structures is likely possible, it is not the main focus of
our research as we are mainly interested in the optimization side of things.
To evaluate the second condition, it suffices to check that just one of the item’s vertices
lies inside the container. Because we also check if there is no intersection between the
meshes, this single vertex implies that the item is situated completely inside the container.
We implement this check by tracing a ray that originates from the vertex in an arbitrary
direction and counting how many times it intersects the surface of the container. An
uneven number of intersections indicates that the vertex is indeed inside the container,
as the container is a bounded polyhedron. This ray-tracing step is also accelerated using
the BVHs.

faster, as the intersection procedure can terminate once an intersecting pair of triangles
is found. For feasible solutions, on the other hand, all pairs of triangles that might
collide must be traversed, which will result in the worst case running time. Algorithm 1

Inflation procedure
With the feasibility function in place, we can now design a bisection search routine that
searches for the maximum feasible scale for a given position and orientation, which we
define as smax . The routine searches this scaling factor in the interval [0, supper ] and
repeatedly bisects this interval by testing if the value in the middle represents a feasible
scale. This is illustrated below in Algorithm 2, where the halving of the search intervals
is represented by halving the step size δs . The search is terminated when the desired
level of precision is reached. This enables the maximal scale to be found in a limited
number of evaluations.

The upper bound on the scale, supper , is an absolute upper bound that is determined by
comparing the diagonal of the bounding boxes of the item and the container. Note that
in our actual implementation, the scaling factor of the items is normalized so that supper
is equal to 1.0. This is achieved by pre-scaling all vertices of the mesh in a preprocessing
step. The goal of this normalization is to achieve equally significant precision ε for all
instances, independent from their relative size. In the final version of the algorithm, the
desired precision ε is set to 10−5 , which results in 17 feasibility evaluations before δs goes
below this value.
In order to guarantee that the final scale is actually the largest feasible scaling factor
possible smax , the bisection search requires a contiguous search space. This means that
if an item is feasible at a certain scaling factor, all scaling factors below should also
yield a feasible solution. This property is satisfied by convex shapes that contain their
coordinate origin, and more generally by items for which every line segment between any
interior point and its origin is contained within the interior of the item 1 . This restriction
implies that every point of an item will lie inside the original one when scaled down and
therefore cannot collide with the container. Examples of these cases are shown in Figure
4a and 4b, respectively.

4.3
Steepest ascent hill climbing
At the placement level, we will test different hill climbing heuristics. These evaluate
solutions with different orientations and positions by performing the inflation
procedure. The first variant we will implement is Steepest Ascent Hill Climbing
(SAHC). In each iteration, this heuristic evaluates all solutions in the neighborhood of
the current best solution and selects the best one. To improve its behaviour, we require
that a solution should at least improve upon the previous scaling factor by a small
value κ. If no improving moves are found in the current neighborhood, the step size for
changes in position and orientation is reduced by multiplying with a factor λ ∈ ]0, 1[.
The pseudocode for this algorithm is given in Algorithm 3.

4.4
Basic and late acceptance hill climbing
The second variant in the experiments is a Basic Hill Climbing (BHC) heuristic. It
considers exactly the same neighboorhood, but immediately continues to the next
iteration upon encountering an improving solution. We expect this variant to be faster,
but the solution quality at termination may be inferior.
Because both the SAHC and BHC will search the solution space very greedily, we wanted
to include a third algorithm that would search more broadly using more computation
effort, in the hope of finding better solutions. This is why we also include Late Acceptance
Hill Climbing (LAHC), introduced in Burke and Bykov (2017).
This heuristic maintains a fitness array that stores the objective function values of
recently visited solutions and accepts new solutions if their objective value is better
than the solution visited L iterations prior, where L is a constant integer value.
We cannot reuse the same neighborhood from before where the step size is reduced after
non-improving iterations, because the LAHC heuristic could accept a solution from the
same neighborhood in later iterations. Therefore, we adopt a different scheme where a
random rotation in [0, θ] or translation in [0, τ ] is sampled and the step size decreases
exponentially, iteration by iteration. While we performed only a few tuning experiments
for this heuristic, we managed to find a configuration that yields better results than both
original heuristics. In this configuration we run the heuristic for 25.000 iterations, use
a fitness array length of L = 12 and the sample step size decreases exponentially from

5
Improvements
In the previous section, we established a two-level approach that optimizes the position
and orientation in the upper level and determines the maximal scale for each assessed
placement in the lower level. However, these levels are not independent of each other and
this approach can benefit greatly from the exchange of information between them. This
section introduces a pair of improvements that reduce the required number of feasibility
evaluations, thus decreasing the computational workload.
5.1
Improvement 1: Fast candidate rejection
The first improvement is to determine if a neighbor can actually improve the current
best solution before calling the inflation procedure. This can be achieved by testing if
the solution is feasible at the threshold scale that would be accepted by the heuristic.
This change is illustrated for the SAHC heuristic in Algorithm 4.
While this version of the heuristic requires an extra feasibility evaluation for improving
neighbors, it saves more evaluations in case of rejections. Because this heuristic
searches the solution in a greedy fashion by only accepting improvements, the majority
of candidate solutions will be rejected, which makes this enhancement very effective.

The same principle can be applied to other local search heuristics as well, as it is typically
easy to derive the threshold for accepting a neighboring solution. In the case of BHC, for
example, this threshold is the previous best scale because any solution with a larger scale
will be accepted. For LAHC it is the value in the fitness array from L iterations ago.
However, it should be noted that this change will be most effective for greedy heuristics,
where most candidate solutions are rejected.
5.2
Improvement 2: Speeding up the inflation procedure
Using the knowledge that sthreshold yields a feasible solution, we can also speed up the
inflation procedure itself. We can achieve this by reducing the search interval [0, supper ]
to [sthreshold , supper ]. Because of this reduction, the desired precision could potentially be
achieved in fewer iterations. Algorithm 5 details how this is implemented.
Algorithm 5 Inflation with a feasible lower bound
procedure inflate(C, I, t⃗, ⃗r, sthreshold )

δs ← supper − sthreshold
while δs ≥ ε do
δs ← δs /2

if feasible(C, I, T (⃗t, ⃗r, s)) then

return s

An interesting observation from the experiments is that this change reduces the required
computation time, but the number of feasible evaluations is not actually reduced all that
much. Because [sthreshold , supper ] is typically in the same order of magnitude as [0, supper ],
it requires an equal number of iterations before achieving the desired precision ϵ. This
can be explained by the fact that supper , determined by the relative sizes of the bounding
boxes, is not a strong upper bound and yields a fairly large search interval.
Nevertheless, this improvement results in a greater speed-up than the limited reduction in
the number of feasible calls might lead one to believe. This gain can be explained by the
fact that the solutions with a scale larger than sthreshold are likely to be infeasible, while
the ones below this threshold are guaranteed to be feasible and take longer to evaluate,
as mentioned in Section 4.1. This means the advantage of exploiting the threshold
scale primarily derives from the fact that some of the worst case evaluations in terms of
execution time are avoided.
19
Attempts were made to determine stronger upper bounds dynamically during the
development of this strategy. For example, we could inflate the item in the simpler
convex hull of the container before repeating it in the actual container with the
resulting upper bound. However, computing this proved to be more expensive than the
benefits we could gain.

Experiments
In this section we will summarize our experimental results. We will compare the results
of our approach against those of a published matheuristic, examine the effectiveness from
the improvements detailed in the previous section and present the results of a sensitivity
analysis. This section only provides a compact summary of the computational results.
Most results we present here are averaged, relative values and they are rounded to three
decimal points for clarity. We provide a data sheet with all the results in full detail as
an attachment.
6.1
Experimental setup
The set of instances used for our experiments is based on the publicly available data
set provided by Silva et al. (2021), as this is the only study from our review in Section
3 that published the objects utilized in their experiments. However, because we are
interested in containers with a high level of detail, and these instances are limited to
1400 vertices at most, we expanded this data set with publicly available rock scans.
These have an increasing amount of detail, from thousands to hundreds of thousands of
vertices. A couple of small objects from Liu et al. (2015) were also added as items, to
better represent very simple instances. Finally, a few interesting shapes were added as
items as well 2 . These are all triangle mesh objects stored as either .obj or .stl files. In
total there are 19 different objects used as containers and 28 as items, resulting in a total
of 532 combinations to benchmark. This large number of combinations is the reason for
only providing a summary of the results in this section. An overview of these instances
and their complexity is given in Appendix A.
Our collision detection approach is implemented in C++ using an in-house library for
the geometric computations. The reported computation times include the preprocessing
operations such as the scaling normalization step and generation of data structures like
the BVHs.

Conclusions and Future Work
In this work we introduced methods to solve single volume maximisation problems
(SVMPs), where the objective is to find the biggest item of predefined shape that fits in
a larger container. The goal of this research was to find generally applicable methods
that produce high-quality solutions to these problems within a reasonable amount of
time. We achieved this with a two-level strategy that maximizes the scale of an item for
a fixed position and orientation using collision detection and bisection search. The
position and orientation are then locally optimized, for which several hill climbing
heuristics were implemented: basic hill climbing, steepest ascent hill climbing and late
acceptance hill climbing. We introduced improvements to this methodology that exploit
the fast rejection of candidate solutions.
28
We compared our methodology against the matheuristic approach introduced in Silva
et al. (2021), which allowed for the best comparison against literature. We also
expanded their data set with more complex instances and made this data publicly
available. Our experiments showed that the basic hill climbing and steepest ascent hill
climbing heuristics can match their matheuristic in terms of solution quality, while
being more than 100 times faster on average. Furthermore, the late acceptance
heuristic proved it is also possible to improve the solution quality while still being faster
than their matheuristic approach. We also observe better performance scaling when
dealing with more complex objects that have an increasing number of vertices.
The evaluation of the improvements shows that we can benefit a lot from the exchange
of information between the two levels of this approach. We achieved this with our
improvements that exploit the geometric properties of the inflation problem to quickly
reject candidate solutions. The experiments also prove that the collision detection
techniques used in this work are very effective in the context of VMPs and could
potentially be used for solving other related cutting and packing problems.
In the future, it would be worthwhile exploring more advanced strategies that use this
methodology as a building block for higher level approaches. While we have achieved
good results in terms of computation time and could match the solution quality of the
matheuristic approach, these are achieved using simple local search heuristics and their
robustness can definitely be improved upon by including them in multi-start or iterated
local search strategies. Additionaly, an expansion of these methodologies to volume
maximization problems with multiple items is certainly possible, so long as the volumes
of the items can be reduced to a single objective function value.

The algorithms in this section further detail the implementation of the intersection checks
described in Section 4.1. Note that we use the previously introduced notations where
∇M denotes the set of triangles of a mesh M and ∆m denotes an individual triangle.
Furthermore, we use BV HM to describe the Bounding Volume Hierarchy of a mesh M
and ∇BV H is also used for the set of triangles in a BVH node, while aabbBV H represents
the bounding volume of a BVH node, as described in Section 4.1. These data structures
are constructed only once, ahead of the optimization.
