Abstract
Reproducing a published 3DGS paper takes weeks of expert engineering before
any extension can be proposed. Prior autonomous-research systems read papers,
draft hypotheses, and write code, but suffer from limited template length, executability, and traceability. Specifically, none has been demonstrated end-to-end in
a live computer-vision subfield with intense activity. SplatLab is an autonomous
research system for 3D Gaussian Splatting that addresses these limitations. Given
a research prompt, a target-paper URL, or no input at all, it reproduces a published
baseline, proposes an extension, trains and ablates across Mip-NeRF 360 and LLFF,
writes the manuscript, and binds every reported number to a logged training run.
Five design choices distinguish it: (i) candidates mutate plugins from a verifiedreproduction backbone matched to published baselines; (ii) a cascade-weighted
Elo tournament across eight islands scores on measured GPU PSNR with verbalgradient feedback; (iii) a standing falsifier subtracts Elo on failure, integrating

training run, and the run cannot terminate while any claim is unbacked; (v) a sevencategory 3DGS taxonomy wraps EVOLVE-BLOCK markers in domain-aligned scaffolds transferable across 3DGS repositories. SplatLab produces manuscripts that
beat their reproduced target by +0.18 to +3.78 dB PSNR, including +3.78 dB on

autonomous compute. Code, all manuscripts, experiment ledgers, and the cross-run
paper store can be found at http://seemandhar.github.io/splatlab.
1
Introduction
Automating scientific discovery is a long-standing research ambition, traceable through early symbolic
systems such as the Automated Mathematician [46] and DENDRAL [6], the conceptual programme
of AI-generating algorithms [7], and a contemporary line of work in which LLMs compose research
ideas, write training code, run experiments, and produce manuscripts [25, 48, 35, 11, 24, 41, 9, 37].
The classical research workflow is bounded by an individual researcher’s time and reading bandwidth,
neither of which scales with the publication rate of an active subfield. Automating parts of the cycle
could return researcher time to ideation and analysis. Computer vision is a natural place to test this:
a single subfield now produces thousands of follow-up papers in a few years, and reproducing one
published method often takes engineering effort comparable to producing a new one.
We are not aware of an autonomous-research pipeline demonstrated end-to-end in a computer-vision
subfield where reproducing a single prior paper takes weeks of expert engineering. Existing systems
edit short LaTeX templates over brief training runs, rank free-text hypotheses without executing a
training loop, or build experimentation on top of LLM-written reimplementations that have not been
externally verified against the target paper [25, 48, 11, 24, 35, 41, 9, 53, 20, 17, 22, 45]. PaperBench
measures this verification gap: across 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight and Oral papers, the strongest
evaluated agent reaches 21.0% average replication [38]. Without a verified reimplementation, an
improvement claim has no fixed external reference: reported metrics may not trace to a measurement
on disk, long-running agents drift from their objective as context fills, and comparisons are sometimes
made against unspecified baselines. Table 2 summarises four common gaps: domain framework,
verified baseline, full-loop refinement, and claim-to-log binding.
Progress on this problem appears to require a domain-specialised research stack constructed on top
of verified reproductions, rather than only improvements in generalist coding ability. Generalist
agentic frameworks [31, 13, 47, 50, 43] were designed for software engineering, where every task
starts from a fresh codebase, not for the iterative refinement atop a verified baseline that scientific
research typically requires. Four design choices follow: choose a single live subfield, build a verifiedreproduction backbone for it, expose a structured modification space to the ideator, and bind every
reported number to a logged GPU run.
We instantiate this for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [19], an active subfield of neural rendering
since 2023. Reproducing a 3DGS paper on top of gsplat [52] requires correct densification
scheduling, opacity reset, spherical-harmonic activation, and view-dependent appearance, none of
which is supplied by the prompt a generalist code agent receives; on a 30-paper 3DGS reproduction
benchmark, current frontier models succeed on roughly 5% of inputs [16]. The verified-reproduction
backbone has two parts: N ERFIFY [16] converts NeRF papers into Nerfstudio plugins matching
expert-written code; S PLATIFY [15] extends the construction to 3DGS through a context-free grammar
over a modular template with nine composable extension hooks. Every candidate that reaches our
experimentation phase mutates a S PLATIFY plugin already verified to train, and match its target.
We present SplatLab, an execution-grounded research system demonstrated in 3D Gaussian Splatting
that produces research manuscripts with verified experiments from a one-line prompt, a target-paper
URL, or no input at all, with every reported number traced to a logged GPU run. SplatLab orchestrates
36 agents across seven phases (research, ideation, implementation, experimentation, analysis, writing,
peer review) without a fixed DAG. Five design decisions distinguish SplatLab from prior autonomousresearch and program-search systems; each is detailed in Section 3. (i) Grounded Elo on real GPU
PSNR. Idea ranking uses cascade-weighted, grounding-conditional Elo scored on measured PSNR
rather than the model-judged proposal scores of AI Co-Scientist [11], with ReEvo-style verbal
gradients [51] fed back to the ideator. (ii) In-loop falsifier coupled to selection. A standing adversarial
2
falsifier extending AIGS [24] runs in-loop; each failed attack deducts Elo, making stress-test survival
a selection signal rather than a post-hoc check. (iii) Claim-backed verification. Every number that
reaches a draft is bound to a logged experiment, and the run cannot terminate while any claim lacks
a backing measurement, a binding absent from prior pipelines [25, 48]. (iv) Verified-reproduction
backbone. Every candidate mutates a S PLATIFY plugin already verified against its target, providing
a fixed reference for every reported gain. (v) Domain-aligned modification space. Seven 3DGS
novelty categories wrap EVOLVE-BLOCK markers and the SPLATIFY island tournament [15] in
category-aligned scaffolds; a cross-codebase auto-mapper places them in arbitrary 3DGS repositories
so the diff space coincides with the field’s modification space.
On Mip-NeRF 360 [3] and LLFF [27], SplatLab produces manuscripts that each beat their reproduced
target by +0.18 to +3.78 dB PSNR, including +3.78 dB on astrophotographic nebula rendering, a
domain not addressed by prior 3DGS work. The research cycle (read, reproduce, extend, train, ablate,
write) compresses from months of graduate-student time to days of mostly autonomous compute,
enabling design-space sweeps not feasible at single-researcher pace. SplatLab, all agents, and the
code can be found at http://seemandhar.github.io/splatlab.
2
Related Work
End-to-end automated research pipelines. SplatLab sits closest to systems that take a goal and
emit a manuscript. The AI Scientist and v2 produce ML papers via fixed templates and agentic
tree search [25, 48]. Agent Laboratory, MLR-Copilot, AI-Researcher, NovelSeek/InternAgent,
Dolphin, and CodeScientist organize the literature-to-paper loop with role-specialized agents [35,
22, 41, 29, 53, 17]; CycleResearcher post-trains an LLM on a research-review-refine cycle, and
AgentRxiv adds a shared preprint pool across runs [45, 34]. AIGS adds a post-hoc FalsificationAgent,
data-to-paper traces claims back to data, Curie embeds rigor through agent control modules, and
PaperBench grades replication against author rubrics [24, 14, 20, 38]. All operate over agent-written
reimplementations whose match to the target is itself unverified; SplatLab requires every candidate to
mutate a plugin already verified to compile, train, and reach the target’s headline. None condition on
a (B, L) pair, none fuse falsification with cascade-weighted Elo, and none allow reviewer-spawned
re-experimentation rather than only prose edits.
Autonomous discovery without manuscript output. A second line of work runs the scientific
loop but stops short of a paper. Google’s AI co-scientist uses a multi-agent Elo tournament over 203
biomedical goals with wet-lab validation in drug repurposing and antimicrobial resistance [11]. The
Virtual Lab designed 92 SARS-CoV-2 nanobodies validated experimentally [39]; Coscientist and
ChemCrow connect GPT-4 to wet-lab and computational chemistry tools [5, 26]. ResearchAgent
and Si et al. study LLM ideation in isolation, with the latter finding LLM ideas judged more novel
but slightly less feasible than expert ideas in a 100+ NLP-researcher study [1, 36]. Wei et al. survey
the landscape [44]; we sit at their Level-3 “full agentic discovery” but instantiate it for graphics
rather than wet-lab biology. Closest to our tournament is the AI co-scientist’s Elo loop, but it ranks
free-text hypotheses rather than compiled training runs against a (B, L) substrate, and updates ratings
uniformly rather than scaling by evaluation depth.
Evolutionary program search and multi-agent infrastructure. SplatLab’s island tournament
with EVOLVE-BLOCK markers builds on LLM-driven program evolution. FunSearch pairs an
LLM with a systematic evaluator on an island-based loop [33]; AlphaEvolve generalizes this to
entire codebases via a Gemini ensemble [30]. EoH co-evolves natural-language “thoughts” and
code, ReEvo introduces verbal gradients (LLM reflections on why one heuristic beat another, fed
back into mutation), Promptbreeder evolves prompts self-referentially, and Mind Evolution scales
inference-time compute through genetic search [23, 51, 10, 21]. We adopt verbal gradients from
ReEvo but route them into the ideator rather than a heuristic mutator, and replace per-individual
fitness with cascade-weighted, grounding-conditional Elo so cheap dry-run wins do not dominate
ratings of full-train candidates against the verified substrate. MetaGPT, ChatDev, AutoGen, and
SWE-agent supply the orchestration primitives we reuse [13, 31, 47, 50], while LLM-as-a-judge,
MARG, and AgentReview inform our reviewer-driven re-experimentation but treat review as terminal
rather than a gate that can demand new substrate experiments [54, 8, 18].

Method
SplatLab is a system for verified evolutionary research over a reproducible substrate. The substrate is
a 3DGS reproduction backbone (SPLATIFY [15]/NERFIFY [16]) whose plugins match their published

input) passes through six stages (grounding, Elo-pre-ranked ideation, EVOLVE-BLOCK implementation,

targets; every extension SplatLab proposes mutates a plugin already verified to match its target. This
scaffolding separates SplatLab from prior systems that pursue unconstrained autonomous discovery
over unverified agent-written code, whose drafts are neither traceable to logs nor reproducible by
retraining. The claim is narrower and stronger: SplatLab performs autonomous iterative discovery
within a live, technically demanding subfield, with every iteration anchored to a measured baseline
and bound to a logged training run.
Formally, given a research prompt, a target-paper URL, or no input at all, SplatLab returns P =
(Ptex , Ppdf , L, C) = W (x; D), where Ptex is the LaTeX source, Ppdf the compiled PDF, L binds
every number in Ptex to a training run on disk, and C is the codebase that produced those numbers.
The fixed context D = (R, B, H, G) supplies the 3DGS literature corpus R, the SPLATIFY backbone
B, the nine EVOLVE-BLOCK hooks H, and the target conference template G. SplatLab produces both
C and L in the same run against the verified B; its writing stage can re-enter implementation to spawn
new training runs.

Elo; Implementation compiles survivors into EVOLVE-BLOCK mutations of the plugin; Refinement
runs a cascade-weighted Elo tournament across eight islands with standing falsification, scored on
real ns-train PSNR; Audit verifies that every reported number traces back to a measurement on the
verified substrate; Synthesis writes the manuscript through reviewer-driven loops that may re-enter

agents; 36 total) with no fixed DAG: the master may revisit any earlier stage on a falsifier veto, audit
failure, or reviewer-spawned ablation.

Stage 1: Grounding. Stage 1 anchors the run in the substrate. Two parallel investigations produce
the thesis spine Γ, the only artefact Stages 2–6 condition on. The first reads the literature: a Researcher
queries arXiv, Semantic Scholar, and Google Scholar and pulls from a persistent cross-run paper
store via RAG, with retrievals written back before Stage 1 closes so the corpus grows monotonically
across runs. Retrieved PDFs are parsed by MinerU [42] and a Limitation Brainstormer crossreferences each paper’s stated limitations against critiques in later work, emitting L-IDs: deduplicated
limitation strings such as L-127: opacity reset destabilises late refinement, each
with its supporting citation set. The second reads the substrate: a Bottleneck Analyst trains the
verified SPLATIFY plugin on representative Mip-NeRF 360 scenes and profiles per-tile loss, PSNR
trajectory, memory, and gradient norms; patterns become B-IDs: measurement-anchored strings
4
such as B-04: PSNR plateau at 25.65 dB on garden, each tagged with the EVOLVE-BLOCK
category it implicates. Γ contains the SPLATIFY reproduction vector V, the L-IDs, and the B-IDs.

signal. Survivors enter a bracket tournament with initial Elo 1200, judged by an LLM-as-Judge along
four axes: novelty against the L-ID citation set, specificity to the B-ID, implementability within
the category, and predicted-delta plausibility. Top-quartile uses multi-turn debate; lower tiers use
single-turn pairwise comparisons. The output passes into Stage 3 where each candidate becomes a
real plugin mutation, and into the GPU-grounded tournament in Stage 4.
Stage 3: Verified-plugin implementation. Stage 3 mutates the substrate. Each surviving idea
becomes a code change to a SPLATIFY/NERFIFY plugin [15, 16] that already compiles, trains, and
matches the target’s published headline; the verified-plugin contract attributes downstream gains to the
proposed mechanism rather than to silent reimplementation drift. A Hook Mapper resolves the abstract
category c into a concrete subset of the nine EVOLVE-BLOCK hooks (densification, regularisation,
loss, rendering, pruning, initialisation, scheduling, opacity, harmonics) and places EVOLVE-BLOCK
markers around the corresponding regions, following [30] but over a verified plugin rather than
agent-written code; for c = open, a cross-codebase auto-mapper locates insertion points that span
existing hooks, compose them, or introduce new ones. EVOLVE-BLOCKs are paired code comments
that delimit regions agents can modify while preserving the surrounding verified implementation.
An Implementer generates the code under three constraints: a Citation Graph retrieves L-ID/B-IDanchored papers and their published implementations as reference code; a Graph of Thoughts [4]
controller structures reasoning into generation, aggregation, and refinement nodes so a failed branch
can be revisited rather than restarted; and a context-free Grammar of SPLATIFY restricts output to
legal hook-level rewrites. Mutations are emitted as structured diffs [30] rather than full rewrites. The
diff is applied, the plugin compiled, and a 100-iteration smoke test trains on a single Mip-NeRF 360
scene; a candidate that compiles, finishes the smoke test stably, and instantiates the requested hooks
clears the contract. Failures return to the Implementer with the build log as a verbal-gradient signal,
capped at a repair budget before logging to the cross-run store as a build-failure negative.
Stage 4: Cascade-weighted tournament. Stage 4 makes the substrate the judge. Each candidate is
trained and scored on real PSNR; ratings are conditioned on the grounding pair (B, L) (benchmark
ID, loss ID), so strength is tracked per evaluation context rather than as a scalar leaderboard.
Each candidate flows through four stages of increasing rigour: smoke (100-iteration sanity check),
probe (full-iteration on one scene), multi-scene (full-iteration on a stratified subset), and the full
benchmark, which only the champion enters. Stage s updates Elo with weight ws ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 1.0}
from smoke through multi-scene, so a smoke win cannot drown out a multi-scene loss; without this
gating, a three-generation eight-island run on Mip-NeRF 360 would exceed multiple GPU-months.

regeneration). Per-island Elo distributions form a Pareto front over (B, L); promotion requires Pareto

Once a champion emerges, the remaining GPU budget splits between chain (sequential variants
extending the winner) and orthogonal (independent branches recombining components from nonchampion islands), shifting from chain-heavy to orthogonal-heavy as the champion’s per-iteration
gain saturates.
Stage 5: Integrity audit. Stage 5 binds the draft back to the substrate. Six audits run between the
Stage 4 champion and the draft; any failure aborts the run as a research log. Provenance: a contenthash chain links every reported number to its (experiment ID, commit, config) triple. Faithfulness: the
Stage 3 rationale is checked against the diff actually applied, catching drift that PaperOrchestra [37]
cannot reach by construction. Novelty: the mechanism is re-resolved against Semantic Scholar with
a query expanded by post-hoc results, catching prior work the Stage 2 Validator missed. Compute
match: the headline metric is recomputed under the baseline’s compute envelope; gains that fail
are reclassified as compute-confounded. Cross-codebase port: the winning hook is ported via the
Stage 3 auto-mapper to a second 3DGS codebase indexed by SPLATIFY; gains that fail are flagged
as codebase-specific. Claim check: every numerical or theoretical claim is resolved against the
experiment ledger; load-bearing mismatches terminate the run, closing the hallucination failure mode
that AI Scientist [25] drafts leave open.
Stage 6: Manuscript synthesis. Stage 6 turns substrate-grounded results into a manuscript that,
when challenged, re-enters the substrate. The (B, L) pair routes the run: a new discovery or beating
SOTA triggers Mode B (manuscript synthesis), otherwise Mode A (research log only). Five writers run
in parallel: Summary Writer (abstract, introduction), Body Writer (method, experiments; consumes
the Stage 4 Lessons File), Related Work (citation floor enforced via the Stage 1 Researcher), Figure
Generator (result plots from the ledger), and PaperBanana [55] (conceptual diagrams). A VLM Figure
Checker audits axis labels, legend completeness, and number-vs-caption agreement; an Ablation
Designer reads the EVOLVE-BLOCK diff and emits leave-one-out ablations, a Failure Analyst surfaces
residual-error heatmaps, and a Visual Comparator tiles qualitative renders against the reproduced
baseline. Three reviewer agents argue under an Editor (Methodology, Domain, Devil’s Advocate);
the panel extends AgentReview [18] by demanding new training runs, not only prose edits. New
experiments can re-enter Stage 4 with the comment as a verbal-gradient seed. The loop terminates
when no reviewer raises a New-Experiment concern.
4
Experiments
We evaluate two aspects of SplatLab: the research process, through run outcomes, component
ablations, failure analysis, and compute costs; and its outputs, through method performance, idea and
manuscript quality, and human and multi-agent peer review. Detailed run outcomes and failure cases
are reported in the appendix.
Experimental Setup. We run SplatLab multiple times: a mix of prompted runs (x is a target-paper
SOTA), single prompt and autonomous runs (x = ∅, the system selects its own target from the
literature); per-run mode and target are listed in supp. None of the runs required manual code
edits after dispatch. Each run’s SPLATIFY plugin or original source code is verified against its
target’s published headline or, against the paper reported numbers, before SplatLab begins (Table 1).
Standard benchmarks are Mip-NeRF 360 [3] (nine unbounded scenes, including flowers and treehill)
and LLFF [27]; every 8th image is held out for testing following [2], with all training runs at 30k
iterations on NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. We test inter-domain discovery on three out-of-distribution
scenes captured for this work and not previously addressed by 3DGS: nebula (astrophotographic),
bubble, and diamond. We report PSNR (dB)↑, SSIM↑, and LPIPS↓.
Our experiments are designed to demonstrate not equalized search budgets, but system capability
under end-to-end operation (with long-run autonomy a critical challenge that we overcome). Besides
scaling, our benefits accrue through the combination of verified reproduction, structured mutation
space and grounded evaluation. Reproduction details, the LLM stack, per-run compute budgets, and
per-scene breakdowns are in the supplementary.

Run outcomes. Across 16 runs, SplatLab trained 198 experiment branches, yielding five verified
positives, five marginal results with appropriately qualified claims, five non-PSNR contributions, and
one recorded failure. The Appendix details selection rejections, training failures, and outcomes by
target regime.
Compute cost. Runs used a 24–48-hour wall-clock budget, averaging 113M API tokens across
approximately 1,000–1,500 LLM calls. GPU consumption ranged from 2–36 GPU-hours per run,
with estimated LLM API costs of $150–250. Detailed accounting appears in the Appendix.
Generated Methods vs. SOTA. We evaluate SplatLab on multiple targets in two regimes. Three
are out-of-distribution scenes (Nebula, Bubble, Diamond) with vanilla 3DGS [19] as the baseline; the
remaining eight target published 3DGS methods on Mip-NeRF 360 and LLFF, where the discovered
variant must beat a tuned, peer-reviewed reference under the original paper’s iteration count, scene
set, and compute budget. Table 1 pairs each baseline with the SplatLab-discovered method on PSNR,
SSIM, and LPIPS; Figure 3 shows the regime-matched qualitative comparison. The discovered
7
method exceeds its baseline on PSNR in every pairing, with gains from +0.18 to +3.78 dB, the
largest on the astrophotographic nebula scene, a regime where 3DGS has not previously been applied.
Multi-seed Robustness. To evaluate seed sensitivity, we reran GNS-AA with five seeds. GNS
achieved 25.95 ± 0.07 dB, while GNS-AA achieved 26.18 ± 0.05 dB, yielding +0.23 ± 0.09 dB
compared with the originally reported +0.18 dB. Other generated studies also use three or four seeds.
Improvements within the estimated noise floor are reported as marginal rather than verified gains.

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting is the dominant route

PixelSplat [6], MVSplat [7], NoPoSplat [52], Splatt3R [37],

Sign-flip and the compression interpretation. Raw attention entropy correlates negatively with rendering residual
on the calibration pixels: tokens with broad, high-entropy
distributions are the ones the decoder gets right. The conventional “high entropy means high uncertainty” direction
is mis-specified for this signal. Together with the calibratable per-Gaussian and round-trip channels, we read the negative correlation as evidence that the decoder compresses information across attention paths, per-Gaussian features, and
the rendering operator, leaving multiple calibratable shadows.
Three calibratable channels in one frozen backbone.
The recent generation built on CroCo-style cross-view
transformers [30, 37, 41, 52] routes information through
cross-attention maps that are normally discarded; perGaussian features are normally aggregated away; and the
rendering equation supports a photometric round trip. Each
channel independently encodes a usable per-pixel uncertainty signal, and the three are best for different calibration
metrics.
We instantiate three lightweight, frozen-backbone posthoc calibrators. AttDis attaches a forward hook to the
last cross-attention layer, computes the Shannon entropy of
each token’s distribution over the context view, and fits a
single-scalar affine map from entropy to predicted residual
on one held-out scene. RasterCal is a small MLP on perGaussian features (covariance trace, scale, opacity, depth,
SH magnitude) that emits one log-variance per Gaussian,
splatted onto a per-pixel sigma map by the unmodified rasterizer. RoundTripCal renders the scene with one input
view withheld, measures the residual on that view, and fits
a single per-scene temperature. A chain extension, HeadVarAttDis, replaces head-mean entropy by the variance of
entropy across attention heads.
Cross-domain evaluation. We use a NoPoSplat checkpoint trained on RealEstate10K, evaluated on MipNeRF 360 [3]. Calibration metrics are well-defined here
regardless of mean accuracy; our claims are about how a
calibrator behaves relative to the same backbone. The large
NLL drop relative to the deterministic constant-variance
backbone is partly an artefact of replacing a degenerate variance with any non-degenerate estimator; AUSE, regressionECE, and Pearson correlation are independent of absolute
residual scale.

• We identify three internal channels of a frozen feedforward 3DGS decoder and show that each carries a calibratable per-pixel uncertainty signal without retraining.

• We document and explain a sign-flip in the attention channel: raw entropy is anti-correlated with the squared residual, consistent with entropy as a marker of redundant correspondence rather than ambiguity.
• We show calibrated uncertainty drives next-best-view selection: AttDis beats a geometric heuristic in mean across

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting. A first wave of
generalizable 3DGS replaced per-scene optimization with
a single decoder pass mapping sparse images to a Gaussian cloud: PixelSplat [6], MVSplat [7], NoPoSplat [52],

redundancy of overlapping primitives, so naive marginalization yields uncertainty that does not match rendered residual [48]. AttDis sidesteps both by reading uncertainty off
attention maps inside a frozen feed-forward decoder.

Calibration of deep models. Temperature scaling [17]

Deep ensembles [29] use K-model disagreement; MCDropout [14] approximates Bayesian inference by stochastic forward passes; evidential regression [1] learns a higherorder distribution. Ovadia et al. [33] benchmark these under

post-hoc affine-fit philosophy to a signal that survives all
three obstacles.
3.2. Where does the uncertainty live?
BayesRays [16] and FisherRF [22] extract per-Gaussian
uncertainty from a fitted Hessian or Laplace approximation, requiring a backward pass after training. ActiveNeRF [34] trains an explicit uncertainty head at scenefit time. Standard post-hoc recipes (temperature scaling [17], MC dropout [14], deep ensembles [29], evidential
heads [1]) either need an existing softmax output, stochastic forward passes, or additional parameters in the loss path.
We ask a different question: is there an internal scalar already produced by a frozen feed-forward decoder that, after a single calibration step, yields useful per-pixel uncertainty? The decoder’s cross-attention weights are produced
for free; their entropy is a 1-D scalar per query token.

3D Gaussian Splatting [23] represents a scene as an unordered set of anisotropic Gaussian primitives rasterised
through a tile-based α-compositor. Kheradmand et al. [24]
reformulated densification as Markov Chain Monte Carlo:
low-opacity primitives are resampled α-proportionally, and
a covariance-shaped Langevin term perturbs every position
on every iteration. The resulting sampler produces a fixedcap distribution and is now a common backbone for followup work.
The method has a known representational ceiling on fine,
elongated structure. The radial profile of an anisotropic
Gaussian is exponential along every principal axis, so a
primitive stretched to cover a long thin element (a branch, a
fence wire, a leaf silhouette) still falls off smoothly across
that element’s narrow dimension. Where the underlying geometry is closer to a step than to a Gaussian profile, the
primitive must either span the discontinuity (blurring the
silhouette) or shrink and leave neighbouring pixels uncovered. At convergence, a small subset of high-aspect primitives carry exactly this kind of structure, and pixel error in
those regions is dominated by kernel mismatch rather than
sampling variance.
We propose a late-stage modification that addresses this
regime directly. Once standard MCMC has converged, we
identify the subset of primitives whose covariance has aspect ratio above a threshold and replace each one by a short
chain of needle-shaped sub-primitives placed along its long
axis, each inheriting the parent’s appearance. Training then
continues for a fixed number of additional iterations under
the unchanged MCMC update. The construction adds no
new learnable parameters and leaves the rasteriser, the relocation proposal, the opacity gate, and the loss untouched.
A methodological aside on cap-mismatch. While auditing our experiments we discovered a configuration failure
mode in an earlier sweep: two arms of a comparison were
inadvertently trained at different primitive caps, producing
an apparent multi-tenth-dB win that disappeared once the
caps were matched. The failure is easy to commit, invisible
in aggregate-only reporting, and inflates per-scene deltas by
amounts comparable to several recent published improvements in this neighbourhood. We document it in Sec. 4.4
as a methodological recommendation, not as a result of the
present method.

Position. Among the four families above (MCMC formulation, optimiser change, primitive change, prior injection), our contribution lives strictly inside the MCMC family. Unlike optimiser-replacement work [18, 21, 48], we
do not alter the descent direction. Unlike primitive variants
[16, 17, 32], we do not alter the kernel. Unlike prior-driven
approaches [4, 29, 47], we add no supervision. We perform
a one-shot edit of the primitive support after equilibrium
and let the unchanged sampler re-equilibrate.
MCMC formulations and densification. Kheradmand
et al. [24] reinterpret 3DGS training as Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics: densification and pruning become
births and deaths, and a fixed-temperature noise term is injected into mean updates. This recasting eliminates many
heuristic clone/split rules [3, 9, 14, 39] and has inspired
related primitive-flow controllers [11, 35, 54, 60]. Earlier
samplers in this family include SGLD [49] and the discretisation analysis of Roberts and Tweedie [46], with preconditioning [42] and momentum extensions [5], building
on simulated annealing [26] and noise-injection analyses
of SGD [40]. We sit strictly inside the MCMC family of
Kheradmand et al. [24]: rather than retune the optimiser or
change the proposal, we make a one-shot edit of the primitive support after equilibrium.

3.4. Why the equilibrium analysis still applies
The MCMC formulation of Kheradmand et al. [24] couples
a relocation proposal q(·) that resamples primitives in proportion to a moment-matched density with a Langevin step
that perturbs positions in place. Detailed balance is stated
on the relocation proposal and is independent of the inplace dynamics. The swap is a one-time deterministic edit
of the primitive set; it does not modify the relocation proposal, the opacity gate, the pruning rule, or the photometric
likelihood, so it cannot break detailed balance. Within the
post-swap continuation the same proposal and likelihood
apply, and the chain re-equilibrates on the larger primitive
set. That perceptual metrics improve while average PSNR
is unchanged is consistent with the post-swap equilibrium
being closer to the true geometry on thin features and approximately conservative on the smooth-region majority.
Table 1 reports per-scene PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS at
cap max=1M. LPIPS improves on every scene; the outdoor mean is −0.041 and the indoor mean is −0.008, an
outdoor gain roughly five times the indoor gain. SSIM gains
on every outdoor scene and is unchanged indoors. Outdoor PSNR is at parity (+0.025 mean, mixed signs); indoor
scenes regress (−0.444 mean, all four negative), pulling the
nine-scene mean to −0.184. We do not claim a PSNR improvement. The late-stage swap reallocates capacity from
blurring across thin features to specialising at them, and the
size of that effect tracks the size of the high-aspect primitive
population.
