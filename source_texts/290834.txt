Abstract
Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting has become a main technique for fast 3D scene reconstruction and editing, leveraging an efficient and flexible explicit representation for high-fidelity real-time rendering. However, the quality of the point clouds used to initialize Gaussians remains a key factor that limits fine-grained geometry reconstruction. To address this limitation, we propose an Iterative Spatial Decomposition (ISD) framework that bridges dense geometric priors from Multi-View Stereo (MVS) with Gaussian Splatting. ISD mitigates the mismatch between dense MVS point clouds and Gaussian sparsity by iteratively partitioning the scene into voxels of adaptive granularity and performing density-aware point assignment. Building on ISD, we introduce Hierarchical Geometric Prior Sampling (HGPS) to substantially reduce redundancy in MVS point clouds while preserving critical details, thereby providing a more robust geometric foundation for reconstruction. We further develop Hierarchical Geometry-aware Initialization (HGI), which uses a voxel-radius-based adaptive parameter initialization and replaces the iterative KNN-based procedure with batch computation, enabling efficient and robust Gaussian initialization. Additionally, we propose a Hierarchical Geometry-aware Densification (HGD) method. By dynamically identifying over-reconstructed or under-reconstructed regions through voxel constraints, HGD enhances detail reconstruction quality while controlling storage overhead. Extensive experiments on Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, and Deep Blending demonstrate significant improvements in rendering quality, achieving state-of-the-art LPIPS performance. These results indicate that our approach effectively alleviates deficiencies in the geometric priors of initial point clouds and recovers richer geometric details.

1 Introduction
In recent years, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has achieved a revolutionary breakthrough in neural rendering thanks to its explicit representation and real-time rendering capabilities [16]. The method employs differentiable Gaussian ellipsoids to parameterize scene geometry and appearance, adopts an alternating strategy for parameter optimization and density control, and incorporates an efficient visibility-aware rendering algorithm. This technology has demonstrated significant advantages in tasks such as robot perception [5, 19, 20], dynamic scene reconstruction [12, 40], 3D content generation [4, 14, 44], and autonomous driving [23, 38].
Despite the impressive performance of 3DGS, its high-fidelity results critically depend on the geometric completeness of the initial point clouds [25]. Current mainstream methods predominantly employ sparse point clouds generated through Structure-from-Motion (SfM) techniques for initialization [32]. However, because SfM relies heavily on feature matching, the resulting point cloud often suffers from severe density insufficiency in weak-texture regions, which leads to significant geometric sparsity [8].
Recently, integrating Multi-View Stereo (MVS) priors has emerged as a primary solution to this sparsity bottleneck [2, 7, 21, 41]. Current works exploring this direction fundamentally diverge into two paradigms: feed-forward networks for sparse-view synthesis, and per-scene optimization for dense-view reconstruction. Our work falls into the latter category. However, directly utilizing massive MVS point clouds for per-scene optimization inevitably introduces extreme GPU memory footprints and inherent spatial redundancies, posing a significant management challenge.
Given the limitations of existing downsampling methods in balancing efficiency and spatial indexing, this article presents an Iterative Spatial Decomposition (ISD) framework. The framework enables batch-based organization and management of point clouds or Gaussian centers, while preserving geometric details through a layered density analysis strategy. Building on this framework, we develop three key modules: Hierarchical Geometric Prior Sampling (HGPS) for MVS point cloud downsampling, Hierarchical Geometry-Aware Initialization (HGI) for Gaussian parameter configuration, and Hierarchical Geometry-Aware Densification (HGD) for adaptive Gaussian distribution refinement.
In summary, our main contributions are:
—
Proposing an ISD method. Distinct from standard linear octrees, ISD structurally adapts traditional spatial indexing into a tensor-friendly, coarse-to-fine iterative pipeline without the need for global sorting. This establishes density-aware adaptive structural correlations between voxels and point clouds/Gaussian centers.
—
Developing a HGPS mechanism to compress MVS point clouds to computationally manageable scales while maintaining scene completeness and detail fidelity, thereby meeting 3DGS initialization requirements.
—
Introducing HGI and densification strategies, leveraging the established adaptive correlations to optimize Gaussian parameter initialization and distribution refinement.
—
Developing and validating a comprehensive Image-to-MVS-to-3DGS pipeline integrating the proposed modules. Extensive experiments demonstrate the pipeline’s advanced performance in generating high-fidelity Gaussian representations.
2 Related Work
2.1 MVS
MVS reconstructs 3D scene representations from multi-view images. Traditional MVS methods [10, 33] rely on hand-crafted operators (e.g., SIFT [24] or DAISY [35]) for feature extraction and patch-based matching to estimate depth or disparity maps. However, these approaches struggle to ensure completeness and accuracy in weakly textured regions or under varying illumination conditions.
In 2018, MVSNet [42] pioneered deep learning-based MVS for sub-pixel precision depth prediction. It leverages differentiable homography warping and bilinear interpolation to construct a cost volume based on the reference camera frustum. The homography warping formula determines the projected position p′ of a pixel p from a source view onto the depth plane d:
p′=KiRi(R1TK1−1pd+(RiTTi−R1TT1)),
(1)
where K1, R1, and T1 denote the intrinsic matrix, rotation matrix, and translation vector of the reference view, respectively, while Ki, Ri, and Ti correspond to those of the ith source view.
Subsequent works, such as CasMVSNet [11], introduced a cascaded structure to progressively refine depth maps from coarse to fine across multiple scales, thereby establishing a dominant framework for subsequent optimization. Recent advances, including MVSTER [37], ET-MVSNet [22], GeoMVSNet [45], and GC-MVSNet [36], have continuously set new reconstruction records on benchmarks such as DTU [1] and Tanks & Temples [18]. However, when directly applying the dense point clouds generated by MVS methods to 3DGS, the excessive point density can result in memory explosion and optimization instability, highlighting the urgent need for an efficient downsampling mechanism.
2.2 3DGS
Recently, 3DGS has replaced implicit MLP-based representations [27, 28] with explicit anisotropic Gaussians, achieving state-of-the-art optimization efficiency and rendering quality performance. A 3D Gaussian is parameterized by its center μ∈R3, rotation matrix R∈R3×3, scaling matrix S∈R3, spherical harmonic coefficients F∈R16×3, and opacity α. Its spatial distribution is given by:
G(x)=exp⁡(−12(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ)),
(2)
where the covariance matrix Σ=RSSTRT is derived from the scaling S and rotation matrices R.
Since its introduction, 3DGS technology has rapidly expanded across various domains. Moreover, extensive research continues to enhance 3DGS performance. To mitigate aliasing artifacts in the original 3DGS, Yan et al. [39] proposed multi-scale 3DGS, maintaining Gaussians at varying scales for consistent scene representation. Mip-Splatting [43] addresses this issue through a hybrid 3D smoothing filter and 2D Mip filter. For redundant Gaussian distributions under extreme viewpoint changes, Scaffold-GS [25] introduces anchor-guided Gaussian placement for compact representations. DashGaussian [6] accelerates optimization by dynamically adjusting the rendering resolution and the number of Gaussians, thereby significantly reducing optimization time while maintaining rendering quality. Vol3DGS [34] computes transmittance by analytically integrating 3D Gaussians, which results in more physically accurate alpha values, thereby improving rendering quality while maintaining rendering speed.
Despite these advancements, the quality of Gaussian initialization remains a critical factor in determining 3DGS rendering performance. Current approaches rely on sparse point clouds generated by SfM, which can introduce geometric distortions in weakly textured regions. Therefore, optimizing the point cloud used for initialization is crucial for enhancing the rendering quality of 3DGS.
2.3 MVS-Assisted 3DGS
Recently, combining MVS techniques with 3DGS has emerged as a promising direction to enhance geometric representation and rendering quality. Current advancements in this domain fundamentally diverge into two distinct paradigms. Table 1 summarizes their key differences across three core dimensions.

Comparison of Two Paradigms Combining MVS and 3DGS
The first paradigm focuses on generalizable feed-forward networks. Specifically, MVSplat [7] is designed for sparse-view inputs, building a cost volume via plane sweeping to directly predict and generate end-to-end feed-forward 3D Gaussian distributions. MVSGaussian [21] proposes a generalizable feed-forward representation that leverages MVS to encode geometry-aware features and decodes them directly into 3D Gaussian parameters for fast reconstruction of unseen scenes. Similarly, Yang et al. [41] propose an adaptive perception-aware feature aggregation module, fusing 2D image features to construct a 3D geometry-aware Gaussian representation with cross-scene generalizability. These networks are explicitly designed for sparse-view inputs to enable fast novel view synthesis, aiming at a different objective from high-fidelity scene reconstruction in dense-view scenarios.
The second paradigm focuses on explicit per-scene optimization, where MVS priors are utilized for initialization before training or as supervision during training. For instance, MVS-Splatting [2] aims to accelerate the per-scene optimization process by estimating and fusing MVS depth maps into dense sets of 3D Gaussians, providing a high-quality explicit geometric initialization for standard training. Operating within this paradigm, our framework takes a different route by directly utilizing dense physical MVS point clouds rather than fused depth maps. We focus on the efficient structural management of these massive point clouds. Distinct from depth-fusion approaches, our ISD framework adaptively downsamples redundant points and maps spatial priors to Gaussian parameters, balancing high-fidelity detail retention with computational efficiency.
3 Method
Our objective is to construct high-fidelity 3D Gaussian models from collections of input images. As shown in Figure 1, the pipeline commences with SfM processing to obtain sparse representations followed by MVS reconstruction, which is augmented by HGPS to preserve geometric details. Notably, we merge the compressed MVS point cloud with the sparse SfM point cloud to combine dense foreground details with broad background coverage. To optimize memory usage, this merging step is performed after the HGPS module has reduced the spatial redundancy of the MVS data. This integrated process produces a unified initial point cloud that captures both fine foreground geometry and background structures. Subsequently, the HGI module is employed to initialize the Gaussian model. During training, the HGD module dynamically regulates the densification process of the Gaussian model. After multiple training iterations, the pipeline ultimately yields a high-fidelity 3D Gaussian model rich in detail. Next, we discuss these key components.

Pipeline overview. The pipeline begins with the input images. First, SfM-based sparse reconstruction is applied to generate an SfM point cloud that captures the basic structural information of the scene. Building upon the SfM results, MVS produces an MVS point cloud enriched with fine details. Next, a HGPS strategy downsamples the MVS point cloud, yielding an HGPS point cloud. Subsequently, the SfM and HGPS point clouds are merged to combine the advantages of both sparse and dense representations. With the merged point cloud, the HGI module initializes the Gaussian model. During training, images are rendered from the 3D Gaussian model, and a loss is computed based on the rendered results. In the backpropagation stage, the HGD module adaptively refines the Gaussian distribution, progressively enhancing the model’s structural details.
3.1 ISD
Traditional point cloud management techniques often suffer from real-time responsiveness and detail retention limitations when processing Gaussian models involving large-scale point cloud data. To overcome these challenges, we propose an ISD method. While ISD utilizes Morton codes for efficient, pointerless spatial hashing—a technique commonly associated with Linear Octrees—it is fundamentally distinct in both its construction algorithm and its application semantics.
A distinct difference exists between ISD and a standard linear octree implementation. While standard high-performance linear octrees are typically constructed by mapping all points to a predefined maximum depth followed by expensive global sorting, the core principle of our method is to hierarchically partition space from coarse to fine granularity through iterative (non-recursive) operations that eliminate the need for global sorting. A notable advantage of this adaptation is its ability to efficiently process all point cloud data in batches, effectively bypassing the overhead of global sorting for massive MVS point clouds.
Specifically, the scene space is subdivided into smaller voxel units during the iterative process. Instead of global tree traversal, ISD evaluates spatial density in parallel. Driven by a density-aware early binding criterion, each point (or Gaussian center) in the point cloud is dynamically assigned to a corresponding level l the moment it becomes the sole occupant of its voxel. Consequently, each level is associated with a specific spatial voxel, whose scale directly provides an adaptive structural correlation for 3D Gaussian parameterization. Each iteration comprises three core phases, which are described in detail below.
3.1.1 Position Mapping.
First, we establish a spatial correspondence between the points and the scene space. Given an input point cloud P∈{pi}i=1N, where each point pi=(xi,yi,zi)∈R3 represents 3D coordinates, and N denotes the number of points, we compute the scene’s geometric center c and its bounding radius r:
c=(xmax−xmin, ymax−ymin, zmax−zmin)/2,
(3)
r=max({|pi−c|}i=1N).
(4)
At level l, the scene is partitioned into 2l×2l×2l voxels. To avoid ambiguity and ensure a self-contained spatial decomposition pipeline, we define the bounds for the iterative levels here. The partitioning process originates from an initial level l0 and iterates progressively up to a predefined maximum level lm, governed by a step size Δl. Here, the world-to-voxel mapping is defined as:
P^=round(2l−1(P−c)/r)+2l−1,
(5)
where P^∈{pi^}i=1N and pi^=(xi^,yi^,zi^)∈{0,1,⋯,2l}3.
3.1.2 Coordinate Encoding.
Following Morton’s space-filling curve theory, we encode 3D voxel indices p^ into compact 1D integers for efficient spatial indexing. For each coordinate axis component p^, we perform (l+1)-bit binary expansion:
x^=∑k=0l2kbk(x),y^=∑k=0l2kbk(y),z^=∑k=0l2kbk(z),
(6)
where bk(.)∈{0,1} denotes the kth bit.
The Morton code m is generated through bit-interleaving:
m=∑k=0l(23kbk(x)+23k+1bk(y)+23k+2bk(z)).
(7)
This encoding uniquely maps 3D coordinates to scalar integer values.
3.1.3 Voxel Binding.
Finally, we bind the points that meet the conditions to the spatial voxel at level l. First, we perform a hash count on all the Morton codes {mi}i=1N, computing the frequency count(mi) of each code. Then, we define the uniqueness mask Ml, whose elements satisfy:
Ml[mi]={1,count(mi)=1,0,otherwise.
(8)
When count(mi)=1, the voxel contains only a single spatial point and does not need further subdivision into smaller voxels. Thus, these points are bound to the current-level voxels.
Moreover, during the last iteration, if count(mi)>1, it means that these points are too close to each other in space. We retain only one point for each unique Morton code to eliminate redundancy, ensuring that all selected points occupy the same space as the original point cloud.

To rigorously evaluate our approach, we compared HGPS against robust decimation algorithms implemented via the standard Open3D library [46], including Curvature downsampling [29], Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR) [31], and explicit Octree-based decimation [26]. Achieving high decimation rates with SOR requires aggressive thresholding, which severely removes background structures and preserves only the densest central clusters, all while incurring a massive computational burden due to repeated neighbor-search queries. Explicit Octree downsampling performs much better structurally but remains computationally expensive. Curvature sampling retains edge features but incurs substantial distortion in core scene regions. In contrast, our HGPS method successfully maintains both global scene structure and fine local details, achieving computational speeds significantly faster than standard Octree and SOR implementations, thereby providing an optimal geometric foundation for efficient 3DGS initialization.

3.3 HGI
Typically, the MVS point cloud is more focused on the central region of the scene—that is, the foreground—while the SfM point cloud, though sparse, covers the scene more broadly and effectively adds background information. In light of this, we combine the downsampled MVS point cloud Pd with the sparse SfM point cloud Ps, forming the initial Gaussian representation P=Pd∪Ps.
Before optimizing the Gaussian model, the input point cloud must be converted into Gaussian representations. We designed a HGI method based on the ISD approach.
The merged point cloud P is converted into Gaussian representations, where each Gaussian is parameterized by its center μ, scaling matrix S, rotation matrix R, opacity α, and spherical harmonic coefficients F. We initialize the scaling matrix based on the voxel size at the point’s assigned level:
S=β⋅vl/2,
(11)
where vl is the voxel size at level l, and β is an enlargement factor ensuring adequate coverage of neighboring voxels.
Algorithm 2 provides a detailed explanation of this process. In the iterative process from low to high levels, we progressively select appropriate points from P as Gaussian centers μ. Then, based on the voxel radius corresponding to these points, we initialize the scaling matrix S. We apply an enlargement factor β to adjust the voxel radius to ensure that the Gaussian distribution adequately covers its surrounding neighboring voxels. As for the rotation matrix, opacity, and other related parameters, we follow the settings in [16], adopting their default values for consistency.
3.4 HGD
The original 3DGS densification strategy relies on a precomputed scene radius to decide whether to split or clone a Gaussian, which often fails to capture fine-grained geometric details due to its global and uniform nature. To enable more precise and adaptive control over the densification process, we propose a HGD strategy. Before detailing this strategy, we first define the key terms utilized in our densification pipeline to ensure self-containment. The view-space positional gradient threshold serves as an indicator to identify regions where the current geometric representation is insufficient. When a Gaussian exceeds this threshold, it reflects a geometric mismatch that falls into one of two categories. Under-reconstructed regions lack sufficient Gaussians to cover local geometric features, requiring the cloning of existing Gaussians. Over-reconstructed regions occur when a single Gaussian covers an excessively large spatial extent without capturing fine details, necessitating a split into smaller Gaussians.
Our method leverages the voxel structure established by the ISD to dynamically identify these two types of regions and adjust the Gaussian distribution accordingly, as outlined in Algorithm 3. The process applies strictly to the candidate set of Gaussians whose view-space positional gradients exceed the predefined threshold in the current training iteration. For each Gaussian Gi∈G, we represent its scaling matrix as S(Gi)∈R3 and the corresponding voxel size at its assigned level l as Sl(Gi)∈R3. Within this specific candidate set, we define the binary conditions for over-reconstruction and under-reconstruction as:
Go={Gi∈G | ∑d=13I(Si,d>Sl,d)>1},
(12)
Gu={Gi∈G | ∑d=13I(Si,d>Sl,d)≤1},
(13)
where I(⋅) denotes the indicator function. If a Gaussian is classified as over-reconstructed, it is split into two sub-Gaussians to better capture the local geometry. Conversely, if a Gaussian is under-reconstructed, it is cloned to compensate for missing details.
Additionally, we remove redundant Gaussians that are too close to each other in the scene space. Specifically, Gaussians that cannot be associated with a unique voxel in the final level lm are discarded. When discarding, we use opacity as the selection criterion and retain the Gaussian with the highest opacity.
4 Experiments
4.1 Experiment Setup
4.1.1 Datasets.
We adopted three widely recognized 3D reconstruction and rendering datasets to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method thoroughly. Mip-NeRF360 [3] is designed for unbounded scenes and supports 360∘ viewpoint rendering, making it suitable for assessing the model’s performance in non-linear parameterization, anti-aliasing, and large-scale scene optimization. Tanks & Temples [18] features a diverse range of complex indoor and outdoor scenes, providing an ideal environment for testing the algorithm’s robustness under complex geometric structures, occlusion handling, and lighting variations. Additionally, the Deep Blending [13] dataset focuses on high dynamic range environments, assessing model adaptability to complex lighting conditions, reflective surfaces, and weakly textured regions. When splitting the training and testing sets, we adhered strictly to the approach recommended by Mip-NeRF360 [3], ensuring alignment with the evaluation standards employed by state-of-the-art methods. The primary evaluation metrics for visual quality are PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.
4.1.2 Implementation Details.
We construct the proposed model by extending the open source 3DGS code with the PyTorch library. To obtain the dense geometric priors, the initial MVS point clouds are generated using the recent RRT-MVS algorithm [15]. Prior to model optimization, the MVS point cloud is preprocessed using HGPS. The relevant parameters are set as follows: Nt=1×106, ls=4, lm=28, and Δl=1. During optimization, the scaling factor β is set to 1.6, derived from the splitting value of the original method. The level step Δl is adjusted to 2, and the number of training iterations is set to 45,000. The loss function follows the definition in the original 3DGS [16] method. To ensure a strictly fair and rigorous comparison, we explicitly state that all baseline methods were reimplemented within the same software framework. Furthermore, core configurations including the total number of training iterations and evaluation views were completely unified across all methods. All experiments were conducted on Linux servers equipped with a single GeForce RTX 5090 GPU.

Its primary contribution lies in structural refinement and redundancy control: it significantly cuts down the total number of Gaussians from 1,944k to 1,825k, reduces training time from 34 minutes to 29 minutes, and further refines detail-sensitive metrics. This demonstrates that HGD effectively mitigates the computational and storage overhead brought by dense MVS priors while maintaining high-fidelity reconstruction.

4.3.4 Robustness to ISD Hyperparameters.
To rigorously evaluate the behavior and stability of the ISD framework, we conducted a comprehensive sensitivity analysis on its execution parameters. Since the ISD framework is deployed in two distinct phases of our pipeline with fundamentally different engineering objectives—preprocessing (HGPS) and training densification (HGD)—we evaluate them independently using structurally symmetrical metrics to observe their isolated and end-to-end impacts.
Preprocessing Phase (HGPS). During the multi-view geometric prior sampling phase, the initial level l0 and maximum level lm merely provide a safety range limit to ensure the actual sampling does not exceed reasonable bounds. Specifically, the initial level l0 simply prevents the starting voxel resolution from being too coarse to adapt to the scale variations of input point clouds. Adjusting l0 within a valid range has no practical impact on the final outcome; therefore, explicit ablation for l0 is fundamentally unnecessary and omitted. The maximum level lm serves as a physical upper boundary. As presented in Table 7, ablating lm simply demonstrates that artificially restricting it to low values (e.g., 10 or 15) forces premature termination, yielding sub-optimal initial point counts (646 K or 856 K). Once lm reaches a sufficient boundary (lm≥20), the loop safely halts and point counts saturate naturally at 1,534 K, fully confirming it acts purely as a safeguard ceiling. The level step Δl, however, controls the sampling precision. A conservative stride (Δl=1) stops precisely at our designated target. In contrast, an aggressive step (Δl=4) causes the loop to skip the optimal stopping condition, prematurely settling for a coarser grid (retaining only 911 K points) and subsequently degrading visual fidelity (LPIPS degrades to 0.134).

The dynamic tracking curves demonstrate that introducing the downsampled MVS prior (+HGPS) provides rich geometric information. To physically support these dense geometric structures, the model inevitably requires a massive number of Gaussians, which naturally results in a higher VRAM consumption compared to the sparse baseline. However, without spatial regularization, this unconstrained densification can lead to structural redundancy. The full pipeline (+HGD) subsequently mitigates this by applying voxel-based physical constraints to dynamically identify over-reconstructed and under-reconstructed regions, removing redundant Gaussians that are too close to each other in the scene space. This mechanism effectively prunes unnecessary Gaussians and limits the storage overhead. Although the final resource consumption of our pipeline remains higher than that of Vanilla 3DGS, this increase is an inevitable and acceptable tradeoff for physically sustaining fine-grained structures and achieving significantly superior rendering quality.

5 Conclusion
In this work, we propose an ISD method. Moving beyond the traditional role of standard linear octrees as mere spatial indexing structures, ISD acts as a tensor-friendly, early-binding framework that bridges spatial decomposition directly with Gaussian parameterization, significantly improving the rendering quality of 3DGS when integrating MVS point clouds. Based on the proposed ISD method, we design three optimization components: HGPS compresses dense MVS point clouds to appropriate scales while preserving critical geometric features; HGI optimizes the initial spatial distribution of Gaussian ellipsoids, providing improved initial values for parameter optimization; and HGD adaptively regulates the cloning and splitting of Gaussians while filtering out redundant Gaussians. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed method effectively enhances rendering quality, particularly regarding fine detail representation.
