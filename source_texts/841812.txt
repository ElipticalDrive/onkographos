A B S T R A C T
We present an eﬃcient dimension-by-dimension ﬁnite-volume method which solves the adiabatic
magnetohydrodynamics equations at high discretization order, using the constrained-transport
approach on Cartesian grids. Results are presented up to tenth order of accuracy. The algorithmic
architecture of this method is very close to that of commonly employed second-order schemes:
it requires only one reconstructed value per face for each computational cell, independently
of the scheme’s order. This property is highly beneﬁcial for the numerical eﬃciency. It results
from reusing the required values already available in neighboring grid cells, in contrast to
standard algorithms that require a number of reconstructions and evaluations which increases
with the scheme’s order of accuracy. At a given resolution, these high-order schemes present
signiﬁcantly less numerical dissipation than commonly employed lower-order approaches. Thus,
results of comparable accuracy are achievable at a substantially coarser resolution, yielding
overall performance gains. We also present a way to include physical dissipative terms: viscosity,
magnetic diﬀusivity and cooling functions, respecting the ﬁnite-volume and constrained-transport
frameworks. Beneﬁts of this method are shown through applications in turbulent ﬂows.
1. Introduction
Turbulence is an ubiquitous phenomenon in nature which still lacks a comprehensive theory. Progress in its understanding can
be achieved by direct numerical simulation (DNS) of the Navier-Stokes equations (NSE) for neutral ﬂuids. For electrically conductive media, such as liquid planetary cores or plasmas (ionized gases), found in many astrophysical environments, the equations of
magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) are a convenient single-ﬂuid description. This approximation is valid on large spatial and long temporal scales as compared to the microscopic characteristics of electric and magnetic interactions between the charged constituents.
Therefore, MHD is widely used when dealing with geo- and astrophysical problems.
In natural systems, the Reynolds number Re, which characterizes the spectral bandwidth of turbulent ﬂuctuations, can be orders of
magnitude above the maximum values achievable by DNS on today’s high-performance computing facilities. A main culprit limiting
the achievable Reynolds number in DNS is insuﬃcient numerical resolution of space and time and the consequential dissipation
introduced via the numerical integration scheme that results in amplitude and phase errors of the numerical solution. Numerical
dissipation is generated by the loss of information due to discretization errors, which is dominant at small scales and represents

A direct reduction of algorithmic numerical dissipation increases the spectral bandwidth of a DNS at the same numerical resolution,
and ideally leads to more accurate numerical experiments at aﬀordable computational cost. For this aim, more accurate Riemann
solvers, which numerically approximate the time evolution of two neighboring ﬂuid states, have been developed [54,63,53,65,49,51].
Another approach is to raise the discretization order, i.e. to go beyond the standard second-order approach by including terms of
higher order in the Taylor expansion of the solution (assumed to be analytic within each computational grid cell), e.g. [20,26,11].
Even though at a given resolution higher-order discretization is computationally more demanding, such schemes can lead to a large
gain in eﬃciency overall. Indeed, higher-order solvers typically require a lower resolution to reach a similar accuracy as compared
to lower-order schemes [66].
However, multi-dimensional polynomial reconstruction becomes increasingly expensive with a growing order of accuracy. A
speciﬁc strategy to alleviate this problem is realized in “dimension-by-dimension” solvers. Instead of solving an expensive 3D reconstruction problem at once, they solve three one-dimensional cost-eﬃcient problems separately. A further gain in performance is
achieved by a dimension-by-dimension approach which requires only one high-order reconstruction and two evaluations along each
dimension [48,16], independently of the scheme’s order, instead of an amount growing with the order of accuracy, associated with
higher-order quadrature.
The present work employs this numerical technique and extends a dimension-by-dimension fourth-order ﬁnite-volume solver for
the compressible MHD equations using the constrained-transport approach [66] up to order ten. The resulting schemes are based on
Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory (WENO) 1D reconstructions, associated with a passage through point values. Volume/area/lineaverage↔point value transformations are derived using the method described in [48,16]. By presenting this method, which, contrary
to standard WENO ones, does not require a growing number of reconstructions with increasing order of accuracy, we intend to make
higher-order numerics more accessible to e.g. practitioners dealing with computational ﬂuid dynamics. Indeed:
1. Implementing higher-order methods is not necessarily much more complex than implementing second-order ones. The method
presented here can upgrade a second-order accurate ﬁnite-volume solver on Cartesian grids to an arbitrarily high-order one
without changing its structure fundamentally. Indeed: (𝑖) the higher-order reconstruction polynomials are evaluated at the same
spatial locations as the second-order one and (𝑖𝑖) the passage through point values is done by two independent algorithmic blocks,
that are added seamlessly in the already existing algorithmic chain. We summarize in this work the theoretical keypoints, present
a complete structure of a solver, provide ready-to-use formulas for the volume/area/line-average↔point value transformations,
and discuss some practical details.
2. Using higher-order methods is not necessarily computationally more costly than employing second-order schemes. Since such
algorithms can allow results of comparable accuracy at signiﬁcantly coarser grid resolutions, they can lead to substantial savings
in computing time. At a ﬁxed grid resolution, the increase in cost when going to higher discretization order remains modest
with the method presented here: on the high-performance computing cluster we used, a right-hand-side computation takes about
three times longer with a tenth-order scheme as compared to a second-order one. This is small, as compared to the increased
numerical cost induced by doubling the linear resolution, which corresponds to an increase by a factor of 16 for 3D problems.
Reducing the numerical resolution while increasing the scheme’s order can thus be beneﬁcial, as far as it is done judiciously
regarding the physical length and time scales of the problem under consideration.
For supersonic ﬂows, where shocks and discontinuities are prevalent, avoiding the generation of spurious oscillations near sharp
gradients is a very challenging issue [20,66,9,67]. This is even more the case when using higher-order numerics, i.e. reconstruction
polynomials of higher degree. The method we present here is shock-capturing and can be made more robust using various techniques
available in the literature (see section 4.3).
The rest of this paper is organized as follows: section 2 describes the MHD equations to be solved as well as the chosen discretization
(ﬁnite-volume in a Cartesian domain and constrained-transport for the magnetic ﬁeld). Section 3 presents how the higher-order
numerical schemes are built. Their numerical accuracy is veriﬁed in section 4 through convergence tests. That section also shows
their shock-capturing ability and presents several examples of application to turbulent ﬂows. Finally, concluding remarks are given
in section 5.

computation is shown in Fig. 1: the solver reconstructs the ﬁelds at the interfaces between cells (HBreco, section 3.1), deduces the
ﬂuxes (HBflux, section 3.2) and from them the temporal derivatives (toHBrhs, section 3.3). These are plugged in a time integrator
(section 3.4).

3.1. Reconstruction module (HBreco)
This subsection describes the reconstruction module (HBreco in Fig. 1), which is composed of two parts (Fig. 2):
1. Binterp, which reconstructs volume averages of the magnetic ﬁeld’s components from the staggered constrained-transport

the internal energy sink (section 3.5.3).
2. WENO, which stands for Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory. It is a reconstruction procedure computing area averages of all
quantities at the cell’s interfaces from their volume average.

3.1.2. Weighted essentially non-oscillatory reconstruction (WENO)
Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory (WENO) schemes allow high-order reconstruction in smooth regions while limiting oscillations in non-smooth ones. They are improvements of the Essentially Non-Oscillatory (ENO) schemes [32]. The ﬁrst WENO scheme
[44], third-order accurate, appeared in the 90s and has rapidly been extended to ﬁfth-order [35]. A method to derive WENO schemes
of arbitrarily high order of accuracy is provided in [57], and high-order WENO schemes, with a discretization order up to 17 are
given in [7,28].
The main idea of a WENO reconstruction is to split the reconstruction stencil in substencils associated with diﬀerent weights [57].

Upgrading a second-order accurate ﬁnite-volume solver on Cartesian grids to higher orders of accuracy can thus be done seamlessly: the reconstructions and evaluations are done at the same locations, i.e. the center of the faces. Higher-order schemes require
simply the integration of two additional blocks (AtoP and PtoA) in the algorithmic chain.

3.4. Time integration: strong-stability preserving Runge-Kutta methods (SSPRK)
The computed right-hand side (RHS, sections 3.1-3.6) is plugged in a Strong Stability Preserving Runge-Kutta (SSPRK) time
integrator (see the diagram for the whole solver, Fig. 7). The SSPRK methods require several iterations, so that the RHS is computed
s times (see the loop with the ending condition it==s in Fig. 7). After this, the simulation time t is updated. If needed, some
diagnostics are written out, e.g. complete state of the system, or only its kinetic/magnetic energy, Fourier spectra, etc. Then, the time
integration procedure is repeated. This is performed until the ﬁnal time tf is reached.
SSPRK methods prevent additional oscillations resulting from inaccuracies in the time integration process [30,29] and are very
valuable in systems exhibiting discontinuities and shocks, e.g. turbulent supersonic ﬂuids.
The second-order and fourth-order time integration methods are taken from [36] (pseudocode 1 with s=10 and pseudocode 3
of that reference, respectively). They are ten-stages single-step SSPRK methods. Ten-stages means that s=10 RHS computations are
needed. Single-step means that the solution at the next instant 𝐮(𝐱, 𝑡 + Δ𝑡) is computed solely from the solution at the instant 𝑡, 𝐮(𝐱, 𝑡).
However, for schemes of order strictly greater than four, no explicit single-step SSPRK method exists [38,56]. For this reason,
multi-step methods, which require the solution at several points in time, have been developed [37,15,31]. Two-step explicit methods,
which require 𝐮(𝐱, 𝑡 − Δ𝑡) in addition to 𝐮(𝐱, 𝑡) to compute 𝐮(𝐱, 𝑡 + Δ𝑡), have order of accuracy at most eight [37]. Hence, for tenth
order of accuracy, three-step methods (at least), which require 𝐮(𝐱, 𝑡 − 2Δ𝑡) as well, are needed.
Consequently, the sixth-order method used is the two-step ten-stages one from [31]. The two-step eighth-order methods presented
in [37] require a smaller time-step as compared to the three-step ten-stages one from [31], so that the latter is used. Finally, for
tenth order of accuracy, a three-step twenty-stages method is used [31]. In the following, we denote the applied time integrators

3.6.4. Remarks for compressible MHD turbulent simulations
We conclude the description of the forcing module with two remarks relevant in the context of compressible MHD turbulence:
• The kinetic forcing occurs through an external acceleration ﬁeld (i.e. it is of the form 𝜕𝑡 (𝜌𝐯) = 𝜌𝐟 ). This allows a broader inertial
range in the turbulent spectra as compared to a forcing through an external force ﬁeld (of the form 𝜕𝑡 (𝜌𝐯) = 𝐠) [39,1].
• The ﬁnite-volume approach guarantees the strict conservation of momentum up to machine precision. However, the statistical
process that represents the turbulence driver can still generate a ﬁnite mean velocity component. The weak mean velocity ﬁeld
is thus removed at each call to the forcing procedure. This is important in the presence of magnetic ﬁelds as those break the
Galilean invariance valid in purely hydrodynamic turbulence.

a domain discretized by 𝑁𝑥 × 𝑁𝑦 × 𝑁𝑧 cells in the 𝐱 -, 𝐲 - and 𝐳 -directions, a run using 𝑐𝑥 × 𝑐𝑦 × 𝑐𝑧 cores assigns a physical domain

As discussed in Appendix B, ADER methods present by construction important advantages: the use of more element-local operations
allows a better use of the caches and limits the amount of MPI communications drastically. At very high discretization order, however,
it is not clear whether ADER methods or the one presented here would be more eﬃcient, since ADER methods require reconstructions
and evaluations in the volume of the computational cells as well.

the physical domain. Nevertheless, in order to keep high order of accuracy, the other algorithmic steps (reconstruction and passage
through point values) need to be modiﬁed, as well. For example, at the boundary, the WENO method of order 𝑛 + 1 requires 𝑚 = 𝑛∕2
values that are outside of the physical domain. A possibility would be to use increasingly directionally-biased stencils (e.g. suﬃciently
far from the boundaries, a WENO reconstruction of order 5 along 𝐱 requires the cells (𝑖 ± 𝛼, 𝑗, 𝑘) with 𝛼 ∈ {−2, … , 2}, but at the ﬁrst

variables, absorbing layers, grid stretching etc., we refer to the literature [19,61,62,24].
3.8. Numerical method: summary
To summarize, the key points of the presented methods are the following:
• The use of one-dimensional WENO reconstruction, which is computationally more aﬀordable than multidimensional versions,
especially with increasing order of accuracy.
• Each cell requires one reconstruction per dimension, evaluated at each face, independently of the scheme’s order. The already
computed area-averages are transformed into point values in the middle of each face up to arbitrarily high order of accuracy.
This leads to a signiﬁcant gain of computing eﬃciency, as compared to standard dimension-by-dimension WENO schemes (see
Appendix B).
• The viscous and resistive terms are formulated and computed in a way respecting the ﬁnite-volume and constrained-transport
frameworks as well as the scheme’s order.
• The internal energy sink and the forcing terms require point value↔volume average transformations, which can be derived to
an arbitrarily high order of accuracy.
• High-order strong stability preserving explicit time integrators are available in the literature. For order strictly greater than four,
these are multi-step methods.
4. Numerical tests
Numerical solvers of order 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 are considered. They are denoted by S2, S4, S6, S8 and S10 and use the method of
the corresponding order for the magnetic ﬁeld volume interpolation (section 3.1.1), the passage through point values (sections 3.2.2
and 3.5.3) and the time integration (section 3.4). The employed WENO schemes (section 3.1.2) are of the next closest odd-order,
that is 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11, respectively. Unless stated otherwise, we use the commonly-chosen parameters (𝑝𝐼𝑆 = 2, 𝜖𝐼𝑆 = 10−6 ) for the
computation of the WENO weights (eq. (28)).
The tests are compiled with the MPI Intel Fortran compiler, with optimization options -O3 -ip -xCORE-AVX512 and run on Xeon

The approach presented in this work is validated in three steps:
1. a convergence test of the ideal MHD equations (𝜇 = 𝜂 = 𝑆𝑒 = 0) through the nonlinear 3D MHD vortex problem (section 4.1),
2. a convergence test for the viscous and resistive terms (section 4.2),
3. a MHD blast-wave, showing the shock-capturing ability of the schemes (section 4.3).

4.3. Validation of shock-capturing ability: 2D MHD blast-wave
The blast-wave test is used to demonstrate the ability of a numerical scheme to handle strong MHD shock waves. √
We consider
the same initial conditions as in [20,66]: a ﬂuid at rest (𝜌 = 1, 𝐯 = 0) with a constant magnetic ﬁeld (𝑏𝑥 = 𝑏𝑦 = 𝑏𝑧 = 2∕2) at an
ambient
√ pressure of 𝑝 = 0.1, in the 2D periodic domain [−0.5, 0.5] × [−0.5, 0.5], discretized in 600 × 600 cells. In the center, for
𝑟 = 𝑥2 + 𝑦2 < 𝑟0 = 0.1, the pressure is 𝑝 = 100. The adiabatic index is 𝛾 = 5∕3 and 𝜇 = 𝜂 = 0.
Fig. 12 shows the mass density at the instant 𝑡 = 0.5. With increasing order, the weaker numerical dissipation leads to a better
resolution of the discontinuities (see subﬁgure (𝑐), especially the inset which shows that the S10 scheme requires about 3 cells to
resolve a certain jump whereas the S2 one requires ∼ 10 cells).
Higher-order reconstruction, however, often results in larger overshoots, undershoots, and oscillations, as well. Thus, in the vicinity
of strong shocks, high-order reconstructions can lead to unphysical states, such as a negative mass density or a negative pressure.
For the test problem presented here, a tiny amount of reconstructions and line-to-point transformations leads to unphysical states for
the S10 scheme. This happens essentially at the very beginning, before 𝑡 ≈ 0.02, with a peak at 𝑡 ≈ 0.0015 at which the mechanism
described below is applied in about one thousandth of the cells.
We make use here of a fallback procedure: if the reconstruction or the passage through point values leads to unphysical values,
then the order of the method is reduced locally until a physical state is reached. A negative density or pressure may appear as a result
of the volume-to-area reconstruction procedure, or the area-to-point value transformation. Thus, after the WENO block of the HBreco
module (see Fig. 2), a positivity check is performed. The area-average of the pressure is estimated in a second-order accurate way,
combining the area-averages of the reconstructed quantities. If, on a certain cell boundary, the area-averaged mass density and/or the
(estimated) pressure is negative, all variables on that face are reconstructed at successively lower order, until both the mass density
and the pressure estimate are positive (e.g., when using the S10 scheme with 11th order WENO, the reconstruction is redone at 9th
order, then 7th order, ... until ﬁrst order, setting the area average equal to the volume average).
Similarly, a positivity check is performed after the AtoP block of the HBflux module (see Fig. 3). Should the mass density or
the pressure be negative for a point, the area-to-point transformation is redone at successively lower-order until the computation of
a physical state.
This strategy is enough for the 2D blast-wave problem presented here, showing that the high-order methods presented in this
work are, in principle, able to cope with shocks. In more stringent situations, this simple approach will not suﬃce. Since this work
aims at giving a ready-to-use presentation of an eﬃcient class of WENO schemes at high-order, we do not consider in more details the
vast ﬁeld concerned with improving the stability in strongly shocked systems. Here are, nonetheless, some possibilities to improve
the robustness of compressible MHD simulations:
• the use of a ﬂattener [18,5,59]: close to strong gradients, the scheme’s order is reduced locally, taking a weighted average of
a higher-order and a lower-order method (the name “ﬂattening” comes from the use of the ﬁrst-order approximation as the
lower-order reconstruction),
• the use of WENO schemes which automatically adapt their order in the vicinity of strong discontinuities [10],
• the Multi-dimensional Optimal Order Detection (MOOD) method [17], which reduces the order locally such that the solution
preserves a discrete maximum principle (the value at a cell cannot become larger – resp. smaller – than the maximum – resp.
minimum – of the neighboring cells). This method is, contrary to the two above ones, an a-posteriori method: the cells for which

4.4. Kelvin-Helmholtz instability
The Kelvin-Helmholtz instability appears in systems with a velocity shear. When the viscosity is suﬃciently small, perturbations
are ampliﬁed and lead the system to a turbulent state. We consider here a 2D hydrodynamic adiabatic (𝛾 = 5∕3) system in the periodic
domain [−0.5, 0.5] × [−0.5 × 0.5] at resolution 600 × 600. Initially, the pressure is constant, 𝑝 = 1∕𝛾 . In the middle, for −0.25 < 𝑥 < 0.25,
the velocity is 𝑣𝑦 = 0.1 and the mass density 𝜌 = 1. In the rest of the domain, the velocity is opposite: 𝑣𝑦 = −0.1 and the mass density
𝜌 = 2. The instability is seeded by adding in each cell, to both 𝐯𝑥 and 𝐯𝑦 , a uniformly distributed random number with peak-to-peak
amplitude 10−4 . For the diﬀerent schemes presented here, the random numbers drawn are the same, so that the initial conditions are
identical.
Fig. 13 presents the mass density at two diﬀerent instants in time for the S2, S4 and S10 schemes, in the absence of physical
viscosity. The higher the scheme’s order, the faster the instability develops into a turbulent state, and the more small scale fractal-like
vortices are visible.
Although numerical dissipation resembles physical dissipation in some ways (e.g. it is linked to the loss of information close to
the grid size and is hence typically dominant at small scales), it generally has a diﬀerent spectral signature that can lead to diﬀerent
dynamics, e.g. regarding nonlinear structure formation and the general evolution of complex ﬂows. This is illustrated in Fig. 14, which
starts with the same initial conditions but uses a ﬁnite physical viscosity 𝜇 ∈ {4 × 10−5 , 2 × 10−5 }. At the higher 4 × 10−5 viscosity, both
solutions with S4 and S10 look very similar. The vortices are however signiﬁcantly sharper with the tenth-order scheme, showing
that the higher numerical viscosity of scheme S4 smears out the vortices. For the S2 scheme, even though this physical viscosity is
above the numerical one (since the perturbations are clearly more damped than in subﬁgure (𝑑) with lower viscosity or Fig. 13),
vortices are not visible.

4.5.2. Hydrodynamic statistically stationary state
In this section, the same hydrodynamic setting as in section 4.5.1 is considered, using the internal energy sink. The turbulent
statistically stationary states obtained by schemes of diﬀerent orders and at diﬀerent resolutions are compared with one another.
We are concerned with ﬁve aspects:
First, we present kinetic energy slices and Fourier spectra using the LLF Riemann solver, with the typical choice for the WENO
weights (𝑝𝐼𝑆 = 2, 𝜖𝐼𝑆 = 10−6 ) in eq. (28), and without physical viscosity. Indeed, setting 𝜇 = 0 allows to estimate the “numerical
viscosity”, and hence ﬁnd a lower bound of achievable physical viscosity for a given numerical setting. Second, to estimate this lower
bound, we present the “eﬀective viscosity” of a scheme as an estimate of the numerical viscosity and measure it for the diﬀerent
schemes at diﬀerent resolutions, comparing as well the LLF with the HLLC Riemann solver. Third, based on the measured eﬀective
viscosity, we estimate the overall numerical cost of each scheme for the simulation of fully developed turbulence and deduce which
scheme’s order is optimal with our given computing architecture. Fourth, we address shortly the question of the attainability of
this lower bound, introducing low physical viscosity. Fifth, and ﬁnally, we vary the free parameter 𝑝𝐼𝑆 and show its impact on the
eﬀective viscosity.
(𝑖) Kinetic energy slices and Fourier spectra using LLF
Fig. 16 compares slices of the kinetic energy, obtained by the schemes S2, S4 and S10 using the LLF Riemann solver. The fourthorder scheme displays signiﬁcantly ﬁner structures as compared to the second-order one, but not nearly as ﬁne as the tenth-order

When using other reconstruction methods with the same order of accuracy, or optimizing the reconstruction parameters, one

suﬃciently smooth problems, one can economize on computing resources by both reducing the number of ﬂoating-point operations
(no need to compute smoothness indicators) and the numerical dissipation.

4.6. Exponential separation of magnetic ﬁeld lines
Higher-order methods can be very beneﬁcial for investigations of magnetic reconnection as well, a fundamental physical process
by which magnetic ﬁeld lines undergo changes in their connectivity.
The behavior of magnetic ﬁelds is fundamentally diﬀerent in 2D and 3D, the former being far more geometrically constrained.
In an ideal evolution, 3D magnetic ﬁelds have the tendency to develop into states of high entanglement, in which ﬁeld lines that are
inﬁnitesimally close at one point become exponentially separated when following them. This behavior is generic and can be derived
from Maxwell’s equations [12].
This has implications for magnetic reconnection in plasmas, i.e. ionized gases, with low resistivity, encountered, e.g., in astrophysical settings or experiments for magnetically-conﬁned thermonuclear fusion, where highly entangled magnetic ﬁelds become
extremely sensitive to even the smallest resistive eﬀects. Indeed, magnetic ﬁeld lines that are very distant at some place in the plasma
can come exponentially close to each other at another location, so that inﬁnitesimally small non-ideal eﬀects can trigger magnetic reconnection and thus give rise to large-scale changes in the magnetic connectivity. This mechanism forms the basis of a topology-driven
magnetic reconnection model [14], and also plays an important role in turbulent reconnection models [40].
The degree of entanglement can be quantiﬁed by the separation of neighboring ﬁeld lines 𝜹(𝑙) along the length of the ﬁeld line 𝑙
with an initial separation of 𝜹0 . As it can vary over several orders of magnitude along the ﬁeld lines, it is convenient to express it in
an exponential form,

5. Conclusion
This work presents a computationally eﬃcient method to implement high-order ﬁnite-volume compressible MHD solvers using
the constrained-transport approach to keep the magnetic ﬁeld solenoidal. This method does not require many algorithmic changes
when compared to second-order numerics: the reconstruction and evaluations occur at the same spatial locations, independently of
the scheme’s order and the required passage through point values can be added seamlessly in the algorithmic chain. Since the amount
of reconstruction and evaluations is independent of the scheme’s order, this method is more eﬃcient than commonly used methods,
which require more quadrature points with increasing order of accuracy. We supply the required explicit formulas up to tenth order
of accuracy.
The consistent inclusion of viscous and resistive terms respecting the ﬁnite-volume and constrained-transport formalism and
preserving the order of accuracy has been shown. The usage of volume-average↔point value transformations allows to handle cooling
functions and forcing terms in a high-order manner as well.
The numerical method is validated by several tests: proper convergence through the advection of a 3D MHD vortex and the
resistive/viscous dissipation of Fourier modes, and shock-capturing ability through a MHD blast-wave. While the increase in computational cost with increasing order is quite modest (about a factor 3 for a right-hand-side computation when moving from second
order to tenth order on the computing architecture we used), the numerical dissipation decreases strongly. For smooth problems,
such as the 3D MHD vortex, this can mean gains in computing eﬃciency of one or two orders of magnitude. For turbulent systems,
an order of magnitude can be expected. Extending existing codes to higher-order can thus be done in a rather straightforward way,
and doing so can signiﬁcantly reduce computing time for given accuracy requirements.
Finally, we remark that the above-mentioned approach of improving numerical eﬃciency by using higher-order schemes at lower
spatio-temporal resolution has to be carried out judiciously, keeping in mind the pertinent physical properties of the system under
consideration. Indeed, even a very high order reconstruction polynomial and a perfect Riemann solver can not recreate the information
lost by coarsening the grid to scales larger than the characteristic distance inherent to a physical process. However, if carried out
appropriately, the present approach allows to minimize the numerical dissipation due to amplitude errors, while having a rather
ﬁne control over the level of small-scale homogenization (i.e. information lost at the grid-cell level). Thus, this kind of numerical
improvements is potentially most attractive for physical systems whose dynamics proceed from larger towards smaller spatial scales,
e.g. turbulent ﬂows.

Appendix B. Computational eﬃciency
The method presented in this work makes use of area-average↔point values transformations based on a Taylor expansion (in the
following TE-WENO), which reuses already reconstructed area averages. This leads to a signiﬁcant gain in computational eﬃciency,
35
Journal of Computational Physics 515 (2024) 113287
J.-M. Teissier, R. Mäusle and W.-C. Müller
as compared to “standard (dimension-by-dimension) WENO” (henceforth STD-WENO) schemes using quadrature rules at the cell
boundaries to compute the ﬂuxes. Another class of eﬃcient methods for solving MHD problems, which do not require SSPRK time
integrators, are the ADER-WENO schemes [8,68,23,21], where ADER stands for “arbitrary high order derivatives”. These methods
exploit approximate solutions of a generalized Riemann problem, so that high order of accuracy in space and time is achieved in a
single step. In Appendix B.1, we compare the asymptotic complexity in terms of the scheme’s order of these three methods: STDWENO, TE-WENO and ADER-WENO.
The asymptotic complexity is however only of limited use in estimating realistic computing time for the orders of accuracy
presented here. Thus, Appendix B.2 presents an estimate of the ﬂoating point operations’ repartition among the diﬀerent algorithmic
steps and suggests a possibility to increase computational eﬃciency further.
On modern supercomputers using many cores in parallel, software (e.g. vectorization, pipelining and other optimizations realized
through the compiler) and hardware optimizations (e.g. size of the diﬀerent caches in the cores, way the communication between
processors is done) play a major role. Estimating the true wall-clock time is thus very challenging. For example, ADER schemes can be
designed so that the cache is used very eﬃciently, and, in parallel implementations, communications between processors are reduced
to a minimum [23]. Thus, additional and concluding remarks are given in Appendix B.3.
B.1. Asymptotic scaling of the number of operations
There are many diﬀerent ﬂavors of WENO schemes and of ADER schemes. In order to compare STD-WENO, TE-WENO and ADERWENO, we set a common ground by considering schemes similar to those described in section 4: the schemes are of even order
𝑛 = 2𝑚, using a (𝑛 + 1)𝑡ℎ order WENO reconstruction, and solve the 3D ideal adiabatic MHD equations (with 𝜇 = 𝜂 = 0). We assume
the same order of accuracy in space and time. The solenoidality of the magnetic ﬁeld is maintained through a constrained-transport
formulation.
For the STD-WENO and ADER-WENO schemes, the quadratures are done using the roots of the Gauss-Legendre polynomials.
Gauss-Legendre quadrature is indeed robust and allows an error term ∼ Δ𝑥𝑛 when using 𝑚 = 𝑛∕2 points per dimension. This is
signiﬁcantly more eﬃcient than using a Newton-Cotes quadrature rule, for which 𝑛 − 1 or 𝑛 − 2 points would be needed, and for
which higher-order approximations are subject to catastrophic cancellation eﬀects.
The time-stepping for TE-WENO and STD-WENO is done using a SSPRK time-integration method with 𝑠 stages, as described in
section 3.4.
In this subsection, we look at the asymptotic scaling for a large scheme’s order 𝑛 ≫ 1 and consider thus only leading order terms.
Let’s ﬁrst estimate the cost of the operations that are common for all three types of schemes:
• the cost of ﬁnding the reconstructing polynomial goes as ∼ 𝑛3 . Indeed, the most expensive operation is the computation of the
≈ 𝑛∕2 smoothness indicators, each involving a sum with ∼ 𝑛∕2 terms that evaluate polynomials of degree ∼ 𝑛∕2 (see eq. (29)).
• evaluating the reconstruction polynomial at a point goes as ∼ 𝑛2 . Indeed, each ≈ 𝑛∕2 weight is associated with a linear combination of the ≈ 𝑛∕2 terms present in each substencil.
• for HLL-type Riemann solvers (such as the Rusanov one), the cost of the evaluation of the ﬂuxes can be made independent of the
number of quadrature points by taking an average (cf [8]).
For TE-WENO, the cost of area-average↔point value transformations goes as ∼ 𝑛2 (see section 3.2.2). In the same way, the cost
of 2D quadrature rules goes as ∼ 𝑛2 .
Based on the above estimates, the (asymptotic) cost for one time-step of the STD-WENO, TE-WENO or ADER-WENO schemes is
derived in the three following subsections. They are then compared to each other in Appendix B.1.4.
B.1.1. STD-WENO: asymptotic scaling
For a STD-WENO scheme, in each cell (𝑖, 𝑗, 𝑘), the following is done, for each dimension:
1. One volume-to-area average reconstruction along, say, 𝐱 , and evaluated at two coordinates: (𝑥𝑖 ± Δ𝑥∕2, 𝑦𝑗 , 𝑧𝑘 ).

B.1.3. ADER-WENO: asymptotic scaling
As described in [68], ADER schemes were originally introduced by [64]. They use an approximate solution of a generalized
Riemann problem at cell boundaries, so that they do not need intermediate Runge-Kutta stages. A more eﬃcient reformulation [21],
based on a weak formulation of the partial diﬀerential equations, is at the core of modern ADER methods [8,68,22]. There are many
diﬀerent types of ADER methods, and we do not enter into the details here.
To estimate their asymptotic scaling, we assume a method similar to, e.g., the ones in [22,8], but without using adaptive mesh
reﬁnement, and using the WENO method described above. It can be described in three steps [8,68]:
1. a reconstruction of the points inside each cell (e.g. at the ∼ 𝑛3 Gauss-Legendre points in the volume),
2. a local space-time predictor that describes the temporal evolution inside a cell in response to the spatial derivatives inside that
same cell. In a certain sense, a space-time reconstruction is performed.
3. a corrector step, where the ﬂuxes from neighboring cells are considered.
We assume that the evaluation of the variables at the Gauss-Legendre points is done through the following steps [22]:
1. One volume-to-area average reconstruction along, say, 𝐱 , and evaluated at the 𝑚 Gauss-Legendre points (𝑥𝑙 ) coordinates.
2. At each (𝑥𝑙 , 𝑦𝑗 , 𝑧𝑘 ), one area-to-line average reconstruction, e.g. along 𝐲 , evaluated at the 𝑚 Gauss-Legendre points (𝑦𝑛 ).
3. At each (𝑥𝑙 , 𝑦𝑛 , 𝑧𝑘 ), one reconstruction along the third direction, e.g. 𝐳 , evaluated at the 𝑚 Gauss-Legendre points (𝑧𝑞 ).
Thus, ∼ 𝑛2 1D-reconstructions and ∼ 𝑛3 evaluations take place, giving ∼ 𝑛5 operations.
The predictor step is done in an iterative way. We do not expect it to cost more than the reconstruction step in ∼ 𝑛5 . As mentioned
in [22], the matrices involved are very sparse and the number of iterations is limited by ∼ 𝑛, with methods to ﬁnd good initial guesses
giving faster convergence [23].
As for the corrector step, it consists of few steps along the time axis [68] (possibly even only one [8]) and does not impact this
scaling.
Hence, the overall scaling for the computation of one time-step goes as:

B.1.4. Comparing the three types of schemes
The above estimates are admittedly of limited applicability (see Appendix B.3) but they permit to make the following points:
• Because their amount of reconstructions and evaluations is independent of the scheme’s order, TE-WENO schemes are always
more eﬃcient than STD-WENO ones.
• At relatively low order of accuracy, ADER-WENO schemes are more eﬃcient than STD-WENO ones. This is because they do
not require several RHS evaluations, but are done in one step as ∼ 𝑛5 , whereas STD-WENO schemes scale as ∼ 𝑛4 𝑠, with 𝑠 the
number of stages in the SSPRK time integrator. Thus, if high-order SSPRK methods can be found with an amount of stages that
is independent of the order of accuracy (e.g. the methods considered here have 𝑠 = 10 up to order 8), there should be an order
of accuracy at which STD-WENO schemes start to be more eﬃcient than ADER-WENO ones – when restraining ourselves solely
to asymptotic scaling considerations.
However, as shown in the next subsection, lower-order terms (in powers of 𝑛) still have a strong weight on the total number
of operations. Furthermore, and more importantly in practical situations: in ADER-WENO schemes, the communication between
processors can be strongly reduced, and many operations can be done element-local wise. This means that ADER-WENO schemes
may use the available hardware more eﬃciently as compared to STD-WENO and TE-WENO schemes, see Appendix B.3.

B.3. Additional and concluding remarks
So far, we considered only the cost of a single time-step, based on the amount of operations. Nevertheless, other aspects play
important, possibly dominant, roles:
• In ADER-WENO schemes, a time accuracy of order 𝑛 requires a Courant number smaller than 1∕(2𝑛 + 1), see [23,21]. When using
a SSPRK time integrator, the stable Courant number decreases with increasing order as well, but it is not clear which type of
schemes would be more advantageous, overall.
• Methods relying on a SSPRK time integrator require communication between processors at each stage. For the TE-WENO method
presented here, the scheme of order 𝑛 requires 𝑛∕2 ghost-cells in each dimension for the reconstruction procedure, and about the
same amount for the passage through point values, also from diagonally-located processors for 𝑛 > 4. ADER-WENO schemes, on
the contrary, can be constructed in a “communication-avoiding” manner [23], since the local space-time predictor does not re38
Journal of Computational Physics 515 (2024) 113287
J.-M. Teissier, R. Mäusle and W.-C. Müller
quire any information from other cells. The few necessary communications can be done using non-blocking MPI communications,
virtually “hiding” their cost.
• More importantly: ADER-WENO schemes, at suﬃciently low order for a given hardware, are “cache-blocking”. Since all the
information needs to be loaded only once per cell and time-step, the time evolution of one cell can possibly be computed relying
only on the L3 and L2 caches of the CPUs [23], before computing the time evolution of the next cell. This is in contrast with
the TE-WENO presented here, that requires computations in the whole domain associated with a CPU before moving to the
next Runge-Kutta stage. The fact that the L3 and L2 caches are not used optimally for the TE-WENO method is illustrated when
comparing Tables 4 and B.8. One reason why the cost for one step is not proportional to the amount of ﬂoating-point operations
is because the caches cannot store that much information at once.
To conclude, this appendix discusses the computational advantage of TE-WENO as compared to STD-WENO. The question whether
ADER-WENO or TE-WENO would be more eﬃcient depends not only on the scheme’s order and the implementation details, but also
heavily on compilation and hardware details. At suﬃciently high orders of accuracy, one might expect that TE-WENO would perform
faster, but concrete measurements are needed to ﬁnd the spot at which one would be more eﬃcient than the other, as a function,
among others, of the cache sizes (cf [23]).
