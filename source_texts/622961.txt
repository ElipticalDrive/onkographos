Motivation
The current ray tracing framework accurately models geometric optics but does not account for wave-optical phenomena such as diffraction and interference. These effects become increasingly important in high-energy laser systems, especially in the presence of finite apertures, beam shaping elements, focal optics, and partially coherent beams.
The Wigner Distribution Function (WDF) provides a phase-space representation of optical fields that naturally bridges geometric and wave optics. Under linear optical systems, the Wigner function is transported along Hamiltonian ray trajectories, making it possible to incorporate diffraction effects into a ray tracing framework without resorting to full field propagation after every optical element.
This could enable OPOSSUM to simulate long and complex laser systems while retaining essential wave-optical effects.
Objectives
Investigate the feasibility of extending OPOSSUM's ray tracing engine with a Wigner function based representation of optical fields.
The proposed workflow is:
Generate the Wigner distribution from an input optical field.
Sample the Wigner distribution into a collection of weighted rays.
Propagate these rays using the existing Hamiltonian ray tracing engine.
Reconstruct physical observables (e.g. intensity, angular spectrum, beam quality) directly from the propagated ray ensemble.
Compare the approach against conventional FFT-based wave propagation.
Proposed Ray Representation
Each ray may contain:
Position
Direction / wavevector
Weight (positive or negative)
Wavelength or frequency
Polarization
Arrival time (for ultrashort pulses)
The ray weights represent samples of the Wigner distribution and therefore naturally encode diffraction and interference effects.
Potential Advantages
Unified framework combining geometric and wave optics.
Reuse of the existing ray tracing infrastructure.
Efficient propagation through large optical systems.
Natural extension to partially coherent beams.
Straightforward support for wavelength-dependent effects and ultrashort pulse propagation.
Possibility to include gain media, apertures, lenses, mirrors, gratings, and other optical components within a common phase-space framework.
Open Questions
How should the initial Wigner distribution be sampled efficiently?
How should positive and negative ray weights be represented numerically?
Which reconstruction method provides the best compromise between accuracy and performance?
How should optical elements modify the Wigner distribution?
How can adaptive or importance sampling reduce the number of required rays?
How does this approach compare with Gaussian beam decomposition, coherent mode decomposition, and FFT-based propagation?
Possible Future Extensions
Adaptive phase-space sampling.
Support for partially coherent sources.
Frequency-dependent propagation and material dispersion.
Time-frequency Wigner distributions for ultrashort pulse simulations.
Gain saturation and nonlinear optical interactions directly within the ray framework.
References
Mout et al., Ray Tracing the Wigner Distribution Function for Optical Simulations.
Alonso, Wigner Functions in Optics: Describing Beams as Ray Bundles and Pulses as Particle Ensembles.
Tappert, Diffractive Ray Tracing of Laser Beams.
