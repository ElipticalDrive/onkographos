Abstract
We have applied high-quality medical imaging techniques to study the structure of the human ankle. Direct volume rendering, using specific algorithms, transforms conventional two-dimensional (2D) magnetic resonance image (MRI) series into 3D volume datasets. This tool allows high-definition visualization of single or multiple structures for diagnostic, research, and teaching purposes. No other image reformatting technique so accurately highlights each anatomic relationship and preserves soft tissue definition. Here, we used this method to study the structure of the human ankle to analyze tendon–bone–muscle relationships. We compared ankle MRI and computerized tomography (CT) images from 17 healthy volunteers, aged 18–30 years (mean 23 years). An additional subject had a partial rupture of the Achilles tendon. The MRI images demonstrated superiority in overall quality of detail compared to the CT images. The MRI series accurately rendered soft tissue and bone in simultaneous image acquisition, whereas CT required several window-reformatting algorithms, with loss of image data quality. We obtained high-quality digital images of the human ankle that were sufficiently accurate for surgical and clinical intervention planning, as well as for teaching human anatomy. Our approach demonstrates that complex anatomical structures such as the ankle, which is rich in articular facets and ligaments, can be easily studied non-invasively using MRI data.
Keywords: ankle, MRI, volume rendering
Introduction
Representing and reconstructing surfaces in three dimensions (3D) have received enormous attention in computer vision research over the past two decades. Significant progress has been made in rendering three-dimensional (3D) objects, expanding the perspective of classical imaging (Jastrow & Vollrath, 2003; Perrone et al. 2003). In particular, 3D digital image processing has significantly advanced the study of human anatomy, producing realistic, interactive features, especially relative to gross anatomy (Trelease et al. 2000; Reidenberg & Laitman, 2002; Cheung et al. 2005; Hayakawa et al. 2007).
Two-dimensional (2D) methods based on X-ray absorption alone do not completely reveal all the complex non-calcified tissues, such as tendons, ligaments, adipose tissue, and cartilage. This limitation is due to the low X-ray absorptive capability of these tissues (Staren, 1980), and their study requires use of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computerized tomography (CT) (Rosenberg et al. 1988). However, routine axial CT scans do not highlight regions of complex anatomy, such as articular facets of the joints or muscle insertions. Therefore, two distinct 3D rendering techniques have been developed to study human anatomy, each with its own unique advantages and disadvantages with respect to image quality (Banerjee et al. 2008; Hua et al. 2008). These techniques have yielded encouraging results, but have several limitations in final image rendering. The first method, shaded surface display (SSD), cannot produce high-quality digital images when wide gaps exist between similar tissues, such as displaced bone fragments in a fracture (Choplin et al. 2004). This may result in image artefacts when the image windows are manipulated (Calhoun et al. 1999). The second, maximum intensity projection (MIP), renders only the maximum attenuation when bones are present, and thus does not allow display of soft tissues. As it is not possible to switch shading on and off, obtained images do not provide in-depth information unless stereo-images are generated (Choplin et al. 2004).
We have developed new technologies and proprietary adjustments of previous 3D reconstruction techniques (Lukas et al. 2004). In a preliminary MRI study on the basal ganglia, we compared manual volumetry based on MRI to that based on non-uniform rational B-spline (NURBS) surfaces. The latter method is considered to be good for quantitative volumetry, offering excellent accuracy and reproducibility of brain measurements (Anastasi et al. 2006). It also provides the flexibility to design several shapes that are widely used in 3D computer graphics (Segars et al. 2004; Anastasi et al. 2006; Silén et al. 2008).
However, segmentation techniques are limited by the loss of digital image integrity after manual manipulation. The signal derived from a manipulated dataset is compromised by data compression and user error, potentially resulting in significant image artefacts. Three-dimensional volume rendering avoids these limitations, producing more faithful representations and deeper views of gray matter structures (Kuszyk et al. 1996; Udupa, 1999). Structures unable to be visualized by other techniques or unclear after resin filling are also better reproduced with 3D volume rendering (Udupa, 1999). For instance, ventricular cerebrospinal fluid obstructs image acquisition and subsequent 3D elaboration in conventional imaging. In contrast, T2 MRI allows the study of ventricular content by 3D rendering analysis. As our previous segmentation techniques did not allow single nuclei highlighting, we searched for a new visualization approach for the central nervous system. Three-dimensional volume rendering better highlighted gray and white matter such as the basal ganglia and cerebral cortex.
Following our neuroanatomical research, we studied the knee joint (Anastasi et al. 2007), which has tissues of differing density. Thus, evaluation of the knee structure is typically performed by MRI for soft tissues and CT for bones (Fishman et al. 1987). Applying an MRI 3D volume rendering method, we simultaneously imaged all the anatomical structures. By assigning a definite tissue type (e.g. ligament) to a specific range of MRI frequencies, selection of the regions or single structure of interest was achieved by simply selecting or deselecting the frequency ranges assigned to tissue types.
We sought to continue our study of anatomical regions to demonstrate the efficacy of our techniques. In the present study, we analyzed the ankle morphology in healthy subjects and one pathological patient. The ankle region is highly complex due to an abundance of connective tissue and articulation. We quantified the relevant structures on MRI volume images and developed specialized opacity and contrast algorithms for high-quality rendering of muscle–tendon–bone relationships. The efficacy of our method was evaluated by study of a semi-rupture of the Achilles tendon. As a comparison, we used CT rendering to evaluate bony structures.
Materials and methods
Seventeen healthy volunteers (age range: 18–30 years, 10 male, 7 female) underwent 1.2T MRI of the ankle in a Sonata Magnet (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Additionally, a 24-year-old male athlete with a semi-rupture of the Achilles tendon underwent both MRI and CT imaging. All subjects gave written informed consent before beginning the study.
MRI technique
Three-dimensional double echo steady state (DESS) sequences were used for cartilage examination. Sagittal, coronal, transverse, and oblique coronal multiplanar reconstruction images were reformatted from MRI, acquired by one stack of 1.50-mm slices. Each sequence was characterized by a TR of 17.81 ms, a TE of 5.11 ms, flip angle of 25%, slice thickness of 1.5 mm, voxel size of 0.6 × 0.6 × 1.5 mm, and a 20% distance factor.
For other images of the ankle, we used turbospin-echo images (TSE-T2) weighted in 512 dpi. These images were characterized by an echo train length of 11, section thickness of 1.5 mm, field of view of 12.75 cm, image matrix of 384 × 384 pixels, bandwidth of 75.8 kHz, section-selection and refocusing-pulse durations of 2 ms, signal acquisition of 2, and a total acquisition time of 21 min.
Digital MRI data were acquired in pixel resolutions of either 256 × 256 or 512 × 512 in 16 bits of gray and converted to 8 bits of gray TIFF files.
Normal MRI DICOM files must be purified by filtering systems to obtain the final high-quality images. Both DESS and TSE-T2 acquisition techniques have been used with good post-processing results, where the exact rendering thresholds are chosen so as to avoid artefacts. The output of the MRI equipment is digital files (DICOM, JPEG, TIFF, etc.). All DICOM images allow inclusion of patient data, anatomical report data, and procedures in the image header.
CT technique
We used a four-channel CT scanner (Mx8000 Quad, Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with the following scanning and reconstruction parameters: peak of 140 kV and 150–200 mA, ultrahigh resolution, beam collimation of 2 × 0.5 mm, effective section width of 0.6 mm, nominal section width of 0.5 mm, reconstruction increment of 0.2 mm, and pitch of 0.825–0.925. The section thickness and reconstruction intervals were selected to produce data with nearly isotropic voxels.
Rendering technique
For the rendering process, we used volview 2.0 graphic software (Kitware Inc., Clifton Park, NY, USA). This volume rendering system enables visualization of data from various imaging modalities (e.g. CT, MRI, functional MRI and confocal microscopy) interfaced with various hardware and software systems [e.g. accelerated OpenGL texturing, RTViz VolumePro 500 (TeraRecon, Inc., Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories, Concord, MA, USA)], volume rendering engine [hardware with real-time 3D volume rendering], and multi-threaded parallel rendering support). It also handles various data file formats (e.g. TIFF and basic DICOM). Multiple image analysis capabilities process and visualize large files of voxel-volume data.
Volume management processing relates image acquisition, resampling, and editing. Acquisition quality is correlated to newer MRI and CT scanners, resulting in larger volumes of high resolution data. These high resolution data allow high-quality 3D rendering, achieving high-definition imaging.
The workstation automatically resamples the volume data by two separate operations. Axial sections are first scaled in the x and y axes, decreasing the volume dataset size (e.g. from 512 × 512 to 256 × 256 pixels). A second operation resamples in the z axis, calibrating in units similar to those of the x and y axes.
The other step of the algorithm involves distinction of the bones, ligaments, and muscles from the MRI image. The algorithm is based on region-growing mathematical morphology operators and anatomical knowledge. Editing is performed for each axial image, outlining the relevant structure and erasing the surrounding structures. Interactive manipulation of the 3D image at the workstation in real-time is also possible. This results in regions of displayed voxels, while unnecessary voxels are suppressed.
Results
Applying volume rendering techniques, we obtained images of the ankle articulation. Shading features were used to simulate an external light source. This was done to give additional depth cues and surface texture to the image, offering a more realistic view of the anatomic structures. In particular, in a lateral image of the right ankle it was possible to highlight structures constituting the subcutaneous stratum of the region surrounding the lateral malleolus, such as the tortuous superficial vessels and the lesser saphenous vein (Fig. 1a). Smaller veins draining into the above vessels and the dorsal venous arch (Fig. 1b) of the foot were also clearly depicted.

Application of our method to the MRI data of a 24-year-old athlete with a traumatic lesion of the Achilles tendon clearly demonstrated pathological changes, including tendon thickening with a partial tear (Fig. 7). Figure 8 shows an extracted image from the DESS sequence of the MRI analysis (0.7-mm slice thickness) in the sagittal section, highlighting the semi-rupture of the Achilles tendon. This image also shows the morphology of the articular cartilage and the soft tissues in a single section. With unmanipulated images, all other anatomical structures are shown but their morphology can only be appreciated in 2D, without depth perception. Use of 3D volume rendering on the MRI sequences clearly showed the tendon rupture in the 3D 360° view, providing further information to orthopaedic specialists. It was also possible to cinematically sequence through a stack of reconstructed images that highlight the articular cartilage as separate from the entire ankle structure (Video S3, published on http://ww2.unime.it/biomorfologiabiotecnologie/nuovo/rendering/Imaging5.html).

Discussion
Here we demonstrate an MRI-based 3D volume rendering technique that allows for high-quality visualization of the fine structural details of the ankle. We compared ankle MRI and CT images from 17 healthy volunteers and one pathological patient. These images are made available to specialists, students, and the general public via the internet for the first time (http://ww2.unime.it/biomorfologiabiotecnologie/nuovo/rendering/Imaging.html). The rendered images provided clearer and more concrete information compared with other 3D procedures, e.g. NURBS, SSD and MIP (Rubin et al. 1996). By varying the algorithms, our 3D volume rendering technique alternated the signal linked to the anatomical structures, such as the subcutaneous layer with its veins (Fig. 1), tendons and muscles (Figs 3 and 5), or articular cartilage (Video S1).
We previously conducted a study using CT followed by MRI acquisition and 3D elaboration which demonstrated that MRI is superior for soft tissues visualization and CT for bone visualization (Pelc & Beaulieu, 2001). However, as shown in the present study, our MRI rendering can simultaneously highlight muscles and bones, permitting analysis of muscle insertions and tendons and their connection to bones. Our results are similar to those of previous studies using volume rendering in three, four or five dimensions (Choplin et al. 2004; Lopes et al. 2008) evaluating separate anatomical parts, common for medical image data (Pfister & Kaufman, 1996; Yoshino et al. 2007; Xie et al. 2008). An important difference with multiplanar MRI is the ability to observe normal or pathological anatomical structures in a single acquisition. This provides 3D-reconstructed images without the use of serial 2D images, which are commonly employed by radiologists. Our method also allows immediate visualization of structures in any desired plane of vision or orientation by individually switching objects on and off (Video S1). Radiologists often find that the multiplanar images are most useful for diagnosing pathology, but clinicians tend to prefer 3D images as they are more familiar with the anatomical representation.
Our method diverges from the first 3D renderings of human structures, given the greater power and programmability of modern graphic processors and software that allow the operators to study objects in real-time (Rusinek et al. 1989). Special rendering (Pfister et al. 1999) or shared memory processor architectures (Lacroute, 1996) are necessary to achieve visualization speeds approaching real-time. However, for volumes characterized by differing densities, such as adjacent tissue structures, one can quickly render the data after transparency and color changes. Image editing steps require an average of 20–30 s, based on the typical processing time of datasets into memory with VolView graphic software.
A main source of error in volume rendering is partial volume averaging, which results when voxels inaccurately represent several adjacent tissue types as the average attenuation of all tissues present. Volume rendering is chosen for its ability to provide a qualitative feel for density changes in the data, preventing the need for classification or segmentation (Jacq & Roux, 1997). Volume rendering allows image rotation, enabling views from any desired perspective. Potentially more interesting is the ability to display multiple tissues and their interrelationships by varying algorithms, which is helpful in isolating and displaying specific structures from skin to articular cartilage.
Due to the long scan time and resulting noise and patient motion artefacts (Kornaat et al. 2005), we did not apply 3D spoiled gradient-recalled echo. We also did not use the signal to noise ratio (Penczek, 2002), because of its unacceptable fat suppression (Gold et al. 2006). The technique presented in this study offers better aspects and fewer limitations than other graphics software, some of which render surface reconstruction with speckle artefacts due to the digital information loss.
Using normalized cross-correlation algorithms, our procedure allowed us to highlight small specific structures better than any surface rendering or binary voxel technique. Both voxel opacity and color properties were shown for the tissues, highlighting anatomical morphology, tissue interrelationships, and pathological changes. Finally, switching off the bone and muscle signals and accentuating the cartilage signal permitted display of the exact morphology of all articular cartilages, in close contact with the articular surfaces. This allows evaluation of the articular structures in subjects with ankle distress or pathology. Our results also revealed the diagnostic efficacy of the technique, highlighting a tendon lesion initially observed as simply a laceration of the third distal portion of the Achilles tendon.
Our laboratory collaborates with the orthopaedics department of our university for bone, tendon and cartilage evaluation, permitting fine analysis of the pathological elements. As part of this collaboration, digital data processing will be used in surgical simulations and in virtual reality arthroscopy ankle simulation, assisting specialists in diagnostics and surgical operations (Bellon & Horwitz, 1992; Sha et al. 2001; Mabrey et al. 2002; Heng et al. 2004; Cannon et al. 2006). Further applications of rendering techniques could define structural characteristics on the stress distribution of the plantar foot surface (Cheung et al. 2005), or the approach for 3D visualization using high-resolution MRI of the temporomandibular joint (Hayakawa et al. 2007).
Computerized tomography rendering is considered a second choice to MRI rendering, as CT cannot be used to evaluate simultaneously the specific anatomical tissues of highly complex structures such as the ankle joint. We are modulating our algorithms to create software that highlights multiple structures of the human body in the musculoskeletal system. We have also created a virtual atlas that will be useful in the study of human anatomy, both for students and specialists, again demonstrating the flexibility of our 3D technique. Some of our images are available at: http://ww2.unime.it/biomorfologiabiotecnologie/nuovo/rendering/Imaging.html. We provide basic graphics software by varying our proprietary algorithms, underlining further capabilities of this new technique, and demonstrating that images of higher realism, detail and quality can be obtained without the limits common to the other graphic reconstruction techniques.
