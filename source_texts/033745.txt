Abstract
Deploying a 3D Gaussian Splatting scene on a phone, a headset or a web page
is governed by how many primitives the model contains, yet no current training
method lets a practitioner specify that number. Mask-based pruning, the leading in-training approach, controls it only indirectly through a constant sparsity
weight, so the delivered size emerges from that constant, the scene and the densification schedule together. Across thirteen standard scenes at the published
setting we measure final sizes spanning a factor of 7.8.
We replace the constant with a dual variable driven by the constraint that the
live population equal a budget supplied by the user. Plain dual ascent is not
sufficient, for a reason that has not previously been reported: although masked
primitives keep receiving gradients and are therefore assumed to be recoverable,
in practice they are not. With deletion disabled and the sparsity pressure released
to effectively zero, the live population ends 16,600 primitives below where it
started, recovering only about 6,500 against a deficit of 207,000. Masking is
a one-way door, so an overshooting controller can never recover, and we measure undershoots of up to 36%. Approaching the budget from above and landing
with a single terminal projection removes the failure entirely: one setting, applied
unchanged to all thirteen scenes at two seeds, delivers exactly the requested number of primitives every time. A paired comparison bounds the quality difference
to [−0.076, +0.085] dB while models are 2.2× smaller and 1.40× faster to
render.

1 Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting [1] represents a scene as an explicit set of anisotropic primitives
and rasterises them directly, reaching real-time frame rates at a quality comparable
to the best neural fields [2–4]. Its explicitness is also its cost. The adaptive density
control that grows the representation is driven by a photometric criterion that does
not know when to stop, and a converged model routinely contains several million
primitives occupying hundreds of megabytes.
That number is what decides whether the model can be used. A scene streamed to
a web client has a download budget, a scene shipped inside a mobile application has
a package-size limit, and a scene rendered on a standalone headset has a fixed videomemory allowance that it must share with the rest of the application. In each case
the engineer knows the budget before training begins and needs a model that meets
it. What current training procedures offer instead is a knob that correlates with size,
leaving the engineer to sweep it, retrain, and hope the result lands close enough —
per scene, because the same knob setting yields different sizes on different scenes.
A large body of work therefore removes primitives. Post-hoc methods score a
trained model and discard the low scorers [5–7]; compression methods quantise or reencode the surviving parameters [8–10]; and in-training methods fold the decision into
the optimisation itself. The most direct of these is mask-based pruning, exemplified by
MaskGaussian [11], which attaches a learnable inclusion probability to every primitive,
draws a hard sample through the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation [12, 13], and multiplies
that sample into the α-compositing weight inside a modified CUDA rasteriser. Because
the backward pass carries a gradient to the mask logit of every primitive, including
those the current sample switched off, the method can in principle revisit its own
decisions. A sparsity term λ m̄2 , with m̄ the mean of the sampled mask, supplies the
pressure that makes the model shrink.
The difficulty is what λ actually controls. It sets a rate of exchange between photometric error and primitive count, not a count. The count that results depends on
how steeply the photometric loss responds to removing a primitive, which varies enormously between a textured outdoor scene and a flat indoor wall, and on how many
primitives densification supplied in the first place. The published value is a compromise, and the original work reports three different settings for three different operating
points, each obtained by hand. Section 3 measures the consequence: at the published
setting, final model size across thirteen standard scenes spans a factor of 7.8. The
same experiment run on a scene with half the texture returns a model of a different
size, and there is no dial to turn.
This is the same structural problem that motivates quantile-based densification [14,
15], where a constant gradient threshold selects an uncontrolled and drifting fraction
of primitives to split. The remedy there is to replace the constant by a statistic of the
current distribution. The remedy here cannot be identical, because pruning removes
rather than adds, and removal in this setting turns out to be irreversible in a way that
shapes the whole design.
2
Masking is a one-way door.
The premise that a masked primitive remains recoverable is central to how MaskGaussian is motivated, and it is the reason the method is presented as making soft rather
than hard decisions. We test it directly. We disable deletion entirely, so that no primitive is ever removed from memory, and we drive the sparsity weight to zero once
the population has been over-pruned. If masked primitives were recoverable, the live
population should climb back. It does not. On playroom the floor engages at iteration 18,000 with 409,706 primitives live, and by iteration 30,000, with λ collapsed to
1.2 × 10−4 and 207,000 primitives sitting masked but alive, the live count stands at
393,077 — lower than when the floor engaged. It falls to a minimum of 386,584 and
then drifts back by 6,493, about 3% of the deficit. Once a mask logit has been pushed
negative the gradient reaching it is far too weak to pull it back within any realistic
schedule. Keeping the primitive in memory does not help, because it never switches
back on.
Contributions.
We make the model size an input to training rather than an outcome, and we do so
in a way that respects the asymmetry above.
• We quantify the control problem in mask-based pruning: at a single fixed λ, delivered
model size varies by 7.8× across thirteen scenes, with a coefficient of variation of
70.9% (Section 3).
• We show empirically that the mask is effectively irreversible, contradicting the
assumption under which soft masking is usually justified, and we show that this
is what makes a naive dual-ascent controller fail, by up to 36%, on exactly those
scenes where the population crosses the budget early (Section 3).
• We propose a controller that is correct under that asymmetry: multiplicative dual
ascent on a log-domain error with an asymmetric release, a setpoint held above the
budget so the population settles from the recoverable side, a deletion floor, and a
single terminal projection onto the budget (Section 4).
• We report that one setting, unchanged across thirteen scenes from three datasets,
delivers exactly the requested size in every scene and at every seed, with models
2.2× smaller than MaskGaussian and 5.0× smaller than 3D-GS. Repeating the
suite at a second seed bounds the paired quality difference against MaskGaussian
to [−0.076, +0.085] dB, an interval an order of magnitude narrower than the effect
a single run can fabricate (Section 5).
• We report two negative results that a controllable budget makes visible: weighting
the sparsity penalty by screen footprint and annealing the Gumbel temperature
both appear to help when model size is left free, and both collapse once size is held
fixed (Section 5.8).
We use tuning-free throughout in a specific and limited sense: no quantity is tuned
per scene. The controller does have constants — a gain, a step bound, a dead band,
a release ratio, an initial multiplier, a setpoint margin and a projection iteration, all
listed in Section 4 — but they take the same values in every experiment reported in
3
this paper, across three datasets and thirteen scenes. What the user supplies is the
budget itself.
2 Related work
Densification control.
Adaptive density control in the original formulation [1] clones or splits any primitive whose accumulated view-space positional gradient exceeds a constant threshold.
AbsGS [14] observes that opposing per-pixel gradients cancel within a primitive and
replaces the signed accumulation with a magnitude one; Pixel-GS [15] weights the
same statistic by projected area; DC4GS [16] conditions it on cross-view directional
consistency. All retain a constant threshold, and consequently the fraction of primitives selected at each step drifts by orders of magnitude over a run and differs across
scenes. Replacing the constant by a quantile of the current score distribution restores
control over growth. Our work is the pruning-side counterpart: the same reasoning
applied to removal rather than addition, with the complication that removal cannot
be undone. Section 5.6 shows that the two budgets cannot be set to the same number.
Reducing the number of primitives.
Post-hoc approaches score a converged model by an importance measure and prune,
sometimes with fine-tuning: LightGaussian [5] uses accumulated blending weight,
PUP-3DGS [6] a Hessian-based sensitivity, and others use spectral [17] or learned [7]
criteria. Mini-Splatting [18] and Trimming [19] reorganise the primitive distribution
instead. These operate after the fact and inherit whatever size training produced. Intraining methods change the population as optimisation proceeds: 3DGS-MCMC [20]
treats primitives as samples from a distribution and relocates rather than deletes them,
and Taming-3DGS [21] caps growth to fit a resource envelope. MaskGaussian [11] is
the method we build on directly. SizeGS [22] also targets a size budget, but does so
by allocating quantisation bit-widths in a post-processing pass over a model whose
primitive count is already fixed; we control the count itself, during training.
Constrained optimisation.
Casting a budget as a constraint and adapting its multiplier is classical [23, 24]. The
particular form we need is dictated by the irreversibility described above: the constraint may be violated in the direction that is recoverable but must not be violated
in the direction that is not, which is why our update is asymmetric and our setpoint is
offset. The resulting loop is closer to an anti-windup regulator [25] than to a textbook
dual ascent.

3.2 What λ does not control
Equation (3) penalises the fraction of primitives retained, scaled by a constant. The
gradient it delivers to a single mask logit is 2λm̄/N , and whether that is enough to
switch a primitive off depends on the photometric gradient competing with it, which
is a property of the scene. Nothing in the formulation refers to a target size.
We measure the consequence directly. Column 3 of Table 1 reports the converged
model size on thirteen standard scenes at the published λ = 5 × 10−4 , all other settings
identical. Sizes run from 388,401 primitives on room to 3,042,282 on bicycle, a ratio of
7.8× with a coefficient of variation of 70.9%. The grey bars in Fig. 2 show the same
data. A practitioner who needs a model of a particular size has no way to obtain one
except by sweeping λ per scene and retraining.
3.3 The mask does not come back
Before designing a controller it is worth asking what can be undone. The justification
for soft masking is that Eq. (2) propagates gradients to switched-off primitives, so
a primitive removed from the image can in principle be reinstated if the pressure is
relieved. We test this in isolation. We disable deletion, so the population is pinned at
B primitives from the moment it would otherwise fall below, and we let the controller
of Section 4 relieve the sparsity weight in the usual way. If the premise held, the live
count would recover towards B as λ fell.
Fig. 4 shows what happens on playroom. From iteration 18,000 the population is
held at exactly 600,000 and λ decays by an order of magnitude to 1.2 × 10−4 , which is
a quarter of the published constant and effectively no pressure at all. The live count

buys back essentially nothing. The gradient that reaches a mask logit through a fully
transparent primitive is real but negligible relative to what is needed to reverse a logit
that has been driven negative under Adam [26] for thousands of steps.

restored. Every design decision in Section 4 follows from this asymmetry.

4.3 Approaching from the recoverable side
A controller that aims exactly at B will, on any scene where the population crosses
the target quickly, overshoot before the loop reacts, and by Section 3.3 that overshoot

probability if it would. The floor alone is not a fix — by Section 3.3 a spared primitive
stays dark — but it costs nothing and it guarantees that the projection below has
material to work with.
4.4 Terminal projection
Soft pressure brings the population close to B ⋆ ; it does not land on B , and Table 4
shows it lands somewhere slightly different on each seed. At iteration Tlock = 28,000 we
therefore project once, keeping the B primitives with the highest inclusion probability
pi = softmax(si )0 and deleting the rest, after which λ is set to zero and no further
pruning occurs. The remaining 2,000 iterations refine a fixed topology.
The projection is exact because of Eq. (7). Since the population is settled near
(1 + µ)B > B , the primitives it removes are live, low-ranked ones rather than longdark primitives whose appearance parameters have gone stale, and there are always
enough of them to reach B . Without the margin the projection can only remove, so
on a scene that has already undershot it does nothing at all, which is exactly what we
observe in Section 5.6.
4.5 Reading out the mask
MaskGaussian exports the primitives selected by a single draw of Eq. (1), which discards roughly 5% of the surviving population by coin flip and makes the delivered size
a random variable. We instead export the mode, {i : pi > 1/2}, and feed the same
quantity back to the controller so that what is measured and what is shipped are the
same object. On its own this is quality-neutral (Table 4); combined with the terminal
projection it makes the delivered size deterministic.

5 Experiments
5.1 Setup
We evaluate on the thirteen scenes standard in this literature: nine from MipNeRF 360 [3], two from Tanks and Temples [27] and two from Deep Blending [28].
Outdoor Mip-NeRF 360 scenes are used at quarter resolution and indoor ones at half;
the remaining scenes are used at native resolution. Every eighth image is held out for
testing. All runs train for 30,000 iterations with the densification schedule, learning
rates and loss weights of MaskGaussian left untouched; the masking rule is the only
thing that differs. The reference 3D-GS row is produced by the same code with masking switched off entirely, so that it differs from the other rows in the pruning rule alone
and not in any incidental implementation detail. We report PSNR, SSIM [29] and
LPIPS [30], the size of the exported point cloud, and rendering throughput measured
on held-out views. Each run occupies one NVIDIA H100. Unless stated otherwise the
budget is B = 600,000 and the seed is 0.
5.2 Cross-scene budget transfer
Table 1 is the main result and Fig. 2 plots the size column. Applied unchanged to
all thirteen scenes, the method delivers exactly 600,000 primitives in every one: the
deviation from the requested budget is zero everywhere rather than merely small, and
no per-scene quantity was tuned.
The two columns on the left show what the alternative is. Neither baseline can be
asked for a size, so each delivers whatever its fixed setting happens to produce: 3DGS ranges from 1.08M primitives on train to 6.04M on bicycle, a factor of 5.6, and
MaskGaussian from 0.39M on room to 3.04M on bicycle, a factor of 7.8. Adding a
sparsity penalty therefore halves the model on average but does not make its size any
more predictable — if anything the spread widens, because the penalty bites differently
in different scenes. Our cross-scene range is 1.000× by construction.
The intermediate column shows why the full design is needed. A controller with
the dual variable but without the margin, floor and projection closes most of the
gap — the range falls from 7.8× to 1.51× — but undershoots by 17.0% on average
and by 36.1% in the worst case, and the failures are structured rather than random.
The five outdoor Mip-NeRF 360 scenes track to within 5.2%; the four indoor scenes
miss by 19 to 25%; the two Deep Blending scenes miss by 36%. The explanation is
Section 3.3 combined with headroom. Outdoor scenes peak at 3.2 to 4.5× the budget
during densification and descend slowly enough for the loop to settle before crossing;
indoor and Deep Blending scenes peak at only 1.4 to 2.8×, cross early while λ is still
rising, and lock in a deficit the controller cannot undo. On drjohnson the live count
falls from 776,373 at iteration 14,000 to 509,051 at iteration 16,000, passing straight
through the budget, and the final multiplier is 3.4 × 10−4 — the loop is asking for
growth it cannot obtain.

5.3 What the budget buys, visually
Fig. 1 compares the three models on one outdoor and one indoor scene. The upper strip
of each block shows the rendered appearance, which is essentially indistinguishable:
on garden the spread between 3D-GS and ours is 0.28 dB across a 9.6× difference in
primitive count, and on kitchen 0.20 dB across 3.0×.
The lower strip shows what the three models are actually built from. Rendering
the learned appearance cannot reveal this, because at full view a five-million-primitive
model places more than ten primitives behind every pixel; we therefore give each
primitive a solid colour and shrink it about its centre so that neighbours separate.
The reference 3D-GS model then appears as an almost continuous sheet of splats,
MaskGaussian thins it, and our model resolves into individually visible primitives
separated by background. The three renders differ in density by exactly the ratio of
their primitive counts, and the appearance strip above shows what that costs, which
is close to nothing.
Note also that the two baselines land where they land: 3D-GS delivers 5.73M
primitives on garden and 1.79M on kitchen, and MaskGaussian 2.79M and 0.83M,
spreads of 3.2× and 3.4× between two scenes at one setting. Our two models both
contain 600,000 primitives because that is what was requested.
5.4 Cost of control
Table 2 aggregates quality and resources over the thirteen scenes. Mean PSNR moves
by −0.014 dB against MaskGaussian and by +0.014 dB against 3D-GS. Our models
match or exceed each baseline on seven of the thirteen scenes, SSIM is unchanged to
three decimal places against both, and LPIPS degrades by 0.010 and 0.012 respectively
— the expected signature of representing the same scene with fewer primitives.
Differences that small invite the question of whether they are real at all, so we
repeated the whole suite at a second seed and compared scene by scene (Table 3). The
paired mean is +0.005 dB with a 95% confidence interval of [−0.076, +0.085] dB, and
t(12) = 0.12. Two things follow. The first is that we can state parity as a bound rather
than as a failure to reject: whatever quality difference exists between our budgeted
models and MaskGaussian, it is smaller than a tenth of a decibel. The second is why
single-run comparisons in this literature should be read with care — changing only
the seed moves PSNR by 0.087 dB on average and by as much as 0.335 dB on kitchen,
which is larger than most of the per-scene differences being compared.
The same table settles the reproducibility question in the other direction. Between
the two seeds, MaskGaussian’s delivered size moves by 1.51% on average and by 4.34%
at worst, while ours does not move at all: every scene returns exactly 600,000 primitives
at both seeds. The size is not merely predictable on average, it is deterministic.
What that quality buys is a model 5.0× smaller than 3D-GS and 2.2× smaller than
MaskGaussian, rendering 2.3× and 1.4× faster, at 142 against 709 and 318 MB on
disk. Training is also shorter, by 1.3× and 1.2×, because fewer primitives are carried
through the second half of the schedule. The averaged compression understates the
effect on large scenes, because the suite contains several scenes that are naturally

smaller than the requested budget: against 3D-GS the reduction reaches 10.1× on

5.5 Controller dynamics
Fig. 3 shows the loop at work. Densification drives the population to three to four times
the budget by iteration 6,000, the multiplier rises smoothly by an order of magnitude
in response, and the population returns to the setpoint by iteration 20,000 and holds.
Once the constraint is satisfied the multiplier relaxes on its own, which is the expected
behaviour of a dual variable at a feasible point.
Panel (b) makes the case against a constant. The two low-gain runs settle at
4.8×10−3 and 9.0×10−3 , ten to eighteen times the published 5×10−4 and, importantly,
at different values for different budgets on the same scene. There is no single constant
to discover; the correct value is a function of the scene and the requested size, which
is precisely what a controller supplies and a hyperparameter cannot.
5.6 Ablation
Table 5 isolates the components on stump. Two of them matter and two instructive
failures are worth recording.
The peak cannot be reached through λ.
We first tried to bound the densification transient by relaxing the controller’s reference
early in training and easing it onto the budget. This raised the peak rather than lowering it, from 4.06 to 4.16 times the budget, because relaxing the reference only delays
pressure, and the peak population is set by densification, not by the penalty: primitives masked off before iteration 15,000 are still resident in memory. The multiplier
has no authority over the peak at all.
The transient is not waste.
We then bounded it at the source, refusing to clone or split while the population is
above the reference. This works exactly as intended — the peak falls from 4.06 to 1.30
times the budget and peak memory from 6.0 to 3.1 GB — and costs 0.96 to 1.07 dB.
Growing to several times the final size and masking back down is doing real work, not
wasting it. This has a direct implication for combining budget control on both ends
of the pipeline: the growth allowance and the final budget are different quantities and
cannot be set to the same number.
The deletion floor is necessary but not sufficient.
Adding the floor to a controller that has already overshot reduces the mean absolute
budget error on the eight failing scenes only from 25.0% to 24.4%. That near-null result
is the experiment reported in Section 3.3: the primitives are alive and they stay dark.
10
The floor earns its place only in combination with the margin, where it guarantees the
terminal projection has material to remove.
5.7 Reproducibility
Table 4 repeats four configurations over four seeds. Delivered size is reproducible to
0.07% under dual control against 2.6% for the baseline, and exactly reproducible once
the terminal projection is added. PSNR is not similarly stable: the seed standard
deviation of the baseline is 0.098 dB and that of the controller 0.077 dB, comparable
rather than better, and the terminal projection raises it to 0.274 dB because the hard
selection amplifies small differences in the inclusion ranking. The practical reading is
that the seed spread is as large as any difference between configurations, so single-run
comparisons between masking variants are not informative — a lesson we learned by
first drawing conclusions from them and then failing to replicate.
5.8 Two directions that do not work
A controllable budget is useful as an instrument as well as a result, because it allows
two configurations to be compared at equal size. Table 6 reports two ideas that look
promising without it.
Weighting the sparsity penalty by projected screen area, so that the objective
tracks rasterisation cost rather than primitive count, appears mildly favourable when
size is free: it reaches a slightly better LPIPS than the baseline. At a matched budget
it collapses to 22.3 dB. The weighting preferentially removes large primitives, which
are the efficient ones — they cover many pixels each — so at any fixed count it keeps
the wrong subset. Annealing the Gumbel temperature appears to gain 0.25 dB, and
the gain is entirely a size artefact: a sharper relaxation makes the ten-sample deletion
test fire less often, so the model simply retains 9% more primitives. At a matched
budget the effect is negative. Neither direction survives contact with a fixed budget,
and neither would have been correctly diagnosed without one.
6 Limitations
Two seeds bound the quality difference but do not resolve its sign. The interval in
Table 3 excludes any effect larger than about a tenth of a decibel in either direction,
which is enough to support the claim we make, but a genuine difference of a few
hundredths of a decibel would remain invisible at this sample size. We also did not
repeat the 3D-GS baseline or the ablations over seeds, so the comparisons involving
them inherit the single-run noise of roughly 0.09 dB measured here.
The budget must be reachable. Our controller can always remove primitives but
cannot compel densification to supply them, so a budget above what adaptive density
control produces for a scene will not be met. All thirteen scenes reached 600,000
comfortably, but a request substantially above a scene’s natural size would require
coupling the budget back into densification, which Section 5.6 shows is not a matter
of reusing the same number.
11
The margin µ and the projection iteration Tlock are constants we did not tune per
scene, and the fact that a single value worked everywhere is evidence but not proof that
they are benign; a scene with an unusually abrupt densification profile could still cross
the budget before the loop engages. Finally, the irreversibility result of Section 3.3 is
measured for this masking formulation under this optimiser and schedule. We believe
the mechanism — a vanishing gradient through a fully transparent primitive — is
general, but we have not tested whether a different relaxation or a periodic logit reset
would restore recoverability, and that is the most interesting thing this work leaves
open.
7 Conclusion
Mask-based pruning decides the size of a 3D Gaussian Splatting model through a
constant that does not refer to size, and the result is a delivered model whose footprint
varies by nearly an order of magnitude across scenes for reasons the practitioner does
not control. Making that constant a dual variable is the obvious correction and is not
sufficient, because masking turns out to be effectively irreversible: a primitive switched
off does not switch back on even when all pressure is removed. Once the controller
is designed around that asymmetry — approaching the budget from the side that
can still be corrected and landing on it with a single projection — a single setting
delivers exactly the requested number of primitives on every scene we tried, at no
measurable cost in image quality. We think the irreversibility result is the more durable
contribution: it is a property of soft masking that its usual justification denies, and
any method that relies on revisiting a masking decision should be checked against it.
