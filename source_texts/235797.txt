While deep learning techniques have gained increasing prominence in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) reconstruction, the denoising of clinical dose CBCT images remains relatively underexplored. This study aimed to propose and evaluate a CBCT image domain denoising network, named CBCT-IDDNet, which won first place in the clinical dose task of the three-dimensional (3D) CBCT Challenge at the 2024 International Conference on Acoustics, Speech, and Signal Processing (ICASSP).
Methods
The proposed CBCT-IDDNet model was based on a 3D Res-UNet architecture and consisted of two components: an encoder and a decoder. The CBCT-IDDNet was trained on data from 800 patients, validated on 100 patients, and tested on 110 patients. On the validation set, the CBCT-IDDNet model was evaluated against five state-of-the-art (SOTA) models using quantitative metrics, including mean squared error (MSE), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), and structural similarity (SSIM). On the test set, the model was compared with six competing models submitted by other participants.
Results
On the validation set, the CBCT-IDDNet model achieved averaged MSE, PSNR, and SSIM values of 0.00089, 38.30 dB, and 0.9486, respectively, outperforming all five SOTA models. On the test set, the model achieved an average MSE of 0.00077, prevailing over all six competing models.
Conclusions
The proposed CBCT-IDDNet model improves CBCT image quality by effectively reducing noise, making it a valuable tool for clinical applications.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is a crucial imaging modality used in various clinical settings, including image-guided radiotherapy (1,2), image-guided surgery (3), stomatology (4), and otolaryngology (5). As a compact X-ray imaging technique, CBCT provides high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) visualization of anatomical structures (6,7). However, the image quality of CBCT is often compromised by noise due to several aspects (8,9), such as inherent hardware limitations, electronic thermal noise, scattering, etc.. This noise can obscure delicate anatomical details, reduce image contrast, and degrade overall image quality, impairing the accuracy and reliability of clinical diagnoses and interventions (10). Therefore, effective denoising of CBCT images is essential for clinical practice.
Over the past decade, deep learning (DL) techniques have become increasingly prominent in the field of computed tomography (CT)/CBCT reconstruction (11-13). Among the various strategies, image domain denoising has gained significant attention, focusing on learning the mapping from noisy images to their corresponding ground truth (GT) images. Notable advancements have been made in this field, showing promising progress. Jin et al. (14) developed the FBPConvNet model, based on a multiresolution U-Net architecture. Chen et al. (15) developed the RED-CNN model, employing a residual encoder-decoder architecture with shortcut connections. Zhang et al. (16) developed the DD-Net model, which consists of several dense blocks and deconvolution layers. Shan et al. (17) developed the MAP-NN model, which consists of several consecutive conveying-link-oriented network encoder-decoder modules. Hu et al. (18) developed the IDU-Net model, employing a 3D U-Net architecture with an added residual learning strategy. Chen et al. (19) developed the FRCNN model, which is trained with a fractional total variation loss to mitigate over-smoothing. Geng et al. (20) developed the CNCL-UNet model, which employs two parallel U-Nets to separately learn the content and noise components of an image, and integrates their outputs through a complementary feature fusion mechanism. Chao et al. (21) developed the Post-AGNet model, employing a symmetrical 3D encoder-decoder architecture with spatial attention modules. Zhu et al. (22) developed the STEDNet model, based on a Swin Transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture. Jian et al. (23) developed a SwinCT model, employing a CNN-Transformer architecture to extract both local and global image features. Despite these advancements, it is important to note that these denoising models are primarily designed for low-dose CT/CBCT images. In clinical practice, however, regular or clinical dose CBCT images are more commonly used (24). There still remains a notable gap in DL research focused on denoising techniques specifically tailored for clinical dose CBCT images.
To advance the frontiers of DL methods for real-world 3D clinical CBCT reconstruction, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) International Conference on Acoustics, Speech, and Signal Processing (ICASSP) initiated a specific Grand Challenge in 2024 (25,26). One of the tasks in this challenge was to denoise CBCT images acquired with a clinical dose. To address this task, we proposed a novel image domain denoising network named CBCT-IDDNet (27). According to the final ranking (23), the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model exhibited robust and superior performance, ultimately winning first place in the challenge.
The highlights of this study are summarized as follows:
We developed an innovative image domain model, CBCT-IDDNet, specifically tailored for denoising clinical dose CBCT images.
Quantitative comparisons on the validation set demonstrated that the CBCT-IDDNet model outperformed five state-of-the-art (SOTA) models from the literature.
Quantitative comparisons on the test set demonstrated that the CBCT-IDDNet model outperformed six competing DL models submitted by other participants.

MethodsData
The ICASSP-2024 3D-CBCT Challenge provided a dataset comprising 1,010 lung nodule patient cases (25,26), derived from the publicly available Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI) dataset (28). The LIDC-IDRI dataset consists of thoracic CT scans aimed at detection, classification, and quantitative analysis of lung nodules. The inclusion criteria for the LIDC-IDRI dataset were as follows: (I) patients underwent diagnostic or lung cancer screening helical thoracic CT scans; (II) among the CT imaging parameters, collimation, reconstruction interval, and slice thickness were all less than or equal to 5 mm; and (III) the CT image quality was sufficient to ensure reliable nodule interpretation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.
According to the data description released by the challenge organizers (25), clean CBCT projections were simulated by performing 3D cone-beam forward projection on thoracic CT images from the LIDC-IDRI dataset using the ASTRA Toolbox (29). The corresponding noisy CBCT projections were generated from the clean projections by adding photon noise, electronic noise, and quantification noise, at levels consistent with those observed in clinical dose acquisitions. Tomographic images of clean projections and clinical dose projections reconstructed with the Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) algorithm (30) were provided, among which the images reconstructed from the clean projections served as the GT. The 1,010 patient cases were divided into three parts: 800 cases for the training set, 100 cases for the validation set, and 110 cases for the test set. The training and validation sets were made available to participants for DL model development and parameter optimization, while the test set was retained by the challenge organizers to evaluate the performance of the submitted models.
Model architecture
Figure 1 shows the architecture of the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model, which is based on a 3D Res-UNet structure. The model takes a 3D patch extracted from tomographic images reconstructed from the clinical dose projections as input and produces a denoised 3D patch as output.
Figure 1
Overall architecture of the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model.
The model comprises an encoder component and a decoder component. The encoder component includes four levels of down-sampling stages, which progressively reduce the input of size D × H × W × 1 to a compact feature representation of size (D/16) × (H/16) × (W/16) × 512. In contrast, the decoder component comprises four corresponding up-sampling stages that restore the encoded representation back to the original resolution, producing the output of size D × H × W × 1. Each stage in the encoder comprises two consecutive 3×3×3 zero-padded convolutional layers with a stride of 1. Each convolutional layer is followed by a batch normalization (BN) layer and a leaky rectified linear unit (LReLU) activation function with a negative slope coefficient of 0.2. A residual learning technique is applied by adding the stage input to the output of the second LReLU activation function. The stage concludes with a down-sampling operation via a 3×3×3 convolutional layer with a stride of 2. Conversely, each decoder stage ends with an up-sampling operation using a 3×3×3 transposed convolutional layer with a stride of 2. Skip connections are used to allow the decoder stages to integrate features from the corresponding encoder stages. Furthermore, the model adopts another residual learning technique by directly adding the model input to the output.
Loss function
The mean squared error (MSE) loss function was utilized to train the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model. This loss function is defined as follows:
LMSE=1MP∑m=1MP1NP‖F(PInput,Θ)−PGT‖22
[1]
Where F represents the CBCT-IDDNet model, PInput refers to the input patch, PGT refers to the GT patch, Θ represents the network parameters, NP is the number of voxels in a patch, and MP is the mini-batch size.
Model training details
The proposed CBCT-IDDNet model was implemented using Python version 3.8.18, PyTorch version 2.0.1, and CUDA version 11.1.89. Training patches of size 128×128×128 were extracted from the reconstructed tomographic images, with a mini-batch size of two patches. The parameters of convolutional and transposed convolutional kernels were initialized using the Kaiming initialization method (31). The Adam optimizer (32) was utilized with an initial learning rate of 10−4, β1 =0.9, and β2 =0.999. To refine the model training process and prevent overfitting, a learning rate scheduler and an early stopping strategy were implemented, both based on the averaged loss value on the validation set. The learning rate was decreased by a factor of 10 if the validation loss did not decrease over 10 consecutive epochs. The training process was terminated if the validation loss failed to decrease over 20 consecutive epochs. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 200. Experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory.
The source code is publicly available at https://github.com/Xuzhi-bjtu/ICASSP-2024-3D-CBCT-challenge-Clinical-dose-CBCT-reconstruction.
Evaluation metrics
The performance of the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model was quantitatively evaluated using three image quality assessment metrics (33): MSE, peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), and structural similarity (SSIM) (34).
The MSE and PSNR are defined as:
MSE=1NI‖I^−I‖22
[2]
PSNR=10log10(max2(I)MSE)
[3]
where I^ and I represent the model-generated axial image and the corresponding GT axial image, respectively. NI denotes the total number of pixels in an axial image, and max (I) denotes the maximum pixel value in the GT axial image.
The SSIM is defined as:
SSIM=(2μI^μI+C1)(2σI^I+C2)(μI^2+μI2+C1)(σI^2+σI2+C2)
[4]
where μI^ and μI denote the mean values of local windows in the model-generated axial image and the GT axial image, respectively. σI^ and σI are the corresponding standard deviations, and σI^I denotes the covariance between the two windows. Constants C1 and C2 are set to (0.01×L)2 and (0.03×L)2, respectively, where L represents the dynamic range of pixel values in the GT axial image (34). In this study, SSIM was computed using a sliding window of size 11×11.
Comparison study
On the validation set, the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model was compared with five SOTA image domain denoising models, including FBPConvNet (14), RED-CNN (15), IDU-Net (18), CNCL-UNet (20), and SwinUNet (35). Specifically, FBPConvNet adopts a two-dimensional (2D) U-Net architecture, while RED-CNN employs a 2D residual encoder-decoder design. IDU-Net is based on a 3D U-Net architecture, and CNCL-UNet comprises two parallel 2D U-Nets coupled with a feature fusion mechanism. These four models have demonstrated promising denoising performance for low-dose CT images in their respective studies (14,15,18,20). In contrast, SwinUNet is built on a 2D Swin Transformer-based U-Net architecture and has shown promising results in natural image denoising tasks (35). As the source codes for FBPConvNet, RED-CNN, and IDU-Net models were not publicly available, these models were re-implemented and fine-tuned based on the methodological descriptions provided in their original papers. Evaluation metrics, including MSE, PSNR, and SSIM, were calculated.
On the test set, the CBCT-IDDNet model was compared with six competing models submitted by other participants. The challenge organizers conducted the comparison, focusing solely on the MSE metric.
ResultsComparison on validation set
Table 2 presents the quantitative results comparing different methods on the validation set. The proposed CBCT-IDDNet model outperformed all other methods. Compared to the averaged metrics obtained by the baseline method (i.e., FDK reconstruction without any DL model), the CBCT-IDDNet model showed improvements of 0.03014 in MSE, 15.75 dB in PSNR, and 0.2822 in SSIM. Discussion
Although many DL models have been developed for CBCT image denoising (14-23), these models are primarily designed for low-dose imaging protocols with reduced X-ray exposure. Denoising methods specifically tailored for clinical dose CBCT images have not been fully explored yet (24). In this work, we developed an image domain denoising model, CBCT-IDDNet, which won first place in the clinical dose task of the ICASSP-2024 3D-CBCT Challenge (25,26).
The performance of the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model was evaluated against five established SOTA models from the literature (14,15,18,20,35) (Table 2), as well as six competing DL models submitted by other participants (Table 3). The results highlight the superior denoising capability of CBCT-IDDNet on clinical dose CBCT images. The model’s lower MSE and higher PSNR values indicate that its delivered images are comparable to the GT with minimal distortion, while the higher SSIM values indicate superior SSIM between the denoised images and the GT. These improvements are crucial for clinical applications, where image quality can directly impact clinical decisions. Beyond numerical metrics, the visual quality of the denoised images plays a significant role in their clinical usability. Figures 2,3 demonstrate that CBCT-IDDNet not only reduces noise more effectively but also preserves critical anatomical details better than its counterparts. This is particularly important in regions with high-frequency structures, such as lung parenchyma and bone structures, where compromised image quality could mislead clinical decisions.
The superior performance of the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model can be attributed to three key methodological factors. First, the use of a 3D network architecture plays a significant role in the model’s success. Unlike 2D networks that process images slice by slice, the 3D network enables leveraging volumetric information, capturing spatial dependencies across adjacent slices (21). This capability is particularly important in CBCT, where maintaining the integrity and continuity of anatomical structures across slices is crucial for clinical accuracy. Second, the incorporation of residual learning techniques allows CBCT-IDDNet to more effectively focus on learning the noise patterns inherent in CBCT images. By directly adding the model input to the output, the model learns to predict the residual noise rather than reconstructing the entire image. This approach not only accelerates convergence during training but also contributes to preserving the integrity of fine details (20). Third, the use of advanced training techniques, such as learning rate scheduling and early stopping, ensured that the model did not overfit the training data while still achieving high performance on the validation and test sets (36). The careful balance between the loss values on the training and validation sets contributed to the model’s robust performance.
The implications of the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model for clinical practice come in two main aspects. First, CBCT-IDDNet is purely in the image domain, making it easy to be integrated into the existing imaging chain. Second, by providing clearer images with reduced noise and preserved details, CBCT-IDDNet has the potential to reduce the cognitive load on clinicians, therefore leading to improved clinical outcomes.
There are two major limitations to this study. First, the proposed CBCT-IDDNet model was trained and evaluated exclusively on a simulated thoracic CBCT dataset provided by the challenge organizers. Future efforts will be directed to test the model’s generalization in real-world settings, i.e., real clinical dose CBCT images acquired from different devices and covering diverse anatomical regions. Second, CBCT-IDDNet operates solely in the image domain, without leveraging information from the projection domain. We will explore the development of an end-to-end trainable projection-image dual-domain DL framework in the future to further enhance overall denoising performance.
Conclusions
The proposed CBCT-IDDNet model effectively denoises clinical dose CBCT images, thereby improving image quality. With the aid of the model, CBCT images have the potential to better support clinical tasks.
