From the maintainer, while reviewing star rendering: introduce an optics model, so that what the sky emits and what an instrument makes of it stay separate. That keeps each part objective, and lets celestiary show what different instruments see.
Why
Today the observer is spread across constants and knobs:
the eye's 10′ patch (EYE_PATCH_SR);
the telescope field's patch over the magnification (Stars through a telescope's field: the eye's patch over the magnification (#192) #198);
sm=, a gain on every star's light;
ev=, exposure compensation;
DISPLAY_GAIN;
the bloom law;
The Sun's activity by date, its corona, eclipses, glare and wind #227's CIE disability glare;
a camera-like response that hasn't been built yet (A camera-like response for telescope fields: Neutral's toe crushes faint values next to bright ones #208).
The scene's light is already physical (HDR.md). The observer isn't one model, and so brightness can leak into size: sprites grew with sm=.
The chain
Scene radiance (physical; done) → optics → detector → display.
Optics:
aperture D and central obstruction;
focal length and field (the FOV), giving magnification and exit pupil;
transmission;
the PSF: diffraction (Airy, or with spider spikes for reflectors), aberrations, and atmospheric seeing (a Moffat or Kolmogorov profile with a FWHM in arcseconds);
the eye's own PSF and its scattering wings (CIE 135/1999; Vos & van den Berg).
Detector:
The eye: photopic and scotopic spectral response (V(λ), V′(λ)) and the mesopic mix; integration time of about 0.1 s; threshold against background luminance (Blackwell 1946; Crumey 2014, which also gives the limiting magnitude from sky brightness); glare.
A camera: QE curve, exposure time, full well and saturation, read and shot noise, and the tone curve. This would make Comparison photo of Jupiter near moon #192's photos reproducible: the occultation photo's camera.
Display: the one tone map, as now.
Shape
One module (e.g. js/scene/observer/) owns the instrument. The renderers read the PSF, collecting area, integration time and threshold from it, never from scattered constants.
The default is the naked eye, dark adapted. The star-rendering work, filed separately under Epic: Physically based light and exposure #109, builds this seam with the eye as its only instance.
Presets: the naked eye (dark- or light-adapted); 7×50 and 10×50 binoculars; a 200 mm Dobsonian; a DSLR with a 50 mm lens; a phone camera; later a space telescope (no seeing; diffraction-limited).
Choosing an instrument is an app in the drawer (Catalogues are scene features; their analysis is an app (dark matter, large-scale structure, globular orbits) #233: catalogues are scene features, and analysis and instruments are apps). Its state goes in apps.observer: tokens, and the scene goes back to the naked eye when it stops.
sm= then becomes the instrument's explicit aperture and integration time; the user's gain stays only as an override. ev= stays as exposure compensation.
Related
#155 (the telescope mode and limiting-magnitude filter: absorbed here), #208 (a camera-like response), #192 (matching photos), #109 (physically based light), #227 (glare), #233 (apps), #98 (Gaia: deep stars only an instrument shows).
Done when
The eye, a pair of binoculars, a Dobsonian and a camera each give a star field whose limiting magnitude matches published values for that instrument and sky brightness (Crumey's model for the eye; aperture and exposure scaling for the rest).
Star images are sized by each instrument's PSF, and their brightness never changes their size beyond the PSF's visible wings.
Comparison photo of Jupiter near moon #192's photo can be reproduced by choosing the camera that took it.
