Variance-aware Differentiable Rendering
By

Doctor of Philosophy in Computer Science

Professor Shuang Zhao, Chair
Differentiable rendering focusing on estimating derivative of forward-rendering result, which
capable of differentiating with respect to a wide range of scene parameters including material
properties and object geometries. In this thesis, we explore different types of variance in
differentiable rendering: variance in gradient estimates in differentiable rendering and inverse
rendering, and differentiating rendering variance.
First, we introduce an efficient technique for estimating boundary integrals in path-space
differentiable rendering, which significantly reduces estimation variance, enabling robust
inverse rendering with complex geometry and lighting.
Second, we introduce an image-space adaptive sampling technique that accelerates inverse
rendering by dynamically adjusting pixel sampling probabilities based on gradient variance
during inverse rendering.
In the end, we establish a framework for differentiating rendering variance with respect to
both scene parameters and sampling probabilities. While traditional differentiable rendering
differentiates pixel values, we differentiate their variance. This introduces the concept of
Variance-aware inverse rendering, which alters a virtual scene and/or an estimator in an
optimal way to offer balance between rendering bias and variance.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Differentiable rendering has emerged as a powerful framework that bridges physically based
rendering with gradient-based optimization. By computing derivatives of rendered images
with respect to scene parameters, such as material properties, object geometries, and lighting
configurations, it enables inverse rendering, where we reconstruct scene parameters from
observed images or target loss functions. This capability has found applications across
computer graphics, computer vision, and machine learning.
However, most physically based rendering techniques rely on Monte Carlo estimation, whose
stochastic nature inevitably introduces variance. In traditional forward rendering, variance
manifests as image noise that can be reduced through advanced sampling or denoising.
In differentiable rendering, variance becomes far more problematic: it propagates into the
computed gradients, causing unstable optimization, poor convergence, and unreliable results
in inverse rendering and machine learning.
Despite the critical importance of variance, most existing differentiable rendering techniques
1
focus primarily on the unbiasedness of derivative estimators, treating variance as an unavoidable quantity to be tolerated. This dissertation takes a fundamentally different perspective:
variance itself becomes the object of study. We investigate how variance arises in differentiable rendering, how the variance in differentiable rendering can be reduced through improved
sampling in Chapter 3, how the variance in inverse rendering can be minimized during optimization in Chapter 4, and how the variance in differentiable rendering can be differentiated
with respect to scene and sampling parameters in Chapter 5. Through these investigations,
we establish a comprehensive variance-aware differentiable rendering framework that enables
stable, efficient, and statistically principled inverse rendering and machine learning.
1.2
Background and Challenges
Monte Carlo rendering expresses image formation as high-dimensional path integrals over all
light transport paths connecting light sources to sensors. Differentiable rendering extends
this formulation to compute derivatives of these integrals with respect to arbitrary scene
parameters including geometry, materials, and illumination. While systems such as Mitsuba
3 Jakob et al. (2022b) and PSDR-JIT Yan et al. (2020) provide unbiased derivative estimates
in differentiable rendering for inverse rendering, their variance can be orders of magnitude
higher than that of forward rendering, particularly when differentiating with respect to
geometric parameters.
Several factors make variance control in differentiable rendering particularly challenging:
• Boundary integrals: Geometric parameters introduce visibility discontinuities, requiring estimation of boundary integrals over silhouette edges. These integrals are inherently
high-variance and difficult to sample efficiently due to sharp visibility changes and specular interactions that create heavy-tailed gradient distributions.
• High-dimensional parameter spaces: Inverse rendering optimizes thousands of corre-
2
lated parameters simultaneously across high-resolution or multi-view images, amplifying
variance and causing erratic convergence when using uniform sampling strategies.
• Lack of theoretical foundations: Traditional differentiable rendering theory focuses
on estimating expectations, not on differentiating or optimizing the variance of estimators
themselves.
These challenges motivate a systematic investigation of variance from three complementary
perspectives: variance reduction in differentiable rendering, variance reduction in inverse
rendering, and differentiating rendering variance, which form the three core contributions of
this dissertation.
1.3
Contributions
This dissertation presents three major contributions that collectively advance the understanding of variance in differentiable rendering. Each contribution addresses a distinct aspect
of the variance problem.
Efficient Estimation of Boundary Integrals The first contribution (Chapter 3) introduces an efficient Monte Carlo technique for estimating boundary integrals in path-space
differentiable rendering. Boundary integrals arise when differentiating with respect to geometric parameters and are notoriously difficult to sample due to their complex domains and
high variance. We develop a novel importance sampling strategy based on primary-samplespace guiding that transforms samples from the rendering integral’s primary sample space
to the boundary integral domain. Additionally, we introduce a multiple importance sampling scheme that combines different guiding strategies and an edge-sorting heuristic that
improves runtime performance. Our technique dramatically reduces gradient variance for geometric derivatives, enabling stable inverse-rendering optimizations in scenes with complex
geometry and global illumination. We demonstrate effectiveness through comparisons with
3
existing methods including the warped-area sampling technique and validate our approach
on challenging reconstruction tasks.
Adaptive Sampling for Accelerated Inverse Rendering The second contribution
(Chapter 4) develops a practical image-space adaptive sampling framework that accelerates
inverse rendering by minimizing gradient variance during optimization. In inverse rendering
from high-resolution or multi-view images, rendering all pixels at every iteration becomes
prohibitively expensive. While uniform pixel subsampling reduces cost, it introduces high
gradient variance that degrades convergence. We formulate pixel sampling probability optimization as a variance minimization problem, deriving an efficient estimator for the gradient variance at each pixel based on both loss contribution and uncertainty in the gradient
estimates. Our method dynamically adjusts sampling probabilities across iterations, effectively performing variance-aware mini-batching that concentrates samples on high-impact,
high-uncertainty regions. We demonstrate significant speedups on material and geometry
reconstruction tasks, outperforming uniform sampling and achieving faster convergence with
lower per-iteration cost.
Differentiating Rendering Variance The third contribution (Chapter 5) establishes a
mathematical framework for differentiating the variance of Monte Carlo rendering estimators. While traditional differentiable rendering computes derivatives of rendered pixel values,
we derive formulations and unbiased estimators for derivatives of pixel variances with respect
to both scene parameters (e.g., surface roughness) and sampling parameters (e.g., Russian
roulette probabilities). Building on the differential path integral framework, we show how
variance derivatives can be expressed as path integrals and estimated using Monte Carlo
sampling. This formulation enables variance-aware inverse rendering, where one can explicitly optimize the bias-variance trade-off by jointly adjusting scene parameters and sampling
strategies. We demonstrate applications including automatic path-space regularization and
optimization of Russian roulette schemes to minimize rendering variance. By making vari4
ance itself a differentiable quantity, this work bridges stochastic Monte Carlo theory with
gradient-based optimization.

Related Work
This thesis builds upon extensive prior work in Monte Carlo rendering, differentiable rendering, and inverse rendering. We organize the related work into several key areas that provide
context for our contributions.
2.1
Forward Rendering and Monte Carlo Sampling
Sampling for forward rendering Monte Carlo sampling of light transport paths has
been an essential component for most, if not all, physics-based forward rendering techniques.
A commonly used approach is local sampling that grows light paths vertex by vertex. This
boils down path sampling to drawing an incident direction given the exitant one (or the other
way around) at each vertex, which is typically achieved by importance sampling local BSDFs.
Previously, a large variety of BSDF models have been proposed (e.g., Phong (1975); Cook
and Torrance (1982); Ashikhmin and Shirley (2000); Oren and Nayar (1994)). Among these
models, microfacet BSDFs (e.g., Cook and Torrance (1982); Ward (1992); Schlick (1994); van
Ginneken et al. (1998); Kelemen and Szirmay-Kalos (2001); Pont and Koenderink (2002);
Walter et al. (2007); Heitz et al. (2016); Lee et al. (2018); Xie and Hanrahan (2018)) have
6
been widely adopted. Importance sampling of these models, therefore, has been studied
thoroughly and can be performed by sampling the underlying normal distributions Walter
et al. (2007); Heitz and d’Eon (2014) or via localized Monte Carlo processes Heitz et al.
(2016).
All these sampling methods are developed for forward rendering and can be repurposed to
estimate interior integrals for differentiable rendering. On the other hand, they are largely
complementary to the estimation of boundary integrals, which is a main focus of this thesis.
Path guiding Several methods (e.g., Jensen (1995); Lafortune and Willems (1995); Vorba
et al. (2014); Müller et al. (2017); Guo et al. (2018); Zheng and Zwicker (2019); Müller
et al. (2019); Droske et al. (2023)) have been developed to accelerate unidirectional path
tracing for forward rendering. Although these techniques are conceptually related to our
work, especially the primary-sample-space guiding approaches we present in Chapter 3, our
techniques focus on different use cases including estimating boundary integrals that are
unique to differentiable rendering and adaptive sampling for inverse rendering.
Adaptive sampling Adaptive sampling has been extensively explored to improve the
quality of Monte Carlo rendering by allocating samples strategically rather than uniformly.
This area has been the focus of numerous previous works Ramamoorthi et al. (2007); Overbeck et al. (2009); Rousselle et al. (2012); Zwicker et al. (2015); Hasselgren et al. (2020);
Salehi et al. (2022); Firmino et al. (2023). However, these works primarily focus on enhancing the quality of forward rendering. In contrast, one of our contributions in Chapter 4 aims
to utilize image-space adaptive sampling to improve the performance of inverse rendering by
strategically allocating computational resources during gradient-based optimization.
7
2.2
Differentiable Rendering
Physics-based differentiable rendering Physics-based differentiable rendering is the
process of numerically computing derivatives of forward-rendering results with respect to arbitrary scene parameters such as object geometries and optical material properties. Although
simple (e.g., one-bounce) light transport can be simulated and differentiated approximately
using soft rasterization (e.g., Liu et al. (2019); Laine et al. (2020)) or analytical methods (e.g.,
Zhou et al. (2021)), physics-based differentiable rendering generally requires estimating (i) interior integrals given by differentiating the integrands of corresponding forward-rendering
integrals; and (ii) boundary ones defined over discontinuities of those integrands.
Interior integrals The interior integrals have been mostly estimated using path sampling
methods developed for forward rendering. Zeltner et al. Zeltner et al. (2021) have studied how
various parameterizations (e.g., “attached” and “detached”) affect the performance of Monte
Carlo estimation of the interior integral. Zhang et al. Zhang et al. (2021a) have introduced
antithetic sampling (at both BSDF- and path-level) for efficiently estimating the interior
integral. Several other approaches have been proposed for differential and antithetic BRDF
sampling methods for estimating BRDF parameter derivatives Su and Gkioulekas (2024);
Belhe et al. (2024). These methods are orthogonal to our techniques and can potentially be
combined with our contributions.
Boundary integrals The boundary integrals are unique to differentiable rendering and
present a significant challenge. A main challenge toward the development of general-purpose
differentiable rendering techniques has been the differentiation with respect to scene geometry, which generally requires calculating these additional boundary integrals.
To address this problem, two classes of techniques have been introduced. The first class
directly samples discontinuity boundaries Li et al. (2018); Zhang et al. (2019, 2020). Specifically, Zhang et al. (2020) have introduced the formulation of differential path integrals where
8
discontinuities are tracked and handled at the path level. Recently, specialized methods Yan
et al. (2022); Zhang et al. (2023) have been developed to better importance sample these
path integrals. The second class of methods Loubet et al. (2019); Bangaru et al. (2020); Xu
et al. (2023) reparameterize boundary integrals and avoid explicit handling of discontinuity
boundaries altogether.
Zhang et al. Zhang et al. (2020, 2021b) have introduced the differential path integral framework that formulates both the interior and the boundary components as full path integrals,
making it possible for the development of sophisticated Monte Carlo estimators for both
components (beyond unidirectional path tracing). However, efficiently estimating boundary
path integrals (by importance sampling full boundary light paths) remains challenging, which
we address in this thesis.
Several approaches have also been proposed to allow differentiable rendering to scale to
complex scenes with large numbers of parameters Nimier-David et al. (2020); Vicini et al.
(2021). We refer the readers to online course materials (e.g., Zhao et al. (2020)) for a more
comprehensive overview of differentiable rendering techniques.
2.3
Variance in Rendering
Variance-aware forward rendering Variance reduction has been a long-standing topic
in Monte Carlo forward rendering. Among the very large body of works on this topic, several
recent methods leverage variance information to enhance various aspects including multiple
importance sampling Grittmann et al. (2019) and path guiding Rath et al. (2020). Our
techniques are largely orthogonal to these methods and focus on variance-aware approaches
for inverse rendering rather than forward rendering.
Differentiating variance Most, if not all, existing differentiable rendering techniques
focus on derivatives of (converged) rendering estimates (e.g., pixel values) rather than their
9
variances. Variance-aware differentiable rendering was recently introduced by Yan et al.
(2024). This technique enables differentiating not only the rendered image with respect to
scene parameters but also its variance with respect to sampling probabilities. In this thesis,
we build upon this work in Chapter 5. We adopt the formulation by Zhang et al. (2020)
to establish a general mathematical framework for the derivatives of rendering variances.
Additionally, in chapter 4, we focus on the estimation of the variance of derivatives for
adaptive sampling rather than only the derivative of variance, enabling new applications in
inverse rendering.
Path-space regularization As a potential application of variance-aware techniques, pathspace regularization concerns modifying path sampling strategies so that rendering variance
is greatly reduced while introducing minimal bias. Previously, several techniques Kaplanyan
and Dachsbacher (2013); Jendersie and Grosch (2019) have been introduced to regularize
the sampling of microfacet distributions by leveraging strategic use of mollifiers for Dirac
deltas.
The path-space regularization technique that is most relevant to our work is the one by Weier
et al. (2021), which reduces rendering variance by altering surface roughness at selected
vertices of each light path. This technique formulates the search of optimal roughness values
as a variance-aware inverse rendering problem and leverages differentiable rendering to solve
the optimization efficiently. Unfortunately, they rely on highly biased variance estimates
which, as we will show in §5.3 and §5.5, can lead to unreliable optimization.
2.4
Inverse Rendering
Inverse rendering aims to estimate the physical attributes of a scene, such as material,
geometry, lighting, and volume, from image(s). These pipelines recover scene parameters by
minimizing a loss function, often leveraging a large batch of input photos for reconstruction.
10
Neural-based methods Mildenhall et al. (2020); Wang et al. (2021); Zhang et al. (2022);
Kerbl et al. (2023) have gained popularity in recent years. In addition, recent advances
in differentiable rendering have driven the development of physics-based inverse rendering
pipelines, as demonstrated by Munkberg et al. (2022); Cai et al. (2022); Hasselgren et al.
(2022); Sun et al. (2023); Yan et al. (2023).
However, these advances present a challenge: when processing high-resolution images or
large image sets, small batch sizes can result in significant variance in gradient estimates.
Our key insight in this thesis mentioned in Chapter 4 is that computing every pixel during
inverse rendering is unnecessary; by strategically allocating samples and leveraging variance
information, we can effectively reduce rendering costs and enhance overall performance.

3.1
Introduction
Provided a virtual scene with fully specified object geometries, material optical properties,
and detector configurations, forward rendering focuses on numerical estimations of a detector’s radiometric response. Differentiable rendering, in contrast, computes gradients of
13
detector responses with respect to differential changes of the scene and has applications in
a wide range of areas such as computational imaging, remote sensing, and computational
fabrication.
Recently, great progress has been made in physics-based differentiable rendering theory, algorithms, and systems Li et al. (2018); Zhang et al. (2019); Loubet et al. (2019); Nimier-David
et al. (2019); Zhang et al. (2020); Bangaru et al. (2020); Zhang et al. (2021b,a); Zeltner et al.
(2021). These advances have made it possible to differentiate a scene representation with respect to arbitrary scene parameters including those controlling global object geometry (e.g.,
the positions of mesh vertices). Mathematically, it has been demonstrated that physics-based
differentiable rendering generally amounts to evaluating interior and boundary integrals.
The interior integrals for differentiable rendering share the same domain as those for forward
rendering. To estimate these terms, previous differentiable rendering techniques have mostly
relied on existing stochastic sampling strategies developed for forward rendering. Recently,
several differentiable-rendering-specific Monte Carlo methods have been developed Zeltner
et al. (2021); Zhang et al. (2021a) with advantages in terms of speed and quality.
In contrast, the boundary integrals are unique to differentiable rendering and defined on
discontinuity boundaries of the ordinary rendering integrals. To handle them, several new
techniques—such as Monte Carlo edge sampling Li et al. (2018), reparameterization of the
ordinary rendering integral Loubet et al. (2019); Bangaru et al. (2020), and differential path
integrals Zhang et al. (2020, 2021b)—have been introduced. Among these techniques, differential path integrals introduced by Zhang et al. Zhang et al. (2020, 2021b) have enabled
the development of sophisticated differentiable rendering algorithms beyond unidirectional
path tracing—similar to ordinary path integrals for forward rendering. Unfortunately, Monte
Carlo estimation of boundary integrals under Zhang et al.’s path-space formulation remains
largely under-explored, making it difficult to solve complex inverse-rendering problems efficiently.
14
In this chapter, we introduce a new technique1 for efficient estimation of these boundary path
integrals. Concretely, our contributions include:
• We develop a primary-sample-space guiding technique for efficient importance sampling of
boundary light paths (§3.3.1). Additionally, we show how multiple importance sampling
(MIS) can be used to combine multiple guided sampling processes (§3.3.2).
• We introduce an optional edge sorting step that can further improve the performance of
our technique (§3.3.3).
To show the effectiveness of our method, we compare gradient images estimated with our
method and previous techniques in Figures 3.6 and 3.7. Additionally, we conduct detailed
ablation studies in Figures 3.8 and 3.9 to evaluate individual components of our technique.
Further, with our improved estimation of boundary integrals, inverse-rendering problems
under challenging conditions, such as environmental and indirect illumination, can be solved
more efficiently. We demonstrate this via several synthetic inverse-rendering examples in
Figures 3.10 and 3.11.

dimension ax ∈ {x, y, z} of the split, we compute fits of F̃ in the two children (Lines 5–8 of

dimension of the primary space, we find 32 to work well for all of our examples.

the node’s axis-aligned bounding box.

3.3.3
Edge Sorting
When the target functions F̃ of Eq. (3.11), F̃light of Eqs. (3.12), and F̃dir of Eqs. (3.13) are
highly discontinuous, the performance of our technique can degrade as the Kd-trees need
to have very large depths to properly approximate the targets. In practice, this is mostly
caused by discontinuities of xB as a function of the primary sample u1 .
To address this problem, we introduce an optional step that can greatly reduce the number
of jump discontinuity points of xB (u1 ). Specifically, we sort the edges to form long “chains”
along which the mapping from u1 to xB is continuous (see Figure 3.4-a). Since finding the
26
optimal edge ordering that results in a minimal number of chains is NP-hard, we instead
traverse the mesh edges in a greedy fashion. As described in Algorithm 2, starting from an
arbitrary mesh edge, we construct a chain of edges by iteratively moving to a neighboring
vertex vnext from the current one vcur . When there are multiple possible neighbors, we pick
the one such that the direction of the edge (vcur , vnext ) is as close to that of the previous
one (vprev , vcur ) as possible (see Figure 3.4-b). We demonstrate the usefulness of our edge
sorting process using a simple scene in Figure 3.5 and will show more ablations in §3.4.
For applications where remeshing can be applied (which is typically the case when solving
inverse-rendering problems), we pre-apply remeshing Jakob et al. (2015) to make the input
mesh field-aligned. This allows our edge sorting process to output fewer longer chains, further
improving the overall effectiveness of our technique.

Discussion and Conclusion
Limitations and future work Our technique focuses on the surface-only light transport.
For scenes with participating media, as demonstrated by Zhang et al. Zhang et al. (2021b),
the dimensionality of the primary-sample space for constructing boundary segments increases
from three to five, making the guiding much more challenging. Generalizing our technique
to support volumetric light transport is an important future topic.
Additionally, our technique focuses on constructing boundary segments, so improving the
sampling of source and detector subpaths may further benefit the performance of our technique.
Lastly, combining our method with Markov-Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling may be
an interesting topic for future exploration.

Inverse rendering is crucial for many scientific and engineering disciplines. Recent progress in
differentiable rendering has led to efficient differentiation of the full image formation process
with respect to scene parameters, enabling gradient-based optimization.

4.1
Introduction
Physics-based forward rendering concerns synthesizing physically accurate images of 3D
scenes. Decades of research efforts on this topic have led to forward rendering techniques
capable of efficiently reproducing complex light transport effects including environmental
lighting, soft shadows, and interreflection.
On the contrary, inverse rendering takes input images of a scene and aims to estimate scene
parameters, including material properties and object geometries. A widely adopted solution
known as analysis by synthesis formulates inverse rendering as an optimization problem
that seeks scene parameters that minimize the difference (quantified by some loss) between
40
input images and renderings of the scene. Recent advances in differentiable rendering Li
et al. (2018); Zhang et al. (2020); Bangaru et al. (2020) have enabled the evaluation of
forward-rendering derivatives with respect to scene parameters, allowing inverse-rendering
optimizations to be solved using gradient-based methods such as stochastic gradient descent
(SGD) and Adam Kingma and Ba (2014).
Many, if not most, practical inverse-rendering configurations use many (e.g., multi-view)
input images. Unfortunately, rendering all the corresponding images for every iteration of
the optimization process can be prohibitively expensive in terms of both compute time and
VRAM consumption. To address this problem, pixel-level mini-batching is a widely adopted
approach in inverse-rendering pipelines Mildenhall et al. (2020); Yariv et al. (2020); Müller
et al. (2022); Nimier-David et al. (2022): at each iteration, the loss is estimated using only
a randomly selected subset of images or pixels.
However, the efficient selection of pixels for mini-batching has received limited attention.
Most existing inverse-rendering pipelines simply adopt uniform sampling when selecting images or pixels. Although this simple strategy is unbiased and works adequately in many
cases, as we will demonstrate later in this chapter, doing so can lead to highly noisy estimates of loss gradients that significantly reduce the convergence rate of inverse-rendering
optimizations.
In this chapter, we introduce a technique that performs adaptive pixel sampling for inverse
rendering. Given a per-iteration sampling budget, our technique allocates samples on each
pixel based on not only its contribution to the loss function but also the forward/differentiable
rendering variance. By offering efficient and low-variance gradient estimates, our method
enables fast inverse rendering using small batch sizes without changing the underlying primal/differentiable rendering procedures.
Technically, this chapter makes the following contributions.
41
• We introduce a technique that adaptively samples pixels to minimize the variance of loss
gradient estimates (§4.2).
• We discuss how the pixel sampling technique can be efficiently implemented and integrated
into complete inverse-rendering pipelines (§4.3).
We demonstrate in §4.4 the effectiveness of our technique using several synthetic inverserendering examples, including SVBRDF reconstruction, shape optimization, and inverse
volume rendering.

For scenes containing objects with greatly varying levels of details (e.g., a mixture of textured

Discussion and Conclusion
Limitations and future work Our method assumes unidirectional path tracing. Therefore, for problems that require using adjoint or bidirectional methods (e.g., scenes with
strong caustic effects), as shown in Figure. 4.9, our method would be inapplicable.
Our
technique performs adaptive sampling in the image space (i.e., for pixels) and currently
60
works only when the underlying primal and differentiable processes use unidirectional path
tracing. Generalizing our technique to support more sophisticated path sampling strategies
such as path-space methods to sample boundary light paths Zhang et al. (2020); Yan et al.
(2022); Zhang et al. (2023) is an important topic for future research.
Also, as discussed in §4.3.1, developing efficient methods to compute (∂I L)j for general losses
such as LPIPS Zhang et al. (2018) is worth exploring in the future.
Conclusion We introduced an image-space adaptive sampling technique to perform pixellevel mini-batching for inverse rendering while minimizing the variance of loss gradient estimates. By considering both mean and variance of the underlying primal and differentiable rendering processes, our technique is a significant generalization of previous methods.
Moreover, we discussed how this sampling technique can be integrated into practical inverse rendering pipelines, allowing smooth convergence with fast rendering per iteration.
We demonstrated the effectiveness of our technique by comparing it with previous methods
using several synthetic inverse rendering examples.

Variance-Aware Inverse Rendering
Monte Carlo methods have been widely adopted in physics-based rendering. A key property
of a Monte Carlo estimator is its variance, which dictates the convergence rate of the estimator. In this chapter, we devise a mathematical formulation for derivatives of rendering
variance with respect to not only scene parameters (e.g., surface roughness) but also sampling
probabilities. Based on this formulation, we introduce unbiased Monte Carlo estimators for
those derivatives. Our theory and algorithm enable variance-aware inverse rendering which
alters a virtual scene and/or an estimator in an optimal way to offer a good balance between
bias and variance. We evaluate our technique using several synthetic examples.

5.6
Discussion and Conclusion
Limitations and future work Our derivations in §5.3.1 rely on the assumption that the
probability density q shares the same visibility-driven discontinuity points as the measurement contribution f . Although this is a mild assumption, it may need to be relaxed to
handle some complex sampling procedures like delta tracking Woodcock et al. (1965). In
addition, although our theory is not restricted to any specific path sampling method, our
analysis in §5.3.2 and implementation focus on unidirectional path tracing. Thus, applying
our theory to more general cases such as bidirectional path tracing (BDPT) could be an
interesting future topic.
Conclusion In this chapter, we derived a path-integral formulation for derivatives of rendering variance with respect to not only scene parameters but also sampling probabilities.
Based on this formulation, we introduced unbiased Monte Carlo estimators for the derivatives. In addition, we discussed an important application of our technique—variance-aware
inverse rendering—which concerns with altering a virtual scene and/or an estimator to offer a good balance between bias and variance. We validated our technique by comparing
derivative estimates to finite-difference references and demonstrated the effectiveness of our
method using several synthetic variance-aware inverse rendering examples.

Chapter 6
Conclusion
While differentiable rendering has become a powerful tool for connecting the rendering process with gradient-based optimization, the stochastic nature of Monte Carlo estimation introduces variance that affects both the stability and accuracy of derivative computation. This
dissertation studies variance in differentiable rendering not as a by-product to be tolerated,
but as a quantity that can be analyzed, differentiated, and optimized.
To summarize, we first reduce variance in boundary integral estimation to improve gradient
quality (Chapter 3), then minimize gradient variance in inverse rendering through adaptive
pixel sampling (Chapter 4), and finally establish theoretical foundations for differentiating
variance and introduce variance-aware inverse rendering (Chapter 5). These contribution
lays the groundwork for a comprehensive theory of variance-aware differentiable rendering
that variance as a important quantity to study in differentiable rendering.

6.1
Future Work
6.1.1
Differentiating Variance for Complex Samplers
The current variance differentiation theory is primarily applicable to path tracing. It would
be valuable to extend this framework to more complex light transport algorithms such as
bidirectional path tracing (BDPT) and path guiding. Additionally, differentiating variance
requires explicit expressions of probability density functions, which makes extending the
approach to delta tracking or complex algorithms in real-time rendering (such as ReSTIR
Lin* et al. (2022); Wyman et al. (2023)) challenging.
6.1.2
Rendering Variance and Denoising
In general, the relationship between rendering variance and denoising performance is not
well studied, as low variance in rendering does not necessarily lead to better denoising performance in practice. A comprehensive study of how variance in rendering, or other error
matrics, affects denoising performance would be valuable for real-time rendering applications.
6.1.3
Minimizing Gradient Variance in Machine Learning
Our image-space adaptive sampling method minimizes gradient variance in inverse rendering
to improve optimization speed. Extending this concept to machine learning, especially for
training with large batch datasets, might further improve general machine learning training
performance.
6.1.4
Differentiable Rendering with Generative AI
Differentiable rendering can benefit to/from generative AI in several ways, such as 3D scene
generation using generative priors, or applying neural materials and shading in inverse rendering pipelines. In addition, neural support is neccessary for next-generation differentiable
