These systems commonly present registered magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as orthogonal 2D slices [6, 13]. The
slices contain rich tissue information, but they do not resemble the exposed cortical surface seen through the surgical

vessel, sulcus, or resection boundary lies within the MRI

Neurosurgical navigation commonly presents magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as 2D slices, making it difficult to
relate the anatomy in those slices to the cortical surface
observed through the surgical microscope. We seek a common 3D visualization that combines a photorealistic reconstruction of the exposed cortex during tumor surgery with
whole-brain MRI context and diffusion tractography pathways. This requires the reconstructed cortex to form a clear
boundary around the brain anatomy rather than a diffuse
volume that obscures internal structures. Conventional 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) can reproduce observed views
but, under limited surgical view coverage, may distribute
opacity through a thick and geometrically ambiguous band.
We introduce height-field 2D Gaussian Splatting (H-2DGS),
a three-stage pipeline that concentrates a robust 3DGS reconstruction, fuses its depth into a shared truncated signed
distance field (TSDF) surface, and represents that surface
with curved, surface-oriented Gaussian primitives. Separate height, color, and opacity bases provide local geometry
and appearance, while a neighbor-transition objective coordinates overlapping patches. The explicit surface further
supports postoperative alignment: MRI-derived depth deforms the reconstruction, and tracked-color supervision with
alternating optimization preserves its appearance. Across
five real neurosurgical cases, H-2DGS gives low multi-view
depth inconsistency, a compact 7.97 MB representation, and
interactive rendering at 29–42 ms per frame. The resulting
sharp, deformable surface integrates clearly with tractography and MRI anatomy, including a postoperative resection
cavity.

We instead aim to place the complementary information
in one 3D scene. Microscope video supplies the current
color and fine surface landmarks of the exposed cortex. MRI
supplies the surrounding whole-brain tissue context, while
diffusion MRI tractography exposes subcortical pathways
that are not visible at the surface. A photorealistic 3D reconstruction can connect what the surgeon sees to these internal
structures under continuous viewpoint changes: the cortex
provides the visible outer layer, the MRI-derived model locates that layer within the brain, and tractography shows the
structures beneath it. The fused views in Fig. 1 illustrate this
intended relationship.
Creating this visualization is not only a registration problem; it first requires an appropriate representation of the visible layer. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [11] is attractive
because it reconstructs realistic appearance, renders rapidly,
and exposes primitives that can be manipulated. It has consequently been adopted for static and deforming surgical
scenes [7, 9, 10, 16, 17, 22]. However, each conventional
3D Gaussian distributes opacity through a volume. With
the narrow camera trajectories typical of microscope video,
Gaussians at several depths can explain the same training pixels. The model may therefore look realistic from observed
views while forming a thick, blurry, or fragmented density
band elsewhere. When tractography is rendered beneath this
band, fibers can be obscured by or visually embedded in
the reconstructed cortex, so the boundary between visible
surface and subcortical anatomy becomes ambiguous.

Medical visualization plays a central role in the interpretation, communication and education of neuroanatomy and
tractography. Interactive visualization is important in, e.g.,
neurosurgical planning, as it enables users to explore anatomical structures and fiber pathways. Modern neurosurgery

We address this representation gap with H-2DGS, which
progressively converts a robust volumetric reconstruction
into a geometrically sharp yet photorealistic surface. Stage I
first uses conventional 3DGS to establish appearance without prematurely imposing a fragile surface. Stage II then
measures the spread of density along camera rays and contracts it toward the visible boundary. Multi-view depth from
this concentrated model is fused into a truncated signed distance field (TSDF), providing a shared surface rather than
unrelated per-view estimates. Stage III discards the volumetric Gaussians and places curved height-field primitives
on the TSDF zero level set. The height field lets each patch
follow local cortical curvature, separate color and opacity
bases retain spatially varying texture, and neighbor-transition
optimization coordinates overlapping patches.
The common 3D view must also remain meaningful when
anatomy changes. Brain motion and tissue removal alter the
relationship between the reconstructed surface and MRI [6];
after resection, the cavity can also invalidate vessel-based
correspondence. For the postoperative case, we therefore
render depth from the MRI-derived mesh and deform the
explicit height-field surface toward that geometry. Depth
determines the target shape, while tracked color supervision,
overlap regularization, and alternating geometry–appearance
updates prevent the alignment from erasing the photorealistic cortical texture. Preoperative vessel-guided alignment
is retained only as a downstream use of the reconstructed
surface.
Our contributions are1 :
• a three-stage conversion from volumetric 3DGS to a thin
TSDF-initialized cortical surface, including a differentiable ray-wise thickness objective;
• a curved height-field Gaussian primitive with spatially
varying geometry, color, and opacity, together with overlap
constraints for locally continuous geometry;
• a depth-supervised deformation procedure that aligns the
photorealistic surface to postoperative MRI geometry
while preserving appearance, enabling unified 3D visualization with whole-brain anatomy and tractography.

Related Work
Gaussian surface representations. Standard 3DGS represents a scene with anisotropic volumetric Gaussians optimized through differentiable alpha compositing [11]. Several alternatives strengthen its geometric prior. 2D Gaussian
Splatting (2DGS) replaces ellipsoids by oriented disks and
uses ray–splat intersection to obtain a more surface-like representation [8]. 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting (3D-HGS) splits
primitives with learned planes to sharpen discontinuities [14],
while Quadratic Gaussian Splatting (QGS) models local geometry with second-order surfaces [15]. These approaches
initialize their designed primitives directly from an input
point cloud. Under limited view coverage, the source geometry can itself be weakly constrained. H-2DGS instead first
concentrates a robust volumetric reconstruction, fuses its
multi-view depth into a shared TSDF, and only then initializes curved surface charts. Per-primitive appearance bases
are related to textured Gaussian representations [19–21, 23],
but our height basis additionally makes each primitive a
deformable local surface.
Surgical reconstruction and anatomical fusion. Neural
and Gaussian representations have been applied to static
and deforming surgical scenes [17, 18, 22]. Most of these
methods prioritize view synthesis or temporal tissue motion.
Cross-modal methods connect intraoperative imagery to preoperative anatomy through inverse rendering or coupled
meshes [4, 5]. Our focus is complementary: we construct a
sharp, compact cortical boundary from limited microscope
views, use it as a substrate for tractography, and directly deform this renderable surface toward postoperative geometry.

Our output is a renderable set of surface patches that must
satisfy three requirements. First, it should reproduce cortical
color and vessel landmarks from the input views. Second,
opacity should be confined to a well-localized surface so
that MRI anatomy and tractography remain visible on the

subsequent alignment.
Directly fitting surface-oriented splats from the sparse input point cloud is brittle in this setting: the same limited
camera coverage that creates 3DGS thickness also leaves the
initial geometry weakly constrained. We therefore separate
appearance acquisition from surface construction. Stage I
obtains a robust photometric reconstruction with flexible
volumetric Gaussians. Stage II makes this reconstruction
a reliable geometric proxy by contracting its density along
observed rays without changing the primitive type. Stage III
fuses the resulting multi-view depths into one surface and replaces the volumetric model with curved height-field patches.
Thus each stage has a distinct purpose: bootstrap appearance,
localize geometry, and encode an explicit surface.

in Fig. 5, fibers appear attached to a defined surface instead
of intersecting a diffuse cloud. The tractography therefore
serves both as a target application and as a visual stress test

We evaluate five MRI cases using real neurosurgical videos:
three preoperative cases and two postoperative cases. For
each case, we select the continuous segment of the surgical
video with the most comprehensive viewpoint coverage and
uniformly sample frames from the original segment, resulting in a continuous sequence of 60–80 images for training.
The data were shared under the approved data-sharing agreement between the participating institutions. All patients
provided written informed consent for the use of their data
for research purposes. All experiments run on an NVIDIA
RTX 3050 GPU with 4 GB memory, 32 GB system memory,
and an Intel i7-12700H CPU. H-2DGS uses 2,400 primitives
per case, with 50 color and 20 opacity bases per primitive.
We compare against 2DGS [8], 3D-HGS [14], and QGS [15],
trained with their released default settings.
PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS evaluate appearance. PSNR and
SSIM use the intersection of valid rendered pixels across
methods. Model size and per-frame time measure efficiency.
For geometry, multi-view depth consistency (MVDC) backprojects valid depth from one evaluation view, reprojects it
into a neighboring view, and reports the median absolute
depth discrepancy normalized by scene extent.

Discussion and Conclusion
A remaining limitation is rendering quality. As indicated
by PSNR and SSIM, H-2DGS does not reproduce the observed images as accurately as the baseline methods. Replacing volumetric support with a thin explicit surface removes geometric ambiguity, but also reduces the rendering
expressiveness available for reproducing high-frequency appearance variations. We introduce spatially varying texture
through the color bases to compensate for this loss, but a
clear photometric gap remains. Future work should therefore
investigate richer geometry-preserving appearance models
that improve rendering fidelity without reintroducing diffuse
volumetric opacity.
Together with the proposed three-stage pipeline, H-2DGS
produces a photorealistic reconstruction of the cortical surface with a sharp geometry and well-defined boundary. This
explicit surface enables direct integration with subcortical
tractography and whole-brain MRI context without the internal anatomy being obscured by diffuse volumetric opacity.
Compared with the baseline approaches, H-2DGS achieves
substantially better geometric generalization across viewpoints: it consistently maintains a localized cortical surface
in uncovered views and obtains lower multi-view depth inconsistency across the five cases (Tab. 1 and Fig. 6).
The clear geometry of H-2DGS also provides a suitable
foundation for deformation. Its surface primitives retain
sufficient geometric flexibility to represent substantial morphological changes, allowing deformation objectives to be
defined directly on the primitive parameters and fitted to
a target geometry. The geometric and rendering flexibility
of H-2DGS is verified in the postoperative case, where the
fitting process is separated into depth-fitting and appearancepreservation stages. The postoperative experiment demonstrates that this strategy can accommodate a pronounced
geometric discrepancy while preserving the photorealistic
appearance of the original reconstruction.
The compact size of H-2DGS follows from its fixed 2,400primitive budget with per-primitive texture on an explicit
surface. We do not match primitive counts to the baselines,
The current pipeline also depends on camera poses recovered by structure from motion (SfM), whose static-scene
assumption can be violated in surgical videos. One additional sequence failed because physiological cortical pulsation introduced non-rigid inter-frame motion, causing SfM
to confound tissue motion with camera motion and produce
inaccurate poses. This error subsequently degraded all reconstruction stages. Potential solutions include obtaining calibrated or externally tracked camera poses from specialized
surgical equipment, representing each primitive’s geometric
attributes as continuous functions of time, and estimating
the dominant pulsation frequency for phase-aware frame selection or motion correction. Addressing both appearance
expressiveness and dynamic tissue motion will be important

the future, combining this approach with cinematic rendering [2, 12] should offer a unique opportunity to improve
