Abstract
Voxel Cone Tracing is an approach that approximates global illumination for real-time applications and has some interesting properties. Global illumination gives photorealistic lighting
results since it simulates how light in real life bounces around multiple times resulting in indirect lighting. Global illumination can be very expensive and a challenge for real-time applications, therefore approximation algorithms such as the Voxel Cone Tracing method make the
computations easier. This method approximates the original scene and creates a voxel field to
retrieve necessary radiance information during the sampling phase where indirect lighting is
processed. Light is approximated by tracing cones that sample the voxel field, rendering realistic approximations with speed necessary for real-time usage. The Voxel Cone Tracing implementation in the report was implemented using C++ with the OpenGL graphics API. The
Sponza model is rendered using Voxel Cone Tracing with sparse 3D textures. The qualities
evaluated were performance, memory consumption, and visual accuracy, and their dependence
on voxel resolution is studied. The qualities evaluated were performance, memory consumption, and visual accuracy. This way the method’s strengths and weaknesses could be explored.
The thesis concludes that Voxel Cone Tracing is a method that warrants further study as advances in modern hardware have improved many of the inherent flaws of Voxel Cone Tracing,
such as memory consumption and speed with higher accuracy renders. As for the visual quality
the foremost unrealistic lighting artifacts were voxel approximation errors, especially around
concavities and unwanted light bleeding from thin materials. The Visual quality was high besides these errors and there was space for further improvements and optimizations to the implementation presented in this thesis. Implementing a more sophisticated voxel storage solution
can lead to reduced memory usage and enhanced performance. Ultimately, voxel cone tracing
emerges as a promising technique that warrants further exploration.

2 Introduction
Producing realistic images and imitating reality is one of the focus points in the world of computer graphics. Therefore, it is important to understand how light works, both in the real world
but also how to mimic it using computers. Simulating realistic light is done by combining several different types of light into one final result. The result is often called global illumination.
Global illumination is the effect of when light spreads throughout a scene and creates realistic
shadows. To get global illumination in real time applications, approximations are required to
get the speed necessary for the program to be interactive. There are many ways to do this, but
this thesis will examine one method, Voxel Cone Tracing. The Voxel Cone Tracing method
was first published back in 2011 by Crassin et al. [1] and has until this day been used sporadically. For example, Voxel Cone Tracing made its way into the popular game engine Unreal
Engine 4 under the name VXGI made by Nvidia, although the latest VXGI update was in 2018
[2]. However, it has never really dominated, as there are many other more prevalent alternatives
for global illumination.
In short, Voxel Cone Tracing compromises and approximates the compute-heavy algorithm
path tracing by tracing singular light photons. Voxel Cone Tracing instead traces cones covering many light particles at the same time. The cones are sent out from a point of interest to
register the light radiated from its surroundings, thus providing the indirect illumination phenomena global illumination. All direct lighting contributes as indirect light and is stored in a
voxel grid using a sparse 3D texture. Through Voxel Cone Tracing interactive speeds are
achieved with realistic results, although with a few light artifacts.

2.1 Problem Statement
The aim of this thesis is to investigate the qualities of Voxel Cone Tracing, which can be narrowed down to its performance, memory usage and image quality. The thesis examines Voxel
Cone Tracing's different qualities and how they change by altering voxel texture sizes and lighting behavior. Where light behavior can be defined as changing the number of light bounces and
comparing static to dynamic scenes. A static scene is defined as a scene where the environment
does not change, and a dynamic scene is a scene which changes each frame. The specific evaluation methods are further presented in Ch. (Chapter 4.2).
The thesis therefore aims to answer the following research questions:
Can an implementation of Voxel Cone Tracing render in real-time providing global illumination with sufficient speed, space consumption and image quality? How do different voxel texture
sizes and light behavior affect these qualities?
The research question can be subdivided to more specific questions that explore the implementation's different settings and the effect on the result.
Q1) How do different voxel texture sizes and light behavior affect performance?
Q2) How do different voxel texture sizes and light behavior affect memory consumption?
Q3) How do different voxel texture sizes and light behavior affect image quality?
2.2 Purpose
The purpose of this thesis is to understand the qualities of Voxel Cone Tracing. It is interesting
to change both the light behavior and voxel data structure size as they both affect the realism
of the results seen in the image quality and the algorithm's performance and memory consumption. Balancing these three quality aspects is a core problem in computer graphics and ultimately defines how good a rendering method is. This thesis tries to pinpoint what qualities this
Voxel Cone Tracing implementation falters or excels.
It is important to understand that there are multiple ways to implement a Voxel Cone Tracer
and many optimizations and variations have come up over the years. After all, 13 years have
passed since this method gained notable attention after Crassin et. al. first presented their paper
“Interactive Indirect Illumination Using Voxel Cone Tracing” [1]. There are some improvements to the traditional Voxel Cone Tracing implementation presented by Crassin used in the
implementation of the thesis evaluations. The improvements are the usage of physically based
rendering techniques and multiple light bounces. The hypothesis is that these improvements
create more realistic lighting.

2.3 Limitations
The implementation of Voxel Cone Tracing in this thesis has some limitations. Only one
method of storing the voxel textures is implemented and evaluated. There are other methods
for storing voxels that are equally promising and further comparisons for the thesis could improve the overall result. Another limitation is that the thesis only implements 1 and 2 light
bounces for the Voxel Cone Tracer. It is possible to extend the method to calculate an indefinite
number of bounces using the same memory consumption as 2 bounces. Further studies upon
the visual results and performance of more bounces and other storage methods should be expanded upon in future papers.
Because of implementing from scratch and the aim of seeking deeper understanding of the inner
workings of our implementation, the time constraints of the thesis created some compromises
regarding the Voxel Cone Tracing implementation. The main compromise is the storage format
of the voxels. This thesis uses a sparse 3D texture which is a good approach although not optimal with storage efficiency in mind. A better approach would be to implement a Clipmap[18][19] or a dynamic sparse voxel octree [1] which could handle dynamic lighting in
scenes more effectively and use less memory, although.

3.1 Introduction to Computer Graphics
3.1.1 Rendering
Rendering is the process of generating an image from a model holding information using a
computer program. The goal of rendering is to provide the user and viewer with the illusion of
watching real objects on the screen of the device. The image on the screen consists of pixels,
evenly distributed tiny colored rectangles. The color of the pixels is generated from a virtual
world, a scene full of objects that contains a lot of information the computer uses to determine
the pixel's colors. [3]
Since this thesis focuses on global illumination, realistic light behavior and the related rendering
process is the focus instead of a stylistic one. In computer graphics, there are a myriad of different methodologies and techniques to render a scene. The area of use for each method can be
generalized to the basic question of if the technique should be used for real-time or offline
rendering.

Offline rendering, while being able to render detailed photorealistic images (see figure 1) the

3.1.3 The graphics pipeline
Vertices hold different attribute information and almost always form in a group of three primitive triangles. These primitives and vertices make up the final textured 3D model in the virtual
world that renders on the screen. The process of the GPU reading the vertex data and consequently rendering the 3D models on a 2D screen follows the graphics pipeline. This transformation is divided into different stages in the graphics pipeline [25][26]. Note that shaders are
programs that run on the GPU instead of the CPU.
Vertex Shader
The Vertex shader handles the vertex attributes such as the position, texture coordinate and
normal vector. Mainly the position coordinates are transformed so that they are positioned correctly in clip space coordinates where the origin is at the viewport, that is the origin of the
camera, and perspective by matrix transformation is applied. Orthographic matrices needed for
voxelization can also be applied here, meaning that perspective is omitted so that objects always
retain their size regardless of their distance from the camera [25][26].
Geometry shader
The geometry shader can modify the primitive geometries or modify them to generate new
geometries, which is also needed during the voxelization process. The geometry shader has the
unique attribute that they handle each vertice of a triangle individually instead of the entire
triangle at once [25][26].
Rasterization
The rasterization is a fixed function process that introduces a grid of pixels based on screen
resolution and converts the primitive to fragments based on its pixel coverage, see Figure 2.

3.2 Global Illumination
3.2.1 Global illumination explained
Global illumination is a name for all methods allowing light to bounce around in the scene,
which creates indirect light. Global illumination creates the effect of realistic lighting in a scene
and thus greatly enhances the realism of a rendered scene. Without global illumination, areas
in a scene that are not directly lit from a light source would be completely black [4]. However,
in the real world these areas would just be darker. To simulate this effect, the primary method
involves allowing light to bounce off surfaces to indirectly light up areas, see figure 4.

3.2.2 Global Illumination problems
Implementing and solving the rendering equation accurately would prove to be very hard,
mainly because of two reasons that will be referenced as a) and b) [4]:
a. The hemisphere that is needed to closely model real-world lighting is computationally
intensive to calculate an integral over every single incoming light direction and do this
for each fragment point on the many surfaces that are rendered. The number of computations that would be needed for this are simply too many and not a feasible way to
calculate the global illumination, at least in real time.
b. The other problem is regarding the equation’s recursive nature. The number of light
bounces is indefinite, and each bounce affects its surroundings. So, for each surface
point p, it is affected by the light bounces from the surrounding surface points n, and in
turn this goes the other way. The emitted light from p will affect the other surrounding
points n. Meaning that the lighting at p and n depend on each other. With two unknown

variables and without knowing the number of times bounced before the rendering equation cannot be solved analytically directly. In other words, the output of the equation is
also the input of the equation, just at another point, see Figure 8.

3.2.3 Global Illumination problem solutions and approximations
The rendering equation is often more of a goal that instructs what should be approximated to
get realistic lighting effects rather than a proposed solution. Approximations or other models
can be used to simplify or adapt the rendering equation. This way computational efficiency can
be assured, and real-time rendering is possible while still providing realistic lighting with global
illumination [4].
In respect of rendering equation problem, a), the Monte Carlo Integration solves this by discretizing the continuous space over Ω to get an approximated computable solution. This is also
what is happening when using path tracing. More precisely by using this integration, one could
trace a specified finite amount of incoming light rays that are set in random directions over Ω.
By tracing back from where the light came from, other surfaces or light sources are reached and
by sampling the lighting information that is stored it will simulate how the sampled light affected the tracing start point. Then the same light ray can potentially continue to be traced by
again bouncing and sampling in a new direction simulating multiple light bounces. Finally, all
light information sampled from the traced light rays are summarized. With this approximation
of Ω change Eq1 to a new approximation of the rendering equation, Eq3 [4][7]:

3.2.4 BRDF and approximation of the rendering equation
Another thing to consider when approximating the rendering equation is how to formulate the
Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function, BRDF. The BRDF oversees calculating how
material responds to lighting based on the surface materials properties.
Choosing a BRDF all comes down to what materials there are and how much information about
them is available in the scene. For example, there could be very complex materials with characteristics not covered in this report like anisotropic reflections or subsurface scattering requiring a different and more advanced BRDF [10][11]. Considering what information, the objects
and their materials in the Sponza scene provide, a simpler BRDF will suffice for this Voxel
Cone Tracing implementation.
BRDF changes depending on if the reflection is diffuse or specular as a Lambertian model is
used for both direct and indirect diffuse reflections. For indirect specular reflections Voxel Cone
Tracing is used while a Phong model is used for the direct specular reflections. This way the
rendering equation can be divided depending on if 𝐿𝑖 (𝜔𝑖 ) is from direct/indirect specular or
direct/indirect diffuse lighting. Two different main lighting calculations can be done. One for
diffuse reflections and one for specular reflection, both considering the direct and indirect lighting for a specified number of bounces as in Eq4. The results of both calculations can be added
together for the final outputted fragment color 𝐿0 (𝜔0 ) and thus approximate the rendering equation with Eq5 [4][7][13]:
final output color = diffuse reflections + specular reflections

3.2.5 The Lambertian model for diffuse reflections
This is the most basic model for generating images that does not depend on the viewpoint coordinates where the camera is. It simply uses the surface normal, light source direction and the
albedo color of each surface [12]. Where albedo is the percentage of light a surface reflects,
hence determining the color of the object seen. In computer graphics albedo is often described
as the texture map that defines the base color of an object before any light or shadows or other
rendering effects are applied. The fact the light reflected is not dependent on viewpoint means
that the same amount of light is reflected with the same amount albedo in every direction around
the hemisphere Ω. This is called diffuse reflections, and they reflect light after some absorption
in different directions depending on the uneven surface and is typical for matte and rough materials, see the right illustration of Figure 11. The different directions are approximated according to Monte Carlo Integration.
The rendering equation is approximated with Eq3, where the light hitting the surface point
𝐿𝑖 (𝜔𝑖 ) is for direct light a coefficient 0 ≤ 𝐿𝑖 ≤ 1, depending on how bright the light source is,
and for indirect light it is the stored lighting data from the other surfaces affecting our point of
interest, see Figure 10. The BRDF 𝑓𝑟 (𝜔𝑖 , 𝜔0 ) is the albedo color from the texture map for direct
diffuse light while the sampled indirect diffuse light is scaled by the PBR roughness texture. In

3.3 Voxel Cone Tracing
3.3.1 Voxelization and storing direct lighting
The solution for the rendering equation problem b) has already been discussed as seen in Ch
3.2.3, such that the direct lighting is calculated first and will contribute to the calculations of
the indirect lighting bounces that follow. It will prove useful to store the direct lighting in a
smart way so that the calculation of the indirect lighting will be more effective. Better still, this
direct lighting is to be averaged and stored more roughly so that the “rough direct lighting”
allows for quicker and larger data samplings. The rough direct lighting is only used to contribute
to indirect lighting while the true direct lighting seen in the final rendered image is still accurately calculated for all fragments viewable on the screen space. The rough direct light is still
calculated across the entire scene since the indirect light is emitted from surfaces even though
they are not seen. This is opposed to the direct light that is only calculated if the surfaces are in
the camera's field of view and not occluded. If the lighting is static the rough direct lighting can
also be precomputed and reused, saving computation time later [1].
16
To store the rough direct light information, voxels are used. A grid full of voxels is created and
the data from the scene full of triangle meshes are converted to the representing voxels, see
Figure 13. Then the voxels data are put in a voxel data structure. All this using GPU shaders,
and under the collective name voxelization. This means that the direct light of a scene is stored
into our voxels to be used later for calculating indirect lightning [1].
Figure 13: Voxelization results of a bunny mesh. [23]
Voxelization can be divided into three steps according to an article from NVIDIA Developer
[15].
The first step is to use the vertex shader to transform the vertex positions into the voxel grid
coordinate system. For this to work, it is important to apply an orthographic projection matrix
in the vertex shader. The orthogonal projection given by the matrix should capture the entire
scene in the visible clip-space, viewable from the camera. This means that the area to be
voxelized is transformed so that it fits into a 3D texture where the voxels will be stored for later
use. Since the entire scene needs to be voxelized, it is important that all primitives, even if
occluded, are handled in the fragment shader. Therefore, before voxelizing the scene all depth
testing needs to be disabled. Afterwards the geometry shader is used to project every primitive
on the same 2D surface, temporarily substituting the correct depth value [1][15].
Step two, during rasterization of the transformed primitives, the rasterized fragment position
together with a belonging correct depth value will exactly match its corresponding voxel grid
coordinate. This means that this fragment data can be directly saved into the voxel grid data
structure using the fragment coordinates from the shader. For this to work, it is important to
make sure that the chosen viewport resolution exactly matches the chosen voxel grid scale,
meaning the size of a fragment matches the size of a voxel. Only then, together with the orthogonal viewport frustum, the coordinates will match [15].
Finally step three, in the fragment shader. For each primitive the direct light information is
calculated and stored in the chosen voxel grid data structure instead of writing it to the 2D
conventional render target screen. The direct light computation is using the previously discussed Lambertian model [15].
17
The data is saved in a sparse 3D texture. This sparse 3D texture’s texels (minimum unit of
texture) stores the color information from the direct lighting. This data structure is a sparse
voxel data structure, meaning that areas without voxels will not take up space in the texture. It
is implemented by using the “GL_ARB_sparse_texture” extension in OpenGL. This extension
allows a 3D texture to be defined as a sparse texture. The implication of this is that chunks of
the texture can be enabled or disabled, where an enabled chunk can save data while a disabled
chunk takes no memory. Areas where there are voxels are enabled consume memory even in
the spaces where there are no voxels saved, so it is important to not enable too much of the
texture. Although only the areas with voxels could be enabled it would be very slow to do as
sparse 3D textures are not optimized for very small chunks allocated [30].
The voxels themselves can affect memory consumption even more if anisotropic voxels are
used instead of isotropic. Anisotropic voxels have different data for each voxel face; hence the
memory usage is increased sixfold for the 3D texture data structure compared to isotropic
voxels that share data for all faces. On the other hand, having six different faces allows for more
accurate illumination and avoids problems such as when the light is only on one side but will
emit on all sides. This is not normally a problem as most voxels are only visible where the light
hits the voxel, but for some thin objects light could hit one side but both sides are lit up without
anisotropic voxels [1].
Often cracks form in between the voxels, see Figure 14 [15].

manner. Based on the axis that presents the largest face area that corresponds to its greatest

Figure 15 and Figure 16.

Even so, gaps can still appear, where the rendered geometry does not fill the voxel fragments

3.3.2 Mipmapping
Mipmapping is also referenced as filtering when down sampling 3D textures. Down sampling
is used to compute an average value of a region of space in a 3D texture. For example, the
average radiance spreading throughout a region of the scene. The down sampling filter is a 2 ×
2 × 2 kernel that averages the values and stores the result in a separate lower resolution 3D
texture in a process called mipmapping. Storing these extra lower resolution textures results in
an extra memory consumption of roughly 33% [18]. By down filtering the stored voxel radiance, high frequency aliasing from sampling is reduced since averaged light information is
sampled compared to high frequency components. Shadows and corners that traditionally provide high frequency details and aliasing are thus smoothened. Generation of mipmapped textures is widely supported on modern GPUs, except for 3D textures. This means that to generate
mipmaps for a sparse 3D texture it needs to be implemented using a compute shader to do the
same thing. The difference in qualities between the filtered 3D textures can be described as a
difference in the level of details, LOD [32].

3.3.3 Cone tracing
Cone tracing can be seen as a method that approximates both Monte Carlo integration and path
tracing, all in a way that respects and satisfies the rendering equation. Different cone apertures
and formations provide different lighting approximations. Important to note, is that the fragment shader is shading what is visible in clip space and using depth test together with applying
perspective compared to the voxelization process. For each visible fragment in the fragment
shader the indirect and direct diffuse light is combined with specular direct and indirect specular
light in a way that approximates the rendering equation, like in Eq5. The four different reflection types and how they relate to cone tracing will be discussed now [1].

Direct Diffuse Illumination
Direct diffuse illumination is calculated with the same Lambertian model covered in Ch 3.2.5
and it is the same calculation as when pre-computing the injected direct lighting into the voxels
during voxelization but without mipmapping. No cones are used for this [13].
Indirect Diffuse Illumination
Indirect diffuse illumination is sampled by sending out cones from every surface in a half
sphere, see figure 17, and collecting the light stored in the voxels in the scene. A few large
cones will be traced and approximate the incoming indirect light all over the hemisphere Ω.
This is reminiscent of the rendering equation problem solution a) discussed in Ch 3.2.2 that
uses path tracing and Monte Carlo integration, to sample from discrete light directions as in
Eq2. But instead of sending out rays at random there are predetermined cones that are traced
that are fixed and symmetrically spaced. This ensures that the cones almost entirely cover the
hemisphere Ω. All the different cone sampled indirect light sums are then accumulated as the
total indirect diffuse light and are scaled. To approximate the rendering equation geometry term
the surface and normal perpendicular cone has some additional weight to it. Notice that the total
weights distributed amongst the cones are still 1. This approximation of the geometry term is
supported by the fact that the cones are a coarse approximation with high apertures making it
harder to pinpoint the incidence angle [1].
Direct Specular Illumination
Direct specular illumination giving shininess is calculated with the same Phong model covered
in Ch 3.2.6. No cones are used for this [13].
Indirect Specular Illumination
Indirect specular illumination is sampled using a smaller specular cone in the viewing direction
reflected about the normal. By using a cone instead of specular rays as discussed in Ch 3.2.6,
the angular aperture of the cone can be changed resulting in varying levels of clarity of the
mirrored reflection. The aperture is based on the roughness of the PBR material texture. This
means that this BRDF allows a more reflective material to have a smaller cone which creates
clearer mirror-like reflections. A rougher material will instead scatter the light more diffusely,

simulated with a wider cone leading to a more approximated indirect specular lighting sample
of the surroundings. [1]

The traced cones are approximating, through voxel sampling, the indirect light from a bigger
area rather than infinitesimal rays that sample from specific singular surface points as used in
path tracing. The main appeal of cone tracing is that the cones can with this approximation
create the illusion that many rays have been cast when only a few cones have been used. Normally when using a path tracer after rays have been cast a denoiser has to be used. A denoiser
is a technique used to decrease grainy spots and discoloration in images. Denoisers are needed
after using a path tracer since the result will be grainy and uneven and the denoiser can smooth
the result out [28]. But now the effects of denoising or doing multiple ray traces is gained for
free by using cone tracing. The sampling results of the cones are already an averaged sample
giving accurate indirect light results because of the technique [1].
By stepping along and “tracing” the cone the light data is sampled from the voxel data for each
cone trace step and so all lighting information within the cone is accumulated. What makes this
method so efficient is that the flexible LOD provided by the mipmap of the voxels is calculated
and scales with the diameter of the cone base. Meaning, even though more voxels fit as the cone
becomes bigger, instead of sampling many small voxels at a bigger cone diameter, a lower
mipmap level is used to sample fewer bigger voxels which are already averaged [1], see Figure

3.3.4 Multiple light bounces
One unique and useful aspect of voxel cone tracing is how easy it is to implement multiple
indirect light bounces and be able to render it at real time speeds. This is due to the nature of
voxels and how this method injects light into them. For a singular bounce only, the direct light
is injected into the voxel and then the standard voxel cone tracing algorithm is run on to get the
direct and indirect diffuse light together with specular light. However, it is possible to instead
of using a fragment shader to render the result of one bounce to the screen to instead use a
compute shader for an additional bounce. Afterwards each voxel in the scene will calculate and
save indirect diffuse light using cone tracing and save the result in another sparse 3D texture.
23
This way another texture with both direct light and an additional light bounce is created. It is
worth noting that only indirect specular light can be calculated using this method as specular
light requires a camera with a viewing direction to calculate [1].
Using this method does require additional memory consumption for two things. Firstly, an additional sparse 3D texture is required to save the result. This method also requires a third texture
to be used, a sparse 3D texture for storing the normal of every voxel so that the compute shader
can perform accurate calculations. Therefore, the memory consumption is tripled for creating
an additional bounce. However, for more bounces than 2 the memory consumption does not
increase further. This is because the original sparse texture used to save direct light can be
reused to store a further additional bounce. The textures can be swapped back and forth to store
an unlimited number of bounces with memory consumption in mind, but the time required does
of course increase per bounce calculated [1].
What is done here in practice is applying the solution to the rendering equation problem b)
discussed in Ch 3.2.2. That is setting a fixed number of bounces and then setting the order of
the calculation, calculating one bounce at a time for each voxel using Eq4. Then store the latest
indirect light information each bounce iteration. The result is that light simulations can get more
realistic global illumination at the cost of more cone tracing passes [1][7].

4.1.2 Voxelization of the scene
To voxelize a scene there are several steps to be taken. The steps taken are a practical implementation of the steps explained in chapter 3.3.1.
The first step is to use the vertex shader to transform the vertex positions from a mesh into the
voxel grid coordinate system. In practice this means that the scene is transformed so the coordinates fit into the sparse 3D texture directly. It is important to disable depth testing before this
step as the entire scene needs to be voxelized and not just the visible textures. Afterwards the
geometry shader is used to project every primitive on the same 2D surface for later.
The second step is during rasterization. The only things that need to be done at this stage from
the users’ end is to change the viewport resolution into the same resolution as the voxel texture
size. A voxel texture size of 5123 needs a viewport resolution of 512x512 at the time of voxelizing. This is because every pixel on the screen at every depth level will be transformed into
voxels. If there is a higher resolution there will be an overlap in voxels and if there is a lower
resolution, then there will be missing voxels. The other thing is that conservative rasterization
needs to be enabled to remove cracks in the voxelization. This step is done behind the scenes
with all the work being done in the first and final step, but it is still important.
The final step is in the fragment shader where the voxels are saved to the texture. In the fragment
shader a shadow map is used to calculate the direct light that is saved in each voxel. This is

Specular radiance
Specular radiance is dependent on where the camera is and how light would bounce on the
surface to hit the camera. The equation can therefore only be done in real time and not precomputed and stored in a voxel texture.
𝑅𝑒𝑓𝑙𝑒𝑐𝑡 𝐷𝑖𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛 = 𝑛𝑜𝑟𝑚𝑎𝑙𝑖𝑧𝑒(𝐼 − 2.0 ∗ 𝑑𝑜𝑡(𝑁, 𝐼) ∗ 𝑁)
where 𝐼 is the incoming camera direction and N is the surface normal. The result is a vector
which points in the direction of the reflected direction of the surface.
Afterwards cone tracing is used to receive the final specular light. Since this implementation
uses PBR, the roughness of the surface is known and therefore the cones diameter is based on
it. The rougher the texture the bigger the diameter. This aims to represent that a rougher more
uneven surface spreads light more than a reflective and smooth surface. The cone is sent out in
the reflected direction from the surface normal with a diameter of close to 1° of the hemisphere
while the surface is completely smooth. This diameter increases with the roughness of the surface. It increases up to a maximum of 180° coverage of the hemisphere when a surface is completely rough to emulate the effect discussed in chapter 3.3.3.

Where 𝑐𝑜𝑛𝑒 𝑡𝑟𝑎𝑐𝑒 is the function implemented based on Figure 21.

function the program does such as rendering, light calculation, voxelization of scene etc. The

6 Conclusion and Future work
6.1 Discussion
Although voxel cone tracing is an old technique that is not used much by the video game industry anymore, mostly because of the memory issues discussed earlier, it is still a technique
that renders mostly realistic lighting with high performance. It is also a technique which is
appropriate for complex scenes with dynamic lighting.
With modern graphics cards having a bigger VRAM capacity and many modern games wanting
to utilize ray tracing there is a possibility that voxel cone tracing might see more mainstream
attention in the future. Especially in video games where the scenes are somewhat small with a
lot of complexity and/or scene variance. Perhaps in the future the inherent memory consumption issue with Voxel Cone Tracing will be completely solved by hardware advancements. But
at the current time many video games will have scenes which are too big to use Voxel Cone
Tracing effectively without some compromises with current hardware.
The results show that voxel cone tracing can create realistic lighting in images with real time
speeds for one light bounce and almost real time speeds for two light bounces, although with
some visual artifacts and a high memory consumption. The most unexpected result was the time
taken to mipmap the voxel textures. As mentioned earlier in Ch 3.3.2, Nvidia does not have a
built-in method for mipmapping a 3D texture on the GPU. Mipmapping therefore had to be
implemented from scratch to run in a compute shader. This result is probably a consequence of
a poor implementation of mipmapping 3D textures as it should theoretically be much faster.
Another somewhat unexpected result is the slow speed of the second light bounce calculation.
Calculating the bounce using the fragment shader and rendering the scene took only a fraction
of the time that the compute shader took to calculate one light bounce. A big part of the difference in computational time is probably that the fragment shader does one cone trace per triangle
in the camera's field of view. This contrasts with the compute shader which cone traces every
single voxel in the scene to make sure that every voxel is as accurate as possible. This means
that the compute shader workload scales linear with the size of the voxel texture while the
fragment shader which does the final bounce scales with the complexity of the scene. The fragment shader computation scales with the number of triangles in the scene and the camera's field
of view while the compute shader scales with the voxel texture size. The implication of this is
that the time required for Voxel Cone Tracing in the fragment shader is mostly dependent on
the complexity of the scene. This contrasts with the Voxel Cone Tracing done in the compute
shader which is mostly reliant on how large the voxel texture size is. For comparison the Sponza
scene has around 260 000 triangles while a voxel texture of size 5123 has around 134 000 000
voxels. Which means that the compute shader has a maximum of around 516 times as many
operations as the fragment shader.
38
6.1.1 How do different voxel sizes and light behavior affect performance?
Table 2 in the results show that the voxel texture size heavily affects the performance in some
key areas, most notably during mipmapping and saving the additional bounce to the voxel texture using a compute shader. This is an expected result as the computational complexity increases linearly as the texture sizes increase. To create an additional bounce using the compute
shader with 2563 voxel texture size the number of calls to the compute shader is 16 777 216.
Compare that to a texture size of 5123 which must do 134 217 728 calculations, an increase of
8 times as many calls. It is important to note that many of the calls to the compute shader result
in early exits as most of the calls will end up in texture spots where there are no voxels saved
and thus no calculations needed.
One solution to the problem discussed is to only call the compute shader for only at spots where
the sparse voxel texture is known to be allocated to reduce redundant GPU work. An implementation of this was initially tested but due to time constraints this was decided against as
some smart solutions are needed. For example, calling for maximum GPU efficiency, few compute shaders with a lot of work is wanted instead of many small work groups. This means that
for a workable implementation there needs to be a system to determine where there are clusters
of voxels in the texture and call the compute shader to calculate a big area instead of individual
voxels.
Overall, the performance speed of the actual Voxel Cone Tracing was incredibly fast for a single bounce. Creating the highest level of detail voxels and then calculating a single bounce took
only a few milliseconds as table 2 shows. As mentioned earlier the performance issues were
mainly the mipmapping of textures at higher resolutions as well as the calculation of a second
light bounce at higher texture sizes. It was expected that these operations would take a big
portion of the rendering time, but the time taken for larger texture sizes shows that the implementation has some problems. Calculating a second bounce in a compute shader should not
take around 50 times longer than calculating the second bounce while using a larger texture in
a fragment shader. In conclusion, the performance of Voxel Cone Tracing with only a single
light bounce is fast at every texture size, but more bounces do not scale as well with an increase
in texture size.
6.1.2 How do different voxel sizes and light behavior affect memory
consumption?
Memory consumption is one of the biggest issues with Voxel Cone Tracing and the results
show this quite clearly. As table 3 shows, using a sparse texture with a voxel texture size of
2563 consumes 25 MB compared to a voxel texture size of 5123 consumes 146 MB. This might
not seem like much, but it comes with a lot of computational cost to update the textures. For
many video games and other real-life usages, the scenes are many times larger than the Sponza
scene used in this thesis. This means that for many other usages it will not be enough with the
smaller textures as the voxels will be more spread out to cover a bigger area. The consequence
of this is a requirement for more voxels which means that the memory consumption can increase
heavily.
39
While calculating multiple light bounces the memory consumption triples as mentioned in
chapter 3.3.4. The reason for this is the need for three sparse 3D textures instead of just one.
Memory consumption is therefore a bigger problem for implementations of Voxel Cone Tracing with multiple bounces. This is probably one of the reasons for poorer performance with
multiple light bounces. While using larger texture sizes, more memory needs to be read and
written to each frame which can often be a big bottleneck. The increased memory consumption
is probably also one of the reasons why almost no implementations of Voxel Cone Tracing use
multiple light bounces. It is worth noting that the memory requirement will always be three
times the normal usage as brought up in chapter 3.3.4. The implications of this are that if the
memory consumption of Voxel Cone Tracing with a single bounce is trivial in the future, then
the extra consumption of multiple bounces would most likely also be trivial.
6.1.3 How do different voxel texture sizes and light behavior affect image quality?
By comparing the side-by-side pictures with different light bounces the observation can be
made that more bounces increase the overall lighting since the indirect lighting contribution has
been increased. This was to be expected since the second light bounce is calculated using information the first bounce created as in Eq4. Furthermore, by comparing the voxel texture sizes
it can be observed that lower voxel texture resolutions tend to lead to too strong ambient indirect
lighting, i.e. a lighter scene. A voxel texture size of 5123 instead still provides indirect lighting
but overall gives a more realistic amount of indirect light even if a bit dimmer. This is to be
expected as smaller voxels more accurately represent the scene. For example, the color bleeding
because of light leaking from the thin curtain objects in figure 25 with a texture size of 5123 is
much less than in the other renders with a lower voxel texture size. This is because the curtain
is more accurately represented by the smaller sized voxels. It was clear that when comparing
the Voxel Cone Tracing renders to the Blender renders that the Voxel Cone Tracing render has
too much light leaking through. Too much light leakage is not a detrimental effect as it can
often look quite realistic, but it is still an error.
Both the frequency, exact locations and amount of miscolor of the visual artifacts change
greatly with the voxel texture size and number of bounces. The gravity of the discolored visual
artifacts increases with two bounces, comparing the images in figure 26. This is because when
calculating the second bounce by cone tracing, the sampled values are already built on cone
trace approximation. Since sampling directions are always fixed and not random, it means that
for each bounce the artifact will continue to sample from the same inaccurate voxel. This sampled inaccurate voxel could for example be a victim of light leakage due to isotropic faces. The
implementation created only implemented one or two light bounces but could easily be increased to more, although at a cost of performance. The one thing to note which was mentioned
earlier is the increased risk for light leaking and artifacts from multiple bounces
The Voxel Cone Tracing implementation has created global illumination as is shown by the
rendered images in the results. Side effects are shown such as smooth shadows, color bleeding,
indirect and direct lighting. All three texture sizes hold up quite well compared to Blender visually but comparing them directly reveals light artifacts that lowers the image quality. It can
also be noted how the artifact locations change between different voxel texture sizes. This is
probably because of different LODs providing different amounts of inaccurate voxels.
40
However, true for all texture sizes is that artifacts almost exclusively appear on concavities and
creases. Ambient occlusion helps reduce this but does not seem to solve this issue entirely.
Perhaps with some further tweaking of parameters this issue could be resolved better in the
future.
6.2 Further improvements
There are many areas of improvement for the current implementation of Voxel Cone Tracing
in this thesis. The key issues as outlined earlier are light leaks, memory consumption and performance handling large 3D textures. With more optimizations the program could probably
reach real time dynamic light with playable frame rates at higher voxel counts with more
bounces. Another big improvement would be to only update the voxels when needed, that is if
there is no possibility of a voxel being changed then there is no need to calculate anything for
that voxel. Finally, a possible big improvement to the compute shader where an additional
bounce is calculated would be to only do calculations on areas which are dense with voxels
instead of sending the compute shader to the entire 3D texture and checking if there is a voxel
there. This would theoretically increase the speed of the compute shader by around 4 times for
5123 textures as the result of table 2 shows that about 27% of the texture is dense with voxels.
Hopefully this method can be returned to later to implement these improvements and compare
the results to further quantify how good different methodologies are.
6.3 Conclusion
Voxel Cone Tracing has recently started gaining traction again utilizing recent progressions in
modern hardware combined with several new techniques for improving the method. A big reason for this is that Voxel Cone Tracing as shown in this thesis can provide realistic lighting
with real time speeds for relatively complex scenes with several interesting properties. The
capability to easily calculate several light bounces and have a method whose computational
cost is mostly fixed to the amount of voxels used instead of the complexity of the scene is
extremely useful in many cases. With further improvements Voxel Cone Tracing could prove
to be extremely useful in the future, especially with less concern for the memory consumption
of the method.
Voxel Cone Tracing is especially worth investigating more in the future for certain types of
games where there are smaller scenes with a lot of change to really use Voxel Cone Tracings’
strengths. Overall Voxel Cone Tracing is a technique which warrants more research and usage.
It has, as previously noted, gained more traction again in later years as hardware has improved,
and hopefully one day Voxel Cone Tracings’ full potential of beautiful and fast lighting can be
implemented in games or other interactive media. For now, it is nonetheless an incredibly interesting method with many unique properties worth investigating further in the future.
