what deferred rendering is
Here's an extremely brief intro: https://youtu.be/nSL8cOxtsz4
Basically you have to set up a renderer with four render buffers and you execute three passes:
Loop over all scene objects and render their depth, normals, albedo colors, and other material metadata (usually a material type id and parameters such as shininess) to the multiple renderbuffers. Important implementation detail; this happens in one step, you can render to multiple buffers simultaneously.
Loop over all lights and render their lighting contributions to a lighting render buffer.
Render the final image in screen space using all the buffers.
That looks something like this (color, normals, depth, lights, final image): http://3.bp.blogspot.com/-RuoVTYRh-wE/Teq7UVwSN8I/AAAAAAAABMY/VlfL8R6wGJA/s1600/deferredshading101.png
Optionally you can run all sorts of screen space effects during or after phase 3, with screen space ambient occlusion being a common example.
comparison to forward rendering
This in contrast with a forward renderer, where you have only one phase where you immediately try to compute the final pixel color including all light contributions for every scene object.
Both approaches have their pros and cons. You can probably see how all your shaders will look wildly differently in a deferred rendering approach, compared to a forward rendering.
Usually in forward rendering people just have a fixed amount of lights uniforms in their mesh shaders, and that's the source of most limitations on dynamic lights. The rest is just prebaked into textures while designing scenes.
In deferred rendering lights and surfaces are rendered independently so the shaders are not limiting anything there. It all depends on how much information you can manage to cram into the four render buffers for the final composition phase.
relationship with transparency
In #108 almar added a "phase 2" to our renderer to blend together transparent objects. It's not really comparable, as his transparency solution could probably be applied equally to a deferred renderer.
I hope you can see now how deferred rendering enables an arbitrary amount of lights to be supported rather trivially, whereas a forward renderer will have trouble supporting that.
shadows
On topic: none of this makes shadow mapping any easier, as far as I know. Deferred rendering does makes SSAO and lots of lights easier.
So I guess that's maybe a question for this issue.. what kind of shadows would be required? There's many different techniques with very different results. This article presents a pretty good overview: https://www.techspot.com/article/1998-how-to-3d-rendering-lighting-shadows/
I guess some combination of SSAO and shadow mapping would make most sense? Not sure what variant of shadow mapping is the current industry staple though...
SSAO
Anyway, SSAO would require you to have normals and depths available for every pixel on screen. That means adjusting all shaders or rerendering the entire scene with a special shader. You can see how in deferred rendering you already have this information available.
For this reason, typically in forward rendering people just add ambient occlusion to some specific materials and shaders, and don't do it in screen space.
Also see this article: https://vr.arvilab.com/blog/ambient-occlusion
Shadow mapping
This requires rendering the scene again from each shadow casting light's point of view and outputting the depth information to a texture. Then you need to use this while rendering everything else so you can figure out if something is reachable by a light source.
There are lots of challenges here though, since this approximates a light source to be point and that type of light source doesn't cast soft shadows.
This topic is too deep for a GitHub post so I'll stop here :)

Great overview @Korijn!
Some practical comments.
Cons and pros of deferred rendering
Some tricks mentioned in the video you linked (like limiting the effect of a light to a certain volume) can be applied to forward rendering too. So I don't see how the performance benefit can be that large. Maybe I am missing something.
If I break down the advantages / disadvantages of deferred rendering:
Pro: The light is calculated per-pixel instead of per fragment (generally less work, but culling already solves most of this?).
Pro: In our case we could calculate lighting on the final resolution (not the high-ress SSAA).
Con: Per-pixel means transparent surfaces cannot be individually lit.
Con: No per-object material properties (unless we output that as well). E.g. standard lighting module not possible / much harder?
Comment: you could still output fragments with a zero-length normal to signal that it should not be lit.
Conclusion: it looks like forward rendering is more suitable for us.
General comments
In #108 we already require that all shaders change how the output fragment is "applied". If we also require specifying a normal here, that opens the door for some interesting post-processing, including SSAO. But let's have that discussion once #108 is resolved (merged or replaced with another strategy).
Once we have lighting, I think it would make sense to provide a function to apply lighting that can be used in all shaders. Once we have that, maybe it's possible to let the renderer decide on doing either forward or deferred rendering without having to change the individual shaders :)
