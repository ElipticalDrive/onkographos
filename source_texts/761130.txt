A BSTRACT
3D Gaussian avatars support fast rendering, however, their real-time animation is
often challenged by the costly neural inference. We address this bottleneck and
show that the animation of pretrained avatar models can be closely approximated
by a linear combination of identity-independent blendshapes. Building on this
finding, we introduce GALA (Gaussian Animation via Linear Approximation), a
distillation method that replaces per-frame heavy neural decoding with a shallow
coefficient predictor and a linear blend. To improve fidelity and reduce memory requirements, we propose to construct the basis using block-local PCA under a rendering-aware metric and a memory budget. Our method learns a shallow MLP network to predict blendshape coefficients and applies to various animation architectures without retraining original models. We validate GALA by
accelerating the inference of three distinct avatar models for 3D animation of
facial expressions and full-bodies with clothing dynamics. Across these models, our distillation generalizes to held-out identities and reduces CPU animation cost by up to three orders of magnitude while preserving most of the rendering quality. Excellent results of our method confirm the shared linear structure of learned avatar representations and enable highly efficient and accurate animation at frame rates reaching up to 60fps on mobile devices. with clothing dynamics (Yu et al., 2025; Fazylov et al., 2025; Kirschstein et al., 2026; Kwon et al.,
2026). However, efficient rendering does not imply efficient animation. Many models evaluate a
substantial neural network at every frame to update Gaussian attributes from expression or pose parameters, and this per-frame computation, which we call the model’s animation path, can dominate
inference cost and limits deployment on CPUs and mobile devices.
The cost of neural animation raises the question of how much of the computation performed by a
pretrained avatar model is necessary at inference time. The complexity of its nonlinear decoder does
not establish that its outputs require an equally complex representation, and deformations across
identities may share a compact linear structure even when the architecture and the training objective
do not impose one. In this work, we show that the animation outputs of diverse pretrained Gaussian avatar models can be closely approximated by an identity-specific neutral avatar and a linear
combination of identity-independent blendshapes, which we obtain by analyzing Gaussian attribute
residuals relative to the neutral state across identities and driving signals. Crucially, a basis extracted
once from a model also captures the animation of identities excluded from basis construction, since
identity-dependent motion is retained through the blendshape coefficients while the deformation
basis is shared.
Linear representations have a long history in face and body modeling (Blanz & Vetter, 1999; Li et al.,
2017b). More recently, Gaussian blendshape methods have demonstrated efficient photorealistic
animation with bases fitted to individual subjects (Ma et al., 2024; Zielonka et al., 2025), while
systems such as LAM adopt a predefined animation rig (He et al., 2025). Our work investigates
a different setting, extracting a reusable basis from a trained model that represents many identities
(Table 1). The basis approximates deformations learned by the original network and can be reused
for new subjects without reconstructing it, where identity independence refers to sharing within one
pretrained model.
Building on this finding, we introduce a Gaussian blendshape distillation strategy, which we call
GALA (Gaussian Animation via Linear Approximation). Our method constructs separate bases for
Gaussian attribute groups and spatial regions using block-local principal component analysis under
a rendering-aware metric, which prioritizes deformations that matter for visual fidelity, and allocates
components within a memory budget according to their estimated image-space error reduction per
stored byte. A shallow coefficient predictor, conditioned on identity and driving signals, then learns
to control the extracted basis, so that animation requires only coefficient prediction and a linear
blend added to the cached neutral avatar. The procedure requires no retraining of the original model.
We apply our approach to three existing models with distinct architectures and training objectives, the hosts of our distillation: AGORA (Fazylov et al., 2025) for head generation, FlexAvatar (Kirschstein et al., 2026) for one-shot head reconstruction, and DynaAvatar (Kwon et al.,
2026) for full-body reconstruction with clothing dynamics. GALA closely approaches animation
accuracy of approximated host models and consistently outperforms other baselines on respective
benchmarks. Crucially, our method increases CPU animation speed of host models by up to three
orders of magnitude and enables on-device photorealistic 3D animation at 60 fps. Figure 1 illustrates
qualitative results of our method. In summary, our contributions are threefold:
1. Identity-independent linearization of neural animation. We demonstrate that animation
produced by complex pretrained Gaussian avatar networks can be closely approximated us2

R ELATED W ORK
Animatable avatar models. Recent work on photorealistic avatars explores several approaches.
3D-aware GANs trained from unstructured images collections generate heads as triplanes (Chan
et al., 2022) or Gaussians (Kirschstein et al., 2024; Hyun & Heo, 2024; Barthel et al., 2025), and
their animatable versions condition generation on the parameters of morphable models through deformation fields or expression branches (Sun et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023; Bergman
et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025; Fazylov et al., 2025). One-shot reconstruction methods regress an animatable head from a single image, either by using 3D GANs during training (Deng et al., 2024a;b;
Tran et al., 2024) or by direct supervised reconstruction from tracked video (Chu et al., 2024; Chu
& Harada, 2024; He et al., 2025; Kirschstein et al., 2026), and diffusion priors pursue the same
goal with synthesised supervision (Taubner et al., 2025b;a). Per-subject methods optimise Gaussian
avatars from videos of particular people (Qian et al., 2024; Giebenhain et al., 2024; Chen et al.,
2024). Work on full-body animation follows similar approaches. One-shot full-body reconstruction
methods (Qiu et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2025; Sim & Moon, 2025) articulate Gaussians by SMPLX skinning (Pavlakos et al., 2019), while DynaAvatar (Kwon et al., 2026) adds pose-dependent
clothing dynamics using a transformer conditioned on pose history. We apply our approach to two
recent host models for head animation AGORA (Fazylov et al., 2025), FlexAvatar (Kirschstein et al.,
2026) and one host model for full-body animation DynaAvatar (Kwon et al., 2026), and demonstrate
significant improvement of their inference speed.
Linear face and body models. Linear representations have a long history in modeling human shape
and appearance. Eigenfaces represent facial images in a low-dimensional PCA subspace (Sirovich
& Kirby, 1987; Turk & Pentland, 1991), while active appearance models capture variations in shape
and texture (Cootes et al., 2001). Three-dimensional morphable models extend this principle to
facial geometry and appearance learned from scans (Blanz & Vetter, 1999). For animation, blendshape models express deformations as weighted combinations of offsets from a neutral shape (Parke,
1972; Lewis et al., 2014), and multilinear models disentangle identity and expression variation (Vlasic et al., 2005; Cao et al., 2014). Modern parametric models, including FLAME (Li et al., 2017b)
and SMPL-X (Pavlakos et al., 2019), combine learned shape and corrective blendshapes with articulated skinning to represent expressive faces and bodies; see Egger et al. (2020) for a review
of 3D morphable face models. These representations provide the driving parameters for the avatar
models considered in our work. We show that the resulting neural animation outputs themselves admit a compact linear approximation: an identity-shared blendshape basis over Gaussian attributes,
extracted from a pretrained network without direct fitting to scans.
Photorealistic blendshape models. Recent work extends blendshape representations to photorealistic Gaussian avatars. Methods such as 3D Gaussian Blendshapes (Ma et al., 2024),
HeadGaS (Dhamo et al., 2024), Gaussian Déjà-vu (Yan et al., 2025), RGBAvatar (Li et al., 2025),
GEM (Zielonka et al., 2025) and WebAvatar (Zhan et al., 2026) learn subject-specific bases over
Gaussian attributes or latent features from monocular or multi-view observations. RGBAvatar
predicts blending coefficients from tracked expression parameters, while GEM extracts a basis
from a trained subject-specific avatar network and predicts its coefficients from images. Similarly,
SqueezeMe (Iandola et al., 2025) distils per-subject full-body Gaussian avatars into linear corrective
bases for efficient headset rendering. These methods establish the effectiveness of linear representations for individual avatars, but require a separate basis for each subject. Fixed-rig approaches such
as LAM (He et al., 2025) instead support efficient animation through a predefined FLAME-based
articulation model. Differently from previous work, our method extracts a shared identity-agnostic
blendshape basis from a pretrained neural model. The basis captures deformations learned by that
model and is reused unchanged for identities unseen during extraction, extending Gaussian blendshape distillation from individual subjects to models of diverse identities.

M ETHOD
Our goal is to accelerate pretrained Gaussian avatar models (host models) by replacing their perframe neural decoding with a fast inference based on linear blendshape representations. We first
introduce a generic host model and its animation parameters in Section 3.1. Section 3.2 then presents
main parts of our method, namely the construction of identity-shared basis and prediction of its
coefficients from animation parameters.
3.1
P RELIMINARIES
We consider avatar models that represent a subject using n 3D Gaussians rendered by differentiable
splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023). Each Gaussian is described by a d-dimensional attribute vector, which
includes position, scale, rotation, opacity, and appearance, with additional skinning weights where
required. Avatar animation is controlled by parameters derived from parametric face or body models
such as FLAME (Li et al., 2017b) or SMPL-X (Pavlakos et al., 2019). We denote the animation
driving input by θ and its neutral setting by θ0 . For models with temporal conditioning, θ includes
the required motion history. An identity descriptor w, either supplied directly or extracted from an
input image, remains fixed throughout an animation sequence. We define pretrained host models
as A(w, θ) → G ∈ Rnd , where the output concatenates the attribute vectors of the n Gaussians.
Importantly, Gaussian indices in the model output G must have consistent correspondence across
variations in identity and animation parameters. This constraint is typically satisfied by existing
models which initialize Gaussians on a shared UV grid or template-anchored point (Kirschstein
et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2025; Fazylov et al., 2025; Kwon et al., 2026).

Because residuals from different identities are factorized jointly, the resulting basis describes shared
animation variation. It can also be applied to identities excluded from extraction, provided that
Gaussian indices retain the correspondence specified in Section 3.1. However, this simple construction has three limitations. Attribute groups have different units and scales, global components incur
storage costs even for localized deformations, and Euclidean attribute error does not directly reflect
rendering quality. We address these limitations through the following modifications.

Per-attribute and spatially local decomposition. We first separate Gaussian attributes into
groups a, each containing da channels, such as position, scale, rotation, opacity, and appearance.

allows their representation capacities to be selected independently.

components over the entire avatar when only a small region contributes to the motion. Following

E XPERIMENTS
4.1
E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP
Host models. We apply our method to three host models with distinct architectures.
AGORA (Fazylov et al., 2025) generates heads with a GAN and animates canonical Gaussians
with a separate convolutional deformation branch conditioned on FLAME expression and jaw parameters. FlexAvatar (Kirschstein et al., 2026) reconstructs a head from a single image and regenerates the attributes of all Gaussians at every frame with a transformer decoder conditioned on a
FLAME-derived expression code. DynaAvatar (Kwon et al., 2026) reconstructs a full-body avatar
with clothing dynamics from a single image and regenerates the Gaussians at every frame with a
dynamic transformer conditioned on the current and 15 preceding SMPL-X poses. See Appendix B
for more details.
Implementation details. The coefficient network of every host model is a shallow MLP, and for
DynaAvatar it also depends on the window of preceding SMPL-X poses that the host model reads.
The evaluation protocol of FlexAvatar fits the identity representation w to the input images of every
test subject, and our model follows it with a per-subject enrollment that fine-tunes the coefficient
network on outputs of the host model. We measure speed as the CPU time of one animation step,
i.e. the computation of the Gaussian attributes Ĝ from the animation parameters θ without rendering,
on the same desktop CPU for all methods. Appendix D and Table 5 give the settings of every host
model.
Training data. Our method generates training data by running host models on samples of their
inputs (wi , θi ). We take these samples from a subset of the training data of the corresponding host
models and use them both to construct the basis and to train the coefficient network. For FlexAvatar
we use 1,200 training identities with 8 expressions each, and for DynaAvatar we use frames sampled
from its training sequences. AGORA is a generative model, hence we sample 10,000 identities from
its latent space and combine each of them with 6 expressions of its training set FFHQ (Karras et al.,
2019). The results in Tables 2–4 and Figure 4 are reported for identities excluded from this data.

Table 2 shows that all three distilled models closely follow their host models and outperform the
competing methods on most metrics. For AGORA, FID increases from 3.17 to 3.47, while the
animation and identity metrics remain within 0.023 of the host model and are the best (AED-jaw,
ID, APD) or second best (AED) among the other animatable models. For DynaAvatar, our model
has a 0.64 dB lower PSNR than its host and outperforms all competing methods on all three quality
metrics, e.g. by more than 1.9 dB in PSNR. For FlexAvatar, our model obtains the best results on 8
of the 12 quality metrics, including one tie. Only LAM is competitive on portrait animation, where
it has a 0.34 dB higher PSNR and the same LPIPS in self-reenactment. On Ava256 single-shot, our
model stays within 0.03 dB PSNR of its host, which outperforms the compared one-shot methods
by more than 3.8 dB PSNR under its published protocol (Kirschstein et al., 2026). The distilled
model therefore retains this lead. With respect to its host, our model stays within 1 dB PSNR in
self-reenactment and within 0.03 dB (single-shot) and 0.2 dB (few-shot) on Ava256.
At the same time, for the three tested host models our method reduces CPU time of their animation
steps as 154→5.5ms, 234→4.4ms and 42.9s→6.1ms (16.1ms with skinning included) respectively
(Table 5). This corresponds to speed-ups of about ×28, ×54 and ×7,000 times, or ×2,700 times
for DynaAvatar when its skinning is included. With an animation step below 7ms on a desktop CPU
for all three host models, our method enables real-time animation, even on a mobile phone, for host
models that were not capable of it before. AGORA runs at 1 frame per second on the phone, and
DynaAvatar, which models garment dynamics, reaches only 0.3 frames per second even on a desktop
GPU, whereas our distilled models run at 60 frames per second on the phone (mFPS in Table 2).
The video on our project page demonstrates all three distilled models running in the web browser
of a mobile phone (Appendix C).

Design choices of the basis construction. We evaluate the representation power of our basis, i.e.
how well it reconstructs the output of the host model independently of coefficient prediction, by the
PSNR between the renderings of our model with projected coefficients c⋆ and of the host model on
held-out identities, at the same memory budget for all compared variants. Table 3a compares our
full method with simple PCA, the single global basis of Section 3.2.1 with the rank given by the
budget, and with variants that revert one design choice while the others are kept. Simple PCA is
9.7, 11.5 and 13.2 dB below our full method for AGORA, FlexAvatar and DynaAvatar. Most of this
gap comes from the two design choices that decide how the memory is spent: a joint basis over all
attributes of a block costs 4.7, 3.6 and 8.8 dB, since a joint component stores all d channels instead
of da and couples attributes with different scales, and scoring the components by explained variance
instead of the value va,j,k costs 5.3, 2.7 and 13.3 dB, since explained variance is not comparable
between attribute groups with different units and spends most of the memory on attributes with little
effect on the image. Rendering-aware PCA itself improves the directions of the basis by a further
0.25, 0.35 and 0.58 dB over Euclidean PCA (β = γ = 0) with the same numbers of components.

Compared with a global basis per attribute group (B = 1), B = 32 local blocks improve PSNR by
8.2, 6.9 and 8.6 dB. A component of a local basis stores nj da instead of nda values, hence more
components fit into the same memory and they can be concentrated in the regions with complex
motion. PSNR increases monotonically with B over the tested range, and we use B = 32 in all
other experiments. For larger B we observed no consistent improvement with predicted coefficients,
since more components make the coefficients harder to regress.
Comparison to alternative distillation. To further demonstrate the value of our linear blendshape
distillation approach, we compare it to a generic method for neural network distillation. Knowledge
distillation (Bucilă et al., 2006; Hinton et al., 2015) trains a smaller student network to reproduce
the outputs of a frozen teacher on the inputs of the teacher. We distill students that are thinner
and shallower copies of the animation network of corresponding host models. We train students of
multiple sizes using the same training data as we use for training our method. Figure 4 compares
this standard distillation with GALA for a range of memory budgets. For all three host models, none
of the distilled networks approaches our method in terms of speed and accuracy. Additional details
of this study are available in Appendix G.
5
L IMITATIONS AND F UTURE W ORK
While GALA already shows excellent results, its coefficient prediction leaves room for improvement. With projected coefficients c⋆ our models reproduce their host models closely, whereas with
predicted coefficients their PSNR with respect to the host model is lower by 5.7 dB for AGORA and
by 12.3 dB for FlexAvatar at a memory budget of 48 MB, and a larger budget mainly improves the results with projected coefficients (Figure 4). Another direction for improvement concerns the spatial
partition, which is currently fixed to B = 32 blocks for all host models. Meanwhile an optimal partition may depend on the motion and could be selected jointly with the numbers of components Ka,j .
Finally, our method inherits the limitations of host models, and our results suggest that avatar models
could be trained directly with an identity-shared blendshape basis, which we leave for future work.
6
C ONCLUSION
We introduced GALA, which replaces per-frame neural decoding of pretrained Gaussian avatar
models with a shallow coefficient network and a linear combination of blendshapes. To the best
of our knowledge, GALA is the first method to extract and leverage a blendshape basis shared
across identities, whereas previous Gaussian blendshape methods require a separate basis for every
subject. We apply GALA to three diverse avatar models for head and full-body animation and

demonstrate excellent computational gains while closely preserving the quality of original models
and outperforming recent baselines. GALA requires no retraining of original models, it accelerates
CPU execution up to three orders of magnitude and enables animation to run at 60 fps on a mobile
phone. These results demonstrate the effectiveness and broad applicability of proposed Gaussian
blendshape distillation for efficient, high-fidelity on-device avatar animation.

H OST M ODELS
We apply our method to three host models, spanning different architectures, training objectives,
and animation mechanisms. Together, these state-of-the-art models cover dedicated deformation
networks, decoders with integrated expression conditioning, and temporally conditioned full-body
animation. Table 5 lists the numbers of Gaussians and the attribute groups of the host models
together with the settings of our distilled models.
AGORA (Fazylov et al., 2025) is a Gaussian avatar GAN trained adversarially on unstructured images of human heads. It follows the dual-branch design of Next3D (Sun et al., 2023) and GAIA (Yu
et al., 2025), using GGHead’s UV-space Gaussian representation (Kirschstein et al., 2024). A mapping network produces an identity code w, and an identity branch generates canonical Gaussian
attributes on a UV grid aligned with FLAME. A separate deformation branch, conditioned on w
and FLAME expression and jaw parameters θ, predicts attribute residuals. The identity branch is
evaluated once per subject, whereas the deformation branch is evaluated at every frame. Identities
can be generated by latent sampling or obtained by image inversion.
FlexAvatar (Kirschstein et al., 2026) reconstructs an animatable head from a single image using an encoder–decoder trained with rendering losses on monocular and multi-view portrait videos.
The encoder produces an identity representation in template UV space. A FLAME-derived expression code conditions the decoder through cross-attention. Each animation frame therefore requires
evaluating the full decoder to regenerate the attributes of all Gaussians. This model tests whether
distillation remains effective when animation is integrated into the decoder rather than implemented
by a separate deformation branch.
DynaAvatar (Kwon et al., 2026) reconstructs an animatable full-body avatar with clothing dynamics from a single image. Image encoders and a static transformer produce identity features on the
SMPL-X template once per subject. A dynamic transformer, conditioned on a window of the current
and 15 preceding SMPL-X poses, regenerates the attributes of template-anchored Gaussians at every frame, which are then posed by linear blend skinning. This model tests our method on full-body
motion with garment dynamics that depend on the pose history. Its neural animation is by far the
most expensive of the three models (Table 5).

Our real-time demos run in a web browser on desktop and mobile devices (Figure 5). They are
built on the open-source WebGL Gaussian splat viewer of Kwok (2023), whose depth sort and splat
rasterizer we keep and whose static point cloud we replace by our animated avatar. The neutral
avatar and the blendshape basis are loaded once as textures. At every frame, the browser reads the
animation parameters of the driving sequence, evaluates the coefficient network as fragment-shader
passes on the GPU, forms the Gaussian attributes as the neutral avatar plus the coefficient-weighted
sum of the blendshapes in a second pass, and hands the result to the sort and the rasterizer of the
viewer. No host network runs in the browser. The FlexAvatar and DynaAvatar demos can show
several avatars at once. The AGORA and FlexAvatar demos use the same viewer and differ only
in the animation parameters they read, the FLAME expression and jaw parameters and the tracked
expression code respectively, and in the sizes of the basis and the network (Table 5). The video on
our project page shows the demos in action.

Inputs and architecture. The identity descriptor w is computed once per identity. For AGORA
it is the latent code of the neutral render. For FlexAvatar it is an embedding of the avatar code
that the host model fits to the input image, produced by a small encoder with layers of 512 and
256 units that is trained jointly with the network. For DynaAvatar it consists of the pooled identity
tokens and the shape parameters of the host’s identity encoder. The animation parameters θ are the
ones the host model reads: the FLAME expression, jaw and eyelid parameters for AGORA, the
articulation code for FlexAvatar, and the window of the current and 15 preceding SMPL-X frames
for DynaAvatar. The two head models use an MLP with two hidden layers on the concatenation of
w and θ. The DynaAvatar network embeds every frame of the window with two layers, applies two
temporal convolutions with kernel size 3 over the window, concatenates the flattened result with an
embedding of w, and predicts the coefficients with two hidden layers. Its output is anchored at a
reference pose, cb = fϕ (w, θ) − fϕ (w, θref ), so that the reference pose gives the neutral avatar for
every subject. The last layer of every network outputs the K coefficients of all attribute groups and
blocks.
Training. The targets are the projected coefficients c⋆ of the residuals of the host model with
respect to the neutral avatar. The distillation loss of Section 3.2.3 is normalized per attribute group
by the coefficient energy of that group on the training data. Every training pair combines an identity
with animation parameters taken from elsewhere: an FFHQ expression for AGORA, an articulation
code sampled from all training identities for FlexAvatar, and a motion window of another training
subject for DynaAvatar, whose 1,035 identity instances are single source images of the 26 4D-Dress
subjects and 136 DNA-Rendering (Cheng et al., 2023) actors in the training data of the host model.

Benchmarks and metrics. Every distilled model is evaluated on the benchmark of its host model.
AGORA is evaluated on FFHQ (Karras et al., 2019) with the protocol of Sun et al. (2023). FID
compares the distributions of generated and real images and measures image quality, AED and AEDjaw measure the accuracy of the expression and jaw animation, ID measures the consistency of the
identity under animation, and APD measures the accuracy of the head pose. DynaAvatar is evaluated
on 4D-Dress (Wang et al., 2024), where PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS measure the pixel-wise, structural
and perceptual similarity of the animated avatar to ground-truth images. FlexAvatar is evaluated on
portrait animation on VFHQ (Xie et al., 2022) and on 3D avatar creation from one or a few images
on Ava256 (Martinez et al., 2024). Here PSNR and LPIPS measure image quality with respect to the
ground truth, AED, APD and the keypoint distance AKD measure animation accuracy, and CSIM,
the cosine similarity of face recognition features, measures identity preservation. For AGORA, the
rows of Table 2a are taken from Fazylov et al. (2025). For FlexAvatar and DynaAvatar, the host
rows of Table 2b and c are measured by us on the checkpoints released by the authors, and the rows
of the compared methods are quoted from the host papers.
Evaluation of DynaAvatar on 4D-Dress. We use the checkpoint released by Kwon et al. (2026)
and follow their evaluation protocol on 83 sequences and 12,200 frames of the 4D-Dress test split.
Our measurement of the host, 23.86 dB PSNR, 0.959 SSIM and 0.063 LPIPS, reproduces the published numbers of 23.74 dB, 0.960 and 0.064 within 0.12 dB. The host and our model are evaluated
on the same renders.
Evaluation of FlexAvatar. On VFHQ we use the authors’ main model FLEX-1 (ckpt-900k)
and follow their released VFHQ-Test protocol. On Ava256 we use the released checkpoint
FLEX-2-avat3r-eval, which according to the repository was not trained on the Avat3r test
identities. The host row, our basis and our coefficient network are all computed from this checkpoint. Kirschstein et al. (2026) specify the structure of the Ava256 protocol, namely one frontal
input frame with 10 expressions and 4 cameras per subject for the single-shot setting and four input
images per subject following Avat3r for the few-shot setting, but not the particular subjects, sequences or expression frames, and the authors did not respond to our request for them. We therefore
instantiate the protocol as follows. Kirschstein et al. (2026) evaluate on 5 subjects that are not identified, so we use all 8 unseen subjects of the authors’ released Avat3r evaluation specification and
the EXP free face sequence of each subject. The input is the subject’s most frontal calibrated
camera at the first frame of the sequence. The targets are evenly spaced frames of the sequence
excluding the input frame, each rendered from the subject’s 4 Avat3r input cameras. The host and
our model are evaluated on exactly the same identities, input images and target frames. Our numbers
for the host consequently differ from those in Kirschstein et al. (2026), e.g. 19.27 versus 16.9 dB
single-shot PSNR over 8 and 5 subjects respectively, and the numbers of the compared methods in
Table 2c are quoted from that paper. Since our method uses the host only through its outputs, the
difference between the host and our model is expected to be unchanged under the original evaluation
subset. The few-shot setting uses the authors’ released evaluation specification for the Avat3r test
identities (Kirschstein et al., 2025) with 8 subjects, 4 input views and 631 target images.
Ablations. Table 7 compares the simple PCA basis with our full method after coefficient prediction, on the benchmarks of Table 2. A coefficient network of the same architecture is trained for each
basis. Our method is better on every quality metric except the identity metrics, by 2.4 dB PSNR for
AGORA, 0.9 dB for FlexAvatar and 0.6 dB for DynaAvatar, and the higher ID and CSIM of simple PCA are a sign of under-animation. For the second rule of Table 4, the ranks Ka per attribute
group are chosen without rendering: among the splits of the memory budget between the attribute
groups, we take the one that maximizes the total value va,j,k of the retained components, the same
rendering-aware value that the greedy allocation uses.

Results. Figure 4 compares these networks with our method for a range of memory budgets. For
all three host models, none of the distilled networks is both faster and more accurate than any
of our models, and among the models that run in less than 80 ms the most accurate one is ours.
For AGORA and DynaAvatar, none of the distilled networks reaches the quality of our model of
Table 2. The best distilled AGORA network obtains an FID of 3.60 in 124 ms, whereas our model
obtains 3.47 in 5.5 ms. For FlexAvatar, only the largest transformer approaches the LPIPS of the
host model. It requires 175 ms, which is 40 times slower than our model. All distilled networks
faster than 50 ms are less accurate than our smallest model, which has a 12 MB basis and takes
2 ms. The time of our method also grows slowly with memory, e.g. a 17 times larger AGORA basis
requires 5 times more time, which we attribute mainly to the memory bandwidth of the CPU. Finally,
the difference between our models with predicted and with projected coefficients shows the effect
of coefficient prediction on the final quality. For larger budgets on FlexAvatar and DynaAvatar,
projected coefficients bring our models close to the host model, which indicates that the remaining
error is mostly due to coefficient prediction.

driven with their released pipelines: RGBAvatar with the drivers tracked by the same tracker and

Results. In self reenactment, the per-subject fits score better: they have a lower AED on the heldout frames of every dataset, and RGBAvatar a higher PSNR on INSTA and VFHQ. This is expected.
Each per-subject fit minimizes a photometric loss on the very sequence that self reenactment scores,
from one video (INSTA, VFHQ) or from 15 views of the capture (Ava256), whereas our model
never sees this sequence and is not optimized for its reconstruction. Our higher held-out PSNR on
Ava256 comes from the camera of the input photo, on which the per-subject fits were not trained.
The intended use of an avatar is cross reenactment, animation by the motion of another person, and
there our model is ahead: it has a lower AED and APD than every per-subject fit on all three datasets
and a higher CSIM on INSTA and VFHQ, whereas GEM keeps a higher CSIM on Ava256. Figures 6
to 8 show why: on their own held-out frames the per-subject fits are close to the ground truth, but
under the motion of the driver RGBAvatar breaks up at head poses absent from its training video
and GEM barely opens the mouth, whereas our model follows the driver. On 4D-Dress, WebAvatar
obtains 22.78 dB, compared with 21.82 dB for DynaAvatar + GALA and 22.93 dB for DynaAvatar.
On their own training frames, RGBAvatar reaches 33.21 dB on INSTA and 23.35 dB on Ava256,
whereas GEM reaches 16.64 dB. Two properties of GEM explain its numbers. GEM does not model
the neck and shoulders, which its pipeline masks out of its training images and which the ground
truth keeps: this region covers 3% of the pixels but 45% of GEM’s squared error, and GEM’s
own training image, scored as a render, reaches 19.3 dB. Its fit is also blurry from its first stage,
which reaches 21.2 dB against its own neck-free training images before the basis and the coefficient
regressor are fitted. On the head region alone, GEM reaches 20.05 dB on its training frames and
RGBAvatar 23.92 dB.

a moving part of the garment for DynaAvatar, which is shown in the canonical A-pose.

rows). The group is driven along the leading eigenvector of the covariance of its coefficients over
