Abstract: The finite element method usually requires regular or strongly regular families
of partitions in order to get guaranteed a priori or a posteriori error estimates. In this
paper we examine the recently invented longest-edge bisection algorithm that produces
face-to-face simplicial partitions of a given bounded polytopic domain in arbitrary space
dimension. First, we prove that the regularity of the family of partitions generated by
this algorithm is equivalent to its strong regularity. Second, we present 3d numerical
examples, which demonstrate that the technique seems to produce regular (and therefore
strongly regular) families of tetrahedral partitions. However, a mathematical proof of this
statement is still an open problem.
25
Keywords: bisection algorithm, conforming finite element method, regular family of
partitions, nested tetrahedral partitions, simplicial elements.

Introduction
When solving problems of mathematical physics by the finite element method, we have to
decompose the investigated domain into small subdomains – called elements. To achieve a
high numerical accuracy, elements should be small. In addition, at those parts where the
exact solution of a particular problem possesses some singularities, we have to use smaller
elements than those at the parts, where the exact solution is smooth enough. In this

The longest-edge bisection algorithm is one of the most popular refinement algorithm
in the finite element method, since it is very simple and can be easily applied also in
higher dimensions. Martin Stynes in his papers [26]–[29] (see also [1] and [24]) examined
the regularity of a family triangulations obtained by a recursive use of the longest-edge
bisection algorithm. This algorithm bisects simultaneously all triangles by medians to the
longest edge of each triangle in a given triangulation (see Figure 1). In this way, a family
of nested triangulations is obtained.

However, this may lead to the so-called hanging nodes and thus refined triangulations
may not all be face-to-face, in general (see Figure 1). Therefore, in [12] a modified version
of the bisection algorithm by medians was introduced, where only simplices sharing the
longest edge of the whole triangulation are bisected at each step (see Figure 2). In this
way, all produced triangulations are face-to-face and moreover, the corresponding family
of triangulations satisfies the minimum angle condition (1) with computable and known
α0 , see [12]. In [8] and [9] we modified the above bisection algorithm so that it may
also generate locally refined simplicial face-to-face partitions. This goal was achieved by
introducing a Lipschitz continuous mesh density function which tell us, which simplices
have to be chosen for bisections. We proved that this generalized algorithm also produces
nested face-to-face partitions.
Bisection-type algorithms are very favourite in finite element community, since they
enable us to perform local mesh refinement quite easily. In [3, 13] local mesh refinements
are generated by means of the famous red refinement. A given tetrahedron is first subdivided into eight tetrahedra by mid-lines (see Figure 3). Then each of its neighbouring

For arbitrary space dimension d, Kearfott [11] proved that the longest-edge algorithm
produces d-simplices whose largest diameter tends to zero. In this algorithm each dsimplex is bisected by a (d − 1)-dimensional hyperplane passing through the midpoint of
its longest edge and through its vertices that do not belong to this edge.
In [2, 10, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18] several other bisection-like algorithms are investigated,
some of them do not necessarily halve the longest edge. The longest-edge algorithm was
originally designed for solving systems of nonlinear algebraic equations (see [25, 7]). Later,
mainly due to effort of Rivara [19, 20, 21, 22, 23], bisection methods started to become
popular also in practical use of the finite element method.

Theoretical background
The convex hull of d + 1 points in Rd for d ∈ {1, 2, 3, . . . }, which are not contained in a
hyperplane of dimension d − 1, is called a d-simplex or just a simplex. The angles between
its (d − 1)-dimensional facets are called dihedral.
Next we define a simplicial partition Th over a bounded polytope Ω ⊂ Rd . We subdivide Ω into a finite number of simplices so that their union is Ω, any two simplices
have disjoint interiors and any facet of any simplex is a facet of another simplex from
the partition or belongs to the boundary ∂Ω of Ω. The discretization parameter h is the
length of the longest edge in a given simplicial partition. We see that simplicial partitions
are face-to-face.
The set F = {Th }h→0 is named a family of simplicial partitions if for any ε > 0 there
exists Th ∈ F with h < ε.

One step of the longest-edge bisection algorithm in any space dimension is defined as
follows:
I) Choose the longest edge ℓ in a given face-to-face simplicial partition.
II) Bisect each simplex S sharing ℓ by a hyperplane passing through the midpoint of
ℓ and the vertices of S that do not belong to ℓ.
From the second point II) we can easily find by induction that all partitions generated by the longest-edge bisection algorithm remain face-to-face. In fact, this algorithm
performs the so-called green refinements.
Theorem 2.1 Let d be an arbitrary space dimension and let F = {Th }h→0 be a family
of simplicial partitions of Ω generated by the longest-edge bisection algorithm. Then F is
regular if and only if it is strongly regular.

Definition 2.3 A d-simplex S is said to be similar to a d-simplex S ′ , if there exists a
nonsingular d × d-matrix A, a vector b ∈ Rd , and a positive constant c such that
1) A⊤ A = c2 I, where I is the identity matrix,

mirroring. Therefore, S and S ′ have the same dihedral angles.

Bisection of tetrahedral partitions
In this section we examine numerically the regularity properties of simplicial partitions
generated by the longest-edge bisection algorithm for d = 3. Obviously, it is enough to
study only particular tetrahedra from some initial face-to-face tetrahedral partition.
Tetrahedra have much more angles than triangles, which obviously complicates the
analysis and numerical implementation in 3d. Moreover, it is often even not possible
to formulate or observe the properties of bisected tetrahedra, which could be natural
analogues of performance of bisections in 2d.
To illustrate the latter fact, we give several observations. Set A = (−1, 0, 0), B =
(1, 0, 0), C = (−2, 2, −1), and D = (2, 2, 1). Then there are the following (dihedral)
angles at edges: 25.21◦ at |CD| = 4.47, 28.56◦ at |BD| = |AC| = 2.45, 53.13◦ at |AB| = 2,
133.09◦ at |BC| = |AD| = 3.75.
Observation 1: The largest (dihedral) angle is not opposite to the longest edge CD.
Observation 2: The tertahedron is obtuse. However, after the first bisection of the
longest edge CD the largest (dihedral) angle increases to 150.79◦. This does not happen
for obtuse triangles in 2d.
Observation 3: If we bisect the edge AD opposite the biggest (dihedral) angle 133.09◦,
then one of the new angles is bigger, namely 140.77◦ .

Conclusions
Our numerical examples of Section 3 indicate that the longest-edge bisection algorithm
seems to produce the regular (and, therefore, by Theorem 2.1, strongly regular) families
of partitions. However, a mathematical proof of this statement is an open problem.
If the family of tetrahedral partitions is proved to be regular, then an analogue of the
minimal angle condition (1) is valid. For d = 3 it says that all dihedral angles and also all
angles between edges are greater than some fixed positive angle α0 . The generalization of
the Zlámal minimum angle condition to an arbitrary space dimension is given in [6].
Regular families of tetrahedral partitions enable us to achieve the optimal interpolation order, to obtain the convergence of the finite element method, to derive efficient
a posteriori error estimates, etc. Subtetrahedra produced by the longest-edge bisection
algorithm thus do not degenerate to a plane when h → 0.
