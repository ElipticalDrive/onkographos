Abstract
Volume rendering [1] is an efficient tool to analyze and understand volumetric data in many scientific applications such
as medical imaging and computational fluid dynamics. Here,
real-time rendering at subsecond image-update rates is the
most important concern for users to interactively explore
their data. However, the use of volume rendering has been
restricted because it is still computationally. expensive.
A number of algorithms have been proposed for accelerating volume rendering. They can be broadly classified
es ray-casting. [2], splatting [3], or shear-warp algorithms
[4]. Among these approaches, the shear-warp factorization
is the most promising because it can realize semi-interactive
rendering of volume data with moderate sise without affecting image quality on a conventional graphics workstation.
By introducing optimisations such es early ray-termination
and run-length encoding to the shear-warp factorization,
Lacroate and Levoy [4] achieved single-processor performance that exceeds the performance of most parallel processing approaches to volume rendering. However, even though
the optimized shear-warp.factorisation algorithm is used,
volume rendering of a large volume data set is still time
con&ming and it is very d&cult to realize the interactive
rendering rate on a single-processor workstation.
The massive parallelism of volume rendering attracts
many researchers to developing parallel processing approaches, Two strategies have been developed to address
this challenge. The first strategy aims at the development
of co-processors or special-purpose rendering engines that
separate real-time image generation from general-purpose

For example, Gelder et al.[7] proposed
an approach that achieved high-quality volume rendering at
nearly interactive speeds with additional hardware support
for texture mapping techniques. Pflster et al.[8] developed a
special-purpose volume rendering machine, Cube-4, that is
predicted to be capable of a 30 fiames/sec frame rate for the
1O243volume data. These are special hardware-assistedvolume rendering systems for interactive volume rendering of
highquality image, but their hardware cost is still high and
rendering algorithms implemented on hardwarelose flexibility.
The second strategy is to develop parallel algorithms
for commercially available multiprocessor systems. Thanks
to the recent VLSI technology development, multiprocessor systems using high-performance superscalar microprocessors as processing elements (PEs) are available at relatively low cost. Their general-purpose property allows users

Volume rendering is an efficient tool to analyze
and understand volumetric data in many scientific applications such as medical imaging and
computational fluid dynamics. This paper presents a data-parallel volume rendering algorithm
for the shear-warp factorization of the viewing
transformation with the binary-swap composit
ing method to achieve real-time rendering. This
algorithm is suited to distributed-memory multiprocessor systems with a message-passingmechanism. Volume is subdivided into subvolumes
to be allocated to PEs. Each PE shears an allocated subvolume and generates a subvolume
image from the sheared subvolume in parallel.
In order to carry out fast compositing of subvolume images, the biiary-swap method is employed, which can keep the overheads due to compositing low. We implement the parallel shearwarp factorization algorithm with binary-swap
cornpositing on the IBM SP2 with 32 PEs, and
show volume rendering of 2562 x 128 to 2563
voxek for a screen of 2562 pixels at 15 to 22
frames/set. As a result, message-passing multiprocessor systems using our algorithm are also
suitable for achieving real-time volume rendering.

Hardware Architecture
A volume visualization system on which we have impk
mented the parallel algorithm consists of the IBM SP2 and
the SGI ONYX that are interconnected through an ATM
network with a bandwidth of 155 Mbps. The main role of
the IBM SP2 is to generate 2D image data based on the
proposed algorithm, and ONYX is only used for displaying the generated images. The IBM SP2 [17] can connect
2 to 512 RISC System/6000 POWER2 processors [18] via a
high-performancecommunication subsystem. Our IBM SP2
has 32 POWER2 nodes at a 15 11sclock. Each node has a
32 KB instruction cache, 64 KB data cache, 64bit memory
bus, and la&bit bus between the data cache and the FPU
(a 2-port bus). All the experiments were carried out during
exclusive reservation (no processes running for other users).
The network subsystem that interconnects 32 POWER%
nodes is based upon a low-latency, high-bandwidth interconnection network called the High Performance Switch (HPS).
The HPS is a bidirectional multistage interconnection network and scales the bisection bandwidth linearly with the
number of nodes while maintaining a fixed number of communication ports per processor node. The HPS operates at
49 MHz, providing a peak bandwidth of 40 MB/set in each
direction.
