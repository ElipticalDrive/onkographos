## Abstract

In volume visualization, a transfer function tailored for one volume usually does not work for other similar volumes without careful tuning. This process can be tedious and time-consuming for a large set of volumes. Leveraging the differentiability of volume rendering, this work presents a novel approach to transfer function optimization based on a reference volume and its transfer function. Using two fully connected neural networks, our approach learns a continuous 2D separable scalar-gradient transfer function that visualizes the features of interest with consistent visual properties across volumes. The resulting optimized transfer function is exportable for further interactions in domain-specific applications. Through two compelling use cases—tracking the aftermath of an asteroid blast near the ocean surface and visualizing brain white matter, grey matter, and cerebral fluid in magnetic resonance (MR) images—we demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, validated through collaboration with domain experts.

During a weekly meeting with Dr. Usman Alim and Joshua Horacsek, the concept of transferring transfer functions emerged as a fresh and unexplored area, offering intriguing research potential for a student like myself. Despite its initial promise, the journey proved far from linear. Initially, our plan involved training generative adversarial networks to forecast effective transfer functions for new volumes. With relentless effort, our weekly meetings with Usman and Joshua evolved into intense discussions spanning every aspect of our work. # Chapter 1

# Introduction

In today's world, where data grows exponentially and complexities in information processing abound, visualization emerges as a crucial tool for understanding and interpreting the world around us. It is the key to unlocking the true potential of information, turning abstract numbers and concepts into tangible insights.

Visualization stands at the intersection of science and art, blending analytical rigor with creative expression. It converts raw data into visual narratives. Whether it is deciphering genetic sequences in a laboratory, plotting market trends in a business setting, or mapping the spread of a global pandemic, visualization helps bridge the gap between complex data and informed decision-making. A glance at an on-point visualization gleans insights that would otherwise remain buried in an impenetrable mass of data.

Creating an effective visualization demands a comprehensive understanding of various factors, including the data's source, its structural characteristics, the purpose of visualization, and the intended audience. In this thesis, we focus our attention on volumetric data, a particularly intricate and valuable form of data representation.

## 1.1 Defining Volumetric Data

Volumetric data is a three-dimensional (3D) representation of objects or phenomena, capturing not just surface details but also the internal composition of the subjects. Unlike 2D images, volumetric data offers a comprehensive view, with depth information that can provide a more holistic understanding of the subject matter.

This form of data can be conceptualized as a collection of samples in the form of (x, y, z, p), where (x, y, z) represents a specific 3D spatial location, and p signifies the value associated with a particular property at

- Easy to Store in Computers: The Cartesian grid structure is akin to a 3D matrix or tensor, making it easy to store and manipulate in computer memory. This compatibility simplifies data storage, retrieval, and processing tasks, facilitating efficient algorithms and computations.

### 1.1.1 Sources and Applications of Volumetric Data

One of the most prominent sources of volume data is medical imaging, where techniques such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Computed Tomography (CT) scans generate detailed 3D representations of internal body structures. This data is crucial for diagnosing diseases, planning surgeries, and monitoring treatment responses. Here, visualization is not just a tool for interpretation; it is a critical component of medical decision-making, enabling healthcare professionals to explore intricate bodily structures and identify abnormalities.

Beyond the medical field, volumetric data forms the foundation for a myriad of simulations, including fluid dynamics simulations and structural analyses. For instance, in computational fluid dynamics (CFD), volumetric data encapsulates essential fluid properties such as velocity and pressure at various points within a 3D domain. These simulations facilitate the examination of fluid behavior, flow patterns, and interactions with solid objects. Visualizing volumetric data derived from such simulations proves invaluable, aiding practitioners in tasks such as locating vortices within fluid flows, or identifying stress concentrations within structures. Additionally, visualization enables researchers to compare and contrast multiple simulation runs or datasets, supporting decision-making processes.

The entertainment industry, particularly in gaming and filmmaking, also leverages volumetric data creatively. Here, scene elements such as water, smoke, fire, and magical effects are created using volumes for realism and artistic expression, adding depth and dynamism to virtual environments.

## 1.3 Insights into Transfer Functions

As previously mentioned, a transfer function serves to map volume properties to optical properties, such as color and opacity. Different mappings can yield varied final outputs and visualizations. Figure 1.5 illustrates the contrasting outputs of volume rendering performed on an MR head scan using three different transfer functions. This visual example shows how changing the transfer function can significantly alter the visual representation and perception of the data.

Therefore, to achieve an informative visualization, an effective transfer function is essential. A welldesigned transfer function can enhance the visibility and interpretability of non-obvious salient features within the data. A good transfer function exhibits the following properties:

- 1. Clear classification: Essential regions or units of interest are colorized in a manner that facilitates easy differentiation.

- 2. Avoids obstruction: Non-essential elements are rendered transparent to prevent them from obscuring areas of interest.
- 3. Appealing and accessible: The final visualization should possess aesthetic qualities while also considering accessibility concerns such as color blindness.

In many volume rendering applications, transfer functions are manually designed. However, this process is not without its challenges:

- Ambiguity in region mapping: It can be unclear which regions of the transfer function correspond to areas of interest, especially for users who are not intimately familiar with the dataset.
- Sensitivity to changes: Certain regions of the transfer function may be highly sensitive to small alterations, potentially leading to drastic changes in the final output.
- Need for higher-dimensional transfer functions: Some phenomena require higher-dimensional transfer functions for effective visualization, increasing the complexity as users navigate a larger parameter space.
- Limited applicability: Transfer functions designed for specific volumes may not be suitable for similar datasets, as changes in the data may necessitate adjustments to the mapping.

The final constraint (limited applicability) highlights the necessity for users to individually create transfer functions for each volume within a dataset of similar volumetric data, presenting a daunting task. This challenge serves as the primary motivation for the thesis, which will be further explored in the subsequent section.

## 2.1 Direct Volume Rendering

The visualization of a function defined on a 3D grid, commonly referred to as a volume, can take on various forms (see Section 1.1.2\). Among these, DVR stands out as a popular technique for visualizing volume data without the need to construct intermediary geometries, as implied by its name "direct". This characteristic distinguishes DVR from other volume visualization methods, such as contour surfaces [Lorensen and Cline, 1998]. At its core, DVR projects a discretely sampled 3D dataset (volume) onto a 2D plane, known as the image plane. This 2D representation can then be presented as the rendered image of the 3D volume. To employ DVR, it is necessary to specify a model that dictates how the volume data interacts with light, encompassing processes like reflection, absorption, emission, and so forth. This section provides a comprehensive overview of DVR from the viewpoint of an emission-absorption model. Based on the seminal work of Max [Max, 1995], the discussion starts in Section 2.1.1 with an absorption-only model, wherein the participating medium solely absorbs light traversing through the volume. Subsequently, in Section 2.1.2, attention shifts to an emission-only model, where the medium exclusively adds light to the ray without al-

### 2.1.1 Absorption-only Model

This section exclusively examines an absorption model, presuming that the participating particles within the volume are black and entirely opaque, absorbing all incident light without any reflection or scattering. For simplicity, a small cylindrical section of the volume is considered, with a base area denoted as E. It is assumed that all particles within this cylinder exhibit an identical spherical shape with a radius of r, as depicted in Figure 1. Let ρ represent the particle density within the volume. The volume of this slab is given by E∆s, yielding a total particle count of N = ρE∆s. For small enough ∆s, it is reasonable to assume that the particle projections on the base of the slab do not overlap. Therefore, the total area occluded on the base of the slab is approximately NA = ρAE∆s; thus, the fraction of the area occluded is ρAE∆s/E = ρA∆s. Let's now consider a light ray, denoted as L, passing through this slab in a direction perpendicular to its base (yellow arrow in Figure 1). The portion of the light that encounters the occluded area is absorbed by the particles, while the remainder continues through the slab. Therefore, the difference in light intensity between its entry and exit from the slab is formulated as follows:



| $\Delta I = I_{out} - I_0 = -I_0 A \rho \Delta s$ | (2.1) |
|---------------------------------------------------|-------|
|---------------------------------------------------|-------|

Here, I0 and Iout are respectively the intensity of light ray L when it enters and exists the slab. As ∆s approaches zero, equation \(2.1\) leads to the following differential equation:


$$\frac{dI}{ds} = -A\rho(s)I(s) = -\tau(s)I(s), \quad (2.2)$$

where s is a length parameter along the direction of light flow, and I(s) signifies the light intensity at distance s. As density ρ is not constant along the light ray's trajectory (unless the volume is homogeneous), ρ(s) emerges as a function that characterizes the density at distance s. The product Aρ(s) is commonly referred to as the extinction coefficient, denoted by τ (s), and defines the occlusion rate. Solving the ordinary differential equation \(2.2\) yields:

$$I(s) = I_0 \exp\left(-\int_0^s \tau(t)dt\right). \quad (2.3)$$

In simple terms, this equation specifies that a light ray traversing through a volume undergoes an attenuation in intensity as it encounters absorbent particles along its path.

### 2.1.2 Emission-only Model

Let's consider the same cylindrical slab from Section 2.1.1. In the scenario where particles within a volume exhibit complete transparency (resulting in zero absorption of light), yet emit diffuse light at an intensity of C per unit projected area, they will contribute a radiant flux of CρAE∆s to the base of the cylindrical slab. Therefore, the differential equation governing I(s) is expressed as:


$$\frac{dI}{ds} = A \, C(s) \, \tau(s). \quad (2.4)$$

The term C(s)τ (s), also referred to as the source term (sometimes denoted as g(s) in certain references), encapsulates this contribution. Solving the differential equation \(2.4\) yields the straightforward expression:

$$I(s) = I_0 + \int_0^s C(t)\tau(t)dt. \quad (2.5)$$

In simpler terms, this equation elucidates that the cumulative light emitted by the particles within the volume adds to the intensity of the light ray as it traverses through the volume.

#### Back-to-Front VS Front-to-Back

For volume visualization, only the rays that reach the camera are of interest, as they are the ones that contribute to the final rendered image. Consider that the light ray L is moving towards the camera. Therefore, I(end) defined by equation \(2.6\), specifies the final intensity of the light received by the camera. This approach is known as the Back-to-Front method, as light originates from the other end of the volume and concludes in front of the camera. However, since only rays that reach the camera are noteworthy, it is possible to reverse the process and shoot rays from the camera toward the scene. These rays are referred to as camera rays. The process is shown in Fig. 2.3. Following the same reasoning as discussed for the back-to-front method, the formula for the front-to-back method is as follows:

$$I_c(q) = \int_0^q C(t)\tau(t)T(0,t) dt, \quad \text{where} \quad (2.7)$$

$$T(a,b) = \exp\left(-\int_a^b \tau(u)du\right).$$

Here, Ic(q) represents the light intensity observed by the camera as the camera ray traverses the distance q. C(t) and τ (t) are respectively emission and extinction coefficients at distance t from the camera, and T(a, b) denotes the transparency of the medium between a and b. Notably, the front-to-back formula does not incorporate the term I0 as it does not initiate from the light source.

For the function Ic(q), end is the point where the camera ray exits the volume. If the volume is transparent then the camera should see through the volume to some extent. In other terms, T(0, end) > 0. In volume visualization, it is customary to set a background color (background light) when visualizing a volume. Consequently, when the volume exhibits transparency, the camera can partially observe the background color, depending on the level of transparency.

Hence, the more accurate formula for front-to-back is as follows:


$$I_c(q) = \begin{cases} \int_0^q C(t)\tau(t)T(0,t) \, dt & \text{if } q < \text{end} \\ B \cdot T(0, \text{end}) + \int_0^{\text{end}} C(t)\tau(t)T(0,t) \, dt & \text{if } q = \text{end}. \end{cases} \quad (2.8)$$

Here B specifies the background color. A question may arise about equation \(2.8\): why incorporate the first condition when the final rendered image is meant to represent the whole volume? Addressing this question necessitates introducing the concept of early stopping. Let's consider a scenario where a volume is nearly opaque. As the camera ray penetrates the volume, it can only penetrate up to a distance f before further visibility is obscured. This situation can be likened to observing a foggy road, where visibility is limited to a specific distance. In this case, T(0, f) approaches zero, signifying that the influence of light and the participating medium beyond this point, from f to the end of the volume (end), becomes negligible. For this reason, there is no need to perform any calculations after the distance f and all that is needed is Ic(f). In Section 2.1.3, I explore how early stopping can be implemented when calculating the light intensity received by the camera through the volume.

The second condition of equation \(2.8\) can be explained by envisioning a semi-transparent volume, where T(0, end) = 0.5. This implies that the camera ray should be able to go through the volume and also observe the background through it. In this case, the second condition of equation \(2.8\) is needed and it results in 0.5 · B + R end 0 C(t)τ (t)T(0, t) dt. Therefore, the background color B is visible with half of its strength.

It is worth noting that both the front-to-back and back-to-front methods yield the same final rendered image. However, for the remainder of this manuscript, the front-to-back approach is employed due to its intuitive nature and ease of implementation.

#### Computing Absorption Emission Model

Since the integral in equation \(2.8\) has no close-form solution, it is approximated using numerical methods. For now, let's set aside considerations of background light and focus on the emission and absorption of light within the participating particles of the volume, as encapsulated by the term R s 0 C(t)τ (t)T(t, s) dt in equation \(2.8\). To compute this integral, it is discretized into N equal segments, each of size ∆t, and then approximated using a Riemann sum. Throughout each segment, the emission (C) and extinction coefficients (τ ) are assumed to be constant. The resulting equation takes the form:


$$I = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i C_i \tau_i \Delta t, \quad \text{where} \quad (2.9)$$

$$T_1 = 1 \text{ and } T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} e^{-\tau_j \Delta t}.$$

For sufficiently small ∆ t, the second-order Taylor expansion of 1−e −τi∆ t ≈ 0+τi∆ t. Therefore, by defining αi = 1 − e −τi∆ t , equation\(2.9\) can be rephrased as:


$$I = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i C_i \alpha_i, \quad \text{where} \quad (2.10)$$

$$T_1 = 1 \text{ and } T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j).$$

Equation \(2.10\) is known as the front-to-back alpha-blending, conventionally formulated as:



| $\bar{C}_i = \bar{C}_{i-1} + (1 - \bar{\alpha}_{i-1})\alpha_i C_i$        | (2.11) |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------|
| $\bar{\alpha}_i = \bar{\alpha}_{i-1} + (1 - \bar{\alpha}_{i-1})\alpha_i,$ |        |

The ealry stopping method, mentioned in Section 2.1.3, can be implemented by setting a cap on ¯α. Whenever its value exceeds a certain threshold, typically 0.99, the calculation is terminated, and consideration of subsequent segments is deemed unnecessary as they have a negligible contribution to the final light

## 2.2 Transfer Function

In the domain of volume visualization, a transfer function serves as the bridge between volume properties V : R 3 → R n, stored within the voxels, and their representation in terms of colors (C) and opacities (α). Therefore, a transfer function F : R n → R 4 takes the volume properties as input and maps them into RGBα. Figure 2.4 provides an illustrative overview of how transfer functions work. It's worth noting that although Fig. 2.4 depicts direct interaction with voxel properties, in practice, transfer functions typically receive samples whose position is specified by the camera ray. These samples undergo tri-linear interpolation between their neighboring voxels before being fed into the transfer function. Further elaboration on this can be found in Chapter 4.

Transfer functions are usually designed manually by experts. However, this manual process has proven to be non-trivial and time-consuming. One of the key factors influencing the level of complexity of extracting features of interest is the underlying properties on which the transfer function is based. For instance, in the context of computer tomography (CT) images, if an expert aims to visualize material boundaries, they might find it more straightforward to do so when the transfer function relies on the first derivative of intensities rather than the intensities themselves. In Chapter 3, I offer a comprehensive literature review encompassing prior studies focused on the design of transfer functions, the selection of properties for the transfer functions, and the automation of transfer function design.

## 2.3 Differentiable DVR

Differentiability is an important attribute of the alpha blending formula (Eq. \(2.11\)), allowing this compositing method to be integrated within deep learning frameworks. This implies, that given a task-specific loss function L defined on the final rendered image, gradients can propagate through this compositing formula to optimize certain parameters with respect to the loss. Let P represent a pixel in the final rendered image of a volume. Using the chain rule, we arrive at the following expressions:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial C_i} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial P} \cdot \frac{\partial P}{\partial C_i}, \quad (2.12)$$

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \alpha_i} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial P} \cdot \frac{\partial P}{\partial \alpha_i}.$$

Here, Ci and αi denote one of the colors and opacities that contributed to the final pixel P. The gradients ∂L ∂Ci and ∂L ∂αi represent the directional vectors for Ci and αi , indicating the paths of local steepest ascent for the loss function. In other words, perturbing the color and opacity along these directions would lead to an increase in the loss. However, since the objective is to minimize the loss, we move in the opposite direction, namely − ∂L ∂Ci and − ∂L ∂αi , to adjust our color and opacity.

To calculate ∂P ∂Ci and ∂P ∂αi , we must differentiate the alpha blending formula:


$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial C_i} = \alpha_i T_i; \quad (2.13)$$


$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial \alpha_i} = C_i T_i + \sum_{j=i+1}^N \frac{T_j}{\alpha_i - 1}. \quad (2.14)$$

As discussed in previous sections, most volumes do not inherently possess visual properties. Instead, a transfer function defines the mapping between volume properties and visual properties. Let Fθ(x) be a transfer function parameterized by θ. Through the chain rule, we can deduce ∂L ∂θ :

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial P} \cdot \frac{\partial P}{\partial C_i} \cdot \frac{\partial C_i}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial P} \cdot \frac{\partial P}{\partial \alpha_i} \cdot \frac{\partial \alpha_i}{\partial \theta}. \quad (2.15)$$

Hence, employing a gradient-based optimization technique allows finding the optimal transfer function that minimizes the loss L. However, it is imperative to note that for ∂Ci ∂θ and ∂αi ∂θ to exist, the transfer function (Fθ) needs be differentiable with respect to θ.

# Related Work

The exploration of volumetric visualization spans more than three decades, witnessing a multitude of proposed methods for effectively representing volume data. Among these techniques, Direct Volume Rendering (DVR) has emerged as a widely adopted approach, distinguished by its ability to generate high-quality images without relying on intermediate geometric representations. Central to the efficacy of DVR is the transfer function, a component responsible for extracting features of interest within the volume while ensuring that less pertinent elements do not obscure them. The quality and utility of DVR are tied to the design and effectiveness of the transfer function.

This chapter provides a concise overview of related works on DVR and transfer functions, shedding light on the changes and ideas that have shaped the field of DVR. It focuses more closely on works aligned with the theme of this thesis, while briefly mentioning or omitting those less directly relevant to the study.

## 3.1 1D Transfer Function

Early studies used 1D transfer functions, mapping scalar values to color and opacity [Max, 1995]. Represented as F : R 1 → R 4 , these functions took scalar values from the volume as inputs and produced optical properties (RGBα values) as outputs. Volume visualization software packages constantly strive to enhance user interfaces (UI) to facilitate the process of tailoring effective transfer functions. ## 3.2 2D Transfer Function

Recognizing the limitations of visualizing a volume's intricate features solely based on scalar values, researchers augmented the transfer function by incorporating additional information from the volume itself.

### 3.2.1 Gradient Based Transfer Function

Gradients (derivatives), being indicative of the rate of change in the immediate vicinity of a voxel in the volume, offer a rich source of information to expand the feature space of transfer functions. The following

#### 2D Separable Transfer Function

A pivotal contribution in this direction was made by Levoy [Levoy, 1988], who used a gradient magnitudeweighted opacity transfer function to depict concentric isovalue contour surfaces within electron density maps. Additionally, this technique found application in rendering boundary surfaces within computer tomography (CT) data. Levoy's work [Levoy, 1988] introduced two distinct formulas—one for visualizing isosurfaces with constant thickness and another for visualizing boundaries between adjacent materials.

For isovalue contours, the work by Levoy [Levoy, 1988] emphasized that the most pleasing images result from maintaining a constant thickness in the transition region. To achieve this, the proposed formula is as follows:


$$\alpha(\mathbf{x}_i) = \begin{cases} \alpha_v & \text{if } |\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_i)| = 0 \text{ and } f(\mathbf{x}_i) = f_v, \\ \alpha_v - \frac{\alpha_v}{r} \left| \frac{f_v - f(\mathbf{x}_i)}{\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_i)} \right| & \text{if } |\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_i)| > 0 \text{ and} \\ f(\mathbf{x}_i) - r |\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_i)| \leq f_v \leq f(\mathbf{x}_i) + r |\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_i)|, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \quad (3.1)$$

Here, fv is the target isovalue, αv is the desired opacity for the contour surface, r is the desired thickness, xi is the location of a voxel, and f(xi) is its corresponding value. In a nutshell, Eq. \(3.1\) utilizes the gradient magnitude of a voxel to estimate its proximity to the desired contour surface. Notably, this method is more accurate for smaller values of r.

To render the boundaries between N materials, where the value for material n is denoted by fn, Levoy's method imposes the precondition that material n can only be in contact with (adjacent to) materials n − 1 and n + 1. The desired opacity for material n is represented by αn. The formula for visualizing these boundaries is expressed as follows:


$$\alpha(\mathbf{x}_i) = \begin{cases} |\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_i)| \left( \alpha_{n+1} \left[ \frac{f(\mathbf{x}_i) - f_n}{f_{n+1} - f_n} \right] + \alpha_n \left[ \frac{f_{n+1} - f(\mathbf{x}_i)}{f_{n+1} - f_n} \right] \right) & \text{if } f_n \leq f(\mathbf{x}_i) \leq f_{n+1}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \quad (3.2)$$

In simpler terms, Eq. \(3.2\) linearly interpolates the opacity for a value that falls between two materials based on its distance to the material values. Notably, boundaries (regions with higher gradient magnitudes) receive higher opacities while homogenous regions remain completely transparent.

Inspired by Levoy [Levoy, 1988], many volume visualization software packages have incorporated interfaces to empower users with control over gradient magnitude. This is typically achieved by enabling users to manipulate two distinct 1D transfer functions—one based on scalar values and the other on gradient magnitude. The resultant opacity of a voxel is determined as the product of its scalar opacity and its gradient magnitude opacity. This approach is commonly referred to as a 2D separable transfer function, given that each transfer function operates independently as a separate 1D transfer function. Refer to Fig. 3.3 for visual representations of the interfaces in volume visualization packages that support 2D separable transfer functions.

## 3.3 Machine Learning and Volume Visualization

Machine learning has made significant contributions across various fields, including visualization, and has notably impacted volume visualization. This section provides an overview of how advances in machine learning and deep learning have influenced the field of volume visualization.

The work by Soundararajan and Schultz [Soundararajan and Schultz, 2015] started by first classifying the volume by mapping the voxels' properties to material probabilities using machine learning and then assigning the classes with visual properties. Tzeng et al. [Tzeng and Ma, 2004] applied the K-means algorithm to perform clustering based on properties defining the transfer function. ## 4.1 Comparison with Conventional DVR

A conventional DVR operates through the following sequential steps:

- 1. Camera Initialization: Initially, a camera instance is created with defined properties such as location, field of view (FOV), focal length, etc.
- 2. Ray Casting: The camera projects rays toward the volume, typically one ray for each pixel in the final image. As these rays traverse the volume, they need to collect samples. There are various methods for specifying sample locations. The simplest and most common approach involves collecting samples at a fixed interval denoted as ∆t.
- 3. Interpolation: The volume's properties are then interpolated (usually tri-linearly) at the sample locations. Since the samples may not fall exactly on voxels' centers, the interpolation approximates the volume properties at the samples' locations.
- 4. Transfer Function Application: The interpolated properties are passed through a transfer function,

These rays gather samples from the volume as they traverse it, as depicted by the circles within the volume. For each sample, the surrounding voxels participate in a tri-linearly interpolated to determine the sample value, donated as P in the figure, which is then processed by the transfer function. The transfer function operates similarly to a lookup table and maps the input volume properties to their corresponding optical properties (color and opacity). Finally, all the samples along each ray pass through the compositing module to generate a pixel in the final rendered image.

- 5. Compositing: Each ray proceeds through the compositing formula (Eq. \(2.10\)) to render a pixel for the final image.

Figure 4.1 provides a schematic representation of this pipeline. In essence, a conventional DVR can be expressed as mathematical functions:

| Rendered Image = VR( $v, c$ , TF). | (4.1) |

Here, VR represents the volume rendering process as a function that takes the volume (v), camera (c), and transfer function (TF) as inputs, yielding the final rendered image as output.

The primary goal of this thesis is to reverse this process. Given an image along with its associated camera parameters and volume, the objective is to deduce a transfer function. This final transfer function is designed to ensure that when the volume is observed using the specified camera, it generates an image that closely resembles the provided image:



| $TF = f_\theta(v, c, \text{Rendered Image})$ | (4.2) |
|----------------------------------------------|-------|
|----------------------------------------------|-------|

Here, the function f represents the proposed pipeline, taking a volume (v), the camera parameters (c), and the rendered image as inputs, with θ denoting the function's parameters, and producing the transfer function as the output.

If the rendered images provided as inputs to Eq. \(4.2\) originate from the same volume as the one being passed to the function, then the problem falls under the supervised category, as we possess ground truth images as reference. Alternatively, it's possible that the rendered images come from a volume similar to the one passed to the function. In this scenario, the problem does not fall within the supervised category; rather, it can be viewed as a style transfer problem between volumes, where the objective is to visualize the features of interest in a volume using rendered images from a similar volume. Throughout this thesis, I refer to these images as pseudo-ground truth, acknowledging that they are not exact ground truth representations.

## 4.2 Pipeline

The previous section discussed the traditional DVR pipeline. Furthermore, it was explained that the primary goal of this thesis is to find an appropriate transfer function for a volume based on ground truth or pseudoground truth images. To achieve this goal, the conventional transfer function (lookup table) is replaced with fully connected neural networks. This modification does not fundamentally change the volume rendering process. Essentially, instead of relying on a lookup table to map volume properties to optical properties, this approach employs a neural network model for this purpose and it is well-established that neural networks are capable of approximating any arbitrary function [Hornik et al., 1989]. However, a crucial difference lies in the optimization process. Utilizing the differentiability of the compositing formula, as discussed in Section 2.3, it is possible to optimize the transfer function for a loss function L defined on the final rendered image produced by our method. Throughout this thesis, the loss functions involve comparing the rendered images with either ground truth or pseudo-ground truth images.

A crucial decision to make involves selecting a set of properties upon which the transfer function should be based, in other words, determining the inputs for the neural networks. As previously discussed in Chapter 3, numerous options exist for the features that can serve as the transfer function's input. To maintain practicality in this work, a widely accepted pair of features, scalars, and gradients, has been chosen. This choice aligns with the preferences of many well-known visualization software packages, including [3D Slicer](https://www.slicer.org/)

### 4.3.1 Loss Function

The loss function, which measures the performance of a model relative to its training data, plays a pivotal role in guiding the optimization process of deep learning networks. However, the meaning of transferring the transfer function varies depending on the specific problem and dataset at hand. Therefore, we refrain from introducing a fixed loss function. Instead, experts from different backgrounds can use their domain knowledge to come up with a loss function that is representative of what they consider as the error in their specific problem. ## 4.4 Background Effect

We observe that having a fixed background color can complicate the creation of an appropriate transfer function due to the heightened ill-posed nature of the problem. In these scenarios, the model might mistakenly assign a high opacity of the background color to regions that should be transparent. While this discrepancy might be less noticeable when the final image is rendered with the same background color, it becomes significant when different background colors are applied.

To provide clarity on this issue, we devised a synthetic volume with a sphere encapsulated within it. This

volume is mathematically expressed as:

$$V(x, y, z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \leq r^2 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \quad (4.3)$$

The reference transfer function maps scalar values below 0.5 to zero opacity, rendering the volume's exterior transparent. The opacity peaks at 0.75 with a corresponding blue hue, and then gradually fades to zero. Using multiple camera positions around the volume alongside the reference transfer function, we generated multiple ground truth images. In the leftmost column of Fig. 4.4, the transfer function is displayed, along with images rendered against black and white backgrounds. The goal is to devise a transfer function for the same volume, ensuring the resulting images closely align (in L1 norm terms) with the ground truth images. Figure 4.4's second column showcases results after learning a transfer function using ground truth images with a black background. As seen, the newly learned transfer function creates images that are comparable when displayed against a black backdrop. However, when set against a white background, inconsistencies are apparent. To address these discrepancies, one could experiment with varied random initializations, craft a broader set of ground truth images from multiple angles, or decrease the learning rate. Nevertheless, these modifications tend to slow down the learning process.

Another solution involves randomly selecting background colors for each camera position during the creation of ground truth images. Consequently, during the learning phase, the appropriate background color is selected according to the camera's position. This modification prevents the model from erroneously correlating certain background colors with regions meant to be transparent. It is especially beneficial when the aim is to transfer a transfer function across different volumes (style transfer). The final column of Fig. 4.4 showcases the results of our model when trained using images with randomized background colors; as evidenced, the final transfer function more closely mirrors the reference function, and the rendered image against a white backdrop more closely aligns with its grand truth counterpart. This technique has been consistently implemented in all the experiments presented in this thesis.

To demonstrate the efficacy of this embedding technique, we conduct an experiment where a cylindrical region of a chest CT volume is masked to a fixed scalar value, 404. This value is close to the intensity of bony material in a CT scan. We formulated a reference transfer function with a spike in opacity between scalars 403 and 404. The resulting images are used as ground truth to assess the performance of our model in reconstructing the reference transfer function under different embedding settings. As figure 4.5 illustrates, increased embedding size L enhances the transfer function's capacity to capture the high-frequency spike. It's worth noting that larger embedding sizes result in noisier transfer functions, but smaller L values yield smoother functions. While our technique diverges slightly from theirs, we similarly use embedding to accelerate our model's convergence on complex high-frequency transfer function details.

### 4.6 Sanity Check

Before proceeding to the transfer of transfer functions between similar volumes, a supervised test is conducted to assess the performance of our method. In this scenario, a transfer function is learned for a volume for which both ground truth transfer function and ground truth rendered images are available. This experiment serves as a sanity check for the method's capability to recover an appropriate transfer function. Furthermore, having ground truths allows for quantitative measurement of the error in our results.

For the experiment, a transfer function is created for a chest CT volume 1 . Subsequently, 2048 ground truth images of size 210×210 are generated by varying camera positions. The selected ground truth transfer function and a rendered ground truth image are depicted in the first column of Fig. 4.6.

The objective now is to reconstruct a suitable transfer function using the ground truth images for the same volume. Since the output images are expected to match the ground truth images pixel by pixel, the L1 distance is employed as the loss function. The L1 loss function is expressed as:

$$L_1(\mathbf{y}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \frac{1}{CWH} \sum_{c, w, h} |\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}| \quad (4.5)$$

where y and ˆy represent the ground truth image and the model output image respectively, and CWH is the product of the number of channels, width, and height of the image. For this experiment, Adam optimizer is used with a learning rate of 0.0005. The second and third columns of Fig. 4.6 display the transfer functions and final images for two different embedding sizes (L = 6 and L = 8). Both transfer functions yield images very close to the ground truth, with their L1 errors specified in the corner of each output image. This experiment demonstrates the satisfactory performance of our model in a supervised environment. In the subsequent chapter, we showcase our model's ability to transfer transfer functions between different volumes.

- L = 8 is computationally and memory-intensive compared to L = 6, leading to potentially longer optimization times and smaller batch sizes.

# Experiments

This chapter presents two compelling use cases that demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed approach. On each application, we collaborated with domain experts to gain a better understanding of how transferring the transfer function would be meaningful within their respective fields. This helps us to tailor loss functions that align with the objectives of our domain experts.

The initial use case (Section 5.1\) involves an ensemble of fluid dynamics simulation volumes for tracking the pressure shock wave resulting from an asteroid impact near the ocean surface. In this section, our domain expert starts with constructing a single transfer function to visualize a wave pattern of interest in an early time step. Then, our method is employed to iteratively refine and optimize transfer functions for proceeding time steps in the dataset. The resulting transfer functions effectively track the desired wave pattern across volumes while maintaining consistent visual properties.

In the second use case (Section 5.2\), our focus shifts to the visualization of medical volumes, specifically white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid in brain magnetic resonance (MR) images. We optimize a scalar-gradient transfer function for one volume based on its segmentation. Then these results are used to visualize other brain volumes. The volumes in this dataset encompass a diverse set of subjects and intensity ranges, acquired from various MR machines.

Both experiments are conducted on a Linux Ubuntu 22.04.3 LTS workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen™ 9 7950X processor, 64GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA A 6000 GPU with 48GB of VRAM. In the case of the asteroid impact, the average time of optimization is around 15 minutes, while for the brain dataset, it is around 12 minutes. It is noteworthy that visually similar results, though with a higher loss value, can be achieved with larger learning rates, reducing the wall time to 5 minutes in both experiments.

## 5.1 Asteroid Impact Simulation Visualization

Gisler et al. [Gisler et al., 2018] investigated the possibility of the creation of tsunamis or similarly catastrophic wave patterns upon an asteroid blast near the ocean surface. They performed a series of 3D simulations of the asteroid blast and kept track of different quantities throughout time. Since most of the earth's surface is covered with water, this simulation assumed the impact takes place in the ocean. Therefore, each simulation of this dataset has only three materials: water, air, and asteroid. In all scenarios, the simulation started with an asteroid falling to the earth from the upper atmosphere at a high velocity. The atmosphere and water start in a static state with no kinetic energy.

This ensemble dataset has three parameters that would vary in different simulation runs. The first is airburst altitude. Airburst is when the asteroid explodes mid-air and before impacting the surface as the result of its interaction with the atmosphere. The dataset contains simulations in which an artificial airburst is created when the asteroid passes through a specific altitude. This synthetic airburst is induced by transferring 10% of the asteroid's kinetic energy into thermal energy, which leads to the disintegration of small-radius asteroids. Larger radius asteroids' cores remain intact after the airburst and impact water (albeit carrying significantly less energy and momentum than the one without airburst). Size is the second variable as it can change the potential harm of the impact drastically. This dataset focused on sizes that after the impact could possibly create tsunamis that could travel 50's to 100's of kilometers. Smaller asteroids that have a very low chance of being harmful and bigger sizes that could destroy the planet entirely were not considered in this dataset. The third parameter is the angle of impact. As the angle of impact gets more oblique, the distance that an asteroid has to traverse in the atmosphere increases and therefore, increases the ablation duration before the impact. This might cause the impactor to shrink in size. In total, 11 scenarios were considered and 7 of those were made publicly available as part of a visualization contest 1 . The names and specifications of the publicly available simulation runs are shown in Table 5.1.

In each of these scenarios, the simulation would track several quantities (such as pressure, temperature, density, etc.) defined in a three-dimensional grid (most data is arranged in a grid of size 300 × 300 × 300) spanning hundreds of time steps. To put the size of this ensemble in perspective, the authors mention that those simulations required months of wall clock time on thousands of cores [Gisler et al., 2018, Patchett et al., 2017].

Processing and visualizing such massive amounts of data poses significant challenges to experts, who despite knowing what to look at might still struggle to discover where to look.
