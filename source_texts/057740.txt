Abstract
Abstract
Inferring scene parameters such as BSDFs and volume densities from user-provided target images has been achieved using a gradient-based optimization framework, which iteratively updates the parameters using the gradient of a loss function defined by the differences between rendered and target images. The gradient can be unbiasedly estimated via a physics-based rendering, i.e., differentiable Monte Carlo rendering. However, the estimated gradient can become noisy unless a large number of samples are used for gradient estimation, and relying on this noisy gradient often slows optimization convergence. An alternative is to exploit a biased version of the gradient, e.g., a filtered gradient, to achieve faster optimization convergence. Unfortunately, this can result in less noisy but overly blurred scene parameters compared to those obtained using unbiased gradients. This paper proposes a gradient combiner that blends unbiased and biased gradients in parameter space instead of relying solely on one gradient type (i.e., unbiased or biased). We demonstrate that optimization with our combined gradient enables more accurate inference of scene parameters than using unbiased or biased gradients alone.

1 Introduction
Monte Carlo (MC) rendering allows us to synthesize photorealistic images from 3D scenes through physically accurate light simulation. Integrating this physics-based rendering into an inverse rendering framework, where we are interested in inferring 3D scenes from user-provided target images, enables the generation of physically explainable outputs. Specifically, the inferred 3D scenes can be transformed into ones that closely resemble the target images through physics-based light simulation.
A typical approach to acquiring such 3D content via MC rendering is to conduct gradient-based optimization, which iteratively updates a part of a scene, i.e., scene parameters, using the gradients of a loss function with respect to the parameters, where the discrepancy between the rendered outputs and target images defines the loss.
Recent sampling methods [Nimier-David et al. 2022; Vicini et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020] have shown that the gradients of a loss function can be estimated unbiasedly and consistently. Therefore, a straightforward approach to obtaining accurate gradients is to use a large number of samples. Unfortunately, using many samples is impractical, as gradient estimation should be performed in each optimization iteration, and this optimization often requires hundreds of iterations. As a result, handling estimation errors in the gradients, i.e., MC noise, without increasing the sample counts, is one of the fundamental challenges in inverse MC rendering.
A tempting alternative to brute-force sampling is to assume that enough spatial coherence exists in the gradients and aggregate these unbiased but noisy derivatives, often called denoising. Adopting such a post-sampling process in either image space [Gu et al. 2024; Hasselgren et al. 2022] or parameter space [Chang et al. 2024] is simple and effective in reducing noise in the input gradients (i.e., unbiased gradients from sampling). However, the resulting gradients can contain nontrivial denoising bias, which prevents scene optimization from accurately inferring scene parameters, as shown in Fig. 1. Consequently, a tradeoff arises between using unbiased or biased gradients in optimization, where the former can lead to noisy results and the latter to overly smoothed outcomes. This tradeoff makes selecting the appropriate type of gradient challenging in practice.
In this paper, we adopt a hybrid strategy that leverages both types of gradient estimates: the unbiased but noisy gradient and the smooth but biased gradient. To robustly utilize these statistically different types of estimates, we propose a gradient combiner that locally blends a pair of unbiased and biased gradients in parameter space so that a gradient-based optimizer can exploit both types of gradient estimates instead of relying on only one type.
We build our gradient combiner on top of the James-Stein estimator [James and Stein 1961], which shrinks unbiased estimates toward biased ones. While this general estimator was previously used to combine unbiased and biased image colors in the context of forward rendering [Gu et al. 2022], we introduce a novel application of this general estimator: combining a pair of gradients for gradient-based scene optimization. Additionally, we propose an application-specific adaptation to the estimator, which enables data-driven adjustment of the blending between unbiased and biased gradients locally in parameter space, guided by error estimation.
We demonstrate that our high-level idea of exploiting both types of gradients through a gradient combiner enables more accurate inference of spatially varying scene parameters, such as textured BSDFs and volume parameters, compared to using either unbiased or biased gradients, as shown in Fig. 1.
2 Related Work
Inverse rendering frameworks (e.g., [Nimier-David et al. 2020; Vicini et al. 2021]) typically rely on gradient-based optimization, which iteratively estimates scene parameters using the gradient of a loss function with respect to the parameters.
Efficient methods for estimating the gradient have been proposed for geometry-related parameters [Bangaru et al. 2020; Li et al. 2018; Loubet et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020; 2019; 2023], volumes [Nimier-David et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2019; 2021b], and BRDFs [Belhe et al. 2024; Zeltner et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021a]. While these sampling approaches allow for the computation of unbiased derivative estimates, such estimates can become excessively noisy unless a large number of samples is used, which leads to unsatisfactory optimization results, as shown in Fig. 1.
Various approaches have been proposed to improve the accuracy of scene inferences by reducing noise in the derivatives. For example, Fan et al. [2024] introduced a path-guiding method that effectively reduces MC noise in the derivatives, particularly in scenes with complex light configurations. Yan et al. [2024] presented computing the derivatives of MC variances with respect to scene parameters and incorporating these variances as a regularization term in a loss function. Nicolet et al. [2021] devised the gradient descent update rule using a mesh Laplacian as a regularizer for robust geometry inference. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that reusing unbiasedly estimated derivatives spatially or temporally can greatly reduce their variances [Balint et al. 2023; Chang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023].
Post-sampling for biased but less noisy gradients. A more aggressive approach is to replace unbiased rendered images with biased ones in the loss function of gradient-based optimizers so that the estimated gradient of the loss becomes less noisy. For example, Nicolet et al. [2023] applied an image-space control variates that temporally aggregates rendered pixel estimates across adjacent optimization steps. Hasselgren et al. [2022] and Gu et al. [2024] employed image-space denoising techniques that spatially blend noisy pixel estimates. While these image-space methods enable the use of a less noisy loss, the gradient of the loss can still be noisy since they do not reduce the variances of the derivatives of rendered images with respect to scene parameters, i.e., sampling noise in the adjoint rendering phase [Vicini et al. 2021]. Alternatively, post-sampling schemes can be applied directly to the gradient of the loss in parameter space. For example, Chang et al. [2024] proposed a cross-bilateral filter that effectively denoises unbiasedly estimated gradients in parameter space.
Adopting these post-sampling techniques within an inverse rendering framework is straightforward, as they do not alter the underlying sampling process. However, a common drawback of these methods is the introduction of bias into the estimated gradient, which can potentially lead to over-blurred inference results (e.g., as shown in Fig. 1). Consequently, in practice, one should decide whether to use such biased post-sampling methods based on the accuracy of the unbiased gradients (i.e., the level of noise in the unbiased gradients). Unfortunately, there is no universal rule for determining which approach is better, as the decision can vary depending on the scene and the number of samples used.
Instead of relying solely on unbiased or biased gradients, we adopt a combination strategy that blends a pair of unbiased and biased gradients while treating the pair as black-box inputs. In this paper, we demonstrate that combining these two different inputs enables more accurate inference of scene parameters than using either input individually.
Combining unbiased and biased estimates in forward rendering. Balancing bias and variance by blending a pair of unbiased and biased estimates has been explored in forward Monte Carlo rendering, where the input pair consists of unbiased and biased pixel estimates [Back et al. 2020; Firmino et al. 2022; Gu et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2021]. As a recent example, Gu et al. [2022] introduced the James-Stein estimator [James and Stein 1961], which enables combined pixel estimates to have lower errors than unbiased pixel estimates. We adopt the same approach of combining unbiased and biased estimates but extend this high-level idea to a different problem: blending a pair of gradient estimates for inverse rendering. Specifically, we present a novel application of the James-Stein estimator for combining gradient estimates and introduce application-specific modifications to make the estimator practical for inverse rendering.

3 Problem Specification and Motivation
We consider an inverse rendering problem where the goal is to infer scene parameters θ that minimize a quadratic loss L, which measures the discrepancy between the rendered image R(θ) with the parameters θ and the target image I provided by the user. Common examples of such loss functions are the L2=n−1∑i=1n(Ri(θ)−Ii)2 and relative L2=n−1∑i=1n(Ri(θ)−Ii)2/(Ii2+0.01), where Ri(θ) and Ii are the i-th values in R(θ) and I, respectively.
Once we have a differentiable rendering framework, e.g., [Jakob et al. 2022], that enables the computation of the derivatives of the rendered image R(θ), i.e., ∂R(θ)/∂θ, we can compute the gradient of the loss L with respect to the parameters θ using the chain rule:
g≡∂L∂θ=∂L∂R(θ)∂R(θ)∂θ.
(1)
This gradient g is then fed into a gradient-based optimizer, which iteratively updates the parameters θ to minimize L.
Unfortunately, in practice, the gradient g cannot be computed without error, as the two derivatives on the right-hand side of Eq. 1 are estimated values through Monte Carlo integration using a finite number of samples. Instead, we obtain a noisy but unbiased estimate g~ of the unknown gradient g when the two derivatives are computed using statistically independent samples [Azinovic et al. 2019; Vicini et al. 2021]. Nonetheless, this unbiased gradient can become noisy unless a large number of samples are used, especially when the dimensionality of the scene parameters θ is high (e.g., parameters for textured BSDFs or volumes).
A general solution to suppress noise in the gradient is to use a moment-based optimizer (e.g., Adam [Kingma and Ba 2014]) that aggregates the gradients temporally during optimization. Fig. 2-(c) shows the optimization result using Adam with the unbiased gradient for an example scenario where a 2D texture is inferred. While the inference result is not far from the target image, it exhibits noticeable artifacts and noise in certain regions, e.g., reflected edges from the environmental light map used in the scene, as seen in the upper part of Fig. 2-(c). It indicates that temporally aggregating unbiased gradients through a moment-based optimizer is insufficient to completely eliminate the negative effects of noise in the gradients, such as deriving the optimization toward undesirable minima.
Motivation of combining the pair of unbiased and biased gradients. An appealing alternative to using the unbiased gradient g~ is to replace it with a less noisy but biased gradient g^ and pass the biased gradient to a moment-based optimizer. Fig. 2-(d) shows the optimization result using the biased gradient computed via the cross-bilateral filter [Chang et al. 2024], which spatially aggregates unbiased derivatives in parameter space. Using the biased gradient instead of the unbiased one enables more accurate inference in smooth areas (e.g., the upper part of the plane in Fig. 2). However, it results in higher errors in high-frequency regions (e.g., the lower part) compared to its unbiased counterpart. It implies that selecting only one gradient type (i.e., unbiased or biased gradients) is practically suboptimal, as their relative accuracies can vary in a data-dependent manner. This motivates us to combine these gradients locally in parameter space to optimize scene parameters more robustly with our combined gradient, which will be presented in the next section.

4 James-Stein Combiner with Unbiased and Biased Gradients
This section presents our gradient combiner (Fig. 3), which takes a pair of unbiased and biased gradients, g~t and g^t, as input and outputs a blended gradient g¯t at each optimization step t. For brevity, we omit the superscript t, denoting the current step, when it is clear from context.
4.1 James-Stein Gradient Combiner
To combine the unbiased and biased gradient pair, g~ and g^, we employ a localized version [Gu et al. 2022] of the James-Stein estimator [Green and Strawderman 1991; James and Stein 1961]. Specifically, for each parameter index c in parameter space, we blend spatially nearby unbiased and biased derivatives using the two gradient inputs:
δc,i(g~,g^;σc2)=g^i+ρc(g~i−g^i)=g^i+max(0,1−(p−2)σc2∑j∈Nc(g~j−g^j)2)(g~i−g^i),
(2)
where the shrinkage factor ρc (0 ≤ ρc ≤ 1) controls the degree of shrinkage of unbiased derivatives g~i to biased ones g^i. This produces combined derivatives δc, i for each parameter index i within a spatial neighborhood Nc around index c. For the neighborhood Nc, we select a 7 × 7 window for 2D parameters (e.g., textured BSDFs) and a 5 × 5 × 5 window for 3D parameters (e.g., volumes).
In Eq. 2, p (p ≥ 3) is the size of Nc, i.e., p=|Nc|, and σc2 is the variance of the unbiased estimates g~i, which determines the shrinkage factor ρc. Technically, the James-Stein estimator assumes that the unbiased derivatives g~i follow a multivariate normal distribution with a shared variance σc2.
Note that the combination in Eq. 2 at parameter index c produces the derivative estimates δc, i (i∈Nc) for its neighboring parameters, not just for the center parameter at c. Therefore, after performing this combination at each parameter index c, we average the multiple estimates at each parameter index i:
g¯i=1|Ni|∑c∈Niδc,i.
(3)
This averaging produces the final output, i.e., the combined derivative g¯i at each parameter index i.
Combining the two input gradients through the shrinkage estimator (Eq. 2) is straightforward, as it treats the processes of generating the inputs as black boxes. However, the unknown variance σc2, which serves as a shrinkage parameter controlling the relative importance of the input pair, should be determined in advance. By assuming that the distribution of the unbiased estimates does not change rapidly over time, we compute the estimated variance si2 of the unbiased derivative g~i as
si2=(g~i−g~it−1)2/2,
(4)
which uses the two samples, i.e., the unbiased derivatives (g~i and g~it−1) in the current and previous optimization step, t and t − 1, respectively. For the first optimization step (i.e., when t = 1), we initialize g~it−1 to zero. We then compute the local mean s¯c2 of the estimated variances si2 (i∈Nc) for each parameter index c as
s¯c2=1|Nc|∑i∈Ncsi2.
(5)
This local mean s¯c2 serves as an estimate of the unknown variance σc2 in Eq. 2. We plug this estimated variance s¯c2 into the localized James-Stein estimator (Eq. 2), i.e., δc,i(g~,g^;s¯c2), and compute the combined gradient g¯ using Eq. 3. We then pass it to a gradient-based optimizer.
Fig. 4 compares the results of 2D texture optimization using unbiased, biased, and combined gradients. As shown, using the combined gradients (Fig. 4-(d)) results in intermediate accuracy compared to optimization using either the unbiased or biased gradients (Fig. 4-(b) and (c)). However, this behavior is not ideal, as we aim to achieve more accurate results than optimization with unbiased or biased gradients alone.
Discussion of the shrinkage parameter σc2. We can consider a better replacement for the unknown variance σc2 as a remedy. In particular, this straightforward adaptation of the James-Stein estimator relies on the assumption of homogeneous variance in the unbiased gradient g~. However, this assumption can be invalid, as the derivatives are computed from a rendered image whose pixel estimates typically exhibit heterogeneous variances. The previous work [Gu et al. 2022], which adapted the James-Stein estimator for forward rendering, determined the shrinkage parameter as a weighted average of estimated variances (e.g., si2 in our case), with the weighting controlled by a neural network. However, such a deep learning–based approach may be too expensive for inverse rendering tasks, unless a highly efficient neural network is designed, as it requires inference at every optimization iteration. In the subsequent section, we propose a simpler alternative that avoids reliance on neural networks.

The straightforward adaptation of the James-Stein estimator (b), using the local mean s¯c, tends to assign high shrinkage factors (i.e., greater weight to unbiased gradients) across parameters, resulting in error patterns (f) similar to those with unbiased gradients (d). In contrast, our approach (c) adaptively adjusts the factors across parameters. For example, it assigns relatively low factors in smooth areas and high factors in high-frequency regions, enabling more accurate optimization (g).
4.2 Adaptive Selection of Shrinkage Parameters
This section presents a process to replace the straightforward estimate s¯c2 (Eq. 5) of the unknown variance σc2 in the James-Stein estimator (Eq. 2). For this purpose, we take inspiration from classical adaptive denoising methods (e.g., [Bitterli et al. 2016; Li et al. 2012; Moon et al. 2014]), which selected an optimal denoising parameter (e.g., the bandwidth of classical filters) from a predefined set of candidates through error estimation.
Specifically, we define a set of candidates per parameter index c, which can replace the unknown variance σc2 in Eq. 2, using the estimated variances (Eq. 4) as
Cc={min(sc2),min(sc2)+s¯c22,s¯c2,s¯c2+max(sc2)2,max(sc2)},
(6)
where min(sc2)=min{si2|i∈Nc} and max(sc2)=max{si2|i∈Nc}. Note that when the homogeneous variance assumption (in Sec. 4) holds, the average sample variance, s¯c2, can be used directly. However, since this assumption may fail (as shown in Fig. 4), we construct a discretized candidate set using the minimum and maximum of the estimated variances si2 (Eq. 4) within Nc.
To select an appropriate candidate from the set Cc, we define an integrated squared error of the gradient combiner δc,i(g~,g^;Cc,k), computed using the k-th candidate Cc,k, as
ξc,k=∑i∈Nc(δc,i(g~,g^;Cc,k)−gi)2.
(7)
The error ξc, k relies on the unknown ground truth derivative gi, which can only be computed with an infinite number of samples. Consequently, the unknown error ξc, k should be estimated by replacing the unknown term gi with a known value.
As a straightforward choice, we might consider using the unbiased estimate g~i in place of the unknown gi in Eq. 7. However, this approach leads to selecting the smallest candidate (i.e., min(sc2) in Eq. 6), which causes the combined estimates δc, i to closely align with the unbiased ones g~i, i.e., overfitting to noise in the unbiased input.
As a workaround, we exploit unbiased derivatives from previous optimization steps, whose noise is uncorrelated with the unbiased input g~ in the current step. Specifically, we temporally accumulate the unbiased derivatives using the first moment estimation used in Kingma and Ba [2014]:
mit(g~)=αmit−1(g~)+(1−α)g~it,
(8)
where α is a constant (e.g., 0.9) that controls the amount of temporal smoothing applied to the unbiased estimates. We set the initial value of the moment to zero, i.e., mi0(g~)=0, and compute a modified moment as m¯it(g~)=mit(g~)/(1−αt) to compensate for the start-up bias caused by this initialization [Kingma and Ba 2014]. This temporal accumulation produces a biased estimate of the unknown derivative gi, as the unknown value can change across optimization iterations. However, this simple heuristic helps mitigate overfitting when we exclude the unbiased derivative g~it from the current step t, i.e., using m¯it−1(g~) accumulated up to the previous step t − 1. This allows us to compute the estimated error ξ^c,k as
ξ^c,k=∑i∈Nc(δc,i(g~,g^;Cc,k)−m¯it−1(g~))2.
(9)
This formulation allows us to evaluate the accuracy of the gradient combiner δc,i(g~,g^;Cc,k) (Eq. 2) for each candidate Cc,k.
Once the error estimation is ready, we compute the combined derivatives δc, i using the optimal candidate, which minimizes the estimated error ξ^c,k (Eq. 9). We then average the combined estimates δc, i using Eq. 3 to determine our final output g¯, which is passed to a gradient-based optimizer.
Analysis of our method with and without adaptive shrinkage parameters. Figs. 4 and 5 compare the scene optimization results obtained using our gradient combiner with and without the adaptive selection of shrinkage parameters. The adaptive selection allows the gradient combiner to guide scene optimization toward producing more accurate results than those achieved using either unbiased or biased gradients alone, unlike the straightforward adaptation of the James-Stein estimator (Sec. 4.1), which replaces the unknown variance σc2 (in Eq. 2) with the mean of sample variances, s¯c2 (Eq. 5).
5 Results
This section demonstrates that combining unbiased and biased gradients yields more accurate inference results for spatially varying scene parameters, such as 2D textured BSDFs and volume parameters, than using either input gradient alone.
We have integrated our method into a recent inverse rendering framework (Mitsuba3 [Jakob et al. 2022]), which generates unbiased gradients using path replay backpropagation [Vicini et al. 2021] and updates scene parameters with the moment-based optimizer, Adam [Kingma and Ba 2014].
We allocated the same number of optimization iterations when comparing our scene inferences with those using the input gradients (i.e., unbiased or biased gradients). The computational overhead of our method is negligible; for example, it is less than 1 ms for parameters of size 1K × 1K on an Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU. Therefore, comparisons conducted using the same number of iterations approximately correspond to equal-time comparisons. For quantitative evaluation, we measured the relative MSE [Rousselle et al. 2011] of images rendered with the inferred scene parameters.
Test settings. We test five inverse rendering scenarios shown in Fig. 8. Specifically, we optimize 2D textured BSDF parameters, including a 1K × 1K albedo for the Frame, a 1K × 1K roughness for the Plane, and a 2K × 2K metallic parameters for the Tray scene. We use a learning rate of 0.02 for BSDF optimization. For the Janga and Dust Devil scenes, we optimize 3D volumetric parameters (albedo and density) using the learning rates provided by Nicolet et al. [2023]. We use 100 iterations for BSDF optimization using single-view inputs, and 1K iterations for volume optimization using eight-view inputs. We use the same number of samples for rendering images R(θ) and computing derivatives ∂R(θ)/∂θ. Unless otherwise specified, we employ a quadratic loss L, such as the L2 loss for 3D volume parameters and the relative L2 loss for 2D BSDF parameters.
Comparisons of scene optimization using different types of gradients. In Fig. 8, we compare the inference results of scene optimization using unbiased, biased, and combined gradients. Specifically, the biased gradients are generated using a state-of-the-art method [Chang et al. 2024], which denoises the unbiased gradients through cross-bilateral filtering in parameter space. We used the bandwidth parameters of the filter as recommended in the original paper. As shown in Fig. 8, scene inference with biased gradients produces smoother results than using unbiased gradients. This approach makes scene optimization robust against noise, particularly for the Frame and Tray scenes. However, it tends to produce higher errors in areas where fine details in the parameters exist. On the other hand, when the biased gradients are combined with the unbiased ones through our gradient combiner, scene optimization becomes more accurate by balancing the errors in the input gradients, specifically the noise in the unbiased gradient and the bias in the biased gradient. An alternative use of the cross-bilateral filter is to denoise the moments of Adam, rather than pre-filtering the unbiased gradients, as demonstrated by Chang et al. [2024]. Additional comparisons and analysis of this alternative approach are provided in the supplemental report.

Analysis of our error estimation. Table 3 compares the accuracy of our scene optimization when selecting the optimal candidate from the candidate set Cc (Eq. 6), guided by either the estimated error ξ^c,k (Eq. 9) or the ground truth error ξc, k (Eq. 7). Note that the ground truth error ξc, k depends on unknown derivatives gi (in Eq. 7), which cannot be computed in practice. Therefore, this test is designed to evaluate the quality of our error estimation by comparing it with its highest possible performance. To compute the unknown derivatives gi, we use 1K spp per optimization iteration. As shown in the table, the gap in the accuracy between scene optimization using the estimated or ground truth errors remains within a moderate range (10% to 40% higher errors when using the estimated error). This indicates that while our error estimation is heuristic, it does not hinder its practical utility, especially when considering the more significant gap between our method and the baselines that use only unbiased or biased gradients.
Limitations and future work. Our gradient combiner assumes the availability of a pair of unbiased and biased inputs (g~ and g^). However, this assumption can be invalid when using a non-quadratic loss (e.g., L1 loss), where the noisy gradient estimate g~ becomes biased, as discussed in [Chang et al. 2023; Nicolet et al. 2023]. While our technique can still be applied using two biased inputs (i.e., a noisy one g~ and a less noisy one g^), the improvement of our method over the baselines (i.e., using either unbiased or biased) can be smaller compared to when a quadratic loss is used, as shown in Fig. 11.
Another limitation of our method is that the accuracy of the combined gradients depends on the accuracy of the input gradients. Specifically, our method can generate undesirable scene parameters in regions where both unbiased and biased gradient-based optimizations fail to infer accurate parameters, as illustrated in Fig. 7.
For future work, we would like to extend our gradient combiner to a broader range of scenarios, such as inferring other kinds of spatially varying parameters (e.g., optimizing geometries from images). Additionally, it would be interesting to explore using other types of biased inputs, such as gradients estimated by neural networks, e.g., [Fischer and Ritschel 2024; Hadadan et al. 2023]. It would also be interesting to explore an improved cross-bilateral filter that adaptively adjusts its bandwidth parameter (e.g., [Li et al. 2012; Rousselle et al. 2013]), rather than relying on a manually chosen bandwidth, as a potentially better-biased input to our method.
Failure case of scene optimization using our method for the Plane scene. Our gradient combiner can fail to enable the optimization to produce accurate scene parameters for specific areas (denoted by the arrows) when both input gradients are erroneous.
6 Conclusion
This paper has introduced a gradient combiner that mitigates the negative effects of using statistically distinct gradients, i.e., noise from unbiased gradients and bias from biased gradients. We demonstrated that the accuracy of scene optimization using these gradients varies depending on the scene, the number of samples, and even locally across parameters. This observation motivated our approach of blending unbiased and biased gradients while locally adjusting the degree of blending between the two inputs, rather than relying on the manual selection of a single gradient type. Although our gradient combiner is built on a classical estimator (the James-Stein estimator), we believe that the high-level idea of combining gradient pairs can inspire alternative strategies for integrating multiple gradient estimators with distinct statistical properties to further improve scene optimization.
