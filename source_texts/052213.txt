A BSTRACT
High-resolution 3D Gaussian Splatting training increases pixel and Gaussian–tile
intersection counts, expanding rasterization caches that coexist with projection
and gradient state at peak memory. A complete-view update aggregates gradients
across the view; each region’s forward and backward caches can be released after
its contribution is collected. BoundedGS separates rasterization and projection
backward passes and bounds the active local rasterization working set according
to its measured load. Candidate-indexed projection and compact gradient storage
address the view-level peaks exposed by this change, while CPU-assisted structure
rebuilding and bounded image supply manage resources outside rendering. In a
resolution sweep at a fixed count of about 6M Gaussians, the measured rendering
working set stays within 1.073–1.077 GiB from 1K to 16K; at 11K, a warm pass
takes 19.79 seconds versus 21.76 for full-frame execution. Across five native 5K
outdoor scenes, full training reduces the mean rendering working set from 9.97
to 1.76 GiB (82.3%), retains similar reconstruction quality, and shortens training
time from 36.7 to 33.9 minutes.

I NTRODUCTION
Training 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 2023) at larger scale involves two distinct
growth factors: scene extent and observation resolution. Larger scenes commonly contain more
Gaussians spread over a wider area, and spatial partitioning can distribute their training cost (Lin
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b). Higher observation resolution increases the resources required by
each training view even when the scene extent and Gaussian count remain approximately fixed.
Extra computation can often be accommodated by a longer run, whereas a memory peak beyond
device capacity prevents that view from being trained. InfiniGS (Liu et al., 2026), for example,
uses image blocks and asynchronous optimization to relieve the memory pressure of high-resolution
training. For a complete-view update on one GPU, the peak also depends on the simultaneous
residency of rasterization, projection, and gradient state. We study how to control this active working
set as resolution grows while retaining efficient execution.
Decomposing the training step reveals that its rendering intermediates need not remain resident
across the entire view. A complete-view update requires the contributions from all image regions to
be aggregated before the parameter update; it does not require every region’s forward and backward
computations to advance in lockstep. A conventional path renders the entire view before computing
the loss and backward pass, keeping the intermediate states needed for regional backward computation alive together. Rasterization, however, is local to the image: given projected Gaussian attributes
and local image gradients, a tile’s forward and backward passes use its own ordered Gaussian list
and pixel state. While the Gaussian parameters remain fixed, the regional gradients can be computed separately and accumulated before the update. Each region can therefore complete its loss
and backward pass and release its local state before the next region runs. Increasing resolution still
creates more pixels and intersections, but limiting the simultaneously active local state controls the
growth of the main rendering caches.

1
We introduce BoundedGS, a bounded-working-set execution method for high-resolution 3DGS
training. Within rasterization, it completes forward and backward computation region by region,
releases intersections, compositing and loss state after extracting each gradient contribution, and
chooses region sizes from their measured load. For attribute evaluation and projection, it conservatively selects candidate Gaussians and can process projection groups in sequence, limiting the
amount of detailed state generated from the global model. The same candidates index compact
parameter-gradient storage instead of allocating complete gradient rows for Gaussians unrelated to
the current view. All regions use one global Gaussian model and the original camera, cover every
pixel, combine gradients and density-control statistics by view, and then perform one parameter update. The memory savings arise from the range and lifetime of computations. Once rendering peaks
are reduced, structure updates and image supply can become the limiting resources; BoundedGS
manages their temporary state as part of the full training system.
In a resolution sweep with the Gaussian count fixed at about 6M, the rendering working set remains
within 1.073–1.077 GiB from 1K to 16K, while the warm pass at the shared 11K setting is about 9%
faster than full-frame execution. Full training on five native 5K outdoor scenes reduces the rendering
working set by 82.3%, with similar final quality and slightly shorter training time. Our contributions
are:
• We identify the memory peak caused by co-resident rasterization, projection, and gradient state in complete-view training, and separate rasterization from projection backward
computation so that local state can be released after its gradient contribution is collected.
• We develop a load-bounded raster-leaf execution path and integrate candidate-indexed projection, compact gradients, structure rebuilding, and image supply into a single-GPU training system.
• We use a 1K–16K resolution sweep at a fixed Gaussian count, component diagnostics, and
full training on native high-resolution scenes to verify a nearly constant rendering working
set, similar reconstruction quality, and training-time performance.

R ELATED W ORK
Representation quality, model capacity, and training efficiency. 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023)
combines explicit Gaussians, adaptive densification, and differentiable rasterization for high-quality
real-time rendering. Subsequent work improves initialization, densification, reconstruction quality,
and convergence (Kotovenko et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2024; Kheradmand et al., 2024; Deng et al.,
2026); reduces model capacity or storage through simplification, pruning, and encoding (Fang &
Wang, 2024; Morgenstern et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025); extends the primitive or representation to structured and dynamic settings (Huang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Lu
et al., 2024); and scales scene extent with spatial partitions and hierarchies (Lin et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2025b; Kerbl et al., 2024). We focus on the temporary memory peak of a training view while
retaining the global model and input resolution.
Rasterization, projection, and intermediate state. Changing the rasterizer’s computation and
data organization is an established route to greater efficiency. gsplat (Ye et al., 2025) reduces training memory with packed projection state and optional sparse gradients for positions, rotations, and
scales in its optimized CUDA rasterizer. StopThePop (Radl et al., 2024) improves view consistency
by hierarchical reordering and culling, changing visibility and compositing order. FlashGS (Feng
et al., 2025), Speedy-Splat (Hanson et al., 2025), and AdR-Gaussian (Wang et al., 2024) reduce
Gaussian–tile work through precise overlap tests, opacity-aware ranges, and early culling. Taming3DGS (Mallick et al., 2024) uses compositing checkpoints for Gaussian-parallel backward computation, while Faster-GS (Hahlbohm et al., 2026) combines bounding, sorting, kernel fusion, backward,
and optimizer improvements. LiteGS (Liao et al., 2025) combines spatial clustering, selection, compact layouts, warp-level gradient reduction, and modified densification. GS-TG (Jo & Park, 2025)
shares sorting across groups of tiles in a rendering accelerator and retains fine-grained participation
of smaller tiles through bit masks. These works improve training or rendering efficiency through
compact states, overlap culling, sorting, and backward kernels. BoundedGS targets the temporary
training-memory peak by controlling which intermediate states coexist, while managing the time
cost of local execution.
2
Scaling resources for high-resolution training. Prior work addresses high-resolution training
through representation size, supervision sampling, and resource distribution. EfficientGS (Liu et al.,
2025a) reduces redundancy through selective densification, pruning, and sparse growth of sphericalharmonic order. Turbo-GS (Dhiman et al., 2026) renders a subset of pixels selected by dilated
sampling for each supervision step and uses a convergence-aware growth budget. HRGS (Li et al.,
2025) starts from a low-resolution global prior and partitions models and observations for highresolution local refinement and fusion. RetinaGS (Li et al., 2024a) distributes Gaussian storage
through model parallelism and combines the rendered contributions of submodels. GGRt (Li et al.,
2024b) performs a full-image forward pass without automatic differentiation and then uses cached
image gradients to recompute backward contributions in blocks for generalizable reconstruction.
Grendel (Zhao et al., 2025) distributes Gaussian and pixel work across GPUs, dynamically balances
load, combines gradients through communication, and supports multi-view batches. InfiniGS (Liu
et al., 2026) combines multi-resolution training with image-block partitioning and asynchronous optimization, adjusting Adam hyperparameters and densification thresholds for resolution and partition
changes. These systems organize high-resolution views through multi-GPU pixel partitions or image blocks; we examine the lifetime of rasterization forward and backward caches within a complete
view on one GPU. CLM (Zhao et al., 2026) and GS-Scale (Lee et al., 2025) extend model capacity
through host offloading, and CLM also reduces projection input and related gradient state by selecting frustum candidates. BoundedGS combines view-level candidates with load-bounded local
rasterization to control the simultaneous residency of Gaussian–tile intersections, pixels, projection
state, and parameter gradients.
Together, these works address high-resolution training through supervision resolution, pixel sampling, model budgets, image blocks, and candidate filtering. BoundedGS retains full-pixel supervision and one update per view, making resolution-dependent rasterization state an active bounded
working set and integrating projection, gradients, and other resource management into the same
training path.

Gaussian and the camera.

Given projected attributes and local pixel gradients, each region uses its own ordered Gaussian lists

Gaussians that may contribute to that view.
3.2
B OUNDED EXECUTION OF RASTERIZATION
BoundedGS separates rasterization and projection backward passes, collecting projected-attribute
gradients and releasing intersection, pixel, and loss caches before projection backward. Neighboring tiles form raster leaves, each processed sequentially through rasterization, loss, and backward
computation.
Nonoverlapping leaves cover every pixel using the original camera and global coordinates. Each leaf
generates and sorts its intersections, composites pixels, computes local loss and backward contributions, and adds them to a shared projected-gradient buffer. Its intersection, sorting, pixel, checkpoint, output, and loss state is then released. Parameters stay fixed until one optimizer update after
all leaves.
For leaf ℓ with Pℓ pixels, weight wℓ = Pℓ /Pv preserves the full-image pixel mean of the additive
L1 term. SSIM uses independent leaf windows, so its boundary treatment differs from full-image
SSIM and changes with leaf layout. Final quality is evaluated on complete images.

The largest leaf sets this part of the peak. Uneven Gaussian coverage makes equal-area leaves

The budget covers intersection, sorting, pixel, checkpoint, and output buffers; projection, gradients,
supervision, and loss follow separate rules. High-load areas receive smaller leaves; low-load areas
retain larger ones, with one 16 × 16-pixel tile as minimum.

BoundedGS caches a CPU tree of leaf boundaries per camera. Later visits recount current load,
further split leaves above budget, and merge siblings when their combined and parent estimates
pass a conservative test that is rechecked before use. Only layout is cached, allowing adaptation
as Gaussians change; resolution or budget changes rebuild the tree. Final counts feed intersection
expansion.
At higher resolution, a fixed budget distributes added pixels and intersections among more sequential
leaves. Leaves passing the budget test bound principal raster allocations, including output; local
supervision, loss, and bounded prefetch remain tied to active regions. Appendix B.3 gives the byte
estimate and partition rules.
3.3
C ANDIDATE - DRIVEN PROJECTION AND GRADIENT STORAGE
After local rasterization reduces leaf-state lifetimes, full-model attributes, projection, and gradients
can set the next peak. Their main storage follows Gaussian count and spherical-harmonic order
rather than image pixels, so smaller leaves do not reduce it. BoundedGS uses view candidates
throughout detailed projection, backward accumulation, and gradient storage.
Before detailed projection, position, opacity, maximum scale, and camera parameters yield conservative screen coverage. Maximum scale bounds covariance independently of rotation, avoiding
full rotated covariances and spherical-harmonic colors during screening. Candidates include every
Gaussian that may affect the current view under the rasterizer, retain uncertain cases, and permit
false positives; detailed projection determines exact intersections. Appendix B.2 gives the construction.
For view v, let Cv be the candidate IDs and nv = |Cv |. Projection reads original parameter rows by
index and internally activates parameters, computes geometry, and evaluates spherical harmonics.
One global model remains; detailed forward and backward states scale with nv , without a full activated tensor or parameter copy. Screening still scans the model but avoids detailed state for every
Gaussian.

Projection backward retains 3DGS’s direct gradient accumulation (DirectGrad), writing to compact
rows indexed by global Gaussian ID. Activation derivatives are computed on that path without a
separate full-model activation graph.
Final gradients for position, spherical harmonics, scale, and rotation retain candidate rows linked to
global parameters by an ID map. Parameters and Adam state keep full layouts; outside-candidate
image gradients are zero. Global opacity regularization requires a dense opacity gradient, added to
image contributions. Candidate indexing shrinks detailed projection and the retained gradient buffer,
while the index and map occupy their own storage.
The optimizer reads compact gradients and IDs directly. For rows outside the candidate set, Adam
still updates the moments and parameters using a zero current image gradient. Gradients and maps
are released after the update. Visibility is combined by union and screen gradients by accumulation
before per-Gaussian densification statistics and scheduled view-level structure updates.
Tile-aligned projection groups can use the same organization. One screening pass builds their candidate indices; each group roots a leaf tree and releases projection state after its leaf passes. Compact gradients use the union of group candidates and accumulate repeated IDs before one update.
Grouping shrinks current projection state while earlier parameter gradients remain live; the main
configuration has one full-image group.

3.4
R ESOURCE PEAKS IN FULL TRAINING
With rendering caches controlled, structure updates and image supply can set new peaks.
Density control changes Gaussian parameters, optimizer state, and statistics together. Rebuilding
after each clone, split, parent removal, and pruning action creates intermediate layouts with concurrent old and new state. BoundedGS instead rebuilds once after view caches are released: density
control selects retained and new Gaussians, and one final mapping constructs their parameters, optimizer state, and statistics. The CPU-assisted path holds the target layout on the host, releases the old
device layout, then uploads the target, trading host memory and transfers for reduced GPU overlap.
Given the same retention and creation decisions, this changes the construction and transfer order of
the layout.
P
For V RGB images with Pv pixels, retaining every decoded uint8 image costs about 3 v Pv
bytes. BoundedGS keeps compressed images on the host and decodes them through a fixed-capacity
prefetch queue, overlapping CPU work with GPU training and bounding decoded-image residency.
Each leaf’s region is packed in pinned memory, transferred as uint8 on a separate stream, converted to floating-point supervision when used, and released after loss backward. Prefetch holds
at most the current and next leaf, tying GPU supervision memory to active regions. Appendix B.5
details image-queue capacity and transfers.

E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP
Datasets and metrics. The full-training comparison uses five outdoor and four indoor scenes from
Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron et al., 2022), and Barn, Family, Train, and Truck from Tanks and Temples (Knapitsch et al., 2017). Mip-NeRF 360 uses the highest-resolution images without downsampling; the Tanks and Temples training images are extracted from the original 4K videos. The three
groups have mean image dimensions of approximately 5041 × 3317, 3116 × 2077, and 3840 × 2160,
denoted 5K, 3K, and 4K below. We report PSNR, SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), LPIPS (Zhang et al.,
2018), pure training time, and the peak temporary rendering working set. Table 1 averages scenes
equally within each group.
Comparison configuration. The main comparison runs full-frame Original and BoundedGS in
the same training framework. Both use the absolute-gradient densification of AbsGS (Ye et al.,
2024), long-axis splitting from ImprovedGS (Deng et al., 2026), and the learnable pruning of
GNS (Deng et al., 2025). Densification ends at 15K steps, followed by gradual pruning to a 50%
retention target, placing the expected rendering-working-set peak near the end of densification. The
two paths share this model-count schedule, CPU-assisted structure rebuilding, and bounded JPEG
image supply; each trains from SfM initialization for 30K steps once per scene. Component and
resolution experiments use short simulations at fixed Gaussian counts, while the resource ablation
covers full 30K-step training. All experiments use an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090.
Memory and timing. The temporary rendering working set includes projection and rasterization
intermediates, supervision and loss caches, and parameter gradients. It excludes parameters, initialized Adam state, and persistent density-control statistics. The main table derives each scene’s
value at its recorded optimization-stage peak; Appendix A.2 gives the exact accounting. These simulations report cumulative time over all training views in their second pass. Pure training time for
the main comparison and resource ablation excludes evaluation and saving, and includes structure
updates and image supply. Structure-update memory and host memory are reported separately.

B.1
S TATE AND EXECUTION ORDER FOR ONE VIEW
Let N be the current Gaussian count and H × W the full image size of the view. Inputs include
the global Gaussian parameters θ, camera, training image, projection-group grid side length g, and
rasterization budget L per leaf. The image is divided into g 2 nonoverlapping groups; Cj denotes
the screened candidate IDs of group j, which is then covered by raster leaves. The main table uses
g = 1 and L = 384 MiB, with one parameter update per training view.
For each view, the system first builds candidate indices for all groups and then processes groups
in row-major order. The current group reads the original parameter rows at its candidate IDs and
projects them once for reuse by all of its leaves. Each leaf recounts intersections under the current projection, completes local rasterization and loss backward, accumulates its contribution to the
group’s projected gradients, and releases its own state. After all leaves in a group, projection backward directly accumulates parameter gradients and the group’s projection and gradient caches are
released before the next group. Global regularization is computed once after all groups, and the optimizer updates after view gradients have been collected. A Gaussian may be a candidate in multiple
groups, whose disjoint pixel regions contribute to the same global parameter row.

the implementation records the event and processes it at this minimum granularity. Each final leaf
is recounted under the current model and camera and follows the original rasterizer’s within-tile
sorting and compositing rules.
For each camera and group, a CPU tree caches the region layout; current leaf loads are recomputed
at every visit. Load increases trigger further subdivision on that visit. When two children are leaves
and both their combined estimate and the parent’s estimate are at most 0.65L, their parent is marked
for possible merger on the next visit. The proposed merger is rechecked before use; changes in
image size, group grid, or budget rebuild the layout.

rows. After releasing the old device layout, it uploads the target arrays, reducing coexistence of
complete old and new GPU structures.
Training images remain as compressed JPEG bytes on the host and are decoded in camera order
through a queue of capacity K = 16. Only the image region needed by the active leaf is uploaded
asynchronously as uint8, converted to FP32 for loss computation, and released afterward.
