### Abstract

The goal of inverse rendering is to decompose geometry, lights, and materials given pose multi-view images. To achieve this goal, we propose neural direct and joint inverse rendering, NDJIR. Different from prior works which relies on some approximations of the rendering equation, NDJIR directly addresses the integrals in the rendering equation and jointly decomposes geometry: signed distance function, lights: environment and implicit lights, materials: base color, roughness, specular reflectance using the powerful and flexible volume rendering framework, voxel grid feature, and Bayesian prior. Our method directly uses the physically-based rendering, so we can seamlessly export an extracted mesh with materials to DCC tools and show material conversion examples. We perform intensive experiments to show that our proposed method can decompose semantically well for real object in photogrammetric setting and what factors contribute towards accurate inverse rendering.

### 1. Introduction

Inverse rendering – given multi-view posed images, we decompose geometry, lights, materials – is a long standing problem in computer vision and graphics. In the era of deep learning, it mainly started in 2D image space [1, 6, 18, 37, 51, 51]. After the great success of neural renderings of implicit representation [45, 60], directly working in 3D space is prominent [4, 5, 25, 35, 47, 63, 65, 67, 68].

However, modeling the rendering equation [30] directly requires sampling lights, which might be prohibitive in memory footprint and computationally expensive when we integrate with the volume rendering [44]. To tackle that problem, there are some approximations proposed: Spherical Gaussian (SG)s [4,65,68], pre-integrated light [5], splitsum [47], and co-located light [3, 64]. Multi-stage training [35, 64, 65, 68] is another form to address the problem. Different direction of approach is using surface rendering [25, 47], but such methods require accurate object mask. None of these approaches do not directly and jointly optimize for the integral of the rendering equation under the powerful and flexible volume rendering. Additionally, prior works are mainly evaluated on synthetic data, which limits practical applicability.

Contribution: In this paper, we propose neural direct and joint inverse rendering, NDJIR [´en(d)Ãi3:r] which light model is tailored to real object in photogrammetric setting. NDJIR directly addresses the integral of the rendering equation and jointly solves the inverse rendering problem with priors. Our key insights are four folds: 1) an indicator function multiplied to the integrand of the specular term allows for simple joint training, 2) directly addressing the integral with importance sampling mitigates degeneration of material networks, 3) introducing Bayesian priors reduces degeneration more, and 4) dense voxel grid feature reinforces more plausible results. We also conducted intensive studies using real datasets. Code is available in https://github.com/sony/NDJIR.

### 2. Related work

Geometry extraction: There are several 3D geometry representations, e.g., mesh, signed distance field (SDF), and volume density. Correspondingly, differentiable renderers were proposed [10, 32, 36, 38, 40–43, 45, 48, 60] in a decade in order to extract such a representation. Notably, NeuS [57] and VolSDF [59] integrates SDF representation with volume rendering [45], SDF is implicit representation and 0 level set corresponds to surface, so we can easily extract mesh once SDF is acquired, and volume rendering [45] mitigates the need of corresponding accurate object masks. As a result, we can extract a mesh given multi-view posed images of an object. Based on the works [45, 57], we jointly decompose geometry, lights, and materials.

Materials and lights estimation: Neural-based materials and lights estimation started in 2D image space [1, 6, 18, 37, 51, 51]. Soon after the success of differentiable rendering of implicit representation [45, 60]; materials and lights estimation is mainly based on neural rendering [4, 5, 25, 35, 47, 63, 65, 67, 68]. Spherical Gaussian (SG)s are often used in [4, 65, 68] for illumination, but it can only represent low to middle frequency and specular materials. Evaluating physically-based rendering (PBR) directly is considered costly, so there are other approximations to PBR: pre-integrated lighting [5], split-sum [47], and co-located light [3,64]. Recently, Hasselgren et al. [25] directly optimizes physically-based rendering using Monte Carlo integration and denoiser on top of rendered image. However, applying denoiser requires full resolution image, which might be bounded by image resolution. Their work relies on DMTet [54] so uses the surface rendering on extracted mesh at each iteration of training, which reduces memory footprint compared to using the volume rendering and may allow for full resolution rendering. Nonetheless, this type of approaches sacrifices level of details of geometry and needs accurate foreground object mask. Contrarily, our approach utilizes the powerfully volume rendering to directly evaluate PBR by circumventing illumination evaluation in 3D space but in pixel space and introducing an indicator function, being able to produce high-poly mesh and high frequent materials.

Acceleration structure: One of shortcomings of the volume rendering is slowness. Prior works to mitigate slow rendering in runtime [19, 27, 39, 58, 61] are based on voxel grid structure, which influences following works for faster training [17, 55, 56, 58]. Density is directly optimized on voxel grid [17, 31, 55] to remove evaluations of MLP. TensorF [8] factorizes voxel grid to vector-matrix low-rank representation to speed up look-up from voxel grid representation. Instant NGP [46] uses hash table on voxel grid to further acceleration. These works aims for novel view synthesis, an acquired underlying geometry is not smooth so can not easily be exported to existing DCC tools as 3D asset. Thus, we use the simple dense voxel grid feature.

### 3. Method

In this section, we formulate the novel forward rendering tailored to inverse rendering of real object. Our proposed method is based on two important prior works: NeuS [57] and Physically-based rendering with Cook-Torrance specular BRDF (Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function) model [12]. These models are briefly described in Sec. 3.1. In Sec. 3.2, we introduce light model specific to photogrammetry setting and integrate the light model to PBR in computationally efficient and trainable way. Even if we can train inverse rendering system, we may not be able to obtain meaningful materials since we only have ground truth color as supervision, there are plenty of solutions for materials to be estimated. Therefore, we pose priors on materials in Sec. 3.4. At the end of this section Sec. 3.5, we elaborate implementation of NDJIR. Note we assume object is opaque and dielectric, and lights are white. Furthermore, we only denote the dimension of sample points along a ray for simplicity, and several input arguments to a function in equations are excluded for visibility in Secs. 3.1 and 3.2. For rigorous inputs and outputs, see Sec. 3.3. More detailed equations and network diagram can be found in the supplementary material.

#### 3.1. Background

Volume rendering with SDF: Given sample points {xi} N i=0 along a ray r, NeuS [57] formulates the volume rendering with SDF s to render a color Cˆ as follows,


$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i, T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad (1)$$

$$\alpha_j = \max \left( \frac{\Phi(s_j, \sigma) - \Phi(s_{j+1}, \sigma)}{\Phi(s_j, \sigma)}, 0 \right),$$

where c is radiance, α is opacity using adjacent SDFs, T is accumulated transmittance, Φ is the cumulative distribution function of logistic distribution with the globally trainable scale σ.

Physically-based rendering: Rendering equation [30] with Cook-Torrance specular BRDF model [12] is the principle of modern graphics engines e.g., [11, 16, 24]. Given a surface point xˆ, normals nˆ, corresponding spatially-varying materials: base color cˆb, roughness αˆr, and specular reflectance fˆ0, viewing direction v, and light direction l over hemisphere Ω+ aligned with nˆ, the rendered color Cˆ(r) is modeled as


$$\begin{aligned} & \underbrace{\frac{\hat{c}_b}{\pi} \int_{\Omega_+} L(\hat{x}, l) (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl}_{\text{diffuse term}} \\ & + \underbrace{\int_{\Omega_+} \frac{D(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) G(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{f}_0)}{4(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})(\hat{n} \cdot l)} L(\hat{x}, l) (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl}_{\text{specular term}} \end{aligned} \quad (2)$$

L is incoming light intensity, D is normal distribution function, G is masking-shadowing function, and F is Fresnel function. With arbitrary two vectors a and b, (a· b) is saturated dot product max(a T b, dot). We use Filament BRDF model [24] for D, G, and F. For space, explicit models of D, G, and F are excluded but in the supplementary.

### 3.2. Joint inverse rendering

NDJIR models the incoming light intensity attenuated by the dot product L(x,l)(n,l) for the diffuse and specular term, respectively:


$$L_P(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{n}) (L_V(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{l}) L_E(\mathbf{l}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) + L_I(\mathbf{x})), \quad (3)$$

|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|

where LV is soft visibility, LE is intensity of environment map, LI is intensity of implicit illumination, and LP is photogrammetric light intensity.

Environment map LE is same as in the classical PBR to query light intensity directionally, and the implicit illumination LI resembles the photon map [28] to query light intensity in space, both of which together captures direct and indirect illumination well. Implicit illumination LI is one of keys to successful training; otherwise, training fails to bad local minima. Notable light model in NDJIR is photogrammetric light LP tailored to photogrammetry setting where a photographer dynamically becomes masks of lights and casts shadows, which is not modeled by classical PBR using a typical pinhole camera model. Photogrammetric light LP is also a importance factor for joint inverse rendering.

Plugging both Eq. \(3\) and Eq. \(4\) to Eq. \(2\), LP is factored out, but still we have the integrals. If we integrate the volume rendering Eq. \(1\) with the physically-based rendering Eq. \(2\) naively, we must sample incident lights per batch, ray, and, point during training, which prohibitively occupies memory footprint and is computationally expensive. Therefore, for computing the specular term in Eq. \(2\), we apply the volume rendering Eq. \(1\) to spatially-varying quantities: roughness αr, specular reflectance f0, analytically computed normals n, photogrammetric light LP , and implicit illumination LI , one exception is computing LV , we apply the volume rendering Eq. \(1\) to x. In other words, we first reduce point dimension by volume rendering and compute the specular term in pixel space. Then, we perform incident light sampling to compute the integrals in Eq. \(2\). This idea is similar to [4]; however, we can directly optimize thanks to the indicator function of Eq. \(6\) and do not use any approximations. For computing diffuse component in Eq. \(2\), we first entangle cb and Lp and utilize the same way as in computing the specular term for the integral then multiply them. Finally, using shorthand notation ˆ· as the volume rendering Eq. \(1\) to each quantity (·) other than c, the rendered color Cˆ(r) of NDJIR is the summation of the diffuse and specular terms formulated as


$$\frac{\widehat{L}_{\text{pr}} \mathbf{c}_b}{\pi} \int_{\Omega_+} \left( L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{l}) L_E(\mathbf{l}) (\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{l}) + \widehat{L}_I(\mathbf{x}) \right) d\mathbf{l}, \quad (5)$$


$$\widehat{L}_p \int_{\Omega_+} \frac{D(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{\mathbf{n}}, \hat{\alpha}_r) G(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{\mathbf{n}}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{\mathbf{f}}_0)}{4(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{v})(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot l)} (6)$$

$$L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, l) L_E(l)(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot l) I(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{\mathbf{n}}) dl,$$

where I(v,l, nˆ) is the indicator function of 1 if ((nˆ · l) ≥ I )∧((nˆ · v) ≥ I )∧ (nˆ · v+l ||v+l||2 ) ≥ I , otherwise 0.

Entanglement of Lp and cb is important; otherwise, training does not successfully start. Indicator function is also necessary for joint-training. As geometry and corresponding normals are changing during training, dot products of numerator in Eq. \(6\) can be negative. Negative values are omitted by the saturation of dot product, but we also have several dot products in denominator of the specular term, so does DGF, such that saturated dot products increase value of the specular term, which results in really large gradients in backward pass. To prevent such gradient explosion, we introduce the indicator function. In that sense, the indicator function can be viewed as hard visibility factor specific to the specular term in joint inverse rendering.

We are mixing the volume rendering and physically based rendering; however, once training starts, the trainable scale σ of Eq. \(1\) is going up, geometry is gradually being formed, then the specular term is considered in training, and rendering is going towards surface rendering. Therefore, NDJIR approaches to LP × PBR computed on surface in the end of training.

#### 3.3. Network

All geometry, lights, and materials are represented by naive multi-layer perceptron (MLP); geometric network is denoted by fG(x) ∈ R DG+1, base color network cb(x,FG) ∈ R 3 , roughness network αr(x,FG, n) ∈ R, specular reflectance network f0(x,FG, n) ∈ R 3 , environment light network LE(l) ∈ R, soft visibility network LV (x,l,FG, n) ∈ R, implicit illumination network LI (x,FG, n) ∈ R, and photogrammetric light network LP (x, v,FG, n, d−1 ) ∈ R. FG ∈ R DG is geometric feature as one of the outputs of fG in addition to SDF s ∈ R. We add the inverse squared distance d −2 as input of Lp, which improves geometric reconstruction empirically.

Choice of inputs is based on our preliminary experiments where normals n captures light distribution spatially. For base color network, we do not expect that light is baked into base color, thus we exclude normals from inputs. Light networks use a single channel output because of white light assumption to reduce ambiguity. For specular reflectance, we use 3 channels in order to increase degree of freedom.

Positional encoding γ(·, `) [45] is applied to the geometric network as γ(x, `G), the environment light network as γ(l, `E), the soft visibility network as γ(l, `V ), and the photogrammetric light network as γ(v, `P ), where ` is the number of frequencies. Results are concatenated to the original input.

Dense voxel grid feature of linear interpolation with grid size G is used but only for x in the geometric network. We tried several variants: the one with varying interpolations (cosine and lanzcos), triplane and triline grid feature similar to tensor factorization [8], and voxel hash grid feature [46]. With varying interpolations achieve similar performance both quantitatively and qualitatively. Triplane and triline grid feature extracts smoother geometry but sometimes bad separation between foreground object and background, depending on scene; also, semantic separation of materials are worse than using dense voxel. With voxel hash feature [46] can not be trained, we conjecture this is due to the simultaneous use of Eikonal regularization [23].

#### 3.4. Loss and prior

Our loss functions are composed of RGB color loss LRGB, Eikonal regularization LE [23], total variation loss LT V [17], base color prior Lcb , roughness prior Lαr , and specular prior Lf0 . These losses are computed as follows.

RGB color loss LRGB is defined as L1 loss of the residual between render color Cˆ(r) and ground truth color C(r):

$$\mathcal{L}_{RGB} = \|\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_1. \quad (7)$$

Eikonal regularization LE is the constraint SDF meets:

$$\mathcal{L}_E = (\|s\|_2 - 1)^2. \quad (8)$$

Total variation loss LT V of voxel grid feature is computed as

$$\mathcal{L}_{TV} = \sqrt{\Delta f_{i+1,j,k}^2 + \Delta f_{i,j+1,k}^2 + \Delta f_{i,j,k+1}^2} \quad (9)$$

where ∆fi+1,j,k is the difference of adjacent features along x axis, same for y and z axes, fi,j,k is positionallyembedded trainable feature corresponding to discretized query point.

Base color prior Lcb is defined spatially between query point and near point perturbed by cb ,


$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{c}_b} = \|\mathbf{c}_b(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{c}_b(\mathbf{x} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{c}_b})\|_1. \quad (10)$$

Even if we can train inverse rendering system jointly, we found roughness and specular reflectance tend to degenerate and outputs either unary (0 or 1) or binary (0 and 1) value(s). This can be mitigated by using smaller MLPs and increasing the number of incident lights to some extent. Even though, roughness and specular reflectance networks would degenerate, so we introduce priors on both networks.

For specular reflectance, we know that specular reflectance of common object is 0.04 [24] as prior knowledge, but we do not know for roughness, so we simply set a certain value rp as prior. Because using a deterministic value is too strong, we pose Laplacian distribution as prior distribution under Bayesian deep learning framework [33].

$$\mathcal{L}_{\alpha_r} = \frac{\|\alpha_r - r_p\|_1}{\sigma_{\alpha_r}} + \log(\sigma_{\alpha_r}), \quad (11)$$

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{f}_0} = \frac{\|\mathbf{f}_0 - \mathbf{0.04}\|_1}{\sigma_{\mathbf{f}_0}} + \log(\sigma_{\mathbf{f}_0}), \quad (12)$$

where the roughness network is forked at the last layer of MLP and outputs σαr additionally and same for σf0 of the specular reflectance network.

Our final objective to be minimized with corresponding weights, AABB-ray intersection mask MA, and denominators N and M is

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{RGB}}{N} + \lambda_E \frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{EMA}}{M} + \lambda_{TV} \frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{TVMA}}{M} \\ & + \lambda_{cb} \frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{cb} \overline{M}_A}{M} + \lambda_{\alpha_r} \frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha_r \overline{M}_A}}{M} + \lambda_{f_0} \frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{f_0} \overline{M}_A}{M}, \end{aligned} \quad (13)$$

where · is the summation of all dimensions.

#### 3.5. Implementation

Our scene is normalized in the same way as [60] such that an object is approximately inside a unit-sphere located on the origin. We put on the origin the voxel grid which min and max coordinates are (−1, −1, −1) and (1, 1, 1), respectively. Grid size is 512, and feature size are 4. During training, we sample 4 camera locations and cast 512 rays at each iteration; if a ray hits the axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) of the voxel grid, we sample 64 points between near and far hits, then upsample 4 times 16 points same as [57]. For background modeling, we use NeRF++ [66] and sample 32 points per ray staring from the far hit of AABB. If a ray does not hit AABB, we sample points only for background modeling from the location of the distance ||camera location||2 − 1 along that ray.

Voxel grid feature is implemented in CUDA [49] and supports double-backward [15]. This is needed; otherwise, extracted geometry is locally jagged. For total variation loss, the asymmetric backward is used in [17], but we found the symmetric backward mitigates the baked light on base color cb, so the symmetric backward is used. When using the voxel grid feature, the extracted mesh is rugged, but applying simple average filter dramatically mitigates and produces smoother mesh. Application of average filters is 2 times as default.

fG has 8 layers and one skip connection at the middle as in [57] with DG = 256. cb and Lp are 4 layers of 256 feature size. The other MLPs have 4 layers of 128 feature size. We use softplus(β = 100) [69] as activation function, and output is ranged in [−1, 1] by the sigmoid function in all MLPs except for LE, σαr , and σf0 which use softplus(β = 1) as output activation function. As in [24], we remap roughness and specular reflectance networks such that actual roughness is αr ← α r and specular reflectance is f0 ← 0.16 × f 2 0 . To prevent 0 division, we clip as max(αr, 0.089). `G = 6, `E = `V = 6, and `P = 4 are set in positional encodings. All networks are initialized using Glorot initialization [21] except for fG where the geometric initialization [2] is used. Voxel grid features are directly initialized by sampling from N (0,(10−3 ) 2 ).

When computing the integrals, we use Monte Carlo integration. The number of incident lights is 128 per pixel. In Eq. \(5\), the uniform sampling is used and the importance sampling in consideration of roughness αr in Eq. \(6\) same as [24]. As suggested in [62], we do not propagate gradients in the sampling process.

n = 10−16 is added to volume rendered normals nˆ, then the result is unit-vector normalized. Even small n produces bias, but this is necessary to prevent 0-division especially at the beginning of training. cb is sampled from Normal distribution N (0,( √ 3 2 G ) 2 ). We set dot = I = 10−8 , λE = 10−1 , λT V = 10−1 , λcb = 10−1 , λαr = 10−5 , λf0 = 10−3 , and rp = 0.5 as default. N is multiplication of the batch size of 4 and sampled pixels of 512, and M is multiplication of the number of rays hitting AABB and sampled points of 128.

For training, we use Adam optimizer [34] with 10−3 weight decay and 5 × 10−4 learning rate, and the warmup [22] is applied for 1.5 % of the total epoch 1500. After the warmup period, the learning rate cosine-decays towards 5 × 10−6 . Training takes 2.8 and 3.7 hours for 49 and 64 images of DTU MVS dataset [29], respectively using A100 GPU.

To extract mesh, we use MarchingCubs algorithm with grid size of 512. For texture baking, we use Blender [11], first we use Smart UV unwrap to get uv-coordinates which is then applied to baking vertex attributes to texture maps in Cycles renderer. To physically-based render extracted meshes with decomposed materials, we utilize Open3D [70] and set the sun light direction above object(s).

### 4. Experiment

Dataset: We use DTU MVS dataset [29] curated by [60]. Dataset contains 15 scenes where real object(s) is captured with a industrial robot. 16 LEDs are located above object(s). 49 and 64 images are given with camera poses.

Evaluation: For inverse rendering of real object, it is hard to evaluate quantitatively in material space, so we compare our method in image space using PSNR and SSIM. Geometrically, we evaluate decomposition quantity with reference given by DTU MVS dataset. In the other cases, we show qualitative comparisons.

Baseline: We use NeuS [57] as quantitative baseline. For qualitative comparison, we implement a method bearing the similar spirit of split-sum [47] and pre-integrated light [5] under our framework. The derivation and more results are found in the supplementary material.

### 4.3. Material conversion

Metallic conversion: Metalness is not modeled in our method, but once specular reflectance is obtained, we can simulate metalness according to the strength of specular reflectance to some extent. Fig. 9 are the results of reflectance-aware metallic conversion. Metal-like objects exhibit more metallic than non-metal PBR.

Rebaking of implicit illumination: We sometimes want to bake light distribution of a captured scene to base color, e.g., 3D printing. Fig. 7 shows the interpolation results between base color and base color × implicit illumination. As seen, when increasing the interpolation coefficient, we can more re-bake light distribution to base color as postprocessing.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Discussion: Here, we mainly discuss limitations of our method. NDJIR sometimes underestimates or overestimates e.g., in Fig. 1, the left cheek and chest of scan69 and the left jaw of scan65. Increasing the number of light samples and introducing Bayesian prior prevents roughness and specular networks from degenerating, but we still have extreme values as in Fig. 3. We believe in that more reasonable priors can mitigate degeneration and extract more plausible results. Dense voxel grid feature produces tiny noisy artifacts and/or non-smooth geometry even if we apply average filters as in Fig. 9, e.g., scissors . Importance sampling of Eq. \(6\), modeling implicit illumination as in Fig. 7, and strong regularization of Eq. \(10\) can make base color flatter in Fig. 8 at the cost of removal of small colors, e.g., dot eyes and thin mouth of scan69. We do not model metalness, so metallic conversion in Fig. 9 is object-agnostic.

Conclusion: We propose NDJIR, neural direct and joint inverse rendering for geometry, lights, and materials of real object tailored to photogrammetry setting. Our proposed method is direct and joint optimization. NDJIR achieves better performances with the prior work and semantically well decomposition of real object.

### S2. Scene layout and point sampling

Fig. S11 shows the scene layout. With the preprocessing [60], we assume that an object of interest is located on the origin and inside the unit-sphere. If a ray hits the axisaligned bounding box (AABB) with min = (−1, −1, −1) and max = (1, 1, 1), we can denote the intersection points as (tnear, tfar) and samples points between them. From tfar, we also sample points for background modeling as in NeRF++ [66]. If a ray does not hit AABB, then we use ||c||2 − 1 as tfar and apply NeRF++ only.

### S3. Monte Carlo integration

We will in detail describe how to compute the diffuse and specular term which contain the integrals. Our implementation is almost same as in [24]. However, to make the paper self-contained, we elaborate Monte Carlo integration.

First of all, the rendering equation [30] with Cook-Torrance specular BRDF model [12] is given:


$$\underbrace{\frac{\hat{c}_b}{\pi} \int_{\Omega_+} L(\hat{x}, l) (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl}_{\text{diffuse term}} + \underbrace{\int_{\Omega_+} \frac{D(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) G(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(\mathbf{v}, l, \hat{f}_0)}{4(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})(\hat{n} \cdot l)} L(\hat{x}, l) (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl}_{\text{specular term}} \quad (\text{S1})$$

where D, G, and F are defined as in [24],

$$\begin{aligned} D &= \frac{\hat{\alpha}_r^2}{\pi ((\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})^2 (\hat{\alpha}_r^2 - 1) + 1)^2}, \\ G &= \frac{2(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})}{(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) + \sqrt{\hat{\alpha}_r^2 + (1 - \hat{\alpha}_r^2)(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})^2}} \quad (S2) \\ &\times \frac{2(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})}{(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + \sqrt{\hat{\alpha}_r^2 + (1 - \hat{\alpha}_r^2)(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2}}, \\ F &= \mathbf{f}_0 + (1 - \mathbf{f}_0)(1 - (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h}))^5. \end{aligned}$$

In case of Filament model [24], we can simplify the shadowing-masking function G in consideration of the denominator of Eq. \(S1\) and without the height correction of microfacet:

$$V := \frac{G}{4(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} = \frac{1}{(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) + \sqrt{\hat{a}_r^2 + (1 - \hat{a}_r^2)(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})^2}} \times \frac{1}{(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + \sqrt{\hat{a}_r^2 + (1 - \hat{a}_r^2)(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2}}. \quad (\text{S3})$$

Thus, the simplified rendering equation is

$$\underbrace{\frac{\hat{c}_b}{\pi} \int_{\Omega_+} L(\hat{x}, l) (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl}_{\text{diffuse term}} + \underbrace{\int_{\Omega_+} D(v, l, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) V(v, l, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(v, l, \hat{f}_0) L(\hat{x}, l) (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl}_{\text{specular term}}. \quad (\text{S4})$$

Accordingly, the diffuse and specular terms of NDJIR forward rendering model become


$$\frac{\widehat{L}_{pb} c_b}{\pi} \int_{\Omega_+} \left( L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{l}) L_E(\mathbf{l}) (\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{l}) + \widehat{L}_I(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \right) d\mathbf{l}, \quad (S5)$$



Figure S10. Network diagram. P.E. means the positional encoding. Voxel indicates the look-up result of the dense voxel grid feature. VR is the abbreviation of the volume rendering, and the dotted lines mean the result of VR. Ranges below some notations are the output ranges.  $\oplus$  and  $\otimes$  is element-wise addition and multiplication, respectively. Note  $\alpha$  and  $T$  go to all VRs, but for visibility, such lines are omitted. Also,  $l$  is the result of the uniform or importance samplings so depends on  $\alpha_r$  and  $\hat{n}$ , but its line is omitted for visibility.


$$\widehat{L}_p \int_{\Omega_+} D(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) V(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \hat{\mathbf{f}}_0) L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{l}) L_E(\mathbf{l}) (\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) I(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \hat{n}) d\mathbf{l}, \quad (\text{S6})$$

For computing the specular term Eq. (S6), we use Monte Carlo integration with importance sampling:


$$\frac{\widehat{L}_p}{N_L} \sum_i^{N_L} \frac{D(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) V(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{\mathbf{f}}_0)}{p(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r)} L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{l}_i) L_E(\mathbf{l}_i) (\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_i) I(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}), \quad (\text{S7})$$

where  $N_L$  is the number of light samples per pixel, and  $p$  is the probabilistic distribution function (PDF). We use  $D(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r)(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})$  for the PDF but consider the reflection of viewing direction  $\mathbf{v}$  around half vector  $\mathbf{h}$ , so the transformed PDF is


$$p = D(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) (\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{h}) \frac{1}{4(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h})}. \quad (\text{S8})$$

Putting Eq. (S8) into Eq. (S7) leads to


$$\frac{\widehat{L}_p}{N_L} \sum_i^{N_L} V(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}, \hat{\alpha}_r) F(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{\mathbf{f}}_0) \frac{4(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h})}{(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})} L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{l}_i) L_E(\mathbf{l}_i) (\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_i) I(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}_i, \hat{n}). \quad (\text{S9})$$

Now, we have the discretized form for the specular term Eq. (S9), the final piece is the way to sample  $l$  according to the PDF. Using the solid angle representation, we can define the PDF as

$$p(\theta, \phi) = \frac{\hat{\alpha}_r^2}{\pi(\cos^2 \theta (\hat{\alpha}_r^2 - 1) + 1)^2} \cos \theta \sin \theta, \quad (\text{S10})$$

where  $\theta$  and  $\phi$  is azimuthal and polar angle, respectively. Applying the inverse transform method, we can sample  $l$  using

$$\begin{aligned} \phi &= 2\pi \zeta_\phi, \\ \theta &= \cos^{-1} \sqrt{\frac{1 - \zeta_\theta}{(\hat{\alpha}_r^2 - 1) \zeta_\theta + 1}}, \\ l &= (\cos \phi \sin \theta, \sin \phi \sin \theta, \cos \theta), \end{aligned} \quad (\text{S11})$$

where  $\zeta_\phi$  and  $\zeta_\theta$  are sampled from the uniform distribution of the range  $[0, 1)$ . Now,  $l$  is defined in the upper-hemisphere on the world coordinate system, so we transform it to the local coordinate system where the normals  $\hat{n}$  is the up-vector:  $l \leftarrow T(n)l$ . As noted in the main script, we do not backpropagate gradients in the sampling process as suggested in [62].



Figure S11. Scene layout. For visibility, we draw the corresponding 2D case.

Since the range of azimuthal angle is as twice as polar angle, we sample two times larger samples regarding θ than φ then use all possible combinations of θ and φ (same as the batch-wise meshgrid operation). For example, when we samples 8 points for the polar angle, the number of light samples per pixel is 8 × 16 = 128.

In summary, for computing the specular term, we sample light l according to Eq. \(S11\), then transform it as l ← T(n)l, finally apply it to Eq. \(S9\).

Similarly, the diffuse term Eq. \(S5\) can be computed by Monte Carlo integration but with uniform sampling:

$$\frac{\widehat{L_p c_b}}{N_L} \sum_{i=1}^{N_L} L_V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{l}_i) L_E(\mathbf{l}_i) (\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{l}_i) + \widehat{L_p c_b \widehat{L_I(\mathbf{x})}}. \quad (\text{S12})$$

Note that the normalization factor π is canceled out, and θ of Eq. \(S11\) becomes

$$\theta = \cos^{-1}(1 - \zeta_\theta). \quad (\text{S13})$$

### S4. Derivation of model with split-sum and PIL

As baseline, we implemented the model which bears the similar spirit of split-sum [47] and pre-integrated light (PIL) [5].

We split the specular term of Eq. \(S1\) into two factors:

$$\int_{\Omega_+} L(\hat{x}, l)(\hat{n} \cdot l) dl \int_{\Omega_+} \frac{DGF}{4(\hat{n} \cdot v)(\hat{n} \cdot l)} (\hat{n} \cdot l) dl. \quad (\text{S14})$$

Then, representing the light integral factor divided by π using pre-integrated neural network L˜(xˆ, nˆ, v, d−2 ), the rendering equation Eq. \(S1\) becomes

$$\tilde{L}\hat{\mathbf{c}}_b + (\pi\tilde{L}) \int_{\Omega_+} \frac{DGF}{4(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{v})(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{l})} (\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{l}) d\mathbf{l}. \quad (\text{S15})$$

Similarly to NDJIR forward rendering model, we entangle the two factors in the diffuse term and apply volume rendering to quantities, then the forward rendering model of split-sum + PIL approximation is

$$\widehat{Lc_b} + (\pi\widehat{L}) \int_{\Omega_+} \frac{DGF}{4(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})(\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} (\hat{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) d\mathbf{l}. \quad (\text{S16})$$

#### S5. Double backward of voxel grid feature

Naive implementation of the voxel grid feature is to use function composition each of which supports auto-grad as implemented in modern neural network libraries [7, 26, 50]. However, such implementation constructs a long and complex computation graph which can not be optimized automatically, resulting in computational overhead in speed and large memory footprint. Thus, we implement specific CUDA [49] kernels to support double-backward [15].

#### S5.1. Forward function

Given query points {xb} = {(xb, yb, zb)}, voxel grid feature FV with grid size G and bounded in min m and max M, we first transform query point to the discrete coordinate system:

$$\bar{x}_b = \frac{x_b - m}{M - m} G = s(x_b - m). \quad (\text{S17})$$

Then, the voxel grid feature outputs

$$\bar{f}(\bar{x}_b) = \sum_{i,j,k} w_{i,j,k}(\bar{x}_b) \mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}(\bar{x}_b), \quad (\text{S18})$$

where wi,j,k(x¯b) is the interpolation coefficient, and fi,j,k(x¯b) is the voxel grid feature queried by x¯b at i, j, k-th location of the discrete coordinate system.

#### S5.2. Grad function

For gradient function, we denote as g (n) gradient operator on n-th order gradient graph. Here, we have two inputs: query point xb and voxel grid feature fi,j,k. Correspondingly, there are two grad functions.

Gradient w.r.t. query point:


$$g^{(1)}x_b = \sum_d g^{(1)}\bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \sum_{i,j,k} s_x \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{\mathbf{x}}_b} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)$$

$$g^{(1)}y_b = \sum_d g^{(1)}\bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \sum_{i,j,k} s_y \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{\mathbf{y}}_b} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)$$

$$g^{(1)}z_b = \sum_d g^{(1)}\bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \sum_{i,j,k} s_z \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{z}_b} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b). \quad (\text{S19})$$

Note that sx, sy, and sz come from the chain rule of the backpropagation, ∂wi,j,k(x¯b) = ∂x¯b ∂wi,j,k(x¯b) ∂x¯b .

∂xb ∂xb Gradient w.r.t. voxel grid feature:

$$g^{(1)} \mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) += g^{(1)} \bar{\mathbf{f}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \quad (\text{S20})$$

We can write a specific form regarding the derivative of wi,j,k(x¯b), but it depends on implementation of interpolation. Further, if we write a specific form, notations become more complicated. Thus, this form is enough to proceed. Note that in case of Straight Through Estimator (STE) [13], ∂wi,j,k(x¯) ∂x¯ = 0 and same for y and z, meaning the normals of the voxel grid feature would be ignored.

#### S5.3. Grad grad function

For gradients of gradients, there are five cases. However, we are only interested in Eikonal regularization [23] thus solely consider two cases.

Gradient of Eq. \(S19\) w.r.t. grad output:

$$\begin{aligned} g^{(2)} g^{(1)} \bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) &= g^{(2)} g^{(1)} x_b \sum_{i,j,k} s_x \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{\mathbf{x}}_b} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \\ &+ g^{(2)} g^{(1)} y_b \sum_{i,j,k} s_y \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{y}_b} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \\ &+ g^{(2)} g^{(1)} z_b \sum_{i,j,k} s_z \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{z}_b} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) \end{aligned} \tag{S21}$$

Gradient of Eq. \(S19\) w.r.t. voxel grid feature:

$$\begin{aligned} g^{(2)} f_{i,j,k}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) + & g^{(2)} g^{(1)} x_b g^{(1)} \bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) s_x \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{\mathbf{x}}_b} \\ & + g^{(2)} g^{(1)} y_b g^{(1)} \bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) s_y \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{y}_b} \\ & + g^{(2)} g^{(1)} z_b g^{(1)} \bar{f}^d(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b) s_z \frac{\partial w_{i,j,k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_b)}{\partial \bar{z}_b}. \end{aligned} \tag{S22}$$

In CUDA implementation, we do not optimize codes, simply parallelize both b and d dimensions, and use the atomic add operation for the respective summations. Note that fi,j,k(x¯b) is the result of look-up, we do not (cannot) backpropagate w.r.t. x.

### S6. More experimental results

### S6.1. More primary result

Decomposed geometry, light, and materials: Figs. S12 to S15 show decomposed components of primary results. Geometry is well extracted, base color is flatter, and comprehensively, combinations of roughness, specular reflectance, and implicit illumination exhibits a photogrammetric settings: high intensity and/or specularity corresponds to lower roughness, low specular reflectance shows low specularity, and implicit illumination captures light distribution spatially.

However, there are several failure cases. 1) Black color of base color are removed, e.g., eyes of scan37, scan55, scan69, scan83, and scan109. This might be mainly due to the implicit illumination and photogrammetric networks. Since either network can be able to output 0, base color can be any values in that case. With strong base color prior, such values are towards colors in neighbors. Nonetheless, this might be trade-off to remove shadows from base color, we can observe clear removals of shadow in base color, e.g, the block shadow in scan40 and hat shadow in scan69. To some extent, we can add black color on base color as seen in the rebaking examples of the main script at the cost of non-flat base color and baked shadow. Further study of better light model is needed in order to mitigate such drawbacks. 2) Quality of geometric reconstruction is highly affected by hight specularity, especially on yellow colors. On some yellow colored regions with high specularity, e.g., in scan63 and scan110, geometric reconstructions clearly worse. Correspondingly, the roughness is intuitively flipped on such regions in scan63. For better geometric reconstruction on such regions, we may need more view-consistent constraints as modeled in [14]. 3) As noted in the main script, we do not model metalness, so decomposing metallic object is not optimal.

Rendered images: Figs. S12 to S15 also show rendered images of primary results. Neural rendered images look real, and PBR images are towards photorealism.

Decomposed environment lights: Fig. S16 illustrates decomposed environment lights of all scenes. All environment lights exhibit non-uniformity and capture the DTU MVS light setting except for the one of scan114 which clearly fails to decompose environment lights and shows strong uniformity.
