Research on Neural Radiance Field Technology: Principles,
Implementation, and Evolution from NeRF to NeRF²
Yanpu Cai
School of Mechanical & Electrical Engineering, Heilongjiang University, Harbin, China
1584081386@qq.com
Abstract. Neural radiance fields (NeRFs) represent 3D scenes as continuous implicit
functions and use differentiable volume rendering to convert 2D observations into 3D
reconstruction and novel-view synthesis. The technology has expanded from static scenes to
dynamic rendering, real-time neural graphics, and 3D generation, and into wireless
communications as NeRF² for radio-frequency propagation modeling. Under a unified view
of optical implicit fields and electromagnetic radiance fields, this paper reviews the
frameworks, core methods, representative results, and limitations of visual NeRF and radiofrequency NeRF², and proposes a task-matched evaluation framework. It covers positional
encoding, sampling, volume rendering, dynamic modeling, and 3D AIGC in the visual
domain, and complex-valued signals, attenuation and reradiation, spatial-spectrum
prediction, and few-shot data augmentation in the radio-frequency domain. Rather than rank
the families by task-independent quality, the paper compares them through output objects,
physical constraints, data requirements, and application objectives, supporting cross-modal
implicit-field research in visual reconstruction, wireless-channel prediction, and 6G digital
twins.
Keywords: NeRF, NeRF², volume rendering, wireless channel modeling, digital twins
1. Introduction
NeRF's main contribution is to represent a 3D scene as a continuous function that maps spatial
position and viewing direction to density and view-dependent color, rather than as fixed grids or
meshes [1]. Differentiable volume rendering and multi-view image supervision enable novel-view
synthesis without explicit 3D annotations. By optimizing geometry, appearance, and rendering
together, NeRF became foundational in 3D vision, robotics, virtual reality, and digital content
generation [2].
Wireless systems must model propagation paths, attenuation, reflection, diffraction, and
multipath in complex environments. Ray tracing is physically interpretable but can be expensive for
large, dynamic, or multi-configuration scenes [3]. 6G digital twins also require joint modeling of
environment, channel, and network state for coverage prediction, channel estimation, and
deployment optimization [4], motivating transfer of neural implicit fields from visible light to radio
frequency.

Research has followed two paths: visual methods improve representation capacity, sampling
efficiency, dynamic-scene modeling, and generative control; wireless methods introduce continuous
fields with complex-valued signals and propagation priors because visual NeRF cannot directly
represent electromagnetic propagation. Both use continuous field functions, neural fitting, and
differentiable rendering, but their outputs differ: visual NeRF predicts images, depth, or geometry,
whereas radio-frequency NeRF² predicts signal strength, spatial spectra, arrival angles, and channel
states.
Based on a systematic review, this paper reorganizes the trajectory of visual NeRF, summarizes
radio-frequency modeling pathways, and proposes an integrated evaluation framework. Visual
development is structured as static representation, efficiency improvement, dynamic modeling, and
3D AIGC; NeRF², NeWRF, R-NeRF, and VoxelRF are reviewed in parallel. The framework covers
visual quality, radio-frequency prediction, computational and data efficiency, physical consistency,
and scene generalization. Literature checking, numerical reporting, and figure and table preparation
follow a traceable scientific-writing workflow [5].
The primary innovations and contributions of this work are as follows:
• Visual NeRF research is reorganized as static representation, efficiency improvement, dynamic
modeling, and 3D AIGC, strengthening the review's structure.
• This review further contributes a systematic survey of representative radio-frequency methods
(including NeRF², NeWRF, R-NeRF, and VoxelRF), distilling their technical characteristics and
development trends.
• A multidimensional framework covers visual quality, radio-frequency prediction, computational
and data efficiency, physical consistency, and scene generalization for horizontal comparison and
future work.

Engineering-wise, visual NeRF has four modules: input receives multi-view images, camera

integrates each ray to form training or novel-view images. Rendering errors backpropagate to close
the observation-encoding-field-rendering-optimization loop.

2.2. Core methods
Figure 1. Schematic of the visual NeRF MLP architecture
2.2.1. Continuous implicit representation and volume rendering
NeRF uses an MLP rather than voxels, point clouds, or meshes. The backbone predicts density and
features from position, then combines them with encoded viewing direction to predict color. Density
is ideally direction-independent, while color varies with direction to model specular and other nonLambertian effects. Volume rendering both maps the field to pixels and transfers pixel errors to
sampled points.
2.2.2. Positional encoding and efficiency improvements
Standard MLPs have spectral bias and struggle with high-frequency texture and geometry from lowdimensional coordinates. Fourier features use sine and cosine mappings to expose higher
frequencies [6]. Multiresolution hash encoding stores learnable features in multilevel tables and uses
lookup/interpolation to reduce query cost, greatly shortening training in Instant-NGP [7]. Fourier
encoding offers interpretable frequency control; hash encoding prioritizes speed and memory
efficiency, so they serve different rapid-training and analysis needs.

2.2.3. Sampling and anti-aliasing
Sampling controls rendering cost. Uniform sampling wastes queries in empty space; hierarchical
sampling uses a coarse network to refine high-contribution regions, while occupancy grids and
empty-space skipping remove invalid samples. Mip-NeRF represents a ray as a conical frustum
across continuous scales to reduce aliasing and stabilize multiscale detail. It reports 17% lower
average error than NeRF, 60% lower error on challenging multiscale data, and much lower rendering
cost at comparable accuracy [8].
2.2.4. Dynamic scenes and 3D generation
Static NeRF assumes positions do not change across observations. Dynamic models add time or
deformation: D-NeRF maps observation space to canonical space to reconstruct rigid and non-rigid
motion [9], while Gafni et al. combine a dynamic field with controllable facial deformation for
monocular-video 4D avatars [10]. Generalization remains limited by motion type, camera trajectory,
and training coverage.
DreamFusion uses a pretrained 2D diffusion prior and score distillation to optimize a NeRF for
text-to-3D generation without large-scale 3D training data [11]. This shifts NeRF from
reconstruction to conditional creation, but multiview consistency, geometric completeness, and
controllability remain open.
2.3. Representative results and limitations
Under the original settings, NeRF achieved high novel-view quality on synthetic and real forwardfacing scenes: PSNR 40.15, 31.01, and 26.50 dB; SSIM 0.991, 0.947, and 0.811; and LPIPS 0.023,
0.081, and 0.250 on three datasets [1]. These values show a balance of pixel error, structural fidelity,
and perceptual similarity. Mip-NeRF confirms the value of multiscale representations for antialiasing and cross-resolution generalization [8].
Visual NeRF has three main limitations. Deep MLP queries make training and inference
expensive, especially as scenes grow. Static-scene assumptions and reliance on accurate camera
poses make occlusion, non-rigid motion, and sparse views difficult. Generative methods add
creative control but remain dependent on the prior and optimization strategy for 3D consistency,
detail, and controllability. The focus has therefore shifted from feasibility to fast, stable, and
controllable reconstruction or generation.
3. Neural radio-frequency radiance fields (NeRF²) for wireless communications
3.1. Overall framework
Visual NeRF predicts optical color and density, not complex wireless amplitude and phase,
multipath interference, or transmitter dependence. NeRF² models radio-frequency propagation as a
continuous spatial field, learns unmeasured received results from sparse measurements, and
incorporates ray tracing as a physical prior [12]. Inputs may include transmitter and receiver
positions, propagation direction, and array configuration; outputs include received signals, RSSI,
spatial spectra, or channel states.
NeRF² queries a field, accumulates rays, models the receiver, and trains against measurements. A
voxel position enters an attenuation network; its features are combined with transmitter position and
measurement direction in a radiation network that predicts complex attenuation and reradiation.

forms a spatial spectrum, and differentiable propagation jointly optimizes the networks using

often accumulates them in the log domain to reduce underflow and vanishing gradients.

3.2.3. From NeRF² to NeWRF, R-NeRF, and VoxelRF
NeWRF targets sparse wireless measurements, using arrival-direction-guided ray casting to
constrain effective paths and predict channels at unvisited positions [13]. R-NeRF models RIS
propagation in two stages, transmitter to RIS then RIS to receiver, to handle RIS position and
complex reflection [14]. VoxelRF makes part of the field explicit as a voxel grid, uses trilinear
interpolation and two shallow MLPs, and skips empty space to reduce queries [15]. They target
sparse measurement, reconfigurable surfaces, and efficiency, respectively, rather than forming a
linear replacement chain.

3.2.4. Physics-constrained data augmentation
NeRF² also proposes turbo-learning: train a field from few real measurements, use it to synthesize
propagation-consistent samples, then mix real and synthetic data for a higher-level communications
task. Unlike unconstrained image augmentation, the synthetic data preserve paths, amplitude
relationships, and spatial structure, reducing measurement cost and label scarcity [12].
3.3. Representative results and limitations
NeRF² reconstructs a continuous radio-frequency field from few measurements and reports usable
results for spatial spectra, indoor localization, and 5G MIMO channel prediction. A microbenchmark
reached about 82% median spectrum similarity. With 10% real and 90% synthetic data, one AoA
experiment improved accuracy by 47.9%, with about 49.5% average improvement across 14 scenes
[12]. Channel prediction improved SNR and SINR by 5.97 dB and 4.32 dB over comparison
methods; Argos 5G MIMO reported 20.87 dB median predicted SNR. These are task-, dataset-, and
setting-specific.
Evidence boundary. The 82%, 47.9%, 49.5%, 5.97 dB, 4.32 dB, and 20.87 dB values refer
respectively to spatial-spectrum similarity, few-shot AoA, 14-scene AoA, channel prediction, and
Argos 5G MIMO; none is a universal wireless performance level.
Later methods emphasize task adaptation. NeWRF combines sparse channels with arrival
direction for unmeasured positions; R-NeRF evaluates different RIS positions with simulated and
measured data and reports lower MAE than NeRF² at low training-data ratios [14]; VoxelRF uses
voxelization and empty-space skipping to reduce training and inference cost for resourceconstrained devices [15]. RF implicit-field research has thus moved from modeling a field to
adapting it to propagation structure, hardware, and computation.

4. Evaluation framework and application scenarios
Visual NeRF and radio-frequency NeRF² differ in outputs, objectives, and data, so evaluation should
use domain-specific metrics within shared engineering dimensions. PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS are
appropriate for images, or auxiliary measures for spectra treated as 2D images, but cannot replace
radio-frequency propagation error. Conversely, RSSI, AoA, SNR, and SINR cannot assess visual
texture realism. Chapter 4 therefore avoids a single cross-domain ranking and pairs domain metrics
with common dimensions [4].

4.2. Radio-frequency modeling evaluation
Spatial-spectrum and RSSI error. Use MSE, MAE, or normalized error for predicted versus
reference power distributions; RSSI, reported in dB, measures signal-strength bias in coverage and
fingerprint localization.
AoA error is the angular distance between predicted and reference arrival directions. For spatialspectrum outputs, also inspect peak direction, main-lobe width, and multimodal structure.
SNR and SINR. In channel prediction, SNR reflects similarity between predicted and true
channels, while SINR includes multiuser interference. Report the dataset, frequency band, antenna
scale, prediction direction, and statistical procedure so task-specific values are not presented as
universal.

4.3. General evaluation dimensions
Computational efficiency. Report training time, single-render or inference time, parameter count,
and GPU or system memory. Hash encoding, occupancy grids, and voxelization improve visualNeRF query efficiency; NeRF² must also report ray-tracing and complex-propagation cost.
Data efficiency. For visual models report training views, coverage, and camera-pose quality; for
radio-frequency models report real measurement points, array configuration, frequency, and
transmitter positions. For synthetic augmentation, report the real-data proportion and synthetic-data
source.
Physical consistency. Visual models should preserve geometry, occlusion, and cross-view
consistency. Radio-frequency models should also satisfy distance attenuation, occlusion, reflection,
diffraction, phase continuity, and plausible energy behavior, distinguishing them from black-box
regression.
Scene generalization. Test unseen camera trajectories, spatial positions, materials, RIS positions,
or transmitter configurations, stating which factors change. Separate results outside the training
distribution; random within-scene splits do not establish cross-scene capability.
4.4. Representative application scenarios
In 3D reconstruction and AIGC, visual NeRF supports novel-view synthesis, free-viewpoint video,
digitization of cultural relics and buildings, product visualization, and text-driven 3D generation.

Surveys classify 3D generation as 3D-native, 2D-prior-driven, or hybrid, placing NeRF both as a
scene representation and as a target of generative optimization [16].
In 6G digital twins, visual NeRF supplies urban or indoor geometry and appearance, while NeRF²
supplies spatial electromagnetic properties. Together they can support coverage, beam configuration,
base-station placement, interference analysis, and network operations. Deployment still requires
unified coordinates, time synchronization, material parameters, and multiband calibration. 6G
digital-twin research coordinates environmental twins, ray-tracing engines, and channel twins [4],
with neural fields as a data-driven complement.
For wireless deployment and RIS optimization, NeWRF, R-NeRF, and VoxelRF emphasize sparse
measurement, reconfigurable propagation, and fast queries. Their shared goal is to reduce
measurement or repeated-simulation cost. Deployment decisions must still rely on measured
calibration, error bounds, failure cases, and system-level communications validation; offline
visualization is insufficient.
5. Conclusions and outlook
This paper reviewed the evolution from visual to radio-frequency implicit fields. Visual NeRF uses a
continuous five-dimensional field and differentiable rendering, then develops through Fourier
features, hash encoding, multiscale anti-aliasing, dynamic scenes, and text-driven 3D generation.
NeRF² adds complex amplitude and phase, ray-propagation priors, attenuation, and reradiation to
predict spatial spectra, RSSI, AoA, and wireless channels. NeWRF, R-NeRF, and VoxelRF show the
need for task-specific designs for sparse measurements, RISs, and efficiency.
The families share a mathematical skeleton but should not be collapsed into one leaderboard.
Visual models target appearance and geometry; radio-frequency models target propagation signals
and physical consistency. Their metrics likewise differ. Future work should (1) jointly represent
visual geometry, semantics, and radio-frequency propagation in a calibratable multimodal field; (2)
support incremental updates and few-shot adaptation for dynamic environments, multiple
transmitters, and reconfigurable surfaces; and (3) combine voxels, tensor decomposition, hash
encoding, and compression to lower radio-frequency-field training and inference cost.
NeRF's value is not only a new rendering method but a demonstration that continuous implicit
representation plus a differentiable physical process can cross modalities. Real-system adoption
depends on jointly validating data efficiency, physical constraints, interpretable errors, and
deployment cost.
