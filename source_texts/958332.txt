Abstract
Color quantization (CQ) is one of the most common and important procedures to be performed on digital images. In
this paper, a new approach to hierarchical color quantization is described, presenting a novel neural network architecture
integrated by a convolutional autoencoder and a Growing Hierarchical Bregman Neural Gas (GHBNG). GHBNG is a CQ
algorithm that allows the compression of an image by choosing a reduced set of the most representative colors to generate
a high-quality reproduction of the original image. In the technique proposed here, an autoencoder is used to translate
the image into a latent representation with higher per-pixel dimensionality but reduced resolution, and GHBNG is then
used to quantize it. Experimental results confirm the performance of this technique and its suitability for tasks related
to color quantization.
Keywords: color quantization, convolutional autoencoder, clustering, self-organization

One of the most common image processing tasks is color
quantization (CQ), which consists of choosing a small but 25
representative set of indexed colors (codebook) to encode
the original digital image with minimal perceptual distortion. This method is widely used to reduce the storage requirements and the transmission bandwidth for color images while maintaining an acceptable image fidelity. In 30
addition, CQ is also considered as a useful lossy compression method, where a codebook (a set of acceptable colors)
can be used to represent the original colors of a digital
image. The quality of the codebook depends on the error between the original image and the resulting image. 35
An optimal codebook should minimize this error, which is
usually measured by the mean squared error criterion.
In the literature, many well-known algorithms have been
proposed to find optimal codebooks such as the k-means
algorithm [1], fuzzy c-means [2], competitive learning [3], 40
self-organizing map [4], and their variants. The SelfOrganizing Map (SOM) [5] was the starting point for
the development of many self-organizing models [6, 7, 8].

Some of them try to face some drawbacks of the original SOM regarding its pre-established network architecture, i.e. topology and number of neurons [9, 10, 11]. The
Growing Hierarchical Self-Organizing Map (GHSOM) [12]
represents a hierarchical extension of the SOM to reflect hierarchical data, where the entire architecture of the neural
network is automatically determined during the unsupervised learning process. The Growing Neural Gas (GNG)
[13] constitutes another successful variant of SOMs that
solves the fixed-network architecture problem by providing
a neuron growth and removal mechanism to automatically
determine the number of neurons during the unsupervised
learning process according to the input data. The GNG
has also a hierarchical extension called the Growing Hierarchical Neural Gas (GHNG) [14], in which a neuron can
be expanded into a new graph in a subsequent layer of
the hierarchy depending on the quantization error associated with that neuron or the graph it belongs to. The
use of the Euclidean distance is prevalent in most of the
proposed algorithms for CQ. However, this distance may
not be the most suitable for all input distributions. In
this sense, a Growing Hierarchical Bregman Neural Gas
(GHBNG) [15, 16] was proposed for CQ, where Bregman
divergences are considered. Note that the classical squared
Euclidean distance is a particular case of the Bregman
divergences[17].

Deep learning has recently achieved great success in the
computer vision field, where the use of autoencoders for
color quantization tasks has gradually been increasing with
promising results [19]. Thus, autoencoders have been successfully applied in dimensionality reduction, compact representation of images, and generative models learning to
extract more compressed codes from images with a minimized loss function [20].
Autoencoders are composed of an encoder and a decoder, and are designed to transform image pixels to data105
in a latent (code) space. Thus, an image is transformed
(via encoder) to the code space, which is processed by a
quantization function to obtain a quantized representation
of the image. After that, this representation is transformed
back (via decoder) to the image space. In the literature,110
different neural architectures have been proposed to learn
a reduced representation of images, such as an autoencoder with pyramidal encoder and convolutional structure
[19], or a symmetric convolutional autoencoder with multiple (down/up)-sampling units and a principal component115
analysis based rotation to generate feature maps with low
dimensions [21, 22].
In this work, we propose a convolutional autoencoder
with a GHBNG[16] to generate hierarchical feature maps
in order to provide different quantization/compression
rates with a single model. Experimental results show an
excellent performance of our proposal, which can provide
a hierarchical, reduced representation of an image with a
tunable compression rate and outperform other alternatives based on single autoencoders.

2.1. Convolutional autoencoders
We use a family of convolutional autoencoders where
the encoder consists of N convolutional layers with 3 × 3
convolutions, each one with stride 2 (i.e. halving the width
and height of the resulting tensor) and adding one more
output dimension (e.g. the first layer will have 3 input dimensions and 4 output dimensions), and each one followed
by a logistic activation layer. For any given encoder, the
decoder consists of N layers of 3 × 3 transposed convolutions (also called deconvolutions [23]), each one with stride
2 (i.e. doubling the width and height of the tensor) and
reducing the dimensionality in 1 dimension. The output of
the encoder is the latent representation of the image, and
for an RGB image represented as a tensor of size 2a ×2a ×3,
the latent representation will be a tensor of size:
L(N ) = 2a−N × 2a−N × (3 + N )
(1)
whose values are in the range 0 . . . 1. During training time,
we minimize the MSE of the input and output images, constraining the autoencoder to learn latent representations
quantized to 8 bits (i.e., the values of the latent representation are clamped to 218 steps before being fed to the
decoder).
While some approaches call for deep autoencoders able
to encode arbitrary images [22], our simpler autoencoder
is trained on a single image, such that each image requires
training a new autoencoder. Consequently, for a given autoencoder architecture, the full representation of the image consists of the latent representation together with the
trained parameters (weights and biases) of the decoder.

The GHBNG model is defined as a hierarchical tree
structure, where each graph is made of H neurons (H ≥ 2)
and one or more directed connections among them. Both
neurons and connections can be created and destroyed during the learning process. It is not necessary that the graph
is connected. The training set for the graph will be noted
S, with S ⊂ RD , where D is the dimension of the input
space. Each unit i ∈ {1, ..., H} has an associated prototype wi ∈ RD and an error variable ei ∈ R, ei ≥ 0.
Each connection has an associated age, which is a nonnegative integer. The set of connections will be noted
A ⊆ {1, ..., H} × {1, ..., H}.

2.3. Chaining convolutional autoencoders and GHBNG
When applied to an RGB image, GHBNG learns a hierarchical model of the colors in the image, i.e., of the
distribution in RGB space of the colors present in the image, with no explicit regard for spatial structure. Thus,
the output is a hierarchical model of the color distribution
of the image together with an image whose pixel colors are
indexed into the GHBNG model.
However, GHBNG is a generic algorithm that can be
trivially adapted to learn such hierarchical models from
higher-dimensional distributions, just by changing the dimensionality of the space. After training an autoencoder
for an image, the latent representation of the image is a
tensor of smaller width and length, but higher per-pixel dimensionality. Each latent pixel encodes color information
from a contiguous patch in the original image, effectively
encoding image textures.
We can feed the latent representation of the image to
GHBNG, which will learn a hierarchical model of the textures encoded by the latent representation. To reconstruct
the image, we just need to first reconstruct an approximation of the latent representation from the hierarchical
model, then use the decoder to recover an approximation
of the image.
This recursive process continues until a prespecified225
number of levels is reached. The elimination of the graphs
with less than 3 neurons and the split of the training set
given by (10) work together in order to attain a parsimonious hierarchy, i.e. one with a reduced number of graphs
and neurons. This is because lower graphs in the tree230
cannot have many neurons, since their training sets are
smaller. It is worth noting that many of the created graphs
will eventually be pruned right after their training, so that
the fact that a graph is created for each unit does not lead
to uncontrolled growth. An example of the tree structure235
of a GHBNG is exemplified in Figure 2.

4. Discussion
In this section, some important aspects of our proposed
model are discussed. The model is composed of two neural
networks that operate in sequence: first, the autoencoder
learns a reduced representation of the inputs. In our experiments, these inputs are the windows that compose the
input image. This way, each window is encoded into a latent space vector. Then the GHBNG learns a hierarchical
representation of the latent vectors so that a hierarchical
codebook is obtained for the distribution of latent vectors.
Such a hierarchical codebook represents the latent vectors
with an increasing level of detail as we go deeper into the
layers of the GHBNG structure (an example can be seen
in Figure 6).
As seen in Figure 7 and Tables 4-7, the performance
of our proposed hybrid model with an autoencoder followed by a GHBNG attains better compressed sizes than
the autoencoder alone. This means that the hierarchical
codebook learned by the GHBNG effectively represents
its input distribution, i.e. the distribution of latent space
vectors produced by the autoencoder. Even in the cases
that autoencoders yield about the same compression ratio,
our hybrid model attains a better image quality as measured by the PSNR and SSIM performance metrics. In

The most influential parameter is N , i.e. the number of515

dimensionality reduction strength given by the N parameter of the autoencoder, while varying degrees of detail are
learned by the levels of the hierarchy of the GHBNG.

performance has been image compression. Experimental

classical hierarchical vector quantization algorithm.

considered in the development of high-quality hierarchical

it is an example of an application of GHBNG, a hierarchical vector quantization algorithm.
