Implementing Volumetric Lighting with Shadow Maps - game-development
Implementing
Volumetric Lighting with Shadow Maps
Volumetric lighting via shadow maps enhances atmospheric realism in
video games by simulating the scattering of light through participating
media like fog, dust, and moisture. This article explores the primary
technical considerations developers face when implementing this
technique, including ray marching efficiency, shadow map precision,
phase function approximations, spatial-temporal filtering, and
downsampled rendering pipelines.
Ray Marching
Resolution and Sampling Artifacts
The core mechanism involves ray marching along the camera's view
vector through participating media, sampling the shadow map at discrete
intervals to determine light occlusion.
Step Count vs. Performance: A low step count
results in severe undersampling, manifesting as distinct banding
artifacts along the light volume. High step counts eliminate banding but
drastically increase texture fetch overhead and GPU fill-rate
demands.
Jittering and Noise: To combat banding without
increasing step counts, developers apply stochastic jittering (such as
blue noise offsets) to the ray march start positions. This converts
coherent banding into high-frequency noise, which is easier to resolve
temporally.
Shadow Map
Precision, Filtering, and Cascades
Using standard shadow maps for volumetric evaluation introduces
specific geometric and precision challenges.
Depth Biasing and Precision: Volumetric rays query
the shadow map continuously along the volume rather than just on opaque
surfaces. Standard depth bias settings tuned for surface geometry often
cause clipping or light leaking near shadow boundaries in the air.
Cascaded Shadow Map (CSM) Transitions: When
traversing across multiple shadow map cascades, sampling transitions can
produce noticeable seams in volumetric density. Implementing smooth
interpolation or cascade blending inside the ray march loop is necessary
to maintain visual consistency.
Shadow Map Texture Lookups: Standard
Percentage-Closer Filtering (PCF) is designed for 2D surface evaluation.
For volumetric rays, point sampling the shadow map depth with subsequent
post-process filtering is generally more performance-efficient than
running multi-tap PCF at every ray march step.
Scattering Functions and
Media Density
Simulating real-world optics requires accurate mathematical models
for how light interacts with particles.
In-Scattering and Transmittance: Light intensity
must decay along the ray path according to the Beer-Lambert law,
calculating both optical depth and transmittance.
Phase Functions: Isotropic scattering scatters
light equally in all directions, which is computationally cheap but
lacks realism. Incorporating anisotropic phase functions, like the
Henyey-Greenstein approximation, allows strong forward scattering that
creates dramatic "god ray" effects when looking toward a light
source.
Heterogeneous Media: While homogeneous fog assumes
constant density, realistic environments require sampling 3D noise
textures (e.g., animated Curl or Perlin-Worley noise) to model shifting
fog pockets, requiring additional texture bandwidth.
Spatial and Temporal
Optimizations
Directly ray marching at native screen resolution is rarely viable
for real-time rendering budgets.
Half-Resolution Rendering and Bilateral Upsampling:
Evaluating volumetric lighting at half or quarter resolution reduces the
pixel shader workload by up to 75%. Reconstructing the final image
requires depth-aware bilateral upsampling to prevent volumetric light
from bleeding across sharp silhouette edges of foreground geometry.
Temporal Accumulation: Temporal Anti-Aliasing (TAA)
or dedicated temporal reprojection smooths the stochastic noise
introduced by jittered ray marching. This requires tracking camera
motion vectors and history buffers while rejecting ghosting artifacts
during rapid light or camera movement.
Frustum Voxel Grids (Froxels): Modern game engines
often decouple ray marching from the screen resolution entirely by
populating a 3D frustum-aligned voxel grid (froxel grid) via compute
shaders. Shadow map lookups and scattering calculations are computed
once per voxel cell and then integrated in a single forward pass,
providing predictable performance scaling regardless of scene depth
complexity.
