Abstrac t
Use of the perspective projection acids important perceptua l
cues for image comprehension . However, it has not been
widely used in volume rendering because of the lack of efficien t
computational algorithms and concern over the nonunifor m
sampling rate imposed by perspective ray divergence . Thi s
paper introduces two new techniques to help make perspectiv e
projection more feasible in rendering volume data . First, a
method is presented for efficient slice-by-slice processing o f
volume data, allowing high resolution data sets by eliminatin g
typical memory constraints . Second, an adaptive "ray
splitting" approach is described which ensures that the entir e
volume is sampled within user-specified limits . Additionally ,
we present results using distributed ray tracing to achiev e
depth of field effects ,
To date, two major stumbling blocks have prevented th e
widespread use of perspective projection . These are the lack o f
algorithms which are both space-efficient and time-efficient ,
and the nonuniform sampling inherent in perpective ra y
divergence . In this paper, we present new techniques fo r
creating perspective images from volume data which addres s
both these problems . In addition, we present our results i n
using lens effects to highlight a portion of the dataset whil e
maintaining a sense of the overall three dimensional structure .

1 Introductio n
In recent years, volume rendering has emerged as a powerfu l
technique for visualizing scientific data . The majority o f
images produced in this manner have employed orthographi c
projections . While quite effective in many circumstances ,
orthographic views have, in a sense, held scientists at a n
infinite distance from their data . Orthographic projection s
also often result in depth ambiguities and reversals of apparen t
depth [Image 1] . Since our visual system is tuned t o
comprehend the perspective views projected onto our retinas ,
scene geometry and depth are more readily apparent i n
simulated perspective views . In addition, data flythroughs an d
lens effects become available to aid in the comprehension o f
spatial relationships within the data when perspective is used .

dimensional grids of volume densities or scalar fields withou t

The volume rendering engine described here follows mos t
closely after the image space mapping technique of [Levoy 88] .

Despite an emphasis on the use of orthographic projection ,
several researchers have explored the use of perspective i n
volume rendering . However, to date none have describe d
efficient, single pass slice-by-slice processing techniques .
Most ray casting implementations have assumed random acces s
to the volume dataset [Schlusselberg 86] [Upson 88 ]
[Sabella 88] [Webber 90] . If the volume data has n voxels on a
side, this approach requires 0(n 3) storage space, which quickl y
outstrips RAM capacity for high resolution arrays . Rando m
access to large virtual memory space causes an unacceptabl e
amount of disk paging . In [Drebin 88] and [Hohne 89], a
perspective view is realized by casting parallel rays into a n
appropriately transformed copy of the volume grid, howeve r
the transformation step is quite expensive . The cell-by-cel l
processing method of [Upson 88] assumes that the data can b e
accessed slice-at-time along any of the three coordinate axes .
This presents a difficulty because volume data is usually store d
so that slice data may be efficiently accessed on only one axis .

Slice-Based Perspective Projectio n
We present an algorithm for slice-based perspective projectio n
in two parts . First, a scheme to allow ray tracing on a slice-byslice basis is introduced . Next, we describe a slice processin g
order which requires that each slice of the dataset be loaded int o
memory only once .
Slice by Slice Ray Tracin g
A typical ray tracer has an outer loop which iterates once fo r
each ray. Each ray is traced until it terminates, yielding a fina l
pixel value . Processing then concentrates on the next ray .
This scheme is extremely inefficient with regard to memor y
access because each ray must take samples from across th e
entire dataset .
To increase efficiency the algorithm is inverted, so that it i s
driven by the data currently in memory . Because we rely o n
trilinear interpolation at sample points, at least two slices o f
data are required in memory at one time . We call each pair o f
slices a slab, The system maintains a queue of rays waiting for
each slab so that they may compute their next sample values ,
This data structure is called a slab queue . During initialization ,

Slab Processing Orde r
Because perspective rays diverge from a single eyepoint, a sla b
processing order which requires that each slab be loaded int o
memory only once is easily defined . We derive a processin g
order for front-to-back processing of ray sample points . Th e
reverse order is optimal for back-to-front processing . Assum e
without loss of generality that the data is accessed as slice s
perpendicular to the z axis . Let E Z be the z-coordinate of th e
eyepoint . Regardless of view direction, all rays emanatin g
from the eyepoint must fall in to one of three categories, First ,
the ray may stay in the z=E Z plane, thus requiring access t o
only one slab . Second, it may diverge in the +z direction fro m
the eyepoint . In this case it requires access to the sla b
containing the z=E 2 plane and to slabs with successively large r
z values, in order . Similarly in the third case, if the ra y
diverges in the -z direction from the eyepoint, it requires acces s
to slabs starting with the z=E Z plane and continuing with slab s
with successively smaller z values . Thus the optimal slab

Of greater concern is the undersampling of the volume data a s
perspective rays diverge . This can result in severe aliasing ,
which is visually unappealing, and more seriously, th e
erroneous omission of small objects from the image entirel y
[Figure 3] . For example, consider the case of a 56-degree fiel d
of view looking directly onto a face of the volume boundin g
box . One ray is shot per pixel through a screen equal i n
resolution to a slice of the dataset . In this case the first slice o f
the dataset is sampled at one ray per voxel, but the last slice i s
sampled at one ray per two voxels in the worst case . Assumin g
that it is desirable to hold the image size constant, one coul d
naYvely eliminate undersampling by firing enough uniformly
distributed rays per pixel so that the back slice is sampled a t
the desired rate [Figure 4] . This simple oversampling wil l
result in blurring in the back of the region rendered, but ever y
voxel is guaranteed to contribute to the final image .
Unfortunately, this approach wastes computation b y
oversampling the parts of the dataset close to the eyepoint .
An alternative approach to maintaining adequate samplin g
rates with perspective projection would be to us e
multiresolution filterings of the data as in the 3D-mipma p
algorithms [Williams 83][Levoy 90] . In these schema, instead
of firing additional rays to obtain the average value across a
larger region, samples are taken from successively blurre d
versions of the volume data . This has the positive effect o f
reducing the number of samples taken during ray tracing . In
particular, it reduces computation effort in distant areas whic h
do not contribute much to the final image . However, it adds a
level of interpolation between the raw data and the ra y
sampling process . It also adds some complexity to the ray
tracer which must pass through several sets of slabs and var y
the sampling rate along the ray direction as it moves t o
different resolution filterings .
We have adopted an adaptive supersampling approach whereby
a ray is split into four divergent rays at the point just before the
volumetric sampling rate would drop below a user-specifie d
threshold . Later, the ray fragments are composited to yield a
final pixel value [Figure 5] . In the current implementation, al l
ray fragments are averaged uniformly during composition ,
however any filter function could be used in its place . As i n
[Levoy 88], the sampling rate along the ray axis is maintaine d
by taking samples at regular intervals along the ray . The
sampling rate in the remaining two dimensions is maintaine d
by considering the projected area of the screen pixel, whic h
grows with distance from the eyepoint . At the point where the
projected area becomes larger than the square of the linea r
sampling rate, the ray is split . This process is continue d
recursively until the ray exits the volume boundary .

The slice processing order presented here is optimized so that
each slice of memory is loaded only once . One could imagin e
alternative slab processing orders which would provide mor e
useful intermediate images for adaptive refinement . On e
technique would be to give each ray an importance weight, an d
to always process the slab with the most weight in its queue . A
ray could be assigned a high weight if, for example, it is know n
to pass through a region of interest .
5 Lens Effect s
If the user defines an area of interest within the volume data ,
one may improve image comprehension by focusing the virtua l
camera on that region . The result is better depth perceptio n
because objects are blurred as distance from the focal regio n
increases . The blurred remnants of distant objects help t o
maintain global perspective .
6 Future Wor k
The slice-by-slice processing technique could be modified t o
take advantage of shared memory coarse-grained parallelism b y
distributing the rays on a given slab queue to several
processors . If memory is not shared, all processors must loa d
the current slab into memory, and thus parallelism should onl y
be used if the slab queue is longer than a certain threshold .
The ray tracing algorithm assumes that the data is stored a s
slices along one axis of the data . This algorithm should b e
extended to allow efficient processing of oct-trees, anothe r
common data structure used in volume rendering .

perspective projection has been presented . By usin g
perspective projection, familiar distortion with depth provide s

projection . In addition, greater freedom in placing th e
eyepoint and the ability to focus the virtual camera allows a
scientist to get " closer " to the data being rendered . By usin g
slice-by-slice processing and volume coherence, the algorithm
runs efficiently even when the full dataset cannot be stored i n
memory, thus eliminating a typical constraint . This factor wil l
