Abstract
We propose a novel reconstruction method for sparse-view X-ray computed tomography (CT) based on projection onto a
learned image manifold. Instead of incorporating a regularisation term into the optimisation objective, we train a neural
network to approximate the distance to a manifold of realistic images, enabling us to project intermediate reconstructions onto
this manifold at each iteration of a generic optimisation algorithm. This approach constrains the solution space to samples
statistically consistent with the distribution of ground-truth images, while still ensuring fidelity to measured projection data.
We evaluate the method on both simulated and real datasets, in 2D and 3D, and observe consistent improvements in
reconstruction quality, as measured by SSIM and PSNR. Our results show that this projection-based strategy effectively
reduces artefacts due to undersampling, preserves fine structural details, and maintains theoretical guarantees.

1. Introduction
X-ray computed tomography is a cornerstone technology in industrial non-destructive testing, enabling
internal inspection of complex parts without altering or damaging the objects under study. It plays a
critical role in aerospace, automotive manufacturing, and materials science, where the ability to detect
structural defects, measure internal geometries, or verify assemblies with micrometric precision is
essential for quality control and safety compliance. A major limitation of standard CT lies in its reliance
on acquiring a large number of projections to ensure high-quality volumetric reconstructions. In industrial
settings, however, acquiring dense tomographic data is often impractical due to time constraints, high
radiation doses, or limited access angles in complex assemblies. These limitations give rise to the socalled sparse-view tomography problem, where only a small subset of projections is available. In such
cases, traditional analytical methods—such as Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK)—produce reconstructions
plagued by severe artefacts, such as streaks and loss of contrast, due to the ill-posedness of the inverse
problem.
To overcome these limitations, recent work has focused on regularised optimisation approaches that
integrate prior knowledge into the reconstruction process. Classical regularisation methods, including
total variation (TV), promote sparsity in a predefined transform domain, such as the Fourier domain or
learned dictionaries [1]. However, they lack the expressiveness to capture the complex structures often
found in real industrial images. More recent advances in machine learning have introduced data-driven
priors, which learn to encode the statistical structure of high-quality images from large datasets [2]. Deep
neural network post-processors have gained popularity, yet they do not inherently ensure that the
reconstructed signals adhere to the underlying physical constraints, which is unacceptable for non
destructive applications [3]. More recent approaches, such as unrolled optimisation methods—including
primal-dual hybrid gradient techniques with learned parameters—integrate data fidelity terms directly
within the optimisation framework alongside regularisation [4,5,6]. Additionally, neural networks can
serve as priors within variational inference frameworks, such as diffusion model based methods [7].
In this work, we explore an alternative approach to leveraging these networks. We build upon the
framework of learned priors and propose a reconstruction approach based on projection onto a learned
manifold. Instead of treating the prior as a regularisation term, we introduce a distance function trained to
distinguish between ground-truth images and noisy reconstructions. This manifold-based approach is
particularly well suited to sparse-view tomography, where the ambiguity of the inverse problem can be

2. Methods
The overarching objective of our approach is to constrain the solution space during the tomographic
reconstruction process by incorporating learned prior knowledge. To achieve this, we introduce a
projection step onto a learned manifold 𝕄, applied at the end of each iteration of a generic iterative
reconstruction algorithm. This projection aims to guide intermediate reconstructions back towards the
manifold of plausible (in our case, from dense trajectories) solutions, thereby improving reconstruction
quality and stability. The central challenge lies in constructing a projection operator 𝑃𝕄 that approximates
the true projection onto 𝕄, despite having access only to samples from the manifold rather than its
explicit representation. Our proposed solution relies on the definition of a pointwise distance function:
𝑑𝕄 (𝒙) = ‖𝑃𝕄 (𝒙) − 𝒙‖.
This distance function has a crucial advantage: using modern deep learning frameworks with automatic
differentiation, we can directly compute its gradient. Since the gradient (or more precisely, the
subgradient) of d indicates the direction of the steepest descent towards the manifold, it can be used to
iteratively refine relaxed projections. Specifically, we have the inclusion:

with α>0 and 𝜕𝑑𝕄 denoting the subgradient of the distance function. This procedure is straightforward to
implement and described in more detail in [8].

adopt a framework inspired by the Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Network (WGAN) formulation

are progressively projected onto the manifold 𝕄. To fully leverage this approach, we employ several
neural networks to approximate the distance to the manifold at each iteration k of the projection
algorithm, which forms our final manifold projection operator 𝑃𝕄 . This operator 𝑃𝕄 can be interpreted as
the composition of several incremental projections; accordingly, we chose to use a series of lightweight

3. Results
We evaluated the performance of our method on a variety of simulated and experimental image datasets
to assess its generalizability and effectiveness in low-projection CT reconstruction.
We first tested our approach on a simple 2D setup consisting of 1000 simulated 128×128 images of
ellipses. These images were reconstructed from only 30 projections using the Feldkamp–Davis–Kress
(FDK) algorithm, producing degraded reconstructions. During training, 200 epochs were performed
between each update of the distribution ℙ𝑘 , resulting in a total of 40 update steps. For the reconstruction
process, we opted for a projected gradient descent scheme. At each iteration, a standard gradient descent
step is followed by a projection onto the learned manifold, as described in the previous section. A total of
150 iterations are performed. The step size is set to 2/L, where L denotes an estimate of the norm of the
projection operator, computed using the power method. The results are presented in Figure 1. The bottom
row displays a zoomed-in view of the bottom left corner region of the reconstruction. It can be observed
that streak artefacts are significantly reduced, which in turn facilitates a clearer visualisation of the
individual ellipses within the region of interest. Initial SSIM and PSNR with FDK were 0.6341 and 27.90
dB, final SSIM and PSNR were 0.9717 and 37.98dB. Qualitative results show a substantial visual
improvement, suggesting that our learned manifold projection effectively suppresses noise and recovers
image structures.
To further evaluate the scalability of our approach, we extended the neural network architecture to handle
larger images of size 512×512 pixels. The network was first trained on a dataset of 5000 simulated
images composed of ellipses. Sparse reconstructions were generated using FDK with only 60 projection
angles. The same training configuration—number of epochs and optimisation parameters—was used as in
the 128×128 setting, both for training and evaluation.

Finally, we extended our method to the three-dimensional case, applying it to volumetric phantom data
composed of ellipsoids within a 5123 voxel grid. To enable efficient processing, the volume was
partitioned into overlapping 643 blocks, and reconstructions were obtained by aggregating the outputs of
local manifold projections within each block. Although the 3D blocks contain the same number of voxels
as the 2D 512×512 slices, the network architecture required modifications to accommodate the volumetric
nature of the data. Specifically, we replaced the 2D convolutional layers with 3D convolutions and added
layers. Thanks to this architectural change, the method generalised well to the 3D setting, yielding
improved reconstruction quality, particularly in regions heavily affected by undersampling artefacts.
However, we observed that convergence in the 3D case was slower and occasionally less stable,
indicating that additional optimisation iterations may be necessary to ensure full convergence in this
higher-dimensional space. The results are presented in Figure 6. A notable artefact in the 3D
reconstruction is the presence of discontinuities at block boundaries, resulting in a mosaic-like appearance
due to the independent local manifold projections. While this effect could be mitigated using a sliding
window approach for smoother patch integration, doing so would significantly increase the number of
manifold projections required, thereby impacting computational efficiency. Finding an effective trade-off
between reconstruction quality and computational cost remains an important direction for future work.

6. Conclusions
In this work, we introduced a novel manifold-based projection framework for solving ill-posed
tomographic reconstruction problems. By learning a distance function that approximates the projection
onto a data manifold, our approach constrains iterative reconstructions to remain close to the distribution
of high-quality ground-truth images, thereby improving the quality and stability of the reconstructions.
Unlike traditional regularisation methods, our framework leverages adversarially learned priors to enforce
data-driven consistency, while preserving fidelity to the physical acquisition model. Through extensive
experiments on both simulated and experimental datasets, in 2D and 3D settings, we demonstrated that
our method consistently enhances reconstruction quality. Notably, it mitigates artefacts arising from
undersampling and reveals fine image details otherwise lost in conventional reconstructions. Theoretical
constraints—such as the 1-Lipschitz condition—were explicitly enforced during training, and empirical
validation confirmed their satisfaction throughout optimisation. These guarantees contributed to stable
convergence and generalisation across datasets. Furthermore, the modular design of our projection
operator enables integration into a wide range of reconstruction pipelines with minimal modification.
Future work will explore more efficient aggregation strategies in the 3D setting to reduce boundary
artefacts, as well as the application of our framework to more complex acquisition geometries. Overall,
our findings highlight the potential of combining variational principles with deep learning-based manifold
priors to advance the state of the art in image reconstruction.
