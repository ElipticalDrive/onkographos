A BSTRACT
The multiscale nature of turbulent reacting flows and the importance of fine-scale structures, including thin reaction layers, pose challenges for super-resolution. In this study, we investigate a
3D super-resolution framework inspired by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), called Neural Field
Super-Resolution (NFSR), to reconstruct fine-resolution fields from coarsened reacting-flow fields.
The proposed formulation adapts the continuous coordinate-conditioned representation of NeRF to
physical flow-field reconstruction by conditioning spatial coordinates with local coarse-grid thermochemical information, while eliminating radiance prediction and volume rendering. Principal
component analysis is used to provide a compact and low-dimensional representation of the thermochemical state, and a hierarchical extension, H-NFSR, progressively reconstructs the solution
through intermediate resolution levels. The framework is evaluated using three-dimensional direct
numerical simulation data for a turbulent premixed hydrogen-air flame from the BLASTNet 3.0
dataset, with an overall spatial coarsening factor of eight in each coordinate direction. The results
demonstrate consistently better prediction measures and a higher ability to reconstruct fine-scale
information than tricubic interpolation on the unseen test realization. The hierarchical formulation
reduces visible coarse-cell imprinting. Training with a mean absolute error loss provides modest
improvements in the overall quantitative performance compared with a mean squared error loss. In
contrast, biased sampling mainly benefits the most localized structures. Three-dimensional isosurface
comparisons further show that H-NFSR recovers much of the small and intermediate features of
the flow, including surface wrinkling and local flame curvature effects. These results highlight
the potential of coordinate-conditioned neural fields as a flexible framework for three-dimensional
coarse-to-fine reconstruction of reacting-flow simulations.
Keywords Turbulent reacting flows, combustion, super-resolution, machine learning, computational methods

Introduction
Turbulent reacting flows involve a wide range of interacting spatial and temporal scales. Thin reaction zones, steep
thermochemical gradients, and highly wrinkled flame structures can require very fine spatial resolution for accurate
numerical representation. Direct numerical simulation (DNS) (Domingo and Vervisch, 2022) provides access to these
fine-scale features, but its computational cost becomes prohibitive for many practical reacting-flow configurations.
Consequently, large-eddy simulation (LES), quasi-DNS (Komen et al., 2017; Zirwes et al., 2020; Abe et al., 2026),
and other coarse-grid approaches are commonly used to reduce the computational requirements. In these approaches,
however, part of the fine-scale spatial information is either modeled or not directly represented on the computational
grid. This has motivated the development of data-driven approaches that seek to recover fine-resolution information
from coarse grid flow representations based on super-resolution.
A number of machine-learning approaches have been developed for flow-field super-resolution. Convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) have demonstrated the ability to reconstruct fine-scale turbulent structures from low-resolution

have been addressed using deconvolution- and CNN-based approaches for quantities associated with scalar transport,
reaction rates, and filtered thermochemical fields (Nikolaou et al., 2018; Lapeyre et al., 2019; Seltz et al., 2019).
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and their super-resolution variants have also been applied to turbulent and
reacting-flow reconstruction, where adversarial training is used to improve the representation of small-scale spatial
structures (Goodfellow et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2018; Bode et al., 2021; Grenga et al., 2023; Nista et al., 2023,
2024, 2025). More recently, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been introduced for flow-field super-resolution,
particularly for problems involving irregular or unstructured meshes (Barwey et al., 2025; Dash et al., 2026), in addition
to techniques based on diffusion models (Huang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026).
Despite their demonstrated capabilities, these approaches rely on different architecture-specific representations of the
spatial field. CNN-based methods generally operate on structured spatial patches or volumes, GAN-based formulations
introduce an additional adversarial training procedure. At the same time, GNN-based approaches require an explicit
graph representation of the computational domain. Moreover, these approaches typically represent the reconstructed
solution on a prescribed discrete grid or graph, rather than as a continuous spatial field. This can limit the direct
evaluation of the solution at arbitrary spatial coordinates and motivates the exploration of alternative super-resolution
formulations based on continuous field representations. In this context, coordinate-conditioned neural representations
provide a natural framework in which the fine-resolution solution can be evaluated directly at prescribed spatial
locations.
An extension of the Neural Implicit Flow (NIF), which was originally developed by Pan et al. (2023), was proposed
by Amarathunga and Echekki (2026) to reconstruct super-resolution fields from coarse simulation data of reacting flows.
NIF provides a mesh-agnostic representation of spatio-temporal flow fields by separating the spatial representation
from temporal or parametric information. In the NIF-based super-resolution framework, coarse-grid thermochemical
information is used to condition the reconstruction of fine-resolution reacting-flow scalar fields. This formulation
demonstrated the potential of coordinate-based neural representations for continuous super-resolution of reactingflow fields. However, the framework relies on a hypernetwork architecture in which the parameters of the spatial
reconstruction network are generated dynamically from the conditioning information. Although this provides a flexible
representation, it also introduces additional computational overhead. These disadvantages encourage the development of
a more direct coordinate-conditioned formulation in which coarse-grid flow information conditions the fine-resolution
prediction without dynamically generating the weights of the spatial network. Such a formulation is particularly
attractive for extending continuous coordinate-based super-resolution to three-dimensional reacting flows.
The Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) model introduced by Mildenhall et al. (2020) provides a useful basis for such a
formulation. NeRF represents a three-dimensional scene as a continuous function of spatial coordinates and employs
positional encoding to improve the representation of high-frequency spatial variation. Although originally developed for
radiance-field reconstruction and novel-view synthesis, the underlying coordinate-conditioned representation provides a
useful framework for physical fields characterized by strong localized spatial variations. This motivates the present
formulation of a NeRF-inspired neural-field framework for three-dimensional super-resolution of reacting flows.
In the current study, a Neural Field Super-Resolution (NFSR) framework is introduced to reconstruct three-dimensional
fine-resolution thermochemical scalar fields from coarse reacting flow data. The proposed framework provides a direct
mapping between coarse-grid information, spatial coordinates, and the corresponding fine-resolution solution, while
avoiding the dynamic parameter generation. A hierarchical extension is also examined to determine whether progressive
coarse-to-fine reconstruction improves the recovery of localized structures and spatial continuity. In addition to the
reconstruction architecture itself, the effects of training-data sampling and the optimization objective are investigated to
determine how these modeling choices influence the reconstruction accuracy.
The training objective is of particular interest because the relative behavior of commonly used loss functions can depend
on the underlying super-resolution formulation. Zhao et al. (2017) demonstrated that networks trained using an L1
loss can provide improved reconstruction quality compared with the commonly used L2 loss for image restoration and
super-resolution problems, including improved preservation of perceptually important structures. Their results also
showed that the relative performance of the loss functions can depend on the convergence behavior of the network
rather than only on the form of the error metric itself. More recently, Balla et al. (2025) adopted an MAE-based
loss (L1 ) for three-dimensional turbulence super-resolution, motivated by previous observations that MAE can better
preserve reconstruction quality and reduce over-smoothing compared with MSE (L2 ) in image-restoration applications.
These observations establish the need for an examination of whether similar behavior is observed in the present
coordinate-conditioned NFSR framework.
The proposed framework is evaluated using three-dimensional DNS data for a turbulent premixed hydrogen–air flame
from the BLASTNet 3.0 dataset (Chung et al., 2026). A spatial coarsening factor of eight in each coordinate direction is
considered, resulting in a challenging reconstruction problem in which a substantial portion of the fine-scale spatial

2.1
NeRF-inspired Neural-field Architecture
The proposed framework is based on an extension of the NeRF architecture (Mildenhall et al., 2020). Figure 1 illustrates
a conceptual representation of the original NeRF architecture, which was originally developed to represent a threedimensional scene as a continuous function of the spatial coordinates and the viewing direction. The network maps
a spatial location, x = (x, y, z), and a viewing direction, d, to a volume density, σ, and the corresponding emitted
radiance, represented by components enclosed within the dashed lines. This mapping can be expressed as

In the original NeRF architecture, the spatial coordinates are first transformed using a positional encoding and passed
through a fully connected spatial multilayer perceptron (MLP), as shown in Fig. 1. The positional encoding maps
the coordinates into a higher-dimensional representation using sinusoidal functions and improves the ability of the
network to capture high-frequency spatial variations. A skip connection is also incorporated within the spatial MLP by
reintroducing the encoded coordinates at an intermediate layer. The spatial MLP predicts the volume density, σ, and an
intermediate feature vector, f . The feature vector is subsequently concatenated with the encoded viewing direction and
passed through a color-head MLP to predict the view-dependent RGB radiance. The predicted density and radiance are
evaluated at multiple points along a camera ray and combined using a differentiable volume-rendering procedure to
construct the final image.
The present study modifies the NeRF architecture to address 1) the implementation of this architecture for superresolution of scalar and velocity fields, 2) the multi-dimensional nature of chemical reacting flow states, and 3) the
inherent challenge of capturing fine-scale structures from coarse-scale fields when such structures originate at the finest
scales.

When adapting the NeRF architecture to reacting-flow super-resolution, the high dimensionality of the thermochemical
state must be addressed. The state may consist of temperature and a large number of species mass fractions, particularly
when detailed chemical mechanisms are considered. Directly reconstructing all of these scalar fields increases the
dimensionality of the super-resolution problem. Alternatively, selecting only a small number of thermochemical scalars
reduces the dimensionality, but may exclude information contained in the remaining correlated quantities. In the
present framework, principal component analysis (PCA) is therefore used to obtain a compact and low-dimensional
representation of the thermochemical state. We have adopted a similar approach in our recent work implementing NIF
for super-resolution, resulting in a computationally efficient reconstruction of species and temperatures (Amarathunga
and Echekki, 2026).
The retained principal components (PCs) combine information from the original thermochemical scalars and allow the
NFSR model to operate on a lower-dimensional representation while retaining information associated with a larger set
of scalar quantities. The thermochemical scalar fields can subsequently be recovered from the reconstructed principal
component fields through the inverse PCA transformation or, more accurately, through a nonlinear mapping using a
simple artificial neural network (ANN), which has been shown to improve the reconstruction of thermochemical scalars
from a reduced set of PCs (Mirgolbabaei and Echekki, 2013, 2015).
Converting the NeRF architecture to a super-resolution architecture also requires a modification to the input and output
information of the network. The present framework adopts the coordinate-based representation, positional encoding,
skip connection, and spatial-feature conditioning used in NeRF. However, important changes are introduced to adapt
the architecture for the super-resolution of three-dimensional reacting-flow fields. Figure 2 illustrates the proposed
NeRF-inspired architecture, hereafter referred to as the Neural Field Super-Resolution (NFSR) model. Unlike the
original NeRF, the present model does not predict volume density or radiance and does not implement ray projection or
volume rendering. Instead, the network directly reconstructs the fine-grid PC fields at prescribed spatial coordinates
using the corresponding local coarse-grid PC information. The spatial coordinates, x = (x1 , x2 , x3 ) = (x, y, z), are
normalized within the local reconstruction region and transformed using positional encoding. For each coordinate
component xp , where p ∈ {1, 2, 3}, the encoding is defined as

2.2
Hierarchical Learning Approach for Super-resolution
Direct reconstruction of a fine-grid solution from a highly coarsened input requires the model to recover a significant
amount of spatial information in a single mapping. This becomes increasingly challenging as the coarsening factor
increases, particularly for reacting-flow fields characterized by thin reaction zones and strong spatial variations. To
address this limitation, a hierarchical learning approach is implemented in the proposed framework. Instead of directly
reconstructing the fine-grid solution from the coarsest input, the reconstruction is performed through a sequence of
intermediate resolution levels.

Data Preparation and Model Training
Dataset description – Premixed Flame H2 –Air DNS
The proposed NFSR framework is applied to a three-dimensional turbulent reacting-flow dataset from the BLASTNet
data collection (Chung et al., 2026). The selected configuration corresponds to a lean turbulent premixed hydrogen–air
flame stabilized in a slot burner geometry. The simulations are performed at atmospheric pressure with an unburned gas
temperature of 300 K. A premixed H2 –air mixture is injected through the central slot with a bulk velocity of 24 m/s and
a turbulence intensity u′ = 2.4 m s−1 . The central jet is surrounded by two laminar coflows of burned gas with a bulk
velocity of 3.6 m/s.
Turbulence at the central inlet is generated using homogeneous and isotropic turbulence based on a Passot–Pouquet
spectrum (Passot and Pouquet, 1987) with an integral length scale of lt = 2 mm. The BLASTNet database contains
this configuration at several global equivalence ratios. In the present work, the leanest configuration, ϕg = 0.35, is
selected to evaluate the reconstruction of highly localized thermochemical structures associated with lean hydrogen
combustion. For this case, the DNS field is represented on a uniform Cartesian grid, 951 × 401 × 201, with a grid
spacing of 80 µm, and the streamwise domain length is Lx = 76 mm.
The chemical kinetics of hydrogen oxidation are represented using the San Diego chemical mechanism of Saxena
and Williams (2006), comprising nine species and 21 elementary reactions. The available solution variables consist
of the three velocity components, pressure, temperature, density, and the species mass fractions of H2 , H, O2 , OH,
O, H2 O, HO2 , H2 O2 and N2 . For the present super-resolution problem, the DNS thermochemical fields provide the
reference fine-grid solution, while the corresponding coarse and intermediate resolution fields are generated by the
spatial subsampling procedure introduced in Section 2.2.
The ϕg = 0.35 dataset contains five statistically stationary snapshots separated by 2 ms. In the present study, three
snapshots are used for training, one snapshot is used for validation, while the remaining snapshot is retained as an
unseen test realization. This separation ensures that the super-resolution performance is evaluated on a turbulent flame
realization that is not included during model training or model selection. The resulting dataset is used to assess the
ability of the proposed hierarchical NFSR framework to reconstruct fine-scale reacting-flow structures from spatially
coarsened fields. However, owing to the limited number of available snapshots, the reacting-flow dataset does not
provide sufficient coverage of the statistical variability even within the selected ϕg = 0.35 configuration. Consequently,
the held-out snapshot should be interpreted as an independent test realization rather than as a rigorously defined test set
representative of the full distribution of this operating condition.
Before data preparation, the computational domain is clipped to reduce the size of the dataset while retaining the
primary flame structure and the surrounding regions required to characterize its spatial development. The domain is
clipped symmetrically in the y- and z-directions, while in the x-direction, which corresponds to the primary flamedevelopment direction, the far-field region downstream of the flame is truncated. Following this operation, the original
951×401×201 domain is reduced to 641×321×193 grid points, corresponding to a physical domain of approximately
51.2 × 25.6 × 15.36 mm3 .
For data preparation, temperature and the eight species mass fractions, excluding N2 , are selected. Each scalar is
first normalized between 0 and 1 using its corresponding minimum and maximum values. PCA is then applied to the
normalized thermochemical state to obtain a reduced representation that retains the dominant variations in the original
scalar fields. The PCs are selected such that at least 99% of the cumulative variance is retained. For the present dataset,
this criterion results in 4 PCs, (ψ1 , ψ2 , ψ3 , ψ4 ), which together account for 99.57% of the cumulative variance.
Once the PCs are obtained, an additional preprocessing step is applied before model training. First, a constant shift
is introduced to ensure that all PC values are positive. The shifted values are then raised to the one-fifth power,
which compresses the dynamic range and reduces the spread of the PC data. The transformed PCs are subsequently
standardized using their respective mean and standard deviation before being used for the training and evaluation of the
NFSR framework.
Following the preprocessing of the PC fields, the computational domain is decomposed into a set of local threedimensional subdomains or blocks. This subdomain-based learning strategy follows the approach adopted in the
NIF-based super-resolution framework (Amarathunga and Echekki, 2026), where the global domain was partitioned
into localized regions and each region was expressed in a normalized local coordinate system. This methodology
enabled the model to learn spatial features based on their relative local structure rather than their absolute position in the
computational domain. Such a representation promotes the learning of reusable local features that may occur at different
spatial locations and under different flow conditions, thereby improving the potential for generalization. Although
this domain decomposition methodology (Amarathunga and Echekki, 2026) was demonstrated for two-dimensional

reacting-flow fields, the same localized learning principle is extended here to the three-dimensional reconstruction

For the present three-dimensional implementation, each local block contains a coarse-grid representation of 5 × 5 × 5
grid points, which corresponds to 4 × 4 × 4 coarse cells. With the overall coarsening factor of 8, the corresponding
fine-grid subdomain contains 33 × 33 × 33 grid points, or 32 × 32 × 32 fine cells. For hierarchical reconstruction, the
intermediate resolution levels therefore consist of the points of the grids 9 × 9 × 9 and 17 × 17 × 17 before reaching
the final representation of the fine-grid 33 × 33 × 33.
Within each block, the spatial coordinates are mapped to a local normalized coordinate system spanning [−1, 1] in each
spatial direction. This local representation enables the NFSR model to learn the spatial structure within the subdomain
independently of its absolute position in the global computational domain. A similar localized learning strategy was
adopted in the NIF-based super-resolution framework for two-dimensional reacting flows Amarathunga and Echekki
(2026). In the present three-dimensional implementation, neighboring blocks are extracted with an overlap ratio of 50%,
such that adjacent subdomains share half of their spatial extent in each overlapping direction. This overlap increases the
diversity of the training data by allowing similar local flow structures to appear at different relative positions within the
blocks and by exposing the model to different combinations of neighboring local features. As a result, the model is
encouraged to learn the underlying local spatial patterns rather than their specific locations within a given block.
2.3.2
Data augmentation in training
To further enrich the flame training data, a data augmentation strategy similar to the one adopted by Chung et al. (2023)
is used. In this approach, three-dimensional fields are subjected to discrete rotations and reflections. The augmentation
is applied stochastically at the batch level during training. For each training batch, a rotation axis is selected randomly
from the x-, y-, and z-directions, while the rotation angle is selected with equal probability from 0◦ , 90◦ , 180◦ , and
270◦ . Thus, each rotation angle has a probability of 25%. Following the rotation operation, a reflection is applied
with a probability of 50%, with the reflection axis randomly selected from the x-, y-, or z-direction. The selected
transformations are applied to the three-dimensional fields within the batch directly through grid-index operations,
avoiding spatial interpolation and the associated modification of the PC fields.
The rotation and reflection operations expose the model to similar local flame structures appearing at different
orientations within the computational domain. Although the present implementation differs slightly from that of
Chung et al. (2023), the overall augmentation strategy follows the same objective of increasing the diversity of spatial
structures available during training. This is particularly important for reacting flows, where flame fronts and localized
high-gradient regions can occur over a wide range of orientations.
In addition to the spatial augmentation, a biased sampling strategy is introduced in the present framework to increase
the frequency at which regions with strong spatial variations are selected during training. For each combination of
snapshot and local coarse-grid window, the population standard deviation of the selected PC fields is evaluated and
averaged to provide a measure of the local variation. The windows are then ranked according to this measure, such that
regions with stronger variations are assigned larger sampling weights. The sampling weights are distributed between 1
and a prescribed bias ratio, rb , according to

2.3.3
Model training setup
To evaluate the effects of the hierarchical reconstruction, biased sampling, and loss function, four NFSR models are
trained. The hierarchical NFSR (H-NFSR) model without biased sampling is selected as the baseline configuration.
The second model retains the same H-NFSR architecture while introducing the biased sampling strategy described in
Section 2.3.2. The third model removes the intermediate reconstruction stages and directly maps the coarsened input
field to the fine-grid solution using a single-stage NFSR model. Finally, the fourth model retains the baseline H-NFSR
architecture but replaces the mean squared error (MSE) loss with the mean absolute error (MAE). These configurations
allow the effects of the hierarchical reconstruction, sampling strategy, and loss criterion to be evaluated independently.

3.3
Effect of Bias Sampling in Model Training
The effect of the biased sampling strategy is evaluated by comparing the baseline H-NFSR model with the H-NFSR
model trained using biased sampling. The architecture, loss function, and training procedure remain unchanged between
the two cases, such that the difference is limited to the sampling probability of the local training subdomains. As
described in Section 2.3.2, the biased sampling strategy increases the frequency at which regions with stronger spatial
variations are presented during training.
Figure 10 compares representative x − y slices at z = 0 from the unseen test dataset for ψ3 and ψ4 . At the global-field
level, both models produce very similar reconstructions and recover the dominant flame-front structures. Therefore, the
effect of biased sampling is considerably less apparent visually than the difference observed between the hierarchical
and single-stage reconstruction strategies.
The quantitative results indicate that the influence of biased sampling is not uniform across the 4 PCs. A complete
comparison of all model configurations is presented in Section 3.7. For the unseen test dataset, the mean NMAE
over the 4 PCs changes only from 1.36% for the baseline H-NFSR model to 1.38% with biased sampling, while the
corresponding mean SSIM increases slightly from 0.9238 to 0.9261. This indicates that biased sampling does not
provide a general reduction in the overall reconstruction error.

behavior of MSE and MAE should be interpreted within the context of the model architecture, sampling procedure, and
reconstruction formulation rather than generalized directly across different super-resolution approaches.

the three-dimensional geometric complexity lost during spatial coarsening. This result is particularly encouraging given
that the model is trained using only three statistically stationary snapshots, which provide limited coverage of the full
range of spatial structures that may occur within the selected operating condition.

similar overall reconstruction capability, with the choice among them depending on the desired balance between
quantitative accuracy, local continuity, and computational cost.
4
Conclusions
This study developed a NeRF-inspired Neural Field Super-Resolution (NFSR) framework for reconstructing threedimensional fine-resolution reacting-flow fields from spatially coarsened solution data. The proposed formulation
replaces the view-dependent radiance prediction of the original NeRF architecture with a coordinate-conditioned
mapping that combines local spatial information with coarse-grid PC fields. A hierarchical extension, H-NFSR,
performs the reconstruction progressively through intermediate resolution levels, while PCA provides a compact and
22
Neural Field Super-Resolution of Reacting Flows
A P REPRINT
low-dimensional representation of the thermochemical state. The framework was evaluated using three-dimensional
DNS data for a lean premixed hydrogen–air flame with an overall spatial coarsening factor of eight in each coordinate
direction.
The results demonstrate that the proposed neural-field formulation can recover fine-scale spatial information that is
not directly represented in the coarse-grid input. Compared with tricubic interpolation, the learned reconstructions
provide improved overall accuracy and structural similarity across the retained PC fields. The reconstructed solutions
recover thin interfaces, localized structures, and small-scale spatial variations that are strongly attenuated in the coarse
representation. These improvements are particularly evident for the higher-order PCs, which contain thinner and more
localized spatial structures.
The reconstruction in the reduced PC space is subsequently mapped back to the original thermochemical variables using
an ANN trained to recover temperature and the eight retained species mass fractions from the four PCs. Compared with
direct inverse PCA, the ANN provides substantially lower reconstruction errors across the recovered thermochemical
quantities. The combined H-NFSR–ANN framework also recovers much of the fine-scale structure of the physical
temperature and OH fields for the unseen test realization. This demonstrates that the spatial information recovered in
the reduced representation can be transferred effectively back to physically interpretable thermochemical quantities.
The model comparisons also clarify the influence of the different components of the framework. The hierarchical
reconstruction improves local spatial continuity and reduces visible coarse-cell imprinting, while the single-stage NFSR
provides comparable global reconstruction accuracy at a substantially lower training cost. Biased sampling provides a
more localized improvement for strongly varying structures, particularly for the higher-order PCs. Training with MAE
provides modest improvements in the overall quantitative performance compared with MSE, although the reconstructed
fields remain visually similar and the MAE-trained model requires a longer convergence time. These results show that
the NFSR framework can be adapted according to the desired balance between reconstruction accuracy, local spatial
quality, and computational cost.
The three-dimensional reconstruction further demonstrates that the capability of the framework extends beyond
individual planar sections. The temperature field recovered using the combined H-NFSR–ANN framework reproduces
much of the surface wrinkling, local curvature, and localized three-dimensional structures observed in the fine-grid
DNS reference but poorly represented in the coarse-grid field. This provides a direct demonstration that the proposed
coarse-to-fine reconstruction can recover physically meaningful three-dimensional thermochemical structure after
transformation from the reduced PC representation.
Despite being trained using a limited number of statistically stationary snapshots, the framework successfully reconstructs the principal fine-scale and three-dimensional features of an unseen test realization. This provides encouraging
evidence that the coordinate-conditioned formulation can learn spatial reconstruction relationships that extend beyond
the individual structures presented during training. However, the limited number of available snapshots does not provide
sufficient coverage of the full statistical variability of the selected operating condition. Future work will therefore
extend the framework to a broader range of snapshots, operating conditions, and reacting-flow configurations, and will
further evaluate its application to independently generated coarse-grid simulations.
Overall, the present results demonstrate that coordinate-conditioned neural fields provide an effective approach for
three-dimensional super-resolution of reacting-flow fields. The proposed NFSR framework, together with the nonlinear
recovery of physical thermochemical variables, provides a flexible coarse-to-fine reconstruction strategy for recovering
fine-scale reacting-flow structure from strongly coarsened representations and establishes a foundation for future
application to high-fidelity reacting-flow simulations.
