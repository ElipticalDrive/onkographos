## Abstract

This paper describes a wavelet–based ray–tracing algorithm that carries out high quality images. Our approach takes advantage of the multiresolution representation of the volume data and the self-similarity of the basis functions. The results are flexible high order approximations of the low albedo rendering equation. We propose accurate integration and evaluation schemes for shading and intensity computation. This paper also describes the calculation of error bounds of the intensity integration. The theory and its implementation are discussed and shaded images are presented.

## 1 Introduction

Volume rendering still represents an emerging and challenging sub–field in visualization. Various techniques have been developed to solve approximations of the adapted intro–differential transport theory model [13].

We can distinguish three categories of algorithms: object–order, image–order and hybrid approaches. Object–order algorithms are based on the spatial structure of volume data. Volume elements are represented as splats within the projection area. New hardware technologies allow the splatting to be performed in a fast and accurate manner [2,14]. Recent approaches for the generation of mathematically optimized splats and algorithms [17] make the splatting available even for low–cost workstations. The main disadvantage to this is the difficult calculations of the ray termination.

This problem can be avoided by image–order algorithms. Rays are casted through each pixel of the projection plane into the volumetric data set [10,16]. These algorithms usually result in high

quality representations of rendered volumes, but the complex calculations for each ray are extremely time and memory consuming. Multiresolution approaches [12,25] accelerate the rendering speed and can help to reduce the required memory.

An example for hybrid rendering shemes is given with shear–Warp factorisation [15], which links object and image–order rendering. It allows to access the volume data by scanlines that are aligned to the pixels of the projection plane and offers an efficient algorithm.

In the following paper we describe a new image–order method which exploits many features of the multiresolution analysis while increasing performance and minimising memory requirements. It enables us to describe the error–bounds analytically and can be controlled by the user. The usage of B–spline basis functions and their corresponding wavelets allows an accurate calculation of the gradient and results in different image representations of the same volume data. The implemented data structures allows fast and efficient data handling.

Our paper is organised as follows: First we give an overview of the mathematical basics of the multidimensional wavelet theory on which our algorithm is based. We focus on cardinal B–spline wavelets and elucidate its advantages. The second chapter links the multiresolution analysis and the volume rendering equation. Furthermore, expressions for the error bounds of different integration methods are given. The implementation and data structures are described in the third chapter. The fourth chapter presents some results. Finally we conclude and discuss some extensions of our approach.

## 2 Mathematical Foundations

### 2.1 Construction of 3D–Wavelets

Wavelets in higher dimensions are easily obtained by a tensor product approach [9]. Thus for the 3D case, a 3D–wavelet can be defined as  $\phi^3(x, y, z) := \phi(x) \phi(y) \phi(z) = \phi \otimes \phi \otimes \phi(x, y, z)$ . In case of (semi–)orthonormal set of functions  $\{\phi(x - p) | p \in \mathbf{Z}\}$ , the set  $\{\phi^3(x - p, y - q, z - r) | p, q, r \in \mathbf{Z}\}$  form an (semi–)orthonormal basis for  $V_0^3 \subset L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$ , where  $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$  denotes the vector space of measurable, square–integrable three dimensional functions  $f(x, y, z)$ . Mallat [18] showed that there exists a multiresolution analysis of  $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$  by translation and dyadic scaling of the so–called scaling function  $\phi$ . In general:

$$(1) \quad \phi_{mpqr}^3(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}} \phi(2^{-m}x - p) \phi(2^{-m}y - q) \phi(2^{-m}z - r) \in V_m^3 \subset L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$$

$$m, p, q, r \in \mathbf{Z}$$

By definition (1) and because of the MRA, it is clear, that  $\{V_m^3 := \text{clos}_{L^2}(\phi_{mpqr}^3 | p, q, r \in \mathbf{Z})\}$  is a nested set of closed subspaces of  $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$ , e.g.  $\dots \subset V_1 \subset V_0 \subset V_{-1} \subset \dots$ . The complement  $U_m^3$  of  $V_m^3$  in  $V_{m-1}^3$  is spanned by the 7 separable functions:

$$\psi_{mpqr}^{3,1}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\phi(2^{-m}x - p)\phi(2^{-m}y - q)\psi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,1}$$

$$\psi_{mpqr}^{3,2}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\phi(2^{-m}x - p)\psi(2^{-m}y - q)\phi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,2}$$

$$\psi_{mpqr}^{3,3}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\phi(2^{-m}x - p)\psi(2^{-m}y - q)\psi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,3}$$

$$(2) \quad \psi_{mpqr}^{3,4}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\psi(2^{-m}x - p)\phi(2^{-m}y - q)\phi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,4}$$

$$\psi_{mpqr}^{3,5}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\psi(2^{-m}x - p)\phi(2^{-m}y - q)\psi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,5}$$

$$\psi_{mpqr}^{3,6}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\psi(2^{-m}x - p)\psi(2^{-m}y - q)\phi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,6}$$

$$\psi_{mpqr}^{3,7}(x, y, z) := 2^{-\frac{3m}{2}}\psi(2^{-m}x - p)\psi(2^{-m}y - q)\psi(2^{-m}z - r) \in U_m^{3,7}$$

In (1) and (2),  $p, q, r \in \mathbf{Z}$  describe translations, whereas  $m \in \mathbf{Z}$  denotes the dyadic scaling factor. The spaces  $\mathbf{U}_m^{3,i} := \text{clos}_{L^2}(\psi_{mpqr}^{3,i} \mid p, q, r \in \mathbf{Z})$  represent the oriented 'detail' information, that can be seen as the difference of information between  $\mathbf{V}_m^3$  and  $\mathbf{V}_{m-1}^3$ . Consequently

$$(3) \quad \bigoplus_{\substack{m \in \mathbf{Z} \\ 1 \leq i \leq 7}} \mathbf{U}_m^{3,i} = L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$$

When the multiresolution analysis is orthogonal, the wavelet decomposition of a given function  $f(x, y, z) \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$  computes the projection into its corresponding spaces, which in return results in the best  $L^2$ -approximation in  $\mathbf{V}$  and  $\mathbf{U}$ . In the more general case of semiorthogonal systems, we can define the projection Operators  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{Q}$  which compute approximations as:

$$(4) \quad \mathcal{P}_{\phi_m^3} f(x) = \sum_{p,q,r} \langle f, \phi_{mpqr}^3 \rangle \tilde{\phi}_{mpqr} = \sum_{p,q,r} \langle f, \tilde{\phi}_{mpqr}^3 \rangle \phi_{mpqr}^3$$

and

$$(5) \quad \mathcal{Q}_{\psi_m^{3,i}} f(x) = \sum_{p,q,r} \langle f, \psi_{mpqr}^{3,i} \rangle \tilde{\psi}_{mpqr}^{3,i} = \sum_{p,q,r} \langle f, \tilde{\psi}_{mpqr}^{3,i} \rangle \psi_{mpqr}^{3,i}$$

The properties (1) and (2) are similar for the so-called dual functions  $\tilde{\phi}_{mpqr}^3$  and  $\tilde{\psi}_{mpqr}^{3,i}$ . For each  $m$  the projection  $\mathcal{P}_{\psi_m^{3,i}} f(x)$  gives localized time-frequency information of  $f$  in the corresponding frequency band (or octave). In the orthonormal case the primary and dual functions are identical. Note, that a strict local support comes along with a global support of the dual counterparts.

A fast and accurate computation of the inner products for the  $n$ -dimensional case, given in (4) and (5), is usually carried out by quadrature mirror pair filter cascades [1].

## 2.2 Cardinal Spline-wavelets

Spline wavelet bases, introduced by [5,21] are representatives of semiorthogonal wavelets. Analytic expressions for scaling and wavelet functions, their duals and derivatives can be given. Furthermore as the order  $j$  tends to infinity they converge pointwise to Gabor functions [20], which is an optimal function in terms of the time/frequency localization. The mother-scaling function of the B-spline wavelets is defined as the  $j$ th order cardinal B-spline function and can be defined recursively by convolution:

$$\begin{aligned}
(6) \quad \phi^{[l]}(x) &:= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi^{[l-1]}(x-t) \phi^{[1]}(t) dt \\
&= \int_0^1 \phi^{[l-1]}(x-t) dt
\end{aligned}$$

with

$$(7) \quad \phi^{[1]}(x) := \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } 0 \leq x < 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

In figure 1 the functional courses for three cardinal B splines are depicted



Figure 1: Different cardinal B-spline scaling functions

a)  $j=1$ , b)  $j=2$ , c)  $j=4$ 

Furthermore these functions are compactly supported with

$$(8) \quad \text{supp}(\phi^{[l]}) = [0, j]$$

and their derivatives satisfy the recursive relation

$$(9) \quad \phi^{[l]'} = \phi^{[l-1]}(x) - \phi^{[l-1]}(x-1)$$

Different biorthogonal cardinal spline wavelet bases can be constructed [3]. Following [4], the minimum support wavelet that corresponds to the  $j$ th order cardinal spline scaling-function is defined as:

$$(10) \quad \psi^{[l]}(x) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} q_{jk} \phi^{[l]}(2x-k) , j \in \mathbb{N}$$

$$q_{jn} := \frac{(-1)^n}{2^{j-1}} \sum_{l=0}^j \binom{j}{l} \phi^{[2j]}(n+1-l)$$



Figure 2: Different B-spline wavelets of minimal support

a)  $j=1$ , b)  $j=2$ , c)  $j=4$ 

It can be verified that

$$(11) \quad \text{supp}(\psi^{[j]}) = [0, 2j - 1]$$

Definitions for the dual functions are given in [5].

This set of functions generate a semiorthogonal multiresolution analysis, e.g.  $U_m = \tilde{U}_m$ ,  $V_m = \tilde{V}_m$  and  $V_m \perp U_m$ . Wavelet and scaling functions that satisfy these identity-relations are semiorthogonal and the corresponding wavelets are often called pre-wavelets.

### 2.3 Boundary Conditions

The upper relations suppose any bases to be defined on the real line. Finite datasets used for volume rendering require a wavelet transform to be confined to an interval. Several constructions for semiorthonormal and orthonormal basis functions on  $[0,1]$  are discussed in this section.

#### 2.3.1 Spatial windowing

A first and most simple approach for handling the WT on an interval is to define a function

$$(12) \quad \tilde{f}(x) := \begin{cases} f(x) & \text{if } x \in [0, 1] \\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases}$$

That extension can be used to apply the WT for the real line, as it was defined above. For many applications this leads to sufficient results, although discontinuities appear at  $f(0)$  and  $f(1)$ , which may cause high frequency bursts in the actual signals. Furthermore, the number of relevant basis-functions will extend  $2^{-m}$  because of the unbounded structure of the decomposition on the real line.

#### 2.3.2 Reflection

A second solution is to reflect the function  $f(x)$  at its edges. The extended function  $\tilde{f}(x)$  can be defined as:

$$(13) \quad \tilde{f}(x) := \begin{cases} \tilde{f}(-x) & \text{if } x < 0 \\ f(x) & \text{if } x \in [0, 1] \\ \tilde{f}(2-x) & \text{if } x > 1 \end{cases}$$

In case of a continuous function  $f(x)$  the modified function  $\tilde{f}(x)$  becomes at least  $C^0$ -continuous. This can also be seen from the view of the bases. Reflecting the function  $f(x)$  is equivalent to expand this function on the real line and to use folded wavelets and scaling functions:

$$(14) \quad \begin{aligned} \phi_{mp}^{fold}(x) &:= \sum_i \phi_{mp}(x - 2i) + \sum_i \phi_{mp}(2i - x) \quad , \quad x \in [0, 1] \\ \psi_{mp}^{fold}(x) &:= \sum_i \psi_{mp}(x - 2i) + \sum_i \psi_{mp}(2i - x) \end{aligned}$$

[8] showed that any given biorthogonal multiresolution analysis (defined by  $\phi, \psi$ ) leads to another biorthogonal analysis on  $[0, 1]$  (defined by  $\phi^{fold}, \psi^{fold}$ ), where the property of an orthogonal MRA is lost during that transformation. The resulting biorthogonal system on  $[0, 1]$  has

1. $2^{-m} + 1$  scaling functions and  $2^{-m}$  wavelets if  $\psi$  and  $\tilde{\psi}$  are symmetric with respect to  $1/2$ .
2. $2^{-m}$  scaling functions and  $2^{-m}$  wavelets if  $\psi$  and  $\tilde{\psi}$  are antisymmetric with respect to  $1/2$

These two cases are illustrated in figure 3 and 4. Note, to ensure that the support of the wavelet and the scaling function is smaller than 1, we have to choose  $m$  as  $-2$ .

### 2.3.3 Repetition

Third, the function  $f(x)$  can be repeated periodically. This appears to be the most natural way from the point of view of sampling theory:

$$(15) \quad \tilde{f}(x) := \begin{cases} \tilde{f}(x+1) & \text{if } x < 0 \\ f(x) & \text{if } x \in [0, 1] \\ \tilde{f}(x-1) & \text{if } x > 1 \end{cases}$$

As before, this transformation can also be considered as a conversion of the basis-functions, namely:

$$(16) \quad \begin{aligned} \phi_{mp}^{period}(x) &:= 2^{-m/2} \sum_i \phi_{mp}(2^{-m}x + 2^{-m}i - p) \\ \psi_{mp}^{period}(x) &:= 2^{-m/2} \sum_i \psi_{mp}(2^{-m}x + 2^{-m}i - p) \end{aligned}$$

The space  $\mathbf{V}_m^{period}$  and its complement  $\mathbf{U}_m^{period}$  are spanned by  $2^{-m}$  functions each.

### 2.3.4 Multiple knots

The fourth possibility for constructions of a B-spline based multiresolution analysis on a bounded interval is to introduce multiple knots at the endpoints.

According to (8), (11) and for the 1D case we recieve

$$(17) \quad \phi_{mp}(x) := 2^{-\frac{m}{2}}\phi(2^{-m}x - p) \quad \psi_{mp}(x) := 2^{-\frac{m}{2}}\psi(2^{-m}x - p) \quad m, p \in \mathbf{Z}$$

so that

$$(18) \quad \text{supp}\left(\phi_{mp}^{[j]}\right) = [2^m p, 2^m(j+p)] \quad \text{supp}\left(\psi_{mp}^{[j]}\right) = [2^m p, 2^m(2j-1+p)]$$

For a given basis function  $f$ , three different classes of functions can be distinguished:

a) Left boundary (LB) functions:  $\{0\} \in \text{supp}(f)$ .

(An associated set of indices is defined as:  $S_m^{LB}(f) := \{p \in \mathbf{Z} | \{0\} \in \text{supp}(f_{mp})\}$ )

b) Right boundary (RB) functions:  $\{1\} \in \text{supp}(f)$ ,

$$S_m^{RB}(f) := \{p \in \mathbf{Z} | \{1\} \in \text{supp}(f_{mp})\}$$

$$S_m^I(f) := \{p \in \mathbf{Z} | \{1\} \notin \text{supp}(f_{mp}) \wedge \{0\} \notin \text{supp}(f_{mp})\}$$

For sufficiently small values of  $m$ , a function can intersect at most one endpoint. In that case, the sets  $S_m^{LB}(f)$ ,  $S_m^{RB}(f)$  and  $S_m^I(f)$  are disjoint and the associated basis functions can be computed separately. Furthermore, due to reasons of symmetry, the basis functions of  $S_m^{RB}(f)$  can obtained from the corresponding functions of  $S_m^{LB}(f)$ .

More precisely, for the B-spline scaling functions we have to choose an  $m_0$  as

$$(19) \quad j < 2^{-m_0}$$

and for the wavelets

$$(20) \quad (2j - 1) < 2^{-m_0}$$

We obtain

$$\begin{aligned} S_m^{LB}(\phi^{[j]}) &= \{p \in \mathbf{Z} \mid -j + 1 \leq p \leq -1\} & \mathbf{V}_m^{LB} &:= \text{span}\{\phi_{mp}^{[j]} \mid p \in S_m^{LB}(\phi)\} \\ S_m^{LB}(\psi^{[j]}) &= \{p \in \mathbf{Z} \mid 2 - 2j \leq p \leq -1\} & \mathbf{U}_m^{LB} &:= \text{span}\{\psi_{mp}^{[j]} \mid p \in S_m^{LB}(\psi)\} \\ (21) \quad S_m^I(\phi^{[j]}) &= \{p \in \mathbf{Z} \mid 0 \leq p \leq 2^{-m} - j\} & \mathbf{V}_m^I &:= \text{span}\{\phi_{mp}^{[j]} \mid p \in S_m^I(\phi)\} \\ S_m^I(\psi^{[j]}) &= \{p \in \mathbf{Z} \mid 0 \leq p \leq 2^{-m} - 2j + 1\} & \mathbf{U}_m^I &:= \text{span}\{\psi_{mp}^{[j]} \mid p \in S_m^I(\psi)\} \\ S_m^{RB}(\phi^{[j]}) &= \{p \in \mathbf{Z} \mid 2^{-m} - j + 1 \leq p \leq 2^{-m} - 1\} & \mathbf{V}_m^{RB} &:= \text{span}\{\phi_{mp}^{[j]} \mid p \in S_m^{RB}(\phi)\} \\ S_m^{RB}(\psi^{[j]}) &= \{p \in \mathbf{Z} \mid 2^{-m} - 2j + 2 \leq p \leq 2^{-m} - 1\} & \mathbf{U}_m^{RB} &:= \text{span}\{\psi_{mp}^{[j]} \mid p \in S_m^{RB}(\psi)\} \end{aligned}$$

To solve the boundary problems, basis functions corresponding to all six classes have to be constructed. Special boundary wavelets and scaling functions have to be introduced to deal with the interval  $[0, 1]$ . We observe, that the basis functions for the  $LB$  and  $RB$ -spaces have to be redefined. Whereas the functions, associated with the  $I$ -space stay unchanged. As a consequence of the symmetries it is sufficient to compute just the left boundary functions and the inner functions. The right boundary functions are generated by using symmetry and reindexing. Doing this computation preassuming  $m_0$  sufficiently small, by referring to (20), leads to all necessary functions that  $\text{span } \mathbf{V}_m$  and  $\mathbf{U}_m$  with  $m \leq m_0$ .

The space  $\mathbf{V}_m$  is generated by

$$\begin{aligned} (22) \quad \mathbf{V}_m &= \mathbf{V}_m^{LB} \oplus \mathbf{V}_m^I \oplus \mathbf{V}_m^{RB} \\ &= \mathbf{V}_{m+1} \oplus \mathbf{U}_{m+1} \end{aligned}$$

From (21) and (22) we have

$$\begin{aligned} (23) \quad \dim(\mathbf{V}_m) &= \dim(\mathbf{V}_m^{LB}) + \dim(\mathbf{V}_m^I) + \dim(\mathbf{V}_m^{RB}) \\ &= (j - 1) + (2^{-m} - j + 1) + (j - 1) \\ &= 2^{-m} + j - 1 \end{aligned}$$

Consequently

$$(24) \quad \dim(\mathbf{U}_m) = 2^{-m}$$

The construction scheme follows the solutions provided by [7] and [24]. Some of the resulting B-spline scaling functions and wavelets are illustrated in figure 6.

### 2.3.5 Meyer's approach

Another approach is to generate orthonormalised basis functions on the bounded interval for  $L(\mathbb{R}^2)$  by using the classical Gram–Schmidt procedure. This was done by Meyer [22], using Daubechies' scaling functions and wavelets. The restriction of functions define the spaces  $V_m^{[0,1]}$ . We set:

$$(25) \quad \mathbf{V}_m^{[0,1]} := \{f \in L^2[0, 1] : f(x) = h(x)|_{[0,1]}, h \in \mathbf{V}_m\}$$

The support of the Daubechies scaling functions is given as

$$(26) \quad \text{supp}(\phi_{mp}) = [2^m p, 2^m(p + 2N - 1)]$$

Because of (26) it follows we have for the left boundary, inner and right boundary index sets:

$$\begin{aligned} (27) \quad \phi_{mp} &\in \mathbf{V}^{LB} \quad \forall p \in [-2N + 2, -1] := S_m^{LB} \\ \phi_{mp} &\in \mathbf{V}^I \quad \forall p \in [0, 2^{-m} - 2N + 1] := S_m^I \\ \phi_{mp} &\in \mathbf{V}^{RB} \quad \forall p \in [2^{-m} - 2N + 2, 2^{-m} - 2N + 1] := S_m^{RB} \end{aligned}$$

These sets are disjoint for all  $m$  with  $m \leq m_0$ . To obtain an o.n. basis of  $V_m^{[0,1]}$  the functions  $\phi_{mp}, p \in S_m^{LB}$  and also  $\phi_{mp}, p \in S_m^{RB}$  have to be orthonormalized among themselves. This can be done by a Gram–Schmidt procedure that produces new boundary functions, because of the disjoint supports and the o.n. properties of  $\phi_{mp}, p \in S_m^I$  an orthonormal basis for  $L^2[0, 1]$  is generated. For the construction of the corresponding wavelets  $N-1$  basis functions have to be generated at each endpoint. These functions are orthogonalized by the Gram Schmidt procedure. For the dimensions we have:

$$(28) \quad \begin{aligned} \dim \mathbf{V}_m^{[0,1]} &= 2^{-m} + 2N - 2 \\ \dim \mathbf{U}_m^{[0,1]} &= 2^{-m} \end{aligned}$$

That yields to an imbalace between these two spaces.

Another disadvantage of Meyers approach is the computation of the adapted filter coefficients near 0 and 1, involving the inversion of a badly conditioned matrix. These disadvantages were avoided by a different approach of Cohen and Daubechies [8].

For further details the reader is referred to the given references.

## 2.4 Interpolating basis functions

As illustrated in figure 1, the cardinal B-spline scaling functions do not have interpolation properties, e.g. we don't have  $\phi^{[i]}(k) = \delta_{k,1}$  for  $j > 2$ . In order to interpolate a given discrete data set  $\mathbf{c}$  we have to calculate a sequence  $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}$ , such that  $c_k = \sum_i \tilde{c}_i \phi^{[i]}_{0i}(k)$ . This can be done by solving the system  $\mathbf{M}\tilde{\mathbf{c}} = \mathbf{c}$ , where

$$(29) \quad \mathbf{M} := \begin{bmatrix} \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & 0 & \phi^{[i]}(1) & \phi^{[i]}(2) & \dots & \phi^{[i]}(j-1) & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \dots & 0 & \phi^{[i]}(1) & \phi^{[i]}(2) & \dots & \phi^{[i]}(j-1) & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \dots & \dots & 0 & \phi^{[i]}(1) & \phi^{[i]}(2) & \dots & \dots & \phi^{[i]}(j-1) & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & & & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

For the values of the cardinal B-spline functions we have:

$$(30) \quad \begin{aligned} \phi_0^{[2]}(k) &= \delta_{k,1} &, k \in \mathbf{Z} \\ \phi_0^{[i+1]}(k) &= \frac{k}{j} \phi_0^{[i]}(k) + \frac{j-k+1}{j} \phi_0^{[i]}(k-1) \end{aligned}$$

Thus for the cubic case and for torus-topology of the data set (case 2.3.3) we have:

$$(31) \quad \mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & & 0 & 1/6 & 2/3 & 1/6 \\ 1/6 & 0 & 0 & \dots & \vdots & 0 & 1/6 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/6 & 0 & \dots & \vdots & 0 & 1/6 \\ 1/6 & 2/3 & 1/6 & \dots & \vdots & 0 & & 0 \\ 0 & 1/6 & 2/3 & \dots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ \vdots & 0 & 1/6 & \dots & \dots & & \vdots \\ \vdots & 0 & \dots & \dots & 0 & & & \\ \vdots & \dots & \dots & \dots & 1/6 & 0 & & \\ \vdots & \dots & \dots & \dots & 2/3 & 1/6 & 0 & \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & \dots & 1/6 & 2/3 & 1/6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Note, that the upper matrix multiplication  $\mathbf{M}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}$  corresponds to a B-spline filter operation on an initial sequence  $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}$ . In order to compute these initial values from  $\mathbf{c}$ , we have to find  $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ , whose columns represent the coefficients of an inverse filter. Solving the interpolation problem can be

considered as an inverse filtering procedure. Figure 7 illustrated both approximating (solid line) and interpolating spline (dotted line) for a given set of samples.

## 2.5 Global and local thresholding

The data compression properties coming along with wavelet theory enable to develop tools to save transmission time, cpu-time and storage. This compression method is linear in time and data-independent. The localisation properties of the wavelet bases ensure that neglecting a particular basis function only influences the area of support in the time/frequency plane. As the order of the B-spline tends to infinity, the B-spline wavelets converge to modulated Gaussian functions. Thus the time/frequency localization for B-spline basis functions and their corresponding wavelets approach to the lower limit on the product of variances (these can be seen as uncertainties) in the time and frequency domain, which has been defined by Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle to  $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ . [20] proved that for a cubic B-spline the variance product is about 5% of the limit that is given by Heisenberg.

The lossy compression is structured as follows: First, the incoming discrete data has to be decomposed. The following filtering step can be applied globally or locally, weighted by a user-defined Gaussian function as it was introduced in [11]. All coefficients that are less than a defined value are neglected. Finally the filtered data can be compressed and stored or transmitted. The scheme is illustrated in figure 8.

The direct rendering method we propose in this section does not require inverse WTs since they are computed by the low albedo equation.

### 3 Ray Tracing in Wavelet Space

#### 3.1 The low-albedo Rendering Equation

The objective of this section is to link wavelet theory and volume rendering. The well known equation for an intensity evaluation for a view ray traversing an volumetric dataset up to an parameter  $t_i$  is called the low-albedo volume rendering integral. It is given as follows:

$$(32) \quad I(t_0, t_i) = \int_{t_0}^{t_i} q(t) \alpha(t) e^{-\int_{s=t_0}^t \alpha(s) ds} dt$$

This term is based on an approximation (multiple and higher order reflections are neglected) of an adapted transport theory model [13] that describes the process of fictitious light particles moving through the data set.  $\alpha(s)$  describes the attenuation of the ray through the volume. The function  $q(t)$  represents the volume source term, that represents the emission of energy in terms of intensity. Both functions are parameterized versions of  $\alpha^3(x, y, z)$  and  $q^3(x, y, z)$  along the viewing ray. They can be defined for RGB-components independently. Usually  $q(t)$  refers to color and shading terms, whereas  $\alpha(s)$  is mapped directly to material densities.

Thus the intensity, associated with a pixel on the projection plane, is computed by choosing  $t_0$  as the parameter of the first intersection of the ray with the volume function and  $t_L$  ( $L \in \mathbb{N}$ ) as the parameter of the last intersection and the calculation of the value  $I(t_0, t_L)$ . For computational reasons we rewrite (32) as:

$$\begin{aligned}
(33) \quad I(t_0, t_i) &= \sum_{l=0}^{i-1} \int_{t_l}^{t_{l+1}} q(t) \alpha(t) e^{-\int_{t_0}^t \alpha(s) ds} dt \\
&= I(t_0, t_{i-1}) + \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i} q(t) \alpha(t) e^{-\int_{t_0}^t \alpha(s) ds} dt \\
&= I(t_0, t_{i-1}) + \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i} q(t) \alpha(t) e^{-A(t_0, t)} dt \\
&= I(t_0, t_{i-1}) + e^{-A(t_0, t_{i-1})} \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i} q(t) \alpha(t) e^{-A(t_{i-1}, t)} dt
\end{aligned}$$

with

$$(34) \quad A(t_k, t_l) := \int_{t_k}^{t_l} \alpha(s) ds$$

Equations (32) and (33) can not be solved analytically in the general case. Different numerical quadrature methods must be applied, depending on both precision and performance requested by the user and of the type of functions for  $\alpha(s)$  and  $q(t)$ . In [23] expressions for the error bounds for several techniques are given precisely.

### 3.2 Projections into wavelet space

To take advantage of the multiresolution analysis, the functions  $\alpha(s)$  and  $q(t)$  are projected into wavelet space. Based on sampled attenuation values  $\alpha_{sampled}^3(x_p, y_q, z_r)$  and the color-values  $q_{sampled}^{3,RGB}(x_p, y_q, z_r)$  ( $p, q, r \in \mathbf{Z}$ ) that result may from a classification process, we set:

$$\begin{aligned}
(35) \quad \alpha^3(x, y, z) &:= \sum_{p,q,r \in \mathbf{Z}} \alpha_{sampled}^3(x_p, y_q, z_r) \phi_{m_0 p q r}^{3,[l]}(x, y, z) \quad x, y, z \in \mathbb{R} \\
q_{RGB}^3(x, y, z) &:= \sum_{p,q,r \in \mathbf{Z}} q_{sampled}^{3,RGB}(x_p, y_q, z_r) \phi_{m_0 p q r}^{3,[l]}(x, y, z)
\end{aligned}$$

Where  $\phi_{m_0 p q r}^{3,[l]}(x, y, z)$  represents the 3D B-spline scaling function of order  $j$ . Applying wavelet decomposition to (35) yields:

$$\begin{aligned}
(36) \quad \alpha^3(x, y, z) &= \sum_{m=m_0+1}^M \sum_{type=1}^7 \sum_{p,q,r \in \mathbf{Z}} d_{mpqr}^\alpha \psi_{mpqr}^{3,type,[l]}(x, y, z) \\
&\quad + \sum_{p,q,r \in \mathbf{Z}} c_{Mpqr}^\alpha \phi_{Mpqr}^{3,[l]}(x, y, z)
\end{aligned}$$

$$(37) \quad q_{RGB}^3(x, y, z) = \sum_{m=m_0+1}^M \sum_{\text{type}=1}^7 \sum_{p,q,r \in \mathbf{Z}} d_{mpqr}^{q_{RGB}} \psi_{mpqr}^{3,\text{type},[i]}(x, y, z) \\ + \sum_{p,q,r \in \mathbf{Z}} c_{Mpqr}^{q_{RGB}} \phi_{Mpqr}^{3,[i]}(x, y, z)$$

for the corresponding volume source terms.

Figure 9 illustrates the process of direct volume rendering in wavelet space. Both RGB and  $\alpha$  values are decomposed separately to receive  $d_{mpqr}^\alpha$ ,  $c_{Mpqr}^\alpha$ ,  $d_{mpqr}^{q_{RGB}}$  and  $c_{Mpqr}^{q_{RGB}}$ . The filtering process represents a user controlled lossy data compression described in section 2.5. The reconstruction and integration step composes the attenuation and volume source term. In the rightmost part of the pipeline computes the integral  $A(t_0, t)$  using (36) and (34), whereas the color source term can be computed by (37). Obviously it is not necessary to perform an explicit reconstruction step before the integrals are computed. In order to speed up the intensity calculation expression (33) is used.

### 3.3 Estimation of error bounds

The numeric techniques for solving the volume rendering integral are based on the Newton–Cotes quadrature formulae. Therefore we assume  $N+1$  evenly spaced parameters between  $t_{i-1}$  and

 $t_i: t_i^0, \dots, t_i^N$  with  $t_i^j := t_{i-1} + j \cdot \Delta t$  and  $\Delta t := \frac{t_i - t_{i-1}}{N}$ 



Figure 10: 2 D projection of the raytracing process ( $N=4$ )

An upper bound for the received error can be given as follows:

Trapezoid Rule:

From (33) we have:

$$(38) \quad \begin{aligned} I(t_0, t_i) &= I(t_0, t_{i-1}) \\ &+ \left( q(t_i) \alpha(t_i) e^{-A(t_0, t_i)} + q(t_{i-1}) \alpha(t_{i-1}) e^{-A(t_0, t_{i-1})} \right) \cdot \frac{(t_i - t_{i-1})}{2} \\ &+ R_I(t_0, t_i) \end{aligned}$$

By subsampling of the interval  $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$  we obtain:

$$(39) \quad \begin{aligned} I(t_0, t_i) &= I(t_0, t_{i-1}) \\ &+ e^{-A(t_0, t_{i-1})} \left[ q(t_i^0) \alpha(t_i^0) + 2 \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} q(t_i^j) \alpha(t_i^j) e^{-A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j)} + q(t_i^N) \alpha(t_i^N) e^{-A(t_{i-1}, t_i^N)} \right] \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{2} \\ &+ R_I(t_0, t_{i-1}, t_i) \end{aligned}$$

The remainder  $R_I(t_0, t_i)$  is given by:

$$(40) \quad R_I(t_0, t_{i-1}, t_i) = \frac{(\Delta t)^2}{12} e^{-A(t_0, t_{i-1})} (q(\xi) \alpha(\xi) e^{-A(t_{i-1}, \xi)})^{(2)} \quad \xi \in (t_{i-1}, t_i)$$

where (2) denotes the second derivative. In general can write the  $n^{\text{th}}$  derivative of a product of functions  $fgh$  as

$$(41) \quad \frac{d^n}{dx} f g h = \sum_{k=0}^n \sum_{i=0}^k \binom{n}{k} \binom{k}{i} f^{(i)} g^{(k-i)} h^{(n-k)}$$

$$\begin{aligned}
(42) \quad A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j) &= \tilde{A}(t_{i-1}, t_i^j) + R_A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j) \\
&= \left[ \alpha(t_{i-1}) + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \alpha(t_i^k) + \alpha(t_i^j) \right] \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{2} + R_A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j)
\end{aligned}$$

$$A(t_0, t_i^j) = A(t_0, t_{i-1}) + A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j)$$

and

$$(43) \quad R_A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j) = (\Delta t)^2 \frac{t_i^j - t_{i-1}}{12} \alpha^{(2)}(\xi) \quad \xi \in (t_{i-1}, t_i^j)$$

Because of the definition given in (35) and the well defined derivatives of B-spline functions, the calculation of the second derivative of  $\alpha$  yields an upper bound for the remainder  $R_A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j)$ .

Substituting this bound into (40) and using (41) gives an upper bound for the approximation error.

To obtain the approximation error of  $\tilde{A}(t_0, t_{i-1})$  we can write:

$$(44) \quad R_A(t_0, t_{i-1}) = \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} R_A(t_{k-1}, t_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} (\Delta t)^2 \frac{t_k - t_{k-1}}{12} \alpha_k^{(2)}(\xi_k) \quad \xi_k \in (t_{k-1}, t_k)$$

where  $(\alpha_k)_{k \in \mathbf{Z}}$  is a set of ordered opacity-functions along the ray.

### Simpson's Rule:

This rule approximates the function by a quadratic spline. The closed form of the integral can be written as:

$$\begin{aligned}
(45) \quad I(t_0, t_i) &= I(t_0, t_{i-1}) \\
&+ \frac{\Delta t}{3} e^{-A(t_0, t_{i-1})} \left[ P(t_{i-1}) + 4 \sum_{k=1}^{N/2} P(t_i^{2k-1}) + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{N/2} P(t_i^{2k}) + P(t_i) \right] \\
&+ R_I(t_0, t_{i-1}, t_i)
\end{aligned}$$

$$\text{with } P(t) := q(t) \alpha(t) e^{-A(t_{i-1}, t)}$$

We also have to examine the integral of the opacity along the ray. We have:

$$\begin{aligned}
(46) \quad A(t_{i-1}, t_j^i) &= \tilde{A}(t_{i-1}, t_j^i) + R_A(t_{i-1}, t_j^i) \\
&= \left[ \alpha(t_{i-1}) + 4 \sum_{k=1}^{j/2} \alpha(t_{2k-1}^i) + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{j/2-1} \alpha(t_{2k}^i) + \alpha(t_j^i) \right] \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{3} + R_A(t_{i-1}, t_j^i)
\end{aligned}$$

$$A(t_0, t_j^i) = A(t_0, t_{i-1}) + A(t_{i-1}, t_j^i)$$

As before we can use the iterative scheme (33) to give an approximation term for the error bounds. The remainder  $R_A$  is defined by:

$$(47) \quad R_A(t_{i-1}, t_i^j) = (\Delta t)^4 \frac{t_i^j - t_{i-1}}{180} \alpha^{(4)}(\xi) \quad \xi \in (t_{i-1}, t_i^j)$$

Thus we receive for the difference between analytic integration  $A(t_0, t_{i-1})$  and its approximation  $\tilde{A}(t_0, t_{i-1})$ :

$$(48) \quad R_A(t_0, t_{i-1}) = \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} (\Delta t)^4 \frac{(t_k - t_{k-1})}{180} \alpha_k^{(4)}(\xi_k) \quad \xi_k \in (t_{k-1}, t_k)$$

where  $(\alpha_k)_{k \in \mathbf{Z}}$  is again a set of ordered functions along the ray.

Finally, this expression can be used to obtain a general expression for the difference in intensity between the analytic integration and its approximation.

$$(49) \quad \begin{aligned} R_I(t_0, t_{i-1}, t_i) &= e^{-A(t_0, t_{i-1})} (\Delta t)^4 \frac{t_i - t_{i-1}}{180} (q(\xi) \alpha(\xi) e^{-A(t_{i-1}, \xi)})^{(4)} \quad \xi \in (t_{i-1}, t_i) \\ &= e^{-\tilde{A}(t_0, t_{i-1}) - R_A(t_0, t_{i-1})} (\Delta t)^4 \frac{t_i - t_{i-1}}{180} (q(\xi) \alpha(\xi) e^{-\tilde{A}(t_{i-1}, \xi) - R(t_{i-1}, \xi)})^{(4)} \end{aligned}$$

As before we can apply (41) to calculate the 4.th derivative to approximate the remainder term.

### 3.4 Illumination and Gradient Computation

Another advantage of our method is the exact calculation of the normal, that is needed for shading. We identify the normalised opacity gradient as the normal, such that

$$(50) \quad N(t) := \frac{\nabla \alpha(t)}{|\nabla \alpha(t)|}$$

The partial derivatives of  $\alpha$  can be computed analytically since it was defined as a linear combination of B-splines. Following (36), the different partial derivatives for a given wavelet decomposition can be expressed as:

$$(51) \quad \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \alpha^3(x(t), y(t), z(t))}{\partial x} &= \frac{\partial \sum_{p,q,r} c_{Mpqr}^\alpha \phi_{Mpqr}^{3,[j]}(x(t), y(t), z(t))}{\partial x} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial \sum_{m=m_0 \text{ type}=1}^M \sum_{\text{type}=1}^7 \sum_{p,q,r} d_{mpqr}^{k,\alpha} \psi_{mpqr}^{3,\text{type},[j]}(x(t), y(t), z(t))}{\partial x} \\ &= \sum_{p,q,r} c_{Mpqr}^\alpha \frac{d\phi_{Mp}^{[j]}(x(t))}{dx} \cdot \phi_{Mq}^{[j]}(y(t)) \cdot \phi_{Mr}^{[j]}(z(t)) \\ &\quad + \sum_{m=m_0 \text{ type}=1}^M \sum_{\text{type}=1}^7 \sum_{p,q,r} d_{mpqr}^{\text{type},\alpha} \frac{df_{mp}^{[j]}(x(t))}{dx} \cdot f_{mq}^{[j]}(y(t)) \cdot f_{mr}^{[j]}(z(t)) \end{aligned}$$

where  $f \in \{\phi, \psi\}$ 

## 4 Implementation

### 4.1 Computation of relevant Wavelet Bases

This section sheds light on the computation of relevant basis functions depending on a given parameter  $t$  on the ray. Relevant in this context comprises all functions that do not vanish at the position  $(x(t), y(t), z(t))$ , e.g.

$$(52) \quad \text{rel}(f, t) := \left\{ (m, p, q, r) \in \mathbf{Z}^4 \mid (x(t), y(t), z(t)) \in \text{supp}(f_{mpqr}^3) \right\}$$

A fast and accurate detection of these parameters is very important to restrict the summations in (36),(37) and (51) to those functions that have support on a given point. For the 1D case we define:

$$(53) \quad \begin{aligned} \text{supp}(\phi) &:= [l_\phi, r_\phi] \\ \text{supp}(\psi) &:= [l_\psi, r_\psi] \end{aligned}$$

The general support for different dilation parameters  $m$  and translation parameters  $p$  takes the following form:

$$(54) \quad \text{supp}(f_{mp}) = \left[ (l_f + p)2^m, (r_f + p)2^m \right] \quad f \in \{\phi, \psi\}, m, n \in \mathbf{Z}$$

Thus for each  $m$  and a given position  $(x, y, z)^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$  the corresponding  $p, q$  and  $r$  must satisfy:

$$(55) \quad \begin{aligned} [x/2^m - r_f] &\leq p \leq [x/2^m - l_f] \\ [y/2^m - r_f] &\leq q \leq [y/2^m - l_f] \\ [z/2^m - r_f] &\leq r \leq [z/2^m - l_f] \end{aligned}$$

### 4.2 Data Structures

An important part of our rendering process is data handling. In order to accelerate computation we use sorted lists for the management of the basis functions. A special data structure is built to save memory space and time. Each of the list elements are sorted in order to the parameter  $t_L$ , the value of  $t$  where the ray leaves the support region of the 3D basis function. A simple mechanism can be applied to compute this parameter and to avoid expensive calculations.

For each wavelet–type and dilation step we use the fact that the supported regions do not differ in their sizes and all boundaries are parallel to the principal axes. Hence, the ray has to be intersected with only three edges of the prototype function  $f_{m000}$  in  $x, y, z$ –direction.

Let a ray be defined by a position  $(ray_{initial,x}, ray_{initial,y}, ray_{initial,z})$  and a direction  $(ray_{direction,x}, ray_{direction,y}, ray_{direction,z})$ , we can compute the parameters of the intersection of the ray with the three ‘leaving edges’ of the supported region as:

$$(56) \quad t_{leaving,i} = \begin{cases} \frac{b_i - ray_{initial,i}}{ray_{direction,i}} & \text{if } ray_{direction,i} > 0 \\ \infty & \text{if } ray_{direction,i} = 0 \\ \frac{a_i - ray_{initial,i}}{ray_{direction,i}} & \text{if } ray_{direction,i} < 0 \end{cases} \quad i \in \{x, y, z\}$$

$$dt_i := \begin{cases} \frac{b_i - a_i}{ray_{direction,i}} & \text{if } ray_{direction,i} \neq 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } ray_{direction,i} = 0 \end{cases}$$

For each parameter  $p, q$  and  $r$  that was detected by (55) the 3 intersection parameters are computed as:

$$(57) \quad \begin{aligned} t_x &:= t_{leaving,x} + p \cdot dt_x \\ t_y &:= t_{leaving,y} + q \cdot dt_y \\ t_z &:= t_{leaving,z} + r \cdot dt_z \end{aligned}$$

The parameter  $t_L$ , associated with a specific basis function  $f_{mpqr}$ , can be selected as:

$$(58) \quad t_L = \min(t_x, t_y, t_z)$$

The situation is shown in figure 11 for a 2D projection.

## 6 Conclusion

We have developed a new image order direct volume rendering algorithm, based on spline wavelet decomposition on the underlaying data. This approach significantly improves the rendering quality and image accuracy. It also reduces the required memory and allows the calculation of the error bounds. The implemented datastructure provides a fast data-access and supports mul-

tiresolution and hierarchical data structure. The users are offered a large degree of freedom, since they can modify compression, speed and accuracy of the rendering process.

This work provides a general framework for further research on multiresolution volume rendering. In the future, we intend to merge our object order algorithm [17] and the work, that is described in this paper to recieve a hybrid system. Furthermore, we also we are also exploring the possible degrees of parallism with minimizing of communication overhead.
