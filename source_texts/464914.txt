Abstract
Performing video restoration in embedded systems is a challenge. Indeed, state-of-theart learning-based methods in video restoration are specifically trained for given tasks,
and often rely on huge networks, which makes them unsuitable when memory
and computing resources are limited. In this paper, we explore the use of deep Plugand-Play (PnP) algorithms for video restoration. We distinguish ourselves from prior PnP
work by directly writing PnP schemes on video sequences instead of separate images.
Our experiments in video deblurring, super-resolution (SR), and pixel interpolation
all show a clear benefit to using a network specifically designed for video denoising,
as it yields better restoration performance than a single image network with similar
denoising performance using the same PnP formulation, and more temporal stability
than other image-based PnP works. Experiments also highlight that, while not always
as effective as state-of-the-art single-task deep networks, our lightweight approach
remains competitive for many video restoration tasks.
Keywords: Video restoration, Bayesian imaging, Proximal methods, Plug-and-Play
(PnP), Denoising, Deblurring, Super-resolution (SR), Interpolation, Convolutional Neural
Network (CNN), Computational photography
1 Introduction
Designing image and video restoration methods for embedded systems is a complicated
process. They typically have additional constraints and requirements when compared
to the literature at large, such as limited storage and computing resources, or desirable
reusability (i.e., using the same method for multiple tasks) and updatability. It is difficult
to design systems that yield noteworthy performance when compared to state-of-the-art
methods for a given task while following some of these principles.
In this paper, we present a potential candidate for video restoration for embedded systems in the Deep Plug-and-Play (PnP) framework. Most inverse problems in video restoration can be written as:

For a long time, Bayesian restoration in digital imaging or video has relied on explicit
priors expressing regularity assumptions on x either in the image space [1–4] or in
transformed spaces [5–7]. When R is known and convex, there are numerous efficient
numerical schemes to find the solutions of (3) [8]. In recent years, learning-based methods and end-to-end neural networks have outperformed traditional image and video
restoration methods [9–14]. Training these networks requires large amounts of data and
computing resources (which tend to increase year over year) and is often specific to a
given degradation model.
Plug-and-Play methods try to reconcile the Bayesian and learning-based worlds,
by combining a likelihood defined explicitly according to (1) with a prior R implicitly
defined by a denoising algorithm. This combination is done within an alternate optimization scheme that splits the data fidelity and regularization subproblems. While PnP
approaches have been thoroughly explored for image restoration problems, there have
been surprisingly few attempts to use them for video restoration, even though the potential applications in this field are numerous. As such, tackling video using a PnP approach
is the subject of this publication. Its main contributions are the following:
• We demonstrate the viability and versatility of the Deep Plug-and-Play framework
for video restoration, where a single video denoising network can be repurposed for
multiple video restoration tasks. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first
one to do so. The resulting method, presented in Sect. 3, has a reduced memory footprint: only the weights of the denoising network have to be stored for several use
cases, which makes the approach very interesting for embedded systems.
• Through several different restoration experiments presented in Sect. 4, our approach
is shown to compare favorably to single image PnP schemes in terms of image quality
and temporal stability, even for restoration problems affecting each frame separately
such as super-resolution or deblurring.
• Although such a comparison is not very prevalent in the Plug-and-Play literature, we
perform the inevitable comparison of our method to state-of-the-art learning-based
ones. Experiments highlight that, while not always as effective as networks specifically trained for each restoration problem, this lightweight approach remain competitive for diverse video restoration tasks.

2 Related works
PnP methods can be traced back to the seminal work by Venkatakrishnan et al. [15]
which uses Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) [16] optimization to
decouple the data and regularization terms. Multiple PnP formulations of optimization
algorithms have been proposed, such as ADMM [15, 17], Stochastic Gradient Descent
(SGD) [18], Proximal Gradient Descent (PGD) [19]. primal-dual methods [20, 21], Iterative Soft-Threshold Algorithm (ISTA) [22, 23], Fast-ISTA (FISTA) [24] or Half-Quadratic
Splitting (HQS) [25]. A large diversity of denoisers have been used for the regularization,
but most recent PnP methods generally use deep neural networks [17, 18, 20, 26, 27].
In [25], an analysis of the efficiency of the different deep denoisers for different image
restoration tasks is provided. Deep plug-and-play methods can be used to solve different
kinds of image restoration tasks, such as super-resolution [28], Gaussian denoising [29],
or image deblurring [30], )and some works have extended the PnP paradigm to Poisson
inverse problems [31, 32]. Theoretical aspects of deep plug-and-play algorithms have
also been studied using bounded denoisers [33], or denoisers with Lipschitz residual [17,
18], with symmetric Jacobian [34, 35], or equal to the gradient of some potential [19].
In a related direction, deep unfolding networks (DUNs) [36–38] translate the truncated
optimization schemes of PnP methods into end-to-end networks and yield excellent
results in fewer iterations, while avoiding manual hyperparameter tuning. However,
DUNs need a separate training for each restoration task, which makes them less suitable
for embedded systems.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first one describing the use of neural
networks in a PnP scheme for video restoration. In [39], the video denoiser FastDVDnet [12] is used in a PnP scheme, but to solve a specific problem of snapshot compressive
imaging where the observation is a single frame. A PnP approach is developed in [40] for
video super-resolution, but employs the patch-based single-image denoiser BM3D [41].

Now, consider that we know how to build a denoiser Dε which can be expressed as the
MAP estimator for a denoising problem (for an i.i.d. Gaussian noise of variance ε) with

3.2 Case of video sequences
In the case of inverse problems on digital videos, we propose to use the previous scheme
directly on a video x for K iterations, i.e., the proximal and denoising steps in the PnPADMM scheme are applied to the whole sequence at each step. This allows considering cases where the operator A cannot be written in a separable way over all images
of the sequence (e.g. temporal blur). This also allows the use of networks specifically
designed for video denoising, such as FastDVDnet [12]. Note that if one uses a singleframe denoiser (applied separately to each video frame), and if the degradation operator
A is separable (i.e., can be written as a degradation on each frame of x separately), iterating PnP-ADMM on a whole video is equivalent to successively running PnP-ADMM on
each frame. This is obviously no longer the case with video-specific denoisers that are
applied to the video in a non-separable way.
Several experiments conducted in the next section compare the performance of
PnP-ADMM using either DRUNet, a single-image denoising network proposed for
integration within PnP-HQS in [25], or FastDVDnet [12], a network designed for
video denoising that leverages a 5-frame sliding window to produce temporally stable
denoised videos with no explicit image alignment. We use these networks off-the-shelf
with training weights provided by their original authors. Before studying the performance of these networks in PnP restoration, we evaluate their video Gaussian denoising
performance as a potential indicator of maximum achievable performance in video PnP
restoration. Also included to our comparison is GS-DRUNet, a single-image denoiser

4 Plug‑and‑play restoration experiments
We are now interested in the performance of our PnP-ADMM method using the networks of Sect. 3.2 for three video restoration problems: non-blind deblurring, super-resolution and interpolation of random missing pixels. We evaluate said performance using
typical reference-based quality metrics [45] such as PSNR, SSIM [46] and LPIPS [47].
These results aim at presenting the viability of our lightweight and multi-task method,
and at evaluating the impact of the chosen denoiser on the final result.
4.1 Optimal setting of PnP parameters
For a given video restoration problem, our formulation of PnP-ADMM has several
parameters that can have an impact on final performance:
• Dε, the Gaussian denoiser used as the proximal operator of the prior term.
• ε, which represents the strength of said denoiser. When the denoiser is designed to
handle varying levels of noise in a non-blind fashion (as is the case of FastDVDnet
and DRUNet), ε can be set in the form of a noise map that is fed as an additional
input to the denoiser.
• α, which represents the tradeoff between the data fidelity and prior terms of Equation (3).
• K, the total number of ADMM iterations. It needs to be large enough to allow convergence of the algorithm, but not too large in cases where convergence is not guaranteed.

4.2.1 Deblurring results
Quantitative results of our PnP-ADMM method for video deblurring on DAVIS-2017test-480p [43] are presented in Table 2. The table also includes comparison to DPIR [25]
and GSPnP [19], two state-of-the-art PnP image restoration methods. DPIR uses DRUNet in PnP-HQS with an annealing strategy on the noise level of their denoiser across
iterations. GSPnP uses GS-DRUNet, the shallower version of DRUNet explicitly trained
as the gradient step in the PGD algorithm. For both methods, we perform PnP restoration of each video frame sequentially, using the parameter values recommended by
their respective authors for deblurring: (σ1 , σK , , K ) = (49, σn × 255, 0.23, 8) using the

We turn off periodical geometric self-ensemble in DPIR as it could also be implemented
in our method, but only yields marginal improvements and is not necessarily relevant to
the comparisons of this article.
With our video PnP-ADMM method, using FastDVDnet instead of DRUNet yields
a higher PSNR/SSIM in almost all videos observed at both noise levels. The performance gap is smaller between PnP-ADMM using FastDVDnet and DPIR even though
it also uses DRUNet; this suggests that using identical denoisers but a different optimization algorithm and a different hyperparameters can lead to different PnP restoration
results. GSPnP also yields better performance than video PnP-ADMM with DRUNet,
with a very similar performance profile to DPIR. Figure 2 showcases a visual comparison of deblurring results. FastDVDnet restores more image detail whereas DPIR yields
smoother frames with somewhat less high frequency content. Videos in the supplementary show that our method with FastDVDnet yields the most temporally stable output.

4.5 Expansiveness of video denoising operators
We have seen with our experiments that the original FastDVDnet can produce satisfactory results and early convergence in video PnP restoration. While this publication
does not aim at providing new theoretical convergence guarantees of PnP methods, we
can still have a better grasp of the empirical stability of our method by looking at the
Monod et al. Journal on Image and Video Processing
(2026) 2026:1
Page 12 of 24
expansiveness of the denoiser being used. First convergence proofs of PnP algorithms
in the literature assumed strong properties on the denoiser, like boundedness or nonexpansiveness. In [17], a fixed-point convergence proof is provided for PnP-ADMM
using a deep CNN denoiser D, if the residual of said denoiser Rσ = Id −Dσ is Lipschitzcontinuous. In practice, the authors train a residual denoising network R to be 1-Lipschitz by performing spectral normalization of each convolution layer of the network,
through a power iteration at each training step, as done in [55]. The Lipschitzness of the
residual is only empirically verified by tracking the expansiveness of R between iterates
on a limited number of examples. Spectral norm as a proxy for the Lipschitz constant
is also found in [56], where convergence guarantees arise if there exists a 1-Lipschitz
operator Q = 2D − Id. In practice, the non-expansiveness of Q is obtained through a
soft constraint during the training of the denoiser D, where it is fine-tuned with a loss
where the reconstruction term is augmented a penalty term enforcing the Jacobian
J(2Dσ −Id) to have spectral norm < 1. The spectral norm is again approximated using
power iterations [55].
In our method, we use of-the-shelf denoising networks simply trained for Gaussian
noise removal, and we have no guarantees that they or their residual are nonexpansive
or Lipschitz-continuous. Let us experimentally study the expansiveness of FastDVDnet
(with its pretrained weights provided by the authors of [12]) for video denoising, and
do the same for DRUNet. To do so, we first set ourselves to compute the spectral norm
of the Jacobian of the denoising operators using power iterations until convergence as
done in [42, 56]. We select 256 × 256 center crops of the first 30 frames of videos of the
DAVIS-2017-test-480p [43] as our evaluation dataset. We also evaluate the expansiveness of the residual of both denoisers at noise levels σn ∈ {10, 25, 50, 100}/255.
As can be seen in Table 7, neither vanilla FastDVDnet nor DRUNet (as discussed
in [19, 42]) are 1-Lipschitz, and neither are their residuals. This means that we cannot
directly build upon the theoretical convergence guarantees of [17] or [56] for PnP video
restoration when using these operators. FastDVDnet consistently has a lower spectral
norm than DRUNet however.

using two different kernels at two noise levels on 4 video sequences, using our method
and DPIR for 2000 iterations on purpose, in order to assess their long-term behavior. We

5 Conclusion
In this work, we have explored the use of Deep PnP algorithms for lightweight and
multi-task video restoration. Our experiments show that using a light and competitive video denoiser (FastDVDnet) as a regularizer outperforms using a state-of-the-art
image denoiser (DRUNet) in PnP video schemes in the vast majority of problems tested
(deblurring, super-resolution, pixel interpolation), both in terms of PSNR and temporal consistency. The performance delta is even larger when the degradation changes
from one frame to another, as in our deblurring and pixel interpolation problems. This
difference is quite remarkable, as FastDVDnet has 13× less parameters than DRUNet.
Promising results for video pixel interpolation paves the way for other video restoration
problems where the degradation operator cannot be separated frame by frame, such as
frame interpolation or spatio-temporal deconvolution. While not as performant as single-task state-of-the-art deep networks, our method handles multiple tasks using one
set of weights of a small denoising network, making it appealing for an on-device implementation in embedded systems, which we intend to experiment with in future work.

Plug-and-play methods are typically not real-time methods, because they imply multiple iterations of an alternate optimization algorithm, and each iteration itself includes
a forward pass through a denoising algorithm. Our method is no exception. That said,
our method is typically faster than DPIR when performing video restoration with similar
initialization, since the number of iterations required by DPIR for a single frame must
be multiplied by the number of frames of the video. In contrast, one iteration of our

We do not feature the runtimes of GSPnP here for two reasons: the first one is that the
method was designed with theoretical convergence guarantees in mind and typically
requires a greater number of iterations than our method and DPIR; the second one is
that a fair runtime comparison would require intensive code refactoring of GsPnP (we
managed to rewrite the code of DPIR to perform one iteration of PnP-HQS on a video
by computing the data fidelity step sequentially for each frame, then the regularization
step by denoising each video frame sequentially; however we could not reproduce this
with GSPnP, and thus have to run the whole GSPnP algorithm separately for each video
frame, which greatly increases runtime).
