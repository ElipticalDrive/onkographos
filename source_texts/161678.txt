## ABSTRACT

In recent years, neural radiance fields have exhibited impressive performance in novel view synthesis. However, exploiting complex network structures to achieve generalizable NeRF usually results in inefficient rendering. Existing methods for accelerating rendering directly employ simpler inference networks or fewer sampling points, leading to unsatisfactory synthesis quality. To address the challenge of balancing rendering speed and quality in generalizable NeRF, we propose a novel framework, NeRF-SDP, which achieves both efficiency and high fidelity by introducing scene depth perception. We incorporate more scene information into the

∗Corresponding Author



radiance field by using our proposed geometry feature extraction and depth-encoded ray transformer to improve the model's inference capabilities with sparse points. With the aid of scene depth perception, NeRF-SDP can better understand the scene's structure, thus better reconstructing the objects' edges with significantly fewer artifacts. Experimental results demonstrate that NeRF-SDP achieves comparable synthesis quality to state-of-the-art methods while significantly improving rendering efficiency. Furthermore, ablation studies confirm that the depth-encoded ray transformer enhances the model's robustness to varying numbers of sampling points.

### 1 INTRODUCTION

Novel view synthesis refers to the process of synthesizing images under any novel viewpoint from a limited number of images captured around the scene. It is an important task in the field of computer vision and computer graphics due to its crucial role in emerging immersive media such as Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality, and has attracted increasing interest from researchers and industry practitioners. In recent years, methods based on Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) [22] have achieved remarkable performances by utilizing multi-layer perceptrons (MLP) to implicitly fit the 3D scene. This kind of implicit representation can obtain photo-realistic results with a simple and differentiable pipeline. Although NeRF's performance is impressive, it is limited to supporting a single scene and lacks generalization ability, which brings significant challenges to its practical applications in production and daily life.

Based on this issue, existing generalizable NeRF algorithms extract features from reference images of the scene and utilize MLPs to decode them, enabling the models to perform novel view synthesis on unseen scenes [5, 9, 15, 20, 32, 35, 42]. Due to the different structures, textures and scales among various scenes, many methods utilize relatively complex designs to achieve better synthesis results but lead to low efficiency during the inference stage [15, 35]. In contrast, some methods have accelerated the rendering process of generalizable NeRF by using simpler networks and trilinear interpolation [5], or by directly reducing the number of sampling points [19]. But these methods can result in severe artifacts, leading to poor image quality. Neither of these approaches has achieved a good balance between efficiency and image synthesis quality.

We propose a new generalizable NeRF, namely NeRF-SDP, by introducing geometry feature extraction and depth-encoded ray transformer. NeRF-SDP can significantly accelerate the rendering process by reducing sampling points while maintaining comparable rendering quality to other state-of-the-art methods. In the next paragraphs, we will elaborate on our proposed pipeline to address the shortcomings of existing methods.

First, existing algorithms rely solely on appearance information to infer query points, leading to ambiguous explanations of the scene [37]. To this end, we introduce a geometry feature extraction module for accurate reconstruction. Concretely, we extract additional relative depth maps of reference images via a pre-trained monocular depth estimation model and insert them into the feature extraction network to obtain geometry features. As far as we know, NeRF-SDP is the first to use the estimated depth during the feature extraction stage in generalizable methods.

Next, due to different structures and scales among various scenes, generalizable NeRFs usually infer query points without the positional information of them, while only relying on features from reference images. In order to make full use of the coarse structure of the scene calculated by multi-view stereo matching, we propose the depth-encoded ray transformer, namely DERT, for structure-aware reconstruction. To be specific, we encode relative distances between query points and the estimated depth into the ray transformer to help NeRF-SDP to infer the density of the points. Benefiting from

this, NeRF-SDP has a deeper understanding of the structure of scenes with stronger inference capabilities, and becomes robust to the varying number of sampling points, thus can adaptively adjust the rendering speed and quality by taking different number of sampling points on the rays. To our best knowledge, we are the first to combine depth with ray transformer and eliminate the sensitivity to varying number of sampling points introduced by the ray transformer in generalizable NeRFs.

Finally, by utilizing volume rendering, the target view can be synthesized based on the inferred density and color of each query point. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

(i) We propose a novel approach that utilizes monocular depth estimation for geometry feature extraction, effectively boosting the qualitative and quantitative performances of generalizable NeRF.

(ii) We propose a novel depth-encoded ray transformer, which not only improves the scene synthesis quality but also alleviates the sensitivity of transformer to the number of sampling points.

(iii) We construct an efficient generalizable neural radiance field, namely NeRF-SDP, which can significantly improve rendering efficiency while maintaining comparable rendering quality to other state-of-the-art methods.

### 2 RELATED WORKS

### 2.1 Generalizable NeRF

In recent years, the introduction of NeRF [22] has provided a new paradigm for the implicit representation of 3D scenes. To eliminate the limitation of NeRF in reconstructing single scene, many researchers have focused on improving the generalization capability of the model [5, 9, 15, 18–20, 32, 35, 39, 40, 42]. Generalizable NeRFs are mainly achieved by combining the ideas of other kinds of view synthesis methods with NeRF's scene representation. One widely used approach is to adopt the ideas of image-based rendering [2–4, 10, 14, 31], using features extracted from reference images instead of fixed 5D coordinates to make NeRF generalizable [9, 32, 35, 42]. In addition, researchers have combined the idea of continuous random sampling in NeRF with other scene representation methods [18]. Due to the differences in content among different scenes, using a simple MLP to process texture features of reference images is difficult to generalize to complex scenes. Therefore, more prior knowledge of the scene [5, 15, 20, 27, 39] and more complex network structures [15, 20, 35] have been taken into consideration to boost analysis and reasoning capabilities, leading to lower rendering efficiency. Some methods use simple networks [5] or extremely sparse sampling points [19] to accelerate rendering speed, but leading to unsatisfactory quality for complex scenes.

## 2.2 NeRF with depth priors

Depth priors have been introduced to aid the learning process of neural radiance fields, allowing NeRF to adapt to sparse view inputs [6, 7, 11, 34], or improve the training and inference speed [23, 37], or extend for reconstruction of dynamic scenes [1, 38]. In addition, many generalizable NeRFs have also integrated depth priors for novel view synthesis [15, 19, 24, 30, 39], where the extracted depth is utilized to guide the sampling process [19, 24, 30, 39], or termed as a criterion for judging the visibility of reference viewpoints [15, 24]. However, these methods ignore the fact that depth

maps contain valuable information about scene geometry and fail to fully leverage it. Additionally, the rough surfaces of a scene can provide useful clues for inferring the density of sampling points.

Considering these factors, we propose geometry feature extraction and the depth-enhanced ray transformer. This not only improves the quality of scene synthesis but also solves the problem of the model's hypersensitivity to sampling points. Concurrent work [8] also uses the idea of extracting geometry features based on monocular depth estimation for panoramic image reconstruction, indirectly demonstrating the effectiveness of this method.

### 3 METHOD

As shown in Figure 2, given a set of  $N$  reference views  $\{\mathbf{I}_i\} = \{\mathbf{I}_1, \dots, \mathbf{I}_N\}$  captured around the scene, we propose a generalizable NeRF algorithm called NeRF-SDP, which can synthesize images at any virtual viewpoint via: (i) Perform feature extraction on  $\{\mathbf{I}_i\}$  and the relative depth maps  $\{\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_i\}$  inferred by a monocular depth estimation network, obtaining texture feature maps  $\{\mathbf{F}^{tex}\}$  and geometry feature maps  $\{\mathbf{F}^{geo}\}$ . (ii) Construct a cost volume under the target view and infer the depth  $\mathbf{D}$ , confidence interval  $\mathbf{S}$ , and a 3D feature volume  $\mathbf{F}^{cv}$  for ray sampling and inferences. (iii) Fuse the features, predict the volume density through the depth-encoded ray transformer, and use color blending to infer the color. Then the target view can be rendered by volume rendering.

#### 3.1 Feature Extraction

Given  $N$  reference images  $\{\mathbf{I}_i\}$ , in order to make NeRF model generalizable, most existing methods only extract the texture features  $\{\mathbf{F}^{tex}\}$  from color images using the 2D CNN network  $\mathcal{G}^{fe}$ :

$$\mathbf{F}_i^{tex} = \mathcal{G}^{fe}(\mathbf{I}_i) \quad i = 1, \dots, N \quad (1)$$

In fact, interpreting the scene solely based on color information may cause certain ambiguities [37]. It is more challenging for generalizable NeRF to cope with complex scene with few reference images, since the scene structure is judged only dependent on the texture features, leading to artifacts due to limited reference information. To this end, we propose the geometry feature extraction module. Concretely, we utilize pre-trained DPT [26], a monocular depth estimation model, denoted as  $\mathcal{H}^{mono}$  to estimate the relative depth map  $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_i$  of each reference image  $\mathbf{I}_i$  as geometry prior and input it to the shared feature extraction network  $\mathcal{G}^{fe}$  to calculate the geometry feature map  $\mathbf{F}_i^{geo}$ :

$$\mathbf{F}_i^{geo} = \mathcal{G}^{fe}(\mathcal{H}^{mono}(\mathbf{I}_i)) \quad i = 1, \dots, N \quad (2)$$

Thus, we can now concatenate the texture and geometry feature map of the  $i$ -th view to form the total feature map  $\mathbf{F}_i^{fe}$ , as prior information for the subsequent reconstruction.

#### 3.2 Multi-View Stereo Matching

To perceive the coarse scene structure under the target view and provide scene priors for the sampling process to skip the blank areas, inspired by works [5, 15, 19], we construct a cost volume based on multi-view stereo matching [29, 41] under the target view. Following [41], we set the depth resolution to be  $L$  layers and warp the volume to each reference view  $i$  to extract features and

obtain a feature volume  $\mathbf{V}_i^f$  using  $\mathbf{F}_i^{fe}$ . The cost volume  $\mathbf{V}^c$  can be constructed by the feature volumes  $\{\mathbf{V}_i^f\}$ :

$$\mathbf{V}^c = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{(\mathbf{V}_i^f - \tilde{\mathbf{V}}^f)^2}{N} \quad (3)$$

Here,  $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^f$  denotes the mean feature volume of  $N$  views, and the square is an element-wise operation. Then we use a 3D CNN network to convert it into a depth probability volume  $\mathbf{V}^p$  and a 3D feature volume  $\mathbf{F}^{cv}$ . The estimated depth  $\mathbf{D}$  and confidence interval  $\mathbf{S}$  can be calculated using the depth probability volume  $\mathbf{V}^p$ :

$$d = \sum_{j=1}^L p_j d_j \quad s = \sum_{j=1}^L p_j (d_j - d)^2 \quad (4)$$

Here,  $d$  and  $s$  represent the estimated depth and confidence interval on ray  $\mathbf{r}$ , respectively.  $p_j$  and  $d_j$  represent the depth probability in  $\mathbf{V}^p$  and the depth value at the  $j$ -th layer on ray  $\mathbf{r}$ , respectively.

#### 3.3 Depth-Encoded Ray Transformer

We introduce a novel depth-encoded ray transformer, denoted as DERT, to infer the volume density of each query point. First, we need to aggregate features from  $N$  views for each point. Following [25], a global-aware feature fusion strategy is adopted: For a given query point  $\mathbf{p}$ , we warp it into the feature maps  $\{\mathbf{F}_i^{fe}\}$  ( $i = 1, \dots, N$ ) and obtain  $N$  feature vectors  $\{\mathbf{f}_i^{fe}\}$ . Then the mean  $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{fe}$  and the variances  $\mathbf{f}_{var}^{fe}$  of these features are calculated as the global information across reference views and the differences between different views. After that, we use  $\text{MLP}^{fe}$  to infer the mixing weights  $\{w_i\}$  of the views and compress the features. The unique feature  $\mathbf{f}^{fe}$  corresponding to each query point can be obtained by fusing features from different views:

$$w_i, \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_i^{fe} = \text{MLP}^{fe}(\mathbf{f}_i^{fe} \oplus \tilde{\mathbf{f}}^{fe} \oplus \mathbf{f}_{var}^{fe}) \quad \mathbf{f}^{fe} = \sum_{i=1}^N \tilde{w}_i \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_i^{fe} \quad (5)$$

Here,  $\tilde{w}_i$  is the normalized mixing weights along the view dimension from  $w_i$ . We also linearly interpolate the 3D feature volume  $\mathbf{F}^{cv}$  and concatenate the 3D feature  $\mathbf{f}^{cv}$  with the unique 2D extracted feature  $\mathbf{f}^{fe}$  as the basis for inferring volume density.

Different from other methods that use the vanilla transformer architecture [20, 30, 33, 35], we introduce the distances between the query points and rough surface in the estimated depth into the encoding as prior position information. To account for varying scene scales, we utilize relative depths rather than absolute values.

Specifically, suppose  $M$  points  $\{\mathbf{p}_m\}$  ( $m = 1, \dots, M$ ) are sampled on the ray  $\mathbf{r}$ , with their depths from the target camera denoted as  $\{t_m\}$ . The estimated depth of the ray is  $d$ , and the depth of near plane and far plane of the scene is  $t_{near}$  and  $t_{far}$  as Figure 4, respectively. The length of the feature is  $K$ . Then, the position encoding  $\mathbf{E}_m$  of DERT for each point on the ray can be represented as:

$$\delta_m = \frac{t_m - d}{t_{far} - t_{near}} * u \quad \omega_k = \frac{1}{10000^{2i/K}} \quad (6)$$

$$\mathbf{E}_m = \begin{cases} \sin \omega_k \delta_m & \text{if } k = 2i \\ \cos \omega_k \delta_m & \text{if } k = 2i + 1 \end{cases} \quad (7)$$

Here,  $u$  is a hyperparameter used to control the range of  $\delta_m$ , and  $\omega_k$  represents the frequency coefficient on the  $k$ -th element. By element-wise addition of the relative depth encoding  $E_m$  and the feature of the point  $f_m$ , then using the self-attention mechanism and feed-forward network for inference, we can predict the volume density value  $\sigma_m$  for each query point.

### 3.4 Color Blending and Volume Rendering

Following [35], we utilize color blending to fuse original colors in different views to the query points. First, we concatenate the

compressed and perception-aware features  $\tilde{f}_i^{fe}$ , the mixing weight  $\tilde{w}_i$  and the view directions' similarity  $\cos \theta_i$ . Then the weight  $w_i^c$  of the  $i$ -th views can be obtained by  $MLP^{cb}$ , and the colors can be calculated in a weighted manner:

$$w_i^c = MLP^{cb}(\tilde{f}_i^{fe} \oplus \tilde{w}_i \oplus \cos \theta_i) \quad c = \sum_{i=1}^N \tilde{w}_i^c c_i \quad (8)$$

Here,  $\cos \theta_i = d_i^{ref} \cdot d^{tar}$  represents the dot product between the  $i$ -th reference view direction  $d_i^{ref}$  and the target view direction  $d^{tar}$ .  $\tilde{w}_i^c$  represents the normalized weight from  $w_i^c$ .  $c_i$  refers to the original color in the  $i$ -th reference view. After the two stages above, given a ray  $r$ , and  $M$  query points on the ray, we can obtain the volume density  $\{\sigma_j\}$  and the color  $\{c_j\}$  for each point. Then, the color of the ray  $C(r)$  can be calculated through the volume rendering formula [16, 22].

### 3.5 Sampling and Optimization

The estimated depth  $D$  and confidence interval  $S$  provide the coarse surface of the scene. Therefore, NeRF-SDP can accelerate the rendering speed by reducing the number of sampling points and eliminating the iterative process. Similar to [19], we only sample points around the estimated depth. To be specific, for a ray  $r$ , assuming the estimated depth is  $d$  and the confidence interval is  $s$ , we directly sample  $M$  points uniformly from the depth range  $[d-s, d+s]$ , denoting the depth of the query points as  $\{t_j\}$ :

$$t_j = d-s + \frac{2s}{M-1}(j-1) \quad (9)$$

With this sampling strategy, we can skip the coarse sampling process and reduce sampling in blank areas. Two additional losses are considered to constrain the estimated depth. As ground truth depth is difficult to obtain, the losses are optimized in a self-supervised manner. Concretely, we use the disparity smoothness loss  $\mathcal{L}_{smooth}$  based on the color image  $I^{GT}$  and the relative depth map  $\tilde{D}^{mono}$  predicted from monocular depth estimation under the target view:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{smooth} = & \frac{1}{HW} \sum_r \left[ \|\partial_x \hat{d}\| (e^{-\|\partial_x I(r)\|} + e^{-\|\partial_x \tilde{D}^{mono}(r)\|}) \right. \\ & \left. + \|\partial_y \hat{d}\| (e^{-\|\partial_y I(r)\|} + e^{-\|\partial_y \tilde{D}^{mono}(r)\|}) \right] \end{aligned} \quad (10)$$



Figure 5: Qualitative Comparison on LLFF dataset [21]: The subscripted numbers represent the number of points used for rendering. GRF, MVSNeRF, ENeRF and IBRNet all produce severe artifacts near the edges of objects. NeRF-SDP exhibits high synthesis quality, with fewer artifacts near the edges of objects, while maintaining the geometry contours of objects well.

Here,  $\hat{d}$  represents the disparity on ray  $\mathbf{r}$ , where  $H \times W$  is the resolution of the image. In addition, we also use  $\mathcal{L}_{std}$  to constrain the size of the confidence interval, encouraging the depth estimation network to produce more accurate results:

$$\mathcal{L}_{std} = \frac{1}{HW} \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \Delta s \quad \Delta s = \begin{cases} s - s^{max} & s > s^{max} \\ 0 & s \leq s^{max} \end{cases} \quad (11)$$

Here,  $s$  represents the confidence interval on ray  $\mathbf{r}$ , and  $s^{max}$  is a hyperparameter to constrain the size of the confidence interval. The main loss we use is the L2 loss between the rendered image and the ground truth image, denoted as  $\mathcal{L}_{render}$ . Finally, the total loss is composed of the three parts mentioned above:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{render} + \lambda_{smooth} \mathcal{L}_{smooth} + \lambda_{std} \mathcal{L}_{std} \quad (12)$$

## 4 EXPERIMENTS

### 4.1 Implementation Details

We inherit a pre-trained DPT [26] model for monocular depth estimation of the reference views. The other parts of NeRF-SDP are jointly trained with random initialization. Specifically, we utilize a UNet [28] with residual architecture [13] to extract 2D features, and use MVSNet [41] to process the cost volume. Notably, to eliminate checkerboard artifacts caused by the deconvolution layers, we replace them with combinations of upsampling and convolution layers. Three datasets: LLFF [21], IBRNet [35], and Google Scanned [12] are used to jointly train the model on a single Nvidia Geforce RTX 3090 for 250,000 steps, with a batch size of 500. We use Adam optimizer [17] and set the learning rate of Res-UNet and other parts as  $1 \times 10^{-3}$  and  $5 \times 10^{-4}$  severally. During the first 170,000 steps, our proposed auxiliary losses and rendering loss are jointly optimized, where  $\lambda_{smooth} = 0.5$ ,  $\lambda_{std} = 0.1$  and  $s^{max} = \frac{1}{6}(t_{far} - t_{near})$ . And for the later 80,000 steps, only the rendering loss is considered for optimization. We set  $L = 16$  in the cost volume and set  $u = 64$  in DERT. The dimensions of 2D and 3D features are set to 24 and 8 respectively.

The results presented in Table 2 have demonstrated that the proposed geometry feature extraction can significantly improve the synthesis quality of both NeRF-SDP and IBRNet. ### 5 CONCLUSION

We propose NeRF-SDP, an efficient and high-fidelity generalizable neural radiance field that enhances the model's inference capabilities by incorporating depth perception of the scene. By extracting geometry features from monocular estimated depth maps and combining depth information from multi-view stereo matching with ray transformers, we provide more scene information for the model, so as to deepen its understanding of the scene's structure with sparse sampling points. Experiment results demonstrate that NeRF-SDP achieves synthesis quality comparable to state-of-the-art methods and significantly outperforms them in rendering efficiency.
