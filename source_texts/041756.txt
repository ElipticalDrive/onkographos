Method for Visualizing Radiation Data using Unreal Enginea
M. Scott Greenwood* and Michael B. R. Smith
Oak Ridge National Laboratory, 1 Bethel Valley Road, Oak Ridge, TN 37831, *greenwoodms@ornl.gov
INTRODUCTION
The field of radiation transport modeling represents
many complex scenarios and many software exist for
performing the calculations and visualizing the results. A
premier radiation transport code used in the nuclear industry
is the SCALE nuclear software suite which has tools for
visualizing 2D projections of geometries, source terms, and
transport tallies.1 However, the 3D combination of transport
geometries and their corresponding cartesian mesh tallies is
not a standard feature in any radiation transport software suite
at this point. 3D results produced from transport simulations
can be visualized and analyzed with third-party tools such as
VisItb and ParaViewc however, a streamlined method for
combining transport geometries and 3D results is still
somewhat elusive for the industry. Furthermore, while these
third-party tools do provide movie making capabilities,d they
do not readily lend themselves to producing cinematicquality presentations for nonexpert audiences or for
interactive demonstrations such as that achieved with virtual
or augmented reality. Developing new and efficient
visualization toolsets for scientific data in nuclear and other
industries is imperative for staying current in the rapidly
evolving computational landscape of the twenty-first century.
Premier data visualization techniques continue to be
developed in such disciplines as geographic information
systems,2 medical imaging and surgical training,3 and
emergency response4 and the nuclear industry should not fall
behind in this effort. While previous work to visualize
radiation data has been undertaken by various research
groups5,6, this paper endeavors to establish a simple workflow
from a specific and popular transport code to a high-quality
visualization software to be made available for the nuclear
engineering community at large.
This paper describes efforts to develop a workflow to
produce high-quality, interactive visuals from data created by
radiation-transport tools like SCALE/MAVRIC1 and to link
them to the Unreal Engine® 3D video game development
software produced by Epic Games.7
Unreal Engine
Unreal Engine® is a game engine developed by Epic
Games with an emphasis on photo-realistic and interactive
3D environments. Since its debut in 1998, the engine has
continued to evolve, pushing the boundaries of visualization
and cinematic experiences of video games to the big screen
and across all platforms (e.g., personal computers, consoles,
and mobile). Perhaps the most renowned product created with
Unreal Engine to-date is the immensely popular video game
Fortnite. However, in addition to video games, the engine is
used throughout the movie industry in the creation of many
of the big-budget films (e.g., Disney’s Rogue One: A Star
Wars Story and The Mandalorian). More recently, Epic
Games has actively sought to expand its influence beyond its
existing user base—especially to non-entertainment
industries—through initiatives like the Unreal Academy
Enterprise conference/training and the Epic Games
MegaGrants program.e
Unreal Engine was selected for this work because of its
intuitive interface, technological maturity, and status as an
open-source softwaref for non-commercial/development
applications. The expectation is that efforts to couple this
complex and powerful software with scientific modeling and
simulation workflows will provide new opportunities to
generate valuable visualizations and demonstrations for the
radiation transport community. This work used Unreal
Engine 4.25 (UE4).
METHODOLOGY
The methodology developed here sought to minimize the
number of steps required to bring simulation data into the
UE4 environment. Furthermore, the authors sought to avoid
any proprietary software, unorthodox data formats, and other
post-processing constraints to avoid introducing more
opportunities for error and increasing workload and process
management.

WORKFLOW
Process to Import Data to UE4
It was determined that MAVRIC includes a utility for
converting native mesh tallies into VTK file formats which
successfully rendered-pointwise data, however this proved
too computationally expensive for rapid visualization and
other solutions were investigated. ParaView® served as the
intermediary and was used to split the VTK file into contour
files saved in the PLY file format. As a final step, the PLY
file formats were imported into Blender® and saved as
GLB/GLTF files.g UE4 has native support for GLB/GLTF
files and were therefore then imported as static mesh files into
UE4. This workflow is summarized in Fig. 2. The process to

Visualization to Production
Once data are imported into UE4 as static meshes and
the desired materials and method for using the meshes have
been selected for the application, it is time to produce the
visualization of interest. This portion of the workflow
consists of building a visual environment, or level, (e.g.,
importing CAD drawings and other assets) and positioning
the mesh at the desired location in the level. Fig. 6 shows the
imported MAVRIC radiation data with a user-selectable
color, number of contours, opacity, and emissivity. These
meshes are then placed in a level (i.e.: Martian landscape)
with an MSL Rover CAD geometry.

From this simple beginning, more complex features
tailored to the specific application can be added. Examples
may include labels, emissivity, colors, multiple meshes,
event-triggered behaviors, user interactions, and more.

Once the level is created, virtual cameras are placed into
the level and can be maneuvered on rails or by camera booms
if a cinematic shot is needed. If the intent is for the level to
be driven by the user like augmented reality, then characters
may be placed and controls or heads-up displays with
selectable filters and menus may be added. The value of each
of these items is completely dependent on the audience and
on the application and should be adjusted accordingly.
LESSONS LEARNED
Fig. 5. Example of a blueprint that enables a user to select
different mesh contours and visualization features.
The principal lesson learned during this process was
regarding the proper method to load large data sets into UE4.
The first attempt in bringing radiation data into UE4 was
approached creating an actor at the x, y, z location of each
data point with the data point value setting a visual parameter
such as opacity, color, emissivity, or a combination thereof.
However, it was quickly determined that UE4 was not meant
to handle so many actors at once (i.e., ~1,000,000 actors).
Attempting to do so prohibitively slowed UE4 renderings.
The second attempt was a variation of the first where a
similar method was applied but the dataset was scaled to a
smaller, but representative subset of points. This significantly
decreased the number of actors to be visualized (i.e.:
~100,000). The actors were then generated using a procedural
method in the Level Blueprint which provided a visualization
that no longer caused significant lags during play mode once
the initial execution of the level logic to visualize the scene
was completed. However, the generation of the level in the
procedural method was still too cumbersome, and the method
prevented any serious attempts to interact with the data.
A third method, which was explored but not enacted,
involved point cloud particle systems. The use of a particle
system is a powerful visualization capability built into UE4
that takes advantage of modern GPU capabilities. However,
while point clouds can generate the shapes of interest, the
individual points cannot be controlled or positioned.
Therefore, the density of points would have to be
significantly greater than the number of radiation data points
to capture the shape, and the individual behavior (e.g.,
material) of each particle would still not be able to be
controlled.
The method described in this paper represents an
effective technique for achieving efficient computational
performance and high-quality, cinematic visuals for radiation
transport data. It provided accurate representations of the
original radiation data (hundreds of thousands to millions of
data points) by relying on the capability to generate and use
complex static meshes with little computational overhead.
This method also allows significant liberties to be taken in
the way that the data is presented and combined with CAD
drawings and landscape, allowing users to render radiation
transport data in a new, useful, and exciting manner.
CONCLUSION
This paper summarizes the workflow developed to
visualize complex radiation data sets (or other similarly
structured datasets) analyzable in a software such as
ParaView®. The workflow consists of relying on standard
file formats and open-source tools to convert the radiation
data from SCALE/MAVRIC to a format importable by the
open-source UE4 game development software. Once in UE4,
the advanced visualization and interaction software enables
broad ranges of demonstrations and other cinematic works to
be generated. It is expected that this capability will enable a
broad range of interesting and impactful visualization efforts
for the nuclear industry and beyond.
