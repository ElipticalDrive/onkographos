Vanishing Volumes: Constraining Reconstructions of
Fine-Structured Objects from Coarse Segmentation
O. Camilleri1 , M. Volino1 and A. Hilton1
1 Centre for Vision, Speech and Signal Processing (CVSSP), University of Surrey, Guildford, Surrey, U.K.
Abstract
Recent advances at the interface of machine learning and 3D reconstruction, ranging from volumetric methods like NeRF and
Plenoxels to point-based primitives such as Gaussian Splats, have enabled high-fidelity 3D modeling from images. Despite this
progress, capturing assets through the isolated optimization of segmented objects remains fundamentally under-constrained.
Additionally, thin structures pose a difficult challenge for segmentation methods. In this work, we explore necessary constraints
and combine priors from monocular depth as well as visual hulls to overcome their respective failure modes, producing highquality object-centric reconstructions in the face of erroneous segmentation. This is demonstrated using synthetic scenes, all
exhibiting fine structure, which we openly release along with coarse and ground truth segmentation masks. Furthermore, we
show that segmentation failure can act as a useful signal to guide sampling and further enhance detail preservation.

1. Introduction
Modern 3D reconstruction research has produced a diverse set of
representations, spanning volumetric approaches such as neural
radiance fields (NeRFs) [MST∗ 20], faster, voxel-based radiance
fields like plenoxels [YFT∗ 21], and point-based techniques such as
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [KKLD23]. These methods are capable of modeling complex geometry and appearance from 2D observations, producing convincing novel view synthesis (NVS) and
ultimately help bridge the gap between traditionally captured media and computer graphics pipelines.
To facilitate editing, such representations may be decomposed into
discrete objects that can be manipulated and stored as modular assets. Objects are often captured using segmented training images,
drawing on the vast amount of research in the 2D domain. Despite
significant progress in 2D segmentation, current models still struggle to capture fine details like hair strands or intricate foliage, especially within complex, cluttered scenes. These conditions can result in missed regions as well as unwanted scene elements within
predicted masks. For high-fidelity applications, such errors can
be detrimental. More fundamentally, optimizing single objects is
highly under-constrained and prone to nonsensical reconstruction,
despite being considerably more efficient than full-scene training.
To address both issues, we propose a framework that combines
monocular depth estimation and visual hull-guided sampling; we
demonstrate that each strategy helps combat the other’s failure
modes and that together, they provide strong constraints which enable efficient, high-quality reconstruction of isolated objects, even
in the face of segmentation errors. We adopt voxel-based radiance
fields as our base representation as they build truly volumetric,
photo-realistic representations in minutes. Additionally, their regular structure makes it straightforward to define discrete objects and
extract mesh assets through methods like marching cubes [LC87].
Our key contributions are summarised below:
• A robust framework for reconstructing fine-structured objects
with high fidelity from coarse segmentation across viewpoints.
• A novel strategy which exploits segmentation failure to identify
and help prioritize fine structure.
• We release a multi-scene synthetic benchmark featuring objects
with thin structure, including coarse and ground-truth segmentation to facilitate evaluation of reconstruction methods under
imperfect segmentation.
2. Related Work
2.1. Object-Centric Representations
Motivated by applications like scene editing and mixed reality, several prior works have proposed methods for capturing standalone
radiance field assets. A prominent, early example is ObjectNeRF
[YZX∗ 21] which adopts two separate branches for scene and object rendering. Later, DM-NeRF [WCY22] proposes a method that
learns spatial codes for individual 3D objects using 2D supervision, isolating them for transforms like translation and rotation.
While direct media applications of such technology are clear (some
of which are explored in [BCE∗ 24]), elements of object-centricity
have also been adopted within wider pipelines. For example, TI-

errors and, for efficiency, ignore background pixels to not waste
compute. Addressing both challenges would greatly enhance compositional scene representations.
2.2. Incorporating Extra Constraints
Foreground-only capture and segmentation error introduce reconstruction ambiguities that require additional constraints. Generic
measures, like sparsity regularisation, can help tackle floating density in ambiguous scenarios but are not tailored to the fundamental
problems in question. Visual hulls have been used as a stronger
constraint to guide scene sampling [KIT∗ 21], therefore restricting
density placement and speeding up reconstruction. This work acknowledges intrinsic visual hull limitations, however, like the inability to model concavities and over-estimation in real-world settings. Parallel research has also turned to depth-based priors, although these strategies remain largely disparate. A notable example is DäRF [SPA∗ 23] which uses monocular depth estimates for
robust geometry reconstruction and artifact removal. Despite encouraging results, in this paper we show that depth alignment can
be cheated in this specific, object-centric context.
3. Method
Our methodology (summarised in Fig. 2) applies complementary
constraints to reconstruct objects given weak supervision: coarse
segmentation and no sampling of background regions. While
monocular depth estimates provide geometric priors, we demonstrate how radiance fields can “cheat” alignment. Visual hulls,
which are themselves notorious over-estimators, help overcome
depth ambiguity by defining a minimum distance between the camera and scene structure. Both coarse and refined masks are then
used to identify fine structure for biased sampling. Key details and
additional steps are described in the following subsections.
3.1. Base Representation
Feature Grids. Grid-based radiance fields are volumetric representations, formed by discretizing space into a regular grid structure,
storing learned features at vertices. Our implementation closely follows Plenoxels, which models scenes as grids of volumetric opacity, σ, and view-dependent colour, c, in the form of spherical harmonic coefficients (9 per color channel). These features are queried
using trilinear interpolation and then volume rendered into images.
For all scenes, our implementation used grids of size 5003 .

3.2. Robust Object Capture
Identifying Objects. Segmentation masks were generated using
Segment Anything 2 (SAM2) [RGH∗ 24]. To correct for missed
object pixels, like small structures, we slightly expand masks by
convolving them with a 4 × 4 dilation kernel prior to training.
This leaves over-estimation as the primary source of error. For efficiency, we cast rays through only true-valued mask pixels. To
sharpen object capture, we apply a softplus remapping to density:
′
σ =softplus(ασ + β). During training, α and β gradually increase
from 1 to ∼8 and from 0 to ∼2, respectively. The latter shifts the
softplus transition zone, boosting gradients for low-density regions
while the former sharpens the transition from “empty” to “solid”.
In our experiments, this process can make it easier to isolate confident structure as a prominent, non-zero peak in density is induced,
as illustrated in Fig. 3, which can be used to guide thresholding.
Priors. Even with perfect masks, rendering only foreground objects is an ill-posed problem with degenerate solutions. To mitigate
this, we obtained depth estimates, D̂, via Depth Pro [BDG∗ 24] for
all but one scene, which used Depth Anything V2 [YKH∗ 24]. A
Pearson correlation (ρ) loss then guides rendered depth, D, as it
is cheap to compute as well as shift and scale invariant: Ldepth =
λ(1 − ρ(D, D̂)). We decrease λ from 8×10−2 to 1.6×10−2 during
training. While depth estimates provide a geometric prior, rendering only targets prevents supervision of “incorrect” rays, allowing
the model to “cheat” depth integrals by distributing false density
along the ray, resulting in physically inaccurate density that still
obeys correct ordering. An example of such cheating is shown in

While visual hull-guided sampling has been employed in isolation,
they are a coarse technique; as maximally consistent volumes, visual hulls are known to over-estimate [Lau94], particularly in the
case of concave structures. In practice, such visual hull errors may
be compounded by poor masks and incomplete viewpoints. Depth
information is used to help to reduce degeneracy and prevent erroneous background pixels from clouding the visual hull. Together,
these two strategies provide a robust set of reconstruction constraints. The dilation described in the previous section also exposes
a very thin boundary around objects, revealing depth discontinuity.
Leveraging Failure. In our experiments, segmentation overestimation often stems from thin structures that are hard to segment. Such regions tend to carry high semantic value despite occupying minimal space, making them especially important to prioritise when sampling is expensive. To bias sampling towards these
areas, we subtract refined masks from coarse ones and clip values <
0, locating over-estimation. We then dilate the result to expand into
nearby structure and reapply the refined mask. This isolates regions
of "true" structure that are bounded by SAM2 over-estimation.
4. Results and Evaluation
4.1. Data
Our methodology is evaluated on a set of object-centric scenes
which we name "Plant", "Flower", "Fence", and "Dino Skull" according to their contents. These scenes consists of 52-65 training images each, rendered using the 3D creation software Blender
[Ble25]. The public dataset also includes camera intrinsics, camerato-world poses, coarse segmentation obtained directly from SAM2,
and ground-truth segmentation generated using source geometry.
As each object possesses some form of thin structure, many SAM2
masks contain over- or under-estimation errors, examples of which
are shown in Fig. 1. While real data would be preferable, this inherent difficulty motivates the use of synthetic data, allowing us
to quantitatively evaluate reconstruction robustness using groundtruth masks which would not be possible using real scenes. Ultimately, this dataset serves as a specialized benchmark that enables
researchers to assess the robustness of object-centric reconstruction
techniques or build mask refinement strategies.
4.2. Reconstruction
After training, we remove mask over-estimation by sampling the
scene and counting pixel rays that intersect with dense voxels.
To evaluate reconstruction, we compute the intersection of union
(IoU) between these refined masks and ground truth. IoU serves
here as a proxy measure of geometry recovery, not a segmentation
benchmark - results are shown in Tab. 1. Improvements over SAM2
show that our method produces meaningful geometry and reduces
the influence of erroneous background pixels. While average IoU
gains for Dino Skull and Flower were marginal – less than 0.01 for
the former - these scenes contained more subtle segmentation error. Additionally, the point clouds (produced by thresholding dense

4.3. Cues from Segmentation Failure
To demonstrate how refined masks can be used to isolate thin structures, we follow the procedure defined in Section 3.2 for the Plant
scene. By detecting over-estimation and expanding it into confident object regions, sampling bias maps are created. Such a map
is applied to an example view and shown in Fig. 6. It is clear that
fine structure like stems and leaves dominate this bias map while
central, dense foliage and the plant pot are neglected - regions that
require less dense sampling in inference. While sample points can
be concentrated along rays from non-zero map pixels, fine structure could be prioritized in other ways, perhaps by adjusting the
underlying grid resolution in these regions.
5. Conclusions and Future Work
We have presented a framework that allows for the robust objectcentric capture of 3D assets from poorly segmented images. Together, correlation with monocular depth estimates and visual hullimposed sampling limits appear to tackle ambiguities inherent to
object-centric training as well as those brought by coarse segmentation. This was demonstrated quantitatively with IoU and SSIM improvements as well as visually with point clouds that show significant reconstruction improvements over an under-constrained baseline. Given the goal of forming modular 3D assets, such qualitative results are especially revealing. Furthermore, we illustrate a
promising extension, exploiting over-estimated masks to identify
fine structure candidates for biased sampling, helping further enhance the capture of thin-structured objects. For practitioners, this

framework and our illustration of failure modes can help efficiently
build high-fidelity 3D assets from challenging, “in-the-wild” 2D
captures. Such volumetric assets may then be converted into standard mesh formats for integration into modern computer graphics
pipelines.
Although our results are promising, there are many avenues for
further work. Firstly, our method relies on high-quality depth estimation. While we have demonstrated that recent models provide
powerful priors in under-constrained regimes, monocular depth estimations rely on 2D, textural cues and can therefore degrade when
encountering difficulties like reflections or transparency. In these
cases, it would be interesting to probe the limits of depth reliance –
perhaps unconfident/erroneous views can be discarded without significantly loosening constraints. Additionally, estimations from alternative methods that consider multiple views, like Structure from
Motion, could be used. While we chose a voxel-based method due
to its speed, volumetric nature, and history in computer graphics,
much of this procedure should be directly applicable to NeRFs, and
this would make interesting future work. Furthermore, our dataset
can be used to assess the robustness of other representations, like
3DGS. Finally, our demonstration of fine structure identification
could be bolstered with further practical experimentation.
