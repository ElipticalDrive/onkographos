Abstract
Implicit Neural Representations, particularly Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), have shown strong potential for medical image arbitrary-scale super-resolution (MIASSR), with Cube-based NeRF establishing a zero-shot framework for continuous reconstruction. However, existing methods still face challenges in high-frequency fidelity and sampling efficiency for complex anatomical structures. We propose Z-AdaNeRF (Zero-shot Adaptive NeRF), an uncertainty-driven framework that improves the CuNeRF optimization process through three coordinated components. Progressive Grouping Frequency Masking (PGFM) gradually activates frequency groups to stabilize fine-detail learning; Heteroscedastic Uncertainty Modeling (HUM) introduces an uncertainty-weighted rendering objective; and Adaptive Texture-Sensitive Sampling (ATSS) allocates training samples toward structurally informative regions. Under the controlled ATSS ablation, the proposed sampling strategy reduces wall-clock training time by 21.1%. Experiments on KiTS19, COVID-DS36, and the public MSD dataset show consistent improvements over CuNeRF in most evaluated settings, particularly at larger upsampling scales, while retaining per-volume zero-shot optimization.

1.1 A.1 Preliminaries NeRF & CuNeRF
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [25] encode a scene with an MLP that maps a 3D position \({\textbf{x}}\) and view direction \({\textbf{d}}\) to color c and density \(\sigma \). For a pixel ray r, K samples along the ray are composited by volume rendering to predict \(\hat{C}(r)\):
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{C}(r)&= \sum _{i=1}^{K} T_i \, \alpha _i \, c_i, \end{aligned}$$
(17a)
$$\begin{aligned} T_i&= \prod _{j=1}^{i-1} \bigl (1 - \alpha _j\bigr ), \end{aligned}$$
(17b)
$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _i&= 1 - \exp (-\sigma _i \, \delta _i) \end{aligned}$$
(17c)
where r is a ray sample, \(T_i\) the accumulated transmittance, \(\alpha _i\) the opacity, \(T_i\alpha _i\) the termination probability at point i, and \(\delta _i\) the step size. The network is trained with an \(\ell _2\) photometric loss:
$$\begin{aligned} L_{\text {photo}} = \sum _{r \in R} \Vert C(r) - \hat{C}(r) \Vert _2^2 \end{aligned}$$
(18)
While effective for natural scenes, NeRF can produce grid artifacts on medical data. CuNeRF therefore adopts a physics-inspired formulation tailored to volumetric imaging. It replaces 1D ray sampling with 3D cube sampling: for a location \(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t\), points are uniformly drawn inside the axis-aligned cube \(B(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, l/2)\) of side length l as
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{{\textbf{x}}}_{i} \sim {\mathcal {U}}\left[ B\left( \hat{{\textbf{x}}}_{t},\frac{l}{2}\right) \right] \end{aligned}$$
(19)
For volume rendering, leveraging approximate isotropy, CuNeRF rewrites NeRF’s line integral as a spherical integral. Assuming the cube density \(\sigma \) depends only on the \(\ell _p\) distance \(r = \Vert \hat{{\textbf{x}}} - \hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t \Vert _p\) to the center, the triple integral becomes (in spherical coordinates) the following numerical approximation:
$$\begin{aligned} C(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, l)&= 4\pi \int _0^{\hat{r}} \frac{r^2 \, \sigma (\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, r) \, c(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, r) \, dr}{\exp \!\left( 4\pi \int _0^r s^2 \, \sigma (\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, s) \, ds\right) }, \end{aligned}$$
(20a)
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{C}(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, l)&= 4\pi \sum _{i=1}^N \frac{r_i^2 \, \bigl (1 - \exp \!\left( -\sigma _i \, (r_{i+1} - r_i)\right) \bigr )}{\exp \!\left( 4\pi \sum _{j=1}^i r_j^2 \, \sigma _j \, (r_{j+1} - r_j)\right) } \, {\textbf{c}}_i \end{aligned}$$
(20b)
where \(\hat{r} = \bigl \Vert ( \tfrac{l}{2}, \tfrac{l}{2}, \tfrac{l}{2} ) \bigr \Vert _p\) is the maximum distance within the cube, and \(\hat{C}(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, l)\) denotes the predicted color at the cube center \(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t\).
Optimization mirrors NeRF’s coarse-to-fine two-MLP scheme but in cube space. Each cube collects \(N_c + N_f\) samples, allocating \(N_f\) proportionally to the estimated intra-cube distribution. Coarse samples are obtained via inverse transform sampling (ITS) [25]; fine points are then selected by a hierarchical sampler \(\zeta _p(\cdot )\),
and a regularization term \(\lambda = \bigl \Vert \textrm{gt} - \tilde{{\textbf{C}}}(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t, l) \bigr \Vert _2^2\) mitigates overfitting at the coarse stage.
For free-view rendering, aligned with clinical multi-plane workflows, CuNeRF generalizes fixed-view rendering to dynamic plane rendering. Given position \(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_t\) and view \({\textbf{d}}\), a reference plane \(P_o\) at \(\hat{{\textbf{x}}}_o\) is translated by \(M_T\) and rotated by \(M_R\) (Rodrigues formula [24]) to yield
$$\begin{aligned} P_t = M_T \, M_R \, P_o \end{aligned}$$
(21)
For arbitrary-scale rendering, CuNeRF performs scale-aware sampling. Given a target scale \(\delta \), we compute a scaling matrix \(M_S\), sample in the transformed coordinates \(M_S \, P_t\), and render slices at the specified resolution.
1.2 A.2 Uncertainty-Guided Importance Fusion (UIF)
To further enhance the semantic awareness of the sampling policy throughout training, we introduce an uncertainty-guided fusion mechanism that dynamically augments the regional importance map. Activated in the mid-to-late training stages, this mechanism fuses an uncertainty heatmap to emphasize “high-confusion” areas, enabling an active-learning style of fine-grained sampling. Specifically, let \({\mathcal {U}}(x,y;z)\) be the uncertainty map at location (x, y) on slice z, estimated via an exponential moving average (EMA) [27] of the accumulated predicted variance during training:
$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal {U}}(x,y;z) = \operatorname {EMA}\big (\beta (x,y;z)\big ) \end{aligned}$$
(22)
where \(\beta (x,y;z)\) denotes the predicted uncertainty measure at the coordinate (e.g., output variance or inverse confidence). This map is suppressed early and gradually activated once the frequency-mask progress reaches \(\tau \ge T_{\text {switch}}\). At that point, the original importance map \({\mathcal {R}}(x,y)\) is expanded as
$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal {R}}'(x,y) = {\mathcal {R}}(x,y) + \kappa (\tau ,z)\cdot {\mathcal {U}}(x,y;z) \end{aligned}$$
(23)
where the fusion weight \(\kappa (\tau ,z)\) is a dynamic gating function that combines training progress, spatial coverage, and the structural informativeness of the uncertainty map:
$$\begin{aligned} \kappa (\tau ,z) = w_u \cdot \text {ramp}(\tau ;T_{\text {switch}},\delta _r) \cdot g_{\text {cov}}(z) \cdot g_{\text {ent}}(z) \end{aligned}$$
(24)
Here \(\operatorname {ramp}(\tau ;T_{\text {switch}},\delta _r) = \min \!\bigl (1,\max \!\bigl (0,\tfrac{\tau - T_{\text {switch}}}{\tau _r}\bigr )\bigr )\) controls the gradual introduction of uncertainty; \(g_{\text {cov}}(z)\) is a coverage gate based on the fraction of already-sampled points on the current slice to avoid over-focusing on sparse regions early; \(g_{\text {ent}}(z)\) is an entropy gate computed from the normalized entropy of \({\mathcal {U}}(\cdot ,\cdot ;z)\) to avoid being misled by flat uncertainty maps; and \(w_u\) is the base fusion weight (an extension of \(\eta \) from Eq. 23).
The final importance map \({\mathcal {R}}_{\text {final}}\) is then used as the base weighting in the augmented probabilistic sampling of Eq. 16, further biasing the sampling distribution toward regions that are structurally salient yet uncertain, thereby improving convergence in bottleneck areas, in spirit similar to proposal/importance sampling ideas explored for accelerating NeRF rendering [4, 29].
1.3 A.3 Online Small-Step Fine-Tuning (OSF)
To mitigate the sensitivity of manually set hyperparameters across different data distributions and training phases, we design an online adaptive tuning mechanism that dynamically adjusts key sampling parameters, improving robustness and generalization. We optimize the ATSS hyperparameters \(\varvec{\theta }\) (cf. Equation 12) in real time. The updates are driven by the entropy feedback of the current sampling distribution. The normalized entropy of the probability vector \({\textbf{p}}\) over the adaptively sampled region is
$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal {H}}({\textbf{p}}) = - \frac{\sum _{i}p_i\log p_i}{\log n_{\text {a}}} \end{aligned}$$
(25)
If \({\mathcal {H}}({\textbf{p}})\) is too low, sampling is overly concentrated, risking overfitting or neglect of potentially important regions; if \({\mathcal {H}}({\textbf{p}})\) is too high, the focus is insufficient and sampling becomes nearly random. Therefore, every \(K_a\) sampling steps we adjust parameters by
$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \varvec{\theta }&\leftarrow \operatorname {clip}\big (\varvec{\theta } + \Delta \varvec{\theta },\, \varvec{\theta }_{\min },\, \varvec{\theta }_{\max }\big ),\\ \text {with}\quad \Delta \varvec{\theta }&= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} -\delta _{\text {lr}}, & {\mathcal {H}}({\textbf{p}}) < h_{\text {low}} \\ +\delta _{\text {lr}}, & {\mathcal {H}}({\textbf{p}}) > h_{\text {high}} \\ 0, & \text {otherwise} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(26)
where \(\delta _{\text {lr}}\) is the update step size; \(h_{\text {low}}\) and \(h_{\text {high}}\) are entropy thresholds; and \(\operatorname {clip}(\cdot )\) keeps parameters within a preset feasible range (e.g., \(\beta \in [0.05,2.0]\)) and within bounded relative change from initialization. This mechanism establishes a self-regulating loop that automatically balances exploration and exploitation during training, particularly suitable for multi-center medical datasets with substantial anatomical variability.

We also investigated the convergence performance of the PGFM module over short training cycles on the COVID-DS36 dataset, keeping all parameters except for the training epochs consistent with the experimental setup described in Sect. 4. The results demonstrate that prematurely exposing high-frequency information to the network can accelerate improvement in non-perceptual metrics, yet at the cost of reduced visual authenticity in the final reconstructed images. Moreover, the abrupt transitions inherent in conventional piecewise gating functions conflict with the network’s progressive learning behavior. This exploratory study aims to identify an information release schedule that aligns with the network’s learning demands at different stages. We find that PGFM, guided by a scheduling function characterized by "gentle slopes during initial and final stages and steep transitions in the middle," more effectively achieves superior output quality (see Table. 6).
