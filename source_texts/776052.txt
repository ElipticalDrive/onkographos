Grid layouts in atmosphere and ocean models
Diagram of the five Arakawa grids, A to E: where the height and the two velocity components are defined on the grid of a numerical weather, climate, or ocean model.
The Arakawa grids are five ways, labeled A to E, of arranging the velocity components and the variables that describe the mass of a fluid, such as its depth or pressure, on the rectangular grids of weather, climate and ocean models.[1]: 295 [2]: 155–156
The A grid defines all the variables at the same points. The other four are staggered grids, which define different variables at different points.[1]: 294–295 The grids are named after Akio Arakawa, who devised them with Frank Winninghoff at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) in the 1960s and described them with Vivian Lamb in 1977.[1]: 295 [3]: 33 [4]: 17 [5]: 109–110 [6]: 180
Staggering lets a model compute pressure gradients and the divergence of the flow over one grid interval instead of two, which improves the accuracy of short waves at no extra computational cost.[5]: 109 [7]: 56–58 The choice of grid matters greatly for the inertia–gravity waves through which the atmosphere and the ocean adjust to geostrophic balance, because their dispersion depends on how the variables are arranged.[2]: 155–156 Arakawa and Lamb found that the C grid simulates this adjustment best, unless the radius of deformation is about the grid spacing or smaller, then on such coarse grids the B grid can do better.[6]: 190 [1]: 299–300 The C grid has often been chosen for grid-point atmospheric models. Ocean models, whose radius of deformation is much smaller, long used the B grid, but many now use the C grid.[8]: 52 [9]: 252 [10]: 536 [11]: 3167
Background
[edit]
When solving differential equations using a finite difference method, each variable is represented by its values at a set of grid points, and derivatives are approximated by differences between neighboring values. If all the variables are defined at the same points, the simplest centered difference at a point spans two grid intervals, from the point before it to the point after it.[1]: 290 [5]: 108–109
In the shallow water equations, for example, the velocity changes in response to the gradient of the fluid depth, and the depth in response to the divergence of the velocity. If the depth is defined halfway between velocity points, both derivatives can be computed over a single grid interval. For these terms, this is equivalent to doubling the resolution, and it reduces their discretization error by a factor of four, with no extra grid points or computation.[1]: 290–291 [5]: 109 [7]: 56–58 Advection terms still need differences over two intervals though.[5]: 109
Because the differences span shorter distances, a model on a staggered grid may need a shorter time step to remain stable by satisfying the CFL condition.[7]: 58 Some time-stepping schemes avoid this, but it is often impossible to choose a single staggering that improves the accuracy of every term.[12]: 154–156
Staggering also removes spurious solutions. On an unstaggered grid, centered differences link each point only to points two intervals away, so the grid splits into sets of points that evolve independently of each other.[1]: 293–294 One result is a stationary checkerboard pattern in the depth, with no motion at all, that the momentum equations cannot detect.[1]: 294–295
For gravity waves traveling along a grid line, the shortest wave that the grid can represent, two grid intervals long, does not move at all on an unstaggered grid, whereas on a staggered grid it moves at 64% of its correct speed.[12]: 154 Such spurious solutions, which fail to propagate or have no counterpart in the original equations, are called computational modes.[8]: 45 [4]: 3
The shorter half of the gravity waves that an unstaggered grid can represent also have group velocities of the wrong sign so they carry energy in the wrong direction. Waves that have spurious propagation characteristics like this are sometimes called parasitic modes. On a staggered grid the group velocity always has the right sign.[8]: 45–47
Large-scale flows in the atmosphere and the ocean are close to geostrophic balance, in which the Coriolis force balances the pressure gradient. When this balance is upset, inertia–gravity waves radiate the imbalance away and leave a balanced flow behind, a process called geostrophic adjustment.[6]: 179–180 How well a model simulates geostrophic adjustment depends strongly on how its variables are distributed over the grid points, because each arrangement gives the inertia–gravity waves different dispersion properties.[6]: 180 [2]: 155–156
The five grids
[edit]
Arakawa and Lamb compared the five ways of distributing the fluid depth h and the velocity components u and v over a square grid, using the simplest second-order finite-difference scheme for each one.[6]: 181–182 Where a term needs a variable at a point where it is not defined, the scheme averages the values at the nearest points where it is.[1]: 295–296 Grids B and E, which keep the two velocity components together, are called semi-staggered, while grids C and D are fully staggered.[13]: 376 The descriptions below place h at the center of each grid cell. The grids are also sometimes drawn with h at the corners, as Arakawa and Lamb drew them, but the arrangements are equivalent.[6]: 181
A grid
[edit]
The A grid defines u, v and h at the same points, and is the only unstaggered one of the five.[1]: 294–295 It is simple, and higher-order schemes are easy to build on it, but all its differences span two grid intervals. Neighboring points are not coupled through the pressure gradient and the divergence, so a checkerboard pattern can develop, which models suppress with high-order diffusion.[5]: 109 Such grid-scale noise cannot take part in geostrophic adjustment.[14]: 1373
B grid
[edit]
The B grid defines u and v together at the corners of the cells. The Coriolis force, which couples the two components, needs no averaging, but the pressure gradient and the divergence are averaged over two neighboring differences.[1]: 296 As a result, a checkerboard pattern in the height exerts no pressure gradient force, and a checkerboard pattern in the velocity causes no divergence, so both are decoupled from the rest of the solution.[3]: 34–35 The shortest distance over which the B grid takes differences is the diagonal of a cell, 2 times the grid spacing.[5]: 111
Kirk Bryan built his 1969 ocean model on the B grid, which was thought to be better suited to the coarse resolutions of the time. The ocean models descended from it, sometimes referred to as being part of the Bryan–Semtner–Cox lineage, kept the B grid even at finer resolutions.[15]: 221 In 2013 the Modular Ocean Model and the Parallel Ocean Program used the B grid.[10]: 536
C grid
[edit]
The C grid defines each velocity component at the middle of the cell faces across which it flows. u is defined on the faces between neighboring cells in the x direction, and v on those between neighboring cells in the y direction. The pressure gradient and the divergence are then computed over a single grid interval with a compact stencil, and the divergence falls at the center of each cell, where h is.[12]: 167 [16]: 473 The C grid also simplifies the computation of advection and friction, because the velocity that carries properties across the cell faces is the velocity the model predicts.[15]: 221 The Coriolis force, however, needs v at the u points and u at the v points, which the scheme takes as the average of the four nearest values.[1]: 295–296 [5]: 110
When the grid spacing is large compared with the radius of deformation, this averaging lets grid-scale noise persist in the divergence of the flow.[17]: 1928 The same arrangement, with the pressure at the cell centers, is the standard staggered grid for computing incompressible flows in computational fluid dynamics, used for example in the marker-and-cell method.[18]: 256–259, 266
The general circulation model of UCLA has used it since its third version, built in the mid-1970s, from which the US Navy's NOGAPS forecasting system evolved.[19]: 159–160 The C grid is also used in the Advanced Research core of the Weather Research and Forecasting Model,[9]: 252 [20]: 3, 61 in almost all models of convective storms.[21]: 246 As of 2013, the C grid was also used in the HyCOM, MITgcm, NEMO, POM, and ROMS ocean models.[10]: 536 As of 2019, version 6 of the Modular Ocean Model, the ocean component of the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory's OM4.0 ocean and sea-ice model, also uses the C grid.[11]: 3167, 3173
D grid
[edit]
The D grid switches the positions of u and v on the C grid, so that each velocity component lies along a cell face instead of across it.[1]: 296 The vorticity, rather than the divergence, then falls at the center of each cell.[16]: 473 Geostrophic balance takes its simplest form on the D grid, which is why Arakawa first chose it for the UCLA model.[3]: 31–32 But the Coriolis force, the pressure gradient and the divergence all need averaging, and of the grids A to D it represents inertia–gravity waves least accurately.[6]: 182 [1]: 299
Staggering the variables in time as well as in space, as Arnt Eliassen proposed, makes all its differences span a single grid interval with leapfrog time stepping, and Shian-Jiann Lin and Richard B. Rood took up a similar idea in a global shallow-water model in 1997.[5]: 111 They predicted the winds on the D grid, which suits the computation of vorticity and of balanced flow, but computed the time-centered winds that carry the mass on the C grid, which avoids the two-grid-interval gravity waves that the D grid alone would produce.[22]: 2483–2486
E grid
[edit]
The E grid places h and the velocity points alternately, like the squares of a checkerboard, so that it is the B grid rotated by 45°.[6]: 181 [1]: 295 Arakawa and Lamb chose its spacing so that it has as many points as the other grids in a given area.[6]: 183 No term needs averaging on the E grid,[14]: 1374 and inertia–gravity waves disperse on it as they do on the B grid rotated by 45°, so the two are often treated together as the B/E grid.[23]: 50 [13]: 377 Both grids are made of two interleaved C grids that the pressure gradient and the divergence do not connect: a disturbance at one height point sends gravity waves only through its own subgrid, and the two subgrids are coupled only through the slower Coriolis and advection terms.[13]: 377
The E grid was used by the Eta model of the US National Centers for Environmental Prediction.[5]: 111
History
[edit]
Yale Mintz and Akio Arakawa built a series of general circulation models of the atmosphere at UCLA beginning in 1961.[19]: 158 The first generation used the D grid. Once moisture was added to the model, noise appeared in its pressure and temperature fields and spread with an unphysically large group velocity.[3]: 31–32 Arakawa then studied, with Frank Winninghoff, how geostrophic adjustment works on five arrangements of the variables, each with the simplest centered differences.[3]: 32–33 In his 1968 doctoral thesis at UCLA, Winninghoff showed that how well a finite-difference scheme simulates geostrophic adjustment depends strongly on how the variables are distributed over the grid points.[6]: 180, 265 Arakawa's 1972 report on the design of the model labels the five arrangements A to E.[24]: 35–37 Arakawa judged the C grid the best for geostrophic adjustment and the D grid the worst, but because the C grid has difficulties of its own, notably with the higher vertical modes, he chose the B grid for the second generation of the model as a compromise.[3]: 33–34 [24]: 44–45, 62 The third generation, built in the mid-1970s, returned to the C grid, which all later versions have used.[3]: 25, 36 [19]: 159–160
Fedor Mesinger and Arakawa described the comparison of the grids in a 1976 monograph for the Global Atmospheric Research Program, by which time the C grid was used both in the UCLA model and in the operational forecasting model of the British Met Office.[23]: 45–50 Arakawa and Vivian Lamb published it in 1977, as part of their description of the third-generation UCLA model. They concluded that, for geostrophic adjustment, only one distribution of the variables was satisfactory: the C grid.[6]: 180, 190, 262 On it they built a scheme for the nonlinear shallow water equations that conserves total energy and momentum, and also enstrophy when the flow is nondivergent. Conserving both energy and enstrophy prevents a spurious cascade of energy to the smallest scales in long integrations.[6]: 177–178, 201–207, 262–263 The grids are named after Arakawa.[1]: 295, 315
Differences and averages
[edit]
Arakawa and Lamb wrote their schemes with two operators, a difference and an average of neighboring values. In the x direction, for a variable q at grid index i, j the difference operator is
(δxq)i,j=qi+12,j−qi−12,j,
and the averaging operator is
(q‾x)i,j=12(qi+12,j+qi−12,j).
Likewise for δy and q‾y in the y direction, with q‾xy=q‾x‾y=q‾y‾x.[6]: 182
The indices count grid intervals, so that a half-integer index i+12 is a point halfway between the two grid points i and i+1. Each operator produces a value halfway between the two points it uses. There, the difference divided by the grid spacing d approximates the derivative ∂q/∂x to second order in d.[1]: 290–291
Each equation of a scheme is evaluated where the variable that it predicts is defined, and the operators bring the other variables to that point. That is possible because the operators take in values at the halfway points and produce values at the integer index points, and vice versa. The equation for v is the same with x and y interchanged, and the divergence in the equation for h uses the same differences and averages as the pressure gradient.[6]: 181–182 [1]: 294–296

Shallow-water equations on the grids
[edit]
Arakawa and Lamb, following Winninghoff, compared the grids using the shallow water equations linearized about a fluid at rest on an f-plane,
∂u∂t=fv−g∂h∂x,
∂v∂t=−fu−g∂h∂y,
∂h∂t=−H(∂u∂x+∂v∂y),
where f is the Coriolis parameter, g the gravitational acceleration, and H the mean depth of the fluid.[6]: 180–181 They replaced only the space derivatives by differences, leaving "the time-change terms in differential form".[6]: 183 Each grid then gives semi-discrete equations, continuous in time and discrete in space:[6]: 181–183

Inertia–gravity waves
[edit]
Arakawa and Lamb judged each of the five grids by how it represents inertia–gravity waves, which carry energy away from a disturbance while the fluid adjusts to geostrophic balance.[6]: 180
Dispersion relation
[edit]
For waves of the form
(u,v,h)=(û,v̂,ĥ)ei(kx+ly−νt),
with wavenumbers k and l and frequency ν, each derivative in the shallow-water equations becomes a factor: ∂/∂t becomes −iν, ∂/∂x becomes ik, and ∂/∂y becomes il. The equations then reduce to three algebraic equations for the amplitudes,
−iνû−fv̂+igkĥ=0fû−iνv̂+iglĥ=0iHkû+iHlv̂−iνĥ=0
which have a solution other than zero only if their determinant vanishes
ν[ν2−f2−gH(k2+l2)]=0.
The root ν=0 is a steady flow in geostrophic balance. The other two solutions are inertia–gravity waves, with[1]: 276–277 [6]: 183
ν2=f2+gH(k2+l2).
The frequency increases with the wavenumber unless the radius of deformation λ=gH/f is zero, so the group velocity of these waves is never zero. This is what allows them to carry the energy of an imbalance away during geostrophic adjustment.[6]: 183 [1]: 276
On the grids
[edit]
The same substitution can be made in each grid's semi-discrete equations, with each variable sampled at its own points. A difference δx then multiplies the wave by 2isin⁡(kd/2), and an average in x multiplies by cos⁡(kd/2). For example, the difference across two intervals on the A grid gives isin⁡(kd)/d whereas the derivative gave ik.[12]: 154
On the C grid, the pressure gradient and the divergence are single differences, and the Coriolis terms are averages over four points. Its three equations are therefore those above with (2/d)sin⁡(kd/2) instead of k, (2/d)sin⁡(ld/2) instead of l, and fcos⁡(kd/2)cos⁡(ld/2) instead of f in the Coriolis terms. Their determinant then gives the dispersion relation[1]: 297–298 [14]: 1372
(νf)2=cos2(kd2)cos2(ld2)+4(λd)2[sin2(kd2)+sin2(ld2)].
Each of the other grids gives its relation in the same way, from its own differences and averages. For waves much longer than the grid spacing, every grid's relation tends to the exact one.[1]: 297

Comparing the grids
[edit]
In one dimension the B grid is the most satisfactory, and the C grid is as good when λ/d is sufficiently larger than 1/2.[6]: 185 On the A and D grids, the shortest waves have group velocities of the wrong sign, so they carry energy the wrong way and solutions on these grids may be noisy.[25]: 228 [23]: 49
In two dimensions, the frequency on the B grid has false maxima for waves traveling at some angles to the grid lines. The frequency on the C grid, like the exact frequency, has no false maxima when the radius of deformation is twice the grid spacing, so that no wave on the C grid then carries energy the wrong way.[23]: 50 When the grid spacing is larger than the radius of deformation, the relation of the B grid keeps much the same shape, but that of the C grid flattens.[6]: 184 [14]: 1376 Arakawa and Lamb concluded that the simulation of geostrophic adjustment is best with the C grid, "except for abnormal situations in which λ/d is less than or close to 1".[6]: 187–190
The difference comes from the terms each grid must average. On every grid except C, the pressure gradient is averaged, so it vanishes for a height field that alternates in sign from one grid point to the next. On the C and D grids, the Coriolis force vanishes for such a pattern in the velocity. Geostrophic adjustment cannot act on these patterns.[3]: 33 Waves much shorter than the radius of deformation are controlled by gravity, through the pressure gradient and the divergence, which the C grid computes over a single interval. Waves longer than the radius of deformation are controlled by the Coriolis force, which the B grid computes without averaging. The C grid is therefore the better choice when the grid spacing is much smaller than the radius of deformation, and the B grid when it is much larger.[1]: 298–300 The change from one regime to the other is gradual: when the radius of deformation is between half and twice the grid spacing, the two grids perform about equally well.[26]: 99
Which regime applies depends on the fluid. In the atmosphere, the radius of deformation is about 3000 km for the external mode, far larger than the grid spacing of any practical model, and the C grid has often been the choice for atmospheric models.[5]: 110 [3]: 34 [8]: 52 In the ocean it is only about 10–30 km, which models long could not resolve, so many ocean models used the B grid. As resolution has improved, some have turned to the C grid.[8]: 52 [17]: 1928 [14]: 1374 The radius of deformation is also much smaller for the higher vertical modes of a three-dimensional model. Randall summarized that "the C grid does well with deep, external modes but has serious problems with high internal modes, whereas the B grid has moderate problems with all modes".[14]: 1376 A model on the C grid therefore needs a horizontal resolution fine enough for its vertical resolution,[4]: 18 and this is one reason some atmospheric models use the B or E grid.[5]: 110–111
Geostrophic adjustment
[edit]
Arakawa and Lamb tested the schemes on a one-dimensional problem of geostrophic adjustment, which they solved as a Fourier integral in which each wavenumber oscillates at its own frequency. They evaluated the same solution with the frequencies of the exact equations and of each scheme.[6]: 185–187 A mound of fluid released from rest in two dimensions can be treated in the same way, because the equations are linear and their Fourier modes evolve independently.[27]: 132 The linearized equations conserve the potential vorticity
q=ζ−fhH
where
ζ=∂v∂x−∂u∂y
is the vorticity.[27]: 129 A Fourier mode of the height that starts at rest with amplitude ĥ0 therefore keeps the vorticity
ζ̂=fH(ĥ−ĥ0).
Differentiating the equation for h with respect to time and using the other two then gives
d2ĥdt2+ν2ĥ=f2ĥ0,
where ν is the frequency of the inertia–gravity waves and ν2=f2+gHK2 with K2=k2+l2. The solution that starts from rest is
ĥ(t)=ĥ0[f2ν2+(1−f2ν2)cos⁡(νt)].
The first term is the height that remains in geostrophic balance, ĥ0/(1+λ2K2). Features much wider than the radius of deformation keep most of their height, and much narrower ones lose most of it.[27]: 133 The second term oscillates, and summed over all wavenumbers it is the part carried away by inertia–gravity waves.
A mound that is symmetric about its center can instead be built from circular modes, J0(Kr), where J0 is a Bessel function of the first kind and r is the distance from the center. A circular mode is the average of the plane waves of wavenumber K traveling in every direction,[28]: 135 and the frequency ν depends only on K, so a circular mode evolves in the same way as a plane wave.
The amplitudes of the circular modes in an initial height h(r,0) are given by its Hankel transform,[28]: 136 and each then evolves as above. Horace Lamb solved the waves that spread from a symmetric disturbance on deep water in this way, with each circular mode oscillating at its own frequency;[28]: 429–430 here
h(r,t)=∫0∞[f2ν2+(1−f2ν2)cos⁡(νt)]J0(Kr)KdK∫0∞h(r′,0)J0(Kr′)r′dr′.
For a Gaussian mound of height h0 and width σ,
h(r,0)=h0e−r2/2σ2,
the inner integral is h0σ2e−σ2K2/2, and
h(r,t)=h0σ2∫0∞e−σ2K2/2[f2ν2+(1−f2ν2)cos⁡(νt)]J0(Kr)KdK.

Related grids
[edit]
Models also stagger their variables in the vertical. Most define the vertical velocity at the boundaries between layers (vertical cell faces) and the other variables in the middle of the layers, an arrangement introduced by Edward Lorenz in 1960. The Lorenz grid allows a spurious computational mode in the temperature. The grid introduced by Jule Charney and Norman Phillips in 1953, with the temperature at the layer boundaries, does not have this spurious mode.[5]: 111–112 [2]: 155 The Charney–Phillips grid gives slightly more accurate wave propagation, while energy is easier to conserve on the Lorenz grid.[29]: 163
Other arrangements avoid some of the weaknesses of the five grids. The Z grid, proposed by David Randall in 1994, predicts the vorticity and the divergence instead of the two velocity components, and defines them at the same points as the height.[14]: 1371, 1376 It needs no averaging, and its inertia–gravity waves behave well whatever the value of λ/d.[14]: 1376 [4]: 18 But then the velocity must then be recovered by solving Poisson equations at every time step.[4]: 3
The C–D grid of Adcroft, Hill and Marshall takes the C grid and adds a second set of velocity components placed as on the D grid, so that the Coriolis terms need no averaging. It removes the C grid's noise at coarse resolution, at the cost of twice as many velocity variables.[17]: 1928–1930, 1933, 1935
Variables can be staggered in the same ways on the hexagonal, triangular, and other polygonal grids that some global models use to cover the sphere.[29]: 163 [8]: 52 C grids on such meshes can keep most of the desirable properties of the C grid on a rectangular mesh if the mesh has an orthogonal dual, such as a Voronoi diagram and its Delaunay triangulation, but triangular and hexagonal C grids also support spurious computational modes.[4]: 4, 16 The Model for Prediction Across Scales, for example, uses a C grid on a centroidal Voronoi mesh of mostly hexagonal cells..[30]: 3090–3091, 3093
