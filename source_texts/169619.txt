# Automatic Size and Variance Determination for Better Surface Splatting Quality on Close-up View

Kar-Weng Ban Faculty of Information Technology Multimedia University (Cyberjaya Campus) 63100, Cyberjaya, Selangor, Malaysia [kwban@mmu.edu.my](mailto:kwban@mmu.edu.my)



Figure 1. The Happy Buddha rendered using polygon rendering (left) and surface splatting using our automatically computed splats' size and variance (right). Each figure insets the close-up view of the head of the Happy Buddha.

### Abstract

Surface splatting has been proven to be a viable alternative to traditional polygon rendering for highly detailed models. In terms of rendering performance, surface splatting has surpassed polygon rendering when it fully exploits today's graphics processing units (GPU). However, in terms of rendering quality, surface splatting could still be inferior to polygon rendering when the model is viewed close-up and when the size and variance of each splat is not computed properly. In this paper, we present a method that improves the image quality of surface splatting to the level comparable to polygonal rendering by automatically finding a suitable size and variance for each splat.

Keywords: splat size and variance determination, surface splatting, point-based rendering, GPU, real-time rendering

# 1. Introduction

Polygon is traditionally used as the basic primitive for representing surface in computer graphics. However, it becomes inefficient when used to represent highly detailed surface due to the processing of tiny triangles that contributes trivially to the rendered image. As a result, a new primitive called splat has slowly become the preferred alternative over polygon for representing such highly detailed surface.

Splat is a planar ellipse defined on the tangent plane at each point. It is used to specify the coverage of the point, as well as to determine how the point's attributes are blended across the neighboring points. Hence, each splat is associated with parameters that define its size, as well as a Gaussian blending kernel that weighs the point's contribution with respect to its distance from the neighboring points. Using these splat data, a smooth and continuous surface is generated by accumulating and blending the contribution of the points in image-space. This rendering technique is known as surface splatting.

In the past when the graphics pipeline is fixed and unchangeable, surface splatting is much slower than polygon rendering as the graphics pipeline is designed primarily for polygon rendering. Now with the advent of programmable shaders, surface splatting can fully utilize the power of today's GPU and performs better than polygonal rendering for complex surfaces. This can be seen in the works by [Ren et al. 2002], [Botsch and Kobbelt 2003], [Botsch et al. 2004], and [Botsch et al. 2005].



Figure 2. The close-up view of Happy Buddha rendered using surface splatting without using proper size and variance for each splat. Using variance that is too large exhibits noticeable splat discontinuity (left), whereas using size or variance that is too small causes aliasing effect (right). 

Although surface splatting has surpassed polygon rendering in terms of performance through hardware acceleration, the quality of surface splatting could still be inferior to polygon rendering especially on close-up view when the size and variance are not properly determined. Figure 2 illustrates the outcome of surface splatting as a result of poorly chosen size and variance. In contrast to polygon representation, the splat-based representation of the surface exhibits noticeable discontinuities between the overlapping splats on close-up view. Such quality defect can be very disturbing for applications that require good rendering quality on close-up view.

In this paper, we present a method that improves the quality of surface splatting by automatically determining the suitable size and variance for each splat such that it is comparable to polygon rendering even on close-up view. For each splat, the size is determined by calculating the distance from itself to its k-th nearest neighboring splat, and the variance is calculated with respect to the size. We use the GPU splatting method in [Bostch et al. 2004] and [Botsch et al. 2005] for the rendering.

#### 2. Previous Work

The use of points as the rendering primitives was first proposed in [Levoy and Whitted 1985], but it was not found to be practical until the advent of QSplat point rendering system [Rusinkiewicz and Levoy 2000] and the introduction of Surfels [Pfister et al. 2000]. Later, [Zwicker et al. 2001] proposed the surface splatting framework, in which the surface local region is approximated by planar discs or ellipses in object space and then rendered by accumulating and blending the visible splats. Surface splatting is capable of producing high quality image, but it was implemented in software and hence putting huge processing task on the CPU.

Subsequent work in point-based rendering has been built on top of surface splatting to improve its efficiency by exploiting hardware acceleration. [Ren et al. 2002] pioneered the three-pass rendering method (two geometry pass and one image pass). They represented each splat with a quad and mapped it with an elliptical alpha-texture. To discard fragments belonging to the splat's exterior, the alpha test was set. The drawback of this method is huge memory consumption as there are four times more data to be processed. [Dachsbacher et al. 2003] and [Botsch and Kobbelt 2003] solved this problem by using image-aligned squares and circular object-space splats respectively. However, they both suffered from depth inaccuracy which causes holes when viewed under extreme viewing angle. This problem was solved by [Zwicker et al. 2004] and [Botsch et al. 2004], with the latter achieved better efficiency through the use of ray casting method.

There are several contributions that particularly addressed the determination of the size and variance to improve splatting quality. [Zwicker et al. 2001] and [Ren et al. 2002] used a global variance v = 1 for every splat if the points are approximately unitspaced, or the local variance v = 1/h2 where h is the maximum distance between points in a small neighborhood. For the splat size, they use 1 for all splats. These methods could exhibit splat discontinuity on close-up view when applied to irregularlysampled model. [Pajarola et al. 2004] proposed a new blending kernel that supports plateau-like blending kernels with variable sharp drop-off. This blending kernel has parameters to control the slope and the size of the blending kernel. However, user adjustment is required to tweak the parameters to produce quality splatting. [Botsch et al. 2004] and [Botsch et al. 2005] applied perpixel Phong shading to splatting to enhance the rendering quality, but it is not suitable for surfaces of non-shiny materials such as wood. Furthermore, the per-pixel normal generated in [Botsch et al. 2005] depends heavily on the splat's variance. Using inappropriate variance for each splat can break the smoothness of normal interpolation among overlapping splats.

We believe that determining suitable size and variance for each splat are vital to the improvement of splatting quality, and that the determination process can be automated. In the following section, we describe how we determine the values of these attributes.

# 3. A Description of Our Method

Our method begins with a surface S given by a set of points P = {pi} whose density satisfies the sampling criterion mentioned in [Amenta et al. 1998]. For each point pi , we want to generate a circular Gaussian splat si = (ci , ni , ri , vi ) where ci , ni , ri , and vi represents the center, normal, size, and variance of the splat respectively. Since we use circular splat, the size is simply the radius of the splat. We set ci = pi , and assume that ni has already been computed. Our focus is to determine the ri and vi for each splat such that the quality of surface splatting is comparable to polygon rendering on close-up view.

#### 3.1 Splat's size determination

Let Nk (si ) be the set of k-nearest neighboring splats to si measured by Euclidean distance between two splats denoted by d(si , sj ) = ||cj – ci ||. To achieve hole-free rendering, ri should be sufficiently large such that si overlaps all splats in Nk (si ). Some of the previous works optimized the number of splats that sufficiently covers the surface, which indirectly caused small overlapping area among the splats. We do not follow this approach because small overlapping area results in less blending among the splats, which can lead to disturbing visual artifacts (as shown in Figure 2) on close-up view. Instead, we set ri using the following equation:

| $r_i = \max(d(s_i, s_j))$ | $s_j \in N_k(s_i)$ | (1) |

#### 3.2 Splat's variance determination

Our approach towards determining vi is done as follows. Let Gi (r) be the 1D Gaussian function defined for splat si with respect to distance r from the splat's center ci

$$G_i(r) = A e^{\frac{-r^2}{2v_i}} \quad 0 \leq r \leq r_i \quad (2)$$

where A represents the amplitude of the Gaussian function. We want to find vi such that

$$G_i\left(\frac{r_i}{2}\right) = \frac{A}{2} \quad (3)$$

This expression can be simplified and eventually leads to

$$v_i = \frac{r_i^2}{-8\ln\frac{1}{2}} \quad (4)$$

where vi only depends on the splat's size ri . This allows the variance for each splat to be computed automatically. For the sake

of convenience, we rewrite equation (4) to

| $v_i = \frac{r_i^2}{k}$ | where $k = -8\ln \frac{1}{2}$ | (5) |

|                         |                               |     |

#### 4. Rendering

Our rendering approach mostly follows [Botsch et al. 2004] and [Botsch et al. 2005], so we will only describe the approach in brief. More information can be found in the papers mentioned above.

To render each splat, we send one vertex through the graphics pipeline. We set the OpenGL point size to the projected size of the splat, which is computed by perspectively foreshortening the splat size using the depth value of the splat center as shown in following equation

$$\text{size} = 2r \cdot \frac{n}{z_{\text{eye}}} \cdot \frac{h_{vp}}{t-b} \quad (6)$$

where n, t, b are the near, top, and bottom of the view frustum respectively, hvp is the viewport height, and zeye is the distance between the camera and the splat's center. As a result, an imagespace square of size x size pixels is generated.

For each pixel generated, we perform what we called as On Splat Test (OST) to determine whether the pixel is within the screenspace projection of the splat. Pixels that pass the test will be further processed whereas pixels that fail test will be discarded. To do this, we cast a ray from the pixel location towards the splat and find the intersection point q between the ray and the splat using the equation

$$q = q_n \cdot \frac{c^T n}{q^T n} \quad (7)$$

where c and n are the splat's center and normal vector in eye coordinate, and qn is the corresponding 3D point on the near plane that intersects the ray, which is computed as

$$q_n = \begin{pmatrix} x \cdot \frac{w_n}{w_{vp}} - \frac{w_n}{2} \\ y \cdot \frac{h_n}{h_{vp}} - \frac{h_n}{2} \\ -n \end{pmatrix} \quad (8)$$

where w{n,vp} and h{n,vp} denote the width and height of the near plane and viewport, respectively. Now, we can easily determine whether the pixel passes the OST for circular splat with the following equation:

$$\|q - c\| \leq r \quad (9)$$

For each pixel that passes OST, the depth value is equal to the depth value of the splat's center, which will cause the overlapping splats to blending incorrectly. As pointed in [Botsch et al. 2004], we can correct the pixel's depth value zvp by

$$z_{vp} = \frac{1}{q_z} \cdot \frac{fn}{f-n} + \frac{f}{f-n} \quad (10)$$

#### 4.1 Implementation

Our rendering approach uses OpenGL graphics pipeline and is implemented with GLSL to exploit hardware acceleration. Each splat has these attributes: center, color, normal, size, and variance. Thus, we require 11 floating points for each splat. We package these attributes of all splats into an OpenGL Vertex Buffer Object (VBO) to avoid data transfer overhead between the main system and the graphics card.

Similar to most of the hardware accelerated splatting algorithms [Ren et al. 2002, Botsch and Kobbelt 2003, Botsch et al. 2004, Botsch et al. 2005], we uses two geometry pass and one image pass to render the splats. We will only briefly describe the operations done in every pass as most of the details can be found in [Botsch et al. 2004] and [Botsch et al. 2005]. In contrast to [Botsch et al. 2005], we do not apply the anisotropic anti-aliasing filter.

Visibility Pass: The purpose of this pass is to render a depth image that contains the depth values of the visible splats. This depth image is used in the next pass to accumulate visible splat's attributes. In the vertex shader, we send one vertex together with the splat's attributes except the color. The splat's center is transformed to window coordinates and the normal is transformed to eye-space. The image-space square size of the splat is computed using equation (6) and back-facing splats are discarded. In the fragment shader, we perform OST on the generated fragment. The perspective-correct depth value is computed and stored in the depth image for fragments that pass the test.

Attribute Pass: In this pass, we accumulate the attributes of the visible splats using an OpenGL Frame Buffer Object (FBO) so that lighting and shading can be done in the next pass. This pass is similar to the visibility pass except for several operations. Before the execution of this pass, we first disable the depth writing, then we enable alpha blending and set the blending function using glBlendFuncSeparate(GL\_SRC\_ALPHA, GL\_ONE, GL\_ONE, GL\_ONE). This will allow the attributes to blend properly. In the vertex shader, we do the same operations as in the vertex shader of visibility pass, except that we are sending all the splat's attributes to the rendering pipeline. In the fragment shader, instead of computing the depth value, we compute the weighted normal, color, and position q (7) for fragments that pass OST and store them in separate color buffer in the FBO.

Shading Pass: In this pass, we render a window-size rectangle to send each pixel through the pipeline. This time, we enable depth writing and disable alpha blending. We also enable multitexturing so that the color buffers in FBO can be accessed by the programmable shaders. The vertex shader uses default OpenGL vertex processing, whereas the fragment shader normalizes the weighted normal, color, and position accessed from the color buffers, and performs per-pixel shading.

### 5. Experimental Results

We experiment our approach on several 3D models by computing the size and variance for each splat using equation (1) and (5) respectively, and we render the models using the approach described in Section 4.

Figure 3 and 4 illustrates the rendering outcome of Dragon and Hand model respectively. In each figure, the top row shows the output rendered using polygon rendering, whereas the bottom row shows the output rendered using surface splatting with the each splat's size and variance computed using equation (1) and (5). For each row, the left side illustrates the full view of the model, while the right side illustrates the close-up view of the model of the small rectangle window on the left side. From these figures, the rendering outcome on distant view between polygon rendering and surface splatting is hardly distinguishable. However, on closeup view, the color blends more smoothly with surface splatting compared to polygon rendering. Furthermore, using today's GPU and employing programmable shaders, we found that surface splatting performs better than polygon rendering. For the Dragon and Hand model, the average elapsed time taken per frame is less than 0.3 milliseconds for surface splatting, which highly surpasses polygon rendering which takes more than 20 milliseconds on the same models.

To gauge the effectiveness of our size and variance determination method, we tried different configurations of splats' size and variance on the Bunny model and compare its quality with the one rendered using our configuration. Figure 5 presents the rendering outcomes of Bunny whose splats' size and variance are generated using 9 different configurations, in which (e) shows the outcome that uses our configuration ri and vi as shown in equation (1) and (5). For images above (e), which are (a), (b), and (c), the model's splats' size is less than our configuration (75% of ri ) whereas for images below (e), which are (g), (h), and (i), the splats' size is larger (125% of ri ). On the other hand, images on the left of (e) use larger variance than vi , while those on the right of (e) uses smaller variance than vi . To do this, we change the constant k in equation (5) to the variable kc , and vary kc to adjust the variance value.

From the results presented in Figure 5, our automatic size and variance determination methods seemed to strike the balance between blurriness and aliasing. The Bunny models that are represented with splat's size smaller than our configuration exhibit disturbing aliasing effect (Figure 5(a), (b), and (c)), while those that use splat's size larger than our configuration shows high level of blurriness (Figure 5(g), (h), and (i)). Furthermore, models whose splats' variance is higher than our configuration displays noticeable discontinuities among the overlapping splats, while models whose splats' variance is lower than our configuration causes aliasing effect. We did not manage to prove theoretically that our method will strike the balance for all types of model.

#### 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we have demonstrated a method to improve the quality of surface splatting to the level comparable to polygon rendering on close-up view by automatically determining the suitable size and variance for each splat. Although we did not manage to establish theoretical guarantees to prove the optimality of our method, our experiments show positive result on the models we have tested. More importantly, our method is automated and thus does not require manual adjustment from the user to improve the quality of the rendered image.

There are still a lot of works that needs to be done. First of all, our method can be applied to elliptical splats. Using elliptical splats is better than circular splats as it can approximate the local surface more accurately. In addition to that, our optimal splat's size does not augur well with sharp features on the surface. This can be improved by clipping splats near the corners and edges of the model [Zwicker et al. 2004]. Besides that, the blending of Gaussian splats results in noticeable blurriness in the rendered image. We think that it may be possible to reduce the blurriness using some image processing techniques.
