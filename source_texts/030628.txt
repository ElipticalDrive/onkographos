Abstract
Gaussian Splatting (GS) is a promising scene representation for visual localization and SLAM. Recent works have
explored loop closure detection via Gaussian registration,
improving map consistency and accuracy. However, achieving reliable registration given two GS representations from
different acquisitions remains challenging. In this paper,
we propose a complete pipeline to perform the matching
and registration given two GS maps. The proposed method
is grounded in generating orthographic bird’s-eye views
(BEVs) of optimized Gaussian models. The proposed approach leverages photometric and geometric information
extracted directly from the GS to provide a trade-off of accuracy and invariance to different viewing changes (e.g.,
as types of GS maps, seasons, or illumination). Unlike existing 3D registration methods, which become inefficient
as the number of Gaussians grows, our approach leverages 2D orthographic renders thus considerably reducing
the registration complexity. Experiments on two public
datasets demonstrate that our method achieves higher accuracy than several existing baselines, while also maintaining better registration results when dealing with GS maps
learned by different techniques (e.g., 3DGS to LightGaussian), or GS maps presenting viewing changes such as varying illumination conditions. Source code is available at:
https://gitlab.inria.fr/tangram/bev-splatreg
1. Introduction
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) has advanced significantly with the emergence of neural rendering
techniques. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [25] have enabled photorealistic scene reconstructions, while 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [14] has recently improved rendering
speed and memory efficiency, offering compact and scalable scene representations. Several works have explored
integrating 3DGS into SLAM pipelines, especially with
RGB-D data [36]. Matsuki et al. [24] introduced the first
SLAM system relying solely on GS as a 3D representation for both monocular and RGB-D inputs. However, these
systems lack essential capabilities for ensuring global consistency, such as loop closure and map-level relocalization.
This motivates the need for robust 3D registration methods
that can align independently reconstructed GS maps–an essential step for enabling loop closure, relocalization, and
collaborative mapping in GS-based systems.
While Bird’s-Eye-View-based methods have shown effectiveness for loop closure and map alignment [4, 12, 23]
in traditional SLAM pipelines, they are typically designed
for point cloud data and do not leverage the photorealistic scene representations enabled by neural rendering. This
opens the door for exploring Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) style
registration directly on 3D GS models, which offer both geometric structure and visual richness. On the other hand, to
address 3DGS registration, several methods rely on a twostage pipeline using the 3DGS model alongside the original camera poses. GaussReg [5] uses learning-based point
cloud registration on Gaussian centers to obtain an initial
pose, which is then refined using volumetric features extracted from overlapping camera subsets. LoopSplat [39]
performs loop closure using NetVLAD descriptors to find
visually similar keyframes and assesses alignment using the
residuals from rendered Gaussian splats. PhotoReg [37]
goes further by leveraging 3D foundation models to estimate 3D structure from images, enabling coarse alignment
without depth sensors, and refines poses via photometric
loss. However, all these methods depend on the original
camera extrinsics and assume access to a specific GS training pipeline, which limits generalization.
In contrast, our work targets a more flexible and selfcontained solution: registration directly from the GS models alone, without requiring access to original training poses
or images. This is particularly important when such meta-

To address this, we propose a BEV-based approach for
aligning 3DGS maps. Inspired by bird’s-eye view (BEV)
techniques used in LiDAR-based loop closure [12, 19, 22,
23], we generate orthographic projections from the GS
models along a common known direction. This direction
is often the gravity vector, which is known in many datasets
captured with a vertical image plane; however, it can also
be reliably estimated in urban environments using vanishing
point (VP) detection, achieving high registration accuracy,
as shown in the experiments section of this paper. Orthographic projection allows us to estimate 2D in-plane transformations (rotation and translation), as well as the camera
BEV height. This reduces the 6-DoF registration problem
to 4 DoF and enables efficient alignment. An overview of
this approach is shown in Fig. 1. Optionally, a final refinement step can be applied to further optimize a full 6-DoF
registration by minimizing the similarity between renderings of the Gaussian Splatting models. Unlike typical BEV
methods that rely on point density or elevation maps, our
method leverages the anisotropic Gaussians on color intensity and Gaussian density, presenting a trade-off between
appearance distinctiveness and semantic invariance to scene
changes.
Our method is validated across indoor and outdoor environments, and we show that it remains robust when applied to GS models generated by different reconstruction
pipelines, as well as under changing weather conditions.
These experiments highlight the potential of GS-based registration methods that are input-agnostic, storage-efficient,
and applicable in real-world SLAM systems. To the best
of our knowledge, we are the first to propose a registration method for Gaussian Splatting scenes that does not rely
on training camera poses, demonstrating its effectiveness in
both indoor and outdoor environments. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• We propose a lightweight 3D registration method of two
GS maps based on orthographic BEV renders of GS models. Our model estimates 4 DOF: the BEV camera height,
followed by 2D in-plane transformation (rotation and 2D
translation). In addition, we show how gravity vectors
can be robustly obtained from zenith VP detections, and
an optional refinement step is proposed for more accurate
registration.
• Our method demonstrates robustness to variations in the
underlying 3D Gaussian splatting reconstruction pipeline,
addressing the challenge of aligning GS models generated
using different algorithms (e.g., 3DGS or LightGaussian).
• We validate the approach on multiple public datasets,
including both indoor and outdoor scenes, and provide
comprehensive experiments demonstrating its accuracy
and robustness under diverse conditions.
2. Related Work
2.1. Point Cloud Registration
3D point cloud registration estimates the spatial transformation between two overlapping point clouds captured from
different viewpoints. Classical methods typically follow a
two-step pipeline: correspondence searching using handcrafted features (e.g., FPFH [31], SHOT [35]), followed by
transformation estimation. Techniques like RANSAC [10]
offer robustness to outliers, while ICP [2] and its variant colored ICP [26] iteratively refine alignment using geometric
and color information. Fast Global Registration [38] (FGR)
formulates the problem as a robust global optimization
for improved scalability and convergence domain. Recent
learning-based approaches, such as GeoTransformer [28],
use geometry-aware transformer architectures and overlapaware losses to improve matching. Despite these advances,
classical methods remain strong baselines, especially in
scenarios with limited supervision or real-time constraints.
These methods can struggle with noise, occlusion, and density variation, as shown in the experiments section.
2.2. Bird’s Eye View (BEV) Representations
Bird’s Eye View (BEV) representations have recently
gained attention for their ability to compress 3D point cloud
information into 2D while preserving spatial structure. This
projection simplifies computation and provides robustness
to viewpoint changes, making it attractive for tasks like loop
closure and localization [16, 17, 19, 22, 23]. Most existing
BEV-based methods are LiDAR-centric and focus primarily
on place recognition or coarse localization. Examples include ScanContext [16], ScanContext++ [17], ContourContext [13], BVMatch [22], and other global descriptor methods [19, 23]. These approaches typically operate on elevation or density maps derived from LiDAR scans but do not
estimate 3D transformations. MapClosure [12] take a step
further by computing 2D relative poses between local map
projections using density images, which can then be refined
with geometric registration techniques. This demonstrates
the potential of BEV projections to support accurate point
cloud alignment, particularly when combined with classical registration methods. In parallel, image-based BEV
methods have emerged in the context of cross-view geolocalization and autonomous driving. These methods aim to
learn BEV features from RGB images captured by ground
or multi-camera rigs. Notable examples include BEVCV [32], which transforms ground-level images to BEV
for geo-localization, U-BEV [3] and BEVSegFormer [27]
for BEV-based scene segmentation and relocalization, and
BEVFormer [20], which use spatiotemporal transformers to
build BEV representations from sequential image inputs.
Incorporating suitable BEV-based image descriptors suitable to registration pipelines with scene changes remains
an open and promising research direction.
to the 3DGS model. Typically, a coarse-to-fine strategy
is adopted: an initial alignment is followed by refinement
guided by known camera viewpoints. For instance, GaussReg [5] performs learning-based point cloud registration on
the Gaussians using GeoTransformer [28] to obtain a coarse
pose, which is then refined using volumetric features extracted from overlapping camera subsets. LoopSplat [39]
addresses loop closure by leveraging NetVLAD-based [1]
descriptors to identify visually similar viewpoints between
submaps, treating registration as a keyframe localization
problem. Residuals from rendered Gaussian splats are used
to evaluate alignment quality. PhotoReg [37] removes the
need for depth sensors by using 3D foundation models to
reconstruct scene geometry from 2D image pairs, enabling
coarse alignment even with minimal overlap, followed by
photometric refinement. However, these approaches rely
on the availability of the training-time camera poses during
registration. This dependency poses practical limitations –
especially when extrinsics are not preserved or when avoiding storage of auxiliary training data is desirable. Moreover, they have a high sensitivity to GS changes (e.g., type
of GS map), and none of these methods operate directly on
the 3DGS model alone, motivating the development of approaches that perform registration purely on the Gaussian
representation.

3.2. BEV Rendering and Matching
Principle: Inspired by BEV properties for localization [12,
19, 22], our method leverages the efficient rendering capabilities of Gaussian splatting to compress 3D scene information into a orthographic 2D BEV render. An orthographic
camera is solely defined by its projection direction. In our
method, the projection of the GSs is done along a preferred
global direction, often the vertical axis in our experiments,
though the method is not restricted to this choice. To ensure
a consistent scale across different scenes, we define the resolution per pixel in metric units and generate BEV images
with a fixed metric resolution. We also limit the scene area
which is projected in accordance with the camera path. As
a result, while the generated BEVs can be of different size,
they can be matched by a 2D isometric transform Rx + T
due to the fixed metric resolution. In addition, using orthographic projection along the same direction avoid perspective distortions typically introduced by traditional rendering
perspective methods.
Gravity detection: We assume that a dominant scene direction is known—typically the gravity vector. This assumption holds for many datasets where the image plane
is aligned vertically. Alternatively, the gravity vector may
be estimated through detection of the vertical VP (zenith), a
task that is generally straightforward in man-made environments where many lines converge toward it. For this purpose, we rely on the robust a-contrario method [33], which
identifies the zenith as the VP supported by the largest set
of converging segments. In practice, one zenith is estimated
for each image in the GS dataset. Some estimates, however, may be unreliable, as near-vertical structures such as
fences, railings, or trees can produce misleading directions.
To mitigate this, we select a representative gravity vector by
computing the medoid [34] of all estimates, i.e., the vector
minimizing the sum of cosine distances to the others —note
that the same procedure can be applied to a gravity vector
from an IMU.
Rendering Orthographic BEV: To generate the BEV orthographic renders, we position a virtual camera above
each scene using a systematic approach designed to ensure
comprehensive coverage and consistency across different
submaps. We start by defining a fixed spatial metric resolution per pixel, which guarantees a uniform level of detail for
all scenes. This resolution then defines the rendered image
size. To prevent excessively large renders caused by outlier points in the GS maps, we discard the Gaussians that
are significantly distant from a fixed threshold relative to
the median position of each GS scene centroid. Additionally, a nearest-neighbor filter removes isolated Gaussians
that could introduce noise in the final render. After cleaning the Gaussian cloud, we compute its bounding box in the
camera coordinate system associated with each GS and proceed with the rendering. From each cleaned GS model, we
render color, depth, and Gaussian density images to capture both photometric and spatial information in the BEV
domain.
Matching BEVs: After generating the orthographic renders
for the Gaussian Splatting submaps GA and GB , we employ
a feature-based matching approach to compute the rigid
transformation between them. We adopt SuperPoint [7]
for keypoint detection and description, followed by LightGlue [21] for feature matching, processing the density and
RGB renders in parallel. The matches obtained from both
modalities are then fused to compute a set of joint correspondences. We fuse matches from both density and RGB
renderings to combine the appearance invariance of density
(e.g., to viewing changes between the GS maps) with the
discriminative capabilities of RGB, improving robustness
in ambiguous or low-texture scenarios. The 2D rigid transform is finally estimated using a RANSAC-based alignment strategy. This initial 3-DOF transform then allows
to perform depth map alignment to obtain height estima-
tion. Unlike traditional alpha blending used in depth and
color rendering—which may introduce errors when Gaussian opacities are low and viewpoint changes are extreme—
we propose an adapted depth rendering approach. Instead of
integrating over all Gaussians, we define the depth at each

4. Experiments
We evaluate our registration method through a series of experiments designed to assess its accuracy, robustness, and
generalizability to different types of GS maps. First, we
compare our approach against several baseline methods on
two standard public datasets in sections 4.5 and 4.6 to establish performance under typical conditions. Next, we
test the robustness of our method across various types of
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) reconstructions, examining
its adaptability to different scene representations in section
4.7. We further evaluate performance under varying lighting
conditions to demonstrate invariance to appearance changes
in section 4.8. Finally, we conduct ablation and sensitivity studies to analyze the impact of key components in our
pipeline and to validate robustness against depth noise and
parameter variations in Sec. 4.9.

4.2. Metrics
We evaluate our method using the same registration metrics
adopted by previous works [5, 9]. Specifically, we measure
Relative Rotational Error (RRE) between the two maps,
Relative Translation Error (RTE) and registration success
ratio. As defined in GaussReg [5] and NeRFuser [9], we
computed this last metric as the proportion of successful
registrations (SR), where a registration is considered successful if the RTE is inferior to 0.2 and RRE remains below
5 degrees. This metric provides an overall measure of the
robustness and reliability of the methods. It is important to
note that the reported metrics are directly tied to the success
ratio. Specifically, they are only calculated for successfully
registered scenes, meaning that focusing solely on the metrics without considering the SR may provide a misleading
interpretation.

scenarios, with particularly strong gains observed in challenging low-overlap conditions (0.2 and 0.4) while maintaining a competitive runtime. The results obtained with the
estimated gravity, denoted as Ours + VP, demonstrate that
comparable accuracy can be achieved without prior knowledge of the reference direction. The final row, Ours + refinement, reports results after the refinement stage, showing noticeable improvements in both rotation and translation accuracy. This step, however, incurs a relatively high
computational cost due to the use of a Powell optimization
implemented in Python. These experiments illustrate that
our method delivers high-quality registrations, which can be
further refined when required. Finally, regarding memory
footprint, the average GPU memory footprint of the registration process was 1.5 GB. Additional details are provided
in the supplementary material.
4.6. Comparison on ScanNet
Table 2 summarizes the registration performance of various methods across different overlap ratios on the ScanNet
dataset. For these interior scenes, we do not use estimated
gravity, as the images often have narrow fields of view that
make reliable VP estimation difficult. Our approach consistently achieves the highest SR across all overlap levels,
indicating superior robustness in challenging indoor environments. Additionally, our method yields the lowest RTE
and RRE for nearly all overlap ranges, demonstrating precise and reliable pose estimation. Notably, in scenarios with
high overlap (0.6), our method attains perfect success (SR
= 1) with minimal translation and rotation errors. The runtime of our method on ScanNet is significantly lower than
on Tanks and Temples, mainly due to the smaller number
of Gaussians in indoor scenes. Other methods also benefit
from faster runtimes, likely due to the more discriminative
indoor geometry. Even in low-overlap cases (0.2), where
registration is typically more difficult, our approach maintains high success and significantly better accuracy compared to other methods. Although the GaussReg [5] model
was trained on ScanNet data, we observed that the performance reported in their paper could not be fully reproduced
using the publicly available code and weights.
4.7. Cross GS Comparison
Recent advances in Gaussian Splatting have led to more
compact and efficient representations, such as LightGaussian [8] and SuGaR [11], which improve scalability, memory efficiency, and geometric fidelity. These improvements
raise the question of how well registration methods generalize across different GS variants, especially in practical scenarios where submaps may originate from different reconstruction pipelines. To explore this, Table 3 reports performance in two settings: intra-variant matching within 3DGS,
LightGaussian, or SuGaR models, and cross-variant matching between them. This evaluation provides insight into the
robustness of competitors under architectural variations in
the GS representation. Our method maintains strong performance within each GS type, achieving significantly better results than existing methods. In the cross-model setting—where submaps are reconstructed with different variants such as 3DGS, LightGaussian, or SuGaR—we observe
a performance drop, which is expected due to differences in
point distribution, density, and surface alignment (some examples are shown in Fig. 1 of the supplementary). Nonetheless, our approach still outperforms the strongest considered
baselines.
4.8. Lighting Condition Robustness
To evaluate robustness under real-world lighting changes,
we perform experiments on the first two scenes from
the NeRF-OSR [30] dataset: Galerie Europa and Ludwigskirche. For each scene, we select a sequence of images
taken at different times of the day with significant lighting
variations– such as transitions from gray to sunny weather,
or sunrise to sunset (some examples are provided in Fig.
2 in the supplementary). We compare our method in two
configurations (with RGB only and RGB-density information) against state-of-the-art methods. As shown in Tab. 4,
our approach maintains perfect registration accuracy across
all tested conditions, while existing methods fail or degrade
significantly in the presence of strong illumination changes.
4.9. Ablation and Sensitivity Analysis
Ablation on feature modality. To assess the contribution
of the different input modalities used in our 2D matching
pipeline, we conduct an ablation study comparing performance with RGB-only inputs versus the combined use of
RGB and density projections derived from the 3DGS models. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, while the RGB-only variant
already achieves strong performance across most settings,
the addition of density maps consistently improves both registration accuracy and success rate. This gain is particularly
noticeable in challenging scenarios involving low-overlap
regions or significant appearance variations. The density
images, which encode structural and spatial information inherent to the Gaussian representation, complement the photometric features from RGB and help disambiguate regions
with appearance changes. These results confirm that leveraging both modalities leads to more robust and accurate registration.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we present a novel registration method for
Gaussian Splatting models based on bird’s-eye view projections, enabling robust map alignment without relying on
original images and camera poses. Our extensive evaluation across multiple datasets indicates that our approach
consistently outperforms existing methods in terms of accuracy (RRE, RTE) and robustness (SR) in both indoor
and outdoor scenarios. The proposed method generalizes
well across different types of Gaussian Splatting reconstructions, and outperforms other registration methods on
cross-model registration between standard 3DGS, LightGaussian and SuGaR. Furthermore, our approach remains
competitive under challenging lighting and weather conditions, where other methods experience significant degradation. These results highlight the effectiveness and practical
applicability of our BEV-based registration method.
While our method shows strong performance on multiple scenarios, future work will focus on improving robustness in cross-representation scenarios and scaling to very
large datasets, for example, through adapting the method
to recent hierarchical Gaussian representations [15]. These
extensions could directly benefit SLAM pipelines, particularly for relocalization across time and varying conditions,
enabling more reliable long-term operation.
