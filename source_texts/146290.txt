A BSTRACT
Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) promises instant scene reconstruction with high-quality novel-view synthesis. Yet, fine-grained geometry and appearance remain difficult to recover, motivating us to revisit how feed-forward
models represent, aggregate, and refine detailed visual information. We introduce FineSplat, a feed-forward 3DGS framework built around fine-grained image
patches. Since global multi-view attention becomes prohibitively expensive at
such high token resolutions, we develop a dual-grouping strategy that combines
two complementary forms of locality: view-wise grouping enables efficient interaction among neighboring views, while 3D spatial grouping reconnects geometrically related tokens that may never have interacted under view-wise grouping
by lifting them into the 3D scene space. We further leverage reconstruction error
as direct feedback, providing an explicit rendering-based signal to recover finegrained structures and appearance, thereby overcoming the limitations of onepass feed-forward prediction. Finally, a compact Gaussian generation scheme
preserves the benefits of fine-grained features without unnecessarily increasing
the number of primitives. Together, these designs enable FineSplat to recover
richer geometry and appearance while remaining efficient and compact, achieving
state-of-the-art results by a substantial margin, under the same or smaller Gaussian
budgets than existing methods.

I NTRODUCTION
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025b) has emerged as
a powerful representation for high-quality and real-time novel view synthesis, combining an explicit
3D scene representation with efficient differentiable rasterization. Since conventional 3DGS relies
on iterative per-scene optimization to achieve high reconstruction quality, it typically requires substantial computational resources for every new scene, limiting its scalability and applicability. To
overcome these limitations, recent works have explored feed-forward 3DGS, where a single network
forward pass directly predicts Gaussian representations from a set of input images. These approaches
have rapidly advanced in both posed (Charatan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a; Ziwen et al., 2025;
Kang et al., 2026a;b) and pose-free (Zhang et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025a; 2026; An et al., 2025)
settings, enabling fast reconstruction with promising generalization. However, replacing iterative
per-scene refinement with a single feed-forward prediction places a much greater burden on the network, requiring it to resolve complex multi-view ambiguities and recover fine-grained scene details
all at once. As a result, existing feed-forward approaches still exhibit a considerable reconstructionquality gap compared with per-scene optimization methods, particularly in recovering fine-grained
geometry and appearance details.
Many recent feed-forward 3DGS methods build upon powerful 3D foundation models, benefiting
from their strong geometric priors and multi-view reasoning capabilities. However, these models
commonly inherit the relatively coarse patch resolution of their geometry backbones, typically using 14 × 14 or 16 × 16 image patches (Wang et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026d).
Such large patches provide broader spatial context that supports large-scale geometric reasoning
and helps disambiguate cross-view correspondences, particularly when fine patches contain insufficiently distinctive local visual cues. However, compressing a broad image region into a single
token can discard the high-frequency cues needed to reconstruct thin structures, sharp boundaries,
textures, and other fine-grained details. Moreover, attention between coarse tokens operates at a limited spatial resolution, which can make it more difficult to precisely localize corresponding image
regions across views. Finer patch tokens, particularly with attention between them, may therefore
offer a promising avenue for preserving richer local visual cues and enabling more spatially precise
cross-view interactions.
Yet, finer tokenization introduces a fundamental computational challenge. Most multi-view transformers rely on global self-attention across tokens from multiple input images, whose quadratic
complexity quickly becomes prohibitive as the number of views, image resolution, and token density increase. A practical alternative is to restrict the attention scope. For example, MVP (Kang
et al., 2026b) partitions input views into small groups and performs fine-grained multi-view attention only within each group, substantially improving the efficiency of high-resolution multi-view
reconstruction while maintaining strong accuracy. Such view-wise grouping is particularly effective
when the inputs are sequentially captured and nearby views are likely to observe overlapping scene
regions. However, the restriction is ultimately imposed in view space rather than scene space: tokens corresponding to the same 3D surface may fall into different view groups and therefore never
directly interact. Consequently, any fixed view grouping inevitably creates blind spots, excluding
potentially relevant correspondences simply because they lie across group boundaries.
In this work, we propose FineSplat, which addresses this challenge through a dual-grouping strategy
that exploits two complementary forms of locality. First, we perform fine-grained multi-view interaction within small groups of neighboring views, enabling efficient attention over high-resolution
tokens. We then lift the resulting tokens into 3D space using their predicted scene locations and regroup them according to 3D spatial proximity. This allows tokens from different, potentially distant
view groups to interact when they correspond to nearby regions of the underlying 3D scene, recovering geometric relationships missed by view-wise grouping alone. By combining view-wise locality
with 3D spatial locality, FineSplat enables rich interactions among fine-grained tokens without the
prohibitive cost of global multi-view attention.
A faithful 3D reconstruction should reproduce the input images when rendered from their viewpoints, which provides a natural signal for assessing its quality. Conventional per-scene optimization
repeatedly exploits this signal, comparing rendered and observed images to identify residual errors
and progressively correct the scene representation. A one-pass feed-forward model, in contrast,
typically produces its reconstruction without an opportunity to inspect and correct its own errors.

Motivated by this observation and recent work (Xu et al., 2026), we explicitly leverage this readily
available feedback as a direct signal in the second stage of our proposed framework. This provides
the network with a second chance to recover structures and fine details that were missed in the first
stage, without resorting to costly iterative optimization.
We further introduce a compact Gaussian generation scheme that predicts only a small number of
Gaussians from each patch token, rather than densely generating pixel-aligned Gaussians. This
design allows the model to retain the benefits of fine-grained image features without increasing
the number of Gaussian primitives. Together, the fine-grained representation, dual-grouping strategy, reconstruction error-driven correction, and compact Gaussian generation enable substantially
improved reconstruction quality while maintaining an efficient and compact 3D representation. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art rendering quality by a
substantial margin in both posed and pose-free settings, while using the same or fewer Gaussians
than existing methods.

R ELATED W ORKS
Feed-forward 3DGS and compact Gaussian representation. Feed-forward 3DGS methods
amortize per-scene optimization (Kerbl et al., 2023) by directly predicting Gaussians from input images in a single forward pass. One common design is pixel-aligned prediction, where each pixel is
unprojected along its camera ray into one Gaussian, using epipolar attention (Charatan et al., 2024),
cost volumes (Chen et al., 2024a; Xu et al., 2025), or large transformers (Zhang et al., 2024; Ziwen
et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026a). This keeps the geometry well-conditioned
and helps preserve fine appearance, but the number of Gaussians grows linearly with resolution and
view count, often producing many redundant primitives in overlapping regions (Park et al., 2025;
Kim et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2026). In contrast, non-pixel-aligned methods predict a compact
set of Gaussians from learnable tokens, clustered scene features, 3D anchors, or voxels (Ren et al.,
2026; An et al., 2025; Veicht et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Moreau et al., 2026; Zhang et al.,
2026a; Itkin et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026b), but lose the explicit pixel-ray
correspondence, which tends to hurt fine detail and makes optimization harder. Between these extremes, patch-aligned methods predict Gaussians at a reduced spatial resolution (Kang et al., 2026a;
Xu et al., 2026). In our framework, we extend this patch-aligned approach by generating only a
small number of Gaussians along the rays of each sub-patch, and further compact the representation
by pruning Gaussians according to their rendering contribution to the context views.
Pose-free feed-forward 3DGS and geometry foundation models. Geometry foundation models (Wang et al., 2024; Leroy et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; 2026d; Lin et al., 2025) estimate
camera poses and dense geometry directly from unposed images. Building on such models, posefree methods jointly predict camera poses and Gaussians (Ye et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025; Jiang
et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2025), mostly in a pixel-aligned manner on top of the
geometry backbone. However, these backbones operate on relatively coarse image tokens, which
are well suited for global geometric reasoning but may discard local appearance cues important for
fine-grained Gaussian reconstruction. To compensate, existing methods often fine-tune the backbone end-to-end so that its pretrained features adapt to appearance reconstruction. This adaptation
is computationally expensive, and the model still relies on representations shaped by the coarse tokenization, which may limit its ability to recover fine-grained appearance. Recent works instead
decouple geometry estimation from appearance modeling (Zhang et al., 2025; Jeong et al., 2026).
We follow this decoupled design for pose-free reconstruction, using a 3D foundation model, such as
DA3 (Lin et al., 2025), to predict the camera poses while FineSplat performs fine-grained Gaussian
prediction.
Combining image-space and 3D locality. Many useful cues for 3D reconstruction, such as dense
image features and rendering errors, are naturally defined in image space, where they are grouped
by proximity in the image plane rather than in the underlying scene. Several works therefore bring
such image-space cues into 3D and process them according to 3D spatial locality. ODIN (Jain et al.,
2024) alternates 2D within-view layers with 3D cross-view attention over unprojected features, and
DITR (Knaebel et al., 2026) projects DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) features onto 3D points and
injects them into a Point Transformer V3 (Wu et al., 2024) backbone. MoGe-3 (Kong et al., 2026)

Iterative refinement. Iterative minimization of an objective has long been a standard approach
to solving vision problems (Lucas & Kanade, 1981; Sun et al., 2010), and learning-based methods
have sought to emulate this process by repeatedly updating an initial estimate. Such learned iterative
refinement often outperforms single-step regression and has been successfully applied to a broad
range of applications, such as optical flow (Teed & Deng, 2020), human pose estimation (Newell
et al., 2016; Carreira et al., 2016), stereo matching (Lipson et al., 2021), SLAM (Teed & Deng,
2021), structure-from-motion (Li et al., 2025), multi-view stereo (Wang et al., 2022), and multiview 3D reconstruction (Burzio et al., 2026). For Gaussian splatting, prior works refine Gaussians
with a single-step or multiple-step network (Chen et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2026a),
learn updates from rendering gradients (Chen et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2025; 2026; Pearl et al., 2026)
or rendering errors (Xu et al., 2026), or fine-tune Gaussian tokens for each scene (Ren et al., 2026).

M ETHOD
Given N context views with or without known camera poses, FineSplat predicts a set of 3D Gaussians in a feed-forward manner. Stage 1 divides each context view into fine-grained 4 × 4 patches
and encodes them with attention of varying scope, where group-wise attention over a few views
keeps fine-grained multi-view reasoning affordable. Stage 2 decodes these tokens into an initial set
of Gaussians, renders them back into the context views, and uses the resulting error maps to update
the tokens through 3D spatial grouping, which lets tokens that fall in different view groups interact
once they are lifted to 3D. This dual-grouping strategy makes fine-grained encoding tractable, with
3D spatial grouping complementing view-wise grouping by connecting tokens across view groups.
Figure 2 gives an overview of the two stages.

The fixed view grouping strategy inevitably leaves blind spots: fine-grained tokens in different
groups cannot directly interact, potentially missing relevant observations from other input views.

3D spatial grouping stage.
3.2
S TAGE 2: 3D SPATIAL TOKEN GROUPING
Starting from the fine-grained tokens z (0) produced by the first stage, we first decode an initial set
of 3D Gaussians. Specifically, a lightweight Gaussian head D, implemented as a linear decoder,
maps each token to K Gaussian primitives and their attributes. We then use the predicted Gaussian
centers to lift the corresponding tokens into 3D space, where tokens are regrouped according to their
spatial proximity in 3D world coordinates. We then perform self-attention within each 3D group,
enabling fine-grained interactions between geometrically related tokens even when they originate
from different view groups. To further refine the current estimate, we augment this interaction with
reconstruction-error information obtained by rendering the input views. The updated tokens are
subsequently decoded into a new set of Gaussian primitives, and this process of Gaussian prediction,
3D grouping, error-guided interaction, and token update is repeated for multiple steps.

3.3
PATCH - ALIGNED S UB - PIXEL G AUSSIAN R ECONSTRUCTION
To obtain a dense Gaussian representation while preserving the spatial structure of image features,
we predict four Gaussians for each 4 × 4 fine-grained token, assigning each Gaussian to one of
four non-overlapping 2 × 2 sub-patches. Rather than constraining a Gaussian to the fixed center
ray of its assigned sub-patch, we learn a two-dimensional image-space offset ∆ = (∆x , ∆y ) that
adaptively shifts its ray within the corresponding region. Specifically, for a sub-patch with stride
s = 2, we bound the offset as δx = 2s tanh (∆x ), δy = 2s tanh (∆y ), such that δx , δy ∈ [−s/2, s/2].
This guarantees that each Gaussian remains within its designated 2 × 2 sub-patch while allowing
continuous sub-pixel localization. Given the center (uc , vc ) of each sub-patch, the displaced image
coordinate is (u, v) = (uc , vc ) + δ, which is unprojected using the predicted camera intrinsics. We
then transform and normalize this direction using the camera-to-world rotation, with the camera
translation serving as the corresponding ray origin.

E XPERIMENTS
4.1
I MPLEMENTATION D ETAILS
Setup. We train a single model only on the DL3DV (Ling et al., 2024) dataset, sampling the number of input context views from {6, 12, 24, 32} for each training scene, and use this one checkpoint
for all evaluations without dataset-specific fine-tuning. On DL3DV, we evaluate on 140 test scenes
with N ∈ {6, 12, 24} context views, selected via farthest point sampling with maximum temporal
frame gaps of 50, 100, and 150, respectively. We further evaluate zero-shot generalization on 1,600
RealEstate10K (RE10K) (Zhou et al., 2018) test scenes with N = 6, and on Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron
et al., 2022) with N ∈ {32, 64, 128}, following the view indices from Veicht et al. (2026). MipNeRF 360 is a demanding target for a model trained on DL3DV: it covers both indoor and outdoor
scenes, its scenes are unbounded, its cameras orbit the scene rather than sweep through it, and it
uses far more context views than the 32 seen during training. We hold out 8 target views on all three
datasets. For a fair comparison, each method encodes and renders at its native training resolution,
and all rendered outputs are resized to 224 × 224 for evaluation, following Ye et al. (2026) (252
× 252 results are provided in Appendix B.3). We report PSNR, SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), and
LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), averaged over all test scenes.

Supervision and scene normalization. FineSplat is trained without any depth supervision, using
only a mean squared error term and a perceptual term on the rendered views. Several of the baselines
instead rely on depth: AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), DepthSplat (Xu et al., 2025), NoDrift3R (Sun
et al., 2026), DA3 (Lin et al., 2025), and ZipSplat (Veicht et al., 2026) all use a depth objective during
training. Since feed-forward reconstruction is scale-ambiguous, we bring each scene to a canonical
scale using only the context camera poses: poses are expressed in the mean context camera frame and
translations are rescaled so that all context camera centers lie within [−1, 1]3 . The same transform is
applied to the target poses, both ground-truth and DA3-predicted. ZipSplat instead derives the scene
scale from depth at evaluation time, unprojecting per-view depth maps predicted by DA3 and taking
the median distance of the resulting points to the first context camera. Details of the scene scale
normalization are provided in Appendix A.3. Since we evaluate on the same Mip-NeRF 360 scenes
and view indices as ZipSplat, the two differ only in how this scale is obtained: its normalization
depends on a dense depth prior, whereas ours needs only camera positions and transfers directly to
predicted poses.
Baselines. We compare against the pose-free methods AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), C3G (An
et al., 2025), DA3 (Lin et al., 2025), YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026), NoDrift3R (Sun et al., 2026),
2Xplat (Jeong et al., 2026), and ZipSplat (Veicht et al., 2026), as well as NoPoSplat (Ye et al.,
2025a), which additionally requires ground-truth camera intrinsics. For pose-dependent methods,
we compare against DepthSplat (Xu et al., 2025) and MVP (Kang et al., 2026b).
4.2
N OVEL VIEW SYNTHESIS
Benchmark performance. As shown in Table 1, FineSplat consistently outperforms previous
state-of-the-art methods in both posed and fully pose-free settings across all input-view configurations. Compared with 2Xplat, ours improves PSNR by up to +2.88 dB in the posed setting and
+2.75 dB in the pose-free setting, while using about 4 times fewer Gaussians. Note that we compare
with the 2Xplat variant that does not leverage view-dependent opacity for a fair comparison. In addition, ours remains substantially more stable as the number of views increases, which we attribute to
our two-stage dual-grouping formulation with fine-grained tokens that better disambiguates redundant and highly overlapping regions across views, reducing conflicting Gaussian predictions. Moreover, some methods achieve competitive PSNR but relatively high LPIPS, whereas ours achieves
both high PSNR and low LPIPS. We attribute this to predicting Gaussians along the rays of finegrained patches, which better preserves the fine structure of the scene beyond photometric accuracy.
Figure 3 shows representative qualitative results.
Cross-dataset generalization. On RE10K, as shown in Table 2, ours achieves the best performance across PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS in the posed setting while using substantially fewer Gaussians, and it is also the best among the zero-shot methods in the pose-free setting. Our model has
only seen DL3DV, whereas the methods shown in gray are trained on RE10K itself. Although differences in training data make direct comparisons less controlled, these results nevertheless demonstrate compelling reconstruction quality and cross-dataset generalization. Qualitative comparisons
are shown in Figure 4.
On Mip-NeRF 360, presented in Table 3, FineSplat achieves the highest PSNR at every view count
in both settings, by 2.49 to 3.33 dB in the posed setting and 0.88 to 1.49 dB in the pose-free setting.
It also makes the best use of additional views: from 32 to 128 context views the baselines gain at
most 0.5 dB or lose ground, while ours rises by 1.32 dB in the posed setting and 1.00 dB in the
pose-free setting, with fewer Gaussians than the pixel-aligned baselines. We attribute this to Stage
2, which lets tokens observing the same region exchange information in 3D instead of producing
redundant predictions. Figure 5 presents the qualitative results.

Cross-group interactions in 3D space. Stage 2 combines two error signals for each token. The
self error feature eself provides a direct signal from the patch where the token is predicted, while the
aggregated error feature eagg averages the error features over all context views in which the token
is visible, including views outside the view group defined in Stage 1. Tokens then exchange these
signals with their neighbors in 3D space through serialized attention (Wu et al., 2024). Stage 2
consists of four attention blocks and each block uses a different one of the four space-filling curves,
so that each token meets a different set of neighbors. Figure 6 visualizes the window of a query
token, which is a single 4×4 patch in the source view. Its neighbors come from many different
frames yet lie close to the query in 3D, showing that tokens observing the same surface can exchange
information across views and view groups.

setting yields no further gain.
Efficiency. Figure 7 plots reconstruction time against quality for a representative pixel-aligned
model, YoNoSplat, and a patch-aligned model, ZipSplat. Both operate on 14 × 14 patches, while
FineSplat works with much finer 4 × 4 patches and remains the fastest of the three with 6 and 12
input views. With 24 views it is still faster than ZipSplat and costs moderately more time than
YoNoSplat, which produces four times as many Gaussians. Across view counts FineSplat returns
the largest quality gain per unit of time, leading the stronger of the two baselines by 2.6 dB with 6
input views and by 4.6 dB with 24 views. This efficiency comes from our dual-grouping strategy.
Instead of attending globally at every layer, Stage 1 exchanges information across neighboring views
in 2D and Stage 2 across spatially nearby Gaussians in 3D. Observations of the same surface from
different views are therefore consolidated rather than left as redundant or conflicting Gaussians.

C ONCLUSION AND D ISCUSSION
We presented FineSplat, a feed-forward 3DGS framework that preserves fine-grained visual information through high-resolution patch representations while avoiding the prohibitive cost of global
multi-view attention via a dual-grouping strategy that combines view-wise and 3D spatial locality. Together with the reconstruction feedback and compact Gaussian generation, we achieve strong
novel-view synthesis quality with a small primitive budget.
While our results suggest that fine-grained multi-view interaction is an important ingredient for
high-quality feed-forward reconstruction, we do not claim that fine patches are universally preferable: sufficiently large foundation models trained at scale may compensate for coarse tokenization.
Nevertheless, our findings indicate that, in the multi-view transformer, revisiting token granularity
and explicitly enabling fine-scale cross-view interaction can be highly effective. We also deliberately avoid relying on pretrained foundation-model features for Gaussian prediction, using DA3
only for camera pose estimation. In our preliminary experiments, combining such features with finegrained tokens did not show any noticeable improvement. Exploring how coarse, semantically and
geometrically rich foundation-model features can be more effectively integrated with fine-grained
local representations, particularly to improve generalization, remains a promising direction for future work.

A.1
A RCHITECTURE
Camera pose estimator. In the pose-free setting, camera poses are predicted by a pose estimator
initialized from DA3-Giant (Lin et al., 2025), following Jeong et al. (2026). It consists of 40 transformer layers with a hidden dimension of 1,536 and 24 attention heads, each with a head dimension
of 64. The predicted cameras are used to condition both Stage 1 and Stage 2.
View-wise grouping network. Stage 1 is based on MVP (Kang et al., 2026b) and adopts a 14layer hierarchical transformer that takes 4 × 4 image patches as input. As the tokens become coarser
through the layers, both the feature dimension and the range of attention increase. The first two
layers operate on 4 × 4 patch tokens with a feature dimension of 256, and both use frame-wise
attention within each view. The next four layers operate on 8 × 8 patch tokens with a feature
dimension of 512 and consist of two pairs of a frame-wise attention layer followed by a view-wise
group attention layer. For the group attention, the views are split into non-overlapping groups of two
frames that are adjacent in frame index. The final eight layers operate on 16 × 16 patch tokens with
a feature dimension of 1,024 and consist of four pairs of a frame-wise attention layer followed by a
global attention layer across all views. All attention modules use a head dimension of 64.
Error features. Stage 2 operates on the 4 × 4 patch tokens produced by the DPT head of Stage 1.
W
The error feature map is computed at the same H
4 × 4 resolution and has 256 channels. It consists of
a 128-channel error branch and a 128-channel image branch. The error branch combines the spaceto-depth pixel error with the feature difference of a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 (He
et al., 2016), using the conv1, layer1, and layer2 features (256 channels in total). The image branch
projects the 256-dimensional finest-level features. For each token, the error feature of its source
view and the mean over the views that pass the frustum and occlusion tests are concatenated and
projected to 256 channels. The 1024-dimensional token is projected to 512 channels, concatenated
with its error feature, and fused into a 512-dimensional point feature.
3D spatial grouping network. Stage 2 follows Point Transformer V3 (Wu et al., 2024) for 3D
serialization and grouping, and consists of four pre-norm transformer blocks with a feature dimension of 512 and an MLP ratio of 4. Self-attention is computed in a 256-dimensional subspace with
16 heads of dimension 16. The GS tokens lifted into 3D are voxelized on a 3843 grid over the
scene bounding box and serialized along four space-filling curves, namely Z-order, Hilbert, and
their transposed variants, with one curve assigned to each block. Each block then computes local
attention within groups of S = 8 consecutive points. A final linear layer maps the output back to a
1024-dimensional residual that updates each GS token. Stage 2 has 13.2M trainable parameters and
is applied with T = 4 steps, sharing the same weights across all steps.
A.2
T RAINING
FineSplat is trained in three phases. Stage 1 is trained first, Stage 2 is then trained on top of the frozen
Stage 1, and finally the whole model is fine-tuned end-to-end. In all phases, each training scene
samples its number of input views from {6, 12, 24, 32} with {6, 6, 6, 1} target views, respectively,
and the per-GPU batch is {4, 2, 1, 1} scenes. All models are trained on NVIDIA B200 GPUs.
Stage 1. Stage 1 is first trained with pixel-aligned Gaussian prediction for 120K iterations. The
pixel-aligned decoder is then replaced with our patch-aligned Gaussian decoder, and the model is
fine-tuned on 8 GPUs for an additional 80K iterations. The remaining setup, including the camera
losses on the predicted poses and intrinsics, follows Jeong et al. (2026).

A.4
O CCLUSION T EST FOR E RROR F EATURE AGGREGATION
To build eagg , each token gathers error features only from the views that actually observe its surface.
If another surface lies in front of the token in a view, the error at the projected pixel belongs to
that occluding surface and should not be assigned to the token. We therefore select views with a
frustum test and a depth test before averaging. Both tests use the same context cameras that place
the Gaussians, which are the ground-truth cameras in the posed setting and the predicted cameras in
the pose-free setting.
Frustum test. Each token j is represented by pj , the mean position of its four Gaussians (Eq. 2).
For every context view n we transform pj into the camera frame, which gives depth djn . We then
project it with the intrinsics to a pixel ujn = πn (pj ) = (u, v). The view passes the test if the point
lies in front of the camera (djn > 0) and ujn falls inside the image (0 ≤ u < W , 0 ≤ v < H).

B.1
E VALUATION -T IME P OSE A LIGNMENT
Pose-free reconstruction is inherently ambiguous, as multiple 3D scenes and camera configurations
can explain the same unposed inputs. Thus, the reconstructed scene may not be perfectly aligned
with the ground-truth evaluation frame. Following prior pose-free protocols (Wang et al., 2021; Fan
et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025a), we freeze the reconstructed 3D Gaussians and optimize each target
camera pose for 200 iterations using an L2 loss. This evaluation-time pose alignment (EPA) is used
only for pose-free settings. When ground-truth camera poses are available, we directly use them
without additional alignment, following YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026).

B.3
E VALUATION R ESOLUTION AND R ESIZE F ILTER
Images in the datasets are downsampled from their original resolution for both training and evaluation, and the reported metrics can vary noticeably depending on the image resolution and the
resizing filter used in this process. As shown in Figure 9, the same image yields visibly different
results under different filters. In the main paper, each method performs Gaussian reconstruction and
rendering at its native training resolution, which is 256 × 448 for DepthSplat, 224 × 224 for C3G,
YoNoSplat, MVP, NoDrift3R, and 2Xplat, 252 × 252 for ZipSplat, and 448 × 448 for AnySplat.
Only the final rendered and ground-truth target images are then resized to a common resolution of
224 × 224 for metric computation, following the evaluation protocol of Ye et al. (2026). Since our
method operates natively at 224 × 224, it uses the smallest input resolution among the compared
methods, so this protocol gives it no advantage from a larger input resolution or field of view. For
this resizing, we use PIL Lanczos as the default filter, following the protocol adopted by all baselines (Chen et al., 2024a; Xu et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025a; Jiang et al., 2025; An
et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026), except ZipSplat (Veicht et al., 2026).
We additionally report results at a different resolution and with a different resizing filter, following
the protocol of ZipSplat (Veicht et al., 2026). Specifically, we fine-tune our model at 252 × 252 and
perform both reconstruction and rendering at this resolution, where both the input images and the
ground-truth target images are resized with bilinear interpolation with anti-aliasing (Bilinear+AA).
Only ZipSplat is also trained on images resized with Bilinear+AA, while our model and the other
methods are trained on images resized with Lanczos. Since the evaluation protocol is identical, the
results of the baselines in Table 5 are taken from Veicht et al. (2026).

C.2
E RROR F EATURE C OMPOSITION
We examine the contribution of the two error features in Table 7. Removing either feature degrades
the quality in all settings, indicating that the two features provide complementary information. Removing eself causes the largest drop, from 0.44 dB with 6 views to 0.62 dB with 24 views in PSNR.
Since eself is sampled at the patch where the token is predicted, it provides the most direct signal of
how well the token explains its own observation, and the model relies on it most heavily. Removing
eagg leads to a smaller but consistent drop, which grows with the number of views from 0.09 dB
to 0.17 dB. This suggests that eagg becomes more useful as each token is observed by more views,
which we examine further below.
C.3
O BSERVATIONS B EYOND THE V IEW G ROUP
To examine whether this growing gain comes from observations outside the view group, we restrict
eagg at inference time to the views within the token’s own view group in Table 8. As the number of
views grows, most visible views lie outside the group, from 55.5% with 6 views to 84.0% with 24
views. We therefore compare this setting with a control that removes the same number of visible
views at random. Both settings discard the same number of observations, yet removing the views
outside the group consistently causes a larger drop, and the difference widens with the number of
views, from 0.04 dB with 6 views to 0.17 dB with 24 views. This shows that observations from other
view groups carry information that the views within the group cannot provide. Together with the
results in Table 4, these results indicate that FineSplat gathers observations beyond the view groups
of Stage 1 and exchanges them among tokens that are close in 3D, which keeps its quality stable as
the number of input views increases.

The pose-free setting shows the same trend from a lower starting point, as reconstruction quality
depends on how well the geometry of an unfamiliar domain is recovered from estimated poses. At

and normalizes scenes with context camera poses only. Even so, the gap closes quickly, and with

anchor tokens in 3D, indicating that the benefit of 3D spatial grouping carries over to domains and
view counts well beyond the training distribution.
