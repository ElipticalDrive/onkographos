ABSTRACT
We present a Monte Carlo Ray tracing model called WARPE (Waveform Analysis and Ray Profiling for
Exploration) to study the travel-time in turbid plane-parallel media with possible interaction at interfaces.
It is an efficient model with a fast computation time guaranteed by ray batch parallelization. This model has
been validated for both spatial and time dimension of the radiative transfer using robust reference models. An
application to explore the influence of the radiative parameters has been conducted and reveal that optical
depth, single scattering albedo, directionality of the medium, refractive index and extinction coefficient have a
crucial role on the time-resolved reflectance. This is illustrated by peak features representing the various back
and forth travels of the light in the medium for both diffused and unaffected rays. The top reflection at the first
interface and the background scattering are described as well. This model shall be used with inversion method
in order to interpret real data to improve the understanding of the microphysics of turbid media with interfaces.
1. Introduction
Turbid plane-parallel media are common in artificial products (such
as industrial glass or thin film), biological tissues (such as skin), or natural environments (such as turbid water, or ice). Accordingly, modeling
light interactions with such media have a wide range of applications in
various fields, such as atmospheric science [1–3], planetary surfaces [4]
or medical applications [5]. Turbid plane-parallel media are assumed
to be laterally infinite with homogeneous light scattering and absorbing properties. This simplification eases the formulation of boundary
conditions and facilitates both analytical approximations and numerical
solutions of the radiative transfer equation [6].
Among the numerical approaches used to study the radiative transfer equation, Monte Carlo Ray tracing simulation is a method used
for its versatility. These models describe these phenomenon as pack
of photons traveling in the medium undergoing various processes [7].
Each photons undergoes a sequence of randomly determined events
under geometric optic approximation. Simulating the medium and the
associated properties is crucial, as light scattering by composite particle
may varies from isotropic to forward [8]. [9] presented a Monte Carlo
Ray tracing model studying scattering and polarization as well with
large particles of complex shapes. Scattering can also be supported by
aggregates which leads to larger backscattering the more complex they
are [10]. [11] chose to represent the medium as an irregular grid unit
representing the grains with various positions and sizes, improving the
modeling of more realistically granular media. The medium can also
include interfaces resulting in refraction and reflection at its boundaries, according to Snell–Descartes Law and Fresnel coefficients [12].
Generally, Monte Carlo methods are focused on the path of the light and
the energy distribution in the spatial dimension. Still, some take into
account the temporal dimension for the light travel-time, sometimes
also called waveform, to study the photon migration in 3D media for
medical imaging [5], in a multilayer medium [13], to analyze the
effect of short light pulse in a strong scattering medium with large
particles where a time delay can be observed [14], or to explore the
low single scattering albedo regime [15]. However, those solutions
are not adapted to plane-parallel turbid media including interaction
at interface. A recent generation of instruments (such as lidar [16–18]
in the scope of remote sensing) have now the capability to estimate
the travel-time in the medium, unveiling new possibilities in the study
of light interactions with matter. Dedicated models already exist, e.g
for the study of a vegetation canopy [19] or the simulation of full
waveform LiDAR data [20]. In this work, we aim at providing a model
adapted to study the microphysics in the media capable to simulate
time-resolved photon distribution whatever the medium.

WARPE’s implementation is described in Fig. 3. The initialization
starts with the definition of geometric parameters, considering 𝑁𝑟𝑎𝑦𝑠
rays with a trajectory defined by the incidence angle 𝜃𝑖=0 , the azimuthal

described in Section 2.3.1 update 𝜃𝑖 , 𝜙𝑖 , 𝜏𝑖 . Firstly rays may be reflected
or transmitted and refracted. The latter rays travel thought the medium
and might be scattered and/or absorbed along the path. Among the
unabsorbed rays reaching the bottom interface, some are reflected back
in the medium which reproduce the previous step and can undergo
reflection at the top interface and so on, others are transmitted and
refracted outside the medium at the bottom. These rays then reach
the bottom condition (Section 2.3.1) where according to the surface
albedo, a fraction will be absorbed the other reflected. The reflected one
may re-enter the medium by transmission and refraction or be reflected
again at the bottom interface.
The simulation ends when more than 99.99% of rays are out of the
computation, either due to absorption or leaving at the top interface.
This means that less than 0.01% of the rays are still in the medium,
mostly on nearly horizontal trajectories. This limit is set arbitrarily to
save computation time while having a negligible impact on the simulated reflectance. Setting 100% would slightly improve the accuracy at
a heavy computation cost (depending on the parameters). At the end
of the computation, WARPE records the path of every ray, absorbed or
not. Usually we only focus on rays leaving the medium at the top that
can be compared to measurements. In the final stage, each ray has its
own number of total iteration 𝑁𝑒𝑣𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑠 .

3.1. Scattering/absorbing medium without slab boundary condition
3.1.1. BRDF validation with DISORT
The first step to validate the model is to ensure that WARPE is able
to simulate the energy distribution in a scattering absorbing medium
without interaction at interface. We compare our model with the
numerical solution DISORT [21] which is commonly used in radiative
transfer problems. It is based on the discrete ordinate method proposed
by [6]. It computes a numerical solution of the radiative transfer
equation in a plane-parallel medium. We test several sets of parameters
such as optical depth, single scattering albedo and surface albedo. The
incident angle is 50◦ and the emergence angle records the energy
distribution between 2.5◦ and 82.5◦ every 10◦ for azimuthal angle of
0◦ (forward direction) and 180◦ (backward direction). The comparison
between WARPE and DISORT is displayed in Fig. 5. We see for various
sets of parameters an excellent agreement between WARPE and DISORT
with RRMSE around 0.03. Since the diffusion is anisotropic (𝑔 = 0.8)
the reflectance is more intense for the forward geometry (0◦ –80◦ ). This
effect is more visible as the optical depth is important, as expected. For
low optical depth (𝜏 = 0.1), this process is less efficient due to fewer
scattering (𝜔 = 0.7).

5. Conclusion
Differences observed between WARPE and the various validation models used in this work are all due to the stochastic nature of the Monte
Carlo method. A higher numbers of rays tends to minimize these variations, the noise and so the RRMSE. The balance between computation
accuracy and computation time is also important because we neither
want too much stochastic errors or too long computation time. This
balance depends on the selected number of rays computed but also on
the parameters. RRMSE is reduced by more than 50% From 1 million
WARPE is a Monte Carlo tool simulating the travel-time in turbid
plane-parallel media with or without interfaces. Its numerical efficiency
is assured by an original parallelization method by groups of rays.
We validated WARPE by comparing with reference models corresponding to various media situations. WARPE shows good agreement
with these models with its accuracy guaranteed with the low RRMSE.
