Modeling a Forged Part – Airplanes in 3D
Skip to content
There are dozens forged or cast parts, like the one below, in an aircraft. Forming their shapes with polygon meshes and Bevel modifiers is difficult, because of their complex, rounded edges. Usually it takes many hours, in the worst cases – it is impossible. (For example: in 2018 I could not model in Blender this propeller governor in “one piece”).
Figure ‎128-1 An example of a forged part
In this post I am proposing a new workflow for recreating these machine parts in Blender. It is based on a custom modifier named Fuse with Collection. This modifier uses the new SDF Geometry Nodes, introduced in Blender 5.1. It converts a group of simple solids into voxel volumes, merges (“fuses”) them into a single volume, performs a Laplacian smoothing, then reconstructs the outer boundary of the result as a manifold mesh. Although it sounds like a complete abstraction, Fuse with Collection coupled with a Decimate and an eventual Boolean modifier creates an effective “stack” for modeling cast or forged machine parts.
In production, a blank is cast or forged with rounded edges and an excess of material in certain areas, for further processing. This excess is then cut away and drilled to produce the finished part. In this workflow we will use the Fuse with Collection (SDF) modifier as the “foundry” that creates the initial blank, and then we will apply the Carve with Collection (Boolean) modifier as the machining stage.

Just in case, here are two blueprints from AirCorps Library that I used in this process: forging (87-37-536) and machining (87-37-525).
Let’s start with the blueprint of the forged blank for the part from Figure ‎128‑1:
Figure ‎128-2 Analyzed blueprint of a forged blank
As you can see, the depicted object has two symmetry planes. On the same drawing I marked in colors the four basic solids of this element:
Base triangle, marked in blue;
Large cylinder, marked in red;
Small cylinder(s), marked in green;
Side (half) cylinders, marked in gray;
The forge formers move along the vertical axis (marked as Z in the picture above). Thus, the vertical surfaces are tapered (about 10%), as you can see in detail a-a. This is a technological restriction. For the same reason cylinders are capped with cones.
Create the first solid. Name this object B.650.Fitting. In this post we will transform it into the final part. In principle, it can be any of the four shapes identified in Figure ‎128‑2. I suggest picking for this role the most complex of these volumes: the triangle (shape 1):
Figure ‎128-3 Analyzed blueprint of a forged part
Create this shape from a plane, extruding the vertical faces. Then move their bottom edges on the XY plane a little bit outside. This creates the “tapering” effect (Figure ‎128‑3a). Mirror this mesh along the X and Z axes (Figure ‎128‑3b). Assign to this object (not its mesh – this is important!) a temporary material, for example – Artificial.Red (Figure ‎128‑3c)
Now go to the Modifiers tab and pull down the Add Modifier list. Select the Fuse with Collection modifier from the bottom of the Unassigned submenu:
Figure ‎128-4 Adding [Fuse with Collection] modifier
You can also add it in the Geometry Nodes modifier, selecting the Fuse with Collection node group from the list in its panel.
The initial effect is too strong (Figure ‎128‑5a). This is because of large voxels (0.3” – see Figure ‎128‑5b). When you decrease their size 6 times, to 0.05” (Figure ‎128‑5d), the object retrieves its original shape (Figure ‎128‑5c):
Figure ‎128-5 Controlling the voxel size
Note the number of elementary triangles in these meshes. For the larger voxels (0.3) it is 1 110. For the smaller voxels (0.05) it is 14 510. Later in this section we will try to reduce this latter number.
Fuse with Collection modifier converts the object mesh volume into “voxel cloud”, then builds the resulting mesh on the boundaries of this “cloud”. That’s why the result has smooth edges and its mesh is built from multiple triangular faces. There is nothing to merge (“fuse”), because we did not assign it any collection, yet.
There are two other parameters in this modifier:
Smoothing iterations: this is the number of so-called Laplacian smoothing, applied to the voxel cloud before it is converted to the mesh. When you set it to 0, the edges of the resulting mesh are “beveled” by single voxel-wide faces. Increasing this number enlarges these chamfers into multi-voxel rounding (as in Figure ‎128‑4). This smoothing also “relaxes” resulting mesh, removing eventual self-generated creases. Value of 3 is a good start for the forge / cast models. (More iterations mean higher CPU load).
Vary face sizes factor: if 0, the resulting mesh is built of small, voxel-size faces. This is a sensitive factor: increasing it to 0.01 or 0.02 reduces the initial number of faces 3 or 4 times. Unfortunately, higher values of this coefficient create visible “bumps” on non-orthogonal edges.
To learn more, switch your 3D viewport to Wireframe view, Object Mode, and experiment with these two parameters. Set one of them to 0 and check the full range of the other. Then try the reverse.
Create now a new auxiliary collection named B.650.Attach. Place in this collection a cylinder corresponding to solid 2 from Figure ‎128‑2. Name this object B.650.002:
Figure ‎128-6 Creating the central cylinder object (2)
Switch the default viewport display mode of this object to Wireframe. Assign B.650.Attach to Fuse with Collection modifier in the Join with collection field:
Figure ‎128-7 Joining central cylinder with the base object
In response, Blender merges these two solids into a single volume. Any change in the source objects is immediately reflected in this result, as in the case of the Boolean modifier. Unlike the Boolean results, Fuse with Collection smooths all intersection edges – just like in the real cast/forge.
Set B.650.Fitting object as the parent of B.650.002, to avoid unexpected deformations.
Now repeat these steps, placing in the B.650.Attach collection solids 3 and 4 from Figure ‎128‑2. Name these objects B.650.003 and B.650.004:
Figure ‎128-8 Joining with solids 3 and 4
Note that they are mirrored along the local axes of their parent (object B.650.Fitting).
For dents, create another collection: B.650.Subtract. Place there a wedge-like object and named it B.650.005. I applied to this object a Bevel:Weight modifier to recreate the top-view contour of the dent depicted in Figure ‎128‑2:
Figure ‎128-9 Forming the dent volume
However, there is an issue: this volume shares one side with the central cylinder. I assigned the A, B, C and D vertices to a vertex group named Cylindrical. Then I added a Shrinkwrap modifier before the Bevel modifier. It projects this group onto the B.650.002 surface (Figure ‎128‑10):
Figure ‎128-10 Projecting one of the mesh faces onto cylinder surface
Then move vertex A along local Y axis to align edge AE with the corresponding outer edge (Figure ‎128‑11a):
Figure ‎128-11 Adjusting mesh faces to the cylinder surface
Finally, I inserted a few new edge loops into the side faces, to recreate the bent shape of this cylindrical surface (Figure ‎128‑11c).
I could not round (bevel) the edges of the bottom face of this mesh. Increasing their bevel weights decreases radii along the vertical edges, changing the contour of this solid in the top view. (This and other Bevel issues are the reason that I turned to volumetric SDF modeling.) I left them sharp, so the Fuse with Collection modifier will smooth them with the uniform (“voxel”) radius.
I could also taper sides of this dent volume (by 10% – see Figure ‎128‑2) using a Simple Deform modifier. However, since I already resigned from the larger fillets along its bottom face, I also skipped this feature. Anyway, it makes a minor difference, negligible for such a (relatively) small detail.
Mirror object B.650.005 with a Mirror modifier along the X and Z axis (Figure ‎128‑12a). Then assign its collection (B.650.Subtract) as the Subtract collection of the Fuse with Collection modifier (Figure ‎128‑12b, c):
Figure ‎128-12 Creating dents in the object block
This step completed the blank part depicted in the blueprint from Figure ‎128‑2.
Make sure that all objects from the “join” and “subtract” collections of the Fuse with Collection modifier are parented to the modified object (in this case: B.650.Fitting).
Then you can hide these collections (in this case these are: B.650.Attach, B.650.Subtract). The obtained “raw result” requires some final touches.
The mesh generated by Fuse with Collection has all its edges sharp. Switch shading of the B.650.Fitting object to Shade Auto Smooth. Increase the threshold of its Smooth by Angle modifier to 45º and turn on the Ignore Sharpness option:
Figure ‎128-13 Switching resulting object to “auto smooth” shading mode
At this moment, the mesh of the B.650.Fitting object is built from 38 648 triangles. Let’s reduce this number.
Add a Decimate modifier (Generate : Decimate) after the Fuse with Collection. Reduce its Collapse Ratio to 0.25 (Figure ‎128‑14a):
Figure ‎128-14 Reducing number of the mesh faces with [Decimate] modifier
Figure ‎128‑14b) shows the result. Without any visual degradation, this Decimate modifier reduces the number of mesh triangles to 9 662. You can decrease its Ratio even further, to 0.15 (5 796 triangles). This number is higher, but comparable with the same part modeled in the classic way (4 320 triangles).
You can also try the Planar option of the Decimate modifier. With the Angle Limit between 3-5º it can produce even lower number of faces for shapes without oblique edges.
Working with these modifiers requires setting names for multiple auxiliary objects and their meshes. To reduce this “bookkeeping” work I found a Blender extension (add-on) named Auto Sync Object Data Names. It automatically renames the single user meshes after their object names.
Now we will remove the “excess of the material” from certain areas of this object. Figure ‎128‑15 shows the original blueprint that describes machining of the forged blank. I marked the planned operations on this drawing. We will skip most of the holes, because in our model they are occupied by bolts. The only exception is the large central opening for the drag truss (1), because it was visible in the rear of the finished part:
Figure ‎128-15 Analyzed blueprint of the finished part
Except hole 1, we need to make just four planar cuts (3, 4, 5a, 5b). Some of them (4, 5a) will leave a trace of the tool rounded corner. In principle we can also cut the tapered fragment (2). However, I decided to model this detail (see Figure ‎128‑16)
Creating another auxiliary collection, with the same prefix as the main object: B.650.Trim. Place there a cylinder that will “drill through” B.650.Fitting (i.e. cut out the hole 1 from Figure ‎128‑15). This is the first “cutter”. Name this auxiliary object X.650.001:
Figure ‎128-16 First of the “cutter” objects and its collection
Note that I shortened the central cylinder of the fitting body (B.650.002) according to the blueprints and modeled the tapered fragment at its top. It was much easier than modeling a flat object with a conical hole, required for the Boolean difference used in the Carve with Collection modifier. Anyway, in real life this fragment probably was grinded, which produced a rounded edge along its base.
Now go to the Modifiers tab and pull down the Add Modifier list. Select the Carve with Collection modifier from the bottom of the Unassigned submenu (as in Figure ‎128‑4).
You can use the standard Boolean:Difference modifier instead, with Operand Type set to Collection. Carve with Collection is its node-based, simplified version, created solely for this workflow. The key difference is that in the Boolean modifier you must take care of the materials of the cutter objects. In the case of Carve with Collection you can specify a single material for all, in the modifier panel (see Figure ‎128‑19).
Assign to this modifier the B.650.Trim collection (Figure ‎128‑17a):
Figure ‎128-17 Results of the [Carve with Collection] modifier
As you can see (Figure ‎126 17b), this is a specialized Boolean difference operator. It subtracts from the modified object all manifold solids grouped in the assigned collection. In the case when you want to use multiple cutter objects, Carve with Collection is more convenient than the standard Boolean modifier.
Now you can trim the B.650.Fitting object by adding subsequent solids to the B.650.Trim collection. For clarity, in the subsequent illustrations below I am showing only the newly added one:
Figure ‎128-18 Applying the planned planar cuts
These cutters are simple, usually mirrored boxes. Following Figure ‎128‑15, I rounded some of their edges.
Carve with Collection modifier requires manifold meshes. Otherwise, it will display an error message.
In addition, in the modifier panel you can set a different material for the carved surfaces. In the case below the base material assigned to the modified object is Artificial.Red. The carved areas are in Artificial.Blue:
Figure ‎128-19 Assigning different material to the cutout surfaces
This option seems to be a fancy feature, but see how it looks in a more realistic arrangement:
Figure ‎128-20 Marking cutout surfaces with bruised metal material
The raw forged or cast parts are quite rough. The machined surfaces are smoother and more reflective. This simple setting can make the modified element more realistic in the renders.
Ensure that all objects from the associated collection (in this case it was B.650.Trim) are parented to the modified object (here: B.650.Fitting).
Described workflow is intended for recreating various small mechanical parts. As you can see above, they are covered by an object-projected material, so do not require laborious UV-unwrapping. Just form the shape, trim the bases and assign the standard materials. The auxiliary collections remain hidden. (They are easily manageable, if consequently named after their main objects).

Quick answer:
This would mean converting original documentation of this aircraft into the modern CAD format. Much more work (by a magnitude) than my humble project. CAD systems require from you a higher precision, because their output is used for machining real parts.
Longer answer:
The better choice would be a NURBS-based designer tool, like Rhino3D. However, using an external tool in your workflow introduces disadvantages:
It would work for a “waterfall” workflow, while my case resembles a “spiral”: I often must modify once completed parts. For example – when I found that I made a mistake in reading dimensions. It usually occurs when I am fitting the parts together.
requires learning of a part of another large system. CAD programs are extensive, their workflow differs from the “artistic tools” like Blender. For example FreeCAD is parametric. This introduces another layer of unnecessary complexity!
eventual mesh import issues
What’s more, no free CAD tool exists that would provide the key functionality that I need for my workflow: filleting of the real, complex cases of the edge corners! Fusion360 from Autodesk provides better tools, because it uses the solutions “honed” by the industry programmers for the last 40 years. However, edge filleting is always an additional, often tricky, step in the CAD systems (and similar tools, like Rhino3D) . The proposed workflow, based on the SDF with the Laplacian smoothing, creates these fillets as a “natural” byproduct. The key points for the method presented in this post:
it is relatively simple
fully “reversible” (CSG – based) – i.e. ideal for eventual further modifications
quick
creates results of moderate polygon count.

For the assumed level of details, I often need to pick up missing detail drawings of various bushings, bearings, etc. from the AirCorps Libary. It occurs that in this process I used most of the blueprints of this assembly. Basing on this this experience I exaggerated in the “quick answer”, writing about whole aircraft :).
The original models of these forged parts were made from wood, often twice of the original size. The negative molds were machined from these models using an analog tool (a kind of pivoted arm that traced the “wooden “positive” model). As you expect, most of their rounding was shaped “by hand”. (I saw some of similar industry models. They were made by experienced craftsmen who used a little putty, if any. They resemble an abstract furniture). Each of the resulting forged parts contains a few of planes/holes that require high tolerance, which are machined later.
Side note: the forged parts were economical for the mass-production. Corresponding elements in the prototypes look different, because they were welded from steel cuts of various shape and sizes.
No doubt that a NURBS-based CAD system is better for your mechanical models. The workflow proposed here is quick, easy for later modifications, but creates solids that look good in visualizations. The resulting surface created by the Decimate modifier is quite “rough”, and it is visually smoothed thanks to the shading trick generated by the following Auto Smooth modifier. The parts of the scale models for the 3D printing require a smoother geometry.

Indeed, after writing my previous response I realized that 3D printing also has the natural “rounding” (like every physical process). I suppose that its radius is somewhat larger than the thickness of the single filament layer (it also depends on the local heat dissipation). In the case of modeled parts the typical 1/16R rounding that occurs in these aluminum forges perfectly corresponds to a “sharp” (0.1mm) 3D-printed edge of a 1:32 scale model. This means that for the most 3D-printed aircraft parts sharp edges are OK, unless you are making a 1:18 giant. The eventual tank models, like the 1:32 cast-armor Sherman variants, are a different story.
