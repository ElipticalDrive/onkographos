Abstract
Indirect illumination involving with visually rich participating media such as turbulent smoke and loud explosions contributes
significantly to the appearances of other objects in a rendering scene. However, previous real-time techniques have focused
only on the appearances of the media directly visible from the viewer. Specifically, appearances that can be indirectly seen over
reflective surfaces have not attracted much attention. In this paper, we present a real-time rendering technique for such indirect
views that involves the participating media. To achieve real-time performance for computing indirect views, we leverage layered
polygonal area lights (LPALs) that can be obtained by slicing the media into multiple flat layers. Using this representation,
radiance entering each surface point from each slice of the volume is analytically evaluated to achieve instant calculation. The
analytic solution can be derived for standard bidirectional reflectance distribution functions (BRDFs) based on the microfacet
theory. Accordingly, our method is sufficiently robust to work on surfaces with arbitrary shapes and roughness values. In
addition, we propose a quadrature method for more accurate rendering of scenes with dense volumes, and a transformation of
the domain of volumes to simplify the calculation and implementation of the proposed method. By taking advantage of these
computation techniques, the proposed method achieves real-time rendering of indirect illumination for emissive volumes.

Physically accurate rendering of inhomogeneous media presents a
challenging problem in computer graphics owing to the complicated behavior of light within the media. Light within inhomogeneous media undergoes phenomena such as emission, absorption, and scattering that vary from point to point owing to the nonuniformity of the participating media. These complicated behaviors

To address this problem, this paper presents a real-time rendering technique for indirect illumination that involves emissive participating media. The key idea of the proposed method is to divide
the media into thin slices of volume. If the light contribution from
each slice can be efficiently computed, then the rendering of indirect illumination from the volume can also be accelerated. In our
method, the volume is divided by a set of parallel planes that are
perpendicular to a straight line from a point of reflection. Considering a line parameterization along this straight line and assuming
that the light contribution from a cutting section is a function of
the line parameter, we found that the contribution from the slice of
volume can be represented in an analytic form. Given that the cutting section of an emissive participating medium can be considered
as an area light, we can compute its light contribution efficiently
using modern approaches [HDHN16; LDSM17]. In this paper, we
refer to these virtual area lights as layered polygonal area lights
(LPALs). Using the LPALs, the problem of calculating the indirect illumination can be simply formulated as a discrete integral.
For this integral, we also propose a quadrature method that is particularly beneficial to volumes with high density. In addition, we
introduce a technique to transform the domain of the volume that
can significantly simplify the computation and implementation of
the proposed method. The computational efficiency and simplicity
of implementation will be beneficial to not only computer games
but also other interactive applications including VR contents, e.g.,
for experiencing the evacuation in the event of fire.
Our technical contributions are summarized as follows:
• A new representation of volumes with LPALs, which can be obtained by slicing the volumes of participating media. This representation can be derived from the volume rendering equation as
a consequence of reasonable approximations;
• A quadrature method for efficiently calculating the light contributions from a set of thin slices of the volume;
• A technique to transform the rectangular bounding domain of the
volume to simplify the computation and implementation of the
proposed method.
2. Related Work
Participating media, e.g., clouds, haze, smoke, fire, and explosions,
are important elements in computer graphics. Therefore, the realistic simulation of participating media has been intensively studied
in the long history of computer graphics. A sophisticated survey of
rendering techniques for participating media is available in the literature [CPP*05; GNJJ08]. Here, we limit our scope to rendering
techniques for inhomogeneous media and provide a brief introduction to physics-based and real-time rendering techniques.
2.1. Rendering inhomogeneous participating media
The light transport in a participating medium is physically described by the radiative transfer equation (RTE) [Cha60], which
indicates that the intensity of light is attenuated by absorption and
the direction of light is altered by scattering. A representative approach for simulating the behavior of light based on the RTE is ray
marching [PH89], which regularly separates the light trajectory in
regular strides and integrates the light contribution along the trajectory. However, it is well known that the ray marching exhibits
a bias in inhomogeneous media. Therefore, recently proposed
techniques [YIC*10; STM11; SGM*17; KHLN17; SHZD17] are
mostly based on delta tracking, also known as Woodcock tracking [WMHL65; RSK08], in which the spatially varying occurrence
frequency of light interaction is balanced by stochastically rejecting
a sampled distance of the mean free path. These techniques are typically used with Monte Carlo path tracing methods [Kaj86; LW96]
to integrate the light contributions over the medium.
Another representative approach to integrating the light contributions is volumetric photon mapping (VPM) [JC98]. This
is a two-pass algorithm in which volumetric photon maps are
built in the first pass, and the light flux of photons stored in
the map is integrated in the second pass by path tracing to obtain a rendering image. While the original VPM collects the
contribution from photons in a small spherical volume around
each vertex on a light ray, VPM was later extended to cylindrical regions [JZJ08], higher-dimensional elements [BJ17], and
was even combined with other path tracing techniques such as
bidirectional path tracing [KGH*14] and gradient-domain radiance estimation [GHV*18]. Recently, a learning-based path tracing technique, i.e., path guiding [VKŠ*14; MGN17], has been reformulated for volume rendering [HZE*19]. Furthermore, a rendering technique for emissive inhomogeneous volume was proposed
by Yuksel [YY17] in the context of many-light rendering techniques [DKH*14].
While the above techniques are primarily for physically accurate
simulation of light transport with reasonable computational time,
our purpose is to obtain a realistic appearance of emissive volumes
in real time.
2.2. Real-time volume rendering
For real-time rendering of participating media, physical accuracy in
the simulation of light transport is often relaxed to reduce the computation complexity. Given that an analytic solution is obtained for
single scattering effects, a common approach to reduce the computational cost is to neglect the influence of multiple scattering.
This approximation can be adequately applied when the participating media are optically sparse or have low scattering albedos.
Following this assumption, ray marching [PH89] and its extension [ZRL*08; Muñ14] have been proposed for efficient rendering.
However, these previous studies focused on rendering appearances
of media that are directly visible by the viewer.
To more explicitly consider multiple scattering, traditional narrow beam theory [Ish78] has been applied to interactive and realtime rendering. Following this theory, a number of approximations

3. Indirect Illumination with Layered Polygonal Area Lights
Suppose a rendering scene with a reflective surface and an inhomogeneous self-illuminating volume located as illustrated in Figure 2. As shown in Figure 2(a), the reflected radiance at a vertex
on a surface can be simply calculated by integrating the incident
radiance from the self-illuminating volume. In this process, we are
required to calculate the incident radiance from every incident direction, for example, by using ray marching. However, this simple
approach results in prohibitively high computational cost because
the number of incident directions for a glossy surface is not as low
as can be treated in real time. Another alternative is to represent the
emissive volume as a set of virtual point lights (VPLs), as shown
in Figure 2(b). To maintain a feasible computational cost, we have
to approximate the emissive volume with a small number of VPLs.
However, by using only a few VPLs, a fine appearance, which is

reflected over a smooth surface, cannot be appropriately obtained.
Thus, it is still challenging to achieve real-time performance for
rendering the reflected appearances of emissive volumes over reflective surfaces with arbitrary roughness.
To address this problem, we focused on the fact that ray marching can be applied to a purely smooth surface to calculate the reflected appearance of the volume. This owes to the property that the
regular reflection direction of view direction is unique on a purely
smooth surface. In ray marching, radiance at discrete “points” on
a straight ray are summed up. The key idea of our study is to sum
up the radiance on “planes” that are perpendicular to a straight line
from the point of reflection. If the radiance from each plane can be
efficiently evaluated, then we can also efficiently calculate the overall reflected appearance as well. The overview of our algorithm is
illustrated in Figure 2(c). In our algorithm, the volume region is
sliced with a set of planes depicted by red line segments. While
these parallel cutting sections can be arbitrarily chosen, we practically use the cutting sections that are perpendicular to the straight
line connecting the point of reflection and the gravity center of the
volume domain. Given that we consider the self-illuminating volume, each of these planes can be regarded as an area light, i.e., an
LPAL. For a single LPAL, the radiance is attenuated by the volume
in the front, which is illustrated as a green region in Figure 2(d). As
shown in this figure, we denote a plane at distance s from the point
of reflection as A(s), the point of intersection as v// (s), and the surface normal at the point of reflection as n(xi ). If the radiance and
attenuation for the LPAL on A(s) can also be represented as functions of the distance s, then we can accumulate the radiance from
the entire volume by simply integrating the product of the radiance
and attenuation with respect to s.

4. Results and Discussion
The second problem involves the implementation of LTC-based
area light rendering. To correctly evaluate the incident light intensity, the area light transformed into the clamped cosine space
must be clipped when part of it penetrates under the plane (see
Figure 6(b)). While this can be accomplished by intersection tests
as well as vertex sorting, its computational cost is not low enough
for real-time rendering. In the implementation provided by the authors, all combinations of penetrated and non-penetrated vertices
are handled in different calculation branches. With simple arithmetic, the number of valid combinations of penetration is obtained
as N(N − 1) + 2. Therefore, considering that a triangle, quadrangle,
pentagon, and hexagon can be given by simple slicing, we need to
list up to 80 different calculation branches in the shader program.
This is not suitable in terms of implementation or performance.

4.1. Comparison of quadrature rules
As a quadrature method, we propose an approach that explicitly
considers the effect of light extinction in the domain of a volume. Thus, the proposed method can more appropriately handle
the exponential attenuation of light intensity in a thin volume slice.
To reveal this property more clearly, we first conducted a simple experiment with an artificial light intensity function and constant extinction coefficients over the volume domain. Figure 10 depicts the results of this experiment. In this experiment, we used
L(s) = −(s − 1)2 + 1 and a constant σ̄t (s). As mentioned previously, the calculation done by the proposed method and trapezoid
rule are equivalent when σt = 0. We can also observe this behavior
in Figure 10. The accuracy of the proposed quadrature method is
approximately the same as that of the trapezoid rule when the value
of σ̄t is low. By contrast, when the value of σ̄t increases, the accuracy of the proposed quadrature method improves significantly,
and it becomes comparable to the Simpson’s rule that imposes a
more complicated computation than ours owing to the dual integral
in Equations 2 and 4.
The good property of the proposed method for dense volumes
is particularly beneficial for rendering emissive volumes such as
flames inside a dense smoke. We also conducted an experiment to
demonstrate the above property in practical rendering. Figure 11
shows a comparison of rendered images using different quadrature
rules. In this comparison, the density of the smoke part was scaled
to change the magnitude of attenuation. As shown in this figure,
when the smoke density is low, all of the quadrature methods, i.e.,
trapezoid rule, Simpson’s rule, and our method, produce almost the
same rendering images. However, when the smoke density is scaled
up to be higher, the results of the trapezoid and Simpson’s rules
turn out to be worse. By contrast, the proposed quadrature method
successfully preserves the visual quality in rendered images.

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a new real-time rendering technique for
participating media indirectly visible over reflective surfaces. The
proposed method represents an inhomogeneous volume using multiple layers of area lights that we refer to as layered polygonal area
lights (LPALs). This simple but powerful representation enables us
to evaluate the light contribution from a volume slice between two
adjacent LPALs immediately by using an analytic form. Thus, the
evaluation of the radiance reflected on the surface has been significantly accelerated. Furthermore, the proposed technique can be
applied to the reflection over surfaces with arbitrary shapes and
roughness. We also propose an approach to slicing volumes using
a rotated rectangular volume domain, which shortens the computational time and simplifies the implementation of our method. Even
though the proposed method is not strictly based on pure physics
