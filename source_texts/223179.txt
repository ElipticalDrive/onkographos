We propose a hybrid 3D modeling and rendering approach called
SPLASH to combine the modeling flexibility and robustness of
SPLAts together with the rendering simplicity and maturity of
meSHes. Together with this novel SPLASH concept, we also propose a system turning a 3D point cloud, obtained for example
through an SfM (Structure from Motion) approach, into a multitextured hybrid 3D model whose shape is described by a triangle
mesh plus a collection of elliptical splats.
Although traditionally there has been more research on realistic rendering of 3D polygon meshes or volumetric models, as the popularity of 3D point clouds is increasing, so is the interest on ways
to render (and model) them properly. The most trivial way to render a point cloud is to draw a single colored pixel per 3D point,
but this is too naive an approach which invariably leads to big ugly
holes (gaps between rendered points). The next step is to model and
render each point as a splat: a surface patch, normally elliptical,
which can be solid-colored [Zwicker et al. 2001] or textured [Sibbing et al. 2013]. These approaches produce good results with very
dense point clouds, like those obtained with expensive laser scanners, but also lead to holes when dealing with sparse ones, like those
yielded by cheaper RGB-D sensors or SfM techniques.

Other approaches try rendering point clouds by meshing them first,
and then using the well-established 3D triangle rendering pipeline.
In fact, “meshing the point cloud” might be misleading because the
points in the original cloud need not be vertices of the mesh replacing it, and are actually not, in general, for some surface reconstruction techniques, either based on Poisson analysis [Kazhdan et al.
2006; Qian 2014], or on Bayesian statistics [Jenke et al. 2006], or
on intrinsic properties of the point cloud, such as the uniformity degree [Lin et al. 2004]. All the 3D reconstruction systems referenced
above work relatively well with unordered, and even generally lowresolution point clouds, but struggle with point clouds that are noisy
and/or too heterogeneous in their sampling: holes still appear in areas with very low point density.
Introduction: Vision and Related Work
3D point clouds are hardly new, especially the ones acquired thanks
to traditional LiDAR-like remote sensing based on sophisticated
laser scanners, but are becoming more and more popular thanks
to both inexpensive RGB-D sensors, such as Microsoft Kinect, and
SfM (Structure from Motion) systems [Snavely et al. 2006]. The
former capture usually noisy color plus depth information of a real
3D scene from a particular viewpoint. The latter infer (again, with
inherent inaccuracies) the 3D positions and colors of many feature

The initial 3D point cloud may be reasonably dense in some areas but is typically very sparse in others, either because the corresponding regions of the real 3D object contain objectively almost
no sharp features to be detected, or because the scene illumination,
as subjectively captured in the 2D pictures, has hidden them from
the feature detector. In any case, the initial point cloud is definitely
noisy, so we start by filtering it to remove statistical noise. We then
analyze this filtered point cloud in the hope of partitioning it, as
much as possible, into sub-clouds corresponding to simple geometrical shapes such as planes, cylinders, etc. Finally, we replace all
sub-clouds matching simple shapes with a triangle mesh, but leave
isolated points be, and model them using elliptical splats. The shape
of the mesh portions of the resulting hybrid model may of course
be further refined, but so may be the shape of the splat portions.
And the same goes for the appearance: in particular, in our system we apply to the whole hybrid model a seamless multi-texturing
technique originally conceived for 3D meshes [Pagés et al. 2015]
which works equally well with splats, as illustrated by Figure 1.

Proposed System Description
The initial, 3D noisy point cloud fed into our system undergoes a
process consisting of the following stages: 1) filtering of the original point cloud through statistical noise removal; 2) coarse normal
vector estimation for every point; 3) segmentation of the cloud into
sub-clouds roughly matching simple geometrical shapes; 4) normal
refinement; 5) replacement of all points in the set of sub-clouds
with a triangle mesh through Poisson-based surface reconstruction;
6) “splatification” of the remaining isolated points; and 7) Multitexturing of both mesh and splat regions of the hybrid 3D model.

2.1
Statistical Noise Removal
Our least squares error approximation results from PCA (Principal
Component Analysis) of a covariance matrix created with the point
neighbors, the neighborhood size determining the smoothness of
the resulting set of normals: if too few neighbors are considered,
the set of normals is too noisy, but if too many are, it may be too
smooth, which is also bad because edges are not sharp enough. In
any case, since the next step does not require particularly smooth
transitions across sub-clouds, for this first normal estimation we
use small neighborhoods, which logically means faster analysis.
Point clouds resulting from SfM approaches typically contain two
kinds of points: the vast majority correspond to truly interesting and
distinct features of the foreground 3D object to be reconstructed,
but some others correspond to semantically meaningless features
automatically detected in the background. For instance, in the cases
of 3D reconstruction of buildings and human avatars, that we have
explored quite extensively, true feature points would be window
corners or face wrinkles, and spurious ones could be false/irrelevant
edges of a surrounding cloud/tree or piece of furniture that happen
to be detected in two or more pictures. There is noise in both kinds
of points: the reconstructed 3D position and color of the true ones
are subject to some small error inherent to the capture process, but
the spurious points must be filtered out altogether because they are
much more harmful for the subsequent steps in our process.
2.3
When reconstructing certain 3D objects such as most buildings, we
may assume that they are mainly composed of planes (or pseudoplanes including more than just completely flat surfaces), cylinders
or other simple geometrical shapes. With this assumption in mind,
we try to segment the point cloud into a set of sub-clouds matching,
within some tolerance, planes, etc.
To get rid of those mostly isolated spurious points, we use a statistical outlier removal technique based on the assumption that the distribution of point-to-point distances in a well-behaved point cloud
must be Gaussian, so that any points whose own local distance
distribution is wildly different from the global one may be removed [Rusu et al. 2008]. This filtering technique was originally
developed for dense point clouds obtained with LiDAR scanners,
but we can confirm that it works as well with sparser point clouds
obtained via SfM, as illustrated by Figure 2, which shows the difference between a noisy point cloud and the filtered one.
2.2
Simple Geometry Shape Segmentation
Our approach is based on an iterative RANSAC model which uses
discarded outliers in the current iteration but considers them for the
next [Schnabel et al. 2007]. However, not every point considered as
inlier by RANSAC is necessarily included in a candidate sub-cloud:
inliers whose normal differs too much (e.g., by more than 30◦ ) from
the average normal of the considered sub-cloud are discarded, but
only in the sense of being rejected as members of that sub-cloud.
Indeed, although we use RANSAC, which was designed to remove
outliers, we delete no points from the cloud after the first statistical
noise filtering step, and especially not the complete outliers rejected
from all successively detected sub-clouds, since they are eventually
turned into splats (see Section 2.6).

2.4
Normal Refinement
Although coarse normal estimates suffice for the previous step,
since they are compared within a rather ample tolerance, and in fact
without paying attention to their orientation, the next step requires
that the set of normals be as smooth as possible, while preserving
sharp edges. This is why a normal refinement step is needed to not
only make more accurate estimates of the normals but also fix their
orientation (i.e., “flip them”) in some cases.

per sub-cloud has two advantages: building neighborhoods (to then
calculate the normal through PCA) in an already segmented region
is faster, and the points from any potential adjacent sub-clouds cannot influence the normals estimated for the points of the considered
one, so that sharp edges across sub-clouds are perfectly preserved.

2.6
Although this normal refinement process is fast and mostly correct,
there is still another issue: PCA only guarantees a correct direction
of the normals, but sometimes does yield normals with the wrong
orientation (we assume that the reconstructed 3D object has an inside and an outside, and that all normals must point outwards). To
solve this problem, we make sure that the dot product of the vector pointing from a point to any of the cameras seeing it and its
PCA-estimated normal is positive.
2.5
Splatification of Remaining Isolated Points
After having partitioned as much as possible the filtered point
cloud into sub-clouds matching simple shapes, our system turns
into splats the still isolated points that were rejected from all subclouds. The “splatification” of a point consists in modeling it as an
ellipse approximating locally around that point the underlying surface, which again results from neighborhood analysis (of course,
although splats are only generated for the points marked as complete outliers, the whole filtered point cloud is used as input in this
step to take advantage of all the geometric information available).
Surface Reconstruction
We use the same PCA techniques applied before to extract only
the normal, but record here as well the two tangential directions for
each neighborhood. We orient again the normal to point towards the
outside of the object, using the corresponding camera position, and
scale the tangential vectors adequately by their variance to define
the major and minor axes of the ellipse. Once all isolated points
have been “splatified”, we optionally decimate the set of splats to
reduce their number and overlaps while avoiding holes.
Once all normals are refined, we replace all segmented sub-clouds
with a 3D mesh thanks to a Poisson surface reconstruction technique [Kazhdan et al. 2006] which produces almost always closed
surfaces, so no holes appear in the mesh, even in regions of the
cloud with very low point density. However, this is a mixed blessing because sometimes regions are closed when they should not
(e.g., roofs for which there is no data in the original point cloud), so
this step also requires some post-processing to remove parts of the
generated mesh from the final model.
2.7
To do so, we enforce a reasonable distance from the resulting 3D
mesh to the set of sub-clouds used for the surface reconstruction:
if a vertex of the mesh is too far away from the point cloud, it is
removed (and so are all triangles including that vertex). Thanks to
this, artifacts such as roofs and hidden walls are removed correctly,
although holes in the point cloud due to windows still lead to closed
portions of the mesh.
Multi-texturing
The last stage deals with the appearance of the hybrid 3D model
whose shape is determined by the previous steps. Given an input
point cloud obtained with a set of calibrated images (e.g., with an
SfM approach, or with RGB-D sensors acquiring the 3D scene from
different viewpoints), the photographs or video frames leading to
that point cloud may be used to texture the resulting model.

We have introduced the novel SPLASH concept, which combines
the best of 3D splats and triangle meshes, and a system to model and
render, with much greater photo-realism, any point cloud extracted
from calibrated images, as is done by SfM techniques. The main
advantages of our approach are its flexibility and adaptivity to the
shape complexity of the reconstructed 3D object: in the case of
buildings or other geometrically simple shapes, the mesh portion of
the model is the predominant one, even after mesh decimation; in
the case of more intricate surfaces, splats outnumber triangles, but
are still more compact than isolated points.
