### Abstract

Immersive video communication requires photorealistic, compact dynamic scene representations that support efficient rendering. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a promising approach, but dynamic 3DGS remains difficult to compress due to its dense primitives and spatiotemporal redundancy. Anchor-based formulations improve compactness through sparse scaffolds that share geometry and appearance across primitives. However, existing designs typically deform a single canonical scaffold and condition each primitive solely on its associated anchor, limiting their ability to model non-local dynamics and disocclusions and exploit inter-anchor correlations. To address these limitations, we propose SAGA, a volumetric video codec built on Sparse Anchor-assisted GAussian splatting representations. SAGA represents a dynamic scene using hierarchical sparse anchors in 4D space-time. Coordinate-based INR decoders generate fine anchors and Gaussian primitives from features obtained through kernel interpolation among nearby anchors, enabling parameter sharing across spatiotemporal structures. A fixed-size memory with a novel orthogonality-informed slot-update strategy further captures long-range dependencies among unstructured anchors for entropy coding. Empirically, SAGA achieves PSNR-based BD-rate reductions of 90.88%, 64.30% and 65.14% relative to GIFStream, TED-4DGS, and OMG4, respectively, on the Neu3D testset.

## 1 Introduction

Immersive visual content is increasingly important for applications such as telepresence, freeviewpoint video, virtual and augmented reality, and digital media production [1, 6]. It provides a stronger sense of presence, richer spatial understanding, and greater viewpoint flexibility than conventional 2D media. However, these benefits introduce substantial challenges. While 2D video mainly records view-specific projected appearance on the imaging plane, free-viewpoint volumetric video requires dynamic scene representations that support rendering from multiple viewpoints, further increasing the demands on storage, transmission, and rendering. Consequently, compact dynamic scene representation has become central to scalable immersive video systems, where fidelity, rendering efficiency, and compressibility must be jointly optimized.

For compact representation and compression of volumetric visual content, prior work spans traditional geometry- and video-based pipelines [40, 39, 1, 6] and neural scene representations [34, 85, 48]. Among neural methods, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have advanced novel-view synthesis [56] and compression [74, 83, 25, 80] by modeling scenes as continuous volumetric radiance fields, but their reliance on volume rendering remains costly for real-time immersive applications. In contrast, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [32] uses an explicit, rasterization-friendly representation with efficient training, high visual fidelity, and substantially faster rendering. Consequently, 3DGS compression (e.g., [54, 11]) has become an active research direction for reducing storage costs and enabling practical immersive visual communication.

Following advances in static 3DGS compression [54, 11, 12, 10, 72, 58], GS-based volumetric video coding has attracted increasing attention [67, 24, 41]. Compared with static scenes, dynamic 3DGS must represent changes in geometry, appearance, and visibility over time, introducing additional parameters and spatiotemporal redundancy. Existing methods broadly fall into frame-by-frame approaches [67, 24, 38, 78], which encode temporally correlated Gaussian frames, and canonicalto-deformation approaches [13, 41, 23] that model video- or chunk-wise temporal variations from a shared canonical representation. While effective, frame-wise methods exploit temporal redundancy via predictive or correlated coding, which can complicate random access and accumulate drift under long prediction chains; canonical-to-deformation methods rely on a single reference that can be affected by abrupt motion, disocclusion, or topology changes.

To address these limitations, we propose SAGA, a Gaussian Splatting-based volumetric video compression framework using a hierarchical sparse 4D anchor scaffold. SAGA organizes anchors directly in spacetime, allowing features to be shared across spatial and temporal neighborhoods without deforming a single canonical scaffold. Fine anchors and Gaussian primitives are generated through a unified interpolation-and-decoding mechanism: neighboring parent-anchor features are aggregated at each target coordinate and fed, together with positional embeddings, into shared coordinate-based implicit neural representation (INR) decoders. This enables parameter sharing at multiple granularities while retaining local flexibility to represent dynamic scene content. A lightweight local hash-based residual branch further refines primitive attributes in normalized anchor coordinates to recover high-frequency details. To complement this local feature sharing, we introduce a long-context entropy model that summarizes previously decoded anchor information in a fixed-size memory bank. Orthogonality-guided slot updates incorporate new context while limiting repeated writes of information aligned with the previous slot state. Our main contributions are as follows:

• We propose a novel GS-based volumetric video compression framework with hierarchical sparse 4D anchors. By organizing anchors directly in spacetime, our formulation combines local adaptability to geometry and visibility changes with long-range temporal sharing, enabling bidirectional redundancy reduction and flexible non-canonical modeling of complex dynamics.

• We leverage coarse-to-fine and inter-anchor correlations through position-interpolated hierarchical features. These features condition both fine-anchor and Gaussian decoding, allowing them to

- aggregate neighboring-anchor context rather than relying on isolated anchor features.
- We introduce an efficient long-context entropy model for unstructured 4D anchors. It distills decoded spatiotemporal contexts into a compact memory bank whose slots are updated with an orthogonality-guided rule, capturing long-range dependencies under bounded complexity.

Experiments on Neu3D [45], Panoptic Sports [29], and MPEG MIV Common Test Condition (CTC) [6] datasets show that SAGA achieves favorable rate-distortion performance against existing GS-based and MPEG-standard volumetric video codecs.

### 2 Related Work

Volumetric video compression has traditionally focused on explicit scene data and multi-view signals, such as point clouds and multi-view videos. Representative standardization efforts include V-PCC [63, 26], G-PCC [39], and V3C [1], covering video-based and geometry-based coding of volumetric content. For immersive multi-view video, TMIV [1, 6] provides reference software that exploits inter-view redundancy and packs texture and geometry into video atlases. Video-based volumetric codecs can further leverage mature 2D coding tools and ecosystems, such as HEVC [66] and VVC [7], with performance typically assessed under common test conditions and view-synthesis evaluation protocols. Beyond standardized pipelines, learning-based methods have also been explored for explicit 3D data, especially point clouds, using learned geometry transforms, entropy models, temporal prediction, motion compensation, and residual coding [59, 16, 28, 64]. More recently, coordinate-based and implicit neural representation (INR) codecs have emerged for multi-view and immersive video compression, such as MV-HiNeRV [34], MV-IERV [85], and MV-MGINR [48].

Compression for NeRF-based methods has evolved along a clear methodological progression. Early work improved compactness through end-to-end optimization [4] and more structured radiancefield parameterizations, including sparse grids, explicit voxels [81], tensor decompositions [68, 9], multiresolution hash encodings [57], and plane-based factorization [8, 18]. Later works treat NeRFs more directly as compressible representations, introducing transform coding [60, 37], quantization [42, 31], entropy-constrained coding [37, 43], and learned codec regimes [43, 31, 71, 17, 83, 25]. These studies show that compactness strongly depends on structured field parameterization, but they remain primarily designed for volumetric radiance fields, whose rendering is generally less direct for real-time immersive applications than explicit Gaussian rasterization.

Compression for GS-based methods extends compact static 3DGS representations to dynamic volumetric video, driven by advances in GS compression [54, 11, 12, 10, 67, 24, 41] and multiview datasets [29, 62, 45, 19, 2]. For static 3DGS [32], existing methods reduce redundancy through compact parameterization [79, 14], pruning [61, 22, 35], quantization [70, 58, 15], context modeling [49, 12, 72, 50, 76], and rate-distortion optimization [54, 11, 12, 10]. Feed-forward methods [53, 10, 78] further avoid extensive per-scene overfitting. For dynamic scenes, GS-based codecs can be broadly grouped into frame-wise predictive approaches and temporally shared representations. The former encode timestamp-dependent Gaussian sets using online updates, motion compensation, GoF-based inter-frame coding, residual prediction, or temporal pruning and quantization [67, 20, 24, 78, 21, 52, 27]. The latter model temporal evolution with canonical deformation, feature streams, motion-decoupled trajectories, or structured UV/attribute atlases [41, 82, 84]. Beyond volumetric video, generative GS-based approaches have also been explored for 2D video compression [51, 69].

### 3 Method

### 3.1 Preliminaries

Gaussian Splatting represents a 3D scene as learnable anisotropic Gaussian primitives {gj} N j=1. Each primitive is parameterized as gj = (µj , qj , sj , αj , cj ), where µj ∈ R 3 is the center, qj denotes the rotation quaternion, sj is the scale, αj is the opacity, and cj encodes view-dependent color/appearance. Its covariance is Σj = Rj diag(sj ) 2R ⊺ j . Given a camera, each Gaussian is projected to a 2D elliptical footprint Gj (p). After depth sorting, the color values at pixel p are computed by differentiable front-

ScaffoldGS [54] organizes 3D Gaussians with a sparse set of anchor points. Each anchor i is associated with a position xi , an anchor feature fi , a learnable scaling factor li , and K learnable local offsets Oi = {oi,k} K k=1. For a visible anchor, ScaffoldGS spawns K neural Gaussians whose centers are given by:

µi,k = xi + oi,k ⊙ li (1)

The remaining Gaussian attributes, including opacity, color, rotation, and scale, are not independently stored for each spawned Gaussian. Instead, they are decoded on the fly from a view-adaptive anchor feature together with the camera-anchor distance δi and viewing direction d⃗ i :

{αi,k} K k=1 = Fα(fˆ i , δi , d⃗ i), {ci,k} K k=1 = Fc(fˆ i , δi , d⃗ i),

with {qi,k} K k=1 and {si,k} K k=1 predicted analogously by Fq and Fs. Thus, ScaffoldGS can be viewed as an anchor-conditioned neural Gaussian generator, where sparse anchors provide local context and offsets, while lightweight MLPs instantiate view-adaptive Gaussian attributes.

#### 3.2 Overview

The proposed SAGA framework is illustrated by Figure 2, which consists of two major stages: representation and compression, which we describe in detail below:

Representation. To exploit correlations among unstructured 4D anchors, we leverage a unified interpolation-and-decoding mechanism across the hierarchy. For each fine anchor, the neighboring coarse anchors of its parent are aggregated via kernel interpolation, as detailed in subsection 3.3, producing a coordinate-adaptive latent feature f˜ c(u mi f ), which includes a positional embedding of u mi f . The fine-anchor offset o¯mi is part of the encoded scaffold and is used, after positional embedding, as the local coordinate input to the shared INR decoder. Specifically, Ffine predicts the

remaining fine-anchor attributes as

(f mi f ,l mi f ) = Ffine f˜ c(u mi f ),PE(o¯ mi) . (2)

Here f mi f and l mi f are the features and the support of the fine anchor, respectively. PE(·) denotes positional embedding [65]. Gaussian primitives are generated from the fine-anchor scaffold in the same local-coordinate manner. Each fine anchor carries a set of primitive offsets {o¯mij} Nmi j=1 in its local coordinate system, obtained as o¯mij = Foff(f mi f )j + r mij , where the shared predictor Foff provides a layout template and only the residuals r mij are transmitted as part of the scaffold. For each primitive, we recover its 4D coordinate u mij g from o¯mij and the parent fine anchor, interpolate neighboring fine anchors to obtain f˜ f(u mij g ), again fused with PE(u mij g ), and decode its attributes as

θ mij geo = Fgeo f˜ f(u mij g ),PE(o¯ mij ) , c mij c,s = Fcolor f˜ f(u mij g ),PE(o¯ mij ), d⃗mij c , τs − τ mij . (3)

Here θ mij geo includes opacity, spatial scaling, base rotation, temporal-opacity parameters, and polynomial motion/rotation coefficients following STG [46]. These coefficients are evaluated at timestamp τs to obtain primitive position and orientation, while Fcolor predicts view- and time-dependent color. Each primitive further carries a learnable binary mask mmij [12], optimized with a straight-through estimator, which gates its opacity during training and to remove/exclude invalid Gaussians. The retained primitives are rasterized to produce ˜Ic,s.

Compression. Parameters are quantized with learned quantization step sizes, using softrounding [33] during training, followed by a short quantization-noise stage. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote the tuple of quantized parameters to be entropy coded as Zˆ = (zˆmeta, zˆcoarse, zˆfine, zˆprim, zˆmask, zˆnet), including meta information, coarse/fine anchor symbols, primitive offset residuals, primitive masks, and network parameters, with the latter also covering the INR-based hyperprior [23]. We encode zˆmeta independently and compress zˆnet, which includes the decoders and the entropy model itself; the remaining symbols are entropy modeled hierarchically, as shown in Figure 2, conditioned on the hyperprior, previously decoded neighbors, and a memory bank. Within the coarse level, and likewise for the primitive offsets, symbols are partitioned into Kc parity-based coding groups according to the integer 4D cell coordinates of their anchors. For the n-th anchor with cell coordinate v ℓ n ∈ Z 4 , the group index is

γ(v ℓ n ) = ω ⊺ (v ℓ n mod 2), ω = (1, 2, 4, 8)⊺ , (4)

so that the k-th coding group is Gℓ,k = n | γ(v ℓ n ) = k for k = 0, . . . , Kc − 1, with Kc = 16. The groups are decoded sequentially over k, while all symbols within the same group are decoded in parallel. Fine-anchor offsets are decoded in a single step conditioned on their parent coarse anchors. Decoding the full scaffold therefore requires 2Kc + 1 sequential steps.

### 3.3 Hierarchical 4D Anchor

Initialization and Optimization. We initialize the hierarchy from a unified 4D candidate pool. The sequence is split into groups of pictures (GOPs), and a short spatio-temporal Gaussian (STG) [46] warm-up is fitted to each GOP as a proposal stage. For every provisional Gaussian q, one gradient pass over the training views accumulates its screen-space gradient magnitude Sq and a temporally normalized response Dq that favors short-lived content. Gaussians whose Sq or Dq exceeds the corresponding upper quantile, and whose opacity is non-negligible, become candidates with importance

wq = min max Sq QS , Dq QD ¯d dq , wmax , (5)

where QS and QD are the corresponding quantile thresholds, dq is the camera distance of candidate q, and ¯d is a reference camera distance used for normalization. A candidate is treated as static when it barely moves and remains visible for at least half a GOP. Static candidates are placed at the middle timestamp and de-duplicated across GOPs, while dynamic candidates are mapped to sequence time.

The resulting pool is P = {((xq, τq), hq, wq)}q, where (xq, τq), hq, and wq denote the 4D location, color, and importance, respectively. We build a greedy dyadic 4D tree using the descriptor

ϕq = [xq, λτ τq, λhhq] , (6)

so subdivision reflects spatial, temporal, and appearance variation. The leaf with the largest importance-weighted descriptor scatter (sum of squared deviations) is split next, until the coarseanchor budget or maximum depth is reached. A cell is split in space only, unless the weighted temporal spread within it exceeds a threshold, in which case it is also halved in time.

Leaf cells automatically become coarse anchors. For leaf m, u m c is the cell center, l m c is the cell halfsize, refined during training by a learned log-scale correction, and f m c is a learnable feature initialized from the weighted mean colour of its candidates. Fine-anchor capacity is allocated according to

Am = P q∈Pm wq α (Hm + ϵ) β , (7)

where Pm is the candidate set in cell m and Hm is its importance-weighted descriptor variance. The allocation scores are converted into integer counts Nm subject to P m Nm = Nbudget, Nmin ≤ Nm ≤ Nmax. Given Nm, weighted k-means++ seeding selects representatives, which are stored as normalized offsets, i.e., o¯mi = u mi − u m c ⊘ l m c , where ⊘ denotes element-wise division.

Geometry Coding and Decoding Order. We use c and f to denote the coarse and fine anchor levels, respectively. Each coarse center is quantized on a 2 Bc grid per dimension and mapped to a 4D Morton key κ c m by interleaving the coordinate bits of (x, y, z, τ ). The sorted keys are stored with Elias–Fano coding, followed by the entropy-coded cell depths and fine-anchor counts Nm, so the tree layout is encoded losslessly. Fine anchors are stored as offsets o¯mi from their parent, quantized and entropy-coded with the parent coarse anchor as context.

Attributes are decoded level by level, so each level is conditioned on the preceding one: coarse features and support deltas, then fine offsets, then Gaussian masks and offsets. Within the coarse and Gaussian levels, symbols are split into 16 groups by the parity of their 4D cell index (for a Gaussian, the local cell of its fine anchor). Group k is decoded in parallel, conditioned on its decoded neighbours in groups smaller than k, which bounds the sequential depth to 16 steps per level.

Kernel Interpolation. Let ℓ ∈ {c, f} denote the source level, used to decode fine anchors from coarse anchors (ℓ = c) and Gaussian primitives from fine anchors (ℓ = f). Each source anchor b has position ub, support lb, and feature fb. A query at uq belongs to a source anchor aq (its parent or owner) and has support lq, set to 1 4 laq for fine anchors and laq for Gaussians. Its candidates are the M nearest 4D neighbours of aq by center distance, which the decoder recomputes for free and which also provide the entropy-coding context of the coarse and Gaussian levels. Among them, the K closest with dqb < ρ2 form N (q) (always including aq), and their features are blended , as illustrated in Figure 3 (b):

f˜ ℓ(uq) = MLPℓ h X b∈N(q) softmaxb −dqb/T fb ; PE(uq) i, dqb = (uq−ub)⊘(lq+lb) 2 Λ , (8)

where [· ; ·] denotes concatenation, PE is a sinusoidal encoding, MLPℓ is a two-hidden-layer ReLU MLP per level, ⊘ is element-wise division, and Λ = diag(1, 1, 1, λt) weights the temporal term.

#### 3.4 Hierarchical Context Modeling

Our entropy model augments the local hierarchical context with a fixed-size memory bank to capture long-range dependencies among sparse 4D anchors. Let r index the sequential decoding steps induced by the hierarchy level and modulo group; for example, one step may decode the symbol blocks associated with Gℓ,k, where ℓ ∈ {c, f} and k is the modulo-group index. We denote the blocks decoded at step r as Br. All blocks in Br are decoded in parallel and read from the same previous memory state Sr−1 = [sr−1,1, . . . , sr−1,L], where L is the number of memory slots.

For each symbol block g ∈ Br, we form a memory query token qg from its known side information and read the memory as h mem g = Readϕ(qg,Sr−1). The entropy parameters are then predicted by (µg,σg) = Eϕ(e hp g , h parent g , h prev g , h mem g ), where e hp g is the hyperprior embedding, h parent g is aggregated from decoded coarser-level symbols, and h prev g uses only symbols from earlier modulo groups within the same coding stage. No symbol from the current batch Br is used to predict another symbol in the same batch, preserving parallel entropy decoding.

After all blocks in Br are decoded, their quantized values and side information are converted into decoded tokens {z˜g}g∈Br and aggregated into a batch write token ar = Aggϕ ({z˜g}g∈Br ). The memory bank contains L slots, and p ∈ {1, . . . , L} indexes an individual slot. For each slot p, a slot-specific candidate write vector and gate are predicted from the same batch write token: (vr,p, wr,p) = W p ϕ (ar). To avoid repeatedly writing information already represented by the previous slot, we remove from the candidate write vector the component parallel to that slot:

v ⊥ r,p = vr,p − ⟨vr,p, sr−1,p⟩ ∥sr−1,p∥ 2 2 + ϵ sr−1,p. (9)

Each memory slot is then updated from its own previous value by combining the previous slot with the gated orthogonal write:

sr,p = Norm sr−1,p + wr,pv ⊥ r,p , p = 1, . . . , L. (10)

After all L slots have been updated, the new memory state is formed as Sr = [sr,1, . . . , sr,L]. Here sr−1,p is the previous value of slot p, v ⊥ r,p is the orthogonalized write content for that slot, wr,p controls the slot-specific write strength, and sr,p denotes the updated slot. All slot updates are computed with respect to the previous memory state Sr−1 and the batch-level write token ar.

#### 3.5 Rate-Distortion Optimization

After the proposal warm-up and hierarchy construction, the provisional flat anchors are discarded, and only the hierarchical 4D anchor scaffold is optimized for compression. We train SAGA with a rate-distortion objective over the quantized transmitted symbols Zˆ:

L = Drender + λrR(Zˆ) + λALA + λmaskLmask. (11)

Here R(Zˆ) is the entropy-estimated bitrate under the hierarchical coding order, and λr, λA and λmask control the relative strengths of the bitrate penalty, anchor regularization, and mask regularization terms, respectively. Quantization and the rate term are enabled after an initial reconstruction stage, with λr ramped up linearly. The rendering loss follows common 3DGS practice, with Drender = (1−η)∥ ˜Ic,s −Ic,s∥1 +η(1−SSIM( ˜Ic,s, Ic,s)), where ˜Ic,s and Ic,s are the rendered and ground-truth frames; during training, ˜Ic,s passes through a per-camera affine color correction that is the identity at test time. The anchor regularization constrains local offsets to remain within their parent-anchor coordinate ranges. We define OA = {o¯mi}∪ {o¯mij} for the coarse-to-fine and fine-to-primitive local offsets, and use LA = |OA| −1 P o¯∈OA ∥ ReLU(|o¯| − ρA)∥ 2 2 , where ρA is the allowed normalized boundary. This prevents the hierarchy from degenerating into unconstrained flat anchors.

#### 4.2 Experimental Results

Novel View Synthesis. As summarized in Table 1, we first evaluate SAGA as a compact dynamic GS representation before quantization and entropy coding. Across Neu3D and Panoptic Sports, SAGA provides a favorable fidelity-size trade-off, with its small variant achieving the smallest reported raw model size among compared methods. On Neu3D, the medium variant already surpasses all evaluated baselines in PSNR with a 17.37 MB model, while the large variant further achieves the best PSNR and LPIPS at 32.81 dB and 0.019, respectively. On Panoptic Sports, even the small variant outperforms all evaluated baselines in PSNR and SSIM, using only 8.7 MB. Increasing model capacity further improves reconstruction quality, although the LPIPS remains higher than that of 4DGS and E-D3DGS. These results show that the proposed spacetime anchor hierarchy and inter-anchor decoding provide a compact representation with competitive reconstruction quality before entropy coding; additional gains from quantization and entropy modeling are evaluated in Table 2.

Volumetric Video Compression. As summarized in Table 2, SAGA achieves consistent ratedistortion improvements over the evaluated GS-based volumetric codecs across the available comparisons. Compared with GIFStream [41], SAGA obtains large BD-rate reductions across all three datasets, with at least 67.09%, 67.10%, and 45.87% savings measured by PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, respectively. The gains also extend to recent baselines on Neu3D, with PSNR BD-rate savings exceeding 60% over both TED-4DGS and OMG4, indicating that the compression advantage is not limited to a single baseline. Beyond GS-based methods, SAGA also outperforms both TMIV configurations on MPEG MIV. Even against the VVEnc configuration, it achieves a PSNR BD-rate reduction of 18.41%, with larger savings of over 70% under SSIM and LPIPS. Together, these results demonstrate improved compression efficiency under both distortion-based and perceptual quality metrics. The corresponding rate-distortion curves and qualitative comparisons are provided in Figure 4 and Figure 5, with the latter further illustrating that SAGA preserves finer texture details at lower bitrates than GIFStream and OMG4.

### 4.3 Ablation Study

To investigate different design components in SAGA, we created the following variants: V1 removes the coarse-to-fine scaffold and uses flat anchors; V2 disables inter-anchor feature interpolation and decodes finer representations only from its own anchor; V3 removes the residual hash grid; V4 disables MLP compression; and V5 replaces the proposed orthogonality-guided memory update with a non-orthogonal gated residual update. The full model is used as the anchor for BD-rate computation. As shown in Table 3 (b), each ablated variant incurs BD-rate loss, demonstrating the effectiveness of the corresponding component. Removing the hierarchy or inter-anchor interpolation substantially degrades RD performance, supporting the importance of both components, while the 8.17% loss of V5 further confirms the contribution of the orthogonalized memory update.

Impact of Memory Slot Size. We also ablate the impact of memory slot size on compression performance and decoding speed, as shown in Table 3 (c). It can be observed that naively increasing the memory slot size offers diminishing returns in compression efficiency; this indicates that the long-context spatiotemporal dependency is already well captured with fewer memory slots, i.e., no more than 256. We hence choose 64 slots as the default for real-time decoding, while larger memories provide better compression at a substantial throughput cost.

### 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we presented SAGA, a GS-based volumetric video compression framework built on hierarchical sparse 4D anchor scaffolds, which exploit inter-anchor redundancy and model local dynamics at variable granularity. Sparse coarse anchors decode fine anchors, which instantiate Gaussian primitives via learned offsets, and learnable masks prune redundant primitives. A longcontext entropy model further summarizes decoded contexts into bounded memory for efficient entropy-parameter prediction. Experiments on Neu3D, Panoptic Sports, and MPEG MIV CTC benchmarks demonstrate that SAGA outperforms existing GS-based codecs in compression efficiency, with average BD-rate reductions (measured by PSNR) of 64.30%, 65.14%, and 90.88%, over TED-4DGS, OMG4, and GIFStream on the Neu3D testset, respectively.

Limitations and Future Work. SAGA still requires per-scene optimization, which may incur notable encoding time for long or complex sequences, and fast motion, topology changes, and sparse camera coverage may also challenge reconstruction. Future work will explore faster encoding, adaptive anchor allocation, and standardized bitstreams for scalability, random access, and deployment.

### A Broader Impact

This work improves the efficiency of volumetric video compression, with potential benefits for telepresence, remote collaboration, education, cultural preservation, digital media production, and immersive communication. By reducing storage and transmission costs while retaining real-time decoding, SAGA may make high-quality immersive media more accessible under limited bandwidth and compute budgets, and may reduce the infrastructure and energy costs of large-scale volumetric video delivery.

At the same time, more compact and realistic volumetric representations may increase risks related to privacy, unauthorized capture, surveillance, and deceptive or non-consensual synthetic media, especially when the content contains identifiable people or private environments. Responsible deployment should therefore consider consent, access control, provenance tracking, watermarking or authentication, and dataset governance. Our work focuses on compression and reconstruction quality on established research datasets, and does not develop tools for identity manipulation, text-driven content synthesis, or surveillance.
