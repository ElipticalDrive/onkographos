Holographic displays are a transforming technology for immersive virtual and
augmented reality systems. Exploring accurate yet efﬁcient computergenerated holography (CGH) algorithms for three-dimensional (3D) content is
a valuable research ﬁeld. Recent advancements in layer-based CGH may
exhibit limited capacity to convey comprehensive 3D information in accurately representing tilted angular spectrum and realizing realistic defocus blur.
Alternative approaches based on point clouds and light ﬁelds may demand
signiﬁcant computational resources for preparing adequate target data for
optimization. In addition, most existing CGH algorithms rely on heuristics to
encode complex amplitudes into phase-only holograms for display, which can
be highly ill-posed. Here we investigate an innovative CGH framework that
overcomes these challenges using a unique combination of mesh-based
representation, tilt-angle tailored wave propagation modeling, and complexvalued optimization, alongside a learning-empowered display calibration
scheme using camera feedback. The resulting expanded hologram encoding
capabilities enable the delivery of natural 3D depth cues, including smooth
defocus blur and view-dependent effects. Experimental results conducted on
our holographic near-eye display prototype demonstrate unprecedented full
3D visual quality, representing a signiﬁcant advancement in creating immersive visualization experiences.
Holography, offering immersive visual experience with rich depth
cues, represents a transformative technology for near-eye displays
(NEDs) toward a wide range of applications, spanning from entertainment and education to remote operation assistance and mixed
reality telepresence1–3. One signiﬁcant challenge associated with offthe-shelf NEDs is the essential issue of vergence-accommodation
conﬂict (VAC)4, which can lead to visual fatigue and various side effects
due to the restriction of having only one single focus depth available.
In this context, holography stands out for its capability to encode and
reconstruct the complete wave ﬁeld information, aka., full 3D information. Advancements in digital computers and spatial light modulators (SLMs) over the past decade have facilitated the development
of full-color computer-generated holographic (CGH) displays5–8. Various algorithms have been proposed to tackle the two primary challenges in CGH: numerical wave ﬁeld diffraction representation and
hologram encoding.
Classical algorithms for modeling numerical diffraction to simulate wavefront propagation within 3D space commonly rely on the
Fresnel integral and Fourier transform9. These algorithms are adapted
to different representations of 3D models (refer to Fig. 1b for visualization). Point cloud data, which is straightforward, simple, and widely
favored in CGH, suffers from an inherent trade-off between computational efﬁciency and accuracy10,11. On the other hand, RGB-D representations using the angular spectrum method (ASM)12 as the

Wave ﬁelds rendered from meshes typically exhibit fewer speckle
artifacts and discontinuities compared to point clouds or RGB-D
models. Despite these advantages, mesh-based CGH algorithms have
only been demonstrated with limited objects in relatively low image
quality due to challenges such as high computational overhead in
afﬁne transformation16, handling textures17,18, and managing
occlusions19,20.
Phase-only SLMs are the dominating wave modulation platform in
holographic displays, delivering stable performance, improved light
efﬁciency, and lightweight design towards dynamic wearable
displays21,22. Within this context, the primary challenge of hologram
encoding lies in solving the ill-posed inverse problem of generating a
phase-only hologram (POH) that faithfully reconstructs the desired
wave ﬁeld. Double-phase amplitude coding (DPAC)23 represents a
direct encoding method that is computationally efﬁcient, but suffers
from reduced light efﬁciency and high-order artifacts24. In contrast,
iterative approaches commonly employ a variety of non-convex optimization strategies25, including phase retrieval techniques26,27 or
gradient-based optimization methods 28,29. These iterative approaches
typically yield superior phase-only solutions compared to direct
methods but rely heavily on the ﬁdelity of the wave propagation
model. Besides the widely adopted ASM, advanced modeling techniques like high-order diffraction simulation24 and camera-calibrated
propagation, either online or ofﬂine13,29,30, have been explored to
faithfully represent the physical setup. However, most optimization
methods focus solely on constraining the target amplitude11,29, as
human vision primarily perceives intensity information. Notably, while
this strategy enhances the reconstruction quality on supervised
planes, it fundamentally compromises 3D visual cues such as defocus
blur and continuous depth cues.
In this work, we investigate a set of innovative techniques aimed at
tackling the aforementioned challenges, as shown in Fig. 1. Our
methods encompass a designed perspective-to-orthogonal smooth
wave ﬁeld rendering pipeline (POS-WRP), which involves encoding
mesh scenes into wave ﬁelds with ﬁne-grained textures, photo-realistic
shadings, and perspective depth cues. In addition, we introduce the
phase-aware gradient descent (PAGD) algorithm, which is an iterative
approach for computing physically accurate 3D holograms with
complex-valued wave ﬁelds-aware optimization, supporting continuous depth perception. Moreover, we unveil an enhanced Flying
Planes mesh-wave ﬁeld-hologram dataset, tailored for true 3D holographic display calibration, effectively bridging the disparity in wavefront propagation between simulation and physical experiments.

Texture baking31 involves encoding shading information from a
scene into a texture for subsequent rendering. In this study, as shown
in Fig. 2a, the input 3D scene comprises meshes and lighting data
captured through a perspective camera. We employ physically based
rendering (PBR) pipelines32 to bake the rendering outcomes onto a
predeﬁned output texture, yielding a texture containing the physical
illumination of a scene (see Fig. 2e, left), which subsequently serves as
the amplitude of the wave ﬁeld across the mesh facets. Occlusion
handling is naturally integrated during baking, where any obstructed
portion of a facet is rendered black, indicating zero amplitude. It is
worth noting that the occlusion modeling in PBR adheres to the
principles of geometric optics32. While diffraction at occlusion edges is
not explicitly accounted for, the facet size and spatial sampling rate
render the visual loss negligible. This assumption enhances computational efﬁciency compared to a wave-based masking approach during wave propagation19,33.

projection space is orthogonal and differs from the perspective projection in the scene space, we transform both the original mesh and the
baked texture into a projection-corrected form, as shown in Fig. 2b.

Our 3D mesh-based holographic display framework operates through
a two-stage process. Initially, the input mesh scene undergoes rendering into a wave ﬁeld on the hologram plane, which is subsequently
followed by an optimization to convert the wave ﬁeld into a phase-only

methodology for encoding meshes with realistic shadings into the
wave ﬁeld, utilizing a robust rendering scheme, simultaneously conducting wave propagation of the facets in parallel.

Phase-only hologram synthesis
The PAGD algorithm is proposed to encode the complex wave ﬁeld
obtained by POS-WRP into phase-only holograms for 3D holography
with continuous depth cues. A camera-calibrated, learned model is
employed to enhance the precision of PAGD, with the goal of reconstructing a 3D wave ﬁeld with accurate phase information.
Amplitude-based optimization encounters three primary limitations. First, the number and positions of target planes are typically set
manually, leading to a trade-off between computational complexity
and the continuity of depth cues. For example, Fig. 3 demonstrates
that phase information can help reduce the number of required
supervised planes while maintaining equivalent imaging quality. Second, amplitude-based multi-layer representations are constrained to
encoding scenes from a single viewpoint, whereas complex ﬁelds can
capture directional light ﬁeld information at each pixel in 3D space14.
Lastly, even for a single viewpoint, discrete multi-plane amplitudes
only provide axial information, resulting in unrealistic defocus blur
during the reconstruction of 3D scenes.
Phase information is essential for reconstructing wave ﬁelds that
propagate beyond the principal optical axis, which is critical for rendering accurate depth cues. For instance, in Fig. 4, a scene comprising
three identical patterns at varying depths propagates at an angle of 1°
relative to the optical axis. Supervision is applied to the 1st and 3rd
planes, with the middle plane set as a held-out example. These simulation results indicate that phase-aware supervision offers a more
accurate reconstruction, highlighting the value of phase for authentic
3D wave ﬁeld reconstruction.
While phase carries important information for the target wave
ﬁeld, supervising it presents challenges due to the non-uniqueness of
phase distribution for a given target. We note that, for any target wave
ﬁeld derived from meshes, there are two inherent shift invariances that
must be considered. Firstly, any global shift in the phase of the entire
ﬁeld does not alter the wave ﬁeld. Secondly, the phase value on each
pixel can be shifted by 2π without changing the target ﬁeld. Consequently, the target ﬁeld derived from the mesh represents one of the
feasible solutions subject to these two shift-invariances. The proposed
solution to address this challenge involves introducing a complexvalued scale factor.

View-dependent holography is a direct outcome of the proposed
PAGD method. Leveraging the forward model’s capability to reconstruct the phase of the wave ﬁeld, it effectively encodes the propagation characteristics of the target wave ﬁeld into the phase via blazed
gratings. Unlike existing 2.5D holography using RGB-D representations, our approach embeds depth information into the third
“dimension”, phase. In contrast to the OLAS method15, our method
excels in modeling aberrations through the forward model, resulting in
higher image quality. Furthermore, in comparison to alternative endto-end optimization strategies for view-dependent holography37, our
proposed method offers distinct advantages. It requires training the
forward model only once, eliminating the need for speciﬁc data per
viewpoint during training. Additionally, our method does not necessitate precise modeling of the ﬁlter to replicate the physical characteristics of an iris.
By reconstructing the wave ﬁeld’s phase, our method facilitates
3D imaging with continuous depth cues across multiple viewpoints.
Speciﬁcally, the left side of Fig. 7c, a 3D scene is depicted featuring two
objects situated at distinct depths. We observe that the object occlusion alters with varying viewpoints. Meanwhile, the right side of Fig. 7c
exhibits a 3D outdoor scene encompassing a detailed background,
illustrating our method’s ability to support depth cues and detailed
texture variations across viewpoints.

mesh-represented scenes using an optical medium. Padmanaban et al.14
and Chakravarthula et al.15 transformed light ﬁelds into view-dependent

target wave ﬁeld’s phase, supporting view-dependent holographic displays leveraging blazed gratings (see Section “Methods" for details).
These view-dependent holograms are optimized based on the target
complex wave ﬁelds encoded with multi-view information.

Recent studies on end-to-end holographic display frameworks29
have demonstrated enhanced display quality by integrating tailored
wave propagation with physical display systems. However, this tightly
coupled approach may result in reduced robustness, necessitating
extensive manual ﬁne-tuning for optimal holographic reconstruction.
The interdependent nature of end-to-end frameworks could limit the
model’s scalability and interpretability. To the best of our knowledge,
although phases are intensively investigated to encode and represent
3D information in classic holography38, serving as a vital link between
recording and reconstruction stages for depth information transmission, their signiﬁcance appears somewhat overlooked in end-to-end
designed holographic displays.
Unlike amplitude-only end-to-end optimization techniques, our
approach adheres to the well-established physical two-step hologram process involving wave ﬁeld recording and holographic
reconstruction. These stages are interconnected through phase
information, with the recording and reconstruction processes digitized using the suggested POS-WRP and PAGD methods, respectively.

Implementation details
Software implementation. The initial scene containing meshes and
textures utilized in this study was created using Blender48. The POSWRP method utilizes OpenGL in conjunction with customized vertex
10
Article
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-74557-0
and fragment shaders to produce perspective-correct meshes and
remap textures. Spectral remapping is accomplished through grid
interpolation in PyTorch. To tackle anti-aliasing, the wave ﬁeld is
magniﬁed by a factor of two in both dimensions, and a ﬁll factor of 0.93
is applied.
To train the camera-calibrated wave propagation model, a total of
5860 phases are captured. These phases encompass 2D phases generated via stochastic gradient descent, higher-order gradient descent,
and the double-phase amplitude encoding technique, as well as true
3D phases derived from the proposed Flying Planes mesh-wave ﬁeldhologram dataset. Each phase is recorded at eight target planes,
positioned at distances of 10.00, 10.07, 10.14, 10.21, 10.28, 10.35, 10.42,
and 10.49 cm from the SLM. The region of interest (ROI) is deﬁned as
880 × 1600 due to the laser’s uniformity and the modulator’s efﬁciency. The model is trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with a
batch size of one for twenty epochs, utilizing the Adam optimizer with
a learning rate of 0.0005.
Hardware setup. A benchtop holographic display prototype is assembled to validate the proposed methodology, as depicted in Fig. 7b.
Holograms are modulated using a HOLOEYE Pluto-2 phase-only SLM,
with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a pixel pitch of 8μm. Illumination is
provided by an RGB single-mode ﬁber-coupled laser (Fisba ReadyBeam)
with principal wavelengths of 639.0, 524.9, and 445.8 nm, respectively.
The laser beam traverses a collimating lens, a neutral density ﬁlter, a
linear polarizer, and a beam splitter. The collimated, uniform beam, with
the required polarization for the SLM, is incident perpendicularly onto
the SLM. Subsequently, the modulated waves pass through an eyepiece
and a camera lens before being recorded by a FLIR Grasshopper 3 sensor
with a resolution of 1920 × 1200 in the RAW16 format. The eyepiece is a
Nikon 50 mm SLR lens, while the camera lens is a Canon 35 mm SLR lens,
with its focus distance adjusted using an Arduino microcontroller. In this
setup, the wave propagation model is trained without the inclusion of an
optical ﬁlter. However, to capture view-dependent holograms, an optical
ﬁlter, speciﬁcally a pinhole, is positioned at the Fourier plane in front of
the camera lens to mimic the pupil.
Prior to image acquisition, calibration procedures are executed
for both the SLM and the camera. The SLM’s voltage response is calibrated using vortex patterns, with manual ﬁne-tuning carried out
through the calibration software supplied by the manufacturer. The
camera is conﬁgured to focus on eight target planes, and depth calibration is conducted by displaying a set of ten holograms at the
respective depths to determine the necessary offsets for precise
focusing. To minimize jitter in the captured images, a homographybased afﬁne transformation is applied49, ensuring dataset stability
through image alignment.
