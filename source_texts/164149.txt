# Mesh-represented and learning-empowered hologram synthesis for full 3D holographic displays

Xiangyu Men[g](http://orcid.org/0009-0002-8828-7036) [1](http://orcid.org/0009-0002-8828-7036),2, Wenbin Zho[u](http://orcid.org/0009-0001-4487-2877) 1,2 & Yifan Pen[g](http://orcid.org/0000-0003-0667-2599) 1

Holographic displays are a transforming technology for immersive virtual and augmented reality systems. Exploring accurate yet efficient computergenerated holography (CGH) algorithms for three-dimensional (3D) content is a valuable research field. Recent advancements in layer-based CGH may exhibit limited capacity to convey comprehensive 3D information in accurately representing tilted angular spectrum and realizing realistic defocus blur. Alternative approaches based on point clouds and light fields may demand significant computational resources for preparing adequate target data for optimization. In addition, most existing CGH algorithms rely on heuristics to encode complex amplitudes into phase-only holograms for display, which can be highly ill-posed. Here we investigate an innovative CGH framework that overcomes these challenges using a unique combination of mesh-based representation, tilt-angle tailored wave propagation modeling, and complexvalued optimization, alongside a learning-empowered display calibration scheme using camera feedback. The resulting expanded hologram encoding capabilities enable the delivery of natural 3D depth cues, including smooth defocus blur and view-dependent effects. Experimental results conducted on our holographic near-eye display prototype demonstrate unprecedented full 3D visual quality, representing a significant advancement in creating immersive visualization experiences.

Holography, offering immersive visual experience with rich depth cues, represents a transformative technology for near-eye displays (NEDs) toward a wide range of applications, spanning from entertainment and education to remote operation assistance and mixed reality telepresence1–3 . One significant challenge associated with offthe-shelf NEDs is the essential issue of vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC\)4 , which can lead to visual fatigue and various side effects due to the restriction of having only one single focus depth available. In this context, holography stands out for its capability to encode and reconstruct the complete wave field information, aka., full 3D information. Advancements in digital computers and spatial light modulators (SLMs) over the past decade have facilitated the development of full-color computer-generated holographic (CGH) displays5–8 . Various algorithms have been proposed to tackle the two primary challenges in CGH: numerical wave field diffraction representation and hologram encoding.

Classical algorithms for modeling numerical diffraction to simulate wavefront propagation within 3D space commonly rely on the Fresnel integral and Fourier transform9 . These algorithms are adapted to different representations of 3D models (refer to Fig. 1b for visualization). Point cloud data, which is straightforward, simple, and widely favored in CGH, suffers from an inherent trade-off between computational efficiency and accuracy10,11. In contrast, light field supports multi-view hologram synthesis by employing the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)14,15, albeit at the cost of a trade-off between spatial and angular resolutions. Besides these representations, meshes are extensively employed in various 3D applications due to their detailed geometric description of scenes crafted by artists. Wave fields rendered from meshes typically exhibit fewer speckle artifacts and discontinuities compared to point clouds or RGB-D models. Despite these advantages, mesh-based CGH algorithms have only been demonstrated with limited objects in relatively low image quality due to challenges such as high computational overhead in affine transformation16, handling textures17,18, and managing occlusions19,20.

Phase-only SLMs are the dominating wave modulation platform in holographic displays, delivering stable performance, improved light efficiency, and lightweight design towards dynamic wearable displays21,22. Within this context, the primary challenge of hologram encoding lies in solving the ill-posed inverse problem of generating a phase-only hologram (POH) that faithfully reconstructs the desired wave field. Double-phase amplitude coding (DPAC\)23 represents a direct encoding method that is computationally efficient, but suffers from reduced light efficiency and high-order artifacts24. In contrast, iterative approaches commonly employ a variety of non-convex optimization strategies25, including phase retrieval techniques26,27 or gradient-based optimization methods 28,29. These iterative approaches typically yield superior phase-only solutions compared to direct methods but rely heavily on the fidelity of the wave propagation model. Besides the widely adopted ASM, advanced modeling techniques like high-order diffraction simulation24 and camera-calibrated propagation, either online or offline13,29,30, have been explored to faithfully represent the physical setup. However, most optimization methods focus solely on constraining the target amplitude11,29, as human vision primarily perceives intensity information. Notably, while this strategy enhances the reconstruction quality on supervised planes, it fundamentally compromises 3D visual cues such as defocus blur and continuous depth cues.

In this work, we investigate a set of innovative techniques aimed at tackling the aforementioned challenges, as shown in Fig. 1. Our methods encompass a designed perspective-to-orthogonal smooth wave field rendering pipeline (POS-WRP), which involves encoding mesh scenes into wave fields with fine-grained textures, photo-realistic shadings, and perspective depth cues. In addition, we introduce the phase-aware gradient descent (PAGD) algorithm, which is an iterative approach for computing physically accurate 3D holograms with complex-valued wave fields-aware optimization, supporting continuous depth perception. Moreover, we unveil an enhanced Flying Planes mesh-wave field-hologram dataset, tailored for true 3D holographic display calibration, effectively bridging the disparity in wavefront propagation between simulation and physical experiments. These elements are co-optimized via a learning-based calibration strategy with camera feedback. ### Results

### Rendering wave field of mesh-represented scenes

Our 3D mesh-based holographic display framework operates through a two-stage process. Initially, the input mesh scene undergoes rendering into a wave field on the hologram plane, which is subsequently followed by an optimization to convert the wave field into a phase-only hologram. The proposed perspective-to-orthogonal wave field rendering pipeline (POS-WRP) outlined in this subsection describes the methodology for encoding meshes with realistic shadings into the wave field, utilizing a robust rendering scheme, simultaneously conducting wave propagation of the facets in parallel.

With spectral resampling and ASM (refer to "Methods" for details), each tilted facet of a mesh scene can be propagated to the hologram plane, thereby creating a wave field representation of the entire scene. However, relying solely on the vanilla propagation presents challenges in maintaining consistent illumination, shading, occlusion, and, particularly, perspective depth cues. This issue arises because the wave field modulated by the SLM propagates solely within the paraxial region in an orthogonal manner (refer to Fig. 2). To overcome these limitations, we introduce an innovative wave field rendering pipeline that incorporates the aforementioned factors into a projection-corrected mesh and textures. Specifically, this approach involves applying a perspective-to-orthogonal transformation to both mesh coordinates and baked textures to retain the perspective cues. The resulting meshes with unified textures are concurrently processed through tilted plane propagation, incorporating an additional phase smoothing step.

Texture baking31 involves encoding shading information from a scene into a texture for subsequent rendering. In this study, as shown in Fig. 2a, the input 3D scene comprises meshes and lighting data captured through a perspective camera. We employ physically based rendering (PBR) pipelines32 to bake the rendering outcomes onto a predefined output texture, yielding a texture containing the physical illumination of a scene (see Fig. 2e, left), which subsequently serves as the amplitude of the wave field across the mesh facets. Occlusion handling is naturally integrated during baking, where any obstructed portion of a facet is rendered black, indicating zero amplitude. It is worth noting that the occlusion modeling in PBR adheres to the principles of geometric optics32. While diffraction at occlusion edges is not explicitly accounted for, the facet size and spatial sampling rate render the visual loss negligible. This assumption enhances computational efficiency compared to a wave-based masking approach during wave propagation19,33.

For maintaining consistency in rendering results under orthogonal projection during wave field propagation from the SLM, where the projection space is orthogonal and differs from the perspective projection in the scene space, we transform both the originalmesh and the baked texture into a projection-corrected form, as shown in Fig. 2b. To ensure accurate depth perception, it is essential to set the scene depth of meshes appropriately during the 3D scene setup to align with the post-transformation depth distribution using linear matrices.

In the process of converting the coordinates of vertices from scene space to SLM space, the baked texture in the scene space should be remapped into the SLM space, otherwise, the rendered image would be distorted (refer to Fig. 2g). This involves defining Mortho, Vortho, and Portho for the pipeline to render with orthogonal projection. Customized shaders are utilized to map the texture coordinates based on the coordinate transformation matrices. The pipeline eventually outputs rendering results that are stored in a target texture, corresponding to the texture for vertices in SLM space. In addition, the shader is enhanced to perform perspective-correct interpolation for the texture34. The remapped texture may appear slightly "twisted", as shown in Fig. 2e, but it delivers the correct visual output after orthogonal projection (refer to Fig. 2c). For further insights into texture remapping, refer to the supplementary material.

With the projection-corrected meshes and textures, each visible facet can be represented as a plane wave field, where the position is determined by vertex coordinates and the amplitude by textures, accounting for occlusion effects. Assuming an initial phase of zero before tilted propagation, each plane wave is then rotated and propagated to the hologram plane, as described in Section "Methods". On the hologram plane, the phase values of the facet wave fields are aggregated into a histogram with a shift to center the distribution peak. This strategy facilitates the generation of a relatively smooth target phase (see Fig. 2d), which helps mitigate artifacts in neighboring regions of adjacent facets stemming from hardware constraints such as SLM crosstalk.

### Phase-only hologram synthesis

The PAGD algorithm is proposed to encode the complex wave field obtained by POS-WRP into phase-only holograms for 3D holography with continuous depth cues. A camera-calibrated, learned model is employed to enhance the precision of PAGD, with the goal of reconstructing a 3D wave field with accurate phase information.

Amplitude-based optimization encounters three primary limitations. First, the number and positions of target planes are typically set manually, leading to a trade-off between computational complexity and the continuity of depth cues. For example, Fig. 3 demonstrates that phase information can help reduce the number of required supervised planes while maintaining equivalent imaging quality. Second, amplitude-based multi-layer representations are constrained to encoding scenes from a single viewpoint, whereas complex fields can capture directional light field information at each pixel in 3D space14. Lastly, even for a single viewpoint, discrete multi-plane amplitudes only provide axial information, resulting in unrealistic defocus blur during the reconstruction of 3D scenes.

Phase information is essential for reconstructing wave fields that propagate beyond the principal optical axis, which is critical for rendering accurate depth cues. For instance, in Fig. 4, a scene comprising three identical patterns at varying depths propagates at an angle of 1° relative to the optical axis. Supervision is applied to the 1st and 3rd planes, with the middle plane set as a held-out example. These simulation results indicate that phase-aware supervision offers a more accurate reconstruction, highlighting the value of phase for authentic 3D wave field reconstruction.

While phase carries important information for the target wave field, supervising it presents challenges due to the non-uniqueness of phase distribution for a given target. We note that, for any target wave field derived from meshes, there are two inherent shift invariances that must be considered. Firstly, any global shift in the phase of the entire field does not alter the wave field. Secondly, the phase value on each pixel can be shifted by 2π without changing the target field. Consequently, the target field derived from the mesh represents one of the feasible solutions subject to these two shift-invariances. The proposed solution to address this challenge involves introducing a complexvalued scale factor.

Box 1 outlines the proposed optimization pipeline that transforms the rendered wave field into a phase-only hologram. This process begins with the input mesh scene and the specified illumination and camera settings, which are then processed with the POS-WRP to generate the target wave field.

Subsequently, as illustrated in Fig. 5a, the rendered wave field is propagated to the near, medium, and far planes. During each iteration, the phase is randomly initialized and propagated to the same target planes using either the vanilla ASM or the camera-calibrated wave propagation model. Next, the complex-valued scale factor is adopted to account for the deviation in light source intensity and shiftinvariance characteristics of the target phase, which is calculated by solving the following minimization problem

$$\text{argmin}_s \|s \cdot u_r - u_t\|_2^2. \quad (3)$$

### View-dependent holography with PAGD

Recently, Matsushima et al.36 realized view-dependent holograms for mesh-represented scenes using an optical medium. Padmanaban et al.14 and Chakravarthula et al.15 transformed light fields into view-dependent holograms. To this end, the PAGD algorithm is able to reconstruct the target wave field's phase, supporting view-dependent holographic displays leveraging blazed gratings (see Section "Methods" for details). These view-dependent holograms are optimized based on the target complex wave fields encoded with multi-view information.

Given n viewpoints indexed i = 1, …, n with viewing angles θi, ηi, respectively, the target wave field can be constructed through the coherent superposition of wave fields of all viewpoints as

$$u_{\text{target}}(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i(x, y) u_{\theta_i, \eta_i}(x, y), \quad (6)$$

where uθi, ηi ðx, yÞ represents the blazed grating for view angles of θi, ηi, and ui(x, y) is the wave field for ith viewpoint. The resulting target wave field encapsulates information from multiple viewpoints. From "Methods", the target spectrum is a superposition of spectra with corresponding shifts. As shown in Fig. 7a, a physical iris is set in front of the camera to simulate the pupil, acting as a filtering mechanism. Positioned at the Fourier plane, this iris functions as a mask on the target spectrum. By adjusting its position, only waves corresponding to specific angles can traverse the filter, enabling the camera to record the wavefront from a particular viewpoint.

Although PAGD can directly optimize holograms with utarget, the physical iris is modeled as a spectral filter to improve image quality. This strategy involves defining a mask function on the Fourier plane. Given the wave field u(x, y), the filtered wave field ubiðx, yÞ can be derived as

$$\hat{u}_t(x, y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\mathcal{F}[u(x, y)] \text{Mask}_{(u_{\theta_t}, v_{\theta_t}, r)}(f_x, f_y)\},$$
where  $\text{Mask}_{(u_{\theta_t}, v_{\theta_t}, r)}(f_x, f_y) =$  (7)
$$\begin{cases} 1, & (f_x - \frac{\sin \theta_t}{\lambda})^2 + (f_y - \frac{\sin \theta_t}{\lambda})^2 \leq r^2, \\ 0, & \text{others.} \end{cases}$$

Following Eq. (7), the loss function is computed by comparing the filtered wave field obtained from the forward model against the target wave field.

View-dependent holography is a direct outcome of the proposed PAGD method. Leveraging the forward model's capability to reconstruct the phase of the wave field, it effectively encodes the propagation characteristics of the target wave field into the phase via blazed gratings. Unlike existing 2.5D holography using RGB-D representations, our approach embeds depth information into the third "dimension", phase. In contrast to the OLAS method15, our method excels in modeling aberrations through the forward model, resulting in higher image quality. Furthermore, in comparison to alternative endto-end optimization strategies for view-dependent holography37, our proposed method offers distinct advantages. It requires training the forward model only once, eliminating the need for specific data per viewpoint during training. Additionally, our method does not necessitate precise modeling of the filter to replicate the physical characteristics of an iris.

By reconstructing the wave field's phase, our method facilitates 3D imaging with continuous depth cues across multiple viewpoints. Specifically, the left side of Fig. 7c, a 3D scene is depicted featuring two objects situated at distinct depths. We observe that the object occlusion alters with varying viewpoints. Meanwhile, the right side of Fig. 7c exhibits a 3D outdoor scene encompassing a detailed background, illustrating our method's ability to support depth cues and detailed texture variations across viewpoints.

### Discussion

The presented POS-WRP method can facilitate rendering complexvalued wave fields from mesh-represented 3D scenes containing finegrained textures. This scheme delivers high-fidelity wave field data with continuous depth cues and reduced speckle artifacts. Subsequently, the wave fields are encoded into phase-only holograms using the proposed PAGD optimizer for 3D holographic displays.

Recent studies on end-to-end holographic display frameworks29 have demonstrated enhanced display quality by integrating tailored wave propagation with physical display systems. However, this tightly coupled approach may result in reduced robustness, necessitating extensive manual fine-tuning for optimal holographic reconstruction. The interdependent nature of end-to-end frameworks could limit the model's scalability and interpretability. To the best of our knowledge, although phases are intensively investigated to encode and represent 3D information in classic holography38, serving as a vital link between recording and reconstruction stages for depth information transmission, their significance appears somewhat overlooked in end-to-end designed holographic displays.

Unlike amplitude-only end-to-end optimization techniques, our approach adheres to the well-established physical two-step hologram process involving wave field recording and holographic reconstruction. These stages are interconnected through phase information, with the recording and reconstruction processes digitized using the suggested POS-WRP and PAGD methods, respectively. The proposed mesh-based CGH approach leverages the widespread adoption of mesh-based representations in modern computer graphics32 and the availability of abundant 3D data. Furthermore, realtime rendering pipelines31 offer hardware and software acceleration for efficient mesh rendering. Although GPU parallelism has been utilized to enhance efficiency, achieving real-time hologram generation remains a goal for future exploration. Specifically, the time for generating one phase-only hologram from a mesh scene is on average 54.96 s (24.96 s for POS-WRP and 30 s for PAGD). ### Methods

### Preliminary of wavefront propagation

Both wave field diffraction and hologram encoding necessitate wavefront propagation. Illustrated in Fig. 8, propagating a tilted facet to a hologram plane involves two essential operations: rotation and parallel propagation. The parallel propagation of a wave field is typically modeled using a free-space propagation operator, notably the angular spectrum method (ASM)12,46, resulting in a wavefront f(x, y; z) as

fðx, y; z0Þ = F 1 fFff ðx, y; 0ÞgHðu, v; z0, λÞg, ð8Þ

where F and F 1 denote the Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms, respectively. H(u, v; z0, λ) denotes the transfer function incorporating the propagation distance z0 and the wavelength λ.

To address the rotation of the wave field, a practical strategy involves integrating the ASM with coordinate transformation in the Fourier domain47. In this context, we denote the wave field as f(x, y) in the global coordinate system and equivalently as gðbx,byÞ in the local coordinate system. The transform between these coordinate systems is defined as T−1 =[aij]fori, j = 1, …, 3, resulting in v = Tvb, where v and vb represent the identical vectors in the global and local coordinate systems, respectively. We further denote F(u, v) and Gðub, bvÞ as the spectra of f(x, y) and gðx b,byÞ, respectively. The relationship between the unknown F(u, v) and the provided Gðub, bvÞ can be established through spectral remapping, as follows

where 
$$\begin{cases} F(u, v) = G[\alpha(u, v), \beta(u, v)], \\ \alpha(u, v) = a_{11}u + a_{12}v + a_{13}w(u, v) \\ \beta(u, v) = a_{21}u + a_{22}v + a_{23}w(u, v) \end{cases} \quad (9)$$

The wave field f(x, y) can be obtained by applying the inverse Fourier transform to F(u, v). To address limited bandwidth and enhance efficiency, we employ the transformation u0 = u u0, v0 = v v0 for ½u0, v0, w0-T = T½0, 0, λ 1 T . Substituting this transformation into the inverse Fourier transform yields the following

$$f(x,y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(u', v') | J(u', v')|\} \cdot e^{i2\pi(u_0x + v_0y)}, \quad (10)$$

where Jðu, vÞ = ∂ðα, βÞ ∂ðu, vÞ represents the Jacobian of the transformation in Eq. (9). Further details are available in the supplementary material.

The term ej2πðu0x + v0yÞ corresponds to a blazed grating, which redirects the propagation away from the principal optical axis back to the facet normal. In the envisioned wave field rendering scheme, the camera consistently records waves propagating from the facet towards the camera, rather than along the facet's normal. Consequently, the blazed grating term can be omitted as it does not match the intended propagation direction.

In the realm of view-dependent holography, the blazed grating serves as a means to scatter light in a particular direction. We employ blazed gratings to alter the propagation direction of the wavefront, a concept that is elucidated in this subsection. Specifically, a twodimensional blazed grating can be defined as

$$u_{\theta, \eta}(x, y) = e^{i2\pi(\frac{\sin \theta}{\lambda}x + \frac{\sin \eta}{\lambda}y)}, \quad (11)$$

where θ and η represent the tilt angles of the x axis and y axis, respectively, while λ denotes the wavelength. The blazed grating denotes a plane wave propagating along the direction characterized by tilt angles θ and η. Here we denote uθ = sin θ λ , vη = sin η λ as the frequencydomain coordinates of the blazed grating, establishing a connection between spatial angle and angular frequency.

When considering a wavefront u(x, y), its multiplication with the blazed grating can be represented as u0ðx, yÞ = uðx, yÞuθ, ηðx, yÞ, where u0ðx, yÞ retains the same amplitude as u(x, y) but propagates in the direction rotated by angles θ, η. Note that the amplitude of the blazed grating is always 1. Performing the Fourier transform on u0ðx, yÞ yields Ffuðx, yÞuθ, ηðx, yÞg =Uðf x uθ,f y vηÞ, indicating a shift in the frequency domain.

Blazed gratings are constrained by a sampling limit imposed by the feature sizes of SLMs, which restricts the maximum tilt angle of the blazed grating. Let Δx denote the feature size, representing the spatial sampling interval along the x-axis of the SLM. The spectral half bandwidth of the SLM plane is computed as Bx = 1 2Δx. To prevent distortion, the frequency-domain coordinate uθ must adhere to uθ < Bx, leading to the requirement θ < arcsin λ 2Δx. Similarly, for the y-axis, we obtain η < arcsin λ 2Δy. Given the specifications of our prototype, it is deemed more suitable to set the maximum tilt angle to 1. 5°.

To tackle anti-aliasing, the wave field is magnified by a factor of two in both dimensions, and a fill factor of 0.93 is applied. To train the camera-calibrated wave propagation model, a total of 5860 phases are captured. These phases encompass 2D phases generated via stochastic gradient descent, higher-order gradient descent, and the double-phase amplitude encoding technique, as well as true 3D phases derived from the proposed Flying Planes mesh-wave fieldhologram dataset. Each phase is recorded at eight target planes, positioned at distances of 10.00, 10.07, 10.14, 10.21, 10.28, 10.35, 10.42, and 10.49 cm from the SLM. The region of interest (ROI) is defined as 880 × 1600 due to the laser's uniformity and the modulator's efficiency. The model is trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with a batch size of one for twenty epochs, utilizing the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0005. Hardware setup. A benchtop holographic display prototype is assembled to validate the proposed methodology, as depicted in Fig. 7b. Holograms are modulated using a HOLOEYE Pluto-2 phase-only SLM, with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a pixel pitch of 8μm. Illumination is provided by an RGB single-mode fiber-coupled laser (Fisba ReadyBeam) with principal wavelengths of 639.0, 524.9, and 445.8 nm, respectively. The laser beam traverses a collimating lens, a neutral density filter, a linear polarizer, and a beam splitter. The collimated, uniform beam, with the required polarization for the SLM, is incident perpendicularly onto the SLM. Subsequently, the modulated waves pass through an eyepiece and a camera lens before being recorded by a FLIR Grasshopper 3 sensor with a resolution of 1920 × 1200 in the RAW16 format. The eyepiece is a Nikon 50 mm SLR lens, while the camera lens is a Canon 35 mm SLR lens, with its focus distance adjusted using an Arduino microcontroller. In this setup, the wave propagation model is trained without the inclusion of an optical filter. However, to capture view-dependent holograms, an optical filter, specifically a pinhole, is positioned at the Fourier plane in front of the camera lens to mimic the pupil. Prior to image acquisition, calibration procedures are executed for both the SLM and the camera. The SLM's voltage response is calibrated using vortex patterns, with manual fine-tuning carried out through the calibration software supplied by the manufacturer. The camera is configured to focus on eight target planes, and depth calibration is conducted by displaying a set of ten holograms at the respective depths to determine the necessary offsets for precise focusing. To minimize jitter in the captured images, a homographybased affine transformation is applied49, ensuring dataset stability through image alignment.
