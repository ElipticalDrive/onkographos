Pushing the Limits of Gaussian Splatting for Spatial Storytelling
A speculative presentation of a gaussian splat using Threebird, The New York Times' in-house collaborative 3D authoring tool.
Advancements in capturing and rendering 3D scenes have the potential to create spatial stories faster and more easily than has previously been possible. Building off of our earlier experiments with photogrammetry and neural radiance fields (NeRF), R&D, with support from the Graphics Department, set out to understand gaussian splatting and continue to explore the future of photography and 3D media. Modern gaussian splatting caught our eye after "3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering" was named one of the best papers of SIGGRAPH 2023. While photogrammetry is a tried-and-tested method, it’s extremely labor- and compute- intensive, and it reliably fails to reconstruct certain textures and settings. NeRF technology addresses some of these concerns, but is extremely difficult to deliver these radiance fields to audiences. We wanted to see how splatting fared under similar circumstances.
In addition to exploring workflows and pipelines for capture, processing and delivery, we aimed to interrogate a few big questions that could unlock splats for spatial stories at The New York Times:
Can we generate splats with high levels of photorealism and geometric accuracy?
Can we generate splats from suboptimal captures that don’t work with traditional methods?
Can we reliably deliver 3D splatting scenes to web and mobile devices, as well as display them in augmented or mixed reality through headsets?
The short answer to all of these questions is “yes, we can.” Gaussian splatting is still a developing field within spatial capture, but it offers many advancements over photogrammetry that we expect will continue to improve.

What are Gaussian Splats?
Gaussian splats represent a 3D object or scene differently than the photogrammetry methods we have been using. Instead of creating a mesh — a virtual object with defined volume and photographic textures mapped onto it — splats encode 3D scenes as a point cloud made up of gaussian functions. Each gaussian function can be envisioned as a 2D disc assigned to a point in 3D space with attributes for orientation, color, transparency, and size. The gaussian function determines how quickly the density of each disc falls off from the center point. In aggregate, these 2D discs blend together to create smooth visual transitions and give the point cloud the appearance of a solid volumetric object.

Compared to photogrammetry and neural radiance fields, gaussian splatting as a technique and medium has intrinsic benefits:
The ability to render elements that photogrammetry traditionally cannot, such as fine detail, texture, transparency and reflection
The ability to capture scenes much faster than photogrammetry, opening up the possibility of capturing humans in novel poses.
The flexibility to use much less input data than photogrammetry, allowing recreations from a wider variety of sources and situations.
The ability to be compressed into much smaller files than photogrammetry models of equivalent scenes with no noticeable decrease to visual fidelity.
Splats are 3D point clouds made up of many 2D discs.
Taking these benefits all together, the point-cloud-based representation of splats has a number of advantages for spatial journalism. Splats combine the photo realism of NeRF with photogrammetry’s ease of interaction and delivery. Additionally, because of their novel encoding method, complex scenes can be compressed down to file sizes that work on older devices and over poor network connections. Compared to traditional high-resolution photogrammetry methods, which can use tens of thousands of images, gaussian splatting generally only requires several hundred images. Objects or scenes that previously required multiple days for capture, processing and publishing with photogrammetry can potentially be published the same day they were captured, even within hours of capture.
Because of these benefits, splats could be useful for modeling everything from food to fashion to people, for stories on breaking news deadlines and for long form features.
With proper processing, gaussian splats (left) can approach the fidelity of the source media (right), especially when representing complex scenes with fine detail, transparency and reflection.
Capturing and Processing People and Environments
For recreating humans, we experimented with a multi-camera rig to minimize the potential for movement during capture. For capturing environments, the rig allows a single person to capture a space more efficiently than a group can — which was our previous workflow when capturing for photogrammetry. When capturing a person and their surrounding environment, the highest fidelity is achieved when devoting time to a dedicated human capture and then to a second dedicated scene capture. After processing, these two captures are then composited together in post-production.
While the rig produced consistently high quality results, we experimented with a number of capture and process methods ranging from all-in-one mobile apps, to more complex modeling software on Mac, PC and in the cloud. We address all of these techniques more fully in our Field Guide to Gaussian Splatting. For mobile capture, apps Polycam and Scaniverse are excellent splatting tools with a low barrier to entry. Scaniverse in particular is notable due to its integration of onboard smartphone LiDAR sensors for increased geometric accuracy, and because of how quickly it processes splats locally on your smartphone.
In general, however, high resolution video processed on a powerful PC with Reality Capture and PostShot provided the best balance between ease of capture and quality. The resulting model could then be cleaned up and cropped using the SuperSplat web editor, before being imported into our 3D WebGL storytelling tool Threebird. We also experimented with compositing and combining splats in Blender and Unreal, which is possible when necessary, but can be quite buggy and frustrating due to the third party plugins needed to render splats in those programs.

Delivering Splats to the Web and Beyond
Splatting is progressing rapidly. At the start of our exploration presenting splats on the web was difficult. But today there is a robust and active open source library for rendering splats in the browser. R&D integrated this library with our in-house collaborative 3D storytelling tool, Threebird, giving our journalists the ability to experiment with using splats for spatial journalism in the same creation environment they currently use for spatial storytelling.
Delivering splats online outside of existing splatting platforms requires similar technologies to those used to deliver photogrammetry models online — at the very least, you’ll need to be familiar with WebGL and Three.js to get started.
Threebird allows journalists at The New York Times to tell spatial stories with splats.
Splats can be compressed into much smaller file sizes than traditional photogrammetry models with minimal impact on delivery quality, allowing the potential for more stable delivery to mobile devices and users on a range of connections. Additionally, the optimization and compression workflow for splats is much faster and more straightforward than traditional photogrammetry methods. Using SuperSplat a 200+MB scene can be opened, cleaned of superfluous points, and exported to a file only a few megabytes in size in hours or even minutes. This is significantly faster than the weeks or days that a complex photogrammetry file might take to optimize.

Creating Splats From Pre-Existing Visuals
We also explored creating splats from suboptimal footage like video originally captured for a documentary, as well as found media. In breaking news scenarios especially, a journalist is less likely to have the time to perform a methodical 3D capture. We wanted to assess whether meaningful recreations can be made from footage captured for other news gathering purposes to explore if and when splatting might fit into news workflows.
A reconstruction made from helicopter footage captured for a traditional documentary style video. The source footage needed manual preprocessing to ready it for splatting.
Using footage that New York Times video journalist Emily Rhyne filmed while reporting with John Branch for Ghosts on the Glacier, an investigation of a camera recovered from a failed mountaineering expedition, we attempted to create accurate splats of the glacier and surrounding mountain. Results were mixed. Drone footage captured from a relatively low altitude yielded a very usable, but limited, view of a part of the glacier key to the story. While footage captured from afar with a Canon cinema camera from a journalist in a helicopter yielded a more complete model of the mountain, but lacked the fidelity of the closer drone footage.
A key learning from this experiment is how well splats reconstruct a 3D scene from viewpoints that roughly align with the camera capture path. A finished splat always looks best when viewed from roughly the same angle and distance of its source images. Trying to view a splat from a camera position far from the original source will result in a drop off in accuracy and fidelity. While this is mainly a problem when creating splats from suboptimal footage, this is true of all splats, and should be kept in mind when mapping virtual camera paths to mitigate issues in display quality.
A partial reconstruction made from drone footage captured for a video documentary.
Assessing Accuracy in Splats for Spatial Journalism
When recreating and representing spaces, objects and people in three dimensions, there are many ways to judge accuracy. Some of these have objectively measurable attributes, for instance: height, width and depth, that can be checked against a ground truth. However, there are also elements of a scene that are more subjective. Similar to still and video photojournalism, color and exposure for a given scene might have a range of acceptable values, and instances when you might alter those values in line with journalistic ethics for editorial clarity. For example: brightening shadows or recovering highlights to show detail. Lastly, there are difficult to quantify attributes: how well a 3D representation recreates things like texture, fine detail, and the effects of light on transparent and reflective surfaces.
Photogrammetry (left) can render high-resolution scenes, but takes a long time and struggles with fine details. Gaussian splatting (right) can render the transparent glass dome as well as the bonsai leaves and legs of the stool and tripod.
The biggest appeal when using splats for spatial storytelling is their ability to render those difficult to quantify attributes. A splat will model reflection, transparency, and fine detail, and a photogrammetry model will not. So, splats are ideal for storytelling purposes where this type of visual fidelity is important: rendering humans, fashion, food, architecture and any other scenario that would benefit from that type of photorealism.
For color and exposure, following standard best practices for photo and video journalism — as well as those that yield ideal photogrammetry models — produce the best results. Most important is manually locking in exposure and white balance prior to capture, or if needed, batch processing images or video afterward to ensure uniform color temperature and exposure.
The gaussian splat of this test scene conveys a variety of textures, detail, transparencies and reflections that a photogrammetry model cannot. This scene is also geometrically accurate.
Finally, while splats are highly accurate when it comes to rendering the way humans perceive complex 3D scenes, they don’t create a mesh with measurable dimensions in the way that a photogrammetry model does, so currently, splats are not an ideal 3D recreation method for visual forensic endeavors where pinpoint geometric accuracy is paramount. However, there are ways to increase the geometric accuracy of a splat.
Using a 3D reference object with known dimensions (a Rubik’s cube is a good low-cost option that can function as an ad-hoc color chart as well), splats can be rescaled after the fact with reasonable accuracy. Additionally, our testing of commercially available splatting solutions showed that Scaniverse consistently produces accurately scaled splats in near-real-time in the field, aided partially by taking advantage of onboard LiDAR sensors in newer smartphones.
Combining these techniques, we tested a simple workflow to marry high fidelity photorealism and geometric accuracy. First we used Scaniverse to create a rough but geometrically accurate model, allowing us to assess the scene before creating a second more complex capture. In post production, both of these splats can be imported into the same editing software, and the more detailed capture can be accurately scaled using the reference model.
Journalists needn't necessarily choose between one technique or the other. Creating splats from datasets originally used or intended for photogrammetry is very feasible.
The Big Picture
Gaussian splatting provides many significant improvements over photogrammetry, and splatting is likely to be an incredibly useful tool for spatial journalism. The ability to create high quality models with limited data quickly, to display visual information impossible for photogrammetry to convey, and to compress those scenes to files easily consumed on a mobile device and connection are massive advancements in democratizing the creation and delivery of 3D stories.
While splatting is powerful, the technique still has room to grow. Improving geometric accuracy and scale would be a massive step forward. There are limited libraries for displaying and delivering splats online and in headsets, and desktop 3D authoring tools currently require additional proprietary plugins to work with splats natively, and even then, are quite buggy. In the future, journalists should be able to work with splats as easily as any other spatial media.
Looking Ahead
The available software and capabilities for gaussian splatting are evolving quickly, and R&D plans to continue to explore this space. Want to get started yourself? Check out our Field Guide to Gaussian Splatting.
