Abstract—We present ChromaGS, a method for real-time,
language-guided color editing of animatable 3D Gaussian head
avatars. Given a trained animatable avatar, users can instantly
modify the color of semantic regions through natural language,
with edits applied at render time and no retraining required.
Our key insight is to augment each Gaussian primitive with
learned soft assignments to semantic regions and decompose
colors into region-level base colors and Gaussian-level residuals.
This decomposition enables coherent color transfer: modifying a
region’s base color propagates naturally through all associated
Gaussians while preserving fine appearance details encoded in
residuals. A two-stage language pipeline translates text instructions into target colors, supporting both absolute specifications
and relative adjustments. Unlike generative editing methods that
may introduce unintended modifications, our approach provides
deterministic, precisely localized semantic control. Experiments
demonstrate faithful appearance preservation and intuitive interaction across diverse subjects. Project page and code are available
at: https://a-canela.github.io/chromags/

Our contributions are: (1) a semantic Gaussian representation with learned soft region assignments enabling localized

II. R ELATED W ORK
Photorealistic 3D head avatars have become essential for
applications ranging from telepresence and virtual reality
to film production and gaming. Recent advances in neural
radiance fields [1] and 3D Gaussian splatting [2] have enabled
high-fidelity avatar reconstruction from monocular video, with
animatable variants supporting real-time expression-driven
rendering [3], [4].
However, editing the appearance of these avatars remains
challenging. Users often want to modify attributes-eye color,
skin tone, hair color-for creative customization or virtual tryon, without altering identity. Existing generative editing methods based on diffusion models, while powerful, often introduce
Text-Guided 3D Gaussian Editing. Several methods address
text-guided editing of 3D Gaussian scenes. GaussianEditor [6],
Instruct-GS2GS [7], and 3DSceneEditor [8] leverage diffusion
priors or open-vocabulary grounding for general scene editing,
while LangSplat [9] embeds CLIP features into Gaussians for
open-vocabulary queries. For head avatars, HeadStudio [10],
HeadArtist [11], and HeadSculpt [12] generate stylized heads
from text via score distillation, and GaussianAvatar-Editor [5]
and FaceG2E [13] apply diffusion-based optimization for
text-guided appearance changes. These generative approaches
require per-edit optimization, may introduce unintended modifications, and cannot guarantee deterministic outputs. In con-

Palette-Based 3D Recoloring. A complementary line of work
decomposes 3D scene appearance into a small set of palette
colors that can be interactively edited. PaletteNeRF [14] and
RecolorNeRF [15] introduced this idea for NeRF, representing
each point’s color as a linear combination of learnable palette
bases. PaletteGaussian [16] extends the palette paradigm to
3D Gaussian Splatting, supporting manual, image-driven, and
text-driven palette edits. While effective for global and regionlevel recoloring of static scenes, these methods do not model
animation, expression-dependent appearance, or semantic regions of a face, all of which are central to our setting.
Semantic Editing in 3D Gaussian Scenes. Gaussian Grouping [17] and OpenGaussian [18] assign semantic labels to
Gaussians for segmentation-based scene editing. However,
these methods target general static scenes and do not handle
animatable avatars or language-driven color specification. Our
approach combines learned semantic assignments with a color
decomposition tailored for animatable head avatars, enabling
region-specific editing that remains consistent across expressions and viewpoints.
Semantic Segmentation and Language-Guided Editing.
Vision-language models like SAM [19] and SAM3 [20] enable
open-vocabulary segmentation via text prompts. For textdriven manipulation, diffusion-based approaches [21], [22]
enable powerful but stochastic editing. We leverage SAM3
for semantic mask extraction and propose a deterministic
language-guided color editing pipeline.

B. Text-Guided Color Editing
To enable intuitive color specification, we propose a twostage architecture that decouples semantic parsing from color
resolution, improving robustness by assigning simpler, welldefined tasks to each component.
Semantic Parsing. We fine-tune Flan-T5-Small [23] to parse
natural language instructions into a structured intermediate
format. Given input text (e.g., “red matte lipstick”), the model
outputs a structured string: region, color, type (e.g., lips, red
matte, absolute). Multiple edits are separated by semicolons.
This simplified output space (region names, color descriptors,
and edit types) is significantly easier to learn than direct RGB
prediction.
Color Resolving. The structured output is processed by a
deterministic color resolver that handles two types of instructions:
Absolute. For absolute color instructions (e.g., “olive skin”),
we encode the color description using a pretrained sentence
encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) [24], [25] and find the closest
match via cosine similarity against pre-computed embeddings
of region-specific palettes. These palettes contain anatomically
plausible colors (e.g., Fitzpatrick scale [26] tones for skin,
natural iris colors based on melanin distributions) and serve
as the target color space for each region.
Relative. For relative instructions (e.g., “darker”, “more saturated”), we convert the current region color to HSV and
apply deterministic modifiers (e.g., reducing V for “darker”,
increasing S for “more saturated”).

IV. E XPERIMENTS
Implementation Details. We implement ChromaGS on top
of an animatable Gaussian splatting framework [4]. We define
K = 6 semantic classes (hair, eyebrows, eyes, lips, skin,
plus background). Training runs for 50k iterations on a single
NVIDIA RTX 5090, taking approximately 15 minutes.
Datasets and Evaluation Setup. We evaluate on scenes from
FaceScape [28] and INSTA [29], featuring diverse skin tones,
eye colors, and hair styles. Each scene is edited with 14 diverse
color edits spanning hair, eyes, skin, lips, and eyebrows, yielding approximately 10k edited frames per method at 512 × 512
resolution.

(2) CLIP Score↑: alignment between rendered image and
target text prompt, averaged across three CLIP backbones
(ViT-L/14, ViT-B/16, ViT-B/32); (3) Context Preservation↑:
computed as 1 − LPIPS outside the target region, measuring
how well non-target areas are preserved after editing.

A. Comparison with Baselines
We compare against GaussianAvatar-Editor [5], a textguided Gaussian head avatar editing method using diffusion
priors, and InstructPix2Pix [22], an image-conditioned diffusion model that applies text-guided edits frame-by-frame.
While other text-guided 3D editing methods exist, they target
static scenes rather than animatable avatars [6], [17], [7], [8],
[16] or edit generated heads instead of reconstructions from
real video [10], [11], [12], [13], making a direct comparison
neither applicable nor fair; the selected baselines represent the
closest available approaches to our setting. Fig. 5 shows color
editing results across multiple subjects and semantic regions.
Our method produces consistent, localized color changes that
preserve identity and fine details (e.g., specular highlights,
texture variations). Edits propagate naturally across different
expressions and viewpoints due to the 3D Gaussian representation.
Table II presents quantitative comparisons using the pretrained scenes publicly released by GaussianAvatar-Editor [5].
Our method achieves substantially higher identity and context preservation scores, reflecting its ability to confine edits
precisely to the target region. The baselines obtain slightly
higher CLIP scores despite producing visibly less accurate
edits (Fig. 5). This apparent paradox arises because CLIP measures holistic image-text similarity: a diffusion-based method
that tints the entire face green when asked for “green eyes”
produces a stronger global color signal that CLIP rewards,
even though the edit is spatially incorrect. Our method, by
design, restricts color changes to the semantically correct
region, yielding a subtler global shift that CLIP is less sensitive
to, a trade-off we consider desirable.
Beyond metric performance, the methods differ substantially
in practical usability (Table I). GaussianAvatar-Editor couples
editing and training into a single optimization loop of ∼800 s,
which must be repeated for every new edit. Our method
shares a comparable one-time training cost, but decouples
editing entirely: once trained, any color change is applied in
∼50 ms (which mainly corresponds to the language model
inference mapping the text prompt to color parameters), three
orders of magnitude faster, enabling interactive exploration of
diverse edits. InstructPix2Pix operates frame-by-frame with
no 3D awareness, which not only limits throughput but also
introduces severe temporal flickering across the sequence.
B. Ablation Studies
Semantic Supervision. Table III and Fig. 6 show that omitting
semantic supervision (λsem = 0) prevents Gaussians from
learning distinct region assignments, causing edits to erroneously affect unrelated facial regions. This confirms Lsem
is essential for regularizing primitives into coherent semantic
clusters, effectively anchoring color modulation to the intended
targets through robust semantic disentanglement.

residual-based decomposition struggles with such intricate
details. Additionally, color resolution is constrained by curated

VI. C ONCLUSION
We introduced ChromaGS for real-time, language-driven
color editing of animatable 3D Gaussian avatars. By coupling
learned segmentation with color decomposition, our framework enables localized, deterministic control without requiring per-edit optimization. Our two-stage pipeline effectively
bridges natural language with precise absolute and relative
color adjustments. Ultimately, replacing diffusion-based optimization with a single forward pass allows ChromaGS to
make high-fidelity avatar customization practical for interactive gaming and virtual try-on.
