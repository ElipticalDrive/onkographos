Abstract—We present ChromaGS, a method for real-time, language-guided color editing of animatable 3D Gaussian head avatars. Given a trained animatable avatar, users can instantly modify the color of semantic regions through natural language, with edits applied at render time and no retraining required. Our key insight is to augment each Gaussian primitive with learned soft assignments to semantic regions and decompose colors into region-level base colors and Gaussian-level residuals. This decomposition enables coherent color transfer: modifying a region's base color propagates naturally through all associated Gaussians while preserving fine appearance details encoded in residuals. A two-stage language pipeline translates text instructions into target colors, supporting both absolute specifications and relative adjustments. Unlike generative editing methods that may introduce unintended modifications, our approach provides deterministic, precisely localized semantic control. # I. INTRODUCTION

Photorealistic 3D head avatars have become essential for applications ranging from telepresence and virtual reality to film production and gaming. Recent advances in neural radiance fields [1] and 3D Gaussian splatting [2] have enabled high-fidelity avatar reconstruction from monocular video, with animatable variants supporting real-time expression-driven rendering [3], [4].

However, editing the appearance of these avatars remains challenging. Users often want to modify attributes-eye color, skin tone, hair color-for creative customization or virtual tryon, without altering identity. Existing generative editing methods based on diffusion models, while powerful, often introduce unintended modifications, struggle with precise localization, and cannot guarantee deterministic outputs [5].

We present ChromaGS, enabling real-time, language-guided semantic color editing of 3D Gaussian head avatars (Fig. 3\), where users specify modifications through natural language and see results instantly.

Our key idea is to augment Gaussian primitives with semantic awareness and decompose their colors in a way that enables coherent region-level editing while preserving fine details. A language interface translates text instructions into target colors, supporting both absolute and relative modifications.

Our contributions are: (1) a semantic Gaussian representation with learned soft region assignments enabling localized color editing; (2) a color decomposition that separates base colors from residual details; and (3) a two-stage language pipeline for robust, intuitive color specification. Experiments demonstrate instant, deterministic color editing with faithful appearance preservation across diverse subjects.

# II. RELATED WORK

Text-Guided 3D Gaussian Editing. Several methods address text-guided editing of 3D Gaussian scenes. GaussianEditor [6], Instruct-GS2GS [7], and 3DSceneEditor [8] leverage diffusion priors or open-vocabulary grounding for general scene editing, while LangSplat [9] embeds CLIP features into Gaussians for open-vocabulary queries. For head avatars, HeadStudio [10], HeadArtist [11], and HeadSculpt [12] generate stylized heads from text via score distillation, and GaussianAvatar-Editor [5] and FaceG2E [13] apply diffusion-based optimization for text-guided appearance changes. Palette-Based 3D Recoloring. A complementary line of work decomposes 3D scene appearance into a small set of palette colors that can be interactively edited. PaletteNeRF [14] and RecolorNeRF [15] introduced this idea for NeRF, representing each point's color as a linear combination of learnable palette bases. PaletteGaussian [16] extends the palette paradigm to 3D Gaussian Splatting, supporting manual, image-driven, and text-driven palette edits. While effective for global and regionlevel recoloring of static scenes, these methods do not model animation, expression-dependent appearance, or semantic regions of a face, all of which are central to our setting.

Semantic Editing in 3D Gaussian Scenes. Gaussian Grouping [17] and OpenGaussian [18] assign semantic labels to Gaussians for segmentation-based scene editing. However, these methods target general static scenes and do not handle animatable avatars or language-driven color specification. Our approach combines learned semantic assignments with a color decomposition tailored for animatable head avatars, enabling region-specific editing that remains consistent across expressions and viewpoints.

Semantic Segmentation and Language-Guided Editing. Vision-language models like SAM [19] and SAM3 [20] enable open-vocabulary segmentation via text prompts. For textdriven manipulation, diffusion-based approaches [21], [22] enable powerful but stochastic editing. We leverage SAM3 for semantic mask extraction and propose a deterministic language-guided color editing pipeline.

# III. METHOD

# A. Semantic Masks Extraction

For each semantic class k and each training frame, we use SAM3 [20] with text prompts to generate binary masks Mk. Iris masks are obtained from eye masks by detecting ellipses and warping an annular-shaped template. Masks are made mutually exclusive by prioritizing smaller regions (e.g., iris over eye sclera) to ensure proper layering. SAM3 exhibits strong temporal consistency across frames due to its concept-level understanding; moreover, since our optimization aggregates supervision from hundreds of frames, occasional segmentation noise is naturally absorbed during training.

# B. Text-Guided Color Editing

To enable intuitive color specification, we propose a twostage architecture that decouples semantic parsing from color resolution, improving robustness by assigning simpler, welldefined tasks to each component.

Semantic Parsing. We fine-tune Flan-T5-Small [23] to parse natural language instructions into a structured intermediate format. Given input text (e.g., "red matte lipstick"), the model outputs a structured string: region, color, type (e.g., lips, red matte, absolute). Multiple edits are separated by semicolons. This simplified output space (region names, color descriptors, and edit types) is significantly easier to learn than direct RGB prediction.

Color Resolving. The structured output is processed by a deterministic color resolver that handles two types of instructions:

Absolute. For absolute color instructions (e.g., "olive skin"), we encode the color description using a pretrained sentence encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) [24], [25] and find the closest match via cosine similarity against pre-computed embeddings of region-specific palettes. These palettes contain anatomically plausible colors (e.g., Fitzpatrick scale [26] tones for skin, natural iris colors based on melanin distributions) and serve as the target color space for each region.

Relative. For relative instructions (e.g., "darker", "more saturated"), we convert the current region color to HSV and apply deterministic modifiers (e.g., reducing V for "darker", increasing S for "more saturated").

# C. Semantic-Aware Gaussian Splatting

Parametric Face Model. We build upon animatable 3D Gaussian representations [4] where Gaussians are bound to FLAME [27], enabling expression-driven animation together with linear blend skinning.

Gaussian Model. Each Gaussian primitive gi is characterized by position µi , scaling σi , rotation quaternion qi , opacity αi , and spherical harmonic color coefficients ci . To enable localized color editing, we augment each Gaussian with semantic logits si ∈ R K, where K is the number of predefined semantic classes. Soft class probabilities are obtained via softmax: pi = softmax(si).

We supervise semantic assignments using the 2D masks from Sec. III-A. During training, we render semantic probabilities using differentiable Gaussian rasterization:

$$\hat{M}_k(\mathbf{u}) = \sum_i p_{i,k} \cdot \alpha_i \cdot G_i(\mathbf{u}), \quad (1)$$

where Gi(u) is the 2D Gaussian evaluated at pixel u, and pi,k is the probability of Gaussian i belonging to class k. We apply a pixel-wise cross-entropy loss against blurred target masks:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{sem}} = -\frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{\mathbf{u} \in \Omega} \sum_k \tilde{M}_k(\mathbf{u}) \log \tilde{M}_k(\mathbf{u}), \quad (2)$$

where Ω is the foreground region and M˜ k are soft targets obtained by applying per-class Gaussian blur (with classspecific σk) to the binary masks and renormalizing across classes. This softens the supervision at region boundaries, where mask annotations are inherently imprecise, and provides smoother gradients than hard labels.

Color Decomposition. We decompose Gaussian colors into two components: (1) class-level base colors bk, computed as the mean color from pixels within class k masks across all training frames; and (2) per-Gaussian residuals c res i encoding local details such as texture, shading, and specular highlights. The residuals are represented as learnable spherical harmonic coefficients that are combined with expression-dependent blendshapes via linear blend skinning [4]: c res i = c 0 i + P l wl c l i , where c 0 i is an expression-independent base residual, {c l i } are learned blendshape bases, and wl are projected FLAME expression coefficients. This allows appearance details to vary naturally across facial expressions.

The final rendered color combines base colors, semantic probabilities, and residuals:

$$\mathbf{c}_i^{\text{final}} = \sum_k p_{i,k} \cdot \mathbf{b}_k + \mathbf{c}_i^{\text{res}}, \quad (3)$$

where pi,k = softmax(si)k are the semantic probabilities.

To edit a region at inference, we replace its base color with a target value:

$$\mathbf{c}_i^{\text{edited}} = \mathbf{c}_i^{\text{final}} + \sum_k p_{i,k} \cdot (\mathbf{b}_k^{\text{target}} - \mathbf{b}_k), \quad (4)$$

where b target k = bk for unedited regions, so only modified regions contribute to the color shift while residuals preserve local details. The soft semantic assignments ensure smooth transitions at region boundaries.

Cross-Region Adaptation. Each semantic class is a weighted combination of mask channels, where the weights act as userexposed parameters, providing a straightforward yet effective mechanism to encode prior knowledge about anatomical color correlations between regions. #### D. Training Objective

The full training objective combines photometric reconstruction with semantic supervision:

$$\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{rgb}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{rgb}} + \lambda_{\text{sem}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{sem}}, \quad (5)$$

where Lrgb is the standard L1 photometric loss and Lsem is the cross-entropy semantic loss. We use λrgb = 1.0 and λsem = 0.1 in all experiments.

### IV. EXPERIMENTS

Implementation Details. We implement ChromaGS on top of an animatable Gaussian splatting framework [4]. We define K = 6 semantic classes (hair, eyebrows, eyes, lips, skin, plus background). Training runs for 50k iterations on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090, taking approximately 15 minutes.

Datasets and Evaluation Setup. We evaluate on scenes from FaceScape [28] and INSTA [29], featuring diverse skin tones, eye colors, and hair styles. Each scene is edited with 14 diverse color edits spanning hair, eyes, skin, lips, and eyebrows, yielding approximately 10k edited frames per method at 512×512 resolution.

Metrics. We measure: (1) Identity Preservation↑: Arc-Face cosine similarity between edited and unedited renders; (2) CLIP Score↑: alignment between rendered image and target text prompt, averaged across three CLIP backbones (ViT-L/14, ViT-B/16, ViT-B/32); (3) Context Preservation↑: computed as 1 − LPIPS outside the target region, measuring how well non-target areas are preserved after editing.

#### A. Comparison with Baselines

We compare against GaussianAvatar-Editor [5], a textguided Gaussian head avatar editing method using diffusion priors, and InstructPix2Pix [22], an image-conditioned diffusion model that applies text-guided edits frame-by-frame. While other text-guided 3D editing methods exist, they target static scenes rather than animatable avatars [6], [17], [7], [8], [16] or edit generated heads instead of reconstructions from real video [10], [11], [12], [13], making a direct comparison neither applicable nor fair; the selected baselines represent the closest available approaches to our setting. Fig. 5 shows color editing results across multiple subjects and semantic regions. Our method produces consistent, localized color changes that preserve identity and fine details (e.g., specular highlights, texture variations). Edits propagate naturally across different expressions and viewpoints due to the 3D Gaussian representation.

Table II presents quantitative comparisons using the pretrained scenes publicly released by GaussianAvatar-Editor [5]. Our method achieves substantially higher identity and context preservation scores, reflecting its ability to confine edits precisely to the target region. The baselines obtain slightly higher CLIP scores despite producing visibly less accurate edits (Fig. 5\). This apparent paradox arises because CLIP measures holistic image-text similarity: a diffusion-based method that tints the entire face green when asked for "green eyes" produces a stronger global color signal that CLIP rewards, even though the edit is spatially incorrect. Our method, by design, restricts color changes to the semantically correct region, yielding a subtler global shift that CLIP is less sensitive to, a trade-off we consider desirable.





Fig. 6. Ablation: semantic supervision. Without Lsem, edits spread to unrelated areas: blue eyes tint the hair and tongue, blonde hair bleeds into the face and clothing, and red lipstick affects the skin color as well. Our full model produces clean, confined edits.

Beyond metric performance, the methods differ substantially in practical usability (Table I\). GaussianAvatar-Editor couples editing and training into a single optimization loop of ∼800 s, which must be repeated for every new edit. Our method shares a comparable one-time training cost, but decouples editing entirely: once trained, any color change is applied in ∼50 ms (which mainly corresponds to the language model inference mapping the text prompt to color parameters), three orders of magnitude faster, enabling interactive exploration of diverse edits. InstructPix2Pix operates frame-by-frame with no 3D awareness, which not only limits throughput but also introduces severe temporal flickering across the sequence.

### B. Ablation Studies

Semantic Supervision. Table III and Fig. 6 show that omitting semantic supervision (λsem = 0) prevents Gaussians from learning distinct region assignments, causing edits to erroneously affect unrelated facial regions. This confirms Lsem is essential for regularizing primitives into coherent semantic clusters, effectively anchoring color modulation to the intended targets through robust semantic disentanglement.

### V. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Localization quality depends on the underlying semantic segmentation [20]. Our approach enables uniform shifts but precludes texture/material modifications (e.g., metallic skin, freckles) and geometric edits (e.g., reshaping features). Highfrequency structures like hair may lack photorealism, as residual-based decomposition struggles with such intricate details. Additionally, color resolution is constrained by curated

- VI. CONCLUSION We introduced ChromaGS for real-time, language-driven color editing of animatable 3D Gaussian avatars. By coupling learned segmentation with color decomposition, our framework enables localized, deterministic control without requiring per-edit optimization. Our two-stage pipeline effectively bridges natural language with precise absolute and relative color adjustments. Ultimately, replacing diffusion-based optimization with a single forward pass allows ChromaGS to make high-fidelity avatar customization practical for interactive gaming and virtual try-on.
