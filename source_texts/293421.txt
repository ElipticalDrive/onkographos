Abstract
Adaptive volumetric finite element meshing is a critical step in computer-aided
engineering and analysis that dictates the computational budget of a given problem.
It traditionally requires iterative PDE solvers or heavily supervised, data-driven surrogates trained on large-scale simulation data. While Multimodal Large Language
Models (MLLMs) excel in 2D visual tasks, their zero-shot capability to semantically ground regions based on geometric understanding and physics remains an
open question. This work explores a significant question: can the high-level semantic understanding of off-the-shelf MLLMs serve as a viable, zero-shot geometric
proxy for finite element mesh refinement? We introduce GReFEM (Geometric Reasoning Enhanced Multimodal LLMs for Finite Element Meshing), a framework that
utilizes MLLMs to visually localize stress-critical regions based on physics-guided
textual prompts. To bridge the gap between 2D MLLM pre-training and 3D geometries, we introduce orthoViews, a trained view-selection module that assists these
MLLMs by maximizing the observability of key geometric features. We evaluate
GReFEM by computing finite-element error at stress concentrations, on diverse
CAD mesh geometries under multiple loading cases, with MLLMs and prompt
variants, against blind and load-informed geometric heuristics, solver-informed
Zienkiewicz–Zhu (ZZ) refinement, and a retrained learned adaptive-meshing baseline, all at a matched element budget. Without any FEM solve or simulation training
data, GReFEM lowers the critical-region peak von Mises stress error relative to a
blind geometric heuristic and to refinement driven by a coarse FEM solve, closing
58% of the gap between the unrefined mesh and a reference-solve oracle, while a
hand-derived analytical load-informed heuristic remains stronger and the learned
baseline performs slightly better overall. With this we investigate their performance
as semantic assistants in automated simulation workflows. Code is available at
https://github.com/kbali1297/GReFEM.

Despite these advances, the role of LLMs in fine-grained numerical simulation workflows remains
largely unexplored. High-fidelity finite element simulation fundamentally relies on discretization
of the problem domain, where mesh resolution must be adaptively concentrated in regions of high
physical variation. While existing approaches adapt LLMs to reason about surface meshes Wang et al.
[2024], Fang et al. [2025] and CAD representations Wu et al. [2023], Alrashedy et al. [2024] of 3D
objects, comparatively little work investigates their effectiveness in guiding volumetric discretization
required to resolve localized physical phenomena while controlling computational cost.
Traditional adaptive volumetric meshing methods rely on PDE solvers to compute error indicators that
identify regions requiring refinement (Zienkiewicz and Zhu [1992], Babuška and Rheinboldt [1978]).
This tightly couples meshing to solver availability and computational expense. In practice, however,
experienced engineers frequently anticipate regions requiring refinement directly from geometric
structure, loading directions, and boundary conditions, without explicitly solving for field quantities.
Several works exploit such geometry-driven heuristics Bronson et al. [2012], Gkrispanis [2024], but
typically assume structured CAD representations that explicitly encode high-level geometric features.
In this work, we investigate a fundamental question: can off-the-shelf MLLMs act as zero-shot
semantic assistants to emulate expert, geometry-driven meshing intuition? To systematically explore
this, we introduce GReFEM as a framework to evaluate MLLM spatial grounding in 3D engineering
environments. Because MLLMs often struggle with precise pixel-level localization (Fig. 2), we
propose grid-based region localization as a solution and integrate orthoViews, a trained view-scoring
module to maximize geometric observability. This enables us to reliably project coarse 2D MLLM
inferences back to the 3D domain, steering volumetric mesh refinement without PDE solvers or
explicit CAD annotations.
Through rigorous experiments across CAD geometries, multiple loading conditions, and state-of-theart MLLMs, we present the following contributions:
(1) A Semantic Evaluation Framework: We introduce GReFEM (with orthoViews) as a CADagnostic probing pipeline that translates geometric instructions grounded in problem physics into
localized 3D volumetric mesh refinement anchors. We score every refinement policy by the downstream finite-element error it produces at a matched element budget, namely the peak von Mises
stress and the strain energy inside the stress-critical region of a high-fidelity reference solve.
(2) Analysis of Instruction-Driven Grounding: Prompt content measurably steers where MLLMs
refine. Describing only the load (Load-Only) raises recall and anchor count but lowers anchor

Related Works
Adaptive Meshing Adaptive meshing non-uniformly allocates resolution to minimize finite element
simulation costs. Traditional pipelines rely on computationally expensive, solver-in-the-loop error
estimators tailored to the governing PDEs ( Babuška and Rheinboldt [1978], Babuvška and Rheinboldt
[1978], Zienkiewicz and Zhu [1992]), though some geometry-driven heuristics avoid explicit PDE
solves using mathematical criteria ( Rivara [1984], Shewchuk [2002], Bronson et al. [2012]). Recently,
deep learning has emerged as a surrogate, using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to learn h-adaptive
sizing fields ( Khan et al. [2024], Freymuth et al. [2025]) or r-adaptive topological optimizations
( Pfaff et al. [2020], Rowbottom et al. [2024]). While these supervised architectures achieve high
accuracy, their strict reliance on massive datasets of converged FEM solutions prevents zero-shot
generalization. Rather than proposing a data-hungry supervised model, GReFEM explores a new
solver-free paradigm: evaluating whether the semantic reasoning of off-the-shelf MLLMs can act
as a natural language-guided proxy for mesh refinement, significantly circumventing the need for
offline FEM training data. Furthermore, supervised GNNs fundamentally solve a different problem:
they act as end-to-end surrogates for a specific, pre-defined PDE and boundary condition distribution.
Adapting a GNN to a new physical phenomenon (e.g., thermal stress instead of linear elasticity)
or a fundamentally different load type requires complete model retraining and a newly generated
FEM dataset. In contrast, GReFEM acts as a domain-agnostic semantic interface. By shifting
the refinement logic from learned network weights to expert-provided natural language prompts,
GReFEM can dynamically adapt to entirely new loading scenarios without a single parameter update.
3D Spatial Reasoning with MLLMs Extending vision-language models to 3D reasoning remains
challenging due to limited 3D training data. Several approaches address this by directly training
LLMs on structured 3D representations, such as 3D feature fields Hong et al. [2023], point clouds
( Xu et al. [2024], Batra et al. [2025]), or radiance fields Kerr et al. [2023]. Alternatively, other works
infer 3D understanding from 2D projections without explicit 3D structures. This includes predicting
3D bounding boxes Cho et al. [2024], optimizing view selection for 3D QA Wang et al. [2025],
regressing geometric attributes via 3D query tokens Yang et al. [2026], estimating absolute distances
from synthetic datasets Chen et al. [2024], progressive prompting for trajectory imagination Ma et al.
[2024], and leveraging video sequences for 3D grounding Zheng et al. [2025]. Our work follows
3
this latter 2D-to-3D projection paradigm; however, rather than targeting general semantic scene
understanding, we uniquely leverage intelligently ranked 2D orthographic views to infer mechanically
relevant 3D features for downstream engineering discretization.

Method
We propose GReFEM, a semantic spatial grounding testbed framework applied to adaptive finite
element meshing. Rather than relying on solver-based error estimators, GReFEM leverages pretrained
MLLMs and problem specific instructions to identify mechanically salient regions using only visual
observations of geometry. GReFEM operates solely on triangulated surface meshes and requires no
access to B-Rep entities, STEP files, or simulation feedback.
4.1
Problem Formulation
We consider a solid domain Ω ⊂ R3 with boundary ∂Ω represented by a triangulated surface mesh
M = (V, F ). The objective is to generate a volumetric finite element mesh that locally refines regions
expected to exhibit large stress gradients under mechanical load, while maintaining coarse resolution
elsewhere to control computational cost. Given M, the method predicts a sparse set of surfacelocalized refinement points P = {pi ∈ R3 }N
i=1 , corresponding to geometrically or mechanically
important features, such as sharp edges, fillets, holes and load-bearing faces. The resulting size field
h : Ω → R+ , as constructed according to equation 6, is provided to a standard tetrahedral meshing
algorithm in Gmsh, which deterministically generates a conforming volumetric mesh.

4.3
Inference Pipeline
Figure 1 summarizes the GReFEM inference pipeline, which consists of three stages: region proposal,
feature detection, and surface projection.
Region Proposal Each selected view v ∈ V ∗ is overlaid with a uniform grid Gv . A multimodal
LLM predicts a subset of salient grid cells Cv ⊂ Gv , corresponding to
S geometrically or mechanically
significant regions. Proposals are aggregated across views via C = v∈V ∗ Cv
Feature Detection Each cell in C defines an image patch processed using classical vision operators
and mesh-ray projection to recover precise feature locations in the 2D views. We specifically spot
features resembling internal corner edges, extruded contours/bosses, holes and fillets that are key
geometric discontinuities in a load bearing structure. This stage converts coarse LLM output into
accurate 2D feature pixels Qc = {qc,j ∈ R2 }.
MV-RaySeg (Multi-View Ray Segment Sampling) Given a feature point q ∈ R2 detected in a view
v, we recover its corresponding spatial projection on the 3D surface by ray–mesh intersection under
known camera parameters. Let πv−1 denote the inverse projection for view v, and let dv ∈ R3 be the
associated view direction. The corresponding ray is defined as r(t) = πv−1 (q) + t dv , t ∈ R.
We compute the complete ordered set of ray–mesh intersections with the surface mesh M, Tq =
{ti | r(ti ) ∈ M}, with t1 < t2 < · · · < t|Tq | . Under standard assumptions on watertight meshes,
consecutive intersection pairs (t2k−1 , t2k ) define ray segments
that lie inside the solid. For each
S
such segment, we sample a finite set of 3D points Pq = k {r(t) | t ∈ [t2k−1 , t2k ]}, which yields
a spatially extended set of surface-adjacent anchor points corresponding to the 2D feature q. This
formulation allows a single 2D detection to influence multiple depth-aligned surface regions, which
is essential for internal cavities and edges.

Experiments
Training We train the orthoViews view-selection module on a dataset consisting of 5k diverse
CAD parts from ABC CAD dataset Koch et al. [2019] and FreeCAD Riegel et al. [2016] spanning
mechanical components with varying geometric complexity (e.g., extrusions, holes, fillets, and loadbearing features). Each object is represented during training by both its triangulated surface mesh and
the corresponding STEP file. Specifically, we leverage high-level geometric primitives available in
STEP (e.g., planar faces, cylindrical axes) for training by scoring each view by the number of features
aligned in its view direction. During inference, our framework can work solely with spherical views
6
of surface meshes of 3D objects. For more information, the reader is encouraged to read the appendix
section on orthoViews. Training the network on the entire dataset for 4 epochs took a couple of hours
on one NVIDIA H100 GPU.
Experimental Setup We evaluate on 156 CAD parts from the ABC Koch et al. [2019] and
FreeCAD Riegel et al. [2016] datasets (Appendix B), disjoint from the orthoViews training and
validation splits, each under five loading scenarios, uniaxial (compression, bending, torsion) and
multi-axial (bending-compression, torsion-compression), giving 780 paired geometry–load cases.
We use three prompt templates: Load+Features, Load+Features+Heuristics and Load-Only. For
the Load+Features prompt we provide the model with the loading instruction prompt and the key
geometric features that could be stress critical under the load (Internal corner Edges, Corner Edges of
extruded or Protruding parts, Holes and Fillets). The model is tested on the ability to correctly identify
the cells under each feature enough to localize it using contour and depth detection CV pipeline.
For the Load+Features+Heuristics we provide additional load based hints to spot stress critical
regions in addition to the input provided in the Load+Features prompt. For the Load-Only prompt,
we merely describe the loading on the object and instruct the MLLM to recommend cells containing
the stress-critical geometric features. All the cells obtained are given to contour and depth detection
algorithms in feature detection part of the pipeline to compute anchor points in these 2D views that
will be later projected onto the 3D mesh. We evaluate four MLLMs, Gemini-3-flash-preview, ClaudeHaiku-4.5, GPT-5.4-mini and Qwen3-VL-235B, all accessed through the OpenRouter (OpenRouter,
Inc. [2026]) API and all given the top five orthoViews views, without domain-specific training or PDE
solves. The minimum element size is hmin = 0.2 hmax for all refined meshes, and Dmin = 2hmin ,
Dmax = 4hmin define the element size field of Eq. 6. Anchors within 6hmin of the loaded faces are
ignored by the mesher for every policy alike, so that error differences arise from interior refinement
and not from differences in the imposition of boundary conditions. Every (geometry, load) case is
scored against an independent reference solve on a uniform mesh with hfine = hmax /16, capped
at 6 × 106 tetrahedra (≈ 5 × 106 typical; linear elasticity, E = 210 GPa, ν = 0.3), through the
critical-region errors of Eqs. 4 and 5.
Qualitative Results In the qualitative examples, the GReFEM predicted refinement locations align
with stress-critical regions. Notably, the predicted points are not uniformly distributed across all
geometric features such as holes or internal corners, but are selectively localized to those features that
should be mechanically activated under the prescribed loading conditions (for instance Figure 3c).
In particular, while multiple concave features may be present in the geometry, GReFEM, when
provided with physics guided prompts about the problem and physics, attempts to strongly highlight
those regions that correspond to dominant stress concentrations in the ZZ stress field, rather than
marking all such features indiscriminately, as seen in Figure 6 in the appendix.
Refinement Policies at a Matched Budget Adaptive meshing is fundamentally a resourceconstrained optimization problem: introducing too many refinement points severely degrades downstream solver efficiency. We therefore compare seven refinement policies at a matched element
budget, so that differences reflect where smaller elements are placed rather than how many are
spent: (i) Coarse, the unrefined mesh produced by the pipeline (a fixed floor, not budget-matched);
(ii) GReFEM, our framework with trained orthoViews selection and zero-shot MLLM inference;
(iii) Geometric heuristic (blind), the load-agnostic detector of concave sharp edges, fillets, and
holes based on dihedral angles and discrete curvature, independent of loading; (iv) Load-informed
heuristic, the same geometric candidates re-weighted by a solver-free closed-form nominal-stress
estimate (per-layer voxel cross-section properties A, Iz , J along the loading axis), so the loading case
decides which candidates are refined; (v) ZZ from coarse solve, anchors placed at the top-percentile
ZZ error points of a single coarse FEM solve; (vi) ZZ from fine reference solve, anchors at the
top-percentile ZZ points of the ≈ 5 × 106 -element reference solution, an oracle reference; and (vii)
AMBER Freymuth et al. [2025], a learned sizing-field AMR method retrained from scratch with
load-case conditioning on 440 expert meshes derived from reference solves. Each anchor-based
baseline is reduced to GReFEM’s anchor count for that object, and the hmin of every refined baseline
is calibrated iteratively to GReFEM’s element count (10% tolerance); AMBER emits a continuous
field, matched via a global scaling factor. The load-informed heuristic and AMBER are detailed in
Appendix A.3.

Localization Overlap versus Downstream Accuracy
For measuring the degree to which MLLMs localize refinement points based on prompt variations and
number of input views, we take the ZZ stress indicator field ηZZ of the reference solve and extract the
points above its 99th percentile (after the same 2% boundary trim as Ωcrit ). Recall@r is the fraction

Appendix Table 4 reports anchor-level Precision@r, Recall@r and F1@r for all twelve MLLM–

5.3
Analysis of Zero-Shot Semantic Grounding
The Impact of Physics-Guided Prompting A central hypothesis of this study is that MLLMs
can translate expert-provided textual cues into load-dependent spatial selections. We compare
three prompt variants: Load+Features (loading conditions plus the stress-critical feature types),
Load+Features+Heuristics (adding load-specific spatial hints), and Load-Only (loading conditions
only, asking for “stress critical” regions). The effect of the feature list is consistent across all four
MLLMs: without it (Load-Only) the models propose more anchors (+0.7k to +2.1k per case),
recall rises (e.g. GPT-5.4-mini 0.644 → 0.777) and precision falls by 0.014–0.039 (Appendix
Table 4). Where Load-Only can be budget-matched (Gemini, GPT-5.4-mini) the broader selection
costs downstream accuracy: peak-stress error rises from 0.627 to 0.649 and from 0.629 to 0.664.
Adding load-specific heuristics to the feature list makes the selection slightly more conservative:
anchors per case fall by up to 0.5k, precision rises marginally (+0.002 to +0.008), and recall drops
for Gemini, Claude, and Qwen (0.008–0.061; unchanged for GPT-5.4-mini). Downstream, the effect
has no consistent sign: peak-stress error falls for Gemini and GPT-5.4-mini (−0.008, −0.013) and
rises for Claude and Qwen (+0.009, +0.003).

Limitations Our study has several limitations. A load-informed heuristic built from hand-derived
beam-theory formulas for each loading case outperforms GReFEM, GReFEM does not outperform
the retrained learned baseline AMBER, and 42% of the gap to the reference-solve oracle remains. The
MLLMs rely on the classical feature-detection stage to turn coarse grid-cell proposals into anchors,
and on the presence of recognisable geometric features. GReFEM decides only where to refine; the
refinement magnitude (hmin , Dmin , Dmax ) and the final mesh generation follow fixed rules. Our
evaluation covers linear elasticity under five displacement-driven load cases on ABC and FreeCAD
parts. Within these bounds, the results indicate that off-the-shelf MLLMs, without task-specific
training, can serve within GReFEM as solver-free, prompt-conditioned refinement policies and as an
interface between human physical intuition and low-level numerical discretization.

We introduced orthoViews as a view ranking module that sorts the rendered views in the order of
most to least informative views considering the stress critical features like internal edges, holes and
notches. We curated a dataset of 5k CAD geometries from open online sources like FreeCAD Riegel
et al. [2016] and ABC Autodesk CAD dataset Koch et al. [2019].

11 elevation angles (-90 to 90 degrees in intervals of 18 degrees, with just one azimuth angle for the
extremes), yielding a total of 110 views per mesh.

Expanded Downstream Evaluation
This appendix expands the matched-budget downstream study of the main text over the full 156-part
(× 5 load) benchmark. All errors are relative to the ≈ 5 × 106 -element reference solve and measured
inside the critical region Ωcrit .
Load-informed heuristic. This policy keeps the same geometric candidates as the blind heuristic
and changes only which of them are refined, using a solver-free estimate of nominal stress. We voxelfill the part and slice it into thin layers along the loading axis y (clamped at ymin , loaded at ymax );
for each layer we compute purely geometric section properties, area A(y), centroid (x̄, z̄), second
moment of area Iz (y), and polar moment J(y). Each candidate is assigned to its nearest layer and
weighted by the textbook nominal stress for the load case: w = 1/A (compression, net-section stress),
w = |x − x̄|/Iz (bending, σ = M c/I), and w = r/J (torsion, τ = T r/J), with r the distance to the
layer centroid; combined loads sum the normalised terms. We keep the top stress-weighted 30% of
candidates and density-filter to the matched anchor budget. Geometry thus decides where refinement
may go and beam-theory stress decides which locations matter; this prior wins where closed-form
reasoning is valid but must be re-derived by hand for each load family.
Learned-AMR baseline (AMBER). We retrained AMBER Freymuth et al. [2025], an iterative
graph network that predicts a continuous sizing field, from scratch on this task: 440 expert adaptive
meshes (88 ABC training parts × 5 load cases, disjoint from all 156 evaluation parts), each expert
mesh built by projecting the fine reference solve’s top-percentile ZZ refinement points onto the
coarse-resolution mesh, trained to its validation plateau on H100 GPUs (our 440 expert meshes are
16
4–20× more than the original AMBER datasets), with load-case conditioning added. At test time
its sizing field is globally scaled to GReFEM’s element budget at each sweep level; it fails to mesh
≈ 6% of cases. The final-step scale is calibrated by a secant search (up to 10 iterations, 5% tolerance),
which brings 95% of AMBER’s meshes within 10% of GReFEM’s element count at every sizing
level; the remainder are geometries on which the predicted field cannot be meshed. Across the sizing
sweep (Table 2) the two policies are comparable: their mean errors differ by at most 0.031 in peak
stress and 0.012 in strain energy at every level. A zero-shot, prompt-based policy thus lands in the
same accuracy range as a sizing field supervised on 440 expert meshes; on a narrow part family with
abundant expert meshes, a supervised sizing field would likely pull further ahead.

Compression: For the top and bottom views, only consider the BH
features. All edges or surfaces along the loading direction are not

Bending: For the top and bottom views, only consider the BH features.

stress concentrations. Geometric features ICE, BH, TH too close to
the axis about which load is applied are usually not stress critical, in
the case of bending loads features away from the axis are more likely to
be stress critical.
Torsion: Geometric features ICE, BH, TH too close to the axis about
which load is applied are usually not stress critical, in the case of
torsion loads features away from the axis are more likely to be stress
critical.
Bending-Compression: For each load (bending or compression), only
consider the BH features when viewed parallel to the loading direction.
All edges or surfaces along the loading direction for each load are
not stress critical but perpendicular to the respective loads are.

bending load is applied are usually not stress critical, in the case
of bending loads features away from the axis are more likely to be
stress critical. Consider stress critical areas taking care of both the
loads.

too close to the axis about which load is applied are usually not stress
critical, in the case of torsion loads features away from the axis are
more likely to be stress critical. Also, features along the loading
direction of the compressive load are not stress critical as they
do not create stress concentrations. Consider stress critical areas
