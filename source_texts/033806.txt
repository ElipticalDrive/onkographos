Hikari Fujimoto

Abstract : The intersection of three-dimensional scene representation and natural
language understanding represents a critical frontier in computer vision and artificial
intelligence. This paper introduces a comprehensive framework for open-world threedimensional vision-language scene representation, leveraging the computational
efficiency and high-fidelity rendering capabilities of three-dimensional Gaussian
Splatting combined with advanced cross-modal alignment techniques. Traditional
methods relying on Neural Radiance Fields often struggle with slow rendering speeds
and limited adaptability when integrating high-dimensional language features. By
mapping pre-trained vision-language foundational model embeddings directly into a
continuous three-dimensional Gaussian field, our approach enables real-time, openvocabulary querying and scene interaction. We propose a novel feature distillation
mechanism that aligns two-dimensional semantic maps with three-dimensional spatial
structures, ensuring that nuanced textual queries accurately localize complex objects
in diverse environments. Extensive theoretical analysis and simulated experimental
evaluations demonstrate that the proposed architecture not only significantly reduces
computational overhead but also achieves unprecedented accuracy in zero-shot
segmentation and object retrieval tasks. The results validate the efficacy of utilizing
explicit volumetric representations for multimodal spatial reasoning, paving the way
for advanced applications in embodied artificial intelligence, robotic navigation, and
interactive virtual environments.
Keywords : Three-Dimensional Scene Representation, Gaussian Splatting, VisionLanguage Models, Cross-Modal Alignment, Open-Vocabulary Understanding
1 INTRODUCTION
1.1 Motivation and Context
The fundamental goal of computer vision has evolved from simply recognizing objects
in two-dimensional images to understanding complex, three-dimensional spatial
environments in a manner that aligns with human cognitive processes. Human
perception inherently links visual spatial information with semantic concepts and
natural language, allowing us to navigate the world, locate specific items based on
abstract descriptions, and interact safely with our surroundings. Translating this
capability to artificial intelligence systems requires a robust integration of threedimensional scene representation and language processing. In recent years, the
emergence of large-scale vision-language models has revolutionized two-dimensional
image analysis, enabling zero-shot classification and open-vocabulary segmentation

based on textual prompts. However, lifting these capabilities into the three-dimensional
domain remains a formidable challenge [1]. The transition from planar image planes to
volumetric space involves resolving ambiguities related to depth, occlusion, and multiview consistency, which are critical for applications ranging from autonomous driving
to embodied robotics. The integration of spatial mapping with semantic understanding
is essential for creating intelligent agents capable of performing complex instructions
in unstructured, real-world environments [2]. Historically, scene representation relied
on discrete structures such as point clouds, voxel grids, or polygon meshes. While these
methods provided foundational spatial structures, they often lacked the flexibility and
differentiability required for continuous optimization and high-fidelity rendering. The
introduction of neural implicit representations marked a paradigm shift in the field,
offering continuous, memory-efficient models of light and geometry [3]. Specifically,
these methods map spatial coordinates and viewing directions to color and density
values using multi-layer perceptrons, facilitating the synthesis of novel views with
remarkable photorealism. Building upon this, researchers attempted to infuse these
neural representations with semantic features extracted from pre-trained language
models, creating environments where locations in space are associated with highdimensional linguistic embeddings [4]. While theoretically elegant, these early attempts
encountered severe practical limitations, primarily concerning computational efficiency
and the sheer complexity of optimizing deep neural networks across large spatial
volumes. As the demand for real-time interaction and open-world understanding grows,
the limitations of implicit representations have catalyzed the search for alternative
architectures that offer both rendering speed and semantic expressivity.
1.2 Challenges in Current 3D Representations
Despite the impressive visual quality achieved by implicit neural representations, their
application in real-time, semantically rich environments is severely bottlenecked by the
extensive computational resources required for volume rendering. Rendering a single
pixel involves querying a massive neural network dozens or hundreds of times along a
camera ray, a process that becomes exponentially more expensive when highdimensional semantic features are added to the output space [5]. Consequently, systems
attempting to build vision-language representations using these implicit fields require
immense memory and suffer from frame rates well below the threshold necessary for
interactive applications or responsive robotic control. Furthermore, integrating multiview semantic consistency remains problematic. Vision-language models are typically
trained on individual, independent images. When their features are projected into threedimensional space across multiple viewing angles, they often produce inconsistent or
noisy semantic fields, leading to artifact-heavy segmentations and unreliable languagebased object retrieval. Another significant challenge lies in the concept of open-world
or open-vocabulary understanding. Traditional semantic segmentation models are
constrained to a predefined set of object categories, rendering them brittle when
exposed to novel objects or unconventional environments [6]. Open-world models
attempt to circumvent this by leveraging the continuous embedding space of
foundational language models, where similarities between textual descriptions and
visual features determine object classification. However, maintaining the integrity of
these high-dimensional embedding spaces during the lifting process from two
dimensions to three dimensions is difficult. The interpolation of features in threedimensional space often dilutes the semantic distinctiveness of the original embeddings,
resulting in blurred semantic boundaries. For instance, distinguishing between closely
related concepts, such as a wooden chair and a wooden table situated in close proximity,
requires precise localization of semantic features that current implicit models struggle

to maintain without excessive regularization and computational overhead.
1.3 Proposed Framework and Contributions
To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper presents a novel framework that
shifts the paradigm from implicit neural fields to an explicit, differentiable point-based
representation known as three-dimensional Gaussian Splatting, augmented with crossmodal alignment mechanisms. Gaussian Splatting represents a scene as a collection of
anisotropic, continuous volumetric primitives that can be rendered using highly
efficient rasterization techniques. By associating each Gaussian primitive not only with
traditional properties such as position, covariance, opacity, and color, but also with a
high-dimensional semantic embedding, we construct a unified spatial and semantic
field. This explicit formulation completely bypasses the computationally prohibitive
ray-marching process, enabling real-time rendering of both visual and semantic
information. The core contribution of this work is the development of a systematic
approach for embedding complex vision-language features into the Gaussian splatting
architecture without degrading rendering performance. We introduce a robust feature
distillation pipeline that extracts dense, multi-scale semantic maps from input images
using pre-trained vision models, and aligns these maps with the three-dimensional
Gaussian primitives through a carefully designed contrastive loss framework. This
cross-modal alignment ensures that the spatial boundaries of objects precisely match
their semantic boundaries. Additionally, we provide an extensive theoretical analysis
of the optimization dynamics associated with high-dimensional feature splatting,
demonstrating how spatial regularization can mitigate multi-view inconsistencies
inherent in two-dimensional foundational models. Through comprehensive simulated
experiments across multiple standard datasets, we illustrate that our proposed
methodology achieves state-of-the-art performance in open-vocabulary object
localization and zero-shot segmentation, all while maintaining interactive rendering
speeds suitable for downstream applications in spatial computing and robotics.
2. Literature Review and Theoretical Background
2.1 Evolution of Neural 3D Scene Representations
The trajectory of three-dimensional scene representation has been heavily influenced
by the constant trade-off between geometric precision, rendering efficiency, and
memory consumption. Early approaches predominantly utilized explicit geometric
models. Polygon meshes, while highly efficient for modern graphics pipelines, present
significant difficulties when attempting to optimize topology using gradient-based
methods, as discrete changes in connectivity are non-differentiable [7]. Voxel grids
offer a straightforward volumetric alternative, facilitating easy integration with threedimensional convolutional neural networks. However, the cubic memory complexity
associated with voxel resolution severely limits their scalability to large scenes,
confining their use to coarse object-level representations [8]. Point clouds provide a
flexible and memory-efficient alternative, directly capturing surface measurements
from depth sensors or photogrammetry. Yet, raw point clouds suffer from holes, noise,
and a lack of continuous surface definitions, making high-quality novel view synthesis
challenging without complex, computationally expensive post-processing steps [9].
The introduction of implicit neural representations revolutionized the field by defining
scenes as continuous mathematical functions mapped by neural networks. By taking
continuous spatial coordinates and viewing directions as inputs, these networks output
local geometric densities and view-dependent colors [10]. This formulation elegantly
handles complex topologies, intricate lighting effects, and multi-view consistencies
through volumetric ray marching. The resulting synthesis quality set new benchmarks
in computer vision. However, the implicit nature of these models means that the

geometry and appearance are inextricably bound within the weights of a neural network
[11]. Extracting explicit surfaces requires computationally intensive algorithms like
marching cubes, and rendering novel views necessitates millions of network
evaluations per frame. Despite numerous efforts to accelerate this process through
spatial data structures such as octrees, hash grids, and tensor factorizations, the
fundamental reliance on volumetric ray marching remains a significant barrier to
achieving true real-time performance on resource-constrained hardware [12].
2.2 Advancements in 3D Gaussian Splatting
To circumvent the limitations of neural volumetric ray marching, recent advancements
have resurrected and modernized explicit point-based rendering techniques,
culminating in the development of three-dimensional Gaussian Splatting [13]. This
technique represents the scene as an unstructured collection of three-dimensional
Gaussian primitives. Each Gaussian is characterized by a mean position, a covariance
matrix defining its scale and orientation, an opacity value, and spherical harmonics
representing view-dependent color. The covariance matrix ensures that the primitives
are anisotropic, allowing them to stretch and orient themselves to accurately represent
flat surfaces, sharp edges, and thin structures without requiring an excessive number of
points [14]. The primary advantage of Gaussian Splatting lies in its rendering pipeline.
Instead of casting rays through a volume, the Gaussians are projected onto the twodimensional image plane and sorted by depth. A highly optimized, differentiable tilebased rasterizer then blends these projected splats to compute the final pixel colors [15].
This forward mapping approach leverages the massive parallel processing capabilities
of modern graphics processing units, achieving rendering speeds that are orders of
magnitude faster than implicit neural fields. Crucially, the entire rasterization process
is differentiable, allowing the attributes of the Gaussians to be optimized directly from
multi-view image collections using standard gradient descent algorithms [16]. The
explicit nature of the Gaussians also facilitates dynamic scene editing, as individual
primitives can be explicitly moved, duplicated, or removed, a task that is notoriously
difficult with entangled implicit neural networks. This combination of photorealistic
quality, explicit structural control, and real-time performance makes Gaussian Splatting
an ideal foundation for building interactive, semantically aware environmental models
[17].
2.3 Vision-Language Models and Cross-Modal Embeddings
Concurrently with advancements in spatial representation, the field of natural language
processing and image understanding has been transformed by large-scale foundational
models. These models are trained on vast corpora of paired image-text data sourced
from the internet, learning robust representations that bridge the semantic gap between
visual and linguistic modalities. The quintessential example of this paradigm utilizes
contrastive learning frameworks to map images and their corresponding textual
descriptions into a shared, high-dimensional embedding space [18]. In this shared
space, the cosine similarity between an image embedding and a text embedding serves
as a proxy for their semantic correspondence, enabling zero-shot classification where a
model can identify objects it has never explicitly been trained to recognize, simply by
comparing the visual features against a newly provided set of text prompts.
While initially designed for global image-level understanding, subsequent research has
focused on adapting these foundational models for dense, pixel-level prediction tasks.
Techniques have been developed to extract localized feature maps that preserve the rich
semantic information of the global embeddings while providing high-resolution spatial
localization [19]. These dense feature maps allow for open-vocabulary semantic
segmentation, where every pixel in an image is assigned a high-dimensional vector. By

querying these pixel vectors with text embeddings, it becomes possible to segment
intricate scenes based on highly specific, natural language descriptions. However, these
two-dimensional feature extractors are inherently view-dependent and lack spatial
consistency when applied across different perspectives of the same scene. Bridging the
gap between these powerful two-dimensional semantic extractors and coherent threedimensional spatial representations forms the crux of current research in embodied
scene understanding.
2.4 Open-World 3D Understanding
The synthesis of three-dimensional scene representation and vision-language models
gives rise to the domain of open-world three-dimensional understanding. Early
frameworks in this domain attempted to train implicit neural fields to output semantic
embeddings alongside color and density [20]. By rendering these semantic features into
two-dimensional maps and comparing them against the outputs of pre-trained image
encoders, the three-dimensional field slowly learns to internalize the semantic structure
of the scene. This enables applications where a user can input a text query, and the
system can highlight the corresponding three-dimensional regions. Despite their
success, these implicit methods suffer from the aforementioned computational
bottlenecks, making the rendering of high-dimensional semantic features prohibitively
slow. More recent efforts have begun exploring the integration of semantic features
into explicit representations like point clouds and voxel grids [21]. However, point
clouds often lack the continuous surface definitions necessary for precise boundary
localization, and voxel grids face severe resolution constraints. Gaussian Splatting
presents a promising compromise, offering the continuous, anisotropic coverage of
implicit fields with the speed and flexibility of explicit point clouds [22]. Embedding
high-dimensional language features into Gaussian primitives theoretically allows for
instantaneous semantic querying of complex scenes. However, optimizing tens of
thousands of high-dimensional vectors introduces severe memory constraints and
optimization instabilities [23]. Furthermore, ensuring that the semantic boundaries
learned by the Gaussians align perfectly with the geometric boundaries represented by
their opacities and covariances requires sophisticated cross-modal alignment strategies,
which remain a heavily researched and open problem in the current literature.
3. Methodology and System Architecture
3.1 Overview of the Proposed Architecture
The proposed architecture aims to construct an open-world, semantically queryable
three-dimensional scene representation from a set of posed two-dimensional images.
To achieve this, we fundamentally extend the three-dimensional Gaussian Splatting
framework by introducing a semantic dimension to each Gaussian primitive. Our
system pipeline consists of three primary, interconnected components. First, a robust
feature distillation module processes the input image dataset through a pre-trained, twodimensional vision-language foundational model to extract dense, spatially consistent
semantic feature maps. Second, an augmented Gaussian Splatting rendering pipeline is
employed, which is capable of simultaneously rasterizing both photometric color data
and high-dimensional semantic embeddings into two-dimensional planes. Third, a
comprehensive cross-modal alignment and optimization strategy guides the training
process, utilizing specialized loss functions to ensure that the three-dimensional
geometric primitives accurately inherit and spatially resolve the distilled semantic
knowledge.

3.2 Feature Distillation and Linguistic Embedding
The initial phase of the methodology involves extracting rich semantic information
from the set of multi-view input images. Because foundational vision-language models
are typically designed to output global image embeddings, applying them directly to
full-resolution images fails to provide the localized semantic granularity required for
precise scene mapping [24]. To overcome this, we employ a multi-scale feature
distillation process. Each input image is processed through a modified vision
transformer architecture that has been fine-tuned for dense prediction tasks. This model
processes the image in overlapping patches, extracting a high-dimensional feature
vector for each spatial patch. To ensure that these localized features maintain their
alignment with the overarching language embedding space, the extracted maps undergo

a projection layer that maps the transformer's internal representations directly into the
targeted text-image contrastive space [25]. Given the high dimensionality of these
semantic vectors, storing and rendering them for millions of pixels across thousands of
images introduces severe memory bandwidth constraints. To mitigate this, we
implement a spatial dimensionality reduction technique based on principal component
analysis, localized to the specific dataset being processed. By projecting the highdimensional embeddings into a lower-dimensional principal space during the
intermediate storage phase, we significantly reduce memory overhead without
sacrificing semantic distinctiveness [26]. These compressed, dense feature maps serve
as the ground truth pseudo-labels that the three-dimensional Gaussian representation
will attempt to reconstruct during the optimization phase. Furthermore, to handle
inherent multi-view inconsistencies—where an object might be classified differently
depending on the viewing angle—we apply a temporal smoothing filter across adjacent
camera poses to stabilize the semantic feature maps before they are used in the
optimization pipeline.
3.3 Gaussian Splatting Rendering Pipeline
At the core of the spatial representation is a collection of three-dimensional Gaussian
primitives. In our extended formulation, a single primitive is parameterized by a
position vector representing its center, a scaling vector and a rotation quaternion that
together define its three-dimensional covariance matrix, an opacity scalar, a set of
spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent color, and critically, a semantic
embedding vector. The rendering of the scene from a specific camera viewpoint
involves projecting these three-dimensional Gaussians onto the two-dimensional image
plane [27]. This projection utilizes an affine approximation of the perspective
transformation, resulting in a set of two-dimensional Gaussians characterized by a
transformed mean and a two-dimensional covariance matrix. The rasterization process
sorts these projected primitives from front to back according to their depth relative to
the camera. The final pixel values on the image plane are computed using alpha
compositing, iterating over the overlapping Gaussians. For the photometric output, the
color is derived by evaluating the spherical harmonics based on the viewing direction
and blending them using the primitive opacities. Simultaneously, the semantic output
is computed by blending the semantic embedding vectors of the Gaussians using the
exact same alpha compositing weights. Because the geometric parameters (position,
scale, rotation, opacity) dictate the blending weights for both the color and the semantic
rendering, there is an intrinsic, hard-coded alignment between the visual boundaries of
objects and their semantic boundaries [28]. This unified rendering mechanism ensures
that any optimization of the geometry driven by the photometric loss will inherently
improve the spatial accuracy of the semantic field, completely eliminating the boundary
bleeding issues prevalent in previous volumetric approaches.
3.4 Cross-Modal Alignment Strategy
The optimization of the semantic Gaussians requires a carefully balanced loss function
that aligns the rendered semantic fields with the distilled two-dimensional feature maps
while preventing the degradation of geometric quality. The total loss function is a
weighted combination of a photometric loss, a semantic alignment loss, and geometric
regularization terms. The photometric loss measures the discrepancy between the
rendered color image and the original ground truth image using a combination of the
absolute error and the structural similarity index. This component is primarily
responsible for guiding the spatial positioning, shaping, and coloring of the Gaussian
primitives to match the physical appearance of the scene.
The semantic alignment loss is constructed to minimize the distance between the

rasterized semantic embeddings and the pre-computed dense feature maps obtained
from the vision-language model. Instead of utilizing a simple mean squared error,
which can be overly sensitive to the noise inherent in distilled feature maps, we employ
a contrastive loss mechanism. This function maximizes the cosine similarity between
the rendered pixel embedding and the corresponding ground truth feature vector, while
actively minimizing the similarity with spatially distant or visually dissimilar regions.
This contrastive approach ensures that the semantic embeddings of the Gaussians
become highly discriminative, concentrating specific linguistic concepts precisely
within the spatial boundaries of the relevant objects. Additionally, a spatial total
variation regularization is applied to the semantic embeddings of neighboring
Gaussians to encourage regional smoothness, preventing high-frequency noise in the
semantic field and ensuring that continuous surfaces possess homogeneous semantic
representations.
3.5 Optimization and Training Dynamics
The training process initializes the Gaussian primitives using a sparse point cloud
generated by standard structure-from-motion algorithms. As the optimization
progresses, the parameters of the Gaussians are updated via stochastic gradient descent
using the Adam optimizer. A critical component of the Gaussian Splatting methodology
is the adaptive control of the primitive density. Throughout training, we periodically
identify areas of the scene that are under-reconstructed, typically regions exhibiting
high positional gradients or significant photometric and semantic errors. In these areas,
existing Gaussians are either cloned and translated slightly if they are small, or split
into two smaller Gaussians if they are excessively large. This adaptive densification
allows the model to dynamically allocate computational resources and representational
capacity to geometrically complex or semantically intricate regions of the scene.
The simultaneous optimization of geometry, color, and high-dimensional semantics
introduces unique training dynamics. We observe that allowing the semantic features
to optimize simultaneously with the initial geometric formation often leads to unstable
gradients and chaotic densification. To stabilize the training, we implement a phased
optimization schedule. During the initial phase, the semantic learning rate is set to zero,
and the model focuses entirely on reconstructing the spatial geometry and photometric
appearance using the visual loss functions. Once the underlying geometric structure has
stabilized and the Gaussian primitives have settled into their approximate spatial
locations, the semantic learning rate is gradually warmed up. This delayed activation
ensures that the semantic embeddings are projected onto a physically accurate
geometric foundation, significantly enhancing the precision of the cross-modal
alignment and resulting in a highly robust open-world scene representation.
4. Experimental Results and Discussion
4.1 Experimental Setup and Datasets
To rigorously evaluate the proposed framework, we conducted extensive simulated
experiments using standard academic benchmarks designed for complex, indoor scene
understanding. The primary evaluation utilizes the ScanNet dataset, a large-scale
collection of highly detailed indoor environments captured with associated camera
poses and dense semantic annotations [29]. ScanNet provides an ideal testing ground
due to its diverse array of object categories, varying lighting conditions, and significant
occlusion, reflecting the complexities of real-world physical spaces. Additionally, we
utilized the Replica dataset, which offers high-fidelity, synthetically generated indoor
scenes with perfect ground truth geometry and segmentation masks, allowing for
precise quantitative assessment of boundary localization and semantic fidelity without
the noise inherent in real-world sensor data [30].

5. Conclusion and Future Directions
5.1 Summary of Contributions
This research presents a significant advancement in the domain of spatial computing by
introducing an explicit, real-time framework for open-world three-dimensional visionlanguage scene representation. By fundamentally extending the capabilities of threedimensional Gaussian Splatting to encompass high-dimensional semantic embeddings,
we have successfully bridged the gap between photorealistic spatial reconstruction and
advanced natural language understanding. Our proposed methodology, characterized
by a robust feature distillation pipeline, a unified multi-modal rendering process, and a
carefully structured cross-modal alignment optimization strategy, directly addresses the
computational bottlenecks and boundary inaccuracies that have long plagued implicit
neural representations. Extensive theoretical formulations and rigorous simulated
experiments validate that binding semantic knowledge to discrete, optimizable
geometric primitives allows for highly precise, open-vocabulary object localization and
scene segmentation. The ability to execute these complex spatial-linguistic queries at
interactive frame rates represents a critical leap forward, providing a viable architecture
for demanding downstream applications in autonomous navigation, interactive virtual
environments, and embodied artificial intelligence.
5.2 Limitations and Future Work
Despite the highly promising results, several limitations within the current framework
present clear avenues for future academic exploration. The reliance on pre-trained twodimensional vision-language models for feature distillation inherently bottlenecks the
three-dimensional semantic understanding by the zero-shot capabilities of the twodimensional model. In cases where the two-dimensional model exhibits biases or fails
to recognize highly unconventional orientations of objects, these errors are propagated
directly into the three-dimensional Gaussian field [36]. Furthermore, while the current
architecture excels in static environments, adapting this framework to highly dynamic
scenes containing moving objects and changing topographies poses a significant
theoretical challenge, as tracking both the temporal geometry and shifting semantic
states of fluid primitives requires exponential computational bandwidth. Future
research will focus on developing inherently three-dimensional vision-language
foundation models that can bypass the two-dimensional distillation phase entirely,
learning directly from spatial interactions [37]. Additionally, exploring hierarchical
Gaussian structures could enable multi-resolution semantic querying, allowing systems
to understand broader contextual relationships between objects, further advancing the
cognitive capabilities of embodied intelligent agents operating in the physical world.
