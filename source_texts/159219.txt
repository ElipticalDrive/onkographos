Digital holographic displays are dynamic three-dimensional display systems that compute a hologram for a virtual scene and present it on an electronically addressed optical modulator. In this article, “digital” means computer-generated holography (CGH) and usually an SLM-driven light engine: software calculates a phase or amplitude pattern, a silicon-based liquid-crystal or micromirror device modulates coherent light, and diffraction reconstructs a view-dependent wavefront. That scope is narrower than optical holographic displays, which covers the broader family of recorded, static, analog, and hybrid holographic techniques.
The attraction of digital holography is that, in principle, it can reproduce the light wave that would have come from a real object: stereo disparity, motion parallax, occlusion, shading, and accommodation can all be encoded in one optical field rather than faked by a flat screen.[1] The practical reality is more constrained. Current systems must divide limited SLM pixels, coherent-light power, refresh rate, computation, and eye-safety budget among field of view, eyebox, image size, color, brightness, and speckle control. The central engineering problem is therefore not whether holography can encode natural 3D cues, but how much of that ideal a real CGH display can deliver at useful size, cost, and robustness.
Definition and Scope
An SLM-based digital holographic display synthesizes a hologram in real time or near real time. The core component is a spatial light modulator (SLM): an electronic panel whose pixels alter a property of the incoming light, usually optical phase, amplitude, or polarization. The SLM acts as a rewritable hologram “screen.”[2] When illuminated by a laser or another sufficiently coherent light source, the displayed pattern diffracts the light into a three-dimensional reconstruction. Driving electronics, a hologram-computation pipeline, and projection or near-eye optics determine whether the output becomes a tabletop image, a headset image, a small free-space projection, or a laboratory measurement field.
This page focuses on dynamic CGH displays: systems where a computer calculates the hologram and an SLM or closely related electro-optic device updates it. It does not treat every product marketed as a “hologram” as a digital holographic display. Many convincing 3D displays use lenticular optics, parallax barriers, tracked stereoscopy, volumetric voxels, fog screens, or reflective theatre illusions without reconstructing a coherent optical wavefront. Those systems are valuable neighbors in the display taxonomy, but they solve different optical problems.
The distinction matters because a true digital holographic display is a wavefront display, not merely a view display. A stereoscopic monitor sends one flat image to each eye. A light-field display sends many directional rays or views. A swept-volume or laser-induced plasma display places luminous points at physical depths. A Pepper's Ghost installation reflects 2D imagery from glass or film into a theatrical “floating” image. A digital holographic display instead tries to form the complex optical field itself, so the viewer's pupil samples the same kind of phase-dependent light distribution it would receive from an object.
Most SLM-based holography requires coherent or partially coherent illumination because interference and diffraction depend on phase. The layout commonly includes red, green, and blue laser sources; beam-shaping and polarization optics; one or more SLMs; lenses or waveguides; spatial filters to suppress unwanted diffraction orders; and sensors or calibration cameras. Phase-only liquid-crystal-on-silicon (LCoS) SLMs are common in research because they can redirect light efficiently, while digital micromirror devices (DMDs) offer high speed but normally operate as binary or amplitude modulators.

Historical Context and Evolution
The idea of displaying 3D images by reconstructing optical wavefronts traces back to Dennis Gabor’s mid-20th-century work. Gabor carried out his basic holography experiments in 1948 while working on wavefront reconstruction, and he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1971 for the holographic method.[3] High-quality optical holograms became practical only after lasers supplied coherent illumination in the 1960s. In 1962, Emmett Leith and Juris Upatnieks produced early laser holographic images, helping move holography from a reconstruction principle into a visible display medium.[2]
The specifically digital branch emerged as computer graphics and Fourier optics converged. A. W. Lohmann and colleagues pioneered computer-generated holograms in the 1960s, but the early patterns were usually printed or fabricated rather than updated electronically. The next step was electroholography: a computer-generated hologram refreshed by a modulator quickly enough to behave as a display. In the early 1990s, MIT Media Lab holovideo systems demonstrated real-time computer-generated holographic scenes using scanning optics and acousto-optic modulation rather than a modern flat-panel SLM.[4]
During the 1990s and 2000s, increasing graphics performance and better liquid-crystal modulators made SLM-based CGH more plausible. Researchers explored horizontal-parallax-only displays, tiled “hogel” architectures, multi-plane approximations, phase-retrieval algorithms, and time-multiplexed color. These approaches acknowledged that a full human-scale holographic television would require an enormous space-bandwidth product, so many prototypes traded vertical parallax, viewing angle, or image size for a workable demonstration.
In the 2010s and 2020s, the field split into several routes. One route improved bench-top and near-eye CGH with GPUs, neural holography, calibration cameras, and better SLM driving. Another route used holographic optical elements and waveguides for augmented-reality optics, sometimes with conventional microdisplays and sometimes with true CGH image formation. A 2024 Nature demonstration, for example, combined full-colour 3D holographic AR with metasurface waveguides and a lensless holographic light engine.[5] In 2025, Stanford and Meta researchers reported a compact mixed-reality prototype using synthetic-aperture waveguide holography; the paper describes an SLM-based holographic display with steered illumination to increase effective étendue and eyebox size.[6]
At the same time, commercial “holographic” monitors and AR products often chose non-holographic routes because they are manufacturable sooner. Microsoft’s HoloLens line popularized the language of holograms for AR overlays, but its display architecture is based on microdisplays and waveguides rather than per-pixel CGH wavefront reconstruction. Sony’s Spatial Reality Display, launched commercially in 2020, uses eye-sensing light-field display technology with a high-speed vision sensor and micro-optical lens, not a laser-illuminated CGH SLM.[7] Looking Glass Factory likewise sells glasses-free 3D displays in the light-field family, even when marketing language evokes holograms.[8]
Core Mechanisms and Processes
At the heart of SLM-based holography are interference, diffraction, coherent light, and numerical propagation. A hologram is a pattern that transforms one optical field into another. In a display, the desired field comes from a 3D model, point cloud, captured light field, depth map, or target image stack. Software computes the complex field that should exist at the SLM plane, then encodes an approximation that the available hardware can actually show.
There are several common ways to compute CGH. Fourier and Fresnel propagation methods treat the scene as a wavefront or as a collection of point emitters and propagate the field mathematically to the SLM. Ray-based and point-source methods build the hologram from many elementary wave contributions. Iterative phase-retrieval methods, such as Gerchberg–Saxton and hybrid input-output variants, repeatedly propagate between the SLM plane and the target image plane while enforcing the constraints of the SLM. Newer neural and differentiable approaches optimize the displayed phase pattern against a camera-calibrated model of the actual optical system, which can reduce speckle, compensate aberrations, or generate acceptable holograms faster for known scene classes.
A difficulty appears immediately: the ideal computed field has both amplitude and phase, but many SLMs control only one. A phase-only LCoS panel can shift the phase of each pixel over a range close to 2π, but it cannot independently set brightness at that same pixel. An amplitude modulator can block or pass light, but it wastes power and does not directly supply phase control. Encoding methods such as kinoforms, double-phase encoding, off-axis carrier gratings, and multi-SLM arrangements translate the desired complex field into a realizable pattern.
Spatial light modulators
The SLM determines much of the display’s envelope. Key specifications include pixel count, pixel pitch, phase stroke, fill factor, refresh rate, polarization behavior, diffraction efficiency, wavefront flatness, and drive nonlinearity. Phase SLMs based on liquid crystal can be efficient but may refresh more slowly than DMDs. DMDs can switch at high rates but are naturally binary-amplitude devices. Some research systems use two modulators in sequence, a modulator plus a static diffractive optic, or a scanned illumination source to recover degrees of freedom that a single panel lacks.
Imperfections are not small details. Pixel crosstalk, dead zones between pixels, nonuniform phase response, temperature drift, and polarization leakage can create ghost images or reduce contrast. The undiffracted zero-order beam often appears as a bright central spot unless filtered or algorithmically suppressed. Practical systems therefore include calibration: a camera observes test holograms, the phase response is estimated, and the CGH algorithm compensates for hardware behavior.
Space-bandwidth product and viewing geometry
A fundamental limit is the space-bandwidth product (SBP), the number of independent optical samples available to describe image extent and viewing angle. In a simple SLM display, the pixel count and pixel pitch set the available angular spread. The SLM pixel pitch acts like a diffraction grating period; the approximate diffraction angle scales with wavelength divided by pixel pitch. For green light near 530 nm and an 8 μm pixel, the first-order half-angle is only a few degrees. Wider field of view requires smaller pixels, more pixels, tiled modulators, scanning, or optical tricks that trade some other resource.
This limit explains why digital holography is more plausible first in near-eye displays than in room-scale projections. Near the eye, a small physical aperture can subtend a useful field if the eyebox is managed carefully. For a wall-sized multi-view display, the same SLM limitations become severe: many people, a wide viewing zone, and a large image volume all demand far more optical degrees of freedom.
Hologram encodings and color
Common encoding strategies include off-axis holograms, which separate the reconstructed image from the zero-order and conjugate image; Fresnel holograms, which model finite-distance propagation; and kinoforms, which are phase-only holograms optimized for efficiency at a target wavelength. Multi-plane holography divides a scene into depth slices and optimizes a pattern or sequence for those planes. Time-multiplexed holography rapidly displays several patterns within one perceptual frame, trading refresh rate for color, depth, speckle averaging, or view zones.
Color adds a second layer of complexity. Because diffraction angle depends on wavelength, a hologram calculated for red light does not automatically reconstruct correctly in green or blue. Many displays use separate red, green, and blue lasers and either separate optical channels, time-multiplexed SLM patterns, or wavelength-compensated algorithms. Full-color holography must also manage speckle correlations, chromatic aberration, eye-safety limits for multiple wavelengths, and the much higher bandwidth of three color channels.
Artifacts and image quality
Laser coherence produces speckle: granular interference noise that can make otherwise sharp holograms look dirty or unstable. Speckle can be reduced through partially coherent illumination, vibrating diffusers, angular diversity, wavelength diversity, polarization diversity, time averaging, or algorithms that optimize perceived image quality rather than exact field reconstruction. Each method trades against brightness, optical complexity, or image sharpness.
Other artifacts include the zero-order spot, twin image, ghost diffraction orders, color fringing, SLM grid texture, reduced contrast from stray light, and accommodation errors when only a few depth planes are represented. For near-eye displays, pupil swim, eyebox uniformity, and retinal safety also become design constraints. High-quality digital holography is therefore a co-design problem: optics, hardware calibration, CGH algorithms, content representation, and human visual perception must be tuned together.
Representative Examples and Case Studies
Digital holography has many prototypes but few mass-market true-CGH products. The examples below are best read as a taxonomy of approaches rather than a consumer buying guide.
MIT holovideo was an early proof that computed holograms could move. The Media Lab systems of the early 1990s generated changing holographic scenes using real-time computation, laser illumination, scanning optics, and electro-optic/acousto-optic modulation.[4] They were not modern compact SLM panels, but they established the electroholographic ambition: video-rate 3D imagery from computed wavefronts.
Bench-top SLM holography in universities and optics labs remains the main source of true digital holographic display demonstrations. Typical systems use one or more LCoS SLMs, RGB lasers, Fourier lenses, spatial filters, calibration cameras, and GPU-generated phase holograms. Some show small floating objects, some target holographic projection onto a screen or diffuser, and some are built as near-eye demonstrators. Their recurring pattern is consistent: convincing depth over a small viewing zone, with trade-offs in color, brightness, speckle, and field of view.
Near-eye holographic AR and VR prototypes are now among the most important research routes. The 2024 full-colour metasurface AR display showed that holographic light engines can be combined with thin waveguide optics for glasses-like form factors.[5] The 2025 Stanford/Meta synthetic-aperture waveguide work is significant because it explicitly tackles étendue and eyebox, two constraints that often prevent small SLM displays from being usable as head-worn displays.[6] Stanford’s public report framed the prototype as a step toward lightweight mixed-reality glasses using artificial intelligence and holograms, while still presenting it as research rather than a shipping product.[9]
Commercial light-field and tracked-stereo systems show why terminology must be handled carefully. Sony’s Spatial Reality Display is a strong example of a real, sold 3D display that is not a digital holographic display in the strict CGH sense: Sony describes a high-speed vision sensor and a micro-optical lens that support glasses-free 3D visualization for a single tracked viewer.[7] Looking Glass Factory’s light-field displays similarly target glasses-free multi-view visualization for product design, medical data, art, and signage; the company’s documentation labels the family as light-field displays, not laser-illuminated SLM holography.[8] In 2025, Looking Glass announced Hololuminescent Displays for standard-video-to-3D experiences; they may be commercially important for 3D signage, but they remain better treated as light-field or layered display products than as true CGH displays.[10]
Medical and engineering visualization often uses the word holographic for a broader class of 3D workstations. Holoxica, for example, develops holographic and 3D visualization tools for medical scans, including software for viewing MRI, CT, ultrasound, and PET data in 3D.[11] Such systems illustrate the demand pull: clinicians and engineers want intuitive spatial visualization of volumetric data. Whether the display engine is true CGH, light-field, stereoscopic, or another 3D method is a separate technical question that should be stated explicitly rather than hidden under marketing language.
Methods of Study
Researching digital holographic displays spans optics, computer graphics, electrical engineering, and human perception.
Optical simulation usually begins with scalar diffraction theory, Fourier optics, and numerical propagation. Researchers simulate how a candidate SLM pattern will propagate through lenses, apertures, waveguides, and the viewer's pupil. For more demanding systems, models include polarization, chromatic dispersion, coherence, aberrations, SLM nonlinearity, and metasurface or volume-holographic grating behavior.
Algorithm development tests how quickly and accurately holograms can be generated. GPU implementations of Fresnel transforms, point-source accumulation, and iterative phase retrieval compete with neural holography, differentiable optics, and learned priors for common content types. Evaluation is not just mathematical error: a hologram that minimizes wavefront error may not minimize perceived speckle, depth ambiguity, or visual fatigue.
Experimental characterization uses cameras, wavefront sensors, photodetectors, spectrometers, and calibrated eye models. Researchers measure resolution, modulation transfer, contrast, brightness, diffraction efficiency, color error, speckle contrast, field of view, eyebox, focal behavior, and zero-order leakage. For near-eye displays, they also measure pupil-dependent image quality and whether the virtual image remains stable as the eye moves.
Calibration and compensation are central methods because ideal CGH assumes ideal optics. A practical system records its own output and updates the CGH model to compensate for SLM phase response, lens aberration, waveguide nonuniformity, or laser instability. Camera-in-the-loop optimization and differentiable optical models have become important for this reason.
Human-subject studies examine whether holographic cues actually improve comfort, depth judgments, or task performance. Researchers compare holographic, stereoscopic, light-field, and varifocal displays for vergence-accommodation conflict, perceived realism, visual fatigue, and depth accuracy. The result is often application-dependent: a small near-eye hologram used for design inspection faces different perceptual requirements than a public tabletop display or surgical planning workstation.
Content generation is another research area. A holographic scene can be generated from a CAD model, polygonal graphics, a depth image, a point cloud, a captured light field, or a volume scan. Turning that representation into compact, real-time CGH video remains difficult because holograms are data-heavy and computation-intensive. Compression and streaming standards for holographic video are therefore an open part of the display problem, not merely a media-distribution afterthought.
Debates and Open Questions
The recurring debate is ideal physics versus deployable systems. Holography can represent all depth cues in principle, but real displays must ration pixels, photons, time, and computation. Near-eye systems can sometimes hide limitations with eye tracking, foveated rendering, pupil steering, or head-coupled optics. Public multi-view displays cannot rely on those assumptions as easily.
Space-bandwidth product remains the hard bottleneck. Tiling SLMs, scanning illumination, using metasurfaces, or fabricating smaller-pitch modulators can expand the envelope, but none removes the basic requirement for enormous optical degrees of freedom in large, wide-angle, high-resolution holography. A future consumer holographic TV would need a very different manufacturing and computation profile from a small laboratory near-eye prototype.
Speckle, brightness, and safety form another linked constraint. More coherent laser power can improve brightness, but it can also increase speckle and eye-safety risk. Reducing speckle with diffusers or angular diversity can reduce efficiency. Near-eye systems must satisfy retinal exposure limits under normal use and foreseeable faults; public displays must also handle reflections, bypassed enclosures, and long viewing times.
Cost and manufacturability are unsettled. True CGH displays need high-quality modulators, coherent light sources, precision optics, calibration, and substantial compute. Simpler stereoscopic or light-field systems can be cheaper and good enough for many tasks. The strongest argument for digital holography is not generic “3D wow,” but use cases where correct focus cues, compact optics, or wavefront control produce value that view-based methods cannot match.
Content and standards lag behind hardware demos. Conventional video standards encode pixels on a plane. Holographic displays may need wavefront data, depth-aware imagery, layered fields, point clouds, neural scene representations, or device-specific phase patterns. That raises questions about compression, latency, interchange formats, authoring tools, and whether holograms should be streamed as physical wavefronts or generated locally from higher-level 3D scene descriptions.
Finally, terminology remains a practical problem. The word “hologram” is culturally powerful, so companies use it for Pepper's Ghost, light-field monitors, AR overlays, volumetric displays, and true CGH. A useful technical article should not police marketing for its own sake, but it should keep the optical distinctions visible so readers can compare systems honestly.
Significance and Applications
Digital holographic displays matter because they turn computation into controlled wavefronts. If the engineering constraints are solved well enough, they could provide 3D imagery without special glasses and with more natural focus behavior than ordinary stereo displays. That makes them especially relevant to tasks where spatial judgment matters: surgical planning, anatomical education, microscopy visualization, molecule and protein inspection, engineering design, architecture, remote manipulation, and high-end simulation.
Near-eye AR and VR are currently one of the most plausible application paths. Holographic light engines and holographic waveguides could make headsets thinner while supporting focus-correct imagery. They also fit naturally with eye tracking, foveated rendering, and adaptive optics: the display can spend optical and computational budget where the viewer is actually looking. For VR, holographic near-eye displays could reduce vergence-accommodation conflict; for AR, they could place virtual content at believable depths in the real world rather than on a fixed focal plane.
Professional visualization is another likely path. A medical team inspecting a heart scan, an engineer studying airflow around a turbine, or a geoscientist examining subsurface data may benefit from directly perceiving depth relationships. In these settings, a costly prototype can be justified more easily than in consumer entertainment, and the content already exists as 3D data. The caveat is that many near-term deployments may use light-field, stereoscopic, or volumetric systems under the “holographic” label because they are easier to ship.
Entertainment, telepresence, and public displays remain aspirational. Life-size holographic calls, holographic concerts, and room-scale shared 3D workspaces are culturally compelling, but they require large display area, wide viewing zones, high brightness, robust safety, and content pipelines that do not yet exist at consumer scale. For the foreseeable future, “hologram” in concerts and retail often means Pepper's Ghost, transparent LED, projection mapping, or light-field signage rather than dynamic CGH.
The technology also has significance beyond displays. The same SLMs, CGH algorithms, and wavefront-shaping techniques appear in optical tweezers, microscopy, lithography, optical communications, beam steering, and adaptive optics. Digital holographic displays are therefore part of a wider photonics toolkit: their hardest display challenges push modulators, diffractive optics, and computational optics in ways that can spill over into measurement, manufacturing, and communication.
