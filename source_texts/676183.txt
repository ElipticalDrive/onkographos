# Abstract

Rendering is traditionally seen as a forward process that generates an image from a description of a virtual scene. In recent years, this scope was significantly broadened and rendering algorithms have been used to solve several challenging problems across computer vision, medicine, and other domains. This development is based on the ability to differentiate rendering algorithms, which means being able to quantify how variations of the virtual scene affect a generated image. Among others, this allows solving inverse problems, i.e., finding a representation of an environment that explains given (image) observations, automatically using gradient-based methods.

In rendering, one typically distinguishes between physically-based algorithms that are costly but accurately simulate light transport, and rendering algorithms that are focused on interactive performance but use various approximations for light transport. The reality of differentiable rendering has shown that both classes have their merit, and even further that inverse applications have brought up rendering algorithms that are specifically tailored to certain optimization tasks. Regardless of the application, efficiency is a universal goal in differentiable rendering, among others, because it enables faster exploration of larger solution spaces and even broader applicability. In this thesis, we tackle efficiency in three ways unique to differentiable rendering.

First, we make building blocks from real-time rendering available to differentiable rendering by ensuring that derivatives are well-defined while maintaining efficiency. This enables using cheap approximations for certain light transport effects in differentiable renderers. For one, we show that one of the oldest approximations for shadows, shadow maps, can be adapted to work in differentiable rendering frameworks. And further, we introduce a more general family of moment-based approximations to differentiable rendering. We provide a robust and fast implementation that is based on a proof of differentiability, and show that the resulting techniques yield significantly higher accuracy than existing approaches while being equally efficient.

Second, we reduce the computational cost and memory requirements of differentiating physically-based rendering algorithms. This not only improves efficiency but also broadens their applicability, making them practical in new application domains. More specifically, we revisit an algorithm for efficient reverse-mode differentiation, and introduce a variant that correctly accounts for derivatives with respect to scene geometry. We then turn our focus towards time-resolved rendering and show how the same algorithm can be extended beyond the steady-state setting, introducing time-resolved path replay backpropagation. We demonstrate that this algorithm has broad applications beyond graphics, enabling efficient and fully-differentiable path tracing for geometric acoustics.

Lastly, we develop a rendering algorithm that exploits the structure of inverse applications, using efficient approximations of light transport tailored to the task. We demonstrated this with the example of 3D surface reconstruction from images, by combining efficient triangle rasterization with the expressiveness of neural rendering. This design enables efficient 3D reconstruction in environments with arbitrary materials and illumination, without having to resort to costly light transport simulations.

# Zusammenfassung

Rendering wird traditionell als ein Vorwärtsprozess betrachtet, der ein Bild aus der Beschreibung einer virtuellen Szene generiert. In den vergangenen Jahren hat sich der Anwendungsbereich deutlich ausgeweitet und Rendering-Algorithmen wurden eingesetzt, um verschiedenste Probleme im maschinellen Sehen, in der Medizin, oder in anderen Feldern zu lösen. Diese Entwicklung basiert hauptsächlich darauf, Rendering-Algorithmen differenzieren zu können, also quantifizieren zu können, welche Auswirkungen Veränderungen der virtuellen Szene auf das generierte Bild haben. Dies ermöglicht unter anderem, inverse Problemstellungen – also das Finden einer Szene, die eine vorgegebene Beobachtung (z. B. ein Bild) besonders plausibel wiedergibt – automatisiert mit Hilfe von Gradientenabstiegsverfahren zu lösen.

Typischerweise wird beim Rendering zwischen zwei Klassen von Algorithmen unterschieden: physikbasierte, die kostspielig sind, aber dafür genaue Ergebnisse liefern, und solche mit Fokus auf interaktive Anwendungen, die zwar sehr effizient sind, dafür aber nur approximative Ergebnisse erzeugen. Die Vergangenheit hat gezeigt, dass für differenzierbares Rendering beide Klassen eine Daseinsberechtigung haben, und darüber hinaus sogar gänzlich neue Rendering-Algorithmen entwickelt wurden, die an gewisse Problemstellungen gekoppelt sind. Unabhängig von der Anwendung ist Effizienz ein allgemeines Ziel, denn dadurch werden das noch schnellere Ergründen noch größerer Lösungsräume und ein weitreichenderer Einsatz ermöglicht. In dieser Arbeit beschäftigen wir uns auf drei Arten mit der Effizienz von differenzierbarem Rendering.

Zuerst ermöglichen wir den Einsatz diverser Komponenten aus der Echtzeitgrafik in diesem Kontext, indem wir deren Differenzierbarkeit und Effizienz sicherstellen. Das erlaubt es, günstige Approximationen gewisser Lichttransporteffekte in differenzierbaren Renderern einzusetzen. Genauer zeigen wir zunächst, dass einer der ältesten Algorithmen zur Erzeugung von Schatten, "Shadow Mapping", für das differenzierbare Rendering angepasst werden kann. Des Weiteren führen wir eine allgemeinere Familie von Approximationen, basierend auf Momenten, ein. Wir stellen eine robuste und performante Implementierung bereit, welche auf einem theoretischen Beweis der Differenzierbarkeit basiert. Wir zeigen, dass diese Techniken im Vergleich zu existierenden Ansätzen eine deutlich höhere Genauigkeit liefern, bei vergleichbaren Kosten.

Zweitens reduzieren wir den Rechenaufwand und den Speicherverbrauch, die zum Differenzieren physikbasierter Algorithmen benötigt werden. Das erhöht nicht nur die Effizienz dieser Algorithmen, sondern erweitert auch deren Anwendbarkeit und Praktikabilität in neuen Feldern. Wir schauen uns zunächst einen existierenden Algorithmus für effizientes Differenzieren im Rückwärtsmodus an und führen eine neue Variante ein, die korrekte Ableitungen bezüglich der Szenengeometrie erzeugt. Wir wenden uns dann dem zeitaufgelösten Rendering zu und zeigen, dass derselbe Algorithmus dahingehend erweitert werden kann. Wir zeigen, dass unser Algorithmus, Time-Resolved Path Replay Backpropagation, ein breites Anwendungsspektrum außerhalb der Computergrafik hat und für effizientes, differenzierbares Path Tracing im Kontext von geometrischer Akustik verwendet werden kann.

versen Problems ausnutzt und effiziente, anwendungsspezifische Approximationen für Lichttransport verwendet. Wir zeigen das am Beispiel der 3D-Oberflächenrekonstruktion aus Bildern und kombinieren effiziente Dreiecksrasterung mit der Ausdruckskraft von neuronalem Rendering. Dieses Verfahren ermöglicht eine effiziente 3D-Rekonstruktion in Umgebungen mit beliebigen Materialien und Beleuchtungsbedingungen, ohne dass kostspielige Lichttransportsimulationen erforderlich sind.

# Introduction

Computer graphics has traditionally seen rendering as a forward process: generate an image from a description of a virtual scene, which includes the geometry of objects, their materials, and camera parameters. The main artifact of interest has been the image, which serves as the final output in typical applications for rendering systems, like movie production, visualization, or video games.

In recent years, it has become clear that rendering algorithms have a much broader scope. Instead of merely serving as image factories, they have been used to solve challenging problems across computer vision, medicine, robotics, manufacturing, and computational design more generally. This particular development is based on the ability to relate the outputs back to the inputs. For rendering, this means being able to quantify how variations of the virtual scene affect the generated image (see Fig. 1.1\). The reason this is useful is that many tasks in the above domains can be formulated as inverse problems: given an observation, for example, a photograph, what (real-world) environment has likely led to it? Fundamentally, the idea is to take a virtual scene and adjust its parameters until rendered images match the observations, in which case the virtual scene provides a possible explanation. A key realization is that when the effect of scene parameters on the generated image can be quantified, this process can be done in a structured and automated manner.

If seen abstractly, rendering algorithms are simply mathematical functions that map some inputs (the parameters of the scene) to some outputs (the image). The relation between inputs and outputs of a function is described by the derivatives. Differentiable rendering seeks to apply this to rendering algorithms, so that the primary artifact of interest is not only the image but also its derivatives with respect to scene parameters. Given the complexity of modern renderers, it might appear infeasible to differentiate these systems. However, on the lowest level, all of them simply perform a sequence of numerical computations according to mathematical models and equations, most of which can be viewed and differentiated in isolation.

The various building blocks of rendering algorithms have traditionally differed depending on the application, although they have been converging in recent years. On the one hand, physically-based rendering aims at generating images that faithfully reproduce the appearance of the real world. It simulates the propagation of light through a scene and its interaction with objects by solving the physical equations of light transport. This enables accurate reproduction of various phenomena, including soft shadows, interreflection, and caustics. The accuracy comes at the price of efficiency, such that these systems are typically used in offline scenarios, for example, movie production. On the other hand, various rendering algorithms focus on efficiency and interactive scenarios,

The reality of differentiable rendering has shown that both classes have their merit, and the appropriate balance between physical accuracy and efficiency strongly depends on the specific inverse application. Even further, many solutions rely on rendering algorithms that are tailored to the requirements of the task at hand. While these methods often build upon components of traditional rendering systems, they may also introduce new approximations or techniques that are primarily motivated by the optimization objectives rather than forward image synthesis.

Regardless of the application, improving the efficiency of differentiable rendering algorithms in terms of runtime and memory requirements is a universal goal. First, reducing computational cost lowers energy consumption, which is increasingly important in the context of climate change and power-efficient computing. Second, as improvements in hardware continue to slow down, progress increasingly depends on developing more efficient algorithms and software. Third, even with current hardware capabilities, efficient methods enable more rapid exploration of a larger space of possible solutions. Lastly, efficiency is a deciding factor in the practicability of solutions, so increasing efficiency also broadens the range of potential applications.

While efficiency in differentiable rendering shares the challenges of traditional forward rendering, as it also involves image synthesis, it comes with its own set of challenges and opportunities. In this thesis, we focus on the latter and tackle efficiency in three ways specific to differentiable rendering:

- 1. We make building blocks from real-time rendering available by ensuring that derivatives are well-defined while maintaining efficiency. This enables using cheap approximations for certain light transport effects in a differentiable renderer.
- 2. We reduce the computational cost and memory requirements of differentiating physically-based rendering algorithms. This not only improves efficiency but also broadens their applicability, making them practical in new application domains.

- 3. We develop rendering algorithms that exploit the structure of inverse applications, using efficient approximations of light transport tailored to the task. This solves inverse problems in complex environments without explicitly simulating light.

# Chapter 2

# Background

Images of virtual scenes can be generated in different ways: physically-based rendering (Section 2.1\) simulates light according to physical principles, whereas efficient real-time systems commonly make simplifying assumptions to achieve interactive performance (Section 2.2\). While these approaches differ in accuracy and computational cost, both ultimately produce images as the result of a numerical computation. Differentiable rendering (Section 2.4\) uses this perspective and applies techniques for differentiating programs (Section 2.3\) to traditional rendering algorithms to be able to compute derivatives of rendered images with respect to arbitrary scene parameters.

# 2.1 Physically-based Rendering

Physically-based rendering (PBR) is the process of simulating light transport in virtual scenes to synthesize images that reproduce the appearance of the real world. While light has particle and wave characteristics, simulations often ignore wave effects, following the principles of geometric optics: light travels along straight lines in homogeneous media, it can be absorbed or reflected, and it refracts when experiencing a change in velocity. Many effects of light transport can be convincingly reproduced using this model (Fig. 2.1\), and it also serves as the theoretical framework for this thesis. In this section, we will provide a brief overview of relevant concepts and refer readers to Pharr et al. [2016] for a more comprehensive treatment.

To simulate light transport, the virtual environment, or scene, first requires a mathematical and digital representation (Section 2.1.1\). Only then can the physical equations of light transport (Section 2.1.2\) be solved computationally (Section 2.1.3\).

## 2.1.1 Representation of a Virtual Scene

In a common abstraction of the real world, a virtual environment, or scene, is assumed to consist of surfaces, i.e., infinitely thin shells that represent the boundaries of objects, which are made of materials that determine how a surface interacts with incident light. Emitters radiate light while sensors register it to produce an image or any other form of measurement. This thesis is most concerned with surface light transport, so we do not discuss participating media (e.g., fog or fire). The aforementioned concepts are briefly introduced in the following.

#### Emitters

Emitters generate the light that is simulated. Generally, objects in the scene or parts of them can be characterized as emitters, in which case they are referred to as area emitters.

# 2.2 Real-Time Rendering Techniques

Accurate simulation of light transport (Section 2.1\) has traditionally been used in "offline" settings, such as movie production, due to the high computational cost. Applications targeting interactive or real-time performance, for example, visualization or video games, instead rely on simplifying assumptions and approximations of light transport. In these contexts, it is often sufficient to use techniques that produce images that appear convincing to human observers, even if they are not physically accurate, yet are orders of magnitude more efficient. Many of these approaches are shaped by the historical development of real-time rendering, which has traditionally relied on rasterization of triangle meshes. Instead of tracing rays for individual pixels of the camera, rasterization takes a primitive-centric perspective and efficiently determines pixel coverage by projecting triangles to the image plane of the camera, a process that is highly optimized on modern GPUs. As a result, numerous lighting and visibility approximations have been designed specifically to integrate well with rasterization-based pipelines.

## 2.2.1 Point Light Sources

Physically accurate emitters are typically modeled as area light sources that emit radiance from a finite area in the scene, which belongs to the surface of some object. While this representation closely follows the nature of real light sources, evaluating their contribution generally requires integrating incident radiance over the emitter area. Many real-time systems approximate illumination using simplified models that assume light is emitted from a single point in space1 . This greatly simplifies computations and is easy to integrate into rasterization-based rendering.

## 2.2.2 Shadow Mapping

#### Automatic Differentiation

In many cases, one would much rather use automatic differentiation (AD). This not only avoids error-prone and tedious derivations but also greatly improved accessibility in recent years: several modern libraries (e.g., PyTorch [Paszke et al., 2019], JAX [Bradbury et al., 2018], or Dr.Jit [Jakob et al., 2022a]) expose automatic differentiation tools for machine learning and other optimization tasks. Notice the distinction between automatic and symbolic differentiation: symbolic methods apply differentiation rules to the mathematical expression of a function to obtain an expression for the derivative, while automatic differentiation evaluates derivatives of a function implemented as a computer program. The details of automatic differentiation have been covered by many textbooks (e.g., by Griewank and Walther [2008]), so we only give a high-level overview here.

#### Finite Differences

Finite differences (FD) are a common numerical method for approximating directional derivatives. The general idea is to estimate derivatives by evaluating the function with slightly perturbed inputs. For example, the derivative in direction v can be approximated using central differences:

Jf (x) v ≈ f(x + ℎv) − f(x − ℎv) 2ℎ (2.34)

where ℎ is a small step size that controls the amount of perturbation. While finite differences are easy to implement because they do not require knowledge about the internals of a function, they come with several limitations. For one, the choice of step size ℎ requires an intricate balance: if ℎ is too small, floating point rounding errors dominate the result due to catastrophic cancellation, but if ℎ is too large, the approximation is inaccurate. Moreover, approximating gradients of scalar-valued functions with many inputs is prohibitively expensive because the derivative for each input would require at least one2 function evaluation. In this thesis, finite differences will primarily serve as a tool for validating derivatives computed using automatic or manual differentiation.

### 2.4.1 Applications

The applications of differentiable rendering are too numerous to cover exhaustively. Instead, each of the main chapters will address the relevant background separately, so we will only briefly discuss the general context here. Broadly speaking, the applications in this thesis fall somewhere between computer graphics and 3D computer vision, many of which have the same general structure: given a reference image Iref (or measurement), find a representation of the environment that gives rise to this observation.

With some rendering algorithm ��(��) at hand, this task may be viewed as inverse rendering, i.e., inverting the rendering process to obtain parameters for a virtual scene as a representation of the environment. However, �� is generally not invertible, so instead, this is cast as a minimization problem of the form

�� ★ = arg min �� ∈R�� ∥��(��) − Iref∥ 2 2 . (2.39)

Notice that this is a special case of Eq. \(2.36\), and the objective could really be any function that measures the difference between the rendering output and the reference (including neural network-based objectives [Johnson et al., 2016]). This approach to the above task is also known as analysis-by-synthesis.

It naturally generalizes to multiple references, and a recurring application in that regard will be multi-view 3D reconstruction: given multiple photographs of a scene (typically with known or estimated camera parameters), recover the scene geometry. With known camera parameters, one can render images from the reference perspectives, compare the rendered images to the reference photographs, and update a representation of the scene geometry (e.g., a triangle mesh) using gradient descent (also see Chapter 7\). In general, it is common for some of the scene parameters to be fixed (e.g., known or estimated beforehand), and only a subset is optimized.

Apart from inverse applications, we will occasionally cover others that are not based on matching a given reference. For example, in Chapter 6, scene geometry is optimized to improve certain acoustic characteristics of a room (in that context, ��(��) generates an acoustic measurement from the scene).

Unfortunately, in most of the applications, we cannot hope to find a global minimum because the objectives are generally non-convex. Even worse, many of the local minima found using differentiable rendering are undesirable: for example, if the task is to reconstruct the surface of an object from real-world images, the renderings might agree with the reference images, but the recovered surface is physically implausible. The application-side drives a significant part of the research on better-suited objectives and robust optimization techniques (e.g., for the evolution of geometry [Luan et al., 2021; Nicolet et al., 2021])

Differentiable rendering comes in many flavors, from general differentiable renderers to application-specific systems. For the most part of the thesis, we will be concerned with general, application-independent techniques, but we will always relate back to applications by evaluating our methods and algorithms in respective settings.

## 2.4.2 Discontinuities and Visibility

There is often a distinction made between physically-based differentiable renderers based on ray tracing with Monte Carlo methods (Section 2.1\) [Nimier-David et al., 2019; Jakob et al., 2022b; Yan et al., 2020; Li et al., 2018b], and rasterization-based differentiable renderers with approximations (Section 2.2\) [Ravi et al., 2020; Fuji Tsang et al., 2022; Laine et al., 2020; de La Gorce et al., 2008]. Regardless of whether one is interested in one or the other, a major challenge shared by both are parameter-dependent discontinuities 4 .

Discontinuities arise because several computations within a renderer involve discrete decisions. For example, a triangle either covers a point in the image or it does not, and a point in the scene is either visible to a point light source, or it is not. Special care is required when differentiating with respect to parameters that affect these decisions. Unfortunately, this case is common: many interesting applications involve optimization of geometry, for example, the vertices of a triangle mesh for 3D reconstruction – and the vertex positions directly affect occlusion relations in the scene.

### 2.4.3 Path Replay Backpropagation

When the task is to differentiate physically-based renderers (Section 2.1\), a natural choice would be automatic differentiation (Section 2.3\) because even if parameter-dependent discontinuities are present, automatic differentiation can be used to compute the interior term of the derivative (Section 2.4.2\). However, tape-based automatic differentiation requires immense amounts of memory to store the computational graph of complex light transport simulations. In practice, this limits its applicability to simple scenes, short light paths, and low sample counts.

Nimier-David et al. [2020] and Stam [2020] showed that the derivatives of light transport simulations can be computed using another form of transport simulation. Building on this idea, Vicini et al. [2021] proposed path replay backpropagation (PRB), a (reversemode) differentiation algorithm that computes derivatives using constant memory and linear time in path length. This approach makes it possible to differentiate complex simulations without storing large computational graphs spanning the full computation.

Path replay backpropagation is an important component in Chapter 6, where we extend the same algorithm to time-resolved energy transport. Our derivation is based on a recursive formulation introduced in the following. Besides supporting the derivation in Chapter 6, we believe it also provides another helpful perspective on the algorithm.

Many inverse rendering applications are concerned with recovering scene information solely from images captured in the real world. If this is to be solved using differentiable rendering, one seeks a renderer that can reproduce the various effects of physical light transport, as the goal is to match real-world images with renderings of a virtual scene. A natural choice are physically-based differentiable renderers [Li et al., 2018a; Nimier-David et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020; Jakob et al., 2022b] as they adhere to the equations of light transport (Section 2.1\). However, as in forward rendering, the accuracy of these sophisticated systems comes at the price of efficiency.

The full picture shows that inverse rendering and other applications require differentiable renderers that strike a good balance between accuracy and efficiency, for example, when the renderer is only one of many components in a larger machine learning pipeline [Gao et al., 2022; Lattas et al., 2022]. Unsurprisingly, many techniques from real-time rendering have found their way into differentiable rendering, as highly realistic images can be efficiently generated by approximating light transport.

Despite the advances of modern real-time systems, and the potential to leverage additional information in optimizations, surprisingly little effort has been spent on approximating global effects like shadows or indirect illumination in differentiable rendering: The predominant approach for scenes with triangle meshes is differentiable rasterization with purely local shading models [Loper and Black, 2014; Chen et al., 2019, 2021]. While this might lead to acceptable outcomes in some applications, ignoring effects like shadows in inverse settings can easily degrade solutions [Munkberg et al., 2022] and is generally only valid in very restrictive setups (e.g., with co-located camera and light [Zhang et al., 2022; Luan et al., 2021]).

In this chapter, we show how shadows can be efficiently generated in differentiable rendering of triangle meshes (Fig. 3.1\). Our central observation is that one of the oldest and most widely used approximations, shadow maps [Williams, 1978] (Section 2.2.2\), can be adapted to work in differentiable rendering frameworks. Our main idea is similar to Primary Discontinuities Secondary Discontinuities the original one: exploit efficient rasterization from the light's point of view. For differentiable shadows, this means that existing differentiable rasterizers handle discontinuities of primary visibility along primitive borders; we use this machinery to handle discontinuities of secondary visibility along shadow borders. In contrast to similar work [Lyu et al., 2021; Hasselgren et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2025], our approach (1) computes shadows directly from the triangle mesh instead of proxy geometry, (2) differentiates visibility with respect to movement of geometry, and (3) supports high-frequency shadows from point light sources. We demonstrate at several inverse graphics problems that differentiable shadow mapping is orders of magnitude faster than differentiable ray tracing, with similar accuracy – while differentiable rasterization without shadows often fails to converge.

## 3.1.2 An Inspiration from Real-Time Rendering

Smoothing (hard) shadow boundaries is commonly encountered in traditional real-time rendering as a way to (1) increase the realism, since most real-world shadows have some amount of softness, and (2) remedy aliasing at shadow boundaries resulting from the discretization of the shadow map (see [Hasenfratz et al., 2003] for an overview). While our goal is the same, the motivation is different: in differentiable rendering, smoothing is not optional, but a necessity for non-zero spatial and geometric shadow derivatives. #### Visibility Derivatives in Concurrent Work

In concurrent work, Wang et al. [2023a] ("SunStage") and Karunratanakul et al. [2023] ("HARP") propose differentiable shadow mapping for facial capture and hand reconstruction, respectively. Both constructions use a sigmoid-based visibility test, giving as a reason that this ensures "smooth gradients", and Karunratanakul et al. [2023] explicitly note that the shadow mapping implementation is differentiable with respect to geometry. However, as we have discussed in Section 3.1.1, a sigmoid test amounts to radially smoothing visibility: the resulting visibility remains discontinuous across shadow boundaries, and the derivatives with respect to scene geometry do not reflect the variation of the shadow outline. ### 3.3.2 Face Reconstruction from Profile Shadows

While the variation within certain classes of objects is governed by pose, such as in articulated objects, deformations are best described by morphable models. A prominent example for morphable models is face geometry [Egger et al., 2020]. Our idea is to reconstruct face geometry from one (or more) shadows of the face. We base the experiment on the publicly available morphable model assembled by Li et al. [2020] (other models would work as well).

The first experiment uses synthetic reference shadows that are generated by rendering a known state of the morphable model. This allows comparing the optimization result not just based on images but also geometry (see Fig. 3.10\). We find that using more shadows only slightly increases the runtime (the time is not linear in the number of light sources, because we have one base pass and multiple shadow passes) but significantly improves the accuracy (see Table 3.2\).
