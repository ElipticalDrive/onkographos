Abstract: Double-Blind Fourier Holography (DBFH) is a phase-retrieval method that has been
shown numerically to address wavefront-sensing challenges for future segmented-aperture space
telescopes, including the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO). DBFH uses a set of carefully
selected pupil-plane amplitude masks and their corresponding focal-plane intensity measurements
to construct an algebraically linear inverse problem, enabling computationally efficient and
stable reconstruction of the complex pupil field. Here, we present the first experimental
demonstration of DBFH using a spatial light modulator and an HWO EAC5-like segmentedaperture geometry. We reconstruct challenging pseudo-random wavefronts and compare DBFH
with the established Focus-Diverse Phase Retrieval (FDPR) method. The two methods achieve
comparable reconstruction precision, while exhibiting distinct strengths and limitations. We
further demonstrate DBFH wavefront sensing in the presence of a binary source, a scenario that
is not naturally accommodated by conventional FDPR. These results provide experimental proof
of concept and establish technology readiness level 3 for DBFH as a wavefront-sensing method
for space telescopes with segmented primary mirrors.
1.
Introduction
Space telescopes provide unparalleled opportunities to address some of the deepest unanswered
questions in astronomy: How did the Universe begin? What drives cosmic expansion? Are there
habitable planets beyond our own, and does life exist elsewhere in the Universe? We are fortunate
to be witnessing a remarkable era in astronomy, in which successive generations of flagship space
telescopes continue to deliver new capabilities and insights, bringing us closer to answering these
fundamental questions. The recent launch of the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope marks the
latest chapter in this progression, while attention is already turning toward the next generation of
flagship observatories. Looking forward, the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO), planned
for launch in the 2040s, will have as one of its primary goals the direct imaging of potentially
habitable, Earth-like exoplanets and the characterization of their atmospheres for possible signs
of life [1]. Building on strong heritage from previous missions like James Webb (JWST) and
Roman space telescopes, current HWO concepts combine a large, segmented primary mirror
with high-performance coronagraphy instrument to suppress light from a host star and enable
detection of extremely faint nearby planets. Achieving the required coronagraphic contrast places
stringent demands on the stability of the telescope optical system, including picometer-scale
wavefront stability over relevant observation timescales [2, 3]. Meeting this requirement will
require both highly stable structural and thermal designs and sensitive wavefront-sensing and
control techniques.
Focal-plane wavefront sensing is particularly attractive for space telescopes because it measures
aberrations through the same optical path used to form the science image, thereby reducing
reliance on separate metrology systems. Focus-Diverse Phase Retrieval (FDPR) [4, 5] is one of
the most established approaches to focal-plane phase retrieval. In FDPR, intensity measurements
acquired with known amounts of defocus, are used to estimate the pupil-plane wavefront. FDPR
techniques have played an important role in space-telescope wavefront sensing and are currently
used for fine phasing of JWST, where nanometer-scale residual wavefront errors have been
demonstrated [6, 7]. FDPR is typically formulated as a nonlinear inverse problem and solved
through iterative numerical optimization over an aberration model, often expressed using a
truncated Zernike basis, and can incur substantial computational cost.
Double-Blind Fourier Holography (DBFH) provides an alternative approach to phase retrieval.
DBFH was first introduced by Raz et al. for one-dimensional signals [8, 9] and subsequently
extended to lensless imaging [10]. Variants of the method have since been applied to problems
including coherent diffraction imaging of nanoparticles using X-ray free-electron lasers [11] and
attosecond pulse characterization [12]. More recently, DBFH was proposed as a focal-plane
wavefront-sensing method for segmented space telescopes and was numerically investigated
under conditions relevant to HWO [13].
In the wavefront-sensing implementation of DBFH, the pupil is divided into complementary
regions using known amplitude masks, and focal-plane intensity measurements are acquired
for different pupil configurations. Linear combinations of these measurements provide the
interference cross term between the fields originating from the complementary sub-apertures. A
compact-support constraint encodes the known finite spatial extent of each sub-aperture, requiring
its associated pupil-plane field to vanish everywhere outside that region. The measured cross
term, together with the known compact-support constraints imposed by the pupil masks, forms an
overdetermined system of linear equations whose solution yields the complex focal-plane fields.
A straightforward inverse Fourier transform then recovers the complex pupil field and hence the
wavefront. DBFH retrieves the wavefront phase in situ from in-focus intensity measurements
using a non-iterative linear reconstruction, without requiring the deliberate defocus used in FDPR
or restricting the wavefront to a low-dimensional aberration basis.
In this work, we present the first experimental validation of DBFH on an HWO-like segmented
aperture. Using an optical testbed based on a programmable spatial light modulator (SLM),
we generate a 37-segment aperture based on the HWO EAC5 geometry and impose controlled,
nontrivial wavefront errors across the pupil. We reconstruct these wavefronts using DBFH and
compare the results directly with FDPR under similar experimental conditions. We further
investigate the dependence of DBFH reconstruction error on signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and
compare the measurements with numerical predictions. Finally, we demonstrate DBFH wavefront
sensing in a field containing two mutually incoherent point sources. Because DBFH uses in-focus
intensity measurements, the two PSFs can remain spatially separated on the detector and their
wavefront estimates can be independently recovered and combined.
This manuscript is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical formulation,
assumptions, and implementation of DBFH for wavefront sensing. Section 3 describes the
experimental setup and data-acquisition procedure. Section 4 presents the experimental DBFH
reconstructions, comparison with FDPR, and dependence on signal-to-noise ratio. Section 5
demonstrates wavefront sensing using a binary source, and Section 6 summarizes the results and
discusses their significance.

3.
Experimental Setup and Data Acquisition
We used the experimental setup shown in Fig. 1 to validate the DBFH methodology. The
system is based on a spatial light modulator (SLM), which facilitates the implementation of both
DBFH and FDPR on a pupil representative of the HWO EAC-5 architecture. This configuration
enables a direct comparison of the two wavefront sensing techniques under identical experimental
conditions.
The optical bench consists of a source module, a programmable phase modulator, and an
imaging arm. The source module comprises a 635 nm laser diode (Thorlabs CPS635R) coupled
to a 10 µm pinhole, which acts as a spatial filter. The diverging beam propagates 500 mm
before being collimated by an achromatic doublet (Thorlabs AC254-500A). The pupil shape and
wavefront aberrations are introduced using a Hamamatsu X15213-01 liquid crystal-on-silicon
(LCOS) spatial light modulator (SLM). The device provides programmable phase modulation
through a two-dimensional array of addressable electrodes that locally modify the refractive index
of a liquid-crystal layer. The SLM comprises a 1024 × 1272 pixel array with a 12.5 µm pixel
pitch, corresponding to a maximum active aperture of approximately 12.8 mm. The collimated
beam is incident on the SLM at 5◦ to the surface normal. A linear phase ramp is superimposed
on all phase patterns, steering the first diffracted order by 1.45◦ . This separates the modulated
beam from unwanted light arising from the finite pixel fill factor, specular reflection, and higher
diffraction orders, allowing these components to be rejected with a spatial filter. Following the
SLM, the beam is relayed by a second achromatic doublet (Thorlabs AC254-500A) and directed
to the imaging arm via a 50:50 plate beamsplitter (Thorlabs BSW10R). The reflected beam is
focused onto the detector by a plano-convex lens (Thorlabs LA1708). Images are recorded using
a Point Grey Flea3 CMOS camera with a 1280 × 1024 pixel array and a pixel pitch of 5.3 µm. For
a 12 mm pupil diameter, the image sampling is approximately 4.08 pixels per 𝜆/𝐷. Pixel binning
is subsequently applied to achieve near-Nyquist sampling. The optical bench also incorporates
a truth-sensing arm, which was not directly used for the measurements reported in this work.
Its purpose is to independently verify the wavefront generated by the SLM. The arm employs a
200 × 200 pyramid wavefront sensor, enabling high-resolution wavefront characterization.

Compared with FDPR, DBFH has several qualitative shortcomings that must be clarified.
First, due to the finite full-well capacity, the in-focus inputs of DBFH require a much higher
number of frames to achieve a similar count of photo-electrons, compared to FDPR’s defocused
images. This means that readout noise and dark-current which grow linearly with the frame count
need to be suppressed by high full-well capacity and low-noise sensors (as can be expected for a
flagship space telescope). The higher frame count may also extend the duration of acquisition
session, perhaps conflicting with source stability. This was in fact a limiting case in our bench
experiment, which prevented us from reaching higher number of useful co-adds. Second, as
DBFH is essentially an interferometric method, its algebra combines intensities from separate
measurements. Exposure normalization, source brightness, detector gain, background subtraction,
and pupil-mask throughput must therefore remain consistent.
DBFH offers several practical and computational advantages over FDPR. Its reconstruction is
formulated as a linear inverse problem, enabling efficient and deterministic recovery without
iterative nonlinear optimization. It directly solves for the complex pupil field, and does not
assume a uniform pupil illumination or a low-dimensional aberration model. In applications
where introducing controllable phase elements into the beam is undesirable, DBFH provides an
in-situ alternative based solely on amplitude masking.
5.
Binary Source
To better constrain WFS algorithms, it is a common practice to use a single bright and unresolved
point source, such as an isolated reference bright star. This often requires a space telescope to
point away from the science object of interest and to an ideal calibration scene. The appearance
of multiple mutually incoherent PSFs in the FOV, or even incoherent background from dust will
limit the ability of any interferometric method. Since DBFH consumes only in-focus images, the
sensor area occupied by the PSF of a single source is significantly smaller compared to defocused
images. While working with focused PSFs is disadvantageous in terms of finite full-well capacity
and accumulation of read-out noise, it does allow accommodating multiple non-overlapping
PSFs in the FOV. Here, we demonstrate DBFH wavefront sensing using an equal-brightness
binary source.
Individual stars in a binary system are mutually incoherent, so their focal-plane intensities add
directly. A binary scene can therefore be synthesized by shifting and summing independently
acquired single-source images with matched exposure and photon statistics. This procedure
correctly reproduces Poisson noise, since the sum of independent Poisson variables is itself
Poisson-distributed. It does, however, overestimate the readout-noise contribution and is therefore
justified only when Poisson noise dominates. Using offset copies of the same input images
amounts to adding only redundant information (does not contribute new information about the
wavefront) and is not expected to improve the phase RMSE. We chose to sum a 100 co-adds

by a cyclic averaging of the estimated wavefronts. Alternative approaches include registering
and summing the centered PSFs before applying DBFH, or constructing a joint linear system
containing measurements from both sources. These latter two methods require accurate knowledge
of the relative source displacement, whereas independently reconstructed wavefronts can be
detrended before averaging.

6.
Conclusions
In this work, we experimentally validated DBFH as a focal-plane wavefront-sensing method for
future segmented space telescopes, with particular relevance to the Habitable Worlds Observatory.
Using a programmable SLM, we applied and reconstructed nontrivial segmented-aperture
wavefronts and compared the DBFH estimates with both the known SLM phase and FDPR
reconstructions, a widely used benchmark for focal-plane wavefront sensing. For the test phases
considered, DBFH achieved reconstruction precision comparable to FDPR.
We further evaluated DBFH over a range of exposure levels and found that the measured phase
RMSE followed the numerical prediction in the Poisson-noise-dominated regime. Compared
with FDPR on equivalent inputs, DBFH achieves comparable WFE precision. This comparison
highlighted the distinct trade-offs between the two methods, including in-focus versus defocused
measurements and amplitude masking versus phase modulation. Because DBFH is formulated
as an algebraically linear inverse problem, it offers computational advantages over iterative
phase-retrieval approaches. Finally, we demonstrated that the smaller area occupied by the
in-focus PSF on the detector allows spatially separated stars within the same field of view to be
processed independently. The additional statistically independent measurement from a companion
source improves the reconstruction precision. Together, these results establish an experimental
proof of concept for DBFH and support its advancement to TRL 3 as a wavefront-sensing method
for segmented space telescopes.
