# Abstract

Stochastic order-independent transparency enables efficient and elegant rendering of primitive-based radiance fields like 3D Gaussian Splatting models, but remains impractical due to the inherent visible noise in the output. We propose a principled approach to minimize high-frequency noise, addressing its sources at the representation and image synthe- sis level. During stochastic rendering, our history-based spatial resampling scheme drastically accelerates image convergence, while temporal importance resampling ensures coherence under camera movement. During training, a color regularizer implicitly reduces the variance along view rays in the 3DGS models. With these properties, our optimized, Vulkan-based renderer effectively mitigates output noise at low and high sample counts, achieving a substantial 13 dB PSNR increase in quality over previous stochastic methods at 1 sample per pixel and quickly converging to sorted 3DGS with an average L1 error of less than 10−4 .

# 1. Introduction

The rapid evolution of novel view synthesis has been largely driven by the advent of radiance fields and, more recently, primitive-based representations in particular. Methods such as 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [11] have demonstrated exceptional visual fidelity and real-time rendering capabilities by modeling complex scenes as unstructured collections of volumetric primitives. Traditionally, fast rendering of these representations relies on alpha-blending sorted primitives using a tile-based rasterizer.

Stochastic order-independent transparency (OIT) presents an elegant, efficient alternative for rendering primitive-based radiance fields. By probabilistically sampling visible primitives rather than sorting them, stochastic OIT dramatically simplifies and accelerates the rendering pipeline. However, despite its structural advantages, this approach has remained largely impractical for novel view

synthesis. The stochastic nature of the visibility evaluation inherently introduces high-frequency noise in the resulting images (cf . Fig. 1\). Across novel view trajectories, this uncorrelated noise manifests itself as severe temporal flickering and "boiling" artifacts [28], to which human visual perception is sensitive. Mitigating this issue and suppressing these artifacts below visible thresholds requires accumulating hundreds of samples per pixel, which directly negates the performance advantages of sorting-free rendering [13].

In this work, we propose solutions that make stochastic order-independent transparency practical for high-fidelity rendering by aggressively tackling noise at both ends of the pipeline: training and inference. All of our contributions are implemented in a high-performance Vulkan-based renderer:

- Color Variance Regularization: A training objective that penalizes high-variance primitive configurations, implicitly conditioning the 3D model to produce less noise during stochastic sampling.
- History-Based Spatial Resampling: An efficient, inference-time resampling technique to massively accelerate convergence of image synthesis with minimal bias.
- Temporal Consistency: We demonstrate that under mild assumptions, spatio-temporal resampling can be implemented as a recursive exponential moving average filter to efficiently mitigate temporal artifacts. Stratifying samples using per-primitive Spatio-Temporal Blue Noise further improves convergence.

We demonstrate that our combined approach successfully renders clean, high-fidelity images at low sample counts, achieving higher frame rates than vanilla 3DGS with nearindistinguishable quality.

# 2. Related Work

3D Gaussian Splatting and the Sorting Bottleneck. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has emerged as a highly efficient representation for real-time novel view synthesis [11]. Traditionally, it relies on a tile-based rasterizer that strictly sorts semi-transparent primitives based on a per-primitive depth to perform deterministic alpha-compositing. However, this global sorting operation is computationally expensive, scales poorly with increasing scene complexity, especially on low-end hardware, and introduces temporal discontinuities known as "popping" artifacts when slight camera movements alter the primitive order. Several methods have attempted to address these limitations. StopThe-Pop [22] eliminates popping through computationally intensive per-pixel sorting based on the maximal Gaussian response. Alternatively, exact volumetric intermixing can be achieved by ray tracing of constant density ellipsoids [15] or by reconstructing the transmittance function from analytic moments [18]. Other approaches approximate alphablending via proxy-guided weighted blending [14] or orderindependent accumulation with a K-buffer [6] to bypass full radix sorting. Weighted sum rendering [3, 8] completely eliminates sorting during training and inference at the cost of reduced image quality.

Order-Independent and Stochastic Transparency. To fundamentally circumvent the sorting bottleneck, recent literature has adapted stochastic order-independent transparency (OIT). Originally introduced to 3D graphics by Enderton et al. [4], stochastic transparency replaces sorted alpha-blending with a randomized Monte Carlo estimator. StochasticSplats [13] successfully integrates this estimator into 3DGS by keeping each fragment as an opaque sample with probability equal to its alpha value, completely bypassing depth-sorting while accurately blending overlapping Gaussians. Similarly, Gaussian Point Splatting [24] treats Gaussians as discrete point clouds, sampling pixelsized opaque points independently and splatting them via 64-bit atomics to achieve massive parallelism. While these approaches eliminate the need for sorting, they inherently introduce severe high-frequency noise into the rendered images and must rely on expensive sample accumulation or temporal anti-aliasing (TAA) [10, 36]. Stochastic approaches can also be applied to ray tracing 3D Gaussians for relightable reconstruction [35] and semi-transparency [31]. Stochastic Gaussian Splatting [27] moves the stochasticity into the Gaussian parameters themselves in order to estimate reconstruction uncertainty.

Denoising and Resampling. Early real-time denoising relied heavily on analytical spatial and temporal filters, most notably Spatiotemporal Variance-Guided Filtering (SVGF) [28] to accumulate temporal history while preserving edges. As deep learning matured, denoising networks such as kernel prediction networks [5, 17, 32], neural bilateral grids [16], and Deep Learning Super Sampling [19, 20] gained popularity. Spatiotemporal Reservoir Resampling (ReSTIR) [34] and descendant methods [7, 12, 21, 37, 38] directly improve the quality of the Monte Carlo integration in a principled manner before the denoising step. By leveraging temporal history and spatial neighbors, the impact of each individual sample is amplified.

# 3. Preliminaries

In this section, we detail how the stochastic paradigm diverges from traditional 3DGS rendering.

# 3.1. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)

3D Gaussian Splatting represents complex scenes as an unstructured collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians [11]. Each Gaussian primitive i is defined by a set of optimizable parameters {µi , Σi , σi , ci}, which encode its mean position



Figure 2. Illustration of deterministic and stochastic rendering of 3D Gaussians.

(µi), 3D covariance matrix (Σi), opacity (σi), and viewdependent color (ci), typically modeled using spherical harmonics.

To synthesize a novel view, these 3D Gaussians are "splatted" to a screen-space 2D Gaussian distribution G2D and depth zi using an affine approximation for projection [40]. We denote by G(x, y) the set of Gaussians intersecting the pixel (x, y), by αi(x, y) = σi · G2D i (x, y) the alpha-blending factor of Gaussian i, and by Cx,y the final pixel radiance, which is classically computed by evaluating the volume rendering equation through deterministic alphacompositing [11]:


$$C_{x,y} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{G}(x,y)} c_i \alpha_i(x,y) \prod_{k \in \mathcal{G}(x,y) \mid z_k < z_i} (1 - \alpha_k(x,y)), \quad (1)$$

where zk < zi denotes all intersecting primitives located in front of primitive i along the ray. In standard 3DGS, primitives are sorted by zi prior to blending, which simplifies the transmittance Ti(x, y) to Q k<i(1 − αk(x, y)).

While this sorted alpha-blending ensures a stable and noise-free image, the global sorting operation imposes significant computational overhead and memory bandwidth bottlenecks, limiting scalability, especially on low-power devices, and producing temporal popping artifacts [22].

## 3.2. Stochastic and Order-Independent 3DGS

StochasticSplats [13] fundamentally alters the rendering pipeline by adapting stochastic order-independent transparency (OIT) [4]. Rather than sorting primitives globally, stochastic transparency replaces deterministic alphablending with an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator. The two approaches are contrasted in Fig. 2.

The core idea is to probabilistically assign fully opaque samples to a pixel according to the fragments' alphablending factors, evaluating the color without any sorting. The radiance Cx,y is estimated by randomly and independently sampling N Gaussian indices r 1 , . . . , rN from a probability mass function Px,y:

| $\hat{C}_{x,y} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N c_{rj}, \quad \text{with } r^j \sim P_{x,y}, \quad (2)$ |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

$$P_{x,y}(i) = \begin{cases} \alpha_i(x,y) T_i(x,y) & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{G}(x,y), \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}$$

i.e., each Gaussian is sampled with exactly its blending weight from Eq. \(1\). A single draw r ∼ Px,y thus yields an unbiased one-sample estimate cr of the pixel color, Er∼Px,y [cr] = P i Px,y(i) ci = Cx,y.

In practical hardware implementations, r is sampled by leveraging standard, unsorted rasterization with a Z-buffer. For each fragment, a uniform random number ui ∼ U(0, 1) is sampled; the fragment is kept as fully opaque if ui < αi(x, y), and discarded otherwise. The Z-buffer then retains the nearest kept fragment, whose index r therefore follows Px,y.

Gaussian Point Splatting produces samples from the same underlying distribution Px,y using a massively parallelized point-sampling technique. As such, its output is near-identical to StochasticSplats except for small biases introduced by approximations. Further details can be found in Sec. 11.

Temporal Reuse. Because 1-sample stochastic splats produce noisy raw frames, temporal accumulation is essential for stability. Existing methods address this in two distinct ways. StochasticSplats maintains an unweighted Cumulative Moving Average (CMA),

$$\hat{C}_{x,y}^t = \left(1 - \frac{1}{N_{x,y}^t}\right) \hat{C}_{x',y'}^{t-1} + \frac{1}{N_{x,y}^t} c_{r^t},$$

where r t ∼ P t x,y is the sample drawn in frame t, (x ′ , y′ ) is the reprojection of (x, y) into the previous frame, and Nt x,y = N t−1 x′ ,y′ + 1 counts the accumulated samples, resetting the sample counter Nt x,y ← 1 whenever relative depth error exceeds a threshold τ .

Gaussian Point Splatting instead uses a fixed-weight exponential moving average (EMA) with a bounded temporal window, borrowing AABB color clamping from standard temporal anti-aliasing (TAA) [10, 26] to suppress ghosting by restricting reprojected history to within γ standard deviations of the local color mean.

# 4. Method

The noise produced by stochastic 3DGS methods [13, 24] is a direct result of the variance in the Monte Carlo estimator



Figure 3. By default, 3DGS optimization blends disparate colors to match the ground truth. With λvar > 0, the blended colors align to reduce color variance.

Cˆ x,y, which determines its root-mean-square error:

$$\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{C}_{x,y} - C_{x,y}\|^2]} = \sqrt{\text{Var}[\hat{C}_{x,y}]} = \frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}[c_r]}}{\sqrt{N}}. \quad (3)$$

To reduce this error, either the color variance Var[cr] at each pixel must be decreased or the sample count N increased.

## 4.1. Color Variance Regularization

Previous stochastic methods for 3DGS [13, 24] reduce the error by increasing N through expensive supersampling or artifact-prone temporal accumulation, while ignoring the variance of the sampled colors Var[cr], which directly represents the power of the noise produced by the renderer:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Var}_{r \sim P_{x,y}}[c_r] &= \mathbb{E}_{r \sim P_{x,y}}[\|c_r - \mathbb{E}_{r \sim P_{x,y}}[c_r]\|^2] & (4) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{r \sim P_{x,y}}[\|c_r - C_{x,y}\|^2] & (5) \end{aligned}$$

$$= \sum_{i \in \mathcal{G}(x,y)} P_{x,y}(i) \cdot \|c_i - C_{x,y}\|^2. \quad (6)$$

Our key insight is that standard, deterministic 3DGS training produces significantly higher color variance Var[cr] than necessary to represent the scene at high fidelity. To minimize Var[cr] during optimization, we add a color variance term Lvar normalized by the resolution H × W to the loss function:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}} = \frac{1}{H \cdot W} \sum_{x=1}^W \sum_{y=1}^H \sum_{i \in \mathcal{G}(x,y)} P_{x,y}(i) \cdot \|c_i - C_{x,y}\|^2. \quad (7)$$

Intuitively, this encourages all Gaussians that splat onto the same pixel to share the same color, instead of matching the ground truth by blending disparate colors. Figure 3 illustrates how Lvar aligns colors splatted onto a pixel. Note that Px,y(i) is already computed for each Gaussian during deterministic 3DGS rasterization and is commonly referred to as the Gaussian weight.

Our complete loss function is:

$$\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{var}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{var}} + (1 - \lambda) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\ell_1} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}}, \quad (8)$$

with hyperparameters λ, λvar and photometric losses Lℓ1 ,LD-SSIM. The resulting models significantly reduce variance in the estimator and image noise with no modifications to the rendering procedure. For efficiency, the expectation Cx,y can be replaced with the ground truth color C gt x,y during training, assuming Cx,y ≈ C gt x,y for a converged model.

While this training objective has already found application in the context of sparse-view reconstruction [9] and mesh extraction [23, 39], its significant applications to stochastic rendering have thus far been unexplored.

## 4.2. Spatial Resampling

By exploiting the fact that color distributions are similar across neighboring pixels (e.g., Px,y(i) ≈ Px+1,y(i)), the information gain from each individual sample can be amplified by mixing the colors of neighboring samples. Naive reuse causes blurring artifacts, which can be mitigated by computing resampling weights that minimize the introduced bias.

Neighbor Reuse. Let N (x, y) define the set of neighboring pixels around (x, y) from which samples will be reused. The final rendered color for a single sample is then given by:


$$\hat{C}_{x,y}^{\text{spatial}} = \frac{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}(r_n) \cdot c_{r_n}}{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}(r_n)}, \quad (9)$$

where rn ∼ Pn is the sample chosen at pixel n and wn→x,y is the spatial resampling weight. Here and in all following sums over N (x, y), the pixel's own sample r ∼ Px,y is included with weight wx,y→x,y(r) = 1; |N (x, y)| counts only the neighbors. The normalization factor is imposed by self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS). Equation \(9\) can also be interpreted as a convolution of the 1-sample image with a dynamic kernel defined by N and wn→x,y. If wn→x,y is chosen simply as a Gaussian falloff according to pixel distance, this operation is equivalent to a Gaussian blur, which successfully reduces noise but converges to a biased, blurry result.

To achieve noise reduction while minimizing bias, the expected value of the weighted samples from pixel n must match the expected value of the target distribution at pixel (x, y):

$$\mathbb{E}_{r_n \sim P_n}[w_{n \rightarrow x, y}(r_n) \cdot c_{r_n}] = \mathbb{E}_{r \sim P_{x, y}}[c_r]. \quad (10)$$

By expanding both expectations into their respective sums over the domain G, we obtain:

$$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{G}(n)} P_n(i) \cdot w_{n \rightarrow x, y}(i) \cdot c_i = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{G}(x, y)} P_{x, y}(i) \cdot c_i. \quad (11)$$

For this equality to hold for any arbitrary signal ci , the corresponding terms inside the summations must be equal.

Equating the coefficients yields:


$$P_n(i) \cdot w_{n \rightarrow x,y}(i) = P_{x,y}(i) . \quad (12)$$

Solving this directly provides the unbiased resample weights: wn→x,y(rn) = Px,y(rn) Pn(rn) . If wn→x,y(rn) > 1, then rn is more likely to be sampled at pixel (x, y) than at pixel n, and vice versa. The assumption G(x, y) = G(n) used in Eq. \(12\) is justified in Sec. 10.

Approximating wn→x,y. While Px,y(rn) Pn(rn) represents the ideal, unbiased weights, they are difficult to compute in practice due to their dependence on occlusion from closer primitives:


$$\frac{P_{x,y}(r_n)}{P_n(r_n)} = \frac{\alpha_{r_n}(x,y)}{\alpha_{r_n}(n)} \prod_{\substack{k \in \mathcal{G}(x,y) \\ \wedge z_k < z_{r_n}}} \frac{1 - \alpha_k(x,y)}{1 - \alpha_k(n)}. \quad (13)$$

The first term accounts for the change in 2D Gaussian opacity between the two pixels and is evaluated directly by sampling the 2D Gaussian G2D rn on neighboring pixels. However, the second term o k n→x,y, representing the change in occlusion of rn between pixels, presumes knowledge of the set of occluding Gaussians {k ∈ G(x, y) | zk < zrn }, which would require an expensive global sorting step or second sampling pass.

Our key insight is that the expected number of occluding Gaussians on each pixel is very small (cf. Fig. 16\) if the scene is trained with Lvar. As such, this product is dominated by a small subset of Gaussians that have a high probability of being sampled because they represent the foreground. In turn, it is highly likely that these important occluding Gaussians were sampled recently on the same pixel. In essence, the samples we draw from each pixel act as importance samples for Gaussians that are likely to be dominant occluders. We can therefore approximate the change in occlusion by taking the product not over all Gaussians splatted onto the pixel, but only over those that have been sampled on the same pixel in the last K iterations.

To this end, for each pixel (x, y), we store the unique values of o k x,y→n for all neighbors n (computed in an extra rendering pass), the index k, and the corresponding depth zk across the K previous samples. Using the set Kn stored at the source pixel n, we estimate wn→x,y as:

$$w_{n \rightarrow x, y}(i) = \frac{P_{x,y}(i)}{P_n(i)} \approx \frac{\alpha_i(x,y)}{\alpha_i(n)} \prod_{\substack{k \in \mathcal{K}_n \\ \wedge z_k < z_i}} o_{n \rightarrow x, y}^k. \quad (14)$$

Due to the unbounded support of the Gaussian kernel, as K → ∞, {k ∈ G(x, y) | zk < zi} ⊆ Kn holds with probability approaching one and the equality becomes exact. Figure 4a sketches the computation of this reuse weight.

Note that the stored buffer of past samples is deduplicated: we do not weight the importance of a Gaussian by its sample frequency (which would introduce substantial noise), but rather take the condition that it was sampled at least once as a signal to include its occlusion factor.

## 4.3. Spatio-Temporal Resampling

Color distributions are coherent not only across spatial neighborhoods, but also temporally along motion trajectories. While evaluating full spatio-temporal resampling weights across multiple frames is intractable for real-time rendering, we show that under mild assumptions, full spatio-temporal resampling reduces to an efficient, closedform recursive filter.



for varying λvar ∈ [0, 5].

Temporal Importance Resampling. The spatial reuse weights wn→x,y extend to the temporal domain by evaluating the target-to-proposal PDF ratio:

$$w_{n \rightarrow x, y}^{\tilde{t} \rightarrow t}(r_n^{\tilde{t}}) = \frac{P_{x,y}^t(r_n^{\tilde{t}})}{P_n^{\tilde{t}}(r_n^{\tilde{t}})}, \quad (15)$$

where r t˜ n is the Gaussian sample drawn at pixel n on prior frame t˜ ∈ T . The full spatio-temporal SNIS estimator is then:

$$\hat{C}_{x,y}^t = \frac{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} \sum_{\tilde{t} \in \mathcal{T}} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}^{\tilde{t} \rightarrow t} (r_n^{\tilde{t}}) \cdot c_{r_n^{\tilde{t}}}}{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} \sum_{\tilde{t} \in \mathcal{T}} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}^{\tilde{t} \rightarrow t} (r_n^{\tilde{t}})}. \quad (16)$$

Assuming sample relevance decays by a per-frame factor δ t x,y ∈ [0, 1] shared by all samples in the history, the spatiotemporal weights factorize as:


$$w_{n \rightarrow x, y}^{\vec{t} \rightarrow t}(r_n^{\vec{t}}) = w_{n \rightarrow x, y}^{\vec{t}}(r_n^{\vec{t}}) \prod_{u=\vec{t}+1}^t \delta_{x, y}^u, \quad (17)$$

This formulation is illustrated in Fig. 4b.

Theorem 1. Under the assumption of a per-frame temporal discount shared by all history samples (cf. Eq. \(17\)), the spatio-temporal reuse estimator

$$\hat{C}_{x,y}^t = \frac{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} \sum_{\tilde{t} \in \mathcal{T}} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}^{\tilde{t} \rightarrow t} (r_n^{\tilde{t}}) \cdot c_{r,\tilde{t}}}{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} \sum_{\tilde{t} \in \mathcal{T}} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}^{\tilde{t} \rightarrow t} (r_n^{\tilde{t}})} \quad (18)$$

can be expressed as an exponential moving average filter:

$$\hat{C}_{x,y}^t = \frac{\delta_{x,y}^t \cdot D_{x,y}^{t-1}}{D_{x,y}^t} \cdot \hat{C}_{x,y}^{t-1} + \frac{\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}(x,y)} w_{n \rightarrow x,y}^t (r_n^t)}{D_{x,y}^t} \cdot \hat{C}_{x,y}^{t,spatial}. \quad (19)$$

A detailed proof is provided in Sec. 14.1. Theorem 1 reduces spatio-temporal SNIS to a two-state recursion per pixel: the running estimate Cˆt x,y and the accumulated weight mass Dt x,y = δ t x,yDt−1 x,y + P n w t n→x,y(r t n ).

At steady state (Dt x,y ≈ Dt−1 x,y ), the recursion in Theorem 1 is equivalent to an EMA filter with history weight δ t x,y ∈ (0, 1), commonly used in TAA [10, 36]. Both prior temporal schemes are special cases: without spatial reuse (P n w t n→x,y = 1), δ t x,y = 1 yields the CMA of StochasticSplats (Sec. 3.2\), whose depth reset corresponds to δ t x,y = 0, and a constant δ t x,y < 1 yields the fixed-weight EMA of Gaussian Point Splatting. We use the steady-state form, so only the running estimate needs to be stored:


$$\hat{C}_{x,y} = \delta_{x,y} \hat{C}_{x,y} + (1 - \delta_{x,y}) \hat{C}_{x,y}^{\text{spat}}, \quad (20)$$

with a fixed nominal history weight δ t x,y = ¯δ that is reduced only by soft history rejection (Eq. \(21\)).

Moving Camera. Theorem 1 is stated for a fixed pixel. With a moving camera, we apply Eq. \(20\) along the motion trajectory and read the history at the reprojection (x ′ , y′ ) of (x, y) into the previous frame, i.e., Cˆt−1 x,y becomes Cˆt−1 x′ ,y′ (Sec. 8\). This is exact only if the reprojection is exact and the surface remains visible; otherwise, the fetched history is an approximation, and the history weight δ t x,y controls how much of it is kept. The occlusion buffer of Sec. 4.2 is kept in screen space and not reprojected, so under motion it may contain occluders of nearby surface points.

Soft History Rejection. Prior temporal approaches for stochastic splatting present a clear trade-off. Pure depthtested CMA maintains an unbounded sample history that causes persistent ghosting whenever depth differences are small, while its hard binary resets trigger visible popping on foliage and fine geometry. In contrast, standard TAA color clamping directly clamps Cˆt−1 x′ ,y′ to a local neighborhood bounding box, which clips valid Monte Carlo variance tails, introduces bias, and interferes with spatial reuse distributions [29, 30, 36].

To avoid both issues, we replace color clipping and binary depth resets with history weight modulation [30, 36]. We leave the historical color Cˆt−1 x′ ,y′ unclipped, and instead change the history weight δ t x,y based on the L∞ distance m of the reprojected history Cˆt−1 x′ ,y′ from the mean of the current samples crq in a 3 × 3 neighborhood (in YCoCg space), normalized by velocity-adaptive confidence halfextents (Sec. 8\):


$$\delta_{x,y}^t = \bar{\delta} \cdot \min \left( 1, \frac{1}{m} \right), \quad (21)$$

where ¯δ is the fixed nominal history weight. For outlier samples (m > 1), this smoothly shifts history retention toward the new spatial estimate (δ t x,y → 0) without distorting valid color distributions. Implementation details and our velocity-adaptive confidence bounds are described in Sec. 8.

Per-Primitive Spatio-Temporal Blue Noise. Instead of independent white noise, we sample the fragment visibility thresholds ui (Sec. 3.2\) from a precomputed Spatio-Temporal Blue Noise (STBN) volume [33]. To decorrelate overlapping fragments at every pixel, each primitive applies an R2 low-discrepancy spatial offset [25], combined with a golden-ratio Cranley-Patterson rotation [2] to prevent temporal repetition. This per-primitive stratification preserves blue-noise spectral properties and estimator unbiasedness, resulting in improved quantitative metrics compared to white noise and visibly smoother images (cf . Fig. 24\).

# 5. Evaluation

For the evaluation, we use the StochasticSplats method of drawing samples and render each sample individually (i.e., no batching) for minimal latency.

Variance-Quality Trade-off. While Lvar effectively mitigates noise, it also dilutes the training signal of the ℓ1 and SSIM photometric losses, resulting in a compromise between noise and visual fidelity. To examine this trade-off, we train the scenes from the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset [1] for 15k iterations using original 3DGS training and then another 15k iterations from the same checkpoint with varying values of λvar. The results in Fig. 5a show that color variance as defined in Eq. \(4\) can be decreased significantly with minimal impact on converged image quality. On the other hand, there appears to be a hard lower limit around Var ≈ 0.01 on color variance for each scene, beyond which severe quality loss occurs.

The quality of stochastic rendering for these models with varying sample count is reported in Fig. 5b. The results show that with increasing sample count, the optimal value of λvar decreases. The reason for this is that error is dominated by noise at low sample counts and by bias at high sample counts. A small amount of variance regularization leads to almost universal gains, significantly outperforming baseline trained models across all image metrics for at least the first 200 samples. For the following evaluations, we use models trained with λvar = 1 as a compromise between fast and correct convergence.

Spatial Reuse. To validate the effectiveness of the spatial reuse component, we evaluate quality metrics over all test poses of the Mip-NeRF 360 scenes on models trained with λvar = 1 and rendered stochastically with up to 100 samples. As baseline methods, we use unmodified StochasticSplats outputs and various fixed 3 × 3 normalized blur kernels and compare against our kernel with dynamic reuse weights for the four direct pixel neighbors with varying buffer sizes K. Figure 6 shows the resulting difference in quality metrics compared to deterministic α-blending. For low sample counts, spatial reuse and blurring achieve an immediate and significant quality improvement over StochasticSplats. However, the convergence of the naive blur kernels quickly plateaus at a blurry and biased result, while our method continues to increase in quality, outperforming all baseline methods for 100 samples even with a buffer size of only K = 4.

Performance. While the quality gains for integrating spatial reuse are significant, they also come at a small cost in performance: Fig. 7 compares the performance overhead of spatial reuse against basic stochastic and deterministic blending, implemented in the same optimized Vulkan renderer for fair comparison and run on an RTX 5090. The results show that even for relatively large buffer sizes (K < 14), our method achieves higher frame rates than deterministic α-blending. The overhead of spatial reuse increases slowly until K = 14, at which point the shared memory is filled up.

In order to evaluate the performance-quality trade-off for spatial sample reuse, we introduce the excess quality factor: An excess quality factor of Q means that enabling spatial resampling achieves the same quality as accumulating Q · N samples in the time it takes to draw N samples (with M < N samples due to the resampling overhead). As such, any Q > 1 is a net-gain in quality at identical convergence time. Figure 8 shows the excess quality factor achieved for various values of buffer size K and sample count. # 6. Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated that the severe highfrequency noise inherently produced by stochastic orderindependent transparency (OIT) rendering methods can be effectively suppressed during both training and inference time with minimal bias. By introducing color variance regularization during optimization and a history-based spatial resampling scheme during inference, our method achieves clean, high-fidelity rendering from just a single primitive sample per pixel.

Different Primitives. Our method is not limited strictly to 3D Gaussian Splatting. The principles of our approach could be used on any semi-transparent rasterized primitive that typically requires computationally expensive depthsorting, extending the benefits of efficient stochastic rendering to a broader range of representations.

## 6.1. Limitations

Despite the massive acceleration in convergence and reduction in noise, our approach introduces a few notable tradeoffs:

- Quality Metric Trade-off: Our color variance loss (Lvar) effectively mitigates noise but also dilutes the training signal of the L1 and SSIM photometric losses, resulting in a slight decrease in peak quality metrics for fully converged models.
- Overhead of Reuse: While spatial resampling avoids the exhaustive global sorting of primitives, populating and reading from the K-buffer to evaluate neighboring samples takes additional memory and computational time during inference.
- Loss of Strict Unbiasedness: Standard stochastic transparency is unbiased. However, to maintain high performance, our spatial reuse implementation approximates the exact transmittance using only a limited set of K previous samples. Because of this approximation, the spatial resampling is no longer strictly unbiased, exchanging a small degree of mathematical correctness for significantly accelerated convergence.

# 6.2. Future Work

Future research should explore dynamic or adaptive sizing of both the spatial neighborhood N (x, y) and the K-buffer capacity. By intelligently allocating K-buffer size based on local scene complexity or depth variance, it may be possible to reduce the memory overhead and time required for sample reuse without sacrificing noise reduction. Additionally, investigating adaptive weighting for the variance loss could help minimize the trade-off in photometric quality metrics, allowing for optimal visual fidelity across varying primitive distributions. A fast approximation of the full spatiotemporal SNIS estimator could further improve the temporal quality as well.

Algorithm 1 details the sampling procedure introduced in StochasticSplats to sample r ∼ Px,y.

#### Algorithm 1 StochasticSplats Sampling [13]

1: r ← ∅, zr ← ∞ ▷ ∅: background, c∅ = cbackground 2: for each Gaussian i ∈ G(x, y) in unsorted order do 3: ui ∼ U(0, 1) 4: if ui < αi(x, y) and zi < zr then 5: r ← i 6: zr ← zi 7: end if 8: end for 9: return r, zr

If no fragment survives, Algorithm 1 returns the background ( Q r = ∅). This happens with probability k∈G(x,y) (1 − αk(x, y)), the mass that is missing from Px,y; the color variance in Eq. \(4\) and Lvar assume full coverage, where this mass vanishes.

Algorithm 2 gives a full overview of how one frame is rendered with spatial resampling. It is written in scatter form: each pixel (x, y) weights its own sample r for every neighbor n with wx,y→n(r), using its own buffer Kx,y, and accumulates the result at n. Up to the order of summation, this equals the gather form of Eq. \(9\), in which the sample of each neighbor n is weighted by wn→x,y(rn) using Kn, plus the pixel's own sample with weight one.

# 8. Temporal Recurrence and Soft Rejection Implementation

This section provides implementation details for the temporal recurrence pass of Sec. 4.3, executed immediately after spatial resampling (Sec. 4.2\) in the same compute pipeline.

Reprojection and History Fetch. For each pixel p = (x, y) with depth zp of its sampled fragment, we reconstruct its view-space position and reproject it into the previous frame's screen space using the prior view-projection matrix:

$$p' = (x', y') = \pi_{t-1}(\pi_t^{-1}(p, z_p)). \quad (22)$$

The screen-space motion magnitude is v = ∥p − p ′∥2. If p ′ lies outside the viewport or within two pixels of its boundary, temporal accumulation is bypassed and the spatial estimate Cˆt,spatial p is output directly. Otherwise, we retrieve the historical radiance Cˆt−1 p′ = SAMPLE(It−1, p′ ) using Catmull-Rom bicubic sampling to minimize interpolation blur.

Neighborhood Moments in Color Space. To estimate the local radiance distribution, we evaluate the first and second moments of the current samples crq over a 3×3 window N3(p) centered at p:

$$\bar{C}_p = \frac{1}{9} \sum_{q \in \mathcal{N}_3(p)} c_{r_q}, \quad (23)$$

$$s_p = \sqrt{\max\left(0, \frac{1}{9} \sum_{q \in \mathcal{N}_3(p)} c_{r_q}^2 - \bar{C}_p^2\right)}. \quad (24)$$

Moments are computed channel-wise in YCoCg space to decouple luminance variance from chromaticity. The confidence half-extents are:

$$e = \max(\gamma s_p, e_{\min}), \quad (25)$$

where emin = (0.01, 0.005, 0.005) prevents degenerate zero-volume bounds on flat regions.

Velocity-Adaptive Confidence Scaling (γ). Stationary regions require wider confidence bounds to preserve accumulated history and avoid premature rejection, whereas moving regions require tighter bounds to flush stale history across disocclusions. Following established real-time practice [36], we dynamically scale γ with screen velocity v:

$$\gamma = \text{lerp} \left( \gamma_{\text{static}}, \gamma_{\text{mot}}, \min \left( 1, \frac{v}{v_0} \right) \right), \quad (26)$$

where lerp(a, b, θ) = (1 − θ) a + θ b, γstatic = 4.0 covers 99.99% of a normal distribution on stationary surfaces, γmot = 1.0 tightens the rejection envelope under movement, and v0 = 4.0 px is the velocity threshold.

Statistical Distance and Soft History Blending. Given the discrepancy vector d = Cˆt−1 p′ − C¯ p, we compute its L∞ distance from the neighborhood mean, normalized by the confidence half-extents so that m = 1 on the boundary of the confidence box:

$$m = \max \left( \left\lfloor \frac{d_Y}{e_Y} \right\rfloor, \left\lfloor \frac{d_C}{e_C} \right\rfloor, \left\lfloor \frac{d_G}{e_G} \right\rfloor \right). \quad (27)$$

When m > 1, the historical sample is an outlier. Rather than projecting Cˆt−1 p′ onto the box boundary along d/m (which biases color ratios), we compute a continuous retention factor ρ = 1/m ∈ (0, 1) and attenuate the history weight as in Eq. \(21\):

$$\delta_p^t = \begin{cases} \bar{\sigma} & \text{if } m \leq 1, \\ \rho \bar{\sigma} & \text{if } m > 1, \end{cases} \quad (28)$$

with nominal history weight ¯δ = 0.70, i.e., the new spatial estimate receives a nominal weight of 1 − ¯δ = 0.30. The final temporal radiance is obtained by blending the unclipped historical color directly, which is Eq. \(20\) with the history read at p ′ (Sec. 4.3\):

$$\hat{C}_p^t = \delta_p^t \hat{C}_{p'}^{t-1} + (1 - \delta_p^t) \hat{C}_p^{t,\text{spatial}}. \quad (29)$$

#### 9.3. Pareto Analysis

The parameter K in our spatial reuse component represents specific trade-offs between fast and exact convergence. Each combination of sample count and K yields a specific image quality in a certain amount of sampling time. To evaluate the trade-off, we find the Pareto-optimal combinations of K and sample count, i.e., those combinations that produce a specific image quality (measured across different metrics) in the shortest rendering time. Figure 21 compares the quality/time trade-off against standard StochasticSplats (our Vulkan reimplementation) with and without a blur kernel. StochasticSplats has the fastest rendering time, but is immediately overtaken in quality by the noise reduction enabled by using a Gaussian blur. Initially, the blur kernel wins out due to its low overhead, but all quality metrics agree that after 5 samples its quality is superseded by spatial reuse with a small buffer. At this point, the more accurate convergence of the spatial reuse weights overcomes the small performance overhead to achieve optimal quality. With more time to render, increasingly large buffer sizes become optimal, which provide better convergence at higher performance cost, until the unbiasedness of pure stochastic rendering catches up in quality, which happens for L1 at 136 samples and for LPIPS at 33. In PSNR and SSIM, a large reuse buffer remains dominant for 256+ samples. In summary, the optimal value for buffer size scales with the available amount of time for the image to converge. For practical rendering, a buffer size of K ∈ [4, 8] is a reasonable choice, representing the optimal configuration between ∼ 5–50 samples.

The heatmaps in Fig. 20 show a more detailed view of the relationship between buffer size, sample count, and quality: as the number of samples increases, the isolines become increasingly curved, meaning that small sample buffers require rapidly increasing amounts of samples to match the quality of larger buffer sizes. For instance, with |N | = 4, 256 samples are required with K = 1 to achieve the same PSNR quality as just 31 samples with K = 16. We also find that except for very low sample counts, a smaller kernel size achieves better quality overall, validating our design decision to choose |N | = 4.

# 10. Bias

In this section, we examine the sources of bias that stochastic sampling and spatial resampling introduce in comparison to standard 3DGS.

Self-Normalized Importance Sampling. Using P n∈N(x,y) wn→x,y(rn) as a normalization factor introduces bias for finite samples, because the denominator is a random variable. This bias decreases as O(1/N) with the number of samples, so the estimator is consistent. As the evaluation demonstrates, the achieved reduction in variance far outweighs the impact of the introduced bias.

Tile-Based Splatting. In Eq. \(12\), we make the assumption that G(x, y) = G(n) for all n ∈ N (x, y). In tile-based rasterization, this holds as long as n is in the same 16 × 16 tile as (x, y). Otherwise, it is possible for a Gaussian to be sampled on a border pixel and then propagated to a neigh-

# 11. Gaussian Point Splatting Evaluation

The stochastic rendering approach in Gaussian Point Splatting [24] replaces traditional alpha-blended rasterization with a massively parallel stochastic point-sampling technique. Instead of sorting and sequentially evaluating semitransparent Gaussians, the renderer samples opaque, pixelsized points and splats them directly into a 64-bit atomic depth buffer. Because threads sample points independently, multiple points from the same Gaussian can collide on the same pixel, which would normally reduce the effective opacity. To counteract this, the method treats the sampling as a Poisson point process and dynamically sets the number of points to sample, ng, using a Poisson distribution with a rate parameter Λ. The authors derive a corrected probability density function, f(q) = − ΛA ln(1 − α(q)), which concentrates more points toward the center of the Gaussian. This mathematical correction ensures that the probability of splatting exactly zero points into a subpixel of area A is precisely 1−α(q), perfectly preserving the original Gaussian's intended opacity while achieving high scalability.

The rendering procedure of Gaussian Point Splat-

ting produces stochastic OIT samples almost identical to StochasticSplats and achieves very similar base quality. As such, all of our components can be used with GPS as a "sampling backbone", while also benefiting from its scalability. Figure 23 shows a comparison between Stochastic-Splats, GPS, and our spatial reuse applied to GPS samples. Just as with StochasticSplats, sample reuse leads to significantly faster convergence.

# 13. Gradients of the Color Variance Loss

The color variance loss Lvar represents the average weighted RGB variance for each pixel:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}} = \frac{1}{H \cdot W} \sum_{x=1}^W \sum_{y=1}^H \sum_{i \in \mathcal{G}(x,y)} P_{x,y}(i) \cdot \|c_i - C_{x,y}^{\text{gt}}\|^2. \quad (30)$$

P We derive the gradients of the per-pixel term Lx,y = i∈G(x,y) Px,y(i) ∥ci −C gt x,y∥ 2 ; the gradient of Lvar is their average over all pixels. The gradients with respect to viewdependent color can be computed using the chain rule (similar to the derivation in [23]):

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{x,y}}{\partial c_i} = 2P_{x,y}(i)(c_i - C_{x,y}^{\text{gt}}). \quad (31)$$

We can compute the gradient with regard to αi(x, y) as follows, with Gaussians indexed in depth order as in deterministic training:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{x,y}}{\partial P_{x,y}(i)} = \|c_i - C_{x,y}^{\text{gt}}\|^2, \quad (32)$$

$$\frac{\partial P_{x,y}(i)}{\partial \alpha_i(x,y)} = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j(x,y)) = T_i(x,y), \quad (33)$$

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{x,y}}{\partial \alpha_i(x,y)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{x,y}}{\partial P_{x,y}(i)} \cdot \frac{\partial P_{x,y}(i)}{\partial \alpha_i(x,y)} \quad (34)$$

$$= \|C_i - C_{x,y}^{\text{gt}}\|^2 T_i(x,y) \cdot$$

This gradient does not account for the effect of αi(x, y) on subsequent Gaussians j > i, which include (1 − αi(x, y)) in their weight Px,y(j). Because αi(x, y) < 1 by definition, ∂Px,y(j) ∂αi(x,y) = − Px,y(j) 1−αi(x,y) holds, giving, with j ranging over all Gaussians in G(x, y) behind i:

$$\begin{aligned} & \sum_{j>i} \|c_j - C_{x,y}^{\text{gt}}\|^2 \left( -\frac{P_{x,y}(j)}{1 - \alpha_i(x,y)} \right) \\ &= -\frac{1}{1 - \alpha_i(x,y)} \sum_{j>i} P_{x,y}(j) \|c_j - C_{x,y}^{\text{gt}}\|^2. \end{aligned} \quad (35)$$
