1. Introduction
The rapid evolution of novel view synthesis has been largely
driven by the advent of radiance fields and, more recently,
primitive-based representations in particular. Methods such
as 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [11] have demonstrated
exceptional visual fidelity and real-time rendering capabilities by modeling complex scenes as unstructured collections of volumetric primitives. Traditionally, fast rendering of these representations relies on alpha-blending sorted
primitives using a tile-based rasterizer.
Stochastic order-independent transparency (OIT)
presents an elegant, efficient alternative for rendering
primitive-based radiance fields. By probabilistically sampling visible primitives rather than sorting them, stochastic
OIT dramatically simplifies and accelerates the rendering
pipeline. However, despite its structural advantages, this
approach has remained largely impractical for novel view

Order-Independent and Stochastic Transparency. To
fundamentally circumvent the sorting bottleneck, recent
literature has adapted stochastic order-independent transparency (OIT). Originally introduced to 3D graphics by
Enderton et al. [4], stochastic transparency replaces sorted
alpha-blending with a randomized Monte Carlo estimator.
StochasticSplats [13] successfully integrates this estimator
into 3DGS by keeping each fragment as an opaque sample with probability equal to its alpha value, completely
bypassing depth-sorting while accurately blending overlapping Gaussians. Similarly, Gaussian Point Splatting [24]
treats Gaussians as discrete point clouds, sampling pixelsized opaque points independently and splatting them via
64-bit atomics to achieve massive parallelism. While these
approaches eliminate the need for sorting, they inherently
introduce severe high-frequency noise into the rendered
images and must rely on expensive sample accumulation
or temporal anti-aliasing (TAA) [10, 36]. Stochastic approaches can also be applied to ray tracing 3D Gaussians for
relightable reconstruction [35] and semi-transparency [31].
Stochastic Gaussian Splatting [27] moves the stochasticity
into the Gaussian parameters themselves in order to estimate reconstruction uncertainty.
Denoising and Resampling. Early real-time denoising
relied heavily on analytical spatial and temporal filters,
most notably Spatiotemporal Variance-Guided Filtering
(SVGF) [28] to accumulate temporal history while preserving edges. As deep learning matured, denoising networks
such as kernel prediction networks [5, 17, 32], neural bilateral grids [16], and Deep Learning Super Sampling [19, 20]
gained popularity. Spatiotemporal Reservoir Resampling
(ReSTIR) [34] and descendant methods [7, 12, 21, 37, 38]
directly improve the quality of the Monte Carlo integration
in a principled manner before the denoising step. By leveraging temporal history and spatial neighbors, the impact of
each individual sample is amplified.
2. Related Work
3D Gaussian Splatting and the Sorting Bottleneck. 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has emerged as a highly efficient representation for real-time novel view synthesis [11].
Traditionally, it relies on a tile-based rasterizer that strictly
sorts semi-transparent primitives based on a per-primitive
depth to perform deterministic alpha-compositing. However, this global sorting operation is computationally expensive, scales poorly with increasing scene complexity,
especially on low-end hardware, and introduces temporal
discontinuities known as “popping” artifacts when slight
camera movements alter the primitive order. Several methods have attempted to address these limitations. StopThePop [22] eliminates popping through computationally intensive per-pixel sorting based on the maximal Gaussian response. Alternatively, exact volumetric intermixing can be
achieved by ray tracing of constant density ellipsoids [15]

3.2. Stochastic and Order-Independent 3DGS
StochasticSplats [13] fundamentally alters the rendering
pipeline by adapting stochastic order-independent transparency (OIT) [4]. Rather than sorting primitives globally, stochastic transparency replaces deterministic alphablending with an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator. The two
approaches are contrasted in Fig. 2.
The core idea is to probabilistically assign fully opaque
samples to a pixel according to the fragments’ alphablending factors, evaluating the color without any sorting.

Per-Primitive Spatio-Temporal Blue Noise. Instead of
independent white noise, we sample the fragment visibility thresholds ui (Sec. 3.2) from a precomputed SpatioTemporal Blue Noise (STBN) volume [33]. To decorrelate overlapping fragments at every pixel, each primitive
applies an R2 low-discrepancy spatial offset [25], combined with a golden-ratio Cranley-Patterson rotation [2] to
prevent temporal repetition. This per-primitive stratification preserves blue-noise spectral properties and estimator unbiasedness, resulting in improved quantitative metrics compared to white noise and visibly smoother images
(cf . Fig. 24).
Performance. While the quality gains for integrating spatial reuse are significant, they also come at a small cost
in performance: Fig. 7 compares the performance overhead of spatial reuse against basic stochastic and deterministic blending, implemented in the same optimized Vulkan
renderer for fair comparison and run on an RTX 5090.
The results show that even for relatively large buffer sizes
(K < 14), our method achieves higher frame rates than
deterministic α-blending. The overhead of spatial reuse increases slowly until K = 14, at which point the shared
memory is filled up.
In order to evaluate the performance-quality trade-off for
spatial sample reuse, we introduce the excess quality factor: An excess quality factor of Q means that enabling spatial resampling achieves the same quality as accumulating
Q · N samples in the time it takes to draw N samples (with
M < N samples due to the resampling overhead). As such,
any Q > 1 is a net-gain in quality at identical convergence
time. Figure 8 shows the excess quality factor achieved
for various values of buffer size K and sample count. The
largest benefit is observed for low sample counts, with up

6. Discussion
In this work, we have demonstrated that the severe highfrequency noise inherently produced by stochastic orderindependent transparency (OIT) rendering methods can be
effectively suppressed during both training and inference
time with minimal bias. By introducing color variance regularization during optimization and a history-based spatial
resampling scheme during inference, our method achieves
clean, high-fidelity rendering from just a single primitive
sample per pixel.
Different Primitives. Our method is not limited strictly
to 3D Gaussian Splatting. The principles of our approach
could be used on any semi-transparent rasterized primitive
that typically requires computationally expensive depthsorting, extending the benefits of efficient stochastic rendering to a broader range of representations.

6.1. Limitations
Acknowledgements
Despite the massive acceleration in convergence and reduction in noise, our approach introduces a few notable tradeoffs:
We thank Alex Evans for his valuable help in the early
stages of the project.
• Quality Metric Trade-off: Our color variance loss (Lvar )
effectively mitigates noise but also dilutes the training
signal of the L1 and SSIM photometric losses, resulting
in a slight decrease in peak quality metrics for fully converged models.
• Overhead of Reuse: While spatial resampling avoids
the exhaustive global sorting of primitives, populating
and reading from the K-buffer to evaluate neighboring
samples takes additional memory and computational time
during inference.
• Loss of Strict Unbiasedness: Standard stochastic transparency is unbiased. However, to maintain high performance, our spatial reuse implementation approximates
the exact transmittance using only a limited set of K previous samples. Because of this approximation, the spatial
resampling is no longer strictly unbiased, exchanging a
small degree of mathematical correctness for significantly
accelerated convergence.
6.2. Future Work
Future research should explore dynamic or adaptive sizing
of both the spatial neighborhood N (x, y) and the K-buffer
capacity. By intelligently allocating K-buffer size based on
local scene complexity or depth variance, it may be possible
to reduce the memory overhead and time required for sample reuse without sacrificing noise reduction. Additionally,
investigating adaptive weighting for the variance loss could
help minimize the trade-off in photometric quality metrics,
allowing for optimal visual fidelity across varying primitive distributions. A fast approximation of the full spatiotemporal SNIS estimator could further improve the temporal quality as well.

9.3. Pareto Analysis
The parameter K in our spatial reuse component represents specific trade-offs between fast and exact convergence. Each combination of sample count and K yields a
specific image quality in a certain amount of sampling time.
To evaluate the trade-off, we find the Pareto-optimal combinations of K and sample count, i.e., those combinations that
produce a specific image quality (measured across different
metrics) in the shortest rendering time. Figure 21 compares
the quality/time trade-off against standard StochasticSplats
(our Vulkan reimplementation) with and without a blur kernel. StochasticSplats has the fastest rendering time, but is
immediately overtaken in quality by the noise reduction enabled by using a Gaussian blur. Initially, the blur kernel
wins out due to its low overhead, but all quality metrics
agree that after 5 samples its quality is superseded by spatial reuse with a small buffer. At this point, the more accurate convergence of the spatial reuse weights overcomes
the small performance overhead to achieve optimal quality.
With more time to render, increasingly large buffer sizes become optimal, which provide better convergence at higher
performance cost, until the unbiasedness of pure stochastic rendering catches up in quality, which happens for L1 at
136 samples and for LPIPS at 33. In PSNR and SSIM, a
large reuse buffer remains dominant for 256+ samples. In
summary, the optimal value for buffer size scales with the
available amount of time for the image to converge. For
practical rendering, a buffer size of K ∈ [4, 8] is a reasonable choice, representing the optimal configuration between
∼ 5–50 samples.
The heatmaps in Fig. 20 show a more detailed view of the
relationship between buffer size, sample count, and quality:
as the number of samples increases, the isolines become increasingly curved, meaning that small sample buffers require rapidly increasing amounts of samples to match the
quality of larger buffer sizes. For instance, with |N | = 4,
256 samples are required with K = 1 to achieve the same
PSNR quality as just 31 samples with K = 16. We also
find that except for very low sample counts, a smaller kernel size achieves better quality overall, validating our design
decision to choose |N | = 4.

11. Gaussian Point Splatting Evaluation
The stochastic rendering approach in Gaussian Point Splatting [24] replaces traditional alpha-blended rasterization
with a massively parallel stochastic point-sampling technique. Instead of sorting and sequentially evaluating semitransparent Gaussians, the renderer samples opaque, pixelsized points and splats them directly into a 64-bit atomic
depth buffer. Because threads sample points independently,
multiple points from the same Gaussian can collide on
the same pixel, which would normally reduce the effective
opacity. To counteract this, the method treats the sampling
as a Poisson point process and dynamically sets the number
of points to sample, ng , using a Poisson distribution with
a rate parameter Λ. The authors derive a corrected proba1
bility density function, f (q) = − ΛA
ln(1 − α(q)), which
concentrates more points toward the center of the Gaussian.
This mathematical correction ensures that the probability of
splatting exactly zero points into a subpixel of area A is precisely 1 − α(q), perfectly preserving the original Gaussian’s
intended opacity while achieving high scalability.
