Abstract
In the contemporary world, there is an increasing demand for image and video data. The
storage and transmission of such voluminous amounts of data has proven to be a significant
challenge. Given the likelihood of continued growth in this trend, the development of
new methods for compressing image and video data has become imperative. Recent
advancements in neural network-based compression techniques offer potential solutions
to this problem. It has been demonstrated that neural networks possess the capacity
to learn nonlinear transformations, complex probability distributions, and various other
components that are essential for image and video compression. The utilization of neural
networks facilitates the direct acquisition of image properties from image and video data,
thereby enabling the development of methods for their integration within the encoder.
In this thesis, we propose a novel neural network-based image compression framework
and a set of variable bitrate solutions designed for this framework. In addition, the
aforementioned solutions have been integrated into the JPEG AI standard platform. The
superiority of the technology has been validated through subjective visual experiments.
A preliminary investigation into the human visual system reveals that humans exhibit
heightened sensitivity to luminance relative to chrominance. The distribution of spatial
pixels in images also reveals a high correlation between chrominance and luminance distributions. It is hypothesized that a conditional color separation compression framework
capable of processing luminance and chrominance in parallel will emerge as a result of
the observations outlined above. This framework employs a more complex neural network for luminance, ensuring quality, while assigning a less complex neural network to
chrominance to reduce computational complexity. In order to maintain the reconstruction
quality of chrominance, we employ luminance as auxiliary information for chrominance.
This framework has been demonstrated to achieve a 42% reduction in complexity, with a
concomitant loss of only 4% in objective quality. The framework’s adoption by JPEG AI
is indicative of its noteworthy balance between complexity and performance.
Subsequent to the aforementioned steps, we implemented a three-dimensional quality
map of equivalent dimensions to the latent tensor within the proposed framework. This
approach was undertaken to enable the model to operate with variable rates. The map
can be derived by expanding two maps with different functionalities. The channel-wise
quality map provides overall rate control, and the spatial quality map enhances the region

1 Introduction
As one of humanity’s fundamental means of understanding the world, images are being captured, transmitted, and stored across devices and networks at an unparalleled
scale. The advent of smartphone photography, social media sharing, cloud-based photo
albums, mapping and satellite imagery, multimodal sensing for autonomous driving, and
specialized applications in medicine and remote sensing are all contingent on image data
to catalyze innovation. Given the constraints of relatively limited computational power,
bandwidth, and storage resources, the development of efficient image compression technology is imperative for ensuring user experience, reducing system costs, and enhancing
service coverage and sustainability.
As demonstrated in the overviews of Ma et al. [19] and Birman et al. [20], the increasing demand for media has resulted in the rapid development of both traditional and
learned image codecs. In 2017, Ballé et al. published a paper [21] on an end-to-end
trained image compression network. This work was seminal in stimulating widespread
research in neural-network-based image and video compression. In Ballé’s work [21], a
typical learned image compression framework is introduced. This framework is capable
of mapping images to latent variables via learnable analysis transforms. It can then produce bitstreams through quantization and entropy modeling. The synthesis transform
decodes quantized latent variables to reconstruct images. Subsequently, based on [21],
Ballé proposed a learned image codec with a hyperprior network in [22], and Minnen et
al. proposed a learned image codec with an autoregressive context model in [23]. The
aforementioned studies provide a fundamental framework for learned image codecs. Following the contribution of the researchers, several of the learned image codecs exhibited
superior performance in comparison to conventional codecs such as Joint Photographic
Experts Group (JPEG) [24], Joint Photographic Experts Group 2000 (JPEG 2000) [25],
Better Portable Graphics (BPG) [26] (an intra coding of High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC) [27]), and Versatile Video Coding (VVC) [28] (intra coding of state-of-the-art
video coding standards).
The publications of the learned image codec can be categorized as follows:

Notwithstanding the continuous evolution of learned image compression, several persistent challenges remain:
1. Firstly, image compression codecs typically employ a uniform process logic for disparate color components. However, the human visual system exhibits distinct sensitivities to each color component. Consequently, the application of a uniform process
logic/complexity to components for which the human visual system exhibits minimal sensitivity may result in redundancy. The issue of redundancy in the process
between different color components remains unaddressed.
2. Secondly, the learned image codec continues to demonstrate challenges in providing a
continuous rate. Initially, in the context of image coding, researchers are required to
train multiple models to provide discrete rate points, which vary for each test image.
This challenge stems from the difficulty in achieving alignment with the target rate
when rate matching is necessary. Subsequently, the architecture of the codecs that
possess continuous variable rate functionality is too complex for industrial areas to
3
implement in their products. Furthermore, the variable rate performance of the
learned image codec is not as effective as that of the classic codecs.
The present thesis is chiefly concerned with the issue previously mentioned. With
regard to the redundancy between color components, a novel learned image compression
framework was proposed in [2]. The proposed framework is based on the conditional
autoencoder structure. In the proposed framework, the different color components are
processed in parallel, and the human-insensitive component is assigned a lower level of
complexity. Furthermore, when processing the less complex component, the auxiliary
information from the higher complex component is utilized to enhance the performance
of the less complex component.
In order to enhance the variable rate performance in the domain of image compression
and to provide a more variable rate functionality, we have proposed several solutions.
In the study [3], an efficient bit rate matching algorithm was proposed that enabled the
codec to reach the target rate within fewer searching iterations while maintaining variable
rate performance without a decline. Additionally, a spatial rate distribution function was
proposed in [4] to enhance the quality of the region of interest. It was observed that VVC
possesses a more flexible spatial rate distribution than the proposed model. Furthermore,
the application of VVC’s spatial rate distribution strategy on the proposed model led to
enhanced coding efficiency.
The advancement of the field of learned image compression has prompted the establishment of standards, leading to the formulation of the first learned image compression
standard, JPEG AI learning-based image coding system (JPEG AI) [46]. In order to
enhance the practicality of our solution for learned image compression for industrial applications, we have integrated the proposed method from this thesis into JPEG AI. The
majority of the experiments were conducted on the JPEG AI platform. Our proposed
framework and methods are also adopted by JPEG AI, and we implement some adaptations for JPEG AI. We then publish an overview of variable rate in JPEG AI [5]. In [5],
we introduce the 3D quality map-based variable rate coding for JPEG AI in depth.
The final step in this study is to assess the visual quality of the proposed framework by
implementing the proposed variable rate coding solutions. Two distinct visual quality assessments have been organized. The initial assessment focuses on the high fidelity quality
range, as we proposed in [6]. The results of the first assessment indicate that the proposed
model demonstrates superior performance for the majority of the test data. The second
visual quality is characterized by a wide quality range, as we proposed in [7]. According
to the results of the second visual quality assessment, the proposed model demonstrated
superior performance in the wide quality range for the majority of the test images. For
the test image for which the proposed model exhibited suboptimal performance, an analysis was conducted, revealing enhanced background texture but not specific object detail.
The result of the visual quality assessment indicates that the implementation of distinct

2 Technical Background
The present chapter delineates the technical background of the thesis. The introduction
will commence with an examination of learned image codecs. Furthermore, a specialized
learned image codec framework, designated as a conditional variational autoencoder, will
be introduced. In addition, a specialized learned image codec framework, designated as a
conditional variational autoencoder, will be introduced.
The subsequent section will introduce the color handling process, which is comprised
of two distinct subsections. The initial part pertains to the transformation of colors
through the utilization of an efficient color format. This approach enables the acquisition
of optimal visual quality in images and videos while minimizing the required data storage
space. The subsequent subsection involves a cross-component process, which further
reduces redundancies in information across disparate color components.
Furthermore, the present solution for the variable rate coding in classic and learned
image codecs is hereby introduced.
The fundamental experiment platform of the thesis will be introduced in closing. All
contributions and experiments are conducted on the JPEG AI standardization verification
model.

2.1.2
Conditional Autoencoder Framework
A particular framework called conditional autoencoder is widely used in image and video
compression. A conditional autoencoder retains the structural integrity of the autoencoder. Additionally, it has the capacity to utilize auxiliary information to enhance the
quality of reconstruction. The compression of images can be facilitated by the incorporation of auxiliary information derived from the content of the images themselves. With
regard to the process of video compression, auxiliary information may be derived from
preceding frames.
In video compression area, Ladune et al. proposed a new inter-frame coding technique [43] that utilizes two complementary autoencoders, called MOFNet and CodecNet.
MOFNet employs the classical autoencoder framework and the function of MOFNet is
to compute and convey the optical flow v, and the pixel-wise weight α. The objective
of CodecNet is to transmit the current frame xt , conditioned on its prediction x̃t . The
structure of CodecNet is a conditional autoencoder, which is a direct extension of image
coding autoencoders with both the frame and its prediction as inputs. Consequently, the
encoder can learn a non-linear mixture of xt and x̃t . The same principle is employed for
the decoder, which utilizes the latent representation from xt and x̃t as input, enabling it
to invert the transform performed by the encoder.
As illustrated in Fig. 2.2, CodecNet incorporates an auxiliary encoder to process the
current frame’s prediction x̃t . This auxiliary encoder maintains a similar architecture to
the encoder, facilitating the generation of a latent representation of the prediction that is
equivalent in size to the latent representation of the current frame xt .

2.2
Color Handling Process
Color information constitutes a critical dimension of visual data. However, its redundancy
and perceptual characteristics necessitate specialized processing in compression systems.
10
2. Technical Background
The human visual system exhibits a higher degree of sensitivity to luminance information
in comparison to chrominance. This characteristic is exploited by compression systems
through various color space transformations and chroma subsampling techniques. The
objective is to utilize a reduced number of bits to represent color while minimizing perceptual quality degradation. The selection of color space and processing methods exerts
a direct influence on the compression ratio and the visual quality of reconstructed images
or videos.
This section provides a comprehensive review of the fundamental and state-of-the-art
techniques for color space transformation and color component processing in image and
video coding.
2.2.1
Color Space Transformations
The underlying principle guiding the implementation of color space transformation is to
mitigate the statistical redundancy present among color channels. As demonstrated by
Malvar et al. in the research [48], there is a strong relationship between the red (R), green
(G), and blue (B) channels in natural images. The conversion of data into a luminancechrominance space, such as YUV, facilitates the separation of intensity information (luma)
from color information (chroma). This separation enables more efficient compression
because the chroma components can be subsampled without significant perceptual loss,
leveraging the physiological characteristics of the Human Visual System (HVS).
The luminance component (Y) is calculated through a weighted sum of RGB values (vY = 0.2126vR + 0.7152vG + 0.0722vB in BT.709 standard), reflecting the HVS’s
heightened sensitivity to luminance variations compared to chromatic changes [49]. This
differential sensitivity originates from retinal ganglion cell distributions, where luminance
pathways dominate spatial resolution [50]. Chrominance components (U and V) encode
color differences:
• vU /vB − vY : Blue luminance deviation.
• vV /vR − vY : Red luminance deviation.
where vY/U/V and vR/G/B are the values from an image of YUV or RGB format.
Empirical studies confirm that humans can tolerate 2×-4× greater spatial aliasing in
chrominance channels without perceptible quality degradation [51]. This neurophysiological constraint justifies chroma subsampling techniques, which optimize bitrate while
preserving perceptual quality.

YUV 4:4:4: Preserves full chroma resolution (1:1 chroma:pixel ratio), essential for
professional imaging where color precision is critical [52].

2.2.2
Cross-Component Process
Human perception research [57] by Gegenfurtner et al. established that chroma and
luma information share neural pathways in visual processing. This physiological reality
translates into mathematical correlation in video signals:
• Spatial Consistency: Edges and textures manifest simultaneously across components.
• Temporal Correlation: Motion characteristics are consistent across color planes.
• Statistical Dependence: Coefficient distributions show similar patterns in transform domains.
The aforementioned features inspired the researcher in the field of video codecs. The
breakthrough came with the formalization of Cross-Component Prediction (CCP) introduced in HEVC. CCP signifies a paradigm shift in the field of video compression, fundamentally challenging the conventional approach of processing color components independently. The fundamental premise acknowledges the existence of substantial redundancy
between the luma (Y) and chroma (U, V) components due to their common presence
in scene content and the sharing of structural information. This correlation engenders a
substantial amount of coding opportunities, which CCP exploits through the use of sophisticated mathematical models. CCP represents a concrete manifestation of the in-loop
luma-to-chroma prediction approach, exhibiting a balanced trade-off between complexity
and compression efficiency enhancement. The following features are attributed to CCP:
• Linear prediction without offset, low-cost reconstruction rule.
• Forward-adaptive signaling, limiting the decoder’s complexity.
• Operating on prediction residuals, limiting the delay between parsing and reconstruction.
• Adaptive at transform unit level, higher flexibility in combination with residual
quadtree.

application of prediction residuals, it can be concluded that the expectation, or the mean,

2.3.1
Solution in the Classic Codecs
The initial implementation of a variable rate function within the JPEG [24] standardization is a notable milestone in the field of image compression. In this instance, quantization
is employed to regulate the rate of the bitstream.
As illustrated in Fig. 2.9, the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)-based processing
steps in JPEG involve the Forward DCT (FDCT) and the Inverse DCT (IDCT). JPEG
is capable of encoding images block by block. Each block is 8 × 8 pixels, and the encoded
block contains 64 DCT coefficients.
Subsequent to the output from the FDCT, each of the 64 DCT coefficients undergoes
uniform quantization in conjunction with a 64-element quantization table. This table must
be specified by the application as an input to the encoder. Each element can assume any
integer value within the range of 1 to 255, thereby specifying the step size of the quantizer
for its corresponding DCT coefficient. The objective of quantization is to achieve further
compression by representing DCT coefficients with no greater precision than is necessary
to attain the desired image quality.

2.4.1
Scope and Development
The consumption of online media is increasing, leading to a growing need for high-quality
content with lower bitrates. The increasing need for media has resulted in the swift
advancement of traditional and neural network-based image codecs, as shown in [19]
and [20]. In terms of rate distortion performance, the early NN-based codecs such as
Ballé et al. proposed [21], and [22], are comparable to traditional standards such as
JPEG [24], JPEG2000 [25] and BPG [26] (an intra coding of HEVC [27]). Furthermore,
the recently proposed NN-based image codecs such as Koyuncu et al. proposed [1], have
already surpassed the intra coding of state-of-the-art video coding standards, such as
VVC [28]. The performance of learned image codecs has prompted the community to
consider establishing a new standard for learned image codecs.
In 2019, the JPEG standardization committee initiated a new project called JPEG
AI [64]. In [64], the scope of JPEG AI is described as follows:
The scope of JPEG AI is to create a learning-based image coding standard that
provides a single-stream, compact compressed domain representation targeting both human visualization, with a significant improvement in compression
efficiency over commonly used image coding standards at equivalent subjective
quality, and effective performance for image processing and computer vision
tasks, with the goal of supporting a royalty-free baseline.
The development of the project is the result of the joint efforts of the International
Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), the International Organization for Standardization
(ISO), and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). An overview of existing
solutions [65] showed that neural-based solutions are mature enough to compete with
conventional image codecs. In light of this, the Call for Evidence (CfE) [66, 67] was
established in 2020, followed by the Call for Proposals (CfP) in 2022 [68]. Ten teams
participated in CfP and demonstrated superior results [69] to those achieved by VVC [28]

2.4.2
Basic Framework
JPEG AI employs a multi-branch decoding framework [73], allowing a single codestream
to be reconstructed in multiple ways. A trade-off exists between complexity and quality in
each case. This adaptability ensures broad support across several devices and applications.
Following the process of entropy decoding, which retrieves quantized residual samples and
reconstructs latent samples, the core decoding engine defines three synthesis (inverse)
transforms. Each of these transforms is capable of producing a reconstructed image.
Conformity testing, which is still in development, explores the possibility of standardcompliant decoding without requiring bitexact reconstruction. By supporting multiple
synthesis transforms and providing flexibility in reconstruction accuracy, JPEG AI enables
vendors to optimize implementations to best suit their device capabilities and application
needs.
The high-level diagrams of the JPEG AI encoder and decoder are illustrated in Fig.
2.14. The encoder initiates the conversion process by converting the source image to the
YUV color space as defined in the BT.709 standard, a format that is internally supported
by the JPEG AI codec. This process entails the decomposition of the image into its
primary and secondary color components.
The concept of separately processing different color components is called Conditional
Color Separation (CCS), as proposed by us in [2]. The details of CCS will be introduced
in the next chapter. These components are then subjected to a series of compression
steps, which include analysis, transform, latent domain prediction, hyper-encoding, and
residual coding utilizing an arithmetic encoder.
The encoder employs convolutional and non-linear activation layers to decorrelate
the source image, thereby producing a latent representation y. Two possible analysis
transforms are described in the standard, with and without an attention model. This
latent representation is subsequently processed into a hypertensor z, which is encoded
prior to residual computation. This enables efficient latent domain prediction and the
creation of an entropy coding probability model.
The hyper-tensor z is quantized to ẑ and compressed using an arithmetic encoder with
a probability model obtained from the trained model, which is shared between the encoder

and decoder. Subsequently, latent domain prediction computes a residual r, which is then
subtracted from a prediction obtained from y.
Finally, the residual r is quantized to r̂. This residual is encoded using an arithmetic
coder with entropy parameters derived from the hyper-scale decoder, producing stream-r.
As illustrated in Fig. 2.14, the module pertaining to the overall variable rate functionality
has been omitted. The simplified gain unit, which was proposed for the overall rate
control, is situated prior to the quantization of the residual.
The decoding process mirrors the encoding process, albeit in reverse order. Initially,
stream-z is parsed, and the hyper-scale decoder generates the entropy probabilistic model,
which provides the parameters for residual decoding.
It is imperative to note that this operation is performed at the encoder and decoder
to ensure the precise congruence of the model across both sides. Subsequently, stream-r
is parsed, and residual r is recovered through the process of arithmetic decoding. Subsequent to this, latent domain prediction is executed using ẑ with a hyper-decoder and
a multistage context model, leveraging previously decoded information. In conclusion,
one of the three synthesis transformations previously mentioned produces the decoded
image. The primary component is processed independently, while the secondary component incorporates latent representations from the primary and secondary components as
auxiliary input.

2.4.4
Performance
JPEG AI employs BD-rate to evaluate the objective performance. The BD-rate is a metric
employed for the evaluation of the coding efficiency of lossy image and video codecs. It
quantifies the average bit-rate difference as a percentage for a given decoded quality. A
negative BD-rate indicates that the codec requires fewer bits to achieve equivalent visual
quality, whereas a positive BD-rate signifies that more bits are necessary to attain the
same level of quality.
The results of the JPEG AI BD-rate performance test are presented in Table 2.2. The
performance of JPEG AI is compared to HEVC, VVC, and six configurations of JPEG
AI. HEVC is the default image coding format in Apple products and is now widely used.
VVC is the most recent generation of video coding standards, configured as an anchor in
Table 2.2.
As mention in subsection 2.4.2, JPEG AI is capable of multi branch encoding and
decoding. The reference software contains two examples of an encoder. The first encoder,
designated as Enc0, is the simplest. The second encoder, termed Enc1, incorporates attention mechanisms and transformers. It has been demonstrated that a stream generated
by any of the encoders can be decoded by any decoder.
As delineated in the standard, three decoders are specified for the purpose of obtaining
a reconstructed image. The standard delineates three decoders (Dec0, Dec1, and Dec2)

This chapter is intended to provide a comprehensive overview of the technical background
of the thesis-related topic in image and video compression technology.
It has been demonstrated that certain image codecs, when utilized, exhibit superior performance in terms of BD-rate when compared to conventional codecs. A prominent framework within the domain of learned image codecs is the Variational Autoencoder (VAE). The VAE framework underlies the operation of a codec, which typically

incorporates a transform-coding mechanism capable of learning a non-linear transform of
the image into a latent representation. This representation is then transmitted through
the use of a learned probability model. Typically, VAEs can be trained end-to-end with a
suitable loss function. This loss function can take into account the rate of distortion and
the various types of distortion.
Another variation of the VAE framework is known as the Conditional Autoencoder
(CAE) framework. In the context of the CAE framework, auxiliary information is typically available to facilitate transform coding. The auxiliary information also has its
corresponding latent representation, which is used in the decoding process. The coding
efficiency can be further improved with the help of the auxiliary information. In this chapter, we overview many different types of CAE frameworks, and their auxiliary information
can come from the neighboring area or another frame.
In the context of the color handling process, researchers have determined that the
human vision system demonstrates heightened sensitivity to luminance in comparison to
chrominance. This phenomenon provided the impetus for the separation of luminance and
chrominance information from an RGB image, thereby resulting in the development of a
novel color format known as YUV. In the YUV format, the Y component contains the
luminance information, and the UV component contains the chrominance information.
By enhancing the Y component and down-sampling the UV components, it is possible to
obtain a color format that preserves more luminance information. This, in turn, ensures
optimal visual quality. Concurrently, this approach reduces the chrominance information,
leading to a reduction in data requirements.
This chapter also presents a variety of approaches to reducing the discrepancy between
luminance and chrominance or leveraging the relationship between them. Among these
approaches are several methods of cross-component processing. Notably, some of these
processes are founded on classical mathematical equations, while others are grounded in
Neural Networks (NNs). The neural network-based structure under consideration comprises convolutional layers. The utilization of convolutional layers is predicated on the
extraction of feature information.
We proceed to provide an overview of the variable rate solution in image codecs. In
this section, we first provide an overview of the variable rate function in classic image
codecs and video codecs. The basic idea is to use different quantization parameters to
provide different rates. This is a very straightforward way. Nonetheless, variable rate is
a challenging topic for learned image codecs. The rationale underlying this phenomenon
is that researchers must develop multiple networks to provide discrete rate points. These
networks utilize distinct trade-off parameters in their loss functions. In addition, certain approaches have been demonstrated to achieve continuous rate points by tuning the
quality map in the latent domain.

3.1

The use of auxiliary information in the autoencoder can help to improve the coding
efficiency. This concept has been successfully applied in both video and image compression
domains, as proposed in [43], and in [45]. The results of the studies conducted by [43]
and [45] demonstrate that conditioning an image autoencoder yields superior compression
ratios and enhanced reconstruction quality. In view of the extant literature, a conditional
autoencoder framework for image compression was proposed. This framework is referred
to as conditional color separation (CCS). In the context of our CCS framework, the
primary and auxiliary information are derived from distinct color channels. This approach
entails the utilization of a primary color component to expedite the processing of nonprimary color components.

3.1.1
Framework Introduction
As illustrated in Fig. 3.1, an example of the CCS framework is demonstrated. In this
particular instance, the input data is converted from the RGB format to the YUV420
format. The YUV format is comprised of three components: Y is the component of the
image that contains the luminance information, while U and V are the components that
contain the chrominance information. In the YUV420 format, the U and V components
are sub-sampled by a factor of two in each spatial direction. Given that the Y component
contains four times more samples, it is selected as the primary component.
Prior to processing, the non-primary components U and V are concatenated, and the
primary component Y is downsampled to match the height and width of the concatenated UV components. Each group of components is processed by a dedicated encoder,
decoder, hyperprior, autoregressive context model, arithmetic encoder, and arithmetic
decoder. The processing of Y occurs concurrently with the processing of UV, resulting
in the generation of two distinct bitstreams. The UV components are coded with the
Y component’s auxiliary information. The Y component and its latent representation
will be concatenated with the UV components before the encoder and the decoder. In
instances where Y and UV possess disparate dimensions, the Y component will undergo
a process of downsampling prior to the concatenation step.

3.3
Three-dimensional Quality Map
In this section, the fundamental concept of the Three-Dimensional (3D) quality map will
be introduced. The utilization of our proposed quality map [10] is intended to facilitate the
provision of variable-rate support in JPEG AI, and this technology is adopted by JPEG
AI. A 3D quality map possesses the same dimensions as the latent tensor because the 3D
quality map is required to provide per-element control for the latent tensor. However,
the transmission of an additional quality map of equivalent dimensions to the latent
tensor can necessitate a substantial amount of data transmission. In order to minimize
the transmitted data while maintaining sufficient rate control functionality, the proposed
approach involves extending two distinct types of quality maps to generate the final 3D
quality map. A 3D quality map of dimensions identical to those of the latent tensor can
be generated through the extension of a channel-wise quality map, a spatial quality map,
or a combination of both.
The subsequent subsection will delineate the process of extending different quality
maps to a 3D quality map.
3.3.1
Extension of Channel-wise Quality Map
The channel-wise quality map is employed to facilitate an overall rate control, thereby
enabling the model to achieve a continuous variable rate. By collaborating with an efficient
bit rate alignment algorithm, the model is capable of precisely matching to a targeted
rate point with optimal performance.
In JPEG AI, we proposed to initially transform the input RGB data into various
YUV formats. Subsequently, the internal process will encode the YUV format, as the
CCS framework permits the Y components to be directly set as the primary component.
The utilization of disparate input YUV formats gives rise to varying internal process
tensor dimensions.
As previously stated in Subsections 3.2.1 and 3.2.2, as illustrated in Fig. 3.5 and 3.7,
respectively, various input YUV formats necessitate distinct pre-processing procedures for
54
3. Part 1
the secondary component network. The disparate allocation of down-sampled convolution
layers in the primary and secondary networks results in the output latent tensors from
the primary and secondary color components exhibiting equivalent spatial dimensions.
To elaborate, the YUV formats possess disparate dimensions for their respective Y components, measuring [1, H, W ], and UV components, measuring [2, ceil(H/cver ),
ceil(W/chor )]. This discrepancy in spatial dimensions is indicative of the fact that the
UV components are subject to variation in accordance with cver and chor . However, it
has been demonstrated that the output tensor of the primary component network has
size [CY , h4 , w4 ], which is spatially equal to the output of the secondary latent tensor
size[CUV , h4 , w4 ], where h4 = ceil(H/24 ) = H/16, and w4 = W/16. Within the latent space,
the number 160 is assigned to the luminance channel, denoted by CY . The number 96 is
assigned to the chrominance channel, denoted by CUV .
The variable rate functionality in JPEG AI employs distinct scaling for each element
of the latent tensor. Consequently, the utilization of two 3D quality maps with dimensions
(CY , H/16, W/16) and (CUV , H/16, W/16) is necessary. It is evident that these two 3D maps,
which possess equivalent spatial dimensions, differ in terms of their channel size.
In order to ensure the proper decoding of the bitstream by the synthesis transform
transformation network, it is necessary to signal the two 3D quality maps. Nonetheless,
the signaling of a quality that is equivalent in size to the latent tensors resulted in an
excess of bits. To circumvent this issue, the 3D quality map is generated through the
extension of either a channel-wise quality map or a spatial quality map.
As illustrated in Fig. 3.18, the process of extending the channel-wise quality map
to a 3D quality map is depicted. A channel-wise quality map is represented by the
tuple [1, 1, C], where C denotes the latent channel number. By extending the channelwise quality map in the spatial domain, a 3D quality map can be generated. It has been
established that the latent channel number is distinct for the primary and secondary color
components. Therefore, it is imperative that each color component possess its respective
channel-wise quality map, thereby facilitating the generation of the 3D quality map.
3.3.2
Extension of Spatial Quality Map
A spatial quality map is employed as a means of facilitating flexible spatial bit allocation.
The utilization of a spatial map enables the allocation of additional bits to the region of
interest. Consequently, the quality of ROI will be enhanced.
As stated in subsection 3.3.1, the primary and secondary latent tensors possess equivalent spatial dimensions. Specifically, the primary latent tensor is characterized by dimensions (CY , H/16, W/16), while the secondary latent tensor is defined by dimensions
(CU V , H/16, W/16). It is evident that the 3D quality map for different color components
can be derived from the same spatial quality map.

Variable Rate Feature
As proposed in JPEG AI, in order to cover a large bit rate range, four models have been
trained for a specific Lagrange multiplier βtrain determining rate-distortion trade-off as
described in (2.5a). A higher value of βtrain yields a higher quality reconstructed image,

The rationale behind utilising four models is explained in Fig. 4.3, which depicts two
distinct lines. The green line illustrates the process of employing four models to construct
an optimal variable rate envelope. The curve represents four points, each denoting a
model that has been trained with a distinct βtrain and encoding with the βtest = βtrain .
These points are referred to as anchor points.

• It is feasible to utilise a single model to encompass a broad bit rate range.
• The single model exhibits optimal performance when the bit rate range is close to

• If the single model provides a bit rate that is either too low or too high in comparison to the anchor point, the resulting performance will not meet the desired

Increasing the quantization step size will not result in improved model performance

point from the single model, the performance decline is more rapid than when providing
a lower bit rate," is a contributing factor.
4.2
Efficient Bit Rate Matching
In the context of assessing visual quality, Bit Rate Matching (BRM) emerges as a critical
component. This is particularly salient when researchers seek to draw parallels between
the visual performance of two codecs. In order to ensure optimal alignment, it is imperative to adjust the bit rate of two reconstructed images to a commensurate level. It is
important to note that attaining an exact match in bit rate is often unfeasible. In this
particular instance, a threshold of the bit rate difference is employed.
66
4. Part 2
In the JPEG AI requirements, the BRM condition is deemed to be satisfied when the
generated rate differs from the target rate by less than 10%. Given that four models are
trained in JPEG AI, the objective of BRM is to identify the model and the corresponding
βtest values that align with the target rate. The BRM algorithm is designed with the
variable rate feature in JPEG AI as its foundation. This section will present an overview
of the variable rate feature and the BRM algorithm.
In JPEG AI, the BRM process is described in the previous proposed work [3], it is
comprised of three distinct parts: model selection, βtest searching, and βtest validation. In
the model selection stage, BRM must locate the particular model that offers the target
rate. In the βtest searching phase, the ultimate βtest is determined for the selected model.
In the βtest validation phase, the codec computes the rate and loss for the βtest . This
subsection provides a detailed account of each BRM step.
4.2.1
Model Selection
The BRM within the prior version of JPEG AI software selects the candidate model with
the minimum loss. This model indicates the rate range that can cover the target rate.
The codec assigns distinct minimum and maximum βtest to each model. Consequently,
each model possesses the capacity to proffer a distinct rate range through the employment
of the minimum and maximum βtest .
In the event that the target rate is situated within the rate range of one model, that
model will be selected as the candidate model. In the event that the target rate falls
within the overlapping areas of multiple models’ rate ranges, they will all be considered
as candidates. As illustrated in Step 1 of Fig. 4.4, the selected candidates are presented.
After the selection of the candidate models has been made, each model must identify
its respective βtest to achieve the target rate. This phenomenon is illustrated in step 2
of Fig. 4.4, and the algorithm for the search for the βtest is presented in the subsequent
subsection. Subsequently, the relevant βtest for each candidate model is identified, and
the corresponding loss of that βtest is determined. The loss is determined by comparing
the reconstructed image with the original image and is a combination of the MSE and
the MS-SSIM loss with varying weights. The model that exhibits the lowest loss and its
corresponding βtest will ultimately be selected.
In the context of prior art regarding model selection, a range of models are considered as potential candidates, and the final model selected is the one that exhibits the
minimal loss. However, this approach presents two drawbacks. Initially, in the event of
a substantial number of candidates, each candidate necessitates a βtest search process for
the target rate, a process that is notably time-consuming. Secondly, it is imperative for
each candidate to calculate the loss at the conclusion of the process, thereby extending
the runtime of the decoder and metric calculation.

5 Spatial Quality Map for Region of
Interest Enhancement
As stated in the preceding chapter, the channel-wise quality map was introduced for the
purpose of facilitating the overall rate control. In this chapter, we propose an alternative
type of quality map in [4], that can be extended to a 3D quality map. This map is referred
to as the spatial quality map.
In the initial section, the underlying motivations and the implementation of the spatial
quality map will be introduced. The spatial rate distribution of VVC and JPEG AI will
be analyzed, and it will be determined that VVC possesses a more flexible spatial rate
distribution. Therefore, the decision was made to implement a spatial quality map in
JPEG AI. The primary challenge in implementing a spatial quality map is the signaling
of the bits necessary for its creation, given its nature as a two-dimensional tensor. The
utilization of a series of bit-saving implementations was proposed, and its introduction is
scheduled in this chapter. In conclusion, the subjective result will be demonstrated using
the spatial quality map to enhance the region of interest. Subsequently, the objective
result will be exhibited through the implementation of a VVC-like spatial rate distribution.
The overall quality enhancement will be evident through the utilization of a more flexible
spatial rate distribution.
5.1
Motivation and Implementation
In this section, the motivation and implementation of a spatial quality map will be introduced. First, the rate distribution between VVC intra as a classic image codec and
JPEG AI as an learned image codec will be analyzed. It was found that VVC intra has a
more complex rate distribution than JPEG AI because it has a more flexible block size.
Then, a spatial quality map for JPEG AI was proposed to improve its spatial rate distribution flexibility. Subsequently, the implementation of the spatial quality map will be
introduced, including the integerization of the value and the utilization of a joint spatial
quality map for different color components. The implementation’s objective is to provide
a flexible spatial rate distribution and to conserve the bits that require signaling.

5.1.2

As demonstrated in the preceding section, VVC exhibits a more flexible spatial rate
distribution. To that end, a spatial quality map was devised for the codec, with the aim
of facilitating the spatial distribution of bits in the JPEG AI VM.
This spatial quality map assigns different quantization steps to the various areas of the
image, thereby enabling the allocation of more bits to areas assigned a lower quantization
step, which in turn facilitates a superior reconstructed quality. Conversely, areas assigned
a higher quantization step receive fewer bits, resulting in a lower reconstructed quality.
The spatial quality map within the codec is predefined in order to guarantee accurate
decoder processing. It thus follows that the quality map must be incorporated into the
bit stream in order to ensure correct decoding. Consequently, the spatial quality map is
also subjected to an entropy encoding process.

5.3
Summary
In this chapter, we delineate the technical nuances of the spatial quality map. At the
chapter’s inception, we conducted an analysis of the spatial rate distribution between the
classic codec, VVC intra, and the learned image codec, JPEG AI. Our analysis demonstrated that the conventional codec manifests a more adaptable spatial rate distribution,
a finding that contradicts the predictions of certain researchers. Therefore, we proposed
the incorporation of a spatial quality map into the JPEG AI framework. This incorporation would enhance the capacity of the JPEG AI framework to attain a more flexible
spatial rate distribution.
A challenge was encountered during the implementation of the spatial quality map.
Specifically, a two-dimensional matrix must be signaled, which can result in significant
data usage. To address this challenge, we initially employed an integer quality index in
replacement of the signaling of floating-point values. Furthermore, a shared spatial quality
map was employed for the luminance and chrominance components. In this instance, only
a single spatial quality map was required, thereby allowing for the conservation of bits by
eliminating the need to transmit two spatial quality maps.
Subsequently, the result evaluation of employing the spatial quality map was introduced. Initially, the subjective result of utilizing a spatial quality map of an image was
presented. Then, the objective result was demonstrated by employing the spatial rate
distribution strategy of VVC. The implementation of a more sophisticated spatial rate
distribution strategy has been demonstrated to yield notable advantages.

6.1.1
Experimental Platform
Following the finalization of JPEG AI, it became the prevailing state-of-the-art image
compression standard, characterized by its neural network structure. JPEG AI employs
neural network-driven models to achieve higher compression efficiency while maintaining superior visual fidelity, thereby signaling a transformative shift towards AI-powered
end-to-end image compression. However, this method generates novel artifacts that are
different from traditional blocking and ringing distortions, while achieving competitive
performance compared to classic approaches. Objective metrics, including SSIM, MSSSIM, PSNR, and VMAF, have been employed by researchers to assess the efficacy of
compression algorithms. Nevertheless, the artifacts yielded by learned image codecs demand exhaustive subjective studies to evaluate their effect on perceived image quality, as
proposed in [92] and [93].
In the aforementioned studies, subjective quality assessment was predominantly reliant
on single- or double-stimulus category ratings or user preference. However, as interest
grows in the perceptual evaluation of high-fidelity compressed images, new methodologies
are being introduced. Advances in scientific research and the maturation of researchderived technologies have prompted stakeholders from relevant industries to incorporate
these methodologies in the development of their products and services. From this perspective, the establishment of international standards enables the global industry to reach
a consensus on the most effective methods and approaches to be employed. A multitude

The JPEG AIC-3 test methodology employs boosting techniques, specifically Boosted
Triplet Comparisons (BTC), to enhance the visibility of subtle distortions. These include
zooming, in which the plain images are cropped to half their size and upscaled using

effect, in which the reference and distorted images alternate at 10 Hz, with each image displayed for 100 milliseconds per cycle. The flickering technique is implemented in
JavaScript and applied in real time when the image triplets are shown to the participants.
In the BTC, observers are tasked with identifying the image that exhibits the most prominent flicker. In cases where a definitive decision is not possible, the option of selecting
"Not sure" is provided, as illustrated in Fig. 6.3.

Data Cleansing
To ensure reliability, a sample of subjects was screened using the JPEG AIC-3 method,
with responses evaluated based on accuracy and consistency. Subjects who scored below
the established threshold values were designated as "screened."
For the accuracy: This metric was computed using only comparisons where both
images were encoded with the same codec. The response was considered accurate when
the image with the lower bitrate was identified as exhibiting greater distortion. Responses
designated as "Not sure" contributed a score of 0.5. To reflect the perceptual strength
of each trial, response contributions were weighted according to the magnitude of the
distortion difference.
For the purpose of achieving consistency: Given that the questions were presented
in symmetric pairs, intra-batch consistency was evaluated by comparing responses across
these matched pairs. A score of 1 was assigned when both responses were identical. In
the event that one response was designated as "Not sure" while the other was not, the pair
received a partial score of 0.375. Pairs that exhibited contradictory directional choices
received a score of 0. The final consistency score was weighted based on the magnitude
of the distortion difference.

utilized. The thresholds were determined to be 0.6563 for PTC and 0.6992 for BTC,

Following the selection of the test image, the target rate point was considered. The objective of the experiment is to assess visual quality over a broad range of rates, necessitating
the preparation of rate points that span from severely distorted to nearly lossless images.

obtained for each image.
Given the eight test images and five distinct codecs employed in the visual quality

employed to select four to five rate points from a range of two to eight for each image,
with the objective of eliminating the poor and excellent quality categories. This approach
is employed due to the limited visibility of visual quality differences between different
codecs in this area.

7 Summary and Outlook
This thesis addresses the challenge posed by the learned image codec, a critical component
in the ever-growing demand for image data. We have proposed a conditional color separation framework for the learned image codec and have provided a variable rate solution
for our framework. Moreover, the proposed methodology has been adopted by JPEG AI,
which is the first neural network-based image compression standard. We subsequently assess the visual quality of JPEG AI. In the following section, we provide a concise summary
of the results obtained from each component of our proposed framework and methods.
Conditional Color Separation Framework
The primary focus of this thesis is the conditional color separation framework, which
is predicated on the conditional autoencoder concept. A conditional autoencoder must
possess auxiliary information to assist the codec, thereby enhancing coding efficiency. In
the context of video coding, auxiliary information is typically derived from other frames
to facilitate the prediction of the current frame. As illustrated in the image codec, when
an image is encoded block-wisely, auxiliary information can be derived from neighboring
pixels. Typically, a conditional autoencoder necessitates two encoders: one for the original
image or frame and the other for the auxiliary information. This configuration results in
a high degree of complexity.
Through a comprehensive understanding of the human visual system, it was determined that humans exhibit heightened sensitivity to luminance relative to chrominance.
Leveraging this insight, the Conditional Color Separation (CCS) framework was developed. The CCS framework constitutes a conditional autoencoder, with the condition
stemming from the inter-color component. Within the context of the CCS framework,
two parallel process lines are employed. The first process line is designated for the primary component, typically responsible for the luminance aspect. The second process line
is allocated for the secondary component, which is generally associated with the chrominance. The encoding and decoding of the secondary component is conditional upon the
primary component. Concurrently, the less complex network is utilized for the secondary
color component, while the more complex network is allocated for the primary component.
The utilization of the CCS framework facilitates the design of networks in a manner that
120
7. Summary and Outlook
enables components to exhibit a trade-off between coding efficiency and complexity. It has
been demonstrated that the less complex network is still capable of acquiring information
from the primary component in order to maintain adequate performance levels.
The initial implementation of the CCS framework is based on Cheng’s model [29].
The CCS framework utilizes the YUV format as the input data format. This decision is
informed by the simplicity of setting Y as the primary component in YUV and the presence of a more complex network. Through the strategic manipulation of the complexity
levels of the primary and secondary color components, we have identified a configuration
that exhibits an optimal balance between complexity and performance. Moreover, our
comparative analysis with the parallel processing framework reveals the critical role of
condition in CCS in ensuring the maintenance of coding efficiency.
Subsequently, the optimal trade-off between complexity and coding efficiency led to the
adoption of the JPEG AI standard. It has been demonstrated that the CCS framework
has been adopted by JPEG AI as its foundational model. To support a variety of data
formats, a pre- and post-processing procedure has been proposed to accommodate distinct
input formats. Furthermore, JPEG AI exhibits multi-level complexity, with a dedicated
detailed framework introduced for each distinct complex encoder or decoder.
Variable Rate Functionality
A critical issue for the learned image codec is the variable rate function. In the classic
variational autoencoder framework based learned image codec, a single model is capable
of providing one rate point, the rate point is dependent on the Lagrange multiplier in the
loss function. The Lagrange multiplier is a tool employed for the purpose of calibrating the
trade-off between rate and distortion. Typically, multiple models are trained to provide
a series of points on a RD curve. The interpolation function is then utilized to estimate
coding efficiency across a broader range of rates. If a specific rate point is of interest that
is not included in the RD curve, it is necessary to train an additional model. However,
the final rate for this model is not predictable based on the different test data.
In order to address this issue, researchers have proposed various solutions. Among
these, the gain unit-based solution proposed in [41] has been identified as a particularly
promising approach. This solution offers the advantage of continuous rate and is also relatively straightforward to implement. In [41], a trainable matrix, designated as the gain
unit, is associated with the model. The gain unit consists of multiple channel-wise quality
maps, with each channel-wise quality map having the same length as the latent channel
number. The gain unit facilitates the provision of multiple rate points from a single model.
The quantity of rate points is equivalent to the number of channel-wise quality maps contained within the gain unit. Furthermore, the model possesses the capacity to attain
any rate point between two specified rate points by employing a fine-tuning factor. The
technology was integrated into the JPEG AI, and the simplification implementation from
7. Summary and Outlook
121
us was executed to ensure the compatibility of the simplified gain unit with both software
and hardware. The implementation necessitated only the transmission of a single integer number, and the model in JPEG AI demonstrated the capacity to support continuous
rates. The transmitted integer number is associated with the trained channel-wise quality
map. Subsequently, the channel-wise quality map is expanded to a 3D quality map of a
same size to the latent tensor. The latent tensor undergoes modification by the 3D quality
map, thereby facilitating differential overall rate control. To provide enhanced variable
rate performance, the training strategy was also designed to ensure optimal variable rate
functionality. It is important to note that in the context of JPEG AI, the fundamental CCS framework provides two bitstreams for different color components. Therefore,
two channel-wise quality maps with different lengths are employed in the latent domain,
resulting in flexible rate allocation for the different color components.
As previously stated, the 3D quality map is the fundamental module for the variable
rate function. It can be expanded by the channel-wise quality map. Furthermore, the
3D quality map can be expanded by a spatial quality map, which possesses the same
spatial dimensions as the latent tensor. The spatial quality map is employed for regionof-interest enhancement. The spatial quality map contains a greater number of elements
than the channel-wise quality map. We provide the implementation of the channel-wise
quality map that reduces the number of bits required. This implementation involves the
transmission of the quality index (an integer number) in the spatial quality map. Each
index corresponds to a specific quantization step. To further minimize the number of
signaled bits, we employed a shared spatial quality map for different color components.
Researchers ascertained that the luminance and chrominance components exhibit consistency in the spatial domain. This finding indicates that utilizing the same spatial rate
distribution is sufficient to ensure quality. Furthermore, in the context of JPEG AI, where
the latent tensor of luminance and chrominance possesses equivalent spatial dimensions,
it is more expedient for us to implement a shared spatial quality map.
In order to execute a comparison of the performance of two different codecs in a fair
manner, it is necessary to take into account not only the BD-rate, but also the precise
bit rate matching (BRM) of the two codecs in question. The process of BRM is often
characterized by its substantial time demands. The underlying logic involves the iterative
process of evaluating all rate control parameters to identify the parameter that most
closely aligns with the target rate. In order to address this issue, an efficient bit rate
matching algorithm was proposed by us. This algorithm has been shown to provide more
than four times the speed of prior art without any loss. The proposed BRM method is
comprised of the following components:
• A model selection strategy that considers the variable rate feature of the learned
image codec. This strategy can determine the chosen model from candidates with
a single round of calculation.

• Rate control parameter prediction that uses statistical analysis of the rate control
parameter and actual rate. This can help us find the suitable rate control parameter
with fewer iterations.
• A simplified rate control parameter validation that skips redundant calculations.
The proposed efficient BRM algorithm enables us to provide ideal BRM results at a
speed that is four to six times faster than previous methods.
Visual Quality Assessment
In the domain of image compression, the bit rate matching is typically employed for
the purpose of quality assessment. To ensure a valid comparison, different codecs are
required to provide the test sequence at the same rate. The objective quality assessment
can be efficiently conducted by calculating the bit rate across various objective metrics.
Conversely, the assessment of visual quality necessitates the utilization of a formal testing
platform, wherein real participants are tasked with evaluating distorted images.
Firstly, we collaborate with the JPEG AIC, which is the standard for visual quality
assessment. In this visual quality assessment, the emphasis is placed on the evaluation of
visual quality within the high fidelity range. A test was administered to 259 participants,
encompassing five test images. The present visual quality assessment involves a comparison of the JPEG AI high profile in tools on configuration with other image formats,
including JPEG, JPEG 2000, AVIF, JPEG XL, and VVC. The results of this comparison
indicate that JPEG AI demonstrates superior performance in the majority of test images.
Secondly, a visual quality assessment has been organized according to the JPEG AI
standardization. This assessment is intended to serve as a verification test. In this visual
quality assessment, the focus is on the wide quality range. The test contains eight images
ranging from high distortion to nearly visually lossless. The target rate points for each
image were selected using the simple test evaluating Mean Opinion Score (MOS) for each
image. Four to five rate points were chosen from totally distorted to visually lossless.
In this study, two distinct profiles were utilized: the main profile without tools and the
high profile with tools. These profiles were employed as the JPEG AI configurations. To
facilitate a comprehensive comparison, the reference codes were set to VVC, JPEG XL,
and AVIF. The visual quality assessment was conducted by 28 participants from various
institutes. In the majority of test images, two configurations of JPEG AI demonstrated
superior visual quality in comparison to competing test images. In the test image where
JPEG AI exhibited substandard performance in comparison to alternative codecs, it was
also observed that JPEG AI can attain higher quality outside the region of interest.
7. Summary and Outlook
123
Overall Conclusion
In this thesis, we introduce a novel framework for the learned image codec, which we have
termed conditional color separation. This framework is predicated on the finding that
human vision is more sensitive to luminance than to chrominance. We have proposed
a conditional autoencoder that can separate and process different color components. In
addition, it can process the secondary component conditional on the primary component.
The allocation of greater complexity to the primary component and lesser complexity
to the secondary component, achieved through flexible allocation, enables the realization
of an optimal trade-off between coding efficiency and complexity. The adoption of conditional color separation as a framework for JPEG AI standardization, the first neural
network-based image compression standardization. The implementation of adjustments
to the standardization is initiated with the adoption of the conditional color separation
framework as the base framework for JPEG AI.
The second topic pertains to the provision of optimal rate control functionality for the
conditional color separation model in JPEG AI. The 3D quality map has been proposed as
a means to ensure precise control of the latent tensor. The dimensions of the 3D quality
map are equivalent to those of the latent tensor, with the potential for augmentation
through the incorporation of two distinct quality maps: a channel-wise quality map,
which facilitates overall rate control, and a spatial quality map, which is employed for
the enhancement of the region of interest. In addition, an adaptive training strategy was
proposed to ensure the provision of the ideal variable rate potential. Furthermore, an
efficient bit rate matching algorithm was developed to assist the codec in attaining its
target rate with greater efficiency and without compromising performance.
The conditional color separation model is finally analyzed using two visual quality
assessment procedures. The variable rate functionality enables precise rate matching and
the provision of favorable test results. In summary, we initially present a novel framework
for the learned image codec. Subsequently, we standardize it and incorporate variable
rate functionality. Finally, we conduct a visual quality assessment to demonstrate the
efficacy of our model.
In light of the findings presented in this thesis, it is anticipated that future developments in neural network-based image format conversion will lead to the effective elimination of redundancy among various components. This, in turn, is expected to result in
enhanced complexity allocation and performance enhancement.
