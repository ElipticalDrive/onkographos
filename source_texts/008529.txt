Abstract
This paper proposes a novel method for accelerating ray tracing of animated scenes in which objects are moved, added, or
deleted. The method uses two trees with different structures. The first tree is a hierarchical grid tree that is easily generatable
from the primitive list and modifiable according to its change. The second tree is a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) tree,
constructed from the first tree with intra-node agglomerative clustering. When the scene is modified, the first tree is updated,
and then, the second tree is partially rebuilt for accessed nodes in the first tree. The resulting BVH tree is deterioration-free;
it always coincides with the tree generated by frame-wise rebuilding. For applications where a small portion of the scene is
modified between frames, the tree formation cost of the proposed method is much lower than frame-wise rebuilding. Experiments showed that the quality of BVH trees generated by this method is comparable to those created by the binned surface
area heuristic approach, and that the tree formation speed is faster not only for dynamic scenes but also for static scenes.
Keywords Ray tracing · Bounding volume hierarchy · Hierarchical grids · Agglomerative clustering
1 Introduction
Beside its excellent capability for handling complex lighting
phenomena, ray tracing has a noticeable property in its rendering performance. With an appropriate precomputed data
structure for acceleration, the rendering time in ray tracing
is sublinear against the number of objects in the scene [1, 2].
This implies that ray tracing offers efficient image generation
for large-scale scenes without relying on complicated culling techniques and can therefore be a good alternative for
polygon rendering in interactive applications like computer
games.
Ray tracing, however, has so far been considered to have
difficulties in efficient management of the acceleration data
structures for dynamic scenes, in which objects are moved,
deformed, newly added, and deleted between frames. The
following aspects should be considered carefully in such
applications.

Good tracing performance. Looking at recent projects on

Fast construction/modification of the tree structure. The

however, this requires linear computational cost at the least

where a part of scene geometry is modified, the cost for
updating the tree structure should ideally not be affected too

Stability of performance. In interactive applications, frame
rate should be kept constant as much as possible. Incremental tree updates (e.g., [5, 8–10]) often cause unpredictable
deterioration of the tree quality, thus affecting the flatness
of the frame rate. Although there are techniques that measure tree quality and fix the tree occasionally [8, 9, 11], they
might lead to an unexpected pause in an interactive session.
This paper proposes a new method for constructing and
updating BVH trees for dynamic scenes with arbitrary types
of scene modification. While it uses a hierarchical grid tree
as a base structure, it eventually generates an ordinary BVH
tree, and thus, standard traversal engines can be used in rendering. The method constructs a BVH tree from scratch in
O(N log N) time, and updates the tree in O(M log N) time,
where N and M represent the total number of primitives and
the number of scene modifications (i.e., primitive insertions
and deletions), respectively, and therefore, the tree update
cost for dynamic scenes with few updates is much lower than
that of rebuilding approaches. The quality of the resulting
BVH tree is competitive compared to that created by the
binned SAH approach [6]. There is no temporary degradation of the tree quality; the generated BVH tree is always
“fresh” in the sense that it coincides with the one generated
by frame-wise rebuilding.
2 Background
Various acceleration data structures for ray tracing have
been proposed so far. Wald et al. [12] give a good survey of
related techniques, except for recent ones. Commonly known
acceleration structures are grids, BVH trees, and kd-trees.
2.1 Grid‑based approaches
Grid-based approaches [13, 14] decompose the scene space
into cells at regular intervals. For adapting to object coherence in the scene, grids are often organized hierarchically
[15]. This hierarchical grid structure can be viewed as the
generalization of the octree.
An advantage of grids in the context of dynamic scenes
is that it is easy to incrementally insert or delete objects,
because, once the scene space for an animation is defined,
relative positions among objects and grid cells are fixed.
Reinhard et al. [16] discusses a constant-time algorithm for
inserting an object to a hierarchical grid by storing objects
not only to leaf cells but also to cells at an intermediate level.
A disadvantage of grids lies in their ray tracing performance; it is generally recognized that grids are slower than
other approaches. The author also tried the grid structure
directly for acceleration, but could not obtain performance
better than moderately optimized BVH trees. Two reasons

2.2 Bounding volume hierarchy
Bounding volume hierarchy accelerates ray tracing through
a nested structure of bounding volumes, most commonly,
axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABBs). In the early period
of ray tracing, BVH trees have been manually constructed
based on scene graphs, but later more efficient trees came
to be generated automatically. For static scenes, one of the
well-known construction methods is the sweep algorithm
[5], which recursively subdivides the set of objects with a
split plane that yields the best estimated SAH cost among
candidates positioned at the centroid of every object.
To handle dynamic scenes, the easiest way is to construct
the tree from scratch for every frame. This requires a faster
construction algorithm. The binned SAH algorithm [6],
which restricts the splitting candidates to several positions
at regular intervals, is much faster than the sweep algorithm
while the degradation of the tree quality is known to be
small. Lauterbach et al. [7] proposed an even faster algorithm called LBVH, which utilizes Morton code for constructing trees based on spatial median splits. Pantaleoni and
Luebke [17] proposed a method called HLBVH, which uses
LBVH for the bottom part of the tree and applies the sweep
algorithm for the top part. Garanzha et al. [18] improved the
efficiency for finding good split planes in GPU implementation using an auxiliary grid structure. This grid structure is
transient data prepared for each frame and not like the one
used in this paper, which is kept over frames.
Since the quality of trees generated by LBVH or
HLBVH is somewhat lower than the sweep algorithm,
there is motivation for the development of strategies for
improving the tree quality. Ganestam et al. [19] succeeded
in generating trees a little better than the sweep algorithm
with less construction time by forming mini-trees for
object groups and constructing the top part of the tree
using them. For optimization, mini-trees with bad quality
are broken into finer ones. Karras and Aila [20] proposed
a method for improving the quality of an existing tree by
forming a treelet, a small subset of nodes itself forming
a tree, from each node as its root and applying dynamic
programming for optimizing the structure. Domingues
and Pedrini [21] achieved faster construction by replacing
dynamic programming with fast agglomerative clustering
[22]. Meister and Bittner [23] applied a faster agglomerative clustering algorithm to the entire tree, by which a

When only a partial set of objects are moved or
deformed in dynamic scenes, the refitting technique [5] is
a possible solution. This technique updates the AABBs of
the BVH tree nodes related to the scene change, but keeps
the same tree structure. This enables quick updating but
often leads to significant degradation of tree quality if the
degree of scene change is large. Therefore, various methods for recovering the quality have been proposed. Lauterbach et al. [11] proposed to monitor tree quality based on
the surface areas of AABBs and rebuild the tree when
the quality goes under a threshold. Larsson and AkenineMöller [8] proposed to compute a volume-based efficiency
measure for each updated node and lazily reconstruct those
subtrees whose measure values are below a threshold.
Yoon et al. [9] selectively restructure the tree by checking
the overlaps of AABBs as well as restructuring benefit for
node pairs. Kopta et al. [10] discuss tree rotations as well
as the split and merge of leaf nodes after refitting. Note
that these methods allow temporary degradation of the tree
quality, and therefore, the stability of trace performance
is not well guaranteed. Moreover, the refitting technique
cannot be used for scenes where new objects are added or
objects are removed.
The incremental BVH tree construction proposed by
Bittner et al. [24] might be applicable for dynamic scenes.
They successively add an object to the tree by using a
branch-and-bound method for finding the best position in
terms of global SAH. Occasionally, the tree is refined for
better quality. Unfortunately, the branch-and-bound search
is computationally expensive, and moreover, the tree quality
is possibly instable, since the resultant tree varies depending
on the order of object insertion.
3 Grid‑induced boundary volume hierarchy
2.3 Kd‑trees
Kd-trees recursively subdivide the scene space with axisaligned split planes. Fast construction methods for kd-trees
are known [25]. Kd-trees had once been believed to be
the best acceleration structure; however, they seem to have
been replaced by BVH trees recently. One reason for this
is that the refitting technique for dynamic scenes can only
be used in BVH trees. Another possible reason is that the
duplicated intersection calculation issues, as in grids, also
exist in kd-trees. As for refitting, Yang et al. [26] recently
proposed an alternative approach that redistributes primitives while keeping the same tree structure but optionally
expanding leaf nodes if their size becomes large as the
result of the redistribution. This approach is faster than
rebuilding the tree; however, it leads to the degradation of
the tree quality.
As addressed in the previous section, the acceleration structure that provides the easiest way of incremental modification for dynamic scenes is a grid; however, using the
grid directly for rendering results in poor performance. To
achieve tracing performance comparable to high-quality
BVH trees, this paper proposes a method that uses a hierarchical grid as a base structure and translates it into a BVH
tree. When a partial set of objects in the scene is modified,
the method first updates the grid and then reconstructs the
BVH tree only for the modified part, by which efficient
updates are achieved. The method is named Grid-induced
Boundary Volume Hierarchy (GBVH).
3.1 Outline of the method
GBVH uses a pair of trees: a grid tree and a BVH tree
(Fig. 1). These two trees share the same set of leaf nodes,
each of which contains a small number of primitives (typically triangles). During generation of an animation, both of
the trees stay in memory. For accelerating intersection calculation in the tracing phase, only the BVH tree is consulted.
The grid tree represents a hierarchical grid that recursively subdivides the scene space (Fig. 1a, b). Each interior node in the grid tree is called a grid node. This method
assumes that a bounding box (not necessarily being a tight
bounding box) of the whole animated scene is known in
advance. The top-level grid subdivides such a bounding box
into cells (typically at ­43 resolution). Then, similarly to the
hierarchical grid proposed by Jevans and Wyvill [15], each
cell that contains a large number of primitives is subdivided
further. While their grid structure allows multiple references
to each primitive, the grid tree here is based on each primitive’s centroid and therefore only has a unique reference to
each primitive. This makes the construction or update of the
tree much easier.
The BVH tree (Fig. 1c) is constructed based on the grid
tree. Each interior node in the BVH tree is called a BVH
node. As the figure shows, each grid node corresponds to a
disjoint subset of BVH nodes that forms a treelet1 in the BVH
tree. The connection structure between grid nodes is preserved as the connections between treelets in the BVH tree.
When the BVH tree is constructed for frame 0 (i.e.,
the beginning frame) of an animation, a full grid tree is
built, and then, each grid node is translated to its corresponding treelet to form a new BVH tree. Between frame
i and frame i + 1, the grid tree is first updated for every
primitive modification between the frames. During this

3.3 Building and updating the grid tree
While a hierarchical grid structure for accelerating ray
tracing is known [15], it seems that its incremental update
algorithm has not been described in past literature. This
subsection presents an incremental update algorithm for
the grid tree, in which the tree dynamically grows or
shrinks in response to scene modification.
In this paper, only two basic operations for manipulating the grid tree are defined, that is, inserting a primitive
to the tree and deleting a primitive from the tree. Building
of the initial tree is done by inserting all the primitives in
the tree to an empty tree. When a primitive is moved or
deformed, it is first deleted and then reinserted.
The insertion and deletion procedures are shown in
Fig. 2. These procedures work so that the number of primitives in each leaf node does not exceed a predetermined
constant Nleaf, unless the level (i.e., distance from the root)
of the leaf node reaches a limit Lmax. (Leaf nodes at level
Lmax may thus contain more than Nleaf primitives.) Limiting the height of the tree is necessary for guaranteeing the
termination of the insertion procedure.
The detailed behavior of the above procedures is as follows. When a primitive is inserted to a grid tree, it is first
checked whether the tree is empty, and if so, a leaf node
is created (lines 5–6). If the root of the tree is a grid node,
the primitive counter is incremented (line 8), the dirty flag
is set (line 9), and the insertion procedure is recursively
called against the cell that contains the centroid of the
primitive. If the root of the tree is a leaf node, the primitive is first inserted to the leaf and when the resultant leaf
node is too large (i.e., breaking the conditions described
above), it is converted to a grid node by recursively calling
the procedure (lines 15–18). When a primitive is deleted
from the tree, the type of the tree root is first checked
(line 22). If it is a grid node, its status is updated, and
the deletion procedure is recursively called (lines 23–25).
Then, if the number of primitives in the grid goes below
the threshold, the grid node is converted to a leaf node or
an empty tree (lines 26–31). If the root of the tree is a leaf
node upon deletion, the primitive is directly deleted from
the node (lines 33–36). Note that the structure of the grid
tree obtained by these procedures, ignoring the order of
primitives in each leaf node, is uniquely determined by
the current set of primitives in the tree; it does not depend
on its update history (for example, insertion order) up to
that point.

defined as the surface area of the tight axis-aligned bounding box enclosing both x and y. The treelet is constructed
according to the merging process.
Since the BVH nodes are allocated and freed more frequently than other types of nodes, allocating them individually might be inefficient. The number of BVH nodes in
the treelet equals the initial number of clusters minus one.
Therefore, BVH nodes can be allocated in a batch once the
actual number of nodes is determined (i.e., just before calling build_treelet at line 15).

The generation of the BVH trees (Fig. 3) can be parallelized easily. Since the subtree generation process for each cell

The parallelization of the grid construction and update

make the processing easier, grid nodes until some level Lp

3.6 Computational complexity
4.1 Performance for static scenes
It is not hard to show that the average-case height of the grid
tree containing N primitives is O(log N), and therefore, the
complexity of inserting or deleting M primitives into the grid
tree is O(M log N), as long as Lmax described in Sect. 3.3 is
set so that it is not reached for most primitives.
The computational cost for translating the grid tree to
the BVH tree is as follows. Since the number of cells for
each grid node is fixed, the translation cost for each node
is O(1). When M insertions/deletions are applied, the number of dirty nodes in the grid tree is O(M log N), because
the number of leaf nodes to be updated is at most M and
the number of grid nodes on the path from the root to each
leaf node is O(log N). Therefore, the total translation cost is
O(M log N). The summed-up cost for grid tree manipulation
and translation is also O(M log N).
For evaluating the construction speed and quality of trees
for generating a single-frame image, the GBVH algorithm
was compared with the binned SAH [6] and LBVH [7] algorithms. The author also implemented these algorithms with
C++, Embree, and OpenMP in the spirit of keeping the code
optimization level the same as GBVH. For the binned SAH
algorithm, 16 bins were used for finding split planes, and
task-based parallelization was applied for recursively constructing trees. The recursion was terminated if the estimated
cost of creating a BVH node is higher than that of creating
a leaf node, or the tree depth reaches 30. For computing the
SAH cost, KT = 1 and KI = 0.2 were used. The LBVH algorithm was implemented with parallel radix sort provided by
Embree for sorting primitives by Morton codes and with
task-based parallelization for recursive tree construction.
Its recursion was terminated if the number of primitives
becomes 8 or less, or the tree depth reaches 30.

4.2 Performance for dynamic scenes
The evaluation of the GBVH method for dynamic scenes
was performed by comparing it with two typical methods
for handling dynamic scenes: (a) a method where the initial
BVH tree is constructed by the binned SAH algorithm, and
then the tree is updated with BVH refitting and (b) a method
where the BVH tree is rebuilt every frame with the LBVH
algorithm. Table 3 shows the results for generating animations using dynamic scenes shown in Fig. 4.
Observing the trace speeds, it was turned out that the
GBVH method constantly provides good-quality BVH
trees (in the powerplantD scene, however, the binned SAH
approach was slightly better, which was probably due to
the same reason described in the previous subsection). For
the museum scene from the BART benchmark [27], the
tree quality is extremely degraded in the case of the refitting approach. This is because the scene contains many
13
primitives moving largely and randomly (related discussions
are found in [5]).
As for the times for the construction and update of trees,
the results show that the refitting approach is generally faster
than the other approaches. However, since the whole tree is
traversed for bounding box updates in the refitting approach,
the GBVH method, which traverses only the updated part of
the tree, becomes superior for the large-sized, powerplantD
scene. Comparing the LBVH-based rebuilding approach and
the GBVH method, the GBVH method is faster in the case
when the percentage of moving primitives is low (i.e., M
described in Sect. 3.6 is sufficiently smaller than N). For
example, for the kitchen and powerplantD scenes, the
GBVH method is more than 3.5 and 63 times faster than the
rebuilding method, respectively. If the percentage of moving primitives is high, the tree rebuilding approach with the
LBVH-based rebuilding method is more efficient in tree
building, but the quality of generated trees is lower as mentioned above.
Comparing the total time for generating animations, the
GBVH method outperforms the other two methods for all
the tested dynamic scenes. The instability of trace performance as found in the refitting approach is not observed in
the GBVH method.
The percentage of dirty nodes shown in Table 3 is the
average ratio of the number of dirty grid and leaf nodes
to the total number of nodes in the grid tree. This value is
considered to indicate how the incremental update in GBVH
performs better than complete rebuilding. As the results
show, it is highly related to the percentage of the moving
primitives in the scene.

4.3 Memory requirements
5 Conclusion
Since the GBVH method uses two different indexing structures simultaneously, the increase of memory requirements
might be a concern. The bottom rows of Table 3 show the
peak memory usage for each category of nodes in the GBVH
method. These are the “net amount” sizes of the information shown in Table 1 and do not include implementationdependent overheads such as unused entries in primitive
arrays, unused parts in SIMD vectors, or memory management information.
As can be seen from the table, the total memory requirements for grid nodes are close to those for BVH nodes.
Thus, the amount of memory required for indexing structures could be doubled in comparison to methods that uses
BVH trees only. However, since a quite large percentage
of memory is used by leaf nodes, which have the geometry information of primitives, such an increase is generally
insignificant in practical applications. Moreover, since the
grid tree is not accessed in the tracing phase, performance
degradation due to the increase of memory access bandwidth
should not occur.
This paper has described a novel method called Gridinduced Boundary Volume Hierarchy for accelerating ray
tracing of dynamic scenes allowing arbitrary forms of scene
modifications. When a small fraction of the scene was modified, the method showed more than an order of magnitude
improvement in the speed of tree formation while keeping
time increase in the tracing phase to a negligible level. Such
improvement would have a significant impact on the total
performance for large-scale scenes and should be even more
emphasized if hardware acceleration is employed in the tracing phase.
In future work, the performance of the GBVH method on
GPUs should be investigated. In addition, the generation of
better BVH trees especially for large-scale scenes should be
considered by, for example, adaptively choosing grid resolution. Another interesting topic would be on-demand, lazy
construction of trees during the tracing phase through the
use of the GBVH framework.
