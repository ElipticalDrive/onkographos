Abstract
The topic of this thesis originates from the fields of 3D video and video compression. It is particularly related to multiview video plus depth (MVD) as a
3D video representation that comprises both texture and geometry information
of the scene, on the one hand, and to the H.265/High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC) standard, on the other hand. Video coding algorithms like HEVC
are highly optimized for the statistics and the subjective perception of natural
video and, consequently, not fully suitable for the efficient compression of depth
signals.
In this thesis, a rendering-optimized coding method for depth signals as part of
the 3D video extension of HEVC (3D-HEVC) is developed and studied. The approach is motivated by the observation that the characteristics of depth signals
differ from those of natural video signals. In this regard, the results of a comparative, statistical analysis reveal that depth signals particularly feature sharp edges
along object borders and large areas of nearly constant or slowly varying values,
while video signals contain more higher frequency textures. Another relevant
aspect is that distortions in video and depth signals have a considerably different effect on the perceived 3D video quality. The depth information in MVD
serves to render perspectively consistent views, such that depth errors impair
the resulting subjective quality only indirectly through geometry distortions in
rendered views.
In consideration of these specific depth characteristics, a consistent coding algorithm is developed based on the approach of approximating the signal of a depth
block by geometric modeling functions. The coding algorithm essentially consists of two complementary modules that have been optimized for a high depth
coding efficiency in terms of minimal artifacts in rendered views, namely a set of
intra and inter-component prediction modes and a residual coding method in the
spatial domain. The advantages of the developed method are demonstrated by a
detailed study of different configurations and applications. Overall, a considerable improvement in the quality of synthesized views is achieved by preserving
relevant depth features substantially better. This applies to both extending and
completely replacing the conventional intra coding by the geometry-based depth
coding method — the former has been adopted in the 3D-HEVC standard and the
latter bridges the gap to 3D computer graphics applications.
Kurzfassung
Das Thema dieser Arbeit stammt aus den Fachgebieten 3D-Video und Videokompression. Sie bezieht sich einerseits auf Multiview Video plus Depth (MVD)
als 3D-Repräsentation die sowohl Textur- als auch Geometrieinformationen
der Szene umfasst und andererseits auf den H.265/High Efficiency Video
Coding (HEVC) Standard. Algorithmen zur Videokodierung wie HEVC sind
hochgradig auf die Statistik und die subjektive Wahrnehmung von natürlichem
Video optimiert und infolgedessen nur bedingt zur effizienten Kompression von
Tiefensignalen geeignet.
In dieser Arbeit wird ein Rendering-optimiertes Kodierverfahren für Tiefensignale als Teil der 3D-Video Erweiterung von HEVC (3D-HEVC) entwickelt und
untersucht. Der Ansatz ist durch die Beobachtung motiviert, dass sich die Eigenschaften von Tiefensignalen von denen natürlicher Videosignale unterscheiden.
Diesbezüglich zeigen die Ergebnisse einer vergleichenden, statistischen Analyse, dass sich Tiefensignale insbesondere durch scharfe Kanten entlang der Objektgrenzen und große Bereiche mit annähernd konstanten oder sich langsam
verändernden Werten auszeichnen, während Videosignale mehr höherfrequente
Texturen aufweisen. Ein weiterer wichtiger Aspekt ist, dass sich Verzerrungen
in Video- und Tiefensignalen sehr unterschiedlich auf die wahrgenommene 3DVideoqualität auswirken. Die Tiefeninformation in MVD dient dazu perspektivisch konsistente Ansichten zu rendern, so dass Fehler in den Tiefen die resultierende subjektive Qualität nur indirekt durch Geometrieverzerrungen in den
gerenderten Ansichten beeinträchtigen.
Unter Berücksichtigung dieser spezifischen Tiefenmerkmale, wird ein konsistenter Kodieralgorithmus, basierend auf dem Ansatz, das Signal eines Tiefenblocks
durch geometrische Modellierungsfunktionen zu approximieren, entwickelt.
Der Kodieralgorithmus besteht im Wesentlichen aus zwei aufeinander abgestimmten Modulen, die für eine hohe Kodiereffizienz, im Sinne von minimalen
Artefakten in gerenderten Ansichten, optimiert wurden, nämlich einer Reihe von
Intra- und Interkomponenten-Prädiktionsmodi und einem Verfahren zur Restfehlerkodierung im Ortsbereich. Die Vorteile des entwickelten Verfahrens werden durch eine ausführliche Untersuchung unterschiedlicher Konfigurationen
und Anwendungen aufgezeigt. Insgesamt wird eine erhebliche Verbesserung
der Qualität von synthetisierten Ansichten erzielt, indem relevante Tiefenmerkmale wesentlich besser erhalten bleiben. Dies gilt sowohl für die Erweiterung als
auch das vollständige Ersetzen der konventionellen Intrakodierung durch das geometriebasierte Tiefenkodierverfahren — ersteres ist in den 3D-HEVC Standard
übernommen worden und letzteres schlägt eine Brücke zu 3D-ComputergrafikAnwendungen.

Overview
The topic of this thesis originates from the fields of video compression and 3D
video.
1.1.1
Video compression
Video compression algorithms are essential for the transmission and storage of
motion video, as the data rate of raw video is significantly higher than the data
rate and capacity of available transmission channels and storage media, respectively. In principle video compression approaches can be grouped into lossless
and lossy algorithms. Lossless means that redundancies in a video signal are
exploited for reducing the data rate, but without modifying the signal, i.e., the
original and decompressed video are identical. Consequently, the compression
ratios that can be achieved by lossless algorithms are limited. Lossy algorithms,
on the other hand, achieve significantly higher compression rates by allowing deviations between original and decompressed video signals. However, this means
that certain information is irreversibly lost. The challenge for lossy compression
algorithms therefore is finding a good tradeoff between quality degradation and
compression rate, also referred to as rate-distortion optimization.
Applications like television broadcasting and internet video streaming typically use standardized video coding solutions that provide interoperability between different devices and services. Currently, the most relevant video coding standards are H.264/Advanced Video Coding (AVC) [ITU 2009] and its successor H.265/High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC) [ITU 2013]. A comprehensive overview of the technical features of AVC and HEVC can be found in
[Wiegand 2003] and [Sullivan 2012], respectively. Both build upon lossy compression algorithms with a hybrid architecture and figure 1.1 illustrates the fundamental block-level processing structure, also referred to as video coding layer.
According to [Sullivan 2012], the encoding algorithm typically proceeds as follows: Each input picture is split into blocks, which represent a square or rectangular region, and the exact block partitioning is transmitted to the decoder. All
blocks of the first picture of a video sequence and the first picture at so-called
random access points are coded using intra-picture prediction, while all remaining pictures of a sequence or between random access points are typically coded
using inter-picture temporal prediction for most blocks.
Intra-picture coding uses only spatial prediction within the same picture and is
thus independent of other pictures. The encoding process consists of choosing or

1.1.2
3D video
The human visual system gives us the ability of a binocular disparity or parallax perception, i.e., providing depth information when viewing a scene with
both eyes. In this context the term stereopsis refers to the perception of
depth and 3D structure obtained on the basis of visual information of two eyes
[Howard 1995]. The basic principle of stereopsis has been known since 1838
when Charles Wheatstone published his fundamental paper about binocular vision [Wheatstone 1838].
Conventional image and video processing is based on two-dimensional scene
representations and consequently lacks of disparity or parallax information —
although occlusions and shadows implicitly provide some depth cues. In contrast to that, 3D image and video processing is based on representations that
explicitly contain three-dimensional visual information and thus provide the illusion of depth perception. A comprehensive overview on 3D video processing
and representation can be found in [Smolic 2011] and [Müller 2011], respectively.
Figure 1.2 illustrates the fundamental 3D video processing structure for the following classes of 3D scene representations.
• Image-based representation:
The multiview video (MVV) format consists of multiple camera views of the
scene without geometry information. A widely-used version of MVV is the
stereo video format with two horizontally arranged views.
• Depth-based representation:
The multiview video plus depth (MVD) format consists of MVV with additional scene geometry information in the form of per-sample depth or dis-

Regarding the sender-side processing, [Smolic 2011] distinguishes between lowlevel and high-level algorithms. The low-level algorithms apply to MVV data
and camera geometry, including color correction, filtering, camera calibration,
rectification, segmentation, and feature extraction. The high-level algorithms, on
the other hand, deal with extracting 3D geometry information, including depth
estimation for MVD and visual hull reconstruction for MVG (e.g. shape-fromsilhouette [Laurentini 1994]).
Before going into detail on 3D video coding, the receiver-side processing and
applications shall be explained. As already mentioned, the two major fields of
application for the considered 3D scene representations are 3D video and FVV.
3D video refers to applications that offer a 3D depth impression of the observed
scenery by providing two pictures that match the left and right eye perspective
(stereopsis). FVV, on the other hand, refers to applications that allow the user to
interactively select the camera perspective (viewpoint and direction) the scenery
is shown from. This feature is well-known from computer graphics applications
like video games.
3D video and FVV obviously do not exclude each other, but can well be combined within a single system. Both applications require the same functionality,
namely providing views of the 3D scene from different perspectives or viewpoints. In case the required view is not contained in the MVV data as an original
camera view, it has to be synthesized or rendered. The rendering capabilities
of the considered 3D scene representations differ considerably. For MVV additional views of the scene can be generated via image-based rendering (IBR)
to a limited extent — the lack of 3D geometry information leads to perspective
distortions. For MVD, on the other hand, perspectively consistent views of the
scene can be generated via depth-image-based rendering (DIBR) within a range
around the original cameras. Regarding MVG, arbitrary views of the scene can
be generated by classical computer graphics methods, namely mesh-based rendering in combination with view-dependent interpolation of the MVV textures
[Smolic 2004]. Hence, geometry-based representations additionally enable applications that combine 3D video and 3D computer graphics elements.
Efficient compression and transmission of the considered 3D scene representations is a key aspect for the successful implementation of 3D video and FVV
applications. Regarding image-based representations, the multiview video coding (MVC) extension of AVC [ITU 2009] was an important milestone. MVV
shows the same scene from two or more different perspectives and consequently
contains a large amount of redundant information. [Merkle 2016] explains that

1.2.1
Motivation
The subject of this thesis is the efficient compression of MVD for 3D video applications, with the main focus on rendering-optimized depth signal coding in the
8

context of 3D-HEVC. Given that video codecs like HEVC are highly optimized
for the statistics and the subjective perception of natural video, specific depth
compression algorithms have the potential for improving the coding efficiency.
The solutions presented in this thesis are essentially motivated by the following
presuppositions:
• The characteristics of depth signals differ from those of natural video signals. The example in figure 1.3 indicates that depth signals particularly feature sharp edges and large areas of nearly constant or slowly varying sample
values, representing object borders and object and background areas, respectively.
• The effect of depth distortions on the perceived 3D video quality differs from
that of video distortions. The depth information serves to render perspectively consistent views, such that the subjective quality is not directly related
to depth distortions, but indirectly perceived through geometry distortions
in rendered views.
1.2.2
Main contributions
In this thesis, it is demonstrated that the specific signal and distortion characteristics of depth can be successfully exploited with the presented approach, namely
rendering-optimized depth signal coding for 3D video based on geometric primitives. The main contributions, including related conference and journal publications, are as follows:
— Depth-based 3D video processing. The relation between depth coding artifacts and the resulting subjective quality of rendered views is investigated and
a conclusive evaluation strategy is derived. In contrast to video coding, depth
distortions only indirectly affect the resulting video quality via DIBR, which has
to be considered for evaluating the efficiency of depth compression algorithms.

views.

the variation of 3D surface meshes extracted from depth images and the distortion of intermediate views rendered from texture and depth images. For

1.2.3
Practical importance
In practice most video transmission systems are compliant to the specification
of a video coding standard. In recent years the Joint Collaborative Team on
Video Coding (JCT-VC) — a group of video coding experts from ITU-T Study
Group 16 (VCEG) and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 29/WG 11 (MPEG) — has developed the
HEVC standard. In a second, overlapping phase the Joint Collaborative Team
on 3D Video (JCT-3V) developed the HEVC extensions for MVV and MVD coding, namely MV-HEVC and 3D-HEVC. Regarding 3D-HEVC, the standardization
project began with a Call for Proposals (CfP) in 2011, the JCT-3V was established
in 2012, and the coding standard was finalized in 2015.
Several ideas described in this thesis have been successfully proposed to the
JCT-3V as technical contributions to 3D-HEVC. The following achievements
have been made:
• A set of prediction modes based on non-rectangular block segmentations and
the constant segment value residual coding method were included in the best
performing proposal to the CfP, which was consequently selected as the development model of 3D-HEVC.
• Two prediction modes based on non-rectangular block segmentations and
constant offset residual coding, including a number of refinements and optimizations, have been accepted for the final 3D-HEVC standard [ITU 2015].
• Seven international patents have been filed and granted: Two on compression and view extraction for 3D video, four on coding modes using nonrectangular block segmentations, and one on constant segment value residual
coding.

Organization
The development of rendering-optimized coding solutions for depth signals is
a complex challenge in the field of 3D video coding. Relative to conventional
video coding, a variety of additional effects and dependencies have to be taken
into account for depth-enhanced 3D video. To address all relevant aspects, this
thesis is organized as follows:
In chapter 2 Geometry of Three-Dimensional Scenes, the geometrical relation between a three-dimensional scene and the two-dimensional images of the scene
is introduced. Based on the definition of a camera model, the fundamental equations for perspective projection of 3D scene points to 2D sample positions in the
image plane of a camera and vice versa are derived. Regarding applications, the
principle of camera calibration for deriving intrinsic and extrinsic parameters
and the epipolar geometry of two or more camera views for image rectification
are introduced.
In chapter 3 Multiview Depth Signals, the extension of multiview video by scene
geometry information is introduced, elaborating on the three topics of representation formats, provision methods and signal characteristics. First, the two formats MVD and MVG are defined, with the scene geometry represented by depth
images or disparity maps and polygon meshes, respectively. Regarding application, the provision of depth data via capture and estimation for natural scenes
and depth rendering for synthetic scenes is explained. Finally, the specifics of
depth signals are analyzed and compared to video signals, including the development of appropriate methods based on the co-occurrence of sample values.
Chapter 4 Depth-Image-Based Rendering is about the functional advantage of
MVD regarding 3D video solutions, namely DIBR for synthesizing perspectively
correct virtual camera views of the scene. Based on the mathematical description of virtual camera geometry and projective point correspondence, the main
components of the rendering process are explained in detail, considering the differences between general and rectified camera views. Regarding application, the
problems and solutions for realizing an efficient DIBR algorithm for 3D video
based on rectified camera views are discussed.
In chapter 5 Depth Signal Coding, the developed algorithms, methods, and applications for depth signal coding in the context of a MVD coding framework
are presented, targeting efficient depth compression by new and optimized approaches that consider the specific signal characteristic and application purpose.
First, the approach of geometric depth modeling is introduced, namely, plane fitting for blocks with a planar characteristic and wedgelet and contour segmenta-

Chapter 2. Geometry of Three-Dimensional Scenes
Figure 2.1: Projective geometry example: picture of two parallel rails intersecting
at point at infinity.
The general mathematical framework for describing the perspective projection
of three-dimensional objects to a two-dimensional plane is referred to as projective geometry. In 3D video and computer graphics, perspective projection
serves as a mathematical model for how 2D images of 3D scenes are formed, e.g.
by defining the geometric relation between points in the 3D scene and their projection on the 2D image plane of a camera that captures the scene from a certain
perspective.
For describing the projective geometry in a unified way, the Euclidean (affine)
geometry needs to be extended by the points at infinity or ideal points
[Hartley 2004], referred to as projective completion. In case of a threedimensional space the set of points at infinity is called plane at infinity. A
typical example for illustrating the effect of projective completion is the rail
track in figure 2.1: Considering the rails as two parallel lines that define a plane
means that they would not intersect in Euclidean geometry, albeit they meet
at the vanishing point in the picture. Projective completion, however, allows
adding the same point at infinity to each of the lines. This point at infinity is part
of the plane at infinity and corresponds to the vanishing point in the picture,
such that the parallel lines intersect in projective geometry.

Representation
According to the camera projection derived in section 2.1, the mapping between
image points p and points in space P is only unique, if the distance Z of P is
known. This type of geometry data is typically referred to as scene depth. Each
camera in a multiview scenario records the values of the color signal at image
points or pixel positions p, resulting in the MVV representation that consists of
sequences of color images, showing the scene from multiple viewpoints.
The following subsections introduce different representations for extending
MVV by the scene geometry information, i.e., multiview depth signals. Depth
images in section 3.1.1 and disparity maps in section 3.1.2 have in common that
the scene geometry is represented by one value for each pixel position in all
MVV images. This kind of representation is referred to as multiview video plus
depth (MVD). In contrast to that polygon meshes in section 3.1.3 represent the
scene geometry independent of the MVV images by global 3D scene points and
their connectivity. This kind of representation is referred to as multiview video
plus geometry (MVG).

Depth provision
This section presents methods for providing multiview depth signals. According to [Müller 2011], the depth information can be obtained in different ways,
namely by depth capture and depth estimation for natural content and by depth
rendering for computer graphics content.
3.2.1
Capture
The most obvious solution for providing depth data of a 3D scene is directly capturing the depth with suitable sensors. Depth sensing is a fundamental challenge
of computer vision, including applications like object reconstruction and robotic
navigation. Depending on the requirements of individual applications, different
classes of depth sensors and cameras need to be distinguished. In the context
of MVD, the main approaches for capturing per-sample depth are referred to as
time-of-flight [Yang 2007, Zhu 2008] and structured light [Scharstein 2003]:
— Time-of-flight. The principle of a time-of-flight camera is illustrated in figure 3.4: a light signal is emitted, reflected by the surface of the 3D scene and
detected with a sensor. By precisely measuring the round-trip time of the light
signal t, the distance ds between surface of the scene object and time-of-flight
camera can be directly calculated via the speed of light c as ds = c · 2t (assuming that emitter and detector have an infinitesimal distance within the camera
compared to ds ).
Based on the time-of-flight principle, different implementations of depth sensors
and depth cameras can be distinguished. One group are infrared time-of-flight
cameras. They either operate with pulsed light, measuring the time difference,
or with continuous wave modulation, measuring the phase shift. These cameras capture the depth of the complete scene with a single measuring cycle, thus
achieving sufficient frame rates for MVD. Albeit the raw results tend to have
high noise levels, infrared time-of-flight cameras are well applicable for MVD,
as the results can be improved by combined video and depth calibration and
post-processing, as for instance shown in [Zhu 2008].
Another group are laser range scanners, which allow for a very precise and
reliable measurement of the scene depth and are therefore frequently used in

— Structured light. The principle of structured light is illustrated in figure 3.5:

Dynamic, time-varying 3D scenes would require recording video and structured
light simultaneously, which is hardly possible due to projection of the light pattern. The typical application of structured light thus is producing detailed 3D
data of static scenes or objects.

3.2. Depth provision

Depth estimation based on the fundamental strategy described above tends to
lead to unsatisfying results. Typical problems are errors from occlusions, noise
and inconsistencies from ambiguous textures, and a lack of precision. Thus,
depth estimation algorithms try optimizing the estimation quality with different strategies (a comprehensive overview can be found in [Scharstein 2002]).
Basic optimizations are the choice of shape and size of the matching window and
the similarity metric. Another improvement is sub-sample accuracy: disparities
based on sample positions as δ0,1 = u1 − u0 potentially lead to depth images
where only a coarse subset of the possible depth values d ∈ [0 .. dmax ] actually
occurs. Using sub-sample accurate disparities helps increasing the matching and
the depth precision. Further optimizations try reducing noise and inconsistencies by advanced search strategies, such as hierarchical refinement with different
matching window sizes or extension of the matching function by criteria for the
consistency with neighboring values. Moreover, temporal and inter-view consistencies are usually considered for MVD.
3.2.3
Depth rendering
For synthetic, computer-generated 3D scenes, depth information is included in
the MVG representation in the form of a triangular mesh. Both MVG and MVD
include multiple video views of the scene (MVV), but differ in the way the scene
geometry is represented.
Converting MVG to MVD means generating a depth image or disparity map (according to sections 3.1.1 and 3.1.2, respectively) from a triangular mesh (according to section 3.1.3) for each camera view of the MVV sequences. This conversion is closely related to the Z-buffering method used in 3D computer graphics
for rendering 2D views of 3D scenes or objects based on polygon meshes, with
the result often referred to as depth map.
Given MVG in the form of MVV with calibrated camera views according to section 2.2 and a triangular mesh with set of vertices V and set of faces F according
to section 3.1.3, the sample-accurate depth images for MVD are derived according
to the following principle: First, a special Z-buffer is generated for each camera
view and then the Z values are converted to depth values d.
As illustrated in figures 3.1 and 3.2, the MVD representation assumes a world
coordinate system with a camera-oriented Z axis. In case a different coordinate system is used for vertices v, their coordinates have to be converted to the

vertex positions P = (X, Y, Z, 1)T .

The reason for using depth-enhanced representations like MVD instead of videoonly representations like MVV for 3D video is the functional advantage they provide regarding the quality of synthesized or rendered intermediate camera views.
MVV data is limited to the original camera perspectives of the 3D scene. However, providing a good 3D video quality with a realistic and non-disturbing 3D
impression requires views of the scene at intermediate camera positions. Typical applications are baseline adaption for stereo viewing, support of multiview
displays, and FVV.
Algorithms for rendering synthesized views of the 3D scene at camera positions
that are not originally captured are referred to as image-based rendering (IBR) for

information have to be distinguished. Given two original camera views C0 and

video signals of the two original views. Due to the lack of geometry information, this is realized by 2D image interpolation in the case of IBR. In contrast to
that, DIBR employs 3D scene rendering, using the available geometry information for compensating the perspective deviation between the camera views by a
3D projection of each original view prior to the image interpolation.

Chapter 5
Depth Signal Coding
This chapter presents algorithms, methods, and applications for depth signal
coding in the context of a MVD coding framework, targeting efficient depth
compression by new and optimized approaches that consider the specific signal
characteristic and application purpose. First, the approach of geometric depth
modeling is introduced, namely, plane fitting for blocks with a planar characteristic and wedgelet and contour segmentations for blocks with sharp edges.
Following this principle, a full coding solution for depth is derived, including intra and inter-component prediction as well as constant offset residual coding in
the spatial domain. Regarding application, the problems and solutions for integrating the developed methods as efficient depth coding tools in a HEVC-based
coding framework are explained in detail.

Chapter 5. Depth Signal Coding
In principle depth sequences are monochrome video sequences that can be
compressed with existing video coding solutions, such as the HEVC standard
[ITU 2013]. However, section 3.3 showed that some signal characteristics differ
considerably for video and depth. This provides the opportunity of improving
the coding performance for depth signals by approaches and methods that are
adapted to specific depth signal features.
The approach presented in this chapter follows the principle of representing the
signal of a depth block by modeling functions based on geometric primitives.
The resulting depth coding solution features novel intra and inter-component
prediction modes along with a residual coding method in the spatial domain.
According to [Merkle 2016], the related state of the art comprises approaches for
non-rectangular block segmentation and wedgelets, inter-component prediction,
and residual coding for depth:
• [Morvan 2006] proposed platelet-based coding of depth maps. By modeling
the signal of depth blocks using piecewise-linear functions and omitting the
residual signal, a bit rate gain of about 25% compared to JPEG2000 is reported.
For the same method, [Merkle 2009a] reported that the bit rate is about 25%
higher than that of AVC intra-only coding, while leading to a better quality
of rendered views using the coded depth maps.
• [Liu 2011] proposed a trilateral filter together with a sparse dyadic mode for
depth intra coding. The latter combines rectangular and diagonal block partitions with an inter-component predicted contour refinement. The resulting
piecewise-constant depth signals are reconstructed from predictively coded
constant partition value offsets — a similar approach has later been applied in
[Merkle 2013] for residual adaptation of inter-component predicted wedgelet
and contour segmentations. As an extension of MVC, about 1.5 dB total gain
in rendered view quality is reported.
• [Oh 2006] proposed inter-component prediction for motion vectors by using
the motion vectors of the corresponding video block also for the depth block.
As an extension of AVC, bit rate gains of about 10% are reported for low rates,
while a worse performance is achieved for high rates. [Winken 2012] proposes inter-component prediction for motion vectors by inheriting motion
and block partitioning from video to depth. As an extension of multiview
HEVC, about 10% reduction of the depth bit rate is reported. [Mora 2014]
proposes inter-component prediction of quadtree partitions using the video
quadtree as a reference for limitation and prediction of the depth quadtree,

5.3.1
Multiview and 3D video coding extension
According to [Merkle 2016], the MVC extension of the AVC standard [ITU 2009]
was an important milestone for efficient compression of MVV signals. By adding
the concept of disparity-compensated prediction, inter-view dependencies are
efficiently exploited with MVC, resulting in a significant coding gain compared
to simulcast coding [Merkle 2007a].
As described in [Sullivan 2013], a multiview extension of HEVC (MV-HEVC)
[ITU 2014] has been developed by the JCT-3V for supporting stereo and multiview applications through efficient compression of MVV signals. The architecture of MV-HEVC is very straightforward, using the same design principles as
for the prior MVC extension.

5.3.2
Specific depth coding tools
Section 5.2 introduced a number of depth coding approaches. Their application
in the 3D-HEVC coding framework requires a careful integration and optimization, for example avoiding conflicts and inefficiencies in existing coding features
or balancing bit rate saving and complexity. Apart from the new depth coding
tools described in more detail below, the depth coding performance of 3D-HEVC
is further improved by particular configuration of HEVC features, such as disabling deblocking filter and samle adaptive offset (SAO) for all depth layers.

function that allows balancing distortions in depth and synthesized views by
using a weighted average of both.
It is important to note, that the difference between conventional and VSO distortion affects RDO considerably: Conventional distortion measures the deviation
of a signal from a reference signal by metrics like the SAD. Consequently, RDO
results in the closest approximation of the original signal that can be achieved
by a certain number of bits. In case the number of bits is not restricted, the distortion has its minimum at zero, meaning that signal dˆ and reference signal d
are identical. For VSO, however, RDO results in the closest approximation of a
synthesized reference picture that can be achieved by a certain number of bits.
Since the view synthesis is a non-bijective function, depth signals different than
the original can also result in a zero distortion. In extreme cases, the local distortion of a block can be even below zero (see calculation of SVDC above, where
D1 is not necessarily smaller than D2 ).
C. Residual estimation and signaling
Section 5.2.4 introduced COR as an alternative residual coding method for
geometry-based depth coding modes. As COR and TQR not necessarily exclude
94
Chapter 5. Depth Signal Coding
each other, the implementation is realized as an additional residual coding feature, allowing to configure the encoder and decoder to either use COR or TQR or
both. In case both are enabled, the prediction error of a block is first minimized
in the spatial domain by COR and only the remaining residual is coded by TQR.
However, a RDO-based evaluation shows that this is only beneficial for some
BSM mode blocks.
Two main requirements need to be considered for implementing COR efficiently:
One is the computational complexity of encoder estimation and the other the cost
for signaling the values to the decoder.
Regarding estimation of optimal COR values at the encoder, the RDO process
strongly depends on whether VSO is used or not. Without VSO the optimal
values can be systematically derived from the difference between original and
predicted signal according to equations 5.14 and 5.15. However, these values
do not necessarily result in the minimum VSO distortion. Thus, the encoder
estimation process is extended by a search of optimal COR values with respect
to minimum VSO distortion. For balancing coding efficiency and complexity,
a fast search strategy is applied. First, the range of candidate COR values is
restricted as follows: given original values dk and predicted values dp,k with
k ∈ {1, 2} for BSM and k = 1 for Plane/DC∗ and other intra modes, respectively,
the range is set as dˆr,k ∈ [0 .. 1.5 · (dk − dp,k )] with dp,k + dˆr,k ∈ [0 .. 2n − 1]
for bit depth n. This means that a smaller range of candidate values is tested,
if the difference between predicted and original value is smaller and vice versa.
Based on dp,k , the search tests all combinations of candidate COR values for BSM
and all candidate COR values for Plane/DC∗ and other intra modes, respectively,
for minimum VSO distortion. To reduce the complexity overhead, an optimized
search strategy is applied: In a first step, a coarse subset of candidate COR values
is tested, using a step size of four, followed by a refinement step with candidates
in the range ±3 around the best coarse values.
For signaling the COR values to the decoder, two aspects are important. One is a
coding tool referred to as depth lookup table (DLT) [Jäger 2012], which reduces
the amplitude of COR values for depth sequences that are not fully exploiting
the range of depth values [0, dmax ], e.g. being coarsely quantized, by mapping
them to an index in a table of valid depth values.
The second aspect is the transmission of COR values in the bitstream. Based
on the probability distribution of COR values, which has a Laplace characteristic, efficient binarization methods have been studied in JCT-3V. As a result, the
solution presented in [JCT3V-H0131 2014] has been adopted, showing a good
tradeoff between efficiency and throughput. Altogether the signaling of COR

5.3.3
Codec integration
Starting from the fundamental approach of geometric depth modeling in section 5.1, a consistent set of depth intra coding tools based on geometric primitives has been developed in section 5.2, with application and implementation
details presented in section 5.3.2. This set of tools, consisting of the combination
of Plane, DC* and BSM prediction modes and COR residual, will be referred to as
geometry-based depth intra (GDI). Compared to conventional intra coding, the
unique feature of GDI is that the reconstructed blocks have a defined geometry,
namely a plane or segment-wise constant depth signal.
According to [Merkle 2016], the reference framework for integration of
geometry-based depth coding is the 3D-HEVC codec introduced above, but
without those tools that methodically overlap with GDI, which are referred
to as depth modeling mode (DMM) and segment-wise DC coding (SDC) in
[Sullivan 2013]. DMM partly corresponds to BSM and SDC partly corresponds
to COR (including DLT). More details on the relation between reference framework and 3D-HEVC can be found in section 6.1.1.
Given this reference, integration of GDI requires the following changes for depth
intra blocks:
• Prediction modes: According to section 5.3.2, the Plane and DC∗ mode are
strictly geometry-based versions of the Planar and DC mode and consequently integrated as an alternative coding process that replaces the regular HEVC process for depth blocks. In contrast to that, the four BSM modes
differ significantly from angular prediction in HEVC and are consequently integrated as an additional coding process according to section 5.3.2, including
support of inter-component prediction for depth.
• Residual coding: According to section 5.3.2, COR is integrated as an alternative residual coding process for depth intra blocks, including support of GDI
and regular prediction modes.

• Configuration and signaling: In case depth intra blocks shall be restricted
to GDI, the codec is configured to fully replace the regular intra prediction

most probable mode coding) by an alternative process. Otherwise, if depth
intra blocks are not restricted to GDI, the codec can be configured in different

by simply switching the role of columns and rows. The resulting and final
set of vertices is illustrated in figure 5.16(c).
It is important to note, that the meshes for this set of vertices and for the full
set of vertices describe geometrically identical surfaces in 3D scene space — the
collinear and coplanar vertices defined above are redundant and thus removing
them does not introduce any distortion.
According to equation 3.13, the number of triangles t for such a reduced set of
vertices depends on the total number of vertices n and the number of vertices
b on the convex border. Compared to the full set of vertices the range of these

t ∈ [2 .. tmax ],
n ∈ [4 .. nmax ],
(5.17)
b(n) ∈ [max(n − nmax + bmax , 4) .. min(n, bmax )],
considering that the set consists at least of the four corner vertices (n ≥ 4)
and that the convex border is restricted to vertices representing samples on the
border of the depth image.
In contrast to this general approach, GDI is explicitly designed for supporting
mesh extraction with a very small number of triangles. Given a decoded GDI
depth block of size N ×N , the geometry-based models in combination with COR
and disabling smoothing/deblocking filtering of reconstructed samples always
result in a plane or segment-wise constant depth signal. In consideration of this
defined geometry, GDI blocks can be represented by a reduced set of vertices
with a considerably less complex derivation process than the regular algorithm
introduced above.

different or identical value can be identified by elementary logical operations
on the binary pattern values instead of full sample value comparisons.
Chapter 6
Experimental Results
This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the developed depth signal coding methods. Based on a number of codec configurations together with a
well-defined evaluation setup, the different effects on the coding and rendering
performance are analyzed. Regarding objective results, the overall performance
and specific effects for individual sequences, bit rates and viewpoints are evaluated. Moreover, results for subjective quality and mesh extraction are presented.
Finally, a statistical analysis elaborates on aspects like the distribution of bit rates
and intra modes, influence of the residual coding methods, and the effect on signal characteristics and features.

6.1
Coding configuration
In this section, the configuration of coding experiments and the evaluation setup
are defined.
6.1.1
Software framework
The reference framework for implementation and evaluation of GDI is defined as
3D-HEVC without GDI-related depth coding tools (see section 5.3.3). The software implementation of this reference will be referred to as 3DR and is based on
version 11 of the 3D-HEVC reference software (HTM-11.0) [JCT3V-G1100 2014],
which is backward compatible to version 1 of the HEVC standard [ITU 2013].
Based on 3DR, the GDI coding tools are integrated according to section 5.3.3.
Moreover, the decoder of the software framework is extended by the mesh extraction method introduced in section 5.3.4.
For assessing the different effects of GDI relative to the reference, the following
configurations of the software framework are evaluated:
• GDI substitution of 3DR (GDX): In this configuration the set of depth
intra coding modes in the reference is fully replaced by the alternative set
of modes, namely Plane, DC∗ and four BSM modes, such that depth intra
blocks are restricted to these new modes. Furthermore, residual coding is
fully replaced by COR in a way that TQR is disabled for depth intra blocks.
• 3D-HEVC standard implementation (3DH): In this configuration the set
of depth intra coding modes in the reference is modified in a way that the
two BSM modes Wie and Ccp can be selected and signaled as alternative
modes. Furthermore, residual coding is extended by COR, which can be
selected and signaled as an alternative to TQR for all depth intra blocks
and as an additional option for BSM modes.
• GDI extension of 3DH (3DX): In this configuration the set of depth intra
coding modes in 3DH is modified in a way that all four BSM modes can
be selected and signaled as alternative modes, while Planar and DC mode
are replaced by Plane and DC∗ , respectively. Furthermore, residual coding
is restricted in a way that COR can only be selected and signaled as an
alternative to TQR.

Chapter 7
Summary and Conclusions
This chapter summarizes the work presented in the main chapters of the thesis,
including discussion of results, main achievements and conclusions.
134
Chapter 7. Summary and Conclusions
In this thesis, a rendering-optimized depth signal coding method for 3D-HEVC is
developed and studied. Depth-based 3D video coding differs from conventional
video coding particularly with regard to the depth signal characteristics and the
effect of depth distortions on the perceived 3D video quality. These specifics
have been considered for developing and optimizing the presented depth coding algorithms and as a result, significant coding gains in synthesized views are
achieved. For addressing all relevant aspects of such a 3D video coding solution, the thesis is divided into five main chapters, which can be summarized as
follows:
— Chapter 2: Geometry of Three-Dimensional Scenes. The principle of
3D video is based on the geometrical relation between a 3D scene and the 2D
camera images of the scene. For this purpose, section 2.1 defines a camera model
and derives the fundamental equations for perspective projection of 3D scene
points to 2D sample positions in the image plane of a camera and vice versa.
For applying perspective projection in 3D video solutions, the intrinsic end extrinsic camera parameters need to be known. These are usually estimated by
camera calibration and section 2.2 describes a method based on the point correspondences of a calibration pattern. The acquisition of 3D video typically comprises multiple cameras. For this purpose, epipolar geometry is introduced in
section 2.3, defining the projective geometry between the image planes of two
or more camera views and its application to image rectification for calibrated
cameras.
— Chapter 3: Multiview Depth Signals. 3D video solutions can be categorized
by the representation format, namely, those based on stereo and MVV and those
that additionally use scene geometry information, i.e., multiview depth signals.
The reason for including depth information is that the distance Z in the scene
space is required for perspective projection between 3D scene points and and
2D image points. Section 3.1 defines the corresponding formats, distinguishing
between MVD with the scene geometry represented by per-sample depth or disparity data and MVG with the scene geometry represented by a polygon mesh.
Basically, MVD stores the continuous value Z as a quantized, discrete value d for
each sample in each video view, while MVG stores the continuous coordinates
(X, Y, Z) of 3D scene points globally, i.e., independent of the video views.
For applying MVD in 3D video solutions, the depth data has to be obtained and
section 3.2 describes and discusses different approaches for depth provision. One
is capturing the depth signal together with the video signal using structured light
or time-of-flight cameras, however, in practice both techniques are not fully suitable for MVD. An alternative approach is depth estimation, basically using the
135
MVV signals of two views for finding corresponding points by stereo matching
algorithms. In practice, advanced depth estimation algorithms show satisfying
results, albeit prone to errors caused by occlusions and inconsistencies. Regarding computer-generated 3D scenes, precise depth data for MVD can be rendered
from polygon meshes via Z-buffering.
Developing efficient compression algorithms requires a good understanding of
the signal characteristics and statistics. For this purpose, a comparative analysis
of the video and depth signals in MVD is conducted in section 3.3, including the
development of qualitative and quantitative evaluation methods based on the cooccurrence of sample values. The analysis reveals that depth signals particularly
feature sharp edges and large areas of nearly constant or slowly varying sample
values, while video signals contain more higher frequency textures.
— Chapter 4: Depth-Image-Based Rendering. By including the depth signals
in MVD, advanced 3D video applications are enabled, as perspectively consistent
virtual camera views of the scene can be synthesized via DIBR. The main steps
of the DIBR process are projection, hole filling, and interpolation. Depending
on whether the camera views are rectified or not, the operating mode of DIBR
differs considerably.
The projection between original and virtual camera views is defined in section 4.1, including derivation of the camera parameters and the projective point
correspondence. For non-rectified cameras, corresponding points are related via
3D scene positions, while their relation simplifies to horizontal disparity shifts
for rectified cameras. This affects both projection and hole filling and section 4.2
defines and explains the specific processing for each scenario, highlighting the
integrated, line-wise operation for rectified camera views. The interpolation is
specified in section 4.3, basically superimposing the projected pictures of both
original views by depth-dependent, weighted averaging.
In the context of 3D video coding, DIBR is an essential tool for evaluating the
effect of depth distortions on the perceived 3D video quality. For this purpose,
the 3D-HEVC reference DIBR algorithm for rectified views is introduced in section 4.4, highlighting the problems and solutions for achieving an efficient rendering application that produces visually satisfying results.
— Chapter 5: Depth Signal Coding. Video coding algorithms like HEVC are
highly optimized for the statistics of natural video signals and consequently not
fully suitable for depth signal coding. The developed depth coding solution is
based on the approach of approximating the signal of a depth block by geometric
modeling functions, considering the specific signal and distortion characteristics
136
Chapter 7. Summary and Conclusions
of depth. The corresponding modeling functions are defined in section 5.1, comprising bisegmentation models of type wedgelet and contour, adapted to depth
blocks with a steplike characteristic (representing object edges), and plane models, adapted to depth blocks with a constant or planar characteristic (representing
object and background areas).
For deriving an efficient compression algorithm from this geometry-based modeling approach, novel and optimized depth coding tools are developed. As a result, section 5.2 presents a set of intra- and inter-component prediction modes,
namely Plane, DC∗ and BSM, and a suitable residual coding method, namely
COR. These GDI coding tools are designed for both an efficient approximation of constant, planar and steplike depth blocks and a smooth integration into
3D-HEVC.
The application of GDI within the 3D-HEVC framework is covered in section 5.3, including several refinements and optimizations, such as wedgelet pattern lookup tables with a fast estimation strategy, residual estimation and signaling for COR, and the interaction with VSO. Moreover, for supporting applications that combine 3D video with 3D computer graphics content, a mesh extraction algorithm, that integrates directly into the decoding process of 3D-HEVC,
is developed.
— Chapter 6: Experimental Results. For analyzing the different effects of GDI
on the coding and rendering performance, the following three configurations,
defined in section 6.1, are evaluated:
• GDX corresponds to the configuration in [Merkle 2016], fully substituting
regular depth intra coding by GDI.
• 3DH corresponds to the 3D-HEVC standard, using a subset of GDI as additional depth intra coding options.
• 3DX corresponds to an extension of 3D-HEVC, using the full set of GDI tools
as additional depth intra coding options.
The objective results in section 6.2 show that GDI leads to significant coding
gains in synthesized views, reducing the all-intra bit rate by about 8% for GDX
and by about 9% for 3DH and 3DX. The gains are in the same range with and
without VSO, confirming that GDI is a rendering-optimized depth coding solution, independent of whether the distortion in synthesized views is considered for encoder RDO decisions or not. Regarding computational complexity,
the evaluation shows that GDI results in an overall increase of encoder and decoder runtimes (except for the 3DH decoder), but with considerable deviations
for the different configurations.
137
The sequences-specific results indicate that factors like scene complexity and
quality of depth signals affect the coding efficiency of GDI, with higher gains
for HDQ than for LDQ sequences. Moreover, the regular characteristic of R-D
curves for synthesized views demonstrates that the performance of GDI is consistently good for the whole range of bit rates. This consistent behavior is also
observed for the quality of views rendered at different positions, which is especially important with regard to auto-stereoscopic displays and FVV applications.
The subjective results in section 6.3 show the effect of GDI on the quantity and
type of coding artifacts, highlighting that distortions around edges, which particularly degrade the perceived 3D video quality, are significantly reduced.
The mesh extraction results in section 6.4 show the advantage of GDI with regard
to applications that combine 3D video with 3D computer graphics content: GDX
requires about 88% less triangles for representing the scene surface by a mesh
than the reference.
For an in-depth assessment of the effects of GDI, additional statistical results are
presented in section 6.5. The bit rate distribution shows that the depth portion
is only about 5-10% of the total bit rate, confirming that the gains by GDI can be
considered as relatively high.
Regarding prediction modes, the distribution of block sizes shows that GDI leads
to more larger blocks than the reference, confirming its quality to closely approximate the signal of a depth block for predictive coding. Moreover, the distribution
of prediction modes shows that BSM modes are mainly applied along edges and
that Plane and DC∗ mode of GDI are more suitable for depth coding than the
regular Planar and DC mode.
Regarding residual coding, the statistical analysis confirms that COR is a very
efficient depth coding tools, especially in combination with the BSM prediction
modes. The comparison of GDX and the reference shows that even fully replacing TQR by COR has no undesired effects on the residual distribution. In case
both residual coding methods are supported (3DH and 3DX), the distribution between TQR and COR strongly depends on the whether VSO is used or not — with
VSO the COR portion is significantly higher than the TQR portion.
Finally, the effect of compression on the depth signal characteristics identified in
section 3.3 is analyzed. The comparison of GLCM diagrams and features shows
that GDI preserves the original depth characteristics considerably better than
the reference.

Chapter 7. Summary and Conclusions
Conclusions and perspectives
The main achievement of this thesis is the rendering-optimized depth signal coding method for 3D-HEVC referred to as GDI. The underlying approach of approximating the signal of a block by geometric modeling functions is adapted
to the specific depth signal characteristics and the effect of depth distortions on
the quality of rendered views. Consequently, the development of this 3D video
coding solution involves analyzing the signal characteristics of MVD as well as
evaluating the resulting quality via DIBR. In conclusion, GDI results in a considerably higher quality of synthesized views by preserving relevant depth features
substantially better than the reference.
The general advantage of GDI is demonstrated by different configurations and
applications. In this regard, the following achievements should be highlighted:
• The performance of GDI is consistently good, independent of whether the
view synthesis distortion is considered for encoder RDO decisions or not.
• The application of GDI as part of the 3D-HEVC standard [ITU 2015] confirms its practical importance. The new methods smoothly integrate into the
HEVC design as additional depth coding options. As a result, significant coding gains in synthesized views are achieved without increasing the decoder
complexity.
• The application in [Merkle 2016] utilizes GDI as a standalone coding method
that replaces HEVC intra coding for depth. This solution has the advantage of
bridging the gap between 3D video and 3D computer graphics applications.
By restricting depth intra blocks to GDI, mesh extraction can be efficiently
supported. As a result, surface meshes with significantly less triangles and at
the same time considerable coding gains in synthesized views are achieved.
Given that the intra-picture coding algorithm derived from the geometry-based
modeling approach yields the above-mentioned advantages, an interesting direction for further research would be the extension to inter-picture coding. The
results for random access configuration indicate, that inter pictures already benefit from GDI indirectly (higher quality intra reference pictures and higher portions of intra blocks in inter pictures). However, adapting the geometry-based
modeling approach to motion- and disparity-compensated prediction could be
challenging.
Another interesting direction for further research could be upcoming 3D video
applications that support an extended viewing range, such as a 360 degree
panoramic view, and therefore involve combined or hybrid depth-/mesh-based
139
representations of the scene geometry. In this regard, the geometry-based modeling approach could serve as a starting point for developing appropriate coding
algorithms, for instance as a successor to 3D-HEVC.
