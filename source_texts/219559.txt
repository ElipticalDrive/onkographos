[Received February 24th, 1910.—Read March 10th, 1910.]
WHEN plane waves fall on a sphere of perfect conductivity, it is known
that the character of the secondary disturbance due to the obstacle is
determined by the relative magnitudes of the wave length of light and
the radius of the sphere. Investigators hitherto have confined their
attention, in the case of a plane wave train, to obstacles of small size in
comparison with the wave length. The most exhaustive direct treatment
of the problem appears to be that of Sir J. J. Thomson,* who has expressed
the Cartesian components of the electric polarisation radiated from the
sphere in the forms of harmonic series. These series are very cumbrous
in form, and, although they readily yield a physical solution for a very
small obstacle, their use has hitherto been restricted to this special case.
The special problem of a very small obstacle was first solved by Lord
Rayleigh,t and has been the subject of a more detailed treatment, based
on Lamb's I general solution of the equations of wave motion, by Love.§
But for the other extreme case in which the obstacle is very large, which
has many important applications, no solution has been given. In the
present communication, an examination of the effect radiated by such an
obstacle is given, restriction being made to points of space whose distance
from the obstacle is very great in comparison with its radius, which itself
bears a large ratio to the wave length of the incident train. The series
given by Sir J. J. Thomson are taken as the starting-point of the investigation. The results are very different from those of a corresponding small obstacle. In the latter case, the mean energy scattered by
the sphere is closely dependent on the wave length, being inversely proportional to its fourth power. But when the radius is a large multiple
of the wave length, this dependence may be expected to vanish. This
conclusion is confirmed by the results of the paper.
