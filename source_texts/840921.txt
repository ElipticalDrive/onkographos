Ray-driven projectors trace one line per detector cell, through its center. When detector cells are large compared to the voxels, these lines miss voxels between them. The back projection then covers the image unevenly, and reconstructions show patterns from the sampling. This PR adds MultiRay, a geometry wrapper that traces several rays per cell and averages their line integrals.
g = MultiRay(circular_cone_beam(...), JitteredPoints(2, 2; seed=1); weights=GaussianWeights(0.4))
op = JosephProjector(image, g)
What's included
MultiRay(geometry, points; weights) for ConeBeam, ArcConeBeam and ParallelBeam.
The data keep their size. The projection of a cell is the weighted mean of its rays' line integrals; the back projection is the exact adjoint.
It works with Siddon and Joseph, on every device, with masks, subset and adapt.
Point strategies (positions in cell units, with the cell center at 0 and the edges at ±½):
RegularPoints(su, sv): the subcell centers.
JitteredPoints(su, sv; seed): each regular point moved randomly within its subcell (Cook 1986).
RandomPoints(n; seed).
Points(offsets).
Weighting strategies: UniformWeights, GaussianWeights(σu, σv). The weights are normalized per cell.
cell_ray(g, cu, cv, k): the ray that ends at any position on the detector, in cell units. On arc detectors the position is an angle about the source, so the rays sample the curved cell.
Random positions without a random number generator: the hash pcg4d (Jarzynski & Olano 2020) of cell, view, ray and seed. It's ported from the authors' MIT-licensed HLSL, with their notice in src/geometry/pcg4d.jl.
The positions are identical in the forward and back projection, on every device and across calls, so the operator is a fixed matrix.
Subsets keep the original view numbers, so their rows are those of the full operator.
2D images: the projectors keep the rays in the image plane (offsets along u only). Without this, rays would leave the single voxel layer, and Joseph would interpolate it down.
Implementation: the number of rays per cell is a type parameter. The kernels are unchanged: a per-element function dispatches on the geometry and loops over the rays. A MultiRay has no ray(g, i), so projectors that trace only one ray per cell reject it instead of ignoring the extra rays.
GaussianPoints(n, σu, σv; seed): normally distributed points; all rays count equally.
AbstractPoints and AbstractWeights: custom strategies, in your own package, need one method each. The functions they use are declared public (Julia's public), with cell_ray, trace_element, visit, Gather and Scatter documented.
Images with one layer of voxels: this also holds for 3D images with nz = 1.
Docs: "Write a Sampling Strategy", "Comparing Projectors", navigation with a further level and "Custom Components". A pass over all pages: statements of cause and recommendations need a paper, otherwise the text gives the measured values and setup.

MultiRay wraps a detector geometry with a point strategy (RegularPoints, JitteredPoints,
RandomPoints, Points) and a weighting strategy (UniformWeights, GaussianWeights); Siddon and Joseph
average the line integrals of the rays of each cell with normalized weights, with the exact
adjoint. The number of rays per cell is a type parameter, so one ray per cell compiles to the plain
projector. Random positions come from the hash pcg4d of Jarzynski & Olano (ported under its MIT
license) of cell, view, ray and seed: the same in forward and back projection, on every device and
in subsets. For 2D images the projectors keep the rays in the image plane.
