Introduction
Effects due to participating media are crucial for the appearance of
many objects and materials including skin, clouds, smoke, fruits,
marble, etc. The unique visual characteristics of volumetric lighting attract attention of lighting designers of both real and virtual
sets where participating media (typically smoke or fog) are often
artificially added to achieve specific dramatic effect or accent a particular emotional state. Unfortunately, the physics of the processes
responsible for volumetric lighting is notoriously complex leading
to difficulties in developing sufficiently efficient practical models
suitable for computer graphics. This is especially true for interactive applications such as computer games where participating media
is typically limited to ad-hoc fog effects achieved with the OpenGL
glFog command or its analogs. However, the sole existence of
such specialized command in the core OpenGL set speaks volumes
about the long-standing interest of the interactive graphics community to this problem.
In this paper we present a relatively simple comprehensive model
for non-homogeneous participating media and describe its efficient implementation using programmable graphics hardware. The
model is capable of achieving interactive rates for general media
while including both single and multiple scattering effects.
In designing efficient lighting models for particular types of
computer graphics scenes, it is common to identify several visually important contributions for given conditions and concentrate
on treating only those using specially tailored algorithms. The commonly used separation into direct and indirect lighting with further
division into diffuse, glossy and specular components is a prime
example of such technique. Our approach can be viewed as an application of this strategy to the case of participating media. It is
based on the recently developed path integral formulation [Premože
et al. 2003; Premože et al. 2004] which we modify to use in a hardware implementation. Blurring and attenuation are identified by
this model as the most visually important effects due to multiple
Efficient and visually compelling reproduction of effects due to
multiple scattering in participating media remains one of the most
difficult tasks in computer graphics. Although several fast techniques were recently developed, most of them work only for special
types of media (for example, uniform or sufficiently dense) or require extensive precomputation. In this paper we present a lighting
model for the general case of inhomogeneous medium and demonstrate its implementation on programmable graphics hardware. It is
capable of producing high quality imagery at interactive frame rates
with only mild assumptions about medium scattering properties and
a moderate amount of simple precomputation.

A simple interactive shading model for homogeneous materials
was presented in [Kniss et al. 2002]. This approach captures volumetric light attenuation effects and incorporates volumetric shadows.In this ad hoc model, forward multiple scattering is approximated over a user defined angle. The angle is constant throughout
the volume and determined independently of the phase function. A
key difference is that our model is inspired by the physics of light
transport and is intended for general inhomogeneous media. For
example, the effecive scattering angle is determined locally by the
phase function and the amount of scattering that has occured up to
this point.
Another feature which distinguishes our technique from most
previous methods is that we pay special attention to handling visually important interactions of objects with participating media.
Realistic appearance of objects as seen through the media, volumetric shadows and shadows of the medium onto objects are all
incorporated into our approach.
Computing realistic lighting in participating media has been a long
standing problem in computer graphics. An excellent overview
of global illumination methods in volumetric media can be found
in [Pérez et al. 1997] and the references therein. However, most of
the techniques presented are far from interactive.
With rapid advances in modern graphics hardware, there is an
increasing number of techniques that improve realism in near realtime. Most of the algorithms are based on various physical simplifications (low scattering order), constraints on media type (homogeneous media, diffuse phase function) and illumination. In most
models for interactive graphics the effects of multiple scattering
have largely been neglected. Recently, however, significant steps
have been made to incorporate such effects into interactive rendering algorithms. [Harris and Lastra 2001] provided a real-time
cloud shading algorithm suitable for games. Multiple scattering in
forward direction only is precomputed in a preprocessing step and
stored in impostors. Single scattering anisotropic effects are accounted for explicitly during rendering. [Sloan et al. 2002] precomputed radiance transfer in clouds and stored it in terms of spherical
harmonics. Multiple scattering effects are thus rendered interactively assuming isotropic phase function and distant illumination
(environment map). [Iwasaki et al. 2003] computed second-order
scattering effect in water bodies by slicing the volume horizontally
and calculating the scattered radiance at sampling points on these
planes. The scattered radiance is stored in texture maps that are
later used for rendering. Higher order scattering could be simulated with an extended algorithm at significant cost. [Riley et al.
2004] introduced a special-purpose multiple-model lighting system
that captures atmospheric effects such as rainbows, halos and the
corona. The model incorporates fine angularly dependent effects in
the presence of multiple scattering and was efficiently implemented
on a modern graphics hardware. While angular effects are crucial
in depicting atmospheric phenomena, spatial effects may be of even
greater importance for general arbitrary media.
A considerable amount of work, mostly based on the dipole
approximation to diffusion [Stam 1995; Jensen et al. 2001], has
been done on rendering effects of subsurface scattering in volumetric materials such as human skin and marble. The subsurface
light transport produces distinguishably soft appearance of objects.
Lensch et al [2002] implemented the diffusion approximation in
graphics hardware by first precomputing impulse response to the
incoming illumination at surface points. During the rendering step,
the incident illumination is filtered by these precomputed kernels.
Similar approaches that utilize relatively local nature of subsurface
light transport have been used by Hao et al [2003; 2004]. Mertens
et al. [2003a] described an even simpler algorithm that efficiently
computes subsurface scattering in image space. [Dachsbacher and
Stamminger 2003] render translucent objects with varying light and
material properties using translucent shadow maps. Subsurface

not dynamic, it is useful to compute and store light segment portions of the integrals to accelerate both single and multiple scattering computation. If either the volume density or light direction
changes, this data structure needs to be updated. The `light volume

integrated along the straight line from the voxel to the light. A
3D texture is then created for `light in the GL LUMINANCE format. If the scattering coefficient b depends on wavelength, we use
wavelength-averaged values for the ` computation. This saves both
storage space and computation time and is sufficiently accurate in
most cases, in part because the type of participating media we consider is rarely strongly colored. Full RGB components for both a
and b are used in all other computations.
LightImage. The LightImage is a texture map that stores information about the visibility of the light source from the perspective
of the volume. The image is oriented perpendicular to the light
direction on the bounding box of the `light volume, see Figure 5.
LightImage pixels have a value of 1 if the light is visible from the
volume and 0 if it is completely blocked. This visibility map is
also modulated by the potentially non-uniform spatial distribution
of light source intensity. In the special case where there are no objects obscuring the volume, the LightImage has a uniform constant
value of 1.
The creation of the LightImage is straightforward. An orthographic camera is orientated in the direction of the light. Then, all
the objects in the scene are rendered black, while the bounding box
of the medium is rendered in white. The resulting image is white
only where the volume is visible to the light. Note that objects completely inside the volume do not affect the LightImage and therefore
do not participate in our multiple scattering calculation. However,
they still affect the single scattering computation.

4.3 Objects and Shadows
All objects and the background are rendered into a floating point
pbuffer. A vertex program computes the diffuse surface color. To
avoid clamping, this value is stored in a texture coordinate. A simple fragment program then outputs a final color for the object using
a standard shadow map for object shadows and `light data structure
for projected shadows of the medium. The depth buffer from this
pass is reused during the volume rendering pass. This ensures that
portions of the volume occluded by an object do not contribute to
the final image.
Shadows cast by objects through a medium are an important visual effect for single scattering leading to the well known “godray”
phenomenon. A simple way this can be done is to add objects to
the `light volume by setting the voxel’s `light value very large if an
object lies between a voxel and the light source. This will cause
equation 2 to appropriately dampen single scattering for such voxels. While simple to implement, the results using this method are
121
rather poor due to the low resolutions available with 3D texture
maps. Shadows cast by objects in this case exhibit all the classic
artifacts associated with low resolution shadow maps. To overcome
this problem, objects are ignored during the generation of the `light
volume. Instead, a traditional 2D shadow map is built for objects
in the scene. Shadows are then incorporated into the single scattering pass by scaling radiance by the result of a shadow map lookup.
This ‘hybrid’ approach gives high quality results at a nominal performance cost. Note, however, that objects inside the medium will
be completely ignored in the multiple scattering calculation which
only uses LightImage and `light volume. We found this strategy to
be quite adequate for reasonable size objects (such as game characters) since multiple scattering is exactly the phenomenon which lets
the light to bend around objects and fill in the space behind them.
One way to handle truly large objects embedded in the medium
(such as a wall separating a smoke filled room in half) without the
need for excessive increase in resolution of 3D `light volume is to
split the media into several separate volumes so that these large
objects are outside each of them and therefore, are being properly
handled.

Volume Rendering
Texture based volume rendering [Cabral et al. 1994] is a popular
method for rendering volumes using
graphics hardware. A density volume is created as a 3D texture map.
For each frame, a series of viewaligned planes is generated such that
they are equally spaced along the view direction and intersect the
volume. The vertices of the planes are assigned texture coordinates
that correspond to the density volume. The planes are then rendered in front to back order and the results are blended using an
appropriate compositing equation. Our algorithm is implemented
within this basic slicing plane framework. For simplicity, we refer to all operations as those done on slices. It is understood that
this means that corresponding computation is performed on each
fragment which lies inside medium volume. We also describe each
contribution as a conceptually separate computation even though
in practice we have found that on current hardware it is more efficient to implement the single and multiple scattering computation
(passes 2 and 3 below) within a single rendering pass. In our implementation this two pass method provides a 40% increase in overall
performance over the three pass method.
For each slicing plane, current values for `eye and distance seye
are determined. We then compute the single scattering component

5.1
Limitations
The model proposed in this paper does not account for all scattering events with equal accuracy. It is inspired by the path integral
(PI) framework which relies on a predominance of sufficiently long
paths with a small angle change per scattering event. Backscattering, on the other hand, is largely due to short paths with significant
angle change per event. These are not accounted for, leading to underestimation of backscattering in a PI-based model. To obtain a
more practical result, we modified the original PI expression in an
ad-hoc manner in Section 3. Therefore, applications of our model
should be limited to situations where plausible visual appearance
takes precedence over physical accuracy.

6 Conclusion
M ERTENS , T., K AUTZ , J., B EKAERT, P., S EIDEL , H.-P., AND R EETH , F. V. 2003.
Interactive rendering of translucent deformable objects. In Proceedings of the 14th
Eurographics Workshop on Rendering, 130–140.
We presented a comprehensive participating media model for computer graphics. Special attention was paid to efficient treatment of
multiple scattering and preserving visually important effects due to
objects in the scene. We have shown that the model can be incorporated into programmable hardware-based graphics pipeline and is
capable of achieving high quality results at interactive frame rates.
There are several possible directions for future research. A more
accurate (while still practical) model incorporating backscattering
is desirable. We would like to investigate methods to achieve high
quality results with a smaller number of slices. It would also be
interesting to incorporate truly distributed illumination conditions
(such as those provided by environment maps) into our framework.
One possibility is to use spherical harmonics decomposition to accelerate the necessary angular integration.
