SIMULATIONDEVELOPMENTSAND
REQUIREMENTS
In many systems, scientists and engineers have
been capable of describing the system dynamics on the
basis of control volume analysis and the appropriate
conservation laws. However, the resulting differential
and/or integro-differential equations were sufficiently
complex that only in very specialized circumstances
could one develop closed form solutions for the
equations. Fluid dynamics is a good example, in
which fundamental conservation laws were developed
long ago from fast principles, governing conservation
of mass, momentum and energy, the so-called
Navier-Stokes equations. (Bird et al. 1960) The
equations, however, are non-linear and only in
simplified systems, like potential flow or Poiseuille
flow for unsteady viscous flow in a channel, can the
tools of analysis be used to provide closed form
solutions. When the real world effects of turbulence,
compressibility, multi-dimensionality, et cetera are
considered, the equations become considerably more
difficult. Historically, scientists and engineers have
often relied on experimental measurements to
understand the complexity of such systems. Since the
conservation laws in such instances are well known,
however, an alternative to consider is the development
of a discrete representation of the governing laws, with
unknown variables identified at specified spatial and
temporal locations, and then consider the solution to
the resulting set of often non-linear algebraic
equations,
This approach has been applied in many
different fields of science and engineering. Examples
include solutions to SchrOdinger's equation with
application in quantum chemistry for drug design or
chemical energetics, atomic motion in molecular and
quantum molecular dynamics for applications in
materials science and superconductivity, neutron and
photon transport in fission and fusion nuclear reactor
design, fluid dynamics, structural mechanics and
thermal hydraulics in a variety of mechanical
engineering applications such as aircraft, automobile or
power plant design. For real applications, however,
the number of discrete variables introduced can be
extremely l_ge. (Karin and Smith 1987) For example,
in computational fluid dynamics problems, the number
of desired mesh points in a single spatial dimension
may range from 100 to several hundred, with
computed values of densities, velocities, pressure and
energies at each local cell. This results in computing
several million to several hundred million variables
simultaneously. For transient simulations, this
computational requirement must be satisfied for every
time step, which themselves, often number in the
hundreds or thousands depending on the application,
accuracy desired, and numerical stability of the
solution algorithm.
To provide some perspective on the
requirements, the number of floating point operations
performed in an explicit solution to a finite difference
form of the three-dimensional Navier Stokes equations
for mass, momentum mud energy conservation
equations is roughly 100 floating point operations per
mesh cell. To consider a flow field simulation around
an automobile, using 100 points in each spatial
direction, time steps of roughly a millisecond, to insure
numerical stability, and a simulation time of 30 sec, we
need to perform several trillion operations. Using
today's vector supercomputers, which can perform
effectively at tens to hundreds of millions of floating
point operations per second, the required computing
time is several hours.
In the application of numerical solution
methods to materials science problems, techniques
such as quantum molecular dynamics (QMD) are now
being employed to study electron dynamics, including
linear and nonlinear electron transport, in materials at
trmite temperatures. QMD simulations provide the
real-time dynamics of electrons and ions through
solutions to the time-dependent SchrSdinger and
Newton equations. (Kalia et al. 1990) As noted in
Kalia et al. 1990, comparisons to experiments require
many thousand of particles and millions of time steps,
while a single QMD simulation with just one electron
and a few hundred panicles can take tens of hours on
the Cray-2 vector/parallel supercomputer. If one looks
at the requirements for solving problems of real
interest, one quickly comes to the conclusion that
computing speeds in the teraflop range (more than
1,000 times the speed of the Cray X-MP) will be
required.
From such applications, the need for a
substantial and expanding computational capability has
been identified. For some time, vector supercomputers
have provided part of the solution for this problem,
with another essential part being provided in the
development of numerical algorithms to solve the
resulting set of algebraic equations. (The Federal High
Performance Computing Program) Expectations are
that developments in the software arena will continue
this trend, as will the hardware developments, although
with an important modification. Although current
vector supercomputers have theoretical capabilities of
several hundred Mflops (millions of floating points
operations per second), the physical limitations on
electronic signal propagation times suggest that we are
nearing the peak capabilities of such systems.
The use of parallelism in the number of
processors, however, offers a way to expand the
computational capability in a substantial fashion,
Already today, parallelism in the number of processors
is available on vector supercomputers, such as Cray,
IBM, Alliant, and Convex systems, as well as several
foreign vendors. Massive parallelism is being
explored in both SIMD (single instruction, multiple
data) and MIMD (multiple instruction, multiple data)
architectures, avail_.ble from vendors including
Thinking Machines (The Connection Machine), Intel
(iPSC/860 systems, including the recently announced
Intel Touchstone), Maspar, NCube and BBN (TCS
2000). On all such systems, the ability to scale the
performance with the number of processors is being
demonstrated and suggests the near term possibility of
attaining the required teraflop performance. As the
power of individual processors, appropriate numerical
algorithms which can effectively use parallel systems,
operating systems and scientific and engineering
libraries mature, we can look forward to realizing the
computational performance desired in many of our
significant applications, lt can thus be expected that
the capability to numerically solve extremely complex
scientific and engineering problems in a routine
fashion for both analysis and design is quickly
becoming a reality,
INTERPRETATION AND ANALYSIS THROUGH
VISUALIZATION
As the simulated systems become closer to
reality, the amount of information generated by such
simulations increases as weil. As suggested above, the
amount of data generated is becoming enormous and
mechanisms for the interpretation and assimilation of
the results must be developed. This is the important
role of scientific visualization. The numerical solution
to the governing equations or the mathematical
algorithms employed may require a very fine
discretization structure to converge to a solution. Our
interpretation of the solution, however, requires us to
integrate fire data and see the bigger picture. Our
ability to review, process, and assimilate large
quantities of numerical data is quite limited, and
probably neither necessary or desirable. The physical
reality we wish to explore is, in fact, observed when all
the small piece.s are put l,.,gether. _qVlcCormicket al.
1987)
Interpretation of data from numerical
simulation has and is continuing to progress rapidly.
The examples below show how we have moved from a
relativelysimple use of line drawings to interpret
simulations in a post-processing mode to today's
developing methods which are focused on real-time,
interactive visualization and control of the simulation.
In early applications of visual interpretation of
computed results, simple line drawings were prepared
to illustrate the effect of the computed simulation. In
ANL's nuclear reactor safety programs, for example,
the response of reactor structures to specified loadings
induced by earthquakes, severe accidents, etc. have
been analyzed with time-dependent finite element
codes° The early visual representations consisted of
such line drawings showing the initial geometric
configuration of the analyzed object and the
subsequent progression of the deformation by
re-drawing the object in its distorted geometric
configuration. Such representations have been useful
in both design and analysis, not to mention debugging
of complex computer codes, but they lack the ability to
convey much of the underlying information of the
simulation. In complex analyses, several dependent
variables are generally computed and provided in two
and three dimensions. The ability to illustrate such
scalar and vector fields is clearly desirable. The
aforementioned videotape illustrates the use of visual
representations in a time dependent incompressible
flow field calculation, where the use of vector graphics
for the velocity fields are superimposed on the color
coordinated scalar field for pressure. The simulations
themselves, however, often provide results in three
dimensions and the ability of the graphics display to
allow the analyst to review the simulated results from a
variety of spatial perspectives, either interior or
exterior to the object boundaries, can be very useful in
the assessment of complex phenomena. The videotape
illustrates theability to translate androtatetheviewing
perspective of the user on an application of a fluid
dynamics simulation of flow around an aircraft. By
such movement of the viewing perspective, local
details of high drag positions or indications of high
structural stress can be quickly identified,
The use of such illustrations scratches the
surface for the use of visualization in simulations, both
staile and dynamic. The dynamic analyses are
producing results that are developing in time and the
analyst often has a primary interest in understanding
the dynamics of motions. Although the capability to
review this temporal development by producing film
versions, perhaps in 8mm or 16mm film, has been
available for sometime, the graphics capability of
personal computers and scientific workstations has
now made it possible to review the images generated
by the simulation in either a static or animated fashion,
Low cost software, such as the Imagetool software
produced at the National Center for Supercomputing
Applications at the University of Illinois at
Urbana-Champaign or aPe, produced at the Ohio
Supercomputer Center, run on low cost systems such
as Macintoshes and Sun workstations. By minor
modifications to existing simulation programs, which
are typically written in Fortran, an image f'tle can be
generated and post processed using this software. The
videotape illustrates the results of a multi-component
multi-phase flow simulation of a fluiclized bed
combustion system. (Lyczkowski et al. 1990) Also
illustrated are movies of the actual fluidized bed
system, illustrating our ,ability to confirm that the
numerical simulation is, in fact, a laue representative of
the physical system. Such tools are now being
expanded to handle three dimensions which can
display results in easily pen:eived color formats, as
well as alternative formulations such as contour
surfaces and histograms, 'with the click of a mouse,
Such ease of use and low cost has brought some of the
power of scientific visuNization to the practicing
scientist and engineer.
Most visual representations are the result of
post-processing of the computed data. With the ability
to translate and rotate viewing perspectives and review
sequences of calculated results through animations, the
scientists can quickly determine the success of the
simulation. The ability to control the calculations,
however, is missing. As alluded to earlier, the
complexity of the systems often being simulated
requires the use of hours of high performance
computing time. Despite the expected continued
increase in computing speed, the level of complexity of
the systems being analyzed will always expand faster
than the rate of increase for computer speeds due to
our continuing desia'e to include more of the details of
true physical systems. Thus, we will always be left
with the need to compute for hours on whatever the
current embodiment of the "supercomputer" is.
As we explore theories or optional design
characteristics, however, we would like the ability to
"see" where the computational analysis is taking us,
terminate if necessary or re-initiate with altered
parameters. This ability to see what is being computed
as it is being computed is what we will call "real-time"
animation. To appreciate the computing and data
communications requirements of what is being
requested, recall the visual image associated with our
simulation. It can be represented on a workstation that
may have 1000 by 1000 pixel resolution, requiring one
to three megabytes of data storage depending on the
use of 8 or 24 bits of information to represent color or
gray scale. Thus each image represents several
megabits of data to be transmitted. Thus, although the
ability to calculate simulation results in real time and
create visual images to depict reality is possible, it is
only part of the story, with the storage transmission of
electronic images still to be addressed.
Visualization can certainly tax data storage
facilities and networking. To generate a 15 second
animation at 30 frames per second, at a resolution of
512 x 512, with 8 bit color values, requires more than
117 megabytes of storage. To transfer this amount of
data at Ethernet speeds (assuming 300K throughput)
would take approximately seven minutes. In general,
many applications can compress the data used to
generate the image and use a smaller size image. If the
animation is primarily for viewing at a workstation
instead of a recording, fewer frames may be required.
These considerations reduce network and storage
requirement somewhat.
Data communication developments need to
keep pace with the developments in computer
technology. Networking speeds have progressed
rapidly from the hundreds of bits to megabits to
expected developments in gigabits for both local and
wide area data transmission. This larger capability will
certainly be required for real-time, interactive
v fion.
As important as it is to see the results of
computational analysis in real time, the scientific or
engineering analyst would like to exercise even greater
control than simply observing or terminating the
computation. The ability to communicate with the
computation in a dynamic fashion and effectively steer
the powerful computing engine is also desirable. Thus,
the next logical step is real-time, interactive
computing. Software packages in use at ANL such as
DISSPLA, available through Computer Associates,
have recently introduced dynamic interaction
capability, in which the application program can
interrogate operations by the application scientist to
dynamically control the sequence of calculations of the
program, select alternative physical properties or
boundary conditions, or change assumptions for the
analysis. Such capability is now being provided in a
vendor-inde_ndent form, using the X Windows
system, which is discussed further below,
Thus, wesee substantial progress in our ability
to create and transmit images that graphically display
the results of our simulations. One can only expect
that technology will continue to evolve that will permit
both greater complexity in the physical systems being
simulated and in our ability to visually interpret and
control these analyses,
HARDWARE DEVELOPMENTS AFFECTING
VISUALIZATION
Advances in workstations with increased
processing power and graphics capabilities play a
major role in the availability of visualization tools.
The increase in screen resolution and standard graphics
hardware and the relatively low cost of a typical
scientific workstation increase the availability of
graphics oriented products,
Vendors such as Apple Computer and IBM
have long attempted to meet dae need for workstations
in the price range under $5,000. More recently,
workstation vendors including SUN, Silicon Graphics,
and DEC have inlroduced entry level workstations in
the price range of from less that $5,000 to $10,000.
These products often provide much of the functionality
albeit less performance that their higher performance
counterparts and provide visualization capabilities that
may otherwise be unaffordable in some market
segments.
Vendors such as Stardent, Silicon Graphics,
SUN, AT&T, Evans and Sutherland and IBM also
market high performance graphics workstations in the
price range exceeding $100K. Here is where
state-of-the art visualization is defined and re-def'med.
Their systems typically provide graphics hardware to
address the need to quickly display complex graphical
objects. As workstation technology in general
continues to increase performance and reduce in cost,
many features found in today's high performance
systems will eventually appear in lower cost
alternatives.

SOFTWARETOOLSFORVISUALIZATION
The introduction and acceptance of the X
Windows distributed graphics protocol developed at
M/T has served to standardize and integrate distributed
systems for the transmission and display of graphic
images. X Windows works with TCP/IP and DECnet
Ethernet network transmission protocols. X Windows
employs the network to unify the computational power
of supercomputers and other hosts with the specialized
graphics capabilities of workstations.
Typical
scientific and engineering workstations today provide
X Windows capabilities, (i.e. display manager,
graphics libraries) as a standard feature. Many
graphics applications products likewise work in an X
Windows environment. X Windows enables a host
computer to generate primitives for an image that are
subsequently transmitted over the network and
interpreted by lhc workstation to display the desired
image.
While X Windows is useful for displaying
images, it needs additional capabilities to enable
researchers to work with three-dimensional graphics
and simulations on host computers often generate
three-dimensional objects. It is desirable to use a
standard structure to represent three..dimensional
objects that can be down loaded to workstations to
traverse and view the object efficiently.
An informal body of vendors and universities
have committed to implement such a standardized
capability. This proposed standard incorporates
PILIGS (Programmers Hierarchical
Interactive
Graphics System) with X Windows to form PEX
(PHIGS Extensions to X). PHIGS is an international
standard for three-dimension graphics,
Many organizations, like Argonne, where
simulation is and has been a long standing necessity,
have a large investment in simulation codes that
incorporate graphics libraries such as DISSPLA. The
size of the investment and the complexities of these
systems preclude arbitrarily moving them from the
Vax, IBM, or Cray computers where they now run. It
is extremely desirable to preserve this invesunent and
to realize a significant benefit for some incremental
cost. Local and vendor efforts in the area of
X-Windows graphics interfaces have served to
integrate traditional mainframe applications with
workstations to provide a new level of capability for
visualization. One such capability enables researchers
to view animation at their workstation as the
computation is being performed on a supercomputer or
mainframe,
Public domain applications, such as Imagetool
for the IBM PC and Apple Macintosh, and X-hnage
for X-Windows workstations (ali developed at NCSA),
provide substantial capabilities for two-dimensional
data analysis and animation. These tools require a
modest effort to revise simulations so that appropriate
image data can be generated. Products such as Dicer
from Spyglass Inc. for the Apple Macintosh and public
domain applications such as Data Slice from NCSA for
X Windows workstations provide the additional
capability for 3-D data analysis,
The ability to store complex 3-D objects, rotate
and view from arbitrary angles or perspectives and
produce animation sequences generally requires
significant processor speed, memory, and disk storage,
This requirement currently precludes the use of many
of the lower cost workstations. While available
graphics libraries have enabled programmers to
construct, display, and interact with three-dimensional
objects, products such as SUNVision available from
SUN Microsystems, and AVS, available from Stardent,
reduce the need for programming with a higher level
user interface, Rather than requiring users to write
programs or issue commands, these programs permit
users to interactively select, connect, and otherwise
manipulate objects to exercise the desired program
capabilities,
CONCLUSIONS
Scientific and engineering simulations are
clearly becoming an important part of scientific
investigation
and
engineering
design.
The
developments in fundamental phenomenological
modeling, numerical algorithms and software for
solving equations and computer processing power will
permit us to continue our exploration of ever more
complex systems. Interpretation of the simulated
results is where scientific visualization wiU play an
essential role. The projected improvements in
computing power andnumerical algorithms can be
expected to be matched by increases in workstation
performance will directly enable new visualization
capabilities. Price--performance Of workstations will
make visualizationcapabilitieswidelyavailable.
With
complementary developments in data and image
transmission capability, it is now becoming possible
for the scientific and engineering analyst to construct
and observe numerical "experiments" from their
desktop workstation. Visualization expedites the
analysis process, improves the productivity of the
practicing and can be considered a competitive edge.
Now is the time to investigate and incorporate
visualization to increase the effectiveness of simulation
activities.
Simulation developers and users must consider
how to proceed to realize benefits from visualization.
In addition to understanding the computational
requirements for simulation, it is important to
understand available visualization tools and relative
capabilities, and existing and emerging standards.
Like any new technology, visualization
collaboration carries a significant learning curve with
specialists in graphics and visualization methods can
expedite the process of incorporating visualization
capabilities with simulation applications.
