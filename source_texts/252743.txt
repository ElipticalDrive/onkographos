Continuous photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) imaging generates large volumes of data, resulting in significant storage demands. Here, we propose a high‐fidelity real‐time compression algorithm for PAM data in LabVIEW by combining Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) with adaptive thresholding and Run Length Encoding (RLE), which we term Adaptive Run Length Encoded DCT (AR‐DCT) compression. This algorithm reduces data storage requirements while preserving all the details of the images. AR‐DCT ensures real‐time compression, achieving superior compression ratios (CRs) compared to traditional DCT compression. We evaluated the performance of AR‐DCT using in vivo mouse brain imaging data, demonstrating a CR of ~50, with a structural similarity index of 0.980 and minimal degradation in signal quality (percentage‐root‐mean‐square‐difference of 1.345%). The results show that AR‐DCT outperforms traditional DCT, offering higher compression efficiency without significantly sacrificing image quality. These findings suggest that AR‐DCT provides an effective solution for applications requiring continuous experiments, such as cerebral hemodynamics studies.

1.Introduction
Photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) is a high‐resolution imaging configuration within the broader field of photoacoustic imaging [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In PAM, the imaging area is scanned point by point, either optically or acoustically, to acquire both spatial and axial information from each point [7]. These individual data are then merged to generate a 3D image. A higher number of points in an image results in more detailed sampling of valuable information from the tissue, but this comes at the expense of increased storage requirements [8]. For example, a single volume consisting of 1000 by 1000 points (pixels), with 1000 samples per point and 16 bits per sample, requires approximately 2 GB of storage. In many applications, continuous PAM imaging is essential to monitor, track, and quantify diagnostically relevant parameters under various conditions. For instance, when studying cerebral hemodynamics in mice under conditions such as hypoxia [9, 10], hindpaw electrical stimulation [7, 11, 12], or whisker stimulation [7, 13], images need to be acquired continuously over a period of several minutes. If the frame rate is 1 Hz and images are captured for 2 min, the total storage requirement increases to approximately 240 GB. More often, the data collection is repeated several times (n = 100) to establish statistical relevance [14, 15], adding the storage requirement to terabytes. This substantial data load places a significant burden on computational systems, impeding data capture, storage, and transportation capability in real time.
Razansky et al. [16] implemented an adaptive real‐time signal compression algorithm on photoacoustic tomographic images of a beating heart using wavelet transform (WT). However, their algorithm was more advantageous when signals are low density. PAM signals typically are high density, representing lots of structural details; therefore, an algorithm with minimal fidelity loss is recommended for PAM imaging. Many research groups have implemented deep learning‐based downsampling methods to compress the PAM images [17, 18]. However, those systems (1) require a very large chunk of data for training deep learning models and (2) are specific to certain parameters, i.e., any change in the imaging system characteristics (transducer, field‐of‐view) will affect the outcome of the deep learning models.
In our previous study, we explored transformation‐based compression techniques to reduce the storage size of the PAM data. These techniques resulted in a structural similarity index of approximately 88%, indicating that the compression was lossy [8, 19]. These compression algorithms were streamed line in the flow of data capture and data storage in LabVIEW, to ensure real time data compression.
In this paper, we propose a modification to one of the transformation‐based compression techniques, specifically the discrete cosine transform (DCT). By integrating run length encoding (RLE) and adaptive thresholding, we develop an adaptive run length encoded DCT (AR‐DCT) compression algorithm that can preserve nearly all the image details while significantly reducing the storage requirement, achieving near real‐time compression with minimal loss in image quality. To evaluate the impact of compression on image fidelity, we conducted several quantitative assessments, including parametric feature analysis to examine the preservation of diagnostically relevant information, such as the diameter and intensity of vascular structures. This algorithm was applied in the flow to data acquisition and data storage to ensure real‐time data compression.
2.Materials and Methods2.1.
PAM Imaging Setup
Laser scanning photoacoustic microscopy (LS‐PAM) setup is used to acquire PAM images. Details of the imaging setup are available in our previous publications [20, 21, 22, 23]. Briefly, a pulsed Nd: YAG laser with a central wavelength of 532 nm (S9, RFHLASER, Shenzhen, China) was used as an excitation source with a wavelength of 532 nm and a pulse repetition rate (PRR) of 80 kHz. A two‐dimensional galvanometer is used to optically scan an imaging area of 1.2 × 1.2 cm2. Triangular waveforms are generated in LabVIEW and transmitted to the galvanometer via an analog output card (BNC 2110, National Instruments) to enable bidirectional scanning. A 2.25 MHz ultrasound transducer (C306‐SU, Olympus, USA) with an active area of 0.5 in. is used to detect photoacoustic (PA) signals from the imaging object; please see the rationale for using the C306‐SU in the configuration of our PAM in [7]. The distance and angle of the transducer relative to the imaging object are optimized to ensure uniform sensitivity throughout the imaging area. A total of 800 × 800 points is acquired with a fetch rate of 12.5 μs (PRR = 80 KHz). The detected PA signals from each point are amplified using two low‐noise ZFL‐500LN amplifiers and digitized at a sampling rate of 200 MS/s by a 4‐channel digitizer (CSE1642, GAGE, USA) in LabVIEW. After signal acquisition, data is compressed in real time before being stored. The protocol for PA signal acquisition and compression in LabVIEW is shown in Figure 1a.

2.2.Data Compression Algorithm
We have previously reported that DCT is one of the most computationally light transform‐based algorithms to perform data compression in real time. Briefly, the DCT is applied to the input PA signals and thresholding is performed on the output coefficients of the signals to remove illicit information in the signals (background noise). Anything below the threshold value is zeroed out. By performing thresholding, we are essentially compressing the size of the data to be stored [24, 25]. The higher the threshold value, the higher the CR at the expense of the degradation of image quality [26]. To avoid image degradation or loss of any diagnostically relevant data, we applied an adaptive thresholding method along with a run length encoding technique to compress the data with a higher CR without losing relevant information, as shown in Figure 1b. First, for each acquired PA signal, we apply the DCT. Next, we perform adaptive thresholding on the transformed PA signals. For each signal, we calculate the maximum and mean values of the frequency coefficients. Based on these values, we determine the threshold: if the mean of the coefficients is less than or equal to the 25th percentile of the maximum (Case 1), we choose the 25th percentile of the maximum as the threshold value. Otherwise (Case 2), we select the mean value as the threshold, as shown in Figure 1c. This adaptive threshold criterion was developed empirically, as we found that the information below the 25th percentile of the maximum (Case 1) does not contain any diagnostically relevant features, and all the vascular details are preserved. This criterion was additionally confirmed when we performed parametric feature assessments to attest to the high‐fidelity compression (discussed in next section). Additionally, some signals may contain mostly background noise, where applying a higher threshold (Case 2) helps eliminate most of the noise, thereby improving the signal quality. Following adaptive thresholding, we reduce the precision of the DCT coefficients using bit quantization. This is done by rounding the coefficients to the nearest integer. Lastly, we apply the run length encoding method to the DCT coefficients and store the data. For each signal, RLE produces different lengths of encoded data, depending on the reproducibility of the DCT coefficients. Therefore, to compile variable length encoded data into a single file, data clustering was performed in LabVIEW. This entire data compression process per signal takes 10 μs, enabling real‐time compression while optimizing storage efficiency. The compressed data is stored on the storage device, while decompression is performed offline afterwards.
During decompression, run‐length decoding is applied, followed by inverse DCT to reconstruct the original time‐domain signal. The reconstructed signals are then used for image reconstruction and further analysis (see Figure 1d). This offline decompression approach ensures minimal impact on real‐time acquisition while allowing efficient data retrieval for diagnostic or quantitative assessments.

2.4.Animal Model
For our in vivo experiments, we used a 4‐week‐old CD‐1 mouse weighing 40 g. To perform brain imaging, the mouse was anesthetized with 4% isoflurane via inhalation, followed by maintenance doses of 2%–3% isoflurane. Then, the hair on the skin was trimmed and removed using a hair trimmer and depilatory lotion. Next, the scalp was carefully excised with scissors to expose the skull. The exposed skull was gently rubbed with a cotton swab to remove any debris and soft tissue, and the area was then cleansed with sterile water. Ultrasound gel was applied to the exposed skull to ensure proper acoustic coupling. A 1.2 × 1.2 cm2 area was imaged. After the study was completed, the mouse was euthanized. During the experiment, data was continuously acquired and compressed for 1 min. All procedures were in accordance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and approved by the University of Illinois, Chicago, Animal Care and Use Committee. There were no interruptions during the data acquisition process; each PA signal acquired by our PAM system was compressed in real‐time.
3.Results
We applied our developed compression algorithm on in vivo brain data acquired from a mouse. 800 × 800 points were imaged with 1000 samples per point. The data is acquired with and without our data compression algorithm in the pipeline between data capture and data storage performed in LabVIEW (see Figure 1a).
Figure 2a (i) and (ii) show the original and decompressed images by AR‐DCT, respectively. In Figure 2a (i) three regions are marked by white arrows. PA signals from these regions before (blue line) and after (red line) data compression are compared in Figure 2b (i)–(iii). These points are acquired from distinct backgrounds to show the capability of the AR‐DCT compression algorithm in fully recovering varying quality PA signals after compression. Moreover, the line profiles across brain images in Figure 2a (i) and (ii) are plotted in Figure 2b (iv). These line profiles show no major change in intensity patterns, validating that there is no significant fidelity loss in the images after data compression. The slight discrepancies observed in the line profiles are a result of the lossy nature of DCT compression. While DCT efficiently reduces data redundancy, it introduces minor inefficiencies due to quantization, where lower‐energy frequency components are slightly altered or discarded. This causes small variations in pixel intensity, particularly in areas with fine details or noise. However, these differences remain minimal, as evidenced by the mean squared error (MSE) of 0.0014 and a high correlation of 98.68% between the original and compressed images. These values indicate that, although compression is not perfectly lossless, the structural integrity and overall intensity patterns are well preserved, ensuring that the compression process does not introduce significant distortions in the reconstructed images.

Overall, our developed algorithm achieved a CR of ~46.6 with PRD of 1.345%, SSIM of 0.98, RMSE of 0.019, and Corr of 0.972 from our algorithm. We also calculated the parametric features, and both VDS and VIS were very close to 1 (0.986 and 0.982), respectively. These parameters advocate a superior compression performance to our previously reported compression algorithm that only used DCT and hard thresholding, as shown in Figure 2a (iii). We compared the results of DCT compression with our developed compression technique. For fair comparison, we kept the CR comparable. This quantitative comparison is summarized in Table 1.

From Table 1, we can observe that although the traditional DCT compression method is about two times faster, the fidelity loss in the compressed images is significant (PRD: 67.332% and SSIM: 0.631). To achieve similar quality images (comparable VDS and VIS) with traditional DCT compression, we reduced the threshold level and observed that with a CR of 12.4, traditional DCT showed comparable compression performance to our developed AR‐DCT.
AR‐DCT with adaptive thresholding and RLE significantly outperforms DCT compression by improving compression efficiency and preserving data quality. Adaptive thresholding discards less significant DCT coefficients, retaining only the most important ones, which reduces data size without noticeable quality loss. RLE further compresses the data by efficiently storing sequences of repeated values, particularly zeros, resulting in additional space savings [27]. This combination leads to higher compression ratios while maintaining high fidelity performance (PRD, RMSE, VDS, and VIS).
We developed adaptive thresholding criteria (illustrated in Figure 1c) based on an empirical study where we varied percentile values from 15% to 50% in increments of 0.25% and evaluated the resulting quality metrics after compression using AR‐DCT. To effectively quantify the impact of our adaptive thresholding criteria, we realized that traditional quality metrics, such as SSIM and PRD, were insufficient for accurately assessing the fidelity loss in images. To address this gap, we introduced two new quality metrics, which we named VDS and VIS, to comprehensively evaluate the effects of compression on image quality. Given that PAM is a high‐resolution imaging technique, with resolutions in the few microns, any changes in vascular diameter or intensity due to compression could compromise the informational integrity of the images. For example, evaluating skin diseases, vasodilation and vasoconstriction are crucial parameters, and any compression‐induced changes in vascular diameter could lead to misdiagnoses. Additionally, vascular intensity is vital in PAM images, particularly when acquiring hemodynamic data and quantifying molecular concentrations in tissue. By incorporating these parameters, we were able to establish adaptive thresholding criteria that minimize fidelity loss while maintaining image quality. The compression time for each parameter remained consistent at 10 μs.
Figure 4a illustrates mouse brain images at various thresholding percentiles: no compression (Figure 4a (i)), 25th percentile (Figure 4a (ii)), 30th percentile (Figure 4a (iii)), 35th percentile (Figure 4a (iv)), and 42nd percentile (Figure 4a (v)). A line profile, drawn across the dotted lines in Figure 4a (i), is presented in Figure 4b, showing that as the threshold percentile increases, fine structural details (indicated by peaks and valleys in the line profile) are lost. We calculated SSIM and CR for all percentiles from 15% to 50% and observed that up to the 25th percentile, there was no significant change in SSIM. At the 25th percentile, the CR was 46.6, SSIM was 0.981, VDS was 0.986, and VIS was 0.92. However, beyond the 25th percentile, image quality began to degrade, with SSIM, CR, VDS, and VIS dropping to 0.89, 83, 79.2, and 54.3, respectively, at the 35th percentile. At the 42nd percentile, SSIM decreased to 0.64, CR rose to 132, and the loss of information became more significant (VDS = 0.55 and VIS = 0.32). As a result, we determined that the 25th percentile threshold offered an optimal balance, providing a high CR with minimal loss in image fidelity. We observed that changes in thresholding had a more significant impact on VDS and VIS than on SSIM. While SSIM is a robust metric, it struggles to fully distinguish between vascular structures and noisy background, making it insufficient for a comprehensive assessment of image quality. As a result, manual inspection using VDS and VIS was essential to evaluate the images accurately. The reason for the pronounced changes in VDS and VIS with thresholding is that the DCT transform, while effective, does not always accurately decompose signals into cosine coefficients. A more advanced transformation could help mitigate this limitation and improve the quality of decomposition. One such transform is the WT, that has the potential to preserve more diagnostically relevant features in the images compared to DCT [28, 29, 30]. However, WT methods are computationally more demanding due to their increased complexity in both the transformation and processing stages [31]. This makes them less practical for real‐time data acquisition and storage systems like LabVIEW, where efficient, high‐speed processing is essential for continuous data streams. Moreover, we cannot apply RLE directly to time domain PA signals [32], making the whole compression process lossless, as PA signals in general do not have any repeated patterns. More complex encoding methods can perhaps be applied to these time domain PA signals like Huffman encoding (HE) and Lempel‐Ziv‐Welch (LZW) to make compression lossless. However, Huffman Encoding and LZW are more computationally intensive due to the need for frequency analysis, tree construction (Huffman) [33, 34], and dynamic dictionary management (LZW) [35, 36], which result in higher computational complexity.

We performed DCT and WT compression with RLE, HE, and LZW on PAM data and evaluated their performance using our quality metrics. The results are summarized in the Table 2. For all compression algorithms, the optimal parameters were selected based on our empirical study. Among the methods tested, our AR‐DCT algorithm was the fastest and the only one suitable for integration into a real‐time data acquisition pipeline for compression in LabVIEW (CT = 10 μs, PRR = 12.5 μs). While DCT with HE produced slightly better results, its computational time increased to 14 μs. Significant improvements were observed with DCT and LZW, particularly in terms of CR, but the computational time rose to 30 μs. In contrast, wavelet‐based compression methods performed well in terms of SSIM, VDS, and VIS when compared to DCT methods, though they came with higher computational costs. WT with RLE produced slightly lower CR because the “sym2” wavelet coefficients did not exhibit many repeated patterns that RLE could effectively encode. Additionally, it was about five times more computationally intensive than AR‐DCT. WT with HE performed similarly to DCT with HE, but the best overall performance in terms of all quality parameters was achieved with WT + LZW, which achieved a compression ratio of 86.3, with SSIM, VDS, and VIS values of 0.997, 0.994, and 0.995, respectively. However, its computational time was approximately 10 times longer than that of AR‐DCT.

To further enhance the implementation of these sophisticated algorithms in LabVIEW, future work could focus on optimizing performance through loop parallelization using LabVIEW's multicore support. Additionally, offloading computationally intensive tasks to GPUs via the LabVIEW GPU Toolkit could significantly reduce processing times, enabling faster and more efficient real‐time data compression. This approach would help fully leverage the potential of advanced compression methods like wavelet‐based algorithms, ensuring their seamless integration into data acquisition pipelines.
4.Conclusion
The proposed AR‐DCT algorithm offers a significant improvement in data compression for PAM imaging. By integrating adaptive thresholding with RLE, the AR‐DCT algorithm achieved a higher compression ratio while preserving the critical diagnostic information in the images. Our results demonstrate that AR‐DCT outperforms the traditional DCT‐based compression method in terms of compression efficiency, with a compression ratio of 46.6, while maintaining image quality as evidenced by the high SSIM (0.981) and low PRD (1.345%). Furthermore, AR‐DCT provides real‐time compression, making it suitable for continuous PAM imaging applications that require large volumes of data to be processed and stored efficiently. This method not only reduces storage requirements but also ensures the retention of essential image details, thus improving the practical feasibility of real‐time, high‐resolution imaging in research and clinical settings. While alternative transform‐based methods such as WT could offer even better preservation of diagnostically relevant features, they are computationally more demanding and less practical for real‐time systems like LabVIEW. The combination of AR‐DCT with efficient data storage and real‐time processing in LabVIEW makes this algorithm a valuable tool for large‐scale, continuous imaging applications in biological research.
