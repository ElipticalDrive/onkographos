Parabolic Sphere Tracing Of Signed Distance Fields
For Old Glass Modelling And Rendering
Quentin Huan, François Rousselle, Christophe Renaud
Abstract—We present a method for modeling and rendering irregular and heterogeneous glass objects, with a specific
emphasis on stained glass windows and window works often
encountered in architecture from middle age to 18th century.
The artisanal production of sheet glass results in glass panels
displaying a vast variety of surface and volume irregularities
like bubbles, irregular surface or smoothly varying refractive
index, all of which contribute to the specific visual aspect of old
glass. We propose to account for all the aforementioned effects
in a unified framework based on signed distance functions and
an analytic solution of the ray tracing equations on tetrahedral
volume elements. We demonstrate how to construct an unbiased
estimator for the transmitted lighting produced by such panels
by using Fermat’s principle and results from seismic ray theory.
We use texture coordinates to map arbitrary sections of a
complex glass panel onto the individual faces of a mesh, allowing
the modeling and rendering of complex 3-dimensional objects
composed of colored glass facets such as stained glass windows.
Index Terms—Raytracing, Sampling, Stochastic methods,
Shading
I. I NTRODUCTION
VER the last twenty years, the virtual restoration of
ancient environments that have disappeared or deteriorated has become an essential tool for historians, both as part
of their research and for communicating their results to the
public. This has been made possible not only by a spectacular
increase in the computing capacity of computers but also by
major advances in rendering algorithms. It is now possible
to produce realistic simulations of the lighting prevailing in a
targeted building in order to gain a better understanding of the
conditions in which our ancestors lived and worked.

From time immemorial, buildings have been equipped with
windows to provide light indoor. These have gradually been
fitted with more or less complex glass panels, providing insulation from the outside while still allowing light to pass through.
However, the production of high quality glass is relatively
recent and old glass, as used in windows and stained glass,
had specific visual characteristics that greatly impacted the
lighting of indoor scenes and the way the outside environment
was perceived.
The visual differences between old glass windows and our
modern glazing mostly come from the artisanal manufacturing
techniques that were used by our ancestors for flat glass
panel production. The two main techniques of interest in this
article, which co-existed until the end of the 18th century,
are crown and cylinder glassblowing. Each of which produces
characteristic irregularities, ranging from slight surface deformations to bubbles or even spatial variations in refractive
index. These irregularities, however small, produce distortions
in the perception of the external environment and complex
lighting patterns known as caustics when illuminated by a light
source (see figure 1). In this article we will concentrate on the
cylinder blowing technique, but our results also apply to crown
glass.
Cylinder blown sheet glass (see figure 2) was produced
by blowing a cylinder (a) and cutting its extremities and
transversal side (b). The cylinder was then unfolded in an oven
(c), producing a large sheet of glass with surface irregularities
that gives to cylinder glass its characteristic look. This process
allowed the production of larger glass panels and was widely
used during the 18th century and after.
Bubbles may appear when impurities are present in the raw
materials, from manipulation by the glass-maker or when the
glass paste is insufficiently hot. Once they appear, they often
get elongated during the blowing and shaping of the panel and
may exist in a large variety of size and shape even inside a
single glass piece.
Additionally, the glass paste itself is not guaranteed to be
perfectly homogeneous. This can happen when several glass
pastes of different compositions are mixed inside the crucible,
or when foreign glass filaments inside the furnace get mixed
with the primary glass while still hot and malleable. Due
to their different composition, it results in a continuously
varying index of refraction inside the material. One of the
numerous examples of such phenomena are chords, which are
long refractive streaks that run inside the panel (see figure 3).
The foreign filaments of glass form thin lines of material that
lightly protrude on the surface and locally bend light rays.

The modeling and simulation of patrimonial glass objects
using computer graphics techniques was already tackled by
Kider et al. [1] to simulate the interior lighting that existed
through the 8th and 10th century in the mosque of Córdoba.
The lighting was provided by oil lamp fixtures composed
of a round glass body filled with some amount of water,
producing complex lighting patterns. While their work allows
for a general restitution of the lighting provided by such
objects, they do not explicitly model the fine details of the
glass surface or water ripples, thus failing at reproducing the
lighting in its full complexity. Light response capture and
relighting of stained glass was studied by Thanikachalam et
al. [2]. Their work accounts for the complex optical properties
of stained glass by first constructing a dictionary out of
illumination and light response image pairs acquired in a
controlled environment. They then use this dictionary to build
sparse matrices representing the light response of several
stained glass windows from in situ image acquisition. Their
work however does not support strong directional lighting
and would not be adapted to render scenes like Fig.1 where
most of the lighting is provided by direct sunlight. Grobe et
al. [3] capture the BSDF of antique Roman window glass
by goniophotometric measurements of archaeological samples.
By its nature, their work can reproduce scattering created by
the local micro structure of the glass but fails at reproducing
more global effects produced by medium scale irregularities.
B. Non-linear ray tracing
While rays propagate in straight lines in an homogeneous
medium, some materials such as amber, wax or hot air have
a continuously varying index of refraction that causes rays to
propagate along curves described by the ray tracing equations
of geometric optics [4]. Non-linear ray tracing in rendering
may be done by solving the ray tracing equations using
an explicit Newton or Runge-Kutta method [5][6], but these
techniques may require a large number of steps to produce
images of satisfying quality.

Analytical ray tracing consists in approximating the
medium’s refractive index field (RIF) by a collection of
volume elements (e.g. tetrahedral cells) inside which an analytical solution of the ray tracing equations is known [7]. The
rendering of heterogeneous refractive objects then consists in
tracing rays across the RIF, traversing the cells one after the
other. These traversal steps make extensive use of intersection
tests between rays and cell boundaries, which generally must
be done using a bisection method in absence of closed form
solutions.
Many wave propagation phenomena outside of optics can be
described by the ray tracing equations and have been studied
in depth in other fields of physics. In acoustics, Mo et al.
[8] present a few alternative closed form expressions for ray
trajectories and show how some of them admit a closed form
solution for ray and cell boundary intersections. Comparing
with [7], they show a significant improvement in traversal
performance. In seismology, Virieux et al. [9] used a similar
framework to trace curved rays in earth underground using
a regular tetrahedral mesh. Using a different parametrization,
they establish simple second order polynomial expressions for
ray trajectories in Cartesian coordinates. Because the rays are
piecewise second order polynomials, the intersection with one
of the four planar cell boundaries can be computed by solving
a quadratic equation.
C. Refractive radiative transfer
In presence of spatially varying refractive index, the well
known radiative transfer equation (RTE) [10] is replaced
by the Refractive radiative transfer equation (RRTE) [11].
This equation is similar to the RTE, although the conserved
quantity along the curved rays is the basic radiance defined
by L̃ = η2L(x) where L is the radiance and η is the RIF. In this
context, any variance reduction techniques based on light path
vertices connections become inapplicable since two vertices
may now be connected by one or several curved paths that
are non trivial to determine. In turn, Ament et al. [11] propose
the use of volumetric photon mapping which biases the basic
radiance estimation but greatly reduces variance. Pediredla et
al. [12] tackle this problem frontally by differentiating the
ray tracing equations and designing an iterative procedure to
connect two vertices inside a refractive participative medium,
allowing the use of bidirectional path tracing estimators to
solve the RRTE. Their two-point connection method seems
to be specific to rendering in a medium that has scattering
properties, which is however not generally the case for windows made of clear glass, where the two points to connect
are often separated by a non-scattering heterogeneous medium
and specular interfaces. Additionally, since their work involves
a bidirectional path tracer it cannot sample effectively the
Specular-Diffuse-Specular paths that are often encountered
when rendering caustics.
As demonstrated by Ihrke et al. [6], adaptative wavefront
tracing can also be used to compute illumination produced by
refractive objects by the use of the intensity law of geometric
optics. The 3-dimensional irradiance and directional field of
the illumination is then saved inside a voxel grid. According
3
to the authors, the approach however does not seem practical
for general lighting computation since depending on the scene
complexity and spatial extent, a prohibitively high voxel grid
resolution may be required to store a faithful estimation of the
transmitted or reflected lighting.
D. Signed distance function
Implicitly defined surfaces hold a number of advantages
over explicit surface representations (e.g. triangle meshes)
such as simple interior testing and robust support for boolean
operations used in constructive geometry modeling. We found
these advantages to be valuable for the modeling of hand
blown glass objects since the use of signed distance functions
makes it easier to support slight surface perturbations and
bubbles. We review in the following paragraph a few recent
works on the ray marching of implicit surfaces along curved
rays.
Sphere tracing [13] is a well known iterative procedure to
compute ray intersections with surfaces defined by a signed
distance function (SDF). Seyb et al. [14] show how to extend
sphere tracing to implicit surfaces deformed by a non-linear
transformation D. Instead of stepping along a straight ray until
the deformed geometry is reached, they instead step along a
ray deformed by the transformation D−1 until the undeformed
geometry is intersected. They use a modified version of sphere
tracing where a Runge-Kutta solver with variable step size
both solves the ray trajectory and finds the intersection with
the zero isosurface describing the object. Similarly Pediredla
et al. [12] uses a symplectic integrator to compute a curved ray
trajectory in a heterogeneous participative medium displaying
continuously varying index of refraction. Their solver adapts
its step size to stop the computation where the ray trajectory
intersects the interface of the medium implicitly defined by a
distance function.
E. Caustic rendering
One of the most striking visual effects produced by the specular interaction of light with transparent objects is certainly
the production of complex caustic patterns. These patterns are
notoriously difficult to render by path tracing since variance
reduction techniques based on vertex connections such as Next
Event Estimation (NEE) fail in presence of specular occluders.
Techniques based on photon maps allow a biased estimation of these complex lighting effects by emitting photons
from light sources and then estimating their contribution to
the lighting of a shaded point by kernel density estimation
[15] [16]. Metropolis Light Transport (MLT) and its variants
[17] [18] use the Metropolis Hasting integration algorithm to
locally explore path space, allowing the discovery of sparse,
but highly contributing light paths that characterize caustics.
A numerical solver can also be used to explicitly search
for the valid specular paths connecting two vertices. Pioneer
work from Mitchell et al. [19] uses interval arithmetic and
Fermat’s principle to find all the stationary paths (with respect
to optical path length) connecting a light source to a point
through specular reflection with a curved mirror. Intensity is
computed by applying the intensity law of geometric optics. In

B. Non-linear ray tracing
Chords are results of heterogeneity inside the glass paste
that produces a spatially varying RIF. The slight deformations
of the glass surface produced when a chord is close to a face
can be incorporated into the panel’s SDF. However, it seems
difficult to reproduce the visual effects due to the spatially
varying RIF in this SDF, without first carrying out an inverse
rendering study to match the two effects. In order to obtain
visual distortions as pronounced as those observed in figure
3, the surface would have to be highly deformed where the
chords are located, which is not the case in the samples shown
in figure 1 and 3. The visual result would also likely differ
from the true effect of the chords depending on the angle
of observation relative to the panel. We therefore specifically
model a variable RIF, which involves non-linear ray-tracing.
We chose to make use of the works in theoretical seismology
of Virieux [9] and Farra [24] that demonstrate how a wellchosen expression for the slowness (analogous to the refractive
index of optics) profile within earth underground defined
inside a tetrahedral cell gives simple second order polynomial
solutions for ray trajectories. Rays in the vicinity of a central
curved ray (paraxial rays) can be computed to first order
by updating a ray propagator matrix during ray traversal.
They can be seen as a generalization of the ray differentials
[25] often used in computer graphics and will be a useful
tool for building our two-point tracing method described in
Section IV. Ray propagation inside a complex RIF can then
be simulated by assembling multiple tetrahedral cells and
tracing the piecewise polynomial ray one cell after the other.
The intersection of a polynomial ray subsection with a cell
boundary can be computed exactly by solving a quadratic
equation. This contrasts with other parametrization choices
(such as [8]) that lead to trajectories for light rays that are
expressed in terms of transcendental functions. Their approach
is described in subsections 1 to 4.
One of the main difficulties in dealing with non-linear rays
is the computation of their intersections with scene geometry.
For intersecting the panel’s SDF, we naively step along the
computed parabolic ray trajectory, much like regular sphere
tracing as described in subsection 5.

We detail in this section how to estimate the transmitted
caustic produced by a lighted glass panel, a common case
in ancient architecture where most of the lighting would be
provided by sunlight transmitted across windows. The core
idea of this work is a generalization of the Next Event
Estimation technique [28] which consists in finding a direct
path connecting a receptor point and a point on a light source.
If these paths can be simple to determine in a medium with a
constant index of refraction (two mutually visible points within
the same medium can be connected by a straight line) they
can be difficult to find in the presence of refractive interfaces
or refractive media. In general, multiple paths connecting the
two points exist, each of which have to be found to ensure
unbiasedness. To connect two points across both refractive
interfaces and refractive media, we propose a method based
on Fermat’s principle. However, bubbles will be considered
as opaque obstacles in order to limit the complexity of path
estimation.

V. M ULTI PIECE OBJECTS
Because of the relative difficulty of making large flat glass
sheets, ancient windows were generally composed of several
smaller fragments. The craftsman would cut individual flat
pieces of glass out of a source panel (or possibly multiple
panels which can have different colors) and assemble them
together with lead. We can approximate this workflow by
constructing a mesh that represents the window. Each face of
the mesh will have its UV coordinates mapped on a particular
section of the source panel and a transformation T will be
used to map a 3-dimensional section of the source panel onto
the face (see figure 12). This method is significantly different
from texture mapping as it allows to map a full section of the
source panel (with its corresponding geometric displacement,
bubble distribution and varying index of refraction) onto a face
instead of a 2D texture like a normal map.
This process eases the modeling of complex windows and
stained glass panels where the geometry of the source panels
was often exploited in an artistic way by the craftsman (e.g. by
using the variable depth of the glass to make a color gradient).

VI. R ESULTS
A. Panel modeling
Our geometric framework can represent a wide variety of
panel types such as cylinder or crown blown glass, handling surface deformation, bubbles and heterogeneous index
of refraction. Our work is well adapted to represent clear
glass windows as shown in figure 1, modern style stained
glass windows (see supplementary materials) or more complex
objects made of individual glass facets like in figure 13.
We have implemented our geometric framework inside a
custom shape plugin for the Mitsuba3 rendering engine. All
the path traced renders have been computed using the standard
path tracing integrator of Mitsuba3 using an Nvidia RTX
A6000 GPU.
B. Caustic estimator
We have implemented the transmitted caustic estimator described in section IV-C as an estimator plugin for the Mitsuba3
rendering engine. To our knowledge, methods for computing
caustics that are unbiased and able to take care of both specular
interfaces and spatially varying refractive index do not exist.
The work of Pediredla [12] may seem relevant in this context,
however as it involves a bidirectional path tracer, it cannot
sample effectively the Specular-Diffuse-Specular paths that are
often encountered when rendering caustics (we provide an
illustration of this issue in supplementary material). For this
reason, we have resorted to comparing our caustic estimation
method with the standard path tracing integrator of Mitsuba3.
This comparison will use an eighteenth century restitution
scene of the Notre-Dame Bridge in Paris (see figure 14 and
supplementary materials).
Our solver behaves the best on panels with relatively smooth
surface perturbations and RIF where the number of possible
solutions is small. In this context, our method allows for fast
estimation of the transmitted caustic, which also benefits the
estimation of indirect lighting in scenes where most of the
lighting is provided through a glass panel.

sets of SDF, bubbles and RIF voxel grids.

B. Variance reduction and outlier removal.
Like similar methods based on Fermat’s principle or specular constraints, the performance of our estimator is tightly
bound to the panel geometry and RIF complexity. The solver
may also have low convergence rates for strong geometric displacement or RIF variations, further increasing the estimation
difficulty and resulting in parts of the caustic featuring high
variance or appearing darker than ground truth. The bottom
row of figure 14 illustrates such phenomena on a rougher panel
with stronger surface and RIF perturbations.
The number of possible connections increases dramatically
for panels featuring finely detailed geometric displacements,
making the unbiased inverse probability estimation costly
[29]. This can be alleviated by using a biased inverse PDF
estimation as in [21] which may trade faster estimation for
local energy loss. Recent work exploiting the continuity of the
specular manifold to provide importance sampling of specular
chains [34] may also be explored to reduce the variance of
our estimator.
Our implementation is susceptible of producing outliers that
may be partially eliminated by regularizing the generalized
geometric factor. This can however lead a to local underestimation of the caustic. This phenomenon could also be alleviated
by using a Monte Carlo estimator more robust to outliers such
as [35].
C. Other optical effects
Wavelength-dependent IOR, and birefringence [36] could
be taken into account, preferably with spectral rendering.
This phenomenon, present in modern glasses, has not been
considered in this work. We have no indication that this
property is present in older glasses, but its consideration may
be the subject of future work.
One of the main limitations of our caustic estimation method
is the current lack of support for scattering participative media
inside translucent glass, which would amount to solve the
refractive radiative transfert equation. We believe however
that, in the spirit of [12], the two point tracing procedure
described in section IV could be used to construct a path
tracing estimator able to solve the RRTE inside the glass. This
would open opportunities to render translucent materials in
addition to perfectly transparent ones.
A proper model to capture the full complexity of painted
glass may be investigated to properly model figurative stained
glass, since our model only accounts for transparent, eventually colored, glass.
VIII. C ONCLUSION
We have presented a complete framework to model and render complex hand-blown glass objects using signed distance
functions coupled with non-linear ray tracing. This model handles in a single primitive multiple geometrical characteristics
of ancient glass such as surface displacement, presence of
numerous bubbles and heterogeneous refractive index. The
heterogeneous refractive index of refraction inside the panel
is described by a regular grid of tetrahedral cells for which
the ray trajectories have simple analytical solutions. Our work
puts a special emphasis on clear and stained glass windows, for
12
which we propose an unbiased estimator for the two refractive
bounce transmitted lighting of a light source through such an
object. Complex objects made of individual planar facets can
be assembled on the fly using UV coordinates, where each
facet is mapped to an arbitrary location on a source glass
panel. This process is inspired by the manual assembly of
stained glass where the artist can use the irregular geometry
of the panel to satisfy an aesthetic goal.
