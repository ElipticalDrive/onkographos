ABSTRACT
We present RayGraphy display technology that renders volumetric
graphics by superimposing the trajectories of lights in indoor space
filled with fog. Since the traditional FogScreen approach requires
the shaping of a thin layer of fog, it can only show two-dimensional
images in a narrow range that is close to the fog-emitting nozzle.
Although a method that renders volumetric graphics with plasma
generated using high-power laser was also proposed, its operation
in a public space is considered quite dangerous. The proposed system mainly comprises dozens of laser projectors circularly arranged
in a fog-filled space, and renders volumetric graphics in a fog by
superimposing weak laser beams from the projectors. Compared
to the conventional methods, this system employing weak laser

INTRODUCTION
Over the last half-century, technologies that display three-dimensional
(3D) images in the real world have attracted much attention. Star
SUI ’20, October 31-November 1, 2020, Virtual Event, Canada
Wars: Episode IV - A New Hope [1] released in 1977 had depicted a
futuristic scene in which volumetric graphics portraying Princess
Leia are projected in the air. Numerous subsequent films have also
shown this type of aerial display technology. Researchers have
studied various approaches to actualize the concept referred to as
Augmented Reality (AR) [11] as well. One typical AR approach
is to have the user use devices such as a handheld device with a
head-mounted display (HMD) [35, 38] and observe a real scene with
superimposed computer graphics (CG). However, this approach relies on the user device and this inherently has the problem that
only the user wearing the device can see the virtual objects.
Another approach is to draw the volumetric graphics directly in a
physical 3D space. Volumetric display is a technology that displays
volumetric graphics generated by arranging image elements called
voxels in a 3D space. Major methods in this field include stacked
transparent displays, rotating screens and displays [14, 19]. However, since these methods can display a volumetric graphics only
inside the devices, it is difficult to show them in a real space outside
of the devices and achieve touch interaction with the graphics.
As a more direct way to draw images in real space, aerial display
technologies that draw voxels by inducing plasma in the air with
nanosecond laser have been proposed [20, 33, 36]. Although the
laser-induced plasma technology that draws volumetric graphics in
a space without any light-reflecting material was innovative, these
have problems such as the dangerousness of the high-power laser,
noise when plasma is generated, and low resolutions of the image.
Ochiai et al. [33] studied the aerial display that allows users to touch
the induced plasma by adopting femtosecond laser, but it only covers an area approximately 1 cm3 . In addition, the femtosecond laser
source is categorised to Class 4 in Inter-national Electrotechnical
Commission (IEC) 60825-1:2014 [4], and it is strong enough to cause
damage to the eyes and skin, even if it is a temporary exposure.
FogScreen presented by Rakkolainen [34] is a method that produces a semitransparent screen that comprises water vapor in
the air. Unlike the plasma-based technology, it is not possible for
FogScreen to draw luminous voxels directly at any position without
fog. This walk-through aerial screen technology is very safe and
scalable. This makes it practical and has already been commercialized.
However, this method requires the formation of a thin layer of
fog. When the thickness of this fog screen increases, the trajectories
of lights from the projector shine due to scattering in the fog and
make it difficult to see the projected image. In other words, it can
only cover a narrow range of less than several meters where the fog
emitted from the nozzle can retain its flat shape. In addition, methods that project an image with 3D effects such as depth perception
and motion parallax using depth-fused 3D (DFD) or forward directional scattering were proposed [24, 44]; however, the projected
images are not volumetric graphics that allow users to view the
displayed images from any angle.
We propose RayGraphy, an aerial display technology that generates volumetric graphics by superimposing trajectories of light rays
illuminated from multiple directions in a fog-filled space as shown
in Fig. 1. We aim to construct a new spatial augmented reality platform that allows volumetric graphics to appear directly anywhere
in physical space. Different from a wearable interface, RayGraphy
does not target everyday life usage and is designed towards specific

applications because it requires a fog-filled room. Possible interaction and applications that RayGraphy can be applied are discussed
in Section 6.
In this paper, we implement the desktop size prototype shown in
Fig. 1a that consists of 32 projectors set up in a fog-filled room as the
first step toward our goal. Figure 1 shows the volumetric graphics of
Stanford Bunny [2] generated by the rays of light emitted from each
of the projectors. Conventional aerial imaging technologies [34]
use an image on a fog display where the fog is a thin layer so that
the trajectory of the light rays is not noticeable. In contrast to these,
RayGraphy fills innocuous fog into the space without the need to
generate a specific form and generates images in the air using light
rays.
RayGraphy has the following advantages. First, compared to
FogScreen [34], the projected image is not a two-dimensional (2D)
image but volumetric graphics that can be observed from any direction. Second, RayGraphy can freely control the 3D position of the
image within the angle of the projectors similar to the plasma-based
method [20, 33, 36]. In other words, the proposed method eliminates
complicated fog machines such as those having a large number of
nozzles arranged in a grid [22] or movable nozzles [23] to control
the position of the image. Third, RayGraphy is highly scalable for
projecting images anywhere within the viewing of the projector
in a fog-filled space, unlike conventional methods [22, 23, 44] that
require nozzles close to the projection surface. Furthermore, the
proposed system is quieter and safer than the laser-induced plasma
system, which uses a high power laser to generate plasma with
making noise.
On the other hand, RayGraphy clearly has some disadvantages.
For example, there is very low contrast of the projected images and
the system can only cover the indoor fog-filled space. Moreover,
there is a limitation to the shape and texture of the projected target.
In this paper, we discuss the advantages and disadvantages based
on an implemented prototype and its evaluation.
The remaining sections of this paper discuss the following: First,
we introduce previous research on aerial display technologies including mid-air display, 3D display, and tangible display. Second,
we describe the principles and design parameters of the proposed
method. Third, we introduce the prototype and confirm its characteristics and performance. Finally, we discuss limitations and future
work.

RELATED WORK
We summarize the related work in the fields of fog screen, 3D
display, and levitating display.
2.1
3D Fog Displays
FogScreen proposed by Rakkolainen et al. [34] pioneered a new
field regarding mid-air interaction. FogScreen forms an aerial flat
screen that enables the projection of a high-quality 2D image on
it. Mistable [29] is a tabletop system that combines a horizontal
interactive surface with fog screens. This system has two fog screens
and allows users to view images from two directions at the same
time.
Methods that show users a 3D image on a fog screen have been
researched. For example, Lee et al. [24] studied a method that shows
RayGraphy: Aerial Volumetric Graphics Rendered Using Lasers in Fog
DFD image using two fog screens arranged vertically, but the DFDbased method allows users to see a 3D image only from a very
narrow viewpoint.
Yagi et al. [44] proposed a method that displays a 3D image by
utilizing the forward directional scattering of Mie scattering. Since
fog comprising particles with a diameter of less than several dozen
micrometers has strong forward directional scattering characteristics referred to as Mie scattering, only the users facing the front of
the projector and fog screen can view the image. They displayed
a 3D image with three motion parallax by projecting the image
from three projectors that are offset by 20 degrees. In addition, this
method can theoretically display images with 360-degree motion
parallax by increasing the number of projectors. However, an image projected using this method is simply 2D images discretely
switched for each user horizontal viewpoint and is different from
volumetric graphics that offer correct 3D views for any viewpoint.
Research focusing on the depth of the fog screen has been proposed as well. Lam et al. [22] studied a method using fog screens
arranged in a grid to achieve an aerial screen with depth. Barnum et al. [10] also investigate an approach that generates aerial
multi-layered screens using water droplets. Lam et al. [23] proposed
linearly movable fog emitters to achieve an aerial screen with continuous depth. Although these researches achieved to control the
position of the images by projecting it according to the physical
position of the screen, it is difficult to build the prototype that can
cover entire room because it requires a number of emitters and
large movable mechanism
Some studies have constructed systems that have greater scalability related to their coverage area. An approach projecting an image
on a moving vortex ring ejected from an air cannon is proposed [40].
Lucciola [21] is a system that uses two air cannons. An image is
projected onto where the vortex rings from the cannons collide.
Gushed Diffusers [39] is sufficiently lightweight to be mounted on
a drone, and uses aerosol distribution from an off-the-shelf spray
as a fog screen.
Aerial displays using fog have been presented in the art field.
Light barrier [5] is an installation art that consists of a projector,
convex mirror array, and unformed fog. Light Sculpture - Line [8]
is installation art that draws a 3D image using dozens of steerable
spotlights. Light of Birth [7] also uses dozens of projectors and a fog
emitter. Although these methods can create volumetric graphics by
superimposing light rays similar to the proposed system, they did
not provide and reveal details of the principles, technologies, and
characteristics. In addition, Light barrier only focuses on simple
figures such as circles and toruses. Light of Birth adopts a device that
constantly sprays fog that quickly disappears, similar to traditional
fog screens. Moreover, both of these cannot offer users a 360-degree
viewing angle because the devices are attached to only one side.
2.2
Other 3D Displays
There have been many studies on technologies that do not rely on
goggle devices for 3D display. A major representative approach
among these is to display images based on binocular disparity in
the left and right eyes using a parallax barrier or lenticular lens
array [17, 26]. An approach has been investigated called the light
field display that regenerates discretized rays using a lens array
or multiple liquid-crystal panels [26, 43]. Since this approach uses
SUI ’20, October 31-November 1, 2020, Virtual Event, Canada
a beam of light to reproduce a 3D image, at first glance it seems
similar to the proposed system, but it uses a completely different
technology. The technology reproduces a 3D image by reproducing
the light field with a display; however, RayGraphy reproduces an
image by superimposing diffuse reflections of the trajectory of a
ray of light on the fog.
Instead of a flat display, technologies have been proposed that
make an image observable from 360-degrees. Ito et al. announced
RayModeler [16], which is a cylindrical display with a 360-degree
viewing angle. Makiguchi et al. [28] investigated a 360-degree interactive tabletop display that can display 3D images on itself. Nakamura et al. [31] studied a cylindrical transparent screen based on
holography.
There are methods that show volumetric graphics by arranging
light sources in 3D, and the research field is known as volumetric
display. A method has been proposed that reproduces volumetric
graphics by stacking transparent displays [14]. However, using
this approach it is difficult to achieve high resolution in the depth
direction due to transparency and wiring of the display. There is also
a method based on the afterimage effect to reproduce volumetric
graphics by moving the display or screen at high speed [19]. A
technique was also proposed in which two different frequency
lasers intersect and produce an image on glass [12]. Although these
3D displays can only produce images on a screen, laser-induced
plasma-based displays using nano or femtosecond lasers can emit
light at an arbitrary three-dimensional position [20, 33, 36].
Meanwhile, a 3D printer based on tomography [27] can manufacture 3D objects by projecting multiple angles with dynamic
light patterns on a liquid photopolymer. RayGraphy is different
from this tomography-based method; however, the concept behind
RayGraphy is a common in reconstructing a target by projecting a
low-dimensional image.
2.3
Materials for Levitating Interface
There is a way to physically display images physically in 3D space
using floating material as a screen. Ochiai et al. [32] studied a
method that can form sparse images in 3D space by controlling the
positions of small, lightweight particles with an acoustic-potential
field. LeviProps [30] achieves a floating thin film screen using
acoustic-potential fields
Lee et al. [25] demonstrated ZeroN, a device that controls the
3D positions of magnetic material using a strong magnet. A way
to control lightweight objects using air-jets has been proposed
as well [18]. Smalley et al. [37] presented volumetric display by
controlling small cellulose particles with a photophoretic-trap. One
proposal directly floats a light emitting diode equipped with a
wireless power supply and acoustic-potential field [41].
In contrast to the methods using invisible tethers, an approach
employing self-levitating objects was proposed. BitDrones [13] proposed aerial interaction using a swarm of autonomous controlled
drones. Shooting star [6] by Intel is an extremely large scale aerial
display comprising over one thousand drones. Yamada et al. [45]
also proposed a self-levitating omnidirectional display that comprises drones and a persistent vision display. However, these methods are high cost due to factors such as strict safety management
and operation of a large number of drones.

PROPOSED METHOD
In this section, we explain the mechanism and the architecture of
RayGraphy.
3.1
Light Scattering in Particles
We cannot observe light trajectories in a void where there is no scattering object. When lights pass through particles that are about the
size of the wavelength of visible light, many atoms in them become
scattering sources and emit light. This phenomenon called Mie
scattering has a complex radiation intensity pattern that depends
on the size of the particles. The Mie scattering radiation pattern
can be obtained by analyzing the data using Maxwell’s equations.
Intensity Isc of the scattered light may be expressed as a function
of five variables
Isc = Isc (c, d, θ, λ, n)
(1)
where c is the concentration, d is the particle diameter, θ is the
measuring angle, λ is the wavelength of the light, and n is the
refractive index of the particles relative to the surrounding medium
(for non-spherical particles, shape parameters must be added) [15].
Figure 2 shows arrays of the scattered intensity of unpolarized
light for each diameter simulated with PyMieScatt 1.7.6 [9]. Because
Mie scattering is a complex phenomenon, smaller particles do not
always have lesser directivity of scattering. However, as Fig. 2
illustrates, there is an overall tendency for larger particles to have
greater forward scattering, and the lights hardly diffuse in directions
other than the front.
The method proposed by Yagi et al. the forward directional diffusivity [44] requires strong forward directional scattering, i.e. large
particles, to increase the number of viewpoints. In contrast, our
method needs particles with omnidirectional directional scattering,
i.e. smaller particles, to draw volumetric graphics with the sum of
the light emitted from various directions.
3.2
Mechanism
Figure 3 shows an example using the number of projectors of N = 8.
These projectors are arranged in a circle toward the center. We
adopt laser projectors that can project a focus-free image because
the distance between the target and the projector is not constant.
The area where the system is installed is pre-filled with the harmless fog. Although traditional fog screens use a fog that disappears
in an instant and does not accumulate in the space, RayGraphy
requires that the space be filled with fog while it is working. Smaller
particle size fog tends to drift in the air for longer periods of time
because it falls slowly. Thus, the kind of fog that comprises small
particles floating for long periods of time is better for our use in
terms of not only scattering but available time.

the target from each position as shown in Fig. 3, and the volumetric
graphics are shown by superimposing rays of the 2D images. Space

It is also clear from Fig. 3 and Eq. 2 that RayGraphy generates
many extra light points in the space where there are no target
volumetric graphics. Thus, we considered two methods for the
kinds of images to be projected. The first method is to project
simply 2D perspective images as seen from each position of the
projector. The other method is to project only the outline of the 2D
perspective image from each projector and only the outer surface
of the volumetric graphics as shown in Fig. 3. This method to draw
outlines cannot express the interior of the volumetric graphics.
However, compared to the former method, the number of extra
points of light is reduced, so that volumetric graphics tend to be seen
more clearly. We present a comparison in the Evaluation section.
In addition to the problem of extra light spots, RayGraphy has
limitations regarding the available shapes of the volumetric graphics. For example, when projecting a sphere into the center of the
system, each projector shows a circle. However, since light does
not bend in a space with a uniform refractive index, it can be represented only with a straight line of lights. Therefore, the sphere is
depicted as a polyhedron approximated by the number of projectors
around the Z-axis.
The proposed method in which an object is reproduced using
straight light rays can cause concave areas to be filled. Therefore,
RayGraphy is limited in terms of the shape of the target object. We
also discuss these problems with approximations to polyhedron
and concave shapes in the Evaluation section.

IMPLEMENTATION
This section describes how we implement the prototype shown in
Fig. 1a. We construct the prototype with 32 projection units evenly
distributed in a circle with a 1.2 m diameter.
Figure 4 shows the projection units. A projection unit comprises
a laser projector (Ultimems HD301D1) and a single-board computer
(Element14 Raspberry Pi 3 Model B). The laser projectors have
a 38.8 ◦ (H) x 22.5 ◦ (V) angle of view with 1280 (W) x 720 (H)
resolution and are mounted on a biaxial head. The laser source is
categorized as Class 1, the safest category in IEC 60825-1:2014 [4]
This means that the prototype is safe except for a special case, for
example, that a user intentionally looks at the laser directly for a
long time. However, this also means that the image generated by
the weak lasers can be only seen in a dark room.
The architecture of the system is based on a main-replica method.
The computer as the main and all projection units as the replicas
are connected to the network with a wired Local Area Network
(LAN) cable as shown in Fig. 5. The projector units are connected
to the switching hub in sets of four to reduce the amount of wiring.
The main computer transfers the image files to each projection
unit as a replica in advance, and then it broadcast commands to
them such as load, play, and stop, via User Datagram Protocol
(UDP) communications. The reason why the video is transferred in
advance is that it is difficult to send dozens of high definition images
to the video output of each unit without delay in the prototype.
Besides, doing so prevents delays by adopting a wired LAN and
UDP broadcast function.
The projected 2D perspective images are generated by capturing
3DCG with virtual cameras with the same angle of view and position as the projector. In the case of projecting only the contours,
contour extraction is performed on the 2D image.

Most fog screens [22, 23, 44] are generated using an ultrasonic
humidifier mechanism and pure water. The main reasons for using
this type are the fast-dissipating properties, low cost, and safety
characteristics that they provide. However, this method is not suitable for the proposed system because a larger amount of fog that
consists of smaller slow-dissipating particles is required.
Thus, we used a machine called a smoke machine, which produces a large amount of fog with a special harmless liquid even if
people breathe them. There are two main types of fluid in a fog
machine: One is the oil-based and the other is water-based. The
oil-based ones can produce a finer fog that floats for longer periods
of time than the water-based ones, and so it matches the proposed
method. Unfortunately, oil-based products are flammable and may
cause a risk of ignition. Considering that we operate it in public
spaces, the character becomes a major drawback. Therefore, we
adopted a smoke machine with water-based liquid.

5 EVALUATION
5.1 Fog
We surmised that finer fog is suitable for RayGraphy due to the
characteristics of Mie scattering. Accordingly, we investigated the
differences in the visibility of the laser light in the fine fog.
Unfortunately, information regarding the particle size produced
by the fazer machine FZ700 and FLH4 is not released by the manufacturer. Thus, we confirmed the characteristics by operating the
fog machines and the prototype in a darkroom that has a 4 m (W) x
4 m (H) x 3 m (D) space with less than 10 lx brightness. The target
model is a simple sphere with a diameter of 8 cm, and only its
contour is projected. The camera is fixed at a height of 70 cm at a
position 1.2 m away from the center of the device and loos down at
angle of about 30 degrees.
Figure 6 shows the results of operating the prototype after filling
the room with fog using the machine. As shown in Fig. 6, the
lights from the opposite projector are strongest due to the forward
directional scattering of the fog. In addition, the lights from the
front are also bright, but the these lights gradually darken as the
projector positions shift to the side. In the center of the device,
the sphere is clearly rendered as the sum of the lights from these
projectors.

6 DISCUSSION
6.1 Limitation
As we indicated, RayGraphy generates volumetric graphics but has
limitations such as in terms of contrast, resolution, and concave
shapes. The problem that light not included in the target model
reduces the contrast is also related to human perception. As the
Weber-Fechner law [42] showed, the human senses are said to be
logarithmic; hence it is difficult to clearly sense the little difference
in brightness between the extra light and the one in the target
model.
The easiest solution is to pile a larger number of the devices
and increase the brightness of the intersection of lights. Using
more projectors from various directions improves the resolution
of the volumetric graphics as well. It is also possible to operate the
projectors selectively according to the shape of the model so as
not to fill in its concave areas. Moreover, although textures having
multi-colors are not available in the current prototype, this method
would achieve expressing them.
The price of laser projectors using micro electro mechanical
systems (MEMS) technology has dramatically decreased, and the
projector that we adopt is approximately two hundred dollars. Thus,
we consider that increasing the number of projectors is enough
practical; however, it is best to use a smaller number of projectors
from the viewpoint of cost.
There could be a way to split the images into three primary colors
and project them to produce higher contrast. In other words, this
is an approach to project RGB light from different projector units
and make only their intersections the target color. We estimate that
a change in not only intensity but also hue will make it easier for
people to perceive the difference in the target volumetric graphics.

6.2
Possible applications
As mentioned earlier, RayGraphy focuses on specific applications
because it requires a fog-filled room. Examples of possible applications for RayGraphy are illustrated in Fig. 9. Figure 9a shows
an example of a training room with projectors located in the corners. In this example application, the trajectories of a golf swing
and ball are projected in space, and users and their coaches can
train while watching them. Figure 9b shows a remote collaborative
work application. This application allows a remote instructor to
provide direct instruction to a local user using a 3D cursor displayed in the air. An example of a large-scale implementation in a
stadium is shown in Fig. 9c. Kanede et al. achieved an innovative
free-viewpoint capturing system by installing a large number of
cameras around a stadium [3]. This application enables the display
of volumetric graphics directly at any position in the space above
the stage and audience by installing a large number of devices in a
large venue such as in their study. In order to actualize such applications without smart glasses, a novel method is required that can
safely display aerial volumetric graphics at any position in a wide
area.
In order to achieve interactive applications using RayGraphy,
technology that senses the user input is required. A typical method
for capturing user gestures with high accuracy in a room is to use
cameras; however, rays of light emitted from projectors and fog
filled in the room could disturb image capture and image analysis.
Thus, we propose a method based on infrared (IR) cameras to
sense user gestures in RayGraphy. Since infrared light, which has
a longer wavelength than visible light, penetrates the fog more
easily, IR images are less likely to degrade than RGB images even
in a fog environment. Furthermore, IR cameras cannot capture the
volumetric graphics projected by the projectors that use RGB lasers;
hence, it is possible to detect the user gestures even when he or she
overlaps the projected image.

7
CONCLUSION
We presented RayGraphy, a display technology that renders volumetric graphics by superimposing trajectories of lights in an indoor
space filled with fog. RayGraphy enable the safe rendering of aerial
volumetric graphics in a wide space. We explained the principle and
construction of the proposed system. In addition, we constructed a
desktop prototype to confirm the practicality of the implementation.
We also compared and investigated its fundamental characteristics.
We discussed the benefits of the system and described possible applications including training, telecommunications, and large-scale
performance. The obtained results confirm that RayGraphy has the
potential to become a new spatial platform for ubiquitous environments. Its current limitations and future research directions were
elucidated.
