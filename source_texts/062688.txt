Abstract
Deploying a trained 3D Gaussian Splatting scene under shrinking compute budgets
requires one primitive set to remain effective when rendered at smaller prefixes.
In this continuous level of detail (LoD) setting, the challenge is to decide which
Gaussians should retain capacity as the budget decreases. A pilot study on 3DGSMCMC reconstructions shows that image complexity, computed from the input
views before optimization, predicts where reconstruction error concentrates in
image and 3D space. It also reveals that simple regions contain too many Gaussians
and degrade as much as complex ones at lower budgets, despite being inherently
cheaper to represent. Subsequently, in KoalaGS, during training we use this complexity prior to bias relocation toward poorly reconstructed areas while ensuring
smooth degradation across LoDs. A primitive importance score then enables inference at low-capacity, while preserving the scene structure. Tested across three
datasets, KoalaGS maintains competitive full-capacity quality while significantly
improving quality across the budget spectrum, yielding smoother transitions across
LoDs, and enabling faster progressive streaming.

Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting [1] (3DGS) has made neural scene representations practical for novel view
synthesis in real time. As the representation has matured, deployment under heterogeneous bandwidth
and compute budgets has motivated a growing line of work on continuous level of detail (LoD) for
3DGS, where a single Gaussian set must remain useful when only a leading prefix of its primitives
is retained [2, 3, 4]. The central problem in this setting is determining which primitives should
retain capacity as the budget shrinks. Existing methods answer it implicitly, ranking primitives by
opacity [2] or by learned importance scores [5]. A structural property of the representation suggests
a complementary signal. A Gaussian does not contribute equally throughout a scene, as a single
primitive may account for a broad region of nearly uniform appearance yet resolve only a fraction
of a visually intricate one. We contend that this spatial variation in the value of capacity is what a
continuous LoD policy should exploit, and we show that it can be anticipated from the input views
alone.
We investigate this claim through a pilot study. Prior work already showed that global image
complexity predicts reconstruction error in neural scene representations [6]; however, continuous
LoD requires a localized signal that identifies not only which views are difficult, but where that
difficulty lies within the scene. We therefore measure the correlation between local image complexity
and reconstruction quality in the 2D image plane and, after multiview projection, on the Gaussian
primitives. The correlation persists across DL3DV [7] and multiple complexity functionals in both
domains, supporting complexity as a spatial prior for reconstruction difficulty that is available
before optimization. We then examine how 3DGS-MCMC allocates capacity with respect to this
structure. Gaussian density increases with complexity, approximately exponentially in regions of high

complexity, yet even visually simple regions are represented by many low-opacity Gaussians. Under
truncation according to opacity, quality degrades at comparable rates in simple and complex regions,
showing that opacity alone does not expose the spatial redundancy required for robust continuous
LoD. Together, these observations expose a mismatch between how 3DGS-MCMC allocates capacity
at full budget and how effectively that allocation is preserved under truncation.
Our continuous LoD method for Gaussian Splatting addresses this mismatch. The key insight is that
image complexity, which is predictive of reconstruction error, provides an inexpensive spatial prior for
allocating capacity and selecting primitives as the budget shrinks. During optimization, a relocation
rule biased by complexity steers primitives toward difficult regions, while at inference an importance
score modulated by complexity preserves broad primitives in simple regions and relies primarily on
opacity in complex ones. A residual variance objective further discourages an abrupt degradation as
the budget shrinks. We evaluate the resulting representation along three complementary axes. Our
method matches the best baselines at full capacity, while outperforming all continuous LoD baselines
across the budget spectrum, even in extremely low budget (< 1%). We show that this has great
practical implication in streaming context, where our method improves drastically the time-to-quality
performance.
We summarize our contributions as follows:
• A pilot study on 140 DL3DV scenes showing image complexity predicts where reconstruction
error concentrates, while capacity is allocated in a form that truncation cannot preserve.
• A continuous LoD method combining relocation biased by complexity during training, importance scoring modulated by complexity at inference, and a residual variance objective that
discourages localized degradation across budgets.
• An evaluation of fixed budget quality, integrated spectrum quality, and transition variance,
together with a streaming study showing a better time-to-quality tradeoff.

Related Work
Gaussian Splatting. 3D Gaussian Splatting transformed neural rendering by using an explicit
set of anisotropic Gaussian primitives optimized for visibility-aware rasterization, enabling highfidelity novel view synthesis at real-time frame rates [1]. A large body of literature focuses on
making this representation more stable, efficient, and deployable. On the optimization side, [8]
reformulates densification as probabilistic sampling over primitives, [9] introduces structured viewadaptive anchors, [10] incorporates radiance-field-informed optimization for robust high-speed
rendering, while [11, 12, 13] reduce training cost or remove expensive densification mechanisms
under constrained budgets. A complementary [14, 15] line compresses the trained representation
itself, reducing memory footprint and rendering cost to make Gaussian scenes practical on resourceconstrained devices. In parallel, other works [16, 17, 18] revisit the primitive parameterization,
replacing or constraining 3DGS with 2D variants to improve geometric fidelity and compatibility
with classical graphics pipelines. The flexibility of this representation also enables applications
beyond static novel view synthesis, including dynamic scene modeling [19, 20], progressive and
adaptive streaming [21, 22], mesh reconstruction [23], or higher-level scene understanding through
language grounding, segmentation, and editing [24, 25]. Altogether, these works establish 3DGS as a
general explicit scene representation. Our work employs 3DGS for deployment under heterogeneous
primitive budgets, exposing image complexity as a spatial prior over both densification and ranking.
Level of Detail in Gaussian Splatting. A growing body of work ports classical Level of Detail [26]
into the splatting pipeline. A first category imposes explicit hierarchies over the primitive set and
exposes a small number of discrete operating points [27, 28, 29], with spatial divide and conquer
variants scaling to kilometer sized captures [30, 31, 32]. A second category approximates multi
budget behavior through compression and importance pruning at varying targets [12, 15], where each
operating point is optimized independently and a new model must be retrained for every budget. A
third category pursues continuous LoD over a single primitive set: [3, 4] learn per Gaussian distance
dependent opacity decay, while FlexGS [5] learns a differentiable budget conditioned selector that
achieves strong quality at targeted budgets but requires a fresh forward pass at every LoD change,
precluding progressive streaming. Closer to our setting, Matryoshka-GS [2] introduces a nested
prefix renderable ordering trained via stochastic budget sampling, so that any leading slice yields a
coherent reconstruction. Our work belongs to this last category but departs from prior approaches in
how the ordering is determined: we introduce image complexity as a scene side spatial prior over

Pilot Study
This section studies image complexity as a predictor of 3DGS reconstruction quality, computed from
the input views before any optimization. The outcomes will shape our method, which must decide
where to spend capacity at the moment when reconstruction error, the signal that would otherwise
guide it, is not yet available. The intuition is that deploying under a primitive budget is a spatial
decision. As primitives are withdrawn, some regions tolerate the loss while others do not, so the
capacity that remains should sit where reconstruction error concentrates. Image complexity addresses
this, since prior work [6] shows that it predicts reconstruction error and is read from the input images
before optimization rather than recovered after training. What that work leaves open is whether the
signal is spatial, since an image level score ranks views by difficulty but not where the error lies
within them. We therefore investigate two questions: (i) Does complexity localize reconstruction
error in space, across the image plane and the 3D world? (ii) Are trained models already allocating
capacity where complexity predicts it should? For experimental details we refer to Sec. B.1.

Does complexity correlate with Gaussian density? Since complexity correlates with the reconstruction error, it is reasonable to expect that the optimal ‘per region number of Gaussians’ is already
achieved. Indeed, density control mechanism (pruning, cloning, relocation) for 3DGS are designed to
increase Gaussian density where reconstruction error is higher. To relate density to complexity at the
scene level, we voxelize each scene at a fixed edge length of 0.1 in normalized scene coordinates and,
within every voxel, record the mean complexity, the mean reconstruction quality, and the number of
Gaussians whose centers fall inside. The fixed scale is well defined because DL3DV test scenes are
released in a comparable spatial scale across scenes.
Reporting the resulting Gaussian counts across complexity intervals in Fig. 2a, we observe that more
complex voxels hold more Gaussians, generally following an exponential trend. At the 90th percentile,
the ratio between the least and most complex voxels (13 vs. 119 Gaussians per voxel) is surprising,
since the simplest voxels typically depict uniform color and are expected to be reconstructed with
only a few Gaussians. We therefore question whether the Gaussian budget is correctly utilized across
regions of varying complexity.

complexity signal, readily accessible at training time. Specifically, we apply two simple modifications
to the optimization process of state-of-the-art 3DGS-MCMC [8]. First, we utilize complexity to foster
(re)allocation of the Gaussians in regions with higher complexity (Sec. 4.1). Second, we introduce a
content-aware importance score that retains simple regions at low budgets (Sec. 4.2). We complement
them with a residual variance objective that encourages stable reconstruction error across space
and LoDs. Experiments show that our simple modification boost performance and efficiency. The
proposed LoD is global and view independent, rather than conditioned on camera distance or screen
resolution. The implications are further discussed in Sec. B.2.

each Gaussian gj = (µj , αj , Σj , cj ) is parameterized by its position µj , opacity αj , covariance

4.1
Complexity-aware Adaptive density control
In practice, 3DGS variants rely on opacity as a proxy for this error signal: during optimization new
primitives are concentrated where opacity has accumulated, and regions the optimizer has not yet
resolved remain under-served precisely because they have not been resolved. In 3DGS-MCMC [8],
the probability p to relocate at the coordinate of µj writes: p(gj ) = αj . Given that complexity is a
surrogate of reconstruction errors (cf., Tab. 1), we simply bias this sampling weight towards more
complex regions. We therefore sample relocation destinations with:

p(gj ) = αj · exp C̃j ,
(4)
where C̃j ∈ [0, 1] is the min-max normalized Cj . Note that we utilize exp(·) to mimic the exponential
growing behavior of particles count with complexity, as in the 90th percentile in Fig. 2a. Importantly,
the multiplicative form keeps opacity in the loop since a primitive that has earned its keep should
not be evicted for exploring other regions. To prevent collapse onto fixed complex regions, we apply
complexity dropout, setting C̃j = 0 with probability δ = 0.2 (ablated in Sec. D.2) and thus scoring
that fraction of relocation candidates by opacity alone (δ=0: none; δ=1: all).
4.2
Complexity-aware Level of Details
As mentioned in Sec. 3, under a limited budget (i.e., τ < 1), only Gaussians with the highest
importance score S(·) are preserved. In the literature, this typically resolves to using opacity [2],
i.e., S(gj ) = αj . As shown in Fig. 2b, this degrades low-complexity regions as much as complex
ones, although they should be the cheapest to retain, because opacity captures learned contribution
but not the total spatial footprint over which a primitive contributes. We therefore rank primitives by
combining three complementary signals: opacity, scale, and complexity:

photometric objective, allowing a small high-opacity Gaussian to outrank a moderate-opacity one
that covers a broader region. Scale supplies this missing spatial footprint, while complexity controls
where it should influence the score. In high-complexity regions, exp(C) attenuates the scale term,
avoiding preference for large, potentially blurring primitives and reducing the score toward opacity,
which is most reliable where optimization pressure is concentrated. In low-complexity regions, where
preserving broad spatial support matters more, the second term reduces to λ · s as exp(C) → 1,
restoring weight to large primitives that efficiently cover simple areas despite their moderate opacity.
We note that LoD-awareness requires the optimizer to vary LoD (i.e., τ ) during training. However, the
scoring alone does not guarantee that the resulting render degrades uniformly since the optimization
support varies with τ . Namely, at full-capacity (τ = 1) the optimizer has access to all primitives
to fit the image, while at lower LODs (τ < 1) the optimizer only accesses the surviving primitives
(according to Eq. 5) and may push the latter away from their full-capacity solution. To keep the
resulting deviation spatially uniform, we consider the residual of the reconstruction error between
full-capacity render and lower LODs render, and minimize it’s variance.

5
Experimental Results
We evaluate our method on three datasets [33, 34, 35] and compare its performance against five
baselines [2, 5, 15, 27, 8]. Since performance of LoD-aware 3DGS may vary across LoD, it is
not possible to characterize its performance by a single metric. We therefore assess our method
along three complementary axes: quality at predefined LoD targets (Sec. 5.1), spectrum quality as
the per-metric area under the curve over the full LoD sweep (Sec. 5.2), and intra- and inter-LOD
transition variance (Sec. 5.3), which together capture whether quality is high, whether it is sustained
across the spectrum, and whether it degrades gracefully rather than abruptly. We further conduct an
ablation in Sec. 5.4 to isolate the contribution of each component of our method. We refer the reader
to Sec. C for the experimental setup description and Sec. D for additional experiments. In all tables,

5.1
Quality at Predefined LOD Targets
We first ask whether our method delivers high quality at the operating points a user is most likely to
deploy, therefore evaluating at four log-spaced budgets chosen to probe each decade of the spectrum:
100% reflects full-capacity quality, 10% a moderate compression regime relevant to deployment,
1% an aggressive reduction where ranking quality dominates and an extreme 0.1%, [typically used
when initialising a streaming]. Quantitative results are reported in Tab. 2, while separating partialLoD methods (OctreeGS, FlexGS) as they can only be evaluated at some intervals. Qualitatives
are presented in Fig. 3, and curves are visualized in Fig. 4. A consistent picture emerges across
datasets. At full capacity, all methods are competitive (Fig. 3, top row), with our method performing
generally on par with the strongest baseline on every dataset and confirming that content-aware
modifications introduce no penalty at peak fidelity. As the budget shrinks, the field separates sharply.
3DGS MCMC degrades rapidly and has already collapsed by the 1% threshold, confirming that
opacity-based ranking alone cannot sustain quality under reduction. LightGaussian retains more
quality than opacity-based pruning in most cases, but falls behind both MGS and ours at every
reduced budget across all three datasets. OctreeGS cannot operate below its native discrete levels
and it remains competitive only on the indoor Deep Blending scenes and falls behind on outdoor
scenes. FlexGS, which is not prefix renderable, trades this property for higher quality near the
specific operating points it was optimised for, but degrades unpredictably between them. MGS, our
closest competitor, follows the same trend as our method but consistently lags behind it. Altogether,
content-aware ranking yields a more robust quality versus quantity trade-off than any single-family
representative, both quantitatively and visually. We report the rendering speeds in Sec. D.3 and
further investigate how our importance score and densification behave jointly in Sec. D.4.

5.2
Quality Across the LoD Spectrum
The previous section leaves open how quality evolves across the full LoD spectrum. To address
this, we sample 28 operating points in [0.1%, 100%], ten linearly spaced per interval ([0.1%, 1%],
[1%, 10%], [10%, 100%]) with shared endpoints, and integrate per-metric curves with the trapezoidal
rule to compute interval-wise area-under-the-curve (AUC) score, rescaled per interval for direct
comparison. Since discrete methods cannot operate across the full spectrum, we focus on prefixrenderable continuous LoD methods. From results in Tab. 3, our method consistently achieves the
highest AUC. Since the total AUC is dominated by high budgets where all methods cluster, the subinterval breakdown is more informative. 3DGS MCMC collapses below 10% capacity, yielding the
lowest sub-interval AUC across datasets and confirming that opacity-based ranking alone fails as the
budget shrinks. LightGaussian improves PSNR and generally SSIM over opacity-based pruning, but
remains behind MGS and ours across all metrics in the two lower-budget intervals. Our largest gain
over MGS occurs precisely in the low-budget [0.1%, 1%] regime where ranking quality dominates.
FlexGS performs better at [1%, 10%], but it does not allow full LoD coverage. Besides, FlexGS is
outperformed by ours in other intervals, which directly results from its design as it trained for specific
operating points at the cost of global consistency, producing the irregular degradation curves visible
in Fig. 4. Overall, these results indicate that our method offers the most balanced quality versus
quantity trade-off across the LoD spectrum.

variances on MipNerf(lower is better). Our method consistently outperforms MGS.
method that concentrates errors in localized regions may rank highly while exhibiting abrupt changes
across LoDs. To analyze this, we compute per-pixel error maps following Sec. 3 and derive two
complementary variance measures. Inter-LoD variance captures transitions across levels: for each
patch, we take differences between error maps at consecutive LoDs, compute the variance of these
residuals, and average over all patches. Low values indicate smooth rather than abrupt degradation.
Intra-LoD variance measures spatial uniformity within a level: we compute the variance of each error
map across patches and average over LoDs. Low values indicate that errors are evenly distributed
rather than concentrated in localized regions. Neither measure is meaningful in isolation, as uniformly
poor renderings can also yield low variance. We therefore use them as a complement to the absolute
quality metrics reported above. Further details are provided in Sec. E. Results on MipNerf against
MGS are reported in Fig. 5. Across all intervals and metrics, our method exhibits lower inter-LoD
variance than MGS, with the gap being most pronounced in the [0.1%, 1%] regime where budget
pressure is highest and abrupt transitions are most likely to occur. A consistent reduction is also
observed in intra-LoD variance, indicating that our content-aware ranking not only smooths transitions
across levels but also distributes error more uniformly within each level. Taken together with the
absolute and AUC results, these findings confirm that complexity-aware importance scoring yields a
representation that degrades both more gracefully and more uniformly across the full LoD spectrum.

5.5
Application: time-to-quality in streaming
Our ranking naturally enables progressive streaming, where remote clients render the primitives as
they arrive. Prior 3DGS streaming systems use a few discrete quality layers [21] without characterizing how quality evolves throughout streaming. We therefore measure time to quality: the time to
reach a target rendering fidelity at fixed bandwidth. We simulate 50 Mbps bandwidth (representative
of 5G network), and send each primitive as raw fp32 attributes (236 bytes). We compare against
MGS [2] and 3DGS MCMC [8], which stream incrementally like ours, and FlexGS [5], which must
restream the full set at every budget. On the Bonsai scene of MipNerf [34] (Fig. 6), the prefix methods
receive the full model in 49.1 s, while FlexGS needs 344 s, a 7.0× overhead caused by restreaming.
Our method reaches 25 dB after 1.47 s, compared to 1.96 s for MGS and 9.82 s for 3DGS MCMC,
and 28 dB after 2.95 s, compared to 3.93 s and 14.73 s, respectively. FlexGS is also non monotone
in time, dropping to about 22 dB in the mid budgets before recovering at full capacity. Because our
importance score (Eq. 5) sends large primitives in simple regions first, the client sees a complete,
coarse scene within the first timestamps.
6
Conclusion
We studied how to allocate Gaussian primitives as rendering budgets shrink in continuous LoD
splatting. Our pilot study showed that image complexity, accessible before any optimization, predicts
spatial reconstruction error in 2D and 3D, yet trained models fail to allocate capacity accordingly
in a form that a shrinking budget preserves. Building on these observations, we design a method
allocating primitives based on complexity which determines their survival at inference, using a
variance objective to maintain uniform error across scales. Evaluated on three datasets, our approach
matches the strongest baselines at full capacity, while achieving the highest integrated quality across
budgets, and degrades more gracefully, with the largest gains below 1% capacity and faster time-toquality in streaming.

A
Limitations
We identify three limitations that needs discussing of our approach, namely the optimization stability,
the nature of our complexity prior, and view conditioning.
(i) Our method inherits the stochastic relocation dynamics of 3DGS-MCMC, so results can slightly
vary across runs. This variation does not alter our standing relative to prior work, as our method
maintains its relative position at every capacity level and on every dataset we evaluate. The instability
is moreover present to the same degree in 3DGS-MCMC and MGS, since all three build on the same
relocation mechanism.
(ii) The complexity prior we introduce is computed from the input images alone, and therefore
captures spatial difficulty but neither view-dependent appearance nor semantic saliency. A specular
highlight that shifts with viewpoint, or a small object that is salient despite low pixel complexity,
receives no special treatment under our prior, and the correlations of Tab. 1 should accordingly be
read as holding under standard capture conditions.
(iii) Primitive selection is likewise unconditioned on the viewpoint: the formulation is neither distancenor resolution-dependent, and is thus not designed to suppress aliasing when rendering from distant
viewpoints or at reduced resolution. However, its preference for large-support primitives in simple
regions may reduce the retention of subpixel primitives relative to opacity-only ranking, but this
provides no antialiasing guarantee. Because we modify primitive allocation and ordering without
prescribing a competing filtering mechanism, our representation remains compatible in principle with
dedicated approaches such as the smoothing filter of Mip-Splatting [36], a combination we do not
evaluate here.
B
Implementation details
B.1
Pilot Study
Experimental setup. We run the study on the DL3DV [7] test set of 140 scenes, reconstructed
with 3DGS-MCMC [8], a robust 3DGS approach. Each scene is labeled bounded or unbounded
from the DL3DV metadata and receives 1.0M primitives when bounded and 1.5M when unbounded,
reflecting the larger extent of unbounded captures. All scenes are trained at 960p for 30k steps under
the default 3DGS-MCMC hyperparameters, holding out every 8th frame as the test split. We measure
reconstruction quality with PSNR [37] and SSIM [38], and image complexity with three functionals:
Shannon pixel entropy [39, 37], Laplacian response [37], and delentropy [40]. Throughout our study,
we exclude the particles that do not contribute to the final reconstruction.
3D aggregation of quality and complexity. The 3D fields Q3D and C 3D defined in Eq. 2 of
the main paper are constructed by projecting the per-view 2D maps onto the trained Gaussian set
under multi-view aggregation. To analyse the joint distribution of complexity, quality, and Gaussian
density at the scene level (Fig. 2a), we voxelize each scene using a fixed voxel size of 0.1 in the
Colmap normalised scene coordinates. This fixed scale is well defined because DL3DV test scenes
are released in a roughly similar normalized frame, so a single voxel size yields comparable spatial
granularity across scenes without per-scene tuning. Within each voxel we record (i) the mean local
complexity, (ii) the mean reconstruction quality (PSNR or SSIM), and (iii) the number of Gaussians
whose means fall inside.
B.2
Method
Scope of the LoD Formulation. Our formulation provides global, view-independent LoD through a
single primitive ordering whose prefixes define successive approximations. Unlike camera-dependent
selection over a spatial hierarchy, this ordering is fixed across viewpoints and budgets, enabling
progressive streaming by transmitting additional primitives as the budget increases.
Complexity maps computation. The complexity maps are computed once per scene, before training,
and cached to disk. The computation is implemented in PyTorch and runs on the GPU. Its cost
depends on the complexity functional, the patch size, and the image resolution, and ranges from 20 s
to 60 s per scene on the evaluated datasets. As this is a one time preprocessing step, its overhead is
negligible relative to training. We use a fixed receptive field of 32 pixels in all experiments, with
14
a stride of 4 to 8 depending on the image resolution. This setting yielded consistent complexity
statistics across input resolutions ranging from 960p (DL3DV) to approx. 1.6K (MipNerf360).
Implementation. We implement our method on top of the 3DGS-MCMC version from the
gsplat [41] framework. We adopt delentropy as the complexity functional since it attained the
highest average rank in the pilot study (Tab. 1), although our method is agnostic to this choice and
admits any complexity functional. On a single NVIDIA H100, reconstructing a typical scene with
∼2M primitives completes in 15 minutes. All hyperparameters are listed in Tab. 5.
Training details. We train every scene for 30k steps. Each step renders the training view twice,
once at full capacity and once at a budget τ ∼ U(0, 1) sampled anew at every step, so that a single
optimization covers the whole LoD spectrum. During the first 500 steps we render at full capacity
only, so that the full-capacity solution forms before any prefix is optimized. The total objective
averages the two reconstruction terms with the variance term of Eq. 6, and adds the regularizers of

Experimental evaluation.
C.1
Setup
We follow the evaluation protocol of 3DGS-MCMC [8] and report results on MipNerf360 [34]
with 7 scenes, Tanks and Temples [35] with 2 scenes, and Deep Blending (DB) [33] with 2 scenes,
together covering a wide range of indoor and outdoor capture conditions. We compare against five
baselines spanning the design space of LoD for Gaussian Splatting. Two of them yield a prefix
renderable ordering directly comparable to ours: Matryoshka Gaussian Splatting (MGS) [2], our
closest competitor, and 3DGS-MCMC [8], which we extend with an opacity based ranking. Other two
operate only at restricted points of the LoD spectrum: FlexGS [5], a budget conditioned continuous
LoD method, and Octree-GS [27], a discrete LoD method. The last, LightGaussian [15], is a pruning
method, included to compare our ordering against post hoc decimation at equal capacity. A detailed
description of each baseline and its adaptation to our protocol is provided in Sec. C.2. All models
are trained under identical conditions on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. For fair comparison we fix the total
primitive budget across 3DGS-MCMC, MGS, and our method, while FlexGS and Octree-GS are
reported at their native counts. We report PSNR [37], SSIM [38], and LPIPS [42], which capture the
complementary axes of pixel fidelity, structural preservation, and perceptual similarity.
C.2
Baselines
3DGS-MCMC [8]. We use the gsplat reimplementation with default hyperparameters. Since
3DGS-MCMC is neither LOD-aware at training time nor designed to support budget-conditioned
inference, we evaluate it under LOD by applying the simplest possible reduction strategy: at each
target budget we retain the top-k primitives ranked by opacity and discard the rest. This naive
15
trimming establishes the baseline behaviour of an opacity-only ranking signal absent any LOD-aware
training.
Matryoshka-GS [2]. We use the authors original implementation. One important difference from
their reported setup affects direct comparability: the original paper trains all scenes with a flat
5M-primitive budget for 50k steps. To align with our protocol and with the 3DGS-MCMC budget
convention, we instead train MGS for 30k steps and adapt the primitive budget per scene to match the
3DGS allocation. All other hyperparameters follow the original implementation. Notably by doing,
we observed that the stability has improved over the DB dataset over the official results.
FlexGS [5]. We use the authors’ original implementation and training hyperparameters without
modification. FlexGS is a budget-conditioned continuous-LOD method that learns a differentiable
selector mapping each primitive to a retention score for a target budget, trained in two stages: a first
stage optimises the 3DGS representation, and a second stage trains the selector on top of the frozen
primitives. This design trades global consistency for pointwise quality: FlexGS can outperform
prefix-ranked methods at the specific budgets it is optimised for, but at the cost of (i) roughly doubled
end-to-end training time, and (ii) the absence of a prefix-renderable ordering, since changing the
operating LOD requires a fresh forward pass through the selector to recompute per-primitive scores
rather than truncating a precomputed ranking.
Octree-GS [27]. We use the authors’ original implementation and training hyperparameters without
modification. Octree-GS is a discrete-LOD method that organises primitives into a fixed octree
hierarchy, exposing a small number of predefined operating points corresponding to traversal depths.
This design yields stable quality at its native levels but cannot interpolate between them: budgets
falling between two depths are unreachable, and the coarsest level is bounded below by the structure
of the hierarchy itself.
LightGaussian [15]. We use the authors’ original implementation. LightGaussian is a compression
method rather than an LoD one, pruning primitives by a global volumetric importance measure,
fine tuning the survivors, then compacting them through spherical harmonic distillation and vector
quantisation. We retain only the pruning stage, as the others reduce storage without changing the
primitive count that defines our budget axis. For fairness we initialise from the same 30k step
3DGS-MCMC checkpoints as the other baselines so every method starts from an identical primitive
set. We omit the recovery fine tuning, since fine tuning at each budget yields a separate optimisation
run and a stored model per operating point, which is what separates pruning from a single prefix
renderable ordering.

opacity sampling p(gj ) = αj . We perform a value sweep δ ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1} on the outdoor

D.3
Rendering Speed
We investigate the rendering speed of our method against the baselines at four levels of detail (100%,
10%, 1%, 0.1%). We split MipNeRF360 [34] into indoor scenes (Bonsai, Counter, Kitchen, Room)
and outdoor scenes (Bicycle, Garden, Stump) and report the average frame rate on a single NVIDIA
H100 (Tab. 7). All prefix methods share an identical budget, so their primitive counts match at every
level of detail and speed differences stem only from the size of the retained primitives, whereas
FlexGS [5] operates with a smaller budget and is not directly comparable. At full capacity, our
method is the fastest prefix method, tied with MGS indoors (302 FPS) and ahead outdoors (198 vs.
193 FPS). As the budget shrinks, every method accelerates, but ours does so less and saturates below
1%, reaching 463 FPS indoors and 874 FPS outdoors at 0.1%, against 829 and 1,151 FPS for MGS.
We attribute this to our importance score (Eq. 5), which retains large primitives so that simple regions
remain covered. A large primitive spans more tiles and thus costs more per frame, whereas MGS
favors compact, high opacity primitives that render faster but cover less of the scene, at lower quality
(Fig. 4). Our method is thus slower than MGS at low budgets, a cost we accept in exchange for
coverage and better quality, while at equal quality it requires fewer primitives (Fig. 6). Reducing
the per frame cost of large primitives at the lowest budgets, for instance by bounding their screen
footprint, is a natural direction for future work.

D.6
Coverage Across LoDs
In this experiment, we investigate whether the primitives retained at low budgets still cover the whole
scene. A reduced budget can degrade a rendering in two ways: the scene may be represented at a
coarser resolution, or parts of it may no longer be covered by any primitive, leaving "holes". We
further define such holes as a pixel whose accumulated opacity is α < 0.05 and count such pixels over
all test views at 4 LoDs (100, 10, 1, 0.1). We compare against MGS [2], our closest prefix renderable
competitor, on two MipNerf360 scenes [34]: the indoor Bonsai and the outdoor Bicycle. Tab. 10
shows that coverage is preserved by both methods at high budgets and that the differences emerge as
the budget shrinks. At 100% and 10% capacity, neither method leaves any hole. At 1% capacity, the
two methods are close on Bonsai (2,420 vs. 2,615 hole pixels), whereas on Bicycle MGS already
leaves 5.4× more holes than ours (11,884 vs. 2,204). At 0.1% capacity, our method leaves fewer
holes on both scenes, with MGS producing 4.0× more on Bonsai (83,341 vs. 20,765) and 3.2× more
on Bicycle (116,920 vs. 36,009). We attribute this difference to our importance score (Eq. 5), which
retains large primitives in simple regions, so that areas such as the background remain covered by a
few large Gaussians as the budget shrinks. The same mechanism explains why the gap appears earlier
on Bicycle, whose large and simple outdoor background is the first to be removed by an ordering that
does not account for coverage.
