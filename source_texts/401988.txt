Abstract
Neural volume rendering became increasingly popular recently due to its success in synthesizing novel views of a scene from a sparse set of input images. So far, the geometry learned by neural volume rendering techniques was modeled using a generic density function. Furthermore, the geometry itself was extracted using an arbitrary level set of the density function leading to a noisy, often low fidelity reconstruction. The goal of this paper is to improve geometry representation and reconstruction in neural volume rendering. We achieve that by modeling the volume density as a function of the geometry. This is in contrast to previous work modeling the geometry as a function of the volume density. In more detail, we define the volume density function as Laplace's cumulative distribution function (CDF) applied to a signed distance function (SDF) representation. This simple density representation has three benefits: (i) it provides a useful inductive bias to the geometry learned in the neural volume rendering process; (ii) it facilitates a bound on the opacity approximation error,[p5] leading to an accurate sampling of the viewing ray. Accurate sampling is important to provide a precise coupling of geometry and radiance; and (iii) it allows efficient unsupervised disentanglement of shape and appearance in volume rendering. Applying this new density representation to challenging scene multiview datasets produced high quality geometry reconstructions, outperforming relevant baselines. Furthermore, switching shape and appearance between scenes is possible[p9] due to the disentanglement of the two.

Building a solid 3D object from photographs taken around it is harder than producing convincing new pictures of it. A neural renderer can reproduce photos from unseen viewpoints while its internal density field still yields a poor surface, because nothing forces that density to describe a faithful boundary. VolSDF's move is to let a learned shape generate the density: instead of learning a generic density and thresholding it afterward, the model learns a surface and derives density from it. In the paper's DTU comparison, this gave much better surfaces than NeRF while image-rendering quality stayed comparable.
The pipeline, left to right: input views of a figurine, a sliced volumetric density defined by the SDF, the sliced SDF with gray reconstructed surfaces, and a rendered view. This illustrates the method rather than a quantitative comparison.
[p2]
How shape guides rendering
A signed distance function (SDF) gives every 3D point its distance to the surface, negative inside and positive outside, so the surface is the zero level set. VolSDF passes the SDF through the cumulative distribution function of a Laplace distribution to obtain volume density. Density therefore changes smoothly across the boundary, with the width of that transition controlled by a learnable scale. The result is a geometric bias, and the zero level set becomes a principled surface to extract, unlike an arbitrary threshold on a generic density network. [p3]
Density matters because it determines how likely light is to be blocked along a camera ray; opacity is the accumulated probability of that occlusion. Since the density has this SDF-derived form, the authors can bound the error made when approximating a ray's opacity from a finite set of samples. [p5] They use that bound to guide sampling, in place of NeRF's coarse network that approximates opacity before fine sampling. [p1]
The sampling procedure starts from uniformly spaced samples and a deliberately broadened scale that satisfies the error bound. It adds samples preferentially to intervals with larger estimated error, narrows the broadened scale while preserving the bound, and finally draws fresh rendering samples by inverse sampling from the approximated opacity. In the implementation, it starts with 128 samples, runs at most five iterations, uses at most ten bisection steps when updating the scale, and returns 64 rendering samples. The returned opacity approximation is guaranteed to meet the chosen bound, but the iterative strategy is not guaranteed to converge to the learned scale; in practice the broadened scale reaches it in about 85% of cases. [p6] [p6]
Does the mechanism matter in practice?
The authors test multiview reconstruction on DTU and BlendedMVS, separating surface accuracy from view-rendering fidelity (PSNR). On DTU, averaged over 15 scans, the Chamfer-L1 surface distance is 0.86 mm for VolSDF versus 1.89 mm for NeRF; lower is better. The scans use 49 or 64 images with fixed camera and lighting parameters. VolSDF is trained without object masks, but the surface comparison against NeRF is evaluated inside a mask-defined visual hull; IDR, by contrast, uses masks for training. Rendering quality is reported as comparable to NeRF's, with mean PSNR of 30.38 for VolSDF against 30.65 for NeRF. [p8] [p8]
DTU results by scan with a Mean column: Chamfer distance for IDR, COLMAP (trim-7 and trim-0), NeRF and VolSDF, and PSNR for NeRF and VolSDF. VolSDF's mean Chamfer is lower (0.86 vs 1.89) while PSNR is near-matching (30.38 vs 30.65).
A sampling ablation checks the proposed ray-sampling directly. It compares uniform sampling (256 samples per ray), NeRF-style two-network sampling, and VolSDF sampling after one and after five iterations. In the displayed scene, the Chamfer scores are 1.19, 0.96, 0.89 and 0.70 respectively, so the five-iteration setting is best on geometry, while its PSNR of 31.5 is similar to or slightly above the others (30.2, 31.32, 31.27). This supports the sampling method in that ablation, though it does not isolate the opacity bound as the sole cause. [p12]
Geometry with Chamfer scores (left) and rendered crops with PSNR (right) for Uniform, 2-networks, Ours (1 iter.) and Ours (5 iter.). Five iterations gives the lowest displayed Chamfer score with PSNR at least on par; this is one displayed ablation, not a universal guarantee.
A separate swap test exchanges radiance fields between two trained scenes. Standard NeRF, and a NeRF variant given VolSDF-like radiance inputs including normals, failed to disentangle shape from appearance in the shown scenes, while VolSDF transferred materials between the objects. The authors attribute this to the inductive bias of the density parameterization; the experiment covers particular scenes and setups, and the supplementary material notes that normal information in the radiance field matters. [p9] [p9] [p14]
Where it stops
These are reconstructions from calibrated multiview collections, not recovery of arbitrary unseen geometry. The authors report arbitrary completions in ambiguous unseen regions and difficulty with homogeneous, textureless areas. [p14] An SDF also cannot represent non-watertight surfaces or surfaces with boundaries, such as a plane of zero thickness. [p9]
One field, two jobs
The learned surface is not a mesh bolted on after the fact; it shapes the density that drives volume rendering. In these experiments that single arrangement supports both an explicit surface and view synthesis. [p2]
