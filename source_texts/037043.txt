I found the book easy to read but
rather difficult to review, partly because
the articles are uneven, as might be expected in such a collection. A minor
problem with the book is that a few parts
(for example, references to Commodore-64 computers) will soon be out of
date. Another more significant problem
derives from my outlook on coding
practices. I strongly believe in the use
of modem structured languages such as
Pascal or C rather than Basic. I also
believe that students and researchers
should use off-the-shelf routines, such
as those found in Press et al.'s Numerical Recipes, rather than homemade routines for common numerical manipulations, such as fast Fourier transforms
and spline fits. The articles vary significantly in their adherence to this philosophy.
Most of the authors are from Germany and the U.K. Hence, there are a
few references to BBC Basic and Acorn
Computers, which will be unfamiliar to
American readers. American readers
may also have difficulty following up
on the German- and French-language
references in some ofthe articles. However, the authors have all agreed to provide additional information on the
hardware and software upon direct request, and a list oftheir postal addresses
is provided at the front of the book.
I give this book two stars out of a
possible four. +

have copies of the American Institute of
Physics Handbook and A Physicist's
Desk Reference. These are all excellent
examples of my conception of a handbook-a compilation of formulae, numerical values, figures, tables, and
graphs, with terse but lucid prose written as a reference for someone skilled in
the art, rather than as an exposition for
a newcomer.

The CRC Image Processing Handbook is written in a very different style.
As you might expect from the title, it is
chock full of beautifully presented images (black and white in the text, with a
section of color plates). The images are
used to good effect to illustrate a wide
variety ofimage-processing techniques,
and the images are accompanied by
well-written descriptions of the techniques. It could be read and appreciated
by a non-specialist. Therein lies my
most severe criticism of the book: it
presents descriptions and examples of
the techniques, but, unlike a handbook
by my definition, it does not provide the
detailed information that a reader would
need to implement the techniques in the
real world. On the other hand, it is not a
textbook like Robot Vision by B.K.P.
Hom or Computer Vision by D.H. Ballard and C.M. Brown, both of which
cover fewer topics in much greater detail.
The book does provide a rather
good, though not exhaustive, survey of
the techniques available for image processing. It is incomplete because it does
not cover an important area of image
processing, multiresolution methods,
also known as pyramid processing,
which I know from my own work, and
it undoubtedly misses others that I did
not catch. During my reading, I found
about a dozen typos, ambiguities, and
minor errors. Nevertheless, the author
did a fine job within the bounds of a
survey of450 pages and brought several
techniques to my attention of which I
was previously unaware. I give this
book a three-star rating out of a possible
four. •
