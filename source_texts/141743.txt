Differentiable Voxelization of Surface Representations | alphaXiv

Abstract
Different shape representations facilitate different computations. Surface representations, in particular meshes, are often used for modeling, whereas volume representations are useful for spatial queries such as intersection or containment. Optimizing a surface representation based on a volumetric properties by gradient descent requires the derivatives of the volume relative to its bounding surface. We derive this gradient for winding numbers and show that it can be efficiently computed for volumetric values sampled on a regular grid (voxel representation) and surface parameters based on vertex sets (triangle meshes). This enables an efficient solution for a variety of optimization problems. We demonstrate the practical use of this approach at the examples of deforming meshes to resolve intersections, being manufacturable by cutting with a bandsaw from three directions,[p8] and creating shapes that are close to tiling 3D space.[p10]

Suppose you have designed a 3D object as a triangle mesh and want to cut it from a block of wood on a bandsaw. A bandsaw cuts along straight silhouettes, so you would cut the block from three orthogonal directions. For many shapes, that leaves unwanted material behind: the three extruded silhouettes together cover more than the object itself. Fixing this by eye means reshaping the mesh without ruining its overall form. The paper's move is to let a volume-based measure of the leftover material guide a small deformation of the mesh automatically. [p2] [p8] On the TOSCA cat category, the reported cut-volume difference, measured with the authors' voxel-based objective, improves by 70.775%. That is a category result for their voxel measure, not a claim about physical wood removed or about every shape becoming manufacturable. [p13]
Four examples compared before and after optimization. Red marks material included by the cut shape but absent from the mesh voxelization, and gray voxels show the mesh voxelization. The displayed relative improvements, left to right, are 76.0%, 69.9%, 58.7%, and 62.8%. This is a voxel-based comparison, not a physical cutting experiment.
A mesh that can listen to volume
Meshes describe only a surface, but questions such as "do parts overlap?" or "can this be cut from a block?" are easier to ask of the interior. A voxel is a cell in a regular 3D grid, and a volumetric representation assigns each cell a value. The tool the authors use to define the interior is the winding number, a signed count of how a surface wraps around a point. For a closed surface without self-intersections it acts as an inside/outside indicator, while overlaps can produce values above one. [p1]
The obstacle to optimization is gradients. Point-sampling the raw winding number gives a piecewise-constant signal, so its derivative with respect to mesh motion is zero almost everywhere or undefined at the boundaries. [p3] The paper's key change is to smooth the winding-number field with a filter before sampling voxel values. For the derivative, the general derivation uses Reynolds' transport theorem: the moving boundary contributes a term, while the interior term vanishes because the integrand does not otherwise depend on the mesh parameter. This holds for general surfaces and allows self-intersections. [p3]
The efficient closed-form algorithm is a specialization, for triangle meshes with voxel-aligned box filters. In that case a voxel's filtered value is the average winding number within the voxel. The algorithm clips triangles at voxel boundaries, divides the intersections into sub-triangles, and adds up their signed shadow-volume contributions along grid columns. For the gradient, it integrates the triangles' normal motion over the clipped pieces and maps those contributions back to mesh vertices. Voxelization and gradient share this local clipping structure. [p5] [p6]
Overview of the idea: moving a highlighted mesh vertex changes the voxelization of adjacent triangles, and that dependence is what the gradient captures. The same machinery is applied to resolving self-intersections, bandsaw cutting, and repeatable tilings.
Does the gradient hold up?
The most direct prior comparison is Luo et al.'s Differentiable Voxelization and Mesh Morphing, which samples a non-smoothed winding number, interpolates the samples, and differentiates with automatic differentiation. Djuren et al. argue that this discretize-then-differentiate route does not give the expected derivatives. Because the raw winding number is piecewise constant, finite differences are zero almost everywhere, while the automatic derivatives are spuriously nonzero only very close to the surface. In their illustrated test, the smoothed-winding-number derivative matches the finite-difference reference. [p7] This is the authors' diagnosis from one displayed comparison, not a proof that every prior method or setting fails.
One mesh (7,254 vertices, 2,418 triangles) voxelized on a 32³ grid. The prior method (DOPH) yields only sparse near-surface derivative signals, while the paper's method (DVX) shows a broader pattern matching the finite-difference reference. Labeled timings are 418.4 ms for DOPH and 2.2 ms for DVX.
A smoother gradient could have come at the price of a slow voxelizer. In this single test, on the 2,418-triangle mesh and 32³ grid, voxelization takes 418.4 ms with DOPH and 2.2 ms with the paper's method (DVX). [p7] That is one example; the paper gives no hardware details alongside this comparison. Separately, the authors report sub-second CPU voxelization and backward-mode gradient computation for meshes above 100,000 faces at 1024³ resolution, averaged over 30 runs on an Intel i9-12900K, with an implementation they describe as not fully optimized and not parallelized. [p6] This is a CPU runtime for those operations, not the time of a full optimization loop or a GPU result; an optimized GPU implementation is left to future work. [p7]
A volume objective changes the shape
For cutability, the objective works in three steps. The voxelized mesh is projected along each of three orthogonal directions to obtain filled silhouettes, the silhouettes are extruded, and the resulting cut shape is compared with the voxelized mesh. An as-rigid-as-possible (ARAP) regularizer discourages unnecessary deformation. [p8] The reported relative improvement is the reduction in cut-volume difference between the initial and optimized meshes' voxelized cut shapes. For TOSCA's cat category, the average cut-volume difference goes from 3.425 to 0.454 (both ×10³), a 70.775% improvement. [p13] This is one category's average, not an aggregate over all categories, and it is measured on voxels rather than as physical material. The authors separately demonstrate cutability with physical bandsaw and foam-cutter cuts. [p8]
The same gradient drives two other demonstrations: deforming a mesh to reduce voxel regions with winding number above one (removing self-intersections), and optimizing repeated shapes toward space-filling tilings. Both are approximate. Self-intersection removal works at voxel resolution, so overlaps hidden by voxel averaging in partly occupied cells, or by negative winding numbers from volume inversions, can escape the objective. [p8] The tiling examples may not fill the whole volume or be intersection-free, and the authors call them near-tileable. [p10]
A bridge between shapes
The cutting example works because the voxelization now passes a usable gradient back to the surface: a constraint stated on volume becomes a force on mesh vertices, and the mesh bends to satisfy it.
