The word "voxel" literally comes from "volumetric pixel", so the definition as "a 3d pixel" is pretty apt. Most everything true about pixels is also true about voxels, plus some other stuff.
One way to implement a voxel in OpenGL is to render one cube for each voxel. If you really render a completely separate cube in a separate draw call for each voxel, your game engine will not run fast enough. Instead, you need to batch together lots and lots and lots (preferably millions) of your voxels so that they can be drawn at once.
Doing the batching operations requires that you treat the voxels as something very different to separate cubes: for instance, you might group touching voxels together and remove internal faces.
So once you move to a certain level of performance, you can't really say "voxel == cube". But you can always say "voxel == volumetric picture element".

"A pixel is not a little square and a voxel is not a little cube!" http://alvyray.com/Memos/CG/Microsoft/6_pixel.pdf
Note that terminology has changed since 1995. What Alvy is calling "pixels" most of us these days would call "texels". What does a texel look like? Well, if you point-sample it, it looks like a square. But, then there's bilinear, biquadratic, bicubic sampling. Gaussian sampling. Lanczos sampling. All kinds of wacky custom methods to interpret a grid of samples...
Because that's what pixels and voxels are: Samples of data to be interpreted however you see fit.

This is all still relevant to pixels, too! When rendering an image, we're sampling some continuous image function onto a grid. For the grid values to faithfully reproduce the original signal, we'll have to low-pass it first. And this low-passing is done with a pixel filter, for which we typically choose exactly one of the filters you mention (or Mitchell-Netravali). All anti-aliasing methods are different variants of this filtering; typical renderers (rasterizers and ray tracers) only produce point samples of the image, so they tend to be weighted averages of point samples (like MSAA or TAA or super sampling). Though nowadays we use LCDs where pixels are indeed close to little squares, so the title might have lost a bit of its punch. Still, even if you use a box filter, doing the analysis from the DSP perspective tends to make more sense.

It doesnt have to be.
One optimization is to only draw faces where there is no voxel attached. Not full cubes anymore! You can also collect larger faces and render them together. So a 8x8x8 filled volume would be one cube too, but 512 voxels.
You can also render voxel volumes as https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marching_cubes and other triangulisation methods. Some medical imaging systems use spheres as approximation with LOD to approimate surfaces. If you feel like overkill you can meta-ball them too.
I like to think of voxels being a 3D grid with uniform information. How you RENDER those is up to you!
The simplest version of a voxel rendering is to render them as colored cubes, so yeah.

Voxels are the 3d equivalent of pixels.
Pixels, voxels, and audio are all examples of discrete signals, meaning they take a continuous signal across some space and approximate it with a series of samples. Sampling from a texture (or voxel grid) means to convert this discrete signal back into the approximated continuous one.
Sampling can be done in different ways based on your performance limitations and your desired output. Usually in graphics the two big ones are nearest sampling, where you pick the single closest pixel and the resulting signal looks very blocky, and linear sampling, which does a linear interpolation between the four closest pixels (or 8 closest voxels, or 2 closest audio samples).
There are plenty of other ways to sample discrete signals, but they require more discrete values and don't have hardware support in the GPU, making them a lot slower in graphics work. There are several cubic sampling functions, for example, which could be considered the next step up from linear sampling.
The process of turning a 3d signal's isosurface (the surface where the signal has some threshold value) into a renderable mesh, is a whole other topic. Minecraft is an example of nearest-sampled voxels with an isosurface threshold of 1.0, while Marching Cubes is an example of linear-sampled voxels with whatever threshold the programmer chooses.

OpenGL doesn't know what a voxel is. However, you can use voxels to represent a spatial data set, and you can use OpenGL to visualize the voxels.
In most cases yes, you would use cubes, but I don't think you have to. For example you could probably merge similar voxels if they are next to each other and render more complex shapes.
