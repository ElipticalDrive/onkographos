# Efficient Encoding and Decoding of Voxelized Models for Machine Learning-Based Applications

ABSTRACT Point clouds have become a popular training data for many practical applications of machine learning in the fields of environmental modeling and precision agriculture. In order to reduce high space requirements and the effect of noise in the data, point clouds are often transformed to a structured representation such as a voxel grid. Storing, transmitting and consuming voxelized geometry, however, remains a challenging problem for machine learning pipelines running on devices with limited amount of on-chip memory with low access latency. A viable solution is to store the data in a compact encoded format, and perform on-the-fly decoding when it is needed for processing. Such on-demand expansion must be fast in order to avoid introducing substantial additional delay to the pipeline. This can be achieved by parallel decoding, which is particularly suitable for massively parallel architecture of GPUs on which the majority of machine learning is currently executed. In this paper, we present such method for efficient and parallelizable encoding/decoding of voxelized geometry. The method employs multi-level context-aware prediction of voxel occupancy based on the extracted binary feature prediction table, and encodes the residual grid with a pointerless sparse voxel octree (PSVO). We particularly focused on encoding the datasets of voxelized trees, obtained from both synthetic tree models and LiDAR point clouds of real trees. The method achieved 15.6% and 12.8% reduction of storage size with respect to plain PSVO on synthetic and real dataset, respectively. We also tested the method on a general set of diverse voxelized objects, where an average 11% improvement of storage space was achieved.

INDEX TERMS Voxel grid, feature prediction, tree models, prediction-based encoding, key voxels, residuals, sparse voxel octree.

### I. INTRODUCTION

Remote sensing of environment, enabled by contemporary satellite, airborne, and terrestrial sensor platforms, has led to important advances in environmental monitoring [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], disaster detection and prevention [6], [7], [8], and precision agriculture [9], [10], [11]. It has also generated massive amounts of data at high level of detail. An example of this is detailed mapping of woody vegetation, which can be used to build machine learning-based decision support tools for optimizing management practices in forestry [12], [13]

The associate editor coordinating the review of this manuscript and approving it for publication was Byung-Gyu Kim.

and precision horticulture [14], [15]. For instance, LiDARbased scanning is becoming a viable solution for collecting structural information about individual trees (e.g., the tree branching exposed during its dormant period) in extensive orchards [16], which has compelling applications for both fruit growers and researchers in the field of phenology [17], [18], [19]. The obtained point clouds can be used for estimation of tree's biomass, canopy size [20], [21], density [22], [23], and yield [24], [25]. By analyzing the changes detected in consecutive tree surveys, improved models of plant growth can be built [26], [27], and the effects of different orchard management strategies [28] and interventions, such as pruning [29], [30], on tree development can be evaluated.

In order to train complex machine learning models, such as deep neural networks, it is necessary to maintain large archives of tree point clouds. However, the storage and transfer of point clouds in raw format can be prohibitive for efficient implementation of machine learning pipelines. While there exist several neural network architectures that operate directly on unstructured point clouds [31], [32], they are typically constrained to treat the input in permutation invariant way. This may introduce unnecessary complexity when full precision is not required or justified (e.g., due to noise inherent in sensor data). In such cases, it is practical to convert the point clouds to a structured grid format by voxelization, and store only a binary occupancy value for each voxel. However, a 10003 voxel grid in bitmap format requires more than 100 MiB of storage, which limits the number of grids that can be held in memory at any time. With the increasing size of datasets used in state-of-the-art deep learning algorithms, this becomes a significant limitation.

Fortunately, voxelization of geometric objects typically produces voxel grids with very low percentage of occupied voxels. The sparsity of a grid allows efficient reduction of its storage space by using hierarchical space partitioning structures, such as a sparse voxel octree (SVO) [33] or a sparse voxel directed acyclic graph (SVDAG) [34]. Low memory footprint is especially important for the training of large voxel-based machine learning models [35], where keeping as many training samples in low-latency on-chip memory as possible is desirable. This calls for finding even more efficient ways for representing voxelized geometry, provided that they support fast on-the-fly decoding.

In this paper, we propose a fast multi-level predictionbased method for pointerless SVO (PSVO) encoding of high-resolution voxel grids. The proposed encoding scheme incorporates context-based prediction of intermediate voxels from a sparser grid of uniformly spaced key voxels. Prediction rules are stored compactly as feature prediction tables that determine the target voxel occupancy based on the occupancies of its context voxels. Using the predictions, the original voxel grid is transformed into an even sparser grid consisting of regularly spaced key voxels and prediction residuals at intermediate locations. We show that such transformation allows more efficient SVO encoding of the resulting grids, as well as their fast parallel decoding.

Motivated by the attractive use cases in precision horticulture and forestry, we focus in particular on efficient representation of voxelized tree models, which are among the most structurally complex natural objects. We do, however, demonstrate a more general applicability of the proposed approach for encoding diverse geometrical objects, where substantial storage savings can be also achieved.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we describe related work in voxel grid encoding and compression. Section III describes our proposed prediction-based method in detail, while Section IV reports the experimental results. Section V concludes the paper.

# II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

The most popular hierarchical encoding technique for sparse voxel grids is the SVO [33], where the grid is subdivided recursively in half along the three coordinate axes. The root of the tree represents the whole grid, and stores an 8-bit binary code indicating the occupancy status of its subgrids. Empty subgrids are skipped, while subgrids containing any occupied voxels are subdivided further, terminating at the level of individual voxels. The result is a tree structure in which each node contains the code and an array of pointers (typically address offsets) to its subnodes. Using the pointers allows navigating down a single branch of the SVO without the need to reconstruct the grid completely. This is important particularly in cases when one needs to determine particular voxel's occupancy quickly, such as during rendering.

For the purpose of offline storage, the pointer-based SVO is linearized by a depth-first traversal of the tree, during which the offsets to child node locations in the output stream are calculated. Since the offset value for a particular subnode depends on the cumulative size of preceding siblings' subtrees, the number of bits required for storing different pointers may vary considerably. This was recognized in a recent work by Madoš et al. [36], who presented clustered SVO (CSVO) as an efficient encoding technique for SVOs with variable-size pointers. However, pointers contribute a substantial part to the size of the resulting stream. In cases where individual voxel queries are not required, such as in model archiving, pointers can be omitted in the SVO representation, since the tree structure can be derived from code values alone. By using a pointerless SVO (PSVO), substantial gains in storage space can be achieved.

A conventional PSVO stores all occupied voxels in the leaves of the octree, as illustrated for a 2D case in the bottom left part of Figure 1. Such basic approach can be inefficient for solid geometry with larger clusters of occupied voxels, which occur in the interior of filled objects. In the case of voxelized trees, it can be beneficial to fill the interior of models, which allows fast estimation of tree's total aboveground biomass [37], [38], balance [29], and tree pruning severity [39]. A possible optimization of PSVO that encodes filled geometry more efficiently is a variant of a technique called complete branch optimization (CBO) [40]. It works by storing a single child node with zero code for every internal node that corresponds to a cubic subspace of all occupied voxels. Since zero code in a child node contradicts the occupied child status in the parent node, the decoder can detect such case and interpret it correctly. The bottom right part of Figure 1 shows an example.

SVDAG is an alternative hierarchical data structure for efficient representation of sparse voxel grids [34]. It is particularly efficient for capturing repetitive geometric patterns, which would lead to multiple instances of identical subtrees in an SVO. In later studies, symmetry-aware [41] and pointerless [42] variants of SVDAG were introduced. In natural objects, where exact repetition of structure is rare,



FIGURE 1. A 2D grid example (top) and its encoding using ordinary SVO (bottom left) and improved SVO with complete branch optimization (bottom right).

lossy SVDAGs [43] can be used for template-based geometry approximation, but with highly ramified objects like trees this may lead to loss of important structural information.

Several methods have been proposed for lossless compression of point clouds [44] that carry over ideas from progressive compression of geometric meshes [45], [46]. Among the voxel-based approaches, octrees have been first used for representation of quantized point clouds in [47], however, no prediction was used to reduce the code length. In [48], predictive coding based on planar approximation of the coarse surface was employed in order to predict non-empty cells on the finer level of progressive point cloud reconstruction. In [49], the code value or bit position of the parent node in the octree structure is used as a context for entropy coding of child codes. In [50], another octree-based method for progressive coding of point clouds is presented, where the reordering of child traversal is used to reduce the entropy of occupancy codes. The traversal is done according to the decreasing occupancy probabilities of subcells, which are computed with respect to the distance from a local planar approximation of object's surface. The methods above focus on progressive coding where occupancy predictions are being made between different levels of the octree.

Current state-of-the-art approaches for compression of quantized point clouds employ trained machine learning models as occupancy predictors [51]. Such methods typically incorporate large neural network models that capture occupancy patterns at multiple scales, which enables them to achieve high prediction accuracy. However, the typical decoding times for these methods are not suitable for on-thefly decoding of grids, for instance within another machine learning pipeline or in interactive visualization. Moreover, keeping another neural network model for grid decoding takes up additional memory on the device.

An alternative technique, tailored specifically for compact representation of rasterized shapes, is chain coding. While several kinds of chain codes exist for describing 2D shapes, the number of their applications in 3D is limited [52], [53], although specific variants for geometric tree models have been presented [54], [55].

### III. METHODOLOGY

In this section, we first provide a high-level overview of the proposed methodology, followed by a detailed description of the individual steps.

# A. A CONCEPT OVERVIEW

The overall concept of the proposed method for encoding voxelized tree models is illustrated in Figure 2. In the proposed method, predictions are made on multiple levels using feature prediction tables that map voxel's context into its predicted occupancy. The prediction context represents a certain arrangement of nearby voxels (i.e., context voxels) with already known occupancy values.

### B. FEATURE PREDICTION TABLE CONSTRUCTION

Model-specific feature prediction tables are built by statistical analysis of the object's voxel grid. For every combination of context voxels' occupancy values, the more frequent occupancy value of the corresponding target voxels across the whole grid is determined and used as a prediction in the encoding and decoding stage. A sequence of context voxels' binary occupancies forms an index into a prediction table. This allows storing the feature prediction tables in the output stream in a very compact form.

During encoding, a context-based prediction from the feature prediction table is used in combination with the true value to determine a residual. During decoding,



FIGURE 2. Overview of the encoding method.

the reverse process is employed to infer the true value from the combination of a prediction and a corresponding residual. A single feature prediction table can be used on multiple levels, or a separate map can be built for each prediction level. The number of prediction levels is specified by an input parameter p, which determines the initial stride between the key voxels.



# C. PREDICTION-BASED ENCODING AND DECODING

Prediction-based encoding of the input voxel grid starts at the sparsest level (denoted by L*0) from a set of regularly distributed *key voxels with a selected initial stride s*0 = 2 *p , where p is a method parameter. The occupancy values of these initial key voxels are stored in the output grid directly. They correspond to the corner voxels of a cube with lateral size of *s*0 + 1 voxels (blue voxels in Figure 3\). Their occupancies constitute the initial context for predicting the occupancies of 19 other voxels, which make up the corners of 8 smaller cubes. At each scale, three prediction steps are executed in sequence, each one providing the prediction context for the next one:

- 1) The occupancy of the central voxel (green voxel in Figure 3\) is first predicted from the initial key voxel context using the feature prediction table 1, and the binary prediction residual is stored at the corresponding location in the output grid.
- 2) The occupancies of cube's side centers (pink voxels in Figure 3\) are predicted next using feature prediction table 2 and the residuals stored in the output.
- 3) Finally, the occupancies of cube's edge centers (yellow voxels in Figure 3\) are predicted using feature prediction table 3 and the residuals stored in the output.

Once the processing at level L*0 is finished, the encoded voxels become the key voxels, and the prediction and encoding of residuals are repeated on the next level *L*1 with stride *s*1 = *s*0 2 . The process is repeated *p-times, where on the last level the occupancy of each target voxel is predicted using a subset of its 26-voxel neighbourhood as a context. In general, stride si = 2 p−i is used to encode level Li .

FIGURE 3. Voxel types used in different steps of grid encoding on the same level of recursion: the central voxel (green) is predicted first using the key voxels (blue), which is followed by the prediction of side centers (pink) and edge centers (yellow).

Next, we present a formal description of the encoding process. Let vx,y,z denote a voxel at integer grid coordinates (x, y,z), where x ∈ {0 . . . , X − 1}, y ∈ {0 . . . , Y − 1}, z ∈ {0 . . . , Z−1} and X×Y ×Z is the grid dimension. At level Li with stride si , the key voxels with known occupancy values have coordinates (qx · si, qy · si, qz · si) for qx ∈ {0, . . . ⌊X/si⌋}, qy ∈ {0, . . . ⌊Y /si⌋}, qz ∈ {0, . . . ⌊Z/si⌋},. The key voxels at level Li form the cube corners, which are separated by si − 1 intermediate voxels in each direction.

Let us denote si 2 by ri . The arrangement of context voxels used in the prediction steps is as follows:

- In step 1, the occupancy of each central voxel vx,y,z is predicted using the nearest eight key voxels vx±ri,y±ri,z±ri (Figure 4a\). The feature prediction table 1, used for this step, is 28 = 256 bits in size.
- In step 2, the occupancy of each cube face center is predicted using six context voxels, namely four surrounding key voxels and two nearest voxels determined in the previous step. For instance, the occupancy of voxel vx,y,z , which is the center of a cube face orthogonal to the grid x direction, is done using key voxels vx,y±ri,z±ri and cube centers vx±ri,y,z (Figure 4b\). The corresponding feature prediction table 2 requires 26 = 64 bits.

- In step 3, the occupancy of each cube edge center is predicted using a context consisting of two nearest corner voxels and four nearest face centers determined in the previous step. For instance, the occupancy of voxel vx,y,z , which is the middle voxel of a cube edge parallel to the grid z direction, is done using key voxels vx,y,z±ri and face centers vx±ri,y±ri,z (Figure 4c\). Another 64 bits are required for storing the feature prediction table 3 for this step.

FIGURE 4. Prediction contexts for steps 1–3 of the encoding/decoding iteration.

For non-cubic grids and grids whose dimensions are not equal to 2*d* for some d ∈ Z +, some prediction contexts will include non-existing voxels outside of the grid, whose values are taken to be 0. The feature prediction tables for prediction steps 1-3 are concatenated and the resulting 48 bytes are stored in the output stream as part of a binary header. This can be done for each prediction level separately, such that the total header size amounts to p·48 bytes for feature prediction tables, plus an additional byte for storing the number of levels p. Alternatively, only the top-level feature prediction table can be stored and used for predictions on all levels, which is indicated by storing p = 0.

The output of the above procedure is a new voxel grid in which the initial key voxels' actual values are interspersed among the prediction residuals for all other voxels. The obtained residual grid is then encoded with a PSVO. The PSVO is finally linearized by concatenating the codes in the depth-first traversal order of octree nodes. The resulting binary sequence is stored in the output stream following the header. A novelty of the proposed approach is that prediction contexts on different levels are modelled with different strides (i.e., spacing) between context voxels, but always at full grid resolution. This differs from existing approaches that predict voxel occupancy using a local context of its SVO node, where a different resolution is used at each level of the SVO.

The process of decoding is symmetric to encoding. The value of p and the corresponding number of feature prediction tables are obtained from the input stream's header. The grid of mixed key voxel and residual values is reconstructed from the PSVO code in the rest of the stream. This is followed by the same coarse-to-fine procedure of voxel occupancy predictions, which are combined with the residuals and stored in the corresponding grid cells.



### IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

In this section, we describe the experiments that were performed to evaluate the efficiency of the proposed method. The main performance metric was the storage size reduction obtained by the proposed method with respect to no use of feature-based prediction. Decoding times with and without parallelization were also collected to assess the suitability of the method for on-the-fly reconstruction of voxel grids in a machine learning pipeline.

### A. DATA

Two datasets of tree models were used in the experiments. The first dataset consists of realistic and geometrically versatile tree models generated using the EduApple software [56]. EduApple is a tree growth simulator, which allows extensive customization of generated trees' characteristics through user-specified parameters. Tree growth is guided by the availability of light in different parts of the canopy, but is otherwise randomized in order to support varying tree shapes. By modulating the simulation parameters, a dataset of 400 high-detail synthetic tree models was obtained. These cover a diverse spectrum of tree forms with varying branching complexity, from juvenile trees characterized by sparse two-level branching to fully developed mature trees featuring dense and highly ramified canopy. Some examples of generated tree models are shown in Figure 5.

Tree models were densely sampled to produce the point clouds, which were then voxelized by using each model's bounding box to define the extent of a voxel grid. A 1 cm3 grid resolution was found to be sufficient for preserving enough structural detail and clear separation of smaller twigs. Filling of the tree interior was performed by executing a flood-fill algorithm of the exterior, seeded by an unoccupied edge voxel, and inverting the result. The average number of voxels per grid across all 400 models in the final dataset was 63,406,098, while the average occupancy was 0.277%. The dataset size in raw (uncompressed) form, where each voxel is stored using one bit, was 2.953 GiB. The entropy of the dataset was 0.02839 bits per voxel, which means that 85.823 MiB was the theoretical lower bound for the dataset size when using optimal lossless entropy coding.

However, a more efficient storage can be achieved by exploiting the specific structure and redundancy in the data.

The second tree dataset is a publicly available dataset of 691 single tree point clouds from terrestrial laser scanning [57]. A selection of point clouds is shown in Figure 6. The point clouds were converted to voxel grids with 5 cm voxel lateral size. To reduce the effects of noise, morphological closing with a cubical structural element 5 voxels wide was performed, and the remaining single isolated voxels were filtered out. The raw dataset size with one bit per voxel was 1.407 GiB. The average number of voxels per grid was 17,495,475, with average occupancy 0.558%. The data entropy was 0.04122 bits per voxel, and the lower bound for the size of the entropy-coded dataset was 59.4 MiB.

In order to assess the usefulness of the proposed method for encoding general geometry, we have also tested its efficiency on a small set of publicly available models shown in Figure 7. Both hollow and filled variants of voxelized objects were used. The main properties of the voxelized objects are collected in Table 1.

### 4) DECODING TIME

The encoding and decoding parts of the proposed method are symmetric, and thus require similar amount of time. However, fast decoding is particularly important for on-the-fly voxel grid reconstruction, e.g. within a machine learning pipeline. An experiment was performed measuring the run-time of substeps, namely SVO decoding and voxel grid reconstruction from residuals and feature prediction tables. Parallelization of the latter sub-step on the GPU was realized using OpenMP in combination with the Clang compiler. The complete synthetic tree dataset was decoded in 3.76 seconds, or 9.4 ms per tree, with approximately 4% of the time spent in SVO decoding and the rest in the reconstruction of the original voxel grid from predictions and residuals. The achieved run-times render the proposed approach suitable for on-demand decoding of training batches in machine learning.

- V. CONCLUSION This paper proposed an efficient method for encoding and decoding of voxelized geometric models. The proposed approach uses multi-level prediction of voxel occupancy based on the known context starting from a sparse grid of key voxels, and encodes the residuals with a PSVO. Experimental results showed that storage savings in the range of 10–15% can be achieved with respect to plain PSVO and PSVO+CBO for synthetic and LiDAR-based tree models, while 8–12% gains were demonstrated when encoding general objects. Contemporary machine learning tasks typically require huge quantities of training data which do not fit the working memory of a training device and must be continuously fetched from an external source. Maintaining the training data in on-chip memory in an encoded format and decoding it on-the-fly for repetitive consumption by the training pipeline is therefore an attractive and viable use case. To this end, the decoding should not incorporate time-consuming decompression steps and should be easily implementable on a parallel architecture, such as a GPU, which drives most of today's machine learning tasks. The proposed method fits these requirements well since it can be parallelized in a straightforward manner, achieving average decoding times of less than 10 milliseconds for individual synthetic tree objects. The proposed approach also has some limitations. For instance, the voxel grid is reconstructed directly at the highest resolution by filling gaps at successively smaller strides. As a result, the method does not support progressive refinement, a feature that would make it suitable for rendering at increasing levels of detail [47]. Additionally, while individual voxel queries can be answered using localized grid decoding, efficiently handling a sequence of queries would require a smart caching strategy. Consequently, the proposed method may not be efficient for real-time rendering with ray tracing of large scenes. In future work, the proposed methodology could be extended with more advanced voxel occupancy prediction rules, balancing between the overhead of rule storage and the size of encoded residuals. Another promising direction of research is near-lossless encoding of voxelized objects with constrained local and global distortion to preserve important features for downstream machine learning tasks.
