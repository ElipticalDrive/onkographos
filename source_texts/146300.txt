A BSTRACT
Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructs scenes from images in seconds.
Pixel-aligned methods keep every Gaussian grounded in an observation, but produce redundant, potentially misaligned Gaussians across views. Token-based methods instead aggregate all views into a fixed set of learned tokens but lack explicit
observation support and use a fixed reconstruction budget. We introduce ZipSplat, a
method that predicts a compact, observation-grounded scene representation whose
size is set at inference. It derives each scene token from an image patch rather than
a learned query, and decodes it into freely placed 3D Gaussians. Tokens across
views are merged into a compact set whose size is adjustable at inference. However,
without geometric supervision, decoded Gaussians can drift from their source patch,
and merging can mix tokens from different surfaces. We therefore introduce two
geometric losses to ground and shape this representation. A cone loss ties each
token’s Gaussians to its source patch, and GeoCE aligns token similarity with
3D proximity so that tokens observing the same surface merge together. Building
on these observation–geometry associations, we further introduce TokenBA, a
token-level bundle adjustment that refines scene tokens and camera poses in a few
seconds. ZipSplat requires neither camera poses nor intrinsics, yet outperforms
pixel-aligned and token-based baselines on DL3DV and RealEstate10K, even those
given ground-truth cameras. It further generalizes zero-shot to Mip-NeRF360 and
ScanNet++. Our project page is at https://veichta.com/zipsplat.

I NTRODUCTION
Novel view synthesis has shifted from per-scene optimization of radiance fields (Mildenhall et al.,
2020) and 3D Gaussians (Kerbl et al., 2023) to feed-forward models that predict a Gaussian scene
from a collection of images in a few seconds (Charatan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025;
2026). As these methods mature, performance increasingly depends on the scene representation itself:
how image observations become Gaussians, how many are predicted, and where they are placed.

Most existing feed-forward methods predict one Gaussian per input pixel (Xu et al., 2025; Ye et al.,
2025a; 2026; Jiang et al., 2025), directly grounding each prediction in an image observation. However,
repeated observations of the same surface produce separate, view-specific Gaussians rather than
a shared prediction, creating substantial cross-view redundancy. Errors in the depth and camera
estimates can further misalign these predictions and produce visible artifacts.
Recent methods based on fixed latent queries instead aggregate multi-view observations into shared
tokens before decoding freely placed Gaussians (An et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026; Itkin et al., 2026).
This aggregation can reduce redundancy and cross-view misalignment, but the learned queries have
no explicit link to the observations they represent. Unlike pixel-aligned Gaussians, whose source
view is known by construction, these tokens must learn their observation support implicitly, which
can produce blurry reconstructions. Their reconstruction budget is also fixed by design: a small object
capture and a large scene receive the same number of tokens and Gaussians.
These limitations motivate a representation that grounds each prediction in its source observation
without fixing the reconstruction budget in the architecture. We introduce ZipSplat, which predicts a
compact, observation-grounded scene representation from unposed images. Its reconstruction budget
is adjustable at inference without retraining, letting it match the scene rather than the architecture.
Rather than using learned queries, we derive each scene token directly from its source image patch,
making the token-to-observation link explicit by construction. Each token is decoded into freely
placed 3D Gaussians, but without geometric constraints the decoded Gaussians can drift from the
patch that produced them. A new cone loss addresses this by constraining each token’s Gaussians to
reproject near their source patch, grounding them in their observation.
As views overlap, many patches observe the same surface and their tokens can be merged into a
more compact set. However, merging in feature space does not guarantee that the resulting groups
correspond to the same surface. A new geometric contrastive loss, GeoCE, aligns token similarity
with 3D proximity so that clusters form by surface rather than by viewpoint.
The representation is now compact and grounded, but it remains the output of a single forward pass,
with residual camera and geometry error. The grounding that ties each token’s Gaussians to its source
patch also gives every token a persistent 2D–3D correspondence that can be used for geometric
refinement. We exploit this with TokenBA, a token-level bundle adjustment that refines cameras and
scene tokens through the frozen decoder, improving reconstruction in seconds.
ZipSplat establishes a new state of the art on standard benchmarks using only unposed, uncalibrated
images at inference. It consistently outperforms both pixel-aligned and token-based feed-forward
methods while using substantially fewer Gaussians. It generalizes zero-shot to diverse indoor and
outdoor scenes, and continues to improve as additional views are provided. Ablations validate the
representation and its components, and further experiments characterize the adjustable budget and
cross-dataset generalization.
In summary, we make the following contributions:
We introduce ZipSplat, a feed-forward model that reconstructs scenes from unposed images as
compact, observation-grounded scene tokens, each derived from a source patch and decoded into
freely placed Gaussians. The reconstruction budget is adjustable at inference without retraining.
• We introduce two geometric losses that ground and shape this representation geometrically: a cone
loss that ties each token’s decoded Gaussians to its source patch, and GeoCE, which aligns token
similarity with 3D proximity for cross-view clustering.
• We introduce TokenBA, an efficient token-level bundle adjustment that exploits the resulting
2D–3D correspondences to jointly refine scene tokens and camera poses through reprojection and
photometric consistency within a few seconds.

Token-based Gaussian prediction. Token-based methods remove pixel alignment by decoding freely
placed Gaussians from latent tokens, similar to Scaffold-GS (Lu et al., 2024), which decodes them
from per-scene anchors. TokenGS (Ren et al., 2026), C3G (An et al., 2026), and GlobalSplat (Itkin
et al., 2026) aggregate multi-view features with learned queries, while TokenSplat (Li et al., 2026)
voxelizes and merges image tokens before decoding. These approaches fuse repeated observations
and decouple the Gaussian count from input size, but their query count or voxelization is fixed, so
changing the budget requires retraining. Learned queries also lack explicit supporting observations.
In contrast, ZipSplat clusters image tokens in a learned geometric space, preserving their observation
support while allowing the budget to be adjusted at inference through the clustering ratio.
Post-hoc Refinements. Learned reconstruction systems commonly end with a refinement step:
DROID-SLAM (Teed & Deng, 2021) embeds a dense bundle adjustment layer in its update, and
VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) refines its predicted cameras by bundle adjustment over predicted tracks.
Feed-forward 3DGS methods so far ignore or lack such correspondences, so refinement falls back
to photometric optimization with ground-truth poses. C3G (An et al., 2026) optimizes the decoded
Gaussians as in per-scene 3DGS, while TokenGS (Ren et al., 2026) tunes its learnable queries through
the frozen network, which preserves geometry but trails Gaussian-space tuning in quality. TokenBA
instead uses the built-in 2D–3D correspondences of scene tokens to refine the scene representation
and camera poses, achieving faster convergence and higher quality without pose supervision.

3.1
O BSERVATION -G ROUNDED S CENE T OKENS
Each image patch observes a specific surface region. To give each observation a dedicated token,
a multi-view backbone extracts per-patch visual tokens at multiple layers. These are merged into
geometry tokens g, one per image patch, forming a shared space for clustering, attention, and
geometric supervision. Each geometry token serves as a scene token.
Gaussian decoding. Each scene token represents a surface region whose spatial extent and complexity
vary across the scene. An MLP decoder D maps each scene token zk into G freely placed Gaussians:
\mathcal {G}_k = \left \{ \left ( \gmean _{kj}, \gscale _{kj}, \grot _{kj}, \gopac _{kj}, \gcolor _{kj} \right ) \right \}_{j=1}^{\ngs } = D(\stok _k), \label {eq:decode}
(1)
with the full scene assembled from all K tokens. Free placement lets the token spread its Gaussians
over the observed region rather than anchoring each one to a fixed ray. However, nothing constrains
decoded Gaussians to stay near their source patch: the decoder can place them anywhere in 3D, and
the association between token and observation is not guaranteed.
Cone loss. The cone loss grounds each token’s Gaussians in its source patch (Fig. 3a). For each token
k, it penalizes the reprojection distance between its Gaussian centers and the source patch center:
\loss _{\mathrm {cone}} = \sum _{k=1}^{K} \sum _{j=1}^{\ngs } \rho \!\left ( \left \| \pi _k(\gmean _{kj})-\*{u}_k \right \| \right ), \label {eq:cone}
(2)
where πk projects into the source view of token k, uk is its patch center, distances are measured in
patch units, and ρ is a robust penalty that limits the influence of large residuals. This constraint is only
definable because each token has a known source patch. A frustum loss, as used by TokenGS (Ren
et al., 2026), keeps Gaussians visible but cannot tie them to a specific observation. With the cone loss,
our approach yields an observation-grounded scene representation: each scene token is derived from
a source patch, its Gaussians are tied back to that patch, and these associations persist at inference.
3.2
M ERGING R EDUNDANT S CENE T OKENS
When multiple views cover a scene, many patches observe the same surface from different viewpoints.
Their tokens carry redundant information and can be merged into a smaller set whose size is adjustable
at inference without retraining.
Inference-time clustering. We cluster the geometry tokens into K groups using k-means in cosine
space. Given T patches per view, the budget K is set through the compression ratio
\qr = \frac {K}{\nviews T}, \qquad \qr \in (0,1]. \label {eq:compression_ratio}
(3)

fine detail. Each scene

Token fusion. To recover detail lost during cluster averaging, we refine the scene tokens through
alternating cross- and self-attention:
\stok ^{l} = \operatorname {SA}\!\left ( \operatorname {CA} (\stok ^{l-1},\,\gtok ,\,\vtok _l) \right ), \qquad l=1,\ldots ,L, \label {eq:fusion}
(4)
where the geometry tokens g serve as keys, biasing retrieval toward relevant observations across
views, and the backbone features f l provide the values, processed from deep to shallow layers. A
final residual cross-attention incorporates color features:
\stok = \stok ^{L} + \operatorname {CA} (\stok ^{L},\,\gtok ,\,\vtok ^{\mathrm {col}}). \label {eq:color_ca}
(5)
GeoCE loss. Clustering can merge tokens across views, but nothing guarantees it groups patches that
observe the same surface. GeoCE makes this explicit by shaping the token space (Fig. 3b). During
training, each token’s patch is unprojected to a 3D anchor pi using its median depth. From these
anchors we construct a soft target distribution over token pairs, weighted by 3D proximity:
w_{ij} \propto \exp \!\left ( -\frac {\|\spoint _i-\spoint _j\|^2} {2\sigma _i\sigma _j} \right ), \qquad j\neq i, \label {eq:geoce_target}
(6)
where σi scales with the local patch spacing (Section A.2). Nearby patches get high weight; distant
ones get near zero. GeoCE pushes the token cosine similarities to match this target via cross-entropy:
\loss _{\mathrm {geo}} = -\sum _i\sum _{j\neq i} w_{ij} \log \frac {\exp (\gtok _i^\top \gtok _j/\tau )} {\sum _{k\neq i}\exp (\gtok _i^\top \gtok _k/\tau )}, \label {eq:geoce}
(7)
with temperature τ . Since the target is soft, multiple patches can share high weight when they observe
the same surface, making cross-view merging explicit rather than leaving it to the rendering loss.
Training. The model is trained end-to-end with rendering, geometric, and camera supervision:
\loss = \loss _{\mathrm {render}} + \lambda _{\mathrm {geo}}\,\loss _{\mathrm {geo}} + \lambda _{\mathrm {cone}}\,\loss _{\mathrm {cone}} + \lambda _{\mathrm {pose}}\,\loss _{\mathrm {pose}}. \label {eq:loss}
(8)
The rendering loss combines ℓ1 and perceptual terms on novel target views, while Lpose supervises
the predicted cameras. Coefficients and details are in Section A.4.
3.3
T OKEN BA: R EFINING C AMERAS AND S CENE T OKENS
The feed-forward prediction carries residual camera and geometry error. The patch–Gaussian associations from Section 3.1 persist at inference and provide 2D–3D correspondences for refining both
cameras and scene tokens from the input views within seconds.
Camera update. With the geometry fixed, we update each camera pose Pn using the Gaussian–patch
correspondences. For each Gaussian j of token k with a supporting patch i ∈ Ck visible in view n,
we minimize the robust reprojection error:
E_{\mathrm {cam}}(\pose _n) = \sum _{(k,j,i)} \rho \!\left ( \left \| \pi (\pose _n,\gmean _{kj})-\*{u}_i \right \| \right ), \label {eq:pnp}
(9)
where ρ is the penalty from Eq. (2). Each pose is estimated independently by robust PnP, minimizing
Eq. (9) over all its correspondences with graduated non-convexity, followed by a short photometric
polish to reduce patch-center quantization.

Alternating optimization. Camera correction improves the consistency of rendering gradients, while
token refinement improves the geometry for subsequent camera updates. TokenBA alternates these
steps and concludes with a short joint photometric refinement of tokens and poses, improving the
feed-forward reconstruction within a few seconds (Fig. 3c).

E XPERIMENTS
Implementation Details. We initialize the backbone and camera head from DA3-Giant (Lin et al.,
2026). Features from three backbone layers are merged into geometry tokens and fused into scene
tokens through three cross- and self-attention blocks. Each scene token produces G=32 Gaussians
with degree-1 spherical harmonics. We train on an equal mixture of RealEstate10K (Zhou et al.,
2018) and DL3DV (Ling et al., 2024) at 252×252 resolution for 450K steps on 16 GH200 GPUs.
The input-view count increases from 2 to 24 over the first half of training. Rendering supervision
combines ℓ1 and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) (weight 0.1) on four target views, with auxiliary weights
λgeo =0.05, λcone =0.01, and λpose =0.05. We sample the compression ratio uniformly from [0.2/N, 1],
giving a minimum budget of approximately 2K Gaussians. TokenBA uses up to four alternating
rounds followed by at most 96 joint refinement steps with an ℓ1 objective, adding approximately 1.6 s
per scene at 16 views on an RTX 4090. Further details are provided in Section A.

Results. Table 1 shows that ZipSplat achieves the highest quality in all three settings, including
against methods given ground-truth cameras. On DL3DV, the advantage grows with more views: from
4.45 dB over DA3, which shares our backbone, at 16 views to 8.40 dB at 64 views, with about 24×
fewer Gaussians. Pixel-aligned prediction ties each Gaussian to a single view’s depth and pose errors;
as views increase, these misalignments accumulate rather than cancel, whereas our free 3D placement
avoids this dependence (Fig. 5). Against TokenGS with TTO, the strongest token-based baseline, the
gap widens from 3.38 to 6.33 dB across the same range. Despite its strong PSNR, TokenGS blurs fine
details and misses scene content (Figs. 5 and 8), because learned queries must infer their observation
support implicitly. Our scene tokens start from their supporting patches, preserving content and
producing sharper reconstructions. On RealEstate10K, ZipSplat exceeds TokenGS by 0.91 dB with
4× fewer Gaussians; posed methods lead only in SSIM, likely benefiting from ground-truth intrinsics.
Even without TokenBA, ZipSplat outperforms all evaluated feed-forward baselines in PSNR and
LPIPS on every benchmark (Section F.1).

4.5
A BLATIONS
Table 4 ablates design choices on DL3DV at 6 views; every row is a DA3-Base retrain with the same
training budget. Per-pixel prediction gives 22.62 dB from 381K Gaussians with no budget control.
Fixed learned queries (matching TokenGS (Ren et al., 2026): 4K queries × 64 Gaussians) reach
23.02 dB at 262K Gaussians with their frustum loss, which constrains Gaussians to lie within the
input views, but the budget is locked at training time. Observation-derived tokens make the budget
adjustable at inference: 62K Gaussians reach 22.87 dB, and halving to 31K costs only 0.34 dB. Yet
the frustum loss does not tie tokens to their source observations. The cone loss grounds them and
adds the largest training-time gain (+1.51 dB). It also provides the 2D–3D correspondences TokenBA
relies on: without it, TokenBA collapses to 14.7 dB. GeoCE further improves clustering by making
tokens that observe the same surface merge, adding +0.16 dB at r=0.5 and lifting AUC@3 from
0.669 to 0.686. With grounding established, TokenBA refines cameras and tokens through the learned
correspondences, delivering the large gains in both reconstruction and pose accuracy.
5
C ONCLUSION
We present ZipSplat, a feed-forward model that reconstructs scenes from unposed images as compact,
observation-grounded scene tokens. Each token is derived from an image patch and decoded into
freely placed 3D Gaussians. Tokens across views are merged, removing redundancy and making
the budget adjustable. The cone loss ties each token’s Gaussians to its source patch, which is what
makes the grounding pay off. GeoCE shapes the token space so that merged tokens observe the
same surface. The resulting correspondences enable TokenBA, which refines cameras and tokens
within seconds. Without poses or intrinsics, ZipSplat outperforms baselines with substantially fewer
Gaussians, including those given ground-truth cameras, and generalizes zero-shot to unseen datasets.
Limitations: The camera estimates can degrade on scenes that differ strongly from the training
distribution. Ground-truth cameras close this gap, and TokenBA recovers most of it, but a residual
remains on out-of-distribution captures. The method also assumes static scenes.

Fusion and decoding. Fusion uses three blocks. Each block is a cross-attention (queries: scene
tokens; keys: geometry tokens; values: the visual tokens of one layer, deepest first) followed by
self-attention among the scene tokens. Blocks use 24 heads, MLP ratio 4, GELU, and residual
gates initialized to 0.01. A final residual cross-attention with the color embedding as values adds
appearance. A two-layer MLP decodes each scene token into G=32 Gaussians of 23 parameters.
Reference frame. All geometry is expressed in the coordinate frame of the first input camera. The
scene scale is normalized so that the median distance of the input depth points to the origin is one.
Camera estimation and priors. A two-layer ReLU MLP maps the deepest backbone camera token
to a translation, unit quaternion, and field of view, initialized from the DA3 camera head. Lpose
applies a Huber loss on translation, an ℓ1 chordal loss on rotations, and an ℓ1 loss on the field of view.
When ground-truth cameras are available, DA3’s frozen camera encoder injects them into the camera
token; this path is used with probability 0.2 during training.
Gaussian parameterization. Positions use an inverse-log activation sign(y)(exp |y| − 1) on raw
outputs clamped to [−5, 5], scales use softplus(x−4) clamped to [10−6 , 15], rotations are normalized
quaternions, opacities use a sigmoid with bias −1.5, and colors are degree-1 spherical harmonics
(12 coefficients). All 32 Gaussians of a token start from a shared template with a forward bias of
z=0.5. Squared-hinge penalties keep scales in [10−6 , 0.1], opacities above 0.01, and positions within
a radius of 10. Table 5 varies the number of Gaussians per scene token. G=32 gains 0.34 to 0.36 dB

averaged over a 4×4 pixel grid inside each key patch, and patches with valid depth on fewer than
half their pixels are excluded. The base bandwidth σ is the scene-wise median 3D distance between
adjacent patch points. Per token, σi is log-interpolated from σ (flattest patch) to 0.4 σ (most textured),
ranked by mean image-gradient energy. The temperature is τ =0.1 and the loss acts on ℓ2 -normalized

Cone loss. The robust penalty ρ in Eq. (2) is a hinged Cauchy function of the reprojection distance
in patch units. The hinge sets a zero-cost region within 2 patches, allowing Gaussians to extend past
their observations to cover flat surfaces or occluded regions. Beyond the hinge, the penalty ramps
smoothly (C 1 ) and saturates past 4 patches to limit the influence of distant outliers. Gaussians that
project behind a member view receive a sentinel distance of 20 patches with no lateral gradient. The
loss constrains only the lateral position relative to the supporting patches’ viewing cones and leaves
depth to the rendering loss, with weight λcone =0.01.
A.3
C LUSTERING
We run spherical k-means on the ℓ2 -normalized geometry tokens with cosine assignments and
centroid renormalization after each update. Lloyd iterations are capped at 50 but typically converge
in 2 to 3 iterations at r=0.5 and 7 to 9 at r=0.1. Seeds are sampled without replacement with
probability proportional to (0.05 + ci )2 , where ci ∈ [0, 1] is the per-scene rank of patch i by mean
image-gradient energy. This biases initialization toward textured regions but does not change the
objective. Each scene token is the mean of its members’ geometry tokens, recomputed differentiably
from the detached assignments. During training, r ∼ U[rmin , 1] with rmin =0.2/N , giving at least
about 65 scene tokens at any view count. A fixed Gaussian budget B corresponds to r = B/(GN T ).
A.4
T RAINING
Training is a single stage from DA3-Giant on a 50/50 mixture of RealEstate10K (Zhou et al., 2018)
and DL3DV (Ling et al., 2024) at 252×252. It runs for 450K steps on 16 GH200 GPUs with 24
samples per GPU and 4 target views per sample. We use AdamW (β1 =0.9, β2 =0.95, weight decay
0.05, gradient clipping 1.0) with a base learning rate of 3×10−4 , the backbone at 0.1×, 5% linear
warmup, cosine decay to zero, and bfloat16 autocast. The input view count grows from 2 to 24 over
the first half of training. A single checkpoint, trained on the RealEstate10K and DL3DV mixture, is
used across all benchmarks for evaluation.
A.5
T OKEN BA
TokenBA optimizes the scene tokens Z and the input camera poses under an ℓ1 rendering loss on
the input views, with the decoder frozen. Tokens use Adam with a learning rate of 10−3 during the
alternation and 2×10−3 in the joint tail. The decoder MLP body runs in half precision while its
readout stays in full precision.
Camera update. The correspondences in Eq. (9) follow from the cluster assignments: every Gaussian
of a scene token is paired with the patch center of each member patch. Each pose is solved by
robust PnP with graduated non-convexity, annealing the robust scale from 64 to 12 to 4 pixels over
4 iterations per level on 2048 sampled correspondences. Seven rotation hypotheses per view (the
current rotation and ±40◦ about each camera axis) are solved in one batched call, and the hypothesis
with the most inliers within 2 patches wins. A photometric polish then removes the patch-center
quantization error, running two steps at half resolution and one at full resolution with the aligner
described below.
Photometric aligner. The aligner is a direct Levenberg–Marquardt solver on the photometric residual.
Each step renders at the current pose and takes a Gauss–Newton update on the brightness-constancy
linearization with the analytic image-warp Jacobian, using one render per step and no autograd. The
full configuration uses a four-level pyramid (1/8, 1/4, 1/2, and full resolution) with 12, 10, 8, and
6 steps per level, Gaussian blur at the two coarse levels, and accept-reject steps with a trust-region
clamp on the pose twist. A scan over nine log-spaced global scale factors from 0.5 to 2.0 precedes
the pyramid, and the best full-resolution pose across levels is kept. Inside TokenBA only the two fine
levels run, without verification or scale scan.

Token update and joint refinement. Each alternation round ends with one token refit step. Rounds
stop once the PnP snap moves every pose by less than 0.3◦ , or after four rounds. The joint tail refines
tokens and poses (an se(3) increment with learning rate 3×10−4 ) for at most 96 steps, reusing the
same Adam state. It exits once a block of 8 steps improves the loss by less than 2%.
B
E VALUATION P ROTOCOL
We describe the evaluation setup shared by all experiments: view selection, pose metrics, baselines,
and the resolution and resize choices that affect reported numbers.
B.1
V IEW SELECTION
On DL3DV (140 scenes), every 8th frame is held out as a target, and context views are selected from
the remaining frames by k-means clustering on the 6-D feature vector of camera position and viewing
direction (Ziwen et al., 2025). On Mip-NeRF360 (6 scenes) and ScanNet++ (46 iPhone captures),
targets are selected by farthest point sampling over the camera centers, and context views over the
remaining frames. We evaluate at 16, 32, 64, and 128 input views on DL3DV and at 32, 64, and 128
on Mip-NeRF360 and ScanNet++, with targets fixed across view counts. On RealEstate10K (1,600
scenes) we use the 6-view protocol of YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026) with 8 held-out targets.
B.2
P OSE ACCURACY
We report relative rotation error and translation-direction error over all input-view pairs, pooled over
all scenes before computing the AUC. Pooling produces a single error distribution that weights each
pair equally, which differs from a mean of per-scene AUCs. The main text reports AUC at 1, 3, and 5
degrees, and Section F.5 extends this to 10, 15, 20, and 30 degrees.
B.3
BASELINES
We reimplement each baseline into our codebase and validate it against the original code up to
floating-point precision. All methods are then evaluated under the same protocol: the same view
indices, the same metric resolution, and the same resize filter. Since a predicted scene need not
align perfectly with the ground-truth cameras, we align the target cameras to the predicted scene
before computing metrics for every method, following prior work (Ye et al., 2025a; 2026). Posefree methods (AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), EcoSplat (Park et al., 2026), DA3 (Lin et al., 2026),
C3G (An et al., 2026), YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026)) predict cameras from the input images. Posed
methods (DepthSplat (Xu et al., 2025), ZPressor (Wang et al., 2025b), VolSplat (Wang et al., 2026),
GlobalSplat (Itkin et al., 2026), TokenGS (Ren et al., 2026)) receive ground-truth poses and intrinsics.
Per-scene 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) optimizes 100K Gaussians, initialized randomly around the
camera centroids, for 30K iterations on the context views with ground-truth cameras, providing
an optimization-based reference. Each method’s encoder runs at its own training resolution, and
all renders are scored at a common 252×252 (Section B.4). SparseSplat (Zhang et al., 2026) and
TokenSplat (Li et al., 2026) had no public code at the time of writing.
B.4
R ENDERING RESOLUTION
Methods train at different resolutions and aspect ratios, and scoring at a lower resolution raises PSNR,
since downsampling removes the fine detail that is hardest to reconstruct. To compare fairly, we score
every method at a common 252×252 while still running each encoder at its own training resolution.
Table 6 compares this protocol with scoring each baseline at its own resolution and with running
it entirely at 252. Scoring each baseline at its own resolution would favor the low-resolution ones
and penalize the high-resolution ones: YoNoSplat gains 0.15 dB at its native 224, while AnySplat
loses 0.31 dB at its native 448. Running a baseline entirely at 252 is worse still, costing AnySplat
4.2 to 5.0 dB and YoNoSplat 2.1 to 2.8 dB. Our protocol therefore keeps each encoder at its training
resolution, and the high-resolution baselines keep the detail they encode.

D.2
C LUSTERING METHOD
ZipSplat compresses by running k-means on the geometry tokens and merging each cluster into
one scene token. We test how much each part of this choice matters, namely the initialization, the
space we cluster in, and the clustering itself. Table 11 keeps the network fixed and only changes how
its tokens are grouped, and we compare each row with k-means on the geometry tokens under the
same strided initialization. Complexity-weighted initialization adds 0.3 dB at r=0.25 and 0.1 dB at
r=0.1. The space matters most. At r=0.1, clustering the backbone tokens costs 0.7 dB and the patch
colors 3.7 dB, a per-view grid on image positions costs 1.0 dB, and even the ground-truth 3D points
are 0.1 dB worse than the geometry tokens. The clustering algorithm matters little, since merging
every token into its nearest seed without k-means iterations costs only 0.1 to 0.2 dB. Merging itself is
essential, as keeping only the seed tokens loses 1.8 dB at r=0.25 and 5.7 dB at r=0.1.
