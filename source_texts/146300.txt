## ABSTRACT

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructs scenes from images in seconds. Pixel-aligned methods keep every Gaussian grounded in an observation, but produce redundant, potentially misaligned Gaussians across views. Token-based methods instead aggregate all views into a fixed set of learned tokens but lack explicit observation support and use a fixed reconstruction budget. We introduce ZipSplat, a method that predicts a compact, observation-grounded scene representation whose size is set at inference. It derives each scene token from an image patch rather than a learned query, and decodes it into freely placed 3D Gaussians. Tokens across views are merged into a compact set whose size is adjustable at inference. However, without geometric supervision, decoded Gaussians can drift from their source patch, and merging can mix tokens from different surfaces. We therefore introduce two geometric losses to ground and shape this representation. A cone loss ties each token's Gaussians to its source patch, and GeoCE aligns token similarity with 3D proximity so that tokens observing the same surface merge together. Building on these observation–geometry associations, we further introduce TokenBA, a token-level bundle adjustment that refines scene tokens and camera poses in a few seconds. ZipSplat requires neither camera poses nor intrinsics, yet outperforms pixel-aligned and token-based baselines on DL3DV and RealEstate10K, even those given ground-truth cameras. It further generalizes zero-shot to Mip-NeRF360 and ScanNet++. Our project page is at [https://veichta.com/zipsplat.](https://veichta.com/zipsplat)

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Novel view synthesis has shifted from per-scene optimization of radiance fields \(Mildenhall et al., 2020\) and 3D Gaussians \(Kerbl et al., 2023\) to feed-forward models that predict a Gaussian scene from a collection of images in a few seconds \(Charatan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; 2026\). As these methods mature, performance increasingly depends on the scene representation itself: how image observations become Gaussians, how many are predicted, and where they are placed.

Preprint

Most existing feed-forward methods predict one Gaussian per input pixel (Xu et al., 2025; Ye et al.,
2025a; 2026; Jiang et al., 2025), directly grounding each prediction in an image observation. However,
repeated observations of the same surface produce separate, view-specific Gaussians rather than
a shared prediction, creating substantial cross-view redundancy. Errors in the depth and camera
estimates can further misalign these predictions and produce visible artifacts.
Recent methods based on fixed latent queries instead aggregate multi-view observations into shared
tokens before decoding freely placed Gaussians (An et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026; Itkin et al., 2026).
This aggregation can reduce redundancy and cross-view misalignment, but the learned queries have
no explicit link to the observations they represent. Unlike pixel-aligned Gaussians, whose source
view is known by construction, these tokens must learn their observation support implicitly, which
can produce blurry reconstructions. Their reconstruction budget is also fixed by design: a small object
capture and a large scene receive the same number of tokens and Gaussians.
These limitations motivate a representation that grounds each prediction in its source observation
without fixing the reconstruction budget in the architecture. We introduce ZipSplat, which predicts a
compact, observation-grounded scene representation from unposed images. Its reconstruction budget
is adjustable at inference without retraining, letting it match the scene rather than the architecture.
Rather than using learned queries, we derive each scene token directly from its source image patch,
making the token-to-observation link explicit by construction. Each token is decoded into freely
placed 3D Gaussians, but without geometric constraints the decoded Gaussians can drift from the
patch that produced them. A new cone loss addresses this by constraining each token’s Gaussians to
reproject near their source patch, grounding them in their observation.
As views overlap, many patches observe the same surface and their tokens can be merged into a
more compact set. However, merging in feature space does not guarantee that the resulting groups
correspond to the same surface. A new geometric contrastive loss, GeoCE, aligns token similarity
with 3D proximity so that clusters form by surface rather than by viewpoint.
The representation is now compact and grounded, but it remains the output of a single forward pass,
with residual camera and geometry error. The grounding that ties each token’s Gaussians to its source
patch also gives every token a persistent 2D–3D correspondence that can be used for geometric
refinement. We exploit this with TokenBA, a token-level bundle adjustment that refines cameras and
scene tokens through the frozen decoder, improving reconstruction in seconds.
ZipSplat establishes a new state of the art on standard benchmarks using only unposed, uncalibrated
images at inference. It consistently outperforms both pixel-aligned and token-based feed-forward
methods while using substantially fewer Gaussians. It generalizes zero-shot to diverse indoor and
outdoor scenes, and continues to improve as additional views are provided. Ablations validate the
representation and its components, and further experiments characterize the adjustable budget and
cross-dataset generalization.
In summary, we make the following contributions:
We introduce ZipSplat, a feed-forward model that reconstructs scenes from unposed images as
compact, observation-grounded scene tokens, each derived from a source patch and decoded into
freely placed Gaussians. The reconstruction budget is adjustable at inference without retraining.
• We introduce two geometric losses that ground and shape this representation geometrically: a cone
loss that ties each token’s decoded Gaussians to its source patch, and GeoCE, which aligns token
similarity with 3D proximity for cross-view clustering.
• We introduce TokenBA, an efficient token-level bundle adjustment that exploits the resulting
2D–3D correspondences to jointly refine scene tokens and camera poses through reprojection and
photometric consistency within a few seconds.

Pose-free variants progressively remove camera assumptions \(Ye et al., 2025a; Smart et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2025; Huang & Mikolajczyk, 2025; Kang et al., 2025\), with recent methods jointly estimating cameras and Gaussians from fully uncalibrated inputs \(Jiang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026\). Token-based Gaussian prediction. Token-based methods remove pixel alignment by decoding freely placed Gaussians from latent tokens, similar to Scaffold-GS \(Lu et al., 2024\), which decodes them from per-scene anchors. TokenGS \(Ren et al., 2026\), C3G \(An et al., 2026\), and GlobalSplat \(Itkin et al., 2026\) aggregate multi-view features with learned queries, while TokenSplat \(Li et al., 2026\) voxelizes and merges image tokens before decoding. These approaches fuse repeated observations and decouple the Gaussian count from input size, but their query count or voxelization is fixed, so changing the budget requires retraining. Learned queries also lack explicit supporting observations. In contrast, ZipSplat clusters image tokens in a learned geometric space, preserving their observation support while allowing the budget to be adjusted at inference through the clustering ratio.

Post-hoc Refinements. Learned reconstruction systems commonly end with a refinement step: DROID-SLAM \(Teed & Deng, 2021\) embeds a dense bundle adjustment layer in its update, and VGGT \(Wang et al., 2025a\) refines its predicted cameras by bundle adjustment over predicted tracks. Feed-forward 3DGS methods so far ignore or lack such correspondences, so refinement falls back to photometric optimization with ground-truth poses. C3G \(An et al., 2026\) optimizes the decoded Gaussians as in per-scene 3DGS, while TokenGS \(Ren et al., 2026\) tunes its learnable queries through the frozen network, which preserves geometry but trails Gaussian-space tuning in quality. TokenBA instead uses the built-in 2D–3D correspondences of scene tokens to refine the scene representation and camera poses, achieving faster convergence and higher quality without pose supervision.

## 3 METHOD

Given N unposed and uncalibrated images I = {In ∈ R H×W×3} N n=1, ZipSplat predicts a set of 3D Gaussians \(Kerbl et al., 2023\) and estimates the camera parameters. Each Gaussian is parameterized by a center µ ∈ R 3 , scales s ∈ R 3 , rotation quaternion q ∈ R 4 , opacity α ∈ [0, 1], and color coefficients c ∈ R C . Novel views are rendered via differentiable rasterization \(Ye et al., 2025b\).

Overview. Figure 2 summarizes the pipeline. A multi-view backbone produces geometry tokens from per-patch features. These are clustered into scene tokens, refined through cross- and self-attention, and decoded into freely placed 3D Gaussians, while camera parameters are estimated alongside. We derive each scene token from its source patches, and a cone loss grounds its Gaussians near them, which gives persistent 2D–3D correspondences (Section 3.1\). Clustering removes cross-view redundancy at an adjustable budget, guided by GeoCE (Section 3.2\), and TokenBA refines cameras and tokens via the patch–Gaussian associations (Section 3.3\).

(c) TokenBA refines cameras via robust PnP on the patch–Gaussian correspondences and updates scene tokens through the frozen decoder using photometric error on the input views.

### 3.1 OBSERVATION-GROUNDED SCENE TOKENS

Each image patch observes a specific surface region. To give each observation a dedicated token, a multi-view backbone extracts per-patch visual tokens at multiple layers. These are merged into geometry tokens g, one per image patch, forming a shared space for clustering, attention, and geometric supervision. Each geometry token serves as a scene token.

Gaussian decoding. Each scene token represents a surface region whose spatial extent and complexity vary across the scene. An MLP decoder D maps each scene token zk into G freely placed Gaussians:

\mathcal {G}\_k = \left \{ \left ( \gmean \_{kj}, \gscale \_{kj}, \grot \gopac \_{kj}, \gcolor \_{kj} \right ) \right \}\_{j=1}^{\ngs } = D(\stok \_k), \label {eq:decode} (1)

with the full scene assembled from all K tokens. Free placement lets the token spread its Gaussians over the observed region rather than anchoring each one to a fixed ray. However, nothing constrains decoded Gaussians to stay near their source patch: the decoder can place them anywhere in 3D, and the association between token and observation is not guaranteed.

Cone loss. The cone loss grounds each token's Gaussians in its source patch (Fig. 3a\). For each token k, it penalizes the reprojection distance between its Gaussian centers and the source patch center:

\loss \_{\mathrm {cone}} = \sum \_{k=1}^{K} \sum \_{j=1}^{\ngs } \rho \!\left ( \left \| \pi \_k(\gmean \_{kj})-\\*{u}\_k \right \| ), \label {eq:cone} (2)

where πk projects into the source view of token k, uk is its patch center, distances are measured in patch units, and ρ is a robust penalty that limits the influence of large residuals. This constraint is only definable because each token has a known source patch. A frustum loss, as used by TokenGS \(Ren et al., 2026\), keeps Gaussians visible but cannot tie them to a specific observation. With the cone loss, our approach yields an observation-grounded scene representation: each scene token is derived from a source patch, its Gaussians are tied back to that patch, and these associations persist at inference.

### 3.2 MERGING REDUNDANT SCENE TOKENS

When multiple views cover a scene, many patches observe the same surface from different viewpoints. Their tokens carry redundant information and can be merged into a smaller set whose size is adjustable at inference without retraining.

Inference-time clustering. We cluster the geometry tokens into K groups using k-means in cosine space. Given T patches per view, the budget K is set through the compression ratio

\qr = \frac {K}{\nviews T}, \qquad \qr \in (0,1]. \label {eq:compression\_ratio} (3)

Cluster initialization favors photometrically complex regions to preserve fine detail. Each scene token is initialized as the mean of its cluster members, z 0 k = |Ck| −1 P i∈Ck gi , and retains them as supporting observations. Assignments remain discrete, while gradients propagate through the cluster means during training. A merged scene token inherits the patches of all its members, and the cone loss takes the minimum over them, mini∈Ck ∥πi(µkj ) − ui∥, so each Gaussian must fall into the

Token fusion. To recover detail lost during cluster averaging, we refine the scene tokens through alternating cross- and self-attention:

\stok ^{l} = \operatorname {SA}\!\left ( \operatorname {CA} (\stok ^{l-1},\,\gtok ,\,\vtok \_l) \right ), \qquad l=1,\ldots ,L, \label {eq:fusion} (4)

where the geometry tokens g serve as keys, biasing retrieval toward relevant observations across views, and the backbone features fl provide the values, processed from deep to shallow layers. A final residual cross-attention incorporates color features:

\stok = \stok ^{L} + \operatorname {CA} (\stok ^{L},\,\gtok ,\,\vtok ^{\mathrm {col}}). \label {eq:color\_ca} (5)

GeoCE loss. Clustering can merge tokens across views, but nothing guarantees it groups patches that observe the same surface. GeoCE makes this explicit by shaping the token space (Fig. 3b\). During training, each token's patch is unprojected to a 3D anchor pi using its median depth. From these anchors we construct a soft target distribution over token pairs, weighted by 3D proximity:

w\_{ij} \propto \exp \!\left ( -\frac {\|\spoint \_i-\spoint \_j\|^2} {2\sigma \_i\sigma \_j} \right ), \qquad j\neq i, \label {eq:geoce\_target} (6)

where σi scales with the local patch spacing (Section A.2\). Nearby patches get high weight; distant ones get near zero. GeoCE pushes the token cosine similarities to match this target via cross-entropy:

\loss \_{\mathrm {geo}} = -\sum \_i\sum \_{j\neq i} w\_{ij} \log \frac {\exp (\gtok \_i^\top \gtok \_j/\tau )} {\sum \_{k\neq i}\exp (\gtok \_i^\top \gtok \_k/\tau )}, \label {eq:geoce} (7)

with temperature τ . Since the target is soft, multiple patches can share high weight when they observe the same surface, making cross-view merging explicit rather than leaving it to the rendering loss.

Training. The model is trained end-to-end with rendering, geometric, and camera supervision:

\loss = \_{\mathrm {render}} + \lambda \_{\mathrm {geo}}\,\loss \_{\mathrm {geo}} + \lambda \_{\mathrm {cone}}\,\loss \_{\mathrm {cone}} + \lambda \_{\mathrm {pose}}\,\loss \_{\mathrm {pose}}. \label {eq:loss} (8)

The rendering loss combines ℓ1 and perceptual terms on novel target views, while Lpose supervises the predicted cameras. Coefficients and details are in Section A.4.

## 3.3 TOKENBA: REFINING CAMERAS AND SCENE TOKENS

The feed-forward prediction carries residual camera and geometry error. The patch–Gaussian associations from Section 3.1 persist at inference and provide 2D–3D correspondences for refining both cameras and scene tokens from the input views within seconds.

Camera update. With the geometry fixed, we update each camera pose Pn using the Gaussian–patch correspondences. For each Gaussian j of token k with a supporting patch i ∈ Ck visible in view n, we minimize the robust reprojection error:

E\_{\mathrm {cam}}(\pose \_n) = \sum \_{(k,j,i)} \rho \!\left ( \left \| \pi (\pose \_n,\gmean \_{kj})-\\*{u}\_i \right \| \right ), \label {eq:pnp} (9)

where ρ is the penalty from Eq. \(2\). Each pose is estimated independently by robust PnP, minimizing Eq. \(9\) over all its correspondences with graduated non-convexity, followed by a short photometric polish to reduce patch-center quantization.





Figure 5: Qualitative comparison on DL3DV from 16 to 128 input views. ZipSplat maintains sharp, detailed renders as input coverage increases, outperforming both the token-based TokenGS and the pixel-aligned YoNoSplat and DA3. Scene token update. With the cameras fixed, we refine the scene tokens by minimizing the rendering loss on the input views. Gradients propagate through the renderer and frozen decoder to the tokens, combining evidence from all views. Optimizing tokens instead of raw Gaussians retains the decoder as a learned scene prior.

Alternating optimization. Camera correction improves the consistency of rendering gradients, while token refinement improves the geometry for subsequent camera updates. TokenBA alternates these steps and concludes with a short joint photometric refinement of tokens and poses, improving the feed-forward reconstruction within a few seconds (Fig. 3c\).

## 4 EXPERIMENTS

Implementation Details. We initialize the backbone and camera head from DA3-Giant \(Lin et al., 2026\). Features from three backbone layers are merged into geometry tokens and fused into scene tokens through three cross- and self-attention blocks. Each scene token produces G=32 Gaussians with degree-1 spherical harmonics. We train on an equal mixture of RealEstate10K \(Zhou et al., 2018\) and DL3DV \(Ling et al., 2024\) at 252×252 resolution for 450K steps on 16 GH200 GPUs. The input-view count increases from 2 to 24 over the first half of training. Rendering supervision combines ℓ1 and LPIPS \(Zhang et al., 2018\) (weight 0.1) on four target views, with auxiliary weights λgeo=0.05, λcone=0.01, and λpose=0.05. We sample the compression ratio uniformly from [0.2/N, 1], giving a minimum budget of approximately 2K Gaussians. TokenBA uses up to four alternating rounds followed by at most 96 joint refinement steps with an ℓ1 objective, adding approximately 1.6 s per scene at 16 views on an RTX 4090. Further details are provided in Section A.

Results. Table 1 shows that ZipSplat achieves the highest quality in all three settings, including against methods given ground-truth cameras. On DL3DV, the advantage grows with more views: from 4.45 dB over DA3, which shares our backbone, at 16 views to 8.40 dB at 64 views, with about 24× fewer Gaussians. Pixel-aligned prediction ties each Gaussian to a single view's depth and pose errors; as views increase, these misalignments accumulate rather than cancel, whereas our free 3D placement avoids this dependence (Fig. 5\). Against TokenGS with TTO, the strongest token-based baseline, the gap widens from 3.38 to 6.33 dB across the same range. Despite its strong PSNR, TokenGS blurs fine details and misses scene content (Figs. 5 and 8\), because learned queries must infer their observation support implicitly. Our scene tokens start from their supporting patches, preserving content and producing sharper reconstructions. On RealEstate10K, ZipSplat exceeds TokenGS by 0.91 dB with 4× fewer Gaussians; posed methods lead only in SSIM, likely benefiting from ground-truth intrinsics. Even without TokenBA, ZipSplat outperforms all evaluated feed-forward baselines in PSNR and LPIPS on every benchmark (Section F.1\).

### 4.5 ABLATIONS

Table 4 ablates design choices on DL3DV at 6 views; every row is a DA3-Base retrain with the same training budget. Per-pixel prediction gives 22.62 dB from 381K Gaussians with no budget control. Fixed learned queries (matching TokenGS \(Ren et al., 2026\): 4K queries × 64 Gaussians) reach 23.02 dB at 262K Gaussians with their frustum loss, which constrains Gaussians to lie within the input views, but the budget is locked at training time. Observation-derived tokens make the budget adjustable at inference: 62K Gaussians reach 22.87 dB, and halving to 31K costs only 0.34 dB. Yet the frustum loss does not tie tokens to their source observations. The cone loss grounds them and adds the largest training-time gain (+1.51 dB). It also provides the 2D–3D correspondences TokenBA relies on: without it, TokenBA collapses to 14.7 dB. GeoCE further improves clustering by making tokens that observe the same surface merge, adding +0.16 dB at r=0.5 and lifting AUC@3 from 0.669 to 0.686. With grounding established, TokenBA refines cameras and tokens through the learned correspondences, delivering the large gains in both reconstruction and pose accuracy.

## 5 CONCLUSION

We present ZipSplat, a feed-forward model that reconstructs scenes from unposed images as compact, observation-grounded scene tokens. Each token is derived from an image patch and decoded into freely placed 3D Gaussians. Tokens across views are merged, removing redundancy and making the budget adjustable. The cone loss ties each token's Gaussians to its source patch, which is what makes the grounding pay off. GeoCE shapes the token space so that merged tokens observe the same surface. The resulting correspondences enable TokenBA, which refines cameras and tokens within seconds. Without poses or intrinsics, ZipSplat outperforms baselines with substantially fewer Gaussians, including those given ground-truth cameras, and generalizes zero-shot to unseen datasets.

Limitations: The camera estimates can degrade on scenes that differ strongly from the training distribution. Ground-truth cameras close this gap, and TokenBA recovers most of it, but a residual remains on out-of-distribution captures. The method also assumes static scenes.

## A ADDITIONAL IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

### A.1 ARCHITECTURE

Backbone features. The backbone is a pretrained DA3-Giant \(Lin et al., 2026\) (40 layers, width 1536, 24 heads of dimension 64). We extract features from layers 19, 29, and 39, each comprising a local component from within-view attention and a global component incorporating cross-view information.

Geometry tokens. Clustering requires a space where proximity reflects shared 3D structure. The backbone produces per-patch features at multiple layers, each with a local component f loc l from within-view attention and a global component f glob l from cross-view attention. These are fused per layer as

\vtok \_l = \vtok \_l^\text {glob} + \alpha \_l \cdot \operatorname {LN}(\vtok \_l^\text {loc}), \label {eq:payload} (10)

where αl is a learnable gate initialized to 0.1. A separate two-layer MLP embeds each raw 14×14 RGB patch into a 1536-dimensional color token f col. All streams merge into a single geometry token per patch,

\gtok = \sum \_{l} \beta \_l \cdot \operatorname {MLP}\_l\!\bigl (\operatorname {LN}(\vtok \_l)\bigr ) + \beta \_c \cdot \operatorname {MLP}\_c\!\bigl (\operatorname {LN}(\vtok ^\text {col})\bigr ), \label {eq:merge} (11)

with learnable scales βl , βc initialized to 1/4 and two-layer GELU MLPs. This shared space defines the clustering metric, the cross-attention keys, and the scene token initialization. GeoCE supervises these tokens directly; the per-layer payloads and color embedding supply the attention values.

Fusion and decoding. Fusion uses three blocks. Each block is a cross-attention (queries: scene tokens; keys: geometry tokens; values: the visual tokens of one layer, deepest first) followed by self-attention among the scene tokens. Blocks use 24 heads, MLP ratio 4, GELU, and residual gates initialized to 0.01. A final residual cross-attention with the color embedding as values adds appearance. A two-layer MLP decodes each scene token into G=32 Gaussians of 23 parameters.

Reference frame. All geometry is expressed in the coordinate frame of the first input camera. The scene scale is normalized so that the median distance of the input depth points to the origin is one.

Camera estimation and priors. A two-layer ReLU MLP maps the deepest backbone camera token to a translation, unit quaternion, and field of view, initialized from the DA3 camera head. Lpose applies a Huber loss on translation, an ℓ1 chordal loss on rotations, and an ℓ1 loss on the field of view. When ground-truth cameras are available, DA3's frozen camera encoder injects them into the camera token; this path is used with probability 0.2 during training.

Gaussian parameterization. Positions use an inverse-log activation sign(y)(exp |y| − 1) on raw outputs clamped to [−5, 5], scales use softplus(x−4) clamped to [10−6 , 15], rotations are normalized quaternions, opacities use a sigmoid with bias −1.5, and colors are degree-1 spherical harmonics (12 coefficients). All 32 Gaussians of a token start from a shared template with a forward bias of z=0.5. Squared-hinge penalties keep scales in [10−6 , 0.1], opacities above 0.01, and positions within a radius of 10. A.5

T OKEN BA

TokenBA optimizes the scene tokens Z and the input camera poses under an ℓ1 rendering loss on
the input views, with the decoder frozen. Tokens use Adam with a learning rate of 10−3 during the
alternation and 2×10−3 in the joint tail. The decoder MLP body runs in half precision while its
readout stays in full precision.
Camera update. The correspondences in Eq. (9) follow from the cluster assignments: every Gaussian
of a scene token is paired with the patch center of each member patch. Each pose is solved by
robust PnP with graduated non-convexity, annealing the robust scale from 64 to 12 to 4 pixels over
4 iterations per level on 2048 sampled correspondences. Seven rotation hypotheses per view (the
current rotation and ±40◦ about each camera axis) are solved in one batched call, and the hypothesis
with the most inliers within 2 patches wins. A photometric polish then removes the patch-center
quantization error, running two steps at half resolution and one at full resolution with the aligner
described below.
Photometric aligner. The aligner is a direct Levenberg–Marquardt solver on the photometric residual.

linearization with the analytic image-warp Jacobian, using one render per step and no autograd. The

6 steps per level, Gaussian blur at the two coarse levels, and accept-reject steps with a trust-region
clamp on the pose twist. A scan over nine log-spaced global scale factors from 0.5 to 2.0 precedes
the pyramid, and the best full-resolution pose across levels is kept. Inside TokenBA only the two fine

B

E VALUATION P ROTOCOL

We describe the evaluation setup shared by all experiments: view selection, pose metrics, baselines,
and the resolution and resize choices that affect reported numbers.
B.1

V IEW SELECTION

On DL3DV (140 scenes), every 8th frame is held out as a target, and context views are selected from
the remaining frames by k-means clustering on the 6-D feature vector of camera position and viewing
direction (Ziwen et al., 2025). On Mip-NeRF360 (6 scenes) and ScanNet++ (46 iPhone captures),
targets are selected by farthest point sampling over the camera centers, and context views over the
remaining frames. We evaluate at 16, 32, 64, and 128 input views on DL3DV and at 32, 64, and 128
on Mip-NeRF360 and ScanNet++, with targets fixed across view counts. On RealEstate10K (1,600
scenes) we use the 6-view protocol of YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026) with 8 held-out targets.
B.2

P OSE ACCURACY

We report relative rotation error and translation-direction error over all input-view pairs, pooled over
all scenes before computing the AUC. Pooling produces a single error distribution that weights each
pair equally, which differs from a mean of per-scene AUCs. The main text reports AUC at 1, 3, and 5
degrees, and Section F.5 extends this to 10, 15, 20, and 30 degrees.
B.3

BASELINES

We reimplement each baseline into our codebase and validate it against the original code up to
floating-point precision. All methods are then evaluated under the same protocol: the same view
indices, the same metric resolution, and the same resize filter. Since a predicted scene need not
align perfectly with the ground-truth cameras, we align the target cameras to the predicted scene
before computing metrics for every method, following prior work (Ye et al., 2025a; 2026). Posefree methods (AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), EcoSplat (Park et al., 2026), DA3 (Lin et al., 2026),
C3G (An et al., 2026), YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026)) predict cameras from the input images. Posed
methods (DepthSplat (Xu et al., 2025), ZPressor (Wang et al., 2025b), VolSplat (Wang et al., 2026),
GlobalSplat (Itkin et al., 2026), TokenGS (Ren et al., 2026)) receive ground-truth poses and intrinsics.
Per-scene 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) optimizes 100K Gaussians, initialized randomly around the
camera centroids, for 30K iterations on the context views with ground-truth cameras, providing
an optimization-based reference. Each method’s encoder runs at its own training resolution, and
all renders are scored at a common 252×252 (Section B.4). SparseSplat (Zhang et al., 2026) and
TokenSplat (Li et al., 2026) had no public code at the time of writing.
B.4

R ENDERING RESOLUTION

Methods train at different resolutions and aspect ratios, and scoring at a lower resolution raises PSNR,
since downsampling removes the fine detail that is hardest to reconstruct. To compare fairly, we score
every method at a common 252×252 while still running each encoder at its own training resolution.
Table 6 compares this protocol with scoring each baseline at its own resolution and with running
it entirely at 252. Scoring each baseline at its own resolution would favor the low-resolution ones
and penalize the high-resolution ones: YoNoSplat gains 0.15 dB at its native 224, while AnySplat
loses 0.31 dB at its native 448. Running a baseline entirely at 252 is worse still, costing AnySplat
4.2 to 5.0 dB and YoNoSplat 2.1 to 2.8 dB. Our protocol therefore keeps each encoder at its training
resolution, and the high-resolution baselines keep the detail they encode.

### B.5 RESIZE FILTER

Switching the ground-truth downsampling filter from PIL Lanczos to bilinear with antialiasing changes PSNR by up to 0.98 dB (Table 7\). Results are therefore not comparable across papers unless the filter is reported. We use bilinear with antialiasing for every method, matching how the DL3DV source ground truth is downscaled.

## C TOKENBA ANALYSIS

This section compares TokenBA with the test-time refinement of the baselines, breaks down its runtime, ablates its components, and shows qualitative results with and without it.

### C.1 COMPARISON WITH BASELINE REFINEMENT

Table 8 compares TokenBA with the test-time refinement available to the baselines, ranking feedforward and refined predictions separately. TokenGS optimizes its scene latents at test time, which lifts it by 1.3 to 2.1 dB PSNR from 16 to 64 views. TokenBA lifts ZipSplat by 2.2 to 4.7 dB over the same range, so the gap to TokenGS widens from 2.5 to 3.7 dB before refinement to 3.4 to 6.3 dB after it, although TokenGS receives ground-truth cameras. TokenBA is also 3.2 to 3.5 times faster, taking 1.8 to 12.8 s per scene including the feed-forward pass against 6.3 to 41.4 s for TokenGS with its optimization. Refining the Gaussians of ZipSplat w/o TokenBA directly for 60 s reaches 23.7 to 27.1 dB, 1.0 to 1.9 dB below TokenBA, which needs only 1.6 to 11.7 s of refinement. TokenBA also refines the cameras, raising AUC at 3 ◦ from 0.92 to between 0.95 and 0.98, well above the 0.74 to 0.76 of YoNoSplat before refinement. Gaussian-space refinement also helps the baselines, lifting YoNoSplat by 2.5 dB and TokenGS by 1.8 dB at 16 views, more than TokenGS gains from its own latent optimization, yet both stay 1.9 and 2.8 dB below TokenBA.

### C.2 TIMING BREAKDOWN

The cost of TokenBA is set by how much it renders. Figure 10 times the grid of view counts and Gaussian budgets with and without TokenBA, and the difference between the two panels is the refinement itself. Times follow the protocol of Section E, and the TokenBA panel averages over all 140 test scenes since its early stops make the cost scene-dependent. This difference grows nearly linearly with both the number of views and the number of Gaussians, since every step renders all input views. At 128 views, each doubling of the budget from 65K upward multiplies the cost of TokenBA by 1.6 to 2.0, and at 262K Gaussians each doubling of the views multiplies it by 1.7. Without TokenBA, the three largest budgets at 128 views are up to 1.7 s slower than full capacity. Full capacity keeps every token and skips clustering. A compressed budget first runs k-means on all 41K tokens of the 128 views. With many clusters, each k-means iteration compares every token with every cluster center, and k-means needs 17 to 45 iterations to converge. This costs 1.9 s at 524K Gaussians. ## C.4 TOKENBA BEYOND THE TRAINING RESOLUTION

The feed-forward encoder runs at its training resolution of 252×252, but the TokenBA rendering loss is resolution-agnostic. Refitting the tokens against higher-resolution input views recovers detail that the low-resolution pass cannot resolve. Figure 11 shows this on DL3DV at 64 views: at 252×252, TokenBA sharpens the prediction but leaves fine features such as the statue's face blurred, whereas refitting at 448×448 or 540×540 resolves them. The additional cost is modest – 4.6 times the pixels add only 1.6 times the refinement time (9.7 s to 15.6 s at 64 views). All quantitative results in this paper use TokenBA at 252×252.

## D CLUSTERING ANALYSIS

This section looks at the tokens that ZipSplat clusters and at the clusters it builds from them. We use the two DA3-Base models of Table 4, trained with and without GeoCE, on DL3DV, and render without TokenBA.

### D.1 GEOCE SHAPES THE TOKEN SPACE

GeoCE trains the geometry tokens so that patches showing the same 3D point have similar tokens, even when they come from different views. Figure 13 shows the effect. Without GeoCE, the tokens mostly encode depth and change smoothly across the image, and the backbone tokens behave the same way. With GeoCE, the tokens also tell apart different objects and surfaces at the same depth. To measure this, we take each patch and find the most similar token in the other views. With GeoCE, this token shows the same 3D point in 88% of cases, compared with 79% without GeoCE and 59% for the backbone tokens. Even without GeoCE, the geometry tokens find the same 3D point far more often than the backbone tokens, and their clusters still span several views. The rendering loss alone already shapes this space, since each scene token is the differentiable mean of the geometry tokens it merges (Section A.3\), and the same tokens also serve as the attention keys. ## D.2 CLUSTERING METHOD

ZipSplat compresses by running k-means on the geometry tokens and merging each cluster into one scene token. We test how much each part of this choice matters, namely the initialization, the space we cluster in, and the clustering itself. Table 11 keeps the network fixed and only changes how its tokens are grouped, and we compare each row with k-means on the geometry tokens under the same strided initialization. Complexity-weighted initialization adds 0.3 dB at r=0.25 and 0.1 dB at r=0.1. The space matters most. At r=0.1, clustering the backbone tokens costs 0.7 dB and the patch colors 3.7 dB, a per-view grid on image positions costs 1.0 dB, and even the ground-truth 3D points are 0.1 dB worse than the geometry tokens. The clustering algorithm matters little, since merging every token into its nearest seed without k-means iterations costs only 0.1 to 0.2 dB. Merging itself is essential, as keeping only the seed tokens loses 1.8 dB at r=0.25 and 5.7 dB at r=0.1.

## D.3 COMPRESSION RESULTS

Figure 14 shows the PSNR of ZipSplat for every combination of input views and Gaussian budget. For the same number of Gaussians, more input views give better renders. At 131K Gaussians, going from 16 to 128 views adds 0.9 dB without TokenBA and 4.0 dB with it. TokenBA helps more as the number of views and Gaussians grows, from 1.0 dB at 16 views and 1K Gaussians to 5.7 dB at 128 views and full capacity. Without TokenBA, only budgets of 2K Gaussians or fewer do not improve with more views. Figure 15 shows two views at full capacity and at 4× and 20× compression. What a scene loses depends on its content: large flat regions survive 20× compression almost unchanged, whereas grass and thin structures need over 8K Gaussians, and TokenBA cannot fully recover them.

## E INFERENCE TIME AND RENDERING SPEED

Setup. All measurements run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB) at 252×252 resolution with batch size 1, with the backbone and fusion in bf16. Each configuration averages 15 timed forward passes after 5 warm-up passes on a single DL3DV scene. The forward pass covers the prediction of the Gaussians and excludes data loading, TokenBA and the evaluation renders. We time full capacity (r=1.0), which keeps every token and skips clustering, and a fixed ratio of r=0.5.

Inference cost. Forward time grows with the number of input views (Fig. 16a), from 0.07 s at 6 views to 5.4 s at 192 views at full capacity. Full capacity is as fast as the compressed ratios because it skips clustering, and the rest of the network is shallow. The 40-layer backbone takes about three quarters of the time, and the three fusion blocks, the color attention and the Gaussian head add the remaining quarter even though every token is a query. Compression can only shrink this last quarter, and it first pays for k-means. At r=0.5 this costs little up to 32 views, but the k-means iterations grow with the number of tokens times the number of clusters. Output representation. The Gaussian count sets the cost of the final representation (Fig. 17\). At full capacity it grows linearly with the number of input views, from 62K Gaussians at 6 views to 2.0M at 192 views (183 MB, 40 FPS at 252×252). Halving the ratio halves the storage and nearly doubles the rendering speed, to 92 MB and 74 FPS at 192 views. At 24 views, full capacity renders 249K Gaussians at 228 FPS and r=0.5 renders 124K at 354 FPS, well within the limits of real-time viewers. YoNoSplat produces 9.6M Gaussians at 192 views (539 MB) and renders at 8.9 FPS, about 5× more primitives and 4.5× slower than ZipSplat at full capacity.

## F.2 REALESTATE10K

Without TokenBA, ZipSplat already exceeds TokenGS w/o TTO and YoNoSplat with 62K Gaussians, four and five times fewer. At r=0.1, 6K Gaussians reach the PSNR of the full feed-forward model. Ground-truth cameras add 1.63 dB here against 0.1 dB on DL3DV, in line with the lower pose accuracy at six views (Table 16\).

### F.3 MIP-NERF360

Without TokenBA, ZipSplat exceeds every feed-forward baseline at every view count, and its PSNR rises from 32 to 128 views while DA3, which shares our backbone, declines by 0.86 dB. At the fixed budget of 262K Gaussians, matching TokenGS, ZipSplat stays within 0.15 dB of full capacity at every view count, with five times fewer Gaussians at 128 views. With ground-truth cameras, ZipSplat gains 2.1 to 2.5 dB and exceeds the per-scene 3DGS fit at every view count, so out of domain the gap to oracle cameras is ten times larger than on DL3DV.

## F.4 SCANNET++

ZipSplat without cameras exceeds the per-scene 3DGS fit at every view count, by 2.25 to 3.26 dB. Without TokenBA it also exceeds AnySplat and DA3, although both train on ScanNet++. Ground-truth cameras add 2.99 and 2.42 dB at 32 and 64 views.
