ABSTRACT
In some instances, reconstruction tomography must be carried out from
projections that do not include projection values for all portions of th3
object to be reconstructed. This may occur, for example, when the field of
view of the detector is limited, or when an opaque foreign body is present
within the object. The effects of such incomplete projections upon reconstructions of computer-simulated phantoms were studied, using iterative and
convolution methods. Several methods for reducing the resulting artifacts
and inaccuracies were investigated.
With a limited field of view, when missing projection values were equated
to zero a ring of very high reconstruction values was produced, effectively
shrinking the region of useful reconstruction. This artifact was eliminated
with the convolution method by equating missing projection values to the
outermost given projection values. Better reconstructions were obtained with
a modified iterative method, in which missing projection values were ignored.
Such reconstructions were further improved by applying a priori knowledge of
the true outline of the object.
Opaque foreign bodies oast many linear shadows across the reconstruction.
These were reduced by filling in missing projection values by linear interpolation. Similar results were obtained with the modified iterative method.

this field. A similar situation occurs in radiology when an opaque object,
such as a metallic foreign body, is present in the plane being imaged and
blocks a portion of the x-ray beam.
In previous reports (1,2), reconstructions of real phantoms by the
iterative method were presented in which the projection data were obtained over
a limited field of view. In the present report, reconstructions are carried
out from projections of computer-generated phantoms that are incomplete due to
a limited field of view or the presence of an opaque object. Various forms
of the convolution and iterative methods are used. The effects of incomplete
projections on the reconstructions are evaluated, along with several methods
for ameliorating these effects.
Reconstruction tomography from incomplete projections is of theoretical
as well as practical interest, because it highlights the differences between
the Fourier and the algebraic approaches. In the Fourier approach, as implemented by the convolution method (3), it is shown that, given the set of all
line integrals g in the x-y plane, one obtains exact solutions for f(x,y)
E ± all points in the plane; and given a subset of g obtained by uniform
redial and angular sampling of g, one obtains solutions at discrete points
in the x-y plane that will be very nearly exact, provided that f(x,y) is
band-limited and the sampling of g is sufficiently fine. This method requires
that -the projections g o be complete, since some value must be assigned at each
of the sampling points of g. Given incomplete projections, it is necessary to
estimate each of the missing projection values.
In the algebraic approach (4,5), the non-zero portion of the x-y plane
is digitized into an n x n_ array of squares, and each:square is assigned the
average value of f(x,y) within that square. Each projection value g(r,9)
is represented as the weighted or unweighted sum of the values assigned to
those squares crossed by the line corresponding to g(r,9) [the line along
which f(x,y) is integrated to give g(r,e)L
It thus becomes possible to
express the reconstruction problem in terms of a system of simultaneous linear
equations with the projection values g(r,e) as the given values and the
f(x,y) as unknowns. These equations cannot be solved by matrix inversion
techniques because the matrices are extremely large, and because the equations
are underdetermined and often inconsistent (due to experimental errors).
Solutions can be obtained by iterative techniques (4,6) which are reasonably
representative of "the true values of f(x,y), particularly when f(x,y) is
sufficiently band-limited.
The important characteristic of the algebraic approach with respect to
the present discussion is that it can provide a solution based on the given
projection values, and does not require that any missing values be estimated.
It would thus appear that the algebraic approach, as implemented by the iterative method, is better suited than the convolution method to the problem of
reconstruction from incomplete projections. This study was undertaken to
determine whether this is in fact the case.
An empiric approach was employed in this study. Phantoms were generated
with the computer that are representative of the types of radioactivity distributions encountered in nuclear medicine. Accurate projections of these phantoms
were made from multiple angles (photon attenuation was not simulated). Incomplete projections were simulated by deletion of the appropriate projection
values. Reconstructions were carried out from the resulting projections using
various forms of the convolution and iterative methods. Results were evaluated
by a quantitative measure and by visual inspection of the reconstructions.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Most of the findings to be presented in this report are equally applicable
to the fields of radiology and nuclear medicine. The frame of reference for
this study, however, was that of reconstruction from incomplete projections as
applied to data acquired with scintillation cameras in current use in nuclear
medicine. The spatial resolution of these devices is excellent for very superficial structures, but deteriorates for deep structures, for which it may be
considered of "the order of 12 mm (full width at half maximum of the line spread
function). The effective field of view of currently available cameras is about
25 cm; therefore, a rather coarse array should be sufficient for reconstruction,
particularly since real projection data will be count-limited. In previous
studies Cl 5 2), it was found that adequate reconstructions could be obtained
with data from the scintillation camera stored in a 32 x 32 array; such an
array was used for the present investigation. Since spurious results nay be
obtained when projection data are based on phantoms generated by computer on a
coarse array, the phantoms for this study were generated on a 128 x 128 array.
Another limitation of a coarse array is that it cannot adequately represent
the high spatial frequency components of geometric structures. Therefore the
phantoms used in this study were "smoothed" by convolution with a Gaussian
spread function having a width equivalent to that of the. spread function of the
scintillation camera. The resulting phantoms have sufficiently band-limited
spatial frequency spectra so that they are represented rather well by the
32 x 32 array.

by means of element-by-element multiplication (Fig. 1 ) . When the mask was
generated, it was convolved with the same Gaussian spread function used for the
spheres, so that the high-frequency components introduced by a sharp edge would

Reconstructions were carried out using the 36 projections of each phantom

DISCUSSION
The problem of reconstruction from incomplete projections highlights the
basic difference between the convolution and the iterative methods for reconstruction. The convolution method requires that each projection be uniformly
sampled, and that a value be assigned to each of the sampling points1, hence
incomplete projections must be completed. The iterative method, on the other
hand, converts the reconstruction problem into one of solving a system of
samjltaneous linear equations by a relaxation technique, with missing projection
values treated like missing equations, i.e., they are ignored.
25

projections that are inconsistent with the object being reconstructed, as
illustrated in Fig. 14. Figure 14A is a disk of uniform density, represented
in volumetric form by a cylinder of unit height. The disk is imaged on edge
by a detector with a limited field of view. The dashed region is the reconstructable region, which i? a smaller disk. Figure 14B is the projection of
Fig. 14A, with the missing projection values denoted by dashed lines. If one
completes this projection by setting missing values equal to 0 (method C Q ) ,
one obtains the projection given in Fig. 14D. This projection is inconsistent
with the disk in Fig. IMA. If we let b represent the radius of the outer
disk in Fig. IMA, and a_ the radius of the inner disk, then it can be shown
that Fig. 1UD is the projection of the circularly symmetric density distribution

The iterative method, unlike the convolution method, does not require
completion of incomplete projections. With method C2, the incomplete projection
26
in Fig. 14B can be used as it stands, and as such it is consistent with the
disk in Fig. 1UA, i.e., the disk is a solution for the set of projections
represented by Fig. 1UB. Other objects, among them the object in Fig. 14C,
will also be solutions. The iterative method will find one of these solutions,
but generally not the correct one.
Herman et al. (5) have shown that a number of the ART algorithms,
including partially constrained ART2 (a method similar to ARTA), converge to
the solution of minimum variance, provided that a solution exists. The proofs
do not require some minimum set of projection values, and it would therefore
appear that they are directly applicable to reconstructions *rom incomplete projections. The minimum variance solution is usually preferable to most other
solutions, as it is "least likely to contain artifacts not forced on us by the
data" (5). Methods for completing incomplete projections, on the other hand,
are likely to introduce artifacts.
The iterative method has the additional advantage that it can utilize
a_ priori information about the margin of the object to yield reconstructions of
improved accuracy. When the field of view is limited, the portion of the margin
located outside the reconstructable region cannot be determined from the
projection data; it can be determined, however, with an ancillary device such
as the contour measuring apparatus of Lanzl et al. (11). On the basis of the
information thus obtained, a mask is constructed which is used to trim the
reconstruction array. Such trimming delimits the density distribution outside
the reconstructable region. The reconstruction values produced by the iterative
method are interdependent, since positive errors must be offset by negative
errors so that the projection values will be satisfied. For this reason, errors
outside the reconstructable region lead to errors within this region. Trimming
by methods T, or T~ (at the beginning or at each step of iterative reconstruction)
27
reduces the errors outside, and hence within, the reconstructable region.
Trimming by method T q is ineffective, since it takes place at the completion
of reconstruction, when values outside the reconstructable region can no longer
affect values within.
In the case of an opaque object within the field of view, the convolution
method with missing projection values filled in by linear interpolation
appears, in most instances, to be as good as or better than the iterative method
that ignores missing values. linear interpolation appears to be a reasonably
adequate method for supplying missing projection values; it fails, however; for
a large opaque object in the midst of the hot spot phantom, where the iterative
method appears preferable (Table VII) • The projection values of the hot spot
phantom vary more markedly than those of the cold spot phantom, and when a
significantly long string of these values is missing, as is the case with a
large opaque object, linear interpolation is inadequate. Interpolation with a
higher-order polynomial might give more satisfactory results (but may fail with
noisy data).
It should be noted that the reconstructions carried out by the iterative
method required only about 15-30% more CPU time "than the corresponding reconstructions by the convolution method. This is attributable to the efficiency of
the iterative method when variable ray width was used (1), and to the rapid
convergence, requiring only one cycle of iterations, when projections were used
in pseudo-randar rather than in serial order (9).
CONCLUSIONS
Incomplete projections are obtained when the field of view of the detector
is limited or when an opaque object lies within the field of view. It is possible to carry out reconstructions from such projections by estimating the missing
28
projection values. A preferable method in most instances, however, appears to
be the additive ART iterative method, modified to ignore the missing projection
values.
When the field of view is limited, one can use knowledge of the true
outline of the density or radioactivity distribution to obtain improved reconstructions, by Lfimming the margin of the reconstruction array at the beginning
of, or during, iterative reconstruction. The modified additive ART method,
utilizing such trimming, appears to be clearly superior to any other method for
reconstruction from projections which cover a limited field of view.
