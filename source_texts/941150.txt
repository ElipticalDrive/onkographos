Abstract
In this article, we propose a texture-mapping procedure to enhance visual appearances of volumetric models. To texture
a volumetric model, the proposed method performs an unsupervised training to transform a Self-Organizing Map (SOM)
into a fitting surface at first. Then, another unsupervised training is conducted to UV-parameterize the fitting surface. As
a result, a bridge connecting the volumetric model’s surface and the texture map has been established. In the following
steps, filtering computations are performed in the fitting surface and the texture map to compute texel values for the
model’s surface voxels and to reduce aliasing artifacts. Experiments confirm that the proposed procedure is able to efficiently and smoothly texture volumetric models within acceptable time. The test results also reveal that the quality of the
texturing process is influenced by the filtering algorithms and the topology and resolution of the embedded SOM. Further
trials indicate that texturing results can be improved by using image warping, affine transformations, anchor points, and
region-of-interest selection.
Keywords Texture mapping · Self-organizing map · Voxel-based modelling · Machine learning · Computer graphics
1 Introduction
Texture-mapping is widely used to glue patterns on the surfaces of 3D models such that the visual effects of the models are enhanced (Catmull 1974; Hecbert 2007). Besides,
texture-mapping is also employed to create fine geometrical
structures on the surfaces of 3D objects, for example bumps
(Blinn 1978). Producing global shading effects is another
notable function of texture-mapping, including shadow casting, glossy-surface reflection, and environmental mapping
(Fernando et al. 2001; Miller and Hoffman 1984; Greene
1986). Because of its importance in computer graphics,
numerous algorithms had been proposed to improve the
quality and efficiency of texture-mapping (Hecbert 2007).
In recent decades, advanced graphic processors and video
memory are more affordable. Assisted by these hardware
resources and shading languages, texture-mapping has
become more efficient and capable (Szirmay-Kalos et al.
2022).

In Computer-Aided Design, the de facto representation methods of 3D models are mainly polygon-based, for
examples, the STL and OBJ formats (McHenry and Bajcsy
2008; STL file format 2024). In the STL format, the shape
of a 3D model is specified by a list of triangles. Each triangle is defined by three vertices. The information of a
vertex includes its world and texture coordinates, normal
vector, and optical parameters. In an OBJ file, the model is
presented by a vertex list and a polygon mesh. The vertex
list records the information of the surface vertices while the
polygon mesh contains the connectivity of these vertices
and defines the model’s shape.
Because of the progressions in 3D scanning, medical
imaging, depth field image capturing, machine learning, and
digitalization of 3D models (voxelization), defining models
by using voxels is no longer uncommon (Xu et al. 2021;
Patil and Ravi 2005; Cohen-Or and Kaufman 1995). In this
type of definition, a 3D model is represented by a set of voxels arranged in a regular grid. These voxels record the intensities of the spatial cells occupied by the model. Therefore,
users are able to define not only the boundary but also the
interior intensities of the model. This functionality is hard
to achieve by using polygon-based representations. Hence,
voxel-based modelling possesses advantages over polygon-based modelling in some applications, for examples,

2 Related work and background knowledge
2.1 Texture mapping
Texture-mapping was proposed by Catmull in Catmull
(1974). In this process, UV-parameters (texture coordinates) are assigned to the vertices of the target polygonal
model during the modelling stage. The UV-parameters are
then used in the rendering phase to retrieve texel colors (values) of the surface fragments from the texture map (Catmull
1974; Hecbert 2007). This technique was revised by Blinn to
generate bumps on the surfaces of objects (Blinn 1978). In
Blinn’s work, the texture map contains discrete samples of a
height field. In the rendering stage, the height field samples
are utilized to alter the normal vectors such that wrinkles are
produced on the model’s surface. By storing depth information, brightness of fragments, and images of the surrounding
world in texture maps, we can produce shadows, shades, and
environmental reflections in computer-generated images by
using texture-mapping (Fernando et al. 2001; Miller and
Hoffman 1984; Greene 1986). Since texture-mapping is a
routine task in computer graphics, current Graphical Processor Units (GPUs) support this computation. Hence, we can
utilize shading languages to implement texture-mapping
procedures and accomplish global illumination effects in
real-time (Szirmay-Kalos et al. 2022).
In texture-mapping, the texture is recorded in a 2D image
while the destination surface mesh is formed by polygons in
a 3D space. If the model’s shape is irregular, it is difficult to
Page 3 of 18 97
Virtual Reality (2026) 30:97
smoothly map the polygons onto the texture space. Texturing the target model by brutal force results in distorted patterns. Many methods had been developed to overcome this
problem. In (Bennis et al. 1991), Bennis et al. proposed to
parameterize the surface mesh piece by piece and map the
parameterized surface mesh onto the texture map to reduce
distortion. Fully-automated UV-parameterization is hard to
achieve. In (Maillot et al. 1993), researchers proposed to
design texturing tools and allow users to adjust UV-parameters of vertices interactively via graphical user interfaces
(GUI). Another method was presented in Sun and Bao
(2000) to alleviate texture distortion. The researchers created B-spline patch surrounding the target model, and then
used the UV-parameters of the B-spline patch to parametrize the model.
Splitting the model surface into charts of simple shapes
and separately processing the charts henceforth can reduce
the topological mismatches between the texture map and the
model surface. Two typical researches based on this strategy
can be found in (Liu et al. 2006 ;González and Patow 2009).
Polygonal models are hard to UV-parameterize in nature.
On the other hand, parametric surfaces are associated with
parameterization in their definitions. Parametric surfaces
can be parameterized and flattened by using their inherent parameterization schemes. In the work of Sheffer et al.
(2007), Sheffer proposed to parametrize polygonal surfaces
at first and then texture- map them later. Since our target
models are voxel-based, the afore-mentioned techniques are
not suitable for UV-parameterize their surfaces, we propose
to utilize un-supervised trainings and fitting surfaces to texture voxel-based models.
Recently, innovative algorithms have been proposed to
reconstruct 3D models from multiple view images (Zeng et
al. 2024; Xiang et al. 2021; Maier et al. 2017; Lai et al.
2018). These methods encode not only the geometric information but also the appearance data of 3D models in neural
networks by using supervised machine learning. Thus, they
are able to texture the reconstructed objects in rendering
stage. Some of these methods represent 3D models by using
voxels. It seems their methods can be used to texture volumetric models. Nonetheless these algorithms cannot meet
the requirements of this research. First, instead of depthimages, the input of our texturing process is a voxel-based
model. Second, the texture of our texturing process is kept
in a single 2D image, not in multiple view images. There
is no need to re-generate it. Third, these algorithms utilize
supervised machine learning to carry out texture-mapping.
In our case, the texturing results are not available beforehand. It is impossible to conduct supervised learning for the
texturing process. Fourth, the supervised learning processes
needs numerous learning materials and computational powers. These resources may not be available to the designers
of 3D models. Thus, in this research, we develop a texturing
procedure which can be carried out in a desktop machine
within seconds.
2.2 Voxel-based modelling and texturing
Representing models by using voxels had been proposed for
decades (Requicha and Voelcker 1983; Gibson 1995). However, volumetric models need large memory spaces to store
their definitions. This data format was thus prohibitive for
defining models in the 1980s. As the price of computer memory is getting cheaper and memory capacities are increasing, it is affordable to specify 3D models by using voxels
nowadays. In the work of (Hiller and Lipson 2009; Ueng
et al. 2019; Hong et al. 2021), voxel-based representations
are utilized to specify the models for layered manufacturing.
The supporting software systems for directly manufacturing
voxel-based models have also been developed. In (Ueng et
al. 2018b), Ueng et al. presented a pre-viewing system for
layer manufacturing. In their method, voxel-based modelling is adopted to represent models fabricated by virtual 3D
printers such that the printed models and related parameters
can be predicted in advance.
Three-dimensional scanners and depth cameras are
widely used to capture RGBD images of indoor and outdoor scenes. Traditionally, the resultant depth fields were
kept in point-clouds (Xu et al. 2021; Turkan et al. 2012; Lai
et al. 2019). As point-clouds are unstructured, reconstructing the scenes requires extensive efforts. By using voxels
to record geometric and appearance data and employing
supervised machine-learning, researchers can efficiently
reconstruct and display the scanned scenes (Zeng et al.
2024; Xiang et al. 2021; Maier et al. 2017; Lai et al. 2018).
Hence, voxel-based representation becomes popular in
scene reconstruction.
In (Weng et al. 2002; Shen and Willis 2005), texturing
methods are proposed to color voxel-based models in medical images. In (Weng et al. 2002), the UV-parameterization
computation is achieved by projecting voxels onto the UVmap while in Shen and Willis (2005) the UV-parameterization is realized by using ray-tracing and a regular-shape
intermediate surface. These two researches modify conventional texturing techniques to assign colors to volumetric
models hidden in medical images. In this work, we employ
unsupervised machine learning and filtering methods to UVparameterize and to color volumetric models with no prior
knowledge about their origins and intrinsic properties.
2.3 Self-organizing map (SOM)
SOM is a type of neural network (Vesanto 1999). An SOM
is composed of nodes (neurons) located in a regular lattice.
13
97 Page 4 of 18
The locations, neighborhood information, and connectivity between the nodes are implicitly encoded in the lattice
structure. Thus, it is easy to access the nodes of a SOM. In
order to learn preferences of features, each SOM node contains a weight, which is the target of unsupervised training
processes. After undertaking trainings, the weight will be
gradually modified to resemble a certain data value. As a
result, SOMs are popular tools for data classification, cluster detection, and outlier identification.
Since the lattice of an SOM is a regular grid, it can
be used to form a fitting surface for a 3D object. In this
application, the weights of the SOM nodes are trained to
approximate the coordiantes of the surfcae vertices of the
target object. Once the training process completes, by treating the weights as vertex coordinates and the SOM lattice
as the underlying mesh, the SOM becomes an approximation of the target object (Barhak and Fischer 2001). In this
research, we revise this strategy and build a fitting surface
which bridges the 2D texture map and the 3D volumetric
model surface.

3.1 Surface voxel extraction
We assume that the volumetric model is contained in a 3D
grid, and the void voxels are associated with zero intensities
while the model voxels are given non-zero intensities. Since
the proposed texture-mapping procedure aims to glue patterns on the model’s surface, we have to extract the surface
voxels and exclude interior and dangling voxels at the preprocessing stage.
First, we examine all the voxels and collect those voxels
satisfying the following two conditions: (1) They are model
voxels. (2) They are adjacent to void voxels by sharing at
least one face and at most five faces. Then, these qualified
voxels are kept in a list, called Vsurf in this article. The voxels of Vsurf constitute the surface of the volumetric model.
They will be textured in the incoming computations.
3.2 Fitting surface generation using SOM
The texels of the texture regularly reside in a 2D grid while
the surface voxels of the model are scattered in a 3D grid.
There exist structural and topological disparities between
the 2D texture space and the model surface space. These
disparities prohibit smooth texture mapping. To overcome
this problem, a fitting surface is generated to alleviate these
disparities.
The fitting surface possesses two functionalities: First,
it is a geometrical approximation of the model’s boundary.
Secondly, it encodes a UV-parameterization scheme for the
incoming texture-mapping. These two functionalities are
realized in two unsupervised training processes. The first

In the proposed texturing procedure, the fitting surface
is constructed by using an SOM. The type and resolution of
the SOM are decided by the users. Currently, four types of
SOMs are supported in our texturing procedure, including
the plane, cylindrical, torus, and cubic SOMs. Each node
of an SOM contains two weights, w1 and w2. The former
has three components while the later has two components.
The first weight w1 is engaged in the first training. After the

The second weight w2 is modified in the second training.
Once the second training is completed, its contents are a pair
of UV-parameters.

3.4 Texture-mapping the srface voxels
In recent researches on 3D scene reconstruction, machine
learning is adopted to create transformations between texture spaces and model spaces (Zeng et al. 2024; Xiang et
al. 2021; Maier et al. 2017; Lai et al. 2018). In these algorithms, the texel values of a surface fragment or a voxel are
calculted by using pre-trained neural nets. Alias reduction
is not considered in their texel retrieval computations. It is
well known that anti-aliasing is crucial in texture-mapping
(Hecbert 2007; Meinds and Eisemann 2025). In this work,
we design two filters for surpressing noises and achieving
high-quality texturing results.
Conventional texture-mapping methods perform minification and magnification filtering in the texture space in
the rendering processes. Since the surface of a volumetric
model is composed of voxels, their filtering approaches are

3.4.1 The UV-parameterization filtering in the SOM space
In the proposed texture-mapping method, the surface voxels
are textured one by one. Assuming that the positional vector
(coordinates) of a target surface voxel is v, a BMU in the
SOM is found based on the metric d1 defined in Eq. (1) at
first. As a result, the w1 weight of the BMU is most similar to v than other SOM nodes’ w1 weights. Then, a filter is
employed to compute a pair of UV-parameters for the voxel.
This filter could be the nearest-point filter or the linear filter,

However, the resolutions of the volumetric model and the
texture are usually different and spatial alias may occur if
no suitable filter is employed in the texel retrieval computation. The proposed texturing procedure categorizes the texel
retrieval computation into two types: If the resolution of the
volummetric model is higher, we have a magnification mapping. Otherwise we have a minification mapping.
In each type of mapping, two filters are utilized for calculating the final texel value. They are the nearest-point and
linear filters. In the nearest-point filter, we find the texel
whose UV-parameters are most similar to vector w at first.
Then, the color of the texel is returned as the final texel values. Part (c) of Fig. 7 reveals this filtering technique.
Unlike the nearest-point filter, the linear filter behaves
differently in minification and magnification mapping. In
the magnification mapping, we map the vector w onto the
texture and find the four texels surrounding w. Then, the
voxel’s color is obtained by bilinearly interpolating the colors of these four texels, as shown in part (a) of Fig. 7. In the
minification mapping, a box, centered at the projection of w,
is created. Then, the texel color is set to the average of the
colors of those texels inside this box. This computation is
shown in part (b) of Fig. 7. The width and height of the box
are estimated by the ratios between the resolutions of the
volumetric model and the texture.
3.4.3 Visualization method
Once the texel values have been calculated, we keep them in
the target voxel. Thus, color information of surface voxels
need not to be recomputed in the rendering process. This
methodology is not uncommon in volumetric model creation (Nießner et al. 2013). Once all the surface voxels have
been textured, we collect the external faces of the surface
voxels and careate a polygon-based model. The colors of
these faces are the same as their master voxels, and their
normal vectors are computed according to their orientations.
Thus, their normals are confined to the positive and negative
x-, y-, and z-axes. Then, this polygonal model is shaded by
using surface rendering such that the quality of the textureing process can be visually evaluated.

4.3 Computational cost analysis
The computational costs are influenced by the sizes of
the volumetric modes and the types and resolutions of the
SOMs as revealed by the data in Table 1. The SOM used for
texturing the teapot is a cubic SOM. Its number of nodes
is six times of the cylindrical SOM. Hence, more time is
required to search for BMUs in the training processes and
the total texturing cost is higher.
By comparing the costs of the training and the texturing
computations, we find that the costs of texturing are higher
than the costs of training for the bunny and the vase models.
However, the teapot model consumes more computational
time in the training processes. This is caused by the searching of BMUs and the updating of w1 and w2 weights in the
cubic SOM. In the cubic SOM, the vicinity of a BMU may
cover at most three faces of the SOM, and thus the searching and weight-updating calculations require more computational efforts.

4.7 Fine tuning of texturing process
Our texturing procedure utilizes unsupervised trainings
to generate fitting surfaces and UV-parameterization.
The appearances of textured models are hard to predict
in advance. In order to solve this problem, we adopt the
13
following techniques to improve the proposed texturing
procedure:
1. Region of interest (ROI): Our texturing program allows
users to select a ROI in the texture and another ROI in
the target model surface via a GUI. The texturing process will be conducted within these two ROIs.
2. Texture warping: To accommodate the irregular shape
of the target, our program allows users to warp the textures by using affine transformations.
3. UV-parameter transformations: The UV-parameters can
be transformed to produce repeated, shifted, and rotated
patterns on the target model.
4. Anchor points: Users can select a set of correspondent voxels, SOM nodes, and texels on the model
surface, SOM lattice, and the texture, denoted as
{ V(x,y,z), N(i,j), T(u,v)} . The correspondent relations among these three elements will be preserved in
the training processes, and the texture will not drift on
the model surface.
5. Multi-chart texturing: Users can split the target model
into multiple charts and texture these charts separately.
Hence, the SOMs can produce better fitting surfaces.

5 Conclusion and future work
In this paper, we present a texturing procedure for mapping patterns on volumetric models. The proposed method
employs SOMs to create fitting surfaces and to encode UVparameterization schemes. Thus, the structural and topological disparities between model surfaces and textures
are reduced. New filtering methods are invented to soothe
texturing alias and preserve texture patterns. Experimental
results reveal that the proposed method is capable of adding
fine details on the surfaces of volumetric models. It greatly
enhances the appearances of volumetric models. The experiments also demonstrate the key factors in the texturing
computations. They are the topology of SOM, the filtering
methods, and the resolution of SOM.
In some applications, the textures are placed within designated locations on the models’ surfaces. We demonstrate
that our texturing procedure is able to map sub-textures
onto designated ROIs of the target models. Our texturing
procedure can also warp the textures to accommodate the
shapes of the models. By using affine transformations, the
13
proposed texturing method is capable of producing repeated
and rotated patterns on the target models.
Since, we adopt un-supervised training to create fitting
surfaces and UV-parameterization, the texturing results are
hard to predict. In this research, we try to use anchor points
and multi-chart techniques to solve this problem. However,
more efforts are need to improve the selection of anchor
points and the partition of the models’ surfaces. Compared
with polygonal models, voxel-based models are easier to
edit and remodel. The proposed texturing procedure can be
revised to carve the surfaces of volumetric models to generate fine geometric patterns on 3D models. In future work,
we will extend the functionalities of the proposed procedure
to add geometric features on volumetric models.
