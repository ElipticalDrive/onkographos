Abstract
Per-scene underwater reconstruction supports marine archaeology, seabed survey,
and offshore inspection, but faces two coupled problems: attenuation and backscatter mix water effects with object appearance, and common 3D Gaussian Splatting
renderers encode the medium globally, directionally, or on object surfaces, limiting
spatial queries after camera motion. We propose AquaMass-GS (AM-GS), which
couples an object-Gaussian scene model to normalized free-space water Gaussians. Cell-volume weighting gives direct-extinction and backscatter optical-mass
semantics; analytic finite-path integration with coverage normalization queries
water from camera to visible surface. Separate optimization and querying keep
object appearance scene-tied while representing water by one equivalent coefficient per finite ray segment. On four SeaThru-NeRF development scenes, AM-GS
provides competitive end-to-end novel-view quality, with the highest mean peak
signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and the lowest learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) among six methods; the observed margins over WaterSplatting
(0.282 dB PSNR and 0.0002 LPIPS) are not statistically decisive under paired
uncertainty, and its mean structural similarity (SSIM) is slightly lower. On paired
simulation with hidden clean targets it improves medium-disabled SSIM by 0.1139
over WaterSplatting, and, in a controlled known-optics test using a compact
σ/∆ = 0.30 carrier, it reduces unseen-origin transmittance RMSE by 52.7%
relative to a trilinear voxel carrier.

1 Introduction
High-fidelity underwater reconstruction can turn a sparse camera survey into a navigable three-dimensional map of a submerged site. For seabed mineral exploration, offshore
construction, and archaeological excavation, such a map supports route planning, equipment placement, and risk assessment before vehicles and tools enter the water. Better
virtual inspection can reduce repeated deployments, improve operational accuracy,
and lower the cost of work in environments where every dive is expensive. Recent
work in visual computing has applied Gaussian scene representations to degraded
imagery and scene-aware rendering [1, 2]. We study per-scene underwater novel-view
synthesis, where a renderer is optimized from posed images of one submerged site and
synthesizes held-out views of that same site [3]. This task has two coupled challenges:
first, wavelength-dependent attenuation weakens the object signal, while suspended
particles add accumulated backscatter [4]; these water effects are entangled with geometry, reflectance, lighting, and camera response. Second, camera motion changes the
three-dimensional water path to each visible surface, so the medium must be spatially
queryable independently of the reconstructed object.
The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [5] has brought high-quality novelview synthesis (NVS) to underwater scenes. Recent systems combine surface Gaussians
with physically motivated attenuation and backscatter [6–10]. Their medium is typically
a scene-global depth law, a direction-conditioned neural prediction, an optical variable
stored on reconstructed surfaces, or a positionally interpolated directional feature grid.
Such carriers can explain captured appearance efficiently, but they do not combine
a water representation independent of object sampling with an explicit query over
the finite camera-to-visible-surface path. Consequently, the second challenge remains
unresolved: the renderer lacks a free-space transport carrier whose optical contribution
can be evaluated when the camera origin and traversed path change. We refer to this
as the missing free-space transport query.
To bridge this gap and address both challenges, we propose AquaMass-GS (AM-GS),
shown in Fig. 1. At the representation level, the method couples an object-Gaussian
scene model with a distinct field of normalized free-space water Gaussians whose cellvolume-weighted parameters encode optical mass for direct extinction and backscatter.
At the rendering level, the object branch supplies a detached alpha-weighted surface
endpoint; analytic finite-path integration and coverage normalization then query the
water field from the camera to that visible surface and convert its optical depth
into one per-pixel equivalent transport coefficient for the finite segment. Separating
the water carrier from object sampling reduces the representational entanglement
between object sampling and water transport, while evaluating only the traversed finite
segment handles changes in camera origin and path. In contrast to global or directionconditioned predictors [6, 8] and surface-attached optical variables [10], AM-GS keeps
water transport spatially queryable in free space.

The contributions are threefold:
• We formulate a normalized free-space water-Gaussian field whose cell-volumeweighted direct-extinction and backscatter parameters have per-primitive opticalmass semantics independent of object sampling and lattice resolution.
• We introduce a detached finite-surface query that combines analytic Gaussian
integration with partition-of-unity coverage normalization, recovering the exact
equivalent coefficient for a constant underlying field on every positive-coverage ray
while retaining an existing object renderer.
• We provide matched end-to-end and controlled representation evidence: on four
SeaThru-NeRF development scenes, AM-GS delivers competitive quality, ranking first
in mean PSNR and LPIPS among six methods while WaterSplatting is marginally
higher in mean SSIM; on paired simulation, it improves hidden-clean SSIM by
0.1139 over WaterSplatting; and in a controlled known-optics test with a compact
σ/∆ = 0.30 carrier, it reduces unseen-origin transmittance RMSE by 52.7% relative
to a trilinear voxel carrier.
2 Related Work
2.1 Underwater Formation and Restoration
Physics-based restoration methods estimate how wavelength-dependent attenuation
and backscatter alter a captured image. The revised underwater image-formation model
separates the coefficients governing direct signal and backscatter [4]; Sea-thru combines
range with dark-pixel evidence for water removal [11]. Haze-Lines extends color restoration across different water types and introduces a natural-image quantitative dataset
[12]. Recent learned restoration models use stronger priors and explicit degradation
3
decomposition; for instance, SLURPP combines latent diffusion with transmission and
backscatter prediction [13]. Related visual-computing work also treats adverse illumination as a restoration problem [14]. These methods restore one image or video frame
at a time and do not maintain a view-consistent 3D water volume. In contrast, AM-GS
optimizes one 3D scene from posed multi-view images, renders novel underwater views,
and exposes a medium-disabled object diagnostic.
2.2 Underwater Neural Rendering
SeaThru-NeRF transfers underwater decomposition to neural radiance fields [3]. Underwater Gaussian methods improve rendering speed but encode water in different places.
SeaSplat and Gaussian Splashing apply learned, depth-conditioned attenuation and
backscatter [6, 7], while WaterSplatting and AquaSplatting pair explicit scene Gaussians with auxiliary neural medium fields [8, 15]. 3D-UIR combines pose-conditioned
appearance embeddings, optical parameters, and pseudo-depth supervision [9]. Plenodium introduces a positional medium grid whose spherical-harmonic features depend
on viewing direction [16]; WaterClear-GS stores local spectral optical parameters
on object Gaussians [10]; and UW-3DGS predicts spatially varying attenuation and
backscatter from a factorized voxel volume [17]. UW-GS and OceanSplat focus on
distractor suppression and medium-aware geometry [18, 19]. Recent visual-computing
studies broaden this line of work to degraded-image quality assessment, scene-aware
rendering, and joint depth–image reconstruction [1, 2, 20]. DualPhys-GS evaluates
feature-guided attenuation and scattering on both SeaThru-NeRF and SaltPond [21];
Li and Fu’s Gaussian splat-field framework targets few-shot underwater synthesis [22];
and HydroGaussian adds hierarchical depth regularization and explicit ray–Gaussian
intersections [23]. Unlike these geometry-, quality-, and semantics-oriented extensions,
AM-GS isolates the medium-carrier question: it retains the object renderer and requires
neither monocular-depth nor semantic priors, while assigning optical mass to normalized
free-space water Gaussians and querying them only along the finite camera-to-surface
path.
2.3 Gaussian Scene Representations
3DGS represents radiance with anisotropic surface splats and a depth-sorted tile rasterizer [5]. Mip-Splatting improves scale consistency [24], while 2D Gaussian Splatting
emphasizes geometrically accurate surface elements [25]. These methods target opaquescene appearance rather than participating media. Vol3DGS analytically integrates 3D
Gaussian density to obtain volumetrically consistent alpha values [26]. Building on this
volumetric principle, AM-GS applies analytic integration specifically to normalized
water primitives: object splatting remains unchanged, water mass is assigned by a
finite-volume rule, and finite-path optical depth is mapped to the retained underwater
compositor.

3 Method
3.1 Overview
AquaMass-GS consists of a conventional object-Gaussian set Go and a separate freespace water set Gw . To isolate the proposed water representation, the object branch,
direction encoding, training losses, and underwater compositor follow the released
WaterSplatting implementation [8]. Our change has four stages: a normalized water
field, finite integration to a detached object-surface proxy, unit-density coverage normalization, and equivalent-coefficient composition. Figure 1 states this contract without
depicting water primitives as depth-sorted object events.
3.2 Hybrid Scene Representation
For every scene, both Gaussian sets are optimized from the same posed underwater
images. Object Gaussian j retains the standard mean, anisotropic covariance, opacity
αj , and spherical-harmonic radiance cj of 3DGS [5]. The retained object branch uses
the reference parameterization and densification schedule; our changes begin only when
its splatted surface statistics are queried by the water field.
The water centers are fixed on a face-centered-cubic (FCC) lattice inside the axisaligned bounds of the structure-from-motion points plus a fixed world-space margin. For
N cells per axis, four offsets per cell produce 4N 3 centers. The axis-aligned covariance
is also fixed, with standard deviation s∆ along each dimension. The compact reported
configuration uses s = 1.3; the initial s = 0.7 layout is retained as an equal-capacity
control in Table 5. Only two nonnegative RGB density vectors per center are optimized:
one for direct attenuation and one for backscatter attenuation. Keeping position and
covariance fixed prevents transport mass from changing through geometric resizing.

3.6 Optimization and Outputs
Training retains the reference loss implementation with the configured reg_l2 and
reg_ssim branches. Specifically, the RGB term is the mean squared residual after
division by stopgrad(bI) + 10−3 , and the structural term is one minus SSIM [27] after
applying the same prediction-dependent normalization to both images. Their convex
combination supervises only captured underwater RGB. The RGB and structural
weights are 0.8 and 0.2. The water densities receive a spatial total-variation penalty
of 10−4 , formed from mean absolute first differences along the three lattice axes
plus the within-cell deviation of the four FCC sites. No clean images, calibrated
optical coefficients, or medium masks are used in the real-scene experiments. The
reported compact configuration uses N = 8, hence 2,048 fixed water Gaussians and
12,288 learned RGB transport parameters, and optimizes both transport densities
jointly with the object set for 10k iterations. Adam uses learning rate 2.5 × 10−3
for each transport-density tensor and 10−3 for the retained directional color head,
with optimizer ϵ = 10−15 ; the density rates are constant, while the directional head
decays to 1.5 × 10−4 over the configured 15k horizon. The grid spans the COLMAP
point bounds plus a two-world-unit margin before multiplication by the archived
coordinate scale. Direct and backscatter-attenuation densities initialize respectively to
8
(0.035, 0.020, 0.012)/sc and (0.012, 0.018, 0.025)/sc , where sc is that coordinate scale;
B∞ initializes from (0.08, 0.32, 0.48). The initially frozen N = 16, s = 0.7 configuration
remains in the component, stochastic, numerical, and efficiency audits so the post-hoc
capacity decision is visible. Optimizer decay horizons remain configured for 15k steps.
The real-scene WaterSplatting and Ours runs terminate at 10k as stated in Section 4.1;
the paired-simulation runs use the separately declared 15k protocol.
The renderer exports the supervised underwater image, its attenuated object term,
its accumulated medium term, and a medium-disabled object image. The last output
is obtained by bypassing the learned attenuation and backscatter during rendering, but
its object colors were still optimized only through underwater images. We therefore
call it an object diagnostic; it is neither trained nor evaluated as physical clear-radiance
restoration.

Coordinate scaling
If scene coordinates are scaled by s, Gaussian means and standard deviations scale by
s while density scales by 1/s. Equation 9 is then invariant. All real-scene runs use the
coordinate scale already stored by the shared data parser; no image or test metric is
used to choose it.
Coverage and single-surface identity
For any positive-coverage ray and constant density, Eqs. 10–11 reproduce that density
exactly even when the finite Gaussian sum does not integrate to the geometric path
length. For a non-uniform field and one opaque surface at axial proxy depth z ∗ , with
geometric distance t∗ = z ∗ /dcam

small when visibility is concentrated near one surface and can increase at occlusion

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Real scenes and split
We use Curasao, IUI3-RedSea, Japanese Gardens, and Panama from SeaThru-NeRF [3].
Every method is optimized independently for each scene. Two filename-selected views
per scene are withheld from all training runs, yielding eight full-resolution test images.
All methods are scored against byte-identical decoded targets using one implementation
of PSNR [28], SSIM [27], and AlexNet-LPIPS [29]. PSNR measures pixelwise fidelity,
SSIM emphasizes local structure, and LPIPS measures perceptual feature distance;
higher PSNR/SSIM and lower LPIPS are better. Because the compact water-field
capacity was selected while these scenes were available, the comparison is a controlled
development-set study rather than an independent leaderboard.
Independent RGB-blind confirmation
We separately reserve all three Underwater360 scenes as an independently held-out
evaluation set [30]. The official even/odd split supplies 53/53, 91/90, and 76/76
train/test panoramas for Scenes 1, 3, and 4; every panorama is bilinearly projected
to four fixed 480 × 480, 90◦ perspective faces. The final AM-GS configuration and
WaterSplatting are trained for 10k iterations from the same complete-capture camera
poses and official point cloud. No held-out RGB is decoded before all six checkpoints
are hashed, after which the four faces are averaged per panorama, panoramas are
averaged per scene, and scenes receive equal macro weight. Thus the test is RGB-blind
but not geometry-blind; it measures transfer to new captures, not calibrated water
removal or native spherical rendering.
Compared methods
We retrain the released 3D-UIR [9], SeaSplat [6], UW-GS [18], Gaussian Splashing [7], and WaterSplatting [8] pipelines on the same scene files and test lists as
Ours. Method-native schedules are retained: 30k iterations for 3D-UIR, SeaSplat, and
Gaussian Splashing, 15k for UW-GS, and 10k for WaterSplatting and Ours. Released
preprocessing is retained, but no held-out RGB enters training, depth normalization,
model selection, or pseudo-depth estimation. WaterClear-GS [10], Plenodium [16], and
OceanSplat [19] are discussed as protocol-mismatched context rather than inserted
into this ranking without common predictions and decoded targets. Plenodium is
nonetheless reproduced in the paired simulation of Section 4.5, and the reason the two
studies differ is the comparison protocol rather than a selective omission: its official
implementation requires a vendored Nerfstudio tree with its own gsplat and PyTorch
pins and was compiled for a different GPU architecture, so it cannot be retrained inside
the single environment this table uses, whereas the paired simulation compares lossless
exports rescored in one frozen metric environment and therefore tolerates per-method
training stacks.

Implementation
AM-GS holds the inherited object Gaussians, optimizer, loss, and direction-conditioned
asymptotic-color head fixed so that only the spatial water representation and query
change. The final configuration uses an N = 8 fixed lattice, σ/∆ = 1.3, coverage normalization, and geometric camera-ray distance, corresponding to 2,048 water Gaussians and
12,288 RGB transport parameters. All real-scene results use full input resolution and
seed 0 unless a multi-seed result is explicitly identified. Eighteen analytic-renderer unit
tests covering diagonal rays, finite segments, anisotropic bases, culling, and transformed
cameras agree with independent float64 integration within 2.90 × 10−6 . All reported differences, intervals, and percentages are computed from archived full-precision per-view
or per-field arrays; table cells are rounded only for display.
Paired simulation protocol
We additionally use the official Plenodium Beach and Street simulations at easy,
medium, and hard water levels [16]. Each subset contains 100 posed 512×512 images
and a pose-matched clean counterpart; explicit filenames freeze 50 degraded training
views and 50 held-out views before any full run. The clean images are inaccessible to
training and are read only by the final evaluator. We train Nerfstudio 3DGS [5] for
30k iterations, the official WaterSplatting [8] and Ours implementations for 15k, and
the official depth-assisted Plenodium implementation [16] for 15,001 iterations with
independently inferred Depth Anything V2 priors [31]. Every method receives the same
degraded images, cameras, subset-specific sparse point cloud, and frozen test poses; no
clean target, test image, or test metric selects a checkpoint or hyperparameter. We
report the same PSNR, SSIM, and AlexNet-LPIPS implementations for (i) the rendered
underwater image against its degraded target and (ii) the raw medium-disabled branch
against the clean target. Ordinary 3DGS has no medium-separation branch, so its
underwater RGB is retained as a clearly labeled no-separation reference in the second
test. To remove training-environment differences, all methods export lossless 8-bit RGB
PNGs and the paper tables rescore those exports in one frozen metric environment;
method-native float metrics are retained only as a render audit.
4.2 Real-Scene Rendering Quality
Table 1 reports every scene and all three image metrics instead of hiding structural or
perceptual losses behind macro averages. Ours has the highest average PSNR (27.871
dB) and lowest average LPIPS (0.1389) among the six reproduced methods, while
WaterSplatting has the highest average SSIM by 0.0005. Relative to that baseline, Ours
improves PSNR on Curasao, IUI3, and Japanese Gardens by 1.041, 0.230, and 0.212 dB,
respectively, and is 0.357 dB lower on Panama. Its SSIM is higher on Curasao and IUI3
but lower on Japanese Gardens and Panama; LPIPS follows the same two-win/two-loss
pattern. Thus the main table supports competitive real-scene reconstruction and a
modest average advantage, not uniform dominance over the strongest matched baseline.

4.8 Ablation and Repeatability
Table 5(a) separates spatial transport from the retained directional asymptotic-color
head. At N = 16 with global color, replacing homogeneous transport by spatial
transport raises average PSNR from 27.566 to 27.754 dB but reduces SSIM and increases
LPIPS, showing that real-view metrics alone do not identify the water field. Re-enabling
directional color recovers structural and perceptual quality, while the compact N = 8
carrier gives the best PSNR in this panel. Panel (b) shows that the rendering refinements
are also non-monotonic: the σ/∆ = 0.7 → 1.3 change improves all three point estimates,
coverage normalization gives the highest PSNR and SSIM, and geometric distance
gives the lowest LPIPS but slightly reduces PSNR. The width change is small relative
to the three-seed variation reported below (0.011 dB versus 0.040 dB PSNR standard
deviation, and 0.0012 LPIPS versus 0.0012 standard deviation), so it is not treated as
statistically decisive model selection. We nevertheless use σ/∆ = 1.3 and geometric
distance in the final model because the former is the retained production configuration
and the latter converts camera-space axial depth to the actual distance travelled along
each ray, preserving the physical unit of optical depth; the 0.066 dB PSNR decrease
relative to coverage normalization is an explicit accuracy–consistency trade-off rather
than an unreported selection. Because these are independently optimized checkpoints,
the rows support the final design choice but do not isolate a pure test-time causal gain
or establish that the selected width is superior under stochastic variation.
The final N = 8, σ/∆ = 1.3, POU, geometric-distance configuration is repeated
from scratch with seeds 1 and 2 and combined with the frozen seed-0 run. Across the
runs, Ours reaches 27.849±0.040 dB and 0.1388±0.0012 LPIPS versus 27.533±0.050 dB

4.9 Numerical Properties and Efficiency
The numerical tests separate analytic correctness from image quality. They verify
the homogeneous optical-mass identity, coordinate-scale invariance, finite-segment
integration, and expected error near separated surfaces and occlusion boundaries. For
four known coefficient fields and three severities, water Gaussians reduce direct and
backscatter optical-depth RMSE by 70.06% and 66.87% relative to a direction-only
oracle under identical finite paths. That oracle receives only the unit ray direction and
path length: it fits one RGB coefficient for each sampled image direction jointly over
all camera origins, with neither camera origin nor a 3D query coordinate, and is not an
official WaterSplatting reproduction. These known-coefficient tests are unit tests, not
learned image restoration results. Complete numerical results are provided in Tables 11
and 12 of Appendix C.
The exact final real-scene model reaches 19.12 render-only FPS on one RTX 5090
versus 239.63 FPS for WaterSplatting, while peak allocated memory rises from 1120.2
to 1449.5 MiB. The current dense integration kernel is therefore a representation
prototype rather than a speed contribution. Its cost is dominated by the per-pixel
sum over water primitives, which grows with lattice size; because the reported kernel
is wider than the lattice spacing, support culling cannot reduce that sum without
perturbing the integral, so the practical route to real-time evaluation is quadrature on
a precomputed field, as discussed in Section 5.

5 Scope and Limitations
AM-GS uses a single-scattering emission–absorption model and one visible-surface
endpoint per pixel; multiple scattering, refraction, polarization, unknown camera
response, and separated translucent layers remain outside its scope. Because training
uses only underwater RGB, object radiance and water transport are not fully identifiable,
so the medium-disabled output is a diagnostic rather than calibrated restoration.
Evaluation covers four development scenes, three independent confirmation scenes,
and controlled synthetic fields; broader environments, geometry-aware evaluation, and
dynamic-scene handling remain future work [21]. Finally, the current dense integration
kernel is slower than WaterSplatting, and the reported kernel width explains why
support culling is not the remedy: at σ/∆ = 1.3 the three-deviation support radius is
3.9 lattice spacings across a lattice of eight, so culling leaves most sites in play, and a
tighter cutoff would perturb the integral beyond the tolerance audited in Section 4.9.
That same width band-limits σ D and σ B , which are therefore resolved by a grid
only a few times finer than ∆. Precomputing both fields on such a grid by separable
convolution and integrating by quadrature over the same finite segment would make
the per-pixel cost independent of the number of water primitives; the analytic kernel
would then be retained as the reference against which that fast path is audited.
6 Conclusion
AquaMass-GS replaces inherited predicted transport coefficients with normalized freespace water Gaussians and a finite-segment analytic query while retaining the object
renderer. On four development scenes, the six-method comparison and exact-final threeseed repeat place Ours first in mean PSNR and LPIPS but second in SSIM; complete
frames and fixed target-selected crops retain scene-level losses. On paired simulation,
the medium-disabled branch ranks first in SSIM and second in PSNR/LPIPS, while
Plenodium leads underwater rendering and hidden-clean perceptual distance. Across
24 independently generated known fields, compact Gaussians with σ/∆ = 0.30 reduce
mean fieldwise unseen-origin edited-transmittance RMSE by 52.7% versus a positive
trilinear voxel carrier, although this controlled width differs from the final real-scene
model. Independent-scene and known-water audits delimit this conclusion: the present
evidence supports a queryable water representation and competitive per-scene rendering,
while calibrated physical recovery remains an open problem.

and [1.48, 2.52]; axis scales are independently uniform on [0.18, 0.38], [0.16, 0.34],

origin-only, direction-only, and joint splits, affected pixels are nonempty but not all
pixels, outside pixels are nonempty, affected fraction lies in [0.02, 0.85], and outside
fraction is at least 0.05. The first 24 passing candidates are fixed before any carrier

All training cameras have origin (x, 0, 0) for x ∈ {−0.36, −0.12, 0.12, 0.36}, zero
yaw/pitch, and look along world +z. Each uses a 21 × 21 pinhole grid with tangent half-FOV 0.20, giving 1,764 training rays. The origin-only test uses (x, 0, 0) for
x ∈ {−0.30, −0.18, 0, 0.18, 0.30} with the same orientation and pixels. The directiononly split reuses training origins with subpixel shift 0.43. The joint split uses the
following (x, y, z; yaw, pitch) values, with angles in degrees:

Optimization and aggregation
The positive trilinear carrier has 133 sites and six RGB values per site; each FCC carrier
has 2,048 centers and six values per center. For every field and carrier, deterministic

projected FISTA solves minX≥0 mean(AX − Y )2 + 10−7 mean(X 2 ) for at most 20,000

metrics are arithmetic means; the relative interval resamples 24 paired field reductions
