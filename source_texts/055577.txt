Abstract
Direct volume rendering (DVR) is a commonly utilized technique for three-dimensional visualization of volumetric medical
images. A key goal of DVR is to enable users to visually emphasize regions of interest (ROIs) which may be occluded by
other structures. Conventional methods for ROIs visual emphasis require extensive user involvement for the adjustment of
rendering parameters to reduce the occlusion, dependent on the user’s viewing direction. Several works have been proposed to
automatically preserve the view of the ROIs by eliminating the occluding structures of lower importance in a view-dependent
manner. However, they require pre-segmentation labeling and manual importance assignment on the images. An alternative
to ROIs segmentation is to use ‘saliency’ to identify important regions. This however lacks semantic information and thus
leads to the inclusion of false positive regions. In this study, we propose an attention-driven visual emphasis method for
volumetric medical image visualization. We developed a deep learning attention model, termed as focused-class attention
map (F-CAM), trained with only image-wise labels for automated ROIs localization and importance estimation. Our F-CAM
transfers the semantic information from the classification task for use in the localization of ROIs, with a focus on small ROIs
that characterize medical images. Additionally, we propose an attention compositing module that integrates the generated
attention map with transfer function within the DVR pipeline to automate the view-dependent visual emphasis of the ROIs.
We demonstrate the superiority of our method compared to existing methods on a multi-modality PET-CT dataset and an
MRI dataset.
Keywords DVR · Visual emphasis · Attention · Medical images
1 Introduction
Direct volume rendering (DVR) is a computer graphics
technique for the three-dimensional (3D) visualization of
volumetric data. DVR has broad applications in medicine,
design, engineering, and natural sciences [1] and is included
in modern imaging viewers such as in 3D Slicer [2] and
ImageJ [3] to complement two-dimensional (2D) image
views. A key goal of DVR is to enable users to ‘visually

The visual emphasis on the ROIs has traditionally been
achieved through transfer functions (TFs) specification. In
conventional 1D TFs that associate intensity with voxels,
users should specify an intensity range for each ROI’s voxels and then assign opacity values to the intensity range. As
such, TF specification is inherently incapable of encoding
contextual information, such as the location of the ROIs, and
is applied globally to all regions that share a similar intensity
range. As an example, TF is unable to differentiate lung structures from its adjacent skin tissue in computed tomography
(CT) images as these structures have overlapping intensity
ranges. Hence accurate localization and opacity adjustment
of the ROIs is challenging. Furthermore, TFs are viewindependent, which means that even if users have an ideal TF
for a particular view, repeated TF manipulation is inevitable
since the occluding regions to the ROIs differ in other viewing
directions. There has been a myriad of work in addressing the

shortcomings of 1D TFs, which include multi-dimensional
TFs [1], automated TF optimization [4], and interactive volume clipping [5]. All of these methods, however, are still
view-independent and/or lack contextual information.
There has been an attempt at using segmentation labels
as contextual information for visual emphasis in DVR. Viola
et al. [6] proposed importance-driven visualization to automatically generate ghosted (clear) views of the volumetric
data. All regions are segmented, and the less important
regions are de-emphasized if they occlude the ROIs under
the current viewing direction. This importance-driven compositing process is conducted alongside volume ray casting
and therefore it is view-dependent. However, importancedriven visualization requires segmentations of regions and
the assignment of importance to each region prior to rendering, which is not trivial to obtain and requires a domain
expert.
In this study, we propose a view-dependent visual emphasis method by incorporating the attention mechanism into the
volume ray casting process, which we termed as ‘attentiondriven visual emphasis’. Attention is a biologically inspired
mechanism that identifies important semantics (as ROIs) that
the human visual perception system considers being ‘attentive’ for particular vision tasks [7]. With rapid advances in
deep learning-based image processing research, we can generate attention maps at a deep layer of a convolutional neural
network (CNN) where semantic information is encoded [8].
Such an attention map allows for a delicate depiction of ROIs
and can be used to adjust the opacity of the ROIs and their
surrounding regions, hence enabling attention-driven visual
emphasis. We call this adjustment process attention compositing for DVR.
There was an early attempt to use visual saliency in DVR
by Jung et al. [9] which used a bottom-up saliency map for
the TF specification. Here, the saliency map was calculated
by identifying regions that are distinct from the adjacent
regions, and then dominant saliency regions were used as
labels to maximize their visibility in subsequent TF specification. However, their saliency calculation relied on low-level,
non-semantic features which failed to eliminate the false
positive salient regions without the high-level semantic guidance.
We generate the attention map encoded with semantics
using a weakly-supervised deep learning model that only
requires image-wise labels (e.g., whether the tumor exists
or not). Once trained as a one-off pre-processing for a particular dataset, e.g., magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), an
attention map can be directly derived for new data with similar semantics. Another novelty we introduce is to support
small ROIs that are inherent in medical images. The ROIs
in medical images are typically small regions [10] and are
not well-suited for existing attention maps, e.g., the popular
Grad-CAM [8] that tends to include regions surrounding the

(1) We introduce an attention-driven mechanism designed

comparison experiments against current visual emphasis
baseline methods.
2 Related work
2.1 Visual emphasis of ROIs
The use of TFs is considered the baseline to apply visual
emphasis among ROIs in DVR. The ROIs are specified by the
users in a TF space and the opacities of the ROIs and the context are adjusted to reduce the occlusion. Much research has
been conducted to explore multi-dimensional TF by leveraging visual attributes derived from the images, such as
occlusion spectrum [14] and ROI size [15], in an attempt
to better specify the ROIs [1]. However, those low-level predefined features are carefully designed for particular tasks
that are difficult to generalize and do not contain high-level
semantic information. Furthermore, multi-dimensional TFs
are difficult to manipulate due to their high dimensional
parameter settings and lack of explicit locational information.
Recently, machine learning-based methods have been proposed to automatically extract visual attributes from image
volumes, e.g., rule-enhanced TFs [16], Convolutional Neural
Network (CNN) derived high-level semantic features [17],
and convolutional sparse coding [18], to replace the manually
derived attributes. Ghahremani et al. [19] employed a deep
learning-based segmentation model to segment volumetric

images and use the segmentation output as one dimension of
a 2D TF. Despite the improved performance in TFs specification, these learning-based methods require expensive
voxel-wise labels, e.g., scribbles or segmentation labels, to
learn visual attributes.
Apart from TFs-based methods, there are interactive methods that require manual placement of ROIs in the data space
and then visually emphasizing them. Weiskopf et al. [20]
manually located the ROIs and then cut away obstructing elements using a clipping plane. Islam et al. [5] extended it with
more complex clipping geometries. These user interactions
in volume clippings are non-trivial and repeated manipulations are required if the viewing direction changes. Zhou et al.
[21] adjusted the opacity of the structures according to the
distance of them to the users’ pointed ROIs center. St-Onge
et al. [22] manipulated the rendering parameters within a
manually defined spherical focus region to visually emphasize the ROIs. However, these methods are view-independent
thus all surrounding context information is suppressed. The
visibility of the context which will not occlude the ROIs
under current viewing directions is not guaranteed.
Chan et al. [23] proposed to optimize the rendering
parameters to enhance the perceived visibility, shape, and
transparency quality of the semi-transparent layers in DVR.
Jung et al. [4] optimized the TFs opacity of the occluding
structures to maximize the visibility of the ROIs. An et al.
[24] extended this method to optimize both the TFs opacity and color to maximize the visual saliency of the ROIs.
Viola et al. [6] also introduced an additional importance
compositing step to emphasize the ROIs. If the ROIs with
high importance value were occluded by their front structures
with low importance value, the front structures would be rendered more sparsely to reduce their occlusion. In this way,
a view-dependent visual emphasis on ROIs was achieved.
Zhang et al. [25] also reduced the opacity of the occluding
context of the ROIs in a view-dependent manner on a virtual reality device. Nonetheless, these methods rely heavily
on pre-segmentation, human-annotated bounding boxes, and
manual importance assignments which is costly in medical
imaging.
2.2 Attention
Attention is a cognitive process that allows the user to
selectively focus on regions of interest while diminishing
regions of disinterest [7, 26, 27]. In the human visual system,
attention can be directed by top-down class-aware guidance, which involves identifying important regions based
on high-level tasks like classification, as well as by bottomup class-agnostic guidance, which involves detecting salient
regions that are distinct from their neighboring regions at
a low-level perspective [28]. Several traditional saliencybased bottom-up attention methods have been introduced in
3207
the visualization community to highlight the ROIs. Kim and
Varshney [29] applied a saliency-based operator to enhance
the ROIs in volume visualization. Shen et al. [30] extended
spatial saliency to temporal space for ROIs detection in timevarying volume data. Jung et al. [9] used hierarchical saliency
to detect ROIs in ray profile analysis. However, these bottomup saliency-based methods did not consider the semantic
information and take all the regions that stand out from their
surroundings as the same, e.g., the high-uptake regions in
a positron emission tomography (PET) including the tumor,
heart, bladder, etc. It can be further upgraded to a learningbased top-down attention estimation model to eliminate those
salient false positive regions.
In the medical image processing community, top-down
attention such as CAM [31] has been widely employed for
weakly supervised disease localization [32] and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) [33]. Choe et al. [34]
also analyzed the performance of numerous state-of-the-art
(SOTA) attention-based ROIs localization methods and recommended CAM as the most stable method which is less
sensitive to hyperparameters. However, the ROIs generated
by CAM tend to be too “general” after being up-sampled
to the raw image size. We thus proposed to extend GradCAM [8], a strict generalization version of the CAM [31],
to F-CAM with a self-guidance size constraint loss for more
focused ROIs localization in medical images.
2.3 Deep learning in DVR
Recent advances in deep learning have significantly advanced
computer vision tasks [35] and its adoption in the DVR
research community is being actively explored [36, 37].
Berger et al. [38] trained a Generative Adversarial Network
(GAN) on a set of DVR images generated with different viewpoints and TFs for a single volumetric data. In the inference
stage, the GAN model can directly generate the rendering
results of that data under new viewpoints and TFs without
the ray-casting process. In another study, Hong et al. [39]
combined the usage of GAN and CNN to synthesize images
with the same styles as the reference images under new viewing directions. Weiss and Navab [40] formulated the DVR
into sub-steps, including feature extraction, optical mapping,
and image compositing, and then explicitly replaced them
with learning-based models in a step-by-step manner. Weiss
et al. [41] also introduced a deep learning based importance
network to adaptively sample volumetric data, and a deep
learning reconstruction network to generate high-resolution
DVR from the low-resolution image. These works transform
the rendering into an image generation problem, but these
methods cannot guarantee that the resulting images are without faults such as hallucination. Therefore, they may not be
reliable for medical applications.
123
3208
Recently, Weiss and Westermann [42] proposed differentiable direct volume rendering to reconstruct TFs from a
given reference image. Pan et al. [43] extended this method
into differentiable design galleries, which automatically generate a well-organized latent space with reference images for
user exploration. The TFs were then reconstructed for the
user’s preferred styles and subsequently used to generate the
actual DVR. Although these methods maintain the realism of
DVRs by preserving the standard ray casting process while
optimizing the TFs, constructing a comprehensive training
dataset for all the possible TFs is both challenging and costly
due to the extensive scope of the TFs space.
Cheng et al. [17] and Quan et al. [18] employed learningbased attributes to encode high-level semantic information
for better feature identification. In another study, Nguyen
et al. [44] proposed the use of a 3D U-Net deep learning
model to pre-segment different structures from cryo-electron
tomography images and used it to produce DVRs of the
images. However, such supervised training processes rely
on voxel-wise ground truth datasets which are very costly
to obtain by domain experts. Despite the advancements in
the localization of ROIs using weakly-supervised attention
methods, as discussed in Sect. 2.2, this technique has not
been employed for visual emphasis in DVR.
3 Methods
3.1 Overview
As shown in Fig. 1, our attention-driven visual emphasis has
three steps: (i) We developed a well-designed deep learningbased attention model F-CAM for the voxel-wise localization
and attention estimation of ROIs (Sect. 3.2). It was trained
with only image-wise labels and outputs an attention estimation volume for the ROIs. (ii) We aggregated the occlusion
relationship between the ROIs and the context considering
both the attention values and current TF settings through an
attention compositing module (Sect. 3.3). (iii) We adjusted
the opacity of the occluding structures based on the aggregated occlusion relationship to visually emphasize the ROIs
(Sect. 3.4).
3.2 Attention estimation model
We proposed a Focused-Class Attention Map (F-CAM)
model to generate the attention map. The model took individual image slices of volumetric data to classify whether
the slice contains ROIs or not. We then stacked the resulting
attention maps from all slices to form an attention estimation
volume. The whole F-CAM model is shown in Fig. 2. The top
branch is a classification network. We calculate the attention
map as shown in the middle branch following Grad-CAM

3.3 Attention compositing
The attention compositing aggregated the attention value of
each voxel, the voxel’s opacity assigned by the TFs, and the
occlusion relations among them into a single-dimensional
new attribute—priority. It determined how we adjust the
opacity of each voxel during the rendering for visual emphasis.
As shown in the right side of Fig. 3, the ROI is the middle
part which we want to visually emphasize. R1 and the ROI lie
along the same viewing ray, with R1 occluding the ROI. R4
is the context part that is outside the F-CAM attention region
(indicated as the orange circle around the ROI) but falsepositively detected as the ROI by the TFs (share a similar
intensity range with the ROI). R3 is another context part that
is false-positively detected as ROI by the attention model.
R2 and R3 lie along the same viewing ray and R2 “occludes”
the false positive region R3. We want to de-emphasize the R1
to reveal the ROI. We want to keep the context in R2 since
it does not occlude the ROI but just context in R3. We also
do not want to render the false positive regions R3/4 as the
ROI but keep the useful context in them. To accomplish this,
we first propose a TFs-enhanced attention value Ae for each
voxel which combines the information from both the TFs and

3.4 DVR integration
We then conducted the standard volume ray casting and
adjusted the opacity of each voxel by multiplying it with
the associated priority value. For the ROIs, the opacity of
the ROIs remains high. For the false positive regions R3/4,
their priorities are high thus the adjusted opacities remain
high. The useful context information in these regions is kept.
The region R2 which does not occlude the ROI but occlude
the false positive region R3 is also kept. Only part R1 which
occludes the ROI is de-emphasized since they have low priority values. The ROI is thus automatically identified and
visually emphasized with this attention-driven visual emphasis.
When users alter the viewing directions, such as by rotating volumetric images, the localization of ROIs remains
consistent, but their 3D occlusion relationships with other
structures are dynamically adjusted along the ray casting.
Consequently, the priority values assigned to each region are
also updated, resulting in a view-dependent visual emphasis
of ROIs.

Materials Our evaluation set comprised a single-modality
MRI brain tumor dataset (BraTS 2020) [11–13] and a multimodality PET-CT non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
dataset. These two datasets both contain tumors surrounded
by anatomical structures that occlude the tumor. The BraTS
2020 contains 4 different scans for 369 patients, and we
employed the post-contrast T1-weighted (T1Gd) volume for
the optimal visualization of brain tumors. The tumor core
which entails the enhancing tumor, the necrotic and, nonenhancing part of the tumor, is considered to be the ROI
[11]. The PET-CT dataset is a private dataset that comprises
90 PET-CT scans from the Department of Nuclear Medicine,
Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center, Shanghai, China.
PET offers functional insights, whereas CT provides anatomical details. The interslice distance for PET and CT volumes
was 5 mm and the z-resolution was 172 slices. The original
CT and PET in-plane image sizes were 512 × 512 pixels, at
in-plane resolutions between 0.9 × 0.9 and 1.3 × 1.3 mm.
The tumor contours were manually delineated by expert clinicians. Note that our attention-based visual emphasis method
didn’t rely on voxel-wise annotation for training and instead,
we derived image-wise classification labels (whether this
slice contains a tumor or not) from the segmentation labels.
In both datasets, the horizontal slices containing ROIs were
extracted as positive samples and the same number of slices
without tumors were randomly selected as negative samples to reduce the class imbalance problem. The input size
of the VGG16 classification backbone was 224 × 224. All
slices were center-cropped to 224 × 224 pixels. We chose
to crop instead of resizing the image since the surrounding
area of images of our datasets is generally empty/no meaningful information. We employed online data augmentation
by randomly flipping (horizontal or vertical, flip rate  0.5)
the training images.

Implementation We trained our attention model for 100
epochs with a batch size of 1. We employed the stochastic
gradient descent (SGD) optimizer to minimize the loss mentioned before. The initial learning rate was 0.0001 which
decreased by 0.5 after 10 epochs. We used fivefold crossvalidation to evaluate the capabilities of our F-CAM. We
ensured that training and test sets of each fold did not contain
slices from the same patient. All networks were implemented
with PyTorch on a 12 GB NVIDIA TITAN V GPU. Training took 6 h for each fold. For visualization, the real-time
rendering was implemented within an open-source interactive visualization development framework—Voreen [47]. All
renderings in the comparative experiments were conducted
on a PC with NVIDIA GeForce GTX 980 Ti.
Evaluation metrics We evaluated our attention-driven
visual emphasis method through qualitative assessment of
the two datasets. We further conducted ablation experiments of our F-CAM and attention compositing on the
NSCLC dataset. We measured the commonly used GTknown ROIs localization accuracy of different attention
methods following CAM [31] and ACoL [48]. Briefly, we
thresholded the attention map at 0.5 to generate a binary
foreground/background map as in ACoL [48]. Then the tight
bounding box of the largest connected components of the
foreground map was matched against the ground truth (GT)
using intersection over union (IoU). The attention bounding box was considered a true positive if the IoU passed
the IoU thresholds and the localization accuracy at different IoU thresholds was calculated as in AGXNet [49]. We
also evaluated our attention compositing through qualitative
assessment.

4.2 Ablation experiments
4.2.1 Attention for weakly supervised ROIs localization
We compared our F-CAM with three baselines for the ROIs
localization: (i) Grad-CAM [8] which is the backbone of
our method, (ii) adversarial complementary learning [48]
(ACoL) which interactively masks out the attended regions
on attention maps to guide the model to get a more accurate
and complete attention estimation, and (iii) guided attention
inference network [51] (GAIN) which in turn interactively
masked out the attended regions on raw images.
The GT-known localization accuracy results are shown in
Table 1 using three IoU threshold variations. A higher IoU
threshold means that the evaluation requires more accurate
ROIs localization. Our F-CAM performed better when the
IoU threshold was higher, which suggests that our F-CAM
locates the ROIs more precisely compared to other methods.
This was caused by the fact that with medical images where
the ROIs, such as tumors, are relatively small compared to
those in natural images, the Grad-CAM/ACoL/GAIN tend
to “over-segment” the ROIs. Our self-guided surface loss
penalizes the model if the attention regions are oversized
to constrain it in medical imaging. We also provided qualitative ROI localization results of 3 NSCLC cases with
different weakly-supervised ROIs localization methods in the
Appendix.
We examined the impact of our attention compositing module. Two PET-CT case studies are presented in 2 rows,
respectively, in Fig. 7. The tumor is occluded by the CT lung
structures in all the cases and cannot be accurately visualized using conventional DVR in Fig. 7a. Figure 7b shows the
importance-driven visualization with manual segmentation
labels. Figure 7c shows the importance-driven visualization
results with the manual segmentation label replaced by an
automated attention map. Figure 7d shows the results of our
attention-driven visual emphasis where attention compositing is then applied instead of importance compositing. We
observe that the occluding CT structures are suppressed to
guarantee the visibility of the tumor in Fig. 7b, c, d. However, some non-occluding contexts surrounding the ROIs are
also removed as indicated with red arrows in Fig. 7c. This
was caused by the fact that the ROIs of medical images are
typically small and the attention map encompasses a “larger”
region than the actual ROI due to the weak guidance from
the classification label rather than the fine-grained segmentation label, resulting in the removal of non-occluding CT
structures. During the attention compositing process, we further combine the information from TF specification to filter
out the false positive regions closely surrounding the ROIs
as shown in Fig. 7d. We noted that those cavity regions were
filled with the CT context since there were no tumors.
4.3 Discussion
A key feature of our attention-driven visual emphasis method
is the automatic weakly supervised ROIs localization. Additional TF attributes, e.g., size of the ROIs, were developed
to better localize the ROI among the volumetric data. Our
method provided a new way to automatically locate the ROIs
using a deep learning-based attention estimation model FCAM. It can focus more closely on the ROIs compared to
other attention estimation models, as demonstrated in our
experiments.

Another key feature of our attention-driven visual emphasis method is a new attention compositing module. Although
its localization accuracy is not as precise as of the manual segmentation labels, the attention map and intensity attribute of
each voxel can complete each other. The attention map can be
employed to filter out the unrelated false positive structures
that are difficult to differentiate using 1D TFs, such as the
heart in PET and the vessels in MRI. The TFs are also used
to filter out the false positive regions on the attention maps
which are contextual information but exhibit high attention
values. Besides, our attention compositing ensures a viewdependent visual emphasis solution which is essential for 3D
exploration.
In all visualizations, we employed a simple 1D intensity
TF that is most commonly used in medical DVR. With medical data, it is expected that a TF is designed specifically
for the medical imaging modality (e.g., PET/CT and MRI
require different TFs) as well as dependent on the disease
(i.e., lung cancer PET-CT will need a different TF compared
to liver cancer PET-CT). Despite the use of 1D TFs, our
attention-driven visual emphasis method is not constrained
to it and any multi-dimensional TF can also be employed to
complement the attention map to localize the ROIs.

other attention estimation models, our F-CAM was more
capable of focusing on the ROIs. Our attention composting

Ablation results of the impact of the w in Eq. (3) are shown
in Table 2. We observed that the attention localization accuracy increased as w increased from 1 to 100, and plateaued
when w reached 200. This is expected, as higher w results
in a more polarized soft binarization operation in Eq. (3),
thereby reducing the low-value attention regions surrounding the ROIs. This aligns with the characteristics of medical
images where ROIs are relatively small.
Ablation results of the impact of the λ in loss function are
shown in Table 3. It was observed that the average attention
localization accuracy increased when λ (the weight of the
self-guided size constraint loss) increased from 0.25 to 0.5,
which is indicative of the effectiveness of the self-guided size
constraint loss in model training. The highest accuracy was
achieved when the classification loss and the self-guided size
constraint loss were balanced equally at λ  0.5.
We also considered 3D networks to estimate the attention maps for ROIs localization. However, the localization
accuracy was ~ 4% lower with several shortcomings when
compared to the 2D F-CAM: (1) Class Imbalance. In the
localization of tumors, the issue of class imbalance becomes
more pronounced in 3D networks. Tumors are often small
compared to the entire 3D image volume. This results in
a significant imbalance between positive (tumor) sub-cubes
and negative (non-tumor) sub-cubes when employing a 3D
classification network for attention-based tumor localization.
(2) Lack of Pretrained Weights. While 2D networks benefit
significantly from pretrained models like ImageNet, similar pretrained weights for 3D networks are scarce. This is
detrimental for medical domains with limited data, and (3)
Memory Requirements. The 3D network requires substantially more memory (~ 19 GB) than its 2D counterpart (~
6 GB) due to the additional dimension in the data, which
leads to a substantial increase in the computational resources
needed for training and inference. Given these constraints,
the 2D F-CAM was selected in this manuscript.
