Abstract
This thesis discusses wavelet-based volume visualization and compression. Volume
rendering is an important area in the field of scientific visualization, and it has become
the most useful technique in medical imaging. With the rapid progress in medical
techniques, more and more high-resolution 3D medical data are generated. Unfortunately,
the sizes of these 3D data are usually beyond the ability of most of today's
today’s computers and
networks. To effectively process these data, compression is needed.
A new lossless 3D wavelet-based volume compression algorithm is presented. This
algorithm is a 3D extension of SPIHT algorithm in 2D image compression, and it has the
property of embedded quantization. The algorithm is combined with a scan-converting
volume rendering algorithm to achieve interactive visualization in medical imaging.
In order to process very high-resolution 3D medical data in a hierarchical way, a
"lifting-up"
“lifting-up” scheme is introduced. This scheme sorts the wavelet coefficients not only
according to their values, but also according to their spatial hierarchy in the
multiresolutionally decomposed subbands. The wavelet coefficients with the lowest
resolution are encoded/decoded first. Then, the coefficients at the next resolution level
are transmitted. The refining process continues until all the coefficients at the finest
resolution level are encoded/decoded. The performance of the new algorithm is
presented.

INTRODUCTION
In the field of visualization, we often deal with volumetric data. Volumetric data sets are
being used in an increasing number of engineering and scientific applications, such as
medical imaging, simulations in computational fluid dynamics, material sciences, and so
on. Volume rendering has been historically a subfield of computer graphics research.
Since it was introduced by Levoy [24],
[24], many algorithms have been developed to generate
new visualization features and to improve image qualities and computational efficiency.
The size of volume data has become very large for some high resolution data sets and
the object features to be visualized have also become increasingly complex. The
anatomical volume dataset of the Visible Man [32]
[32] has reached 15GB.
15GB. This is considered
to be huge even for today's
today’s super computers. It is still difficult to load such a data set into
the memory of a commonly available workstation and process it for visualization. A
feasible solution to this problem is to represent the data in a multiresolution form,
possibly with compression as well. Effective compression algorithms can significantly
reduce the size of a volume data set. Furthermore, researchers are looking at hierarchical
methods such that the users can initially render a rough image with small computational
effort in terms of time and space requirements, and then refine the image adaptively with
increased quality as more information becomes available.

1.2 Multiresolution
Multiresolution Representation
In the last two decades, multiresolution representation has been one of the most
actively researched topics in image processing, pattern recognition, computer vision, and
visualization. According to the research in psychophysics and the physiology of human
vision, there is consistent evidence showing that the retinal image is decomposed into
several spatially oriented frequency channels [30].
[30]. A multiresolution representation
provides a simple hierarchical framework for interpreting the image information. There
are several important multiresolution decomposition models. We will discuss three major
methods in the following: Windowed Fourier Transform, Laplacian Pyramid, and
Wavelet Transform.
1.2.1
1.2.1 Windowed
Windowed Fourier
Fourier Transform
Transform
In the traditional Fourier transform, each coefficient is an integral covering the entire
spatial/time domain, thus making the identification of local properties difficult. Unlike
the traditional Fourier Transform, the Windowed Fourier Transform can localize the
frequency information in the spatial domain by using a window function. The spatial
window g(x), used by Gabor [16],
[16], is the standard Gaussian distribution function. The
main drawback of a Windowed Fourier Transform is that the spatial and frequency
domain resolution is constant. If the signal has features at very different scales, it is
difficult to define an optimal resolution for the analysis. If a fixed window size is given,
we will not be able to analyze both the fine and the large structures.

1.2.2
1.2.2 Laplacian Pyramid
The Laplacian Pyramid Scheme [7] is an important early work in multiresolution
decomposition in the area of image processing before the rapid development of wavelet
transformations started in the late 1980s.
1980s. The idea of the Laplacian Pyramid Scheme is to
remove the high correlation and redundancy existing between neighboring pixels at
different scales, in order to achieve compression. A shortcoming of this scheme is the
oversampled information contained in the Laplacian pyramid at each level (except the
lowest resolution level).
1.2.3
1.2.3 Wavelet Transform
As mentioned previously, the Windowed Fourier Transform has difficulty analyzing
both small and large structures because of the constant resolution in both the spatial and
frequency domains. In order to see both the "forest"
“forest” and the "tree"
“tree” clearly, Marlet [18]
introduced the formal wavelet transform to image analysis by decomposing a signal into
a family of base functions, which are the translation and dilation of a unique base
wavelet. The dilation of a base wavelet varies the resolution in both the spatial and
frequency domains. Furthermore, when orthonormal base functions are used, the
oversampling problem associated with the Laplacian Pyramid scheme is eliminated.

1.3
1.3 Progressive Compression and Transmission
Data in volume form could consume an extraordinary amount of storage space. For
efficient storage and transmission of such data, a compression algorithm is imperative.
Basically, the algorithms that compress 3D volumes always originate from 2D image
compression methods. Due to the intrinsic hierarchical nature of the wavelet
mutiresolution representation of images, several new compression algorithms have been
proposed for progressive coding/decoding of wavelet transform coefficients.
A
particularly interesting one called embedded quantization was proposed by Shapiro in
[37].
[37]. Shapiro introduced a new zerotree structure for coding of transform coefficients.
Said and Pearlman [36] improved the original zerotree structure by adopting a new
structure called set partitioning in hierarchical trees. A fundamental property of
embedded quantization is to organize the transform coefficients in the order of their
magnitudes. Furthermore, more significant binary bits precede less significant bits for all
coefficients in the coding stream. This leads to an extremely flexible quantization
capability with a natural support for progressive transmission. To extend the original 2D
images embedded quantization algorithm to a 3D volume, there are several technical
problems to be solved, which will be addressed in Chapter 4.

Scalable compression refers to the generation o f a bit-stream which contains
embedded subsets, each representing an efficient compression of the original image at a
reduced resolution or increased distortion. A key advantage of scalable compression is
that the target bit-rate or reconstruction resolution need not be known at the time of
compression. Another advantage of practical significance is that the image need not be
compressed multiple times in order to achieve a target bit-rate, as is common with the
existing JPEG compression standard. Rather than focusing on generating a single scalable
bit-stream to represent the entire image, EBCOT partitions each subband into relatively
small blocks of samples, and generates a separate highly scalable bit-stream to represent
each so-called code-block. The algorithm exhibits state-of-the-art compression
performance while producing a bit-stream with an unprecedented feature set, including
resolution and SNR scalability together with a random access property. The algorithm
has modest complexity and is extremely well suited to applications involving remote
browsing of large compressed images.

computational complexity in the set partitioning because the more zerotrees that exists,
the less bit we need in transmitting the set partitioning information to the decoder.

Chapter 7
CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Based on the extensive exploration and successful applications of wavelet theories in
image processing, especially in the area of image compression, we believe that there is a
great potential for wavelet theory to be applied to 3D volume compression and
visualization. In this thesis, we developed a volume compression and visualization
system for studies in this direction.

7.2 Future
The wavelet-based volume compression and visualization algorithms presented in this
thesis provide a solid base for further research and experimentation in the following
areas:
•
The volume visualization is performed after the volume is fully recovered from the
wavelet coefficients by the inverse wavelet transform. Theoretically, we can get
better compression ratio of the volume data if we decompose the volume to deeper
levels. A four level wavelet decomposed volume can definitely achieve better
compression ratio than a two level wavelet decomposed volume. However, the
computational effort of the inverse wavelet transform is proportional to the
decomposition level too. If we could meaningfully visualize the volume directly from
the wavelet transform space, we would reduce the processing time significantly.
•
The Volume rendering technique we used is a parallel scan-converting volume
rendering algorithm. It maps slices of the volume to the image plane in the order of
the projected ray. Nevertheless, the refinement of the wavelet coefficients is not in the
order of the projected ray. If we could find a more flexible algorithm that can decode
the code stream according to the order of the projected ray, the rendered image can be
generated much faster.
•
The data structure of hierarchical volume compression used in our thesis is still not
sufficient to support interactive visualization of high resolution volumes, such as the
Visible Man and Woman. Further research may investigate a more flexible and
