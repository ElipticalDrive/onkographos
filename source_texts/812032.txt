Abstract. Neural implicit surfaces can be used to recover accurate 3D
geometry from imperfect point clouds. In this work, we show that stateof-the-art techniques work by minimizing an approximation of a onesided Chamfer distance. This shape metric is not symmetric, as it only
ensures that the point cloud is near the surface but not vice versa. As
a consequence, existing methods can produce inaccurate reconstructions
with spurious surfaces. Although one approach against spurious surfaces
has been widely used in the literature, we theoretically and experimentally show that it is equivalent to regularizing the surface area, resulting
in over-smoothing. As a more appealing alternative, we propose DiffCD,
a novel loss function corresponding to the symmetric Chamfer distance.
In contrast to previous work, DiffCD also assures that the surface is near
the point cloud, which eliminates spurious surfaces without the need for
additional regularization. We experimentally show that DiffCD reliably
recovers a high degree of shape detail, substantially outperforming existing work across varying surface complexity and noise levels.

Introduction
Neural implicit surfaces parametrize a 3D surface as the zero-level set of a scalar
field predicted by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) f (θ, · ) : R3 → R:
\begin {aligned} \label {eq:zero_level} \surface _\theta = \{x : f(\theta , x) = 0\}, \end {aligned}
(1)
where θ are the network parameters. Neural implicit surfaces are naturally suited
for surface reconstruction. In contrast to meshes, they are continuous everywhere
and can represent shapes of arbitrary topology; in contrast to point clouds, they
provide surface normal information and enable occlusion reasoning. The implicit
surface representation is also malleable and allows for complex shape manipulations, such as topological changes [34]. Yet, as Fig. 1 illustrates, optimizing over
such a flexible parametrization of the surface also comes with some difficulties.

State-of-the-art techniques [5, 15, 30] struggle to accurately recover surfaces in
practical scenarios, especially when the sensor data is incomplete and noisy.
Problem setup. We consider the problem of reconstructing an underlying
surface S from an unoriented, sparse and possibly noisy point cloud P = {x̃i }ni=1 ,
contained in a bounding region Ω. Our goal is to estimate the network parameters
θ such that the zero-level set Sθ approximates the true surface, i.e. Sθ ≈ S.
We further require that f (θ, · ) approximates a Signed Distance Field (SDF),
characterized by the eikonal equation:
\begin {aligned} \label {eq:eikonal_eq} \|\grad f(\theta , x)\| = 1. \end {aligned}
(2)

This ensures that the absolute value of the field at any point equals the distance
to the surface. Training f (θ, · ) to satisfy the eikonal equation adds regularity
to the optimization problem by preventing the degenerate solution f (θ, · ) = 0.
Contributions. Firstly, we show that the widely used approach of training the
network output to vanish on the point cloud [6, 15, 30, 32, 35] corresponds to
minimizing only one side of the Chamfer distance – the average distance from
the input point cloud to the implicit surface. We will refer to this quantity as the
points-to-surface Chamfer distance. Although minimizing the one-sided Chamfer
distance works in some scenarios, it may lead to spurious surfaces, see Fig. 1b.
Spurious surfaces are large surface chunks located in the “blind spot” of the
one-sided Chamfer distance. This observation suggests that minimizing the full,
symmetric Chamfer distance could lead to a more accurate surface reconstruction. As a second contribution, we therefore develop a novel loss term corresponding to the “missing” one-sided surface-to-points Chamfer distance. This
term measures the average distance from the implicit surface to the point cloud.
Our proposed loss function combines the two one-sided Chamfer distances into
a single loss function, the Diff erentiable Chamfer Distance (DiffCD). By minimizing the symmetric Chamfer distance, DiffCD reliably mitigates the issue of
spurious surfaces, achieving a substantial improvement in surface reconstruction
accuracy across various types of shapes and noise levels.
As our third contribution, we theoretically justify DiffCD against a popular alternative for mitigating spurious surfaces, the off-surface loss adopted by
SIREN [30]. We prove that minimizing the SIREN loss term is equivalent to regularizing the surface area of the implicit surface. This implies that the SIREN
approach mitigates spurious surfaces at the cost of shrinking the surface area,
resulting in over-smoothing, or even to the surface disappearing.
Overall, our work provides novel theoretical insights into surface reconstruction with neural implicit surfaces. Building on these insights, we develop DiffCD.
It minimizes the symmetric Chamfer distance between the point cloud and the
implicit surface, leading to a more reliable surface reconstruction.

Related work
The two dominant categories of techniques for reconstructing neural implicit
surfaces from point clouds are supervised and optimization-based. Here, we provide a condensed summary of the most relevant previous work. For a broader
overview, we refer interested readers to the recent survey by Sulzer et al. [31].
Supervised methods. Supervised methods approach the surface reconstruction problem by learning from a large collection of point clouds with corresponding ground-truth meshes. Supervised methods typically extract feature vectors
from the point cloud, which are used to predict either occupancy or a signed distance value. Points2Surf [12] employs PointNet [29] and regresses the sign and
distance separately by combining a fine-grained local descriptor with a coarsegrained global descriptor. POCO [7] instead uses point cloud convolutions [8].
Neural Kernel Surface Reconstruction (NKSR) [18] defines the surface as the

zero-level set of a Neural Kernel Field [33]. It relies on PointNet-like [29] encoder to extract a voxel hierarchy of kernel features from the point cloud.
Optimization-based methods. More relevant to our work are optimizationbased methods [3–5, 23, 27, 30]. They reconstruct the surface by formulating
a loss function, which is optimized from scratch at inference time. IGR [15],
Neural-Pull [5] and SIREN [30] all employ different loss functions to ensure that
the zero-level goes through the point cloud points. Leveraging normals in the input significantly simplifies the problem, leading to more accurate reconstruction
with classical [19] and neural-based [4, 15, 28, 30] formulations alike. Surface
reconstruction without normals – the scenario addressed in this work – is highly
ill-posed. It inspires interest in regularization techniques, which typically encourage smooth solutions by means of additional loss terms. Several methods
penalize deviations from the SDF properties, most notably the eikonal equation
[15, 30]. A related property, which can be derived from the eikonal equation,
is that the gradient of an SDF lies in the nullspace of the Hessian [25]. This
property has been used to motivate regularizing the determinant of the Hessian
[35] and the norm of the Hessian-gradient product [32]. Level-set alignment [24]
also makes use of this property indirectly by regularizing the misalignment of
the gradient in the query points and their level-set projections. DiGs [6] goes
beyond SDF properties and minimizes the divergence of the scalar field, which
increases surface smoothness by encouraging flat level sets. PHASE [23] and, as
we will see in Sec. 5, SIREN [30] both include loss functions that regularize the
surface area of the implicit surface. In contrast to regularization, our loss term
is geometric in nature. In fact, we find that tuning the weight of the eikonal loss
is sufficient for adapting our method to various noise levels.
Chamfer distance for shape reconstruction. Point2Mesh [16] and SAP
[28] also develop differentiable variants of the symmetric Chamfer distance, but
use an intermediate mesh representation. In contrast, our DiffCD is defined directly in terms of the implicit surface.

Background
3.1
Chamfer distance
As the base of our analysis and our novel loss formulation, we leverage a widely
used metric in shape reconstruction, the Chamfer distance1 [13]. The Chamfer
distance between two point sets, P1 and P2 , is given by:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:sym-p2p} \symchamfer (\pointcloud _1, \pointcloud _2) = \frac {1}{2}\Big (\chamfer (\pointcloud _1, \pointcloud _2) + \chamfer (\pointcloud _2, \pointcloud _1)\Big ). \end {aligned}
(3)
The term CD→ (P1 , P2 ) is the one-sided Chamfer distance from P1 to P2 . It
simply measures the average distance between each point in P1 and its corre1
The Chamfer distance is not a distance metric in the mathematical sense, as it does
not satisfy the triangle inequality; it is an average over point-to-set distances.

\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:p2p} \chamfer (\pointcloud _1, \pointcloud _2) = \frac {1}{|\pointcloud _1|}\sum _{x_1 \in \pointcloud _1} \underset {x_2 \in \pointcloud _2}{\text {min}} \| x_1 - x_2 \|, \end {aligned}
(4)

one-sided Chamfer distance can be a reliable similarity metric if P1 and P2 have
similar density and size, it can be misleadingly small even in cases of substantial
shape mismatch. Notably, points in P2 which are not the nearest neighbor of
any point in P1 will have no impact on the value of CD→ (P1 , P2 ).
3.2
IGR and spurious surfaces
Let us recall implicit geometric regularization (IGR) [15], which serves as the
base loss function for several follow-up works [6, 30, 32, 35]. In the normal-free
unoriented setting, IGR tackles the surface reconstruction problem by minimizing the following loss function:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:igr-loss} \IGRloss (\theta ) \!=\! \frac {1}{n}\sum _{i = 1}^{n}|f(\theta , \tilde x_i)| + \lambda \Eikonalloss (\theta ). \end {aligned}
(5)
The first term in Eq. (5) ensures that the network output is close to 0 on the
points in the point cloud, x̃i ∈ P. The second term is the eikonal loss. It ensures
that f (θ, · ) approximates an SDF by penalizing deviations from the eikonal
equation in Eq. (2):
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:eikonal-loss} \Eikonalloss (\theta ) = \frac {1}{|\globalsamples |}\sum _{\globalsample \in \globalsamples }(\|\grad f(\theta , \globalsample )\| \!-\! 1)^2. \end {aligned}
(6)
Ps is the set of sample points at which the eikonal loss should be computed. The
hyperparameter λ in Eq. (5) is the eikonal weight and it determines the trade-off
between the two terms. As we discuss in Appendix E, the value of λ can have a
significant impact on the reconstructed surface.
IGR approximates a one-sided Chamfer distance. Using the fact that
f (θ, · ) will approximate the signed distance field of Sθ , we can adopt a geometric
interpretation of Eq. (5). Namely, when the eikonal loss is small, each term in
the sum will approximate a distance to the implicit surface:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:approx-sdf-eikonal} |f(\theta , \tilde x)|\approx \min _{x\in \surface _\theta }\|\tilde x - x\| \text {, when } \Eikonalloss (\theta )\approx 0. \end {aligned}
(7)
Plugging this approximation back into Eq. (5), it becomes clear that LIGR in
turn approximates the average distance between the point cloud and the implicit
surface, which we can identify as a one-sided Chamfer distance (cf . Sec. 3.2).
That is, we have:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:IGRloss-exact-sdf} \IGRloss (\theta ) \approx \frac {1}{n}\sum _{i = 1}^{n}\min _{x\in \surface _\theta }\|\tilde x_i - x\|^p = \chamfer (\pointcloud , \surface _\theta ) \text {, when } \Eikonalloss (\theta )\approx 0. \end {aligned}
(8)

Spurious surfaces. A critical shortcoming of minimizing only one side of the
Chamfer distance is that it cannot account for the surface parts far away from
every point in P. As illustrated in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2, this leads to spurious surfaces
in the reconstructions produced by IGR. Specifically, large blob-shaped artifacts
tend to accumulate near repeated thin structures and in the areas where the
point cloud has missing coverage. Introduced next, our approach addresses this
issue.

DiffCD: A Symmetric Differentiable Chamfer Distance
In order to resolve the issue of spurious surfaces, we propose extending the onesided Chamfer distance in Eq. (5) to a full symmetric Chamfer distance. By doing
so, we explicitly account for the spurious surfaces with the loss function. To this
end, we add the “missing” Chamfer term, namely the surface-to-points Chamfer
distance. The surface-to-points Chamfer distance is the average distance from
the surface to the point cloud:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:s2p-loss} \chamfer (\surface _\theta , \pointcloud ) = \frac {1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^n\min _{j=1,\ldots ,N}\|x_i(\theta ) - \tilde x_j\|, \end {aligned}
(9)

where xi (θ) are points sampled uniformly from the implicit surface Sθ . Note that
Eq. (9) is an explicit loss; it is defined geometrically and does not assume f (θ, · )
to be an SDF. Below, we propose an efficient sampling procedure and a method
for computing the gradients of the surface-to-points loss.
Fig. 2 illustrates a 2D example of jointly minimizing both sides of the Chamfer distance. The sample points xi (θ) depend on the network parameters θ and
are constrained to lie on the implicit surface. By minimizing Eq. (9), the gradient descent will tend to move each surface point xi (θ) toward the closest point
in the point cloud. This surface pulling toward the point cloud will eventually
remove any spurious surfaces by molding them into the existing surface.
Having defined the surface-to-points loss, we introduce Differentiable Chamfer Distance, or DiffCD. Similar to the Chamfer distance for point clouds, we
take the average of the two one-sided Chamfer distances to obtain:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:diffcd-loss} \DiffCDloss (\theta ) &= \frac {1}{2}\bigg (\frac {1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^n|f(\theta , \tilde x_i)| + \chamfer (\surface _\theta , \pointcloud )\bigg ) +\lambda \Eikonalloss (\theta ). \end {aligned}
(10)
Using Eq. (8), we can confirm that Eq. (10) indeed approximates the symmetric
Chamfer distance when the eikonal loss is small:
\begin {aligned} \DiffCDloss (\theta )\approx \symchamfer (\surface _\theta , \pointcloud )\text {, when } \Eikonalloss (\theta )\approx 0. \end {aligned}
(11)
That is, minimizing our proposed loss function Eq. (10) implicitly minimizes the
fully symmetric Chamfer distance between the point cloud and the surface, as
opposed to just the one-sided Chamfer distance.
4.1
Computing gradients
In order to minimize Eq. (9) with backpropagation, we need a method for computing surface point derivatives, ∇θ xi (θ), where θ ∈ R is a scalar network parameter. For this, we use the method for controlling level sets proposed by Atzmon
et al. [2]. The key idea is to make use of the level set equation f (θ, xi (θ)) = 0,
which must hold for all θ. Differentiating both sides of the level set equation
w.r.t. θ and rearranging terms yields the constraint:
\begin {aligned} g^T\der _\theta x_i(\theta ) = -f_\theta , \end {aligned}
(12)
where we have used the shorthands g := ∇x f (θ, xi (θ)) and fθ := ∇θ f (θ, xi (θ)).
That is, the gradient ∇θ xi (θ) lies on a plane with normal g at a distance |fθ |/∥g∥
from the origin. So we can set ∇θ xi (θ) = ξ − fθ g/∥g∥2 , where ξ ∈ R3 is any
vector orthogonal to g.
Similarly to Atzmon et al. [2], we take the minimum norm solution, ξ = 0.
While this choice may seem arbitrary, we find that it has a significant advantage
when combined with gradient descent. Namely, it will naturally lead to linearly
interpolating surfaces when the input point cloud is sparse. To see this, we note
that the derivative of each term in Eq. (9) is:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:sample-distance-grad} \der _\theta \|x_i(\theta ) - \tilde x\| = -f_\theta \frac {(x_i(\theta ) - \tilde x)^Tg}{\|x_i(\theta ) - \tilde x\|\|g\|^2}. \end {aligned}
(13)

The SIREN loss – a surface area regularizer
The off-surface term from SIREN [30] is a widely used alternative to mitigating
spurious surfaces. Here, we will examine this loss term and establish its link to the
surface area, which explain some undersirable side-effects of this regularization
in surface reconstruction.
Let us begin by recalling the regularization term from SIREN [30]:2
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:siren-term} \SSAloss ^\alpha (\theta ) = \frac {\alpha }{2}\int _{\Omega }e^{-\alpha |f(\theta , x)|}\diff x, \end {aligned}
(16)
which penalizes having distance field values close to zero uniformly in Ω. Eq. (16)
is commonly referred to as an off-surface or non-manifold loss [6, 35]. However,
our upcoming analysis reveals that Lα
SSA , in fact, behaves as a surface area
regularizer, when it is combined with the eikonal loss. For this reason, we opt to
refer to it as the SIREN Surface Area (SSA) loss. The full SIREN loss without
normals is given by:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:siren-loss} \IGRSSAloss ^\alpha (\theta ) = \frac {1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^n|f(\theta , x_i)| + \lambda \Eikonalloss (\theta ) + \tilde \mu \SSAloss ^\alpha (\theta ), \end {aligned}
(17)
where µ̃ is a weighting parameter. To ensure consistency of the weighting parameter with SIREN [30], we will from now on work with the rescaled weight
µ = µ̃|Ω|α/2.

close to 1 near the surface, and close to 0 otherwise, with a cut-off distance at
roughly 1/α from the surface. We might therefore already expect the integral

6.1
Implementation details
We compare DiffCD against the loss functions from IGR [15], Neural-Pull [5]
and SIREN [30] (without the normal alignment term). In order to ensure a
fair comparison between loss functions, we implement all methods in a common
framework using JAX [9]. We use the same MLP architecture and training procedure for all methods. See Appendix A for more details. We also compare our
approach with supervised methods Points2Surf [12], POCO [7] and NKSR [18].
For Points2Surf and POCO, we use the checkpoints trained on the ABC dataset
[21]. For NKSR, we also use the model trained on ABC. Since this model requires
normals as input, we use the normal-free model trained on ScanNet [11] in order
to estimate surface normals. We note that this leaves NKSR at a disadvantage
compared to the other supervised methods, which were trained fully on ABC.
We will denote this variant by NKSR† in order to emphasize the distinction.
Parameter settings. As our base setting, we use λ = 0.1, as in IGR [15].
We use µ = 0.033 to keep the same ratio between the point cloud loss and the
SSA loss as in SIREN [30]. We also evaluate SIREN with µ = 0.33 since we
find that the original value is too small to remove the most prominent spurious
surfaces (see Sec. 6.3). As we demonstrate in Appendix E, using larger λ produces

7
Conclusion
We presented DiffCD, a method for fitting neural implicit surfaces to sparse and
noisy point clouds. We analyzed existing methods and showed that they tend
to minimize only one side of the Chamfer distance, which leaves a blind spot
for spurious surfaces. We also showed that the SIREN approach to mitigating
spurious surfaces comes at the cost of regularizing the surface area. By instead
minimizing the full symmetric Chamfer distance, our method explicitly mitigates
spurious surfaces without being coupled with additional regularization.
Limitations and future work. While DiffCD can be tuned to work well
across various noise levels, it does not automatically adapt in cases where the
noise level is unknown or varies significantly across the shape. This stands in
contrast to supervised methods, which can handle multiple noise levels using a
single model, provided the training data is available. A promising future direction
would be to extend optimization-based methods with learned surface priors,
which could help in guiding the optimization process. We also hope that our
analysis of the SIREN [30] loss term in Sec. 5 can be extended to other kinds of
sampling-based surface integrals for implicit surfaces.

Additional implementation details
Network architecture and training. We use the same coordinate MLP
f (θ, · ) for all loss functions, consisting of 8 fully connected layers with 256dimensional features and softplus activations, using geometric initialization as
introduced by Gropp et al. [15]. We also use a skip connection from the input
to the 4th layer. We train each method for 40K iterations of Adam [20] with
a learning rate of 10−3 for the “No Noise” and “Medium Noise” settings, and
5×10−5 for the “Max Noise” setting. We use cosine annealing and a linear warmup spanning the first 1000 iterations.
Batch sampling. Each training batch consists of 5K points x̃i sampled uniformly from the input point cloud P. These points are used to compute the
points-to-surface loss. For DiffCD, we sample an additional 5K surface points
xi (θ) from Sθ following our sampling scheme (cf . Sec. 4). For the eikonal loss,
we use a mixed sampling scheme similar to Gropp et al. [15]. Specifically, we
sample 625 “global” points uniformly from Ω, along with 5K “local” points obtained from a normal distribution centered at each x̃i . The standard deviation
of each normal distribution is determined by the distance from x̃i to its 50th
nearest neighbor in P. Inspired by the implementation of Baorui et al. [5], we
scale each standard deviation by a factor of 0.2, as we find that it tends to improve surface detail. For Neural-Pull [5], we only use the 5K local sample points

as in the original implementation. For computing L̂α

uniformly from Ω following the original paper.
Normalization. Before training, we follow Baorui et al. [5] and compute a

Parameter ablation and eikonal weight smoothing
We find that the value of the eikonal weight, λ, has a significant impact on
the overall smoothness of reconstructed shapes. In particular, larger λ tends to
produce smoother shapes. This is likely due to the fact that non-flat surfaces have
more non-differentiable points in the distance field, where the gradient changes
direction abruptly. Such points are difficult to approximate without violating the
eikonal constraint, and they are therefore naturally avoided by the optimization
process if λ is sufficiently large. We demonstrate this effect qualitatively in Fig. 2
the noisy scenarios. When λ is too small, the field collapses to the degenerate
solution f (θ, · ) = 0. As λ increases, a distinct 0-level set eventually appears.
However, the reconstructed shapes are initially “wrinkly”, since nothing prevents
the surface from overfitting to the noise. With larger λ, the surfaces become
smoother, and the gradient norms concentrate around the value 1. On the other
hand, setting λ too large results in over-smoothing, so the optimal value for any
given shape will lie somewhere in between. We can also note that, in contrast to
LSIREN , increasing λ does not lead to the surface disappearing.

Neural-Pull [5] tackles the surface reconstruction problem by defining a pulling
operation:
\begin {aligned} \label {eqn:pull-operation} \pull {\theta }{x} = x - f(\theta , x)\frac {\grad f(\theta , x)}{\|\grad f(\theta , x)\|} \end {aligned}
(8)

points, xs ∈ Ps , and minimize the average distance2 between the pulled location
