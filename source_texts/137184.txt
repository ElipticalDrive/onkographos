# Neural Field Thermal Tomography: A Differentiable Physics Framework for Non-Destructive Evaluation

Tao Zhong1 , Yixun Hu1 , Dongzhe Zheng1 , Aditya Sood1 , Christine Allen-Blanchette1 1Princeton University {tzhong, ca15}@princeton.edu

# Abstract

Inverse problems for stiff parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs), such as the inverse heat conduction problem (IHCP), are severely ill-posed: the forward map rapidly damps high-frequency interior structure before it reaches the boundary. Soft-constrained physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), which embed the PDE as a residual penalty, suffer from gradient pathology in this regime and tend to fit boundary measurements while leaving the interior field essentially untouched. We propose Neural Field Thermal Tomography (NeFTY), a hardconstrained neural field framework for label-free three-dimensional inverse heat conduction. NeFTY represents the unknown diffusivity as a continuous coordinatebased neural network, and at every optimization step passes the candidate field through a differentiable implicit-Euler heat solver with harmonic-mean interface flux, so that the governing PDE holds exactly on the discretization rather than as a soft penalty. Adjoint gradients propagate the surface reconstruction error back to the network weights at solver-level memory cost, making test-time inversion tractable on a single GPU. Across synthetic 3D benchmarks, NeFTY substantially outperforms soft-constrained PINN variants and a voxel-grid baseline on label-free volumetric recovery, and it transfers to real thermography data, surpassing classical signal-processing baselines in both defect segmentation and depth estimation. Additional details at [cab-lab-princeton.github.io/nefty](https://cab-lab-princeton.github.io/nefty/).

# 1 Introduction

Inverse problems for stiff parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs) are central to scientific machine learning, yet they remain among the hardest to solve from observations alone. The forward heat operator is compact and strongly smoothing: high spatial frequencies in the interior are damped before they reach the boundary, so the map from internal material properties to surface measurements destroys much of the information needed to invert it [16, 19]. Soft physics-informed losses can fit boundary data without recovering the underlying field, because their gradients become poorly conditioned and dominated by the surface signal in this stiff regime [44, 55]. A complementary line of work in differentiable physics builds the discretized solver directly into the optimization loop, so that the governing equations hold as a hard constraint at every step rather than as a residual penalty in the objective [11, 23, 24, 37]. We apply this paradigm to the canonical case of the three-dimensional inverse heat conduction problem (IHCP).

Non-destructive testing (NDT) by pulsed thermography is the working example throughout (Figure 1\). A pulsed laser deposits energy on the specimen surface, and a high-speed camera records the subsequent surface temperature decay; subsurface delaminations, voids, and inclusions perturb the diffusive flux and leave faint thermal contrast on the boundary [28, 41, 46]. Reconstructing the volumetric thermal diffusivity field α(x, y, z) from these surface signals is the inverse heat conduction problem. Unlike hyperbolic ultrasonic or radar imaging [4, 54], the parabolic forward map damps

internal features rapidly with depth, so small surface perturbations can correspond to large interior variations and the inverse map becomes severely ill-posed [29, 43]. Existing approaches do not address this volumetric inversion well. Classical thermography pipelines, including Thermographic Signal Reconstruction (TSR) [49, 50], Pulsed Phase Thermography (PPT) [32], and the Virtual Wave Concept (VWC) [1, 3], transform the temporal data into domains where defect contrast is enhanced, but they reduce to 1D pixel-wise inversions that ignore lateral diffusion and bias estimates for shallow or low-aspect-ratio defects [42]. Soft-constrained physicsinformed neural networks (PINNs) target the volumetric problem in principle, yet they inherit the gradient pathology that limits these methods in stiff transient diffusion: the PDE residual is high and rugged, so the optimizer makes progress on the surface-data term while the diffusivity network saturates at a near-trivial field that explains the boundary measurements without recovering the bulk [55]. Supervised volumetric learning sidesteps these issues but requires 3D ground-truth labels that are unavailable in real NDT.





To this end, we introduce Neural Field Thermal Tomography (NeFTY), a hard-constrained neural field framework for label-free 3D inverse heat conduction. NeFTY represents the unknown diffusivity as a continuous coordinate-based neural network [34, 52] with positional encoding, frequency annealing, and a bounded output. At every optimization step, the candidate field is passed through a differentiable implicit-Euler heat solver with harmonic-mean interface flux, so the heat equation holds exactly on the discretization rather than as a soft penalty. Adjoint gradients propagate the surface reconstruction error back to the network weights at solver-level memory cost, making test-time inversion tractable on a single GPU. On synthetic homogeneous and layered settings, NeFTY reaches volumetric IoU of 0.45 and 0.37, while soft-constrained PINN variants and a voxel-grid baseline remain at or near zero, and it outperforms classical thermography baselines on real PVC thermal data in both 2D defect segmentation and 2.5D depth recovery.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

- We propose a hard-constrained neural field formulation for label-free 3D inverse heat conduction from surface-only thermal sequences, combining a continuous coordinate-based prior with a differentiable implicit heat solver and adjoint gradients.
- We give an empirical and theoretical account of why soft-constrained PINNs fail on stiff parabolic inverse problems, demonstrating across several modern PINN variants that low surface error does not imply correct volumetric recovery.
- We carry out a comprehensive evaluation that pairs synthetic 3D label-free reconstruction with real PVC thermal data, soft-constrained PINN baselines, frequency- and edge-domain diagnostics, component ablations, and a wall-clock and memory analysis of the adjoint solver.

# 2 Related Work

Thermographic Non-Destructive Testing traditionally extracts subsurface defect information through signal-processing transforms applied to surface temperature decays. Thermographic Signal Reconstruction (TSR) [49, 50] fits log-time polynomials to enhance defect contrast, Pulsed Phase Thermography (PPT) [9, 32] maps the decays into a frequency phase domain that suppresses noise and emissivity variations, and the Virtual Wave Concept (VWC) [1, 3, 48] deconvolves the diffusive forward map into a pseudo-wave for depth estimation. Quantitative-thermography refinements push these heuristics further with calibrated depth correlations [31, 42], but the underlying inverse mappings remain pixel-wise and amplify high-frequency measurement noise. In parallel, supervised convolutional pipelines based on U-Net [15, 36, 45, 51] learn defect masks directly from thermal sequences, and several recent surveys [28, 41, 46] catalogue this growing detection-only literature. Both lines stop short of the volumetric inverse problem: signal-processing transforms ignore lateral

heat flux, and supervised models require 3D diffusivity labels that are unavailable in real NDT. NeFTY recovers a full 3D diffusivity field directly from the surface sequence, without volumetric labels and without 1D pixel-wise approximations.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks embed governing equations as soft penalties alongside data terms in scientific machine learning. Standard PINNs [5, 29, 44] jointly minimize boundary fit and a PDE residual, and a series of variants attack the resulting optimization pathologies: separable architectures in SPINN [8] scale up collocation throughput, temporal-causality weighting in Causal-PINN [56] stabilizes long-horizon transients, and dual-cone gradient descent in DCGD [25] resolves residual-versus-data gradient conflicts. Diagnostic studies [20, 55] nonetheless trace recurring failures to the soft-penalty formulation itself, where stiff PDE residuals dominate the data signal and induce spectral bias against deep high-frequency features even with adaptive loss balancing such as GradNorm [7]. A complementary line replaces the penalty with a differentiable solver: discretize-then-optimize [37], differentiable PDE engines [2, 23, 24], and end-to-end differentiable physics for control and robotics [11, 12, 53, 61] all enforce the governing equations exactly at every optimization step. This solver-in-the-loop paradigm has yet to be applied to thermal IHCP. NeFTY closes the gap by composing a coordinate-based diffusivity field with a differentiable implicit-Euler heat solver, so the heat equation holds as a hard constraint at every iteration and gradients propagate through a numerically stable adjoint.

Coordinate-based Neural Fields parameterize a continuous signal as a multilayer perceptron (MLP) over spatial coordinates, typically combined with positional encoding or sinusoidal activations. SIREN [52] and NeRF [34] established neural fields as a high-frequency representation prior for visual signals, and deformable extensions such as Nerfies [38] broadened the class of recoverable scenes. Scientific imaging has begun to adopt the same representation for tomographic and fieldreconstruction problems, including X-ray computed tomography [60, 62], dynamic medical imaging via ProxNF [30], and fluid scalar fields with FluidNeRF [27]. These works contribute a continuous, mesh-free function space, but the representation alone is agnostic to physics: nothing in the parameterization enforces that the reconstructed field obeys a governing PDE. NeFTY treats the neural field strictly as the diffusivity prior and couples it with a differentiable heat solver, so the scientific contribution rests on hard physics constraints rather than on the representation itself.

# 3 Forward Model, Ill-Posedness, and Soft-Constraint Pathology

### 3.1 Forward model and effective-diffusivity parameterization

The conservation form of transient heat conduction in an isotropic medium is posed on a bounded Lipschitz domain Ω ⊂ R 3 , with boundary ∂Ω and observation window Γobs ⊆ ∂Ω, as follows:



| $\rho(\mathbf{x})C_p(\mathbf{x}) \partial_t T = \nabla \cdot (k(\mathbf{x}) \nabla T) + Q(\mathbf{x}, t),$ | $\mathbf{x} \in \Omega, t \in (0, t_{\text{end}}],$ | (1) |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|

with mass density ρ, specific heat capacity Cp, thermal conductivity k, and source Q (Q ≡ 0 post flash). Dividing \(1\) by ρCp and applying the product rule gives the equivalent diffusivity form


$$\partial_t T = \nabla \cdot (\alpha(\mathbf{x}) \nabla T) - k(\mathbf{x}) \nabla T \cdot \nabla \left( \frac{1}{\rho C_p} \right), \quad \alpha(\mathbf{x}) := \frac{k(\mathbf{x})}{\rho(\mathbf{x}) C_p(\mathbf{x})}, \quad (2)$$

valid when ρ, Cp ∈ C 1 (Ω) are strictly positive (Appendix A.1\). When ρCp is piecewise-constant with bulk and defect phases separated by an interface set Σ, the second term in \(2\) vanishes a.e. on Ω \ Σ and concentrates as a singular flux jump on Σ that a finite-volume grid cannot represent [40]. NeFTY therefore parameterizes a single effective field α(x) obeying ∂tT = ∇ · (α∇T) on Ω \ Σ and recovers continuity of the reduced α-flux at cell faces through the harmonic mean.

Proposition 1 (Harmonic mean as discrete interface continuity for the effective-α flux). For a onedimensional cell pair with constant cell-centered diffusivities αi , αi+1*, cell spacing* ∆x*, cell-centered temperatures* Ti , Ti+1*, and effective* α*-flux* jα := −α∂xT*, the unique flux satisfying continuity across the cell face* xi+1/2 is jα = −α¯i+1/2(Ti+1 − Ti)/∆x with α¯i+1/2 = 2αiαi+1/(αi + αi+1).

The proof, multi-dimensional consequence, and high-contrast behavior are in Appendix A; synthetic and real-PVC initial and boundary condition settings are specified in Appendix A.5.

# 3.2 The inverse problem and its ill-posedness

The IHCP recovers α from noisy surface measurements Tˆ(x, ti), x ∈ Γobs, ti ∈ {t1, . . . , tM} ⊂ (0, tend]. Let K(α) := Tα|Γobs×(0,tend) denote the parameter-to-observation operator (with Tα the

solution of \(2\) under fixed initial and lateral boundary conditions) acting on the admissible set A ⊂ H1 (Ω) ∩ L∞(Ω). The reconstruction objective is


$$\begin{aligned}\alpha^* &= \arg\min_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \mathcal{J}(\alpha), \\ \mathcal{J}(\alpha) &= \sum_{i=1}^M \left\| \mathcal{K}(\alpha)(\cdot, t_i) - \hat{T}(\cdot, t_i) \right\|_{\Gamma_{\text{obs}}}^2 + \lambda \mathcal{R}(\alpha),\end{aligned}\tag{3}$$

with ∥ · ∥Γobs the L 2 norm over Γobs, R a regularization functional, and λ > 0. The IHCP is widely recognized as one of the most severely ill-posed problems in mathematical physics [14, 19, 33]; we make the failure quantitative.

Proposition 2 (Compactness and algebraic singular-value decay). Let α0 > 0 be a constant background diffusivity with ∇Tα0 ∈ L∞(Ω × (0, tend)). The Fréchet derivative dKα0 : H1 (Ω) → L 2 (Γobs × (0, tend)) is bounded and compact, with singular values σn ≤ C (1 + λn) −1/2 , where {λj}j≥0 are the Laplacian eigenvalues on Ω under the imposed boundary conditions; on the slab, Weyl's law gives σn ≲ n −1/3 .

Corollary 1 (Hadamard ill-posedness). The pseudo-inverse dK† α0 is unbounded, with σ −1 n ≥ C −1 (1+ λn) 1/2 amplifying noise along the n*-th left singular vector.*

Proof in Appendix B; Figure 2 visualizes the smoothing. NeFTY counters this with a neural-field prior plus frequency annealing (Section 4\) and a total-variation penalty R(α).

#### 3.3 Soft-constraint pathology in stiff parabolic inversion

A standard PINN approximates the inverse map by jointly optimizing two networks: a temperature surrogate Tϕ(x, t) with parameters ϕ and a diffusivity surrogate αθ(x) with parameters θ, against


$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}} = \underbrace{\|T_\phi - \hat{T}\|_{\text{F}_{\text{obs}}^2}^2}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}} + \lambda_{\text{PDE}} \underbrace{\|\partial_t T_\phi - \nabla \cdot (\alpha_\theta \nabla T_\phi)\|_2^2}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}} + \lambda_{\text{IC}} \underbrace{\|T_\phi(\cdot, 0) - T_0\|_2^2}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}}}, \quad (4)$$

with weights λPDE, λIC > 0 and T0 the prescribed initial temperature field. Because Tϕ is parameterized by ϕ alone, ∇θLdata = ∇θLIC = 0, so the diffusivity update reduces to ∇θLPINN = λPDE∇θLPDE, with magnitude controlled pointwise by the Laplacian ∆Tϕ of the under-fitted temperature surrogate. Appendix C gives the full derivation and shows that adaptive loss balancing (GradNorm), separable temperature parameterizations (SPINN), causal weighting (Causal-PINN), and gradient-conflict resolution (DCGD) all preserve this decoupling.

The decoupling is structural: surface observations cannot reach θ except indirectly through the residual penalty. Combined with the unbounded amplification in Corollary 1, this gives a plausible mechanism for the well-documented PINN training pathology in which LPDE shrinks while αθ remains near a trivial constant [20, 55], which we confirm empirically across all four PINN variants in Section 5. NeFTY removes the soft constraint at its source, so surface data propagates to αθ through the adjoint of the discretized heat operator.

# 4 Method

NeFTY recovers the diffusivity field by directly minimizing the reconstruction objective J of equation \(3\), with the heat equation enforced as a hard constraint at every optimization step rather than as a soft residual penalty. The framework couples three components, illustrated in Figure 3: (i) a continuous coordinate-based parameterization αθ(x) that supplies a low-dimensional differentiable prior, (ii) a finite-volume implicit-Euler discretization of the bulk equation \(2\) whose interface flux is restored by the harmonic-mean stencil of Proposition 1, and (iii) a discrete adjoint that propagates the surface-fidelity gradient back to θ through the same sparse system used in the forward pass. By construction, the discrete temperature at each step is the solution of the discretized PDE driven by αθ, so the gradient decoupling of Section 3.3 cannot occur.



Figure 3: NeFTY pipeline. A coordinate MLP fθ maps each grid node x through a frequencyannealed positional encoding γ(·) into a bounded diffusivity αθ(x) (§4.1\). A differentiable implicit-Euler solver with harmonic-mean coefficients evolves the temperature Tn on a Cartesian grid (§4.2\). Surface frames are compared to measurements; the adjoint state µ n is propagated backward through the same sparse system to assemble the gradient on θ at constant memory in time (§4.3\).

#### 4.1 Neural diffusivity field

NeFTY parameterizes the unknown effective diffusivity αθ : Ω → R>0 as a coordinate multilayer perceptron (MLP) fθ : R dγ → R whose input is lifted through a Fourier feature map γ : R 3 → R dγ :

$$\gamma(\mathbf{x}) = (\sin(2^0 \pi \mathbf{x}), \cos(2^0 \pi \mathbf{x}), \dots, \sin(2^{N-1} \pi \mathbf{x}), \cos(2^{N-1} \pi \mathbf{x})), \quad (5)$$

of bandwidth N. The encoding combats the spectral bias of plain MLPs and lets the network represent the sharp interfaces characteristic of subsurface defects [34, 52]. To stabilize the high-frequency components against the algebraic noise amplification of Corollary 1, we adopt the cosine frequencyannealing schedule of Park et al. [38], gradually unmasking encoding bands during training. The network output is hard-bracketed to the physically admissible range

$$\alpha_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \alpha_{\min} + (\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min}) \sigma(f_\theta(\gamma(\mathbf{x}))), \quad (6)$$

with σ(·) the logistic sigmoid; the bounds αmin, αmax > 0 are chosen to span the bulk-to-defect contrast of the application without inducing ill-conditioning in the discrete system below. We instantiate the regularizer in \(3\) as the isotropic total variation R(αθ) = R Ω ∥∇αθ∥ [47], consistent with the piecewise-constant defect structure of the effective-diffusivity model in Appendix A. The full architecture, the cosine annealing schedule, and the discrete TV form are given in Appendix D.1.

#### 4.2 Hard-constrained differentiable physics

We discretize the bulk equation ∂tT = ∇ · (α∇T) on a uniform Cartesian grid covering Ω with spacings ∆x, ∆y, ∆z along the three axes and Nt time steps of size ∆t aligned with the camera frame rate. Let Tn ∈ R Ng denote the discrete temperature at step n on the Ng grid nodes, and let α¯i+1/2 denote the harmonic-mean face-centered diffusivity from Proposition 1. The second-order finite-volume diffusion operator L(αθ) ∈ R Ng×Ng acts at an interior node by

$$[\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)\mathbf{T}]_{i,j,k} = \frac{1}{\Delta x^2} [\bar{\alpha}_{i+1/2}(T_{i+1,j,k} - T_{i,j,k}) - \bar{\alpha}_{i-1/2}(T_{i,j,k} - T_{i-1,j,k})] + (y_-, z\text{-terms}), \quad (7)$$

with lateral periodic and through-thickness adiabatic conditions (Appendix A.5\) closing the stencil at the boundary. The harmonic-mean coefficient is the unique discrete realization of effectiveflux continuity at interior faces for the single-α model (Proposition 1\); an arithmetic mean would spuriously allow heat to leak across the interface [40]. All discrete temperatures here are interpreted as deviations from the ambient temperature (Appendix A.5\), so the implicit-Euler step is affine-free.

To decouple the time step from the diffusivity contrast, we integrate in time with the unconditionally stable implicit Euler scheme [10], advancing Tn to Tn+1 through the sparse linear system



| $\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta) \mathbf{T}^{n+1} = \mathbf{T}^n,$ | $\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta) := \mathbf{I} - \Delta t \mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta).$ | (8) |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|--------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|

The matrix A(αθ) is sparse, symmetric positive-definite for our boundary conditions, and depends on θ only through αθ. We solve \(8\) on the GPU through a fixed number K of unrolled Jacobi iterations [22], expressed as stencil convolutions to fit seamlessly into autodiff frameworks [39]. The full discretization, choice of K, and Jacobi update rule are given in Appendix D.2. At every optimization iteration, the temperature Tn+1 produced by \(8\) satisfies the discrete heat equation up to the iterative solver tolerance.

#### 4.3 Adjoint optimization

Composing the implicit Euler recurrence \(8\) from T0 defines Tn = Tn(αθ) implicitly, hence the discrete analogue Kdisc of the parameter-to-observation map. Let ni ∈ {1, . . . , Nt} be chosen so that tni = ti for each measurement time ti , and let ΠΓ : R Ng → R NΓ denote the row-selector matrix that extracts the NΓ surface samples on Γobs. NeFTY minimizes the discrete reconstruction objective


$$\min_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{disc}}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^M \|\mathbf{\Pi}_{\Gamma} \mathbf{T}^{\mathbf{n}_i}(\alpha_{\theta}) - \hat{\mathbf{T}}_i\|^2 + \lambda \mathcal{R}(\alpha_{\theta}), \quad (9)$$

the discretization of \(3\) with ∥ · ∥ the standard Euclidean norm on R NΓ (equal to the discrete L 2 (Γobs) norm up to the lateral quadrature weight ∆x∆y, which we absorb into the regularization weight λ > 0), and R instantiated as the discrete total-variation regularizer of Appendix D.1; we keep the symbol λ from \(3\) now in its TV-weight role. Because Tn is defined implicitly by the state equation \(8\), gradients dJdisc/dθ obtained by storing the full forward trajectory and applying backpropagation through time cost O(NgNt) memory, which is prohibitive for the Ng ≈ 105 , Nt ≈ 102 regime of pulsed thermography. We use the discrete adjoint method instead [6, 37].

Treat \(8\) as the constraint F n(Tn, Tn−1 , αθ) := A(αθ)Tn − Tn−1 = 0 for n = 1, . . . , Nt, and introduce adjoint variables µ n as Lagrange multipliers for each step. Setting the total derivative of the augmented Lagrangian with respect to Tn to zero yields the backward-in-time recurrence



| $\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta)^T \boldsymbol{\mu}^n = \boldsymbol{\mu}^{n+1} + (\partial \ell^n / \partial \mathbf{T}^n)^T$ , | $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{n+1} = \mathbf{0}$ , | (10) |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|------|
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|------|

where ℓ n collects the data-fidelity terms in \(9\) that depend on Tn (nonzero only at measurement frames n ∈ {n1, . . . , nM}). The adjoint at step n uses the transpose of the same sparse A that drives the forward solve, so a single backward sweep with the same Jacobi inner loop yields all µ n at the cost of one forward pass. The parameter gradient is then assembled by


$$\frac{d\mathcal{J}_{\text{disc}}}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial(\lambda\mathcal{R})}{\partial\theta} + \Delta t \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} (\boldsymbol{\mu}^n)^\top \frac{\partial(\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)\mathbf{T}^n)}{\partial\alpha_\theta} \frac{\partial\alpha_\theta}{\partial\theta}, \quad (11)$$

where ∂αθ/∂θ is the standard MLP Jacobian. Because \(10\) requires only µ n+1 and Tn, the backward sweep has constant memory in Nt. The full Lagrangian construction, the derivation of \(10\)–\(11\), and the consistency with the continuous adjoint of the bulk equation are given in Appendix D.3; the implementation hyperparameters (depth, width, encoding bandwidth N, grid resolution, Jacobi count K, and optimizer schedule) are catalogued in Appendix D.4. Crucially, the data-side gradient on θ is non-vanishing precisely because ΠΓTni is now a function of θ, restoring the dependency chain θ → αθ → Tn → Jdisc that the soft-PINN formulation of Section 3.3 broke.

# 5 Experiments

#### 5.1 Setup

Datasets. Synthetic data are generated with PhiFlow [23], a finite-volume engine independent of the implicit-Euler Jacobi solver of Section 4.2, so reconstruction is not subject to inverse crime; the real PVC datasets [58, 59] provide only 2D defect masks and discrete defect depths from CAD, so real-world evaluation uses projected 2D masks and 2.5D depth annotations rather than 3D volumetric labels. The synthetic benchmark contains 1,000 samples on the unitless slab Ω = [0, 10]2 × [0, 1] at 64 × 64 × 16 resolution, 100 frames at ∆t = 0.05, and one to four subsurface defects per sample, split into a homogeneous and a layered configuration. Full data-generation procedure, dimensional analysis, and compute resources are in Appendix E.

Baselines. On real PVC data we additionally compare to the classical thermography heuristics PPT [26, 32] and TSR [49, 50] and to a Swin-UNETR [21] trained on the synthetic benchmark and transferred zero-shot. ### 6 Conclusion

We presented NeFTY, a hard-constrained neural field framework for three-dimensional inverse heat conduction. NeFTY parameterizes the unknown thermal diffusivity as a continuous coordinate-based field, enforces the governing PDE through a differentiable implicit-Euler solver with harmonic-mean interface flux, and inverts the resulting forward map with adjoint gradients at solver-level memory cost. On synthetic homogeneous and layered settings it reaches IoU 0.45 and 0.37, while four modern soft-constrained PINN variants and a voxel-grid baseline remain at or near zero, and on real PVC pulsed-thermography data it segments defects at IoU 0.431 and recovers depth at Abs Rel 0.465 from surface measurements alone. More broadly, our findings are consistent with a recurring pattern in scientific machine learning: when the forward operator is strongly smoothing, embedding the discretized physics as a hard constraint inside the optimization loop tends to be more reliable than penalizing its residual in the loss.

Limitations and Broader Impact. NeFTY shares the limitations common to test-time inverseproblem solvers: per-specimen optimization at higher latency than supervised feedforward inversion, attenuated quantitative recovery inside very high-contrast voids, and evaluation against projected 2D and 2.5D supervision because public NDT benchmarks do not yet provide 3D volumetric ground truth. Each is addressable with established tooling, including amortized initialization through meta-learning or hypernetworks, preconditioned solvers in the differentiable loop, and paired computed-tomography labels for full volumetric evaluation; we discuss them in detail in Appendix J. The intended use of NeFTY is non-destructive evaluation, where it can support safer infrastructure inspection and earlier defect detection without destructive testing or ionizing X-ray exposure. As with any automated inspection pipeline, its outputs are best treated as decision support rather than a substitute for human qualification of safety-critical components, and reconstructions in regions of low surface sensitivity should be reported with uncertainty alongside the recovered field.

# Appendix

# A Effective Diffusivity Model and Interface Flux

# A.1 Derivation of the diffusivity form

Let ρ, Cp ∈ C 1 (Ω) with ρCp > 0 on Ω¯, and let T ∈ C 2,1 (Ω × (0, tend]) satisfy the conservation form \(1\) with Q ≡ 0:

$$\rho C_p \partial_t T = \nabla \cdot (k \nabla T).$$

Dividing both sides by the strictly positive function ρCp and applying the product rule ∇ · (γv) = γ∇ · v + ∇γ · v with γ = 1/(ρCp) and v = k∇T gives

$$\begin{aligned}\partial_t T &= \frac{1}{\rho C_p} \nabla \cdot (k \nabla T) = \nabla \cdot \left( \frac{k}{\rho C_p} \nabla T \right) - \nabla \left( \frac{1}{\rho C_p} \right) \cdot (k \nabla T) \\ &= \nabla \cdot (\alpha \nabla T) - k \nabla T \cdot \nabla \left( \frac{1}{\rho C_p} \right),\end{aligned}$$

with α := k/(ρCp). The reduction is reversible by multiplying through by ρCp, so the conservation form \(1\) (under Q ≡ 0) and the diffusivity form \(2\) are equivalent on Ω × (0, tend] whenever ρCp ∈ C 1 .

#### A.2 Distributional treatment of the heat-capacity correction

When ρCp is piecewise-constant with values cb > 0 on a bulk subdomain Ωb and cd > 0 on a defect subdomain Ωd, separated by a smooth interface Σ = ∂Ωb ∩ ∂Ωd, the gradient ∇(1/(ρCp)) is a vector-valued distribution supported on Σ:

$$\nabla \left( \frac{1}{\rho C_p} \right) = \left( \frac{1}{c_d} - \frac{1}{c_b} \right) \mathbf{n}_\Sigma \delta_\Sigma, \quad (12)$$

where nΣ is the unit normal to Σ pointing from Ωb to Ωd and δΣ is the surface measure on Σ. The correction term in \(2\) reads

$$-k\nabla T \cdot \nabla \left( \frac{1}{\rho C_p} \right) = -\left( \frac{1}{c_d} - \frac{1}{c_b} \right) (k\nabla T \cdot \mathbf{n}_\Sigma) \delta_\Sigma,$$

which is identically zero on Ω \ Σ and concentrates as a jump in the normal physical Fourier flux q := −k ∂nT across Σ. A finite-volume discretization on a uniform Cartesian grid of spacing ∆x cannot resolve a Dirac measure on a measure-zero interface [40]; dropping this distributional term yields the bulk equation ∂tT = ∇ · (α∇T) on Ω \ Σ, which is the model NeFTY actually solves.

The single-α effective model has its own interface conservation law: across a face where α jumps, the effective α-flux jα := −α∂nT must be continuous, since otherwise temperature would lose mass at that face. The remainder of this appendix shows that the harmonic-mean stencil enforces this jα-continuity exactly. We do not claim that the harmonic mean reproduces the dropped distributional term of the full ρCp model; rather, the harmonic-mean stencil is the unique discrete analogue of the jump condition appropriate to the single-α model that NeFTY parameterizes.

#### A.3 Proof of Proposition 1 (Harmonic mean as discrete interface continuity)

Consider two adjacent one-dimensional cells with center coordinates xi and xi+1 = xi + ∆x, cell-centered diffusivities αi and αi+1, and cell-centered temperatures Ti and Ti+1. Assume each cell has constant diffusivity, with the discontinuity located at the cell face xi+1/2 = xi + ∆x/2. Let Ti+1/2 denote the (a priori unknown) face temperature.

Within the left half-cell [xi , xi+1/2], Fourier's law for the effective α-flux jα = −α∂xT gives a constant flux and a linear temperature profile, so


$$j_\alpha = -\alpha_i \cdot \frac{T_{i+1/2} - T_i}{\Delta x/2}. \quad (13)$$

Within the right half-cell [xi+1/2, xi+1], by the same argument,


$$j_\alpha = -\alpha_{i+1} \cdot \frac{T_{i+1} - T_{i+1/2}}{\Delta x/2}. \quad (14)$$

Continuity of the effective flux at xi+1/2 in the single-α model imposes the same value of jα in \(13\) and \(14\). From \(13\), Ti+1/2 = Ti − jα∆x/(2αi). From \(14\), Ti+1/2 = Ti+1 + jα∆x/(2αi+1). Equating and rearranging,

$$T_i - T_{i+1} = j_\alpha \frac{\Delta x}{2} \left( \frac{1}{\alpha_i} + \frac{1}{\alpha_{i+1}} \right) = j_\alpha \frac{\Delta x (\alpha_i + \alpha_{i+1})}{2\alpha_i\alpha_{i+1}}.$$

Solving for jα,

$$j_\alpha = -\frac{2\alpha_i\alpha_{i+1}}{\alpha_i + \alpha_{i+1}} \cdot \frac{T_{i+1} - T_i}{\Delta x} =: -\bar{\alpha}_{i+1/2} \cdot \frac{T_{i+1} - T_i}{\Delta x}.$$

The expression α¯i+1/2 = 2αiαi+1/(αi + αi+1) is the harmonic mean. Uniqueness follows from the linearity of the two-equation system in (jα, Ti+1/2) with non-degenerate coefficient matrix. □

#### A.4 Multi-dimensional consequence and physical interpretation

In ambient spatial dimension D ∈ {2, 3} on a uniform Cartesian grid, the same argument applied along each principal direction gives the second-order finite-volume diffusion operator L(α) used by NeFTY, defined by its action on a discrete temperature field T at grid node (i, j, k):

$$[\mathbf{L}(\alpha)\mathbf{T}]_{i,j,k} := \frac{1}{\Delta x^2} [\bar{\alpha}_{i+1/2}(T_{i+1,j,k} - T_{i,j,k}) - \bar{\alpha}_{i-1/2}(T_{i,j,k} - T_{i-1,j,k})] + (y_-, z\text{-terms}).$$

The harmonic mean has two physically important properties for thermal NDT. First, α¯i+1/2 is dominated by the smaller of αi , αi+1 when the two are well separated, so an insulating defect cell αi+1 ≪ αi throttles flux at its boundary as physical thermal resistance does. Second, α¯ is exact for piecewise-constant α at cell faces and second-order accurate for smooth α; using the arithmetic mean instead is first-order accurate and overestimates flux across high-contrast interfaces, leading to systematic underestimation of defect depth in inverse reconstruction [40]. NeFTY's adoption of the harmonic mean is therefore the unique discrete analogue of the effective-flux continuity condition for the single-α bulk equation in \(2\).

#### A.5 Initial and boundary conditions: synthetic and real PVC datasets

The bulk diffusivity equation ∂tT = ∇ · (α∇T) is closed by an initial condition and by lateral and through-thickness boundary conditions. The synthetic dataset and the real PVC datasets [58, 59] require different choices, all consistent with the same underlying physics but adapted to the specimen scale, the heating modality, and the dominant non-dimensional groups.

Synthetic dataset. The synthetic data are generated on a unitless slab Ω = [0, L] 2 × [0, H] with L = 10, H = 1, grid resolution 64 × 64 × 16 and 100 transient frames, on micron-scale specimens at microsecond timescales for which the Biot number is small and convective dissipation is negligible. We accordingly impose:

- Initial condition. A spatially Gaussian post-flash temperature distribution localized near the front face,

$$T(\mathbf{x}) = T_{\text{amb}} + A \exp(-((x - x_c)^2 + (y - y_c)^2)/(2w_{xy}^2)) \exp(-z^2/(2w_z^2)),$$

where Tamb is the ambient temperature, A is the deposited pulse amplitude, (xc, yc) is the lateral footprint center, wxy is the lateral pulse width, and wz ≪ H is the absorption depth set by the focused-laser pulse profile.

- Lateral boundaries (∂Ωxy*).* Periodic conditions on ∂Ωxy := [0, L] 2 × {0, L}x × {0, L}y (the x = 0/L and y = 0/L faces), modeling a semi-infinite slab and removing reflections from the truncated simulation grid.
- Through-thickness boundaries (∂Ωz*).* Adiabatic conditions n·(α∇T) = 0 on ∂Ωz (the front z = 0 and back z = H faces), modeling negligible convective loss during the short inspection window.

Real PVC dataset. The PVC-Infrared [59] and PVC-Depth [58] benchmarks consist of pulsed thermography sequences on 100 × 100 × 5 mm PVC specimens with cylindrical flat-bottom holes of varying size and depth, recorded over second-scale dynamics. Three physical adaptations are required so that the same NeFTY solver applies without retraining the ground-truth simulator:

- Initial condition. The Gaussian pulse is replaced by a near-uniform front-face flash T0(x) = Tamb + A exp(−z 2/(2w 2 z )), matching the broad flash-lamp excitation used in the benchmark setups (lateral profile uniform over the inspection footprint, absorption depth wz small relative to specimen thickness).
- Centimeter and second-scale Fourier-number rescaling. The PVC specimens are centimeter-scale at second-scale dynamics rather than the synthetic micron / microsecond regime; the unitless solver is rescaled through the Fourier number Fo = αphys ttotal/L2 0 (full scaling below).

- Convective Robin back-face boundary. The centimeter-scale specimen and second-scale dynamics make convective dissipation non-negligible; on the back face we therefore impose a Robin condition n · (α∇T) = −h (T − Tamb) with h > 0 a calibrated convective coefficient, recovering the asymptotic Biot regime appropriate for the PVC scale. The front face retains the adiabatic Neumann condition; lateral conditions remain periodic on interior crops or are replaced by adiabatic Neumann depending on the recording.

The unitless and dimensional scales are aligned through the Fourier number Fo = αphys ttotal/L2 0 , with αphys the physical diffusivity (in m2/s), ttotal the physical experiment duration (in s), and L0 a characteristic specimen length scale (in m); the unitless solver evolves in (t/ttotal, x/L0) coordinates with effective diffusivity α = αphys ttotal/L2 0 . A single set of unitless solver parameters represents widely different material classes by reinterpreting L0 and ttotal, so the synthetic and real PVC settings differ in their initial-condition shape and back-face Robin coefficient but share the same forward operator K and the same harmonic-mean stencil derived above. Full physical scaling, calibrated h values, and projection from a reconstructed 3D α field to the 2D defect masks and 2.5D depth labels supplied by the benchmarks are documented in Appendix H.

# B Ill-Posedness of the Inverse Heat Conduction Problem

This appendix establishes the compactness and algebraic singular-value decay claimed in Proposition 2. The argument is consistent with the standard treatment of parameter identification in parabolic inverse problems [14, 19, 33]; we present a self-contained derivation on the slab geometry used in our experiments. The proof factorizes dKα0 = B ◦ J, where B : L 2 (Ω) → L 2 (Γobs × (0, tend)) is bounded by an energy estimate plus a parabolic boundary trace, and J : H1 (Ω) ,→ L 2 (Ω) is the compact Rellich–Kondrachov embedding whose approximation numbers along the Laplacian eigenbasis decay as (1 + λn) −1/2 . Truncating the parameter side via J rather than the source side avoids the mode-mixing introduced by multiplication with ∇Tα0 .

#### B.1 Linearization of the parameter-to-observation map

Let α0 > 0 be a constant background diffusivity and let Tα0 (x, t) solve the bulk heat equation ∂tT = α0∆T with the prescribed initial condition T0 and boundary conditions on the slab Ω = [0, L] 2 × [0, H]. Throughout the proof we assume the background satisfies ∇Tα0 ∈ L∞(Q) with Q := Ω × (0, tend). Consider a perturbation α = α0 + ε δα with δα ∈ H1 (Ω) and ε > 0 small. To first order in ε, the perturbation δT = (Tα − Tα0 )/ε solves the linearized PDE in the weak sense


$$\langle \partial_t(\delta T), v \rangle + \alpha_0 (\nabla(\delta T), \nabla v)_{L^2(\Omega)} = - \int_{\Omega} \delta \alpha \nabla T_{\alpha_0}(\cdot, t) \cdot \nabla v \, d\mathbf{x}, \quad (15)$$

for all v ∈ H1 (Ω) compatible with the imposed boundary conditions, with vanishing initial condition δT(·, 0) = 0. We define the source distribution Fδα(t) ∈ H−1 (Ω) by ⟨Fδα(t), v⟩ = − R Ω δα ∇Tα0 (·, t) · ∇v dx, satisfying

$$\|F_{\delta\alpha}(t)\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} \leq \|\nabla T_{\alpha_0}(\cdot, t)\|_{L^\infty(\Omega)} \|\delta\alpha\|_{L^2(\Omega)}. \quad (16)$$

The Fréchet derivative is the linear map dKα0 : H1 (Ω) → L 2 (Γobs × (0, tend)) defined by dKα0 (δα) = δT|Γobs×(0,tend) .

#### B.2 Proof of Proposition 2 (Compactness and algebraic singular-value decay)

The proof has four steps: (i) an energy estimate in L 2 (0, tend; H1 (Ω)) for δT; (ii) a parabolic trace inequality giving boundedness of the auxiliary map B; (iii) a finite-rank approximation through the Rellich–Kondrachov embedding J; (iv) Weyl asymptotics on the slab.

Step 1: energy estimate. Existence and uniqueness of a weak solution δT ∈ L 2 (0, tend; H1 (Ω)) ∩ H1 (0, tend; H−1 (Ω)) to \(15\) with vanishing initial data and source Fδα ∈ L (0, tend; H−1 (Ω)) follow from standard Galerkin / Lions–Magenes parabolic theory [14]. With this regularity, testing \(15\) with v = δT(·, t) is permissible and gives

$$\frac{1}{2} \frac{d}{dt} \| \delta T(\cdot, t) \|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \alpha_0 \| \nabla \delta T(\cdot, t) \|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = - \int_{\Omega} \delta \alpha \nabla T_{\alpha_0}(\cdot, t) \cdot \nabla(\delta T)(\cdot, t) \, dx.$$

Applying Young's inequality |ab| ≤ 1 2α0 a 2+ α0 2 b 2 to the right-hand side and integrating over (0, tend) with the vanishing initial condition,

$$\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla T(\cdot, t_{\text{end}})\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \frac{\alpha_0}{2} \int_0^{t_{\text{end}}} \|\nabla \Delta T\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 dt \leq \frac{1}{2\alpha_0} \int_0^{t_{\text{end}}} \|\delta \alpha \nabla T_{\alpha_0}(\cdot, t)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 dt.$$

With ∥δα ∇Tα0 (·, t)∥L2(Ω) ≤ ∥∇Tα0 (·, t)∥L∞(Ω) ∥δα∥L2(Ω), this yields



| $\  \delta T \ _{L^2(0, t_{\text{end}}; H^1(\Omega))} \leq C_E(\Omega, t_{\text{end}}, \alpha_0) \  \nabla T_{\alpha_0} \ _{L^\infty(Q)} \  \delta \alpha \ _{L^2(\Omega)},$ | (17) |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|

with CE an absolute constant depending only on Ω, tend, α0 (a Poincaré-type constant absorbed into the bound).

Step 2: boundary trace and the auxiliary operator B. The Sobolev trace inequality ∥u|∂Ω∥L2(∂Ω) ≤ CΓ∥u∥H1(Ω) on a Lipschitz domain combined with \(17\) gives

$$\|\delta T\|_{\Gamma_{\text{obs}} \times (0, t_{\text{end}})} \|^2 (\Gamma_{\text{obs}} \times (0, t_{\text{end}})) - C_T^2 \|\delta T\|_{\Gamma^2(0, t_{\text{end}}; H^1(\Omega))}^2 \leq C_B \|\delta \alpha\|_{\Gamma^2(\Omega)}^2.$$

with CB := CΓ CE ∥∇Tα0 ∥L∞(Q) . The map

$$B : L^2(\Omega) \rightarrow L^2(\Gamma_{\text{obs}} \times (0, t_{\text{end}})), \quad B(\delta\alpha) = \delta T|_{\Gamma_{\text{obs}} \times (0, t_{\text{end}})},$$

is therefore bounded, ∥B∥op ≤ CB. The Fréchet derivative is dKα0 = B ◦ J, where J : H1 (Ω) ,→ L (Ω) is the inclusion.

Equipping H1 (Ω) with the spectral norm ∥δα∥ H1 = P j≥0 (1 + λj )|aj | 2 where δα = P j ajϕj , the approximation numbers of J along the Laplacian eigenbasis are exactly (1 + λn) −1/2 : for the L 2 -orthogonal projector PN onto span(ϕ0, . . . , ϕN−1),

$$\|(I - P_N)\delta\alpha\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = \sum_{j \geq N} a_j^2 \leq (1 + \lambda_N)^{-1} \sum_{j \geq N} (1 + \lambda_j) a_j^2 \leq (1 + \lambda_N)^{-1} \|\delta\alpha\|_{H^1(\Omega)}^2.$$

$$\|(\mathcal{D}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha_0} - A_N)(\delta\alpha)\|_{L^2(\Gamma_{\text{obs}} \times (t, t_{\text{end}}))} \leq \|B\|_{\text{op}} \|(I - P_N)(J\delta\alpha)\|_{L^2(\Omega)} \leq C_B (1 + \lambda_N)^{-1/2}.$$

Since approximation numbers and singular values coincide for compact operators between Hilbert spaces [14], this yields



| $\sigma_{N+1}(d\mathcal{K}_{\alpha_0}) \leq C_B (1 + \lambda_N)^{-1/2},$ | $N \geq 0.$ | (18) |
|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|------|
|                                                                          |             |      |

Compactness of dKα0 follows from σn → 0, equivalently the convergence AN → dKα0 in operator norm. The mode-mixing introduced by multiplication with ∇Tα0 in the source Fδα does not affect this argument: the truncation PN acts on the parameter δα before it enters the linearized PDE, and the small-L 2 -tail of δα for δα ∈ H1 -unit-ball produces a small output in L 2 (Γobs × (0, tend)) via the L 2 -bounded map B.

Step 4: slab Weyl asymptotics. For Ω = [0, L] 2×[0, H] with periodic conditions on the lateral faces and Neumann conditions on the top/bottom, the eigenfunctions are tensor products ϕm,ℓ,p(x, y, z) = e 2πi(mx/L+ℓy/L) cos(pπz/H) with eigenvalues λm,ℓ,p = (2πm/L) 2 + (2πℓ/L) 2 + (pπ/H) 2 , indexed by (m, ℓ, p) ∈ Z 2 × Z≥0. By Weyl's law in three dimensions, enumerated in increasing order λn ≍ n 2/3 , so \(18\) reads σn ≲ n −1/3 on the slab. □

# B.3 Proof of Corollary 1 (Hadamard ill-posedness)

By Proposition 2, the singular values of dKα0 tend to zero, so the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse dK† α0 is unbounded on the closure of the range. Concretely, the singular value decomposition

dKα0 (δα) = P n:σn>0 σn⟨δα, vn⟩un has right singular vectors vn ∈ H1 (Ω) in the parameter space and left singular vectors un ∈ L 2 (Γobs × (0, tend)) in the data space, with dK† α0 un = σ −1 n vn for the indices n with σn > 0 and dK† α0 un = 0 on the kernel of dK∗ α0 . A measurement perturbation aligned with a non-degenerate n-th left singular vector un therefore produces an H1 -norm perturbation in the recovered diffusivity of magnitude σ −1 n ≥ C −1 B (1 + λn) 1/2 . The forward problem is wellposed (existence, uniqueness, continuous dependence in the forward direction follow from standard parabolic theory), but inversion fails the continuous-dependence criterion in the sense of Hadamard. □

#### B.4 Discussion: pointwise damping versus operator decay

Two distinct smoothing rates appear in this analysis and should not be conflated. Pointwise in time, the free-space heat kernel Gt(x) = (4πα0t) −3/2 exp(−|x| 2/(4α0t)) governs the impulse response: a localized defect at depth z contributes a surface temperature signal whose amplitude decays Gaussianfast in z, suppressed by exp(−z 2/(4α0t)) at observation time t > 0. Equivalently in the spectral picture, the heat semigroup S(t) = e α0∆t damps the j-th Laplacian eigenmode by e −α0λj t , so a defect supported at frequency λj is exponentially attenuated at any fixed time. These pointwise statements are the depth- and frequency-decay heuristics that motivate the original ill-posedness intuition for IHCP.

The operator decay of dKα0 , in contrast, integrates the boundary trace against the entire time interval (0, tend), and the bound \(18\) is only algebraic in λn. The two rates coincide in the degenerate observation model where the trace is taken at a single final time t = tend, in which case σn(S(tend)) = e −α0λntend recovers the exponential rate; integrating over (0, tend) averages out this exponential factor and yields the algebraic bound proven above.

The practical consequences for IHCP follow from the algebraic operator-rate. Longer observation windows tend and higher-conductivity bulk materials improve early-time signal-to-noise ratio but preserve the algebraic singular-value rate; regularization remains mandatory at any rate, since any consistent reconstruction method must implicitly or explicitly bound the high-frequency content of admissible α. NeFTY combines two such regularizers, namely a continuous neural-field prior with frequency annealing (Section 4\) and an isotropic total-variation penalty R(α) = R Ω ∥∇α∥ aligned with the piecewise-constant defect structure assumed by the effective-diffusivity model of Section 3.1.

# C Soft-Constraint Gradient Pathology in PINNs

#### C.1 Derivation of the gradient decoupling

Write LPINN = Ldata(Tϕ) + λPDELPDE(Tϕ, αθ) + λICLIC(Tϕ) as in equation \(4\). The data term Ldata(Tϕ) = ∥Tϕ − Tˆ∥ 2 Γobs depends on θ only if Tϕ does. Because Tϕ is parameterized by ϕ alone, ∂Tϕ/∂θ ≡ 0, hence ∇θLdata = 0. The same argument applied to LIC(Tϕ) gives ∇θLIC = 0. Therefore

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}} = \lambda_{\text{PDE}} \nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}(T_{\phi}, \alpha_{\theta}).$$

which establishes the gradient decoupling. To bound the residual-gradient magnitude, expand LPDE pointwise:

| $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}(T_\phi, \alpha_\theta) = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} (r_i(T_\phi, \alpha_\theta))^2,$ | $r_i = \partial_t T_\phi(\mathbf{x}_i, \tau_i) - \nabla \cdot (\alpha_\theta \nabla T_\phi)(\mathbf{x}_i, \tau_i),$ |
|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|                                                                                                                    |                                                                                                                     |

where {(xi , τi)} Nc i=1 are the PINN collocation points (using τi for collocation times to distinguish them from the measurement times ti of Section 3.2\). Differentiating with respect to θ and applying the product rule to the divergence,

$$\nabla \cdot (\alpha_\theta \nabla T_\phi) = \alpha_\theta \Delta T_\phi + \nabla \alpha_\theta \cdot \nabla T_\phi,$$

so


$$\nabla \theta_{ri} = -\Delta T_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i, \tau_i) \nabla_{\theta} \alpha_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i) - \nabla T_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i, \tau_i) \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \nabla \alpha_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i). \quad (19)$$

The Cauchy–Schwarz bound ∥∇θLPDE∥ ≤ 2 Nc P i |ri | ∥∇θri∥ combined with \(19\) gives a residualgradient magnitude controlled by the spatial derivatives ∆Tϕ and ∇Tϕ of the temperature surrogate at the collocation points, dominated by ∥∆Tϕ∥ when ∇αθ is bounded. Empirically and analytically, ∥∆Tϕ∥ is large whenever Tϕ has not yet converged, because second-order spatial derivatives of an under-fitted neural temperature field are noisy and large in magnitude.

The decoupling does not by itself prove that αθ collapses to a constant. It does prove that surface observations cannot reach θ except indirectly through the residual term, so the only avenue for Tˆ to influence αθ is via residual gradients that scale with the under-fitted Laplacian of Tϕ. The empirical PINN failure mode reported in Section 5 and observed across PINN training analyses [20, 55] is consistent with this structural channel and is the experimental complement to the present derivation.

### C.2 Coupling with the ill-posedness of Section 3.2

Even if the gradient decoupling derived in Appendix C.1 were absent, recovering αθ through any chain that begins at the surface data would require inverting the parameter-to-observation map K. By Corollary 1, this inverse algebraically amplifies the n-th left singular vector by σ −1 n ≥ C −1 B (1 + λn) 1/2 . In the soft-PINN, the surface data influences αθ only second-hand, by first updating Tϕ through Ldata and then propagating to αθ through the residual term: the high-frequency information needed to localize defects is already attenuated by the smoothing of the forward map before the residual gradient sees it, and must then be re-amplified by an unbounded pseudo-inverse. The result is the experimentally observed pattern in which Ldata converges (the temperature net fits the surface) but αθ remains near a trivial constant; this is the soft-constraint analogue of the data-fit paradox documented in our experimental study (Section 5\) and in independent analyses of PINN training [20, 55].

### C.3 Why standard remedies do not resolve the decoupling

Several SciML methods have been proposed to address PINN training pathologies. We argue that none of them creates a path from Tˆ to θ that bypasses the residual term, so the gradient decoupling continues to apply.

GradNorm [7]. GradNorm rescales the loss weights λPDE, λIC to balance gradient magnitudes during training. Because the rescaling acts on λPDE and not on the parameterization, the identity ∇θLPINN = λPDE∇θLPDE is unchanged. GradNorm changes the relative size of the residual gradient but cannot manufacture a data-side gradient on θ.

SPINN [8]. SPINN replaces the joint Tϕ(x, t) network with a separable product Tϕ(x, t) = P k T (1) ϕ (x) T (2) ϕ (y) T (3) ϕ (z) T (4) ϕ (t), accelerating collocation and reducing the variance of the residual term. The diffusivity network αθ remains independent of ϕ; the gradient decoupling is unchanged.

Causal-PINN [56]. Causal-PINN reweights the residual integrand by a causal weight that emphasizes early times before late times. The weight enters LPDE through a non-negative pointwise factor and again does not change the parameterization. The decoupling result holds.

DCGD [25]. Dual-cone gradient descent projects the data and residual gradients onto a common acceptable cone to avoid destructive interference. By construction it operates on already-computed gradients of Ldata and LPDE separately. Because ∇θLdata = 0 identically, the only gradient applied to θ remains the (projected) residual gradient.

In each case, the structural decoupling persists. NeFTY removes it at the source: the temperature is no longer an independent neural surrogate, but the implicit solution of a discretized PDE driven by αθ, so the chain of dependencies is θ → αθ → T → Ldata and the surface data provides a non-vanishing gradient on θ through the adjoint of the discrete heat operator (Section 4\).

# D Method Details: Neural-Field Architecture, Forward Solver, and Adjoint Gradients

This appendix records the implementation of the three components introduced in Section 4: the neural diffusivity field of Section 4.1, the discrete forward solver of Section 4.2, and the discrete adjoint of Section 4.3. The harmonic-mean stencil derived in Appendix A (Proposition 1\) and the boundary conditions described in Appendix A.5 are reused without further comment. Notation matches Section 4 throughout: θ denotes the MLP parameter set, µ n the discrete adjoint variable at step n (kept distinct from the Laplacian eigenvalues λn of Proposition 2 and from the regularization weight λ in \(9\)), ΠΓ the observation projector onto Γobs, and A(αθ) := I − ∆tL(αθ) the implicit-Euler system matrix. The boldface discrete diffusion operator L(αθ) is distinct from the italic lateral

side length L of Appendix A.5, the boldface Jacobi diagonal D and off-diagonal R of Appendix D.2 are distinct from the italic spatial dimension D and the calligraphic regularizer R of \(9\), and the boldface state residual F n is distinct from the italic source Fδα of Appendix B.1.

#### D.1 Neural diffusivity-field architecture

Coordinate MLP. The diffusivity field is modeled as a fully connected network fθ : R dγ → R with depth LMLP, hidden width WMLP, ReLU activations [35], and a single skip connection that re-injects the encoded coordinate γ(x) at the middle layer to preserve gradient flow [34]. Weights are initialized with Xavier scaling [18]. Concrete values for LMLP, WMLP, and the skip-layer index are tabulated in Appendix D.4.

Positional encoding. Each input coordinate x ∈ Ω ⊂ R D is normalized to [−1, 1]D and mapped to

$$\gamma(\mathbf{x}) = (\sin(2^0 \pi \mathbf{x}), \cos(2^0 \pi \mathbf{x}), \dots, \sin(2^{N-1} \pi \mathbf{x}), \cos(2^{N-1} \pi \mathbf{x})) \in \mathbb{R}^{2^N}, \quad (20)$$

yielding dγ = 2DN. The bandwidth N controls the maximum spatial frequency the network can express; raising N improves the network's ability to resolve sharp interfaces but exposes it to the algebraic noise amplification of Corollary 1 along high-eigenmode left singular vectors.

Frequency annealing. To suppress this amplification during the early phase of optimization, we apply the cosine schedule of Park et al. [38] to the encoding bands. Let β(t) ∈ [0, N] be a trainingstep-dependent annealing parameter; the k-th band (k = 0, . . . , N − 1) of γ(x) is multiplied by

$$w_k(\beta) = \frac{1}{2} [1 - \cos(\pi \operatorname{clamp}(\beta - k, 0, 1))], \quad (21)$$

so that wk = 0 before the schedule reaches band k, ramps smoothly to wk = 1 over a unit-width interval in β, and remains at one thereafter. We linearly increase β from 0 to N over the first TFA optimization steps (frequency-annealing horizon, value in Appendix D.4\), after which all bands are fully unmasked. Compared with binary masking, the cosine schedule avoids abrupt gradient bursts when a new band becomes active and lets the network grow high-frequency detail only after the low-frequency reconstruction has stabilized.

Bounded output. The MLP output is hard-bracketed to a physically admissible range,

$$\alpha_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \alpha_{\min} + (\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min}) \sigma(f_\theta(\gamma(\mathbf{x}))),$$

with σ(·) the logistic sigmoid and bounds 0 < αmin < αmax. The bracketing serves three purposes: (i) it enforces αθ(x) > 0 identically, satisfying the positivity required by the diffusivity form \(2\); (ii) it bounds the diffusivity contrast so that the system matrix A(αθ) remains well-conditioned for the Jacobi inner solver of Appendix D.2; (iii) it prevents the early-training optimizer from probing extreme values that would yield zero gradients through the saturation of the sigmoid or through stiff numerical regimes. Concrete numerical values for αmin, αmax are calibrated once per dataset using the bulk-to-defect contrast described in Appendix A.5, and are reported in Appendix D.4.

Discrete total-variation regularizer. The continuous regularizer R(αθ) = R Ω ∥∇αθ(x)∥ dx is implemented on the same Cartesian grid used by the forward solver, with per-axis dimensions Nx, Ny, Nz and spacings ∆x, ∆y, ∆z formally defined in Appendix D.2. With αi,j,k := αθ(xi,j,k) at grid node (i, j, k), we use the isotropic forward-difference discretization

$$\mathcal{R}(\alpha_\theta) = \Delta x \Delta y \Delta z \sum_{i,j,k=0}^{N_x-1, N_y-1, N_z-1} \left[ (\delta_x \alpha_{i,j,k} / \Delta x)^2 + (\delta_y \alpha_{i,j,k} / \Delta y)^2 + (\delta_z \alpha_{i,j,k} / \Delta z)^2 + \epsilon_{\text{TV}}^2 \right]^{1/2}, \quad (22)$$

where the per-axis forward differences are δxαi,j,k := α(i+1) mod Nx,j,k − αi,j,k, δyαi,j,k := αi,(j+1) mod Ny,k −αi,j,k (lateral periodic wrap, matching the lateral periodic BCs of Appendix A.5\), and δzαi,j,k := αi,j,k+1 − αi,j,k for k < Nz − 1 with δzαi,j,Nz−1 := 0 (the adiabatic and Robin BCs do not constrain α at the front/back faces, so the through-thickness forward difference at the top slice vanishes; the lateral δx, δy contributions on that slice are retained). The smoothing constant ϵTV > 0 has dimension [α]/[length] and ensures differentiability at vanishing gradients (concrete value in Appendix D.4, distinct from the perturbation parameter ε used in Appendix B.1\). The penalty promotes piecewise-constant αθ aligned with the model assumed in Appendix A.2 and supplements the high-eigenmode suppression provided by frequency annealing.

### D.2 Discrete forward solver

Ambient shift. Throughout this appendix and Section 4, the discrete temperature field Tn denotes the deviation from the ambient temperature Tamb defined in Appendix A.5. Under this convention the synthetic post-flash and PVC near-uniform initial conditions of Appendix A.5 have ambient floor zero and the convective Robin condition n · (α∇T) = −h(T − Tamb) becomes the homogeneous form n ·(α∇T) = −h T. All measured frames Tˆ i in Section 4.3 are likewise interpreted as ambientshifted observations Tˆ i − Tamb. The state equation \(8\) therefore retains the homogeneous form A(αθ)Tn+1 = Tn across both datasets, with no affine right-hand side.

Spatial discretization. The bulk equation ∂tT = ∇ · (α∇T) on the slab Ω = [0, L] 2 × [0, H] is discretized on a uniform Cartesian grid with Nx × Ny × Nz nodes and spacings ∆x, ∆y, ∆z aligned with the lateral and through-thickness axes. Continuing the one-dimensional argument of Proposition 1, the second-order finite-volume diffusion operator L(αθ) ∈ R Ng×Ng acts at an interior node (i, j, k) by

$$\begin{aligned} [\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)\mathbf{T}]_{i,j,k} &= \frac{1}{\Delta x^2} [\bar{\alpha}_{i+1/2,j,k}(T_{i+1,j,k} - T_{i,j,k}) - \bar{\alpha}_{i-1/2,j,k}(T_{i,j,k} - T_{i-1,j,k})] \\ &+ \frac{1}{\Delta y^2} [\bar{\alpha}_{i,j+1/2,k}(T_{i,j+1,k} - T_{i,j,k}) - \bar{\alpha}_{i,j-1/2,k}(T_{i,j,k} - T_{i,j-1,k})] \\ &+ \frac{1}{\Delta z^2} [\bar{\alpha}_{i,j,k+1/2}(T_{i,j,k+1} - T_{i,j,k}) - \bar{\alpha}_{i,j,k-1/2}(T_{i,j,k} - T_{i,j,k-1})] , \end{aligned}$$

with each face-centered coefficient α¯ computed as the harmonic mean of its two neighbouring cellcentered values. By Proposition 1 this is the unique discrete realization of effective-flux continuity for the single-α model.

Boundary conditions. Lateral periodicity is implemented by circular padding so that the stencil at i = 0 wraps to i = Nx − 1 and similarly for j. Through-thickness adiabatic conditions (zero-flux Neumann) are implemented by replicate padding on the front z = 0 and back z = H faces, which enforces α¯i,j,−1/2(Ti,j,0 − Ti,j,−1) = 0 at the boundary face by zeroing the cross-face temperature increment. The PVC convective Robin condition described in Appendix A.5 replaces the back-face replicate padding by a ghost-cell update consistent with n · (α∇T) = −h(T − Tamb).

Implicit-Euler step. The state at frame n + 1 is the solution of the sparse linear system



| $\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta) \mathbf{T}^{n+1} = \mathbf{T}^n,$ | $\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta) := \mathbf{I} - \Delta t \mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta),$ | (23) |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
|--------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|

which is unconditionally stable [10] so that ∆t is set by the camera frame rate rather than the CFL bound ∆t ≤ ∆x 2/(2D αmax).

Symmetry, sign, and diagonal dominance. Under the homogeneous boundary conditions enforced by the ambient shift above (lateral periodic, adiabatic through-thickness, and the homogeneous Robin form n · (α∇T) = −h T on the PVC back face), the harmonic-mean construction makes L(αθ) symmetric: each interior face conductance apq := ¯αpq/∆x 2 d (d ∈ {x, y, z} the axis joining grid nodes p, q) appears symmetrically in row p and row q, lateral periodic wrapping preserves this symmetry, and a finite-volume back-face flux balance n · (α∇T) · ∆x∆y = −h Tp · ∆x∆y divided by the nodal volume ∆x∆y∆z yields a strictly positive diagonal sink βp := h/∆z on each back-face Robin row. Writing q ∼ p for the set of interior-face neighbours of node p and βp ≥ 0 for the per-row Robin sink (zero for non-Robin rows), the entries of L(αθ) are

$$[\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)]_{pp} = -\beta_p - \sum_{q \sim p} a_{pq}, \quad [\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)]_{pq} = a_{pq} \geq 0 \text{ for } q \sim p,$$

$$\sum_q [\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)]_{pq} = -\beta_p \leq 0,$$

so the constant-temperature mode is annihilated on rows without a Robin sink and damped at rate βp on Robin boundary rows. Therefore L(αθ) is symmetric and negative semi-definite under purely periodic/adiabatic BCs, and negative definite once at least one βp > 0. Strict diagonal dominance of A(αθ) follows from the identity term and the (non-negative) Robin sink:

$$[\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta)]_{pp} - \sum_{q \neq p} |[\mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta)]_{pq}| = (1 + \Delta t \beta_p + \Delta t \sum_{q \sim p} a_{pq}) - \Delta t \sum_{q \sim p} a_{pq} = 1 + \Delta t \beta_p \geq 1 > 0,$$

holding strictly on every row independently of the conductance pattern, so A(αθ) is symmetric positive-definite and the Jacobi iteration below converges geometrically.

Jacobi inner solver. For autodiff compatibility on the GPU we avoid sparse-matrix factorizations and solve \(23\) by a fixed unrolled number K of Jacobi iterations. With the splitting A(αθ) = D + R where D is the diagonal of A and R the off-diagonal remainder, the iteration with right-hand side b := Tn is



| $\mathbf{T}^{(\kappa+1)} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{R}\mathbf{T}^{(\kappa)})$ , | $\kappa = 0, 1, \dots, K-1$ , | (24) |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|------|
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|------|

initialized from T(0) := Tn (warm-started by the previous frame's solution). Equation \(24\) reduces to a 7-point stencil convolution, expressible as a single fused kernel in standard autodiff frameworks [39] and accelerated with torch.compile just-in-time autotuning. The iteration count K is chosen large enough that the residual ∥AT(K) − b∥ falls below the noise floor of the camera measurements (Appendix D.4\); empirically K = 50 suffices for our grid resolution and ∆t. Because the Jacobi iteration converges to the unique exact solution of \(23\) under the strict diagonal dominance established above, we treat T(K) as the (numerical) exact state Tn+1, and the discrete adjoint of Appendix D.3 below is derived for this exact linear solve. The resulting parameter gradients are exact for the converged objective up to the same iterative tolerance.

#### D.3 Adjoint recurrence and gradient assembly

Constraint and augmented Lagrangian. Set T0 from the prescribed initial condition (Appendix A.5\) and define the state residual at step n by



| $\mathbf{F}^n(\mathbf{T}^n, \mathbf{T}^{n-1}, \alpha_\theta) := \mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta) \mathbf{T}^n - \mathbf{T}^{n-1} = \mathbf{0},$ | $n = 1, \dots, N_t$ | (25) |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------|------|
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------|------|

Define the per-step data term

$$\ell^n(\mathbf{T}^n) := \left\{ \left\| \Pi_{\mathbf{F}} \mathbf{T}^n - \hat{\mathbf{T}}_i \right\|^2 \right. \quad \text{if } n = n_i \in \{n_1, \dots, n_M\}, \\ \quad \text{otherwise,}$$

so the data part of the objective is PNt n=1 ℓ n(Tn) and matches the data-fidelity sum in \(9\). We introduce adjoint variables µ n ∈ R Ng for n = 1, . . . , Nt as Lagrange multipliers of the state residuals, giving the augmented functional

$$\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta; \{\mathbf{T}^n\}, \{\boldsymbol{\mu}^n\}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} \ell^n(\mathbf{T}^n) + \lambda \mathcal{R}(\alpha_\theta) - \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} (\boldsymbol{\mu}^n)^\top \mathbf{F}^n(\mathbf{T}^n, \mathbf{T}^{n-1}, \alpha_\theta).$$

For any {Tn} that satisfy the state equation \(25\), Le = Jdisc(θ) regardless of the multipliers µ n.

Stationarity and adjoint recurrence. Computing dJdisc/dθ via the chain rule and discarding terms that are eliminated by the state equation, it suffices to compute dLe/dθ in the way that does not require the implicit derivatives dTn/dθ. To eliminate them, we choose the multipliers so that the partial derivative of Le with respect to each Tn vanishes. Differentiating,

$$\frac{\partial \tilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \mathbf{T}^n} = \frac{\partial \ell^n}{\partial \mathbf{T}^n} - (\boldsymbol{\mu}^n)^\top \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^n}{\partial \mathbf{T}^n} - (\boldsymbol{\mu}^{n+1})^\top \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^{n+1}}{\partial \mathbf{T}^n} = 0,$$

where we set µ Nt+1 = 0 because no constraint F Nt+1 exists. Reading off the partials from \(25\),

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^n}{\partial \mathbf{T}^n} = \mathbf{A}(\alpha_\theta), \quad \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^{n+1}}{\partial \mathbf{T}^n} = -\mathbf{I},$$

the stationarity condition becomes


$$\mathbf{A}(\alpha_0)^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}^n = (\partial \ell^n / \partial \mathbf{T}^n)^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}^{n+1}, \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}^{n+1} = \mathbf{0}, \quad (26)$$

which is \(10\). The recurrence is integrated backwards in time from n = Nt down to n = 1. The same Jacobi inner solver of Appendix D.2 applies because the finite-volume discretization with diagonal Robin sink βp := h/∆z makes A(αθ) ⊤ = A(αθ) on every dataset (lateral periodic, adiabatic through-thickness, and homogeneous Robin), so the forward and backward sweeps share the same SPD system matrix.

Gradient assembly. With µ n chosen so that ∂Le/∂Tn = 0 for n = 1, . . . , Nt, the total derivative of Jdisc with respect to θ is the partial derivative of Le with respect to θ at fixed {Tn}:

$$\frac{d\mathcal{J}_{\text{disc}}}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial(\lambda \mathcal{R})}{\partial \theta} - \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} (\boldsymbol{\mu}^n)^\top \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^n}{\partial \alpha_\theta} \frac{\partial \alpha_\theta}{\partial \theta}.$$

Because F n = A(αθ)Tn − Tn−1 and A(αθ) = I − ∆tL(αθ) depends on αθ only through the diffusion operator,

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^n}{\partial \alpha_\theta} = -\Delta t \frac{\partial(\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)\mathbf{T}^n)}{\partial \alpha_\theta},$$

so the assembled gradient becomes


$$\frac{d\mathcal{J}_{\text{disc}}}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial(\lambda\mathcal{R})}{\partial\theta} + \Delta t \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} (\boldsymbol{\mu}^n)^T \frac{\partial(\mathbf{L}(\alpha_\theta)\mathbf{T}^n)}{\partial\alpha_\theta} \frac{\partial\alpha_\theta}{\partial\theta}, \quad (27)$$

which is \(11\). The Jacobian ∂αθ/∂θ is obtained by ordinary backpropagation through the MLP at each grid node, and the term ∂(L(αθ)Tn)/∂αθ is a sparse local stencil whose nonzeros are determined by the harmonic-mean derivatives ∂α¯i+1/2/∂αi = 2α 2 i+1/(αi + αi+1) 2 and similarly for ∂/∂αi+1.

Memory cost. The forward sweep produces T1 , . . . , TNt , which the gradient assembly \(27\) consumes one frame at a time during the backward sweep that produces µ n. Because \(26\) requires only µ n+1 and Tn at any given step, only one current and one trailing temperature slice plus the running gradient accumulator need to reside in memory beyond the cost of a single forward solve. The naive backpropagation-through-time alternative would store the entire computational graph of the K unrolled Jacobi iterations at every one of the Nt time steps, with peak memory scaling as O(KNgNt); the adjoint replaces this with O(Ng) at the cost of one backward solve, an exact restatement of the implicit-function-theorem trick in a sequential setting [6].

Sensitivity to initial conditions. If the initial temperature T0 is itself uncertain or learnable, the same calculation under the augmented Lagrangian above (which carries a − P n (µ n) ⊤F n term) gives

$$\frac{d\mathcal{J}_{\text{disc}}}{d\mathbf{T}^0} = -(\mu^1)^\top \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}^1}{\partial \mathbf{T}^0} = -(\mu^1)^\top (-\mathbf{I}) = +\mu^1,$$

where only F 1 = AT1 − T0 depends on T0 . The sign is consistent with the adjoint recurrence \(26\) and the gradient assembly \(27\) under the same Lagrangian convention. We do not exercise this option in the experiments of Section 5, where the post-flash initial profile is calibrated externally per Appendix A.5.

Consistency with the continuous adjoint. The recurrence \(26\) is the implicit-Euler discretization, applied backwards in time, of the continuous adjoint problem associated with the bulk equation ∂tT = ∇ · (α∇T) and the cumulative observation functional P i ∥ΠΓT(·, ti) − Tˆ i∥ 2 : namely, −∂tp = ∇ · (α∇p) + P i 2Π∗ Γ (ΠΓT(·, ti) − Tˆ i) δ(t − ti) with terminal condition p(·, tend) = 0. The discretize-then-optimize formulation we use thus inherits the same backward-parabolic structure as the continuous setting [37], while delivering exact gradients of the discrete objective Jdisc rather than discretized gradients of the continuous one.

Simulation configuration. All synthetic samples are generated on the unitless slab Ω = [0, 10]2 × [0, 1] at 64×64×16 grid resolution. The lateral (x, y) faces use periodic boundary conditions and the through-thickness (z) faces use adiabatic Neumann conditions, both consistent with Appendix A.5. The transient is recorded over 100 frames with ∆t = 0.05. Material properties are sampled from uniform distributions: bulk diffusivity αbase ∼ U(0.1, 0.2), defect diffusivity αdefect ∼ U(0.005, 0.015). Each sample contains one to four ellipsoidal, cylindrical, or box-shaped subsurface defects buried at varying depth. The dataset of 1,000 samples is split into a homogeneous configuration (constant αbase) and a layered configuration (three to four bulk strata along z), and we evaluate every method on the full 1,000-sample benchmark per configuration.

Dimensional analysis. The unitless solver evolves in (t/ttotal, x/L0) coordinates with effective diffusivity αsim = αphys ttotal/L2 0 , the Fourier-number scaling of Appendix A.5. A simulation with αsim ≈ 0.1 corresponds to highly conductive silicon (αphys ≈ 10−4 m2/s) on a microsecond timescale at L0 = 10 µm, or to a resistive polymer (αphys ≈ 10−7 m2/s) on a millisecond timescale at the same length scale; a single set of unitless parameters thus represents widely different material classes by reinterpreting L0 and ttotal.

Defect contrast scaling. The defect-to-bulk diffusivity ratio is set to roughly 1 : 20. We choose this contrast over the realistic air-to-solid ratio of > 1 : 1000 for numerical stability: at 1 : 1000 the linear system A(αθ) = I − ∆tL(αθ) becomes severely ill-conditioned and the iterative solver stalls. At 1 : 20 the surface-temperature signature already saturates against the perfect-insulator limit (further reductions in αdefect leave the boundary thermograms unchanged), while the condition number remains compatible with efficient gradient-based optimization.

#### E.2 Metrics

Synthetic 3D reconstruction. For each test sample, let α ⋆ denote the ground-truth diffusivity field on the Ng = NxNyNz grid and αˆ the recovered field. We report:

- MSE = Ng ∥αˆ − α ⋆∥ 2 2 .
- PSNR = 10 log10 (αmax − αmin) 2/MSE .
- SSIM [57] computed slice-by-slice along z and averaged.
- IoU = |(ˆα < τ ) ∩ (α ⋆ < τ )|/|(ˆα < τ ) ∪ (α ⋆ < τ )| with defect threshold τ = 0.03, chosen as twice the upper end of the defect distribution to provide a clean binary defect mask.

The metrics are computed per sample and averaged over the full 1,000-sample benchmark per configuration.

Real PVC: 2D segmentation. Following the convention of the PVC-Infrared benchmark [59], the recovered 3D αˆθ is projected onto a 2D defect mask through the depth-averaged anomaly contrast described in Appendix H, and IoU and Dice are computed against the 2D ground-truth annotation.

Real PVC: 2.5D depth. For each pixel p assigned to a defect, let d ⋆ p denote the ground-truth depth (in mm) supplied by the PVC-Depth benchmark [58] and ˆdp the predicted depth from the spatial-median anomaly contrast along z (Appendix H\). Let P denote the set of pixels assigned to a defect by the 2D mask above and δp := max( ˆdp/d⋆ p , d⋆ p/ ˆdp) the per-pixel depth ratio. We report the standard depth metrics of Eigen et al. [13]: Absolute Relative error Abs Rel = 1 |P | P p∈P ˆdp − d ⋆ p |/d⋆ p , RMSE = 1 |P | P p∈P ( ˆdp −d ⋆ p ) 2 1/2 , and the threshold accuracies δ < 1.25k := 1 |P | |{p ∈ P : δp < 1.25k}| for k ∈ {1, 2, 3}.

Frequency-domain diagnostics. Defect Edge F1 thresholds the gradient magnitude ∥∇α∥ at 50% of the ground-truth maximum, restricts to a dilated defect-boundary mask, and reports the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Radial power spectrum extracts a tight 16×16×8 crop centered on the defect cluster, computes the 3D Fourier transform, and radially averages the power, with frequencies normalized so that the through-thickness Nyquist limit equals ∼ 8 cycles per unit length.

# F Baseline Implementations

This appendix gives the configuration of every baseline used in Section 5. Unless otherwise stated, all baselines are tuned with the same compute budget as NeFTY on the same hardware (Appendix E.3\).

### F.1 Soft-constrained PINN family

PINN with GradNorm. The vanilla PINN baseline instantiates two networks, a temperature surrogate Tϕ(x, t) and a diffusivity surrogate αθ(x), and minimizes the composite loss of equation \(4\). The PDE residual is evaluated through automatic differentiation at Nc space-time collocation points sampled uniformly per iteration, and the initial-condition penalty matches Tϕ(x, 0) to the prescribed Gaussian post-flash profile of Appendix A.5. The two physics weights λPDE and λIC are dynamically balanced through GradNorm [7], which normalizes per-loss gradient magnitudes to a common scale. We use Tϕ depth 5 and width 128 (4.91 M total parameters across both networks), Nc = 24,576

collocation points per iteration, Adam with learning rate 10−3 , and 22,000 iterations: 2.2× more iterations and 2× more parameters than NeFTY. Despite this advantage, the recovered αθ saturates at a near-trivial constant (Section 5.2\), in line with the structural decoupling proven in Appendix C.

SPINN, Causal-PINN, DCGD. For the three modern PINN variants we follow the official implementations and hyperparameter settings released by the original authors [8, 25, 56], paired with the same diffusivity-network αθ and the same 24,576 collocation points and 22,000 iterations as the vanilla PINN above so that compute is matched across the family.

#### F.2 Voxel-grid optimization

The Grid Opt. baseline replaces the neural diffusivity field αθ of Section 4.1 by a learnable tensor α ∈ R Nx×Ny×Nz (initialized at the bulk mean) and optimizes it through the same differentiable solver and adjoint of Section 4. This isolates the contribution of the continuous coordinate-based prior. Grid Opt. uses the same Adam learning-rate schedule, the same number of iterations (10,000), and the same TV regularizer as NeFTY; without the neural prior it converges to a noisy field with ringing artifacts (Section 5.2\).

### F.3 Supervised U-Net baselines

U-Net (Full). A 3D U-Net [45] adapted for spatiotemporal regression. The input temperature sequence of shape (B, 1, 100, 64, 64) is interpolated along the temporal axis from 100 to 16 depth slices and treated as a (B, 1, 16, 64, 64) volume. The architecture follows a four-level encoderdecoder with channel sizes {32, 64, 128, 256}, double 3D convolutions and max-pooling on the contracting path, and trilinear upsampling with skip connections on the expansive path; the output passes through a sigmoid scaled to [αmin, αmax]. Training uses MSE between predicted and groundtruth α on the full synthetic dataset (defects included), which represents an upper bound consuming the volumetric labels that the label-free baselines never see.

U-Net (Sound-Only). The same architecture trained exclusively on defect-free samples. This baseline probes the susceptibility of purely data-driven inversion to out-of-distribution defects, and is included in the main Table 1 as a data-driven zero-shot reference.

### F.4 Classical thermography heuristics

PPT. Pulsed Phase Thermography [26, 32] computes the per-pixel discrete Fourier transform of the surface-temperature decay, identifies the dominant phase peak at frequency fpeak, and inverts depth through the diffusion-length formula d = 1.82 p αphys/(πfpeak). We use the implementation of the original authors with six target frequencies spanning 0.01-0.5 Hz, matching the PVC-Depth recording rate.

TSR. Thermographic Signal Reconstruction [49, 50] fits a polynomial in log t to each pixel's temperature decay, identifies the time tpeak at which the second log-derivative peaks, and inverts depth as d = 1.8 √ αphys tpeak. We use the standard fifth-order polynomial fit and the per-pixel tpeak extraction.

### F.5 Swin-UNETR (real PVC, zero-shot transfer)

The supervised Swin-UNETR baseline of Hatamizadeh et al. [21] is trained on the synthetic dataset of Appendix E.1 for 500 epochs under MSE supervision (loss converges to 4 × 10−6 ) and transferred zero-shot to the real PVC datasets. Inputs are temporally resampled from the PVC frame rate to the synthetic 100-frame format and amplitude-normalized to the synthetic peak-temperature scale; the predicted 3D αˆ is projected to 2D masks and 2.5D depth through the same procedure used for NeFTY (Appendix H\).

### G.5 Frequency-domain diagnostics: Edge F1 and radial power spectrum

To quantify whether each method recovers sharp defect boundaries (Section 5.2\), we threshold the gradient magnitude ∥∇α∥ at 50% of the ground-truth maximum, restrict to a dilated boundary mask, and compute Edge F1. NeFTY is the only label-free method whose recovered field carries detectable defect-scale gradients; the four PINN variants produce featureless interiors. Figure 9 plots the radial power spectrum, computed over a 16 × 16 × 8 defect crop and radially averaged across the same 1,000-sample benchmark. NeFTY tracks the ground-truth spectrum from the lowest wavenumbers up to the Nyquist limit (∼ 8 cycles per unit length in the z-direction), while all four PINN variants collapse to near-zero power across the spectrum and Grid Opt. falls off above the mid frequencies. #### G.7 Failure mode

We identify one consistent failure mode of NeFTY: when defects are placed shallow and close to the laser-illuminated front surface, the surface-only data become weakly sensitive to the backface diffusivity, and the optimizer occasionally introduces a spurious low-α artifact along the back boundary that is consistent with the surface decay but not with the ground truth (Figure 14\). The defect itself is still recovered at the correct lateral position, so the artifact does not affect 2D segmentation, but it propagates into the depth metric of Table 2 for shallow defects. Stronger back-face priors, a multi-side acquisition that adds back-face thermograms, or a depth-adaptive TV weight that suppresses high-curvature solutions far from observed pixels would all reduce this failure mode; we leave a quantitative ablation of these remedies to future work.

# H Real PVC Data Details

### H.1 Datasets

The PVC-Infrared [59] and PVC-Depth [58] benchmarks consist of 19 and 38 pulsed-thermography sequences respectively on 100 × 100 × 5 mm PVC specimens with cylindrical flat-bottom holes of varying diameter and depth, recorded with a long-wave-infrared camera at second-scale dynamics under flash-lamp excitation. PVC-Infrared annotates each specimen with a 2D binary defect mask derived from CAD drawings and bottom-view inspection; PVC-Depth additionally annotates discrete defect depths. Neither benchmark provides a 3D volumetric diffusivity ground truth, since obtaining such labels requires destructive sectioning or X-ray CT (5-96 hours per specimen [17]). Thermal sequences are temporally resampled and amplitude-normalized as documented in Appendix F.5 for compatibility across baselines.

### H.2 Physical adaptations

The synthetic benchmark of Appendix E.1 is set on micron-scale specimens at microsecond timescales with negligible convective dissipation, while the PVC specimens are centimeter-scale at second-scale dynamics; three physical adaptations of Appendix A.5 are required for NeFTY to apply without re-training the ground-truth simulator.

Initial condition. The Gaussian post-flash profile is replaced by a near-uniform front-face flash T0(x) = Tamb + A exp(−z 2/(2w 2 z )), with wz the absorption depth and A calibrated against the first measured frame; this matches the broad flash-lamp excitation used by Wei et al. [58, 59].

Convective Robin boundary. On the back face we replace the synthetic adiabatic Neumann boundary by a Robin condition n·(α∇T) = −h (T −Tamb), with h > 0 a calibrated convective coefficient. The unitless h is set to match the asymptotic Biot regime of natural convection on PVC over the relevant second-scale window. After the ambient shift of Section 4.2, this becomes n · (α∇T) = −h T , so the discrete implicit-Euler step remains affine-free and the diagonal-dominance argument of Appendix D.2 carries over without change.

Dimensional rescaling. The unitless solver evolves in (t/ttotal, x/L0) coordinates with effective diffusivity αsim = αphys ttotal/L2 0 (Appendix A.5\). For PVC we set L0 = 100 mm (lateral specimen size) and ttotal to the recording window of the benchmark, so the unitless diffusivity ranges of the synthetic benchmark map onto the physical PVC diffusivity range. Lateral boundaries remain periodic when the camera footprint is far from the specimen edge; on cropped specimens we replace lateral periodicity by adiabatic Neumann boundaries.

#### H.3 Projection from 3D diffusivity to 2D and 2.5D labels

The reconstructed 3D field αˆθ(x, y, z) is reduced to the labels supplied by the benchmarks through two complementary projections.

2D defect mask. For each lateral pixel (x, y), we compute the depth-averaged diffusivity α¯(x, y) = N −1 z P z αˆθ(x, y, z) and the normalized one-sided diffusivity deficit d(x, y) = [1 − α¯(x, y)/αbase]+. Because sound pixels should have α¯(x, y) ≈ αbase, we estimate the reconstruction noise floor by σˆ = 1.4826 MAD{d(x, y)}(x,y) and define the mask to be 1{d(x, y) > 2ˆσ}. The same fixed postprocessing rule is applied to NeFTY, Grid Opt., Swin-UNETR, and SPINN; PPT/TSR follow their publication-default segmentation rules.

2.5D defect depth. For each pixel p inside the predicted 2D mask we extract the through-thickness depth ˆdp as the spatial median of the depths of the z-voxels whose αˆθ falls below the bulk by more than the noise floor of the defect-free region. PPT and TSR use their published depth formulas with the calibrated PVC diffusivity; Swin-UNETR uses the same spatial-median rule applied to its supervised prediction.

# I Validation of the Differentiable Heat Diffusion Simulator

We validate the correctness of our differentiable heat diffusion simulator, implemented using an implicit Euler time discretization solved via a Jacobi iterative scheme, through both analytical consistency checks and numerical experiments.

# I.1 Governing Equation and Analytical Behavior

To validate our simulator, we fix α as a constant, set the source term Q in Eq. \(1\) to zero, and prescribe a Gaussian initial temperature distribution. Under these settings the simulator reduces to


$$\frac{\partial T(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial t} = \alpha \nabla^2 T(\mathbf{x}, t), \quad (28)$$

where T(x, t) denotes temperature and α is a constant, isotropic thermal diffusivity.

For an initial Gaussian temperature distribution

$$T(\mathbf{x}, 0) = A \exp \left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\|^2}{2\sigma_0^2} \right), \quad (29)$$

where A > 0 is the peak amplitude, x0 ∈ R 3 the source center, and σ0 > 0 the initial standard deviation along each axis. The analytical solution of Eq. \(28\) remains Gaussian for all t > 0. In particular, the variance along each spatial dimension evolves as



| $\sigma^2(t) = \sigma_0^2 + 2\alpha t$ | (30) |
|----------------------------------------|------|
|----------------------------------------|------|

which implies a linear growth rate

$$\frac{d\sigma^2}{dt} = 2\alpha. \quad (31)$$

This property provides a quantitative criterion for validating the physical fidelity of a numerical diffusion solver.

#### I.2 Numerical Setup

We discretize the spatial domain using a uniform Cartesian grid and advance Eq. \(28\) in time using an implicit Euler scheme. The resulting linear system at each time step is solved using a fixed number of Jacobi iterations, yielding a fully differentiable simulation pipeline.

Periodic boundary conditions are used in the x and y directions, and zero-flux (Neumann) boundary conditions are applied along the z axis. Temperature observations are taken from the top surface of the domain to match the sensing configuration used in thermal imaging.

### I.3 Gaussian Diffusion Rate Verification



Figure 16: Gaussian Diffusion Rate Validation. (Left) Temporal evolution of the temperature variance σ 2 along the x and y directions, measured on the surface for a diffusing Gaussian heat source with constant diffusivity α = 0.1. Both σ 2 x (t) and σ 2 y (t) grow linearly over time and closely follow the analytical prediction σ 2 (t) = σ 2 0 + 2αt. (Right) Comparison between the measured average slope dσ 2/dt and the theoretical value 2α, showing a relative error of 0.16%. This result quantitatively confirms that the proposed simulator reproduces the correct diffusion rate of the heat equation.

To verify the analytical variance growth in Eq. \(30\), we simulate the diffusion of a 3D Gaussian heat source with constant diffusivity α = 0.1 in a large domain. At each time step, we compute the temperature-weighted second moments on the surface,

$$\sigma_x^2(t) = \frac{\sum(x - \bar{x})^2 T(x, y, t)}{\sum T(x, y, t)}, \quad \sigma_y^2(t) = \frac{\sum(y - \bar{y})^2 T(x, y, t)}{\sum T(x, y, t)}, \quad (32)$$

with x¯ = Px T(x, y, t)/ PT(x, y, t) and y¯ defined analogously, the temperature-weighted lateral centroids on the observed top surface.

Linear regression is performed on σ x (t) and σ 2 y (t) after an initial transient period. As shown in Fig. 16, both measured variances exhibit a linear increase over time, with an average slope of 0.1997, compared to the theoretical value 2α = 0.2000. The resulting relative error is 0.16%, demonstrating excellent agreement with the analytical solution.

# J Limitations and Future Work

Test-time optimization cost. Unlike supervised feedforward inversion (e.g., U-Net) that runs in milliseconds, NeFTY relies on per-specimen test-time optimization. Recovering one specimen takes ∼ 10 minutes on a single high-end GPU (Table 9\). This cost is acceptable for offline NDE inspection where accuracy is paramount but limits applicability in high-throughput manufacturing lines. Amortized-inference strategies, for example meta-learning or hypernetworks that predict a good initialization for the neural field, could substantially reduce the number of optimization steps and are a natural follow-up.

Quantitative diffusivity under high contrast. As discussed in Appendix E.1, we operate at a defect-to-bulk diffusivity contrast of ∼ 1: 20. At realistic air-to-solid ratios (> 1: 1000), the linear system A(αθ) becomes severely ill-conditioned and the iterative solver stalls. NeFTY recovers defect geometry reliably in this regime (Section 5.2, Section 5.3\) but recovering the precise quantitative magnitude of α inside high-contrast voids remains hard because the diffusion time inside an insulator scales as L 2/α and saturates for α → 0. Preconditioned or multi-grid solvers within the differentiable loop could extend NeFTY to the regime of stiff air voids.

Lack of 3D real labels. The real PVC datasets used in Section 5.3 provide only 2D defect masks and discrete defect depths from CAD; full 3D volumetric diffusivity ground truth is not available for any public NDT dataset, and obtaining it requires destructive testing or X-ray CT [17]. We therefore evaluate real-world performance through projected 2D and 2.5D labels rather than direct volumetric error. Building a benchmark that pairs surface thermograms with volumetric diffusivity ground truth would constitute a significant standalone materials-science contribution and remains a future direction.
