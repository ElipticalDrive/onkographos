Abstract and Figures
Progress in swept source laser technology and graphics processing unit (GPU) have led to the realization of real time four-dimensional optical coherence tomography (4D-OCT). Various rendering algorithms have been introduced for volumetric OCT leading to enhanced spatial comprehension. These algorithms still cannot provide sufficient visual realism. Shadowing is one of the advanced rendering techniques used to provide realistic spatial comprehension for objects in space. We developed and implemented a shadow extension for ray casting for real-time 4D-OCT, using both a swept source (SS)-OCT and spectral domain (SD)-OCT. Our technique builds upon previously developed ray casting techniques, adding an additional shadowing computation. The shadow extension for ray casting yields greater three-dimensionality of objects via self-shadowing, and also improves depth perception of objects. We imaged a beveled needle hovering over a flat surface and found that the positioning of a beveled needle can be accurately determined in relation to the environment. The shadowing algorithm was implemented using texture memory of the GPU, which realized video rendering rate. The complete processing pipeline requires 47 ms with a volume size of 1024 × 256 × 100, with a 100 kHz sweep rate SS-OCT. This new algorithm may enhance interactive surgical guidance during ophthalmic microsurgery.

ABSTRACT
Progress in swept source laser technology and graphics processing unit (GPU) have led to the realization
of real time four-dimensional optical coherence tomography (4D-OCT). Various rendering algorithms have
been introduced for volumetric OCT leading to enhanced spatial comprehension. These algorithms still cannot
provide sufficient visual realism. Shadowing is one of the advanced rendering techniques used to provide
realistic spatial comprehension for objects in space. We developed and implemented a shadow extension
for ray casting for real-time 4D-OCT, using both a swept source (SS)-OCT and spectral domain (SD)-OCT.
Our technique builds upon previously developed ray casting techniques, adding an additional shadowing
computation. The shadow extension for ray casting yields greater three-dimensionality of objects via self-
shadowing, and also improves depth perception of objects. We imaged a beveled needle hovering over a flat
surface and found that the positioning of a beveled needle can be accurately determined in relation to the
environment. The shadowing algorithm was implemented using texture memory of the GPU, which realized
video rendering rate. The complete processing pipeline requires 47 ms with a volume size of 1024 ×256 ×
100, with a 100 kHz sweep rate SS-OCT. This new algorithm may enhance interactive surgical guidance during
ophthalmic microsurgery.
1. Introduction
Optical coherence tomography (OCT) [1] is an interferometric imag-
ing technique capable of noninvasive acquisition of micrometer-scale
cross-sectional images and provides diagnostic and surgical guidance
in fields such as ophthalmology. Advances in galvanometric, laser, and
camera technologies led to improved acquisition speeds in OCT imaging
[2,3], which facilitates time-resolved volumetric recording. In addition,
the processing time of volumetric data for Fourier domain (FD)-OCT
has been significantly reduced due to the progress in graphics process-
ing unit (GPU) technology, allowing real-time, video-rate volumetric
OCT, which is often cited as 4D-OCT [4–8].
Advances in 4D-OCT have facilitated surgical guidance in oph-
thalmic microsurgery [9–13]. Microscopy, which has been convention-
ally used in ophthalmic microsurgery, provides only an en-face view,
and therefore, surgeons indirectly infer spatial and depth information
via instrument shadowing and stereoscopy [14]. Combining microscopy
with real-time 4D-OCT facilitates accurate spatial inference and depth
analysis for tool precision during surgery [10–13]. Computed tomogra-
phy (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasound have also

plays an important role intraoperatively in ophthalmic microsurgery,
several efforts have been made to utilize computer graphics for en-
hanced volumetric rendering algorithms to achieve visual realism of
space [9,18].
Ray casting, one of the most widely used volume rendering tech-
niques in medical imaging, provides a comprehensive spatial view
with relatively low computational load compared to other rendering
techniques that achieve similar visual quality [19]. Naturally, ray cast-
ing has been applied to OCT volumetric visualization [4–8], however,
implementation of basic ray casting only showed rather hazy surfaces
and poor depth cues. In 2016, Viehland et al. demonstrated that depth
cues may be augmented by edge enhancement, depth-based shading,
Phong lighting, and stereoscopy [9]. However, it is well known that
shadows provide better realism and depth information in 3D imag-
ing [20]. Another recent study proposed real-time corneal segmentation

and needle tracking in volumetric OCT, which also provide quantitative

During the imaging of heterogeneous surfaces, basic rendering tech-
niques such as ray casting and Phong shading alone are often inade-
quate for accurate spatial comprehension of OCT volumetric data for
surgical guidance. Visual realism can be notably improved with shad-
owing, which helps improve human perception of spatial relationships
between objects with depth cues [22]. Advanced rendering techniques
including shadows have already been utilized in CT and ultrasound
[23,24].
In this study, we present the implementation of shadow extension
for ray casting [25] for volume rendering in order to improve visual
realism of OCT volumetric image [26]. The shadow extension for
ray casting technique was built on a single GPU architecture and
processed simultaneously with real-time volume rendering rates using
parallel computing via Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA).
Real time 4D-OCT with shadowing facilitates spatial comprehension
of the surgical sites and determination of the position and pose of
surgical instruments in order to accurately perform delicate tasks such
as ophthalmic microsurgery.

2.1. Implementation of shadow rendering
As shown in Fig. 1(a), the shading effect over a sphere delineates
its 3D structure, without addressing the depth cues. The position of the
shadow on a surface plane, also known as the planar shadow, influences
the perceived position of the sphere. A volumetric OCT image naturally
contains intrinsic shadows created by the source beam, with a generally
fixed direction of the shadow and its darkness. However, adding natural
shadows that can be adjusted as desired provide better realism in
4D-OCT.
GPU-based ray casting has been utilized as the standard method for
interactive volume rendering in real-time 4D-OCT [4–8]. We integrated
a shadowing algorithm into volume rendering software, in addition to
ray casting [25], which we designate as the shadow extension for ray
casting (SERC). Hasenfratz et al. [27] defines shadow as the region of
space with at least one point of the light source occluded, assuming the
occluders as opaque. The following Eq. (1) describes the definition of
the shadow:

2.2. Shadow extension for GPU-based ray casting in real-time 4D-OCT
Our new shadow algorithm has been applied both to a swept source
(SS)-OCT and a spectral domain (SD)-OCT system described previously
[28,29]. The SS-OCT system uses a wavelength swept laser (HSL-10-
100; Santec Corp., Aichi, Japan) centered at 1060 nm with a sweeping
rate of 100 kHz. A high-speed balanced photoreceiver (Tholabs; USA)
detects the interference signal with a bandwidth of 400 MHz. We used
a 12-bit and 1.8 GSPS digtizer (ATS9360; Alazar Technologies Inc.,
Quebec, Canada) and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the axial
resolution were 10.58 μm and 92.33 dB at the 150 μm depth position
in the air, respectively. The SD-OCT system utilizes a superluminescent
diode source centered at 830 nm with a 64 nm FWHM bandwidth (SLD;
Superlum Ltd., Ireland). We used a CMOS line scan camera (Sprint
spL4096-140k; Basler AG, Germany) with a 25 kHz line scanning rate.
The axial resolution and the SNR were 6.4 μm and 91.79 dB at 150
μm depth in the air, respectively. We used these two OCT systems for
real-time acquisition and display of two objects: the omasum of cow
and beveled needles.
For parallel computation using GPU, we used CUDA version 8.0;
the system software was built using Microsoft Visual Studio 2015. The
software was tested on NVIDIA Geforce GTX 780 with 2304 CUDA cores
and Geforce GTX Titan X with 3072 CUDA cores for the SS-OCT and
SD-OCT systems, respectively. For hosting the GPU, we used a desktop
computer with Intel Core i7 CPU with Windows 7 operating system.
We utilized and extended the GPU-accelerated OCT processing soft-
ware capable of video rate volumetric rendering, which has been
reported previously [4]. Jian et al. [4] demonstrated that one of the
main bottlenecks in GPU implementation is the Host-to-Device (H2D)
and the Device-to-Host (D2H) memory transfer. They eliminated mem-
ory transfer latency from host to device parallelizing the data transfer
with the general Fourier domain (FD)-OCT processing kernels. After
the FD-OCT processing using CUDA streams and pinned memory, the
ray casting algorithm was implemented for volume rendering. The
CUDA and OpenGL interoperability eliminated D2H memory trans-
fer, displaying the rendered results directly from GPU. We added a
shadow computation kernel between the FD-OCT data processing and
the ray casting volume rendering to the software provided by Jian et
al. Fig. 2(a) shows the timeline generated by NVIDIA’s toolkit, Visual
Profiler, for one of our experiments.
Following FD-OCT processing, the volumetric data set was passed to
the rendering kernel, which is the shadow kernel and the ray casting
kernel as seen in Fig. 2(b). For SERC calculation, we utilized direct
volume rendering (DVR), which is one of the most widely used methods
for the visualization of a volumetric data set [20,30]. DVR provides
internal details as well as the comprehensive view of objects, compared
with surface rendering. However, DVR is computationally expensive,
which makes it difficult to accomplish an interactive rendering rate,
especially when shadow rendering is included. In order to achieve
an interactive frame rate using DVR, a 3D texture graphics memory
in CUDA programming can be used [31,32]. Texture memory is one
of the constant memories in the GPU, and since it is a read-only
memory, it can be quickly accessed while minimizing memory traffic.
Also, its memory access patterns exhibit great spatial locality, thus
facilitating the computation of the volumetric data set with threads and
utilization of hardware-accelerated interpolation with texture memory.
Both shadows and ray casting were computed using the GPU’s texture
memory. The volume data set following FD-OCT processing was bound
to the 3D texture memory via a 3D array. Shadow ray was cast from
one plane of the volume in an orthogonal direction to the plane on
the opposite side. Intensity value of each voxel was sampled along the
ray direction and shadows were calculated according to the procedure
mentioned in Section 2.1. As mentioned previously, the ray casting
kernel for volumetric visualization was implemented in the last step
of rendering (Fig. 2(b)).

3.2. Improved spatial comprehension by planar shadow
We applied planar shadowing technique to a 22-gauge beveled
needle placed immediately over a human finger, and the results are
presented in Fig. 4(a–b). These volume data were acquired with the
SS-OCT system (1024 ×1024 ×160 voxels) as well. The distance of
the needle to the finger cannot be identified precisely because the OCT
source light is perpendicular to the finger and the shadows formed by
the light appear similar to each other. It is clear that the needle was
moved towards the finger from Fig. 4(a) to (b), as indicated by red
arrows on the figure. A similar needle viewed from the horizontal side
as shown in Fig. 4(c–d), was imaged using SD-OCT with a size of 2048 ×
512 ×200 voxels. In this scenario, the shadow due to the OCT source
light was entirely occluded by the bottom structure. The needle was
moved in the direction away from the viewer, which is visible only by
the planar shadow placed on the left-hand side of the image. In these
examples, the self-shadow algorithm was temporarily turned off since
the metal surface of the needle was highly visible.
3.3. Demonstration of real-time performance
To demonstrate the real-time acquisition and display capabilities,
we performed real-time SS-OCT imaging for a needle on clay using the
100 kHz SS-OCT system, as shown in Video 1 and Video 2. Fig. 5(a–b)
show representative images of Video 1 and 2. In Video 1, the volume
was acquired at 256 A-scans per B-scan and 100 B-scans per volume.
In order to process the entire volume, the complete pipeline re-
quired approximately 47 ms (as shown in Fig. 6(a)), including the
FD-OCT batch processing and the volume rendering, which is much
less than the actual acquisition time of 320 ms. The final display
rate was 3.13 volumes per sec with the acquisition rate limiting the
display speed. The entire SERC (red box in Fig. 6(a)) required 5.8 ms.
Fig. 6(b) presents an expanded view of the tasks included within SERC
algorithm, within which the shadow was computed (the pink square
of ‘d_shadow’) and the ray casting (the pink square of ‘d_render’) was
implemented. The shadow computational kernel takes approximately
1.6 ms. Video 2 displays real-time acquisition and display with a larger
volume size of 512 A-scans per B-scan and 100 B-scans. In order to
process the entire volume, the complete pipeline required approxi-
mately 90 ms and the shadow kernel 3.4 ms. As shown in both videos,
the interactive volume rendering demonstrates an improved spatial
comprehension and more realistic images because of the inclusion of
shadows. The self-shadow algorithm was turned off in these videos due
to the reason stated earlier.
4. Discussion and conclusion
In this work, we developed, implemented, and demonstrated a SERC
rendering algorithm applied to a real-time 4D-OCT software. We tested
the algorithm with a 100 kHz sweep rate SS-OCT system and a 25 kHz
line rate SD-OCT system. The self-shadow effect rendered a three-
dimensional appearance for uneven objects, and provided realistic
depth cues for uneven surfaces such as intestinal villi covered on a
cow’s omasum. The planar shadow projected onto a separate virtual
surface improves spatial comprehension in 3D scenes, enhancing depth
perception of objects such as those with beveled needles. Computed in
a current-generation GPU, the complete processing pipeline required
47 ms with a volume size of 1024 ×256 ×100, adequate to support
real-time volumetric visualization.
Fig. 3 shows implementation of self-shadow and both Figs. 4 and 5
show implementation of planar shadow. These two shadowing schemes
can be applied simultaneously, with time difference not less than 1 ms
when both self-shadow and planar shadow were implemented together,
which can be induced by the varying the performance of GPU with
time. To achieve an interactive rendering rate, the GPU-based direct
volume rendering was applied and the GPU texture memory was used.
We expect that this SERC rendering algorithm in real time 4D-OCT
will play a key role in providing surgical guidance for ophthalmic
microsurgery. Improved visual realism facilitates visualization of ab-
normal neovascularization on the retinal surface or retinal membrane
peels, because self-shadow enhances the three-dimensionality of such
structures. Also, surgeons can obtain enhanced geometric perception
of the precise position and configuration of surgical instruments such
as beveled needles, which is important in ophthalmic microsurgery to
improve surgical performance, with additional depth cues provided by
planar shadow.
Further improvements will be made for realistic volume rendering
in the near future. Currently, a single light source for shadow rendering
is assumed to be located distant to the volumetric data set. Multiple
shadows created by multiple light sources at desired locations may pro-
vide more visually appealing spatial comprehension in specific cases.
Also, other advanced rendering algorithms such as sophisticated ray
tracing can be implemented into the real time 4D-OCT system. For
example, ray tracing technique generates more realistic lighting effects,
concerning the light path reflected between the objects [33]. Additional
optical effects including scattering and dispersion provide highly real-
istic 3D scenes. Ray tracing ensures excellent image quality but entails
expensive computational tasks, which can create latencies in processing
time. To eliminate the latencies, additional GPUs may be considered as
an option, for example, one for the FD-OCT processing and another for
the ray tracing utilizing the method introduced by Zhang et al. [8].
The source code corresponding to the shadow rendering algorithm is
available online at http://kubol.korea.ac.kr.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging technology has significant advantages in in situ and noninvasive monitoring of biological tissues. However, it still faces the following challenges: including data processing speed, image quality, and improvements in three-dimensional (3D) visualization effects. OCT technology, especially functional imaging techniques like optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), requires a long acquisition time and a large data size. Chapter
Oct 2023
Lect Notes Comput Sci
Michael Sommersperger
Shervin Dehghani
Philipp Matten
Nassir Navab
Swept-Source Optical Coherence Tomography (SS-OCT) integrated with surgical microscopes has enabled fast, high-resolution, and volumetric visualization of delicate tissue-instrument interactions. However, some visual features, which provide essential perceptual information in microscopic surgery, are not present in 4D OCT. Such a feature is the shadow of the surgical instruments cast onto the retina by the endo-illumination probe, which is among the most important cognitive cues for perceptual distance estimation. In this work, we propose Semantic Virtual Shadows (SVS), a novel concept to artificially generate instrument-specific shadows in OCT volumes, enabling naturally non-existent but important perceptual cues that are present in microscopic surgery. Semantic scene information is leveraged by considering only voxels associated with shadow-casting and shadow-receiving objects, identified using a learning-based approach and efficient volume processing, respectively. Real-time performance is achieved by a precomputed semantic shadow volume texture that assigns a shadowing factor to each voxel associated with a shadow-receiving object. The novelty of the method includes not only instrument-specific shadowing on the surface anatomy but also exclusively on deep-seated subsurface structures, providing advantages for various vitreoretinal procedures. Our user study indicates the benefits of the method for 4D OCT-guided surgery in several cognitive and performance-specific aspects.

We propose a new method of determining the optical axis (OA), pupillary axis (PA), and visual axis (VA) of the human eye by using dual-depth whole-eye optical coherence tomography (OCT). These axes, as well as the angles “α” between the OA and VA and “κ” between PA and VA, are important in many ophthalmologic applications, especially in refractive surgery. Whole-eye images are reconstructed based on simultaneously acquired images of the anterior segment and retina. The light from a light source is split into two orthogonal polarization components for imaging the anterior segment and retina, respectively. The feasibility of our approach was tested using a model eye and human subjects.

Abstract Ray-based simulations have been shown to generate impressively realistic ultrasound images in interactive frame rates. Recent efforts used GPU-based surface ray-tracing to simulate complex ultrasound interactions such as multiple reflections and re-fractions. These methods are restricted to perfectly specular reflections (i.e., following only a single reflective/refractive ray), whereas real tissue exhibits roughness of varying degree at tissue interfaces, causing partly diffuse reflections and refractions. Such surface interactions are significantly more complex and can in general not be handled by such deterministic ray-tracing approaches. However, they can be efficiently computed by Monte-Carlo sampling techniques, where many ray paths are generated with respect to a probability distribution. In this paper we introduce Monte-Carlo ray-tracing for ultrasound. This enables the realistic simulation of ultrasound interactions such as soft shadows and fuzzy reflections. We discuss how to properly weight the contribution of each ray path in order to simulate the behavior of a beamformed ultrasound signal. Tracing many individual rays per transducer element is easily parallelizable on modern GPUs, as opposed to previous approaches based on recursive binary ray-tracing.

Minimally-invasive microsurgery has resulted in improved outcomes for patients. However, operating through a microscope limits depth perception and fixes the visual perspective, which result in a steep learning curve to achieve microsurgical proficiency. We introduce a surgical imaging system employing four-dimensional (live volumetric imaging through time) microscope-integrated optical coherence tomography (4D MIOCT) capable of imaging at up to 10 volumes per second to visualize human microsurgery. Our novel surgical visualization platform achieves surgeon-interactive 4D visualization of live surgery which could expand the surgeon’s capabilities.

Real-Time Volumetric Imaging of Vitreoretinal Surgery with a Prototype Microscope-Integrated Swept-Source OCT Device
Article
Nov 2017
Michael I. Seider
Oscar Carrasco-Zevallos
Randall Gunther
Cynthia Toth
Purpose
To evaluate the images produced in an initial surgical series of intraoperative near–real-time volumetric swept-source OCT imaging.
Design
Prospective translational study.
Participants
Forty-one consecutive adult patients undergoing vitreoretinal surgery between July 22, 2014, and July 1, 2015, at the Duke University Eye Center who agreed to participate.
Methods
A novel microscope-integrated swept-source OCT prototype captured volumetric renderings of imaging of macular surgery in near-real-time and showed them to the surgeon via a heads-up display through the microscope oculars. Then the images were analyzed formally after surgery.
Main Outcome Measures
Image quality, successful capture of surgical instruments, maneuvers and associated retinal deformation volumetrically over time, and qualitative image analysis.
Results
Volumetric swept-source OCT images were graded as acceptable in 92% of patients. Volumetric imaging of scraping and peeling procedures was achieved in 75% and 78% of patients in whom it was performed, respectively. Imaging provided the surgeon with near–real-time volumetric visualization of the position of the instrument relative to the retinal surface, flap initiation, flap removal, and retinal deformation during instrumentation via a heads-up display.
Conclusions
This volumetric, microscope-integrated swept-source OCT prototype seems to provide high-detail, near–real-time volumetric imaging of delicate maneuvers during macular surgery.
