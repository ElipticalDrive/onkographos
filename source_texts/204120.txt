Vision transformers (ViTs) have attracted increasing attention in visual tasks due to their strong global modeling capability. However, compared with conventional convolutional neural networks, ViTs typically involve substantially more parameters and higher computational complexity, which poses significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained hardware platforms. Model quantization, as an effective model compression technique, can significantly reduce inference overhead; however, existing methods often struggle to strike a good balance between model accuracy and hardware deployment efficiency. To address this issue, this paper proposes a mixed-precision quantization framework for ViTs under hardware constraints. First, based on the distribution characteristics of activation values at different layers, we employ uniform and non-uniform quantization strategies, respectively, to improve the adaptability of the quantized representation. To further guide the reasonable allocation of quantization bit width, we introduce a sensitivity metric based on the trace of the Hessian matrix, which evaluates the sensitivity of each layer to quantization by measuring the impact of quantization perturbations on the loss. Based on this, the mixed-precision bit-width allocation problem is formulated as an optimal binary mask search problem under hardware constraints and solved using integer linear programming to explicitly account for resource constraints in actual hardware deployment. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves an effective balance among model accuracy, model size, and inference latency. Extensive experiments on benchmark datasets such as ImageNet and COCO demonstrate that our approach achieves lower model size and computational cost than existing methods, while maintaining competitive accuracy.

Machine learning applications cover a wide range of deployment scenarios, from large-scale cloud infrastructures to small-scale real-time edge devices. Transformer-based network models were initially developed for natural language processing and have subsequently achieved remarkable performance in various computer vision tasks, including image classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation. However, compared with traditional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Transformer-based models generally involve substantially more parameters, larger model sizes, and higher computational costs. These characteristics make their deployment on resource-constrained edge devices with strict real-time requirements particularly challenging.
To address these challenges, mainstream approaches include network model quantization1,2, knowledge distillation3, model pruning, and lightweight network design. This work focuses on quantization-based solutions. Many existing quantization methods4–6, originally developed for CNNs, cannot be directly or effectively applied to Transformer-based architectures. As shown in Table 1, conventional quantization schemes such as MinMax and EMA suffer from severe accuracy degradation when applied to Vision Transformers (ViTs). Methods specifically designed for ViTs, such as PTQ4ViT7 and ADFQ-ViT8, alleviate this issue to some extent; however, they still rely on floating-point computation during inference by dequantizing weights or activations, and typically leave certain components, such as the Softmax layer, unquantized. As a result, these approaches offer limited inference acceleration. FQ-ViT9 achieves full-network quantization, but does not adequately support low-bit (e.g., 4-bit) or mixed-precision configurations, which restricts its flexibility in meeting diverse deployment requirements in terms of model size, computation, and latency. In summary, existing post-training quantization methods for ViTs exhibit notable limitations. Fully quantized approaches that aggressively quantize all activations, including matrix multiplication layers, to very low bit widths (e.g., 4-bit) often lead to drastic accuracy degradation and even render the model unusable. In contrast, other methods deliberately avoid quantizing sensitive components, such as Layer Normalization and Softmax layers, to preserve accuracy, thereby sacrificing potential efficiency gains. These trade-offs highlight the need for more flexible and hardware-aware quantization strategies for vision transformers.
In this paper, we propose a hardware-friendly post-training mixed-precision quantization framework for ViTs to mitigate the severe accuracy degradation commonly observed under low-bit full quantization. Our goal is to provide a practical solution that balances accuracy with deployment requirements, including model size, computational cost, and inference latency. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
We propose a hardware-friendly post-training mixed-precision quantization framework tailored for ViTs, which enables automated conversion from floating-point models to low-bit integer models while remaining compatible with practical deployment.
We introduce a hybrid precision quantization scheme that leverages Hessian-based sensitivity analysis to characterize layer-wise quantization impact, and formulates bit-width allocation as a hardware-aware optimization problem under real deployment constraints.
Experiments on ImageNet and COCO demonstrate that the proposed method achieves competitive accuracy compared with state-of-the-art quantization techniques, while significantly reducing model size and computational cost. Related work
This work focuses on Transformer-based vision models and model compression. We review the most relevant literature from two perspectives: Transformer-based models for vision tasks and quantization methods for ViTs.
Transformer-based models for vision tasks
The Transformer10 architecture was originally proposed for natural language processing and has since been successfully extended to computer vision, benefiting from its strong global modeling capability. The ViT11 replaces convolutional operations with self-attention mechanisms and demonstrates promising performance on various vision benchmarks. Nevertheless, ViT often relies on large-scale training data and can be sensitive to distribution shifts such as changes in object position or scale.
DeiT12 introduces a token-based teacher–student distillation strategy that enables data-efficient training of ViTs without requiring additional external data. However, it still depends heavily on strong data augmentation, which increases training time and memory consumption. Swin Transformer (Swin-T)13 improves both accuracy and efficiency via hierarchical representations and window-based self-attention, together with relative positional encoding to support variable input resolutions. Despite these advantages, its structural complexity may lead to increased model size and computational overhead.
Several works further target efficient inference of ViTs. CF-ViT14 adopts a coarse-to-fine inference strategy: it first performs a lightweight prediction using shortened token sequences and then selectively refines uncertain cases with informative patches. Efficient-ViT15 improves inference speed and memory efficiency through cascaded group attention modules and a sandwich-like design, though its model size can still be larger than that of some efficient CNN counterparts, which may hinder deployment on resource-constrained devices.
For mobile scenarios, Mobile-ViT16 combines the strengths of CNNs and Transformers into a lightweight architecture suitable for on-device inference, and uses multi-scale processing to accommodate inputs with different resolutions. Despite these advances, Transformer-based models often remain less competitive than mobile-optimized CNNs in terms of the accuracy–efficiency trade-off on edge devices.
Quantization of vision transformers
Quantization methods for neural networks are commonly categorized into Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) and Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)17. Given the high training cost of Vision Transformers (ViTs), this section primarily reviews PTQ-based approaches, which are more practical for real-world deployment.
In terms of mixed-precision quantization, PTQ-ViT18 proposes a mixed-precision quantization strategy that introduces an ordering loss to preserve the relative order of self-attention outputs and leverages the kernel-level properties of features across different layers. Han et al.19 proposed the MixA-Q framework, which leverages the sparsity of activations within Transformer layers to assign different bit widths to important and non-important windows in the Swin architecture. They constructed a dual-branch Swin module to adapt to various quantization paradigms, achieving lossless quantization while remaining compatible with both QAT and PTQ algorithms. Ranjan et al.20 proposed the Mix-QViT interpretable mixed-precision quantization framework, which evaluates layer importance using Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) and quantizes sensitivity through layer-by-layer precision perturbation, using a dual-metric approach to achieve adaptive layer-level allocation. However, PTQ-ViT requires a large-scale calibration dataset and incorporates multiple auxiliary techniques, substantially increasing implementation complexity; MixA-Q is incompatible with ViT architectures beyond Swin Transformers; and Mix-QViT has not yet integrated dynamic mixed-precision quantization under hardware resource constraints.
In the PTQ domain, UQ-ViT21 addresses the issue of extremely uneven dynamic distribution of ViT activations by designing a DeMax mechanism to dynamically preserve the dominant Softmax values, and by employing NormQuant channel quantization and tensor dequantization, thereby ensuring hardware-friendliness; IPTQ-ViT22 designed polynomial-approximated GELU and bit-shifted Softmax for the nonlinear functions in ViT, achieving pure integer quantization inference; PSAQ-ViT V223 generates unsupervised synthetic data based on block similarity metrics and utilizes task- and model-agnostic prior information to enable Transformer quantization without requiring real data or class supervision, making it suitable for data-sensitive scenarios; however, these approaches lack a joint optimization of mixed-precision assignment and hardware resource constraints, which hinders their flexible deployment across various resource-limited hardware.
In the realm of hardware-friendly and integer quantization approaches, I-ViT2 achieves pure integer quantization of ViT through operator re-engineering, thereby improving hardware inference efficiency. Li et al.24 proposed RepQ-ViT, which decouples the quantization and inference processes. It applies channel quantization to activations with significant channel-wise variations following LayerNorm, and Log2 quantization to activations with a Power-of-Two distribution following Softmax. They then use scale reparameterization to convert these quantizers into hardware-friendly layer quantization and Log2 quantization, thereby improving the post-training quantization accuracy of ViT from 4 bits to a practical level and achieving efficient inference while ensuring quantization accuracy. However, these methods lack fine-grained parameter sensitivity awareness and mixed-precision strategies, making it difficult to achieve an optimal balance between accuracy and efficiency under hardware constraints.
Q-ViT represents a representative QAT-based approach. It introduces an Information Rectification Module (IRM) to align full-precision and quantized feature representations, together with a Distribution-Guided Distillation (DGD) loss to reduce performance degradation by matching similarity matrices. However, the inclusion of additional distillation objectives and rectification modules results in increased training complexity and a larger number of hyperparameters.
Notably, most of the methods mentioned above performs quantization on sensitive components such as the Softmax and Layer Normalization layers. To address this limitation, FQ-ViT adopts a full-network quantization strategy for ViTs by applying non-uniform Power-of-Two quantization to Layer Normalization inputs, Log-Int-Softmax to attention maps, and customized non-uniform quantization schemes to other modules. While this design effectively alleviates accuracy loss under full quantization, it also introduces stronger hardware-specific assumptions and increases deployment complexity, which may limit its general applicability across diverse hardware platforms.

FQ-ViT achieves full quantization of Vision Transformer (ViT) models by carefully designing quantization strategies for key components, including the Layer Normalization layer, the Softmax layer, and the attention map. For Layer Normalization, FQ-ViT adopts the Power-of-Two Factor (PTF) scheme, whose core idea is to assign different power-of-two scaling factors to different channels, rather than using a single set of quantization parameters across all channels. This design effectively alleviates the large inter-channel variation commonly observed in LayerNorm inputs.
For the Softmax layer, FQ-ViT employs Log-Int-Softmax (LIS) to enable integer-only computation. By analyzing the distribution of attention maps, it is observed that most values are concentrated around small magnitudes, while only a few outliers approach one. Motivated by this distributional property, FQ-ViT applies Log2-based non-uniform quantization to attention maps. In contrast, the convolutional, linear, and matrix multiplication components are quantized using standard uniform Min–Max quantization.
It is well known that applying conventional full quantization methods to ViTs, especially under low-bit settings, often leads to severe accuracy degradation. As a result, many previous works deliberately avoid quantizing sensitive components such as the Softmax layer. By replacing the standard Softmax operation with Log-Int-Softmax, as illustrated in Fig. 1, FQ-ViT significantly reduces the need for dequantization before the Softmax layer, thereby facilitating fully quantized inference.

LayerNorm quantization. One major source of accuracy degradation in quantized ViTs arises from the large inter-channel variation of Layer Normalization (LayerNorm) inputs, where the dynamic ranges of different channels can differ substantially. Conventional uniform quantization, which applies a single set of quantization parameters to all channels, is therefore insufficient to accurately capture such heterogeneous distributions. To address this issue, FQ-ViT adopts the PTF scheme for LayerNorm quantization. Instead of sharing a single scaling factor across channels, PTF assigns each channel an individual power-of-two scaling factor. This design significantly reduces quantization error caused by inter-channel variability, while preserving computational efficiency by enabling integer-only inference through bit-shift operations, thus avoiding floating-point arithmetic during deployment. Proposed method
Applying full low-bit quantization to Vision Transformers, such as the W4A4 configuration in FQ-ViT, often results in severe accuracy degradation and may even render the model unusable, as shown in Table 1. To address this issue, we aim to develop a balanced intra-layer mixed-precision quantization scheme that mitigates accuracy loss while retaining the efficiency benefits of low-bit inference. From this perspective, the quantization process can be formulated as the problem of selecting an optimal precision assignment under sparsity and deployment constraints. Specifically, we decompose Transformer quantization into two stages: the first stage focuses on identifying an optimal binary mask that determines precision allocation, while the second stage further refines this configuration to recover accuracy.
Inter-layer mixed precision. Existing quantization methods typically adopt a uniform bit width across all layers or assign different precisions at the layer level. In contrast, we observe that different layers can contribute unevenly to the final inference performance. Motivated by this observation, our method assigns higher precision (e.g., 8-bit) to layers that are more sensitive to quantization, while allocating lower precision (e.g., 4-bit or below) to less sensitive layers. This inter-layer mixed-precision strategy enables finer-grained control over the accuracy–efficiency trade-off compared with uniform bit width schemes.
In addition to accuracy considerations, our method explicitly accounts for hardware-specific deployment metrics, particularly inference latency. In practical systems, reducing numerical precision from INT8 to INT4 does not necessarily translate into proportional latency reduction. Some layer configurations may exhibit negligible speedup at lower precision, while others can benefit disproportionately from aggressive quantization. Quantizing layers that are both insensitive to precision reduction and ineffective in improving latency is therefore counterproductive, as it degrades accuracy without tangible performance gains. Consequently, our approach retains higher precision for such components and reserves low-bit quantization for those that offer meaningful efficiency improvements. This design highlights the importance of jointly considering accuracy sensitivity and hardware characteristics, as inference latency is not solely determined by model size or computational complexity.

Experiments
We evaluate the proposed method on two widely used benchmark datasets, ImageNet27 and COCO. We first report mixed-precision quantization results for Vision Transformers on ImageNet. We then investigate different deployment scenarios enabled by the ILP formulation and analyze the corresponding trade-offs among model size, inference latency, and accuracy.
Implementation details
We employ the open-source PULP28 library to solve the integer linear programming problem and obtain optimal mixed-precision configurations. For fair comparison, we follow the same quantization settings as FQ-ViT during the quantization phase. Specifically, all weights are quantized using uniform Min–Max quantization. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and conducted on NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti GPUs.

Conclusions
In this paper, we presented a hardware-friendly mixed-precision quantization framework for Vision Transformers. By formulating bit-width assignment as an ILP problem and incorporating Hessian-based sensitivity analysis, the proposed method enables fine-grained precision allocation under explicit hardware constraints. Extensive experiments on ImageNet and COCO demonstrate that our approach achieves competitive accuracy compared with state-of-the-art quantization methods, while significantly reducing model size and computational cost. The flexibility of the framework further allows effective trade-offs among accuracy, latency, and model compactness, making it well suited for deployment on resource-constrained devices.
Future work will focus on exploring more advanced sensitivity estimation techniques to further improve quantization robustness, including the incorporation of quantitative error propagation modeling into the solution process, as well as extending the proposed framework to other neural network architectures and deployment scenarios. In addition, we plan to investigate tighter integration with hardware-specific profiling tools to better capture real-world latency characteristics and further enhance practical deployment efficiency.
