ABSTRACT
Interventional C-arm imaging is a standard tool for guiding minimally invasive cardiac procedures, as it reduces radiation
exposure by requiring only a limited number of projections compared to computed tomography (CT). However, a major
limitation of this approach is the inability to uniquely reconstruct three-dimensional data from a sparse number of projections,
especially under clinical low-dose conditions characterized by high photon noise. In this work, we investigate a method for 3D
reconstruction based on a limited number of C-arm images (9 views). To address the resulting ill-posed inverse problem and
suppress noise, we employ Total Variation (TV) minimization and consider two alternative ray-tracing approaches within the
solver: Siddon’s (weighted) and Bresenham’s (Binary) algorithms. The methods are evaluated using cardiovascular models
with known spatial geometries. The C-arm imaging process is computationally simulated with Poisson noise, followed by
the reconstruction of the 3D structures. The reconstructed results are then visually and quantitatively compared with the
ground-truth data. The experiments demonstrate a significant geometric failure of Bresenham’s binary approach under noisy
conditions, as evidenced by a mean Dice coefficient of only 0.61 ± 0.05 and an artificial inflation of vessel volume by a factor
of 1.95 ± 0.47. Siddon’s method shows improved performance; combined with TV regularization, it attenuates reconstruction
artifacts while providing improved preservation of morphological structures, achieving a mean Dice coefficient of 0.80 ± 0.06
and limiting average volume deviation to 5%. The results demonstrate that accurate reconstruction of vascular geometry from
low-dose C-arm imaging is feasible, provided that suitable backprojection algorithms and regularization methods are employed.
Introduction
Clinical Background
Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality globally, driving the demand for precise interventional tools1 .
X-ray angiography using C-Arm systems is the primary modality for visualizing coronary arteries during catheterization—a
minimally invasive procedure where a flexible tube is navigated through the patient’s vasculature to the heart to diagnose or
mechanically treat blockages. However, standard 2D projections inherently lack depth information. They capture the cumulative
attenuation of X-rays as they pass through the chest, meaning the clinical input consists merely of 2D grayscale shadows of the
vessel tree. When the complex, multi-branching 3D anatomy (Figure 1) is flattened into these 2D planes, vessel overlap and
foreshortening occur. Foreshortening is a geometric distortion where vessels angled toward or away from the X-ray source
appear artificially shorter than their true anatomical length. This loss of dimensionality creates significant ambiguity in complex
scenarios, such as assessing stenosis severity in tortuous segments or guiding stent placement in bifurcations2, 17 , necessitating
a transition to fully volumetric 3D reconstruction.
The Problem: Sparse-View Reconstruction
The fundamental challenge in 3D reconstruction is the ill-posed inverse problem: recovering the original 3D volume based
solely on its resultant 2D projections. While standard computed tomography (CT) systems acquire hundreds of projection
angles to form a well-posed system of equations, interventional C-arms typically capture a limited number of projections to
comply with the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle for patient radiation safety6 .
Consequently, clinical protocols to minimize radiation often result in sparse-view scenarios. During these procedures, the
C-arm rotates around the patient’s isocenter along a circular trajectory. The number of projections acquired in low-dose C-arm
imaging protocols typically consists of only a fraction of the views captured in conventional CT. In this study, an average of
nine projections was assumed to reflect common clinical practice, representing a practical compromise between minimizing
radiation exposure and ensuring sufficient information for reliable 3D reconstruction. Mathematically, this transforms the task
into an ill-posed, underdetermined problem.
In conventional clinical CT, Filtered Back Projection (FBP) serves as the standard analytical reconstruction algorithm. However,
FBP is fundamentally predicated on the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, requiring hundreds of densely spaced projections.
Under the sparse 9-view conditions evaluated in this study, analytical methods like FBP fail, producing severe aliasing artifacts
due to angular undersampling and resulting in vascular reconstructions that are unsuitable for further clinical analysis or
processing7 .
Therefore, to achieve meaningful geometric fidelity, it is necessary to abandon analytical approaches in favor of iterative
reconstruction techniques. This study investigates the integration of Total Variation (TV) regularization frameworks and the
choice of ray-tracing projector—specifically, the mathematical approximation of the continuous X-ray path through the discrete
voxel grid. We investigate whether the combination of a high-fidelity continuous projector and gradient-domain regularization
enables the recovery of distal vessel connectivity and mitigates the volumetric dilation inherent to clinical noise conditions in
low-dose interventional environments.

Related Work
Historically, 3D reconstruction in medical imaging relied on analytical direct-inversion methods, primarily Filtered BackProjection (FBP)7 . FBP serves as the discrete computational implementation of the mathematical inverse Radon transform19 .
These algorithms are computationally efficient and mathematically exact when provided with a well-posed dataset containing
dense angular sampling (e.g., hundreds of continuous views in conventional Computed Tomography). However, the transition to
2/17
interventional C-arm environments renders these traditional methods inadequate. The physical acquisition of X-ray projections
corresponds to sampling the object’s Radon transform. According to the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, calculating an
exact, artifact-free inverse Radon transform requires a sufficient number of angular views to densely populate the Radon space19 .
In our sparse-view scenario, the large angular intervals leave large gaps in the sampled projection data. This undersampling
violates the Nyquist condition, rendering the inverse problem ill-posed. Forcing a standard analytical inversion on this sparse
data introduces aliasing and streak artifacts that obscure fine coronary vessel connectivity, necessitating the shift toward the
regularized, iterative algebraic methods6 utilized in this study. To address the limitations of analytical methods, the field shifted
toward Iterative Reconstruction (IR) and Compressed Sensing (CS) techniques25 .
Unlike FBP, which executes an analytical direct inversion in a single computational pass, Iterative Reconstruction solves the
linear system Ax = b through a process of successive numerical approximation. Algorithms such as the Simultaneous Iterative
Reconstruction Technique (SIRT)11 execute a continuous cycle of forward projection (Ax), residual error calculation (Ax − b),
and gradient-based back-projection to incrementally update the volumetric data. While this iterative feedback loop allows the
solver to mathematically compensate for the missing angular information, relying purely on unregularized SIRT introduces a
risk of high-frequency noise amplification. If left unconstrained, the successive iterations will eventually begin to overfit the
statistical quantum noise rather than converging on the true physical anatomy, which explicitly necessitates the integration of
the regularization constraint26 discussed in the Methods section.
While compressed sensing (CS) frameworks and total variation minimization have enabled robust sparse-view reconstruction,
their success fundamentally relies on the mathematical accuracy of the underlying system matrix A. If the forward projector
utilizes a coarse, binary approximation (such as Bresenham’s binary method8 ), it feeds inaccurate geometric intersection
data directly into the iterative optimization loop27 . No amount of downstream regularization can correct this fundamental
geometrical error at the root of the system; the solver will successfully minimize the total variation penalty, but it will converge
on a smooth yet morphologically dilated vessel tree.
In recent years, Deep Learning (DL) has emerged as a prominent area of research for solving inverse problems. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and multi-stage architectures10, 16, 33 can learn complex statistical priors from large datasets,
demonstrating the ability to segment and reconstruct coronary arteries by synthesizing plausible vascular structures from sparse
input data. However, the efficacy of these data-driven models is fundamentally limited by the quality of their training data and
their internal physics consistency. While powerful, they often act as non-linear “black boxes” that obscure the fundamental
physical geometry of the reconstruction process and carry a clinical risk of hallucinating non-physical anatomical artifacts.
Because the primary objective of this study is to isolate and evaluate the fundamental mathematical impact of the forward
projector matrix (A), a deterministic and interpretable framework is required. Therefore, we deliberately employ total variation
(TV) minimization. Unlike “black-box” DL models, it provides a mathematically transparent, physics-based regularization
environment, ensuring that any observed differences in the final reconstruction stem from the chosen ray-tracing algorithm28 .
Many DL architectures are trained on Digital Twins—synthetic vascular trees forward-projected into 2D1 . If the forward
projector utilized to generate this training corpus relies on a coarse binary approximation, the neural network learns a
fundamentally flawed representation of X-ray physics. Consequently, the artificial vessel thickening (morphological dilation)
inherent to binary ray-tracing is permanently encoded into the network’s learned parameters, forcing the model to reproduce
these geometric errors even when processing real clinical data29, 30 .
State-of-the-art unrolled networks attempt to bridge this gap by directly integrating the system matrix A into the neural
architecture as a differentiable physical layer4 . In this coupled environment, the mathematical rigor of A dictates the success of
the entire network. Therefore, establishing a physically accurate, anti-aliased forward projector is a mandatory prerequisite for
developing the next generation of reliable, AI-driven interventional reconstruction16 .
While general-purpose algorithms and deep learning models address broad image quality, the reconstruction of coronary arteries
presents unique geometric challenges. Unlike static bones, coronary arteries are narrow, tortuous structures where preserving
connectivity is critical. Recent research has focused on model-based approaches. Techniques using Non-Uniform Rational
B-Splines (NURBS) or centerline extraction have been proposed to mathematically force the reconstruction to resemble a
tubular structure14, 32 . Similarly, Quantitative Coronary Angiography (QCA) methods attempt to infer 3D properties from 2D
projections by assuming circular cross-sections2, 31 .
Despite these algorithmic advancements, a critical limitation remains in how modern reconstruction frameworks interact
with the physical ray-tracing physics. Many contemporary solvers—whether advanced iterative techniques or novel neural
networks—continue to rely on simplified, binary forward projectors to map the 3D domain back to the 2D sensor. Consequently,
any geometric inaccuracies inherent to these coarse binary approximations, such as artificial vessel thickening, are unavoidably
inherited by the final reconstructed volume. Addressing this root morphological error is clinically paramount; as demonstrated
3/17
in recent studies, downstream diagnostic applications that rely on these static 3D models—such as non-invasive Fractional Flow
Reserve (FFR) estimations—are exponentially sensitive to even minor deviations in reconstructed vessel radius3, 30 . Therefore,
before introducing complex solvers, the fundamental geometric accuracy of the underlying system matrix A must first be
evaluated and optimized.
If the underlying forward projector relies on binary voxel sampling, it inherently introduces morphological dilation across the
entire reconstructed volume. This creates an irreducible baseline error; no amount of advanced algorithmic regularization can
accurately recover fine, distal vessel branches if the fundamental physics model fails to calculate exact, sub-voxel intersection
lengths. Therefore, isolating and quantifying the impact of this low-level mathematical dependency—by systematically
evaluating binary versus weighted ray-tracing algorithms—constitutes the primary contribution of this work.
Methods
In a C-arm system, X-rays are emitted from a point source, pass through the patient—where they are attenuated according to
tissue density—and are subsequently detected by a flat-panel detector, forming a two-dimensional projection. To model this
mathematically for 3D reconstruction, the continuous physical space containing the patient is discretized into a 3D voxel grid.
As illustrated in Figure 2, a sparse-view setup involves simulating geometric rays originating from discrete source positions
(e.g., S1 and S2 along a rotation arc) that intersect this voxelized domain. The fundamental task of the forward projection model
is to calculate the cumulative attenuation along each ray path as it travels from the source to a corresponding pixel P on the
detector plane. The geometric magnification of the resulting image and the specific sequence of voxels intersected by each ray
depend on the ratio of the source-to-detector distance (dSP ) to the source-to-object distance (dSO ).
The core component of any 3D reconstruction algorithm is the projector—the mathematical model that simulates how an X-ray
traverses this grid. Specifically, this geometric calculation establishes the exact mathematical mapping between the spatial
domains, determining precisely which discrete 3D voxels contribute to the attenuation intensity recorded at any single 2D
detector pixel. To reconstruct the volume, this process is inverted via back-projection, in which the measured intensity is
distributed along the corresponding ray path within the three-dimensional grid. The accuracy of this process depends on how
the continuous ray path is represented in discrete voxel space.
To investigate this geometric dependency, we evaluate two fundamentally distinct forward projection models. While both
algorithms inherently operate within the discrete integer coordinate space of the 3D voxel grid, they differ fundamentally in how
they assign attenuation weights: the Bresenham algorithm (a binary method) and the Siddon algorithm (a weighted method).
The Binary Approach (Bresenham Algorithm)
Originally designed for rasterizing lines in computer graphics, Bresenham’s algorithm approximates the continuous X-ray path
using efficient integer arithmetic. Rather than calculating exact geometric intersection lengths, the algorithm steps incrementally
along the primary axis of the ray path and maintains an internal error variable. This error term tracks the mathematical
divergence between the true continuous line and the discrete voxel centers. At each grid interval, the algorithm uses this error
term to make a binary decision, selecting the single voxel that best approximates the line’s trajectory. If a voxel is selected, it is
fully incorporated into the ray sum with a normalized weight of 1. Conversely, all other adjacent voxels are entirely excluded
with a weight of 0.
Operationally, this approach utilizes Bresenham’s line algorithm8 to traverse the grid by incrementing along the primary
axis of the ray one integer unit at a time. As illustrated in Figure 3B, this strict binary sampling yields a jagged, aliased
trajectory—commonly referred to as a “staircase” effect—rather than a precise geometric line. Consequently, this binary
approximation introduces a geometric artifact: the simulated ray effectively assumes a non-zero thickness dictated by the voxel
dimensions. During reconstruction, this expanded spatial footprint artificially dilates the structural boundaries, leading to a
significant overestimation of vessel volume.
The Weighted Approach (Siddon’s Algorithm)
Conversely, the Siddon algorithm is referred to as weighted because it computes a continuous, fractional contribution for every
voxel intersected by the ray path. Unlike the binary inclusion criterion of the Bresenham method, this approach calculates
the precise geometric length of the line segment traversed by the ray within each individual voxel. This exact path length,
normalized by the voxel dimensions, serves directly as the voxel’s specific attenuation weight. Computationally, this technique9
analytically determines the exact spatial coordinates where the continuous ray enters and exits each voxel boundary. By

Having established how individual X-ray paths are computationally modeled, we can now formalize the overarching reconstruction challenge. The ultimate goal is to invert this forward projection process to accurately recover the 3D volume from the 2D
projections. However, because interventional C-arm systems rely on extremely sparse data, this inversion is underdetermined.
To understand exactly why analytical methods fail under these constraints, we must examine the mathematical nature of this
specific inverse problem.

The mathematical stability of this linear system is dictated by the ratio of measurements (M) to unknowns (N). In a standard
clinical CT scan, hundreds of projection angles are acquired such that the number of measurements is equal to or greater than
the number of voxels (M ≥ N). This creates a well-posed or overdetermined system with a unique solution.
However, in our interventional C-arm scenario, we are limited to an extremely sparse dataset of only 9 views. Consequently, the
number of unknown voxels vastly exceeds the number of measured pixels (M ≪ N), creating a severely underdetermined and
ill-posed inverse problem. This underdetermination manifests as a high-dimensional null space, meaning that an infinite number
of distinct 3D volumetric distributions for x can theoretically satisfy the equations and yield the exact same 2D projection
data vector b from a restricted set of angles. This mathematical ambiguity renders a direct analytical inversion fundamentally
unfeasible. If standard techniques such as Filtered Back-Projection are applied under these sparse conditions, the violation of
the Nyquist criteria generates severe streak artifacts and aliasing artifacts, because the unregularized mathematics lacks the
constraints necessary to distinguish the true continuous coronary anatomy from an infinite combination of non-physical ghost
solutions7 .

Crucially, the regularization term R(x) operates solely within the 3D volumetric domain and remains identical across both the
binary and weighted reconstruction implementations. This controlled mathematical design ensures that any observed geometric
or morphological differences in the final reconstructions are attributable entirely to the specific mathematical formulation of the
forward projector matrix A (Bresenham vs. Siddon).
This specific regularization choice is fundamentally grounded in Compressed Sensing (CS) theory6, 11, 21 . Classical sampling
theorems dictate that accurately reconstructing a volume from only 9 projection views is impossible. However, CS theory
demonstrates that an undersampled system can be robustly solved provided the underlying data is sparse (meaning it consists
mostly of zeros) in a specific mathematical transform domain. By enforcing this sparsity in the gradient domain, TV

minimization leverages the assumption that biological structures—such as contrast-filled coronary arteries—are piecewisesmooth, consisting of uniform tissue densities separated by sharp boundaries. Consequently, this CS framework allows
the reconstruction to effectively suppress high-frequency noise and undersampling streak artifacts while preserving the
morphological integrity of the vessel walls.
To transition from the raw 2D projection images to the final 3D coronary model, the computational pipeline executes the
following sequential steps:
1. Volume Initialization: Before the solver begins, the 3D volumetric space is initialized as a null vector (x0 = 0). This
establishes a completely blank, non-negative baseline grid.
2. Iterative Optimization: The algorithm enters the optimization loop. In each iteration (k), the current volume guess
(x(k) ) is forward-projected using the system matrix A. The resulting simulated shadows are mathematically compared
against the actual acquired sparse projection set (the 9 views stored in b) to calculate the data fidelity error. The volume
is then updated to x(k+1) using the regularization gradient to minimize this error while enforcing spatial sparsity.
3. Convergence (Stopping Criterion): This iterative loop continues until a strict stopping condition is met. The solver
terminates either when the relative change between consecutive volumetric updates falls below a predefined tolerance
threshold (∥x(k+1) − x(k) ∥2 < ε ), indicating mathematical convergence, or when a maximum allowed number of iterations
(kmax ) is reached.
4. Volumetric Segmentation: Upon convergence, the solver outputs a continuous, grayscale 3D attenuation map. To isolate
the physical geometry of the coronary arteries, a final binarization step is applied. Using Otsu’s thresholding algorithm,
an optimal global threshold is automatically computed to separate the high-intensity contrast-filled vessels from the
low-intensity background tissue, yielding the final discrete 3D morphological model.
To address the challenge of morphologically accurate 3D coronary reconstruction from a sparse set of projection views,
this study employs a digital twin validation framework1 . In this context, a digital twin refers to a completely synthetic,
mathematically defined 3D vascular model utilized for in silico simulation. This controlled computational approach allows for a
quantitative assessment of the reconstruction algorithm’s geometric fidelity. By using a synthetic model, the exact ground truth
3D anatomy is known prior to the experiment—a baseline condition that is fundamentally impossible to achieve in real-world
clinical settings, where the patient’s actual 3D arterial shape is the unknown variable being sought.
The experimental pipeline follows a three-stage workflow:
1. Input (Simulated Acquisition): We begin with high-resolution 3D anatomical models (phantoms). From these, we
mathematically generate a set of sparse 2D projections (simulating the C-Arm X-ray process) to serve as the input data b.
2. Process (Reconstruction): The sparse 2D data is fed into the reconstruction engine. We employ the Simultaneous
Iterative Reconstruction Technique (SIRT) solver11 , comparing two distinct system matrix A configurations: the binary
(Bresenham) projector versus the weighted (Siddon) projector.
3. Output Validation: The resulting 3D volumetric reconstructions (x̂) are mathematically compared against the original
ground truth phantoms. The primary objective is to quantify the geometric fidelity and the extent of morphological
dilation introduced by the specific choice of forward ray-tracing projector.

coronary models (visualized in Figure 4). These models were selected to represent a range of vascular complexities, including
variations in tortuosity, bifurcation angles, and vessel caliber.

To perform the reconstruction, we utilized the ASTRA Toolbox (All Scale Tomographic Reconstruction Antwerp)13 . While
often treated as a “black box” in general imaging tasks, we selected this framework specifically because of its modular
architecture. It allows us to explicitly define the system matrix—the mathematical model of the X-ray geometry—independently
of the iterative solver, while offloading the massive ray-tracing calculations to the GPU for computational efficiency.
The core of this experiment is the comparison of two distinct methods for calculating the elements of the system matrix A.
These weights determine the geometric relationship between a specific X-ray path and a specific voxel.
The baseline forward projector implements Bresenham’s Line Algorithm8 , an approach originally developed for drawing linear
trajectories on discrete raster displays. To model the X-ray trajectory, this algorithm forces the continuous physical ray to snap
to discrete integer grid coordinates using binary, nearest-neighbor logic. Consequently, the resulting system matrix is binary: if
the geometric center of a ray intersects a voxel, it is assigned a uniform weight of 1; otherwise, it is assigned a 0. This binary
classification creates a jagged, “staircase” approximation of the true continuous ray. By mathematically treating a thin, linear
X-ray beam as a blocky chain of uniform cubes, this method artificially widens the intersection profile, directly introducing the
morphological dilation and artificial vessel thickening observed in the final reconstructed volumes.
To construct the weighted system matrix, the algorithm calculates the exact physical intersection length of the X-ray traversing
each discrete cubic voxel, normalized by the physical voxel dimensions. Consequently, the resulting matrix is populated with
continuous floating-point values dependent on the 3D angle of incidence. For example, a ray passing diagonally through the
center of a voxel assigns a proportionally higher geometric weight than an orthogonal ray, while a ray merely grazing an outer
corner yields a weight approaching zero. This continuous geometric weighting acts as an intrinsic spatial anti-aliasing filter.
By mathematically accounting for these partial-volume intersections, the forward projector represents sub-voxel boundaries,
thereby preventing the morphological dilation and artificial vessel thickening produced by binary ray-tracing algorithms.
To solve the inverse problem, we utilized the Simultaneous Iterative Reconstruction Technique (SIRT)11, 20 . Because direct
analytical inversion of the system matrix A is computationally intractable for high dimensional data, SIRT approaches the
solution through successive approximation. Specifically, the algorithm’s update rule employs the transpose matrix AT —which
physically corresponds to the back-projection operator—to map the computed residual error from the 2D projection domain
back into the 3D image volume. Unlike sequential algebraic methods that update the volume ray-by-ray, SIRT computes these
iterative updates simultaneously based on the data from all projection angles. This global update strategy is advantageous for
sparse-view data, as it inherently averages out quantum noise and suppresses the convergence oscillations common in sequential
algorithms.

It is critical to note that the specific geometric model chosen for the system matrix (A) governs both the forward-projection
and back-projection steps defined in Equation 4. If the binary model (Abinary ) is utilized, the residual error is forcefully
back-projected along the same jagged, binary coordinate path. Conversely, if the weighted model (Aweighted ) is employed,
the back-projected error is distributed proportionally across the grid based on exact partial-volume intersections, effectively
mitigating spatial aliasing without falsely implying a continuous spatial domain.
To ensure a fair comparative analysis, all initialization and solver hyperparameters were kept identical across both experiments.
The simulated projection data (b) was generated by forward-projecting the digital twin phantoms from 9 equispaced angles
spanning a 180° arc. This specific degree of sparsity was chosen to emulate a dose- and contrast-limited interventional scenario,
capturing the minimum data required to span the angular domain while freezing cardiac motion. To avoid the well-documented
inverse crime31 of testing an algorithm using the same mathematical model used to generate the data, all ground-truth projections
were generated using the high-fidelity weighted model. Furthermore, to accurately model clinical photon starvation and a
spatially varying, low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), Poisson noise was subsequently introduced to the forward-projected data by
simulating an unattenuated incident X-ray intensity of I0 = 5 × 103 photons per detector pixel.
For the iterative reconstruction pipeline, the 3D volume was initialized as a null vector (x0 = 0). During the SIRT optimization,
a hard non-negativity constraint was enforced to prevent the formation of physically impossible negative attenuation artifacts.
The solver utilized a fixed stopping criterion of 150 iterations—where one iteration encompasses a full forward and backward
cycle across all 9 angles. This threshold was selected empirically, as the relative volumetric change successfully plateaued by
this point, guaranteeing mathematical convergence for both projection models. Finally, the Total Variation (TV) regularization
hyperparameter (λ ) was tuned via a systematic parameter sweep, utilizing the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between
the reconstructed volume and the digital twin ground truth as the quantitative selection criterion. Because an excessive λ
over-smooths delicate distal coronary branches, while an insufficient λ allows Poisson noise to dominate, this mathematically
optimized baseline value was established to balance noise suppression against geometric fidelity, and was held rigidly constant
across all comparative evaluations.

To evaluate the impact of the projector model on reconstruction quality, we employed four complementary metrics. These were
selected to assess distinct characteristics of the output: spatial overlap (Dice, Jaccard), intensity fidelity (RMSE), and geometric
volume preservation (Volume Ratio).

Discussion
Quantitative evaluation of the ray-tracing algorithms under simulated clinical noise conditions revealed substantial geometric
limitations of the binary approach. The binary (Bresenham) method achieved a mean Jaccard Index of 0.44 ± 0.05 and produced
a mean volume ratio of 1.95 ± 0.47 (Table 1), indicating considerable overestimation of vessel volume. Binary ray-tracing
constrains each ray to integer voxel coordinates, introducing spatial discretization errors. In noise-free conditions, these errors
manifest as morphological distortions, including artificial vessel thickening and structural fragmentation, depending on vessel
diameter and orientation.
The performance of the weighted reconstruction framework can be assessed within the broader context of sparse-view
tomographic reconstruction. While recent developments increasingly rely on geometric heuristics or data-driven methods that
incorporate learned statistical priors, the weighted SIRT+TV framework achieved a mean DSC of 0.80 ± 0.06 and a stable
mean volume ratio of 1.05 ± 0.11. These results demonstrate that the combination of a weighted ray-tracing projector and total
variation (TV) regularization provides accurate geometric reconstruction under sparse-view and noisy acquisition conditions.
Furthermore, unlike deep-learning-based approaches, the proposed framework remains fully interpretable, as reconstructed
voxel intensities are directly related to the underlying X-ray attenuation measurements.
The isosurface sensitivity analysis of the representative phantom (Figure 6) further highlights the instability of the binary
model. Under noisy conditions, the binary reconstruction failed to produce a distinct high-accuracy region across the evaluated
threshold range, indicating a strong dependence on discretization artifacts and noise. In contrast, the weighted Siddon projector
combined with TV regularization exhibited a broad stability plateau, indicating that reconstruction accuracy remained stable
over a wide range of threshold values. Such robustness is desirable in clinical applications, where reconstruction quality should
remain stable without extensive parameter tuning.
Clinical X-ray reconstructions generate continuous grayscale volumes representing attenuation coefficients rather than binary
structures. Consequently, the use of binary evaluation metrics such as DSC and Jaccard Index requires threshold-based
segmentation and therefore simplifies the partial-volume effects present in clinical data. Nevertheless, this evaluation remains
relevant because many downstream applications, including fractional flow reserve estimation and automated vessel tracking,
ultimately require a binary representation of the vessel lumen resulting from image segmentation.
To complement the binary evaluation, reconstruction accuracy was also assessed using the root mean square error (RMSE)
metric presented in Table 1. The weighted method reduced the mean density error by more than 50% compared with the binary
baseline (0.03 ± 0.03 versus 0.08 ± 0.04). This improvement is consistent with the visual reconstruction quality observed
in Figure 5. TV Regularization suppresses noise by promoting sparsity in the image-gradient domain, whereas the weighted
projector preserves smooth intensity transitions at vessel boundaries through sub-voxel ray-path modeling. Together, these
mechanisms reduce quantization artifacts and morphological distortions associated with binary ray-tracing.
A limitation of TV regularization observed in this study is the partial smoothing of high-frequency anatomical details. Because
distal coronary branches are extremely narrow structures, the regularization process may suppress these vessels together with
high-frequency noise components. Although the weighted method substantially improves vessel continuity compared with the
binary baseline, minor thinning or discontinuities may still occur in the most distal vessel segments.
This study employed digital phantoms to provide a controlled ground-truth benchmark. Although the simulations incorporated
realistic Poisson noise to emulate low-dose clinical acquisitions, they did not account for cardiac motion or respiratory motion,
both of which are present in clinical catheterization procedures. In addition, the evaluation was intentionally limited to an
extreme sparse-view configuration consisting of nine projections. This experimental design enabled isolation of the geometric
limitations of the projection models and assessment of the regularization requirements of the reconstruction algorithms. Future
work will investigate the robustness of the weighted regularized framework in the presence of motion artifacts and uncalibrated
in vivo C-arm rotational angiography data.
Conclusions
The primary finding of this study is the severe geometric inaccuracy of Bresenham’s binary ray-tracing. In sparse-view
(9-projection) reconstruction scenarios, the binary projector lacks the spatial precision required to accurately reconstruct
14/17
morphological boundaries, particularly for thin vascular structures. As shown in Figure 6, under simulated clinical low-dose
conditions, the binary projector exhibited a substantial reduction in accuracy across all threshold values, never exceeding a
DSC of 0.55, a level generally insufficient for clinical applications. Consequently, the algorithm proved unstable, artificially
enlarging larger structures while failing to preserve finer anatomical details. This fundamental inability to accurately reconstruct
vessel geometry resulted in significant geometric dilation, merging of adjacent parallel vessel branches, and fragmentation of
thin vessel segments, yielding an average volume ratio of 1.95 ± 0.47 (Figure 5B).
In contrast, Siddon’s weighted iterative reconstruction framework combined with total variation (TV) regularization demonstrated substantially greater robustness to clinical noise conditions. The weighted method achieved a mean Jaccard Index of
0.67 ± 0.09 and a mean Dice coefficient of 0.80 ± 0.06, maintaining a broad accuracy plateau that supports its suitability for
automated threshold selection even in highly degraded projection data. TV regularization effectively suppressed background
noise, while the sub-voxel accuracy of the weighted projector preserved vessel boundaries and structural continuity (Figure 5C).
This combination enabled preservation of the tubular morphology and distal branch connectivity that were largely lost in
the binary reconstructions. Nevertheless, the weighted method did not completely resolve the reconstruction of the smallest
vascular structures. Some thin vessels remained partially disconnected, while closely spaced small vessels were occasionally
reconstructed as a single enlarged structure.
The weighted reconstruction framework produced a mean volume ratio of 1.05 ± 0.11, corresponding to a modest geometric
overestimation. This effect is consistent with the boundary-smoothing properties of TV regularization and remained substantially
smaller than that observed for the binary approach. Overall, the results demonstrate that accurate sparse-view coronary
reconstruction under low-dose conditions requires both weighted ray-path modeling and appropriate regularization. Among the
evaluated methods, the combination of a weighted projector and TV regularization provided the highest geometric fidelity and
the greatest robustness to noise.
Future work will focus on extending the proposed framework to uncalibrated in vivo C-arm rotational angiography data,
evaluating its performance in the presence of cardiac and respiratory motion, and systematically investigating the relationship
between reconstruction accuracy, noise level, and angular sampling density. In addition, further research will be directed toward
the development of novel regularization strategies specifically designed to preserve and enhance thin tubular structures while
maintaining effective noise suppression.
