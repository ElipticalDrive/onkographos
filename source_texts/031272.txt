## Abstract

A method is presented to extract space curves, defined by differential invariants, at increasing scales. The curves are considered as the intersection of two iso-surfaces in 3D, so their moving paths or orbits can be explicitly obtained in scale space as the intersection of two iso-surfaces in 4D. This method is based on a novel algorithm to search for iso-surfaces and their intersections in nD. The algorithm is a significant extension of the 3D Marching Lines algorithm with new orientation and implementation considerations. As a result of these considerations, the reconstructed iso-surfaces and their intersections can be proved to have good topological properties; moreover the implementation is quite straightforward.

Specifically, a 4D extension has been implemented to follow spatial curves efficiently via scale. The algorithm automatically finds the connection order of singularities, so tracking remains reliable even if scale is not densely sampled. As an example, the development of parabolic and crest curves across scale is visualized.

keywords: Scale space, Iso-surface detection, Topology, Differential geometry, Singularity theory

# 1 Introduction

In medical image processing, many imaging modalities (such as MR or CT) give images that are piecewise constant, because the intensity is related directly to tissue type. It follows that iso-surfaces correspond (approximately) to tissue regions, so the use of iso-surface techniques [18, 21, 29, 30] is well established. In many cases, the iso-surface can be extracted directly; otherwise a pre-segmentation step is required to determine which part of the image is the object and which part is the background. As 4D images are becoming increasingly common, the challenge of extracting 4D hyper-iso-surfaces and their intersections has emerged. Practical applications consist of processing temporal 3D images like beating heart sequences (3 space dimensions and 1 time), or tracking topological changes of 3D characteristic curves in scale space (3 space dimensions and 1 scale).

The main idea of scale-space theory [19, 31] is to consider an image and its details at different levels. A well-known scale-space property is the “simplification” of the image as the scale is increased, that is, small details are subsequently smoothed out. This means no enhancement and no new extrema creation at larger scales [3] in the 1D case, while in higher dimensions there could be new emerging events [6]; however they are “caused by” what happened at finer scales, in other words, “no spurious detail is generated” [19]. At a coarse scale, only the grossest structures of the original image are conserved, but they are delocalized and may have undergone substantial topological changes (splitting, merging, disappearing). To get precisely located and significant structures, the structures should be tracked from coarse to fine scale, which can be rather difficult in practice. Most work in multiscale image analysis considered 2D images [2, 20, 23, 22, 31], resulting in a 3D scale space. Though this space is Riemannian, many concepts and methods of the 3D space of everyday experience can be applied. However, starting from 3D images one gets a 4D space, and new concepts and algorithms must be designed.

A 4D (or higher dimensional) space is challenging to imagine: though there is nothing special in treating the 4th coordinate mathematically, the way of thinking about orientation needs to be revised: left-right, top-bottom do not exist in 4D contrary to the 3D world. To avoid any mysticism about higher dimensional spaces, an intuitive approach based on dimensional analogy will be used in the sequel.

The aim of this paper is to describe a novel way to extract iso-surfaces and their intersections in spaces of arbitrary  $n$  dimension and to show that the algorithm guarantees the good topology of the result. The good topological properties – such as closeness (no holes), orientability and no self-intersections – are proved by the inductive definition of nD orientation. Similarly, the recursive use of nD orientation ensures an elegantly simple calculation such that sub-tasks are realized in the 2D plane then the sub-results are coherently re-organized into a whole for nD. This gives a uniformed framework to compute the intersection of  $m$  ( $n-1$ )D iso-surfaces in the nD ( $n \geq m, n \geq 2$ ) space, hence the nD algorithm can be regarded as a nice generalization of the well-known “Marching Cubes” [21] and “Marching Lines” [29] algorithms working in 3D.

Since the algorithm correctly finds the connection order of singularities, it can be applied to track space curves at multiple scales efficiently. In previous articles [13, 14] a multiscale representation method, based on iso-surface detection, was described. It was applied to follow 2D corner points at increasing scales, whose evolution was then analysed. Here, a method is presented to investigate the scale-space behaviour of 3D characteristic curves. A motivating example of curve tracking is shown in Fig. 11.

Recent papers deal with a similar subject [16, 17]: the multiscale extraction of height ridges [9] (or generalized maxima) using iso-surface techniques as well. While this article treats the subject from an algorithmic point of view and describes how topological correctness can be guaranteed, the authors of [16, 17] convey a number of mathematical facts including the discussion of several characteristic curves.

The paper is organized as follows. First the construction of some feature curves at increasing scales are described. In the next section, the Marching Lines algorithm [29] – which inspired this work – is briefly summarized, then the extraction algorithm in nD is presented. Next, some implementation details, mainly for the 4D case, are discussed. Finally, some visualisation results are presented.

## 2.1 Mathematical overview

Except for singular (or umbilic) points on a smooth surface, any point  $P$  has two principal directions  $(\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2)$ , where the normal curvatures assume extremum values  $(k_1, k_2)$  [7]. Let  $\mathbf{n}$  denote the normal to the surface at point  $P$ , and  $k_1$  the maximum principal curvature in absolute value ( $|k_1| > |k_2|$ ) with principal direction  $\mathbf{t}_1$ . The Gaussian curvature is  $K_g = k_1 \cdot k_2$  and the mean curvature is  $K_m = (k_1 + k_2)/2$ . Parabolic curves are the loci of a surface where one of the principal curvatures vanishes. They are used e.g., to compute aspect graphs of smooth surfaces [24]. In particular, on an iso-surface they are defined as follows.

Definition 1 (parabolic curves) Parabolic curves on an iso-intensity-surface  $f(x, y, z) = I$  are characterized as the intersection of this surface  $f = I$  and the parabolic surface  $K_g = k_1 \cdot k_2 = 0$ .

Crest curves (also called “crest lines” in the literature [28]) can be intuitively defined as the points of a surface whose largest principal curvature is locally maximal in absolute value, see Fig. 1. They have been used previously to match segmented images [28] when iso-value selection is reliable. Note that crest curves are the union of the strong ridges and ravines discussed in [1, 10].

Definition 2 (crest curves) Curves of extremum curvature on an iso-intensity-surface  $f(x, y, z) = I$  are characterized as the intersection of this surface  $f = I$  and the first/second extremum surface  $e_i(x, y, z) = \nabla k_i \cdot \mathbf{t}_i = 0$  ( $i = 1, 2$ ). Here  $e_i$  ( $i = 1, 2$ ) are called the  $i$ th principal curvature extremum and  $\nabla k_i \cdot \mathbf{t}_i$  denotes a directional derivative computation. Crest curves correspond to maxima in absolute value (i.e., either positive maximum or negative minimum) of the first principal curvature  $k_1$ .

To define these curves one may reason in a different way. Parabolic curves may be considered on a family of iso-surfaces where the intensity threshold  $I$  smoothly changes generating a parabolic surface  $K_g = 0$ . The same applies to the crest surface (or first / second extremum surface) discussion. I think that the resulting parabolic / extremum / crest surfaces are smooth, which is intuitively suggested by the fact that they are defined on a family of iso-surfaces for a continuous variation of the iso-value  $I$  [28]. However, its exhaustive proof is not clear yet, and it is beyond the scope of this paper.

A close look at the computation of the principal curvature extrema  $e_1$  and  $e_2$  reveals that they are not well defined topologically. The principal directions are not vectors, so both  $(\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2)$  and  $(-\mathbf{t}_1, -\mathbf{t}_2)$  are valid choices. Unfortunately, the Poincaré-Hopf index theorem [7] implies (generally referred as the “hairy ape” theorem) that no global solution exists that fixes their orientation on a closed surface. Only a local continuous solution can be found on distinct surface patches not including any umbilic, but one has to cope with the technical difficulty which comes from the discontinuity of the principal directions. Since the principal curvatures and directions are the solutions of a real-valued continuous eigensystem (the Weingarten endomorphism), the eigenvalues are continuous, but the eigenvectors need not be. Consequently,

 $\epsilon_1$  and  $\epsilon_2$  need not be continuous which makes zero testing difficult. The Gaussian extremality  $\epsilon_g = \epsilon_1 \epsilon_2$  is a theoretical solution [26] to this problem, since its sign is independent of the orientation of  $(\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2)$ , assuming that  $[\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2, \mathbf{n}] = \mathbf{1}$ . However, in practice it cannot be applied directly: one still has to locally compute the extremal curves ( $\epsilon_g = 0$ ) defined by either  $\epsilon_1$  or  $\epsilon_2$ . Another continuous formulation of extrema is given in [9], but it ignores the case of umbilic points: thus, there are singular loci where continuity cannot be maintained. The practical extraction of these singular points, the umbilics, have been studied in [25], where a method, based on the computation of the index of the vector field corresponding to principal directions, is proposed.

The robust detection of differential singularities — features which can be defined as zero-crossings of (possibly non-linear) differential expressions — necessitates a scale-space approach as it can associate significance to features not only via thresholding but also via multiscale properties. The singularity set of a differential operator changes with the scale parameter— the orbits of singularity points in the scale-extended space may undergo bifurcations: splitting or merging at catastrophe points. In principle, orbits can be tracked from coarse to fine scale; however in practice, sampling and delocalization effects make this much more difficult. The previous feature curves described above are defined as the intersection of two implicit surfaces. This suggests a natural method to solve the problem of tracking, which will be described in the rest of this paper.

## 2.2 Why and how to use iso-surfaces for tracking features?

The usual approach to multiscale extraction is to detect singularities as local extrema at each level of resolution, then to connect them on adjacent levels. The problems with this are: (1) detection depends on the actual numerical values, which are affected by smoothing; so (2) it is difficult to specify how singularities in adjoining slices should be paired. The first problem can be avoided by detecting zero-crossings, i.e., by the extraction of zero-iso-contours or zero-iso-surfaces starting from 2D, respectively 3D, images. To overcome the ambiguity of pairing, iso-surface extraction is preferable to a sequence of iso-contours [18] (and similarly hyper-iso-surface vs. iso-surface), since the parent-child connection of singularities is established directly from the voxel structure. Here I use the fact that the sequence of iso-contours (iso-surfaces) obtained independently at different scales is equivalent to the iso-surface (hyper-iso-surface) of a multiscale image; assuming, that the same intensity constant  $I$  is used.

To get the orbits of features in scale space, a two-step procedure is proposed. First a multiscale extension image (blurred by a Gaussian density), and an image of scaled differential descriptors (smoothed by the derivatives of the Gaussian) is created from the primal image.1 Then hyper-iso-surfaces are extracted (the algorithm is described in section 4). The one from the multiscale extension shows the development of iso-surfaces as resolution varies; they become more and more “regular”, that is sharp curvatures are attenuated. In the same way as iso-intensity-surfaces extend naturally into hyper-iso-surfaces in scale space, so do the singularities of the primal image (such as curvature surfaces). This means that singularities at different scales can be observed with the help of the zero-hyper-surface extracted from the image of scaled differential descriptors. The intersection of these two hyper-iso-surfaces (one of intensities, the other of zero-crossings) illustrates the orbits of features. For example, curvature extrema points are defined as the intersection of two iso-surfaces, so their orbits can be obtained explicitly in scale space by intersecting two hyper-iso-surfaces. By following the orbits across scale one can examine how bifurcations occur, that is, how a simple feature can turn into several features when the resolution is increased.

The separate computation of multiscale images and hyper-iso-surfaces is acceptable if one is interested in the evolution of iso-surfaces. However, if the main concern is to follow features, when only the orbits are needed, this approach requires lot of unnecessary effort. Thus an alternative way is suggested which consists of the combination of the two previous steps. To do so, the simplification property of scale space is incorporated into the detection of intersection. That is, starting line segments can be found if the first two levels of the scaled images are pre-calculated and the intersection of hyper-iso-surfaces are computed. The rest of the intersected segments can be extracted using their orientation; only voxels in a reached region

### 3 Iso-surfaces in 3D — An overview

There are several techniques to extract iso-surfaces in 3D; a good survey can be found in [18]. Apart from the class of methods which follow curves / surfaces in the tangent direction(s) – which are hard to extend to higher dimensions as the tracking in the tangent space becomes too complicated – there are two families of methods. The first family [30] uses a cuberille representation, voxels being viewed as a pile of “sugar” cubes, each cubic voxel being assigned a boolean value depending on whether the intensity is higher than the iso-value threshold  $I$  or not. The interface (or union of common faces) between connected 1-valued cubes and connected 0-valued cubes constitutes the iso-surface. While this representation ensures the Jordan-Curvedness property, manifold topology is not necessarily maintained, moreover, these methods cannot be extended to compute the intersections of iso-surfaces.

The second family [4, 21, 32] (known as “Marching Cubes” type, following the classical example [21]) uses a beveled-form representation, voxels being viewed as 3D grid points such that 8 adjacent voxels form a cubic  $8\text{-cell}$ . The intensity value of each voxel is then compared to an iso-value threshold  $I$ ; in effect, the intersection of the iso-surface with the edges (2-cells) and faces (4-cells) of this cube is searched. Though attention is needed to correctly find adjacent surface patches [8], this technique guarantees manifold properties. In addition, it can be generalized to compute the intersection curves of two iso-surfaces, whose effective tool is the Marching Lines algorithm (MLA) [29]. By means of interface and orientation conventions the MLA resolves singular situations and ensures good topological properties. The reconstructed discrete surface is oriented, complete i.e. without holes (except at the boundary of the 3D grid) and contains no self-intersections. Also, each intersection curve is oriented (a linked list of 3D points where the order of points reflects the orientation) and closed (if the curve is contained within the 3D grid). Since the MLA is a starting point for the nD extension, the conventions used and the main steps of the algorithm are summarized below; for a proof of correctness see [29].

#### 3.1 Extraction of iso-surfaces

Definition 3 (iso-surface as interface) The iso-surface is the interface between the regions of the image  $f: f \geq I$  (the inside) and  $f < I$  (the outside), where  $I$  is an intensity constant.

According to this convention, each voxel is labeled either positive ( $f \geq I$ ) or negative ( $f < I$ ); to follow the explanation see Fig. 2. If at least one of the voxel labels of the 8-cell differs from the others, the 8-cell will contain some iso-surface. The points at which the iso-surface intersects the edges of the cube, called vertices of iso-patches (2 or 4 per face), are obtained by linear interpolation along the cell edges. To define adjacency relations between vertex pairs on a 4-cell, the two following conventions are used, so that each face of the cube is oriented according to the left-hand convention if it is seen from outside.

Definition 4 (orientation) When following a closed planar curve, the inside of the curve should always be on the left. The surface of a cubic cell is oriented toward the outside of the cell.

On each face, the vertices of iso-patches are labeled with the label of the endpoint of the left-hand oriented edges. The vertices are joined to form iso-segments from points labeled – to points labeled +. The ambiguous case [8] of alternately labeled voxels – giving 4 vertices – on a face is resolved by the mean-value rule.

Definition 5 (mean-value rule) Assuming that 4 interpolated vertices are generated: If the mean-value of a 4-cell is positive ( $f \geq I$ ), two segments are formed to give one positive iso-patch component containing the centre point; otherwise to give two components without the centre point.

The last two conventions ensure that the vertices of a cubic 8-cell (giving directed iso-segments on each face) can be organized into oriented iso-patch cycles.

### 3.2 Extraction of intersection curves

To get the intersection line segments, voxels are labeled according to two images  $f$  and  $g$ . First iso-patch cycles are computed with the intensity values of  $f$ , then one proceeds to get bi-iso-segments (similarly as did on a 4-cell), this time using the iso-value  $J$  corresponding to the second iso-surface  $g$  at the iso-patch vertices. To avoid ambiguity at tangent intersections of surfaces, the order of iso-surfaces is preset.

Definition 6 (intersection order of iso-surface) Let  $S$  ( $f \geq I, f < I$ ) and  $T$  ( $g \geq J, g < J$ ) be two iso-surfaces.  $C(f, g)$  is an iso-contour on  $S$  defined as the interface between the regions of  $S$ :  $g \geq J$  and  $g < J$ .  $C(g, f)$  is similarly defined; the difference between the curves is how they bypass the tangency region.

An important application is to extract feature curves such as parabolic curves, curves of extrema of the principal curvatures at a given scale. Here the values of  $g$  are the corresponding differential expressions with iso-constant  $J = 0$ , while  $(f, I)$  defines a usual iso-surface. The intersection of three iso-surfaces resulting points can similarly be computed [27].

Again, there is an ambiguity in forming bi-iso-segments in case of more than 2 bi-interpolated points. This does not matter for the 3D case; however in 4D it should be considered in a deterministic way; otherwise holes can be created. The connection of consecutive points is proposed (this ensures that bi-iso-segments do not intersect) using the mean-value rule, which is to be applied on the bi-interpolated points.

## 4 nD extension to get hyper-iso-surfaces

The good topological properties of the MLA are ensured with the help of the interface (see Definitions 3,5 & 6) and orientation (Def. 2) conventions. To extend the algorithm, I have to find a dimension-independent

generalization of the orientation conventions, while the others can be used as they are. Indeed, with the help of correct orientation the required topological properties can be easily proved; in addition, the local computation of iso-patches in hypercubes can be simply achieved using a “divide and conquer” strategy.

A discrete nD image is considered as a regular nD grid composed of hypercubes or 2n-cells, which are formed by 2n adjacent voxels. Both the hypercube and its orientation are defined inductively (see Fig. 3):

Definition 8 (orientation of hypercube)

1D arbitrary vector direction (to be fixed)

The intersection of two adjacent nD hypercubes, i.e., a (n-1)D hypercube should have opposite orientation in the two nD hypercubes.

Not only can these definitions be defined inductively, but also the structure of the nD generalized algorithm can be given recursively. The essence of the realization is that the computation is divided into sub-tasks by subsequently  $(n-2)$  times projecting a given  $2^n$ -cell or hypercube to the 2D plane. After that, iso-vertices are localized by linear interpolation, their adjacency relations on each 2D face then on each 3D cube are established with a method similar to the MLA. Finally, these sub-results are re-organized into a whole for the nD hypercube. The orientation plays a key role so that both the top-down decomposition and the bottom-up build should be coherent hence providing the correct result.

In the sequel, I restrict myself to the 4D case, though the statements are valid for any dimension  $n$ .

```

I emphasize that not only the cycles, but also the reconstructed hyper-cycles are oriented, and this orientation is coherent with the orientation of the corresponding hypercubes. That is, adjacent cycles composing the hyper-faces of a hyper-cycle have orientations related with the opposite rule, and likewise: hyper-cycles in adjacent 16-cells also have coherent orientation according to the opposite rule. ## 5 Implementation details

One can argue that processing 4D images cannot be done effectively in practice, because of the inevitably high computation time and the large amount of memory needed. However, the program can be structured in such a way that only two adjacent levels of a 4D image (i.e., two 3D images) are processed at the same time, profiting from the fact that the 4th coordinate is often essentially different from the other three spatial coordinates. For example, in multiscale applications one can suppose that the adjacent levels are closely related; moreover, because of the simplification property of scale space, the levels at finest scales contain much more detail. (It is not clear that similar statements apply to the case of time evolution.)

One approach is to start the algorithm by intensive computation at the two neighboring levels corresponding to the highest scales. Then the obtained (hyper-)cycles are propagated to the next level, that is, the remaining voxels have to be processed only if they are on a reached 16-cell. As scale is increased, there could be new emerging events [6], but they usually are only of small extent and negligible influence on the whole structure; examples of such a creation of corner points in 2D images can be found in [15]. In practice, it is quite rare for fine-to-coarse propagation to miss many features. A possible variant, which is more useful in practice, is the coarse-to-fine propagation. In this case only the “main” features (which exist even at the coarsest scale) are extracted, and this can be viewed as a strength of the algorithm. Accordingly, either case of propagation may miss some features. If all the possible feature curves have to be detected for some reason, a full scan (which can also be organized as a process based on two neighboring levels) or propagation from starting seeds (e.g., specified by the user) can be applied. More details about the implementation can be found in [11].

The propagation can be controlled with two temporary queues (one for the actual level, one for the next), as summarized below.

To start (e.g., at the two finest / coarsest scales), an extensive computation is preformed. For each 16-cell:

- • If a hyper-cycle containing a cycle in the face-cube numbered 7 is found, store into nextQueue the standard origin of the adjacent 16-cell. (This ensures the propagation to the next level; actQueue remains empty.)

To proceed level-by-level, first let actQueue := nextQueue, nextQueue := empty. While there is an elem in actQueue:

- • Take it out and calculate hyper-cycles in the corresponding 16-cell.
- • If a cycle in face-cubes numbered 1..6 is found, store the standard origin of the adjacent 16-cell (if it is not stored yet) into actQueue. (This is necessitated by topological changes between two consecutive levels.)
- • If a cycle in the face-cube numbered 7 is found, store the standard origin of the adjacent 16-cell into nextQueue.

As explained earlier, the 4D algorithm can be traced back to 3D calculations via orientation-preserving bijections. The transformation of hyper-faces can be obtained by a look-up table procedure, as opposed to solving equations. This greatly speeds up the 4D  $\rightarrow$  3D reduction; similarly, other topological information like edge and face connection can also be stored in look-up tables.

Finally a remark: the results are rather difficult to visualize; the best way I have found so far is animation and extensive use of colors.

Note that the generated surfaces are closed, i.e., without holes. Also, the merging, appearance and disappearance of features are easy to investigate.

Tracking extremum-type singularities is more difficult. First, if one wants to follow only maxima – the features that are needed in practice – the reconstructed surface may have holes, that is, its good topological properties are not conserved. Since maxima and minima may merge, and absolute maxima may change to absolute minima (for example, a positive maximum may become a positive minimum), just as in the 2D case [14, 20]. Moreover, if  $|k_1| \approx |k_2|$  the role of two principal curvatures may be interchanged causing a switch between the two principal directions. One solution is to follow both extrema (maintaining the correct topology), and to choose the maxima after extraction. Alternatively, only maxima are followed and one should take care with propagation [11]: holes are allowed if and only if minima or catastrophe points corresponding to merging are detected.

Second, as discussed in section 2.1, principal curvature extrema are not well defined topologically. Since the principal directions are not vectors, both  $(\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2)$  and  $(-\mathbf{t}_1, -\mathbf{t}_2)$  are valid choices. In the case of reversed directions, the sign of the extremum  $e_1$  alters; this may lead to incorrect detections (“phantoms”) if  $e_1$  is computed in a cell in the direction of  $\mathbf{t}_1$  but in a neighboring cell in the direction of  $-\mathbf{t}_1$ . As the “hairy ape” theorem implies, there are singular points, umbilics, where the principal direction fields are not continuous. Nevertheless, far from an umbilic, there are two possibilities to regularize the extraction of crest curves. Either a different formulation can be applied to compute curvature extrema, as is done in [9, 26]. Alternatively, the detection of phantom segments can be avoided by curve following, so the tangent  $\mathbf{t}$  of the extracted line segment is used to orient  $(\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2)$  such that  $\mathbf{t}_2\mathbf{t} \geq \mathbf{0}$  and  $[\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_2, \mathbf{t}] = \mathbf{1}$ . While this is fairly straightforward to implement in 3D [29], that is, in the case of fixed scale extraction, the algorithm is rather complicated in 4D since propagation according to scale and direction need to be achieved at the same time. Moreover, because of the fundamental, unsolvable problem of discontinuity, the 2D manifolds generated by scaled crest curves are not necessarily oriented. Indeed, umbilics are singularities not only in position but also in scale, so crest curves (with possibly different orientations) can split and merge in the neighborhood of umbilics, thus my algorithm can produce holes, see Fig. 9.

Empirically it seems that regularization by scale alone gives similar (not better) results to regularization by curve-following alone. Thus both types of regularization are required if robust detection is to be achieved. Another way could be to find a differential expression which makes possible the extraction of curvature extrema without considering global orientation properties. I am working to establish which approach provides the better results.

## 7 Conclusion

A natural method has been presented to extract and represent multiscale singularity orbits using on iso-surface techniques. It is based on a novel algorithm which efficiently computes iso-surfaces and their intersections in nD. This algorithm guarantees good topological properties – such as closeness, orientability and no self-intersections – of the result. All these topological properties can be easily proved and also the implementation is elegantly simple, due to the proposed inductive definition and recursive use of orientation.

In particular, the algorithm has been implemented in 4D and applied to visualize the development of characteristic curves, defined by differential invariants, in scale space. Since the algorithm automatically finds the connection order of singularities, the proposed method is very convenient both for detection at
