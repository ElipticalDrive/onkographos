Abstract
Keywords
In recent years, neural field-based methods for synthesizing novel
views have gained popularity due to their exceptional rendering
quality and fast training speed. However, the computational cost
of volumetric rendering has significantly increased with the advancement of camera technology and the subsequent rise in average camera resolution. Despite extensive efforts to accelerate the
training process, the training duration remains unacceptable for
high-resolution inputs. Therefore, it’s crucial to develop efficient
sampling methods to optimize the learning process of neural fields
from large inputs. In this paper, we present a new technique called
Superpixel-based Efficient Sampling (SES) to improve the learning efficiency of neural fields. Our approach optimizes pixel-level
ray sampling by segmenting the error map into multiple superpixels and dynamically updating their errors during training to
increase ray sampling in superpixel areas with higher rendering
errors. Compared with other methods, our approach leverages the
flexibility of superpixels, effectively reducing redundant sampling
while considering local information. Our method not only speeds
up the learning process but also enhances the rendering quality
learned from large inputs. We conduct extensive experiments to
evaluate the effectiveness of our method across several baselines
and datasets. The code will be released.

Introduction
Novel View Synthesis (NVS) has always been a hot research topic
in computer vision, with wide-ranging applications in virtual reality, medical imaging, and augmented reality. In recent years, NVS
methods that represent scenes using neural fields have gradually
become prevailing due to their promising results [23–25, 29, 31, 49,
50, 54, 57]. These methods utilize Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)
to represent the scenes and then render them using volume rendering techniques. Compared with traditional Multi-View Stereo
(MVS) methods [5, 7, 9, 13, 16, 17, 43, 48], they can easily handle
textureless surfaces and interpolate unseen areas via regularization
techniques [19]. However, rendering many sampled points using
volume rendering results in significant computational overhead,
which becomes even more pronounced as the image resolution
increases.
For novel view synthesis, the resolution of input images directly
determines the upper limit of reconstruction quality. As camera
technology advances, the resolution of captured images continues
to increase. However, high-resolution images capture more details
and also bring a substantial amount of redundant data. Therefore,
traditional random pixel sampling methods [28, 41] become overwhelmed by the huge number of pixels in high-resolution inputs.
To handle this limitation, [52] employs a patch sampling strategy
combined with an encoder-decoder structure. They use the encoder
to encode 3D geometric information and the decoder to render
high-resolution outputs. [42] introduces color-guided ray sampling.
They guide the sampling of rays by color and depth, allowing them

To address the challenge of large input, we introduce a Superpixelbased Efficient Sampling (SES) method for learning neural fields.
Our core idea is to adaptively increase the number of samples, especially in regions with significant rendering errors, thereby reducing
redundancy in sampling. Simultaneously, our goal is to improve the
sampling of light rays to account for local image details. We start
by using the SLIC algorithm [1] to segment the input image into
various superpixels, each representing unique local information.
Compared to other segmentation methods like quadtree, superpixels can significantly reduce redundancy. In subsequent steps, we
treat superpixels as the smallest processing unit. We set different
sampling probabilities for various superpixels based on their rendering errors. We continuously update them during training and
integrate the rendering errors into the loss function. We apply our
approach to various well-known neural field baselines and validate
it across multiple benchmark datasets. Our image rendering quality has reached state-of-the-art levels at different resolutions, and
integrating our method does not incur additional computational
overhead. In summary, our main contributions can be summarized
as follows:
• We present an efficient and effective sampling method for
learning neural fields from large inputs. By segmenting the
image into superpixels, we reduce redundancy in light rays,
improve sampling efficiency, and consider local sampling
information.
• We introduce a new loss function to speed up the convergence of neural fields in areas with significant rendering
errors.

In graphics research, achieving Novel View Synthesis (NVS) depends on effectively managing intermediate 3D scene representations. The previous efforts have concentrated on investigating
appropriate 3D representations. Mesh-based or pointcloud-based
methods [2, 6, 10, 34, 47] often rely on depth information or Structure From Motion (SFM) [35] techniques for geometric shape restoration, presenting challenges in rendering realistic images. While approaches utilizing multi-plane images (MPIs) [11, 27, 39, 40, 46, 61]
to express scenes as feature maps or stacked images can yield highquality renderings, they constrain the variability of perspectives.
Voxel-based representations [21, 24, 32, 36, 37, 44] offer swift image rendering but face limitations in resolution. Recent methods
represent scenes using neural fields, providing greater flexibility
alongside high-quality image generation [28, 30, 38, 58]. In 2020,
NeRF [28] was introduced, revolutionizing NVS through volume
rendering. NeRF uses neural networks to map 3D coordinates and
view directions to volume density and color. It then uses alpha
blending for pixel color rendering, resulting in impressive outcomes. NeRF’s emergence has significantly advanced novel view
synthesis, 3D reconstruction, and point cloud rendering.
2.2
Neural Fields
Inspired by NeRF, many neural field methods have been proposed
over the past few years. NeuS [49] proposes to represent a surface as the zero-level set of a signed distance function (SDF) and
develop a new volume rendering method to train the neural SDF
representation, leading to more accurate surface reconstruction.
NeILF [56] represents the scene lighting as the neural incident light
field and material properties as the surface BRDF modeled by MLPs.
Therefore, NeILF can estimate material properties even for scenes
with complex lighting and geometry. NeRF-W [26] introduces a
series of extensions to NeRF to address the issues of uncontrolled
images, thereby enabling accurate reconstructions from unstructured image collections taken from the internet. Point-NeRF [53]
uses neural 3D point clouds with associated neural features to
model a radiance field. Point-NeRF combines the advantages of
volume rendering and multi-view stereo. MobileNeRF [8] introduces a new NeRF representation based on textured polygons that
can synthesize novel images efficiently with standard rendering
pipelines. MobileNeRF can achieve interactive frame rates on various computing platforms, including mobile phones. Tetra-NeRF
[20] proposes to use an adaptive representation based on tetrahedra
obtained by Delaunay triangulation instead of uniform subdivision
or point-based representations. Tetra-NeRF enables efficient training and leads to state-of-the-art results. Zip-NeRF [4] addresses the
anti-aliasing of grid-based representations and constructs a technique that combines mip-NeRF 360 and grid-based models. NPBG
[3] presents a point-based approach that uses a raw point cloud as
the geometric representation of a scene and augments each point
with a learnable neural descriptor. 3DGS [18] represents the scene

2.3
Neural Field Training Acceleration
NeRF uses volume rendering to render pixel colors and employs
a two-stage sampling method to bring sampling points closer to
the surface. However, this approach typically requires 1-2 days
or several days for training completion. To accelerate NeRF training, ENeRF [14] uses the geometric features of the scene to direct
surface sampling. Some methods leverage explicit modeling techniques to accelerate scene training. For example, KiloNeRF [33]
breaks down NeRF into multiple small MLPs for quicker training.
In contrast, DVGO [41], Plenoxels [12], and similar works utilize
learnable explicit meshes to speed up training. Instant-NGP [29]
uses multi-resolution hash encoding with a hash table and jump
sampling to quickly approach the scene surface during training.
The works mentioned above concentrate on improving sampling
strategies in 3D space to make them faster. As for 2D space, [60]
uses a quadtree to guide sampling, reducing the number of sampled rays to accelerate training. [42] efficiently samples rays by
filtering them using images and depth information. These methods
significantly speed up NeRF training but may have limitations with
high-resolution input images.

High-Resolution Synthesis
To process high-resolution image inputs and produce high-resolution
images, 4k-NeRF [52] encodes the high-resolution images into feature maps. After training, a decoder decodes these feature maps
into high-resolution images. This method decreases the computational burden during NeRF training. However, jointly training the
encoder-decoder with NeRF still requires a substantial amount of
time. UHD-NeRF [22] reconstructs higher-resolution images using
a combination of neural networks and point cloud representations.
It intends to rebuild the low-frequency components of the scene
using MLP and the high-frequency components using point clouds.
It is important to note that both methods mentioned above utilize
patch sampling to prevent excessive memory consumption. While
patches capture local information in the scene, their fixed shape
may cause fragmented image information.

SLIC: Simple Linear Iterative Clustering (SLIC) [1] is a fast and

3.2
Superpixel-guide Efficient Sampling (SES)
NeRF [28] trains DNNs to predict the color and density information
of a 3D point. Compared with simple regions, DNNs need more
iterations to learn spatial information in complex regions. Previous
methods [42, 60] have attempted multiple sampling in regions with
large rendering errors or drastic color changes while reducing sampling in areas with small rendering errors to achieve better results.
Redundant computations resulting from inadequate partitioning
by quadtrees and other segmentation methods increase with input
scale and are impractical. Methods that guide computation through
color gradients also face challenges in handling large-scale image
blocks. To address these issues, we utilize the SLIC algorithm [1] to
segment images into superpixels, which act as the smallest processing units for guiding sampling. Due to the nature of superpixels,
the issue of inadequate partitioning caused by structures such as
quadtrees is resolved, reducing computational overhead in single
processing instances.

4.1 Implementation Details
Integrate with Existing Baselines
In this section, we introduce how to integrate SES with existing
neural field baselines. We incorporate SES into neural field training
and apply a set of techniques to handle high-resolution inputs,
thereby improving the rendering quality of neural field baselines.
Training Progress. Our training process is illustrated in Fig.
2. Initially, we use a random sampling method for training and
update the errors of superpixels in each training iteration. After
completing the secondary segmentation computation, we use superpixels to guide sampling and ray generation while concurrently
accumulating rendering errors. Upon completion of training for
all rays generated in a single sampling, the superpixel errors are
updated, and sampled rays are regenerated.
Loss Design. We optimize the parameters of the neural fields using pixel-level Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss L𝑚𝑠𝑒 and superpixel
loss L𝑠𝑝 . Our total loss is computed as follows:

have similar tree-like structures to reduce sampling redundancy.

4.3
NeRF Synthetic
The NeRF synthetic dataset [28] is a commonly used benchmark
in the neural field. Using Blender, it renders eight scenes, each
with distinct characteristics, and generates 100 training images per

structural information in high-error areas. Our two-stage strategy
balances stability and rendering quality.

Conclusion
In our paper, we present a method called Superpixel-based Efficient
Sampling (SES). This method uses superpixels to effectively guide
the pixel-level ray sampling process in neural field training. SES
allows for processing large inputs and generating high-quality images. Leveraging the superpixel characteristics, we have developed
a new segmentation, updating, and sampling approach tailored
specifically to them. Our strategy effectively balances the relationship between sampling errors and color information, allowing for
more efficient extraction of information from large inputs. Furthermore, we suggest a new loss function that effectively leverages the
characteristics of superpixels. Through experimental evaluations,
our approach has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance when
compared to existing methods.
Limitation and Future Work Although superpixel-based ray
sampling can enhance image quality, especially for individual objects, our method becomes highly sensitive to parameters as scene
complexity increases (e.g., in the mip-NeRF 360 dataset). Hyperparameters such as the number of superpixels significantly affect the
reconstruction quality. A potential solution is to utilize adaptive
parameters, optimizing hyperparameters during training to avoid
manual settings. Besides, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [18] and
2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS) [15] are becoming popular in neural
field research. Instead of sampling pixels, they sample the image
patches to train the models. Therefore, how to extend our method
to 3DGS and 2DGS is another promising future work.
