Abstract
Multi-scale rendering exposes scale-dependent reconstruction errors in 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) when the rendering resolution differs from the training resolution. Existing
anti-aliasing and multi-scale optimization methods reduce these artifacts, but each Gaussian
usually keeps a fixed color representation and a fixed spatial allocation after densification.
This fixed representation is restrictive at coarse scales because one rendered pixel combines multiple Gaussian contributions into an average color that can differ from the color
assigned to each Gaussian. In addition, Gaussians allocated by fine-scale reconstruction
criteria may provide insufficient coverage for regions with persistent coarse-scale errors.
This paper proposes adaptive-scale color offset and error-guided Gaussian reallocation for
multi-scale 3DGS. The adaptive-scale color offset applies a scale-specific correction to the
shared base color of each Gaussian while keeping its geometry unchanged. Error-guided
Gaussian reallocation shifts low-contribution Gaussians toward high-error regions under a
fixed primitive budget and preserves Gaussians that support coarse-scale rendering. Experimental results show that the proposed method improves multi-scale rendering quality
across rendering scales on Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks and Temples, and Deep Blending, with
larger gains at coarse scales. These results demonstrate that adapting Gaussian color and
primitive allocation together improves scale-consistent 3DGS rendering without modifying
the inference-stage rendering process.

Novel view synthesis (NVS) aims to synthesize images of a scene from viewpoints
unseen during training. NVS supports practical applications such as virtual reality, digital
twins, robotics, and 3D content creation. Neural radiance fields (NeRF) [1] represent a scene
as a continuous volumetric function for novel view rendering. Although NeRF achieves
high visual quality, dense ray sampling increases the rendering cost. 3D Gaussian splatting
(3DGS) [2] reduces this cost by representing the scene with explicit anisotropic Gaussian
primitives and rasterizing them directly. These primitives are optimized to reconstruct a
single scene from training images at the training resolution.
In practical rendering scenarios, the same optimized 3DGS representation is often
used at scales different from the training resolution. This scale mismatch becomes more

pronounced at coarse scales because each pixel covers a larger projected scene area. The
effective sampling density therefore decreases, and aliasing artifacts become more severe.
For this reason, a 3DGS model optimized at the training resolution can lose reconstruction
accuracy at other rendering scales.
Existing multi-scale 3DGS methods address this scale sensitivity mainly through
two strategies: scale-aware filtering [3,4] and multi-scale optimization [5,6]. Scale-aware
filtering constrains Gaussian primitives according to the target sampling rate to suppress
aliasing. The filtering changes the scale-dependent contribution of each Gaussian, while
the Gaussian color remains shared across scales. A complementary strategy is multi-scale
optimization, which trains one shared Gaussian set across multiple target scales. This
optimization balances scale-dependent reconstruction losses, but the shared Gaussian set
still defines per-Gaussian color and primitive allocation independently of coarse-scale
errors. Figure 1 shows visible quality degradation in MS-GS under coarse-scale rendering.
This example motivates a closer examination of Gaussian color and primitive allocation
under coarse-scale rendering.

For Gaussian color, the key issue arises from the mismatch between fixed per-Gaussian
color and the pixel average formed at each rendering scale. At a coarse scale, one pixel
aggregates the projected contributions of multiple Gaussians into an average color. The
average pixel color can differ from the color assigned to each contributing Gaussian. A
single per-Gaussian color therefore only approximates these scale-dependent averages,
which can produce coarse-scale color errors. For primitive allocation, the main issue
arises during densification. Densification adds Gaussians in regions with large fine-scale
reconstruction error, so fine-scale criteria largely determine primitive placement. After
densification, the number of Gaussians is fixed, and later optimization primarily refines
existing primitives. This allocation can concentrate redundant primitives in well-covered
regions, whereas coarse-scale error regions can remain underrepresented.
To address the color mismatch and primitive allocation imbalance, we propose
adaptive-scale color offset and error-guided Gaussian reallocation (ACO-EGR) for multiscale 3DGS. ACO adds a scale-specific color offset to the shared base color of each Gaussian.
At a target scale, the corresponding offset corrects the color used for rendering. This scheme
provides scale-dependent color adaptation without changing Gaussian geometry. EGR
redistributes low-contribution Gaussians toward high-error regions without increasing
the primitive count. The contribution of each Gaussian is measured from its accumulated
rendering weights across training views. Gaussians with large coarse-scale contributions
are retained at their current positions. Low-contribution Gaussians outside this protected

2. Related Work
This section reviews prior studies relevant to scale-dependent rendering in NVS.
The review first covers NeRF methods based on volumetric pre-filtering and scale-aware
sampling. It then summarizes 3DGS methods that modify Gaussian filtering, primitive
selection, or training across scales. Finally, it discusses Gaussian color and primitive
allocation to clarify the motivation for the proposed method.
2.1. NeRF-Based Methods for Scale-Dependent Rendering
NeRF [1] represents a scene as a continuous radiance field parameterized by position
and viewing direction. Novel views are synthesized by integrating sampled color and
density values along camera rays. Although this rendering process achieves high viewsynthesis quality, dense per-ray sampling increases computational cost. To reduce this
cost, NeRF-based methods [7–10] use hash grids or explicit feature structures to accelerate
rendering. These methods lower the sampling cost while preserving the ray-based sampling
structure. When the output scale changes, point-wise ray samples can inadequately cover
the scene area represented by each pixel and cause aliasing artifacts.
Anti-aliased NeRF methods address this issue by incorporating the pixel footprint into
volumetric rendering. Mip-NeRF [11] approximates each pixel with a cone and encodes the
corresponding conical frustum using integrated positional encoding. Mip-NeRF 360 [12]
extends this design to unbounded scenes through a nonlinear parameterization that maps
distant scene regions into a bounded domain. Zip-NeRF [13] applies anti-aliased rendering
to grid-based fields for more efficient training. These methods indicate that scale consistency
in NeRF depends on matching ray sampling to the pixel footprint.
2.2. 3DGS-Based Methods for Anti-Aliased Rendering
3DGS [2] renders anisotropic Gaussian primitives through rasterization for real-time
novel view synthesis. A tile-based rasterizer projects these primitives onto the image plane
and composites their color and opacity contributions through alpha blending. Because
Gaussian parameters are optimized for the sampling pattern at the training resolution,
rendering at a different output scale can introduce aliasing or blur.

Filtering-based methods address scale-dependent artifacts by controlling the projected
footprint of each Gaussian. Mip-Splatting [3] limits high-frequency components near the
training sampling rate with a 3D smoothing filter and a 2D approximation of the box
filter. This band-limiting suppresses aliasing when the rendering sampling rate differs
from the training setting. Analytic-Splatting [14] integrates the Gaussian response over
each pixel footprint to compute an analytic anti-aliased value. Scale-adaptive Gaussian
splatting [15] uses a 2D scale-adaptive filter at test time to adjust the effective Gaussian
scale to the target rendering scale while keeping the trained parameters fixed. Anti-Aliased
2D Gaussian Splatting (AA-2DGS) [16] mitigates 2D Gaussian splatting (2DGS) aliasing via
a world-space flat smoothing kernel while preserving geometric accuracy.
Beyond test-time filtering, multi-scale 3DGS methods construct a shared Gaussian set
across multiple rendering scales. Multi-Scale 3DGS (MS-GS) [5] optimizes a single model
with reconstruction losses evaluated at multiple output scales. MS-GS organizes fine-detail
primitives into hierarchical Gaussian subsets and selects a scale-matched subset for each
pixel to suppress aliasing. These methods share per-Gaussian color and primitive placement
across scales. Coarse-scale color mismatch and allocation imbalance can therefore still occur.
2.3. Color Modeling and Primitive Allocation in 3DGS
Color modeling and primitive allocation have been studied in 3DGS from different
perspectives. Spherical harmonics represent the view-dependent color of each Gaussian,
and appearance embeddings compensate for image- or camera-specific appearance variations. Such color models describe angular or image-level color variation rather than
scale-dependent color changes. NeRF-W [17] associates an appearance embedding with
each training image and estimates an embedding for a novel view. VastGaussian [18] uses
an appearance module during training and removes it at inference. ACO associates an
explicit red-green-blue (RGB) offset with each rendering scale and applies the offset only to
the SH DC coefficient of each Gaussian. The higher-order SH coefficients remain shared
across scales. Since the target rendering scale is specified at inference, the corresponding
offset can be selected directly.
LightGaussian [19] addresses model compression. It prunes low-significance Gaussians, distills the SH representation to a lower degree, and quantizes Gaussian attributes.
Its objective differs from the scale-dependent color correction performed by ACO.
AbsGS [20] adds Gaussians guided by gradient-based criteria, whereas 3DGS as
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (3DGS-MCMC) [21] relocates Gaussians while maintaining
a fixed Gaussian count. Pixel-GS [22] refines the densification criterion with pixel-aware
gradients, and Mini-Splatting [23] reorganizes the Gaussian density under a constrained
budget. These allocation strategies are designed for single-scale training. Such reallocation
can move Gaussians away from regions where they are useful for coarse-scale rendering.
These single-scale allocation criteria indicate that shared 3DGS representations require
primitive placement criteria that reflect coarse-scale rendering errors.

and gradient-based measures for each Gaussian, converts them into LC and HE scores,

For each of the M selected pairs, EGR relocates the LC Gaussian to the location of
its paired HE Gaussian and copies the HE Gaussian’s scale, orientation, and base color.
The relocated Gaussian resets its higher-order SH coefficients and ACO offsets to zero and
clears its optimizer state before subsequent training at the new location. After relocation,
the paired Gaussians share the same location. EGR rescales their opacities to preserve the
original transmittance according to the opacity split of 3DGS-MCMC [21].
The copied attributes serve only as an initialization at the high-error location. The
paired Gaussians retain separate parameters and are independently optimized during
subsequent training, allowing their appearance parameters to become distinct.
EGR operates after densification, once the Gaussian count has been fixed. Reallocation
is performed at fixed intervals within a bounded window, followed by a standard optimization phase without further changes to the Gaussian allocation. Before every round,
the LC and HE statistics are recomputed from the current model using all scale-1 training
cameras. Each relocation therefore reflects the reconstruction state after the preceding
optimization period.
EGR uses the adaptive relocation count, parameter reset, opacity rescaling, and continued gradient-based optimization as its stabilization strategy in place of an explicit rollback
or separate re-correction module. The adaptive relocation count limits the perturbation
introduced in each round, while the parameter reset and opacity rescaling provide a stable
starting state for the relocated Gaussians. Gradient-based optimization resumes immediately after every round and updates the relocated Gaussians before the statistics for the
next round are computed.
4. Experimental Results
This section evaluates the proposed ACO-EGR on three real-scene benchmarks with
MS-GS [5] as the multi-scale 3DGS backbone. The evaluation first describes the experimental setup and then presents the multi-scale rendering performance of ACO-EGR
compared with the retrained 3DGS and MS-GS models. The ablation studies quantify
https://doi.org/10.3390/math14152713

module-level contributions while validating the ACO color-offset configuration and the
EGR preservation strategy.
4.1. Experimental Setup
All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 graphics processing
unit (GPU). The proposed ACO-EGR was implemented on MS-GS [5], which served as
the multi-scale 3DGS backbone. The single-scale 3DGS baseline and the MS-GS backbone
were retrained from their public implementations under the official MS-GS training and
testing protocol. The evaluation used three novel view synthesis benchmarks: Mip-NeRF
360 [12], Tanks and Temples [24], and Deep Blending [25]. For fair comparison, MS-GS and
ACO-EGR were trained with identical experimental settings and densification thresholds.
MS-GS and ACO-EGR were both trained for 40,000 iterations, with densification performed
between iterations 500 and 15,000. EGR performs ten reallocation rounds at intervals of
1000 iterations from iteration 16,000 to iteration 25,000. Reallocation is then disabled, and
standard optimization continues until iteration 40,000. Mip-Splatting, Analytic-Splatting,
and AA-2DGS were trained for 30,000 iterations following their original training settings.
Within each backbone comparison, the baseline and its ACO-EGR variant use the same
iteration budget.
All methods use the same input resolutions, preprocessing, and training and evaluation camera splits. No additional data augmentation is used. Within each backbone
comparison, the baseline and its ACO-EGR variant use the same multi-scale sampling
schedule and loss configuration. Under this setting, performance differences between
the two models can be attributed to the proposed modules rather than to differences in
training conditions.
Rendering quality was evaluated using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) [26], structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [27], and learned perceptual image patch similarity
(LPIPS) [28]. To assess computational overhead, inference-stage cost was measured by
per-frame runtime and model memory usage.

Compared with the retrained MS-GS backbone, the proposed method provides larger
gains mainly at coarser rendering scales. At 1× and 2×, PSNR changes remain small
across the three datasets, with only a 0.02 dB decrease at 2× on Deep Blending. From 4×
onward, ACO-EGR improves PSNR over MS-GS at all available scales. On Mip-NeRF
360, the PSNR gain reaches 1.96 dB at 4× and 2.09 dB at 128×. On Tanks and Temples
and Deep Blending, the gains at the coarsest available scale reach 0.71 and 0.57 dB at 64×,
respectively. These PSNR gains are accompanied by higher SSIM from 4× onward on all
three datasets. The combined PSNR and SSIM improvements indicate stronger coarse-scale
structural consistency rather than only higher pixel-wise fidelity.
LPIPS complements the PSNR and SSIM results by reflecting perceptual differences
that are less directly represented by pixel-wise metrics. Across the evaluated LPIPS scales,
the proposed method generally lowers perceptual error on all three datasets, especially at
scales where local structures become difficult to distinguish. These LPIPS results indicate
that ACO-EGR improves perceptual fidelity beyond the PSNR and SSIM gains, particularly
in texture-rich regions and thin structures.
We assess the consistency of the PSNR gains using paired scene-level statistics on the

as the ACO-EGR result minus the corresponding MS-GS result. This paired difference
removes the scene-dependent PSNR level shared by the two methods and directly measures
the gain obtained by ACO-EGR.

because the reallocated Gaussians require subsequent optimization updates before their
contribution becomes visible. As training proceeds, ACO-EGR reaches a lower L1 loss

between the two loss curves becomes largest near the final iterations. This observed
behavior indicates a lower final training loss with limited fluctuations, providing empirical
evidence of stable training behavior without establishing formal convergence.

Rendering-scale variation affects image-plane spatial sampling, while SH order represents angular frequency with respect to the viewing direction. Because all rendering
scales use the same camera poses, the SH evaluation direction of each Gaussian remains
unchanged across scales.
At coarser scales, each pixel covers a larger spatial region and combines contributions
from more Gaussians. This suggests that the resulting mismatch is more closely related
to changes in aggregated pixel color than to changes in viewing direction. Based on
this reasoning, ACO applies the scale-specific correction to the direction-independent DC
coefficient, while the higher-order SH coefficients remain shared across scales.
To examine this design, Figure 6 reports the mean absolute magnitude of the SH
coefficients across coefficient indices under the MS-GS setting on Deep Blending. The
DC coefficient has a substantially larger magnitude than the higher-order SH coefficients.
The SH coefficient analysis and higher-order offset ablation provide empirical support for
using the DC coefficient as the primary target for scale-specific color correction. Thus, the
DC-only design is motivated by this mechanism and supported by empirical results rather
than by a formal analytical derivation.

two configurations produce similar results because fine-scale details dominate the reconstruction error. As the rendering scale becomes coarser, removing preservation increases
the PSNR loss. The PSNR losses reach 0.79 dB at 16×, 1.09 dB at 64×, and 0.99 dB at 128×,

can reassign Gaussians that retain low-resolution structural information. The preservation
gate protects Gaussians that remain important at coarse scales from being relocated.

supporting coarse-scale reconstruction remain in place. In contrast, the configuration
without preservation in Table 8 retains reallocation while removing this safeguard, which
explains its larger degradation at coarse scales.

5. Conclusions
This paper proposed ACO-EGR for scale-consistent multi-scale 3D Gaussian splatting.
The method is motivated by fixed per-Gaussian color and post-densification primitive
allocation, both of which become more evident at coarse rendering scales. ACO corrects
scale-dependent color shifts through scale-specific DC-term offsets. EGR reallocates lowcontribution Gaussians to regions with persistent reconstruction errors and preserves
coarse-scale structural primitives. Experimental results show that ACO-EGR improves
rendering quality primarily at coarse scales on Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks and Temples, and Deep
Blending under the MS-GS setting. On Mip-NeRF 360, the PSNR gains over MS-GS reached
1.96 dB at 4× and 2.09 dB at 128×, with a marginal increase in inference-time cost. The
ablation studies confirmed that ACO provides the main scale-dependent color correction
and that EGR refines Gaussian allocation in scale ranges where local structure still affects
reconstruction quality. Further work will extend ACO-EGR toward broader scale-adaptive
3D scene representations with more flexible color adaptation and allocation strategies.
