Abstract
While 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) provides high-quality novel-view synthesis, its lack of an inherent hierarchy forces
rasterization effort to scale with fine scene details rather than perceptually visible features. Existing Level of Detail (LoD)
approaches typically address this by training discrete levels as independent models, relying on fixed selection thresholds that do
not ensure smooth perceptual transitions. We introduce Hierarchical Level of Detail (HLoD) for 3DGS, a method that jointly
optimizes Gaussian levels into a single, explicit parent-child hierarchy. To maintain a valid tree topology during adaptive density
control, HLoD performs hierarchy-aware pruning and parent-child regularization, combined with scale safeguards to ensure
spatial consistency across frequency bands. Unlike prior methods, HLoD avoids fixed size thresholds per level, preserving the
ability to assign point density based on local geometric detail. At runtime, an efficient GPU traversal dynamically collapses
fine sibling groups into their trained coarse parents based on local pixel-projection thresholds. Crucially, by integrating this
traversal directly into the optimization process alongside strategies to balance photometric supervision between fine details and
coarse nodes, HLoD maintains perceptually gradual LoD transitions that remain stable under inhomogeneous point distributions.
Our evaluation demonstrates that HLoD achieves competitive reconstruction quality while enabling continuous control over the
performance-quality trade-off along two axes: dynamically adjusting pixel-projection thresholds for spatially local LoD, and
selectively rendering hierarchical subsets for adaptive memory scaling. Achieving these seamless transitions requires minimal
traversal overhead while allowing overall performance to scale dynamically with scene complexity. Finally, we establish that
joint optimization is structurally essential, as post-hoc hierarchical assignments fundamentally fail to preserve visual fidelity.

1. Introduction
Explicit radiance fields such as 3D Gaussian Splatting [KKL∗ 23]
(3DGS) provide high-quality real-time view synthesis, but their
primitive sets are largely unstructured. Adaptive density control
places Gaussians where the photometric loss requires them, yet
the final model does not record which fine primitives refine which
coarser scene elements. This limits controllable level-of-detail (LoD)
rendering: a standard 3DGS model can be rendered quickly, but it
does not directly provide a local mechanism for replacing a group
of fine splats by a compatible coarse representative.
Existing Gaussian LoD methods address this limitation by training discrete, scale-bounded representations. These levels provide
useful operating points for different budgets, but they are typically
stored as independent models. In particular, FLoD [SCSU25] trains
a coarse-to-fine sequence of scale-constrained Gaussian sets, yet the
resulting levels do not contain explicit parent-child relationships.
Consequently, LoD selection is driven by global model choices or
broad camera-distance bands. Such strategies can be efficient, but
they cannot express a local screen-space decision in which one region of a view remains fine while another region of the same view
is replaced by trained coarse ancestors.
We present HLoD (Hierarchical Level of Detail), a training and
rendering pipeline that demonstrates how scale-bounded 3DGS levels can be jointly trained into a single explicit parent-child hierarchy.
The goal is to adaptively reduce the number of rendered Gaussians
through local hierarchical substitution, a capability absent in vanilla
3DGS and independent-level LoD methods. At render time, HLoD
traverses the tree from fine to coarse and collapses a complete sibling
group to its trained parent when all children project below a pixel
threshold. The active representation is therefore selected per group
in screen-space rather than per camera in world space. Homogeneous or low-footprint regions, such as flat walls, floors, or smooth
background areas, can be represented by larger coarse Gaussians,
while foreground structures, silhouettes, foliage, fabric, and other
high-frequency details remain at finer levels within the same view,
which is illustrated by LoD heatmaps. Unlike the depth-ordered
bands of distance-based FLoD [SCSU25], HLoD forms spatially interleaved hierarchy cuts governed by local projected structure rather
than global camera distance.
Maintaining such a hierarchy during optimization is non-trivial.
Adaptive density control freely clones, splits, prunes, and moves
primitives to reduce photometric error, whereas a traversable hierarchy requires stable structural relationships between coarse parents and fine descendants. HLoD addresses this tension through an
append-only training schedule that keeps all previously optimized
levels live while newly spawned Gaussians store explicit parent
links and birth-level tags. Stochastic rendering paths that either restrict traversal to the current level or temporarily cap the hierarchy
ensure that both newly created fine primitives and coarser collapse
parents receive photometric supervision in the roles they will later
play at inference. Hierarchy-aware density control, together with a
scale-upper hinge and a parent-child Mahalanobis regularizer, helps
preserve geometric compatibility under densification and pruning.
Shifting from global distance bands to local screen-space adaptivity introduces a fundamental trade-off between structural flexibility
and rendering dynamics. By design, HLoD maintains fine primitives
whenever locally required, which naturally yields higher active primitive counts than aggressive global selection. Additionally, structural
simplification does not strictly guarantee proportional speedups:
replacing a dense cluster of small children with a broad coarse
ancestor can increase tile overdraw during rasterization. The resulting performance benefits are therefore scene-dependent. While our
streamlined CUDA traversal provides practical speed and memory
gains on several indoor scenes, expansive outdoor scenes can become bottlenecked by rasterization overhead. Ultimately, HLoD
serves as a capability-driven representation that trades raw uniform
speed for granular, screen-space LoD control.
Across a set of real-world captures, HLoD preserves high-fidelity
reconstruction quality while exposing spatially local, multi-level
active sets within a single model. Our evaluations demonstrate that
this structure must be explicitly learned, as heuristic post-training
assignments of parent-child links severely degrade image quality.
Furthermore, by evaluating cumulative prefixes of the hierarchy, we
show that each appended level contributes usable detail. This smooth
progression from valid coarse approximations to high-quality final
renders confirms our representation as a effective cumulative hierarchy. Ultimately, we demonstrate that scale-bounded Gaussian LoD
levels can form a coherent, traversable hierarchy, enabling local
screen-space replacement decisions unavailable to vanilla 3DGS
and independent-level LoD methods. Our contributions are:
• An explicit, traversable hierarchy for 3DGS that enables local
screen-space substitution. By collapsing fine primitives to their
coarse ancestors based on projected size, intermediate levels provide coherent quality-budget tradeoffs that are not achieved by
post-training assignments.
• A fine-to-coarse traversal mechanism integrated directly into the
training process. By balancing photometric supervision between
hierarchy levels, it generates spatially mixed active sets based on
local geometric complexity rather than global camera distance.
• A training schedule using hierarchy-aware density control in
combination with scale parent-child distance regluarisation to
maintain valid hierarchy under adaptive densification and pruning.

2. Related Work
Structured Gaussian Representations. 3DGS [KKL∗ 23] stores
anisotropic Gaussians rendered via front-to-back alpha compositing. While its adaptive density control places detail where photometric loss demands it, the final primitive set remains structurally
flat and unordered with respect to later LoD selection. ScaffoldGS [LYX∗ 24] replaces independent primitives with anchors that
decode small sets of view-adaptive neural Gaussians; however, LoD
remains implicit in learned anchor activation rather than forming
a persistent, traversable hierarchy. A parallel direction improves
the training-time geometry of primitive sets. Lyu et al. [LTC∗ 26]
introduce structure-aware anisotropic splitting driven by a per-axis
frequency-violation metric. While this resolves under-sampled regions earlier, the resulting structure remains flat and lacks the topological relationships required for dynamic LoD collapse.

stratified seed-point anchors. While both ACD and HLoD’s Mahalanobis family regularizer prevent descendants from drifting outside

ancestor’s ellipsoid. This explicit regularization preserves the parentchild spatial proximity strictly required to make coherent, dynamic
tree-traversal decisions at runtime.
Discrete Gaussian LoD. FLoD [SCSU25] is the most direct predecessor to our work. It trains a sequence of Gaussian levels with
specific scale bounds and combines them post-hoc based on camera distance. However, these levels function as discrete entities
lacking explicit parent-child relationships between corresponding
primitives. Octree-GS [RJL∗ 25] arranges anchors into an octree
where finer levels cumulatively refine a coarse base. However, this
hierarchy solely governs anchor activations without establishing a
parent-child tree of Gaussians. LapisGS [SMGO25] and Scalable
Gaussian Soup [SQZ25] propose layered or multi-quality representations tailored for specific deployment constraints. However, none
of these approaches equip a scale-bounded sequence with explicit
parent-child links or support dynamic, local screen-space traversal.
Large-scene LoD. Hierarchical 3DGS [KMK∗ 24] chunks large
captures into a projected-size hierarchy without end-to-end
photometric optimization. Similarly, methods like CityGaussian [LLF∗ 24], VastGaussian [LLT∗ 24], LODGE [KRM∗ 25], Virtualized 3D Gaussians [YXJ∗ 25], and LoD-of-Gaussians [WKR∗ 26]
focus on spatial partitioning, out-of-core CPU-to-GPU streaming,
and memory-bounded rendering for web- and city-scale scenes.
These out-of-core and partitioning strategies are orthogonal yet
complementary to HLoD, which focuses on dynamic screen-space
traversal via photometrically exchangeable parent-child hierarchies.
To address traversal bottlenecks in city-scale Level-of-Gaussians
(LoG) hierarchies, FilterGS [WYT∗ 26] replaces explicit fine-tocoarse tree-walking with parallel GPU filters that select active Gaussians and eliminate redundant Gaussian tile coverage. FilterGS is
complementary to HLoD, operating strictly downstream on the
rendering side of a pre-existing hierarchy without providing photometric guarantees for parent-child coherence. HLoD addresses this
upstream problem by jointly training these relationships, ensuring
a collapsed sibling group is photometrically exchangeable with its
parent. Furthermore, our GPU-fused traversal kernel achieves minimal selection overhead, remaining competitive with parallel filtering
in our targeted single-scene setting.
Non-Hierarchical LoD and Compression. As the primary continuous LoD alternative to HLoD in the single-scene setting, CLoDGS [CSL∗ 26] creates a continuous LoD spectrum by augmenting
each Gaussian with a learnable, distance-dependent opacity decay.
However, this mechanism is purely global: because decay depends
solely on camera distance, far-foreground objects and true background surfaces receive identical reduction. In contrast, HLoD triggers collapses based on the local screen-space footprint of sibling

Frequency-Aware Gaussian Splatting Decomposition [LSA26]
introduces a perceptually driven LoD structure. Instead of grouping
Gaussians by geometric scale, it assigns them to levels of a Laplacian

are orthogonal: a flat, low-frequency background wall at close range
occupies coarse levels in Frequency-Aware GSD but can reach
deeper hierarchy levels in HLoD, whereas a distant, textured surface
behaves oppositely.
Beyond continuous and perceptual criteria, several recent methods address scalability through global ordering or post-hoc compression rather than local hierarchies. Matryoshka Gaussian Splatting [GZA∗ 26] learns a globally ordered list of splats to reconstruct
scenes across different budgets, lacking explicit parent-child topology. Similarly, GoDe [DSRG∗ 25] reorganizes a trained model into
progressive layers for scalable compression, and Iterative Gaussian Synopsis [LZD∗ 26] compresses full-resolution models through
learnable pruning. Like discrete methods, these approaches do not
explicitly optimize for dynamic, localized screen-space traversal.
Scale-aware Filtering. Mip-Splatting [YCH∗ 24] demonstrates
that standard 3DGS aliases or erodes when rendered at sampling
rates that differ from training, mitigating this issue with 3D and
2D smoothing filters. Multi-Scale 3DGS [YLCL24] and LoDGS [YGC25] address these artifacts through scale-specific Gaussian
sets and adaptive filtering. HLoD leverages this insight, drawing its
mip floor directly from the Nyquist-scale criterion of Mip-Splatting
to ensure anti-aliased rendering during hierarchical traversal.
FilterGS targets large-scale traversal acceleration but takes an
existing hierarchy as given. CLoD-GS eliminates discrete levels
entirely but provides no per-group selection guarantee. FrequencyAware GSD reorganises LoD by perceptual frequency rather than
geometric scale. ACD strengthens densification geometry but does
not address hierarchy maintenance.

4. Hierarchical Level-of-Detail Traversal for 3DGS
HLoD introduces an end-to-end trained parent-child hierarchy for
3D Gaussian Splatting, enabling view-dependent LoD within a single model. Our structure allows fine Gaussians to dynamically collapse into coarse parents via efficient edge collapse, seamlessly
adapting to the active camera while preserving the ability to allocate
point density based on local geometric detail. Section 4.1 defines
our data structure and the GPU traversal algorithm that efficiently
computes the appropriate LoD cut for the current view. Section 4.2
outlines the append-only training schedule and the structural safeguards required for topological validity, while Section 4.3 details
the regularizers that ensure perceptual stability across transitions.
4.1. Gaussian Hierarchy Traversal
Our scene parameterization builds upon the 3DGS formulation [KKL∗ 23], where each primitive stores a spatial mean, scaling
vector, rotation quaternion, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent color. Rather than maintaining an unstructured point cloud, we organize the scene as a forest of Gaussians.
Each tree represents a localized, coarse-to-fine hierarchy. At the
coarsest level, each root Gaussian acts as an independent particle.
We use Gl to denote the set of Gaussians across all trees at level l.
To establish this tree structure, we augment each Gaussian g
with two additional properties: a birth level ℓb (g) ∈ {1, . . . , Lmax }
indicating the depth at which the primitive is introduced (where Lmax
is a predefined maximum tree depth), and a parent index π(g) linking
the Gaussian to its coarser ancestor. The entire hierarchy is jointly
trained as a cumulative representation of the scene. Consequently,
rendering at a specific detail level l evaluates the subset G≤l , which
comprises all Gaussians from the root down to level l. By rasterizing

Screen-Space Tree Traversal. To extract a spatially varying representation during rendering, we dynamically traverse the forest to
adapt the local resolution based on the projected screen footprint of
each primitive. Given a view v and a pixel threshold T , the traversal selects a view-dependent active set Av . To determine when a
collapse should occur, we directly compare the threshold T against
the screen-space footprint of each Gaussian, utilizing the rasterizer
radius σpx (g; v) given in Equation (1).
To determine whether a parent or its children are part of Av , we
use a synchronous group collapse rule: a sibling group S with parent
p is collapsed if every g ∈ S satisfies σpx (g; v) < T . When this
condition is met, the sibling group S are added to Av and otherwise
parent p is added. Defining the collapse synchronously maintains a
structural invariant, ensuring a parent is never rendered alongside
a subset of its children. Figure 3 illustrates the mechanics of both
synchronous and partial collapse on a simple scene. If a partial
collapse were permitted, a coarse parent would spatially overlap with
its remaining active children. Such cross-level overlap can introduce
blending artifacts and complicate the joint optimization, hindering
the model’s ability to learn smooth LoD transitions. Relying on
synchronous collapses prevents this overlap while still preserving
spatial adaptivity across the view.
While traversal works in either direction, we default to a fineto-coarse traversal for parallel GPU efficiency. Starting with the
finest-level Gaussians in Av , we evaluate active primitives in parallel at each level. Proceeding bottom-up from level Lmax to level
2, we assign a parallel thread to each active child at the current
depth. These threads concurrently compute their screen-space radius
σpx (g; v) and atomically flag their shared parent p if any sibling in
the group S exceeds the pixel threshold T . A subsequent voting step
applies the group collapse rule to update Av . Finally, the index list is
compacted and passed to the 3DGS rasterizer. Because early culling
filters non-contributing families, the overhead scales with active set
size rather than total scene complexity. We refer the reader to the
supplemental for further implementation details.

We initialize the forest by instantiating the root level from a
Structure-from-Motion point cloud, following 3DGS [KKL∗ 23].
Rather than optimizing discrete levels independently, we jointly
train the hierarchy by integrating the screen-space tree traversal directly into the forward pass. For each training view v, we determine
the active set Av by collapsing the cumulative model G≤l using
a fixed pixel threshold T . This allows the optimization to directly
minimize the photometric error of the locally adapted representation.
Periodically, following FLoD [SCSU25], we increase the maximum forest depth by appending a new level l. For each active parent,
we spawn two child Gaussians that inherit their parent’s parameters
but undergo a scale reduction to capture higher spatial frequencies. The cumulative model is then updated to G≤l = G≤l−1 ∪ Gl ,
expanding the parameter pool for the subsequent training phase.
Finally, we assign the appropriate birth level and parent index to
each new primitive to maintain the explicit topological links.
Relying solely on screen-space traversal during training introduces a structural supervision imbalance. Because the collapse criterion depends on the projected footprint, coarse Gaussians are rarely
optimized unless the training cameras are sufficiently distant. Conversely, newly spawned fine Gaussians may lack sufficient gradients
if they immediately collapse under the pixel threshold T . To decouple photometric supervision from the camera distribution, we
introduce a randomized active-set selection during the forward pass.
At each training step, we randomly modify the active set Av
via one of three strategies. With probability pcur , we bypass the
traversal and restrict Av entirely to primitives born at the currently
optimizing level l, ensuring new Gaussians receive direct supervision regardless of their projected size. Alternatively, with probability
pcap , we artificially truncate the hierarchy by uniformly sampling a
maximum depth M ≤ l and executing the traversal on the restricted
subset G≤M . This forces coarse Gaussians to act as active parents,
exposing them to gradients even from close views. For the remaining
probability, we perform the standard unconstrained traversal. All
three sampling strategies utilize the same photometric loss, enabling
the cumulative model to maintain consistent quality across all LoDs.
Applying 3DGS [KKL∗ 23] ADC and the overlap pruning introduced by FLoD [SCSU25] directly to the Gaussian hierarchy can
inadvertently create orphan nodes. To maintain a consistent tree
hierarchy, we apply pruning exclusively to the newest level of the
model and adjust the removal criteria. We restrict the FLoD overlap
evaluation to consider only same-level neighbors, preventing parentchild pairs from being incorrectly discarded as redundant geometry.
During opacity and overlap pruning, if an operation would eliminate
all children of a given parent, we preserve the single child with
the highest opacity. This safeguard prevents orphaned parents and
ensures overlap evaluation operates strictly across adjacent levels
rather than skipping levels. Before appending a new level, a cleanup
pass removes any low-opacity primitives that survived solely due to
this intervention, yielding a clean structure prior to the next periodic
increase of the forest depth.

localized, coarse-to-fine refinement of the scene. If a sibling group

structural artifacts. To preserve spatial cohesion across the hierarchy, we introduce two geometric regularizers alongside the primary
photometric loss.

5. Results
We first evaluate the quality-performance trade-off of HLoD to validate it as a Level of Detail (LoD) system, demonstrating how its
screen-space aware edge collapses dynamically balance rendering
speed and visual fidelity. Next, we benchmark peak image quality and performance against state-of-the-art baselines. Finally, we
present ablation studies to justify our core architectural choices,
including the structural necessity of joint optimization compared to
naive post-hoc hierarchical assignments.
Implementation. Our implementation is built upon the FLoD codebase [SCSU25], inheriting the original 3DGS CUDA rasterizer without modification to ensure a fair baseline comparison. The geometric
regularizers are implemented in PyTorch whereas the screen-space
tree traversal for dynamic edge collapses is integrated via a custom
CUDA extension. The memory footprint of our hierarchy only requires storing two additional 64-bit integers per Gaussian to keep
track of its level and parent index.
Unless specified otherwise, HLoD inherits default hyperparameter
configurations from FLoD and adopts the standard 3DGS densification schedule within each level. For the hierarchy structure, we use
an initial scale bound of τ0 =0.05, a scale ratio of ρ=2.0, a collapse
threshold of T =9, and a branching factor of two children per level.
Regularization weights are set to λsu =0.01 for spatial regularization
and λmaha =0.005 for Mahalanobis regularization. During traversal, the current-level and capped-traversal sampling probabilities are
pcur =0.03 and pcap =0.05, respectively. Finally, we disable the EWA
screen-space filter to avoid artificially inflating the 2D footprints
with a low-pass filter. This is valid because we utilize the mip-floor
to constrain the size. We refer the reader to the supplemental for a
list of all hyperparameters and training schedules.
Experimental Setup. Our evaluation adheres to the standard
3DGS evaluation protocol for dataset selection, resolution scaling, and train-test splits across all nine scenes from MipNeRF 360 [BMV∗ 22], the Truck and Train scenes from Tanks and
Temples [KPZK17], and the Dr. Johnson and Playroom scenes from
Deep Blending [HPP∗ 18]. Additional experiments are conducted
on the block_all from Matrix-City [LJX∗ 23], as well as the building
and rubble scenes from the Mill-19 [TRS22], where we utilize the
provided train and test splits.

5.2. Quantitative Evaluation
To evaluate peak reconstruction fidelity, we compare the highestquality settings of HLoD (T =3) against the finest discrete levels
of prior state-of-the-art approaches (Table 1). Although HLoD is
optimized at T =9, rendering at T =3 maximizes visual fidelity (see
Table 7) while actively maintaining the dynamic screen-space collapse mechanism. This contrasts with Hierarchical-GS, which must
effectively disable its traversal (T =0) to reach its maximum reported
quality. Across the evaluated datasets, HLoD achieves highly competitive visual metrics for PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. Specifically,
it renders faster than all baseline methods on the Mip-NeRF 360
and Deep Blending datasets. On the Tanks and Temples dataset, it
remains highly competitive in framerate while achieving the best
PSNR among the faster-rendering methods. These results also show
that HLoD delivers a strong performance-quality trade-off even
at high fidelity levels. Regarding the baselines, our reproductions
yielded slightly higher metrics for FLoD and Octree-GS than originally published. Conversely, Frequency-GS [LSA26] exhibited
a drop in peak PSNR when evaluated under the standard 3DGS
lower-resolution training protocol, as opposed to the full-resolution
optimization used in its original publication. This indicates a distinct sensitivity to training resolution. As shown in our qualitative
comparison (Figure 9), this sensitivity manifests as noticeable blurriness along edges and within highly detailed regions. Nevertheless,
Frequency-GS retains strong LPIPS scores, which corroborates the
perceptual benefits of its frequency-supervised training. HLoD, on
the other hand, has a quantitative gap in LPIPS compared to methods
like Frequency-GS. We hypothesize that this perceptual difference
stems from the inherent mechanics of our hierarchical collapse. As
fine Gaussians merge into coarser parents, the resulting edges between adjacent coarse splats might introduce incorrect frequencies
into the image, to which LPIPS appears to be sensitive to. Beyond
these evaluated baselines, our comparative analysis is naturally restricted to public implementations, leaving a direct comparison with
the closed-source CLoD-GS [CSL∗ 26] for future work.

Performance Scaling. Fine-to-coarse traversal remains faster up
to 5 × 107 Gaussians in highly detailed scenes (see Figure 13),
while coarse-to-fine traversal becomes more efficient beyond 7.5 ×

5.4. Limitations
Despite its dynamic scaling capabilities, HLoD presents certain limitations. Maintaining the stability of the learned hierarchy requires
explicit geometric guardrails. Without Mahalanobis and scale regularization, the model suffers from spatial drift and improper expansion into low-frequency backgrounds, which disrupts the intended
frequency separation between levels. Even with these regularizers
active, their soft nature leaves room for minor structural variations.
Consequently, while HLoD generally maintains smooth transitions,
it can still introduce sparse but visible pop-in artifacts during camera
motion. We observe these artifacts exclusively at greater distances
from the camera, leaving foreground objects unaffected. We hypothesize that this behavior stems from inhomogeneous Gaussian
We introduced Hierarchical Level of Detail (HLoD) for 3D Gaussian
Splatting, addressing the lack of inherent hierarchy and overcoming
the limitations of discrete LoD representations. By jointly optimizing Gaussian levels into an explicit parent-child hierarchy, HLoD
provides continuous performance-quality trade-offs that successfully
composite without the systematic seams, coverage holes, and blurred
transition bands typical of distance-based models. Our dynamic
screen-space traversal evaluates local sibling groups rather than the
entire camera view. Consequently, low-frequency regions smoothly
collapse to coarse ancestors while high-frequency features remain
preserved at finer depths. Our evaluations demonstrate that HLoD
achieves highly competitive visual fidelity across PSNR, SSIM, and
LPIPS metrics, while rendering faster than state-of-the-art baselines
on datasets such as Mip-NeRF 360 and Deep Blending. This efficiency is driven by an extremely lightweight traversal overhead of
less than 0.75 ms, which allows the method to convert reductions
in active primitives directly into end-to-end framerate gains. Ultimately, HLoD establishes a highly robust and stable framework that
ensures the computational budget and visual fidelity remain resilient
against aggressive pruning.
