Holography enables modulation of light waves to create three-dimensional (3D) displays with natural depth cues. Previous research on holographic displays primarily focused on synthesizing real images in free space while neglecting the physical display optics. Here, we propose an end-to-end, system-level design framework that accounts for the complete path from the illumination source to the retina by integrating optimized eyepiece optics. Joint optimization of the hologram, eyepiece optics, and component positions across polychromatic wavelengths and multiple depth channels corrects chromatic dispersion, optical aberrations, and geometric distortions while incorporating eyebox deployment, depth representation, and hologram bandwidth, enabling apochromatic and high-performance holographic displays. Two prototypes, a full-color, large depth-of-field (DOF), large field-of-view (FOV) holographic near-eye display and a full-color, large FOV, multi-focus 3D holographic freeform augmented-reality (AR) near-eye display, validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, providing valuable insights for advancing ergonomic holographic AR/virtual reality (VR) systems with realistic visual experiences.

Introduction
Holography enables the precise encoding and reconstruction of light waves based on the principles of interference and diffraction, producing a three-dimensional (3D) display with natural depth cues that are difficult to achieve in traditional displays1. Augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) displays present virtual information to the human eye, advancing information acquisition and interaction for human society and prompting developments across various fields such as education, training, entertainment, navigation2–4. Conventional AR/VR displays typically support only a two-dimensional (2D) image at a single depth plane, resulting in a vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC) that causes visual discomfort and fatigue5. It is essential to construct a complete holographic display system integrating eyepiece optics while accounting for the characteristics of the retinal imaging process, as exemplified by holographic near-eye displays (NEDs) and head-up displays (HUDs), rather than concentrating exclusively on the synthesis of volumetric images in free space, which can substantially broaden the applicability of holography to AR and VR6–9. Integrating holographic displays with AR/VR systems can unlock the potential to present 3D virtual scenes delivered to the human eye, achieving perceptually realistic, immersive and comfortable viewing10–12.
In contrast to optical holograms recorded in holographic photosensitive medium, computer-generated holograms (CGHs), numerically computed based on wave diffraction theory, feature greater flexibility and versatility for representing 3D volumetric scenes, which have been widely used in industry and academia13–15. A CGH is typically loaded onto a spatial light modulator (SLM) device, which diffracts the incident coherent illumination wavefront and reconstructs an aerial 3D volumetric image at a certain distance in space as the wave propagates forward. In general, to fully integrate the holographic display with human vision, the eyepiece optics are indispensable. An intermediate volumetric image is placed near the focal plane of the eyepiece optics, and subsequently re-imaged and magnified16–18. When the human eye is positioned at the conjugate focal plane, a virtual holographic image can be observed, with its perceived depth determined by the lens formula.
Most previous works on holographic displays mainly focus on designing the hologram that accounts for the wave propagation between the hologram plane and the intermediate image in free space17,18, while neglecting the complete formation process of the virtual image presented to the human eye, which involves the residual aberrations and distortions introduced by the physical display optics, and the system structure (e.g., off-axis configuration). Additional off-the-shelf eyepiece optics that are not optimized or tailored for a specific holographic display system, when directly used to deliver display beams to the human eye, would impose critical challenges that degrade display performance due to aberrations and distortions, compromise essential specifications such as FOV and viewing eyebox, and further limit the practical applicability of holographic displays. Furthermore, the depth mapping between the intermediate volumetric image and the perceived virtual image is not tailored from the perspective of the human eye, resulting in a depth perception that is inconsistent with physical, real-world scenes. General wave propagation calculations are more applicable to the free space between the parallel planes, such as the angular spectrum method (ASM)19. However, in the cases of off-axis propagation or involving the physical display optics, the ASM becomes highly complex20.
Some end-to-end camera calibration approaches (ranging from the illumination source to the camera sensor) have been proposed to correct optical deficiencies in the physical display optics and system structure. A practical camera, substituting for the human eye, captures the holographic image points and repeatedly adjusts the phase functions (coefficients) of the corresponding holograms until tightly focused image points are obtained21,22. In addition, some studies directly compute a numerical loss function between the captured image and the target image, such as mean-squared error (MSE), and construct a differentiable model to update the phase pattern of the hologram via gradient backpropagation, aiming to minimize the loss function23,24. While camera-calibration methods can generally achieve high image quality through iterative image acquisition and optimization loops, they fundamentally require a rigidly fixed hardware configuration, in which the spatial coordinates of all optical components must be pre-set and held constant throughout the entire calibration process.
In this work, we propose an end-to-end design framework, incorporating the entire optical path from the illumination source to the retina, to develop complete holographic display systems. A joint optimization is conducted for the hologram, the eyepiece optics, and the positional coordinates of system components, including the hologram plane, the eyepiece optics, and the viewing eyebox plane, to correct chromatic dispersion, optical aberrations, and distortions of the holographic image perceived on the retina during the end-to-end design. The polychromatic wavelengths and multiple depth channels are collaboratively designed to enable an apochromatic and multi-focus 3D display. In addition, the proposed design framework explicitly considers the spatial deployment of an effective viewing eyebox, the frequency bandwidth constraint of the hologram, and the trade-off between the exit-pupil size and the depth-of-field (DOF) range. To validate the effectiveness and advancement of the proposed design framework, two types of display prototypes are developed: a full-color, large DOF, large field-of-view (FOV) holographic near-eye display, and a full-color, large FOV, multi-focus 3D holographic freeform AR near-eye display, with results demonstrating depth perception as well as corrected optical aberrations and distortions.
ResultsDesign principles
This section introduces in detail the principle of the proposed end-to-end design framework, exemplified by a full-color, multi-focus holographic freeform off-axis AR near-eye display system, as shown in Fig. 1a, which comprises a hologram and a freeform half-mirror serving as the eyepiece optics. Without loss of generality, the eyepiece optics can alternatively be a holographic optical element (HOE), metasurface, or diffractive optical element (DOE)25–28. A coherent wavefront illuminates the hologram displayed on the SLM, and after diffraction, it is re-modulated by the freeform optics and directed to the viewing eyebox. When the human eye is positioned within the eyebox region, an aberration- and distortion-corrected, apochromatic virtual image with depth perception is accurately formed on the retina in accordance with the focusing ability of the crystalline lens. The joint design and optimization of the hologram and the eyepiece optics are conducted based on the proposed end-to-end design from the illumination source to the retina, enabling correction of optical deficiencies in the retinal image during the design stage. To more intuitively elaborate the design framework, the side view of this holographic display system is shown in Fig. 1b.

The formation of the intermediate volumetric image is based on the principle of Fresnel holography, in which a coherent illumination wavefront is modulated by a hologram and, after propagating forward over certain distances from the hologram plane, generates the desired image intensity in accordance with Fresnel diffraction18,22, rather than using a Fourier transforming lens as in Fourier holography29,30. Here, an intermediate volumetric image, which intentionally accounts for the overall chromatic dispersion, optical aberrations, distortions, and depth perception within the complete holographic near-eye display system (including the illumination source, physical display optics, and off-axis configuration), is formed to ensure aberration-corrected imaging with depth perception on the retina. To further shorten the focal distance of the intermediate image for a compact form factor, while satisfying the sampling theorem given the pixel pitch of the SLM device, a sub-hologram region smaller than the full width of the SLM is employed, as indicated by the orange-outlined area in Fig. 1b.
The design of computational holographic display systems involves polychromatic wavelength channels and multiple depth planes to realize a full-color, multi-focus display. Typical three-color laser wavelengths corresponding to the red, green, and blue channels are multiplexed to enable color display. In terms of multi-focus functionality, multiple depth planes are sampled in 3D space for the accurate and realistic representation of the volumetric scenes in computational holographic displays, typically involving hundreds of discrete depth levels ranging from the near point of distinct vision (4 diopters, 4 D) to optical infinity (0 D) with respect to the position of the human eye. The multi-focus scenes presented in the holographic display are defined by RGB-D images, which are rendered and computed from the perspective of the human eye and aligned with the real physical world, thus enabling depth perception consistent with that of real-world scenes. The final hologram is computed by summing the sub-holograms of the image points defined by the RGB-D image, as described in later sections.
The performance of the holographic image on the retina, including distortions, (chromatic) aberrations, and depth perception, is determined by the cumulative effects of all system components. Inspired by this, end-to-end ray tracing is proposed in this work, which traces rays from the light source emitting the illumination wave to the retina perceiving the holographic image, incorporating the hologram, physical display optics, and human eye lens, as indicated by the blue arrows in Fig. 1b. To facilitate illustration, only three virtual image points located at different depth planes are depicted here. Ray tracing can effectively characterize the wavefront modulation of the hologram and the display optics, where the diffracted, reflected, or refracted rays are computed using the analytical phase function ϕ(x, y) and surface sag function z(x, y), enabling more efficient system design, analysis, and optimization31. In contrast, general wave diffraction calculations, such as the ASM, may become increasingly complex, especially for the off-axis system configuration.
To obtain an aberration- and distortion-corrected holographic image on the retina, joint optimization is carried out for the phase function ϕ(x, y) of the sub-holograms, the surface sag function z(x, y) of the eyepiece optics, and the positional coordinates of the system components, enabling optimal alignment among the hologram plane, intermediate volumetric image, eyepiece optics, and viewing eyebox plane. The holographic display system across polychromatic wavelength channels and multiple depth planes is collaboratively designed to enable an optimal solution, realizing an apochromatic display with depth perception. The optimization merit function is defined as the transverse ray aberration (TRA) evaluated at the retinal image points, along with penalty terms related to system performance. In addition, several critical system constraints must be incorporated into the joint optimization to ensure the physical feasibility of the holographic display, including the frequency bandwidth constraint of the hologram, the spatial deployment of an effective viewing eyebox, the trade-off between the exit-pupil size and the DOF range, the illumination source configuration, and the system form factor. In this way, a physically feasible holographic display system with corrected aberrations and distortions, as well as depth perception, can be effectively obtained, as shown in Fig. 1b.
The system initialization is constructed based on optical mapping and paraxial optics, providing a physically reasonable starting point for the subsequent joint optimization and enabling stable convergence while avoiding blind exploration of the solution space and tedious trial-and-error. Meanwhile, a progressive optimization strategy is adopted to gradually approach the prescribed specifications and progressively tighten the system constraints, reducing the risk of convergence to local minima and improving the stability of the optimization. Detailed descriptions and formulations of the optimization merit function and system constraints are provided in Supplementary Note 1. In addition, Supplementary Note 2 provides a complete design and optimization example of an off-axis freeform holographic AR near-eye display system, including system initialization, joint optimization strategy, convergence analysis, and optimization flow diagram. This example provides a generalized reference for developing holographic display systems tailored to various design and application requirements.
Holography computation
In Fresnel holography, the hologram H(x, y) is obtained by multiplying the conjugate phase factor of the illumination wave by the superposition of light waves converging at each image point18,22.
1H(x,y)=e−iϕillu(x,y)∑j∈SIAjeiϕj(x,y)
where SI denotes the set of image points whose phase functions are defined at (x, y) across the hologram plane. Aj is the amplitude of the image point Ij. ϕillu(x, y) is the phase function of the illumination wave, which is usually a spherical or planar wave. The phase function ϕj(x, y) of an image point Ij is represented as follows.
2ϕj(x,y)=−2πλ(x−xj)2+(y−yj)2+zj2+ϕ0
where (xj, yj, zj) are the positional coordinates of the image point Ij relative to the hologram plane, and ϕ0 is the initial phase. Equations (1) and (2) describe the point-based method (PBM) commonly used in conventional holographic display design18, which considers only the generation of image points in free space, intrinsically neglecting the complex wavefront modulations introduced by subsequent optics in a practical AR/VR optical system, such as the eyepiece optics and the human eye lens.
In general, to satisfy the sampling theorem during the hologram calculation, the focal distance zj should be greater than Lp/λ, where L is the hologram width. To shorten the focal distance for a compact form factor while avoiding aliasing, a sub-hologram with a width smaller than that of the SLM plane is employed in this work, as indicated by the orange-bordered region in Fig. 1b, instead of utilizing the full width of the SLM. In other words, at a fixed focal distance zj, the hologram region must be confined to a local area.
The local coordinates on the sub-hologram plane are defined as (x’, y’), with the sub-hologram center (xc,j, yc,j) specified relative to that of the hologram plane. The phase function of the image point Ij, represented in the coordinates (x, y), is expressed by Eq. (3) through a coordinate transformation.
3ϕj(x′,y′)=ϕjx−xc,j,y−yc,j,∣x−xc,j∣≤Lsub,x2,y−yc,j≤Lsub,y2
where Lsub is the width of the sub-hologram region.
Characterization of sub-hologram phase
In this work, an end-to-end ray tracing that incorporates both the illumination and diffraction waves of the hologram is implemented, which requires that the phase function design of the sub-hologram accounts not only for the intermediate image point, but also for the illumination wave. The hologram calculation in Eq. (1) can be further revised as follows, and represented in the sub-hologram coordinates (x’, y’).
4H(x,y)=∑j∈SIAjei[ϕj(x′,y′)−ϕillu(x′+xc,j,y′+yc,j)],x′≤Lsub,x2,y′≤Lsub,y2
The exponential phase function is unified as the phase ϕsub,j(x’, y’) of the sub-hologram for the image point Ij.
5H(x,y)=∑j∈SIAjeiϕsub,j(x′,y′)
The phase functions ϕj and ϕillu, corresponding to the image point and the illumination wave, respectively, are analytical and differentiable. In this case, the phase function ϕsub,j of the sub-hologram can be characterized by the XY polynomials.
6ϕsub(x′,y′)=2πλc∑u=0Kϕ∑v=0Kϕ−uBu,vx′uy′v
where λc is the construction wavelength, and Bu,v is the coefficient of the phase term. Kϕ denotes the maximum total order of the hologram phase polynomial. The reference coordinate system lies at the origin of the sub-hologram plane, and (x’, y’) are the local coordinates, as shown in Fig. 1b. The tilt x and y phase terms that shift the image point are intentionally incorporated into the phase function ϕsub(x’, y’), enabling not only aberration correction but also the correction of image distortions introduced by the physical display optics (e.g., short-focus eyepiece or freeform optical combiner) and the off-axis system configuration during the design stage.
In contrast, previous works employed the Zernike polynomials to characterize the phase function ϕsub(x’, y’) of the sub-holograms, which are defined over a circular aperture to ensure orthogonality, but the tilt terms ρsinθ and ρcosθ in polar coordinates were excluded due to their irrelevance to aberrations21,22. The display system design corrected only optical aberrations and did not involve distortion correction. To compensate for the distorted image captured in the holographic display, the input image must be pre-distorted, similar to image processing, based on distortion-mapping data obtained through a post-calibration process32. Moreover, considering that available SLMs typically have a rectangular aperture with pixels arranged and addressed on a rectangular basis, additional coordinate transformation and cropping operations were required when employing the Zernike functions during the hologram calculation33.
The phase function design for a sub-hologram must consider the spatial bandwidth product supported by the SLM device. According to the Shannon sampling theorem, the maximum spatial frequency of the phase function should be less than half the reciprocal of the SLM pixel pitch p; otherwise, severe aliasing occurs due to undersampling. Although the spatial frequency distribution can be analytically computed using Eq. (7), directly constraining its maximum value across the sub-hologram plane during the numerical optimization remains challenging. Alternatively, this constraint can be imposed by applying a low-pass filter (LPF) in the Fourier frequency domain21; however, such an operation still poses difficulties for the optimization process.
The spatial frequency of the phase function ϕsub(x’, y’) along the x’- and y’-axes is represented as follows.

To solve this issue, we instead constrain the deflection angle of the diffracted ray relative to the incident ray over the sub-hologram plane during the end-to-end ray tracing, thereby indirectly limiting the maximum spatial frequency. In theory, the spatial frequency distribution of the phase function directly dictates the directional relationship between the diffracted and incident illumination rays. When an incident ray intersects the sub-hologram plane at coordinates (x’, y’), the first-order diffracted ray is determined by Eq. The z-component direction cosine cosγD, relative to the local z’-axis, can be easily computed from the norm of the wave vector34.

Design pipeline of holographic displays
In this work, the design of holographic near-eye display systems incorporates polychromatic wavelength channels and multiple depth planes to achieve full-color and multi-focus displays, where the set of polychromatic wavelengths is denoted as SW, and the set of sampled depth planes in 3D space is denoted as SD. At each depth plane in the set SD, the number (or spatial resolution) of image points remains consistent, and the set of image points at any given depth plane is denoted as SI. Designing a computational holographic display system through end-to-end ray tracing from the illumination source to the retina and calculating the phase functions of sub-holograms for all image points across the entire depth set SD and wavelength set SW is computationally intractable and inefficient, as the required number of calculations amounts to hundreds of millions.
Considering that the aberrations and distortions between adjacent image points vary slowly and continuously, it is preferable to first design the sub-hologram phase function ϕsub(x’, y’) for hundreds of sampled image points in 3D space, and then apply interpolation to these sampled points to extend the phase coefficients to the entire display domain, thereby significantly improving the design efficiency. In terms of image quality, this interpolation can provide sufficient accuracy for the unsampled image points. To implement this strategy, several representative depth planes are sub-sampled from the depth set SD, forming a subset denoted as Ssub,D. At each depth plane within Ssub,D, a set of image points, denoted as Ssub,I, is sub-sampled from the image point set SI.
For a phase coefficient Bu,v,w of the w-th wavelength channel in the set SW, 2D cubic interpolation is applied to the sub-sampled image points in the set Ssub,I at each sampled depth plane in the set Ssub,D, extending them to the complete image set SI. This operation is performed across all sampled depths in Ssub,D. Subsequently, 1D piecewise cubic Hermite interpolating polynomial (PCHIP) interpolation is applied to the sub-sampled depth planes in the set Ssub,D to extend them to the full depth set SD. The above interpolation process is repeated for all wavelength channels in the set SW.
10Bu,v,wSsub,I;Ssub,D→2DInterpSsub,IBu,v,wSI;Ssub,D→1DInterpSsub,DBu,v,wSI;SD,1≤u+v≤Kϕ,1≤w≤3
where Kϕ denotes the order of the phase function adopted for the sub-holograms, and w represents the index of the wavelength channel. Detailed descriptions of the interpolation are provided in Supplementary Note 3, including the interpolation accuracy and error, ablation analyses of the sampling strategies in the image and depth domains, as well as comparisons of the interpolation methods.
The proposed overall design pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 2b. End-to-end ray tracing enables the complete design of the holographic display system through joint optimization, considering optical aberration and distortion correction, the focusing ability of the human eye lens, and the bandwidth constraints of the hologram. Through the interpolation operation described by Eq. (10), the phase coefficients Bu,v,w of the image point set SI, corresponding to the depth set SD and the wavelength set SW, can be obtained. The 3D volumetric scene presented in the computational holographic display is defined by an RGB-D image, which is rendered and computed from the perspective of the human eye using graphics rendering tools. The rendering camera, serving as the human eye, is positioned at the designated eyebox plane, with its orientation aligned to the chief ray of the central virtual image point. The final hologram H(x, y) is then computed by summing the sub-holograms associated with the phase function coefficients and the image points defined by the RGB-D image that represents the volumetric scene. This computation is significantly accelerated by CUDA-based parallelization implemented with PyTorch tensors on a graphics processing unit (GPU), with hologram synthesis taking approximately 41 s on an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPU. Further acceleration can be achieved by using a deep neural network to learn end-to-end wave propagation, which is worth further exploration in future work. The calculated hologram H(x, y) is converted to phase-only holograms using double phase amplitude coding (DPAC) to accommodate the phase-only SLM device35. Three color-channel phase-only holograms are then loaded onto the SLM for full-color display based on the field-sequential color (FSC) mode36.
Display prototypes and results
To validate the effectiveness and advancement of the proposed design framework, two types of computational holographic display systems are developed: the first system realizes a full-color, large FOV near-eye display by utilizing a single short-focus plano-convex lens as the eyepiece, and the second one achieves a full-color, large FOV, multi-focus freeform off-axis AR near-eye display by incorporating an off-axis freeform half-mirror as the eyepiece optics. For the display system design, a perfect lens with a limiting aperture is placed at the eyebox plane to simulate the focusing capability of the crystalline lens, achieving ideal point-to-point imaging without introducing aberrations and distortions. The image plane of the perfect lens serves as the retina.
The polychromatic wavelengths are 639, 532, and 457 nm, corresponding to the red, green, and blue channels, respectively, constituting the wavelength set SW. The depth set SD, ranging from the near point of distinct vision (4.0 D) to optical infinity (0.0 D), comprises 256 depth levels represented by 8 bits. 7 × 7 sub-hologram points are sampled for each depth plane to perform the aforementioned end-to-end design and joint optimization for the polychromatic wavelength channels and multiple depth planes. The phase function ϕsub(x’, y’) of the sub-hologram is described by the XY polynomials up to the 3rd order; thus, Kϕ is set to 3 in Eq. (10). The higher-order phase terms can also be further utilized without affecting the design principles.
Parameter design for holographic near-eye displays
Several key parameters in holographic near-eye displays, including FOV, DOF, and exit-pupil size, must be properly allocated within the limited étendue of the SLM to ensure an acceptable viewing eyebox and depth perception functionality, which directly affect viewing immersion and realism29,37. Owing to the conservation of étendue, the exit-pupil size is traded off against the FOV, which is primarily limited by the spatial bandwidth product of the SLM device. The FOV is determined by the maximum deflection angle of the diffracted rays relative to the normal of the SLM plane. When a planar wave illuminates the SLM, the deflection angle is limited to arcsin(λ/2p). To enable a larger deflection, a diverging spherical wave emitted from a point light source can instead be employed. It is noted that the point source must be positioned at a distance greater than Lp/λ from the SLM plane to satisfy the sampling theorem, and its position is kept consistent across all polychromatic wavelength channels to facilitate experimental operations. Detailed derivations of this distance limitation for the spherical illumination wavefront are provided in Supplementary Note 1.
Furthermore, there is also a critical trade-off between the exit pupil size and the DOF for the near-eye displays. In the case of the exit-pupil size being much smaller than the eye pupil, the defocus blur of the virtual image point becomes less sensitive to changes in the focus of the eye lens or camera lens, resulting in a large DOF display similar to Maxwellian or retinal projection displays38–40. To present multi-focus displays, the exit-pupil size of the image points should be intentionally extended to further reduce the DOF range in support of this functionality; however, this will compromise the FOV accordingly.
The effective viewing eyebox refers to the spatial region within which the human eye can view the complete holographic image. In this work, to prevent vignetting of light rays, the spatial distribution of the exit pupils corresponding to all image points, that is the viewing eyebox, must be confined within a region limited by the typical eye pupil size (3 mm to 4 mm). When the eye pupil is positioned within this eyebox region, all virtual image points, after being focused by the crystalline lens, will be imaged onto the retina, presenting either sharply focused or defocused blur states depending on their specific depth distances and the lens’s accommodation capability. Detailed explanations and formulations of the above constraints are provided in Supplementary Note 1.
Full-color, large DOF and large FOV holographic near-eye display
In this design, a stock short-focus plano-convex lens with a focal length of 12.7 mm and an F# number of 1 is employed as the eyepiece optics, and a diverging spherical wave serves as the illumination wavefront for the SLM. The optical layout of this holographic near-eye display system is shown in Fig. 3a.
Fig. 3Design of full-color and large FOV holographic VR near-eye display.
a Optical layout of the holographic VR near-eye display system. b MTF curves of the central virtual image point located at the depth plane of 0 D, as the eye’s focusing distance changes from 4 D to − 4 D. c Simulation display results on the retina without correction.
The use of a short-focus eyepiece achieves a diagonal FOV of up to 65°; however, due to étendue conservation, it significantly reduces the exit-pupil size, with a maximum of only 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm. Note that the FOV specification can be further enlarged, but due to the limited FOV of the camera lens, this display is set to 65°. The distribution of the effective viewing eyebox, as shown in Fig. 3a, is centered and confined within the 4 mm eye pupil region. This reduction in exit-pupil size degrades depth perception capability, resulting in a display with a large DOF; consequently, only a single depth plane is incorporated into the system design. At the designed depth plane of 0 D, the modulation transfer function (MTF) curves for the central virtual image point on the retina reach high performance, as shown in Fig. 3b. With the focusing distance changing from 4 D to −4 D, the MTF curves remain largely unchanged, indicating a large DOF range. This display is less susceptible to refractive errors of the eye, such as myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, and presbyopia, facilitating accommodation for a wider range of users without the need for special correction lens. The MTF and DOF behaviors of other sampled image points are consistent with those of the central point.
In this design, a single short-focus lens introduces severe pincushion distortion, which is effectively corrected by pre-shifting the image points during the end-to-end design stage, without pre-processing of the input image. In addition, a single lens rather than an achromatic lens serves as the eyepiece optics, which cannot inherently correct chromatic aberration for full-color displays. To achieve apochromatic imaging on the retina, chromatic aberration must be corrected during the system design. A collaborative optimization method for the holographic display system across polychromatic wavelength channels is adopted to determine the optimal positional alignment and phase functions, achieving effective correction for the chromatic aberration. The spots and positions of the sampled image points on the intermediate image plane and the retina are shown in Fig. 3a, with the image points deliberately exaggerated for illustration here. It is evident that the intermediate image points are intentionally degraded by optical aberrations, chromatic dispersion, and distortions to ensure high-performance imaging on the retina.
As a comparative baseline, this holographic display system is also designed using PBM, in which the positional coordinates of each component are kept identical to those in the proposed design. The PBM-based hologram synthesis can only consider the wave propagation between the SLM plane and the paraxial intermediate image plane, resulting in focused intermediate image points that are then re-imaged by the eyepiece lens. Note that the intermediate image height is set equal to that of the proposed display to ensure a fair comparison. The simulation result of the polychromatic image points on the retina for the PBM-based design is shown in Fig. 3c.

The PBM-based design exhibits severe pincushion distortion and chromatic dispersion, as shown in Fig. 4b. In contrast, the captured full-color virtual image for the proposed design, which incorporates the entire propagation from the illumination source to the retina, is shown in Fig. 4c, where the distortion and chromatic aberration have been effectively corrected. Note that the virtual images of the three-color channels are captured separately and then directly synthesized without any image processing. In terms of optical aberration correction, the two displays present comparable image quality for image points within the small field angle near the paraxial region of the eyepiece lens. However, at large field angles, the proposed display is superior to the PBM-based design in reducing aberrations. Full-color, large FOV and multi-focus holographic AR near-eye display
In this design, an off-axis projection-type system structure is adopted to enable an AR see-through near-eye display, where a freeform half-mirror functions as the eyepiece optics, and a diverging spherical wave is used as the illumination wavefront, as shown in Fig. 5a. The eyebox plane is positioned highly off-axis to avoid light obscuration.

Similar to the phase function design of the sub-holograms, the XY polynomial overlaid onto a conic base surface is used to characterize the freeform surface.

where ξ is the conic constant, and c is the curvature. (x, y) are the local coordinates on the substrate surface, and Bu,v is the coefficient of the polynomial term. Kz denotes the maximum total order of the polynomial.
Compared to traditional optical surfaces, such as spherical or aspheric surfaces, freeform surfaces offer a higher degree of design freedom and aberration correction capability, effectively correcting off-axis, nonsymmetric optical aberrations, and ensuring a compact form factor41,42. To better integrate the display rays from the SLM with the see-through rays from the real-world scenes, a half-mirror coating is applied to the interior surface facing the eyebox plane, while the exterior surface is coated with an anti-reflection coating for the visible light.
In this design, the optimized freeform optics preferably extend the exit pupil size to achieve depth perception functionality, while ensuring a large FOV and effectively correcting aberrations and distortions. The diagonal FOV of this holographic display is designed to reach up to 25.6°. The minimum exit pupil size is greater than 1.1 mm × 1.1 mm, and the spatial distribution of the effective viewing eyebox is shown in Fig. 5c, which is centered and confined within the eye pupil region of 4 mm. For optimal viewing by the human eye, the chief ray of the central virtual image point is incident perpendicular to the eyebox plane, with its exit pupil positioned at the center of the eyebox.
For the central virtual image point located at the depth plane of 0 D, the MTF curves reach high performance, as illustrated by the red lines in Fig. 5b. As the focus distance of the human eye lens changes from 0 D to 4 D, the MTF values exhibit a corresponding degree of degradation, with the depth plane at 4 D presenting apparent defocus blur, thereby demonstrating depth perception capability. The MTF and DOF characteristics of other image points are consistent with those of the central one. In addition, since end-to-end ray tracing is implemented, the depth perception for the virtual image point is computed and rendered from the perspective of the human eye, taking into account the wave modulation of the eyepiece optics and the accommodation ability of the crystalline lens. The proposed design essentially differs from conventional multi-focus holographic near-eye displays that consider only the wave propagation of intermediate volumetric image points in free space, located at different distances that are mapped to various depth planes, while neglecting the effects of the eyepiece and crystalline lens, which results in depth perception inconsistent with the physical real-world scenes.
When a freeform half-mirror is used as the optical combiner, the optical performance (i.e., distortion and aberration) for the real-world see-through paths should also be corrected by optimizing the exterior surface profile SE, as shown in Fig. 5d. The relative positions between the freeform half-mirror and the eyebox plane remain consistent with those of the holographic display system. The distortion grid and MTF curves for the see-through rays are shown in Fig. 5e, f, respectively, and have been effectively corrected. The selected see-through field angle is decided by the clear aperture (CA) of the freeform half-mirror and the eye relief, while an off-axis angle is introduced due to the tilt of the half-mirror.
In addition, to evaluate the sensitivity of image quality to errors in the freeform surface profile and component alignment, a Monte Carlo tolerance analysis is conducted. The nominal holographic display system is randomly perturbed by the positional and orientational parameter deviations of the system components, as well as the sag deviation of the freeform surface, followed by forward end-to-end ray tracing to evaluate the retinal image quality. Detailed tolerance analysis is provided in Supplementary Note 4.

Discussion
In summary, the proposed framework enables end-to-end design and joint optimization of holographic display systems. It incorporates the complete propagation path from the illumination source to the retinal image plane, passing through the hologram plane, the eyepiece optics, and the human eye lens, across polychromatic wavelength channels and multiple depth planes. The illumination wave, hologram phase, eyepiece optics, and the spatial and orientational coordinates of the system components are jointly optimized to realize aberration- and distortion-corrected retinal imaging with depth perception capability, while ensuring physical feasibility in terms of hologram bandwidth, illumination source configuration, viewing eyebox deployment, gaze direction, system structure, obscuration, and form factor. The proposed framework intrinsically differs from the conventional holographic display designs that mainly account for wave propagation in free space and from camera-calibration-based methods that hold component positions constant throughout the calibration process.
Specifically, depth perception is formulated based on retinal image formation through the human eye lens model. This constitutes one of the key contributions of our work. The proposed approach is fundamentally different from conventional holographic display designs, which generally employ rendered varifocal images or focal stacks as optimization targets by forward propagating the wavefield to different distances in free space and mapping them to various depth planes. Such designs characterize depth perception mainly based on wave propagation among multiple planes, and usually capture the holographic image directly in free space, without explicitly accounting for the formation of the resulting retinal image through the eyepiece optics and the eye lens.
Two types of display prototypes, a full-color, large DOF, large FOV holographic near-eye display, and a full-color, large FOV, multi-focus 3D holographic freeform AR near-eye display, have been developed, and the experimental results verify the advancement and effectiveness of the design framework. The proposed method can be further generalized for designing holographic HUDs and waveguide display systems, providing valuable insights for advancing ergonomic holographic AR/VR systems that achieve perceptually realistic, immersive and comfortable viewing.
The proposed design framework is also applicable to holographic display designs in which no intermediate image is formed between the hologram plane and the eyepiece optics, and this is described in Supplementary Note 9. In addition, several aspects can be further improved. The tolerance analysis can be extended to more sophisticated error sources in holographic displays, such as random speckle noise, illumination nonuniformity, encoding method, and phase quantization or nonlinear phase response, which may be further modeled by incorporating random surface error, pupil intensity apodization, and polynomial-based phase tolerance terms, with compensation achieved by updating the hologram phase functions. Deep neural networks can also be incorporated to learn end-to-end wave propagation, thereby accelerating the computational process of hologram synthesis. These extensions would further enhance the robustness and generality of the design framework, advancing the development for computational holographic displays and warranting exploration in future work.
MethodsExperimental setup
The schematic diagrams of the experimental setups for the holographic display prototypes are shown in Figs. 4a,6a. The phase-only SLM utilized in this work is a reflective liquid-crystal-on-silicon (LCoS) device with a resolution of 1920×1080 pixels, a pixel pitch of 4.5 μm, and a bit depth of 8 bits. The illumination module employs three diode lasers for the red (639 nm), green (532 nm), and blue (457 nm) channels, with output powers of 200 mW, 200 mW, and 150 mW, respectively. The diode lasers provide high temporal and spatial coherence, with a coherence length exceeding 50 m and a beam quality factor M2 of less than 1.1. The laser power of each channel is individually attenuated and balanced using neutral density (ND) filters to achieve uniform brightness among the RGB channels. For beam shaping, the emitted laser beams are spatially filtered and expanded using a spatial-filter assembly comprising a microscope objective with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.65 and a 15 μm pinhole, which generates a spherical illumination wave that is directed onto the SLM panel.
Three-color laser beams are combined to realize a full-color display, and a polarizer generates the polarization state required by the SLM. The beam splitter splits the illumination beam and directs it toward the SLM panel. The 4f system comprises two achromatic lenses, and an aperture stop positioned at the Fourier plane (i.e., the Fourier filter) blocks higher-order diffractions. The short-focus plano-convex lens and the cylindrical lens are off-the-shelf products (GCL-010104A, GCL-110314) from Daheng Optics. The freeform half-mirror used in the holographic AR display prototype is fabricated by single-point diamond turning (SPDT) with K2RR_ZEON as the lens material. A half-mirror coating is deposited on the interior surface facing the human eye, whereas the exterior surface is coated with an anti-reflection coating with a reflectivity of less than 2%.
The camera used for capturing the virtual holographic images is an iPhone 12 (Apple Inc.), which provides an aperture-limited imaging condition analogous to the pupil-limited imaging behavior of the human eye, thereby offering a practical capture condition for AR near-eye display evaluation. Since the native iOS camera application does not provide manual focus control with a calibrated distance readout, a third-party camera application (ProCam) is employed to enable manual focus adjustment. For focus calibration, a physical test target is placed at several known distances from the camera lens. At each distance, the software focus slider is manually adjusted until the target appears sharply focused, and the corresponding focus slider value is recorded. This procedure established a calibration curve that maps the software focus-slider values to object distances in free space.
