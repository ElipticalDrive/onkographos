ABSTRACT
The Iterative Optimizing Quantization Technique (IOQT) is a novel method in
reconstructing three-dimensional images from a limited number of two-dimensional
projections. IOQT reduces the artifacts and image distortion due to a limited number of
projections and limited range of viewing angles. IOQT, which reduces the number of
projections required for reconstruction, can simplify the complexity of an experimental set-up
and support the development of techniques to nondestructively image microstructures of
materials without the problems of chemical changes or damage. In this paper, we will
demonstrate the capability of IOQT in reconstruction of an image from four projections. The
advantage of IOQT in using a limited number of arbitrary-angled projections and the
possibility of modification of IOQT are also mentioned.
Key Words: Tomography, limited number of views, iterative optimization technique.
1.INTRODUCTION
We have developed a novel method, called IOQT, to reconstruct three-dimensional
images from a limited number of two-dimensional (2D) projections. Several effective
algorithms have been developed for Computerized Tomography (CT) to reconstruct a threedimensional (3D) image of an object. Most of these algorithms have been based on a huge
number of projections within the entire range of angles necessary to solve the inverse problem
for image reconstruction.1-6 During the last four decades summation techniques, convolution
back-projection, and Fourier methods have been found to be effective techniques for 3D
reconstruction and their applications to imaging techniques in various sciences such as
biomedical and material sciences have increased remarkably. However, many scientists have
experienced the difficulties in obtaining a large number of angular projections for their image
reconstruction studies in diverse fields such as x-ray micro-tomography ( X M T ) for biological
cells, radio astronomy, and geology. The reality is that the practical limitations due to the
limited number of projections and limited range of viewing angles typically used to compute
the 3D density distribution from multiple 2D transmission images of a infinitesimal specimen
can cause artifacts and image distortion. This problem has been investigated for over three
decades with the development of iterative and optimizing techniques in algorithms.
Various methods have been devised to solve the inverse problem for reconstruction with a
limited number of views.7-14 One of the most well-developed techniques is the algebraic
reconstruction technique (ART) introduced by Gordon, Bender, and Herman7, which is an
iterative procedure for recovering objects from their projections. Since ART takes a ray at a
time and corrects the pixels dynamically as the errors are calculated, no averaging is
considered. A modification of this algorithm is the simultaneous iterative reconstruction
technique (SIRT) proposed by Giber@in 1972 which has the advantage of reduced noise levels
due to averaging but the procedure does not always converge. This divergence was
demonstrated by Lakshminarayanan and Lent9 who proposed a new algorithm called the
Generalized SIRT (GSIRT), which utilized ‘pseudo-projections’ of a fictitious object. In 1971,
Gordon and Hermanlo exploited a Monte Carlo algorithm to optimize the image
reconstruction, which quantized a digital picture into picture elements. They demonstrated
how to optimize a reconstructed image without disturbing the original projections by adding
or subtracting a unit (Le. quantized) element in each pixel and also showed the advantage of
averaging the signal values for each pixel to enhance the image,
In this paper, we propose to use IOQT to obtain an accurate image of an object from a
limited number of projections. We perform computer simulations on three sets of four-viewed
projections to demonstrate our algorithm and then explore a real case in which the
experimental data from a test pattern is used to show the high performance of our algorithm.
2. PHILOSOPHY OF IOOT
We developed a unique IOQT algorithm to solve the problem with a limited number of
views, for example four views, which contains the optimization of pseudo-projections as in
GSIRT and the quantization of projections as in the Monte Carlo method. The main intuitive
consideration in the development of IOQT is to treat the object as a group of elements whose
size is determined by the resolution of the projection data. The resolution of the projection data
is determined by the size of the pixels. By using one of the projections as a reference projection,
a fictitious object can be arbitrarily defined that is constrained by this reference projection.
IOQT modifies the known structure of the fictitious object by comparing and optimizing its
four projections to those of the unknown structure of the real object and continues to iterate
until the optimization is limited by the residual sum of background noise.
IOQT is composed of several sub-programs that: (1)acquire four projections from the real
data and the fictitious object, (2) generate an arbitrary distribution to define the fictitious object,
(3) optimize the four projections, (4)generate a new distribution for the fictitious object, and (5)
enhance the reconstructed image. The sub-program for the acquisition of the four projections
from the input real data is simply the function of acquiring the four projections from the data
of the transmitted intensity. The transmitted intensity represents the density distribution, that

Finally, the image reconstruction is enhanced before the next iteration or prior to the
acquisition of the final result. The main purpose of this sub-program is to accelerate the
convergence of the reconstruction. A variety of routines such as a filtering function, removal of
threshold values to reduce unwanted noise, or a smoothing function like convolution can be
applied. For the application of the threshold procedure, we first need to choose a threshold
value. Since the mass quanta were enhanced with the magnification factor f, in order to
amplify the signal intensity, each pixel of the image should have at least the value of f,.
Further we can presume that the pixels which have a lower value than that of f, are not the
ones which contain mass quanta. However, since image reconstruction is never perfect using a
limited number of views, a more representative estimate of the threshold value that we have
chosen to use in our simulations is 50% of f,. Another main procedure of the enhancement
sub-program for our simulations is to smooth the estimated image. Since we consider the
object as a group of discrete elements and the point spread function of the zone plate as a
gaussian profile, it is necessary to smooth the reconstruction by taking the local average value
of neighboring pixels. We typically use a convolution routine with a 2D gaussian function. For

To completely solve a linear equation in the n x n unknowns, we need n x n linear
equations, that is, we need n projection data in n columns, which is impossible in real
experiments. With m projections, m << n, there will be more than one picture with exactly the
same projections. However, the optimization procedure of IOQT can select points with high
probability to be added or removed by comparing the difference between real and pseudo
projections but not those with low probability. After enhancing the reconstruction after each
iteration, the probabilities of each element will be changed with some elements having an
increased probability. At this point, those elements will be selected to be added or removed
within the fictitious object plane. Briefly, the function of optimization in IOQT is to select the
most probable elements by changing their probabilities without disturbing the reference
projection. This optimization process continues for the remaining pseudo-projections until the
reconstruction quality degrades at which time the execution of IOQT should be terminated.

3. PERFORMANCE OF IOOT
We used IOQT for the reconstruction of three simulated objects and one experimental test
pattern. The results demonstrate the ability of IoQT to perform 3D reconstruction with limited
views. In the first simulation, we used a 13 x 13 binary image composed of point objects.
Figure-3 shows how an arbitrarily distributed fictitious object can quickly approach the real
object distribution during only three loops of the three combinations of the optimizing
procedure over a complete iteration. For convenience, the projection angles in this test case
used 0, 45, 90, and 135 degrees. As we mentioned, the projections of the real object are
considered as real experimental projections and that of the fictitious object as pseudoprojections.
The next two simulations provided a more rigorous test of the performance of IOQT by
investigating more realistic examples with consideration of arbitrary projections, gray scale
objects, and noise problems. For both test cases a 50 x 50 binary image of was generated as the
real object. In these examples the finite dimensions of a pixel are taken into account. We have
used the value of 100 for the magnification factor, f,, that of 50 for the threshold value. and
that of 1for the standard deviation of the smoothing gaussian function. Figure4 demonstrates
the good agreement of the reconstruction with the real object showing the internal structure
and the discrepancy of the reconstruction due to the choice of projection angles. In Figure-$b,
with 0,45, 90, and 135 degree projections, the reconstruction is good and shows the internal
structure because the object has a symmetric configuration which is projected into evenly
separated directions. In Figure-4c, with 0, 15/90! and 105 degree projections, we observe the
appearance of artifacts due to the selection of projection angles for an object which is composed
of a large number of pixels. The artifacts are removable by modifying the signal enhancement
sub-program. Figure-5 shows the improvement possible with an improved enhancement
procedure that allows for variations in the threshold value during the image reconstruction for
each iteration. In this case, a threshold value of 20% was initially applied for the image
reconstruction in the first iteration and then increased by 10%for each subsequent iteration due
to the improvement of image perfection. The only difference between the computer
simulations shown in Figure-4c and Figure-5 is the threshold value that was applied to reduce
the residual background. The operation of IoQT was terminated by using the criterion give by
Equation-13. These simulations not only demonstrate the high performance of IOQT in
determining the internal structure of an object, but also indicates how it might be used to select
optimum viewing angles. It is also clear that the optimum signal enhancement technique
depends on the object.
It is an important task to include the problem of noise in the computer simulation since,
in real experiments, random errors like photon fluctuations are produced by unpredictable and
unknown sources. Based on the reference projection which is contaminated by such random
error (i.e. noise), IOQT may generate an improper reconstruction that does not reproduce the
unknown real object. To test the ability of IOQT to overcome this problem, we include a
gaussian random noise distribution in each pixel of the simulated projections; the mean value is
the noise free value of each pixel in the simulated projections and the standard deviation is 10%
of that. The outcome is shown in Figure-6 which demonstrates the agreement between the
simulations with and without noise. This can be understood by noting that two pixels from
different projections, representing the position of a point on the 2D object, cannot have the
same relative (i.e. corresponding) amount of random noise. As a consequence after the
optimization procedure the noise simply becomes part of the residual background and hence
will not effect the quality of the final image reconstruction then would have been obtained in
the case where no random noise was applied. In accordance with this result, we can confirm
that IOQT is not sensitive to the noise in the real data. and that it can reconstruct the image of a
real object with the limited number of views.
In addition to the random noise distribution, it is necessary to investigate IOQT with the
use of a gray scale object for the reconstruction of a real object which is composed of various
materials. As shown in Figure-7a, a gray scaled object having a dimension of 50 x 50 pixels is
composed of several smaller square blocks each having a dimension of 5 x 5 pixels. The
individual pixel intensity within a square block can range from 1 to 7 with all of the pixels in
any given square block having the same value. The intensity of adjacent square blocks differ
by one unit. The image reconstructions shown in Figure-% and 7c correspond to the same set
of projection angles that were used in Figure-4b and 4c, respectively. The differences in the
shape and size of the distribution of pixel intensities within the reconstructed images of the
square blocks clearly reflects the differences in the value of the initial mass (i.e. absorption
coefficient) intensities that were used. The higher the initial mass intensity that was used, the
closer the shape and size of the reconstructed image was to the original square block. The
simulated real projection of this object also included gaussian random noise. Consequently,
these simulations prove that IOQT is a practical method to reconstruct a 3D image of a real
sample with a limited number of views.
Figure-8 represents our first step into the real world of 3D reconstruction with IOQT. We
have used the experimental data taken by Haddad15 (1994) using scanning x-ray transmission
microscope at Brookhaven National Laboratory. We extracted ID projection data from each
projection with angles of 0, 15, and f 5 5 degrees for one of 2D object plane (slice) shown in
Figure-8a which shows the cross-section of a gold test pattern. In Figure-8b which shows the
series of the reconstructions after each iteration, we can see the artifacts and the residual
background gradually disappearing with each iteration and quickly approaching the real test
pattern.
4. DISCUSSION
We have shown the practical and potential application of IOQT for 3D reconstruction with
limited view data. Compared to other algorithms in the reconstruction of limited projection
data, IOQT is a much more applicable and precise technique to identify the internal structure of
an object. Figure-9 demonstrates the comparison of the reconstructions resulting from the use
of three algorithms; IOQT, typical ART, and Back-projection. As demonstrated in this
comparison, the internal architecture is well defined by IOQT and the streak of artifacts shown
in the reconstructions of ART and Back-projection does not appear.
A key element of any optimization techniques is the selection of a convergence criterion
because the reconstruction cannot be uniquely determined by a finite number of projections.
We have empirically found that the criterion for IOQT is consistent with the terminating
criterion defined by Equation-13. Using different combinations of primary distributions for the
fictitious object and reference projections as constraints, the reconstructed images were found
to all converge into a region whose boundaries are defined by the intersection of the four views
of the real object. The differences in the reconstructed images were indistinguishable. If a
reconstructed image did not fall within this boundary then the reconstructed image would not
be representative of the information obtained by all the limited number of views of the real
object. This is exactly the condition that would be required for the standard deviation to be at a
minimum. An example for the highly probable region for solution convergence in the case of
four views using IOQT is illustrated in Figure-10. The reconstruction with IOQT in each
iteration is always convergent because the function of the optimization procedure is to
minimize the difference, Mj = d0j - dif , and to have the estimated image bounded into the
plausible region. However, the sequential ratio for the q-th iteration,

reconstruction as described by Equation-12 in order to facilitate the next iteration, the ratio of

the primary distribution of the fictitious object and reference projections, terminated using the
criterion in Equation-13, would lead to even a better image by reducing the overall number of
artifacts.
5. CONCLUSION
We have demonstrated the benefits of IOQT for image reconstruction with a limited
number of projections. IOQT has the advantages of reducing the number of projections
necessary for 3D image reconstruction thus protecting samples and simplifying the experiment.
Limited number of views is often necessary for applications in biology, geology, oceanology,
astronomy, and especially in studying living biological specimens. Unlike other algorithms,
IOQT can eliminate artifactual streaks often observed in limited view reconstruction. In
addition, it can aid in identifying the artifacts of the reconstruction and allow for modification
of the image. Further, we can obtain a better reconstruction by averaging the reconstruction
with other constraints, i.e. other reference projections. We find that IOQT is a novel technique
that can be successfully used to reconstruct a 3D image with a limited number of projections.
