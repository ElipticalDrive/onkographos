Rendering volumetric phenomena with believable appearance can
add tremendous realism to virtual scenes. We introduce the CommonVolumeShader node, an extension of the X3D standard
which has been specifically designed for physically-based rendering of participating media. CommonVolumeShader allows content authors to specify optical properties in a concise and purely
declarative way and can accurately capture the appearance of many
volumetric phenomena. We demonstrate results with implementations for an interactive ray tracer and a rasterization-based pipeline.
In this paper we propose the CommonVolumeShader node as
an extension of the X3D Programmable Shaders component. It is
inspired by the CommonSurfaceShader node [Schwenk et al.
2010] and provides a declarative (i.e. highly portable) description of volumetric optical properties for physically-based rendering. Surprisingly few parameters are needed to accurately describe many volumetric phenomena, resulting in a compact but still
widely applicable node. As part of an Appearance node, a CommonVolumeShader can be attached to arbitrary geometry and
can be used in combination with other Appearance properties,
e.g. a CommonSurfaceShader node that describes surface appearance. We believe the X3D standard will greatly benefit from
the CommonVolumeShader, because it provides a powerful and
portable way to specify more realistic materials – and thus more
believable virtual worlds.

Introduction
The appearance of many objects in our world depends on the optical
properties of the whole object, including the interior, not just the
surface. Examples include gaseous phenomena like fog, liquids
like milk, and solid materials like marble. Rendering these objects
in a believable and physically-based manner can add tremendous
realism to virtual scenes.

Related Work
Our work is closely related to the development of the CommonSurfaceShader node [Schwenk et al. 2010]. This node provides a slim and declarative shader interface that allows authors
to specify surface appearance in a compact and portable way, can
capture a wide range of materials, and is easy to implement. Our
goal was to develop a node with the same qualities for specifying
volume appearance. Pharr and Humphreys [Pharr and Humphreys
2004] and, more recently, Gutierrez et al. [Gutierrez et al. 2009]
provide excellent surveys on physically based rendering of volumetric effects.
Currently, the X3D ISO standard [Web3D Consortium 2011] offers
little support for specifying and rendering volumetric phenomena.
One option is to use X3D’s Volume Rendering component. However, its main purpose is scientific visualization, primarily of medical data sets, and it is not readily applicable to volumetric phenomena of arbitrary geometry and optical properties (detailed reasons

It follows from the above that many common volumetric phenomena can be accurately modeled by just specifying four optical properties. The CommonVolumeShader node provides X3D with the
possibility to do exactly that.
3.2
The CommonVolumeShader node is a purely declarative shader
node, like the CommonSurfaceShader node. Analogously to
the CommonSurfaceShader, we have decided to group all volume shaders under a common base node, the VolumeShader
node. In addition to the reasons given in [Schwenk et al. 2010], this
design allows us to combine a VolumeShader with a SurfaceShader in a single Appearance instance. (The observant reader
may object that such a combination requires the isSelected
field of two shaders to be true at the same time. This is admittedly a
broad interpretation of the standard, but with our declarative surface
and volume shaders it is not a problem in practice, because they are
complementary.) So an implementation may shade the surface of
an object with a CommonSurfaceShader and the interior with
a CommonVolumeShader, or it may ignore one (or even both) if
it does not support the nodes.
The novel interactive voxel-based rendering techniques [Laine and
Karras 2010; Crassin et al. 2010] are an alternative to the stringent
Volume Rendering component and our classical polygon-based approach, but – at least at the moment – we consider them to be too
much the subject of ongoing research to be considered for an ISO
standard.

Mapping the parameters of a node directly to the underlying physical quantities bears the danger of making them unintuitive and hard
to tweak. However, we believe this is not the case here, because
each parameter controls a distinctly defined aspect of the final appearance and – if one is purely interested in a certain visual effect –
they can simply be treated as colors.
Nested objects require multiple VolumeShader nodes to be active for a given ray segment. However, in order to make implementations easier, we have decided to allow only one active VolumeShader at a time. In addition, each ray must leave the nested volumes in the inverse order as they were entered. This way a simple
stack can be used to keep track of the currently active VolumeShader during ray tracing. The rasterization-based implementation has even stronger restrictions (see below).
The phaseFunction field specifies the phase function to be

3.5
PhaseFunction Node
While ray tracing is the obvious choice to simulate volumetric effects, we have also implemented the technique in a rasterizationbased renderer as a proof of concept. This implementation relies
on depth buffers to calculate the distances light travels through media and therefore cannot handle non-convex, nested, or transparent
objects correctly. Non-convex and nested objects cause trouble if
rays cross the objects surface more than twice, because our depth
buffers can only hold up to two depth values per pixel. In this case
we simply take the nearest and farthest intersection and integrate as
if the ray segment between these points was completely inside the
volume. This gives wrong results, but it helps masking artifacts that
would occur otherwise. Transparent objects would require multiple
depth values per pixel, too. Here, we simply apply an alpha test
with threshold 0.5 to solve visibility. This can cause objectionable
artifacts, but without resorting to complicated techniques for orderindependent transparency it is the best we can do with a standard
rasterization pipeline.
We have modeled the phase function as a separate node to ensure the extensibility of CommonVolumeShader with additional
phase functions. Furthermore, this black box design allows phase
functions to use their own mechanism of retrieving spatially varying parameters, e.g. via 3d textures.

Results
Figure 1 shows the elementary building blocks of a CommonVolumeShader applied to a Text node (which is internally converted into a polygonal mesh). The two renderings with single scattering are particularly interesting. The medium scatters blue light
more strongly than red and green light, therefore the medium appears bluish close to the surface and yellow inside. The rightmost
image has a reduced scattering coefficient, so blue light can penetrate deeper into the medium. (GPU-based ray tracing, 1024 × 768,
12Hz, on a GeForce GTS 450 1024MB, this card was used for all
measurements in this paper.)

Media that exhibit fluorescence or phosphorescence cannot be described accurately because CommonVolumeShader assumes no
energy transfer takes place between wavelengths. However, fluorescence can be faked to some extend for a fixed illuminant by
specifying an emission and absorption behavior that mimics the energy transfer the medium would exhibit under this illuminant.
Figure 2 shows a combination of CommonVolumeShader with
CommonSurfaceShader. Only light that penetrates into the object affects the CommonVolumeShader. The single scattering
simulation leads to a believable attenuation inside of the object,
including realistic self-shadowing. The additional volumetric information gives the object a far more convincing appearance than
could be achieved by a surface shader alone. The combined image
was rendered at 1024 × 768, 2Hz (ray tracing, internal reflection
takes a heavy toll here).
For some applications the physically-based parameters (and the
underlying light transport equation) of CommonVolumeShader
may be a disadvantage, e.g. if an ‘effect shader’ with physically
implausible behavior needs to be specified. Then one has to resort
to explicit shader programs (and accept the potential loss in portability).
The left of Figure 3 shows a single-scattering layer of ground fog.
It was created by simply applying a CommonVolumeShader to a
horizontal plane. A texture was used to create an exponential falloff
of the scattering coefficient with increasing height. The right of Figure 3 uses a VolumeShaderFog to fill the scene with a realistic
fog. Direct light reaches the hallway only through the arcs to the
left, resulting in nice volumetric shadows. (Ray tracing, 1024×768,
left: 5Hz, right: 9Hz.)
Although this is more a limitation of current implementations and
hardware than a limitation of the CommonVolumeShader node,
it ought to be mentioned that simulating volumetric light transport
is a costly process, even with the single scattering approximation
and early ray termination. Especially for interactive applications it
may be necessary to place objects very carefully and tune the step
size manually to get acceptable performance. For rasterization the
issues described in Section 4 have to be considered as well.
Figure 4 shows some combinations of CommonVolumeShaders with CommonSurfaceShaders used to simulate subsurface scattering. Because of the comparatively large scattering coefficients these scenes profit massively from early ray termination.
The ‘planck’ model was rendered at 97Hz (rasterization with early
ray termination; ‘planck’ uses a large 3d texture and was the slowest
of the four).

We introduced the CommonVolumeShader node, a way to specify volumetric light transport with a compact and portable declarative interface. Many natural phenomena can be handled, although
some restrictions for exotic materials exist. We demonstrated results with implementations for ray tracing and rasterization. The
latter suffers from some system-inherent limitations, but even with
these restrictions the CommonVolumeShader is a valuable extension to model volumetric light transport in X3D.
Finally, Figure 5 demonstrates the effects of the phase function. A room is filled with a participating medium by simply defining an appropriate Shape node consisting of a Box
and an Appearance with a CommonVolumeShader. The
room is lit through stained glass and the g parameter of the
HenyeyGreensteinPhaseFunction is varied to show the
resulting difference in scattering behavior. (Ray tracing, 1024 ×
