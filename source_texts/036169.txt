Abstract Surface reconstruction methods based on 3D Gaussian Splatting achieve high-fidelity rendering, but at the cost of
millions of redundant Gaussian primitives, resulting in enormous model storage overhead and slow mesh extraction. Existing
Gaussian pruning methods largely rely on global importance measures and inevitably compromise the geometric integrity of
weakly textured or weakly observed regions, producing holes in the reconstructed mesh. To address this, we propose CPGOF, a center-object-faithful training-time pruning method for Gaussian opacity fields (GOF) that systematically resolves
the tension between Gaussian bloat and center-geometry fidelity in GOF at three levels: theory, mechanism, and application.
At the theoretical level, since existing training-time pruning ignores the statistical instability of newborn Gaussians, we
directly reuse the opacity gradients already computed during backpropagation to construct an exponential moving average
(EMA) importance measure with zero additional cost, and we establish an age-aware mechanism that guarantees the
statistical reliability of pruning decisions. At the mechanism level, we embed a center-protected gradient-EMA importance
pruning (CP-GEIP) module into the standard training loop and, by using a sparse-reconstruction prior to define a 3D spatial
protection box, achieve absolute preservation of the center object together with adaptive pruning of redundant background
Gaussians. At the application level, we perform distance-aware filtering and quadric error metrics (QEM) mesh
simplification on the lightweight Gaussian field to further compress the surface representation. While keeping the native
GOF representation unchanged, the entire pipeline compresses Gaussian model storage overhead and lightens the mesh, and
we validate its center-object fidelity through multi-scene experiments.

1 Introduction
Neural rendering methods based on 3DGS [1] and earlier efficient representations [2, 3] have made
remarkable progress in real-time view synthesis and surface reconstruction. However, their densification
mechanism causes the number of Gaussian primitives to grow explosively from an initial few tens of thousands
to millions, with model storage overhead reaching hundreds of MB, which severely constrains deployment on
storage-limited devices and in real-time rendering pipelines [4–7]. GOF [8] and neural surface reconstruction
methods [9, 10] further introduce an opacity-field constraint to enable high-quality mesh extraction, but they
inherit the densification mechanism of 3DGS, making the volume-bloat problem especially acute. Achieving a
lightweight Gaussian field while preserving the surface-reconstruction capability of GOF has therefore become
a key bottleneck in moving GOF from academic research toward practical applications.
Existing Gaussian-lightweighting methods face two fundamental limitations when tackling this bottleneck.
The first category is post-hoc pruning: LightGaussian [11] computes saliency scores from ray-intersection
frequency, Mini-Splatting [12] performs pixel-level pruning based on color-blending weights, and RadSplat [13]
removes far-field Gaussians via a distance field. These methods implicitly assume that “rendering contribution
is equivalent to geometric importance.” However, weakly textured regions of the scene’s main body (such as
building facades, indoor walls, and road surfaces) and weakly observed regions (such as occluded backsides, the
reverse sides of mirrors and glass panes, and structural interiors) often exhibit low rendering contribution due to
gradient saturation or sparse viewpoint coverage. In global ranking, they are misjudged as prunable background
regions, resulting in geometric holes.
The second category is training-time constraints: Scaffold-GS [14] adopts an anchor-neural-network hybrid
structure, Octree-GS [15] organizes Gaussians hierarchically via an octree, and Compact 3D Gaussian
Representation [16] uses a learnable mask to constrain the number of Gaussians. The research focus of these
methods is to achieve Gaussian lightweighting while preserving GS rendering quality, compressing explicit
parameters by restructuring the Gaussian representation. However, existing work has not evaluated the
compatibility of their representation with subsequent mesh-extraction tasks: fundamental modifications to the
Gaussian structure (such as anchoring or octree-based organization) significantly alter the distribution
characteristics of the opacity field and disrupt the field continuity required for mesh extraction, degrading the
performance of 3DGS-based mesh-extraction methods.

To break out of the above “pruning harms geometry, restructuring harms compatibility” dilemma, this paper
builds upon GOF and proposes CP-GOF, a center-object-faithful training-time pruning method for Gaussian
opacity fields that is compatible with the native GOF representation. During training, a CP-GEIP module is
embedded into the standard GOF training loop to achieve adaptive control of the number of Gaussians and highfidelity preservation of the main-body region; in post-processing, distance-aware filtering and QEM mesh
simplification are performed on the lightweight Gaussian field to further compress the surface representation.
Working together, the two stages achieve model lightweighting while preserving the surface-reconstruction
capability of GOF. Fig. 1 provides an overview of the CP-GOF framework.
Our core contributions can be summarized at three levels. At the theoretical level, we directly reuse the
opacity gradients already computed during backpropagation to construct an EMA importance measure at zero
additional rendering overhead, establish a confidence interval through a gradient-statistics-bias analysis of
newborn Gaussians, and determine the age threshold for training-time pruning (𝑇age = 1500 iterations) via
EMA bias analysis and empirical parameter-convergence considerations, thereby ensuring reliable pruning
decisions. At the mechanism level, we use a sparse-reconstruction prior to define a 3D spatial protection box,
analytically determine the scale factor from the point-cloud coverage ratio, and thereby achieve a balance of
“aggressive background pruning and absolute center preservation”, while introducing growth-trend protection as
a complement to the age mechanism. At the application level, we construct a complete “training-time slimming
plus post-processing refinement” lightweighting pipeline, performing distance-aware filtering and QEM mesh
simplification on the lightweight Gaussian field output by CP-GEIP to further compress the surface
representation. Multi-scene experiments show that CP-GOF effectively compresses Gaussian model size and
substantially simplifies the mesh surface representation while keeping the geometric fidelity of the center object
visually indistinguishable from the original GOF.

2 Related Work
2.1 3D Gaussian Splatting and Surface Reconstruction
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [17–20] achieve high-quality novel-view synthesis via an implicit
volumetric representation, but their MLP-based per-point query mechanism severely limits rendering speed and
struggles to meet real-time requirements. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] replaces the implicit network with
explicit anisotropic Gaussians and achieves real-time rendering through efficient rasterization, quickly
becoming the dominant paradigm in neural rendering [21]. However, the original 3DGS design optimizes only
photometric consistency; its anisotropic Gaussians form ellipsoidal distributions in 3D space and lack explicit
constraints on surface geometry, so the Gaussian centers often deviate from the true surface, and directly
extracting a mesh produces severe artifacts and floating noise.
To remedy this deficiency, subsequent work has attempted to introduce geometric supervision, but all such
methods become trapped in the trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and computational efficiency. SuGaR

[22] binds Gaussians to the mesh surface via a surface-alignment constraint, but its joint optimization
framework requires alternately optimizing Gaussian parameters and mesh topology, incurring heavy
computational overhead, and its surface-alignment regularization tends to over-smooth high-frequency
geometric detail. PGSR [23] introduces planar constraints to improve geometric accuracy in large scenes, but its
core assumption—that the local scene can be approximated as planar—systematically fails on curved surfaces,
vegetation, and unstructured detail regions, leading to geometric collapse in regions of complex topology. 2D
Gaussian Splatting [24] improves depth estimation by projecting 3D Gaussians into 2D disks, but this
dimensionality-reducing representation severs the continuity of 3D space; it not only sacrifices the global
consistency of depth perception, but also requires its mesh extraction to rely on additional post-processing and
struggles to recover complete surface topology.
The essential commonality of the above methods is that they all attempt to “patch” geometric defects within
the original 3DGS representation, or rely on discretization methods such as Marching Cubes during mesh
extraction, or introduce over-smoothing and topological constraints during regularization—thereby introducing
inherent errors such as limited resolution, sharply increasing memory overhead, and loss of surface detail. In
sharp contrast, the Gaussian Opacity Fields (GOF) proposed by Yu et al. [8] fundamentally restructure the
geometric-supervision paradigm: instead of directly manipulating discrete Gaussians, GOF constructs a
continuous opacity field that converts discrete Gaussians into a differentiable implicit surface representation,
achieving—for the first time—continuous modeling of the internal geometric structure of the Gaussian field
without sacrificing rendering efficiency. Furthermore, GOF introduces a depth-normal consistency constraint
that couples the photometric and geometric information of multi-view geometry in a joint optimization, forcing
the Gaussian distribution to converge toward the true surface rather than floating as ellipsoids in space.
Crucially, GOF employs marching tetrahedra to directly extract high-quality triangular meshes; compared to
traditional Marching Cubes, tetrahedral partitioning can adaptively capture complex topological variations and
completely avoids the resolution bottleneck and feature loss introduced by voxel-grid discretization. This design
enables GOF to achieve simultaneous breakthroughs across three dimensions—surface-reconstruction accuracy,
mesh topological integrity, and extraction efficiency—making it the preferred solution and de facto benchmark
for directly extracting high-quality meshes from 3D Gaussian fields.
However, while achieving its breakthrough in reconstruction quality, GOF also fully inherits the
densification mechanism of 3DGS: the number of Gaussian primitives grows explosively from an initial few
tens of thousands to millions through adaptive densification, and the final model storage overhead can reach
hundreds of MB. This “accuracy improvement accompanied by volume bloat” paradox is not merely a matter of
scale; it exposes a deep flaw in the representational efficiency of GOF: its densification strategy is driven by
global photometric error and indiscriminately splits Gaussians in all regions within the view frustum during
optimization, causing a large number of primitives to be allocated to the background, sky, ground, and other
redundant regions far from the object of interest. For center-object-priority scenarios (such as autonomous
driving, industrial inspection, and cultural-heritage digitization), this globally uniform bloat causes severe
resource misallocation—the geometric accuracy of the foreground target certainly benefits, but the number of
Gaussians in irrelevant background regions grows just as exponentially, forming a heavy “geometric-noise
burden.”
The consequences of this bloat are multidimensional and mutually coupled: millions of Gaussians not only
sharply increase model-storage and network-transmission overhead, but also impose enormous computational
pressure during mesh extraction—marching tetrahedra must traverse all Gaussians to build the tetrahedral mesh,
and the linear growth in primitive count translates directly into super-linear deterioration of extraction time.
Meanwhile, the latency of rasterized rendering also rises linearly with the number of Gaussians, severely
constraining the deployment feasibility of GOF on edge devices, in real-time rendering pipelines, and in largescale scenes. Therefore, achieving adaptive lightweighting of the Gaussian field—that is, matching Gaussian
density to geometric importance rather than blindly binding it to photometric error—while preserving the
surface-reconstruction quality advantage of GOF has become a key bottleneck for moving GOF from academic
research to practical applications, and is the central starting point of this work.
2.2 Gaussian Lightweighting Methods
Existing Gaussian-lightweighting methods can be divided into two categories, post-hoc pruning and trainingtime structural restructuring, but neither can simultaneously satisfy the two core constraints of “center-object
fidelity” and “GOF compatibility.”

Post-hoc pruning reduces the number of Gaussians based on global importance ranking after training is
complete. LightGaussian [11] computes saliency scores from ray-intersection frequency, Mini-Splatting [12]
performs pixel-level pruning based on color-blending weights, RadSplat [13] removes far-field Gaussians via a
distance field, and LP-3DGS [25] adopts a learned strategy to predict Gaussian importance. These methods
implicitly assume that “rendering contribution is positively correlated with geometric importance.” However, in
weakly textured regions of the scene’s main body (such as building facades, indoor walls, and road surfaces)
and weakly observed regions (such as occluded backsides, the reverse sides of mirrors and glass panes, and
structural interiors), weak photometric contribution and sparse viewpoint coverage lead to low rendering
saliency, and global ranking misjudges these regions as prunable background, directly producing geometric
holes. Moreover, post-hoc pruning often requires an additional rendering pass or rasterizer adaptation, incurring
high engineering complexity.
Training-time structural restructuring instead attempts to reduce redundancy from the Gaussian
representation itself. Scaffold-GS [14] uses an anchor-neural-network hybrid structure, Octree-GS [15]
organizes Gaussians hierarchically via an octree, and Compact 3D Gaussian Representation [16] constrains the
number of Gaussians via a learnable mask. Although these methods achieve significant compression, GOF
surface extraction depends on the opacity-field continuity of explicit Gaussians, and any fundamental
modification of the native Gaussian representation (such as anchoring, neural-network-based attributes, or the
introduction of non-Gaussian primitives) disrupts the spatial distribution of the opacity field, causing abnormal
gradient flow and mesh-extraction failure. Consequently, existing training-time constraint-based methods are
difficult to apply directly to the GOF pipeline.
In summary, existing Gaussian-lightweighting methods fall into the dilemma of “pruning harms geometry,
restructuring harms compatibility”: post-hoc global pruning inevitably sacrifices the geometric integrity of the
center object because of the spatial mismatch between rendering contribution and geometric contribution;
training-time structural restructuring, by changing the native form of the Gaussian representation, disrupts the
continuity of the GOF opacity field and causes high-quality surface extraction to fail. The essence of this
dilemma is that existing mainstream methods either perform “after-the-fact trimming” once Gaussiandistribution optimization has converged, or “tamper with the representation structure” during training—and
neither achieves the co-evolution of Gaussian count and geometric importance within the training dynamics.
To this end, this paper aims to explore a training-time lightweighting paradigm compatible with the native
GOF mechanism—achieving adaptive constraint on the number of Gaussians and explicit protection of centerregion geometric fidelity without altering its explicit Gaussian representation and opacity-field framework—
thereby breaking the bottleneck in which model lightweighting and high-quality surface extraction cannot be
achieved simultaneously.
2.3 Training-Time Control of the Number of Gaussians
The lightweighting methods above—whether post-hoc ranking or training-time masking—all treat Gaussians
as statistically stable individuals when making pruning decisions, directly retaining or deleting them based on
the current importance measure. However, a large number of newborn Gaussians exist during GOF training
(produced by densification via cloning or splitting); with very few historical gradient records, their EMA
importance estimates exhibit systematic underestimation and high variance. If such Gaussians directly
participate in pruning ranking, the true importance of newborn Gaussians is severely underestimated: those that
“should be retained” are erroneously removed (false-kill), while those that “should be deleted” are erroneously
retained because of an occasional large-gradient observation (false-keep). Existing work has not analyzed this
reliability problem of newborn Gaussians from a statistical perspective, nor provided a minimum age lower
bound for pruning decisions, leaving training-time pruning without theoretical guarantees [26–28].
The above analysis shows that existing methods lack theoretical guarantees for the statistical reliability of
newborn Gaussians. Starting from EMA bias modeling and confidence-interval derivation, this paper establishes
a minimum age lower bound for training-time pruning.

3 Method
3.1 Method Overview
Among existing lightweighting methods, post-hoc pruning (LightGaussian, Mini-Splatting, etc.) requires
modifying the CUDA rasterizer or executing an additional global rendering pass, which is incompatible with the
opacity-field optimization mechanism of GOF training; training-time structural restructuring (Scaffold-GS,
Octree-GS, etc.) changes the explicit Gaussian structure and disrupts the spatial continuity of the opacity field,
causing GOF mesh extraction to fail. This paper therefore focuses on training-time pruning that is fully
compatible with the original GOF training pipeline, and does not conduct direct comparisons with the
aforementioned heterogeneous frameworks.
Our method, denoted CP-GOF, comprises two parts: training-time Gaussian lightweighting and postprocessing geometric simplification. The training part embeds a CP-GEIP module into the standard GOF
training loop, periodically pruning redundant background Gaussians based on importance while absolutely
protecting the center-object region. The post-processing part extracts a mesh from the lightweight Gaussian field
obtained after training, and compresses the number of faces via distance filtering and QEM [29] simplification.

3.2.3 Age Awareness and Growth-Trend Protection
In the early stages of their existence, newborn Gaussians have too short a gradient history, and their EMA
importance estimates suffer from serious statistical bias. This subsection establishes the theoretical necessity of
the age-protection mechanism from three angles—estimation bias, confidence interval, and false-kill rate—and
derives the lower bound of the minimum age threshold 𝑇age .
It should be noted that Eqs. (6)–(8) characterize the response behavior of the EMA estimator to the gradient
sequence. When = 0.92, the EMA weights themselves approach steady state within about 100 iterations
( 100 ≈ 2.4 × 10−4 ). However, newborn Gaussians face not only the transient bias of the EMA estimator, but
also, more critically, the fact that their own parameters (position, covariance, color, opacity) have not yet been
sufficiently optimized. In the GOF densification mechanism, a Gaussian receives a structural update
(cloning/splitting) only once every 100 iterations, after which it must undergo many gradient-descent iterations
before its local geometry and radiance-field distribution stabilize. Therefore, the physical meaning of 𝑇age is not
to wait for the EMA estimate to converge, but to ensure that a Gaussian undergoes parameter optimization over
at least 15 densification cycles (about 1500 iterations), so that its gradient distribution transitions from an “initial
transient” to a “statistical steady state.” Only then do the gradient samples on which the EMA estimate is based
possess stable statistical properties, and only then does the pruning decision become reliable.

iterations and never fully converging—to accumulate in background regions. Although numerous, these
Gaussians fail to form an effective radiance-field contribution because they lack stable gradient optimization;
instead, they disrupt the spatial-distribution balance of the Gaussian field, and their stray radiance propagates
through the rasterization gradient into the interior of the protection box, causing the rendering consistency inside
the protection box to collapse. This is precisely the root cause of the “volume bloat and quality plunge”
phenomenon observed in the ablation study below when age protection is removed. The age-protection
mechanism is not a simple conservative strategy, but a key stabilizer that maintains the balance of training
dynamics and prevents the accumulation of ineffective Gaussians from suppressing effective ones. Fig. 5
visualizes the effect of the age-protection mechanism on training dynamics.

3.2.4 Periodic Pruning Schedule
We set up multiple pruning windows in the middle of training (iterations 7000/12000/18000/24000), pruning
background Gaussians outside the protection box in ascending order of importance, all at a ratio of 35%. After
each pruning, the EMA statistics are reset to prevent stale estimates from contaminating the new set, and
recovery time is left for subsequent optimization. It is worth noting that resetting the EMA statistics after each

3.3 Integration with the GOF Training Pipeline
CP-GEIP is embedded into the GOF training loop in a non-invasive manner. During initialization, after
Gaussians are created, the EMA buffer is started and the protection box is computed from the initial point cloud;
in each iteration, the gradient EMA is updated after loss.backward(); after densification, the birth iterations
of new Gaussians and the dimension of the EMA tensor are synchronized; and during a pruning window, ageaware importance pruning is performed inside and outside the protection box. Here, filter_3D is a boolean
mask tensor used in the GOF training pipeline for 3D spatial filtering (removing floating Gaussians far from the
main surface or of abnormal size), whose dimension corresponds one-to-one to the number of Gaussians. Since
CP-GEIP dynamically changes the number of Gaussians during pruning and densification, the dimension of the
filter_3D tensor must be synchronized accordingly. We explicitly synchronize the slicing and concatenation
of filter_3D in our prune_points and densification_postfix implementations, preventing the
dimension-mismatch crash caused by the dynamic increase and decrease of Gaussians during training.
3.4 Post-Processing Geometric Simplification
On the lightweight Gaussian field produced by CP-GEIP, we perform standard GOF mesh extraction
(marching tetrahedra), followed by two post-processing steps. The first step removes floating fragments far from
the main surface with extremely large areas via distance-aware filtering; the second step applies QEM (Quadric
Error Metrics) mesh simplification, reducing the number of faces through quadric-error edge collapse while
preserving the topology of the center object. Because CP-GEIP has already cleaned up a large number of
background floaters during training, the post-processing QEM is less likely to erroneously collapse the center
structure, creating a synergistic lightweighting effect from training to output.
It is worth noting that the post-processing QEM simplification currently performs aggressive edge collapse
only in the background region, while the center region retains its original topology. This may appear to forgo the
compression potential of the center region, but it is in fact a deliberate concession to geometric fidelity: the face
density of the center object directly determines the reconstruction accuracy of the surface normals, and any edge
collapse may damage sharp features (such as truck-window frames and wheel spokes). Experiments show that,
given that CP-GEIP has already cleaned up the background noise, simplifying the background alone can achieve
a 60.5% reduction in faces, meeting deployment requirements. Adaptive fidelity-preserving simplification of the
center region (such as curvature-aware local QEM) is left as future work.

4.1 Experimental Setup
The experiments are implemented in the Nerfstudio [32] framework. The dataset uses the Truck scene from
Tanks and Temples [33], with COLMAP [30] sparse reconstruction as initialization. The baseline is the official
GOF implementation, using the same configuration (full resolution, decoupled appearance model, 30K
iterations).
The evaluation metrics include novel-view synthesis quality (Test PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS) and Gaussian model
size (MB). For implementation details, training is performed on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090, and the
hyperparameters of CP-GEIP are fixed at = 0.92, = 1.8, and 𝑇age = 1500 (see Sect. 3.2).
In addition, the EMA importance update and periodic pruning operations of CP-GEIP introduce extremely
low computational overhead: because they directly reuse the gradients already computed during
backpropagation, no additional rendering pass is required, and the reduced number of Gaussians after pruning in
fact lowers the computational burden of subsequent iterations. Measurements show that the total training time of
CP-GEIP on the Truck scene is comparable to, or even slightly shorter than, that of the GOF baseline, incurring
no additional time cost from the pruning mechanism.

5 Conclusion
To address the problem of Gaussian-count bloat during GOF training, this paper proposes the CP-GOF
framework, which is fully compatible with the original training pipeline. It solves the statistical-reliability
problem of “when to prune” through an opacity-gradient EMA importance measure and a Gaussian-age
mechanism, solves the center-object-integrity problem of “where to prune” through a COLMAP-prior protection
box, and solves the dynamic-balance problem of “what to preserve” through the synergistic design of age
protection and growth-trend protection, thereby unifying aggressive background pruning with absolute
preservation of the center object.
Experiments show that CP-GOF compresses the Gaussian model size by 20.4% and reduces the mesh face
count by 60.5% with a PSNR loss of only 0.10 dB, while keeping the topological structure of the center object
consistent with the original GOF; in the multi-scene validation on Mip-NeRF 360, the PSNR loss is kept within
0.14 dB and the size reduction rate reaches 14.5% to 32.7%.
The limitations of our method are that the protection box relies on the COLMAP initial point-cloud
distribution and its boundary may be ambiguous in extreme scenes where the center object and background
heavily overlap, and that the age threshold is currently a fixed empirical value whose adaptive-adjustment
strategy remains to be explored. Future work will focus on dynamic optimization of the protection box,
lightweighting extensions to 4D Gaussian fields [34], and the transfer of the age-protection framework to other
3DGS variants.
