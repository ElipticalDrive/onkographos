A flight vehicle with control surfaces can be characterized by its trim table, which provides
control surface deflections and aerodynamic forces as functions of flight condition. Constructing
this table from computational fluid dynamics data is challenging because the trimmed state exists
as a lower-dimensional hypersurface within a larger aerodynamic design space. Traditional
sampling methods, including full-factorial designs and space-filling techniques, perform poorly
for this problem because they aim to cover the full design space rather than concentrate near
the trimmed state. This paper compares three adaptive sampling strategies for constructing
trimmed aerodynamic databases and simulating trajectories: simultaneous contour sampling
based on moment-coefficient entropy, trim IMSPE sampling based on integrated mean squared
prediction error, and trajectory-binning sampling based on flown flight conditions. These
sampling strategies are applied to a demonstration workflow with the High-speed Army
Reference Vehicle (HARV). The trim integrated mean squared prediction error (IMSPE) and
trajectory-binning methods provide the best agreement with a precomputed truth dataset, while
the simultaneous contour method is most effective at reducing trim-space entropy. Finally,
we address the question of convergence when no truth dataset exists and explore potential
convergence criteria for instances in which a reference truth trajectory is not known.

I. Introduction
Constructing high-quality aerodynamic databases with good prediction of aerodynamic forces and moments on the
vehicle throughout the design space is a central step in quasi-steady trajectory simulation. High-fidelity computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) solvers make it possible to capture complex aerodynamic physics and to compute this force and
moment data directly from simulation [1]. However, the cost of each CFD evaluation is high, and the total expense
grows rapidly with the number of design variables used to describe the flight condition and control state. As a result,
building a sufficiently resolved CFD-driven aerodynamic database using gridded sampling alone is computationally
expensive, if not impractical, for vehicles with control surfaces. As a compromise, lower fidelity methods of estimating
forces and moments are often used, including correlations and panel solvers, to populate the design space.
These challenges motivate the use of surrogate modeling together with efficient sampling strategies [2]. Rather
than attempting to evaluate the full design space uniformly, adaptive sampling methods aim to place new CFD samples
where they are expected to provide the greatest improvement to the surrogate model or to a particular downstream
quantity of interest. Gaussian process (GP) surrogate models can provide both predictions and uncertainty estimates,
making them of particular interest for this type of study. While their application to hypersonics is relatively recent, there
is demonstrated value in terms of reduced computational cost and built-in access to information needed for thorough
uncertainty quantification analysis. Multi-fidelity methods [3–5] are well-suited for aerodynamics, where a variety of
code fidelities are available. Co-kriging techniques allow the aerodynamic design space to be densely populated with
cheap models that are anchored to a smaller number of higher-fidelity simulations [6–9]. In this work, however, we focus
specifically on the sampling problem itself: how to place new high-value samples such that a trimmed aerodynamic
database can be constructed accurately and efficiently.
Quinlan et al. introduced sequential sampling for aerodynamic applications, where adaptive sampling methods
optimize new CFD runs based on expected improvement to the surrogate model [10]. Subsequently, these techniques
were generalized to support sample generation based on minimizing the uncertainty of a downstream quantity of interest
[11], rather than simply the uncertainty in the aerodynamic database. By iteratively generating samples and propagating
the uncertainty through a trajectory solver, it is possible to sample only in regions relevant to the vehicle’s flight path
while simultaneously reducing the total aerodynamic-database uncertainty.
A. Trimmed Vehicles
Here, we consider a vehicle with independently actuating control surfaces that can actively shape its trajectory. The
aerodynamic database includes a dimension for the actuation of each control surface, thereby greatly increasing the
complexity of the surrogate modeling problem. However, the essential data used by a quasi-steady trajectory solver is
not the full aerodynamic database, but rather its trimmed aerodynamic database.
Quasi-steady trajectory solvers neglect the transient off-trim behavior of the vehicle as it maneuvers, instead treating
the transition between trim states as occurring instantaneously. This assumption that the contribution of the transient
behavior to the overall vehicle performance is small can be well justified, and is a common one to make in vehicle design
studies. For each flight condition (e.g., Mach-𝛼-𝛽 combination), the trim table gives the control surface deflections
required to drive the aerodynamic moments to zero, together with the resulting forces acting upon the vehicle at that
flight condition. Errors in the aerodynamic surrogate therefore propagate through the trim solve and into the trim table,
which can lead to inaccurate predictions of control deflections, trimmed forces, and feasible trajectories.
The space of trimmed states is a lower-dimensional hypersurface embedded within a larger space parameterized
by flight conditions and control surface deflections. Conventional sampling approaches such as full-factorial grids or
purely space-filling designs are inefficient in this setting because they target a model for the full hypervolume, much of
which is irrelevant to trim or to the subset of trim conditions actually visited during trajectory analysis. More effective
strategies should instead concentrate samples near locations where trim is likely to exist, where uncertainty in the trim

prediction remains high, or where the vehicle is most likely to fly.
B. Contour Estimation
Mathematically, the process of finding the trim condition from discrete aerodynamic samples is referred to as
contour or level-set estimation. Approaches involving surrogate models have been successful in the statistics literature
([12], [13], [14], and [15]). Of particular interest to our work, Cole et al. applies an entropy-based metric with Gaussian
process surrogates for efficient contour identification in the single-response setting [16]. The result is the entropy-based
contour location (ECL) acquisition function, which is defined for a target contour of 0 as

This metric is maximized when the probability 𝑃(𝑌 (𝑥) > 0) = 𝑃(𝑌 (𝑥) ≤ 0) = 0.5. Therefore, the ECL criterion
will be large if the mean of the GP is close to 0 or the prediction variance is high.
A relevant extension is given by [17], who consider targeted sampling for multiple contours in noisy, correlated
simulations. Their approach is based on probabilistic satisfaction of tolerance bounds and allows for the possibility
that no feasible joint solution exists. In contrast, the present work restricts the design space to regions where there is a
strong expectation that the vehicle can be trimmed. [18] recently extended contour estimation techniques to include the
assessment of multiple contours, where their intersection is of interest. In practical terms, the intersection of three
contours, each representing a zero-moment condition about a rotational axis, defines the trim condition. This provides a
natural starting point for the present work, which considers a similar question across all flight conditions.
C. Current Study
In this study, we investigate adaptive sampling methods for trim table generation through the lens of an exemplar
trim-analysis workflow. We consider a maximum-crossrange trajectory optimization problem for the High-speed Army
Reference Vehicle (HARV), a benchmark vehicle for unpowered, maneuvering flight [19]. Trim tables generated
through the sampling methods under study are used as input to a direct collocation trajectory optimization solver with
flight-dynamics equations and constraints, which ultimately determines aerodynamic performance and impact location.
A large CFD database has previously been computed for the HARV and is used as a truth case for comparison. A
high-performing sampling method will approach the performance of the full database with significantly fewer samples.
There are three sampling techniques under study here, alongside random sampling as a control. The first method
uses simultaneous contour information from the aerodynamic surrogate to target regions where the moment coefficients
are near zero and uncertain [18]. The second uses an integrated mean squared prediction error criterion defined on
a surrogate of the trim table. The third uses trajectory binning to emphasize flight-condition regions that are most
frequently visited during a trajectory optimization step.
The contributions of this work are threefold. First, we adapt entropy- and uncertainty-based sampling techniques to
the problem of constructing trimmed aerodynamic databases in a trajectory-analysis workflow, exploiting the fact that
the trimmed state lies on a hypersurface within the larger aerodynamic design space and that only a subset of that space
is operationally important. Second, we compare multiple adaptive sampling strategies that prioritize samples using
contour information, trim-surrogate uncertainty, or trajectory occupancy. Third, we examine practical convergence
criteria for this setting, particularly criteria that do not rely on the existence of a truth data set for comparison.
II. Methodology
A number of sampling methods were evaluated for potential suitability for this study. Factors considered included
effectiveness, scalability, and robustness. Of these, three adaptive sampling methods were selected, each of which uses
information from a different stage of the trajectory simulation workflow. These adaptive methods are compared against
both random and gridded sampling baselines. The methodological focus of this work is the comparison of adaptive
sampling strategies for aerodynamic database refinement in a trim-and-trajectory workflow. In particular, the study
evaluates how sampling criteria defined on the aerodynamic surrogate, the derived trim table, and the flown trajectories
affect both trim-space uncertainty and trajectory prediction quality. This section first defines the trim problem and
aerodynamic design space, then describes the iterative workflow and surrogate models, then presents the adaptive and
baseline sampling methods, and finally summarizes the study design and evaluation metrics.

control surface deflections such that pitch, yaw, and roll moment coefficients are zero.

This study evaluates the trim problem for the High-speed Army Reference Vehicle (HARV) [19], where the
flight-condition space is three-dimensional and the trim solve is performed over four control surface deflections.
Because the HARV has four control variables and three moment constraints, many flight conditions may admit a
one-dimensional set of zero-moment control surface deflections within the four-dimensional control-surface decision
space. The minimum-drag objective is therefore used to select a unique trimmed solution from that feasible set. Because
the trim solve depends on surrogate predictions of the aerodynamic moments, errors in the aerodynamic database
propagate directly into the predicted trimmed controls and trimmed forces, affecting the downstream trim table and
trajectory predictions.
The aerodynamic surrogate is defined over the combined flight-condition and control space. In this study, that space
is spanned by Mach number, angle of attack, sideslip angle, and the four control surface deflections. The surrogate
therefore maps a seven-dimensional input vector,
(𝑀, 𝛼, 𝛽, 𝛿1 , 𝛿2 , 𝛿3 , 𝛿4 ),
to the corresponding aerodynamic force and moment coefficients.
This higher-dimensional structure makes the trim problem challenging from a sampling perspective. The trim table
is defined over the three-dimensional flight-condition space, but each trimmed state is obtained by solving within the full
seven-dimensional aerodynamic design space, so the trim manifold is a lower-dimensional hypersurface embedded in
that larger space. A gridded design over all surrogate inputs becomes prohibitively expensive as dimensionality grows,
and even general space-filling designs may waste samples in regions far from trimmed states or frequently flown flight
conditions. These considerations motivate adaptive methods that concentrate samples where they are most likely to
improve trim and trajectory predictions.
B. Iterative workflow
Each iteration of the adaptive sampling process begins from the aerodynamic database and workflow products
generated in the previous round. The following steps are performed at each iteration:
1) The adaptive sampling method generates a set of new CFD evaluation points in the full seven-dimensional design
space based on the results of the previous round.
2) The aerodynamic coefficients are evaluated, either by running Cart3D directly or by querying the truth surrogate,
depending on the study configuration.
4
3) The newly obtained aerodynamic data are merged with all previously available samples to form an updated
aerodynamic database.
4) A Gaussian process surrogate is fit to the updated aerodynamic database.
5) The trim tool uses gradient-based optimization on the surrogate to determine trimmed control surface deflections
over a prescribed set of flight conditions, producing an updated trim table.
6) The trajectory optimizer uses the trim table to predict vehicle trajectories.
7) The resulting aerodynamic, trim, and trajectory outputs are used to evaluate convergence and assess the
effectiveness of the adaptive sampling strategy.
The adaptive methods differ in the stage of the workflow from which sampling priorities are derived. The
simultaneous contour sampling method operates directly on the aerodynamic surrogate, targeting regions where the
three moment contours are both near zero and uncertain. The trim IMSPE sampling method operates on a surrogate
defined over the trim table and targets flight conditions where trimmed aerodynamic predictions remain uncertain. The
trajectory-binning sampling method operates on the simulated trajectories and emphasizes flight-condition regions
visited most frequently.
Although not every adaptive strategy requires every intermediate workflow product to generate new samples, all
stages are executed at each iteration, either within the sampling process or as post-processing. This allows consistent
evaluation of convergence criteria at the end of each workflow iteration.
C. Surrogate models
Within this workflow, two key surrogate models are created. The first is an aerodynamic surrogate defined on
the full seven-dimensional input space and returns aerodynamic force and moment coefficients as functions of flight
condition and control surface deflections. This model serves as the input to the trim tool and is also queried by the
simultaneous contour sampling method. The second is a trim surrogate built from the calculated trim table. For a
given flight condition, it returns the trimmed control surface deflections together with the associated trimmed force and
moment coefficients, where the moment coefficients are expected to be zero for trimmable flight conditions. This model
is used by the trim IMSPE sampling method.
D. Non-uniform weighted sampling
Two of the adaptive sampling strategies considered here require a scalar weight function defined over the sampling
space. For these cases, a non-uniform weighted space-filling sampling procedure inspired by minimum energy designs
[20] is employed. The procedure constructs a deterministic set of sample locations that balances two competing
objectives: concentrating samples in regions with high weight and maintaining separation between selected points to
preserve space-filling coverage.
At each adaptive iteration, a candidate set of points is generated over the bounded sampling space using a Latin
hypercube design. A nonnegative weight is then assigned to each candidate point according to the sampling criterion of
interest, and candidates with negligible weight are discarded. The final design is constructed sequentially by selecting
points that maximize a weighted distance criterion relative to the points already chosen.
In this work, a MaxPro-style distance metric is used. This metric promotes good low-dimensional projection
properties while still favoring points in high-weight regions. After the sequential construction step, a simulated annealing
procedure is used to locally improve the design.
This framework provides a common mechanism for converting contour-based or trajectory-based importance
measures into actual CFD sample locations. Compared with purely random weighted resampling, the procedure reduces
clustering among selected points while still directing new evaluations toward regions identified as important by the
adaptive criterion.

F. Trim IMSPE sampling
Another method considered is IMSPE-based sampling, which is focused on reducing total predictive uncertainty
within a specified space [11]. This criterion may be applied either in the original aerodynamic design space or in a
lower-dimensional derived space. Because the objective of this work is to construct a trimmed database suitable for
trajectory analysis, IMSPE is applied in the trimmed flight condition space spanned by 𝑀, 𝛼, and 𝛽.

strategy therefore places new samples where uncertainty in the trimmed aerodynamic response remains high.
G. Trajectory-binning sampling
In this sampling method, the flight-condition space defined by 𝑀, 𝛼, and 𝛽 is discretized into bins. For this analysis,
50 bins were used in each dimension. A notional example is shown below for some given 𝛽:

For each adaptive iteration, the reference trajectory is projected into this discretized flight-condition space and
the number of visits to each bin is recorded. These visit counts are normalized to define a weight over the bins, so
that regions encountered more frequently during the reference trajectory are assigned greater importance. A small
default weight is provided for all bins that are not flown through to preserve some exploration outside the currently
flown trajectories.
Candidate flight conditions are then sampled from this weighted distribution, and the trim interpolant is queried
to determine the corresponding trimmed control surface deflections for each selected flight condition. This strategy
therefore prioritizes new CFD evaluations in operationally important regions of the flight envelope rather than in regions
chosen solely by global uncertainty reduction.
H. Baseline methods
Two non-adaptive baselines are included for comparison: gridded sampling and random sampling. Gridded sampling
methods evaluate the Cartesian product of prescribed values in each input dimension and have historically been used to
generate aerodynamic databases. These approaches produce databases that are easy to interpret and can simplify CFD
workflows through mesh reuse at repeated control surface deflections. However, gridded designs restrict sampling to a
discrete lattice in the design space, making them inefficient in higher dimensions and generally less space-filling than
non-uniform or adaptive designs given the same total sample count. In this study, gridded baselines were constructed in
the full seven-dimensional aerodynamic input space using either two or three evenly spaced values per independent
variable, corresponding to 27 = 128 and 37 = 2187 CFD evaluations, respectively. Because gridded sampling does
not support the same fine-grained iterative refinement as the adaptive methods, its use here is limited to fixed-size
benchmark studies.
Random sampling is used as an iterative control baseline. At each adaptive sampling round, new sample locations
are drawn uniformly across the bounded aerodynamic input space and appended to the existing aerodynamic database.
This provides a simple comparison case in which no model-based acquisition function is used. Like the adaptive
methods, the random baseline adds samples in batches and supports round-by-round evaluation of convergence metrics.
I. Study design
The simulation study evaluated sampling performance for generation of an aerodynamic database for the HARV
flight vehicle. To assess both efficacy and variability, each iterative sampling method was tested in fifteen independent
replicate studies. Each study began with a random space-filling design of 30 initial samples.
In the adaptive rounds that followed, the number of new samples increased in stages, with 8 samples per round used
in rounds 2–4, 15 in rounds 5–9, 30 in rounds 10–14, and 40 thereafter, as shown in Fig. 4.
Fig. 4
Sampling schedule used in the iterative studies.
The test problem uses the three independent flight-condition parameters, 𝑀, 𝛼, and 𝛽, and four control-surface
decision variables. Performance was compared across simultaneous contour sampling, trim IMSPE sampling, trajectory8
binning sampling, iterative random sampling, and the fixed gridded baselines. The gridded benchmark studies used 128
and 2187 CFD evaluations, corresponding to two and three samples per dimension in the seven-dimensional aerodynamic
input space. For comparisons based on the truth data, a separate reference dataset built from 5490 space-filling CFD
samples over the full aerodynamic design space was used to construct a higher-fidelity surrogate and associated trim
results.
This setup was chosen to reflect the expected application of this study: a workflow in which samples are added
iteratively until a desired convergence metric is achieved. The objective of the staged sampling is to ensure that both
early- and late-stage convergence effects can be studied at a reasonable computational cost across the 60 total sampling
studies.
J. Evaluation metrics
Sampling performance is evaluated using metrics defined at several levels of the workflow. The primary truth-based
metric is trajectory agreement with the reference dataset, measured by comparing the trajectories produced by each
sampled database against the trajectories obtained from the reference surrogate. Because such truth data are not generally
available in practical applications, additional convergence metrics are also tracked based on changes from one sampling
round to the next.
At the trajectory level, round-to-round change metrics compare the predicted trajectories from consecutive workflow
iterations. At the aerodynamic and trim level, coefficient-based comparisons are made using the weighted root-meansquare error in 𝐶drag evaluated over the trimmed flight-condition set. These coefficient-level metrics provide a supporting
view of convergence when trajectory outputs are noisy or insensitive to remaining aerodynamic differences.
An additional uncertainty-based metric is defined from the moment-surrogate entropy used in the contour analysis.
This metric is reported as the fraction of sampled design-space points whose contour entropy exceeds a prescribed
threshold, and it is intended to measure how much of the trim-relevant region remains unresolved. Taken together,
these metrics allow the sampling methods to be compared in terms of both truth agreement and practical convergence
behavior when no truth dataset is available.
III. Results
The sampling methods are evaluated using the trajectory-, coefficient-, and entropy-based metrics defined in the
methodology section. Because these metrics emphasize different parts of the trim-and-trajectory workflow, they can
lead to different conclusions about the apparent rate and character of convergence. The results are therefore presented in
terms of truth-based trajectory agreement, round-to-round change metrics, entropy throughout the design space, and
supporting coefficient-level comparisons.
Overall, the results indicate that non-gridded and adaptive methods are more effective than coarse gridded sampling
for this problem. However, the differences between adaptive methods are somewhat less clean than originally expected,
especially when looking only at final trajectory metrics. In particular, the simultaneous contour sampling method
does drive down uncertainty in the locations of the trim contours as expected, but this does not translate to strong
performance in matching the truth trajectories.
A. Comparing to truth data
To determine, in a broad sense, which sampling methods performed best, the results were first compared to the truth
database, which was generated through space-filling sampling. A first comparison of convergence toward the truth
trajectories is therefore obtained by comparing the resulting trajectories with those flown by the truth model. While this
cannot serve as a convergence criterion for applications of these methods to a novel problem, it can still be used here to
identify the best-performing sampling methods.

The non-gridded sampling methods have much less error in their trajectories than gridded sampling, even for a
much smaller number of total samples. This highlights, among other things, the effects of nonlinearity in the aerotable,

likely influenced by error sources outside the sampling method itself, including the trajectory solver settling on a locally
optimal solution and coefficient differences that make the truth trajectory either infeasible or less optimal than a nearby
alternative.
The trajectory-binning sampling method gives the lowest median trajectory error for most of the study, approaching
the final trajectory location more quickly than the other methods and reaching this diminishing-returns stage with less
final error. It reaches this common plateau earlier than the other sampling methods, and continues to improve to a lower
error level than the other models. The trim IMSPE sampling method also performs well, while the simultaneous contour
sampling method only modestly outperforms the random sampling method in this truth-based trajectory metric.

B. Comparing to previous iterations
Practical applications of adaptive sampling will not have a truth model available; instead, a stopping criterion must
be calculated from the data. The most natural way to do this is by comparing a quantity of interest from one iteration to
the next. While it cannot directly measure error, it can provide an indication of whether the additional samples are
causing a change to the model results or if the model has converged.
Fig. 8 Comparison of each sampling round against the previous round, shown as the median final total trajectory
error versus the number of samples.
Comparing Figs. 8 and 5 illustrates a key difficulty with comparing one sampling iteration to the next. It is difficult
to determine whether a changing quantity of interest is moving toward its truth value or simply reflecting random noise
and overall modeling uncertainty.
For the present problem, this metric shows some of the same broad trends as the truth comparison: the adaptive
methods generally reduce round-to-round changes faster than coarse gridded sampling. However, the ordering among
the adaptive methods is less clear here, and the metric itself is noisy. It therefore cannot distinguish changes that reflect
genuine model improvement from changes driven primarily by model noise. In particular, a small change from one
round to the next does not always imply that the solution is close to the truth. It could also indicate that the method has
stalled or that the trajectory metric is simply not sensitive to the remaining errors in the model.
This is one of the central difficulties in the present application. The trim workflow can amplify small modeling
differences in a way that is hard to predict from the endpoint trajectory data alone. As a result, a plateau in this metric
could reflect convergence, but it could also reflect a poor sampling method or a trajectory metric that is no longer very
informative.

A key objective of this work is to determine where the trim conditions are and how much uncertainty remains in that
table, so a convergence criterion that evaluates this uncertainty could be valuable. In this framework, the entropy is

As additional samples are added, more of the design space tends toward low entropy values. Most of the space
with high contour values is driven by uncertainty rather than by the known presence of a trim contour, as we see in

space-filling design and evaluate the contour at each point. We can then evaluate the points with an entropy above a
given value to measure what fraction of the space is still classified as highly uncertain. Unlike the trajectory-based
criteria, this metric can be computed without access to truth data and does not depend on selecting a particular quantity
of interest derived from the trajectory.

decrease with the addition of new samples for all of the methods, suggesting a decrease in uncertainty in the trim tables
even as the trajectory error approaches a point of diminishing returns.

E. Computational discussion
There is also a practical tradeoff between sampling effectiveness and the cost to add additional samples. Adaptive
methods, especially the simultaneous contour sampling method, take longer to generate new sample points as the study
progresses.

The cost of the simultaneous contour sampling approach is driven by how sparse the space is with respect to
non-zero contours. As each round of sampling targets the high-entropy regions, the difficulty of finding additional
samples increases with each iteration. However, the trajectory-binning sampling method retains a similar sampling
weight function in the sampling space on each iteration, so finding samples does not become more difficult with each

While the cost to generate samples does increase, even medium-fidelity CFD takes multiple hours of wall time, so
even if a relatively expensive sampling method such as contour MED sampling is used, if it can reduce the CFD cases
needed to characterize a set of reference trajectories, the net computational cost will be reduced. On the other hand,
because these adaptive sampling methods require each study round to finish before the next begins, the wall time to
reach a given number of samples will generally be larger than for a space-filling study, since fewer CFD cases can be run
fully in parallel. This cost could be reduced by choosing more studies per sampling iteration and ending the study once
a more moderate convergence level is reached, yielding faster-turn studies than those considered here.
F. Summary of results
Taken together, these results support a few main conclusions. First, non-gridded methods are preferable to coarse
gridded sampling for generating aerodynamic databases for running quasi-steady trajectories with a high-dimensional
aerotable. Each iterative sampling method was able to generate trajectories with much less error compared to the truth
model than the gridded sampling method.
Second, each of the three adaptive sampling methods has its advantages, but no single adaptive method dominates
every metric. The trajectory-binning sampling method converged more quickly than the other methods to a point of
diminishing returns, and remained closer to the truth trajectory endpoints, but a few sampling iterations generated
trajectories that differed sharply from the truth. The trim IMSPE sampling method had slightly slower convergence to
the truth trajectory endpoints, but did not show the same divergence issues. The simultaneous contour sampling method
performed worse than the others with regard to converging to the truth data, but was successful at driving uncertainty
down much faster than the other methods.
Finally, identifying a reasonable convergence criterion for an adaptive sampling method remains challenging without
truth comparison data. The uncertainty of entropy contours may not correlate particularly well with the error in the
trajectory, and change from one iteration to the next in terminal trajectory locations can be misleading.

IV. Conclusion
This paper examined sampling methods and convergence criteria for iterative aerodynamic database refinement.
For the present problem, the results show that non-gridded and adaptive sampling methods are clearly preferable to
coarse gridded sampling. All three adaptive sampling approaches produced substantially better agreement with the truth
trajectories than the gridded approach, showing the power of adaptive sampling methods for efficient refinement of a
high-dimensional aerotable.
The two strongest-performing sampling methods in this study are the trim IMSPE sampling and trajectory-binning
sampling methods. The trajectory-binning sampling method often reduced trajectory error more quickly and in many
cases achieved the best agreement with the truth data, but it also exhibited several outlier trajectories and occasional
unstable behavior. This suggests that a method focused too strongly on exploitation of the current sample distribution
can be vulnerable to surrogate-model errors or local minima in the trajectory solution process. While the trim
IMSPE sampling method did not converge as quickly, it proved to be more robust across the sampling iterations. The
simultaneous contour sampling method was most effective at reducing uncertainty in the trim-space entropy metric, but
this improvement did not translate into equally strong trajectory-level accuracy.
A central result of this work is that the choice of convergence criterion strongly affects how the sampling methods
appear to perform. Metrics based on round-to-round changes are desired when truth data are unavailable, but they can be
misleading because small changes may indicate either true convergence or stagnation. Similarly, entropy-based metrics
are useful for measuring uncertainty in the trim space, but they did not correlate strongly with trajectory accuracy in the
present application.
As a result, neither of these metrics alone appears sufficient as a general stopping criterion for trajectory-oriented
adaptive sampling. In practice, this suggests using multiple diagnostics rather than relying on any single stopping rule.
Overall, these results support the use of adaptive sampling for aerodynamic database refinement, while also showing that
the most useful sampling strategy depends on whether the goal is robust trajectory prediction, rapid local improvement,
or global reduction of trim-space uncertainty.
Future work should focus on developing convergence criteria that better connect uncertainty in the trim model
to trajectory-level error, and on adaptive sampling strategies that maintain the fast convergence of trajectory-focused
methods while avoiding instability caused by overexploitation.
