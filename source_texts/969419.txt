Comparative Study and Analysis of Advanced Denoising
Techniques for Speckle Noise Reduction in Ultrasound
Images
Dr. Anand B. Deshmukh1, Dr. Sanjay V. Dudul2
1Associate Professor, Department of IT, Sipna C.O.E.T. Amravati, India
2Professor & HoD (Rtd.), Department of Applied Electronics, S.G.B.A.U. Amravati, India
Abstract: Unexpected changes in the brightness and color values in the images causes introduction
of distortion or noise in the image and frames of the video stream. Such random unexpected
variation in the brightness and color values doesn’t carry any intelligence or information in the
image, in fact it degrades the quality of the image. To minimize the effect of such meaningless
information, denoising frameworks are designed. In this research paper we have studied various
denoising frameworks for speckle noise reduction and proposed a de-speckling algorithm. The
performance evaluation is carried out with evaluation parameters such as PSNR and MSSIM.
Keywords: MSSIM, PSNR, Speckle Noise.
I. INTRODUCTION
Noise in an image is the term used to describe undesired and arbitrary variations in pixel, color, or
brightness. Usually, the adjacent pixels are compared to these changes. The image has more noise if
the changes are more erratic. A variety of sources can introduce noise into an image. It may be added
at the time of image capture, editing, or enhancement, or even when the image is being transmitted
via wired or wireless connections (such as Wi-Fi or Bluetooth).
Noise in an image can be caused by problems such as camera shake, malfunctioning sensors, or
switching components. Noise can be introduced during image improvement by utilizing improperly
calibrated automatic features or by modifying settings such as ISO. Changes in the surroundings or
interference during data transfer might potentially produce noise. Since videos are composed of
separate visual frames, the same is true.
Special programs known as denoising algorithms are employed to correct or minimize this noise and
return the image to its original quality.
The way noise is added determines the kind of noise that appears in an image. Gaussian noise, impulse
noise, mixed noise, and speckle noise are examples of common forms. This paper examines speckle
noise, or how it manifests in pictures, and suggests a technique called de-speckling to lessen or
eliminate it.
Speckle noise is frequently observed in artificial images, such as those generated by aerial imaging
systems and RADAR. Multiple reflected waves that return after striking a surface generate random
interference. It deteriorates picture quality and manifests visually as sporadic white, black, or gray
dots.

II. LITERATURE SURVEY
The pervasiveness of noise in image acquisition, transmission, and enhancement makes image
denoising a crucial field of research in digital image processing. In addition to degrading visual quality,
noise can jeopardize crucial image analysis, especially in domains like remote sensing, satellite images,
and medical imaging. Over the years, a variety of methods and strategies have been put forth to
address various forms of noise, such as Gaussian, impulse, and speckle noise.
1.
Conventional Denoising Methods:
In the past, picture denoising techniques have been widely divided into transform domain and
spatial domain approaches. By averaging pixel values according to their surroundings, spatial
domain techniques including mean filtering, median filtering, and Gaussian smoothing seek to
minimize noise. Nevertheless, crucial edge features and structural information are frequently lost
with these techniques. Even though it works well against impulse noise, median filtering [1] can
still obscure subtle details.
Superior performance is provided by transform domain techniques, especially those based on
wavelet transforms [2], which isolate the image into frequency components and reduce noise in
the high-frequency subbands. The basis for wavelet-based denoising was established by Donoho
and Johnstone's wavelet shrinkage technique [3], which uses wavelet coefficient thresholding to
remove noise while maintaining picture properties.
2.
The Anisotropic Diffusion and Speckle Noise:
A type of multiplicative noise, speckle noise is frequently seen in coherent imaging systems such
as MRI, SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar), and ultrasound. Speckle noise is more difficult to remove
than additive Gaussian noise since it is dependent on the strength of the underlying signal. The
Lee filter [4], Frost filter [5], and Kuan filter [6] are early techniques for dealing with speckle noise;
they all work under the statistical presumption of local homogeneity but have a tendency to
weaken edges.
The advent of anisotropic diffusion techniques was a major breakthrough. Anisotropic diffusion,
which was proposed by Perona and Malik [7], maintains edges by altering the diffusion process
according to gradients in the image. Yu and Acton developed Speckle Reducing Anisotropic
Diffusion (SRAD) to apply this idea for speckle noise [8]. SRAD has demonstrated efficacy in
medical imaging applications and is specifically capable of handling multiplicative noise. By
adjusting the diffusion strength throughout the image, it maintains fine structures by smoothing
homogeneous areas while leaving boundaries unaltered.

3.
Techniques for Logarithmic Transformation:
Due to the multiplicative character of speckle noise, many contemporary methods start by
transforming the noise into an additive form via a logarithmic transformation. Since many wellestablished methods are more appropriate for additive noise models, this change streamlines the
denoising process. After the image has been transformed, it can be treated with either linear or
nonlinear filters. Finally, an exponential transformation is applied to return the image to its initial
intensity range.
Despite being mathematically straightforward, this pre-processing step is essential for enhancing
the resilience of the ensuing denoising methods. The transformation greatly enhances methods
like wavelet-based shrinkage or Wiener filtering, particularly when used with adaptive filtering
frameworks [9].
4.
Adaptive and Guided Filtering:
The technique of guided image filtering, which uses a guiding picture—typically the image or a
smoothed version of it—to filter an input image while maintaining edges, has been the subject of
recent advancements. The Guided Filter (GF), first presented by Achim, A., Bezerianos, A.,
&Tsakalides et al. [10], greatly enhances edge-preserving smoothing and has been modified in a
number of ways. By adding local variance and gradient information, the Weighted Guided Image
Filter (WGIF) and the Gradient Domain Guided Image Filter (GDGIF) significantly enhance the GF,
which makes them especially useful for structure-aware denoising applications.
These techniques have been effectively used in noise reduction jobs where edge preservation is
crucial, including in satellite and medical imaging, and they provide better detail retention than
conventional filtering techniques.
5.
Wavelet-Based Frameworks and Hybrid Approaches:
By fusing the advantages of several approaches, hybrid strategies have become a potent strategy.
A popular framework uses wavelet or curvelet decomposition to handle frequency-based noise
reduction after initial preprocessing using anisotropic diffusion or median filtering. The suggested
approach uses two-level wavelet decomposition, which provides a multiscale representation of
the image and reduces noise from high-frequency components while preserving crucial lowfrequency information. By constantly modifying filtering behavior based on local content,
adaptive filters like WGIF and GDGIF, which successfully balance denoising strength and edge
preservation, further improve this system.
Studies like those by Dabov et al. [12] and Achim et al. [11] (BM3D) have shown that hybrid
approaches that combine adaptive filtering with transform domain analysis produce state-of-theart results in denoising tasks, particularly in speckle-affected images.
6.
Evaluation Metrics for Denoising Performance:
Several statistical measures are used to evaluate denoising algorithms' performance
quantitatively. One of the most used metrics is still the Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), which

The suggested denoising method for ultrasound images is a methodical, multi-phase procedure
intended to successfully lower speckle noise while maintaining crucial image characteristics. The
following are the steps that make up the methodology:
1.
Input Image (Black and White, Grayscale, or Color): An input image, which could be in color,
grayscale, or binary (black and white) format, is used to start the process. Although most
ultrasound images are in grayscale, this framework is made to work with a variety of image
formats, increasing its adaptability for a range of uses.

2.
Preprocessing Using SRAD Filter: As a preprocessing step, the Speckle Reducing Anisotropic
Diffusion (SRAD) filter is used. SRAD is an edge-sensitive filter designed to minimize speckle noise
without obscuring crucial elements like small structures and edges. In contrast to conventional
smoothing filters, SRAD adaptively smoothes homogenous regions while maintaining structural
boundaries a crucial aspect of medical imaging by utilizing image gradients and diffusion
coefficients.
3.
Logarithmic Transformation: The image undergoes a logarithmic change during preprocessing.
To facilitate the subsequent steps of separating the noise from the actual image signal, this step
transforms the multiplicative character of speckle noise into an additive form. Additionally, by
compressing the dynamic range of pixel intensities, this change enhances the effectiveness of
later denoising methods.
4.
Two-Level Decomposition: Two-level decomposition of the altered image is performed, usually
with wavelet transformations. Using this method, the image is divided into various frequency
components (detail and approximation subbands) at various scales. While the second level pulls
finer information, the first level catches larger visual structures. Targeted denoising, which
eliminates noise mostly from high-frequency components while maintaining low-frequency
content that contains crucial image information, requires this multi-resolution analysis.
5.
Image Reconstruction: The inverse transform of the decomposition approach is used to recreate
the image in the last stage. The processed subbands are merged back into the full image in this
stage. It is anticipated that the reconstructed image will have substantially less speckle noise
while keeping crucial diagnostic features because of the selective denoising done in the earlier
steps.
This structured pipeline shows an efficient hybrid method for de-speckling ultrasound pictures,
ranging from SRAD filtering to wavelet-based reconstruction. It improves visual quality and
facilitates more precise medical analysis by combining the advantages of wavelet decomposition,
logarithmic transformation, and anisotropic diffusion.

V. CONCLUSION
In this study, we provide a practical approach for eliminating speckle noise from a range of
photographs. Three frequently used test images—Lena, Pepper, Airplane, and an Ultrasound image
(in JPG format)—are used to illustrate the algorithm's performance. These photos are subjected to
speckle noise with different variance levels (ranging from 0.01 to 0.1). Applying a Speckle Reducing
Anisotropic Diffusion (SRAD) filter is the first preprocessing step in the procedure. The multiplicative
noise is then transformed into additive noise using a logarithmic transformation. Then, to maintain
wavelet coefficients while eliminating noise, a combination of Gradient Domain Guided Image Filter
(GDGIF) and Weighted Guided Image Filter (WGIF) is used. Lastly, Variance, PSNR, and SSIM measures
are used to assess the effectiveness of the suggested approach.
