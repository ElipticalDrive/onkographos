ABSTRACT
Graph Signal Processing (GSP) extends classical signal processing to the analysis of signals supported over irregular grids represented by graphs. Sampling and reconstruction are fundamental tools that have received considerable attention in GSP.
Under the assumption that adjacent nodes tend to have similar signal values, a reconstruction method that recovers a signal by minimizing its variation with respect to
the graph structure is proposed. To this end, the mean square error of the recovered
signal is minimized by sampling sets whose spectrum presents high-frequency dominance, a characteristic present in blue-noise patterns that suppress the low-frequency
components by maximizing the inter-sample distance. Blue-noise sampling has many
advantages, including the possibility of finding close to optimal sampling performance
using simple distance concepts. However, the formulation of blue-noise sampling is
restricted to graphs with regular structures, and most applications involve irregular
graphs. Thus, the underlying principles of blue-noise sampling need to be considered
in a broader sense. Another limitation of blue noise is its high computational complexity, limiting its use to small graphs. This work overcomes these limitations and
generalizes blue-noise sampling to large and irregular graphs. First, blue-noise sampling is generalized to irregular graphs by regularizing the weights across the edges of
the graph before sampling, such that the concentration of samples is adapted to the
local density of nodes. Secondly, a new and scalable approach can be easily parallelized
to leverage the massive parallel processing power readily available in commodity hardware is proposed. The proposed method uses graph partitioning algorithms in concert
with vertex-domain blue-noise sampling to develop two sampling schemes that minimize the recovery error and are based on the spatial characteristic of the graph. The
first sampling scheme combines graph partitioning with the void-and-cluster algorithm.
xv
The second approach uses error diffusion in the partitions. Experiments on synthetic
and real data show the effectiveness of these new approaches on very large graphs.
Then, the partitions are used to recover the signal by adding some overlapping, which
induces smoothness in the recovered signal within and between partitions. Finally, the
benefits of blue-noise sampling on graphs are extended outside of GSP to the active
form of semi-supervised learning to reduce the amount of labeled data needed by the
machine learning algorithms to achieve high accuracy.

Chapter 1
INTRODUCTION
In different fields, such as social, financial, ecological, and traffic networks, the
data can be represented by irregular graph structures that model the intrinsic relationships between observations. Signals live in the graphs’ nodes representing information
associated to them; Graph Signal Processing (GSP) has emerged as a tool to analyze these signals by extending concepts of discrete signal processing to graphs. The
interest in reducing the complexity of graph signal analysis through sampling and reconstruction has increased over the past decade [2, 4, 9, 24, 39, 46, 49, 66, 70]. Most of
the existing methods focus on the sampling and recovery of smooth signals where the
signal’s energy tends to be concentrated in a subset of the Laplacian’s eigenvectors [24];
in this case, the sampling problem becomes the optimal row selection of this subset of
eigenvectors [2, 9, 24, 66]. Therefore, the initial work on sampling was based on the
eigendecomposition of the shift operator which led to cubic complexity with number
of nodes, limiting the analysis to small graphs [2, 9, 24, 66]. Eigendecomposition-free
methods have been proposed to alleviate the sampling complexity but they still fall
short for signals on very large graphs [4, 24, 49, 70]. Some sampling approaches that
offer low computational complexity include the Binary Search Gershgoring Disc Alignment method (BS-GDA), which excels in small graphs but underperforms in larger
and denser graphs [4]; the fast sampling method based on the localization operator
(Ed-Free) which requires parameter tuning [49]; and the log-linear algorithm, approximate volume maximization (AVM), which maximizes the coherence and separation
between sampling [24]. Weighted random sampling (WRS) is also a low-complexity
1
sampling method; however, it can lead to high reconstruction errors, especially in approximate bandlimited signals [24, 46]. Recently there has been growing interest on
spatial-based sampling techniques [24,31,38,39]. Some of these approaches include the
void and cluster (VAC) algorithm, which generates high-quality patterns but requires
the computation of the geodesic distances [39], and the Error Diffusion (ED) algorithm
that offers fast sampling but requires vertex ordering which is not clearly defined in
most graphs [31]. Despite the vast amount of research on GSP, mots of these sampling
methods tend to focus on the recovery of strictly bandlimited signals which are hard
to find in real scenarios.
Under the assumption that smooth signals present low-frequency dominance,
Chapter 2 introduces a technique that recovers a smooth signal by minimizing the
strength of the high-frequency components of its spectrum; thus the name Blueness
Minimization Reconstruction (BMR) since color blue is associated with high frequencies. The main benefit of BMR is that it assumes smooth signals and is not limited to
strictly bandlimited models. Nevertheless, the method can recover strictly bandlimited
signals by using some parameter tuning. Chapter 2 proves that the effects of noise on
the proposed reconstruction is minimized by suppressing low-frequency components on
the spectrum of the sampled signal a well-known characteristic of blue-noise sampling
as aforementioned [39].
Blue-noise sampling of graph signals was recently introduced based on stochastic
sampling and concepts of spatial dithering as an intuitive method applied entirely
in the vertex domain [3, 32, 33, 39]. The technique generates sampled signals with
high frequency dominance by maximizing the separation between sampling nodes [39].
However, the current formulation of blue-noise sampling is limited to approximately
regular graphs where the degree distribution presents little to no variation across the
vertices on the graph [39]. In highly irregular graphs, existing blue-noise methods lead
to uniform patterns that fail to represent the underlying graph structure. Assuming
that sampling algorithms should be able to adapt the concentration of sampling nodes
according to the density of the graph, Chapter 2 generalizes the work on [39] to graphs
2
with non-regular node distributions using a regularization of the edges’ weight such
that they represent a more uniform graph where the standard deviation of the degree
distribution is minimized. In the mapped graph, the algorithm in [39] is applied to
generate the sampling pattern.
The main limitation of the methods proposed on Chapter 2 is their scalability which limits the analysis to small graphs. Chapter 3 extends the benefits of the
proposed methods to large graphs by taking advantage of the recent increase in parallel processing resources. More specifically, the sampling and reconstruction of graphs
signals is reformulated using graph partitioning, a technique that divides the graph
into smaller graphs by minimizing the communication between the subgraphs. Then,
the work on Chapter 3 leverages the benefits of graph partitioning to process different
parts of the graphs in parallel.
Having the set of subgraphs induced by the set of nodes in the partitions, sampling and reconstruction methods defined on the vertex domain can be adapted to allow
the parallel processing of different subgraphs; this research focuses on blue-noise-based
techniques, but future work could explore other techniques. Despite being intuitively
simple, the joint use of graph partitioning with blue-noise-based methods still presents
challenges; for example, signals recovered by BMR need to be smooth between partitions which can be forced by sampling all nodes inside the q-hop neighborhood of the
boundary nodes with q being parameter that controls the smoothness of the recovered
signals. However, this solution leads to unnecessary large sampling sets. Instead, this
work proposes an approximate solution where the partitions present some degree of
overlapping, i.e., each partition element is extended such that it includes the q-hop
neighbors of its boundary nodes forming the augmented partitions. Then, the signal
is sampled and recovered using BMR independently on each augmented partition. Finally, in the overlapping regions, the signal value is replaced by the average of the
individual reconstructions inducing smoothness between partitions. The main advantage of the proposed approach is that it allows the parallel processing of the augmented
3
partitions since the recovery of the signal on each element is independent. The reconstruction’s robustness against noise is strongly dependent on the sampling set, and it is
shown that sampling techniques that present high-frequency dominance are beneficial.
Chapter 3 shows that while having an independent sampling pattern on each
augmented partition would minimize the recovery errors, it would also increase the
number of samples in the overlapping regions of the augmented sets. Instead, this work
proposes two sampling schemes that generate a single blue-noise sampling pattern on
the entire graph; thus, they minimize the number of samples. The first approach based
on the VAC algorithm uses error diffusion to generate an initial sampling pattern in the
boundary graph. Then, the initial samples are fixed while sampling the partitions with
VAC; this initial samples generate a repulsion effect avoiding placing samples close to
the boundaries. It is worth mentioning that after sampling the boundary graph, the
partitions can be processed in parallel. The second approach uses the concepts behind
dot diffusion (DD) to connect the partitions and error diffusion (ED) [31, 38]. Dot
diffusion is a dithering technique that divides images into equal size grids and then
processes them independently using ED. Then, an equivalent algorithm on graphs uses
partitioning to divide the graph, and then it samples the individual partitions using the
ED algorithm in [31,38]. This work describes three different forms to obtain the nodes’
numbering required by the ED algorithm; the first method is based on a depth-first
search algorithm; the second uses a breadth-first search-like algorithm, and the final
approach alternates nodes with high and low degrees to avoid processing neighboring
nodes; this last approach leads to the best experimental performance.
After extending the benefits of blue-noise sampling to irregular and large graph
structures, this work considers how to take advantage of this technique in applications beyond signal processing. An example is Active Learning (AL), a form of semisupervised learning where an external agent labels data points selected according to
some heuristic to improve the model training [51]. Sampling is crucial to AL since
the amount of labeled data required by the model to achieve high classification performance depends on its quality. It has been found theoretically and experimentally that
4
sampling sets that maximize the separation between samples minimize the expected
error of the model connecting AL to blue-noise sampling [50]. Therefore Chapter 4
takes advantage of blue-noise sampling algorithms to reduce classification errors in AL
by maximizing data exploration. Furthermore, the method proposed in Chapter 4 combines blue-noise sampling with uncertainty sampling to introduce the model’s feedback
into the graph structure such that the data selection algorithm places more samples
in regions of high uncertainty, which showed experimentally high performance. Chapter 4 presents a set of synthetic experiments that show the benefits of using blue-noise
sampling schemes on an AL framework.

Background
A graph G(V, E) consists of a set of N objects contained in the nodes V and a set
of edges E containing the pairwise relationships between objects. The values associated
with the edges are contained in the weight matrix, W. In this work we consider
weighted undirected graphs W(v, u) = W(u, v2) with no self-loops W(u, u) = 0 . If
the node u is adjacent to v (u ∼ v), then, the value W(u, v) > 0 reflects the degree of
relation between nodes u and v. The set of nodes connected to a node v is known as its
neighborhood, and is denoted by N (v) = {u ∈ V (g)| W(u, v) > 0}. A graph signal is
a function that maps the nodes’ information to the real numbers, x : V → RN , and it
is represented by a vector x ∈ RN , whose entry x(v) represents the value of the signal
on the node v. Signals that have little variation between adjacent nodes are considered
smooth.

Blue-Noise Sampling on Irregular Graphs
Before describing the notion of blue-noise sampling on graphs, the definition of
distance on graphs is required. In a graph, the distance between two nodes Γ(ua , ub )
is defined as the path with minimum length between them, where a path is a sequence
of edges that joins a sequence of nodes (ua , u1 , ..., un , ub ). In binary graphs or graphs
whose edges’ weight reflects the difference between nodes, the length of a path is given
by the sum of the weights of the edges on it. The distance computation needs to
be modified in graphs where the edges’ weights reflect the strength of the connection
between vertices. In this case, the distance corresponding to an edge can be measured
as the reciprocal of the edge’s weight. Then, the length of a path is given by the sum
of the inverse of the weights [13].
15
A sampling pattern on a graph is considered a blue-noise pattern if the collection of m balls Q(si , λ) with radius λ and centered at the sampling nodes on
S = {s1 , s2 ..., sm } form a cover of V, and the cover radius (λ) is the minimum possible
for all subsets with m samples [39]. The characteristics of blue-noise are related to the
k-center problem, where it has been found that the separation between samples should
be maximized [20]. Then, blue-noise patterns promote separation between samples,
resulting in patterns that look like those depicted in Fig. 2.1(b). Note the difference
with Fig. 2.1(a) which shows the spatial representation of a random sampling pattern
on the same network. blue-noise sampling techniques distribute the sampling nodes as
homogeneously as possible, such that the selected nodes are separated by a minimum
distance of λb , known as the principal path length of the pattern. The value of λb is
inversely proportional to the number of samples. As the number of samples increases,
the minimum distance between samples decreases [39].
Not only is the spatial definition of blue-noise sampling intuitive and interpretable, but its spectral characteristics are also associated with good sampling patterns. Blue-noise patterns are characterized by a high-frequency dominance on their
power spectrum. Figure 2.1(c) shows the flat-shaped power spectrum of 100 random
patterns computed using Eqn. (2.3) on a sensor network. Note that in Fig. 2.1(c) all
the frequency components are significant, a well-known characteristic of white-noise. In
contrast, Fig. 2.1(d) shows the graph power spectrum of 100 ideal blue-noise sampling
patterns computed using Eqn. (2.3) on a Sensor Network graph with N = 1000. In this
plot, the high-frequency components are substantially larger than the low-frequency
components; this phenomenon is due to the maximization of the minimum separation
between samples and becomes more pronounced as the sampling density increases. The
color blue is associated with higher frequencies. Therefore, these patterns receive the
name of blue-noise. Since blue-noise patterns suppress the presence of low-frequency
components, they tend to have lower values of redness (Eqn. (2.20)), leading to lower
reconstruction errors.

where ρ(v) is the density of the node, and ρ is the graph’s density. If irr(G) = 0,
then G is a regular graph. Examples of graphs satisfying this condition are the set
of k−regular graphs, such as the Paley graph or the Petersen graph [11]. In general,
common network graphs do not satisfy the condition irr(G) = 0. Almost regular
graphs tend to be more common. These graphs have low values of irr(G) since their
degree distribution presents little variations [11].
Formally, Chung [11] defines approximately regular graphs as a graph where all
but n vertices have the same degree. Then approximately regular graphs tend to have
uniform nodes’ density distribution ρ(v). On the other hand, irregular graphs exhibit
degree variations that lead to higher values of irregularity (irr(G) ≫ 0). Therefore,
different partitions have different isoperimetric dimension, and the conditions on [39]
are not satisfied. Then, patterns generated from equal volume partitions do not minimize the redness and result in low-quality sampling patterns. While there are different
characterizations of irregular graphs [1], here a graph is considered to be irregular if
the standard deviation, σ, of the nodes’ density, ρ(v), satisfies σ ≥ 0.1ρ, where ρ is the
density of G.
This work seeks to generate a good quality sampling pattern on irregular graphs
using blue-noise sampling. To do so, the sampling approach proposed on [39] is applied
to a modified version of the graph that is approximately regular, where the isoperimetric
dimension varies less between partitions. The modified graph is denoted Gar , and it
consists of the same set of nodes VGar = VG and edges EGar = EG but the weights of the
edges are modified according to :
War (u, v) = W(u, v) max {ρ(u), ρ(v)}α ,

(2.29)
where ρ(i) is the density of the node i and α is a variable that controls the uniformity
of Gar . The idea is to modify the weight of the edges such that the degree’s distribution
D(u, u) tends to be constant. The weight of the edges is amplified or reduced depending
on the nodes’ density and the value of α. In general, Eqn. (2.29) increases the distance
between the nodes in denser parts of the graph by modifying the weight of the edges.
The edge’s weight is multiplied by the maximum density of the nodes it connects to
ensure that denser nodes are spread in the resulting graph Gar . The regularization
parameter, α, controls how sparse the regions become.

2.4
Experimental Results
In this Section, the performance of Generalized blue-noise sampling and BMR
is tested on artificial data and in a real dataset [31, 40].
2.4.1
Artificial Graph Signals: Experimental Setup
In the first set of experiments, the performance of GVAC and the BMR is
evaluated and compared to the state-of-the-art techniques in artificial signals using
three synthetic graphs as well as a real traffic network:
• G1: Small World Network graph with N = 1000, with rewiring probability p =
0.8 and average degree d = 80.
• G2: Erdős–Rényi graph with N = 1000, with probability of connection p =
0.1 [41].
• G3: Stochastic Block graph with N = 1024 and k = 5 clusters [41].
• G4: The Minnesota traffic graph with N = 2, 642 [41].

• SM1: the signals are noisy bandlimited. The signals were generated at random

additive Gaussian noise such that the signal-to-noise ratio is SN R = 20dB.
• SM2: the true signals are approximately bandlimited. The GFT coefficients
are drawn from the Gaussian distribution N (1, 0.52 ). Then the signal is passed
through a Butterworth filter H of order n = 8 (n = 1 for G4) and ωc = µk with

BLUE-NOISE SAMPLING AND BLUENESS MINIMIZATION
RECONSTRUCTION ON LARGE GRAPHS
Despite the vast research on graph signal sampling and reconstruction, existing
methods cannot process very large graphs often found in relevant applications, e.g.,
social networks like Facebook, having billions of active users [45]. The advantages of
parallel processing are then of interest in GSP when a complex graph problem could
be potentially broken into hundreds of separate tasks to work on them simultaneously.
This work aims precisely at this goal where parallel processing is used to sample and
reconstruct graph signals by leveraging the concepts of graph partitioning to divide
large graphs into a set of smaller graphs that can be processed independently.
3.1
Graph Signal Sampling and Reconstruction with Partitions
Graph partitioning has been extensively studied; most of the existing algorithms
seek to optimize some objective function, which is often minimizing the communication
between partition elements measured by the total cut [7]:

3.2
Error Diffusion and Dot Diffusion
Error diffusion was initially introduced as an efficient dithering technique that
generates blue-noise patterns using neighborhood operations [17, 32, 33, 67]. In this algorithm, each pixel is quantized at the time. Then, the quantization error is transferred
to the yet non-processed pixels. The original algorithm considered only the immediate
neighbors of the processed pixel in the diffusion step [17], and later work considered
the pixels in the 2-hop neighborhood of the current pixel [23, 68].
Recently, based on the original algorithm, error diffusion was extended to graphs [31,
38]; this work formalizes the formulation of error diffusion and provides a different approach that incorporates partitioning in the sampling process; the extension of error
diffusion to graphs uses the nodes on the graphs as pixels, and the set of edges E as the
structure to diffuse the error. The method initially generates a constant signal x with
amplitude x(v) = m/N and sets the initial the error of the nodes to zero e(v) = 0.
Then, the algorithm defines the order in which the nodes will be processed, known as
the diffusion path (p); after defining the diffusion path, the nodes are processed one
at a time. First, the difference between the signal and the error term is thresholded,
if x(v) − e(v) ≥ t then s(v) = 1 indicating that the node v is sampled, and s(v) = 0
otherwise. Usually, the threshold value is set to t = 0.5. Then, the quantization error,

selects a random node and then processes all its 1-hop neighbors, continuously moving
from the nodes in the l-hop neighborhood to those in the l + 1-hop neighborhood of v.

path; this approach generates an order similar to that of the raster path on [67],
generating a spanning tree path shape. However, a path based on DD that avoids

graphs experimentally.
3.2.1
Dot Diffusion
As aforementioned, DD is dithering that divides the image into equal size grids
and process them using error diffusion; naturally, the extension of this algorithm to
graphs relies on graph partitioning; first, the graph is partitioned into k balanced partitions. Then, assuming that neighboring nodes tend to have similar degree distribution,
the nodes in the partitions are processed by alternating the high and low degree nodes
to avoid processing adjacent nodes consecutively. Given the path, the nodes in the partitions are processed using the error diffusion manner, i.e., quantifying one node at a
time and diffusing the quantization error to its non-visited neighbors. After processing
all partitions, the pattern on G is obtained by concatenating the individual patterns,
such that s(Vi ) = si . The main advantage of DD is that it allows the parallelization
of the sampling process since each partition can be processed independently.

ACTIVE LEARNING WITH BLUE-NOSE SAMPLING
4.1
Active Learning
The large amounts of labeled data required by most supervised learning algo-
rithms aren’t always available. In some cases, creating labeled training sets can be
difficult, time-consuming, or expensive; for example, in speech classification accurate
labeling can take up to ten times the duration of the actual audio [51,77]. Active Learning(AL) emerged as a solution that reduces labeling costs by combining the benefits
of semi-supervised learning with external labeling agents called oracles. Give a pool of
unlabeled data AL continuously selects subsets of unlabeled data and passes it to the
oracle who provides the labels. Then, the new data is added to the training set, and
the machine learning model is retrained [51]. Figure 4.1 illustrates the AL cycle. First,
a few instances are sampled from the unlabeled data pool; then, they are passed to the
oracle whom/which provides their labels. Using this initial training set, a supervised
machine learning model is trained and used to predict the labels of the unlabeled pool.
Then, according to some criteria, the oracle selects and labels a new set of instances;
this new training set is used to re-train the model and improve its performance. The
AL cycle repeats itself until reaching some convergence criteria [51]. While the new
samples can be selected one at a time in some learning models, this would lead to slight
improvement on each iteration [51]. Pool-based is a more efficient AL scenario, where
all the unlabeled data is observed simultaneously, and several samples are passed to the
oracle at once [51]. Note that the efficiency of AL relies on the data selection scheme;
consequently, a wide variety of algorithms that select informative samples have been

CONCLUSIONS
Based on the assumption that the signals of interest are smooth, Chapter 2
introduced a reconstruction approach that minimizes the high-frequency components
of the reconstructed signal’s spectrum. The proposed method presents two benefits;
first, it does not need the signal’s bandwidth estimations; secondly, since it assumes
only smooth signals, the model can recover approximately bandlimited signals. In the
experimental results, the reconstruction method outperformed the reconstruction used
in [4] and Least Squares Minimization [2], showing a more effective way to recover approximately bandlimited signals. The results also demonstrated that the parameters in
Blueness Minimization Reconstruction can be tuned to control the signals’ smoothness
and recover strictly bandlimited signals. Note that despite avoiding eigendecomposition BMR requires the inverse of the perturbed Laplacian matrix, which makes it
computationally expensive.
The reconstruction error of Blueness Minimization is directly connected to the
theory of blue-noise sampling on graphs initially defined for approximately regular
graphs. Chapter 2 generalized blue-noise sampling extending it to irregular graph
structures by mapping them into approximately regular graphs regularizing the edges’
weight. Then, in the mapped graph, a blue-noise sampling algorithm, VAC, is used
to determine the sampling set. Based on the work on [39] Chapter 2 connected the
regularization parameter with the quality of the sampling pattern. The experimental results showed that the proposed sampling approach achieves lower reconstruction
errors than the original blue-noise sampling on irregular graph structures, confirming
the need to include the distribution of the nodes in the sampling process [39]. Also,
88
the model’s performance is comparable to that of methods that focus on the spectral
characteristics of the graph. However, the mapping adds a linear term to the VAC
algorithm, which already has high computational complexity [39].
To extend the benefits of the methods proposed in Chapter 2 to large graphs,
Chapter 3 takes the advantages of parallel processing for graph signal sampling and
reconstruction using graph partitioning to divide the graph structure. Then, Blueness Minimization Reconstruction is reformulated using graph partitioning. This new
formulation allows solving the reconstruction independently for each subgraph, reducing the recovery complexity by k 2 while adding a log-linear term; also, the approach
reduces the space complexity allowing the parallel process on massive graphs.
The effect of noise in the blueness minimization reconstruction with partitions
is minimized by blue-noise patterns. Therefore, Chapter 3 presents two sampling algorithms using graph partitioning that generate blue-noise sampling patterns on large
graphs. The first method is based on the original VAC algorithm and leads to close to
optimal patterns but has higher computational requirements. The second algorithm
uses Error diffusion and graph partitioning to sample large graphs using a Dot diffusionlike algorithm. The node ordering required by ED is connected to the graph filtering
problem, and different heuristics were described to number the nodes. The proposed
methods reduce the complexity of blue-noise sampling algorithms to a fraction of the
original algorithms; aditionally, they allow parallel processing to reduce the sampling
time. The work in Chapter 3 selected blue-noise-based algorithms due to their spatial
definition, but future research could combine other sampling and reconstruction algorithms with graph partitioning. The experimental result showed the benefits of using
parallel processing on GSP, allowing the recovery of signals on graphs with almost a
million nodes.
Finally, Chapter 4 explores the benefits of blue-noise sampling of graphs outside
the signal processing problem providing a theoretical connection between blue-noise
sampling and Active Learning. Chapter 4 proves that blue-noise patterns on graphs
minimize the expected error of an AL framework since they maximize data exploration.
89
The proposed framework creates a graph structure given the input space and uses an
adaptive form of blue-noise sampling to introduce the model’s feedback into the graph
structure. On each iteration, the weights of the edges are regularized based on the
observation’s uncertainty; then, using a constrained form of VAC, the graph is sampled;
this approach places more samples on regions of high uncertainty while preserving some
separation between samples. The experimental results on synthetic data showed the
potential of a blue-noise framework for active learning.
Future research should continue to explore the benefits of blue-noise sampling
on more complex graph structures such as directed graphs and mixed graphs that
use complex weighted networks to represent the different types of edges. Existent
literature on hyper-graph and directed graph partitioning allows the extension of the
methodologies introduced in Chapter 3 to the complex structures. Finally, adaptive
blue-noise sampling used for AL in Chapter 4 should be extended to graph neural
networks for the node classification tasks to exploit the underlying graph structure of
the problem.
