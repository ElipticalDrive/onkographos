Volume rendering vs. slice-by-slice viewing
To understand why 3D matters, it helps to know what your scan actually is. A CT or MRI scanner does not take "a picture." It samples your body in thin parallel layers — slices — and records a number for every tiny cube of tissue in each layer. Those cubes are called voxels (volume pixels). A single study can hold hundreds of slices and many millions of voxels.
There are two fundamentally different ways to show that data.
Slice-by-slice (the flip-book). You look at one layer at a time and scroll through the stack. This is exactly how a radiologist reads — and it is the correct tool for fine measurement and detailed inspection. But it asks you to mentally stack dozens of cross-sections back into a solid shape. For a trained eye that is second nature. For a patient, a slice through the middle of an organ can be almost impossible to connect to where that organ lives in the body.
Volume rendering (the solid model). Instead of showing one layer, volume rendering uses all the voxels at once. It assigns each tissue density a color and an opacity, then casts simulated light rays through the entire dataset to produce a single image of the whole structure — which you can then rotate in real time. Bone can be made opaque and ivory-colored while soft tissue is made translucent, so the skeleton appears inside a ghost of the body. Nothing is invented: every pixel you see is driven by a measured voxel. It is your scan, shown as the three-dimensional object it always was.
Braid's 3D view is true volume rendering, not a cartoon model or a stitched surface mesh. The renderer reads your reconstructed volume directly and lights it in the browser, so what you rotate is the real density data from your study.

Why seeing it in space helps you understand your scan
A flat slice answers "what is on this one layer." A 3D volume answers a question patients actually have: "where is this, and what is it next to?"
Orientation. When a report says a finding is "in the lower pole of the right kidney" or "at the L4–L5 level," a 3D model lets you actually locate that region on your own anatomy instead of guessing which gray blob on which slice it meant.
Relationships. Anatomy is about neighbors. Is a structure pressing on something? Sitting near a vessel? Volume rendering shows those spatial relationships in a way a single cross-section never can.
Scale and shape. A measurement in millimeters is abstract. Seeing the shape and extent of a structure in three dimensions makes "how big" and "what shape" immediately concrete.
Confidence in your own questions. When you can see your anatomy in space, the questions you bring to your doctor get sharper — and sharper questions get better answers.

It runs from your full study, not a single image
The 3D view is only possible because of how Braid handles your data in the first place. Volume rendering needs the whole study: the complete set of slices in a series — because the volume is literally built from all of them. You cannot reconstruct a volume from one screenshot or a single exported JPEG. The geometry that says how far apart the slices are, how they are oriented, and what each voxel measures lives in the DICOM data.
That is exactly what Braid imports. When you drag and drop your DICOM files — a whole CD, a USB stick, or a folder of hundreds of images — Braid reads the full multi-series study, not a handful of cherry-picked pictures. The same complete import that powers the AI analysis is what makes the 3D reconstruction possible. Tools that only accept a flat image or ask you to pick "a few representative slices" structurally cannot build a real volume, because the data needed to do it was thrown away before upload.
So the workflow is simple: import your study once, and you get both the AI's plain-language analysis and an interactive 3D view of the same anatomy, from the same files.
What you can do with the 3D view
Once your volume is rendered, it is interactive:
Rotate the model freely to see it from any angle.
Zoom in to look closely at a region of interest, or pull back for the whole picture.
Adjust how tissues appear so bone, soft tissue, or other densities stand out — the same idea as a radiologist's "windowing," applied to the whole volume.
Move between 3D and the slices. The volume is for spatial understanding; when you want to inspect a specific layer in detail, you can still scroll the individual images. The two views describe the same study from two angles.

Frequently asked questions
Is this real 3D, or just a rotating picture? It is real volume rendering. Braid reconstructs a 3D volume from every slice in your study and lights it in the browser, so what you rotate is your actual measured density data — not a pre-rendered animation or a generic anatomical model.
What scans work best in 3D? Cross-sectional studies that capture a true stack of slices — CT and MRI — work best, because the volume is built from that stack. Studies that are a single flat projection, like a standard chest X-ray, do not contain a volume to reconstruct.
Do I need to install anything? No. The 3D view runs entirely in your web browser. There are no downloads, no plugins, and no specialized workstation software.
Why can't I make a 3D model from a JPEG or a screenshot of my scan? Because a single image has thrown away the depth information. A 3D volume requires the full set of slices and the DICOM geometry that records how they fit together in space. That is why Braid imports your complete study rather than a single picture.
Is my data private when I view it in 3D? Yes. The same privacy model applies to the 3D view as to the rest of Ask AI: your imaging is de-identified on your device before it is transmitted, and personal identifiers are not exposed. You can read the details on our data protection page.
Can the 3D view diagnose me? No. The 3D view is a visualization and exploration tool to help you understand your anatomy. It is not a diagnosis. For a clinical interpretation, request a second opinion from a board-certified radiologist.
Can I view two studies in 3D to compare them? Braid supports importing more than one study. To compare findings across exams from different dates, see Compare CT Scans Over Time, which walks through multi-study analysis.
Does the 3D view work on my phone? The Braid web experience runs in modern mobile and desktop browsers. A larger screen makes rotating and inspecting a detailed volume more comfortable, but the import and analysis work across devices.
