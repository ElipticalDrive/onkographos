Language models’ abilities to interpret tasks and generate programs offer a promising basis for 3D
spatial reasoning. Yet following spatial instructions in 3D scenes requires programs to construct
geometric references that language leaves implicit. In this work, we introduce VOX P ROG, which
formulates 3D object placement through voxel programming: a language model translates task interpretations into executable programs over local voxel views of scene surfaces. Because these views
index surface geometry on regular grids with explicit coordinates and neighborhood relations, an
evidence program can isolate task-relevant regions and boundaries directly from scene meshes, and
a downstream pose program can measure the resulting geometry to compute a target SE(3) pose.
Visual and numerical feedback from execution lets the model inspect intermediate selections and
candidate placements to iteratively revise its code. To evaluate open-ended placement across functional use, geometric fit, and contextual arrangements, we introduce the 71-task VOX P ROG-Bench
benchmark along with an evaluation suite pairing perceptual Intent Alignment and 3D Physical
Validity. Across VOX P ROG-Bench and external benchmarks, VOX P ROG consistently outperforms
relation-based, keypoint-based, and mesh-based baselines. Code will be released.

Introduction
Natural-language spatial instructions often specify goals whose geometric requirements must be inferred
from the scene (Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024). Placing a bottle inside an open
display holder requires identifying the holder’s receiving structure and determining how the bottle should
be positioned and oriented to fit within it. Similarly, extending a row of cups requires inferring the direction
and spacing of the existing arrangement to derive the next placement target. Here, the geometric references
that define the goal are themselves outcomes of spatial computation over the 3D scene.
Existing approaches are limited by both the task requirements their interfaces can express and the geometric
computations their representations support. Predefined task interfaces couple task coverage to task-specific
implementations, limiting generalization to new spatial requirements (Gao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023b;
Ichter et al., 2023). Geometric representations present a different difficulty: sparse keypoints can leave
extended contact regions and local surface shapes under-specified (Huang et al., 2025b; Hua et al., 2025;
Manuelli et al., 2022; Sundaresan et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2024), while raw meshes require programs
to organize irregular surface elements into the regions and references a task needs. Open-ended spatial
reasoning therefore requires both programmatic computation that adapts to free-form instructions and a
structured 3D surface representation that exposes scene geometry directly to code.
To this end, we formulate voxel programming, in which a language model expresses its understanding of a
spatial task as a program that constructs and operates on local voxel views (Fig. 1). By discretizing object
surfaces onto regular grids at task-appropriate resolutions, local voxel views expose spatial coordinates
and neighborhood relations directly as array operations. Generated programs can index these grids to
isolate contact regions, extract boundaries, and measure local extent and orientation, turning a uniform grid
representation into task-specific geometric references for 6-DoF pose computation.

and execution feedback. Starting from the instruction and raycast visual anchors, the model first writes an

We evaluate VOX P ROG on VOX P ROG-Bench, a new benchmark of 71 open-ended placement tasks where
target poses depend on part-level affordances, 3D shape compatibility, and surrounding layouts. Because
valid placements must satisfy both semantic intent and physical plausibility, our evaluation suite pairs a

tasks where object contours govern fit and clearance. Ablation studies further show that constructing explicit geometric evidence before pose synthesis improves both physical validity and intent alignment over
direct mesh programming, while local voxel views further improve intent alignment across all 71 tasks.

Related Work
Spatial Task Specification. Language-guided placement has been studied through layout prediction, rearrangement search, and geometric optimization. Scene graphs encode inter-object relations for scene
synthesis (Chen et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2023; Lin & Mu, 2024; Hu et al., 2024b), while scene-layout
and object-placement methods map language to spatial configurations (Feng et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024;
Öcal et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025a; Abdelreheem et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Kuang
et al., 2026). Rearrangement methods coordinate multi-object structures using learned spatial priors (Liu
et al., 2022; 2023; Tang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024) or pattern-guided search (Chang
et al., 2024). At a finer scale, object manipulation methods formulate 6-DoF pose optimization over sparse
2
semantic keypoints, directional axes, and part-level affordances (Manuelli et al., 2022; Hua et al., 2025;
Huang et al., 2025b; Qi et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025).
While layout, surface-constraint, and keypoint formulations parameterize placement over bounding boxes,
planar support patches, or sparse point and axis primitives, open-ended tasks often hinge on extended surface structures (such as receiving slots, angled ledges, curved rims, or recessed compartments) whose relevant regions depend on the instruction and paired objects. VOX P ROG therefore treats geometric reference
construction as an explicit, programmable stage of task specification: generated code composes spatial selections and neighborhood operations over local voxel views, retaining both the selected voxel regions and
their clipped source-mesh patches for downstream pose measurement so that the same 3D object geometry
can be segmented and parameterized differently across placement tasks.
Language Models for Spatial Computation. Executable programs let language models delegate quantitative calculation and visual queries to external interpreters (Gao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Gupta
& Kembhavi, 2023; Surís et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024a). In robotics, generated code sequences task
plans (Singh et al., 2023), composes perception calls with control primitives (Liang et al., 2023; Mu et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2024), and synthesizes 3D value maps over workspace grids for motion planning (Huang
et al., 2023a). Recent methods further incorporate geometric processing via part-mask refinement (Tang
et al., 2026), primitive fitting (Lin et al., 2026), and frame-grounded tool calls (Chen et al., 2026).
In most programmatic frameworks, however, 3D perception remains encapsulated behind fixed API calls
that return object centroids, bounding boxes, or pre-segmented masks (Liang et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2024),
or operates on coarse workspace grids to score trajectories (Huang et al., 2023a). We develop voxel programming so that generated code can inspect and dissect 3D object surfaces directly at the part level.
By discretizing candidate objects into local voxel views at task-chosen resolutions, VOX P ROG turns 3D
surface inspection into regular-grid array programming while retaining exact correspondence with the underlying mesh triangles. Generated code can thus construct custom surface witnesses on demand, verify
them through visual and numerical execution feedback, and measure both discrete grid bounds and clipped
source-mesh patches to solve for precise, physically valid 6-DoF object placements.

Rather than mapping language directly to 6-DoF coordinates in a single step, VOX P ROG decouples geometric reference construction from pose calculation: an evidence program Pe isolates task-relevant surface
regions on the moved and reference objects, and a pose program Pp measures these constructed surface
regions and their relational constraints to solve for the moved object’s final rigid transformation.
Fig. 2 illustrates this pipeline on an upright plate-placement task. The model first organizes the required
spatial references into a directed evidence graph and grounds visible cues into 3D surface anchors (§3.1).
Operating on local voxel views of scene meshes (§3.2), the evidence program constructs the rack’s adjacent
dividers, lower support rails, and slot region while refining its selections through visual and geometric
selection checks and program revision (§3.3). The verified evidence record and relational claims are then
passed to the pose program, which queries both discrete voxel bounds and clipped source-mesh patches to
align, center, and seat the plate upright within the target receiving slot (§3.4).
3.1
Task Interpretation and Visual Grounding
An LLM formalizes its understanding of the instruction as a directed evidence graph G = (V, E). Each
node v ∈ V specifies either a visible semantic anchor or a derived geometric region to construct, along with
its functional role in the task; each directed edge (u, v) ∈ E indicates that constructing v depends on the
location or geometry of u. In Fig. 2, visual cues on the plate and dish rack initiate a dependency chain that
extracts the plate’s outer rim alongside the rack’s adjacent dividers, support rails, and slot region.
Each node in G is routed according to how its geometry can be acquired. For a directly visible semantic
cue, Locate queries a VLM for a 2D pixel coordinate in the rendered scene image and casts a camera ray
against the scene mesh to recover a 3D surface point as a spatial anchor in A. References that cannot be
reduced to a single clickable pixel, such as an interior slot region, a recessed support ledge, or an occluded
underside, are passed to the evidence program together with their descriptions and upstream 3D anchors,
which localize where on the object mesh the program should inspect and slice the surface.

3.2
Voxel Programming
Generated programs operate on scene geometry through local voxel views. Given a source mesh s and voxel
size h > 0, U = VoxelView(s, h) discretizes the surface of s onto a regular 3D grid at resolution h. On
this grid, the view exposes occupied surface cells as integer coordinate arrays (N, 3) alongside directional
exposure masks and connected-component queries, while a rigid grid-to-world transform maps selected
voxel indices and local bounds back to continuous 3D coordinates for downstream geometric computation.
Each constructed geometric reference forms a witness in the evidence record R. To construct a witness v,
the evidence program instantiates a local view Uv on source geometry sv at resolution hv and evaluates a
programmatic binary mask mv over its surface cells to obtain the selected surface cells Wv :
Wv = {c ∈ Uv : mv (c) = 1}.
Uv = VoxelView(sv , hv ),
(1)
Because Uv is a regular grid, the program can compose mv through standard array operations (axis-aligned
slicing, proximity filters around visual anchors or upstream witnesses, directional exposure queries, and
connected-component labeling) to isolate witnesses such as the rack’s adjacent dividers D, E, lower support
rails S, and combined slot region B in Fig. 2. For each witness v, the entry R.v in R stores these selected
cells Wv , their parent view Uv , the source object sv , and the provenance edges from G.
Parameterizing the voxel size hv per view allows the evidence program to match discretization scale to local
geometric structure without voxelizing the entire 3D scene at a uniform fine resolution. A program can use
coarse grids to localize broad object surfaces and switch to a finer resolution hv when resolving thin wire
dividers, narrow receiving slots, or shallow rims that would merge on a coarse grid, while retaining exact
geometric correspondence with the underlying triangles of the source object mesh.

To tie each witness to its functional role before writing code, the model first states explicit geometric
inclusion and exclusion criteria for each target witness (such as excluding adjacent rack loops or vertical
side walls). When a structural question remains unresolved, the model can execute lightweight probes via
the same voxel API to print numerical statistics (such as voxel counts, component counts, and coordinate
spans) and inspect rendered diagnostic views of intermediate voxel selections.
Once a complete candidate Pe is executed, geometric checks and a VLM critic inspect each constructed
witness for part identity, region completeness, and the absence of unwanted geometry (Fig. 2, center).
When a candidate selection leaks into adjacent structure or remains incomplete, this feedback guides the
model to revise its programmatic selection rules or adjust the voxel resolution hv and re-run Pe .

4
Experiments
We evaluate VOX P ROG on VOX P ROG-Bench against relation-, keypoint-, and mesh-based baselines (§4.1–
§4.2), ablate staged reference construction and local voxel views alongside external tasks from RoboCasa
and Open6DOR v2 (§4.3), and analyze physical and perceptual failure modes (§4.4).
4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmark. We evaluate on VOX P ROG-Bench, a benchmark of 71 individually curated language-guided
3D placement tasks. Each case presents an initial tabletop scene and a free-form instruction specifying the
desired placement of an object. We emphasize diverse instructions that call for fine-grained reasoning about
functional object parts, geometric fit, and contextual arrangements. Although an instruction can involve
multiple spatial cues, we assign each case to one category according to its primary spatial requirement:
• Functional (28 cases): The placement must support a specific use, such as placing a cup beneath a
grinder chute to catch grounds, returning headphones to a stand, or orienting a funnel for pouring.
• Geometric (14 cases): The placement depends directly on object contours, such as nesting an oval bowl
inside a matching container or fitting a baking tray into a narrow box at an angle.
• Contextual (29 cases): The placement takes its spatial cues from the surrounding multi-object arrangement, such as completing a missing place setting or restoring a symmetric tabletop layout.
Scenes are assembled from generated 3D assets through randomized placement and manual refinement.
Detailed instructions, object geometry, and scene context make the intended arrangement well defined, with
one manually annotated ground-truth 6-DoF pose per task. All methods receive the scene meshes, initial
poses, instruction, and target-object identity; ground-truth poses and renderings serve only for evaluation.

Evaluation Suite. We design an evaluation suite around two complementary requirements of object placement: realizing the intended spatial arrangement and maintaining physical plausibility. We evaluate Intent
Alignment via visual comparison with ground truth and Physical Validity via 3D geometric checks.
Intent Alignment measures perceptual similarity using DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023) between full-scene renderings of the predicted and ground-truth placements under matched cameras and lighting. Within each
case, we rank the M compared methods by increasing DreamSim distance and assign rank r the score
100(M − r)/(M − 1), using average ranks for ties. Averaging over cases gives the IA Rank Score (0–100;
higher is better), which measures how consistently a method outranks its competitors across tasks. Each
task contributes equally; scores are compared within the same method pool.
Physical Validity assesses whether the final placement establishes plausible static support and avoids interpenetration with surrounding scene objects. We report the percentage of evaluated task placements whose
final 3D meshes satisfy both static support-contact and collision-clearance checks.
Baselines. We compare VOX P ROG against three language-guided placement approaches. G EN M ANIP
(Gao et al., 2025) constructs placements using predefined spatial relations between target and anchor objects. G EN S IM 2- K PAM (Hua et al., 2025; Manuelli et al., 2022) specifies placement targets through predicted semantic keypoints together with geometric point and axis constraints. D IRECT M ESH generates
Blender Python code for placement and executes it through Blender-MCP1 .
Model Configuration. All compared methods use GPT-5.6-Sol under a shared inference configuration for
both language-guided code generation and visual grounding across all evaluated tasks.
4.2
Results on the Task Suite
Quantitative Results. VOX P ROG achieves the highest IA Rank Score overall (83.10) and across all three
task groups, alongside the highest Physical Validity at 95.8% (Table 1), outperforming D IRECT M ESH
(48.59 / 87.3%), G EN S IM 2- K PAM (42.72 / 77.5%), and G EN M ANIP (25.59 / 73.2%). Although D IRECT
M ESH also writes task-specific Python code over explicit scene meshes, VOX P ROG improves its IA Rank
Score by 34.51 points and Physical Validity by 8.5 points: code generation alone remains brittle without an
intermediate representation that isolates the surfaces relevant to the instruction.
Breaking down performance by category reveals how different representations behave under distinct spatial
demands. On Functional tasks, VOX P ROG reaches 86.90 versus 53.57 for D IRECT M ESH and 34.52 for
G EN S IM 2- K PAM (with Physical Validity rising from 75.0% and 78.6% to 92.9%), as functional use requires isolating mating parts, such as a chute outlet or headphone arch, rather than whole-object bounding
boxes or sparse keypoints. On Contextual tasks, the strongest baseline shifts from D IRECT M ESH (45.98)
to G EN S IM 2- K PAM (54.02), while VOX P ROG retains a clear lead at 77.01. The largest margin occurs on
Geometric tasks: VOX P ROG reaches an IA Rank Score of 88.10 versus 44.05 for D IRECT M ESH, although
both attain 100.0% Physical Validity. The difference lies in resolving the local contours and clearances that
distinguish the intended fit from other physically valid configurations.
Qualitative Results. Fig. 3 exposes the gap between locating an object and identifying its functional contact region. In the headphone case (b), all three baselines bring the earcups against the stand or wrap the
headphones around its post, missing the inner headband support specified by the instruction. VOX P ROG
matches the inner arch to the curved crown, establishing the intended part-level contact.

4.3
Ablation and External Evaluation
Effect of Voxel Programming. To separate the effect of staged reference construction from that of local
voxel views, we compare D IRECT M ESH, M ESH E VIDENCE, and VOX P ROG across all 71 tasks in Table 2.
D IRECT M ESH writes a single placement program directly over the scene meshes; M ESH E VIDENCE shares
VOX P ROG’s two-stage pipeline and execution feedback but constructs witness regions through direct mesh
operations on vertices and faces; VOX P ROG constructs witnesses on local voxel views while retaining
access to the clipped source-mesh surfaces during pose computation.
Within this three-method comparison, moving from single-stage D IRECT M ESH to the staged M ESH E VI DENCE pipeline raises Physical Validity from 87.3% to 95.8% and more than doubles the IA Rank Score
from 26.76 to 56.34. When a single program must simultaneously locate task-relevant surface geometry
and compute 6-DoF coordinates over raw scene meshes, imprecise region selection directly degrades the
resulting placement pose; introducing an explicit evidence stage allows candidate surface regions to be
constructed, verified, and revised through execution feedback before downstream pose synthesis.

Visually Ambiguous Contact. The pot-lid case in Fig. 7b
illustrates why 2D visual inspection alone cannot establish
physical validity in 3D object placement. From the primary
camera view, the lid appears properly seated: it stands upright with its handle facing outward, its circular contour centered between the side supports, and its lower rim aligned
over the receiving rack. A side view of the same final pose
reveals, however, that the lid remains suspended above the
rack, separated by a clear vertical gap from both the base
tray and the inclined backrest. Here, the rack’s opaque front
frame provides a strong visual cue to the destination while
occluding the recessed region where support contact should
occur. Because 2D projection overlap along the viewing ray
can conceal 3D separation under self-occlusion, a singleview visual comparison can accept a physically unsupported
placement even when the target object and its apparent relation to the reference structure look aligned in image space.
Pairing perceptual evaluation with 3D geometric verification
addresses this ambiguity by checking local 3D support contact and interpenetration conditions between the placed object and surrounding scene meshes, making the distinction
between apparent 2D seating in the rendered camera view
and actual physical support in the 3D scene explicit.

Conclusion
We presented VOX P ROG, a framework for 3D spatial reasoning that formulates language-guided object
placement through voxel programming. Rather than parameterizing tasks over fixed spatial relations or
sparse keypoint primitives, VOX P ROG treats the geometric references required by an instruction as intermediate surface structures constructed by executable code. By coupling regular-grid selection over local voxel
views with exact surface normals and patches from the underlying meshes, our two-stage pipeline enables
a language model to isolate functional contact regions, verify them through visual and geometric feedback,
and solve for physically valid SE(3) poses. Across the 71-task VOX P ROG-Bench benchmark and external
tasks from RoboCasa and Open6DOR v2, VOX P ROG consistently outperforms relation-based, keypointbased, and mesh-based baselines in both intent alignment and physical validity. Coupling such programmable surface witnesses with downstream motion generation provides a natural direction for extending
fine-grained geometric reasoning from target placement specification to full manipulation execution.
