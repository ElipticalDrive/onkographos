SPANDY-A MONTE CARLO PROGRAM FOR
GAS TARGET SCATTERING GEOMETRY
by
Nelson Jarmie, J. H. Jett,
A. C. Niethammer
ABSTRACT
A Monte Carlo computer program is presented that simulates a two-slit
gas target scattering geometry. The program is useful in estimating effects
due to finite geometry and multiple scattering in the target foil. Details of
the program are presented and experience with a specific exampis is discus-
INTRODUCTION
A gas target with two slits is used to define the
scattering geometry in many experiments in nuclear
and particle physics. The yield of the scattering experiment depends on the "geometry factor" G, which
is basically a function of the slit sizes and distances.
This factor has been calculated for a variety of approximations, including finite slit sizes, finite beam,
and a divergent beam.1*' The formulas depend on
the detailed shape of the scattering cross section. In
the usual case, calculations are made to second
order and involve the angular slope a' (8) = da/d6
and curvature a"(9) = d'a/dP of the differential
cross section.
Recently, the role of multiple scattering of particles in the exit foil of the gas target has become
potentially important.' Where there is a large
angular curvature in the nuclear cross section, the
particles scattered out of the detection line may not
be compensated for by those scattering in.'* Essentially, the multiple scattering in the foil contributes
to the angular width of the detection system, but the
effect is difficult to calculate analytically or
numerically because of the complicated geometry.
We have written a Monte Carlo type of program
(see Appendix A) to estimate this effect. The
program may also be used to compute detection
geometries not covered by Refs. 1-5 and to check the
accuracy of their formulas. Formulas for the multiple scattering corrections in thin solid targets are
known'10 and are compared with our results. In
many cases, including the experiment" that inspired this work, the solid target formula gives a sufficiently accurate correction for the gas target
geometry.
METHOD
The program simulates the progressive threedimensional history of a scattering experiment (see
Pig. 1). The position and divergence of a single beam
particle are specified by four random numbers; the
position of the nuclear scattering point along the
beam is specified by a fifth random number. A polar
and an azimuthal scattering direction are calculated
using an assumed angular distribution together with
random numbers six and seven. A Gaussian multiple scattering distribution is assumed at the target

The above sequence is run many times until the

The history of each particle is recorded—scattering

resulting spectra are produced. This procedure is
followed for all particles as well as for just the
detected particles. The spread of energy from the
different pathlengths is also recorded.
RESTRICTIONS
The program has few restrictions. At present, the
program assumes a parallel beam. The energy
calculation assumes a simple elastic scattering.
PROGRAM STRUCTURE
Frequent comment cards in the listing (Appendix
A) indicate the details of the calculation. The
program consists of a main program SPANDY and
four subprograms EVALF, SINP, BINTAB, and
SIMPSON.
SPANDY has three sections: (1) preliminary, (2)
main loop, and (3) output. The preliminary section
reads the input information and calculates various
constants to be used in the main loop. In particular,
it computes a cumulative distribution function
(CDF) from the nuclear scattering angular distribution supplied by the user in EVALF. The CDF is
used with a random number to choose a scattering
direction. The main loop makes the calculation
described in METHOD. It is written as efficiently as
possible because most of the computing time is
spent there. The output computes certain final
averages and prints and plots the results.
EVALF contains the analytic form of the angular
distribution of the nuclear scattering.
SINP is used to print the input data.
BINTAB tabulates the particle histories.
SIMPSN is a Simpson integration routine called
by SPANDY in the calculation of the CDF.

OUTPUT
All input information is printed (see Appendix C).
Some diagnostic information may be printed,
depending on the branching in the program. If called
for, the distribution history tables are printed (Appendix D). The final results are printed (see example in Appendix E). Also printed are the mean
angles if the successfully detected particles. The
last random number is given to permit a continuation, if desired.
PHILOSOPHY OF PROGRAM OPERATION
As with all Monte Carlo programs, there is a
trade-off between running time and accuracy. The
acceptance ranges in Cards 4 and 5 must be large
enough to insure that all possible detected events
are physically allowed. However, as the ranges get
larger, there will be more "wasted" trials and more
inefficient running. Proper acceptances must be
chosen by trial and error—the distribution tables
are helpful here. The tabulation should then be
turned off for long statistics runs because it slows
the main loop considerably.

FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS
The following improvements could be made in the
code:
1. Allowance for a divergent beam.
2. Significant reduction in running time.
Sophisticated deprogramming in machine or
assembly languages has not been attempted.
3. Installation of a more general, yet accurate,
way of choosing the angular form of the scattering distribution.
