Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Scene Reconstruction:
Methods, Systems, and Trade-offs
Ashim Dahal, Rabab Abdelfattah, Nick Rahimi
arXiv:2610.17513v1 [cs.CV] 13 Oct 2026
School of Computing and Computer Engineering, University of Southern
Mississippi, Hattiesburg, MS, 39401, USA
Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) allows for high-quality real-time novel-view synthesis.
Since its inception in 2023, its explicit and highly configurable scene representation
has been extended toward sparse-view generation, dynamic reconstruction, efficiency
and compression, streaming, and simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM). Although Gaussian Splatting can provide real-time rendering, rendering an existing representation is distinct from constructing, updating, or maintaining the scene itself.
In this survey, we synthesize current literature, categorize them and provide a connection of current approaches and tools towards an end-to-end pipeline. Explicitly,
we categorize GS scene reconstruction into five overlapping families: single-shot generation, dynamic representations, efficiency and compression, streaming and immersive systems, and Gaussian Splatting SLAM. We distinguish render-only throughput
from task-specific scene generation, update, tracking, mapping, and optimization costs,
and combine controlled sparse-view benchmarking with native evaluations to identify
cross-family quality, latency, memory, and update-cost trade-offs.
Keywords: Gaussian Splatting, 3D Scene Representation, Novel View Synthesis,
Neural Rendering, Multi-View Geometry
1. Introduction
Kerbl et al. [1] introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as an explicit scene representation capable of high-quality interactive novel-view rendering. Subsequent work
rapidly extended this formulation beyond static per-scene reconstruction. Deformable
3DGS [2] and 4DGaussians [3] introduced temporal deformation and spatiotemporal representations, while later methods explored feed-forward sparse-view generation [4, 5], efficiency and compression, streaming, and Gaussian-based SLAM. These
developments increasingly shift the systems bottleneck from rendering an existing representation toward constructing, updating, transmitting, or maintaining the Gaussian
scene. This distinction is important for applications such as free-viewpoint video, immersive media, online mapping, and robotics. In these settings, interactive rendering
alone is insufficient when scene construction, temporal updates, or map maintenance
remain substantially slower than rasterization.

2. Preliminaries
Figure 1 summarizes the end-to-end Gaussian Splatting reconstruction pipeline and
where our five taxonomy families intervene. This section fixes terminology and notation, summarizes the core 3DGS representation and rasterization/compositing used for
real-time rendering, and defines the training regimes, input/supervision assumptions,
and the real-time criterion used throughout. We conclude with a compact tag schema
used to structure the taxonomy and method comparisons.
2.1. Scope and Terms
We use Gaussian Splatting to refer to modern 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1]:
an explicit set of 3D Gaussian primitives parameterized in world space and rendered
by fast (typically differentiable) screen-space splatting with rasterization/compositing.
Under this scope, the primary object is a Gaussian scene representation (centers, covariances, opacities, and appearance parameters), and the key efficiency advantage
comes from rasterizing a finite set of primitives rather than volumetric ray marching. We do not survey NeRFs [11] as a primary topic. We cite NeRF-related work
only when necessary for context (e.g., datasets/metrics) or when a method explicitly
transfers radiance-field ideas into Gaussian representations.
Finally, we use real-time scene reconstruction in a task-specific sense rather than
through a universal FPS threshold. Rendering, scene generation, dynamic or streaming
updates, and SLAM are evaluated using their corresponding units of work and timing
boundaries, as defined in Section 2.7.

2.3. Gaussian Representation
Gaussian Splatting represents a scene as G = {g j }Nj=1 , a set of N anisotropic 3D
Gaussian primitives in world coordinates. Each g j is defined by a mean µ j ∈ R3 , covariance Σ j ∈ R3×3 (commonly parameterized via rotation and per-axis scales for stability), opacity α j ∈ [0, 1], and appearance parameters c j . This explicit primitive-based
representation enables high-throughput rasterization pipelines compared to radiancefield rendering [1].
Methods primarily differ in (i) which parameters are optimized vs. predicted, and
(ii) how time is modeled. Static pipelines often optimize all parameters per scene
with SfM/MVS/depth-based initialization; dynamic extensions introduce time dependence via explicit per-frame parameters, deformation models, or static/dynamic partitions. Online/SLAM-style systems additionally constrain updates to bounded latency
and control representation growth via pruning, keyframes, and local refinement.
Representation complexity and real-time knobs. Runtime and memory are dominated
by the number of primitives N and the appearance bandwidth (e.g., SH degree or feature
dimensionality). Dynamic/streaming settings add growth over time, so many systems
explicitly regulate N and appearance parameters to meet real-time targets.
Representation vs. serialization (file formats). Gaussian scenes may be stored in different encodings (e.g., PLY-like formats or .splat-style binaries). These typically
change serialization/layout and attribute encoding (e.g., quantization, SH/features) rather
than the underlying representation {(µ j , Σ j , α j , c j )}Nj=1 .

2.5. Training
We group common regimes by when optimization/learning happens: per-scene/persequence optimization (offline fitting), amortized feed-forward generation via pretrained models, and dynamic/online settings where temporal modeling and incremental updates impose bounded-latency constraints. Per-scene optimization fits {(µ j , Σ j , α j , c j )}
for a specific sequence [1]; amortized methods predict Gaussians at inference time (optionally followed by brief test-time refinement) [17, 5, 4, 18]. Dynamic methods often
introduce time-dependent parameterizations or deformations and are frequently optimized per sequence [3, 19, 2], while online/SLAM pipelines prioritize training-free or
pretrained components at runtime with efficient map maintenance.
2.6. Inputs and Supervision
We categorize input modalities as multi-view captures, monocular video, and
RGB-D or sensor-augmented streams (e.g., events). Pose assumptions range from
known (calibration/SfM) to estimated/refined (tracked or jointly optimized) to posefree. Supervision is often self-supervised via photometric consistency, but many pipelines
incorporate pretrained priors (especially depth/MVS) to stabilize geometry and accelerate initialization.

3. Methodology
We survey Gaussian Splatting (GS) research toward real-time scene reconstruction
using the task-specific definitions in Section 2.7. Our workflow is: (i) collect papers broadly, (ii) apply inclusion criteria aligned with the GS reconstruction pipeline,
(iii) assign a pipeline-centric taxonomy with consistent tags, and (iv) interpret reported
efficiency using end-to-end-aware conventions.
3.1. Literature Collection and Inclusion Criteria
We prioritize recent work (2023–2025) while retaining foundational baselines needed
for context (datasets, metrics, and core dynamic/NVS references). Literature was identified primarily through Google Scholar and the proceedings of CVPR, ICCV, ECCV,
NeurIPS, and ICLR. Seed queries included Gaussian splatting, Gaussian splatting
for SLAM, efficient Gaussian splatting, fast Gaussian splatting, sparse-view Gaussian
splatting, and zero-shot Gaussian splatting, together with closely related variants for
dynamic reconstruction, streaming, compression, pose-free reconstruction, and realtime reconstruction. Reference lists of relevant papers were also inspected to identify
related and foundational work (backward citation snowballing). We include methods whose primary scene representation is an explicit Gaussian-based model (or a
direct extension) and that materially affect one or more stages of a GS reconstruction pipeline (initialization/generation, temporal modeling, map maintenance, streaming/delivery, compositing, or tracking-and-mapping). We exclude NeRF-style methods
as a primary focus, citing them only when necessary for context or when they explicitly bridge into Gaussian representations. Duplicate versions and works outside the
surveyed reconstruction/system scope are excluded; dataset, metric, and foundational
papers are retained as supporting references rather than surveyed GS methods. Because
the literature search evolved iteratively during preparation of the survey, intermediate
retrieval and screening counts were not recorded prospectively and are therefore not
reconstructed retrospectively.
3.2. Tagging and Taxonomy Assignment
Each paper is annotated using the master tag schema in Table 3 (input/pose/depth
cues, regime, temporal scope, runtime setting, and efficiency levers). We assign each
method to one primary family based on its dominant contribution, while noting meaningful overlaps when a method intervenes in multiple pipeline stages. The five families
in Fig. 2 are constructed to reflect where methods intervene operationally in the endto-end pipeline (Fig. 1), enabling consistent comparison across tables and sections.
3.3. Interpreting Performance Claims
To avoid conflating rendering speed with reconstruction throughput, runtime evidence is interpreted according to the task being measured rather than through a universal FPS or SPS quantity. Render-only results, scene generation, dynamic or streaming
updates, SLAM, and offline optimization follow the timing boundaries defined in Section 2.7. Results are treated as directly comparable only when the dataset, input protocol, resolution, hardware, unit of work, and timing boundary are sufficiently aligned;

4. Datasets and Evaluation Protocols
This section summarizes the datasets, quality metrics, and evaluation protocols
used throughout the survey. Runtime comparisons follow the task-specific definitions
introduced in Section 2.7.
4.1. Datasets and Quality Metrics
Datasets (representative regimes). Although datasets are not the main focus of this
survey, they strongly influence what claims are comparable. Across the literature,
benchmarks cluster into five recurring regimes. (i) Multi-view dome/studio captures provide calibrated multi-camera observations and controlled dynamics, commonly used for high-fidelity dynamic reconstruction (e.g., Technicolor Light Field
[20], Neu3DV [21], Replay [22], NeRSemble [23], PKU-DyMVHumans [24], DiVa360 [25]). (ii) Monocular/handheld sequences emphasize pose uncertainty, motion
blur, and in-the-wild dynamics, often inherited from dynamic NeRF benchmarks (e.g.,
NSFF Dynamic [26], D-NeRF [27], Nerfies [28], HyperNeRF [29]) and more recent
GS/video-generation settings (e.g., Splat4D [30]). (iii) Event+RGB settings target fast
motion and low-light where conventional frames degrade, using event streams as additional supervision or cues (e.g., Dynamic EventNeRF [31], E-4DGS [19]). (iv) Outdoor/driving/drone captures evaluate scalability and SLAM-like conditions, often
with LiDAR/SLAM pose supervision and dynamic agents (e.g., MTGS [32], DroneSplat [33], Wide-Range-4D [34]). (v) Synthetic/physics-based datasets provide controllable dynamics and/or ground-truth structure for stress-testing temporal modeling

4.2. Task-Specific Runtime Metrics
Runtime is reported according to the task being measured. Render FPS (rFPS)
refers only to rendering an existing Gaussian representation. Feed-forward methods
use scene throughput, dynamic and streaming methods use update throughput, and
SLAM methods use tracking or mapping throughput. Offline optimization is reported
using training or optimization time. These quantities are compared only when they
measure the same task under the same timing boundary.
4.3. Evaluation Protocol
Quantitative comparisons require aligned datasets, input settings, resolution, hardware, and timing boundaries. When these conditions are not aligned, results are treated
as method-specific evidence rather than direct comparisons. We therefore report both
quality and runtime together with the experimental conditions under which they were
measured.

Single-shot / amortized Gaussian generation. Feed-forward construction of a usable
Gaussian scene from one or few images, often using depth/MVS priors or learned
regularizers, optionally followed by brief refinement. Poses may be known, refined,
or pose-free, but the defining feature is replacing heavy per-scene iterative fitting with
amortized inference from limited views.
Dynamic representations beyond 4DGS. Methods that treat time as a first-class variable in the scene model (e.g., time-conditioned parameters, deformation/warp fields,
key-time primitives, canonical-plus-delta factorization) or explicitly mitigate dynamic
failure modes such as ghosting, identity swaps, occlusion/disocclusion artifacts, and
temporal flicker. The emphasis is modeling temporal change, not merely delivering or
replaying content over time.
Efficiency and compression. Works whose central goal is reducing compute, memory, or storage/bandwidth while preserving real-time operation, via levers such as pruning/sparsification, quantization/packing, view-adaptive LoD selection, or system/hardware
acceleration. We place a method here when efficiency is the primary objective rather
than a side effect.

6.6. Cross-Family Trade-offs
Across the five families, the main distinction is not the Gaussian renderer but which
term dominates the task latency. Using the notation of section 2.7, a cross-family
decomposition can be written as
tT = tgeom + tpose + tdeform/update + trefine + trender ,
(25)
where only task-relevant terms are active. In single-shot reconstruction, tgeom dominates: table 5 shows that decoder-only rFPS is much larger than scene throughput
because Gaussian construction is more expensive than rasterization. Dynamic methods
such as 4DGaussians [3] and Deformable-3D-Gaussians [2] shift cost toward temporal deformation and per-sequence optimization. Streaming systems expose the same
separation more strongly: in table 10, IGS [61] and 3DGStream [62] render an updated
representation quickly, while the transition itself remains orders of magnitude slower.
Gaussian SLAM adds tpose and map maintenance to this loop, making tracking and
mapping, rather than rasterization, the dominant full-system cost in table 11.
Efficiency methods operate on a different axis. For B(G) ≈ Nbg , pruning reduces
N, quantization reduces bg , and LoD reduces the view-dependent active set Nvis , but
none of these implies a proportional decrease in tT . The shared baseline in table 9
makes this explicit: PUP 3D-GS [57] and Compact3D [58] reduce serialized size by
approximately 90% and 92.66%, respectively, while the pruned PUP model does not
render faster than the vanilla baseline in table 8. Memory is therefore an independent
deployment constraint rather than a proxy for speed; the failed MotionGS [52] execution on an 80 GB A100 provides the complementary case where optimization becomes
infeasible despite the efficiency of Gaussian rasterization. A useful cross-family operating point is consequently
P = (Q, tT , B(G), trender ) ,
18
(26)
and methods should be compared through the resulting trade-off rather than a single
universal FPS or SPS value.
These differences lead to distinct deployment regimes. Feed-forward single-shot
methods are the strongest fit for rapid initialization and interactive preview because
they minimize scene-construction latency, although the controlled results show a direct
quality–latency–memory trade-off. Per-sequence dynamic methods are better suited
when temporal reconstruction quality is more important than initialization time, since
interactive rendering can be achieved after a substantially heavier optimization stage.
Compression and LoD methods are most useful when storage, bandwidth, or rendering
load is the limiting resource. Streaming methods directly target temporal scene delivery, but the measured update rates show that interactive playback and real-time scene
refresh remain different system requirements. Finally, Gaussian SLAM is the natural
family for robotics and online mapping because pose estimation and scene reconstruction are coupled, but the wrapped rates in table 11 show that joint tracking–mapping
latency remains the principal obstacle to video-rate operation. Thus, “real-time” Gaussian Splatting is not a single operating point: the appropriate method depends on
whether the application must optimize reconstruction latency, temporal update latency,
map maintenance, memory/bandwidth, or render-only throughput.
7. Open Problems and Future Directions
We summarize open problems across the major components of a GS reconstruction
pipeline (see Figure 1), focusing on what blocks unified end-to-end real-time systems.
7.1. Reporting vs. Reality: Accepted Gaps
Open problems. “Real-time” is often reported at the stage level rather than endto-end, making cross-paper comparisons fragile. Some works report only submodule
speed (e.g., VGGT-style lifting [71]), while others report FPS under narrow settings
(few views, low resolution) that do not reflect realistic reconstruction workloads [17, 5].
Depth Anything 3 [18] similarly reports per-image throughput, which does not translate
to per-scene throughput for multi-frame/view fusion.
Future directions. Standardize task-specific speed–quality reporting with explicit
input window size, unit of work, timing boundary, hardware, and included pipeline
stages.
7.2. Single-Shot Generators: The Deployment Gap
Open problems. Single-shot GS generators combine heavy backbones with multiview fusion and sometimes refinement, making “speed” hard to interpret. Across
depth-conditioned lifting (DepthSplat [17]), sparse-view fusion (MVSplat [5]), and
learned priors (Splatt3r [44], VGGT-style lifting [71]), runtime often shifts into feature
extraction/fusion that is inconsistently included in FPS reports. InstantSplat [46] and
pose-free lines [72, 73] further depend on iteration budgets and convergence behavior,
while custom CUDA or non-standard rasterization/fusion components (e.g., [12, 16])
can complicate deployment and reproducible timing.
19
Future directions. (i) Co-design for deployment: prefer architectures that map
cleanly to standard inference graphs with predictable memory/batching. (ii) Pruning/LoD as first-class: predict compact Gaussians directly (importance-aware outputs)
rather than generating dense sets and pruning afterward.
7.3. Pruning and Efficiency: An Afterthought
Open problems. Many efficiency levers are applied after dense construction, so
expensive lifting/fusion/optimization is still paid at full cost. Pruning methods remove large fractions post hoc [57, 59]; quantization/packing reduce bandwidth/storage
[74, 75]; and LoD scales playback [76, 56]. These benefits often arrive only after
dense construction, and view-adaptive pruning can introduce popping/flicker without
temporal stabilization [77, 78].
Future directions. (i) Sparse by construction: design generators and online updaters that output compact Gaussians natively. (ii) Temporally stable budgets: learn
selection/budget policies that avoid popping and flicker under fast camera motion.
7.4. Dynamic Streaming: Long-Horizon Stability
Open problems. Long-horizon streaming remains fragile because dynamics introduce identity and lifecycle issues: Gaussians must remain consistent under occlusion/disocclusion, topology change, and re-observation after long gaps. Spawnable/timevarying primitives (e.g., FreeTimeGS [79]) improve flexibility but exacerbate growth
control and bookkeeping. System-wise, streaming pipelines must meet bounded update
budgets and avoid latency spikes under cache misses or viewpoint jumps [62, 63, 61];
many pipelines still resemble offline optimization with real-time playback rather than
truly online capture-to-render reconstruction [3, 2].
Future directions. (i) Lifecycle + map maintenance: explicit birth/death, merge/split,
and re-anchoring policies to stabilize identity and bound growth [79]. (ii) Canonical + residual streaming: compact canonical models plus time-dependent deltas [63].
(iii) Cache-robust updates: schedules/caches that degrade gracefully under viewpoint
jumps [62, 61].
7.5. Rendering: Differentiability
Open problems. Differentiable rasterization enables end-to-end optimization [1],
but full gradients increase memory/overhead and complicate bounded-latency streaming or online updates. Faster rasterizers can prioritize throughput, yet approximations
in compositing/sorting/visibility may introduce haloing, incorrect occlusion, and temporal flicker, especially in dense/dynamic regions [12, 16]. Recent work on compositing correctness (e.g., volumetrically consistent formulations) suggests some artifacts
are rooted in the rendering model itself [15].
Future directions. (i) Train–deploy separation: train with differentiable or surrogate rendering, deploy with faster backends, and control mismatch [12, 16]. (ii)
Selective differentiability: keep full gradients only for keyframes or uncertain regions.
(iii) Correctness complements efficiency: improved compositing/visibility can reduce
artifacts without inflating N [15].
20
7.6. SLAM with Gaussians: The Missing Pieces
Open problems. GS-based SLAM must satisfy tracking deadlines and maintain a map that is both renderable and usable for localization. While recent systems show real-time tracking/mapping [80, 67, 68], long-term consistency (loop closure/relocalization), bounded growth via maintenance, and robustness to dynamicobject corruption remain under-emphasized in in-the-wild settings [69].
Future directions. (i) Loop-consistent Gaussian maps: loop-aware merge/reanchor/de-duplicate updates to preserve render quality and pose consistency. (ii) Maintenancefirst SLAM: pruning, keyframe selection, and local submap management as core components to keep latency stable [68]. (iii) Dynamic-robust mapping: lightweight masking/segmentation and static/dynamic separation to prevent permanent map pollution
[69].
8. Conclusion
This survey reviewed Gaussian Splatting (GS) for real-time scene reconstruction.
Rather than only listing methods, we organized prior work into a pipeline-centric taxonomy and highlighted the trade-offs that determine whether a method is merely realtime in a submodule or deployable end-to-end.
Strengths and value to the field. The main strength of this work is its synthesis
across components. We connect design choices (e.g., temporal parameterization vs.
stability, pruning/quantization/LoD vs. fidelity, and rasterization choices vs. artifacts)
to system-level outcomes such as latency, memory growth, robustness to pose/depth
noise, and long-horizon stability. The taxonomy, comparison framework, and failuremode analysis help researchers position new contributions and practitioners reason
about capture-to-render pipelines.
Weaknesses and limitations. Our taxonomy necessarily simplifies a fast-moving
hybrid space, where many systems combine amortized initialization with refinement or
mix static and dynamic representations. Comparability is also limited: papers report
speed and memory at different stages (e.g., per-image FPS vs. per-scene throughput),
under different input-window sizes and hardware assumptions. Thus, end-to-end conclusions about “real-time” readiness should be read as pipeline-aware guidance rather
than a standardized benchmark ranking.
Future work. The main remaining directions are task-specific standardized evaluation, deployment-aware scene generation, sparse-by-construction representations,
long-horizon dynamic and streaming maintenance, and robust Gaussian SLAM. Together, these problems shift the focus from fast rasterization toward maintaining quality, bounded latency, and manageable state throughout the reconstruction pipeline.
