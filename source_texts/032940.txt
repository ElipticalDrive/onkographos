Abstract—The need for more realistic 3D scene representations
has fomented the development of models for a wide range of
applications. In this context, solutions that attempt to model the
light’s behavior through the plenoptic function have provided
considerable advancements using neural-based approaches, often
presenting a trade-off between rendering time and model sizes. In
this work, we propose a pruning framework to reduce the sizes of
these models by computing the visibility over the training data,
applicable to different 3D scene representations. In particular, we
implement first a solution suitable for the 3D Gaussian Splatting,
and then we exemplify the solution for the Neural Radiance
Fields (NeRF)-style of rendering using PlenOctrees. We show
that our pruning solution produces smaller models in terms of
the number of elements – be they voxels, points, or Gaussians
– with minimal losses in terms of rendering novel views. We
further assess our solution by combining it with state-of-theart (SOTA) compression solutions for both rendering schemes.
Results over the NeRF-Synthetic dataset show comparable metrics to the SOTA for PlenOctrees, achieving marginal gains for
lower bitrates. For 3DGS, the combination of our pruning method
and compression solutions achieves a compression ratio of up to
37.5 times over the uncompressed 3DGS models, with only a 0.5
dB decrease in rendering quality. When compared against other
SOTA compression methods, our solution produces models 1.4
times smaller, with less than a 0.1 dB loss over novel views for
synthetic data, and models 1.9 times smaller with less than 0.2
dB loss when synthesizing novel views on real-world, outdoor
content.

Pruning, Compression
I. I NTRODUCTION
T
HE modeling of three-dimensional (3D) scenes has been
a focal point of study across multiple disciplines. In computer vision, for example, representing a 3D scene structure
from 2D images allows the generation of novel photorealistic
views that can be used for a wide range of applications
related to object recognition and scene understanding, such as
virtual and augmented reality, 3D reconstruction, and medical
imaging. On the other hand, in domains like robotics, a robust
3D representation is paramount to facilitate spatial perception, navigation, and interaction for tasks like autonomous
exploration and industrial automation. Traditional methods for
representation of such scenes include point clouds (PC) [1],
meshes [2], voxel grids [3], and even sets of depth maps [4].
On the other hand, 3D scene rendering has seen considerable advancements using neural-based representations that

parametrize the scene as a description of the light rays in
space, the so-called plenoptic function. Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRF) [5] was among the first exponents, which led to extensions to enhance the method by improving its image quality
[6], [7], adapting it to different image formats [8], [9], making
it more generalizable for sparse inputs [10], accelerating its
training and/or inference speed [11]–[14], among others.
Nevertheless, NeRF’s design for volumetric rendering resulted in the advent of solutions that accelerate the rendering
via a trade-off between the model’s size and speed. 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [15] emerged as an alternative by
representing 3D scenes by using Gaussian kernels, generating
a rendering process that is both fast and with state-of-theart (SOTA) quality without a particular need for a neural
network (NN). However, the tendency of 3DGS optimization
to produce an elevated number of Gaussians to represent 3D
scenes results in large model sizes. This has proved to be a
challenge for the widespread adoption of such models, since
applications like handheld devices and head-mounted displays
work with constrained resources that are not compliant with
the elevated storage and memory footprints. This fomented the
development of solutions that compress them [16]–[19].
In this work, we propose a framework to reduce the size of
3D scene representations for scenes with an “inwards-facing”
disposition, by exploiting their underlying geometry regardless
of their format. This is achieved by using the training views to
compute the visibility of the elements in the scene – be they
points, voxels, or Gaussians, depending on the representation
type – and pruning the ones that are deemed less visible and
thus, less impactful for the rendering quality of the model. We
implement our method with two solutions that use a different
underlying framework to render 3D scenes. In this work, we
primarily focus on 3DGS, where we remove Gaussians to
generate more compact models, thus reducing the memory
consumption of such scenes as well as allowing them to be
rendered faster. Moreover, we also apply our pruning method
in a NeRF-style approach, where we use the PlenOctrees
framework to prune voxels from large octrees.
In short, our solution has the following contributions:
1) A geometry pruning approach that can be applied to
both PlenOctree and Gaussian splatting with minimal
modifications,
a) When used in conjunction with PlenOctrees, our
pruning solution provides an accurate geometry,
closer to the ground-truth than the pruned point
clouds obtained by other existing approaches,
2
b) For the 3DGS, we embed our method inside the
optimization/densification process to reduce the required number of Gaussians to represent the scene,
2) We finally combine our pruning method with existing
compression algorithms for both our solutions implemented with PlenOctrees and Gaussian Splatting, to
achieve even smaller bitrates than in the SOTA.

A. Plenoptic Representations
The ability to model scenes and objects with changing
colors as the viewer changes their viewing angle, i.e. nonLambertian, is highly desired in immersive applications. As
such, the plenoptic function [20] is a mathematical model that
represents a “sea of light” by describing each light ray in the
world as a wave through a set of parameters, generally given
by

This provides a means of representation of the intensity of
each light ray in the world according to the spatial coordinates
(x, y, z), the viewing angle (θ, ϕ), the light wavelength λ and
time t. Such function can be incorporated differently in the
modeling of 3D scenes according to the type of scene representation; 360° videos only have 3 degrees of freedom, using
the angular coordinates θ, ϕ [21]. Light field images define the
light transport in a 3-D scene with a 5D feature space, with
spatial and angular coordinates [22]. Plenoptic point clouds
[23], [24] represent such a model with a collection of vectors,
with an explicit geometry given by a finite set of points in R3
and a finite number of RGB colors as attributes corresponding
to the camera viewpoints during capture, naturally sampling
the viewing direction of the 3D space.

light field. Through the use of multi-layer perceptrons (MLP),
NeRF implements a continuous function F that maps the 3D

C. Speed-up NeRF Variants and Model Compression
Ray-marching through the scene in NeRF’s rendering makes
its training and inference intrinsically slow since every sample
in the ray has to query the MLP to obtain the volume rendering
parameters for the predicted pixel color (Eq. 5).
As a result, different proposals have sought to speed up
the training efficiency and/or the rendering time of the NN’s
evaluation. Pre-baking NeRF’s outputs into a sparse voxel grid
[28], [29], or representing the parameters into octrees [12]
are ways to accelerate the model’s rendering. Alternatively,
optimizing over a sparse voxel grid from scratch [13] or using
auxiliary multi-scale hash grids to reduce the complexity of
the NN’s task [11] can also speed up the training.
However, these grid-based, NeRF-like solutions improve the
rendering speed at the cost of storage space. Hence, solutions
have been proposed to address such weaknesses. The authors
in [30] reduce the grid sizes by learning a hybrid tensor rank
decomposition, while Rho et al. [31] transform the spatial
information into the frequency domain. Re:NeRF [32] applies
voxel pruning via removing and re-including parameters for
any NeRF-like method that minimizes a differentiable loss
function. In addition to pruning voxels, the work in [33] aims
to also compress the scene according to an octree quantization
and intra-frame prediction of the volume rendering parameters
as point cloud attributes by looking at a point’s neighborhood.
D. Gaussian Splatting Compression
3D Gaussian Splatting achieved breakthroughs in the task
of synthesizing novel views by achieving real-time rendering performance while presenting smaller storage footprints
comparatively to other view-dependent representations, such
as voxel-based [24], [34], [35] and the grid-based, NeRFlike approaches [36]. Nevertheless, such Gaussian-based optimization tends to over-parametrize the scene with kernels
to generate high-fidelity renders, increasing the storage and
memory requirements. Moreover, previous work [33] shows
that representing view-dependent colors as a set of spherical
harmonics (SH) coefficients takes up most of the said storage
costs. Hence, Niedemayr et al. [16] proposed to compress
both the SHs coefficients and the Gaussian parameters via
a clustering algorithm based on the sensitivity of the reconstruction quality to the changes of such attributes. This
clustering is combined with quantization-aware training to
compress codebooks of the SHs and Gaussian parameters with
dictionary-based entropy coding. In addition to the attribute
compression, Lee et al. [17] also proposed to reduce the
number of Gaussians during optimization. This is done with
a learnable mask that removes small-sized Gaussians and
those with low opacity. This is combined with a geometry
codebook representation of the Gaussians and a grid-based
neural field to represent the view-dependent colors by feeding
the view direction and feature vectors into a small MLP.
Other solutions to reduce the sizes of 3DGS models include
vector quantization [18], self-organizing grids [19], [37], and
distillation of SH coefficients to a lower degree [19].
These works address the size reduction of models that
follow either a NeRF-like or a splatting pipeline. Our solution
aims to prune the scene models by looking at the scene’s
disposition to the acquired viewpoints, thus being able to be
used for both NeRF-based and splatting-based methods with
minimal adaptations.
III. P ROPOSED F RAMEWORK
Leveraging the scene’s underlying geometry is a way to
orient the pruning while minimizing distortion losses by
exploring redundancies while maintaining the object’s “watertightness”, regardless of the geometry’s representation.
We focus in this work on inward-facing scenes, that is,
the cameras in the training set are arranged to be centered
around an object. As a result, scenes of this type tend to be
less sparse when compared to natural, large-scale ones. This
type of camera arrangement leads to points being visible by
multiple cameras at once. Therefore, one can leverage such a
setup to prune points from the scene based on their visibility
with regard to the training viewpoints. Removing the ones that
are less visible to a given set of cameras can minimize losses
on the 2-D rendered views while working directly on the 3-D
space without the need for ground-truth geometry.

A. NeRF-based Approach
The framework for our solution based on NeRF’s rendering
is based on PlenOctrees [12], an approach that provides
faster rendering by pre-tabulating the NN that predicts the
parameters required for NeRF’s volume rendering. We review
the pipeline of the PlenOctrees method in order to then explain
how our pruning method interacts with the original solution.
The method consists of constructing an octree structure
where each voxel contains the rendering parameters optimized
via rendering loss [12]. To achieve real-time performance,
the ray-marching procedure has to be done directly on the
PlenOctree, which means that the color information in the
voxel has to be view-independent beforehand. Hence, the
NeRF network from which PlenOctree extracts its parameters
is a modified version that outputs spherical harmonics (SH)
coefficients for the color and the density of the soft geometry.
This greatly increases the storage and memory requirements
to execute such an approach, as SHs of order 3 mean that each
voxel stores 16 coefficients per color channel.
In order to reduce the impact of this tradeoff of speed
for size, the original PlenOctrees paper [12] proposes some
techniques to reduce the complexity of the structures. The
work in [33] further explores the search for a more compact
representation by reducing the number of occupied voxels
during optimization, performed by thresholding the octree
based on the σ values. The pruned PlenOctree was then
fine-tuned and compressed as a voxelized point cloud whose
attributes were the SH coefficients and the density.

B. Splatting-based Approach
The 3DGS pipeline, on the other hand, provides an adaptive
splat by representing the 3-D scene via a set of Gaussian kernels. This is achieved by the fact that each one of these Gaussians can be approximately represented by a corresponding
ellipsoid by describing its covariance matrix Σ with a scaling
matrix S and rotation matrix R. This anisotropic modeling
of the covariance allows the Gaussians to be optimized into
different shapes, as opposed to the fixed splats one sees in
voxel-based solutions such as PlenOctrees (Sec. III-A).
To project the Gaussian positions, we use the location
of their centroids as points of reference for the visibility
algorithm. Note that the centroids are the positions where the
density of the kernels is at its highest, that is, where they
appear more “opaque” (see Fig 3).
Our method can be implemented by embedding our pruning
into 3DGS’ optimization pipeline, as seen in Fig. 4. More
specifically, the visibility computation and consequent removal
of Gaussians can be performed jointly with the Adaptive
Density Control block, by removing the occluded kernels every
time the α value of the Gaussians is reset to zero.

The geometry xpc is encoded losslessly in [33], with the
attributes encoded lossily using a modified version of the
Geometry-Based Video Point Cloud Coding (G-PCC) from

orthogonal basis formed by the decomposition into SHs provides a decorrelation between the multiple attributes of P for
each of the color channels that is analogous to the usage of
Karhunen-Loève Transform (KLT) coefficients applied in [40].
In this work, we compress the geometry and multiple
attributes from the Plenoctrees derived PC P using the more
recent Geometry-Based Solid Content Test Model (GeS-TM)
[41] to compress the models. As a branch split from G-PCC,
in this codec the attributes are lossy compressed via the region
adaptive hierarchical transform (RAHT), while the geometry is
either losslessly encoded via octree representation, or encoded
with losses using the triangle soup (Trisoup) coding. Both are
then entropy encoded to create the respective bitstreams.
Once the attributes are decoded and unscaled, the PC P can
be reconstructed into P̂ . From the geometry xpc of P̂ that was
encoded losslessly, one can reconstruct a PlenOctree with the
same structure as the original one, but with lossy reconstructed
attributes σ and SHs. These are encoded with attribute quantization parameters (QP) of six different values, from the lowest
to the highest quantization: (22, 28, 34, 40, 46, 51). This allows
the selection of a certain level of model quality according to
the bitrate constraints of the user.

The parameters are then fine-tuned on the training set,
with the optimization for position, opacity, and scaling for
the Gaussians, as well as the color and codebook entries.
This is done via quantization-aware training with Min-Max
Quantization. In the forward pass, the quantization is simulated
considering a certain number of bits in addition to the moving
average of each parameter’s minimum and maximum values.
The backward pass then ignores the simulated quantization and
calculates the gradient for the parameter without quantization.
Finally, the set of Gaussians and the codebooks for the
SHs and Gaussian shape parameters are compressed with
DEFLATE [42]. By ordering the Gaussians according to their
positions in Z-order in Morton order, one can leverage the
spatial coherence of the attributes with a dictionary-based
entropy coding algorithm (LZ77 [43]).
The second one, proposed by Lee et al. [17], introduces a
mechanism to reduce the number of Gaussians, in addition to
their compression. In combination with our visibility-pruning
strategy, this can lead to representations with even fewer
kernels and, consequently, more compact.

mask during the optimization that removes Gaussians that
minimally impact performance. This is done by targeting the
removal of small-sized kernels at every densification step, due
to their minimal impact on rendering quality performance.

scale attributes of the kernels. The masked scale ŝ and opacity

A. Experimental Setup
1) Dataset: We use in our experiments the NeRF-Synthetic
360 – also known as Blender – dataset from the original
NeRF [5], which contains 8 sequences with splits of 100
images for training, 100 for validation, and 200 for testing with
dimensions of 800 × 800 each and ground-truth camera poses.
The ground-truth point clouds for the geometry assessment
were obtained from the modified NeRF-Synthetic 360 dataset
introduced in [36], available at https://drcfts.github.io/c-ppc/.
Additionally, we also use in our experiments the real-world
dataset of the Mip-NeRF 360 [7], containing 5 outdoor and
4 indoor environments for a total of 9 large-scale scenes.
Each scene has a variable number of camera poses, with the
testing split considered to be every 8th image of the set of the
rendering views, and no validation poses, as is generally done
in the literature [7].
2) Baselines: On the PlenOctrees’ side of our visibilitybased pruning solution, we compare our proposal against the
pruning and compression methods presented in [33], which we
refer to as “Compressed PlenOctrees (C-PO)” in this work. For
the 3DGS’ side, we assess our method against the state-of-theart compression solutions from [16] and [17], which we call
as “Niedermayr” and “Lee” in our benchmark comparisons.
For our proposed methods that use Eq. 7 as their loss
function (our solution in Sec. V-C1 and the combination of
our pruning with the compression from the work in [16]), the
selected value for the β hyperparameter was of 1 × 10−5 . The
λ and λm parameter values used were of 0.2 and 5 × 10−4
respectively, according to the existing literature.
3) Performance evaluation: All the reported PSNR values
are the averages of the individual image PSNRs measured
for the respective color channels at each of the 200 test
poses for the scenes of the NeRF-Synthetic dataset. The
sizes reported for the rate-distortion (RD) analysis of the
compression schemes are given in bytes, while the analysis of
the pruned models is given by the number of retained points.
Reporting the sizes in bytes is relevant for applications where
the efficient transmission of the models over communication
links is required while reporting the sizes as the number of
retained points is a (crude) estimation of the memory size
needed for fast accessing the uncompressed models at the

measuring the error via commonly used geometry metrics,
such as the point-to-point (D1) or the point-to-plane (D2)
errors [46]. Considering a reference PC A and reconstructed
PC B, the D1 and D2 metrics calculate the MSE between
the points and their closest neighbors in the other PC, that is,
from both A to B, and B to A, and taking the lowest value
of both directions as the final result. The downside of such a
symmetric metric is the assumption that both A and B have
close to the same amount of points, and thus the PSNR in both
directions will have closely related values. Here, where B can
have a significantly larger amount of points than A, the PSNR
value from the A −
→ B direction will be considerably higher
than in B −
→ A, as any given point in A will be more likely
to have a corresponding point (or plane) in B. As the number
of points from the pruned PCs B decreases, the PSNR values
in both directions converge, as the values in A −
→ B decrease
and B −
→ A increase. Therefore, the non-trivial analysis of
these metrics led us to benchmark it via ROC curves.

E. Method Scalability
In addition to the limitations of our method for some of the
indoor scenes from the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset presented in
the last Section, the scalability of the solution with regard to
the computational overhead on the training time relies on the
number of Gaussians in the scene as well as the number of
cameras in which to compute the visibility mask O.
As an example, for an NVIDIA A40 GPU, while the training
time for “drums”, the largest scene of the NeRF-Synthetic
dataset, increases from approximately 9 to 11 minutes when
comparing the introduction of our pruning method over the
vanilla version of 3DGS, this value increases from 50 minutes
to just under 3 hours for “bicycle”, the largest scene of the
Mip-NeRF 360 dataset. Note that our method can be sped
up by implementing the computation of the occlusion mask
using CUDA programming, taking advantage of the hardware
acceleration provided by the CUDA kernels.
In terms of inference time, the reduced number of Gaussians
of the reconstructed models with our pruning method makes
the scene rendering faster. As an example, for the “bicycle”
scene of the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset, the vanilla 3DGS achieves
a performance of 45.41 FPS in an NVIDIA A40 GPU, while
the pruned model with our method achieves a rendering speed
of 85.47 FPS for the same scene. When comparing the solution
from Niedermayr et al. [16] for the same scene, we notice
an increase in rendering speed from 79.22 to 102.25 FPS by
applying our pruning method, while in Lee et al. [17] our
solution increased it from 73.82 to 96.58 FPS.
VI. C ONCLUSION
We propose a pruning framework to reduce the size of
3D “inwards-facing” scene representations adaptable to different types of depiction of the geometry, such as NeRFor 3DGS-based methods. This is achieved by computing the
visibility over the training data of the elements of the given
representation, which can be points, voxels, or even Gaussian
kernels depending on the model’s underlying structure. We
implement our solution in two specific scenarios, with the grid
representation of PlenOctrees for the NeRF-based rendering,
and the 3DGS for the splatting-based approach.
We show that our pruning solution produces smaller models
in terms of the number of points with minimal losses in terms
of rendering quality in a novel view synthesis context for both
PlenOctrees and Gaussian Splatting implementations.
We further assess the sizes of our methods by combining
our pruning approach with existing compression methods to
evaluate them over their storage footprints. On synthetic data,
results show comparable metrics to existing solutions on the
PlenOctrees’ representation, achieving marginal gains for the
lower bitrates. On the 3DGS side, the combination of our pruning method and compression solutions achieves a compression
ratio of up to 37.5 times over their original, uncompressed
counterparts for the Gaussian-based scenes, with only a 0.5
dB decrease in rendering quality. While comparing our model
sizes to other SOTA approaches, our solution produces models
smaller by 1.4 times, with less than a 0.1 dB loss over novel
rendered views. We also assess our pruning method over largescale, real-world scenes, where our method produces model
