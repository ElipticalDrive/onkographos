Abstract
Particle-based systems have become a cornerstone in scientific visualization and
computer graphics, enabling the realistic simulation of dynamic phenomena such
as fluid flow and gas behavior. Traditional rendering techniques for particle systems often involve simplistic representations, such as spherical particles or static
points, which fail to convey their interconnectedness and movement. To address
these limitations, this paper presents a novel approach using deformed droplet
sprites for real-time rendering, enhancing visual fidelity while maintaining computational efficiency. The proposed method dynamically shapes particles based on
velocity and forces acting on them, enabling clearer visualization of fluid motion
and trajectories. Experimental results demonstrate that this approach not only
improves the aesthetic quality of fluid simulations but also supports real-time
rendering capabilities, making it suitable for various applications including animation and interactive scientific visualization. This work emphasizes the need for
advancements in rendering techniques within particle systems to bridge the visual
gap between discrete particles and cohesive fluid dynamics, thereby fostering
more compelling and informative representations of simulated environments.

1 Introduction
Particle-based simulation techniques, such as the Distinct Element Method (DEM) [1]
and Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) [2, 3], are extensively utilized for modeling the dynamics of granular materials and fluids, respectively. These methods find
applications across a spectrum, ranging from micro-scale phenomena to macro-scale
interactions, and from discrete particle systems to continuum modeling. In addition to
their scientific applications, these simulations are integral in computer graphics (CG),
powering visuals in animated films and video games. Modern 3D modeling and editing
software, along with game and physics engines, offer robust particle system functionalities. These systems are primarily designed to generate visual effects that mimic
particle-like behaviors such as gaseous phenomena (e.g., sparks, fire, smoke, clouds)
and fluid movements (e.g., waterfalls [4, 5] and splashes), as well as granular motions
(e.g., dust, snow, and leaves). Often, particle systems operate using simplified models
rather than comprehensive physics simulations to balance visual realism and computational efficiency. In many instances, particle systems do not employ a comprehensive
simulation model but rather rely on simplified models due to the substantial computational demands of full physics simulations. In certain scenarios, particle systems may
omit particle-particle interactions to enhance performance efficiency. However, there
is a persistent demand for particle systems to generate large-scale particle motions as
seen in various phenomena. Recently, advancements have been made to improve the
performance of particle simulations. Despite this, rendering techniques, particularly
for particle-based fluid simulations, have not significantly progressed in performance
optimization. A major challenge lies in rendering spherical particles to accurately
represent the fluid’s deformable shapes. For applications requiring non-real-time rendering, such as simulated rain in CG films, handcrafted droplet-like 3D models are
often used instead of particles, though this approach incurs considerable computational
costs. This study presents a novel technique that employs a deformation droplet sprite
to achieve real-time visualization and rendering of particles within fluid simulations.
The results from our experiments indicate that this method is not only lightweight
but also ideally adapted for in-situ rendering of particle-based fluid dynamics. By utilizing a shape that can be deformed, our approach effectively provides an intuitive
framework for illustrating the movement of fluids, capturing their dynamic behavior
in a way that enhances both accuracy and visual appeal. Consequently, this method
significantly elevates the standard of representation seen in particle-based simulations, proving beneficial for various applications, particularly in the realm of computer
graphics where realistic depiction is essential.
The remaining sections of this paper are structured as follows: In Section 2, we
will provide a review of the related literature that underpins our research. Following
this, Section 3 will detail the intricacies of our proposed methodology, laying out the
foundations and innovations therein. In Section 4, we will elaborate on the implementation specifics of our approach, demonstrating how it has been operationalized.
Subsequently, Section 5 will present the results obtained from our experimental evaluations, showcasing the effectiveness of our approach. Finally, we will conclude our
discussions in Section 6, summarizing our findings and suggesting avenues for future
work based on the insights gained from this study.

2 Related work
Various methods for visualizing results from fluid simulations have been explored
extensively over the years. Following the introduction of SPH, particle-based simulations have emerged as a prevalent choice for fluid simulation techniques, finding
extensive application in both scientific research and computer graphics domains. Most
common particle simulations utilize spherical particles as their fundamental elements.
While rendering these particles as spheres is the simplest approach, it incurs a high
cost in rendering a 3D sphere. An alternative technique is to use a sprite (a flat
quad textured to appear spherical) to depict a particle. However, traditional rendering methods for particle-based fluid simulations face two significant challenges. To
begin with, spherical particles are ineffective in authentically mimicking the appearance of liquid. Additionally, a clear contrast exists between a collection of particles
and a unified mass of fluid (e.g., rivers or lakes). Numerous researchers have actively
proposed various innovative methods for effectively rendering the results of particlebased fluid simulations, placing a significant emphasis on achieving realistic visual
representations of fluids [6, 7]. Despite their efforts, there remains a noticeable gap,
with relatively few methods focused specifically on real-time rendering suitable for
interactive applications. Among the prevalent techniques for real-time visualization of
such fluid simulations, screen-space methods stand out as the most commonly utilized
approach [8]. Additionally, the exploration of utilizing ellipses in place of traditional
sphere sprites represents another promising direction aimed at enhancing the depiction of fluids to achieve a more liquid-like appearance. Recently, an advanced method
in anisotropic screen space rendering also has been introduced [9]. This method effectively utilizes elliptical shapes in combination with screen-space techniques, resulting
in faster computations and improved rendering quality, which collectively provide
superior results in the visualization of particle-based fluid simulations. This approach
marks a significant advancement in the field and opens further avenues for enhancing
real-time fluid rendering techniques.
The approach employed in this research is fundamentally grounded in the concept of utilizing ellipse sprites as an alternative to traditional sphere sprites. However,
the methodology in the paper differentiates itself by concentrating specifically on the
behavior of moving particles within the fluid simulation. This innovative strategy
involves rendering droplet-like particles that are directly influenced by the velocity
vectors of each individual particle in the simulation. By doing so, not only is there an
enhancement in the visual representation of the particles, but this technique also significantly contributes to a more accurate visualization of fluid motion. The resulting
effect aims to depict a dynamic fluid environment that mirrors the natural behavior
of fluids, thereby improving both aesthetic appeal and the informative quality of the
simulation. This dual focus not only enhances the visual fidelity of the rendered particles but also facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of fluid dynamics as
they unfold in real-time scenarios.

3 Approach
Visualization and rendering techniques are vital technologies for interpreting scientific
data, particularly in the context of fluid simulation results. These methods not only
play a crucial role in crafting realistic computer-generated imagery (CGI) grounded
in physical simulations but also serve a significant purpose for researchers and scientists. They provide essential insights, allowing these professionals to observe and
identify various physical phenomena and challenges through the analysis of simulation outcomes. Through effective visualization, complex data becomes more accessible,
enabling better understanding and exploration of the underlying processes. A typical
technique for visualizing particles involves rendering them as spheres using a sprite or
billboard, which is a small quad that consistently faces the viewer. Despite not being
an actual spherical geometry, it is represented in 3D space as a sphere. This approach is
akin to mapping a sphere’s texture onto the quad utilizing normal mapping techniques.
Initially, a quad oriented towards the viewer is rendered, followed by the computation
of the normal vectors for the “virtual sphere” on this quad in the U V -space. Subsequently, lighting and shading effects are derived from these normal vectors. All these
calculations take place within the rendering pipeline, facilitated by programmable
shaders, ensuring an efficient visualization process. To achieve lightweight and rapid
rendering, we introduce a deformable droplet constructed within the U V -space of a
sprite. This design simplifies computations by transitioning from 3D (XY Z) space
to 2D (U V ) space. Fig. 1 demonstrates the calculation process for our deformable
droplet, which has been enhanced based on the sphere sprite technique.

Deformation is implemented across the three axes of the droplet’s local coordinate
system. Given that calculations occur within the U V -space, the axes of this system
are defined as the U -axis, V -axis, and N -axis, resembling the tangent space definition
used in normal mapping. This framing allows for effective manipulation of the droplet’s
form during simulations. The droplet undergoes deformation in alignment with the
velocity or force direction, typically resulting in a pull at the rear or tail end and a push
at the front end. To streamline the discussion, this paper focuses solely on velocity
as the primary factor influencing the droplet’s deformation. The velocity of a particle
system or simulation is represented as a vector in three-dimensional space, denoted as
Vworld . To enable deformation within U V -space, this velocity is converted to viewer
coordinates, referred to as Vview . Subsequently, the coordinates of the sprite in U V space undergo a rotation. Let the V -axis be aligned with the mapped velocity vector
Vview ; consequently, any deformation resulting from the velocity will consistently affect
the V -axis of the sprite.

Two user-defined scale factors, denoted as sf (sf > 0) for the scaling of the front
side and sr (sr > 0) for the scaling of the rear side, are established for this context.
These values are not fixed; rather, they can be calculated based on the specific demands
imposed by various applications or desired visual effects. Notably, it is important to
understand that the scaling effects observed along the U -axis and N -axis directions do
not stem from direct external forces. According to the conservation law, the volume of
the droplet remains constant before and after deformation. Consequently, the resulting
scaling effects correlate with the factors sf and sr , as well as the overall volume of the
droplet. The scale factors corresponding to the U -axis and N -axis directions can be
determined based on the following assumptions:
1. The droplet shall become a sphere if no deformation (i.e., sf = sr = 1).
2. The volume of the deformed droplet and a non-deformed droplet (sphere) are
equivalent.
3. There is no anisotropy internal stress. Scale factors in V -axis and N -axis are the
same.
4. The droplet is considered as composed by two half-spheroids with the same length
of the semi-axis a (see Fig. 3).

4 Implementation
Our proposed method is integrated as a rendering component within a dynamic
particle-based simulator utilizing CUDA [10] and OpenGL. The simulation leverages
SPH techniques powered by CUDA, while OpenGL handles the real-time rendering of the simulation outputs. These two elements communicate effectively through
the CUDA-OpenGL interoperability framework [11]. The simulation and rendering
pipeline of the whole program is shown in Fig. 4. The implementation of SPH is derived
from the FluidSurface code in the NVIDIA GVDB Voxels library [12], which serves
7
Fig. 4: The overall simulation and rendering pipeline of our implementation.
as a refined version of the Fluids v3 simulator [13]. Unlike conventional particle-based
renderers that require only the positions of particles from the simulator, our approach
enhances this by transmitting both particle positions and velocities to the renderer,
enabling a more accurate depiction of the deformation of spheroid sprites.
In various rendering scenarios where the proposed method excels, it becomes essential to visualize expansive fluid bodies, such as lakes or rivers, alongside individual
droplets. These larger volumes of fluid are more effectively rendered using isosurface
algorithms, like marching cubes, rather than employing multiple singular spheroids,
which may not capture the depth and detail required. To facilitate this differentiation, we incorporate a selector that intelligently distinguishes between these two
rendering approaches based on the quantity of particles present within interconnected
cells. Conversely, if the count is below the threshold, those particles are excluded
from the marching cubes volume calculations and are instead represented as deformed
spheroids. This method significantly enhances the visual accuracy of fluid dynamics
by effectively distinguishing between dense fluid volumes and isolated particles.
The connection between the SPH simulator and the renderer is seamless, allowing
for efficient integration. Given that the computations involving pressure and force
inherently necessitate data from neighboring particles, the process of counting particles
within each cell can be performed with minimal additional computational expense.
This counting is crucial for determining the behavior of the renderer as well as for
implementing the marching cubes algorithm, ensuring that visual outputs are accurate.
Furthermore, a user-friendly interface has been developed to facilitate the management
of various simulation and rendering parameters. This interface allows users to easily
switch between different rendering methods, providing flexibility and enhancing user
experience while ensuring that simulations can be effectively monitored and adjusted
in real-time (see Fig. 5).

degree of realism in fluid dynamics simulation. The subsequent waterfall scenario
further demonstrates the practical application of these rendering techniques, effectively
depicting the behavior of water as it cascades over cliffs.

6 Conclusion and future work
This research presents a novel approach to real-time particle rendering using
deformable droplet sprites, significantly enhancing the visual fidelity of fluid simulations while maintaining efficient computational performance. Despite its strengths,
the method has limitations, including potential performance drops with extremely
high particle counts and challenges in rendering highly turbulent fluid flows accurately. These issues suggest that while the hybrid rendering technique provides
significant improvements over traditional isosurface methods, there remains room for
enhancement in more complex scenarios.
Future research could explore optimization techniques to minimize performance
overhead, especially in environments with dynamic and varied particle densities. Additionally, the integration of machine learning methods could be investigated to better
predict particle behavior and enhance real-time rendering quality, particularly in
