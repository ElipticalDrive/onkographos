Abstract
Visual quality assessment of dynamic point clouds has become an important
matter for emerging use cases and applications in eXtended Reality (XR).
Understanding how users perceive quality in XR is essential to enable improvements in compression and rendering processes that will improve the
Quality of Experience (QoE) for end users. While numerous studies have
investigated the perceptual quality of static and dynamic point clouds, very
few have examined influential factors exclusive to volumetric media, such as
rendering parameters and lighting, and how they impact the perceived quality and user experience in the context of XR applications with six degrees of
freedom (6DoF) interaction. In this paper, we present Light-DPCVR: a subjective dataset collected through a quality assessment experiment in Virtual
Reality (VR) complemented by gaze and exploration tracking. This study
investigates the role of virtual lighting direction, a rendering parameter exclusive to 3D content, in the perceived quality of rendered dynamic point
clouds coded with Video-based Point Cloud Compression (V-PCC) in VR,
and its influence on observer behavior. Our results reveal how lighting affects
the perception of V-PCC artifacts and user exploration. Furthermore, we assess the efficiency of our test methodology for volumetric videos in VR, and
build a benchmark of objective quality metrics performance on our dataset
and give access to our data to enlighten future development.
Keywords: Virtual Reality, Point clouds, Quality of Experience, Lighting
1. Introduction
Volumetric videos are dynamic sequences of full 3D frames, which makes
them the richest type of media in terms of spatial and temporal dimensions.
Commonly, they can be reconstructed using data captured with synchronized multi-camera setups and depth sensors, and used as fully interactive
6DoF (degrees of freedom) representation of moving natural scenes or humans. Thanks to the ongoing development of hardware and software for
eXtended Reality (XR), numerous use cases are emerging, where dynamic
natural content is captured, transmitted, rendered, relighted and explored.
These include virtual concerts and theaters, but also live interactive broadcasting and immersive telecommunication [1]. In these cases, moving humans
are represented as dynamic point clouds (DPCs) [2], a type of volumetric
video where all frames are point clouds in the same coordinate space. Using
DPCs has advantages in real-time scenarios, as the construction of a point
cloud is usually a step in the process of any 3D reconstruction [3]. Another
motivation for the use of DPCs is the tremendous ongoing development and
standardization efforts for point cloud compression [1]. The high dimensionality of volumetric videos makes them extremely heavy, ranging in gigabytes
even for just a few seconds, which causes issues for storage and transmission. The research community in multimedia compression addresses these
challenges with the development of 3D compression methods for volumetric
data, leading to standards by the MPEG-I group and mostly focused on the
case of point clouds so far. Two approaches have been standardized since
2020:
• Geometry-based (G-PCC): Point clouds are encoded in 3D space, adapted
to sparse and static data.
• Video-based (V-PCC): Point cloud sequences are patched into 2D maps
and encoded with video codecs, adapted to dynamic and dense content.
Based on comparison studies [4, 5], including other 3D compression methods such as Google Draco [6], V-PCC is the best-performing method in terms
of quality, especially for dynamic point clouds. Other ongoing studies are
also investigating learning-based methods [7] or optimized processes [8] to
improve over the standard implementations. These compression methods
2
are among many other processes that can impact the visual aspect of the
data. Their performance is therefore assessed in terms of quality, but simple objective measures such as the PSNR are not sufficient to capture the
quality of visual content as perceived by humans. The expectations of end
users in application also vary depending on the context. The aforementioned
use cases include both applications with high-end quality content and live
applications where DPCs have to be captured, transmitted and rendered in
real-time. The display and rendering parameters such as the lighting, as
well as user freedom, can also have an impact on the experience and quality
perception. For this reason, to consider human perceived quality as part of
the Quality of Experience (QoE), compression methods and other processes
are evaluated in subjective Quality Assessment (QA) experiments where the
quality of a distorted content is defined as the Mean Opinion Score (MOS) of
human observers. The resulting data are also commonly used as datasets for
training and evaluation of new metrics, fueling research in objective QA [9].
To ensure that such experiments produce reliable and exploitable data,
test conditions are standardized for different types of content. However, the
recommendations for point cloud subjective QA [10] are based on standards
for 2D display or static spatial content [11], which are unfit for the evaluation of dynamic point clouds in the context of XR applications. Indeed,
these recommendations suggest avoiding interactive viewing as it would affect the inter-observer variability. But using passive tasks for 3D volumetric
media implies limiting the assessment to a pre-selected set of viewpoints,
which could bias the results and potentially miss important clues on the
preferences of observers, especially for 4D (3D+time) media. To consider
the dimensionality of volumetric videos, recent studies run quality tests in
virtual [12] or mixed [13] reality environments with 6DoF. But even though
some of these works also assess various influential factors such as the viewing
distance and quality switches [14], they all include only geometry and color
compression levels as visual distortions on the data. This is because the
standard implementations for point cloud compression also include only encoding and decoding of color and geometry, while there are other surface and
aspect properties that can be stored as point cloud attributes. Some of these
attributes are essential to improve the rendering options, such as the surface
normals that represent the surface orientation as 3D vectors. These vectors
allow vertex-based lit rendering of point clouds, which is an important depth
cue in 3D and strongly affects the perception of geometry. As a 3D attribute,
normals can be added into any compression method and processed like the
3
color (RGB) attributes and therefore have distortions that will affect the aspect of point clouds rendered with shading [15]. Particularly in the case of
3D content in XR, there is a plurality of rendering parameters that affect the
visual quality. The representation (mesh, point cloud, gaussian splat) [5], the
lighting parameters [16, 17] (intensity, direction, ambient) and the level of
details are examples of influential factors on the quality that are specific to
volumetric media representing natural content. In a 6DoF application context, these parameters could also potentially influence the behavior of users
in the way they experience the media [18].
In this paper, we present a subjective quality assessment study considering one of these rendering variables: virtual lighting direction. We use
V-PCC to distort the color, geometry and normals of DPC sequences and
render them with different lighting angles to cross compression and rendering
distortions. As the source of the shading, the lighting direction is necessary
to reveal the distortion of the normals. Observers view the sequences in a
6DoF virtual room and are tasked with rating their quality on the Absolute
Category Rating scale (ACR) [19]. During the experiment, we also collect
and analyze the gaze and exploration tracking data of the observers. All
the details on the test content and method are in Section 3. Based on the
collected data, we investigate the following research questions:
RQ1: How does lit rendering impact the perceived quality of compressed dynamic point clouds in VR?
RQ2: Is the behavior of observers affected by different lighting directions?
RQ3: Are current subjective test methodologies efficient for the quality assessment of complex rendering parameters on dynamic content in VR?
RQ4: How do virtual lighting parameters impact the performance of objective
quality metrics?
To answer these questions, we use statistical analyses to assess the significance of our test data and the influence of different lighting directions on
the perceived quality of the compressed DPC and on the reliability of our
test methodology (Section 4). We also thoroughly analyze the collected head
and eye tracking data to assess the impact of lighting on the exploration and
gaze behavior of observers in the context of their task (Section 5). Then,
we use our dataset to build a benchmark of objective quality metrics suitable for the evaluation of point clouds with distorted normals, and observe
how their performance is influenced by various lighting conditions (Section
6). Finally we discuss our results and research questions by identifying our
4
key highlights (Section 7) before concluding. To support further research in
this topic, we introduce the Light-DPCVR dataset, which includes all our
stimuli, quality scores and behavioral data, and make it publicly available1 .
2. Related works
2.1. Quality Assessment and User Behavior in VR
Subjective quality assessment of volumetric videos is an emerging research
topic as mainstream use case applications for this type of media are only
starting to appear. The first such experiment for volumetric videos [20] was
conducted using a 2D display, showing DPCs into rendered videos of rotating
cameras. These conditions therefore left aside the factors that could affect the
experience and perception in XR. More recently, the inherent dimensionality
of these applications has raised attention on the behavior of observers when
confronted with 4D content. Among the few QA studies for volumetric videos
in XR, several are also collecting and analyzing the exploration and headtracking data of the observers [12, 21] to look deeper into their experience. All
these experiments are specifically assessing DPCs of humans. These studies
all seem to show that the freedom given to observers is not being exploited,
as they mostly stay in a single position facing the DPC to evaluate. This lack
of motion could have been due to the context of the QA task that demands
focus on the stimuli. Moreover, even though eye-tracking data may have
been collected in these studies, they were not analyzed and discussed in the
papers.
Zhou et al. [18] run a larger study that compares the visual attention
of observers in a task-free and task-dependent context. Additionally to the
exploration and head tracking, they also collect eye tracking data that they
visualize on 3D saliency and fixation maps highlighting the salient areas on
each point cloud frame. Their results reveal that the QA task has a strong
impact on the behavior as could be observed in previous experiments. In taskfree visualization of DPCs, observers navigate the space more widely around
the sequence. The distribution of their positions is more dependent on the
spatial, temporal and visual information of the viewed sequence. Regarding
their gaze data, Zhou et al. also showed a difference in fixation points on
the same sequences with and without task, which is likely due to a stronger

Rossi et al. [21] and Zhou et al. [18] also point out the importance of

data to train and develop objective metrics that can more accurately reflect
the perceived quality.
2.2. Lighting and Volumetric Media Quality
Lighting is an important aspect of rendering for 3D volumetric content
and an important cue for depth perception in 3D. It is characterized by a
large set of parameters that could each affect the perceived quality differently. Previous works have studied the impact of lighting direction [16] on
the perceived quality of 3D meshes, or material properties on objects rendered with Phong shading [22]. Both studies conclude that the assessed
parameters strongly influence the perception of geometry. More recently, another study [23] shows that real room lighting intensity affects the quality
of colored meshes in Mixed Reality (MR). However, all of these studies are
assessing synthetic static meshes, and their findings have not been verified
and validated for reconstructed natural content or other types of representations such as point clouds, and/or volumetric videos. Van Damme et al. [24]
convert compressed point clouds to meshes to enable more efficient rendering
and assess their quality with different protocols in VR. However, the mesh reconstruction introduces further distortion and no advanced rendering options
such as lighting is considered.
Javaheri et al. [25] have shown that introducing lighting (using the points
normals and vertex shading) on compressed point clouds negatively affects
the quality as the shading reveals geometric distortions. However, in this
study, the normals are estimated after decoding based on the distorted geometry, as the compression methods consider only the coordinates and colors
of the points and not the normals. Yet, more recent objective [15] and subjective [17] quality studies show that adding normals in the compression
process and using the decoded normals for the rendering can greatly improve the quality of the shading over using normal estimation, even at low
bitrates. Indeed, using compressed normals cancels the revealing effect that
6
estimated normals had on the compression artifacts of geometry. This paper
also assesses the performance of objective metrics in comparison with their
subjective test results, highlighting the weight of normals in the quality of
lit point clouds and the need for more specific metrics.
However, even though this last paper focuses on representations of humans as point clouds, the content they consider is not dynamic. Also, the
experiment was run on a 2D display with a passive evaluation task, and the
lighting used to reveal the normals was static across all stimuli, with no variation in direction or intensity. So there has so far been no study about the
impact of lighting on volumetric videos that can extend the conclusions of
the literature for static meshes.
3. Subjective test methodology
In this section we describe all parameters of the subjective experiment we
have run. We detail the generation of stimuli, the test setup and methodology, and provide demographic information about our observers.
3.1. Test Data
Figure 1: Example of frames from the selected DPC of the UVG-VPC dataset.
We collected our source point cloud sequences from UVG-VPC [3], a
recent open database for volumetric videos. It contains 12 sequences, all
7
available as raw point clouds, meshes, and clean point clouds with different Levels of Detail (LoD). We selected 6 sequences as cleaned point clouds
(LoD=10) with normals, according to the clustering of all 12 sources presented by Nguyen et al. [13], based on Spatial information, Temporal information and Colorfulness. Fig. 1 shows examples of frames from each of the 6
selected sources. The BlueSpin sequence will be used only for training purposes during the test and will not be part of the main test. As pre-processing,
we corrected the orientation and coordinates of the sequences to align their
origins (bottom-left-back corner), and applied a smoothing filter (smoothing
range = 15 nearest-neighbors) on the normals with Meshlab [26] to make the
surfaces look more natural under lighting.
We use the V-PCC test model [27] to generate different compression levels
for all the sources, as it is the best-performing method for DPCs and the
method used in all other similar subjective studies in XR [11]. We selected 3
sets of parameters from the JPEG recommendations for point cloud coding
test conditions [10] to encode the color and geometry of the sequences, all in
all-intra configuration with different quantization parameters (QP). We also
encoded and decoded normals with the method suggested in [15]: they are
quantized from normalized 3D vector coordinates to RGB color space, and
mapped like the colors in a new video stream. For each level of quality, we
adjusted the QP for the normals in order to maintain them as equally (for r1 )
or significantly less costly (for r3 and r5 ) than the colors in bitrate. Table 1
shows the QP used for each attribute in each compression level. According
to [17], the impact of the selected QP for the normals on the visual quality
is also similar to that of the QP of the colors.

Finally, we cross each of the compression levels with 4 lighting parameters.
Since our focus in this study is to assess the influence of light direction on
quality, we make the rotation of the directional light vary. We rotate the light
around the x-axis at 3 different angular heights above the horizon that we
use as distortion levels: 0° (L0°), 30° (L30) and 60° (L60°). In order to also
consider the so far standard compliant case where only unlit geometry and
8
color distortions are considered, we also include a level LØ that corresponds
to the case where the DPC is rendered with no shading, showing only the
raw colors of the point clouds.
For all 5 sources, we include their reference and their 3 compression rates,
and show each of these sequences to be rated under all 4 lighting parameters
during the test, which results in a total of 80 stimuli.
3.2. Evaluation Methodology
We use the ACR protocol [19] with a 5-level scale for the quality assessment of the DPC. For each stimulus, the observers were first shown the
sequence, rendered 2 meters away facing the starting position, and were free
to move around, before being asked to return to the starting position to give
their rating. Each sequence plays twice in a row (2x10 seconds) to leave more
time for the observer to explore it. While they are viewing the stimuli, we
collect the position, head movement and rotation, and gaze tracking data of
the observers.
The test is split into 3 sessions. First is a training session to familiarize
the observers with the task. It consists of 8 versions of the BlueSpin source in
a pre-defined order. Then, the main test is split into two sessions of 40 stimuli
in random order, separated by a pause during which observers are free to take
a break in order to avoid strong effects of VR sickness and fatigue [28, 12].
The average durations of the sessions were 3.67 minutes for the training, 17.1
minutes for the first test session, 4.36 minutes for the pause, and 17.3 minutes
for the second test session.
3.3. Test Environment and Material
Figure 2: Lit Virtual test room under the 4 different light settings.
The experiment was performed in-lab, in a test room where the observers
could move around freely and comfortably. In both the real and virtual
room, a mark on the ground was there to indicate the starting position to
9
the observers. The head-mounted display (HMD) is the HTC Vive Focus 3
with an eye tracker add-on that had to be calibrated for every observer. The
HMD and its controllers were fully charged before every test, allowing it to
be used wireless. The device was remotely connected to a VR-ready machine
running the test environment application developed in Unity 2022, equipped
with a Nvidia GeForce RTX 3070 GPU. The application was streamed to the
HMD using VIVE Business Streaming over a dedicated WiFi network. The
source Unity project is made publicly available to enable reproducibility2 .
During the test, observers see the sequences in a virtual room with medium
grey walls and variable lighting. All DPCs are displayed in the virtual room
with the same origin, however their centroids are not aligned due to inherent
differences in the content. Sequences are rendered using a vertex shader with
a point size adapted to the LOD and scale to make the frames look watertight
and cast a clean shadow. For LØ, the shader was unlit, using only the vertex
colors at 100% ambient lighting. For L0°, L30° and L60°, we set the ambient
light intensity to 50% to ensure that the shadows on the surface are visible,
and the directional light intensity to 80% to avoid overexposure. Following
the recommendations of [16], the directional light is oriented slightly on the
left of the initial viewpoint (12° around the y-axis). The only lighting parameter that varies throughout the test is the lighting direction around the
x-axis, which is the assessed influential factor. Fig. 2 show the virtual room
and a DPC frame (CasualSquat_0000 ) under all four light settings.
3.4. Observers
We recruited 43 non-expert observers (65% men and 35% women), aged
19 to 66 years (mean of 37 ± 14.4), from a panel of volunteers with no prior
knowledge on the experiment. All observers passed a visual acuity assessment
and the Ishihara test for color vision, to guarantee that they all had normal
or corrected-to-normal vision. After all tests, we discarded the data of 2
observers, due to a technical issue that prevented the registration of their
inputs and behavioral data. We therefore collected subjective quality scores,
exploration and gaze tracking data from 41 valid observers.
3.5. Behavioral Data Processing
The collected behavioral data consisted of eye-gaze and head-movement
recordings, analyzed to identify differences in exploration patterns across
2

10
experimental conditions. Movement data was used to generate heatmaps of
observer positions within the virtual space and to compute distributions of
head rotations along the pitch axis. The gaze data was used to compute the
point clouds saliency maps and visual attention metrics.
The saliency maps are computed using a method conceptually similar to
that described by Alexiou et al. [29]. However, the algorithm is modified as
follows: fixations are detected using an I-VT algorithm [30] with a velocity
threshold of 100◦ /s and a minimum duration time of 80 ms. The method by
Alexiou et al. was originally designed for static point clouds and relies on
an I-DT approach based on spatial dispersion. In the case of dynamic point
clouds, such an approach can fragment or discard smooth pursuit gazes due to
their inherent spatial variability. On the other hand, I-VT algorithms allow to
better detect gazes even on moving salient elements. In this implementation,
all gaze samples belonging to a fixation are treated as independent gaze
events, meaning that no spatial or temporal averaging is applied. Each gaze
sample therefore produces its own set of salient points. The first 400ms of
data are discarded to avoid unintentional gazes as per [28]. Since the real
tracking error could not be directly estimated, we derived it from the results
of previous studies [31, 32], and adapted to our setup by averaging their
findings to reflect our conditions: observers primarily looking in the center of
the viewport, at approximately 2m the stimuli. We obtain a final error value
of 2.5◦ . For each point cloud, observer, and frame, a cone with its apex at the
gaze origin and its axis aligned with the gaze direction is defined using the
above error angle. All points located within the cone volume are retrieved
and assigned weights according to a Gaussian function centered on the gaze
direction vector.
The algorithm produces, for each point cloud, observer, and frame, a
weighted list of salient points. These lists are aggregated across observers
and frames to compute the final saliency maps. The aggregated weights are
normalized by the maximum saliency value per sequence, and the normalized
values are used to color the point clouds according to their relative saliency.
Using the results from the saliency analysis, fixation points for each point
cloud, frame, and observer are extracted. These fixation points are aggregated across all observers and used to compute the Shannon entropy over a
spatial grid within the virtual scene, within a sliding window of five frames,
to quantify the spatial distribution of visual attention. The window size
was selected so that each window included at least 100 fixation points. The
same fixation points are also aggregated and clustered using the OPTICS
11
algorithm, applied over the same five-frame window, with the default parameters of the scikit-learn implementation [33]. From the resulting clusters, the
number of clusters and their average size are computed as indicators of gaze
concentration and dispersion. The metrics on the number of fixations give
insight on whether observers had a more focused or exploring gaze. To complement this measure, the entropy of the fixations gives a broader view on
the variability of gaze positions across observers, with low entropy indicating
common areas of focus and high entropy related to scattered attention. The
number of clusters is related to the amount of distinct hot-spots that are
common across observers and the average cluster size gives information on
how strongly observers attention converges within those hot-spots.
4. Subjective Results
4.1. Mean Opinion Scores
Figure 3: Average MOS (left) and MOS difference with LØ (right) for all and each source
at different V-PCC compression rates. Each curve corresponds to one light setting.
From the raw opinion scores we collected from the experiment, we compute recovered quality scores, further defined as the MOS, using the soft
outlier removal method of the ITU-T P.910 standard [19]. We obtain biassubtracted and consistency-weighed MOS. Fig. 3 shows the evolution of MOS
with their 95% confidence interval through compression levels for the four
12
different light settings we used. As is visible on these figures, remarkably
higher quality scores were given to the stimuli without directional lighting
(LØ). This is not surprising and is consistent with the results of [17] since
the lighting reveals the normals of the DPCs which were also distorted with
V-PCC. The impact of the different lighting directions (L0°, L30° and L60°)
on the MOS is also noticeable from their difference with LØ. For all sources,
we can see that L60° has a stronger negative influence on the quality. It also
appears that the impact on quality of L0° and L30° is lesser for the heaviest compression rate (r1 ) and the reference. Even though L0° tends to be
slightly superior in quality, L0° and L30° are difficult to compare from these
figures.
In order to illustrate the effect of V-PCC compression independently of
the quality of the lit source content, we also compute the DMOS of the
stimuli, as the difference between the MOS for its source (reference with
the same light setting) and its own. Fig. 4 shows the average DMOS of each
compression level for all light settings. Unlike the MOS, the DMOS indicates
that the impact of heavy V-PCC compression (r1 ) on quality is lesser with
lighting, as LØ gives the highest DMOS while L60° gives the lowest. This
suggests that for heavy compression, an inclined directional lighting has a
masking effect on distortion artifacts that are likely hidden by the shading.
Also unlike for the MOS, it appears that L0° has a slightly stronger impact
than L30° and not the opposite, meaning that the masking of compression
artifacts is more efficient with L30° but that its overall effect on perceived
quality is more negative.
From the MOS of DPC sources individually, we can deduce that some
sequences have a better quality than others. While the MOS of CasualSquat,
ElegantWave and Gymnast never reach above 4 even for their unlit reference
version, the MOS of FlowerDance and ReadyForWinter are generally higher,
suggesting that these sequences have a better reference quality that therefore
has a lesser impact on the MOS. Consistently for these sources, we can see
that the DMOS are more heavily impacted by V-PCC compression. However,
we also notice that the DMOS for L30° and L60° are noticeably lower for
these two sources, implying that the masking effect we observe for all DPC
at r1 could extend to lower compression rates [17] depending on the source
content.

4.2. Statistical Analysis
In order to check the validity of our observations on the MOS figures
and provide elements of responses for RQ1 and RQ3, we run a statistical
analysis on our data and identify the significantly different distortion levels
and sequences. To select the appropriate test, we first evaluated Normality
and Homoscedasticity, which are the key assumptions of ANOVA. Since the
normality assumption was violated, the Friedman test, a within-subject, nonparametric alternative to the ANOVA, was used. When the Friedman test
revealed a significant effect, pairwise comparisons were performed using the
Conover post-hoc test with Holm–Bonferroni correction.
Like in similar studies for DPCs in VR [11], the subjects inconsistencies
(x = 0.44 ± 0.18) and subject bias ([−1.15; 0.85]) are very high on our raw
quality scores. Therefore we do not aggregate the data and run the following
analysis for each observer. We first compare the raw quality scores grouped
by reference. The Friedman tests show that there are statistically significant differences in quality among the source DPCs (p-value < 0.001). The
Conover test between pairs of sources show that all pairs (except CasualSquat
and ElegantWave (p-value=0.62)) are significantly different (p-value<0.05)
in quality. This means that the source content has a non-negligible impact
on the quality ratings.

the MOS. Sources with higher quality (MOS>4 for ref_LØ) have more differences (81% of significance for FlowerDance and ReadyForWinter ) while

For all sources, there is no difference revealed between the light directions
(L0°, L30° and L60°) at compression rate r1. Though it does not appear on
the matrix for the full dataset, we notice that all sources have at least one
pair of light settings that are not significantly different within each compression rate, with only ReadyForWinter at r1 having no difference among light
settings at all. Consistently with our observations on the MOS curves, L0°L30° at the same compression rate are not or less different than other light
setting pairs. Between compression rates, the non-significantly different pairs
of distortions are mostly LØ with other light settings at lower compression
rates, or on the contrary, L60° with other lightings at heavier compression
rates. This highlights the generally poor visual quality of L60°, and higher
quality of LØ which does not include shading distortion.
To further validate the reliability of the collected quality scores, we conduct a MOS discriminability analysis. The discriminability of the MOS is
defined as the percentage of stimuli pairs with statistically significant differences between their quality scores. This test has been used in previous
work to compare datasets with various types of stimuli evaluated in the same
conditions (audio vs visual vs audiovisual [34], long vs short haptics [35]),
or datasets with the same type of content evaluated in different light and
environment settings [36]. In this study, it is used to assess whether the
number of observers in our experiment is sufficient or not [11], and if the different light settings influence the discrimination power of the Light-DPCVR
dataset. For each possible number of observers, we generate 100 random
samples of observers and compute the discriminability using a Wilcoxon test
to detect the significantly different pairs (p-value < 0.05) [37].
Fig. 6(a) shows the evolution of the discriminability for different numbers
of observers with the 5-95% percentile intervals. The maximum discriminability of our dataset (max=0.83) is reached with 39 observers. It is included in
the 5-95% percentile interval for 37 observers, meaning that a convergence
to this maximum is noticeable from that point and that this amount of observers is sufficient to obtain meaningful data. These results are consistent
with similar experiments in the literature [11], which show that the ACR
protocol is adapted for the assessment of compressed DPCs, but costly in
observers.
Fig. 6(b) illustrates the discriminability for each light setting separately.
As all curves behave similarly, the lighting does not seem to influence the
requirements in terms of observers. However, the differences in maximum

5. Observer Behavior Results
To investigate RQ2, we present the results of analyses on the collected
exploration and attention data.
5.1. Movement Patterns
In Fig. 7 and Fig. 8, the aggregated displacement of observers is shown
respectively per sequence and per light condition. Heatmaps are used to
highlight the areas where observers most frequently positioned themselves
while observing the point clouds. The plots represent user position within the
virtual room along the Z (forward–backward) and X (left–right) directions,
with color encoding the spatial distribution of users from unvisited (purple)
17
to most frequently visited (yellow) areas. In Fig. 7, the pink points indicate
the centroids of every fifth frame of each point cloud sequence, while in Fig. 8
the points correspond to every fifth frame across all sequences.
The results in Fig. 7 show no consistent pattern in observer movement
across different sequences. Observers do not exhibit distinct movement behaviors depending on the sequence. However, due to the differences in sequence initialization described in Section 3.3, observers’ relative positions
with respect to the point cloud centroids vary across sequences, with the effect being most pronounced in the FlowerDance sequence, which starts closer
to the observers than the others. This effect is also visible in Fig. 8.
This indicates that observers generally preferred a passive viewing behavior, remaining stationary even when the point clouds appeared closer to them.
Similarly, changes in lighting conditions did not cause noticeable movement,
as shown in Fig. 8. Overall, the data suggest that observers remained largely
still during the experiment and did not actively explore the environment and
the stimuli, which confirms the results of previous studies [12, 21, 18]. As
reported by Zhou et al. [18], this behavior may be explained by the tendency
of users in task-dependent conditions to explore the back of human point
clouds less, as the front is perceived first, and is more salient. Moreover,
evaluating multiple variations of the same sequence is not an engaging task,
which further reduce user interest and movement.
In Fig. 9 and Fig. 10, the distributions of head rotations along the pitch
axis are shown, aggregated respectively by sequence and by lighting condition.
Fig. 9 reveals a clear relationship between the visual content of the sequences and the corresponding head rotation behavior. For example, during
the ReadyForWinter sequence, where most movements and salient events
occur near the face, observers tend to maintain their heads level or slightly
tilted upward to focus on facial details. In contrast, during FlowerDance,
which features greater motion, richer detail, and moments when the character faces away from the viewer, observers exhibit a more exploratory head
movement pattern. The CasualSquat sequence, characterized by a downward
squatting motion, similarly produces a noticeable spread of head rotations.
Meanwhile, Gymnast and ElegantWave fall between these extremes, showing
a balance between focused viewing of the face area and broader exploration
of the full point cloud.
In Fig. 10, the influence of lighting conditions on head rotation is less
pronounced, as behavior appears generally consistent across light settings.

5.2. Statistical Analysis
In order to determine whether the extracted metrics differed significantly
between conditions, a statistical analysis was conducted. Following the same
testing pipeline as in Section 4.2, key assumptions of ANOVA and Welch’s

correction for pairwise comparison when significances were revealed. For aggregated metrics, fixation entropy, number of clusters and mean cluster size,
the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied instead. When the Kruskal–Wallis test
rejected the null hypothesis, the Dunn post-hoc test with Holm–Bonferroni
correction was used for pairwise comparisons.

6. Lighting and Objective QA
To assess RQ4, we evaluated a set of objective metrics for point clouds
and 2D videos by testing the correlation of their predicted scores with our
subjective dataset.
6.1. Objective metrics
The best-performing 3D-based metrics of the state-of-the-art are only developed and tested to consider distortions of geometry and color [9] and are
26
Figure 11: Saliency maps of the 50th frame of the FlowerDance sequence under light
conditions LØ, L0º, L30º, and L60º (from left to right) at reference quality.
not trained and designed to account for the distortion of other attributes.
In Tious et al. [15], the implementation of standardized point cloud metrics
of JPEG Pleno [10] is modified to evaluate decoded normals. The attribute
point2point (p2p) metric [38], which usually consists of a PSNR between the
RGB colors of a distorted point cloud and its source, is modified to instead
compare the normals of two point clouds. For the point2plane (p2plane)
metric, which compares the projection of points on their normal plan [38],
and the plane2plane (AngSIM) metric [39], which is the angular similarity
between the distorted and reference normals, normal estimation is removed
and the already distorted normals are used instead. All these standard metrics compute a quality score for a single point cloud frame. When evaluating
DPCs, Ak et al. [40] show that sub-sampling the DPC distorted with V-PCC,
even down to 1 fps, has no impact on the accuracy of metrics, which allows
to speed up metric computations up to 30 times. Therefore, we compute the
aforementioned metrics only for 11 frames of each stimulus (frames 0, 25, 50,
75, 100, 125, 150, 175, 200, 225 and 249), and the predicted score is defined
as the linear average of each evaluated frame. As only the attributes of the
point cloud files are taken as input, the light setting cannot be considered in
the quality score.

point-based metrics is to use 2D-based image and video quality metrics for
projections of the point clouds. Projection-based metrics for point clouds
are usually computed either on multiple views or on rendered videos with

rotating cameras [41]. Some video metrics such as VMAF [42], were shown

7. Discussion
In this section, we address our research questions by reviewing and discussing the most relevant observations on our results. We interpret the answers we found and look into perspectives for the future of quality assessment
of volumetric videos and rendering related impact factors in XR.
30
7.1. Impact of Lighting on the QoE
Like in previous studies assessing unlit and lit point clouds [15, 25, 17],
stimuli without directional lighting (LØ) have a better perceived quality,
which is due to the absence of the normals and their distortions in the rendering, and the representation of natural content already captured with natural
lighting. This is the first study that extends this observation to dynamic
content. However, this study did not aim to compare lit and unlit DPCs
in terms of quality. Even though observers may find unlit stimuli better,
virtual lighting and lit rendering of volumetric videos is essential to use cases
in cultural and creative industries [46, 47].
Regarding RQ1, our results show that light direction has an impact on
the quality and perception of human-centric volumetric videos, as had been
confirmed for synthetic static 3D content [16]. Consistently with the literature, the MOS of our dataset show that different light directions impact the
perception of geometry differently. Light x-angle of 0° and 30° result in a better perception of depth and quality, and seem more natural to the observers.
On the other hand, our results also suggest that shadows have a masking
effect on distortions that is stronger for higher light angles. Indeed, for the
sources with the best quality, L60° has the lowest DMOS among light settings for all compression rates, and only for r1 over all sources. This means
that despite having a clearly negative impact on the perceived quality, higher
lighting angles also make compression artifacts less noticeable. This masking
effect may also have been strengthened by the darkness of shadows in our
study, as we chose a low ambient light in order to make the shading more
visible. In some application contexts with important staging where light is
part of the scene, the use of such extreme lighting conditions could therefore
help optimize the compression. But for research purposes, in future studies
investigating other influential factors in the rendering (reflectance, smoothness, ...), we advise using a lower natural light direction (25°-30°) that will
limit the impact of the light setting and simply be used to reveal the assessed
rendering parameters.
The analysis of our behavioral data also brought elements to answer RQ2.
Previous studies have shown that in the context of a QA task, observers tend
to stay static during evaluation and do not make use of their freedom [12, 21].
They also show that the movements of observers are mostly dependent on
the source content and not of compression. Though our dataset showed that
lighting also does not affect the movements, we do reveal that it has an effect
on the saliency. While compression revealed to have no impact on the visual
31
attention of users [28], we found that the gaze and fixations of observers
will significantly vary across different light settings and direction, depending
on characteristics of the source content. One limitation of these behavioral
results is that they are only valid for the set of human DPCs evaluated.
Although our results are consistent with the literature, further studies would
strengthen our claims on the influence of lighting.
As we have shown that lighting is important for both the perceived quality
and the gaze behavior of users, we strongly encourage further development
of rendering solutions for relighted volumetric videos and point clouds.
7.2. QA Methodology in VR
Our discriminability analyses on the MOS of our dataset (Section 4.3)
shows consistent results for other datasets also collected through subjective
experiments using ACR [11]. We show that different lighting conditions,
despite negatively affecting the statistical power of the dataset, do not impact
the requirement in observers of the ACR test protocol. Therefore, rendering
parameters such as shading and light direction are influential factors that
can be assessed without affecting the efficiency of current subjective test
protocols. Yet, as advised in Section 7.1, light settings like L60° that poorly
affect overall quality should be avoided in subjective tests and more natural
light direction should be prioritized. Our behavioral data also are similar to
those of previous studies [21], showing that observers keep a passive behavior
when assessing volumetric videos in XR with ACR.
However, the characteristics of the source sequences have a significant
impact on the quality ratings and the behavior of observers. Their original
quality, affected by the reconstruction method [3], also has a strong impact
on the quality. Like in the ComPEQ-MR dataset [13], which also uses sources
of UVG-VPC, the quality scores are relatively low (All MOS<4) compared
to other source content. As other experiments and datasets mostly use the
sources of 8iVFB v2 [48], the development of other source databases such
as V-sense VVDB [5] and UVG-VPC are essential. To limit the impact of
the potential quality gap between different sources, it is important to look
into the DMOS when using ACR, which requires to include the references as
stimuli and therefore makes the experiment longer. In our study, including
the references increased the duration of the test by 33% (20 references and
60 distorted stimuli).
Regarding RQ3, while ACR permits the collection of meaningful data, its
limits lie mostly in its cost (in observers and time). Other rating method32
ologies that make abstraction of the source content quality, such as DSIS
or DSCQS where stimuli are directly compared to their references by the
observers, should be investigated [11]. They could be found less costly than
ACR and lower the biases and inconsistencies of observers [49]. As the display
of stimuli is also different in these methodologies (sequential with reference
for DSCQS, side-by-side for DSIS), analyzing their impact on user behavior
could also help comparing them to ACR.
7.3. Lighting impact on Objective QA
As has been shown in previous studies [15, 17], distortion of normals and
shading strongly impacts the performance of objective metrics. The performance benchmark built on the Light-DPCVR dataset is consistent with
these works, showing that modifying standard point-based metrics to measure and consider distorted normals results in an increase of performance.
Even though the different light settings in the dataset introduce more complex rendering distortions that cannot be considered by simple point-based
measures, the updated point-based metrics still outperform the projectionbased method using state-of-the-art video metrics (VMAF, VIF). As an answer to RQ4, the influence of different light directions on the performance
of metrics is unclear and highly dependent on the metric. But the addition
of lighting has a better influence on the performance of point-based metrics
than perceptual projection-based metrics. Further works and comparisons
with more adapted metrics are needed.
By making the Light-DPCVR dataset available, we also open the gate to
further optimization of objective point cloud metrics. As the standard metrics only consider the point properties, regardless of rendering or attention,
their accuracy could possibly be improved. By adding a shading computation
on the normals of a point cloud with a light direction, point-based metrics
could be updated as "shading-weighted" scores. Our 3D saliency maps could
also be used to train saliency-aware point-based metrics. Other well performing metrics of the state-of-the-art that also extract normal features, such as
PointSSIM [50], could also be improved using our dataset and considering
compressed normals.
Even though they seem outperformed, optimizing projection-based metrics is also a relevant research track to investigate. Methods such as VMAF
are among the most promising QA metrics across all media. Such methods
could be fine-tuned using our dataset, or new metric fusion methods specific
to volumetric videos could be developed.
33
8. Conclusion
In this paper, we present a subjective QoE assessment study evaluating
the impact of different lighting directions on the perceived quality V-PCC
compressed human-centered dynamic point clouds and the exploration and
gaze behavior of observers in virtual reality. Analyses on our subjective test
results reveal that light setting has a significant impact on the quality. We
show that standard light directions (0-30° height) are better for the visual
quality and geometry perception, as previously observed for synthetic 3D
objects, while higher angles and more theatrical lighting could have a stronger
masking effect on compression artifacts. Our gaze and behavior data also
reveal that, while the QA task prevented us from noticing any influence
on the exploration, different light settings do affect the visual attention of
observers differently, depending on the characteristics of the source content.
On the test conditions and methodology, we suggest using natural lighting with minimal impact on the quality for future studies that are focused
on other rendering parameters. We also show that despite providing reliable results, using the ACR protocol for volumetric content in VR is costly
and influenced by factors such as the quality of source content. We suggest
investigating other methods in further works.
With a short benchmark on adapted objective quality metrics, we also
show that the JPEG standard point-based measures are the best-performing,
though they could be improved by adding consideration of lighting direction
and saliency. We also encourage further works to improve the performance
of projection-based metrics. By proposing the Light-DPCVR dataset, which
contains all our stimuli, subjective results, behavioral data and saliency maps,
we enable further research related to lighting and other rendering parameters
in the QoE of volumetric videos.
9. Future Works
In future works, we intend to further evaluate and analyze the influence
of lighting and shading on the performance of objective quality metrics. We
intend to explore and optimize metrics by implementing shading-weighted
or saliency-weighted computations, and by fine-tuning or totally retraining
other existing 3D feature-based and projection-based metrics.
In further exploration of the QoE of volumetric videos in XR, we also
aim to run more subjective tests to compare different test conditions and
34
methodologies, in order to find alternatives to ACR. We plan to compare
ACR with other potentially adapted methods like DSIS or DSCQS in terms
of efficiency for data collection, and in terms of influence on observer gaze
and behavior.
