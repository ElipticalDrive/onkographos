Abstract
Recovering 3D geometry is a crutial task in computer vision, essential for accurate
world reconstruction and perception. Modern applications in AR, VR, autonomous
driving, and medical imaging rely heavily on 3D and 4D reconstruction techniques.
This thesis aims to enhance registration methods, which play a key role in reconstruction, by fusing classical multi-view geometry and deep neural networks. We
explore this theme in three primary directions, each distinguished by registration
dimensionality: 3D – 3D, 3D – 2D, and 2D – 2D.
First, we focus on improving the alignment of 3D point clouds in both rigid
and non-rigid scenarios. In non-rigid 3D registration, traditional methods directly
optimise a motion field between a source and target surface. This often leads to
slow convergence and being trapped to local minimums. We introduce a neural
network-based scene flow to initialise the optimisation, providing a more efficient
and robust solution. Additionally, we present a novel surface normal estimation
technique that aids both rigid and non-rigid registration. Unlike conventional
methods that use a fixed global neighbour parameter, our approach employs a
self-attention mechanism to adapt to local geometry variations.
Second, we address the challenge of registering 2D images to 3D Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) through joint optimisation of NeRF and camera parameters.
Original NeRF training mandates pre-processed camera parameters, creating a
bottleneck in the workflow. Our approach allows for end-to-end camera parameter
estimation during NeRF training while reusing the existing photometric loss in
NeRF. We further extend this to account for larger camera movements by incorporating a monocular depth prior.
Lastly, we propose a method for interest point discovery, which is beneficial for
2D image registration. Unlike existing interest point identification methods that
suffer from significant viewpoint changes and occlusion boundaries, we propose
a multi-view interest point discovery approach to address these limitations. Our
method is trained in a self-supervised fashion with pure-geometric constraints that
encourage point identification repeatability, sparsity, and multi-view consistency.
In summary, this thesis explores the fusion of traditional multi-view geometry
concepts with deep learning priors in various registration tasks, including point
cloud registration, image-to-NeRF registration, and image-to-image registration.
Keywords – 3D Registration, 3D Reconstruction, Multi-view Geometry, NeRF, Point
Cloud, Interest Point Discovery.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Bridging the gap between our physical reality and the digital world, 3D modelling
plays a pivotal role in advancing digital and automated technologies. This demand
arises from two key areas. Firstly, precise 3D modelling is vital for automating
complex tasks within three-dimensional environments, evident in applications such
as object detection, environmental inspections, and autonomous driving. Secondly,
it satisfies our inherent need to interact with digital content as intuitively as we
do in the real world, impacting industries like telecommunications, education,
entertainment, and healthcare. With the growing accessibility of computational
resources and advancements in algorithms, 3D modelling has evolved to be more
practical and accurate than ever before.
One way to model the 3D world is reconstructing 3D structures from 2D images. Depending on whether the scene is static or dynamic, this process is termed
as 3D or 4D reconstruction, respectively. Numerous systems have been developed for these purposes. For static scenes, example systems include Simultaneous
Localisation and Mapping (SLAM) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], Structure-from-Motion
(SfM) [8, 9, 10], Multi-view Stereo (MVS) [11, 12, 13], and Neural Radiance Field
(NeRF) [14, 15, 16, 17]. For dynamic scenes, example systems include 4D recon-
6
struction [18, 19, 20] and non-rigid SfM systems [21, 22, 23].
This thesis focuses on a critical aspect of both 3D and 4D reconstruction:
registration. From a dimensionality perspective, we classify the registration task
into three categories: i) 3D – 3D, ii) 3D – 2D, and iii) 2D – 2D. In the 3D
– 3D context, classical algorithms like Iterative Closest Point (ICP) are used for
registering point clouds [24, 25, 26, 27], and level set evolution methods [28, 29, 30]
are employed for aligning implicit surfaces. In the 3D – 2D domain, Perspectiven-Points (PnP) methods [31, 32, 33] and photometric-based approaches [34, 35]
are used to register a 3D model with a 2D image. In 2D – 2D direction, rigid
registration is often achieved using homography and fundamental matrices [36, 37,
38, 39, 40], while non-rigid registration utilises optical flow methods [41, 42].
While classical registration algorithms are known for their lightweight nature
and interpretability they tend to focus on local structures and textures, which
often leads to local minima and slower convergence in the optimisation process.
In contrast, recent developments in deep learning have introduced data-driven
alternatives [43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. By leveraging high-level understanding, these
method offers superior results, but they can sometimes make elementary errors
due to a lack of geometric grounding. Therefore, it is crucial to balance both highlevel and low-level understanding. For instance, high-level information from datadriven approaches, such as categorical or part-based priors, can serve as coarse
registration guidance. Conversely, low-level or piece-wise geometric consistency
can refine these initial results.
Hence, the goal of this thesis is to study how to combine strengths from both
classical and learning-based techniques and develop robust and accurate registration methods. Specifically, Chapter 3 presents our study in 3D – 3D registration:
where i) Section 3.1 discusses how classical point-to-plane ICP registration can
enhance learning-based scene flow estimation, and how learning-based scene flow
7
estimation is beneficial to complex non-rigid registration, and ii) Section 3.2 introduces a novel point cloud normal estimation method that takes advantage of
self-attention [48] to weight points softly in this task. This learning-based method
aids both rigid and non-rigid registration.
Chapter 4 explores 3D – 2D registration between a Neural Radiance Field
(NeRF) and 2D images, in which Section 4.1 proposes a joint camera parameter
estimation and NeRF training pipeline, removing camera pre-processing overhead,
and Section 4.2 shows how adding a monocular depth network improves handling
of large camera movements.
Lastly, Chapter 5 proposes an interest point discover network. We demonstrate
that introducing geometric constraints to interest point discovery network enables
consistent and repeatable interest point predictions under large view angle changes
and occlusions. This further leads to better 2D – 2D registration performance in
term of related pose estimation.
This chapter is structured as follows. We start with Section 1.1 that expands
the key contributions of this thesis, followed by a thesis outline in Section 1.2, and
end with a publication list in Section 1.3.
1.1
Key Contributions
This thesis focuses on enhancing both 2D and 3D registration techniques through
the fusion of deep neural networks and multi-view geometry concepts. Specifically,
our research advances the registration field in three principal directions.
1.1.1
3D – 3D Registration in Dynamic Scenes
Traditional methods for dynamic and non-rigid registration [49, 50, 29, 30] often
suffer from slow optimisation and prone to local minima. These methods typically
8
operate well only when the motion between source and target is small.
Our research tackles these limitations from two perspectives. In Section 3.1,
we introduce a motion prior in the form of scene flow to guide the optimisationbased registration process. This coarse registration serves as a useful initial step
for a second stage non-linear optimisation. The scene flow is estimated using a
neural network trained solely on synthetic data, and we enhance its performance
by incorporating an Iterative Closest Point (ICP) [25] loss.
Normal estimation in point clouds plays a crucial role in surface alignment
loss metrics such as ICP. Existing methods like Principal Component Analysis
(PCA) [51], Nesti-Net [52], and PCPNet [53] are sensitive to the selection of the
neighbourhood range. For instance, flat regions benefit from larger surface neighbourhoods which reduce local noise, while complex regions require smaller neighbourhoods to prevent blurring. In Section 3.2, we introduce a self-attention-based
method for normal estimation that is insensitive to neighbourhood selection. This
approach soft-focuses on relevant neighbour points and substantially mitigates the
sensitivity issue. The method is advantageous for both rigid and non-rigid 3D
registration.
In summary, Sections 3.1 and 3.2 discuss our contributions to enhancing 3D
registration through point cloud scene flow and normal estimation networks.
1.1.2
3D – 2D Registration with NeRF
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) methods [14] offer photo-realistic novel view synthesis by optimising a radiance and a density volume from multi-view images
using a photometric loss function. However, training NeRF requires pre-computed
camera parameters, typically obtained from Structure from Motion (SfM) tools
like COLMAP [9]. This step can be computationally intensive when dealing with
numerous images, adding significant overhead to NeRF optimisation.
9
In this line of research, we focus on the simultaneous training of NeRF and camera parameter estimation, aiming to alleviate this pre-processing burden. Given
that NeRF operates in a 3D volumetric space and the input images reside in a 2D
domain, we frame this as a 3D-to-2D registration problem.
Our work offers two key contributions. First, in Section 4.1, we introduce
NeRF−−, a straightforward yet effective method for jointly optimising NeRF and
camera parameters, both extrinsic and intrinsic, for forward-facing scenes. We
achieve this by simply considering the camera parameters as trainable parameters
and applying the same photometric loss used in the original NeRF framework.
Given that ray sampling in NeRF depends on camera parameters, the rendering
loss can be back-propagated to these parameters during NeRF training. Our approach introduces little overhead and maintains comparable rendering performance
to a NeRF model trained with pre-computed cameras.
Second, in Section 4.2, we extend NeRF−− with NoPe-NeRF, a model that incorporates monocular depth measurements for joint NeRF and camera parameter
optimisation. Specifically, we learn to undistort monocular depth for each input image during joint optimisation, and constrain the optimisation process with
three factors: i) a point cloud registration constraint through chamfer distance,
ii) a depth-based surface rendering loss, and iii) a depth consistency loss between
rendered depth and monocular depth. As a result, NoPe-NeRF can accommodate
more complex camera movements.
In summary, our contributions in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 present a novel 3Dto-2D framework that jointly optimises camera parameters and monocular depth
distortions during NeRF training.

1.1.3
2D – 2D Registration with Interest Point Discovery
The goal of this work is to enhance the process of interest point identification, a
crucial step in affine transformation estimation for image registration. Currently,
local keypoints based on low-level image features are the norm. These are detected
using either traditional algorithms [54, 55, 56] or neural networks [57, 58, 59,
60]. However, a significant limitation of this approach is that these low-level
features are predominantly surface-based, limiting their detectability across various
viewing angles, distances, and lighting conditions. This limitation has led to the
development of interest points at a higher, more abstract level, especially in specific
categories like human and hand imagery [61, 62, 63, 64]. Yet, extending this
abstraction to a broader range of categories remains a challenge.
In response, Chapter 5 proposes a concept of Abstract Interest Point (AIP),
denoting an abstract-level keypoint that is repeatable across diverse viewing conditions and applicable to a wide range of scenes. To train a neural network to
discover AIPs, we design a geometric-based self-supervised training strategy, including a contrastive term and a triangulation term. Our pipeline avoids relying
heavily on local surface texture, instead focusing on broader scene structures, similar to approaches in human keypoint discovery literature but without assumptions
on scene content.
To sum up, our contribution is enhancing image-to-image registration by introducing a multi-view framework for interest point discovery.

Chapter 2
Literature Review
This literature review serves as a high-level overview of work relevant to the theme
of registration. We initially examine common 3D representations in Section 2.1,
followed by a discussion of both 2D and 3D registration techniques in Sections 2.2
to 2.4. As Sections 3.1, 3.2, 4.1 and 4.2 and Chapter 5 each correspond to an
individual research paper, readers are directed to these chapters for more in-depth
literature reviews in each respective field.
2.1
3D Scene Representations
The field of 3D shape representations has evolved substantially since its beginning
in the 1960s and remains a vibrant area of research. Early works started from
techniques like wire-frame models [66], Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) [67],
and parametric surfaces [68], driven largely by applications in computer-aided
design. Subsequently, point clouds [69] and polygonal meshes [70] were introduced
to accommodate more complex shapes. Implicit surfaces and level sets [71] were
developed to naturally handle changes in topology. The Surfel representation [72]
extended point clouds to provide richer surface details without the complexity of
15
mesh affinity.
Recent advancements in deep learning have given rise to neural-based representations, such as the Occupancy Network (OcNet) [65], Neural Radiance Field
(NeRF) [14], and Multi-plane Images (MPI) [73]. These representations offer several advantages including ease of differentiation during training, high-quality view
synthesis, and efficient storage.
In the context of this thesis, level sets, point clouds, and NeRF are especially
relevant. Section 3.1 delves into point cloud scene flow networks, demonstrating improvements in dynamic reconstruction using level set representations. Section 3.2 introduces a network for point cloud normal estimation. Sections 4.1
and 4.2 build upon NeRF, and Section 4.2 additionally incorporates point clouds
back-projected from monocular depth maps. Chapter 5 discusses a self-supervised
interest point discovery method that relies on point clouds for data preparation
and triangulation during training. A general review of these representations is
provided in the subsequent sections, with more targeted domain-specific reviews
available in each specific section.
2.1.1
Point Cloud
A point cloud is a collection of data points defined within a coordinate system.
Although point clouds are versatile in 3D computer vision field, this thesis focuses
on their use as a simple and efficient representation of surfaces. Common ways
to obtain point cloud include a) gathering raw data from depth sensors such as
LiDAR, radar, depth cameras, ultrasonic sensors; b) extracting data from other
representations such as meshes, distance functions; and c) recovering data through
image-based algorithms such as Structure from Motion (SfM) [8, 9, 10] and Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping (SLAM) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. With point clouds,
it is possible to perform and conduct various tasks, for example, relative pose es16
timation [24, 25, 74, 75, 76], shape classification and segmentation [77, 78, 79],
object tracking [80, 81], and scene flow estimation [43, 82, 83]. We refer readers
to [84] for a more comprehensive survey on point clouds and their applications.
2.1.2
Level Set
A level set is a collection of points in a space where a particular function takes
on a specific value. In simpler terms, it’s like drawing a line or a surface where a
function remains constant. For example, on a weather map, a line showing all the
locations with the same temperature is a level set. These sets help visualise and
analyse the behaviour of functions in various fields like mathematics, physics, and
engineering. In this thesis we narrow the scope to its applications in implicit 3D
surface representation [71], which offers more flexibility during 3D and 4D reconstruction. A commonly utilised level set function for this purpose is the Signed
Distance Function (SDF), which assigns positive distance value to points located
between the surface and the observer, and negative distance value to those behind
the surface. Subsequent advancements introduced Truncated SDF (TSDF) [85]
and voxel hashing [86], enabling more efficient storage and faster computational
operations. Level sets are also closely related to recently developed neural representations such as occupancy network[20, 87] and NeRF [14, 88], which all share
similar concept of occupancy fields and density fields. We direct readers to [89]
for a more complete review on level sets and their applications.
2.1.3
NeRF
Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) [14] is a neural volumetric representation that
utilises a Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) to map 3D positions and viewing angles
to volume density and radiance. This allows for the generation of photo-realistic
17
novel views based on a set of input images and corresponding camera parameters. Numerous enhancements have been made to NeRF, including accelerated
training with explicit voxel structures [90, 91, 15], volume decomposition [92, 93],
Gaussian splatting [16]. To eliminate per-scene optimisation, generalisable NeRF
methods [94, 95, 96, 97] have been proposed. Further extensions include, improving
surface reconstruction accuracy [88, 98, 99], dynamic reconstruction [100, 101, 102],
camera parameter estimation [103, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108], and integration with
large language models [109, 110, 111, 112]. NeRF remains a vibrant and rapidly
advancing field. For the latest updates and comprehensive insights, readers are
advised to consult a recent survey [113].
2.2
3D – 3D Registration
Accurately aligning a source and target surface is a crutial step in 3D and 4D reconstruction. In this thesis, Section 3.1 introduces a scene flow estimation method
that provides a motion prior to non-rigid 3D registration in dynamic environments.
Additionally, Section 3.2 presents a surface normal estimation technique that enhances general 3D surface registration. Here, we present a general review of three
related topics in 3D to 3D registration: static registration, dynamic registration,
and surface normal estimation, whereas Sections 3.1 and 3.2 contain more detailed
paper specific literature review.
2.2.1
Registration in Static Scenes
Registration in static scenes primarily involves pose estimation between two surfaces, where one surface is a rigidly transformed version of the other. The objective
is to estimate a rigid transformation for aligning the surfaces.
Numerous methods exist in the literature, depending on the type of 3D rep18
resentations. Examples include registration between two level sets [114, 115, 28],
between level sets and point clouds [116, 117], and between two point clouds [24,
25, 74, 75, 76]. Recent advances also cover neural representations [118, 119].
In this thesis, Section 3.2 introduces a novel normal estimation method that
aids surface-based registration. Meanwhile, Section 4.2 utilises a Chamfer Distance [120] based loss function for point cloud registration. Therefore, we narrow
our focus to 3D registration between two point clouds.
Methods for registration are commonly categorised based on whether correspondence between surfaces is pre-established. First, correspondence-based methods [74, 75, 121] directly compute a rigid transformation when the correspondence
is known. Second, when the correspondence is not specified, Iterative Closest
Point (ICP) methods are widely used for local alignment. For instance, pointto-point ICP [24] minimises the distance between the closest points on the two
surfaces to estimate rotation and translation. Point-to-plane ICP [25] stabilises
the optimisation using point-to-plane distance metrics. Coloured ICP [27] incorporates photometric information. In the era of deep learning, approaches like
PointNetLK [44] minimise feature distances in ICP formulations, while DCP [45]
leverages transformer architectures [48] to register point sets.
2.2.2
Registration in Dynamic Scenes
Dynamic scene registration entails non-rigid alignment between source and target
surfaces. Two primary categories exist: intrinsic methods, which work in transformed domains, and extrinsic methods that function in 3D space R3 . This thesis
emphasises the extrinsic approach. A more comprehensive survey can be found
in [122].
The essence of non-rigid registration is estimating a displacement field linking
source and target surfaces. Simple methods employ scene flow, depicted as 3D
19
translation vectors [123]. Affine fields [124] extend simple scene flow, capturing
local deformations like rotation and scaling. These methods, however, need to
model motion for each point individually, overlooking spatial correlations among
neighbouring points and leading to expensive computation cost.
To introduce spatial smoothness and improve efficiency, Deformation Graphs
[125] use a sparse set of nodes to model the displacement field of a surface. Each
node has an affine transformation attached that transforms a local region and is
linked to neighbour nodes, enabling local smoothness enforcement. Despite its
efficiency, defining and maintaining the graph is challenging.
Recently, the integration of neural networks into the non-rigid 3D registration
domain has seen notable progress, particularly in areas like scene flow estimation. This includes advancements in point clouds [43, 126, 127, 128], occupancy
fields [20], and NeRFs [101, 102]. In Section 3.1, we explore how scene flow estimation networks and conventional surface registration techniques can be mutually
beneficial. For instance, using network-estimated scene flow as an initial step in
dynamic reconstruction tasks can enhance registration convergence. Conversely,
incorporating a point-to-plane Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration objective
during scene flow network training can significantly improve the training efficiency
and scene flow prediction accuracy.
2.2.3
Point Cloud Normal Estimation
Normal estimation is crucial for tasks like surface reconstruction [129, 130], rendering [131], and registration [25]. Classical methods like PCA [51] employ eigen
decomposition on the covariance matrix of a local point cloud patch to find the
normal vector. Jet [132] uses a truncated Taylor expansion to estimate both the
surface normal and principal curvatures.
Recent learning-based methods like PCPNet [53] use PointNet [77] on a local
20
patch to directly regress a surface normal. Nesti-Net [52] employs a Mixture of
Experts (MoE) system [133, 134] that processes various neighbour scales around
a target point, with each expert specialising in different geometric properties.
Although these learning-based approaches outperform PCA and Jet, they require larger models and longer inference times. They also limit information flow
due to their manual handling of multiple scales. These shortcomings are addressed
in Section 3.2 via a new attention-based lightweight normal estimation technique.
2.3
3D – 2D Registration
The task of aligning a reconstructed 3D model with a 2D image is known as 3D
– 2D registration. The Perspective-n-Point (PnP) algorithm [31, 32, 33, 135] is
a conventional method in this area, mapping a 3D point cloud to corresponding
2D points on an image plane. However, PnP is dependent on estimated correspondences, often obtained through feature matching, which can be both slow and
prone to errors.
An alternative approach is intensity-based registration, which registers two
entities using photometric information, such as RGB, gray-scale, or object silhouettes. This line of work is initially developed for medical applications such as
image-guided therapy (IGT) [34, 136, 137, 138], which aligns 3D volumes such as
computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) volumes and
2D scans such as X-ray. Similar idea has also been incorporated into Simultaneous
Localisation and Mapping (SLAM) systems such as DTAM [35], LSD-SLAM [5],
and DSO [139].
With the advent of neural representations like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF),
which require costly pre-processing of camera parameters, there has been a growing focus on reducing this computational burden. Section 4.1 introduces our
21
approach that jointly optimises a NeRF model and camera parameters, minimising the overhead while maintaining high-quality novel view synthesis. Section 4.2 extends this work by incorporating monocular depth to accommodate
larger camera movements. Evolving from our research, notable contributions in
this domain include SPARF [107], which leverages dense correspondence from
optical flow networks to estimate poses in sparse views pose scenarios, and RoDynRF [140], which focuses on optimising camera parameters in dynamic NeRF
setups. To further facilitate large scale 3D understanding, there is a growing interest in replacing per-scene pose optimisation with generalisable pose estimation
pipelines [141, 142, 143, 144, 145, 146], and with pose-free pipelines, for instance,
LEAP [147].
Additional methods in 3D – 2D registration include absolute pose regression [47, 148, 149] and scene coordinate regression [150, 151, 152]. In this thesis
we work closely with NeRF-based registration, and we refer readers to [138, 153]
for more detailed review in 3D – 2D registration domain.
2.4
2D – 2D Registration
Registering two images involves estimating a transformation that aligns one image
with another. This field can be categorised based on the type of transformation
involved: either rigid or non-rigid.
In the context of non-rigid transformations, the problem is often framed as
motion estimation. Various techniques have been proposed for this, including
optical flow [41, 42, 154, 155, 156], diffusion process [157], level sets evolution [158,
159], and correspondence flow [160, 161, 162, 163, 164].
Conversely, estimating rigid transformations between two images is equivalent to estimating epipolar relationships. This is typically parameterised using
22
either fundamental matrices or homography transformations [36, 37, 38, 39, 40].
A common pipeline for these estimations includes interest point detection, feature
extraction, feature matching, and ultimately solving a set of linear equations. In
Chapter 5, we introduce a method for discovering highly repeatable interest points
from multi-view inputs, contributing to the initial stage of image-to-image registration. We direct readers to extensive reviews [165, 153, 166] for a full landscape
in this topic.

This chapter concerns non-rigid 3D registration, a key component in dynamic
reconstruction systems. Considering two surfaces, a source surface and a deformed
target surface, we aim to recover a non-rigid transformation between these two
surfaces. Classical methods in this area mainly include level set evolution [28,
29, 30], non-rigid ICP [49, 50, 19], and scene flow estimation [167, 168, 29, 30].
However, due to the inherent complexity of non-rigid registration, which involves
a high degree of freedom in optimisation, the methods mentioned above can only
handle only limited motion between two surfaces.
We address this issue from two perspectives. First, in Section 3.1, we propose to
initiate classical non-rigid registration with a scene flow initial solution. The initial
solution is estimated from a point cloud neural network. We additionally enhance
the scene flow estimation network by introducing several geometry constraints
during training. Second, in Section 3.2, we contribute to the 3D registration task,
including both rigid and non-rigid, by introducing a novel point cloud normal
estimation method. Our method consists of a self-attention based network that
24
weights each point softly during normal estimation, resulting in more accurate
normal estimation and faster surface registration.
In summary, we introduce three contributions in non-rigid registration. First,
we propose a two-stage non-rigid reconstruction pipeline, where the first stage
provides a initial alignment through a point cloud scene flow estimation network,
and the second stage conducts a classical optimisation-based refinement. Second,
we enhance existing scene flow network generalisation capability by introducing a
self-supervision signal from classical ICP. Third, we introduce a novel point cloud
normal estimation method, further contributing to non-rigid 3D registration task.
3.1
FlowNet3D++: Geometric Losses For Deep
Scene Flow Estimation
In this section, we present our research paper on scene flow estimation. Our
work builds on FlowNet3D [43], the pioneering point cloud scene flow estimation
network introduced in 2019. FlowNet3D estimates 3D scene flows, describing
the movement of each point between two point clouds. It works by first using a
PointNet-style encoder to down-sample and extract local features from both point
clouds. These features are then aggregated via a neural network to determine
the implicit correspondence between the point clouds. Finally, the network upsamples and decodes this correspondence into scene flow vectors. FlowNet3D is
trained with L2 loss on the FlyingThing3D [169] synthetic dataset using ground
truth scene flows.
While this pipeline demonstrates the feasibility of estimating scene flow between two point clouds using a neural network, we enhance the existing FlowNet3D
model by integrating geometric principles, specifically the point-to-plane distance
and angular alignment. These geometric constraints not only improve the accu25
racy of scene flow estimation but also contribute to training stability and faster
convergence.
In addition to enhancing scene flow estimation, we propose improving 3D-3D
registration in dynamic scenes by incorporating our improved scene flow network
into an established dynamic reconstruction pipeline [29]. We employ our network
as an initial step, followed by further refinement stages. This two-stage approach
notably elevates the accuracy of dynamic reconstruction.
In summary, our contributions to the field of non-rigid 3D registration are
twofold. First, we introduce two additional loss terms to enhance the training
of scene flow estimation networks. Second, we integrate our improved scene flow
network into an established dynamic reconstruction pipeline, resulting in increased
accuracy in dynamic reconstruction.

Abstract
We present FlowNet3D++, a deep scene flow estimation
network. Inspired by classical methods, FlowNet3D++ incorporates geometric constraints in the form of point-toplane distance and angular alignment between individual
vectors in the flow field, into FlowNet3D [21]. We demonstrate that the addition of these geometric loss terms improves the previous state-of-art FlowNet3D accuracy from
57.85% to 63.43%. To further demonstrate the effectiveness of our geometric constraints, we propose a benchmark
for flow estimation on the task of dynamic 3D reconstruction, thus providing a more holistic and practical measure
of performance than the breakdown of individual metrics
previously used to evaluate scene flow. This is made possible through the contribution of a novel pipeline to integrate
point-based scene flow predictions into a global dense volume. FlowNet3D++ achieves up to a 15.0% reduction in
reconstruction error over FlowNet3D, and up to a 35.2%
improvement over KillingFusion [32] alone. We will release
our scene flow estimation code later.
1. Introduction
Scene flow is defined as a 3D vector field that provides
a low level representation of 3D motion. It is analogous to
optical flow, which describes the pixel movements on a 2D
image plane. Optical flow can be considered the projection
of scene flow into 2D. Applications such as object detection,
object tracking, point cloud registration, correspondence estimation and motion capture can benefit from this low-level
information for better performance.
Although the vector field representation is simple, scene
flow estimation is far from an easy task. This is due to the
requirement of accurate depth estimation and also the need
to deal with occlusion. Traditionally, scene flow estimation is computed by optimising photometric error [21], or
through matching hand-crafted features [3], each applied
over multiple view geometry or RGB-D images. With the
fast development of deep learning, some works bring CNN
to scene flow estimation. This allows for the scene flow estimation to benefit from the semantic information and powerful deep feature extraction. Recently, PointNet [5] and
PointNet++ [29] enabled the direct point cloud processing
for deep learning. These works are particularly interesting
since they are point cloud-based networks that can directly
process the rich 3D geometric information, rather than implicitly learning 3D geometry from 2D images. Built on
top of PointNet++, FlowNet3D [21] tackles the scene flow
estimation problem on point cloud directly, achieving the
state-of-art scene flow estimation results. Despite the impressive results of FlowNet3D, point cloud based scene flow
estimation is still at the very beginning of its development.
FlowNet3D trains the network with a naive supervision signal, which is the L2 loss between predicted flow and ground
truth vectors.
In this work, we apply geometric principles from classical point cloud registration algorithms in order to mature
deep scene flow estimation beyond the simple L2 norm between prediction and ground truth. In particular, we investigate two geometric constraints including: 1) point-toplane distance and 2) the cosine distance between predicted
flow vector and ground truth vector. The point-to-plane distance is a common loss term in Iterative Closest Point (ICP)
[2, 26] algorithm, which is known for fast convergence. Cosine distance can penalise the angle between two vectors directly. As a result of this, cosine distance encourages correct
alignment of our predicted scene flow vectors, not only that
they lie on the L2 norm surface. The application of these
geometrically principled constraints not only demonstrates
improved accuracy over the state-of-art, but also improved
convergence speed and stability of training.
Further, we introduce a novel benchmark for investigating the practical performance of scene flow estimators,
through the proxy task of dynamic 3D reconstruction. Application of scene flow to dynamic reconstruction provides
a holistic combination of the individual metrics previously
used to evaluate 3D flow estimation. We contribute a novel
pipeline for integrating point-based scene flow into a global
dense volume.
To summarise, our main contributions are:
1. We improved the state-of-art point cloud-based scene
flow estimation accuracy from 57.85% to 63.43% by
combining point-to-plane loss, cosine distance loss
with L2 loss.
2. We propose an average angle error metric to evaluate
the flow direction deviation to supplement End Point
Error (EPE), which is not sufficient alone to evaluate
the angle difference between two vectors.
3. We propose dynamic 3D reconstruction, alongside
a novel dynamic integration pipeline, as a benchmarking task for scene flow estimation. 3D reconstruction provides a holistic and inherently geometric measure of flow estimation performance.
Within our deformable scene flow benchmark task, our
FlowNet3D++ achieves up to 15.0% less reconstruction error than FlowNet3D, and up to a 35.2% improvement over KillingFusion.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: in
Section 2, we briefly describe related work. Section 3 describes our modifications to FlowNet3D, as well as our
method to integrate the sparse scene flow vector field in
dense dynamic reconstruction. In the experiment section,
we evaluate the effect of adding different geometric constraints to scene flow estimation and our dynamic reconstruction results with several public datasets. Section 6 concludes our work and describes potential future work.
2. Related Work
2.1. Tradition Scene Flow Estimation
Scene flow is a low-level representation of 3D motion of
points within a scene. It is a 3D extension from the 2D optical flow [3], which itself describes the pixel movements on
a 2D image plane. Many works have focused on estimating
scene flow using multi-view geometry [35] by associating
salient image key points. Later works [27, 11, 37, 36]
tackle this problem with joint variational optimisation of
image registration and motion estimation. [37] compute
dense scene flow from stereo cameras and achieved 5fps
on a CPU. SphereFlow [10, 15] is the first real-time scene
flow estimation system using RGB-D input. [16] proposed
to process rigid and non-rigid segments differently.
2.2. Deep Flow Estimation
The recent development in deep neural networks provides an alternative to address the problem of associating
points over deformed depth maps. One group of deep methods can be viewed as the successors of the classic 2D optical flow methods. For example, FlowNet [7] and its variants [12]. Instead of using hand-crafted feature for tracking
pixel locations, these methods rely on learned deep features
for tracking and then back-project into depth maps to fetch
the 3D scene flow. For better training and evaluation, Mayer
et al [24] created three synthetic scene flow datasets. They
also proposed a network for disparity and scene flow estimation. [30] assume a dynamic scene contains foreground
objects and background and apply instance segmentation
masks over foreground to treat foreground and background
differently.
Similarly, [31] developed a neural network that jointly
estimates object segmentation, trajectories of objects, and
the object scene flow from two consecutive RGB-D frames.
Ilg et al. [13] proposed a network based on FlowNet [7] to
estimate occlusions and disparity together. [23] integrates
three vision cues to estimate scene flow for rigid objects
in self-driving tasks. The three vision cues are segmentation masks, disparity map, and optical flow and they are extracted by existing networks, i.e. Mask R-CNN [9], PSMNet [4], and PWC-Net [34].
All the above approaches are mostly image-based so
that appearance features can be conveniently extracted using 2D convolution. However, some sensory data such
as laser scanners is unstructured and therefore conventional convolution is not applicable. To address the problem, Behl et al [1] evaluated the performance of scene
flow estimation when integrating bounding box and integrating pixel-wise segmentation to scene flow estimation
pipeline. [1, 22, 8] are designed for scene flow estimation
on the point cloud. PointFlowNet [1] proposed to estimate
scene flow, ego-motion and rigid object motion at the same
time. In comparison to the PointFlowNet, FlowNet3D [22]
and HPLFlowNet [8] are more general scene flow estimation frameworks that do not rely on rigid object assumption. More specifically, FlowNet3D extracts features with
PointNet++ [29], mixes features and computes a coarse
scene flow using a flow embedding layer, and propagates
coarse scene flow to finer level using a set-upconv layer.
HPLFlowNet, instead of using PointNet++, states that using permutohedral lattice[20] and Bilateral Convolutional
Layer (BCL) [17] can improve global information extraction and faster performance.

3.2. Scene Flow for Dynamic 3D Reconstruction
The performance of flow field estimation on a dynamic
scene is normally evaluated through the counting of inliers,
which are determined through a set threshold. However, this
evaluation scheme depends heavily on the threshold, which
must be set heuristically. We propose to benchmark scene
flow framework based on a state-of-the-art dynamic 3D reconstruction system, so that the scene flow can be evaluated
by viewing a 3D model. This provides a more holistic performance measure, as well as a practical application for 3D
flow estimation.
Dynamic 3D reconstruction is recently introduced for recovering non-static objects, including deformable objects
such as moving animals or human beings [25, 14, 32, 33, 18,
19]. KillingFusion [32] and its variant SobolevFusion [33]
represent the state-of-the-art dynamic fusion method directly estimating a dense vector field between two TSDF
volumes. However, this variational optimisation process is
easily trapped in local minimum when the search space is
large. Our benchmark framework, which can also be considered as a dynamic reconstruction system, significantly
outperforms KillingFusion in terms of quality by a 35.2%
reduction in mean error.

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced FlowNet3D++, which to
the best of our knowledge is the state-of-art point cloudbased deep scene flow estimator. We contribute two principled geometric constraints that each improve the accuracy of the state-of-art of point cloud based deep scene flow
from 57.85% to 63.43%. We also contribute a novel geometric based scene flow benchmark pipeline in dynamic
reconstruction context. Within our deformable scene flow
benchmark, our FlowNet3D++ achieves up to 15.0% less
reconstruction error than FlowNet3D, and up to a 35.2%
improvement over KillingFusion alone.

Neighbourhood-Insensitive Point Cloud Normal Estimation Network
In this section, we present our research paper on point cloud normal estimation, an
essential step for surface registration and reconstruction. This topic is particularly
relevant to ICP algorithms, which play a key role in our scene flow network training
as detailed in Section 3.1.
Traditional techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [51] and
Jet [132] requires a manually tuning of a global hyper-parameter, specifically the
local neighbourhood radius, for optimal performance. This poses a challenge when
handling scenes with complex geometries. Subsequent methods [53, 52] address
this limitation by utilising multi-scale patches at a cost of complex system design.
We introduce a novel self-attention-based normal estimation network. This
architecture allows each point contributes softly in normal estimation task, offering more accurate results while eliminating the need for manual neighbourhood
range tuning and the use of multi-scale patches. Moreover, our design leads to
significantly smaller model size and faster inference time. We further demonstrate
the effectiveness of our model in the point-to-plane ICP registration task, showing
faster convergence without neighbourhood range tuning.

Abstract
We introduce a novel self-attention-based normal estimation network that is able to
focus softly on relevant points and adjust the softness by learning a temperature parameter, making it able to work naturally and effectively within a large neighbourhood
range. As a result, our model outperforms all existing normal estimation algorithms
by a large margin, achieving 94.1% accuracy in comparison with the previous state of
the art of 91.2%, with a 25x smaller model and 12x faster inference time. We also
use point-to-plane Iterative Closest Point (ICP) as an application case to show that our
normal estimations lead to faster convergence than normal estimations from other methods, without manually fine-tuning neighbourhood range parameters. Code available at
https://code.active.vision.
Introduction
Normal estimation is an important low-level task that forms the foundation for many highlevel applications, such as tracking, reconstruction, and rendering. Principle Component
Analysis (PCA) [11], the classic normal estimation method, estimates a normal vector at a
point by collecting its k nearest neighbours (k-NN) and fitting a plane on them. It is worth
noting that, the neighbourhood range k, is a global hyper-parameter requiring manual tuning
for PCA to achieve best accuracy. In practice, a single global k is often not suitable to
an entire scene, where different regions have different geometric structures. Therefore, an
algorithm that can determine the neighbourhood range adaptively based on local geometric
structures and is insensitive to the neighbourhood variance is desirable, as downstream tasks
can enjoy high-quality normal estimations without manually tuning neighbourhood range
parameters.
One possible approach to achieve this is to identify appropriate neighbourhood scales to
estimate normals under different geometric properties. One line of research [2, 8] aims to
solve this problem by integrating information from multi-scale patches. Here, the experts
that specialise at small scales are responsible for sharper regions and the experts that specialise at large scales are responsible for smoother regions. A scale manager can choose to
weight estimations from different experts. This approach is effective and does not require
a manual fine-tuning of the neighbourhood range, but it comes at the expenses of a large

neural network, i.e. for a model that processes L scales, the model is L times as large as a
single scale model, for example, the 7-expert Nesti-Net [2] model is about 1.1GB.
In this paper, we propose a normal estimation network that selects neighbour points softly
according to its local geometric properties, by applying a multi-head self-attention module
to the neighbour points around the current point. Our model allows a soft selection of neighbour points by learning a temperature parameter that controls the softmax function within
the attention module. As a result, our method does not need a carefully picked k. Given a
fairly large neighbourhood range, for example, setting k to 30, 40, or even 50 when the best
k for PCA is 8, our network is able to focus on relevant points adaptively and outperforms all
existing normal estimation algorithms consistently, with a significantly smaller model and
faster inference time. We also provide a point-to-plane ICP [6] application study to demonstrate how downstream algorithms like ICP can benefit from our neighbourhood-insensitive
normal estimation.

Related Work
In this section, we present approaches related to our work, starting with neural network point
cloud reasoning, moving to normal estimation from point clouds and ending with selected
attention-based methods.
Neural Network Point Cloud Reasoning Learning-based 3D point cloud reasoning is
an active research area. Su et al. [20] first proposed to process point clouds using CNNs
on their 2D projections. This method can extract semantic information but lose geometric
details significantly. Qi et al. [18] proposed to analyse point clouds using volumetric representations so that 3D CNNs could be applied directly. This method preserves 3D geometric
structures but has an expensive computational cost. Geometric details are also lost during
the volumetric quantization. To overcome these issues, PointNet [5] was proposed to consume point clouds directly. The follow-up work PointNet++ [19] improves the performance
using a hierarchical strategy. More recent works, such as DGCNN [25], PointCNN [14], and
KPConv [21] combine graph neural networks and deformable convolutions with point cloud
processing. These approaches focus on standard tasks like classification and segmentation.
The next section focus on approaches for normal estimation.
Point Cloud Surface Normal Estimation PCA [11] is a classic method for normal
estimation. Given a target point and its neighbours, PCA treats these points as samples from
a multivariate distribution, of which the three spatial coordinates x, y, z are three independent
random variables. By applying eigen-decomposition to the covariance matrix of this point
cloud patch, the eigenvector that has the smallest eigenvalue points to the normal direction or
its reverse direction. Jet [4] estimates a normal vector by fitting a truncated Taylor expansion
to the local point cloud patch. Geometric properties such as surface normal and the two
principle curvatures can be computed from the Taylor expression.
Recently, learning-based point cloud normal estimation approaches have been proposed.
PCPNet [8] applies PointNet to a local patch and regresses a surface normal directly. To
adapt to local geometries better, it also introduces a multi-scale model that runs PointNet [5]
at three different local neighbourhood scales. Similarly, Nesti-Net [2] is a Mix of Expert
3
Z. WANG AND V. PRISACARIU: NEIGHBOURHOOD-INSENSITIVE NORMAL
(MoE) [9, 12] system that processes 7 different scales of neighbours around a target point.
Each expert learns to specialise at regions with certain geometric properties and outputs confidences at inference time. To obtain the final result, a manager network integrates normal
estimations from all experts and outputs a final result. Both methods tackle normal estimation by exploring more scale levels and balancing results in the multi-scale inferences. This
line of approaches achieves better performance than classical PCA and Jet but also requires
a large model and a long inference time. The multi-scale setting also prohibits information
flow among sub-networks. Our method improves both performance and efficiency by using a single network that learns to select point information softly and can adapt to different
geometries without quantizing a local patch to a number of scales.
Attention Mechanism Attention was first introduced by Bahdanau et al. [1] to handle long-range dependencies in language processing tasks together with Recurrent Neural
Networks (RNN). Self-attention or intra-attention [7, 15] was proposed to understand relationships within a sequence itself. Vaswani et al. [22] designed multi-head attention and
replaced RNNs with self-attention to enable parallel processing, which is difficult to achieve
in RNNs. Recently, attention mechanisms have been introduced to the point cloud domain
to explore relationships within a point cloud. These methods use attention mechanism to enhance semantic understanding over the entire point cloud. [26, 27] uses attention mechanism
to sample points. Wang et al. [23] employs self-attention in semantic segmentation tasks.
DCP [24] applies attention mechanism to find correspondences between two point clouds in
a registration task. Inspired by prior work, we propose applying attention to normal estimation, a task that requires an understanding of local geometric structures.

local patch, as input and outputs the predicted surface normal for this local patch as a three

pipeline, elaborates our key attention module, and provides implementation details in the
end.

In this section, we demonstrate two benefits of our neural network: 1) our method is much
less sensitive to the neighbourhood range in comparison with other approaches. 2) our
method achieves state of the art performance with a much smaller model and a much faster

Attention Weights Analysis
In this section, we discuss why the performance of our network does not degrade when the
number of neighbours are increasing, as in PCA. In fact, our network performs better when
more neighbours are available. To explain this, we show 4 attention maps from our models
in Fig. 4. The four models we use to generate these attention maps are trained and tested
using local patches contain 5, 10, 25, and 50 neighbours and achieves PGP10 92.5%, 93.0%,
94.0%, and 93.3%, from left to right, while the best PCA performance is 91.4%. In an
attention map, each local patch is represented by a row of pixels and attention weights are
colour-coded by pixel intensity.
From the attention map over 5 and 10 neighbours, (a) and (b), we can see that the network
pays more attention to neighbours that are close to the patch centre and ignores points farther
than 8th neighbours. This agrees with the PCA performance in Fig. 2. From the attention
map over 25 and 50 neighbours, (c) and (d), we observe that the network pays extra attention
to points at the right-end in a row. These are points far from the patch centre. To illustrate
this better, we show the attention weights predicted by our network in 3D in Fig. 5. These
attention weights visualisations suggest that the network learns to identify geometric properties around the current point using points from larger scales, by inspecting, for example,
whether the patch contains a sharp edge or a corner. Therefore, our network maintains a relatively stable performance despite different numbers of neighbours by focusing on relevant
points.

4.5
Application Study: ICP Point Cloud Registration
In this section, we demonstrate how other downstream algorithms can benefit from our
neighbourhood-insensitive normal estimation network. We take the point-to-plane ICP [3, 6]
algorithm as an example and show that, by simply setting the number of neighbours k to a
large enough number, for example, k = 50 in our network, ICP with our normal estimation
converges faster than with other normal estimation methods. Moreover, ICP with classical
normal estimation methods like PCA requires fine-tuning k manually to achieve best performance, which is still outperformed by our network.
ICP Preliminary ICP is a widely used point cloud registration and pose estimation
method. Many variants have been proposed in past decades. The original point-to-point
ICP [3] optimises the distance between closest point pairs directly. The point-to-plane
ICP [6] is proposed to improve the converging speed and robustness against noise. Given
two point clouds, a source point cloud Xs and a rigid transformed destination point cloud
Xd = Tds Xs , ICP computes the rigid transformation Tds ∈ SE(3) to register Xd and Xs iteratively. The point-to-point energy is defined as
E pt−pt = ∑(kxs − xd k2 )
(4)
and the point-to-plane energy is defined as
E pt−plane = ∑(nd · (T̃ds xs − xd ))2
(5)
where T̃ds denotes the estimated transformation, xs is a point in Xs , xd is the corresponding
point in Xd , and nd is the surface normal at xd .

the point-to-plane ICP converges faster than using normals from PCA [11] and Nesti-Net [2].

evaluating registration results. A perfect registration should produce E pt−pt = 0 but the
E pt−plane can still be larger than zero because the normals estimations are not perfect. We
stop
to 10−5 . For simplicity, we set the rotation angles beset the stopping threshold E pt−pt
tween Xs and Xd to 10◦ in all three axes (x, y, z), and the translation between Xs and Xd
to [0.01, 0.01, 0.01]. We scale all point clouds to a unit sphere and shift them to the point
cloud centre before running ICP. We use the Levenberg-Marquardt [13, 16] solver to optimise E pt−plane . Our ICP code comes along with the training code at https://code.

5 Conclusion
In this work, we propose an attention-based normal estimation network that is insensitive
to neighbourhood range and provides state of the art normal estimation performance. We
achieve this by applying an attention module to the local neighbourhood so our network
can focus on relevant points and integrate information softly. With a learnable temperature
parameter, our network can also control how attentive it should be under current neighbourhood settings. As a result, our network is 12x faster and 25x smaller than the previous state
of the art models [2] and provides 2.9% higher accuracy (PGP 10) on the PCPNet dataset [8].
We also use point-to-plane (ICP) as an application to show that our normal estimations lead
to faster convergence than normal estimations from other methods, without manually finetuning neighbourhood range parameters.

Chapter 4
3D – 2D Registration with NeRF
This chapter addresses the problem of aligning a set of 2D images with a global
3D model, namely 3D – 2D registration. Representative approaches in this field
include Perspective-n-Point (PnP) [31, 32, 33], absolute pose regression (APR) [47,
148, 149], scene coordinate networks [150, 151, 152], and photometric methods [34,
136, 137, 35, 139]. However, these methods primarily rely on classical representations such as point cloud and voxel.
Recently, the advent of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) has significantly improved performance in various 3D tasks, including novel view synthesis [14, 15, 92],
3D reconstruction [88], and generative models [112]. One limitation in NeRF
training is that it often requires a time-consuming pre-processing step to estimate
camera parameters for input images, for example, camera extrinsics and intrinsics.
This chapter presents a series of two papers aimed at eliminating the need for
this pre-processing step. In Section 4.1, we propose to optimise camera parameters
along with NeRF training. In specific, our method consider camera parameters
as learnables during NeRF training and update them with photometric loss during NeRF training. Since the photometric loss is shared with the original NeRF
pipeline, our method add little overhead to NeRF training, while removing the
59
pre-processing step.
In Section 4.2, we propose to extend Section 4.1 to handle larger camera motion
by leveraging monocular depth. We integrate monocular depth with joint camera and NeRF training pipeline from three perspectives: i) providing geometry
prior to NeRF training, ii) supporting a 3D registration between depth projected
point clouds, and iii) enabling surface rendering in addition to original volumetric
rendering. Since monocular depth can be estimated efficiently, our method introduces little overhead while dramatically enhance the performance under large
camera motion.
In summary, we propose a line of work in joint camera parameter estimation
and NeRF optimisation, contributing to the theme of 3D – 2D registration with
NeRF representation.

NeRF−−: Neural Radiance Fields Without

In this section, we present a paper that focuses on aligning 2D images with Neural
Radiance Fields (NeRF) volumes. Standard NeRF training employs ray casting
and volumetric rendering to achieve multi-view photometric consistency. This
approach inherently depends on pre-computed camera parameters.
In this work, we eliminate the requirement for such pre-computation, streamlining the NeRF training process. Our approach treats camera parameters as
trainable variables during NeRF optimisation. This allows us to recover camera
parameters by back-propagating the photometric error in the ray-casting procedure. As a result, our method integrates seamlessly with the existing NeRF training pipeline, introducing minimal computational overhead while providing comparable novel view synthesis quality to the original NeRF method that requires
60
pre-defined camera parameters.
Moreover, we contribute a synthetic dataset to aid in evaluating the performance of concurrent camera parameter and NeRF optimisation. Existing datasets
fall into two main categories: one includes real-life images with pseudo ground
truth camera parameters obtained from SfM [9] or SLAM [6] systems; the other
contains ground truth camera parameters but with over-simplified synthetic scenes.
Our dataset fills this gap by offering both accurate ground truth and photo-realistic
scenes, thereby facilitating assessment of joint estimation techniques.

Abstract
Considering the problem of novel view synthesis (NVS) from only a set of 2D
images, we simplify the training process of Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) on
forward-facing scenes by removing the requirement of known or pre-computed
camera parameters, including both intrinsics and 6DoF poses. To this end, we propose NeRF−−, with three contributions: First, we show that the camera parameters
can be jointly optimised as learnable parameters with NeRF training, through a
photometric reconstruction; Second, to benchmark the camera parameter estimation
and the quality of novel view renderings, we introduce a new dataset of path-traced
synthetic scenes, termed as Blender Forward-Facing Dataset (BLEFF); Third, we
conduct extensive analyses to understand the training behaviours under various
camera motions, and show that in most scenarios, the joint optimisation pipeline
can recover accurate camera parameters and achieve comparable novel view synthesis quality as those trained with COLMAP pre-computed camera parameters.
Our code and data is available at https://nerfmm.active.vision.

Introduction
Generating photo-realistic images from arbitrary viewpoints not only requires to understand the 3D
scene geometry but also complex viewpoint-dependent appearance resulted from sophisticated light
transport phenomena. One way to achieve this is by constructing a 5D plenoptic function that directly
models the light passing through each point in space [1] (or a 4D light field [2, 3] if we restrict
ourselves outside the convex hull of the objects of interest). Unfortunately, physically measuring such
a plenoptic function is usually not feasible in practice. As an alternative, Novel View Synthesis (NVS)
aims to generate the unseen views from a small set of images captured from diverse viewpoints.
Recently, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [4, 5, 6, 7] and Multi-Plane Images (MPI) [8, 9, 10, 11,
12, 13] have demonstrated that, by optimising a volumetric scene representation on a sparse set of
images, one can re-render the scene from novel viewpoints with impressive visual quality, including
sophisticated view-dependent effects, such as specularity and transparency.
One limitation for these methods is the requirement of the camera parameters of the images at
the training time, which is rarely accessible in real scenarios, e.g., images taken with a mobile
phone. In practice, these methods usually pre-compute the camera parameters via conventional
techniques. For example, NeRF and its variants [4, 5, 6, 7] adopt COLMAP [14] to estimate the
camera parameters (both intrinsics and extrinsics) for each input image. This pre-processing step,

Our goal in this paper is to investigate novel view synthesis for forward-facing scenes with unknown
camera parameters. We introduce NeRF−−, a framework that treats camera poses and intrinsics as
learnable parameters, and jointly optimises them with a 3D scene representation (as shown in Fig. 1).
Given only a sparse set of images captured in a forward-facing setup, our system can be trained
1) to estimate camera parameters for each image and 2) to learn a volumetric scene representation
through a photometric reconstruction loss, achieving high fidelity NVS results comparable to existing
two-stage COLMAP-NeRF pipelines while removing the pre-processing step for camera parameters.
In order to establish a comprehensive evaluation of the method, we make two additional contributions:
1) we introduce a high-quality path-traced synthetic dataset, named Blender Forward Facing (BLEFF),
to benchmark camera parameter estimation and novel view synthesis quality, which is publicly
accessible for future research; 2) we conduct thorough analyses on our method and COLMAP-NeRF
under various camera motions, showing that both systems can tolerate up to ±20◦ of rotation and
±20% translation perturbations, and that the joint optimisation is more favourable than COLMAP in
translational perturbations, but less competitive in rotational perturbations.
2 Related Work
We divide the related work roughly into two categories, one assuming camera parameters are input to
NVS representations, and the other jointly estimating camera parameters with an NVS representation
from uncalibrated images.
With Known Camera Parameters Images and their camera parameters are usually required by
many novel view synthesis systems. In this line of work, classical methods Light Field Rendering [3]
and Lumigraph [2] model a simplified plenoptic function. [18] integrates sophisticated hand-crafted
material BRDF models into Signed Distance Functions (SDF). FVS[19] and SVS[20] combine meshes
and deep features from images. Recently, dense volumetric representations have been proposed
to enable smooth gradients for photometry-based optimisation and has shown to be promising for
photo-realistic novel view synthesis of highly complex shapes and view-dependent appearance.
These representations include Soft3D[21], Multi-Plane Images (MPI)[8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13], Scene
Representation Networks (SRN)[22], implicit surface [23, 24], and Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)[4,
6, 7, 5]. Despite these approaches differ in the way of scene representations, they all require input
images to be associated with known camera parameters.
With Unknown Camera Parameters The second line of work, mostly consisting of SLAM and
SfM systems, aims to reconstruct the scene directly from RGB images by jointly estimating camera
parameters and 3D geometries. For example, MonoSLAM[25] and ORB-SLAM [26] reconstruct
a point cloud map and estimate camera poses in real-time by associating feature correspondences,
Similarly, DTAM [27], SVO[28], and LSD-SLAM [29] approach the SLAM task by minimising a
photometric loss. SfM systems Bundler[30] and COLMAP [14] can reconstruct a global consistent

4.1 Camera Parameters
Camera Intrinsics can be expressed as a focal length f and principle points cx and cy for a pinhole
camera model. For simplicity, we consider the centre of sensor as the camera principle points, i.e.,

input images are taken by the same camera. Thus, estimating the camera intrinsics only refers to
finding the focal length f in this case.

Implementation Details We implement our framework in PyTorch following the same architecture
as original NeRF, except that, for computation efficiency, we: (a) do not use the hierarchical sampling
strategy; (b) reduce the hidden layer dimension from 256 to 128; and (c) sample only 1024 pixels
from every input image and 128 points along each ray. We use Kaiming initialisation [44] for the
NeRF model, and initialise all cameras to be at origin looking at −z direction, with focal length f
to be the image width. We use three separate Adam optimisers for NeRF, camera poses and focal
lengths respectively, all with an initial learning rate of 0.001. The learning rate of the NeRF model is
decayed every 10 epochs by multiplying with 0.9954 (equivalent to a stair-cased exponential decay),
and learning rates of the pose and focal length parameters are decayed every 100 epochs with a
multiplier of 0.9. All models are trained for 10000 epochs unless otherwise specified. More technical
details are included in the supplementary material.

Breaking Point in Forward-Facing Scenes
The forward-facing assumption we made, which in an ideal case, assumes all cameras looking to −z

Setup Three groups of perturbation experiments are conducted in this section (Fig. 8): a) full 6DoF,

Result With full 6DoF perturbation added, both NeRF−− and COLMAP start breaking when
translation noise > ±20% and rotation noise > ±20◦ . Our method is favourable than COLMAP in
large translation perturbations, but underperforms COLMAP in large rotation cases. Two reasons
account for this result. First, it is well known that feature descriptors can handle appearance changes
better than photometric methods, hence the COLMAP’s superior performance in large rotation
perturbation. However, feature-based method can also be vulnerable to degenerate cases, resulting in
the failure cases in translation perturbation experiments, as will be discussed in Section 6.3.2.

Rotational and Translational As shown in Fig. 8c) and
t000 r000 column, COLMAP gives inaccurate camera parameter estimations in all translational experiments. Similar results are also observed in rotational motion. Fig. 10
presents two examples from the RealEstate10K dataset,
where COLMAP fails to initialise or produces inaccurate
estimations. For explanation, these results are known as
degenerate cases while solving fundamental matrices from
the point correspondences [15, 17]. Moreover, in [16],
Torr et al. further point out that these degenerate cases
are even more difficult to identify when matching outliers exist. Consequently, feature-matching-based SfM
and SLAM methods tend to be vulnerable to these cases,
whereas our photometry-based method handles them well.
Track-to-object We observe an inaccurate joint optimisation from our model, when a camera is set to track to an
object during capturing, even when the camera rotation is
well below our breaking point (±20◦ ). Fig. 11 illustrates
such a situation. One hypothesis is, such track-to-object
motion results in an effect that the object position and
the image composition remain almost unchanged during
capturing, which is not a problem for systems that work
with high-res images directly, e.g., COLMAP and SLAM
systems. However, our method is supervised from the
photometric loss between captured images and synthesised images. When the model is trained from scratch,
synthesised images are blurry during most of the training
time, producing little supervision for camera parameter
optimisation.

Our system faces two limitations. Firstly, as motioned

exploring relative pose estimation between image pairs and temporal relationships in sequences might
enable the processing of such 360◦ scenes.
7 Conclusions
Our contribution in this work is threefold. First, we present NeRF−−, a framework that jointly optimises camera parameters and scene representation for
forward-facing scenes, eliminating the need of pre-computing camera parameters,
while achieving view synthesis results on par with the COLMAP-enabled NeRF
baseline. Second, we introduce the BLEFF datasets for evaluating such jointly
optimised novel view synthesis system with ground-truth camera parameters
and high-quality path-traced images. Third, we present extensive experimental
results and demonstrate the viability of this joint optimisation framework under Figure 12: Failed
different camera trajectory patterns, even when COLMAP fails to estimate the Lego 360◦ scene.
camera parameters. Despite its current limitations discussed above, our proposed
joint optimisation pipeline has demonstrated promising results on this highly challenging task, and
advanced one step towards end-to-end novel view synthesis on more general scenes.

B.4
Evaluation Details
Since our optimised camera parameters might lie in different spaces from the ones estimated using
COLMAP, for evaluation, we first align the two trajectories globally with a Sim(3) transformation
using Umeyama algorithm[47] in an ATE toolbox [43], followed by a more fine-grained gradientdriven camera pose alignment by minimising the photometric error on the synthesised image, while
keeping the NeRF model fixed. Finally, we compute the metrics between the test image and our
synthesised image rendered from the best possible viewpoint. Simply put, all the above mentioned
processing aims to eliminate the effect from camera mis-alignment and make a fair comparison on
quality of the 3D scene representation.

Appendix C Broader Impact
Joint Optimisation Method Our method focuses on simplifying the classical two-stage NVS
systems, by jointly optimising a NeRF model and camera parameters for forward-facing images.
We expect our method to be applied in a wide ranged of applications, for example, AR and VR
content creation, 3D modelling, camera pose estimation and etc. As this method is designed to be
trained on a per-scene basis and is designed for static scenes and cannot handle dynamic objects
such as animals or humans, we expect the potential negative social impact (i.e., bias in models and
human privacy) that can be introduced by our method is very limited, as long as the image capturing
procedure confronts local legal requirements.
BLEFF Dataset We also introduce a synthetic dataset BLEFF, which contains high-quality pathtraced images rendered from 3D Blender models. The BLEFF scenes are selected to cover various
common real-life subjects, such as furniture, decorations, toys, rooms, or outdoor landscapes and
etc. There are two main purposes for this dataset: 1) to evaluate pose estimation and NVS rendering
quality as the same time; and 2) to evaluate the robustness of a joint optimisation system similar to
ours. Therefore, we expect our dataset to be beneficial to other joint optimisation systems (NVS and
camera parameters) in the future.

NoPe-NeRF: Optimising Neural Radiance
Field with No Pose Prior
While Section 4.1 demonstrates the feasibility of jointly estimating camera parameters and NeRF, its applicability is constrained to scenes with limited camera
motion for two primary reasons: i) NeRF training suffers from inherent shaperadiance ambiguity [170], which is further amplified while optimising additional
camera parameters, and ii) existing methods estimate camera poses for individual
images without accounting for their relative transformations.
Although classical epipolar geometry can potentially address the second limitation, it requires a series of pre-processing steps including interest point detection,
feature matching, and transformation estimation. This contradicts our goal to
eliminate complex and time-consuming pre-processing.
To overcome both challenges and avoid complex pre-processing, we introduce
monocular depth estimation into the joint camera and NeRF optimisation pipeline.
This integration serves two purposes: i) it offers robust geometric cues for NeRF
training and camera parameter optimisation, and ii) it allows NeRF, which acts as
a global volumetric model, to correct distortions in monocular depth estimation.
As a result, monocular depth and NeRF complement each other.
Specifically, we integrate monocular depth from three perspectives: i) explicitly optimising distortion parameters for each depth map, un-distorting during
NeRF training; ii) employing on-fly un-distorted depth for surface rendering and
3D registration between adjacent images, enhancing camera parameter estimation by considering relative transformations; and iii) supervising NeRF rendered
depth with monocular depth to resolve shape-radiance ambiguity. As a result, our
method effectively handles large camera motions and outperforms existing methods in both novel view rendering quality and camera pose estimation accuracy.

Abstract
Training a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) without precomputed camera poses is challenging. Recent advances
in this direction demonstrate the possibility of jointly optimising a NeRF and camera poses in forward-facing scenes.
However, these methods still face difficulties during dramatic camera movement. We tackle this challenging problem by incorporating undistorted monocular depth priors.
These priors are generated by correcting scale and shift
parameters during training, with which we are then able
to constrain the relative poses between consecutive frames.
This constraint is achieved using our proposed novel loss
functions. Experiments on real-world indoor and outdoor
scenes show that our method can handle challenging camera trajectories and outperforms existing methods in terms
of novel view rendering quality and pose estimation accuracy. Our project page is https://nope- nerf.
active.vision.
1. Introduction
The photo-realistic reconstruction of a scene from a
stream of RGB images requires both accurate 3D geometry
reconstruction and view-dependent appearance modelling.
Recently, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [24] have demonstrated the ability to build high-quality results for generating
photo-realistic images from novel viewpoints given a sparse
set of images.
An important preparation step for NeRF training is the
estimation of camera parameters for the input images. A
current go-to option is the popular Structure-from-Motion
(SfM) library COLMAP [35]. Whilst easy to use, this preprocessing step could be an obstacle to NeRF research and
real-world deployments in the long term due to its long processing time and its lack of differentiability. Recent works
such as NeRFmm [47], BARF [18] and SC-NeRF [12] propose to simultaneously optimise camera poses and the neural implicit representation to address these issues. Nevertheless, these methods can only handle forward-facing scenes
when no initial parameters are supplied, and fail in dramatic
camera motions, e.g.a casual handheld captured video.
This limitation has two key causes. First, all these methods estimate a camera pose for each input image individually without considering relative poses between images.
Looking back to the literature of Simultaneous localisation
and mapping (SLAM) and visual odometry, pose estimation
can significantly benefit from estimating relative poses be-
tween adjacent input frames. Second, the radiance field is
known to suffer from shape-radiance ambiguity [56]. Estimating camera parameters jointly with NeRF adds another
degree of ambiguity, resulting in slow convergence and unstable optimisation.
To handle the limitation of large camera motion, we seek
help from monocular depth estimation [22, 28, 29, 52]. Our
motivation is threefold: First, monocular depth provides
strong geometry cues that are beneficial to constraint shaperadiance ambiguity. Second, relative poses between adjacent depth maps can be easily injected into the training
pipeline via Chamfer Distance. Third, monocular depth is
lightweight to run and does not require camera parameters
as input, in contrast to multi-view stereo depth estimation.
For simplicity, we use the term mono-depth from now on.
Utilising mono-depth effectively is not straightforward
with the presence of scale and shift distortions. In other
words, mono-depth maps are not multi-view consistent.
Previous works [9, 17, 48] simply take mono-depth into
a depth-wise loss along with NeRF training. Instead, we
propose a novel and effective way to thoroughly integrate
mono-depth into our system. First, we explicitly optimise
scale and shift parameters for each mono-depth map during NeRF training by penalising the difference between
rendered depth and mono-depth. Since NeRF by itself is
trained based on multiview consistency, this step transforms
mono-depth maps to undistorted multiview consistent depth
maps. We further leverage these multiview consistent depth
maps in two loss terms: a) a Chamfer Distance loss between
two depth maps of adjacent images, which injects relative
pose to our system; and b) a depth-based surface rendering
loss, which further improves relative pose estimation.
In summary, we propose a method to jointly optimise
camera poses and a NeRF from a sequence of images with
large camera motion. Our system is enabled by three contributions. First, we propose a novel way to integrate monodepth into unposed-NeRF training by explicitly modelling
scale and shift distortions. Second, we supply relative poses
to the camera-NeRF joint optimisation via an inter-frame
loss using undistorted mono-depth maps. Third, we further
regularise our relative pose estimation with a depth-based
surface rendering loss.
As a result, our method is able to handle large camera
motion, and outperforms state-of-the-art methods by a significant margin in terms of novel view synthesis quality and
camera trajectory accuracy.
2. Related Work
Novel View Synthesis. While early Novel View Synthesis (NVS) approaches applied interpolations between pixels [3], later works often rendered images from 3D reconstruction [1, 6]. In recent years, different representations
of the 3D scene are used, e.g.meshes [30, 31], Multi-Plane
Images [42, 60], layered depth [43] etc. Among them,
NeRF [24] has become a popular scene representation for
its photorealistic rendering.
A number of techniques are proposed to improve NeRF’s
performance with additional regularisation [13, 26, 55],
depth priors [7,32,48,54], surface enhancements [27,44,50]
or latent codes [41, 45, 53]. Other works [2, 10, 25, 34] have
also accelerated NeRF training and rendering. However,
most of these approaches require pre-computed camera parameters obtained from SfM algorithms [11, 35].
NeRF With Pose Optimisation. Removing camera parameter preprocessing is an active line of research recently.
One category of the methods [33, 39, 61] use a SLAM-style
pipeline, that either requires RGB-D inputs or relies on accurate camera poses generated from the SLAM tracking
system. Another category of works optimises camera poses
with the NeRF model directly. We term this type of method
as unposed-NeRF in this paper. iNeRF [51] shows that
poses for novel view images can be estimated using a reconstructed NeRF model. GNeRF [21] combines Generative
Adversarial Networks with NeRF to estimate camera poses
but requires a known sampling distribution for poses. More
relevant to our work, NeRFmm [47] jointly optimises both
camera intrinsics and extrinsics alongside NeRF training.
BARF [18] proposes a coarse-to-fine positional encoding
strategy for camera poses and NeRF joint optimisation. SCNeRF [12] further parameterises camera distortion and employs a geometric loss to regularise rays. GARF [4] shows
that using Gaussian-MLPs makes joint pose and scene optimisation easier and more accurate. Recently, SiNeRF [49]
uses SIREN [37] layers and a novel sampling strategy to alleviate the sub-optimality of joint optimisation in NeRFmm.
Although showing promising results on the forward-facing
dataset like LLFF [23], these approaches face difficulties
when handling challenging camera trajectories with large
camera motion. We address this issue by closely integrating mono-depth maps with the joint optimisation of camera
parameters and NeRF.
3. Method
We tackle the challenge of handling large camera motions in unposed-NeRF training. Given a sequence of images, camera intrinsics, and their mono-depth estimations,
our method recovers camera poses and optimises a NeRF
simultaneously. We assume camera intrinsics are available
in the image meta block, and we run an off-the-shelf monodepth network DPT [7] to acquire mono-depth estimations.
Without repeating the benefit of mono-depth, we unroll this
section around the effective integration of monocular depth
into unposed-NeRF training.

4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We conduct experiments on two datasets Tanks
and Temples [15] and ScanNet [5]. Tanks and Temples:
we use 8 scenes to evaluate pose accuracy and novel view
synthesis quality. We chose scenes captured at both indoor
and outdoor locations, with different frame sampling rates
and lengths. All images are down-sampled to a resolution
of 960 × 540. For the family scene, we sample 200 images
and take 100 frames with odd frame ids as training images
and the remaining 100 frames for novel view synthesis, in
order to analyse the performance under smooth motion. For
the remaining scenes, following NeRF [24], 1/8 of the images in each sequence are held out for novel view synthesis,
unless otherwise specified. ScanNet: we select 4 scenes for
evaluating pose accuracy, depth accuracy, and novel view
synthesis quality. For each scene, we take 80-100 consecutive images and use 1/8 of these images for novel view
synthesis. For evaluation, we employ depth maps and poses
provided by ScanNet as ground truth. ScanNet images are
down-sampled to 648 × 484. We crop images with dark
orders during preprocessing.
Metrics. We evaluate our proposed method in three aspects. For novel view synthesis, we follow previous methods [12,18,47], and use standard evaluation metrics, including Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) [46] and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [57]. For pose evaluation, We
use standard visual odometry metrics [16, 38, 58], including the Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) and Relative Pose
Error (RPE). ATE measures the difference between the estimated camera positions and the ground truth positions. RPE
measures the relative pose errors between pairs of images,
which consists of relative rotation error (RPEr ) and relative
translation error (RPEt ). The estimated trajectory is aligned
with the ground truth using Sim(3) with 7 degrees of freedom. We use standard depth metrics [8,19,20,40] (Abs Rel,
Sq Rel, RMSE, RMSE log, δ1 , δ2 and δ3 ) for depth evaluation. For further detail, please refer to the supplementary
material. To recover the metric scale, we follow Zhou et
al. [59] and match the median value between rendered and
ground truth depth maps.
Implementation Details. Our model architecture is based
on NeRF [24] with a few modifications: a) replacing
ReLU activation function with Softplus and b) sampling
128 points along each ray uniformly with noise, between
a predefined range (0.1, 10). We use 2 separate Adam optimisers [14] for NeRF and other parameters. The initial
learning rate for NeRF is 0.001 and for the pose and distortion is 0.0005. Camera rotations are optimised in axis-angle
representation ϕi ∈ so(3). We first train the model with
all losses with constant learning rates until convergence.
Then, we decay the learning rates with different schedulers
and gradually reduce weights for the inter-frame losses and
depth loss to further train for 10,000 epochs. We balance
the loss terms with λ1 = 0.04, λ2 = 1.0 and λ3 = 1.0. For
each training step, we randomly sample 1024 pixels (rays)
from each input image and 128 samples per ray. More details are provided in the supplementary material.

4.5. Limitations
Our proposed method optimises camera pose and the
NeRF model jointly and works on challenging scenes where
other baselines fail. However, the optimisation of the model
is also affected by non-linear distortions and the accuracy of
the mono-depth estimation, which we did not consider.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we present NoPe-NeRF, an end-to-end
differentiable model for joint camera pose estimation and
novel view synthesis from a sequence of images. We
demonstrate that previous approaches have difficulty with
complex trajectories. To tackle this challenge, we use
mono-depth maps to constrain the relative poses between
frames and regularise the geometry of NeRF, which leads
to better pose estimation. We show the effectiveness and
robustness of NoPe-NeRF on challenging scenes. The improved pose estimation leads to better novel view synthesis
quality and geometry reconstruction compared with other
approaches. We believe our method is an important step
towards applying the unknown-pose NeRF models to largescale scenes in the future.

A.1. Dataset

ScanNet
We select sequences containing dramatic camera motions from ScanNet [5] and Tanks and Tamples [15] for
training and evaluation. Tab. 7 lists details about these sequences, where Max rotation denotes the maximum relative rotation angle between any two frames in a sequence.
The sampled images are further split into training and test
sets. Starting from the 5th image, we sample every 8th image in a sequence as a test image. However, this leads to a
change in the sampling rate in the temporal domain among
training images. We found that the rotation errors are often
higher than average at these positions where the sampling
rate changes. In order to study the effect of the sampling
rate changes, for scene Family in Tanks and Temples [15],
we sample every other image as test images, i.e. training on
images with odd frame ids and testing on images with even
frame ids.

A.2. Training Details
During training, we sample 1024 pixels/rays for an image and we sample 128 points along each ray for our approaches and all baselines. For all approaches, we use the
same pre-defined sampling range (i.e., near and far) and
sample uniformly between this range. During scheduling,
the learning rate of NeRF model decays every 10 epochs
with 0.9954, and the learning rate for the camera poses decays every 100 epochs with 0.9. As the scene scales can be
arbitrary, the optimised scale parameter of the depth map
during training is also arbitrary. To avoid scale collapsing
(all scales reduced to 0.0) during training, we manually set
the scale of the depth map for the last frame to 1.0. We
also use the normalised point clouds when computing the
inter-frame point cloud loss.
A.3. Test-time Optimisation
During the evaluation for novel view synthesis, following our baselines NeRFmm [47], BARF [18] and SCNeRF [12], we run a test-time optimisation to align the camera poses of the test set by minimising the photometric error
on the synthesised images, while keeping the trained NeRF
model froze. Although all these baseline methods have their
own way to align camera poses (discussed below), all of
them fail to align camera poses in complex camera trajectories in ScanNet and Tanks and Temples.
To fairly evaluate all methods in challenging camera trajectories, we propose to align test camera poses by first initialising from learned poses of adjacent training images, followed by a test-time optimisation. We shorthand this alignment as Neighbour + opt. In practice, we find this initialisation is robust and provides the best alignment for all
approaches. All results in our main paper are evaluated in
this way.
The following paragraphs outline previous alignment
methods, and we show a comparison for all method with
a ScanNet scene in Tab. 8.
Identity + opt. BARF [18] uses test-time optimisation
to identify poses for the test frames, where all poses are
initialised with identity matrices. This initialisation works
well for simple forward-facing scenes, but not for complex
trajectories. The optimisation is sensitive to the learning
rate, and can easily fall into local minima when the target
pose is far from the identity initialisation.
Sim(3) + opt. In NeRFmm [47], the poses are first initialised using Sim(3) alignment with an ATE toolbox [58].
Then, an additional test-time optimisation is used to further
adjust the test poses. This initialisation works well when the
learned poses can be aligned precisely to COLMAP poses
(Ours in Tab. 8). However, incorrect pose estimations can
affect the Sim(3) alignment.
Sim(3) + no opt. In SC-NeRF [12], the test poses
are identified using a Sim(3) alignment between COLMAP
poses and the learned poses. And no test-time optimisation
is used. However, the results are biased toward COLMAP
estimations, and misalignment can affect the view synthesis
quality significantly.

2D – 2D Registration with
Interest Point Discovery
This chapter addresses the issue of image registration, specifically the alignment
of two images via affine transformation estimation. As outlined in Section 2.4,
numerous methods exist for this purpose; however, our focus lies on the field of
interest point detection, a crucial element in estimating fundamental matrices and
homography transformation.
The ability to handle significant viewpoint changes is a key property desired in
interest point detection. Existing methods [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60] have largely
been confined to detecting points within a single image. Although this single-view
approach allows efficient pipelines, it imposes bottlenecks when dealing with extensive viewpoint alterations. This has led to higher-level interest point development,
particularly in human and hand imagery [61, 62, 63, 64], but broadening this to
more general scenes is challenging.
To address these limitations, we introduces a novel interest point discovery
network designed to handle input of multi-view images. This multi-view framework
enables the network to distinguish local image appearance from factors such as
texture and 3D shapes.
98
Specifically, our contributions are twofold: First, we introduce the concept of
Abstract Interest Point (AIP) in this chapter, representing a high-level keypoint
consistent across various conditions and applicable to diverse scenes. Second, we
present a self-supervised approach to train a network for this task. Our training
approach involves a geometric-based self-supervised strategy with a contrastive and
triangulation term. This method focuses on overall scene structures rather than
local surface textures, similar to human keypoint discovery but without specific
scene content assumptions.
Consequently, our multi-view interest point discovery method significantly enhances the repeatability of identified interest points, which is beneficial to the
theme of 2D – 2D registration.

We re-visit the fundamental challenge of interest point

1. Introduction
In summary, we present two major contributions. First,
we introduce a novel concept of abstract interest points
(AIPs) that are highly repeatable across various viewing
conditions and generalizable to different scenes, moving beyond low-level image feature-based keypoints. Second, we
propose a novel self-supervised strategy to identify AIPs effectively. This strategy comprises two terms: a contrastive
term that ensures AIPs to be repeatable and sparse, and a triangulation term that encourages AIPs to be multi-view consistent. As a result, we show AIPs offer superior keypoint
identification performance in terms of repeatability and related pose estimation, as well as more accurate point tracking in comparison with modern 2D trackers.
The rest of this paper is structured as follows: We first
review related work in Sec. 2, then delve into our methodology in Sec. 3, followed by a discussion of our results and
limitations in Sec. 4, and conclude in Sec. 5.
Interest points provide a sparse set of anchors upon which
to establish reliable visual correspondence, and are therefore essential elements in computer vision. A core property of good interest points is their repeatability: points
should be consistently identified at the same position under a variety of view points, lighting conditions, and partial occlusions. The most common interest points are local
keypoints, i.e., based on low-level image features such as
blobs and corners, and detected with either hand-written algorithms [14, 29, 50] or neural networks [6, 12, 37, 60].
One major limitation of depending on low-level image
features for interest point detection is the fact that these features are mostly at physical surfaces and are therefore only
reliably detectable within a limited range of viewing angles,
viewing distances, and illumination conditions. This motivates the pursuit of interest point at a higher level, i.e.,
abstracted beyond low-level image features, and thus more
repeatable to detect. For specific categories such as human and hands, abstract interest points have been widely
adapted, in either human annotated or self-supervised manner [18, 48, 49, 58]. Generalizing beyond designated categories, however, remains a challenge for existing abstract
interest point detection methods.
In this paper, we aim to identify abstract interest points
(AIPs) that are highly repeatable across a wide range of
viewing conditions yet remain generalizable to diverse
scenes. It is difficult to directly define an algorithm for detecting good abstract interest points, and direct supervision
is non-trivial even when using neural networks to learn the
detection function. Instead, we identify the desired properties of interest points and design losses to encourage the
discovery of interest points with these properties.
Specifically, we want points to be 1) geometrically consistent, in the sense that multiple detections of the same interest point triangulate to a single 3D point, and 2) repeatable, in the sense that multiple observations of the same
scene from disparate viewing angles and / or under different illumination should lead to the detection of the same
interest points.
The former property is encouraged by detecting AIPs in
a multi-view image series and penalizing geometric inconsistency using differentiable triangulation [35]. The latter
property is encouraged by a loss that induces clustering of
keypoint detections in 3D. It is this cluster-inducing loss
that makes the detected interest points abstract – in order to
reliably detect the same points in many views of the same
scene, the network must avoid over-indexing on local surface texture and instead identify scene structure that is more
broadly observable. This approach echoes similar ideas in
the human keypoint discovery literature [18, 48, 49, 58],
but in contrast to this prior work we do not make any assumptions on scene content.
2. Related Work
Three groups of prior work are reviewed: 1) generic interest point identification, which are broadly applicable across
various scenarios; 2) categorial interest point identification,
which are tailored for recognizing interest patterns in specific categories; and 3) point tracking, which are designed
to track 2D query points across a series of frames.
Generic Interest Point Identification. A long history of
works exist on detecting interest point with low-level image features using hand-crafted rules [14, 25, 29] or neural network [6, 9, 52, 54]. More recently, progress has
been made towards discovering interest points with selfsupervision, further reducing the level of human input to the
learning process. To construct the self-supervision, existing works assume the availability of pixel-level correspondence, which is established either with homography (therefore limit to planar scene) [2, 24, 41, 56, 57] or other lowlevel feature based algorithm such as optical flow or structure from motion [12, 33, 37, 54, 59, 60]. In other word,
the target interest points that these self-supervised discovery methods are trying to find, are either by-definition on
physical surface, or derived from low-level image features
that are mostly at physical surface. Over-indexing on local
surface textures is not ideal, as it places an inherent limit on
the range of viewing angle / distance / illumination condition that the interest points can be detected from.
Categorial Interest Point Identification. There has been
long interest in identifying interest point for specific categories, e.g., human [4, 11], face [5, 36] or hand [3, 55].
To avoid laborious manual labeling and to facilitate utilizing of large amount of unlabeled in-the-wild data, various
self-supervision attempts have been made using single im2
age input [18, 19, 49, 58] or multiple-view [46]. Overall,
this line of work heavily relies on category priors to limit
the solution space. In contrast, we aim to discover interest
points that are more generic and can be applied to a variety
of scenarios.
Point Tracking. Establishing correspondence is a fundamental challenge in vision tasks. While explicit feature
matching methods [27, 39, 45] are viable for tracking query
points, our method aligns more closely to flow-like point
tracking approaches. For example, optical flow [8, 15, 16,
30, 44, 47] and correspondence flow [17, 21, 22, 28, 53]
methods estimate displacements between adjacent frames.
Particle video methods [7, 13, 20, 38, 61] track query points
in a window of consecutive images. While point tracking
methods produce outputs in similar forms to ours, they primarily address the challenge of effectively tracking a query
point, without particular consideration for repeatability and
distinctiveness. In contrast, our method emphasizes not
only tracking but also driving query input to more repeatable and distinctive points.

discover a new tracklet within some proximity of the seed,

repeatable. We refer the discovered new tracklet or these

5. Conclusion
In conclusion, this paper presents a novel method for detecting abstract interest points (AIPs) on general scenes.
The self-supervised training approach that only focus on
geometric consistency successfully circumvents the limitations of traditional local keypoint methods that rely heavily
on low-level image features. The discovered AIPs demonstrate remarkable repeatability across varying viewpoints
and conditions, maintaining generalizability across diverse
scenarios.
Test Data. We choose 14 videos from Aria Pilot (AP) [31]
as the testing data. These videos, captured in real-world
indoor settings, feature significant camera motion and have
pseudo ground truth camera parameters provided. To ensure
a more accurate evaluation, dynamic segments within these
videos have been excluded.

a motion-assisted KLT tracks these initial points across

KLT has prior knowledge of relative camera motion between frames, therefore is able to 1) conduct a 1D search

Our network g consists of two primary components, genc for
image encoding, and goffset for offset regression. To enhance
efficiency and ensure faster runtime, we build a compact
network with a total of 15.2M parameters.
Image Encoder genc utilizes a U-Net structure with a VGG11 backbone to extract two feature maps for input images in resolution H × W . Specifically, feature maps
at the last two U-Net layers, Flayer=−1 ∈ RH×W ×32 and
Flayer=−2 ∈ RH/2×W/2×64 , are bi-linearly interpolated for
per-point feature vector f . In addition, two Fourier positional embeddings, a frame order embedding with 16 dimensions and a relative pixel coordinate embedding with
128 dimensions, are appended to f , leading to a final perpoint feature vector f with dimension 240.
Seed Tracklets Sampling. In Sec. 3.1 we present our
self-supervised training with a pair of tracklets. During
training, we adopt a sliding window strategy to enable
batched processing. In specific, each training step starts by
sampling a pair of sliding windows, each consisting of Nimg
frames that partially cover the same 3D space. We then take
all seed tracklets that have a minimum observation threshold m out of window size Nimg from each window. Next,
the tracklets from both windows are paired by checking the
3D distance between 3D points of corresponding tracklets,
and only those within the 3D contrastive radius re = 0.3m
are kept as a part of the training batch. We found m = 5
and Nimg = 9 providing sufficient tracklets pairs that can be
triangulated reliably.
Offset Regressor goffset includes two parts, a self-attention
module, which reasons relationships among features fpi
from input views, and a MLP-based regression head, which
makes the final offset predictions. The self-attention module is implemented with xFormer [23], and is stacked with
6 encoder layers. Each layer has 8 attention heads and
480 embedding channels. A 4-layer MLP then projects the
per-point features transformed by the attention module to 2
channels in a normalized range (−1, +1), which is further
converted to pixel space with a scale factor 12. This leads
to a max offset range of ±12 pixel around 2D points on a
seed tracklet in one prediction step. With multiple iterative
steps, 3 in our experiments, our method is able to discover
AIPs that are significantly distant from seed tracklets.
Training Schedule. Our model was trained with 192
V100 GPUs for 5 days (0.5 day for bootstrap and 4.5 days
for actual training) on the 27 hours of processed video. To
speed up convergence, a 15-epoch bootstrap stage is conducted first, where the pulling component in Eq. (8) is
applied to seed tracklet pairs that are sampled from predefined 3D tracklet clusters. The 3D clusters were obtained using off-the-shelf 3D clustering algorithm HDBSCAN5 . This bootstrap stage ensures the pulling behavior
between two tracklets emerges early. However, the clusterfixed training has clear limitation as well, due to the strong
bias introduced by the initial cluster assignment for seed
tracklets. Therefore, after 15 epochs of bootstrapping, we
switch to cluster-free self-supervision, where seed tracklet
pairs are sampled based on the 3D contrastive radius re .
The cluster-free self-supervision either enhances or corrects
bootstrapped predictions. In total, our model is trained for
500 epochs.

A.3. Inference
Inference Time Input. During the inference phase, our
model processes the input video with a sliding window approach. Our method discovers AIPs by taking input of a
window of consecutive images, along with a set of seed
tracklets associated with this window. We apply the same
window size Nimg = 9 (as training) during inference.
Non-Maximum Suppression We adopt a common nonmaximum suppression (NMS) module6 that takes input
points with associated scores. For each AIP, we derive a
score from the local density of all AIPs. Specifically, an
AIP is scored by counting the number of nearby AIPs at
the last image of current sliding window, where the nearby
range for counting is identical to NMS range. We set it
to 5-pixel for all experiments. Those that form condensed
clusters with high consensus will survive from NMS, resulting in a sparse set of highly scored AIPs. Meanwhile
those that latch to arbitrary places with low consensus will
be scored lower, therefore either be suppressed during NMS
or get ranked too low to pass top-K budgeting.

Chapter 6
Conclusion
This thesis explored various avenues in both 3D and 2D registration. Over the
course of this DPhil study, there has been a notable shift in the research landscape, transitioning from classical representations like point clouds and level sets,
to neural representations such as occupancy networks and Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRF).
Initially, our work focused on developing techniques for point cloud registration
in both rigid and non-rigid contexts, as discussed in Sections 3.1 and 3.2. We
demonstrated advancements in 3D rigid and non-rigid registration through point
cloud scene flow estimation and point cloud normal estimation methods.
Subsequently, we turned our attention to 3D – 2D registration challenges,
particularly in Sections 4.1 and 4.2. Here, we introduced a joint optimisation
pipeline for Neural Radiance Fields and camera parameters. Our work began with
NeRF−− for forward-facing scenes and was extended in NoPe-NeRF to accommodate larger camera movements and additional mono-depth estimations.
Lastly, we revisited a low-level task of interest point detection in Chapter 5.
Aimed at addressing long-term localisation and mapping, we designed a multiview, self-supervised interest point discovery pipeline that identifies highly repeat-
113
able and multi-view consistent interest points.
Overall, this thesis contributes to the evolving field of image and shape registration, offering new methods and insights across a range of scenarios and representations.
6.1
Achievement
We are grateful to see that our research contributions have received positive recognition within their relevant research communities.
In Section 3.1, we enhanced a scene flow estimation network and introduced
the concept of using scene flow estimation as an initial step for complex non-rigid
registration, improving dynamic reconstruction outcomes. Published in 2020, this
work has earned over 90 citations and has influenced subsequent studies in dynamic
reconstruction [171] and scene flow estimation networks [172, 173].
Our research, presented in Section 3.2, was among the first to introduce the
concept of point soft-weighting in normal estimation tasks. This work was honoured with an oral presentation at BMVC 2020 and has spawned follow-up research
such as [174, 175, 176].
The work in Section 4.1 was the first effort in the area of joint NeRF and
camera parameter optimisation. Our subsequent work in Section 4.2 received a
highlight presentation at CVPR 2023 and expands upon Section 4.1 by introducing a novel way of integrating monocular depth, addressing challenges from larger
camera movements. This line of research has inspired various directions such as
joint camera and NeRF optimisation [177, 107, 141, 145, 178, 108], lens modelling [179, 105, 180], as well as became a default camera refinement module in
dynamic NeRF [140, 181] and city-scale NeRF [182]. Moreover, the GitHub repos-
114
itories12 for Sections 4.1 and 4.2 have collectively received over 750 stars and have
been cited more than 280 times within two years of release, establishing a solid
reference in this evolving field.
Lastly, Chapter 5 outlined our ongoing work in the field of interest point discovery, where we propose a novel multi-view setup to address this challenge. This
work is currently under peer review. We expect that this work will be particularly
valuable for long-term mapping and localisation research.
6.2
Future Work
Current registration methods predominantly depend on geometry and appearance
correspondences, which are vulnerable to challenges such as motion blur, sensor
noise, and varying lighting conditions. A promising direction for future research
is to incorporate high-level semantic from recent advancements in representation
learning, including MAE [183], DINO [184, 185], and large vision and language
models [186, 187]. Merging semantic information with low-level appearance details
could result in more robust registration performance in challenging conditions.
Additionally, in this thesis, we demonstrated that integrating classical multiview geometry principles can significantly improve learning-based registration algorithms. This integration leads to benefits such as accelerated training processes,
enhanced accuracy, and improved consistency across multiple views. Hence, similar idea can be employed to augment large-scale vision and language models such
as CLIP [186], StableDiffusion [187], and Zero-1-to-3 [112], by serving as an extra
self-supervision mechanism that encourages more consistent results in multi-view
setups.
