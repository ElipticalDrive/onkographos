Abstract
Creating photorealistic human heads typically involves
trade-offs between representation power, efficiency, and
generalizability. Traditional mesh-based solutions relying
on 3DMMs generally sacrifice representation power but are
efficient to render. On the other hand, NeRF-based methods
improve representation power and fidelity while sacrificing
efficiency. Gaussian splatting methods attempt to resolve
these trade-offs by offering both high representation power
and efficiency with real-time rendering. However, their efficiency nonetheless depends on the number of rasterized
Gaussians, and the time needed to optimize and prepare the
head (the enrollment time) is often high. In light of this, we
propose EGGHead, which introduces a novel Gaussian allocation algorithm that efficiently assigns Gaussians to the
head’s semantic regions given a fixed Gaussian budget. Our
algorithm reduces the number of Gaussians needed while
minimizing the impact on representation power. EGGHead
operates in a single forward pass and outperforms previous single-image face modeling methods in reconstruction
quality, novel view synthesis, and rendering speeds.
1. Introduction
Modeling photorealistic human heads has received
widespread attention from the computer vision and

graphics communities. It has a wide array of applications, including movie production [31], digital humans
in AR/VR [25, 70, 75, 90], and downstream tasks in
the face domain [57, 58]. There are several important
axes to consider when designing a head reconstruction
method, including representation power, the efficiency of
the 3D representation, and data requirements. An ideal
method produces a high-quality head that captures fine
identity details, enables real-time generation and novel
view synthesis, and requires minimal data.
Traditional head representation methods adopt meshbased approaches that rely on 3DMMs [10] to model the
face. Although generally efficient to rasterize and render,
and despite a plethora of single-image based methods [6,
53, 83, 85, 86], these 3D representations generally lack high
representation power due to reliance on PCA bases, carrying over artifacts from intrinsic decomposition into rendering. The follow-up methods [25, 37, 39, 70, 71, 97] relied
instead on the implicit NeRF [65] representation, which allowed for higher representation power thanks to per-pixel
raytracing, the ability to model view-specific texture variations, and the volumetric rendering design. However,
NeRF-based methods sacrifice efficiency since the raytracing inference stage is often costly. More recently, the face
modeling community has favored mesh-driven 3D Gaussian
Splatting [45] (3D-GS): a hybrid explicit-implicit representation that allows for both high fidelity and real-time rendering.
Gaussian Splatting, however, is not without limitations.
While considered a representation that enables real-time
rendering, the efficiency is still affected by the number of
Gaussians used to represent the head. In practice, highquality Gaussian heads require a large number of Gaussians
to capture fine geometric and appearance details. Reducing
the number of Gaussians is thus an important priority, but if
done naively will simply reduce the head’s fidelity. To address this trade-off between fidelity and efficiency, we propose a novel Gaussian Allocation Module (GAM), which
efficiently allocates a fixed budget of M Gaussians while
minimizing the impact on the head’s fidelity.
Moreover, 3D-GS traditionally relies on monocular or
multi-view image/video inputs, is trained per subject instead of being generalizable, and requires an expensive optimization for each subject ranging from a few minutes to
one hour [16, 73, 90]. GPS-Gaussian [96] resolves some of
these limitations by producing a generalizable method that
takes a stereo pair of images as input and predicts Gaussian
attributes. However, it requires a monocular video as input,
since the method depends on the nearest-neighbor stereo
pair for each queried viewpoint. Moreover, it makes the
naive assumption of assigning one Gaussian to each pixel,
which will naturally reduce the rendering speed. We instead reduce this requirement from a monocular video to a
single image while also proposing a generalizable method
given one image as input. Our novel GAM improves rendering speed by allocating Gaussians over semantic regions
rather than on a per-pixel basis, achieving high fidelity with
fewer Gaussians. In addition, we employ Voronoi tessellation [11] to guide Gaussian attribute computation in a
single-view setting with sparse Gaussians. Finally, we introduce a lightweight, network-free Face Symmetry Module
to render occluded face regions, avoiding expensive postprocessing networks and diffusion-based multi-view generation during inference.
Our contributions are thus as follows:
⋄ We propose EGGHead, a single-image and generalizable Gaussian head model that outperforms existing approaches in rendering quality and speed while achieving
competitive enrollment time.
⋄ We introduce a Gaussian Allocation Module (GAM)
that allocates Gaussians under a fixed budget with minimal impact, employ Voronoi tessellation to guide Gaussian shape computation in a single-view setting with sparse
Gaussians, and incorporate a lightweight Face Symmetry
Module to improve head completeness without relying on
additional networks.
⋄ We demonstrate superior head representation capabilities and fidelity among single-image head reconstruction
methods, both quantitatively and qualitatively.

2. Related Work
Problem Formulation. Given a single source image Is ,
our goal is to reconstruct a photorealistic head of an unseen
subject. To tackle this non-trivial task, we employ the 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) pipeline [45]. 3D-GS models
Face Modeling. Existing 3D face representations include
explicit mesh-based methods [6, 8, 9, 38, 53, 56–59, 77, 83–
Generalizable Gaussian Splatting. Although 3D-GS is
traditionally optimized per scene or subject, generalizable
3D-GS methods have recently gained attention. GPSGaussian [96] synthesizes unseen identities but assigns one
Gaussian per pixel, limiting efficiency. GAGAvatar [17]
predicts Gaussians from a single image using FLAME [55]
and dual lifting, but its additional neural renderer may
compromise identity consistency. Diffusion-based methods [63, 82] achieve high quality but rely on costly multiview generation. In contrast, EGGHead uses GAM to
merge Gaussians in fidelity-insensitive regions, avoiding
dense per-pixel allocation, and multi-view generation.
Efficient Gaussian Splatting. Several methods improve
the efficiency of 3D-GS by compressing Gaussian parameters or pruning low-impact Gaussians. Compression methods [54, 66, 67] reduce storage and rendering costs by removing parameter redundancy, while pruning-based methods [23, 28, 69] reduce the Gaussian count. However, they
still rely on costly multi-view optimization. In contrast, our
GAM operates from a single image and directly allocates a
compact set of Gaussians.
Symmetry in Face Modeling. Face symmetry is a strong
prior that preserves the naturally symmetrical structure of
human faces, especially in the ill-posed single-image setting where occluded regions rely on face priors to be properly reconstructed. While previous methods [7, 20, 85] impose symmetry as a loss function to guide networks toward
symmetric 3D face reconstruction, EGGHead introduces a
lightweight Face Symmetry Module that directly mirrors
visible Gaussians across the facial symmetry plane to complement occluded regions without additional networks.

3.1. Gaussian Allocation Module
Our objective is to efficiently allocate a fixed Gaussian budget while preserving rendering quality. We begin by assigning one Gaussian per pixel, compute the error between
adjacent Gaussian pairs based on their mean texture difference, and iteratively merge the pair with the lowest error
until reaching the target number of Gaussians M . After
each merge, all impacted neighboring Gaussians have their
mean texture error updated with the newly merged Gaussian. To prevent oversized Gaussians and sparsely covered
regions, we set a maximum threshold such that no Gaussian
can occupy more than 5∗B
M pixels, where B is the number
of foreground pixels in the binary face mask.
The resulting positions of these per-pixel Gaussians form
a pseudo ground-truth position map in pixel coordinates.
In practice, we convert these pseudo ground-truth position
maps into continuous density maps and use them to train a
network to directly predict a Gaussian position density map
given a source image. We then sample our M Gaussians
from the predicted density map (See Sec. 3.5). Using the
predicted depth map D together with a scaling factor simg ,
the sampled Gaussian centers (im , jm ) can be lifted from

High-fidelity facial rendering typically requires a large
number of points to capture appearance details [17]. However, our approach reduces the number of Gaussians for efficiency, and to ensure robust generalization across a vast
range of identities, we adopt a source-view reconstruction
setting instead of video-based supervision. This inherently
limits the ability to recover facial details during novel view
synthesis. To address this, we introduce a refinement network. To maintain overall efficiency, we adopt a lightweight
U-Net style module. The network takes the coarse output

3.6. Face Symmetry Module
While our method covers visible regions of the head from
the input image well, occluded regions also need to be handled to improve the completeness of the reconstructed head.
Human faces typically have sufficient bilateral symmetry to
serve as a practical prior. We leverage this symmetry to generate the missing side of the head from a single input image.
This design avoids the need for diffusion-based multi-view
generation [63, 82] or an additional neural network [17, 51],
making the method computationally lightweight.

the Gaussians across this plane to create a complementary

original and mirrored Gaussians leads to conflicts in overlapping regions, so we assign low opacity to mirrored Gaussians in overlapped regions and increase their opacity with
distance. This yields a smooth transition between the two
sets and avoids visible seams. Fig. 3 shows the processes
described in Sec. 3.5 and Sec. 3.6.

Sampling Strategy. We compare our allocation strategy
(adaptive sampling) with three alternative sampling strategies (uniform, landmark, and parsing-guided) in reconstruction quality across Gaussian budgets M . Uniform sampling
uniformly samples the head region, and landmark sampling allocates more samples around facial landmarks [48].
Parsing-guided sampling leverages face parsing [92] and allocates more Gaussians to targeted regions such as eyes and
hair using fixed budgets for each semantic region. We evaluate under three settings of M : 12k, 8k, and 4k. As shown in
Tab. 5, our adaptive sampling generally outperforms other
strategies, with lower SSIM than uniform sampling. Fig. 7
visualizes improvements in reconstruction quality. Our allocation algorithm captures fine details such as hair strands,
eyes, and teeth more faithfully than uniform sampling. Although landmark sampling yields higher facial detail by allocating more samples around facial landmarks, it results in
blurred regions elsewhere, such as hair. We also find that a
fixed, deterministic budget for each facial semantic region
cannot outperform our adaptive sampling that adjusts for

5. Conclusion
We’ve proposed EGGHead, a single-image generalizable
head modeling method that offers fast enrollment and rendering while maintaining high reconstruction quality. Our
Gaussian allocation algorithm assigns appropriate numbers
of Gaussians to different semantic head regions, preserving visual fidelity even with lower Gaussian budgets. Our
feed-forward design combines a Gaussian allocation and
density prediction network with a Gaussian attribute regressor, eliminating expensive per-subject optimization typically required in 3D-GS. In addition, Voronoi tessellationguided attribute prediction improves structural accuracy under sparse single-view settings, while the Face Symmetry
Module enhances completeness without additional heavy
networks. We hope our method encourages future work
on efficient, high-quality, and generalizable Gaussian heads
from single images.
Limitations. Our model does not decompose texture into
albedo and illumination and thus cannot support relighting.
Moreover, pseudo-ground truth for Gaussian allocation is
derived from single in-the-wild FFHQ images. Multi-view
images of the same identity would provide more reliable
supervision and higher-quality ground truth. Uneven lighting can also challenge the face symmetry assumption, causing incorrect reflection of shadows or overexposed regions
under non-diffuse lighting. This can be mitigated by preprocessing the input image with an off-the-shelf delighting
method [35, 60] during enrollment. Finally, fully frontal inputs may prevent complete reconstruction of the back of the
head, as symmetry-based completion of occluded regions
benefits from a partially visible face profile.

8. Edge-aware Alignment
In addition to Gaussian merging, we incorporate an edgeaware alignment to ensure smoother transitions near internal boundaries in GAM. Specifically, we extract internal
edges from the source image using the Canny edge detector [12]. For each Gaussian center, we then compute its
distance to the nearest internal edge and determine its symmetric position P̃ across the nearest edge.
P̃ = 2 · proj(P ) − P,
(17)
where proj(P ) denotes the projection of P onto the nearest
edge. We pair P and P̃ across the boundary while preserving the total budget M . This alignment produces smoother
transitions along the edges and preserves overall rendering
consistency while reducing discontinuities.

11. Expression Editing
Although our framework is trained for source-view reconstruction with static Gaussians, it enables expression editing at inference time by deforming Gaussians bound to the
FLAME mesh [55]. Specifically, each Gaussian center is
bound to its closest point on the source FLAME mesh and
deformed using the displacement between the source and
driving meshes. Compared to GAGAvatar [17], EGGHead
is able to match the expression in the driving image more
closely. For example, in the second driving image shown
below, notice that GAGAvatar’s result still has an open
mouth whereas EGGHead is able to appropriately close
the lips. GAGAvatar also modifies the gaze direction incorrectly (See the first driving image). In terms of efficiency, we find that editing expressions has very little impact on the rendering speed (121.19 FPS → 119.21 FPS),
and EGGHead remains real-time. The enrollment time increases from 1.54 seconds to 2.14 seconds due to binding
and fitting Gaussians to the source mesh, but it remains
shorter than those of E3DGE [52] and G-NeRF [98].

16. Potential Societal Impacts
Creating photorealistic human heads always has the potential for generating malicious edited content of real subjects.
We certainly do not condone such misuse. One potential way to address this problem is to train deepfake methods [1, 3, 4, 18, 32, 41, 61, 68] that can distinguish our
generated heads from real images using some of our synthesized heads as part of training data.
