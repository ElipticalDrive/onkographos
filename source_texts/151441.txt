Introduction
Rendering commonly refers to the mapping of parameters describing a scene, such as geometry, reflectance properties, location and
characteristics of emitters as well as sensors, to the signal at the
sensor. If we summarize the scene parameters as a set of values
𝝅, we can express the rendering process as the computation of
the vector-valued function 𝑅(𝝅). In any application that requires
finding, adjusting, or optimizing 𝝅 based on requirements or goals
for the rendering output, it has proven extremely useful to know
the Jacobian J𝑅 (𝝅): As long as the objective can be modeled as a
scalar (and continuously differentiable) loss function L, optimizing
the function L (𝑅(𝝅)) can be performed with gradient-based methods. The need for gradients of the rendering function, especially
in the context of neural networks in vision tasks, has led to the
development of differentiable rendering.
Great progress has been made in differentiable rendering of light
in recent years (Section 2.2), in particular using the method of path
tracing [Kajiya 1986; Veach 1998]. In this work, we make use of these
recent techniques to simulate the propagation of sound. Although
differentiable acoustic rendering has recently appeared in the literature [Stanziola et al. 2023; Zhi et al. 2023], to our knowledge, we
present the first fully differentiable acoustic path tracer.
While both light and sound are waves and exhibit very similar
phenomena, the importance of various aspects in applications is very
different. Some aspects are often ignored in the simulation of both
types, such as nonlinear interactions or variations in propagation
speed. Diffraction or interference is generally more important for the
simulation of sound propagation, because many objects interacting
with audible sound are at the scale of the wavelengths. Nevertheless,
the simulation of sound propagation has many applications even
when ignoring interference and diffraction effects (Section 2.1). The
main difference for the simulation of sound and light is propagation
over time. For the simulation of light, we commonly assume instantaneous propagation. The function 𝑅(𝝅) is resolved in the angular
domain, but not in time. Conversely, for sound, it is often acceptable
to model omnidirectional receivers without any spatial resolution,
but it is crucial to resolve the time domain.
After introducing some background (Section 3), we develop extensions to differentiable path tracing that enable proper handling
of time (Section 4). We adopt a functional perspective, where quantities are represented as continuous functions of time, and show
that algorithms from differentiable light transport can be naturally
cast in this framework. In principle, the derivatives of the rendering function 𝑅, no matter how complex, can be determined using
automatic differentiation [Jakob et al. 2022b,a; Nimier-David et al.
2019; Zhang et al. 2020]. In practice, however, the problem is difficult
because the complexity of 𝑅(𝝅) makes tape-based automatic differentiation infeasible for all but the most simplistic problems. We
propose a time-resolved extension of Path Replay Backpropagation
(PRB) [Vicini et al. 2021], which is essential for efficient differentiable
simulations as derivatives can be computed with constant memory
and linear time. Despite handling (discretized) functions over time,
we show that this time-resolved backpropagation algorithm has
only minimal overhead over its light transport equivalent.
We continue by verifying our implementation (Section 5), first
showing that the forward simulation reproduces established simulation software based on geometric acoustics. Then, we demonstrate
that the differentiable simulation not only correctly accounts for the
effect of moving geometry on the time but also that time-resolved
Path Replay Backpropagation efficiently computes derivatives that
are equivalent to tape-based automatic differentiation.
We show several applications and inverse acoustics problems
for which gradient-based solutions are enabled by differentiable
acoustic path tracing (Section 6), including the optimization and
recovery of reflectance properties of surfaces or the position of
sound sources and geometry to improve the sound distribution.
We conclude by discussing the limitations inherited from the light
transport PRB method and those resulting from ignoring the wave
nature of sound and how they might be overcome (Section 7).
2
Related Work
The cross-fertilization between graphics and acoustics has a long
history, with advances in one field being brought to bear on the
other [Liu and Manocha 2020]. Graphics techniques have been used
in acoustics mostly for improved efficiency of computation, for example by exploiting GPUs [Mehra et al. 2012; Savioja et al. 2010], in
particular for interaction in virtual environments [Funkhouser et al.
1998; Manocha and Lin 2009; Mehra et al. 2015; Taylor et al. 2009;
Tsingos et al. 2001; Yeh et al. 2013] and precomputation-based methods for fast synthesis as needed in games [Raghuvanshi and Snyder
2014, 2018]. Physically-based simulation has been extended from
generating images of the scene to also generating sounds from the
interaction of objects [James et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2018; Zheng and
James 2010, 2011]. Here, we focus on applications that involve either
inverse problems (such as recovering scene parameters from observations) or optimization (such as improving application specific
properties by adjusting scene parameters).
In the following, we briefly review geometric techniques for room
acoustic simulation and how they are currently being extended
towards differentiable sound rendering. Then we provide a brief
summary of differentiable rendering for light transport simulation.
2.1
Room Acoustic Simulations
Wave-based simulation. Wave-based methods aim to simulate the
entire sound pressure field by solving the linear wave equation
numerically and include wave phenomena such as interference,
scattering and diffraction. Finite Element Method (FEM), Boundary
Element Method (BEM), and Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD)
have been applied to acoustic problems [Aretz 2012; Hamilton and
Bilbao 2017; Raghuvanshi and Snyder 2014; Southern et al. 2013;
Wang et al. 2018; Ziegelwanger et al. 2015]. Although providing the
highest physical accuracy, the use of wave-based methods in room
acoustics is currently limited to rendering lower frequencies due
to their excessive memory requirements and computation times. In
addition, the boundary conditions of the differential equation, i.e.,
the acoustic impedances of the surfaces, are sometimes difficult to
specify correctly in realistic environments.
Geometrical acoustics. Geometrical acoustics (GA) approaches approximate solutions, with much higher efficiency than wave-based

methods. GA assumes that sound propagates in straight lines and
inherently neglects wave phenomena such as diffraction and interference, unless these are approximated using simplified models. This
is a high-frequency approximation, where diffraction becomes less
relevant and energy-based (incoherent) summation can be assumed.
Additionally, GA methods assume a homogeneous and isotropic
medium with a constant speed of sound, and they do not account
for structure-borne sound. Despite these limitations, GA methods
are widely used in room acoustics due to their computational efficiency and ability to handle complex geometry. They are particularly effective for simulating sound propagation in large spaces,
such as concert halls and auditoriums, where wave effects are less
pronounced. GA approaches include the Image Source Model (ISM)
[Vorländer 2020, Ch. 11.4] and acoustic Path Tracing [Cao et al.
2016; Kiminki 2005; Schröder 2011; Siltanen et al. 2007]. The ISM
represents the analytical solution for the sound propagation of point
sources with ideal specular reflecting surfaces. Typical limitations
include the absence of scattering effects and an exponential growth
in complexity with the number of surfaces and the maximum order
of reflections, making it primarily feasible for scenarios involving
a low number of bounces or simple geometries such as a shoebox,
where no visibility checks are necessary [Allen and Berkley 1979].
Path tracing is a stochastic approach that does not suffer from the
exponential growth in complexity that ISM encounters, making it
applicable to arbitrary scene geometries. It is commonly used for
simulating the diffuse late reverberation in rooms but requires a
large number of rays to adequately simulate early specular reflections. Widely used room acoustical simulation software such as
RAVEN [Schröder 2011], EASE [AFMG 2023], or ODEON [Naylor
1993] use combinations of image sources and path tracing.
The method presented in this paper is based on a path tracer with
no complementary ISM.
Automatic acoustic optimization. In addition to the manual optimization of simulation and model parameters to achieve acoustic
goals [Labia et al. 2020] and the use of genetic algorithms [Christensen et al. 2014; Pilch 2020], inverse methods have also been applied in the field of room acoustics: Early work proposed an inverse
method based on the boundary element method to estimate material properties [Nava et al. 2006]. Dühring et al. [2008] minimized
noise levels in a room by topology optimization. [Dokmanić and
Vetterli 2012] used the finite element method to approximate the
wave equation to locate sound sources. Pelzer and Vorländer [2013]
inverted the effects of sound propagation along pre-computed paths
to optimize absorption and scattering coefficients in a hybrid ISM/path tracing model. Hölter et al. [2023]; Lemke et al. [2022]; Stein
et al. [2019, 2020]; Lemke et al. [2025] propose adjoint-based FDTD
approaches for the optimization and interpolation of sound source
directivity and acoustic boundary conditions. Recent developments
include the implementation of a modular and differentiable acoustic
simulation of the wave equation, called j-Wave, by Stanziola et al.
[2023]. Zhi et al. [2023] present a fully differentiable Image Source
Model, suitable for gradient-based optimization of scene parameters,
including the scene geometry – but it is limited to shoebox rooms.
Our approach can optimize arbitrary scenes and parameters by
propagating derivatives through an acoustic path tracing simulation.

Differentiable Rendering
Differentiable rendering is encountered in the solution of inverse
graphics problems: the state of a scene or more generally a system, defined by a set of parameters, is inferred from given observations. The
goal is to minimize the difference between renderings of the scene
and the observations. Differentiable rendering enables a gradientbased solution by making the rendering process differentiable; the
parameters are iteratively updated to minimize the difference.
Physically-based and Approximate Methods. Approaches to differentiable rendering can be categorized according to how accurately
they model energy transport. At one end are approximate methods,
where some are based on neural rendering, e.g., for 3D reconstruction [Li et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2021; Worchel et al. 2022; Yariv et al.
2020] or view synthesis [Mildenhall et al. 2020]. Others use real-time
graphics techniques or simplifying assumptions for efficient, approximate rendering [Kerbl et al. 2023; Luan et al. 2021; Lyu et al. 2021;
Munkberg et al. 2022; Worchel and Alexa 2023]. At the other end are
physically-based methods that perform a differentiable simulation
of light transport [Jakob et al. 2022b; Li et al. 2018; Nimier-David
et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020].
Our approach lies at the physically-based end of the spectrum:
we perform a differentiable simulation of sound propagation, based
on the physical foundation of geometric acoustics.
Time-Dependent Energy Transport. Many differentiable light transport simulations assume instantaneous energy transfer, such that
the propagation speed is ignored. A notable exception are methods
for differentiable transient rendering, which consider settings where
the propagation of light cannot be assumed to be instantaneous.
One line of work builds differentiable transient rendering on a
three-bounce assumption [Choi et al. 2023; Plack et al. 2023], which,
however, is too restrictive for general applications in acoustics. In
contrast, we consider time-resolved differentiable rendering for an
arbitrary number of bounces.
Most closely related to our work are approaches for physicallybased differentiable transient rendering [Wu et al. 2021; Yi et al.
2021]. They combine path-space differentiable rendering [Zhang
et al. 2020] with time-dependent energy transport. However, their
systems rely on forward-mode differentiation (on the CPU), which
is only suitable for few parameters and simple applications.
Practical differentiable rendering systems use backward-mode differentiation ("backpropagation"), but a naive implementation based
on automatic differentiation quickly becomes memory-bound as the
computational graphs in path tracing are large and complex. Our
contribution is orthogonal to existing work: while prior work is
mainly concerned with handling discontinuities in the path-space
form, we investigate efficient backward-mode differentiation for
time-resolved physically-based differentiable rendering.
Efficient Backward-Mode Differentiation. Efficient methods for differentiating light transport simulations are based on the observation
that computing the derivatives of a light transport simulation may
be seen as another special form of transport [Nimier-David et al.
2020; Stam 2020; Vicini et al. 2021]. This simulation can be solved
with the algorithm from the forward simulation; potentially re-using
information, as in Path Replay Backpropagation (PRB) [Vicini et al.

Implementation and Validation
Our method is implemented on top of Mitsuba 3 [Jakob et al. 2022b],
a system for differentiable, physically-based light transport simulation. We found that most of its components are readily suitable for
the simulation of sound, such as the different shapes and emitters.
We implemented a custom sensor, a (possibly omni-)directional Microphone, and a custom film, a Tape. We model point sources using
small spherical (omnidirectional) area emitters; directional patterns
to model speaker membranes are left to future work.
The core of our method is implemented in three custom integrators. AcousticPRB implements temporal Path Replay Backpropagation for efficient and time-sensitive gradient estimation of scene
parameters using the three-point form parametrization described in

Applications
In this section, we present some example applications of differentiable acoustic path tracing with more complex scenes to demonstrate the capabilities of our method and to give an idea of the
possibilities of solving inverse problems in room acoustic design.
6.1
Optimization of Material Parameters
A common first step when building a room model for acoustic
simulations is fitting the acoustic properties of the model’s walls
(absorption and/or scattering) so the simulation matches a measurement. With Temporal Path Replay Backpropatation, we can
efficiently perform this task, even for many material parameters
simultaneously. Because the geometry stays fixed, no discontinuities occur and AcousticPRB calculates the correct gradients. As
objective function we use the squared error between the current
and target ETC, see Eq. (21).
6.1.1 Simple Scene. Although it may seem simple, finding the material parameters of all six surfaces (walls, floor, and ceiling) within
a shoebox scene is a challenging task when relying solely on one
measured ETC. Here, we fit the model to a rendered reference ETC,
ensuring that any discrepancies arise from the computed gradients
rather than measurement-simulation discrepancies.
The scene is initialized with uniform absorption coefficients and a
uniform scattering coefficient. The absorption factor attenuates the
energy of all paths that hit the corresponding surface (Section A).

6.2.3 Optimal Ceiling Reflector Angles. This experiment addresses
a common challenge in the acoustic design of rooms for speech
and music: achieving both high intelligibility and clarity, while providing a uniform listener experience. Typically, it is desirable to
maximize clarity measures such as 𝐶 80 , i.e. to maximize the energy
within the first 80 ms after the direct sound, while minimizing the
variation in energy across listener positions. In concert halls, ceiling
reflectors are often used to direct sound to the audience. Finding the
optimal shape and positioning of these reflectors is not a trivial task,
as the reflected sound reaches the audience not only via first-order
reflections, but also via later, higher-order reflections. We demonstrate that our method can, in this complex setting, simultaneously
optimize the angle of one reflector positioned above the stage and
three additional reflectors positioned above the audience. The scene
geometry is taken from the BRAS Benchmark [Aspöck et al. 2020;
Brinkmann et al. 2021, CR4]. It is shown in Fig. 12 (top left).
The objective function that both maximizes the clarity and minimizes the variance across listener positions can be formulated as a
joint loss function, as proposed by [IEC 60268-16 2020]:
L (𝐸) = std(𝐸 early ) − mean(𝐸 early ).
(39)
This formulation assumes that the clarity depends only on the early
energy 𝐸 early , which arrives within 80 ms after the direct sound and
the late energy remains unchanged. This is a reasonable assumption
since we only adjust the position of reflectors and don’t introduce additional absorptive surfaces. This allows us to restrict the maximum
rendering length, which speeds up computation times.

Discussion
This work transfers the principles of differentiable path tracing
from light transport to sound propagation for the backpropagation
of time-dependent gradients to scene parameters. It extends Path
Replay Backpropagation [Vicini et al. 2021] to allow for efficient,
time-dependent derivatives, addressing an open problem previously
stated in the context of differentiable transient rendering [Wu et al.
2021; Yi et al. 2021]. The experiments with our implementation have
demonstrated that differentiable path tracing of sound propagation
is feasible and enables the optimization of various scene parameters,
such as material properties and geometry, to achieve desired acoustic
characteristics in room environments. This opens up applications
that appear to go significantly beyond traditional techniques in
acoustics. Our implementation can be easily extended to model
properties that are currently not supported, such as sources or
receivers with arbitrary directional characteristics.
Biased gradients. An apparent limitation are the biased gradients
produced by the AcousticPRB integrator when visibility is affected
by scene parameters. As shown by Worchel and Finnendahl [2025],
there is currently no implementation that simultaneously handles
discontinuities and is PRB-based, so a combination of the two will
clearly benefit differentiable acoustic path tracing.
Reflection model. An important ingredient to make gradient descent successful is smoothing – this is not only true for time but also
for modeling reflection behavior. Reflection models that consider
more than ideal diffuse and ideal specular reflections are rarely seen
in geometrical acoustics. At the same time, it seems plausible that
Phong-like models for the specular-diffuse transition are not only
necessary to make gradient descent useful, but also provide a more
realistic model of sound reflections in general. It seems worthwhile
to develop such models for acoustics, in order to reconcile actual
measurements with the desired efficiency of evaluation. We want
to emphasize that in our implementation, the reflection model is
nothing but a property of the scene, which could be modeled with a
fixed but parameterized function. With this setup, the model could
be matched against physical measurements using differentiable path
tracing and gradient descent.
Wave effects. Our current implementation is based on path tracing,
which ignores all effects related to the wave nature of sound. While
this is consistent with ray tracing techniques used in acoustics, it
is nonetheless a more serious simplification of physical phenomena compared to the simulation of light transport. The literature
ACM Trans. Graph., Vol. 44, No. 4, Article 82. Publication date: August 2025.
in acoustics, but also in graphics, has shown many efficient techniques for incorporating wave effects into the simulation [Kirsch
and Ewert 2023; Pohl and Stephenson 2014; Steinberg et al. 2024;
Svensson and Calamia 2006]. It will be worthwhile to explore how
such approaches could be integrated into the Monte Carlo simulation framework. For improved fidelity and simulation in arbitrary
frequency bands, full spectral path tracing could be implemented as
suggested by Radziszewski et al. [2009]. Spectral rendering with arbitrary sensitivity spectra and logarithmic sampling in octave bands
has already been successfully applied to the forward rendering pass
[Jüterbock et al. 2024].
Time-Dependent Backward-Mode Differentiation and Path-Space
Rendering. Our approach is connected to differentiable transient
rendering, which is commonly derived from a path space formulation [Wu et al. 2021; Yi et al. 2021]: although our formalism is
based on the classical rendering equation with a spherical integral,
our implementation uses a surface integral, whose derivatives are
closely related to the interior derivatives of the path-space form.
We see an exciting avenue in combining our insights with the
path space formalism and its recent advances [Zhang et al. 2023],
which may lead to more robust discontinuity handling in differentiable acoustic simulations on the one hand, and more efficient
differentiable transient rendering on the other hand.

Room Acoustics Metrics
In room acoustic design and architectural acoustics, sound propagation can assumed to be linear and time-invariant (LTI). Rooms
are therefore often described by their Room Impulse Response (RIR)
ℎ(𝑡), which is the response of the room to an acoustic impulse, such
as an idealized popping balloon. The RIR contains information about
the energy and arrival time of direct sound and reflections, and is
the central basis for deriving room acoustic metrics.

response’s phase information, because they quantify the temporal
energy distribution in some way, so they can be derived from the
energy time curve, which is the squared impulse response 𝐻 (𝑡) =
ℎ 2 (𝑡). In our method, we simulate the ETC directly (see Eq. (5)).
