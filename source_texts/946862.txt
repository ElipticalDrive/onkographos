# Abstract

In this paper, we propose to integrate superpixel-guided sampling into the framework of 3D Gaussian Splatting for novel view synthesis. Given a sequence of frames, where each frame is a pair of RGB-D image and the camera pose that captures the image, we first select the keyframes. Then, we decompose each keyframe image into superpixels, back-project each superpixel's center into 3D space, and initialize a 3D Gaussian at the back-projected position. This superpixel-guided sampling produces a set of sparse but welldistributed Gaussians, which enables the optimization procedure to converge quickly. The experimental results show that with a significantly reduced computing cost, we can synthesize a novel view the quality of which is comparable to that generated by the state-of-the-art methods.

# 1 Introduction

Novel view synthesis refers to the task of generating a new image with an arbitrary camera pose by using a set of source images and their camera poses. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [14] optimizes a neural network to encapsulate a 3D scene to synthesize a novel view, but both training and inference are computationally demanding. Addressing this challenge, Müller et al. [15] proposed a multi-resolution hash encoding technique, which enables real-time rendering, but it remains unsuitable for large-scale mapping due to the exponential increase in the size of the hash table.

More recently, Kerbl et al. [11] proposed a novel method for representing and rendering the radiance field, named 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). It obtains the 3D point cloud of the scene with COLMAP [17], which uses SIFT [13] for Structure from Motion (SfM). Figure 1a shows SIFT features (in blue dots) extracted from an input image, and Figure 1b shows the 3D point cloud (in green dots) generated via SfM. Note that the majority of 3D points are located at the features of the scene, i.e., at the edges and corners. A Gaussian is initialized at each 3D point, and all of the Gaussians are optimized. Figure 1c shows the optimized Gaussians projected onto the image plane, where all Gaussians are uniformly scaled by 3 and painted in green just for visualization purposes. Figure 1d shows the result of rendering with the optimized Gaussians.

In Figure 1c, it can be observed that the optimized Gaussians are well distributed, i.e., they are no longer concentrated on the edges and corners of the scene. It implies that many Gaussians have been shifted toward the surface areas that are deficient in features. On the other hand, the number of Gaussians has been significantly increased during optimization by the so-called densification process, which populates the "empty spaces" with new Gaussians. In general, the densification process increases the number of Gaussians dozens of times. In 3DGS, the optimization procedure coupled with densification is costly.

Such a problem of 3DGS can be resolved by distributing the "initial Gussians" appropriately over the space. For this purpose, we propose to decompose the input image into superpixels [19], as shown in Figure 1e, where the dots represent the superpixel centers. Then, each superpixel's center is back-projected into 3D space, as shown in Figure 1f, and a Gaussian is created at the 3D position. Our initial Gaussians are distributed better than those of 3DGS presented in Figure 1b.

It is important to note the key strengths of our well-distributed initial Gaussians. Firstly, whereas the edges and corners of the scene are located in the superpixel boundaries, the superpixel centers are located away from them. Therefore, our method shifts the Gaussians far less than 3DGS. Secondly, we have fewer empty spaces than 3DGS and therefore the need for densification is reduced. These strengths enable our optimization procedure to converge several times faster than 3DGS. Figure 1g visualizes the optimized Gaussians, and Figure 1h shows the result of rendering with the optimized Gaussians. Even though the computation time is significantly reduced in our method, the rendering quality is comparable to that of 3DGS shown in Figure 1d.

# 2 Related Work

# 2.1 Neural radiance fields

View synthesis has seen significant advancements with the introduction of NeRF (standing for Neural Radiance Fields) [14]. NeRF utilizes a deep neural network, specifically a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) model, which takes 5D input (for spatial coordinates and viewing directions) to compute 3D color values. This approach enables highly detailed and photorealistic rendering from sparse input images, setting a new benchmark in capturing intricate details and view-dependent phenomena in synthesized views.

Further enhancing the practicality of neural rendering, Müller et al. [15] introduced Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (INGP), a method for rapid training and real-time rendering. INGP leverages a multiresolution hash encoding, significantly speeding up the rendering process without sacrificing quality. This development marks a stride towards real-time applications in the fields such as virtual reality and interactive 3D modeling. In a similar vein, the work of Chen et al. [4] introduced an innovative approach known as NeuRBF (standing for Neural Radial Basis Function Fields). This method utilizes adaptive radial basis functions to enhance spatial adaptivity and detail representation in neural fields. NeuRBF demonstrates its effectiveness by encoding a broader range of frequencies, leading to improved accuracy and compactness in applications such as 2D image fitting and 3D reconstruction, thus marking another significant evolution in the field of neural rendering.

# 2.2 Explicit scene reconstruction and rendering

The evolution of 3D rendering and surface representation in computer graphics has been marked by several key developments. Pfister et al. [16] introduced the concept of surfels, small surface patches that serve as fundamental rendering primitives. This approach diverged from traditional polygon-based rendering, often offering a more detailed and flexible representation for complex geometries. Expanding on this idea, Zwicker et al. [26] developed a technique known as surface splatting. This method projects surfels onto the image plane and blends them, enhancing the rendering of detailed surface features and soft shadows. Surface splatting shows how surfels could be effectively utilized for high-quality renderings of intricate scenes.

Further advancing these techniques, the concept of 3DGS (standing for 3D Gaussian Splatting) [11] emerged as a significant innovation. This approach uses 3D Gaussian functions to represent surfels, providing a robust mathematical model for the spatial distribution of surfel attributes. 3DGS excels in rendering soft transitions and fine details, offering improved accuracy and realism, especially in complex scenes where traditional methods may struggle.

In order to improve 3DGS, several approaches are introduced. Zhou et al. [7] present a structured lower-dimensional feature field, which allows for more efficient feature field distillation of 3DGS. This approach supports various applications, such as semantic segmentation, language-guided editing, and both promptable and promptless instance segmentation. Mini-Splatting [7] incorporates strategies for densification and simplification through intersection preservation and sampling. GaussianRoom [22] enhances the geometry represented by Gaussians, which in turn improves the

efficiency of the signed distance field by guiding its point sampling. Several works observe the original 3DGS method fails to accurately represent surfaces. Huang et al. [10] reconstruct geometrically accurate radiance fields by using a set of 2D oriented planar Gaussian disks which provide view-consistent geometry for modeling surfaces. SuGaR [8] extracts mesh from 3DGS scene, encouraging the Gaussians to align well with the surface of the scene. These methods inspire that optimizing a 3DGS scene benefits from the provided geometrically-accurate Gaussians, which are affixed to the surfaces.

There are some efforts to compress the 3DGS scene by pruning the Gaussians. In order to minimize the number of Gaussians, Fan et al. [6] propose a method named Light Gaussian. It transforms

However, this approach requires a pre-optimized Gaussian scene and involves complex computations to identify the least significant Gaussian points for pruning. Furthermore, they propose a compact representation by employing a grid-based neural field. However, this approach requires an additional effort, i.e., training of codebooks to represent the geometric attributes of the Gaussians through vector quantization [23] for memory efficiency. We challenge this issue by sampling filtered 3D Gaussians in advance of optimization, rather than pruning them after or during optimizing a 3DGS scene.

# 3 Method

Figure 2 provides an overview of our method. The input to our system is a sequence of pre-captured frames, where each frame is a pair of RGB-D image and camera pose. Traversing the frames, we select keyframes. Then, for each keyframe, superpixel-guided sampling is performed to generate the 3D point cloud. The 3D Gaussians are initialized at the 3D points and then optimized over iterations.

## 3.1 Keyframe selection

In our method, a set of Gaussians is newly created "per frame" via superpixel-guided sampling. If we process all frames, the accumulated number of Gaussians will become excessively large. To avoid it, we opt to take a keyframe structure, which is commonly adopted in SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping) systems.

Given a frame sequence, a frame is taken as a keyframe if its camera pose is significantly different from the last keyframe's. In the current proof-of-concept implementation, the keyframe selection criteria are simple: 0.4 radians for the orientation disparity and 0.5 meters for the position difference.

#### 3.2 Superpixel-guided sampling

Our goal is to decompose an input image into superpixels, which help the optimization procedure converge as quickly as possible. Among dozens of superpixel algorithms [19], we adopt SLIC [1] method for its speed and efficient memory usage. It is also easy to implement within the Python framework.

When a 3D Gaussian is projected onto the image plane, the projected Gaussian's shape is elliptical; more generally, it is convex. Figure 3a and Figure 3b show two exemplar sets of superpixels generated by SLIC. As the superpixels presented in Figure 3b are mostly convex, they would suit better our goal and therefore are preferred to the superpixels in Figure 3a. By controlling the parameters of SLIC, we make the superpixels as convex as possible.

In Figure 3b, the superpixels' centers are depicted as red dots. An RGB color is sampled from each center, and its depth value is sampled from the same position of the depth image. The depth pixel is back-projected into the 3D camera space and is then transformed to the world space using the camera pose. Figure 3c shows the resulting 3D point cloud, where a 3D Gaussian will be initialized at a point.

Our superpixel-guided sampling results in well-distributed initial Gaussians. They have two desirable characteristics that can save the optimization cost significantly: (1) Unlike in 3DGS, the initial Gaussians are not concentrated on the edges and corners of the scene. Consequently, they would be shifted much less than in 3DGS. (2) In principle, we have few apparent "empty spaces," and therefore, unlike 3DGS, we do not highly depend on the expensive densification process.

However, unnecessarily many superpixels are often generated by SLIC. For example, see the ceiling area in Figure 3b. It is mostly textureless and therefore does not require so many superpixels. In order to reduce the number of initial Gaussians, we combine adjacent superpixels of similar colors, as shown in Figure 3d, where the combined superpixels are visualized in the same color.

Unfortunately, such a combining step may produce concave superpixels. In order to resolve this side effect, we approximate each concave superpixel by a polygon and then decompose it into convex polygons [9]. Such convex decomposition may often result in too many fragments. We then replace them by the original superpixels generated by SLIC. Figure 3e shows the result, where the convex

In our method, the Gaussians are optimized in the same way as in 3DGS. For comparison purposes, it is also made to iterate 30,000 times. However, the density control is made in a much shorter period, i.e., between the 500th and 1,600th iterations, as illustrated in Figure 4-(b), resulting in 11 density controls in total. In general, our optimization procedure converges quickly, thanks to the well-distributed initial Gaussians, and therefore we can significantly reduce the iteration count. This will be discussed at the end of Section 4.

#### 4 Experiments

# 4.1 Baseline methods

Our goal is to make a compact Gaussian representation of the scene. Sharing the same goal as our method, several methods have been proposed to compress the 3DGS scene. Among them, two representative methods are selected as the baselines:

- Compact Gaussian proposed by Lee et al. [12] takes the point cloud generated by COLMAP as input (as the original 3DGS does) and applies a learnable volume mask to filter out insignificant Gaussians during optimization.
- Light Gaussian proposed by Fan et al. [6] prunes the least significant Gaussians by operating directly on the Gaussians pre-optimized by the original 3DGS.

With the baseline methods, our experiments are made using Replica dataset [18] and NeRF-synthetic dataset [14], and the results are presented in Sections 4.2 and 4.3, respectively.

#### 4.3 NeRF-synthetic dataset

Eight scenes in the NeRF-synthetic dataset are used (Chair, Drums, Ficus, Hotdog, Lego, Materials, Mic and Ship). Each scene is associated with two frame sequences, each with 200 frames. We denote them as ��1 and ��2. In ��1, a frame consists of an RGB-D image and the camera pose that captures the image. In ��2, a frame consists of an RGB image and its camera pose.

4.3.1 Input data. As discussed in Section 4.2, the baseline methods as well as our method perform scene optimization with ��1, but the input data and their sources are different. Table 4 presents the statistics of the input data. Whereas Compact Gaussian takes as input 24,347.5 points generated by COLMAP, Light Gaussian takes 264,747 Gaussians pre-optimized by 3DGS.



For NeRF-synthetic dataset, our keyframe selection criterion is relaxed, i.e., the position difference is changed from 0.5 meters to 2.0 meters whereas the orientation disparity remains the same, 0.4. Then, our method selects approximately 30 keyframes out of 200 frames in ��1. Finally, we exclude the pixels with the inverse depth zero, which correspond to the background, and run the superpixelguided sampling to obtain 16,271.63 points on average.

4.3.2 Evaluation. The optimized scenes are rendered with the camera poses in ��1. The rendered images are provided in a supplementary material, and Table 5 shows the evaluation results. The PSNR

score of our method (32.47) is comparable to that of Compact Gaussian (32.72) whereas our method and Compact Gaussian have the same scores for both SSIM and LPIPS. With respect to the number of optimized Gaussians and the optimization time, our method outperforms the baselines, but Compact Gaussian consumes the least memory. It is worth noting that our variant denoted as "Ours w/o dens" excels Light Gaussian across all metrics.

In general, the images in NeRF-synthetic dataset are more textured than those in Replica dataset, posing a challenge to our method. Therefore, our PSNR scores are the highest for Chair, Ficus and Hotdog, all of which have simple textures. On the other hand, NeRF-synthetic dataset is associated with simpler lighting environments than Replica dataset, which allows the baselines to compress the scenes effectively. This explains why our method has bigger data size in spite of the smaller number of Gaussians.

# 5 Additional Experiments

For further investigation, an additional experiment is made with the Mip-NeRF360 dataset [2], where unlike in the Replica and NeRFsynthetic datasets, each scene consists of a sequence of RGB frames (not RGB-D frames) and their camera poses. On the other hand, the compact 3D Gaussians generated by our method are successfully ported into an immersive VR environment. This section reports the results.

# 5.1 Mip-NeRF360 dataset

Nine scenes in Mip-NeRF360 are used (Bicycle, Bonsai, Counter, Flowers, Garden, Kitchen, Room, Stump and Treehill). They are all real scenes unlike the synthetic scenes in the Replica and NeRFsynthetic datasets.

5.1.1 Input data. As listed in Table 7, Compact Gaussian takes as input 48,697.67 points generated by COLMAP, and Light Gaussian takes 2,838,710.44 Gaussians pre-optimized by 3DGS. Our method requires depth images, but Mip-NeRF360 contains only RGB images. Therefore, we generated them using the RGB images with 2DGS [10]. When we run the superpixel-guided sampling using the depth images, we obtain 52,364 points on average. The scenes in Mip-NeRF360 vary in scale, and we use an adaptive keyframe

selection criterion so that a keyframe is selected out of about five frames.

5.1.2 Evaluation. The optimized scenes are rendered with the given camera poses, and the rendered images are provided in a supplementary material. Table 8 shows the evaluation results. The overall scores of our method are better than those of Compact Gaussian and comparable to those of Light Gaussian. With respect to the number of optimized Gaussians, optimization time and data size, our method outperforms the baselines. Note that our variant denoted as "Ours w/o dens" optimizes quite a small number of Gaussians (45,163) with little compromise in rendering quality.

# 5.2 VR application

Recently, commercial tools such as Blender [3], Unreal [5] and Unity [20] began to support 3DGS. Figure 7 shows a VR scene, where the 3D Gaussians optimized by our method are rendered using Unity. In such a VR environment, users can walk through the environment, as demonstrated in the supplementary video.

Unfortunately, it is challenging to import the customized compression representations generated by the baselines into Unity. Therefore, we chose the original 3DGS [11] and imported its Gaussians into Unity. Then, using Unity Profiler, the GPU memory usages are measured for the original 3DGS and our method. Table 9 shows the results for the Replica, NeRF-synthetic and Mip-NeRF360 datasets. Since our method requires significantly less GPU memory, it benefits resource-limited VR applications.
