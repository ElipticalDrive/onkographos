Abstract
CCS Concepts
In this paper, we propose to integrate superpixel-guided sampling
into the framework of 3D Gaussian Splatting for novel view synthesis. Given a sequence of frames, where each frame is a pair of
RGB-D image and the camera pose that captures the image, we
first select the keyframes. Then, we decompose each keyframe image into superpixels, back-project each superpixel’s center into 3D
space, and initialize a 3D Gaussian at the back-projected position.
This superpixel-guided sampling produces a set of sparse but welldistributed Gaussians, which enables the optimization procedure
to converge quickly. The experimental results show that with a
significantly reduced computing cost, we can synthesize a novel
view the quality of which is comparable to that generated by the
state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction
Novel view synthesis refers to the task of generating a new image with an arbitrary camera pose by using a set of source images
and their camera poses. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [14] optimizes a neural network to encapsulate a 3D scene to synthesize a
novel view, but both training and inference are computationally
demanding. Addressing this challenge, Müller et al. [15] proposed a
multi-resolution hash encoding technique, which enables real-time
rendering, but it remains unsuitable for large-scale mapping due to
the exponential increase in the size of the hash table.
VRST ’24, October 09–11, 2024, Trier, Germany
More recently, Kerbl et al. [11] proposed a novel method for
representing and rendering the radiance field, named 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS). It obtains the 3D point cloud of the scene with
COLMAP [17], which uses SIFT [13] for Structure from Motion
(SfM). Figure 1a shows SIFT features (in blue dots) extracted from
an input image, and Figure 1b shows the 3D point cloud (in green
dots) generated via SfM. Note that the majority of 3D points are
located at the features of the scene, i.e., at the edges and corners.
A Gaussian is initialized at each 3D point, and all of the Gaussians
are optimized. Figure 1c shows the optimized Gaussians projected
onto the image plane, where all Gaussians are uniformly scaled by
1 and painted in green just for visualization purposes. Figure 1d
3
shows the result of rendering with the optimized Gaussians.
In Figure 1c, it can be observed that the optimized Gaussians are
well distributed, i.e., they are no longer concentrated on the edges
and corners of the scene. It implies that many Gaussians have been
shifted toward the surface areas that are deficient in features. On
the other hand, the number of Gaussians has been significantly
increased during optimization by the so-called densification process,
which populates the “empty spaces” with new Gaussians. In general, the densification process increases the number of Gaussians
dozens of times. In 3DGS, the optimization procedure coupled with
densification is costly.
Such a problem of 3DGS can be resolved by distributing the
“initial Gussians” appropriately over the space. For this purpose,
we propose to decompose the input image into superpixels [19], as
shown in Figure 1e, where the dots represent the superpixel centers.
Then, each superpixel’s center is back-projected into 3D space, as
shown in Figure 1f, and a Gaussian is created at the 3D position.
Our initial Gaussians are distributed better than those of 3DGS
presented in Figure 1b.
It is important to note the key strengths of our well-distributed
initial Gaussians. Firstly, whereas the edges and corners of the scene
are located in the superpixel boundaries, the superpixel centers are
located away from them. Therefore, our method shifts the Gaussians far less than 3DGS. Secondly, we have fewer empty spaces
than 3DGS and therefore the need for densification is reduced.
These strengths enable our optimization procedure to converge
several times faster than 3DGS. Figure 1g visualizes the optimized
Gaussians, and Figure 1h shows the result of rendering with the
optimized Gaussians. Even though the computation time is significantly reduced in our method, the rendering quality is comparable
to that of 3DGS shown in Figure 1d.
2 Related Work
2.1 Neural radiance fields
View synthesis has seen significant advancements with the introduction of NeRF (standing for Neural Radiance Fields) [14]. NeRF
utilizes a deep neural network, specifically a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) model, which takes 5D input (for spatial coordinates
and viewing directions) to compute 3D color values. This approach
enables highly detailed and photorealistic rendering from sparse input images, setting a new benchmark in capturing intricate details
and view-dependent phenomena in synthesized views.
Further enhancing the practicality of neural rendering, Müller
et al. [15] introduced Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (INGP),
Kim et al.
a method for rapid training and real-time rendering. INGP leverages a multiresolution hash encoding, significantly speeding up the
rendering process without sacrificing quality. This development
marks a stride towards real-time applications in the fields such as
virtual reality and interactive 3D modeling. In a similar vein, the
work of Chen et al. [4] introduced an innovative approach known
as NeuRBF (standing for Neural Radial Basis Function Fields). This
method utilizes adaptive radial basis functions to enhance spatial
adaptivity and detail representation in neural fields. NeuRBF demonstrates its effectiveness by encoding a broader range of frequencies,
leading to improved accuracy and compactness in applications such
as 2D image fitting and 3D reconstruction, thus marking another
significant evolution in the field of neural rendering.
2.2
Explicit scene reconstruction and rendering
The evolution of 3D rendering and surface representation in computer graphics has been marked by several key developments. Pfister et al. [16] introduced the concept of surfels, small surface patches
that serve as fundamental rendering primitives. This approach diverged from traditional polygon-based rendering, often offering a
more detailed and flexible representation for complex geometries.
Expanding on this idea, Zwicker et al. [26] developed a technique
known as surface splatting. This method projects surfels onto the
image plane and blends them, enhancing the rendering of detailed
surface features and soft shadows. Surface splatting shows how
surfels could be effectively utilized for high-quality renderings of
intricate scenes.
Further advancing these techniques, the concept of 3DGS (standing for 3D Gaussian Splatting) [11] emerged as a significant innovation. This approach uses 3D Gaussian functions to represent surfels,
providing a robust mathematical model for the spatial distribution
of surfel attributes. 3DGS excels in rendering soft transitions and
fine details, offering improved accuracy and realism, especially in
complex scenes where traditional methods may struggle.
In order to improve 3DGS, several approaches are introduced.
Zhou et al. [7] present a structured lower-dimensional feature field,
which allows for more efficient feature field distillation of 3DGS.
This approach supports various applications, such as semantic
segmentation, language-guided editing, and both promptable and
promptless instance segmentation. Mini-Splatting [7] incorporates
strategies for densification and simplification through intersection preservation and sampling. GaussianRoom [22] enhances the
geometry represented by Gaussians, which in turn improves the
efficiency of the signed distance field by guiding its point sampling.
Several works observe the original 3DGS method fails to accurately represent surfaces. Huang et al. [10] reconstruct geometrically accurate radiance fields by using a set of 2D oriented planar
Gaussian disks which provide view-consistent geometry for modeling surfaces. SuGaR [8] extracts mesh from 3DGS scene, encouraging the Gaussians to align well with the surface of the scene. These
methods inspire that optimizing a 3DGS scene benefits from the
provided geometrically-accurate Gaussians, which are affixed to
the surfaces.
There are some efforts to compress the 3DGS scene by pruning
the Gaussians. In order to minimize the number of Gaussians, Fan
et al. [6] propose a method named Light Gaussian. It transforms

after or during optimizing a 3DGS scene.
3
Method
Figure 2 provides an overview of our method. The input to our
system is a sequence of pre-captured frames, where each frame is
a pair of RGB-D image and camera pose. Traversing the frames,
we select keyframes. Then, for each keyframe, superpixel-guided
sampling is performed to generate the 3D point cloud. The 3D
Gaussians are initialized at the 3D points and then optimized over
iterations.
3.1
Keyframe selection
In our method, a set of Gaussians is newly created “per frame” via
superpixel-guided sampling. If we process all frames, the accumulated number of Gaussians will become excessively large. To avoid
it, we opt to take a keyframe structure, which is commonly adopted
in SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping) systems.
Given a frame sequence, a frame is taken as a keyframe if its
camera pose is significantly different from the last keyframe’s. In the
current proof-of-concept implementation, the keyframe selection
criteria are simple: 0.4 radians for the orientation disparity and 0.5
meters for the position difference.
3.2
Superpixel-guided sampling
Our goal is to decompose an input image into superpixels, which
help the optimization procedure converge as quickly as possible.
Among dozens of superpixel algorithms [19], we adopt SLIC [1]
method for its speed and efficient memory usage. It is also easy to
implement within the Python framework.
When a 3D Gaussian is projected onto the image plane, the projected Gaussian’s shape is elliptical; more generally, it is convex.
Figure 3a and Figure 3b show two exemplar sets of superpixels generated by SLIC. As the superpixels presented in Figure 3b are mostly
convex, they would suit better our goal and therefore are preferred
to the superpixels in Figure 3a. By controlling the parameters of
SLIC, we make the superpixels as convex as possible.
In Figure 3b, the superpixels’ centers are depicted as red dots.
An RGB color is sampled from each center, and its depth value is
sampled from the same position of the depth image. The depth pixel
is back-projected into the 3D camera space and is then transformed
to the world space using the camera pose. Figure 3c shows the
resulting 3D point cloud, where a 3D Gaussian will be initialized at
a point.
Our superpixel-guided sampling results in well-distributed initial Gaussians. They have two desirable characteristics that can
save the optimization cost significantly: (1) Unlike in 3DGS, the
initial Gaussians are not concentrated on the edges and corners of
the scene. Consequently, they would be shifted much less than in
3DGS. (2) In principle, we have few apparent “empty spaces,” and
therefore, unlike 3DGS, we do not highly depend on the expensive
densification process.
However, unnecessarily many superpixels are often generated
by SLIC. For example, see the ceiling area in Figure 3b. It is mostly
textureless and therefore does not require so many superpixels.
In order to reduce the number of initial Gaussians, we combine
adjacent superpixels of similar colors, as shown in Figure 3d, where
the combined superpixels are visualized in the same color.
Unfortunately, such a combining step may produce concave superpixels. In order to resolve this side effect, we approximate each
concave superpixel by a polygon and then decompose it into convex
polygons [9]. Such convex decomposition may often result in too
many fragments. We then replace them by the original superpixels
generated by SLIC. Figure 3e shows the result, where the convex

4 Experiments
4.1 Baseline methods
Our goal is to make a compact Gaussian representation of the scene.
Sharing the same goal as our method, several methods have been
proposed to compress the 3DGS scene. Among them, two representative methods are selected as the baselines:
• Compact Gaussian proposed by Lee et al. [12] takes the
point cloud generated by COLMAP as input (as the original
3DGS does) and applies a learnable volume mask to filter
out insignificant Gaussians during optimization.
• Light Gaussian proposed by Fan et al. [6] prunes the least
significant Gaussians by operating directly on the Gaussians
pre-optimized by the original 3DGS.
With the baseline methods, our experiments are made using
Replica dataset [18] and NeRF-synthetic dataset [14], and the results
are presented in Sections 4.2 and 4.3, respectively.

4.3
NeRF-synthetic dataset
Eight scenes in the NeRF-synthetic dataset are used (Chair, Drums,
Ficus, Hotdog, Lego, Materials, Mic and Ship). Each scene is associated with two frame sequences, each with 200 frames. We denote
them as 𝑆 1 and 𝑆 2 . In 𝑆 1 , a frame consists of an RGB-D image and
the camera pose that captures the image. In 𝑆 2 , a frame consists of
an RGB image and its camera pose.
4.3.1 Input data. As discussed in Section 4.2, the baseline methods
as well as our method perform scene optimization with 𝑆 1 , but the
input data and their sources are different. Table 4 presents the
statistics of the input data. Whereas Compact Gaussian takes as
input 24,347.5 points generated by COLMAP, Light Gaussian takes
264,747 Gaussians pre-optimized by 3DGS.
For NeRF-synthetic dataset, our keyframe selection criterion is
relaxed, i.e., the position difference is changed from 0.5 meters to
2.0 meters whereas the orientation disparity remains the same, 0.4.
Then, our method selects approximately 30 keyframes out of 200
frames in 𝑆 1 . Finally, we exclude the pixels with the inverse depth
zero, which correspond to the background, and run the superpixelguided sampling to obtain 16,271.63 points on average.
4.3.2 Evaluation. The optimized scenes are rendered with the camera poses in 𝑆 1 . The rendered images are provided in a supplementary material, and Table 5 shows the evaluation results. The PSNR
score of our method (32.47) is comparable to that of Compact Gaussian (32.72) whereas our method and Compact Gaussian have the
same scores for both SSIM and LPIPS. With respect to the number
of optimized Gaussians and the optimization time, our method outperforms the baselines, but Compact Gaussian consumes the least
memory. It is worth noting that our variant denoted as “Ours w/o
dens” excels Light Gaussian across all metrics.
In general, the images in NeRF-synthetic dataset are more textured than those in Replica dataset, posing a challenge to our
method. Therefore, our PSNR scores are the highest for Chair, Ficus and Hotdog, all of which have simple textures. On the other
hand, NeRF-synthetic dataset is associated with simpler lighting
environments than Replica dataset, which allows the baselines to
compress the scenes effectively. This explains why our method has
bigger data size in spite of the smaller number of Gaussians.
Novel views are synthesized with the camera poses in 𝑆 2 . The
rendered images are provided in a supplementary material, and
the evaluation results are enumerated in Table 6, which can be
discussed in the same manner as Table 5.
