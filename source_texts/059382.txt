Abstract
Background
Physics-based differentiable rendering (PBDR) aims to propagate gradients from scene parameters to image pixels or vice versa. The physically correct gradients obtained can be used in various applications, including inverse rendering and machine learning. Currently, two categories of methods are prevalent in the PBDR community: reparameterization and boundary sampling methods. The state-of-the-art boundary sampling methods rely on a guiding structure to calculate the gradients efficiently. They utilize the rays generated in traditional path-tracing methods and project them onto the object silhouette boundary to initialize the guiding structure.
Methods
In this study, we propose an augmentation of previous projective-sampling-based boundary-sampling methods in a bidirectional manner. Specifically, we utilize the rays spawned from the sensors and also employ the rays emitted by the emitters to initialize the guiding structure.
Results
To demonstrate the benefits of our technique, we perform a comparative analysis of differentiable rendering and inverse rendering performance. We utilize a range of synthetic scene examples and evaluate our method against state-of-the-art projective-sampling-based differentiable rendering methods.
Conclusions
The experiments show that our method achieves lower variance gradients in the forward differentiable rendering process and better geometry reconstruction quality in the inverse-rendering results.

1. Introduction
Differentiable rendering has become a focal point of research and exploration in contemporary virtual and augmented reality, computer vision, and computer graphics. The role of rendering algorithms is to map a variety of scene parameters onto the colors of the image pixels. On the other hand, differentiable rendering algorithms are designed to propagate the gradients of image pixels back to their corresponding scene parameters, which enables a broad array of applications, such as 3D reconstruction, inverse rendering, and novel view synthesis.
Various differentiable rendering algorithms have been proposed to learn the underlying scene representations. These include NeRF-like methods [1], methods based on 3D Gaussian Splatting [2], techniques that leverage traditional rasterization [3], and a vast array of studies based on neural networks [4]. In this study, we focus on physics-based differentiable rendering (PBDR) algorithms [5,6]. PBDR aims to propagate the gradients of image pixels to scene parameters such as intensity of emitters, albedos of materials, and positions of vertices in the triangle meshes, in a physically correct way.
Compared to other differentiable rendering algorithms, the advantage of PBDR is that its corresponding primary rendering process (global illumination algorithms) strictly obeys the rules of geometric optics in the real world [7]. Thus, PBDR can provide high-quality gradients of scene parameters, enabling the optimization used in inverse rendering applications to converge to a valid result [8].
The main challenge in PBDR involves handling high-order discontinuities encapsulated within the render integral. These discontinuities result in an additional boundary integral during the gradient calculation [9]. Consequently, if we directly apply the automatic differentiation technique, biased gradients will be obtained. Current PBDR algorithms can be divided into two categories based on how the additional boundary integral is handled: reparameterization and boundary sampling methods.
Reparameterization methods [6,10, 11, 12] use a warp field estimated using Monte Carlo estimators to reparameterize the boundary integral. This converts the boundary integral into an interior integral, thereby avoiding explicit sampling of discontinuities. However, the additional Monte Carlo step used to estimate the warp field introduces additional variance into the final gradient estimator, which is the main drawback of reparameterization methods.
Boundary sampling methods [5,9,13,14] calculate an additional boundary integral. Analogous to traditional global illumination algorithms, the Monte Carlo estimator and importance sampling scheme are indispensable components for efficiently estimating the boundary integral. Specifically, light paths in which one of the segments is tangential to the geometric boundary must be sampled. However, it is difficult to sample boundary paths according to their contributions in unidirectional algorithms because this process must be capable of sampling silhouette edges as viewed from any arbitrary shading point in the scene.
State-of-the-art boundary sampling methods [5] construct boundary paths from the middle and expand them outward in a bidirectional manner. Consequently, every path generated in this manner is a valid boundary path. Thus, the corresponding importance sampling scheme can be divided into the following two steps. First, the middle segment is sampled. In the second step, a complete path can be constructed in the traditional manner.
A guiding data structure is required in the first step to perform importance sampling of middle segments. The current state-of-the-art method accomplishes this by using the rays generated in traditional path-tracing algorithms and projecting them onto the geometric boundary to initialize the guiding structure. This projection operation typically performs well without requiring changes to many scene configurations. However, this can be inefficient in scenes with small-area emitters, such as mobile phone flashlights, which resemble point lights. This inefficiency stems from the projection operation not explicitly accounting for the emitter positions, which reduces the likelihood of projected rays intersecting these small-area emitters.
In this study, we not only use rays spawned from sensors, as in previous methods, but also employ rays from emitters to initialize the guiding structure. We generate the seed rays by first randomly sampling points on the emitters, and then connecting them to randomly sampled points on the differentiating geometry. We then replace some of the seed rays, which were originally generated from the sensors, with rays generated from the above process. The adjusted seed rays are then projected onto a geometric boundary to initialize the guiding structure. The superiority of our method is demonstrated through a series of forward differential rendering and inverse rendering experiments.
2. Related work
Recently, significant advancements have been made in the field of physics-based differentiable rendering [5,6]. The main challenge in PBDR is the high-order discontinuities in the render integral that are used to calculate the colors of the image pixels. The discontinuity mainly originates from the discontinuous visibility function, and the discontinuity in the visibility function arises from the geometric parameters. Depending on whether the discontinuity is handled implicitly or explicitly, current PBDR techniques can be divided into two categories: reparameterization and boundary sampling methods.
2.1. Reparameterization methods
Reparameterization methods attempt to avoid sampling the discontinuities contained in the render integral explicitly by reparameterizing the integral domain, which converts the additional boundary integral into an interior integral by applying Stokes’s theorem such that we can use the sampling techniques developed for primary rendering algorithms. After reparameterization, the location of the geometric discontinuity remains static, irrespective of changes in the scene parameters. Thus, the automatic differentiation technique can be used directly to obtain the correct gradients.
Loubet et al. [10] conducted the first study in the reparameterization category of physics-based differentiable rendering. They discovered that the shifts in the geometric discontinuities within a small spherical integral domain could be approximated using a first-order spherical rotation transformation. For scene functions with wide support, the scene function is processed with a convolution operation using the von Mises-Fisher distribution, such that inside the kernel function's narrow support, a spherical rotation is still capable of offering a good approximation of the movement in geometric discontinuities. Given the small domain for reparametrizing the convolved scene function, auxiliary rays must be emitted to determine the reparameterization scheme. The principal disadvantage of this reparameterization technique is that the ultimate Monte Carlo estimator carries a bias owing to the approximation employed.
Bangaru et al. [11] proposed the first unbiased reparameterization method for PBDR. Rather than directly determining the appropriate changes in variables, they estimated a warp field using a Monte Carlo estimator, which could then be used to accomplish the reparameterization task. To ensure the validity of the reparameterization, the value of the warp field at the geometric boundary must exactly match the movement of the geometric discontinuities. The warp field, 𝑉(direct), obtained by applying automatic differentiation techniques directly to the ray tracing process, satisfies the above requirement only on the occluder side. To satisfy the above requirement on the occluder side, they convolved the warp field 𝑉(direct) with a weight function, which was estimated by tracing additional rays, to obtain the final warp field. Their work was extended by Xu et al. [6] using more advanced primary Monte Carlo rendering techniques such as bidirectional path tracing. The main drawback of reparameterization methods is the additional variance introduced during the reparameterization step. This problem can be solved if an analytical reparameterization scheme becomes available.
2.2. Boundary sampling methods
Li et al. [9] pioneered the field of PBDR using a physics-based differentiable-rendering technique for scenes comprising only triangle meshes by introducing an edge-sampling method. This technique is remarkable because it also represents the first use of a boundary sampling method in the PBDR.
They discovered that the discontinuity of the integrand prevents the automatic differentiation technique from being directly applied to compute the gradient of the pixel intensity. They employed the Heaviside step function to break the discontinuous integrand into smaller parts, where the relevant function remains continuous. To obtain the differential of the rendering integral, they used the property of the Heaviside step function, whose derivative is the Dirac delta function. This allows them to convert the 2D integral into a 1D integral (the additional boundary integral), where the integral domains are over the triangle edges corresponding to the discontinuity locations of the scene function. With this physically correct formula, an efficient importance sampling scheme is still needed to put this theory into practice. They used pre-computation to perform importance sampling of the boundary integral corresponding to the primary visibility, and a 6D Hough tree for higher-order visibility cases.
However, this pioneering importance sampling scheme fails to handle scenes of high complexity efficiently and struggles to accurately reflect the shape of the contribution function. Consequently, this leads to a Monte Carlo estimator characterized by a relatively high degree of variance
Zhang et al. [20] extended the edge-sampling method to scenes that include participating media. However, their work remained limited to the framework of sampling edges from given shading points, which is a strategy deemed inefficient from a modern viewpoint.
By engaging a transport relation borrowed from the realm of fluid mechanics, Zhang et al. [13] built a mathematical foundation central to modern boundary sampling methods. Additionally, they proposed a multi-dimensional variant of the boundary integral, which introduces the novel concept of generating silhouette paths ‘from the middle’. This substantially accelerates the boundary integral computation.
Zhang et al. [15] extended this method to scenes containing participating media. Yan et al. [14] employed adaptive data structures to guide the sampling process within a boundary sample space. In addition, they proposed an edge-sorting algorithm to reduce the high frequencies of the corresponding contribution functions.
Zhang et al. [5] included a re-derivation of the local formulation for the perimeters of shapes with discontinuous normal and a novel proposal for the local formulation of the interior for shapes with smooth normal. Collectively, these derivations formed the theoretical backbone of their study. Unlike previous methods, the method for initializing the guiding structure is determined by the primary rendering process. Specifically, they first gather the rays created by path-tracing algorithms, which intersect with the differentiated geometries. They projected these rays onto the corresponding geometric boundaries. The contributions of the projected rays were cumulatively added to the guiding structure. The main challenge in boundary sampling methods is efficient sampling of the boundary sample space. The currently used data structures exhibit sparse and high-frequency features, even though the boundary sample space is intrinsically smooth.
3. Method
3.1. Preliminaries of boundary sampling methods
Before discussing the specifics of our method, it is necessary to provide a brief summary of the foundational theory of the boundary sampling methods proposed by Zhang et al. [13].
The solution to the well-known rendering equation [16](1)𝐿𝑜⁡(𝑝,𝜔𝑜)=𝐿𝑒⁡(𝑝,𝜔𝑜)+∫𝑆2𝑓⁡(𝑝,𝜔𝑜,𝜔)⁢𝐿𝑖⁡(𝑝,𝜔)⁢|cos⁡𝜃|d𝜔is usually expressed in Neumann series: 𝐿𝑖=∑∞𝑁=1𝑃𝑁, where 𝑃𝑁 denotes the radiance contribution from paths of length 𝑁.(2)𝑃𝑁⁡(𝑝,𝜔0)=∫𝑆2×⋯×𝑆2𝐹⁡(𝑝,𝜔0,…,𝜔𝑁−1)⁢d⁢𝜔𝑁−1⋯d⁢𝜔1.
The resulting radiance function 𝐿𝑖 is then convolved with the pixel reconstruction filter function to obtain the final intensity of pixel 𝑗.(3)𝐼𝑗=∬ℎ𝑗⁡(𝑥,𝑦)⁢𝐿𝑖⁡(𝑥,𝑦)d𝑥⁢d⁢𝑦.
The rendering equation can also be expressed in the surface form [7] as follows:(4)𝐿⁡(𝑝,𝑝′)=𝐿𝑒⁡(𝑝,𝑝′)+∫𝐴𝑓⁡(𝑝,𝑝′,𝑝″)⁢𝐿⁡(𝑝′,𝑝″)⁢𝐺⁡(𝑝′,𝑝″)⁢d⁢𝐴⁡(𝑝″),where function 𝐺 is a geometric term. By expanding the formula, we obtain the widely used path integral.(5)𝑃𝑁⁡(𝑝,𝑝0)=∫𝐴×⋯×𝐴𝐹⁡(𝑝,𝑝0,…,𝑝𝑁−1)⁢d⁢𝐴𝑝𝑁−1⋯d⁢𝐴𝑝1.
After having made these preparations, we start to calculate the derivative of the integral. Typically, a scene may contain many parameters {𝜋0,…,𝜋𝑛} needed to be differentiated. Without loss of generality, we consider only one parameter 𝜋 to be differentiated at a time.
By applying Cartan’s magic formula, Stokes’s theorem, and suitable parameterizations, the derivative of the rendering integral can be written as [13](6)∂𝐼∂𝜋=∫̂Ω∂∂𝜋⁢̂𝑓⁡(̅̅̅̅̅𝒑)⁢d⁢𝜇⁡(̅̅̅̅̅𝒑)+∫∂̂ΩΔ⁢̂𝑓𝐾⁡(̅̅̅̅̅𝒑)⁢𝑉Δ⁢ℬ𝐾⁡(𝒑𝐾)⁢d⁢𝜇′⁡(̅̅̅̅̅𝒑)where the first integral is obtained over an ordinary path space and can be calculated using automatic differentiation techniques. The second integral (boundary integral) is evaluated over the boundary path space. The paths required for the second integral must be tangential to one of the geometries differentiated at one of the ray segments. The integrand in the second integral consists of the radiance difference between the foreground and background at the tangent point as well as the dot product of the velocity of the tangent point and the normal vector.
To evaluate the boundary integral efficiently, the paths in the boundary path space must be constructed effectively. Instead of explicitly searching for paths to a specific shading point, contemporary methods generate paths from the middle. Specifically, the tangent segment of a path is first created and then extended bidirectionally to complete the construction. For compatibility, the boundary integral can be expressed in a multidirectional form [13] as follows:(7)∫ℬ∫Δ⁢ℬ[∫̂Ω̂𝑓𝑆⁡𝑑⁢𝜇⁡(̅̅̅̅̅𝒑𝑆)]⁢̂𝑓𝐵⁡[∫̂Ω̂𝑓𝐷⁡𝑑⁢𝜇⁡(̅̅̅̅̅𝒑𝐷)]⁢𝑑⁢𝑙⁡(𝒑𝐷0)⁢𝑑⁢𝐴⁡(𝒑𝑆0).
The bidirectional extension process for constructing such paths can be effectively implemented using traditional path tracing techniques. However, the efficient sampling of tangent segments presents a new challenge in PBDR. Zhang et al. [13] addressed this problem by leveraging the low-dimensional nature of the tangent segment space, which is three-dimensional. For a scene comprising solely triangular meshes, the first dimension encompasses all triangle edges on the geometry being differentiated. Given a point on an edge of a triangle, the remaining two dimensions are defined over the unit spherical domain. They then create a 3D grid in the boundary sample space, referred to as the guiding structure, to facilitate the important sampling of the tangent segments.
Initially, they select a set of points within this 3D grid and employ techniques such as photon mapping to evaluate the corresponding weights, thereby initializing the guiding structure. During the boundary integral estimation process, the importance sampling of tangent segments using this guiding structure is performed, and the boundary integral is calculated using eq. (7).
3.2. Projective-sampling-based boundary sampling methods
The primary distinction between projective-sampling-based boundary sampling methods [5] and other boundary sampling techniques is the initialization of the guiding structure, which is crucial for reducing the overall variance of the gradient estimator. Projective-sampling-based methods leverage the information gathered during the primary rendering process to initialize the guiding structure, thereby significantly enhancing its quality.
Specifically, rays are first emitted from the sensor within a scene, and those occluded by geometries differentiated along some of the path segments during the primary rendering process are recorded as seed rays. These seed rays are then projected onto the geometric boundaries, ensuring that they are tangential to the geometry, using the JUMP and WALK strategies for triangular meshes. During this projection process, the origins of the rays remain fixed and their directions are determined based on the results of the projection operation. Next, the locations of these projected rays within the guiding structure are computed, and their corresponding contributions are evaluated using the integrand in eq. (7) to initialize the guiding structure.
To demonstrate the inherent simplicity of the boundary integral, Zhang et al. [5] derived a local formulation of eq. (7) as follows:∂𝐼∂𝜋=∫∂𝒜∫𝑆2𝐿𝑖⁡(𝒙𝑏,𝝎)⁢𝑊𝑖⁡(𝒙𝑏,𝝎)⁢sin⁡𝜙⁢(∂𝜋𝒙𝑏·𝒏𝑏)⁢d⁢𝝎⁢d⁢𝑙⁡(𝒙𝑏)+(8)∫𝒜∫𝑆1𝐿𝑖⁡(𝒙𝑏,𝜙)⁢𝑊𝑖⁡(𝒙𝑏,𝜙)⁢𝜅⁡(𝜙)⁢(∂𝜋𝒙𝑏·𝒏𝑏)d𝜙⁢d⁢𝐴⁡(𝒙𝑏).

3.3. Bidirectional projection
Unlike previous methods that only project rays generated from sensors, we fully utilize rays spawned from both sensors and emitters, as illustrated in Figure 2.

Our first principle for the generation of seed rays is that upon projection, these rays should provide the maximum possible contribution to the additional boundary integral, as outlined in eq. (8).
When the scene contains small-area lights, particularly at a distance, the seed rays generated from the sensors struggle to contribute meaningfully to the boundary integral in eq. (8) upon projection, as illustrated in Figure 2. In this case, the use of the rays emitted by small emitters as seed rays for subsequent projection operations is more efficient.
Specifically, the algorithm used to generate the seed rays comprises four steps. First, given the total number of seed rays needed to be generated, we assign the rays to the emitters in the scene according to their intensity and distance from the shape being differentiated. In practice, the emitter that is closer to the shape and has stronger intensity is dispatched with a greater number of rays. After determining the number of rays that an emitter must generate, we first sample a triangle on the emitter according to its area. Then, we uniformly sample a point within the selected triangle to serve as the starting point for the seed rays. The third step in our algorithm involves determining the endpoint of the seed rays. This is accomplished in a manner similar to the second step: we select a triangle on the shape being differentiated based on its area and then uniformly sample a point within that triangle. The final step is to generate seed rays from the sensor side and combine the rays from two different sources. In all the experiments discussed in this paper, we use a split in which 30% of the rays are emitted by the emitters and 70% of the rays are spawned from the sensor. The subsequent projection operations and importance sampling processes are conducted in the same manner as defined by Zhang et al. [5].
4. Results
We implemented our method on top of cuda_ad_rgb backend from Mitsuba 3 [17] using the underlying framework based on Dr. Jit [18] for forward/reverse-mode AD and GPU kernel compilation. All experiments were conducted using an NVIDIA RTX 3090 graphics card and OptiX [19] to perform ray-triangle intersection queries.
We now demonstrate the practical advantages of our method over Zhang et al.’s [5] method on different data structures (3D grids and octrees) used to guide the sampling process with several differentiable and inverse-rendering examples. We conducted all the comparisons with their method using the same number of samples per pixel.
In Figure 3, we compare the derivative images computed using our method with those computed from the state-of-the-art projective-sampling-based boundary sampling methods from Zhang et al. [5]. For all the scenes, our results closely match the ground-truth derivatives generated using the finite-difference method.

The superiority of our method stems from the fact that the seed rays generated explore the boundary sample space more effectively. Consider the scene configuration shown in Figure 3. The seed rays generated by Zhang et al. ’s method contribute to the final guiding structure only if they are emitted from the sensor and directly hit the background plane without intersecting any other geometries as the first ray segment. These rays are then reflected by the plane, subsequently affecting the geometry differentiated as the second ray segment. Such seed rays are projected onto geometric boundaries with their origins kept fixed to identify possible contributions to the guiding structure. Only the projected rays that intersect the scene emitter, which is a small-area light, will finally contribute to the guiding structure.
As the size of the emitter area decreases, the likelihood of the projected rays intersecting the emitter also decreases. Within this limit, as the emitter area approaches a point light, the probability of intersection becomes zero.
However, some of the seed rays generated by our method are emitted directly from the emitter, making them unaffected by the size of the emitter area. Consequently, all the successfully projected rays contribute to the guiding structure, resulting in a better sampling pattern of the boundary sample space and improved differentiable rendering results, as shown in Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5.
5. Conclusion
The main discovery of the projective-sampling-based boundary sampling method by Zhang et al. [5] was that the estimation of the boundary integral in eq. (8) can greatly benefit from information gathered during the primary rendering process via the projection operation. This phenomenon can be understood as follows. The boundary paths that contribute the most to the integrand in the boundary integral on a global scale may contribute less on local scale, that is, for some specific camera views. Although their projection operation generally works well without requiring adjustments for many scene configurations, it struggles with scenes containing small-area lights, such as mobile phone flashlights or small bulbs that resemble point lights. To increase the likelihood of projected rays intersecting these small lights, we incorporated rays emitted from these small-area emitters as part of the seed rays during the projection operation, thereby creating a higher-quality guiding structure for the subsequent importance-sampling process.
Whether we use a projection or simple uniform sampling strategy to initialize, the guiding structure does not appear to be a bottleneck for boundary-sampling methods. The main inefficiency of the boundary sampling methods for PBDR is the sparse and high-frequency features of the underlying guiding structures built for triangle meshes, which are used to perform importance sampling of the boundary sample space. Current state-of-the-art methods parameterize the 3D boundary sample space using the first dimension to represent all the edges of the meshes, whereas the remaining two dimensions parameterize the unit directions. Imagine these meshes using single-point light; the silhouette edges contributing to the boundary integrals are continuously connected on the mesh. However, this property is lost during the aforementioned 1D parameterization. These continuously connected silhouette edges are broken into many parts in this parameterization, leading to sparse and high-frequency features in the underlying guiding structures. Nevertheless, the corresponding boundary sample space is intrinsically smooth because the silhouette edges are continuously connected. A more dedicated data structure that fully utilizes the intrinsically smooth nature of the boundary sample space using a new parameterization scheme will make a significant leap forward in the field of PBDR.
