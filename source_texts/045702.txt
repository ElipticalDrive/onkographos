Holography is capable of rendering three-dimensional scenes with full-depth control and delivering transformative experiences across numerous domains, including virtual and augmented reality, education, and communication. However, traditional holography presents 3D scenes with unnatural defocus and severe speckles due to the limited space bandwidth product of the spatial light modulator (SLM). Here, we introduce Motion Hologram, a holographic technique that accurately portrays photorealistic and speckle-free 3D scenes. This technique leverages a single hologram and a learnable motion trajectory, which are jointly optimized within a deep reinforcement learning framework. Specifically, we experimentally demonstrated that the proposed technique could achieve a 4- to 5-dB PSNR improvement of focal stacks in comparison with traditional holography and could successfully depict speckle-free, high-fidelity, and full-color 3D displays using only a commercial SLM. We believe that the proposed method promises a prospective form of holographic displays that will offer immersive viewing experiences for audiences.

INTRODUCTION
Since its first inception in 1947 by Dennis Gabor, holography (1) has been hailed as the “Holy Grail” of information display. Unlike existing three-dimensional (3D) display techniques such as stereoscopic displays (2), volumetric displays (3), and light-field displays (4), holographic displays (5–7) offer pixel-level depth control, aberration correction, and a compact form factor, which promise an immersive and photorealistic viewing experience to the audience. Computer-generated holography (CGH) (8–11) is one of the most popular and prospective techniques in holographic displays. It digitally synthesizes holograms using computational methods and dynamically reproduces the wavefronts of 3D virtual objects by coherently illuminating the spatial light modulators (SLMs).
Recently, CGH algorithms (12–18) have achieved remarkable breakthroughs with the aid of exploding artificial intelligence technology, especially for its application in augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR). Despite these advancements, current CGH still faces notable challenges that restrict its usage in practice owing to hardware limitations. First, a single 2D SLM can hardly be used to accurately render high-fidelity 3D scenes with natural focus cues in traditional holography due to limited bandwidth (15). The 3D scenes reconstructed by traditional CGH systems often exhibit unnatural artifacts and laser speckles, which seriously compromise the viewer’s experience. Second, the current optical system design lacks a unified paradigm that can effectively integrate it with hologram generation algorithms to further unlock the full potential of CGH to deliver photorealistic 3D images.
To address the first issue of limited bandwidth, several techniques (19–27) have been proposed and most of them are rooted in the concept of multiplexing. First of all, one straightforward implementation is to spatially tile multiple SLMs (19, 28, 29) to enhance the space bandwidth product, thereby achieving realistic 3D displays. Nonetheless, this approach would inevitably increase system complexity and computational overheads. Second, time-multiplexed holography (25, 26), which displays multiple frames with distinct speckle patterns in rapid succession to break the temporal coherence of light source, allows the human viewers to perceive realistic 3D displays without artifacts through subjective visual averaging. However, most commercially available SLMs have a refresh rate of merely 60 Hz, which is inadequate to meet the requirements of time-multiplexed methods when being operated at video rate. While some researchers (20, 21, 27) have proposed to use high-refresh rate devices such as ferro-electronic liquid crystal on silicon and micro-electromechanical systems, these systems are less mature in performance due to the limited pixel counts and shallow bit depths (30). In addition, Nam et al. (23) introduced a polarization-multiplexed metasurface to enhance the space bandwidth product of SLM, despite the final displayed multiplane images only exhibit marginal quality improvement.
For the second issue, most hardware systems and software algorithms are designed separately in traditional holography, or more generally in optical tasks, which fails to fully unlock the potential of the hardware (31–33). A novel and promising approach is to solve the systems and algorithms within an end-to-end differentiable framework in the computational display and imaging fields (34–38). For instance, Tseng et al. (37) devised a differentiable holographic image formation model that jointly learned a diffractive optical element (DOE) with the hologram pattern to expand the field of view by 64 times. Despite these inspiring attempts, this differentiable architecture might not be the ideal and universal solution. First, it requires the hardware configurations to be differentiable; however, not all system components can be described by differentiable mathematical models, such as scanning devices and illumination sources (39–41). Second, the current pipeline is mostly demonstrated in single hardware optimization tasks, such as the design of DOE and metasurface, despite of the fact that real-world systems are more complicated and usually involve multiple devices (42). Recently, reinforcement learning (RL) (43) has emerged as an effective way to optimize the complex and nondifferentiable scenarios (44–46). Specifically, RL is a reward-based learning method (47) that identifies the optimal strategy in the parameter space through trial and error without the need of gradient information (48), which appears as a promising candidate to codesign a complicated system.
Here, we proposed a CGH technique called Motion Hologram to disrupt the spatial coherence of light source using RL, which can thus render photorealistic and speckle-free 3D scenes by globally designing the holographic system. We reappraised the role of system motion, which was commonly believed detrimental in imaging and display, and conceptualized that the inherent speckles in holography could be neutralized and photorealistic 3D display could be achieved by spatially multiplexing the holograms via proper system motions. Specifically, a system design paradigm that leverages RL to jointly generate the phase-only hologram and system’s motion trajectory was introduced, which promises an unparalleled viewing experience. To showcase the superior performance of the proposed technique, we conducted numerical simulations and experimental reconstructions alongside traditional and time-multiplexed holography methods. We developed a benchtop prototype and demonstrated that a 4 to 5 dB improvement in the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) is achieved compared with traditional holography, which also surpasses that of state-of-the-art time-multiplexed holography. Furthermore, our method is capable of accurately reconstructing full-color and high-fidelity multiplane images with realistic defocus in visual effects. We believe that the proposed method promises a prospective form of holographic displays that reconstructs photorealistic and speckle-free 3D scenes and provides a blueprint for computational design in other research areas.
RESULTSMotion Hologram
Traditional holographic display uses a collimated laser beam to illuminate a phase-only SLM, and thus an optical field is generated in the SLM plane. This field is then modulated by the hologram and propagated over a specific distance to reconstruct desired 2D images or 3D image stacks. It is common to use the angular spectrum method (ASM) (49, 50) to calculate the propagation of complex waves from the SLM plane to the target plane, whose expression is given as follows

where urecon(x,y) is the optical field in the target plane, ϕ(x,y,λ) is the phase-only hologram loaded on the SLM. λ is the wavelength, fx and fy are spatial frequencies, Z is the propagation distance between SLM and target plane, H{·} is ASM’s transfer function, and F{·} denotes the Fourier transform. The reconstructed images ∣urecon(x,y)∣ can be obtained by taking the absolute value of urecon(x,y).
For 3D displays, ASM can also be used to solve the inverse problem of optimizing ϕ for a focal stack intensity Itargetj located at the set of distances zj, j=1,2,…,J. The objective is to solve the following optimization problem
(3)ϕ=argminϕL(∣ureconj∣,Itargetj)
where Itargetj is usually rendered based on incoherent wave propagation (20, 25). In traditional holography, a single hologram is used to directly control the 3D volume of light with natural focus cues. However, this task often requires a larger space bandwidth product than what the SLM can offer, which leads to 3D reconstructions with uncontrollable speckle noise and unnatural ringing artifacts.
To address this challenge, our strategy is to generate multiple replicas of the image with identical spatial speckle patterns, such that the speckles could be reduced when these images are spatially overlaid and averaged. Differentiating from the aforementioned space- or time-multiplexed holography, which requires multiple or high-speed SLMs to generate different patterns at the same location to smooth out the speckles, we aim to use motion to synthesize a specific form of “motion speckle:” By superimposing and averaging each replica along the motion trajectory, we could effectively eliminate spatial speckles and display high-quality 3D scenes with realistic defocus. Furthermore, unlike previous works on CGH algorithms, we develop an innovative design paradigm that leverages deep RL to jointly optimize the hologram generation and motion planning to deliver unparalleled display quality in an end-to-end manner.
The overall framework is divided into two stages: The hologram generation and motion planning are first jointly optimized using RL framework, as shown in Fig. 1A. Once the optimization of the first stage is complete, holograms for new scenes are optimized individually on the basis of the optimized trajectory, as illustrated in Fig. 1B. In the first stage, the deep agent πθ interacts with the environment, in this case, the Motion Holography, in a sequence of actions a, states s, and rewards r. Specifically, the deep agent πθ consists of two networks: an actor network and a critic network. We define the action a as the combination of a specific direction (d) and a number of pixels (p) of each movement performed by SLM and the state s represents the current position of the SLM and the number of steps (k).

In the joint optimization stage, we first initialize the state sk−1 and feed it to both the actor and critic networks. The actor network is expected to generate the next action ak for Motion Holography based on the received state sk−1. Next, the motion planning of the SLM is updated according to the newly generated action ak, and the phase-only hologram ϕmotion is reoptimized on the basis of the latest motion trajectory in Motion Holography. Once the Motion Hologram ϕmotion is optimized, the immediate reward rk and the new state sk are fed to the critic network and the actor network to collect the next round of action, reward, and state. After iteration between the deep agent πθ and Motion Holography, a complete motion trajectory is thus obtained. The motion trajectory and the corresponding cumulative reward R can then be expressed as follows
(4)Trajectory={a1,a2,a3,…,aK}(5)R=r1+γr2+γ2r3+…+γK−1rK=∑k=1Kγk−1rk
where K is the total step of motion termination and γ∈[0,1] is a discount factor, which aims to reduce the impact of future rewards. The goal of agent is to strategically choose actions to maximize the expected cumulative reward R. Therefore, we solve the following optimization problem in the proposed framework
(6)ϕmotion,Trajectory=argmaxϕmotion,Trajectory∑k=1Kγk−1rk
For Motion Holography framework, the hologram is digitally shifted to a specific position according to the motion trajectory, which emulates the physical movement of the SLM in the real world, to create the spatial replicas of the hologram. Then, each hologram is propagated in free space using ASM model or camera-in-the-loop (CITL) model (12) to reconstruct a 3D scene with the same motion speckle pattern but at slightly shifted spatial locations. Last, the reconstruction result umotionj is obtained by superimposing and averaging all replicas. We optimize the Motion Hologram using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) solver to solve the following optimization problem
(7)umotionj=1K∑kfmodel{Trajectory(ϕmotion),zj}(8)ϕmotion=argminϕL(∣umotionj∣,Itargetj)
where we use the DeepFocus (51) network to retrieve multiplane images stack Itargetj with natural focus cues from red-green-blue (RGB)–depth images. More detailed information can be found in Materials and Methods and the Supplementary Materials.
The details of deep agent πθ architectures are implemented using multilayer perceptrons (MLPs). The actor network learns a policy to make decisions, while the critic network outputs the value vk to evaluate the actions employed by the actor based on the reward. The immediate reward rk can be evaluated by
(9)rk=rkall+W×rkin−focus
where rkall represents the improvement in PSNR of the entire reconstructed 3D scene after each action is taken, rkin−focus is a metric to measure the increment in PSNR after each action only in the focal region considering that the reconstruction quality in this area remains our primary target, and W gives the weight assigned to rkin−focus.
Here, the proximal policy optimization (PPO) (52) method is used to maximize the expected reward R. Specifically, the framework is jointly optimized in an automated differentiation software (53) and then backpropagated through the neural network to update πθ. The network architecture and optimization process are detailed in Materials and Methods. In this work, while the joint optimization is finished, we also use the SGD solver to optimize Motion Holograms for other 3D scenes based on the same optimized trajectory without the need for reoptimization of the motion planning. More detailed discussions can be found in the Supplementary Materials.
Simulation results
We first compared the focal stacks obtained by traditional holography, time-multiplexed holography, and proposed holography in simulation, as shown in Fig. 2. It is observed that the 3D scenes reconstructed by traditional holography present speckles and chromatic aberrations, no matter where the camera is focused (Fig. 2, B and F). In addition, a relatively lower-resolution rendering for in-focus regions and unnatural ringing patterns in out-of-focus regions (the zoom-in images in Fig. 2, B and F) lead to an unpleasant viewing experience for the audience. In contrast, the proposed method leverages motion to synthesize unique “motion speckles” (fig. S2) to reconstruct high-quality and speckle-free 3D scenes with higher-resolution and photorealistic contents (Fig. 2, C and K) via temporal averaging. It is worth noting that the proposed holography can deliver visual effects comparable to those of the current state-of-the-art time-multiplexed holography (Fig. 2, D and L).

The quantitative results are provided in Fig. 3A for comparison purpose. The PSNR of images reconstructed using traditional holography is 23.22 dB, while the proposed holography achieves PSNR of 33.22 dB, which represents a meaningful improvement of 10 dB. Similarly, the structural similarity index measure (SSIM) of focal stacks using the proposed holography is 0.92, which outperforms the traditional holography by a large margin of 0.47 SSIM. We further conducted and compared two-frame and four-frame time-multiplexed holography to verify the superiority of the proposed method. The results reveal that our method outperforms the two-frame time-multiplexed holography in PSNR and SSIM by 5.36 dB and 0.17, respectively. In addition, the proposed holography also demonstrates display quality that is equivalent to that of the four-frame time-multiplexed holography in the quantitative metrics.

Model analysis
In this section, we conducted a detailed model analysis to examine the role of each action in the optimized trajectory and demonstrate the effectiveness of the reward function. First, the cumulative rewards obtained at each step along the optimal trajectory are shown in Fig. 3B. It is clear that the cumulative rewards generally increase with the number of steps; however, the correlation is not perfect and the benefits tend to be marginal under certain circumstances. For example, after taking the third and seventh steps, the total reward is calculated to be 10.77 and 17.09 dB, which equates to a reduction of 0.38 and 0.07 dB from the cumulative rewards of the former steps, respectively. This interesting phenomenon occurs because the optimization goal of the proposed joint framework is to maximize the cumulative reward after taking the terminal step rather than the incremental gain at each individual step, as illustrated in Eq. 6.
Second, we examined the impact of rkin−focus on the final trajectory. Figure 3C illustrates the trajectories obtained with and without rkin−focus, which are labeled as optimized trajectory and nonoptimized trajectory, respectively. It is worth noting that the nonoptimized trajectory for SLM is only extended in one direction, which might cause motion blur and lower sharpness in the reconstructed 3D scene. To further analyze this result, we conducted quantitative and qualitative analyses, which are presented in Fig. 3 (D and E). When rnin−focus is not enforced as an additional reward function, the total reward obtained by using nonoptimized trajectory is 10.76 dB, which is even 0.84 dB higher. However, a simple visual inspection would tell a difference story: Despite of its relatively higher quantitative metrics, the 3D reconstructed scene based on nonoptimized trajectory not only exhibits undesirable motion blur in the in-focus regions (as indicated by blue arrows) but also introduces ringing artifacts at the boundaries between the in-focus and out-of-focus regions (as indicated by red arrows). In Fig. 3D, when rkin−focus is incorporated, the proposed method achieves a higher total reward with optimized trajectory than with nonoptimized trajectory. Furthermore, the proposed method successfully renders high-fidelity and photorealistic 3D scenes without motion blur and ringing artifacts using optimized trajectory in Fig. 3E.
Last, we compared the reconstructed scenes using traditional holography, traditional holography with optimized trajectory, optimized holography with random trajectory, and proposed holography. Random trajectory also consists of eight displacement actions randomly selected from the action space. Please note that traditional holography with optimized trajectory simply overlays and averages reconstructed images from preoptimized holograms using traditional holography along the motion trajectory. The qualitative and quantitative results in simulations are presented in Fig. 3F. While the traditional holography using optimized trajectory partially suppresses speckles, the reconstructed images suffer from lower contrast and resolution due to the nonoptimized speckle patterns (Fig. 3F, column 2). Although the optimized holography using random trajectory could enhance the contrast and resolution of the reconstructed scene to some extent, it is evident that the reconstructed scene still cannot perfectly render high-fidelity contents (red arrows) due to the lack of optimized trajectory, such as the bunny’s eyes and grass. In comparison, the proposed holography effectively depicts high-fidelity and photorealistic 3D scenes through the joint optimization of hologram generation and motion planning in the RL framework. Furthermore, the quantitative analysis reveals that the proposed method achieves a cumulative reward of 22.3 dB in the bunny scene, which surpasses the traditional method using an optimized trajectory and the optimized holography using a random trajectory by 13.8 and 5.3 dB, respectively. More reconstruction results using the optimized holography with random trajectory can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

We qualitatively and quantitatively assessed and compared the experimental results with those of traditional holography, time-multiplexed holography, and proposed holography. In line with the simulation results, the proposed holography not only perfectly generates speckle-free and higher-contrast multiplane image stacks (Fig. 4, C and G) but also naturally portrays 3D defocusing effects over the entire volume (Supplementary Movies), which ensures a more realistic viewing experience. In comparison, traditional holography suffers from speckle noise and low contrast in the reconstructed 3D scenes (Fig. 4, B and F). Moreover, in certain scenes, such as the cave (Fig. 4, J and N) and the flowers (fig. S15), traditional holography fails to accurately depict 3D defocus behavior, which causes vergence-accommodation conflict for audiences. Quantitatively, the proposed holography achieves PSNR of 25.62 and 24.17 dB in the reconstructed bamboo and bunny scenes, which showcase robust and notable improvements of 3.92 and 4.8 dB over traditional holography, respectively. To our best knowledge, the proposed holography represents the highest performance reported thus far in 3D holography using a single SLM and a single laser source. Furthermore, the proposed method also meaningfully matches the performance of time-multiplexed holography in both visual quality and evaluation metrics.
DISCUSSION
In summary, we introduced a CGH technique, Motion Hologram, to achieve photorealistic and speckle-free 3D displays. This innovative form of holography leverages motion to disrupt the spatial coherence of the light source and synthesize unique speckle patterns, which will then be averaged over all reconstructed images along the motion trajectory to attain this purpose. An RL-based framework was proposed to jointly optimize the hologram generation and motion planning for the first time in an end-to-end manner to ensure the excellent display quality of the contents. Extensive simulation and experimental results in addition to a thorough analysis are provided to verify the effectiveness of the proposed technique. To date, no CGH algorithm is available to achieve experimental results comparable to those presented in this work using merely a single pair of commercial SLM and light source. We believe that our approach offers a feasible solution to address the long-standing issues in the holography community and potentially makes holographic displays a viable technology, in particular for emerging VR and AR applications.
The proposed approach generates holograms using the optimization-based method to ensure superior display results. However, this technique cannot achieve real-time hologram generation. To address this issue, we have implemented the neural network-based Motion Holography (fig. S7A) and achieved hologram generation at a frame rate of 71 FPS. However, compared with images reconstructed using the optimization-based method, those from the network-based framework exhibit relatively low contrast and resolution (fig. S7B). We suggest that this is because learning-based CGH methods lack the ability to model the relationship between diffusive 3D wavefronts with random phases and high-frequency hologram patterns (54, 55). We believe that if this challenge is properly addressed, our framework can be fully implemented using neural networks to render speckle-free and photorealistic 3D scenes.
Furthermore, the current prototype still uses digital ways to shift the hologram according to the learned motion trajectory, and the reconstructions are obtained offline. However, it is not nontrivial to extend the approach to physical domain: commercial piezoelectric actuators (PK3HUC2, Thorlabs, US) can achieve a resonant frequency and a maximal displacement of 41 kHz and 30 μm, respectively. Therefore, it is feasible to use piezoelectric actuators to achieve the movement of the image during displacement. However, it should be noted that an entire electromechanical system needs to be developed to serve this purpose and there are complicated engineering challenges that require a substantial amount of time to be effectively addressed. Here, we have conceptually validated the effectiveness of our method through extensive simulations and experiments. We plan to address these engineering issues in the future. More detailed discussion can be found in the Supplementary Materials.
MATERIALS AND METHODSSimulation configurations
We use DeepFocus (51) to synthesize target focal stacks with natural focus cues. In simulation, seven target images are rendered as a volume, and their distances are measured to be −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, and 3 mm away from the conjugate plane of the SLM. The wavelengths of light sources are 680, 520, and 450 nm, which correspond to red, green, and blue laser diodes, respectively. The resolution of the hologram is 2048 × 1152 with a 3.74 μm pixel pitch, while the resolution of the target image of interest is 1920 × 1080. All simulations are optimized and tested in PyTorch using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU Card.
We randomly select 50 test images from the DIV2K validation set (38), the Big Buck Bunny video, which comes from www.bigbuckbunny.org (2008, Blender Foundation) under the Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 license (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/), and the MPI Sintel dataset (39) to evaluate the generalization capability of the proposed method. To assess the quality of the reconstructed images, we use widely used evaluation metrics such as PSNR and SSIM (40), which provide quantitative measures to evaluate the fidelity and similarity of the reconstructed images compared with the ground truth.
PPO model architecture and training configurations
Within the RL framework, we restrict the movement directions d∈{up,down,right,left} and the pixel value per move p∈{1,2,3}, which means that the number of valid actions is 12 in this case. Both the actor and critic networks are three fully connected layers. Specifically, for the actor network, the first layer consists of 3 input nodes and 64 output nodes, and the second layer maintains 64 nodes for both input and output. The last layer is a fully connected layer with 64 input nodes and provides a single output for each valid action. Both first and second layers use the ReLU activation function, while the last layer uses the Softmax activation function.
For the critic MLP, the first layer and the second layer are identical to the first and second layers of the action network. The last layer has 64 input nodes and 1 output node. To train the deep agent, the learning rate is set to be 1 × 10−4 for actor MLP and 3 × 10−4 for critic MLP, respectively, both of which are optimized by using the Adam optimizer with β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999 to update network parameters. The total reward is calculated after taking eight steps, and the environment is then reset for the next iteration. We collect 160 pairs of data at a time in the buffer to train the actor and critic networks with 200 epochs. The clip parameter is 0.2 and the discount factor is 0.99. The complete set of hyperparameters used for training deep agent is listed in the Supplementary Materials. The RL framework is trained on the NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU.
Experimental setup
The schematic of optical setup is shown in Fig. 5 and the real-world setup can be found in fig. S11, which includes a HOLOEYE GAEA-2 phase-only liquid crystal on silicon with a resolution of 3840 × 2160 pixels and a pixel pitch of 3.74 μm. This SLM has a refresh rate of 60 Hz (monochrome) and 8-bit depth. The laser used is a single-mode fiber-coupled module, which consists of three laser diodes optically aligned at wavelengths of 680, 520, and 450 nm. Specifically, to avoid chromatic artifacts, the emission intensities of the red, green, and blue channels of the multiwavelength laser diodes are set to 60, 20, and 10% of their respective maximum values of 40 mW in our optical setup. It should be noted that in the implementation, the color images are captured by individually exposing each wavelength and then merged in postprocessing. The collimating lens is an achromatic doublet with a focal length of 100 mm and we use the beam expander to ensure that the laser incident on the SLM is as uniform as possible. The holographic setup includes two polarizers, where the first polarization filter is used to control the intensity of the laser and the second polarization filter is used to match the polarization direction of the SLM. All images are captured by a charge-coupled device sensor (BFS-U3-31S4M-C, FLIR, US) with a resolution of 2048 × 1536 pixels and a pixel pitch of 3.45 μm. We use nearest-neighbor interpolation to double the size of the hologram, effectively alleviating the cross-talk that occurs between neighboring pixels on the SLM. Therefore, to ensure that the camera captures the complete reconstructed plane, we set the resolution of the hologram to 1024 × 576 and the resolution of the reconstructed image to 960 × 540 in the experiment. We provide a 4F system, where the first achromatic lens has a focal length of 60 mm, and the second achromatic lens has a focal length of 50 mm. An iris is positioned at the Fourier plane to block excessive light diffracted by the grating structure and higher-order diffractions.

An RGB laser module is coupled to a single-mode fiber to illuminate our phase-only SLM through a collimating lens, and two polarizers. We provide a 4F system, where a pinhole is positioned at the Fourier plane to block excessive light diffracted by the grating structure and higher-order diffractions. The sensor is used to capture multiplane images by moving the three-axis stage.
System calibration
We use the CITL optimization (12, 29), which is used to correct the mismatch between the optical forward propagation in the real world and ideal wave propagation in the simulation, to further enhance the experimental results, as shown in Fig. 6. A detailed explanation of the calibration procedure is given in the following paragraphs.
Fig. 6.Illustration of the CITL model.
The phase pattern displayed by the SLM is adjusted by learnable amplitude and phase and then is processed by a CNN. The resulting complex-valued wave field is propagated to all target planes using a conventional ASM wave propagation operator. The wave fields are processed again by a shared CNN at each target plane. The loss function is calculated as the mean squared error between the adjusted target amplitude and the amplitudes captured at different planes using the optical system. Image credits: A. Treviño.
We adopt the approach (15) to design a real-world forward model where unknown parameters are learned from a dataset of experimentally captured SLM-image pairs. Specifically, we use CNNSLM in the SLM plane to assist the ASM model to address the nonuniform intensity distribution of the laser source and nonlinear mapping from voltage to phase delay of the SLM. In addition, a CNNTarget in the target plane is used to handle optical aberrations and camera sampling issues in the holographic system. We further introduce asrc and ϕsrc to learn the content-independent spatial variations in amplitude and phase of the incident source field at the SLM plane. In conclusion, our learnable propagation model can be expressed as
(11)fmodel(ϕ,z{j})=CNNTargetfASMCNNSLM(asrceiϕsrceiϕ),z{j}
To train fmodel{·}, we randomly generate 2400 holograms using traditional and proposed methods, and then capture the intensity of holograms in seven target depth planes for each color channel. During the hologram generation process, we randomize the learning rate, the optimization iteration, and the initial random phase distribution to ensure the generalization of the learned propagation model (15). Both CNNSLM and CNNTarget are implemented using U-Net (56) with two input and output channels for the real and imaginary components of the fields. For training hyperparameters, the batch size is 1 and the initialization learning rate is 3 × 10−4. We train the network for 100 epochs, using the AdamW (57) optimizer with momentum of (0.9, 0.999), and then use the cosine decay strategy to decrease the learning rate. All experiments are trained and tested using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU Card.
