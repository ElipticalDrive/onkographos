Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) are revolutionizing diverse fields such as autonomous driving, education, and virtual reality (VR). As their applications expand, the ability to accurately evaluate the quality of NeRF-generated content becomes essential. Currently, there are only a few datasets for NeRF quality evaluation. Also, while existing quality datasets primarily utilize processed video sequences (PVS) as stimuli, real-world scenarios often involve uneven camera trajectories, underscoring the need for alternative approaches to subjective quality assessment. This study proposes a quality dataset for assessing the quality of NeRF. The dataset was generated by varying quality parameters in SOTA NeRF models to create different quality levels. A subjective experiment was conducted to obtain human opinion scores for the distorted NeRF. The subjective data were processed in accordance with International Telecommunication Union (ITU) guidelines to derive mean opinion scores (MOS. The datasets and findings not only offer insights into the performance of NeRF models but also serve as valuable resources for developing quality assessment models.
Keywords: Subjective study, NeRF, QoE, Image sequences, Quality models

Type of dataThe dataset comprises synthesized images in .PNG format and, .CSV file of scores for each stimuli. A reference folder is provided for all reference images employed to create the dataset.
Data collectionA remote subjective experiment is conducted involving 86 participants. Human opinion scores were gathered using a customized web-based form and processed in accordance with the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) standards. Specifically, the ITU-R BT.500-15 standard was employed for the experiment to post-process the human opinion scores and the reliability of the subjective data was also estimated by means of confidence intervals. The stimuli were designed as image sequences using four widely state-of-the-art (SOTA) NeRF models: DVGO, InstantNGP, TensoRF, and Plenoxels. The stimuli in the dataset included sixteen scenes: all eight synthetic scenes (Chair, Mic, Hotdog, Materials, Drums, Ficus, Lego, and Ship) from the NeRF Synthetic 360° dataset, and eight realistic scenes with four scenes (Fortress, Trex, Horns, and Room) from the LLFF dataset and four scenes (Truck, M60, Train, and Playground) from the Tanks & Temples dataset. Key parameters of the selected NeRF models were systematically adjusted to generate the stimuli, creating a range of quality levels for comprehensive subjective evaluation. These combinations resulted in a total of 102 stimuli.
Data source locationAnnotators who performed manual NeRF stimuli annotations online were based in Vietnam. All other data were generated from computer processing.

1.Value of the Data
•
This is the first NeRF quality dataset with image sequences as stimuli to guide researchers working in development of NeRF models. The MOS scores can also be employed by developers and researchers to evaluate the performance of new full reference quality model.
•
The stimuli in the dataset may be employed to train deep-learning quality models for quality assessment of NeRF.
•
The dataset gives insights into the performances of the 4 SOTA NeRF models, DVGO, InstantNGP, Plenoxels, and TensoRF, as well as the performances of different NeRF scenes (synthetic and realistic).
•
The dataset, along with the corresponding subjective MOS scores, has been made publicly available for download to support further research and development in this area.
2.Background
Understanding user perspectives is essential for evaluating the effectiveness and applicability of NeRF-generated contents. Currently, just a few subjective quality assessments [[1], [2], [3], [4],6] have been conducted (refer to Table 1) to aid the quality evaluation of rendered views from NeRF models. For instance, in [1], an online subjective experiment was carried out to assess the quality of NeRF viewpoint videos. In [[2], [3], [4],6], the authors relied on controlled laboratory environments for the subjective quality assessments. In [5], the focus was to develop a no-reference (NR) quality metrics for NeRF.

Existing objective quality evaluations in NeRF are quite mixed. For example, the results of full reference (FR) objective evaluation in [3] show that PSNR has the best performance on fieldwork stimuli. Meanwhile, the authors in show that DISTS and PieAPP metrics are better than PSNR using the same subjective data from [5]. Beyond quality evaluation, several datasets [[7], [8], [9], [10]] have also been proposed to evaluate the performance of existing methods based on NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for autonomous driving systems. Similar investigations have been explored using images and videos generated from text prompts, to identify which generative models perform best in terms of visual appeal [11], and perceived quality [12]. In addition, recent study also identified the quality aspects that play a role in influencing user acceptance of generated videos [13].
3.Data Description
The directory contains three zip files and a CSV file. The zip files include the distorted dataset, the reference folders, and an additional zip file named subjective_stimuli. The latter file contains the image sequences from the distorted folder organized alongside their corresponding reference images from the reference folders. The synthesized NeRF stimuli are organized into subfolders, where folder names encapsulate detailed information, including the NeRF model, the scene type, and specific experimental settings (e.g., the selected option and the part of the altered model). The settings for each NeRF model are presented in later section of this paper. The subfolders contain the image sequences in .PNG extensions, and 102 subfolders are within the dataset directory. The reference directory comprises 16 subfolders corresponding to the number of scenes employed to create the dataset. Each folder holds the reference images. The number of images in each reference subfolder matches the corresponding images in the distorted subfolders, ensuring consistent objective quality evaluations. The CSV file contains metadata, with columns listing the folder names of the stimuli, corresponding folder names of references, and Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) ranging from 0 to 10, formatted to four decimal places for precision. In addition, the CSV file contains prediction scores from off-the-shelf full-reference objective quality models in columns 4 through 19, enabling comparisons between subjective and objective quality assessments.
4.Experimental Design, Materials and Methods4.1.Subjective test4.1.1.Test stimuli
Given that video outputs from NeRF often face challenges, such as uneven camera paths, especially in realistic scenes, we created a dataset using image sequences as stimuli instead of videos to mitigate this issue. We employed 8 synthetic scenes from the NeRF synthetic dataset [14]. The rest 8 scenes are the realistic scenes taken from LLFF [15] and Tanks & Temples (T&T) [16] datasets. Each scene from the NeRF synthetic dataset has 200 training images and 100 testing images. Each image has a spatial resolution of 800×800 pixels. The number of images for Horns, Fortress, Trex, and the Room scenes is 62, 42, 55, and 41, respectively. About 80% of the images are used for training, while 20% is set aside for testing. Images of LLFF scenes have the same spatial resolution of 1008×756 pixels. Scenes such as playground, train, truck and M60 were collected from T&T dataset and their spatial resolutions are of 1008×548, 982×546, 980×546 and 1077×546 pixels respectively. The data format of T&T employed for the development of this dataset is similar to those employed in previous study [2].
To generate stimuli, we selected 4 NeRF models namely DVGO [17], InstantNGP [18], Plenoxels [19] and TensoRF [20]. The outputs are made up of image sequences. Each image sequence from synthetic scenes follow a 360° camera trajectory around the scene's object, whereas each image sequence of an LLFF dataset follows a forward facing trajectory and those of T&T follow an outside-looking-in camera trajectory.
To produce sequences with different degrees of quality, key parameters of the NeRF models were altered. All parameter settings for each NeRF model can be found in Table 2. Note that InstantNGP is not used with LLFF dataset due to format related issue. In Table 2, using instantNGP on synthetic scenes, the default value of hashmap size of the hash encoding and the grid resolution are altered. The default hashmap size and dense grid resolutions are 19 and 16, respectively. Other selected NeRF models have their different configurations. The grid resolutions of the coarse and fine scene representation in DVGO are altered, producing varying degrees of quality. For instance, to produce distorted stimuli with synthetic scenes, both fine and coarse model representations are altered in DVGO. However for realistic scenes (T&T and LLFF datasets), only the parameters of the fine scene representation is altered. In the TensoRF model, both CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) and Vector matrix (VM) decomposition are toggled with Spherical harmonics (SH) and MLP employed as different choice to run the model. In TensoRF model, scenes rendered by CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) occupy fewer memory space but have reduced quality compared with Vector matrix (VM) decomposition. The plenoxels model is designed to represent the radiance function using a set of SH basis functions. The default value of the SH parameter is altered from 9 to 4, 1 respectively. Two arbitrary options with varying quality levels are selected to design the dataset. The training of each NeRF model is carried out on a computer equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.

4.1.2.Test procedures
As mentioned, the dataset contains 102 NeRF stimuli, each stimulus is an image sequence that containing synthesized images. For the subjective experiments, both the reference and synthesized images were placed side-by-side (refer to Fig. 1). There were instructions on how to download the dataset, how to open and view the image sequences. Similar to [1], subjects were introduced to a sample of reference and distorted image using a scene with hotdog and a chair object. We require subjects to have a laptop/desktop with 2K (2560×1440 pixels) display size and advised them not to resize the viewing window. We also provided two video tutorials on how to view the image sequences on Macintosh and Windows OS.

The double-stimulus impairment scale (DSIS) method is the standard test approach for this experiment. The subjects watch a rendered image and the corresponding reference image side by side. Subjects were asked to rate the image sequences on a scale of 0 to 10 using the 11-point absolute category rating (ACR) scale (refer to Fig. 2) which is more flexible and shows variability. An illustration of the subjective experiment can be seen in Fig. 3. A score of 0 represents a distorted sequence without any similarity with the reference sample. A remote subjective experiment was conducted with college students as subjects. A previous subjective study involving 120 participants [1] was conducted using the test stimuli from [2]. The study employed the Mean Opinion Score (MOS) as the rating method and demonstrated in Fig. 4 that a sample size of approximately 30 human annotators or above is good for a remote subjective experiment. A total of 86 subjects participated in the subjective study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. The subjective experiment took approximately 14 days to complete. Similar to [1], a web UI was employed to collect the opinion scores.

Fig 44.2.Data analysis
A data analysis was conducted on the opinion scores obtained from the subjective experiment. As part of the pre-screening process, seven (7) rows with empty and inconsistent entries were identified and removed from the subjective data. The annotation process was divided among the subjects. Subjects 1 to 31 annotated 46 out of the 102 synthesized image sequences, while subjects 32 to 79 annotated the remaining sequences. After combining all the scores, post-screening procedures were performed to ensure the reliability of the collected scores. We employed the ITU-R BT.500-15 [21,22] standard for post-screening, and the MOS scores are computed. The confidence intervals (CI) of the MOS scores are presented in Fig. 5. The pattern observed in Fig. 5 aligns with findings in [23] and [24], where the CI is narrower at the extremes of the scale. This indicates that participants provided more consistent ratings for stimuli of very low or very high quality. For this dataset, the CI values are below 0.8, with a maximum MOS of 10. These values are comparable to or smaller than the CI values reported in [23] and [24], which had a maximum MOS of 5. The distributions of the MOS scores are shown in Fig. 6 using a histogram. The histogram in Fig. 6 shows that 34 stimuli have MOS scores between 4 and 5. It also shows that a small fraction of the stimuli (4 stimuli) have MOS scores below 2. The relationships between the mean MOS scores across different NeRF models and scenes employed to design the dataset are presented in Figs. 7 and 8. In Fig. 7, image sequences created from InstantNGP model achieved the best mean MOS scores, followed by those created with the TensoRF model. It also shows that image sequences created with the DVGO model yield the lowest mean MOS scores. The performance of the DVGO model aligns with the findings in [4], where the average DMOS values were employed. The distributions in Fig. 8a and b revealed that synthetic scenes tend to result in higher MOS scores compared to realistic scenes. However, an exception is observed with the Horns scene from the LLFF dataset, which achieves MOS scores comparable to those of synthetic scenes such as Chair, Lego, and Ficus. In addition, scenes from the T&T dataset, such as M60, Playground, and Train, consistently yield lower MOS scores and occupy the lower left end of the graph in Fig. 8a. In future work, a similar experiment will be conducted for 3DGS-based methods.
