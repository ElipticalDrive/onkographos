Abstract
Abstract
As a continuous representation function, implicit neural representation has found widespread applications in various research fields such as computer graphics and computer vision. In recent years, many researchers have utilized implicit neural representation for data compression. However, current data compression methods based on implicit neural representation face several challenges, with a critical issue being the inability to adaptively allocate network parameters based on the complex features in the data. This limitation leads to problems such as attention dispersion, decreased reconstruction quality, and increased training costs. To address this challenge, this paper draws inspiration from adaptive grid partitioning and proposes an adaptive volumetric compression method based on implicit neural representation. In this paper, octree is employed to conduct domain non-uniform decomposition of the data, and training a network model for data compression on each leaf node, allowing the network to focus attention on complex data regions during training. The block-wise training approach reduces training time and lowers training costs while achieving the same compression rate. Finally, the effectiveness of our proposed method has been demonstrated through several qualitative and quantitative experiments.

1 Introduction
With the continuous advancement of computer graphics and computer vision, implicit neural representation has found widespread applications. In computer graphics, implicit neural representation is employed to achieve realistic visual effects. For instance, in ray tracing, implicit neural representation is utilized to represent complex geometric shapes and material properties in a scene, thereby enhancing rendering realism[17]. Implicit neural representation is also extensively applied in 3D shape generation and reconstruction[21]. By learning implicit functions, models can generate or reconstruct complex 3D objects without explicitly representing their surfaces[16]. Implicit neural representation can also be applied to compress volume scalar fields[3, 11, 13, 25].
However, current data compression methods based on implicit neural representation face a prevalent and critical challenge: the inability to adaptively allocate network parameters based on the complex features in the data. This results in issues such as attention dispersion, decreased reconstruction quality, and increased training costs during the training process. Traditional uniform decomposition methods may fail to fully utilize the locality properties of the data space. Moreover, the uniform allocation of network parameters may cause the model to inefficiently focus on the most important parts of the data, leading to attention dispersion and decreased reconstruction quality during the training process.
To address this challenge, this paper draws inspiration from adaptive grid partitioning to cope with the complex structures and features in the data[14, 23]. This paper proposes an adaptive volumetric data compression method based on implicit neural representation. Specifically, this paper employs octree algorithms to perform domain non-uniform decomposition on the data. Then, it trains a separate implicit neural representation network model for each leaf node corresponding block to compress volume scalar fields. The aim is to more effectively allocate network parameters through adaptive spatial decomposition, concentrating attention on the complex feature regions of the data, thereby addressing issues such as attention dispersion and decreased reconstruction quality during the training process. Block-wise training reduces training time and decreases training costs while achieving the same compression rate.
Summarizing, the main contributions of our approach are:
(1)
Using octrees for domain non-uniform decomposition, and training a network model for data compression on each leaf node, enabling the network to focus attention on complex data regions during training.
(2)
The block-wise training approach of non-uniform decomposition reduces training time and lowers training costs while achieving the same compression rate.
2 Related Work
2.1 Octree-Based Non-Uniform Domain Decomposition
Octree is a commonly used data structure for representing three-dimensional space, recursively dividing space into eight equally sized cubic subregions[7, 15]. This structure provides convenience for organizing and managing large-scale three-dimensional data effectively. However, in certain cases, especially when dealing with complex data structures, traditional uniform decomposition methods may fail to fully exploit the spatial locality. To handle structurally complex data more effectively, non-uniform decomposition of octree is a good solution.
For example, in meteorology, non-uniform decomposition of meteorological fields can provide finer resolution in simulating meteorological phenomena[6]. This method helps in predicting weather events more accurately. In fluid dynamics, turbulent regions may require higher resolution to capture finer fluid behaviors. Therefore, adopting the non-uniform decomposition of octree structures allows for more flexible adaptation to these local properties, enabling resolution enhancement within the regions of interest. In computer graphics, non-uniform decomposition of octrees is often used for rendering complex scenes. By adaptively allocating resolution to different parts of the tree based on the observer’s viewpoint and scene characteristics, rendering engines can provide high-quality images in critical areas while maintaining lower computational costs in less significant areas. Yu et al. implemented real-time rendering of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) using PlenOctrees[29].
This paper applies octrees to domain non-uniform decomposition in implicit neural representations for data compression, focusing attention on regions of complex data during network training.
2.2 Implicit Neural Representation
Implicit Neural Representation (INR) offers a novel approach for representing complex 3D scenes and shapes without explicitly parameterizing their surfaces. NeRF represents scenes as continuous volumetric functions learned from images or 3D point clouds[17]. By modeling radiance as a function of position and viewing direction, NeRF enables high-quality view synthesis and scene reconstruction. DeepSDF learns continuous Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) to represent shapes[20]. By encoding shapes as SDFs, DeepSDF facilitates shape generation, editing, and reconstruction tasks. Occupancy Networks model 3D shapes as occupancy functions, indicating whether points in space are occupied or free[16]. This approach enables efficient and accurate 3D shape reconstruction from sparse or incomplete data.
A key advantage of these implicit representations is their potential for high data efficiency and compression. Traditional 3D data representations, such as meshes or voxel grids, often require substantial storage space and computational resources. In contrast, INR methods compress complex geometries and scenes into compact neural network parameters. This compression is particularly advantageous for applications with limited storage or bandwidth.
2.3 Volume Scalar Field Compression
Volume scalar field compression plays a pivotal role in domains such as scientific visualization, medical imaging, and computer graphics. Its aim is to reduce the storage requirements and computational complexity of volumetric scalar data while preserving critical features and minimizing information loss. Traditional methods for volume scalar field compression include differential encoding, which exploits spatial and temporal coherence in volumetric data by encoding the differences between consecutive data points. Common techniques include delta encoding and predictive coding. Wavelet transform methods decompose volumetric data into multiple resolution levels using wavelet transforms[8, 9, 19, 22], facilitating efficient compression by discarding high-frequency components while retaining crucial structural information. Advanced approaches in volume scalar field compression include tensor decomposition methods, leveraging the low-rank structure of volumetric data tensors to achieve significant compression ratios. Techniques such as Tucker decomposition and tensor approximation are employed to reduce storage requirements while maintaining data integrity[4]. Deep learning methods have also shown promising results in volume data compression[1, 2, 12, 18, 26, 27, 28]. Utilizing autoencoder architectures and generative adversarial networks (GANs)[5, 24], these methods learn compact representations of volumetric data, leading to improved compression performance and reconstruction quality. Y. Lu et al. employed implicit neural representations for compressing volumetric scalar fields, termed the neurcomp method[13]. This method involves representing a scalar field as a learned function, with a neural network mapping points from the domain to an output scalar value. By ensuring the number of weights in the neural network is smaller than the input size, compressed representations of scalar fields are attained, framing compression as a form of function approximation. The TINC method proposed by Runzhao Yang is a tree-structured implicit neural compression technique[25]. It employs octree algorithms for uniform data decomposition and utilizes parameter sharing, as well as inter-layer and intra-layer parameter allocation methods, to ensure continuity between blocks and eliminate both local and non-local redundancies. However, the TINC method requires training an implicit neural representation model for each node, which demands significant memory usage, longer training time, and higher training costs.

3 Data Partitioning
In this paper, the dataset is non-uniformly decomposed using the octree algorithm. The octree nodes have attributes including center (the center coordinates of the node block), size (the size of the node block), depth (the depth of the node), value (the new value assigned to the node), and a list of child nodes. The center of the node serves as its identifier, facilitating the recombination process after each block training session. The value is assigned based on the depth of the node, with the new value being twice the depth of the current node. This value is used for visualization in Paraview, providing an intuitive understanding of the dataset decomposition.
Whether each octree node continues to be decomposed depends on two conditions: The first condition is the variance of all data within the current node. If it exceeds the set threshold, further decomposition continues; if it is lower than or equal to the threshold, decomposition stops. The second condition involves setting a minimum resolution for the node. Even if the variance of the data within the node exceeds the threshold, if the resolution of the node block is lower than or equal to the set minimum resolution, decomposition ceases. Regarding the determination of the threshold, we first calculate the variance of all the data in the original data, which is a reference value, and the threshold value is chosen to be less than this value, and by adjusting the threshold value and observing its effect on the octree structure and the number of nodes, we finally choose a threshold value that can effectively distinguish the details and maintain a reasonable number of nodes. Since the distribution of values in each dataset is different, it is not possible to fix a threshold value, and it needs to be flexibly adjusted according to different datasets.
Through the aforementioned decomposition approach, the complex regions of the dataset are ultimately partitioned into finer blocks, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
After partitioning, the voxel values of all leaf nodes in the octree are saved. They are stored with the center coordinates of each block serving as the index. These center coordinates serve as the identifier for each block. After data decomposition using the octree algorithm, a separate implicit neural representation model is trained for each block of the established octree. Partitioning the complex regions of the data into finer blocks and training a separate network for each of these blocks will further concentrate training attention on details.
This paper utilizes octree algorithms to perform domain non-uniform decomposition of the data, training a separate network for compression on each leaf node. This allows for efficient allocation of network parameters and focuses attention on complex feature regions of the data. This approach resolves issues such as attention dispersion and decreased reconstruction quality during the training process.
4 Data Block-based Implicit Neural Representation Model
As the scale of data continues to grow, traditional neural networks face immense computational and memory pressure when dealing with large-scale datasets. Implicit neural representation models based on data blocking address this challenge by partitioning data into smaller blocks, enabling more efficient and flexible learning of the data’s implicit representations. The data block-based implicit neural representation model is illustrated in Fig. 1. The neural network consists of an input layer, three residual blocks, and an output layer, where each residual block contains two fully connected layers. The initial learning rate is adjusted based on the network parameters, and the network is trained using the Adamax optimizer. Each implicit neural representation model takes as input all leaf nodes corresponding to blocks obtained from octree decomposition of the dataset, with voxel values of these blocks normalized to [ − 1, 1]3. To ensure consistency in neural network optimization, different data partitions also undergo normalization using the same maximum and minimum values. The normalization’s maximum and minimum values are based on the overall maximum and minimum values of the dataset, and each normalized block serves as input to the network. The output of the network is a compressed representation of the input data, which is the predicted values of voxel compression at leaf nodes. The neural network undergoes continuous training, gradually reducing the value of the loss function until convergence, indicating the end of training. After each leaf node is trained, reorganization is performed based on the central coordinates of the leaf nodes.

5 Experiments
5.1 Implementation Details
The experiments in this paper were conducted on three datasets: Magnetic Reconnection Simulation (512×512×512), Rayleigh-Taylor Instability (1024×1024×1024), and Duct Flow (193×194×1000). For experimental convenience, the Rayleigh-Taylor Instability (1024×1024× 1024) was cropped to a size of 520×520×520. Our network is trained on the dataset using the Adamax optimizer. The initial learning rate is determined based on the number of model parameters. The specific adjustment methods and the selection of learning rates are provided in Sec.5.2.
5.2 Learning Rate Adjustment Strategy
The initial choice of learning rate is crucial for training deep learning models. If the learning rate is too large, it may lead to gradient explosion or unstable training. Conversely, if the learning rate is too small, the training process may become slow or the model may get stuck in local minima. In this paper, experiments were conducted to adjust the learning rate based on the number of model parameters to find the most suitable learning rate for different model parameter counts. The Duct Flow dataset was used for testing, and the results are shown in Fig. 3. The line chart on the left shows the results of training as a whole. Depending on the compression ratio, the model parameter counts are 370,495, 246,445, and 184,111 for compression ratios of 100:1, 150:1, and 200:1, respectively. Learning rate adjustments were made for these three parameter counts, ranging from 1e-7 to 1e-4. As shown in the line chart, the PSNR is highest when the learning rate is 1e-6 for these three parameter counts. Furthermore, it can be observed that as the number of parameters decreases, the learning rate should be increased appropriately, and better experimental results can be achieved. The bar chart on the right shows the results of block-wise training. After blocking, the data was divided into three different block sizes, and experiments were conducted with a compression ratio of 200:1. The parameter counts for block-wise training were 22,998, 2,878, and 372, respectively. Based on the aforementioned conclusion that decreasing model parameter counts requires an appropriately increased learning rate, four adjustment methods for block-wise training were designed. The first method sets the learning rate to 1e-3 for all three parameter counts; the second method sets the learning rate to 1e-2 for all three parameter counts; the third method sets the learning rate to 1e-2 for the parameter count of 372 while keeping it at 1e-3 for the other two parameter counts; and the fourth method sets the learning rate to 1e-3 for the parameter count of 22,998 while keeping it at 1e-2 for the other two parameter counts. These four adjustment methods correspond to the bar chart on the right side of Fig. 3 from bottom to top. It can be seen that the fourth method yields the highest PSNR. Similar experiments were conducted on the Magnetic Reconnection Simulation and Rayleigh-Taylor Instability datasets, and the results conformed to the same pattern. Therefore, the following conclusions were drawn. Generally, if the number of parameters is less than 10k, the learning rate is 1e-2; if the number of parameters is between 10k and 100k, the learning rate is 1e-3; and if the number of parameters is between 100k and 1M, the learning rate is 1e-6. And as the number of parameters decreases, the learning rate should be increased appropriately, and better experimental results can be achieved.

5.5 Network Weight Quantization
The network model was further compressed using network weight quantization in this paper. Given that the majority of network parameters are concentrated in the intermediate layers, with fewer parameters in the first and last layers, only the intermediate layers were subjected to weight quantization. We apply k-means clustering to the weights of each intermediary layer individually. The number of clusters is determined by the desired precision for the weights; specifically, if we aim to represent the weights with b-bit precision, we set the number of clusters to 2b. Once clustering is complete, we assign each weight to its corresponding b-bit index. These indices, along with the cluster centers in floating-point precision, are then stored as the quantized weight representation. For the selection of the b value, experiments were conducted on three datasets, each with a compression ratio of 150:1 before weight quantization. The maximum b value in the experiment is determined by the network weights, where the maximum b value for the 512×512×512 and 520×520×520 datasets can be 7, and for the 193×194×1000 dataset, it can be 6. Different b values were set for the three datasets to observe the changes in compression ratio and PSNR, as shown in Fig. 4. To ensure the visual fidelity of the weight quantized results, the b value selected in this paper is the maximum usable b value for each dataset. Further compression using weight quantization was then applied to models with compression ratios of 150:1 and 200:1, and the compression results are shown in Fig. 6.
6 Limitations
One of the limitations of this paper’s method is that the training of small-scale data will be limited, because this paper’s method needs to partition the data according to the complexity of the data, and if the original data is small in size, the partitioning will make the chunks at the complexity of the data very small, which will lead to too little information that can be learned, thus affecting the effect of training. In addition, the chunking training in this paper leads to a slight boundary artifact problem in the final visualization results.
7 Conclusions
This paper’s contributions include utilizing octrees for domain non-uniform decomposition, and training a network model for data compression on each leaf node, enabling the network to focus attention on complex data regions during training. The block-wise training approach shortens training time and reduces training costs. Furthermore, block-wise processing opens up possibilities for future parallel training[10]. In the future, placing different blocks on different GPUs for parallel training and introducing relevant acceleration technologies[30] will make the time advantage even more significant.
