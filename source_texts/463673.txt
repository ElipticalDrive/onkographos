Résumé en Français
La caractérisation tridimensionnelle (3D) à l’échelle nanométrique constitue
aujourd’hui un enjeu majeur dans de nombreux domaines, en particulier en science des
matériaux, en nanoélectronique et, plus largement, dans toutes les situations où des
structures complexes ne peuvent pas être décrites fidèlement à partir d’images
bidimensionnelles (2D). Les observations 2D classiquement utilisées bien que souvent
suffisantes, souffrent d’effet de projection et de superposition qui limitent fortement
l’interprétation des morphologies internes ou des distributions chimiques des
échantillons analysés. Dans ce contexte, la tomographie électronique (electron
tomography, ET) s’est imposée comme une technique de choix pour l’imagerie 3D à
l’échelle nanométrique, voire atomique dans certains cas. Réalisée dans un microscope
électronique en transmission (transmission electron microscope, TEM), elle consiste en
la reconstruction d’un volume 3D d’un échantillon à partir d’une série d’images 2D
acquises à différents angles d’inclinaison de l’objet étudié. Les progrès instrumentaux
en microscope électronique, tels que les correcteurs d’aberrations et le développement
d’approches analytiques comme le STEM-EDX ou STEM-EELS, ont fortement élargi le
champ d’application de l’ET, aussi bien en science des matériaux qu’en sciences du
vivant.
Malgré son potentiel, l’ET reste confrontée à plusieurs limitations expérimentales et
algorithmiques. D’une part, la géométrie du microscope, du porte-échantillon et de
l’échantillon lui-même empêchent généralement l’acquisition sur la plage angulaire
idéale complète, ce qui crée un coin manquant (missing wedge, MW) dans l’espace de
Fourier et induit des artéfacts d’élongations dans les reconstructions. D’autre part, le
temps d’acquisition et la dose électronique reçue par l’échantillon constituent des
contraintes fortes, en particulier pour les acquisitions analytiques, souvent bruitées et
nécessitant une forte dose d’électrons. La réduction du temps d’acquisition, du nombre
de projections (en augmentant l’incrément angulaire), ou de la plage angulaire
conduisent à des reconstructions dégradées. Les méthodes de reconstruction
classiques, telles que FBP et SIRT génèrent des reconstructions fortement dégradées
lorsque ces conditions d’acquisition complexes sont réunies. Des approches issues de
l’acquisition comprimée (compressed sensing, CS), améliorent partiellement la qualité
des reconstructions, mais montrent encore des limites dans les scénarios les plus
complexes, notamment en présence de fort bruit ou de plage angulaire très restreintes.
Cette thèse s’inscrit précisément dans ce contexte et vise à explorer l’apport des
approches d’apprentissage profond (deep learning, DL) pour dépasser les limites des
méthodes de reconstructions classiques en ET.
Le manuscrit commence par présenter les principes fondamentaux de
l’apprentissage profond et leur intérêt pour les problèmes inverses en imagerie. Les
réseaux de neurones artificiels profonds se sont imposés ces dernières années comme
9
des outils particulièrement efficaces pour résoudre des problèmes de reconstruction et
restauration d’images à partir de données incomplètes ou dégradées. Deux grandes
familles d’approches peuvent notamment être distinguées : les méthodes supervisées,
qui nécessitent des jeux de données d’entraînement comportant des données de
référence de haute qualité, et les méthodes non supervisées, plus attractives lorsque de
tels jeux de données sont indisponibles. Parmi ces dernières, ce travail accorde une
attention particulière au Deep Image Prior (DIP), qui consiste à exploiter l’architecture
d’un réseau de neurone non entraîné comme un apriori implicite pour reconstruire des
images ou des volumes à partir des données acquises potentiellement dégradées.
L’approche DIP est particulièrement intéressante pour la tomographie électronique, car
les jeux de données de référence sont souvent rares, difficiles à obtenir et les
échantillons étudiés sont divers et variés.
Le travail de cette thèse propose d’étudier deux stratégies principales
d’apprentissage profond pour la reconstruction et restauration de données en
tomographie électronique dans le cas d’acquisitions dégradées. La première méthode
est une approche supervisée de restauration post-reconstruction : un réseau de
neurone de type U-Net est entraîné à corriger des reconstructions obtenues par une
méthode classique, à partir de données simulées dans lesquelles les conditions
d’acquisition sont dégradées et connues. La seconde approche consiste à utiliser la
méthode DIP adaptée pour la reconstruction en tomographie électronique. Le réseau
est optimisé directement sur les données de projections limitées et dégradées, sans
apprentissage préalable, afin de reconstruire un volume cohérent avec les projections
qui sont connues. Cette seconde approche est ensuite étendue à une version multicanaux, pensée pour les données multidimensionnelles typiquement obtenues en
tomographie analytique, où plusieurs cartographies élémentaires peuvent être
reconstruites conjointement. L’idée est alors d’exploiter les corrélations structurelles
entre les canaux pour améliorer la robustesse et la cohérence des reconstructions.
Avant toute validation expérimentale, ces approches sont évaluées et étudiées sur
des données simulées représentatives de plusieurs scénarios d’acquisition dégradés en
tomographie électronique. Les tests des deux approches portent tout d’abord sur des
simples particules simulées, avec trois scénarios d’acquisitions dégradées. Puis
l’approche DIP seulement est portée sur des fantômes plus complexes représentant des
nanocubes de type cœur-coquille, où les plages angulaires et les niveaux de bruit
varient. Les résultats sur les particules simulées montrent que les deux approches
d’apprentissage profond améliorent nettement les reconstructions par rapport aux
méthodes classiques en limitant les dégradations dans les reconstructions, et en
particulier les artéfacts de MW. Dans le cas des structures cœur-coquille, l’approche DIP
se révèle flexible est adaptée à des géométries inconnues ou complexes, fournissant des
reconstructions de très bonne qualité, dans tous les scénarios d’acquisition dégradés
envisagés. La version multi-canaux de DIP donne des résultats particulièrement
10
intéressants sur les données corrélées en améliorant la restitution globale des formes
et la cohérence entre les deux volumes reconstruits. Cependant, l’approche DIP se
montre moins stable et sensible aux choix de ses hyperparamètres. Ces résultats sur
données simulées permettent de montrer la fiabilité des méthodes étudiées, avant de
poursuivre avec leur application sur des données expérimentales.
La partie expérimentale de la thèse constitue le cœur de ce travail. Un premier cas
d’étude concerne la reconstruction morphologique de nanoparticules de platine
observées en STEM-HAADF, avec plusieurs plages angulaires volontairement dégradées.
Dans ce scénario relativement simple, où les reconstructions sont affectées
principalement par des artéfacts de MW, les deux approches d’apprentissage profond
surpassent également les reconstructions classiques SIRT et CS. Les volumes obtenus
sont plus propres, plus fidèles géométriquement, ce qui les rend plus facilement
segmentables et analysables. Les deux méthodes atteignent des performances
comparables, avec toutefois des légères limites spécifiques observées. L’approche
supervisée est moins robuste lorsque l’incrément angulaire est élevé, tandis que DIP
montre ses limites lorsque la plage angulaire est extrêmement limitée. Ce premier
exemple valide l’intérêt des méthodes d’apprentissage profond pour la reconstruction
de volumes dont les projections ont été acquises dans des conditions dégradées.
Le second cas d’étude porte sur la tomographie analytique STEM-EDX appliquée à
des dispositifs microélectroniques de type PCRAM. Il s’agit ici d’un problème plus
complexe typique de la tomographie analytique, combinant plage angulaire limitée,
bruit important, échantillon complexe et plusieurs cartographies chimiques à
reconstruire. Seule l’approche DIP est comparée avec les approches classiques dans ce
cas d’étude, dans sa version simple, ainsi que multi-canaux. De plus, un même
échantillon est reconstruit dans plusieurs cas d’acquisition avec des plages angulaires
différentes et des nombres de frames différents. Les résultats montrent que les
variantes de DIP surpassent nettement les approches classiques, tant pour la réduction
des artéfacts de missing wedge que pour la restauration de la forme globale de
l’échantillon. En particulier, la version multi-canaux produit des reconstructions plus
lissées et souvent plus robustes. Les résultats sont également étudiés en termes de
conservation des ratios d’intensité entre les différents canaux, permettant une
quantification élémentaire, en utilisant l’approche de Cliff-Lorimer. Quels que soient les
scénarios d’acquisition simulés, l’approche DIP fournit des reconstructions de très
bonne qualité, qui respectent les rapports d’intensité entre les différents éléments. Ces
résultats démontrent le potentiel de DIP pour la reconstruction de données
spectroscopiques fortement dégradées, cas où les méthodes classiques restent très
limitées.
Le troisième cas d’étude traite de tomographie STEM-EELS pour la reconstruction
d’un nanocube cœur-coquille d’oxyde de fer. L’acquisition considérée ici est
particulièrement complexe, seulement neuf projections sont disponibles et les données
11
sont fortement bruitées. Dans ce contexte, les reconstructions SIRT et CS sont très
dégradées, à cause du faible nombre de projections. Les approches DIP, et en particulier
l’approche multi-canaux, permettent de mieux restituer la géométrie du nanocube, de
réduire les artéfacts dus au faible nombre de projections, et de mieux définir les
interfaces entre le cœur et la coquille. La reconstruction multi-canaux est également la
seule à faire apparaître correctement une fine structure externe associée au cœur,
illustrant le bénéfice de la reconstruction conjointe dans les cas où les volumes sont
effectivement corrélés.
Enfin, la thèse ouvre une perspective vers des modes d’acquisition plus récents tel
que la ptychographie tomographique, qui repose sur des données 4D-STEM et est
capable d’atteindre des résolutions atomiques. Des résultats préliminaires montrent
que l’approche DIP peut également améliorer l’interprétabilité des reconstructions
ptychographiques limités en plage angulaire, même si les échantillons considérés sont
particulièrement complexes et que les artéfacts de missing wedge ne sont pas
complètement supprimés. Cette ouverture suggère que les approches développées
pourraient s’étendre à d’autres modalités plus complexes en microscopie électronique.
Finalement, ce travail montre que les approches d’apprentissage profond, et plus
particulièrement DIP, constituent une alternative très prometteuse aux méthodes de
reconstruction classiques en tomographie électronique. Ce travail montre qu’à partir
d’acquisitions de données fortement dégradées (limitées en plage angulaire, en
incrément angulaire, et bruitées) des volumes peuvent être reconstruits de manière
fiable grâce à des approches bien choisies. L’approche supervisée est particulièrement
efficace lorsque des données simulées réalistes peuvent être générées et lorsque les
objets à restaurer sont plutôt simples. L’approche DIP quant à elle se distingue par son
caractère versatile, sa flexibilité et sa capacité à s’adapter à des géométries plus
complexes, sans nécessité de phase d’entraînement dédiée. La version multi-canaux
apparaît également adaptée aux acquisitions en tomographie électronique analytique,
où la reconstruction simultanée de plusieurs volumes peut améliorer leur qualité. La
thèse souligne cependant certaines limites : la convergence de DIP n’est pas forcément
garantie, le choix des hyperparamètres reste délicat, et un risque d’instabilité de la
méthode peut subsister lors de l’optimisation. Ces points constituent des perspectives
d’amélioration importantes, de même que l’extension de la méthode à des volumes plus
complexes, multidimensionnels ou à d’autres modalités d’acquisition.

General introduction
The mathematical foundations of tomography were established in 1917 by Johannes
Radon, who introduced the idea of reconstructing a function from its integrals along
straight lines (1). This formulation, later known as the Radon transform, became the
basis for tomographic imaging, which consists in reconstructing three-dimensional (3D)
volumes from sets of two-dimensional (2D) projections. Tomography became a practical
imaging technique only later in the 1960s, particularly with the development of early
computers, and the first major applications in medical x-ray computed tomography.
Electron microscopy was developed in the 1930s with the construction of the first
transmission electron microscope (TEM). Electron tomography (ET) was developed later,
notably through the pioneering work of DeRosier and Klug, who performed one of the
first tomographic reconstructions in a TEM in 1968 (2). This work opened the way to the
3D visualization of nanoscale biological objects. Since then, ET has become an important
tool for the characterization and analysis of specimens in both the life sciences and
materials science, down to the nanoscale.
Modern TEMs offer high spatial resolution, and their capabilities extend well beyond
conventional imaging modes. In particular, high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF)
imaging provides contrast information sensitive to atomic numbers, while analytical ET
gives access to chemical information. Although 2D TEM imaging already provides
valuable information, some specimens require a full 3D analysis. For example, catalyst
nanoparticles or semiconductor devices often present complex internal organizations
that cannot be fully understood from 2D projections alone. This limitation can be
addressed by combining tomographic acquisition and reconstruction in the TEM.
However, electron tomography reconstruction is an ill-posed inverse problem, and
two major challenges can be identified in the reconstruction process:
•
An incomplete angular range leads to degraded 3D reconstructions. Ideally,
tomographic projections should be acquired over the full angular range
around the specimen. In practice, this is often prevented by the geometry of
the sample holder or by shadowing effects from the support grid.
•
The dose limitation can be a major constraint during the acquisition. In order
to avoid damaging beam-sensitive samples, it is often necessary to reduce
the overall number of projections or the acquisition time, which generally
results in additional noise or artefacts in the projections.
Classical reconstruction techniques are reliable and perform well when a sufficient
number of high-quality projections is available. However, when the constraints
mentioned above become significant, the resulting reconstructions may suffer from
streaking artefacts, missing wedge elongations and an overall loss of fine details. To
15
improve the quality of the reconstructions, more advanced approaches have been
developed, including compressed sensing-based methods. These techniques
incorporate prior information on the object during the reconstruction process and can
improve the reconstruction quality in complex acquisition settings. Nevertheless, they
remain insufficient in the most challenging scenarios, especially when several
degradations are combined, which motivates the development of more advanced
reconstruction strategies.
Over the last decade, the rise of deep learning (DL) has completely transformed the
field of inverse problems in signal and image processing. Neural networks-based
methods have shown outstanding performance for tasks such as image denoising,
reconstruction, inpainting or segmentation. Their strength lies in their ability to learn
powerful implicit priors from the data while enabling fast inference once trained. In the
context of ET, these approaches offer the possibility of drastically improving
reconstruction quality from original projections data that are limited and degraded.
However, ET also presents specific challenges for DL applications, notably the difficulty
of obtaining large, high-quality reference data sets for the training of the models, as well
as the large diversity and complexity of specimens and acquisition conditions.
In this thesis, we investigate several DL-based methods to address the intrinsic
challenges of electron tomography reconstruction. In particular, both a supervised
restoration approach and an unsupervised method based on the Deep Image Prior (DIP)
framework are considered. A multidimensional extension of DIP is also proposed for
multi-channel reconstruction. The objective is to enable high-quality tomographic
reconstructions from data acquired with a limited number of projections and under
dose-constrained conditions. Both supervised and unsupervised learning methods are
investigated. The proposed approaches are evaluated on both simulated and
experimental data sets under a variety of acquisition scenarios. The experimental
applications include reconstructions from conventional STEM-HAADF imaging, as well as
spectroscopic modalities.
The manuscript is organized as follows.
The first chapter introduces electron tomography, its main fields of application in
the life sciences and materials science, and the principal challenges related to
acquisition geometry and electron dose. It also reviews recent DL-based approaches
proposed for ET reconstruction.
The second chapter presents the fundamentals of deep learning, including
optimization, the main components of neural networks, the different training paradigms
and modern DL approaches for inverse problems, as well as their potential interest for
ET.
16
The third chapter introduces the mathematical formalism of tomography through
the Radon transform, describes the experimental principles of TEM acquisition, and
reviews the classical reconstruction techniques used in ET.
The fourth chapter presents the proposed DL methods for ET reconstruction and
restoration, and evaluates their performance on several simulated data sets in order to
highlight their strengths and limitations.
The fifth chapter applies these methods to several challenging experimental data
sets acquired with different imaging modes. The studied specimens include catalyst
nanoparticles observed by STEM-HAADF, PCRAM devices characterized by STEM-EDX,
and iron oxide nanocubes analyzed by STEM-EELS. Finally, a brief perspective on
applications to ptychographic data is provided.

Chapter 1: An overview of advances in
electron tomography for nanoscale 3D
characterization
A large number of materials and biological specimens present three-dimensional
(3D) structures at the nanoscale level, with internal organization or chemical
composition that are not entirely describable from two-dimensional (2D) observations
alone. While 2D imaging techniques provide valuable information, they suffer from
projection effects with overlapping features that prevent a faithful observation of
complex structures. Therefore, the access to 3D information has become essential in
many fields, such as microelectronics.
Transmission electron microscopes (TEMs) are among the most widely used tools for
nanoscale characterization in both materials and life sciences. With their capability to
surpass the resolution of light microscopes by using electrons, TEMs have shown the
capability to provide 2D structural and chemical information down to sub-nanometer
scale. Conventional TEM imaging generates 2D projections of 3D objects, limiting the
observation of 3D characteristics. Electron tomography (ET) is a technique that enables
the 3D visualization of objects by reconstructing a volume from a set of 2D projection
images acquired at different tilt angles around the object in the TEM.
The range of applications for ET has risen significantly in fields such as
microelectronics and nanotechnology, where devices can reach dimensions of only a
few nanometers. In parallel, electron microscopy has benefited from significant
technical advancements, including the development of advanced electron sources and
the introduction of aberration correctors in the late 1990s (3–5). These innovations have
significantly enhanced the spatial resolution and analytical capabilities of modern
electron microscopes. Since then, ET has emerged as an essential technique for 3D
characterization at the nanoscale.
This chapter provides an overview of 3D imaging techniques, with a focus on ET. The
main fields of application and developments of ET are then presented, followed by a
consideration of its classical limitations and the usual strategies to address them. Finally,
recent applications of deep learning (DL) for ET data reconstruction and restoration are
reviewed, before introducing the objectives of this thesis work.
1.1. 3D characterization of materials
Three-dimensional imaging techniques are essential for a comprehensive
characterization of complex specimens, because they provide an access to the internal
structure of objects, beyond the information available from classical 2D images.
18
Standard 2D imaging techniques consist in recording projections of specimens onto a
plane, which leads to the loss of depth information and makes the interpretation of
ambiguous features challenging. By contrast, 3D imaging provides true volumetric data
which offers a more effective and interpretable way of analyzing the internal structure
of complex specimens.
Figure 1:1 presents an overview of the most commonly used 3D imaging techniques
in materials and life sciences, spanning sample sizes from a few millimeters to a few
nanometers (6). Within this range, ET covers spatial resolutions between 1 and 10 nm
and can even achieve atomic scale resolutions in specific cases, while remaining a nondestructive technique.
Figure 1:1: Comparison of the possible resolution and analyzed volumes with different 3D
characterization techniques.
The following section briefly introduces alternative 3D imaging methods before
introducing the fundamental principles of ET in more details.
1.1.1. Destructive 3D characterization techniques
1.1.1.1.
FIB-SEM tomography
Focused ion beam (FIB) microscopy is now a commonly used tool for the preparation
of specimens. In FIB-SEM (scanning electron microscope) tomography, the instrument
operates in a dual-beam manner, integrating a FIB and a scanning electron microscope
(SEM). It enables 3D volumetric visualization through slice-by-slice analysis as depicted
in Figure 1:2 (7). The electron beam column is oriented vertically, while the ion beam
column is positioned at a tilt angle of roughly 50°. The generation of a 3D image is
achieved through sequential removal of layers of the sample by the ion beam, while the
electron beam performs the imaging. This repeated procedure, referred to as “slice-and19
view”, produces a series of images that can be reconstructed into a 3D volume. This
method is relatively simple to use, versatile and is well suited for the analysis of
micrometric-sized samples with a typical spatial resolution in the range of 5-10 nm. As a
result, FIB-SEM tomography is an extremely important technique commonly used for
the analysis of microscopic biological samples (cells, tissues, etc.) and material samples
(fuel cells, batteries, microelectronics components, etc.) (8). Nevertheless, FIB-SEM
tomography also has limitations. It is inherently a destructive technique and can be timeconsuming, as the cutting and imaging of large volumes can take up to a full day,
depending on the material, the instrument, and the image quality (9). The technique can
fill a gap between X-ray-based techniques, which offer sub-micrometer resolution and
TEM tomography, which provides nanometric to atomic scale resolutions.
Figure 1:2: Simplified representation of a FIB-SEM 3D.
1.1.1.2.
Atom probe tomography
Atom probe tomography (APT) is a technique capable of reconstructing samples at
the atomic level. The technique requires the utilization of field ion microscope (FIM) in
combination with a mass spectrometer. The sample, which is prepared as a thin needle,
is positioned within the FIM and a high voltage is applied to it. Then, by applying a
voltage or laser pulse, the atoms at the extremity of the probe are evaporated and are
subsequently detected by the spectrometer. It is possible to determine the chemical
nature of the atoms as well as their position from this measurement. Finally, it is possible
to recover the geometry and chemical nature of the sample atom by atom, using the
information obtained through the acquisition process (10). APT is a quantitative
technique capable of providing chemical information with very high sensitivity, in the
range of parts per million, and is also the 3D approach offering the highest spatial
resolution, reaching the atomic scale (11). However, it should be noted that APT is a
destructive method. One limitation of the technique is the distortion of the
reconstructed volume because of the differences in evaporation fields between the
different observed regions. In addition, not all types of specimens can be prepared into
a needle shape nor demonstrate a sufficient voltage resistance. Figure 1:3 presents a
simple illustration of the schematic of the APT (12).

1.1.2. Non-destructive projection-based 3D techniques
In tomography, 2D projections are typically collected at multiple angles around a
specimen of unknown structure or chemical composition, as illustrated in Figure 1:4
(10). A 3D volume is then obtained by applying a dedicated reconstruction algorithm to
the set of 2D projections. Tomography is a versatile technique that is applicable to a
variety of physical signals, such as x-rays, electrons or neutrons to name a few. The
tomographic reconstruction process relies on a fundamental principle known as the
projection requirement (13). We begin this section with a brief overview of x-ray
tomography, before discussing ET in greater detail, including its fields of application,
acquisition strategies and associated acquisition challenges. Details on tomographic
reconstruction and ET principles are presented more thoroughly in Chapter 3.
Figure 1:4: Schematic representation of the principle of tomographic acquisition and reconstruction.
21
1.1.2.1.
X-ray tomography
Historically, x-rays radiographs were the first transmission images used for
tomography. At the micrometric and nanometric scale, three setups are mainly used to
perform x-ray tomography experiments: synchrotrons, x-ray micro-tomographs and
scanning electron microscopes (SEM). In synchrotrons, which are large instruments, xray beams can be precisely controlled and made monochromatic, achieving spatial
resolutions down to approximately 50 nm (14). X-ray micro-tomographs are routinely
used to achieve micrometer scale resolutions, with the latest instruments reaching
resolutions on the order of 50-100 nm (15). SEMs can also be used to generate x-rays by
excitation of a specimen of known composition with the electron beam, enabling SEMbased x-ray tomography, with spatial resolutions of approximately 50 nm (16). However,
to achieve smaller spatial resolutions, tomography performed in a transmission electron
microscope is required.
1.1.2.2.
Electron tomography and fields of application
Transmission electron microscopes are powerful instruments used for the
characterization of nanomaterials, in which a beam of electrons is transmitted through
a sample in order to create 2D images. A detailed description of the TEM operating
principles, components and acquisition modes is presented in Chapter 3. We focus here
on the various applications of ET. The origins of 3D electron microscopy in the TEM are
conventionally attributed to the seminal paper (2), in which the 3D structure of the
bacteriophage T4 tail was reconstructed from a single 2D image by exploiting prior
information on the symmetry of the specimen. The authors, DeRosier and Klug, also
introduced the idea of reconstructing a 3D object from a set of 2D images, now referred
to as projections. Following this pioneering work, the technique was further developed
and applied across several fields including both materials and life sciences. Although the
fundamental principles of the method itself remain the same between the two domains,
the typical specimens of interest and the mode of imaging are usually different in
materials and life sciences (17,18). Historically, the life sciences community began
applying the principle of ET for the 3D visualization of biological macromolecules in the
1970s.
1.1.2.2.1.
Life sciences
In life sciences, bright-field (BF) TEM imaging is the prevalent technique due to the
fact that the specimens under study are typically non-crystalline with low scattering
properties. The observed images can then be regarded as accurate projections of the
objects, with mass-thickness and phase contrast being the primary factors considered in
the formation of images (10).
Cells are complex 3D systems with a dynamic internal organization linked to their
function and state. When analyzing such specimens, conventional TEM is limited by its
22
projections of 3D objects onto 2D images. The use of ET enables the visualization and
understanding of complex cellular and subcellular structures (17). For instance, ET has
been used to characterize and reveal antibody transport across epithelial cells (19) or to
investigate the interaction between bacteria and intestinal epithelial cells (20).
The combination of ET with cryogenic imaging conditions, without fixation or
dehydration of the samples, led to the development of cryogenic ET (cryo-ET), which
enables the 3D visualization of biological specimens in their hydrated native state. CryoET makes it possible for the 3D observation of a variety of specimens across several
levels of organization, from macromolecular machines to subcellular structures
(17,21,22). It has been used in various scenarios, such as the observation of distinct
macromolecular complexes in vitrified eukaryotic cells at a resolution of 5 to 6
nanometers in an unperturbed cellular environment (23), as well as the observation of
the 3D density map of intact microtubules doublets with a 3 nm resolution (24).
To further enhance reconstruction quality, a widely used approach in the life
sciences is the sub-tomogram averaging, in which multiple copies of similar structures
are extracted, aligned, and averaged to produce a reconstruction of higher quality (25).
The approach enable sub-nanometric resolution in several studies, including the
determination of the structure of immature HIV-1 capsid within virus particles at 8.8 Å
resolution (26), the construction of an atomic model of immature HIV-1 particles with
3.9 Å resolution (27), or the resolution of the structure of Ebola virus nucleocapsid
structure at 6.6 Å (28).
In parallel, single-particle cryo-ET reconstruction has enabled the near-atomic
resolution structural determination of large complexes, such as the mammalian
transient receptor potential (TRP) channel resolved at 3.4 Å, providing an understanding
of unique TRP channel function (22). Current developments in ET for life sciences
prioritize the acceleration of acquisitions and the development of more automated
acquisition and reconstruction approaches (25).
1.1.2.2.2.
Materials science and (HA)ADF-STEM
In materials science, BF-TEM was also initially used for observations of a variety of
specimens such as carbonaceous weakly scattering materials (29,30), or the SBA-15
mesoporous silica materials (31). However, BF-TEM imaging of highly scattering
samples, which are commonly studied in the physical sciences, has shown to lead to the
violation of the projection requirement. In contrast to biological samples, material
specimens are generally more tolerant to higher electron doses, making possible the use
of a larger range of imaging modes, providing not only morphological, but also chemical,
electronic, magnetic or crystallographic information. Therefore, in scanning
transmission electron microscopy (STEM), annular dark field (ADF) and especially high
angle annular dark field (HAADF) imaging have become central to ET for materials
23
science (10,13,18). These modes provide signals whose contrast is directly related to
specimen properties and are generally considered to satisfy the projection requirement.
HAADF-STEM tomography was applied for the 3D characterization of a large variety
of samples in the past years, such as catalysts, fuel cells components and nanoparticles
(32–36), normally not accessible with conventional imaging techniques. Advances in
electron microscopy instrumentation and methodologies have also led to the possibility
of reaching atomic resolution 3D imaging, with various objectives such as, determining
the crystallographic structure of silver nanoparticle (37), resolving the 3D structure of
gold nanoparticles at 2.4 Å resolution (38), observing dislocations in platinum
nanoparticles (39) or estimating the coordinates of individual atoms in tungsten (40).
These studies had applications in several fields such as catalysis, nanoscience or solidstate physics. Current and future objectives focus on the analysis of more complex
systems or amorphous materials, prone to radiation damage by improving electron
detectors, STEM imaging methods and reconstruction techniques (41).
1.1.2.2.3.
Analytical electron tomography
A second important reason for the widespread use of STEM is its ability to acquire
multiple signals simultaneously, making possible the multimodal analysis of specimens.
In particular, STEM allows for the combination of morphological imaging with analytical
techniques, enabling the mapping of physical properties, such as local chemistry.
Analytical techniques have played an important role for the chemical characterization
of materials in electron microscopy, so they were naturally extended to ET. Analytical
electron tomography (AET) produces tomograms that are multidimensional, with
additional dimensions beyond the spatial domain. The most commonly used techniques
are energy electron loss spectroscopy (EELS) and energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy
(EDX or EDXS) (42). This section presents applications of STEM-EDX and STEM-EELS
combined with ET.
STEM-EDX was used to reveal the 3D elemental distribution in organic and inorganic
core-shell nanowires (43), as well as to highlight the segregation of silver and gold in
bimetallic nanoparticles (44). As an example, the corresponding 3D reconstruction
showing the segregation of the two elements is presented in Figure 1:5. It also enabled
the analysis of nanoscale particles used as a cathode material for lithium ion batteries
and highlighted the segregation of nickel on their surface (45). EDX was also used in
combination with ET for the characterization of devices in the semiconductor industry.
In (46), a method for the observation of a 28 nm FDSOI transistor by STEM-EDX was
presented, showing the possible use of such 3D chemical characterization in almost
routine analysis. In (47), the STEM-EDX and STEM-HAADF acquisition from an arsenicdoped silicon fin-shaped structure were performed simultaneously in order to observe
complementary information (visible in Figure 1:6): the roughness of the Si structure was

characterized by STEM-HAADF tomography while STEM-EDX tomography was used to
observe the profiling of the arsenic dopant.

In parallel to STEM-EDX, STEM-EELS tomography was employed in a range of

voxel-wise spectroscopic reconstruction. This demonstrated the complementarity of the

Extension to ptychography
In the late 2010s, the 4D-STEM technique was made possible with the development

1.1.2.3. Challenges and limitations of the method and classical
solutions
As demonstrated in the previous section, ET is a powerful characterization technique
employed across a wide range of acquisition scenarios and specimen types. The
technique has been improved over the years and is now commonly used for 3D
characterization at the nanoscale. However, several challenges and limitations that
impact the quality of the reconstructions must be considered during the acquisition
process. This section briefly describes the typical issues encountered during a TEM
acquisition: the limited angular range and the electron dose. In both acquisition
scenarios, the classical reconstruction techniques perform poorly and generate 3D
26
volumes that might suffer from noise, or from unwanted artefacts. These limitations lie
at the core of this work and justify the need for advanced reconstruction techniques.
1.1.2.3.1.
Geometry of the acquisition
During a tomographic acquisition, the geometry of the TEM, the specimen, or the
sample holder can prevent the specimen from being tilted over the full ±90° angular
range ideally required for single-axis tomography (10). This incomplete angular sampling
results in missing information, referred to as the missing wedge (MW), and leads to
characteristic reconstruction artefacts. To mitigate these effects, both instrumental and
algorithmic solutions have been proposed.
In order to prevent the appearance of MW artefacts, one solution is to use a FIBprepared needle-shaped sample in combination with a specifically developed “on-axis”
tomographic holder (56–60). In this configuration, the sample can rotate freely, avoiding
MW. Moreover, this geometry allows the specimen thickness to remain constant at
every tilt angle, ensuring a constant resolution in the projections throughout the
acquisition (61). A second solution is to perform a dual-axis tomography acquisition,
which consists in performing two acquisitions in two perpendicular directions of the
specimen plane, in order to reduce the MW to a missing cone of information (62–64).
However, this method is more complex to implement and combining both acquisitions
accurately is not easy. In addition, the electron dose applied to the sample is also
doubled because of the repeated acquisition.
Algorithmically, some approaches have been developed in order to remove the MW
artefacts. For instance, (65) proposed a total variation (TV) based sinogram inpainting
technique. However, the approach was tested on quite simple simulated and
experimental data. In addition, the technique does not perform as good when the data
is noisy or when more than two thirds of the angular range is unavailable. The GENFIRE
algorithm, proposed in (66), is an approach that performs iterations between the Fourier
space and the real space. The method has shown good reconstruction results and
robustness to missing data scenarios with a ±60° acquisition range. In (67), the authors
proposed a novel technique (QURT) by arranging gray-level quantisation units in 3D
image space with a non-linear discrete processing. The technique presented
reconstruction with correction of the MW artefacts for angular ranges down to ±60°.
However, the method requires a large computational power and was also tested on
quite simple data sets. Overall, algorithmic reconstruction approaches capable of
handling angular range below ±60° with complex realistic samples remain rare.
1.1.2.3.2.
Electron dose and acquisition time
Two significant challenges in ET are the electron dose received by the sample as well
as the acquisition time, which are inherently linked. In comparison with classical TEM
observations, both the acquisition time and electron dose are multiplied by the number
27
of projections acquired in a tomographic experiment. These acquisition conditions might
lead to beam-induced damage and degradation of the specimen. Beam damages are
influenced not only by the overall cumulative electron dose, but also the dose rate, as
the instantaneous electron beam can degrade the sample, even when the overall dose
remains moderate.
Two main approaches have been used to reduce both the electron dose and
acquisition time. The first consists in acquiring fewer projections around the specimen,
thereby performing sparse acquisitions. The second consists in reducing the effective
acquisition time for each projection. This leads to a tradeoff between sparsity and
acquisition time, and both strategies produce degraded reconstructions when classical
reconstruction techniques are used.
A number of acquisition strategies have been established with the purpose of
performing rapid tomographic acquisition. For in situ studies typically, the continuous
fast ET (CFET) and the incremental fast ET (IFET) are characterized by rapid tilting
between consecutive tilt angles for the IFET and uninterrupted tilting during the
acquisition for CFET (68). However, these approaches often lead to specimen
movements in a perpendicular direction to the tilt axis over the full tilt range, which can
only be partially compensated. To maintain satisfying image quality, manual correction
of the stage movements might be required during the acquisition, increasing the overall
acquisition time. As a consequence, these types of approaches might restrict the
possibility of high tilt range acquisitions leading to more MW artefacts.
One of the main fields of research in the algorithmic side has been the development
of compressed sensing (CS) reconstruction techniques, which incorporate a prior
knowledge into the reconstruction process and have been vastly adapted and applied
to the field of ET. CS is used for the reconstruction of signals from undersampled data.
A more developed and precise formulation of the CS approaches will be presented in
Chapter 3. In the last decade, CS-based methods have been applied to the
reconstruction of samples from challenging acquisitions in ET. In 2011, (69) presented
the reconstruction of iron oxide nanoparticles, from a few acquired projections, using a
CS-based reconstruction technique with a TV regularization, which was the first
application of CS to ET. The approach was further studied and compared to classical
reconstruction technique in (70). Following this work, the method has demonstrated its
efficiency in several scenarios such as for atomic resolution ET of gold nanorods (71),
the study of the cloudy zone of meteorites in (72) or the reconstruction of core-shell
nanoparticles in (73). The approach was also further applied with an improved
regularization technique called high order TV for the reconstruction of mesoporous
boron nitride nanoparticle (74). Another regularization of interest classically used in CS
is based on the wavelet transform, which has been used several times in CS
reconstruction for ET. For example, it was adapted in (48), for the reconstruction of
28
metal nanoparticles or in (75), for the determination of the structure of biological
specimens.
The CS-based approaches using additional regularization also demonstrated to be
suitable for analytical ET, where noise is usually present in the data. As an example, 3D
chemical composition of phase-change materials (76) and 3D oxidation state mapping
of iron oxide core-shell nanocubes in (52) were both reconstructed using STEM-EELS
tomography and CS-based methods. The total nuclear variation (TNV) regularization was
used in (77) in order to perform a joint EDS and HAADF-STEM reconstruction of a PMOS
finFET device. This regularization encourages the EDX reconstruction to overlap its edges
with the more accurate HAADF reconstruction. In (78), a total generalized variation
regularization was implemented in order to perform combined multimodal HAADF-EDX
observation and reconstruction of a Si-Na-Yb needle-shaped sample. In (79), a similar
approach, called the HAADF-EDX bimodal tomographic (HEBT) technique, was proposed
by taking advantage of the information in both HAADF and noisy EDX data in order to
reconstruct silver-gold nanoparticles.
Over a decade of developments, CS-based reconstruction techniques have greatly
improved the quality of the reconstruction obtained from ET acquisitions. However, the
approach still presents limitations in specific acquisition contexts. In (80), CS-TV based
techniques were applied on a large number of simulated structures, with different
angular ranges, tilt increments and noise levels. Two main conclusions were made from
this study. First, the CS approach is robust only to a small missing angular range of 60°
or less. Second, for a fixed electron count, and therefore a fixed noise level, increasing
the number of available projections does not necessarily improve the reconstruction
quality. These results show that CS-based methods still remain sensitive to the most
challenging acquisitions scenarios int ET.

In the meantime, the rapid expansion of the field of machine learning (ML) research
has led to its wide usage in a variety of scientific environments. ML techniques focus on
algorithms that learn to solve problems from known data sets and can generalize
robustly to new, previously unseen data. Notably, DL approaches, which use neural
network architectures, have undergone rapid expansion across numerous fields of
application. DL has become a standard procedure to solve imaging problems, such as for
electron microscopy challenges (81,82). ET has naturally been impacted by the novel use
of DL approaches for data processing, restoration or reconstruction. In particular, for
the problem of tomographic reconstruction and restoration from degraded acquisitions,
three general ways in which DL can be applied to tomographic imaging can be identified.

The number of studies focusing on applying novel DL approaches to the field of ET is

a ±60° acquisition range and suffer from the classical MW artefacts. This work performs
an iterative refinement of the reconstructions of biological particles using raw
tomograms as input information and neural networks, thereby improving image quality.

well because biological tomograms contain recurrent patterns, allowing the network to
exploit similarities in the data. In (85), a similar method called DeepDeWedge uses raw

called generative adversarial networks (GAN), such as (86), where a GAN was used in
conjunction with a cryo-EM physics simulator in an approach called CryoGAN, to
perform single-particle reconstruction. In (87), a GAN-based approach was used in a
combination of six neural networks to perform simultaneous denoising and simulation
of realistic cryo-ET data that could be used for neural network training. In a recent work
(88), the authors applied a combination of deep image prior (DIP) and coordinate
networks, which uses an untrained neural network as a prior, to perform
reconstructions from cryo-ET experiments. The reconstructions from phage particles
acquired with a ±60° angular range focused on preventing MW artefacts. This approach
has the advantage of being versatile and adaptative to various acquisitions scenarios.

1.2.2. Deep learning in x-ray tomography
While the work presented in this manuscript is completely focused on the
application of DL approaches to ET data sets, several methods of interest have also been
developed and applied to x-ray tomography. The challenges encountered in x-ray
tomography can be very similar to those from ET, making it relevant to present some of
these techniques. This list is non-exhaustive but rather presents some interesting
approaches employed only for x-ray tomographic reconstruction. For instance, (97) has
shown that an encoder–decoder network architecture like the U-Net, which had been
mainly employed for image segmentation tasks, is also efficient for the removal of usual
CT sparse-view artefacts usually visible in tomographic acquisitions. In (98), a wavelets33
based network used in a supervised manner was used for multi-scale wavelet domain
learning in the context of the denoising of low-dose CT reconstruction. In TomoGAN (99),
synchrotron data were restored from sparse and noisy acquisitions with a GAN-based
approach. GANs have also been employed for the purpose of direct reconstruction of
tomographic data from degraded sinograms in (100) without any prior training. In this
work, ptychographic tomography data were acquired on a ±70° angular range, and MW
artefacts were removed in the reconstruction process. Finally, a number of studies have
explored DIP and related implicit representations inspired approaches for x-ray
tomography. They have been applied in several scenarios such as low-dose and sparseview acquisitions (101,102) as well as for limited angular-range of ±60° (103,104) but
also for reconstruction with very limited angular-range, down to an available range of
only 45° (105). These works highlight the strong potential of the DIP framework for a
variety of challenging tomographic reconstruction scenarios.
From the review of existing DL-based works applied to tomographic challenges,
some interesting points can be raised. Several approaches operate in a supervised
manner, and necessitate training data sets for their models, making them less easily
applicable in heterogeneous scenarios. The approaches developed for ET in life sciences
are performing greatly but are usually specifically developed to single-particle
reconstructions. Many approaches are GAN-based, but these networks are quite
complex to use and ensuring their convergence is not straightforward. Finally, the family
of approaches based on DIP, which has already demonstrated efficiency in challenging
x-ray tomography reconstruction scenarios, appears promising for further applications
to ET. Because these methods require no prior training, they can be applied to a variety
of specimens. In addition, they seem to be well suited for the denoising of tomographic
data, as well as for the reconstruction and completion of sinograms in the case of sparseview and limited-angle acquisitions.
1.3. Objective of the thesis work
From this review of the literature, the family of approaches based on DIP appears
particularly promising for ET. In contrast to many supervised methods, DIP does not
require large training data sets and can be more easily adapted to the wide variety of
specimens, acquisitions conditions, and imaging modes encountered in ET. In addition,
it appears well suited for challenging reconstruction scenarios involving limited angular
ranges, sparse acquisitions, and noise. For these reasons, this work focuses primarily on
the use of DIP-based approaches, including their application to several ET imaging
modes.

Chapter 2: Introduction to deep learning
The field of artificial intelligence (AI) designates a broad field that contains a wide
range of computational methods designed to perform a variety of complex tasks (Figure
2:1). Inside AI, the subset of machine learning (ML) corresponds to the set of techniques
that enable algorithms to learn patterns from the data. Deep Learning (DL) is
characterized by the use of deep neural networks, which are models that extract
features from the given input data. The notion of learning arises from the optimization
of the parameters of the networks using the training data, while the term “deep”
originates from the network architectures that employ many stacked layers.
Figure 2:1: Subsets of approaches within the field of Artificial Intelligence (AI). Machine learning (ML)

data.
DL methods have undergone major developments across many scientific fields,
including electron microscopy and tomography, because of their ability to efficiently
solve inverse problems. In particular, they have been used for the reconstruction and
restoration of tomographic data acquired under degraded conditions. Such approaches
can be used to suppress noise in reconstructions or to correct artefacts resulting from
sparse and limited angular range acquisitions. In this context, DL methods offer a
promising alternative to classical reconstruction techniques and make it possible to
address challenging ET scenarios.
This chapter aims at providing the DL background to understand the methods
developed and used in this thesis. We first introduce a short history of DL before
reviewing the fundamental principles of neural network training and optimization. Then,
the core components of convolutional neural networks used in this work are introduced.

Finally, several training paradigms and modern DL approaches are presented, with
attention to their relevance for electron tomography application.
For a more comprehensive presentation of DL, the reader may refer to the handbook

2.1. Origins of deep learning
With the rapid development of computer science and the availability of powerful
computing machines, researchers have looked at mimicking characteristics of human
intelligence such as learning and reasoning. In the second half of the 20 th century,
several works presented the premises of what would become artificial neural networks
(ANN). In 1989, LeCun et al. (109) successfully trained a neural network to recognize
handwritten postal ZIP codes using the backpropagation algorithm, which was one of
the first demonstrations of supervised DL.
A major breakthrough happened in 2012 with the work of Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and
Hinton (110) who trained a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) using advanced
computing power (GPUs). This model achieved unprecedented results and
outperformed classical computer vision methods (111). This moment is regarded as the
beginning of the modern DL era. At the time, these models were often seen as “black
boxes” because of their complexity, their large number of parameters, and the limited
comprehension of their high performance.
Following this early development, DL has demonstrated high efficiency in the
interpretation of high-dimensional data as well as its ability to extract complex features
in such data (112). It has demonstrated proficiency in several tasks including image
processing, natural language processing or speech recognition. Compared to earlier
techniques, DL makes particularly effective use of large data sets and recent advances
in computational power. In the last decade, the field of DL has undergone rapid
development, both in terms of research activity and investment. The creation of opensource tools such as PyTorch, TensorFlow or JAX made easier the application, the
training and the deployment of neural network models for both experts and nonspecialists of the field. The next section presents the core principles underlying the
training and usage of neural networks.

2.3. Neural network components
Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are the main building blocks of modern DL. They
are made of several layers, placed end-to-end, with trainable weights that are optimized
to learn features and characteristics from the data. The ANNs architectures have evolved
considerably over the last years, but the core principles and computational components
remain mostly unchanged.
In our work, the proposed methods rely on neural networks based on the wellknown U-Net architecture, which has become a standard architecture for image
processing over the last decade (120). Originally introduced for medical image
segmentation, the U-Net is based on an encoder-decoder structure, connected through
skip connections. This architecture has been widely applied to 2D and 3D image
reconstruction, restoration or segmentation problems. In addition, various extensions
of the original architecture have been proposed, incorporating recent developments in
DL architectures.
This section introduces the main building blocks of neural networks, which are
typically present in the U-Net architecture used in our work.

perceptrons are stacked together to form a deeper architecture. A perceptron takes an

2.3.2. Convolutional layers
With the rapid development of DL for image processing tasks, more suitable
approaches than the perceptron layers were required. The perceptron layers, being fully
connected layers, take vectors as input, making them poorly adapted for image data,
without flattening them, and therefore destroying their spatial arrangement. In
addition, fully connected layers have a fixed input size, and their number of parameters
scales with the size of the input, which is impractical for large images.
CNNs were introduced in (109), as a way to address these limitations and to exploit
the spatial structure of images. They are based on the use of a convolutional layer, which
applies a convolution kernel with learnable parameters to the input, therefore using
fewer parameters than the perceptron blocks. Convolutional layers have since become
the standard neural network building block for image and signal processing, and they
form one of the core components of architectures such as the U-Net.
A convolutional layer is defined by the size of its kernel (typically 3 × 3 for an image)
and by the number of input and output channels. They can be seen as a specific case of
perceptron layers, whose formal operation is:
𝑓(𝑥) = 𝑊 ∗ 𝑥 + 𝑏
(2.12)
where 𝑊 is the convolution kernel, 𝑏 is a bias term and ∗ is the convolution operator.
In addition to the kernel size and number of channels, three important
hyperparameters exist:
-
The padding represents the number of zero added around the input tensor.
Padding is typically used to preserve the spatial dimension between the input
and the output,
-
The stride represents the step size of the scanning operation along the input
tensor. A standard value for the stride is 1, and a larger stride will reduce the
spatial resolution of the output,
-
The dilation specifies the spacing between the elements of the kernel, its
standard value is 1 and corresponds to a standard convolution. A higher value
allows for the kernel to detect a larger area, without increasing the number of
parameters.
As with perceptron layers, a non-linear activation function is applied on the output
of convolution layers to introduce nonlinearity in the model.

2.3.3. Non-linearities (activation functions)
Following the analogy between artificial neural networks and biological neurons,
each unit requires an “activation”. Activation functions are applied element-wise after
a convolutional or fully connected layer in order to introduce non-linearity in the model.
These non-linearities help the network to model more complex structures in the data.

2.3.4. Pooling
Many neural network architectures reduce the spatial dimensions for the feature
maps at various stages in the process to focus on higher-level structures and reduce
computational cost. Pooling layers achieve this by downsampling feature maps, which
generate smaller representations that contain only the most meaningful information.
Several pooling strategies exist, and the most commonly used are:
•
The maximum pooling, which keeps the maximum in each region, focuses on
the most prominent features.
•
The average pooling, which computes the mean value in its region, produces
smoother feature maps.
•
The minimum pooling, which selects the smallest value.
Pooling operations can be seen as a type of convolution operation with specific
kernels and strides. A visualization of different pooling operations is shown in Figure 2:3

the pooling layers progressively decrease the spatial resolution of the data, while the
number of features maps increases, allowing them to extract abstract representation of
the data.
2.3.5. Dropout
Dropout is a regularization technique used to improve the generalization capability
of the network. It consists in randomly setting a fraction of the neurons to zero at each
iteration. By temporarily dropping these neurons, the network is able to learn
independent representations instead of relying too much on some specific neurons. This
technique reduces the overfitting in neural networks, i.e., prevents the model from
being too specialized for a specific training data set and improves its performance on
new unseen data. Dropout is applied only during the training of the network, and no
neurons are dropped during the testing or application of the network.
2.3.6. Normalization
Normalization layers are an important class of neural network components that
helps the optimization process and the deep network to converge more reliably. Batch
normalization (127) was one of the first commonly used methods and has been followed
by the development of other variants such as layer normalization (128), instance
normalization (129), and group normalization (130). These techniques also address the
training of networks with smaller batch sizes.

2.3.7. Residual
Residual layers (or residual blocks) were developed to solve the vanishing gradient
problem and to enable the training of deeper neural network (131). The idea is to make
the gradient easier to propagate through the network, by enabling a more direct path
through the network, known as a skip connection. For a transformation 𝐹 applied to an
input 𝑥, a residual layer computes:
𝐻(𝑥) = 𝐹(𝑥) + 𝑥
(2.19)
Here, 𝐹 consists of convolutional layers followed by non-linearities.
Residual blocks were popularized in ResNet (131), where it was demonstrated that
very deep CNNs could be trained efficiently by using these blocks. Without residual
connections, such deep networks could not be trained because of optimization
difficulties.
Although the skip connections in the classical U-Net architecture are not residual
connections in the strict sense, the two concepts are related. In U-Net, skip connections
concatenate information from the encoder to the decoder, rather than adding the input

of a block to its output. They also facilitate information propagation through the
network, address the vanishing gradient problem, and help to preserve fine details that
might be lost during the downsampling process.

2.4. Training paradigms
With the rapid development of deep neural networks and the variety of their
possible applications, several advanced training paradigms have emerged. The choice of
paradigm depends strongly on the target problem, as well as on the availability and
quality of the training data.
2.4.1. Supervised learning
Supervised learning is the classical and most used way of training neural networks.
It relies on using large high-quality data sets, where each input 𝑥𝑖 is associated with a
paired ground truth 𝑦𝑖 . The objective of the network is to learn a mapping 𝑓𝜃 : 𝑥 → 𝑦 that
minimizes a loss function depending on the task. Supervised learning performs well
when large, high-quality labeled data sets are available. However, such data sets are not
always accessible, which can limit the applicability of these approaches. Typical
applications of supervised learning include image classification, object detection or
image restoration and reconstruction.
In ET, the use of supervised learning can be challenging because experimental
specimens are highly diverse in shape, structure, and acquisition conditions. Therefore,
it might be complex to create a large training data set required to use supervised
approaches.
2.4.2. Unsupervised learning
In unsupervised learning, the model receives only input data, without any groundtruth labels, and must learn useful or underlying representations on its own. The
objective is not to predict labels, but rather for the model to identify and capture the
hidden distribution of the data. Unsupervised learning is particularly efficient when highquality training data sets are unavailable or hard to obtain. It can typically be used for
clustering, feature extraction or data generation.
46
2.4.3. Self-supervised learning
Self-supervised learning is a paradigm that can be seen as the intersection between
supervised and unsupervised learning. Rather than relying on labeled data, selfsupervised approaches generate implicit labels from the unstructured data themselves.
The objective is to learn meaningful representations without requiring external
annotation or labeling. It is particularly efficient when large amount of unlabeled data
without associated reference are available. For example, it has been especially
successful in natural language processing but is also used in image processing.
2.5. Modern deep learning for inverse problems
This section provides a brief overview of several modern DL approaches for inverse
problems. These methods were considered in the context of this thesis, even if they
were not all ultimately used in the proposed ET framework. They illustrate the diversity
of DL-based strategies for image reconstruction and restoration.
2.5.1. Deep image prior
Deep Image Prior (DIP) is an approach introduced for image restoration without
requiring any additional training data beyond the image itself (132). The core idea is that
a randomly initialized neural network and its architecture can act as an implicit prior for
solving inverse problems. The method relies on the fact that CNNs tend to generate
more easily images that are smooth and structured rather than random noise.
Conceptually, DIP represents a particular use of neural networks that departs from
the classical DL paradigms. It is often classified as an unsupervised learning approach,
because only a single observation is used as input. However, in contrast to standard
unsupervised methods, DIP does not actually learn a general representation of the data
distribution, but rather optimizes a network directly for a single measurement. For this
reason, it is sometimes defined as a single-instance optimization method.
DIP was originally proposed for tasks such as denoising, super-resolution and
inpainting, and was later extended successfully to several reconstruction problems,
including tomography (101). This is the main approach investigated in this thesis. Its
formulation and practical use are described in more detail in Chapter 4.
One known limitation of DIP is the instability of the optimization process. In practice,
the quality of the results can vary significantly depending on the choice of
hyperparameters. In recent years, several works have proposed replacing standard
convolutional architecture with implicit neural representations (INRs) and coordinatebased MLPs (133,134). While classical DIP reconstructs images on a fixed pixel grid, INRbased approaches learn a continuous mapping from spatial coordinates (𝑥, 𝑦, 𝑧) to
signal intensities. As a result, they can produce reconstructions that are not intrinsically
tied to a fixed resolution and may offer improved efficiency and flexibility.
47
2.5.2. Unrolled networks
Algorithm unrolling, or unfolding, refers to a family of approaches that combine
classical iterative reconstruction algorithms with DL. Traditional neural networks
achieve strong empirical performance but often lack interpretability, while classical
iterative methods are more explicit and physically grounded. The main idea of unrolling
is to take a fixed number of iterations of an optimization algorithm (such as gradient
descent or primal-dual methods) into a sequence of neural network layers.
In such architectures, some components of the original algorithm are replaced by
learnable blocks, often CNNs. Unrolled networks typically alternate between data
consistency steps and learned regularization blocks, combining interpretability and
reconstruction performance. These approaches have been successfully applied to
various imaging techniques. In MRI, for example, physics-based information was
integrated into a primal-dual unrolled scheme to enable accelerated reconstruction
(135). Similarly, unrolled networks were proposed for low-dose CT (129), where CNNsbased priors were embedded into iterative schemes and outperformed classical
methods.
2.5.3. Plug-and-Play priors
Plug-and-Play (PnP) methods constitute another important family of approaches for
inverse problems. Unlike deep unrolling, which generally requires end-to-end
supervised training, PnP methods incorporate a pretrained denoiser in place of a
regularization operator within a classical iterative reconstruction algorithm.
The key advantage of PnP methods is that the denoiser is trained independently of
the final inverse problem. This provides strong flexibility, as the same denoiser can
potentially be reused for different acquisitions settings without retraining. These
approaches have been successfully used for MRI applications such as in (136) for highly
accelerated non-cartesian reconstruction, in (137) where a denoiser was replaced by an
artefact removal network, or in CT (138), where a restoration network was incorporated
into PnP scheme for sparse-view reconstruction.
2.5.4. Diffusion models
Diffusion models have recently emerged as powerful generative priors for inverse
imaging problems, achieving state-of-the-art performance in many applications. Their
core idea is to learn complex data distributions through an iterative denoising process.
A forward diffusion process gradually degrades a clean image with gaussian noise until
it becomes pure noise, while a reverse process is learned by a neural network to
progressively remove this noise.
Because this reverse process involves repeating denoising operations, pretrained
diffusion models encode a strong prior over clean images. Originally developed for
48
image synthesis and video generation, diffusion models are now being adapted to a wide
range of inverse problems. In RePaint (139), for example, the standard diffusion process
was modified for image inpainting by incorporating a mask during the process. In (140),
diffusion models were applied to limited angle CT reconstruction from only one third of
the full angular range (60°), using conditionally trained models combined with physical
forward models to recover fine details in the reconstructions.
Diffusion models have been used as pretrained priors in zero-shot settings (141).
These methods are conceptually related to DIP. In both cases, the inverse problem is
solved without training a task-specific supervised network for the degradation under
consideration. While DIP relies on the implicit prior induced by the untrained neural
network optimized directly on the data, zero-shot diffusion methods rely on a diffusion
model prior pretrained over clean images. The model is then incorporated only at
inference time, enabling the same pretrained diffusion prior to be easily applied to
multiple unrelated inverse problems.
Recent work has also extended diffusion-based priors to tomographic reconstruction
(142). In particular, scalable 3D diffusion priors have been proposed for CT
reconstruction using local 3D patches, showing that diffusion models can be adapted to
high-resolution 3D tomographic reconstruction.
2.5.5. Conclusion on the presented approaches
In this work, we decided to focus primarily on the DIP technique, which is well suited
for ET applications. In particular, DIP offers several advantages. First, it does not require
any external training data, which makes it particularly attractive for ET applications.
Then, it is a versatile method applicable to a variety of specimens and acquisition
conditions independently, with very few changes in the optimization process. Finally,
even if the optimization must be performed separately for each reconstruction, it is
easily applicable for 3D volumetric data observed in ET.
In contrast, the other DL approaches presented above appear less adapted for the
objectives of this work. Unrolled networks generally require supervised training,
therefore depending on the availability of high-quality training data, which is a major
limitation in ET. Plug-and-Play methods are flexible, but they rely on pretrained
denoisers, whose training might require specific data and adaptation to the target
degradation in each reconstruction scenario. Diffusion models have presented strong
results in reconstruction problems, but they remain computationally expensive,
especially for 3D applications. In addition, their application to ET is less straightforward
than other approaches.
Overall, the choice of DIP in this work is motivated by its training-free nature, its
versatility, and its simplicity of use, making it a perfect candidate for ET applications.
49
2.6. Conclusion
This chapter introduced the main concepts of DL required for the methods
developed in this thesis, from basic components of neural networks to several modern
strategies for inverse problems. Among these methods, DIP occupies a central place in
our work. By exploiting implicit regularization by a neural network, DIP makes it possible
to reconstruct high-quality images without large training data sets. In the context of ET,
this constitutes a major advantage. For this reason, both a supervised restoration
strategy and a DIP-based reconstruction approach are investigated in this thesis, in order
to address a broad range of ET scenarios.
It is also worth noting that recent representations such as INRs may offer promising
perspectives beyond conventional CNN-based DIP approaches, notably in terms of
flexibility and computational efficiency. More generally, the methods briefly reviewed
in this chapter illustrate how DL has moved progressively from post-processing-based
approaches to powerful reconstruction frameworks, which incorporate learned or
implicit complex priors. The concepts introduced here are central to the simulation
results presented in Chapter 4 and experimental results presented in Chapter 5.

3.2. Acquisition in the transmission electron microscope
TEMs are versatile instruments capable of retrieving morphological, structural or
chemical information down to the atomic scale, as introduced in Chapter 1. Until the
early 20th century, the maximum achievable resolution was limited by the wavelength
of visible light. In 1924, De Broglie presented his seminal works on the wave-particle
duality, demonstrating that electrons are associated with a wavelength being inversely
proportional to their momentum. This breakthrough paved the way for the
development of high-resolution microscopes based on the use of highly accelerated
electrons, whose small wavelengths enable the potential for imaging beyond the limits
of light microscopy. The first TEM was presented in 1932 by Knoll and Ruska, and sublight resolution was achieved the following year (146).
54
Tomography in the TEM relies on the fundamental projection requirement, which
states that the signal in each projection must vary monotonically with a physical
property of the object to be suitable for the tomographic reconstruction.
Electron-matter interactions in the TEM are central to image formation and to
specimen characterization. These interactions are at the basis of several physical
phenomena that can be used for imaging and for ET, while ensuring the fulfillment of
the projection requirement. This section first provides an overview of TEM operation and
of the electron-matter interactions responsible for image formation. Then, it describes
the main operating modes of the TEM. Finally, a discussion of the key challenges
associated with tomographic acquisition and reconstruction is provided. The objective
of the section is to present a concise description of the TEM while emphasizing the
constrains and limitations relevant to ET (10,143,144).
3.2.1. Imaging with electrons
As mentioned previously, by accelerating electrons to sufficiently high acceleration
voltages, typically 60-300kV in modern TEMS, their associated wavelengths become
extremely small, theoretically allowing spatial resolutions down to the picometer, below
interatomic distance. In practice, however, the achievable resolution is not limited by
the wavelength of the electrons, but by the instrumental factors, including lenses,
mechanical stability, source coherence and detector performance. The electron optical
system, composed of condenser lenses, objectives lenses, projector lenses and
apertures, plays a central role in beam shaping and image formation (147).
An additional advantage of using electrons lies in the variety of interactions
occurring between the highly accelerated electron beam and the specimen. A simplified
overview of these electron-matter interactions is presented in Figure 3:5. Electrons
transmitted through the sample may undergo elastic scattering (without energy loss) or
inelastic scattering (with energy loss). These interactions generate multiple signals,
which can be exploited for specimen characterization in the TEM. Therefore, TEM
experiments make possible the observation of a range of different signals, giving access
to structural or chemical information, depending on the imaging mode and detector
configuration. With many of these signals being able to satisfy the projection
requirement, they are at the basis of ET experiments.

3.2.2. TEM imaging modes
Modern TEMs commonly support two principal operating modes: conventional
transmission electron microscope (CTEM) and scanning transmission electron
microscope (STEM). The fundamental difference between these modes lies in the image
formation process. In CTEM, a parallel electron beam illuminates the sample, and the
image is directly formed through the optical system from the transmitted electrons. In
STEM, a convergent electron beam is scanned across the specimen and signals are
recorded pixel by pixel by the detectors. The STEM operating mode is the primary
acquisition technique used in this work.
3.2.2.1.
CTEM Mode
In CTEM mode, the microscope operates in an analogous manner to an optical
microscope. The parallel electron beam, generated by the electron gun at the top of the
column and shaped by the condenser system, is directed through the sample. The
transmitted electrons collected by the objective lens form an image magnified by
projector lenses before being recorded on a camera or screen. This is historically the
first operating mode of the TEM (2).
However, the CTEM technique exhibits some limitations for tomography, for
example the projection requirement is not fulfilled for strongly scattering or crystalline
specimens. In such cases, diffraction contrast causes the image intensities to not present
monotonic dependence of the specimen mass or thickness, with the transmitted
intensity varying strongly with the orientation of the sample. In addition, CTEM images
offer only a low sensitivity to the chemical nature of the samples.
Despite these limitations, CTEM is still dominant for non-crystalline samples and
weakly scattering specimens, mostly seen in life sciences, where the dose efficiency is
an advantage for beam sensitive specimens (10). It is generally accepted that CTEM is
56
capable of accurately reconstructing the exterior morphology of homogeneous objects,
but remain less accurate for their internal features due to diffraction effects (148,149).
3.2.2.2.
STEM Mode
In the STEM acquisition mode, the incident electron beam is focused into a
convergent beam and scans the surface of the sample, while images are acquired pixel
by pixel using the different detectors placed along the column. Figure 3:6 (150) presents
the different detectors and components associated with STEM imaging. With its pointby-point acquisition, the STEM mode is adequate to acquire simultaneous signals
containing structural or spectroscopic information on the sample, making it of interest
for ET applications.
Figure 3:6: Schematic representation of a Scanning Transmission Electron Microscope.
Among the most used detectors are the bright-field (BF) and dark-field detectors
that include ADF and HAADF. The main difference between these detectors lies in the
scattering angles of the signals they collect. In materials sciences, STEM-HAADF imaging
is the most used method because high-angle scattered electrons are mostly incoherently
scattered, producing images with intensities linked to specimen thickness and atomic
number, and less affected by diffraction contrast (151). As a result, STEM-HAADF images
generally satisfy the projection requirement to a good approximation and are therefore
relatively straightforward to interpret. This imaging mode, commonly referred to as Z57
contrast imaging, is particularly efficient for distinguishing between materials with
sufficiently different atomic number, even without spectroscopic analysis.
3.2.3. Analytical electron tomography
STEM mode also has the advantage of being used for the production of images
sensitive to the chemical composition and structural properties of the sample. This is
possible due to the electron-matter interactions, described previously in Figure 3:5. In
particular, chemically sensitive signals arise from inelastic interactions between the
incident electron beam and the atoms of the specimen.
In the inelastic interactions, part of the energy of the electrons of the beam is
transmitted to electrons in the sample, making it pass to another excited state. These
interactions are schematically represented on Figure 3:7 (152). This inelastic excitation
leaves a vacancy which is filled by the relaxation of an electron of higher energy level. In
this process, a photon of energy level fixed by the difference between the initial and
final transition level is emitted. These events generate two linked signals that are able
to be processed in AET:

Both STEM-EDX and STEM-EELS techniques generate multidimensional

acquisitions of spectral data at each tilt angle and pixel position with these modes, it is

An application of both methods applied to different tomographic acquisition scenarios

3.2.3.1.
EELS
During inelastic scattering, a portion of the energy of an electron from the incident
beam is transmitted to an electron in an atom of the specimen. These energy losses are
measured by dispersing the transmitted electrons in energy using a spectrometer
positioned below the sample, as illustrated in Figure 3:6. The spectrometer is made of
an electromagnetic prism, for energy dispersion of the beam, optical lenses and a digital
camera (154).
For a reliable interpretation of EELS spectrum, it is required to minimize the multiple
inelastic scattering through the sample. This requires for the specimens to be sufficiently
thin, with acceptable thickness depending of the material, the observed elements as
well as the accelerating voltage of the electrons (155).
EELS analysis provides the access to several properties such as the specimen
thickness, elemental composition and chemical environment. The spectrum is usually
divided into two regions: the Low-Loss region, up to 50 eV, associated with the valence
electrons, and the Core-Loss region, corresponding to high energy losses and to innershell transitions.
Extracting valuable quantitative information from EELS spectra require several
processing steps (background subtraction, deconvolution, curve fitting) that can be
performed using dedicated software, such as Gatan Microscopy Suite (GMS) or using
Python libraries such as Hyperspy (156).
3.2.3.2.
EDX
During inelastic scattering, an incident electron can transfer energy to an atom in
the specimen, creating a vacancy. When this vacancy is filled by an electron from a
higher energy level, a characteristic x-ray might be emitted. Analysis of these
characteristic x-rays provides information on the chemical composition of the sample.
The are collected using one or several x-ray detectors that are placed within the
microscope column, as shown in Figure 3:6.
In EDX spectra, peak intensities are associated with the number of atoms interacting
with the electron beam and x-rays emitted while it passes through the sample, allowing
for elemental quantification. However, the efficiency of x-ray detection is a limiting
factor of the technique. It strongly depends on detector position, geometry and
technology. The x-ray generation relies on a random physical process, and even with
multiple detectors, only a fraction of the emitted x-rays is collected. As a result, EDX
59
spectrums are affected by significant Poisson noise, due to the nature of the experiment
(157).
The EDX spectrums consist of several discrete peaks at characteristic energies
corresponding to specific electronic transitions. Elemental peaks extraction and
quantification can be performed using different methods such as with Python libraries
like Hyperspy, or with manufacturer-provided software such as Esprit from Brucker or
Velox from Thermo Fisher.
3.2.4. Challenges for the acquisition and reconstruction
In the context of the present work, our primary focus lies in the reconstructed
volumes, rather than the acquisition process itself. Even if the principle of tomography
is relatively simple to understand in theory, its application to TEM is not straightforward
and several challenges arise during the acquisition, that find repercussions in the
reconstruction process. These limitations include the accessible angular range, the
electron dose received by the sample and the misalignment of the projections. Because
tomographic reconstruction is the central focus of this work, taking these challenges
into account is crucial for the processing of our data. In particular, this work focuses on
limitations related to angular range, sparsity of the acquisition, and electron dose. The
question of alignment is not addressed here because reliable methods exist. This section
presents more thoroughly the causes of these different limitations and their impact on
the tomographic reconstructions.
3.2.4.1.
Electron dose, tilt increment and acquisition time
By design, a tomographic acquisition requires the recording of projections over a
large tilt range. Compared to a conventional 2D STEM acquisition, it implies an increase
in both the acquisition time and the electron dose received by the specimen. In materials
science, samples are commonly, though not always, more beam-resistant than, for
example, biological specimens. For morphological imaging, such as STEM-HAADF,
acquiring a 2048 by 2048 pixels image typically takes from a few seconds to a few
minutes.
In contrast, spectroscopic imaging (EDX and EELS) is more challenging because a full
spectrum must be acquired at each pixel in the image, therefore increasing the
acquisition time drastically. For instance, a 100 by 100 pixels STEM-EELS map may
require 15 minutes to acquire. To limit the acquisition time and the accumulated
electron dose, it is common to increase the tilt increment, from a few degrees, to 5°, 10°
or even 15°, or to decrease the dwell time. Figure 3:8 presents an example of a
reconstruction obtained with a simulated 10° tilt increment where undersampling
artefacts are visible. Fine details in the central region are lost, and streak artefacts
appear as bright lines due to the insufficient number of projections.
60
Figure 3:8: Example of a sparse acquisition with a tilt increment of 10° (left), corresponding
reconstruction (middle) and reference high-quality reconstruction (right). Only a small amount of the
projections is available in this scenario. It leads to the presence of artefacts in the SIRT reconstruction.
However, for spectroscopic data, in addition to increasing the tilt increment,
reducing the acquisition time leads to very noisy projections which further degrade the
quality of the tomographic reconstructions. In practice, determining practical dose limits
for ET is challenging, because no universal quantitative method exists to assess the
extent of radiation damage and the sensitivity to beam irradiation depends on the
nature of the material under investigation (3). Therefore, selecting appropriate
acquisition parameters requires a careful compromise between the minimization of the
electron dose and the high quality of the projections, to guarantee a reliable
tomographic reconstruction. Ultimately, this compromise depends on the objective of
the experiment and the nature and scale of the features of interest to observe.
3.2.4.2.
Angular range
An important challenge in ET arises from the physical constraints imposed by both
the TEM column and the specimen geometry. As illustrated in Figure 3:9 (adapted from
(158)), the tilt range is limited by possible collisions with the surrounding components
of the microscope, such as the objective lens polepieces. The gap in the chamber is
typically around 2-5 mm and, combined with the geometrical constraint of the sample
holder, the specimen cannot be freely rotated over the full 180° angular range ideally
required for single-axis tomography, and projections are therefore often acquired over
a reduced range, typically on the order of ±60° to ±70° (10).
61
Figure 3:9: Representation of the limitation in the rotation of the sample in the TEM. The rotation is
physically limited by the geometry of the TEM.
In addition to the limited angular range, shadowing effects can occur for thicker slablike specimens or supporting grids. At high tilt angles, the increased thickness of the
effective projected specimen might generate less usable projections due to image
blurring or beam broadening leading to a loss of resolution in STEM. Therefore, the
acquisition process requires an inevitable compromise between maximization of the
effective angular range and occlusion of the sample while tilting it.
As mentioned in Chapter 1, when the complete angular range is not accessible, part
of the information in the Fourier domain is missing, corresponding to the non-acquired
projection angles. In real space, this leads to elongation artefacts in the reconstructions,
commonly referred to as missing wedge artefacts (Figure 3:10).
Figure 3:10: Example of an acquisition with a limited angular range of ±60° (left). The projections before
-60° and after +60° are missing. This leads to degradations in the reconstruction (center) in the form of
horizontal MW artefacts compared to the reference high-quality reconstruction (right)
In this context, the interest in needle-shaped specimens mounted on on-axis
tomography holders which prevent MW artefacts has grown (49,57,58,159). However,
62
needle preparation is not always possible, depending on the material, region of interest
and experimental conditions. For example, in situ sample holders are not adapted for
needle-shaped samples.
In this work, we focus on the reconstructions obtained from limited-angle
acquisitions, such as samples prepared as FIB lamellae, where shadowing effects or MW
are inevitable. Therefore, developing strategies capable of reducing these effects is a
central objective of our work.
3.2.4.3.
Alignment
Several reasons can lead to a misalignment of the projections during the acquisition
process, including mechanical instabilities in the TEM that can induce minor shift of the
specimen during tilting or inaccuracies in the tilt mechanism that might cause deviations
in the tilt angle. As a result, the rotation axis of the projection series might be
inaccurately defined, leading to degradation in the reconstructions, as disclosed in
Figure 3:11.
Figure 3:11: Example of a misaligned sinogram (left), the corresponding reconstruction (center) and highquality reference (right).
Correcting these misalignments generally involves two separate steps. The first
consists in correcting the translation between the projections, to ensure that they are
rightfully aligned with one another. The second step consists in compensating for the
misalignment of the tilt axis and may require applying a shift and tilt of the entire tilt
series at once.
Numerous alignment methods have been developed to perform the alignment of
the projections, depending on the specimens and the complexity of the misalignment.
The two predominant methods are marker-based alignment (160) and cross-correlation
(161,162). The alignment by markers, using easily identifiable gold markers is frequently
employed in the biological sciences, but is not always practical, especially in materials
science applications where introducing markers might be difficult. Cross-correlation
methods estimate a relative shift between consecutive projections by computing their
63
cross-correlation and identifying their maximum values. However, the approach might
not be exact, especially for slab-like or low-contrast specimens.
A procedure specially adapted for needle-shaped samples was proposed recently
(163). This approach combines cross-correlation between neighboring projections, a
common line algorithm to compute the precise shift correction along the tilt axis
direction, and intermediate reconstructions to determine the correction perpendicular
to the tilt axis. This automatic approach has been shown to be fast, very efficient and
produces reliable alignments for various data sets. It is also available through a userfriendly software interface.
Beyond its original application to needle-shaped specimens, it has also
demonstrated good performance for correcting the relative shifts between projections
in other types of samples, which motivated its use for the alignment of the data sets
studied in Chapters 4 and 5. However, the correction of the tilt-axis position itself
appears to be less reliable for non-needle-shaped specimens. Therefore, in this work,
the correction of the tilt-axis shift was performed manually using the software interface.
3.3. Reconstruction techniques
Over the years, several techniques have been developed to reconstruct a 3D volume
from a set of 2D projections in the different fields that rely on tomography, including ET
or X-ray CT. The existing techniques can be separated into two different categories,
analytical and algebraic approaches.
3.3.1. Analytical approaches
For several years, analytical reconstruction techniques have been the standard
approach employed by the community. These methods can be split into two categories:
the Fourier methods and the approaches based on the backprojection.
3.3.1.1.
Fourier techniques
The simplest approach of performing tomographic reconstruction would be to utilize
the central-slice theorem by performing the 1D Fourier transform of each projection and
then applying the inverse 2D Fourier transform to recover the 2D object. This method is
straightforward in principle but it requires an additional interpolation or gridding step
to map the known 1D Fourier values onto the cartesian grid of the 2D Fourier space. This
is a challenging process and might lead to degradations in the reconstructions (10).
Therefore, this process has not generally been used in ET. More advanced methods
based on sophisticated gridding strategies or Fourier domain formulations have been
developed, but these approaches are not used in this work (164–167).

3.3.3.2.
Wavelet based regularization
The wavelet transform is widely used in signal processing to obtain sparse
representations of signals and to perform multiscale analysis. Wavelets are especially
well suited to represent information on a sparse basis and have been successfully
applied to several inverse problems such as for MRI reconstruction. A detailed
introduction on wavelet representations can be found in (186).
The wavelet transform decomposes a signal or image into approximations at
multiple scales by using scaled and translated version of a mother wavelet. Each
daughter wavelet is a compressed or expanded version of the mother wavelet, which is
used to extract high-frequency details between consecutive levels of approximation.
Through iterative decomposition of signals, their representations contain both general
structural information and sharp features.
In CS-based ET reconstruction, wavelet regularization promotes sparsity in the
wavelet domain. In practice, several wavelet families can be used, including Haar,
Daubechies or biorthogonal wavelets, with the appropriate choice depending on prior
knowledge of the structure of the objects to reconstruct. For example, the Haar wavelet
behaves similarly to a multiscale gradient and is well suited for piecewise constant
objects, in an analogous way to TV regularization. Biorthogonal wavelets are appropriate
to represent slightly anisotropic features. For strongly anisotropic features, transforms
based on anisotropic basis such as ridgelets, curvelets or shearlets have proved to
perform better reconstructions (187).
Overall, CS approaches based on TV or wavelet regularization can improve the
quality of the reconstruction in challenging scenarios, typically from sparse acquisitions
or when the electron dose is slightly reduced. However, they might generate
oversmoothed reconstructions when the tilt increment is too high and if too much noise
is present. Moreover, these methods do not compensate for large MW artefacts often
observed in tomographic acquisitions (75,80,188).

The classical U-Net architecture consists of four encoding blocks, each of them

of feature channels (16, 32, 64 and 128) respectively followed by ReLU activation

encoding path to progressively reduce spatial resolution.
In the decoding path, 2 × 2 upsampling operations are performed to recover the
spatial resolution. Skip connections are added between corresponding encoding and
decoding blocks, allowing the feature maps from the encoder to be concatenated with
those of the decoder. Finally, two single convolutional layers with 1 × 1 kernel and linear
activation are added at the output to recover the final image.

too many iterations cause the network to progressively fit noise and artefacts, ultimately
resulting in degraded images. This behavior happens because the network initially
captures the dominant structure and overall shape of the object, and only later begins
to model finer-scale features, such as noise, as the optimization proceeds.
Therefore, the optimal stopping point must be adapted to each reconstruction
scenario and data set. In practice in this work, for the reconstruction of complex
experimental specimens (such as those from spectroscopic acquisitions), the network is
intentionally run for a large number of iterations while saving intermediate
reconstructions. After optimization, both the evolution of the loss function and the
intermediate reconstructions are inspected to select the most visually accurate
reconstruction. In contrast, for simpler reconstructions with smoother optimization
behavior (such as grayscale piecewise-constant data), the reconstructions
corresponding to the minimal loss values are selected.

acquisition scenario. However, it remains difficult to assert that one method is
significantly outperforming the other across all situations.
Both supervised restoration and DIP reconstruction demonstrate strong similar
performance, with slight differences. Their primary differences lie in their applicability
and technical constraints. The supervised approach requires a training phase with a
training data set, on the order of tens of minutes on a modern GPU, after which it can
be quasi instantly applied to similar data sets with minimal parameter tuning. In
contrast, the DIP approach is more versatile and does not require additional data, but it
demands higher computational cost and manual hyperparameter tuning. Moreover,
optimization must be performed independently for each image, typically requiring
several minutes per reconstruction.
86
It is also important to remember that the supervised approach benefits from access
to hundreds of training examples, whereas DIP relies solely on the single degraded
image being reconstructed. In this sense, the comparisons made in this section should
be viewed in a practical perspective. It highlights the interest of the supervised
restoration for repeated applications with similar types of data. In the meantime, it
demonstrates the effectiveness of DIP and its strong potential for ET reconstruction.
Therefore, supervised restoration may be particularly suitable for repeatable
applications to data sets sharing similar features and shapes, while DIP is better suited
for unique or highly challenging reconstruction problems that necessitate manual fine
tuning. In particular, as data complexity increases, for example in multidimensional
spectroscopic acquisitions, generating realistic simulation data and training robust
supervised models becomes increasingly difficult. In such cases, the flexibility of DIP
might become a significant advantage.
The following section focuses more thoroughly on the DIP approach, as well as its
multi-channel version presented above and its application to different acquisition
scenarios. In particular, we investigate the influence of sparsity, noise, and limited
angular range on a multidimensional simulated data set more representative of ET
spectroscopic experiments.
4.2.3. Evaluation of DIP for the reconstruction of simulated core-shell
nanocubes
After comparing the two DL-based approaches, we came to the conclusion that both
techniques perform relatively similarly, but that DIP is more versatile, and better suited
to complex ET scenarios. In this section, we apply DIP to a new simulated data set
consisting of two volumes, in a similar manner to spectroscopic acquisitions. These two
volumes are reconstructed under several simulated acquisition scenarios combining
noise and limited number or projections (MW and sparsity artefacts). Both the classical
DIP approach, and the multi-channel version are applied to this data set, with the aim
of assessing the faithfulness of DIP reconstructions under various challenging conditions.
In the work of (80), mentioned in Chapter 1, limitations of CS-based methods for ET
reconstruction were highlighted under different scenarios by combining various angular

using the DIP technique.

4.2.3.2.
Implementation details of the techniques
For the reconstruction of the phantom data, we compare the classical reconstruction
techniques (SIRT and CS-TV) with the single-channel DIP-TV reconstruction and the
multi-channel DIP-TV reconstruction. The parameters for each method were manually
fined-tuned for every acquisition scenario.
The SIRT and CS-TV reconstructions are performed using 50 iterations. A positivity
constraint is applied in the case of SIRT and the TV regularization parameter is manually
optimized for each scenario, with optimal values typically found around 10-2.
Both DIP approaches are implemented using U-Net architectures. For the singlechannel reconstructions, the same U-Net architecture as the one used for the particles
reconstruction using DIP is adopted, consisting of 4 encoding and decoding blocks with
numbers of channels (16, 32, 64 and 128) respectively. For the multi-channel
reconstruction, the network depth is increased to 5 blocks with (16, 32, 64, 128 and 128)
channels. This deeper architecture is adopted to accommodate for the increased
complexity of the joint reconstruction process.
In both DIP versions, the additive noise regularization proposed in the original DIP
work is removed, as preliminary results showed that it introduces noise and artefacts in
the reconstructions. A total of 500 iterations is performed for each acquisition scenario.
The TV regularization weights are manually fine-tuned, with optimal values found
around 10-9.
To assess the quality of the reconstructions, both qualitative and quantitative
evaluations are conducted. Visually, we focus on specific structural features, including
the correction of MW elongations, the suppression of sparsity related artefacts, and the
reduction of noise. Quantitatively, for the first scenario (various angular ranges and tilt
increments), the SSIM metrics are computed to evaluate the structural similarity in the
reconstructed volumes. For the scenario involving different noise levels, PSNR is
additionally computed to quantify the denoising performance. The resulting metrics are
visualized in graphs showing their evolution as function of the decreasing angular ranges
and increasing noise levels.

4.3. Limitations of the DIP technique and of the multi-channel approach
Despite the promising performance of DIP ET reconstruction, several limitations
were identified during its application to simulated data.
97
4.3.1. Optimization stability and hyperparameters sensitivity
A first limitation concerns the stability of the optimization process. As discussed
earlier, the quality of the reconstructions strongly depends on the choice of the network
architecture and of several hyperparameters, including the number of iterations, the
network size and depth, and the regularization weight of the TV regularization. There is
no theoretical guarantee that the optimization will reliably converge to the most faithful
reconstruction. Therefore, final manual fine-tuning is often required.
Among these hyperparameters, the number of iterations is the most critical. As
already observed in the original DIP work, too few iterations lead to incomplete and
poorly defined reconstructions, whereas too many iterations progressively make the
network fit noise in the data. In practice, this issue is partially addressed by saving
intermediate reconstructions during the optimization and selecting the one that seems
to appear the most faithful.
The choice of the network architecture also has a significant impact. In this work, the
U-Net is used with four depth levels, similar to the original DIP article. The most
important parameter seems to be the number of features maps, the number of
parameters in the network. If it is too small, the reconstruction may lack fine details and
sharp features. Increasing its size improve its capability to generate trustworthy larger
volumes. However, this also increases the computational cost and make the
optimization process slower. Therefore, the network size is chosen as a compromise
between best reconstruction quality and lower computation time.
The TV regularization weight plays a role similar than for CS reconstruction methods.
If it is too small, it has little effect on the reconstruction. If it is too large, it makes
suppresses important sharp details. Its value is adapted to the acquisition scenario and
to the structure of interest.
Several strategies have been proposed in the literature to mitigate these issues,
including early stopping criteria (195) and joint optimization of the input noise alongside
the network parameters (196). While theoretically appealing, our experience suggests
that these approaches do not consistently yield significant improvements for ET
reconstructions compared to careful hyperparameter tuning of the standard DIP
framework. Therefore, the robustness and stability of DIP remain practical concerns that
must be taken into account when using the method.
As a result, the robustness of DIP remains a practical concern, particularly for
complex data sets reconstruction.
4.3.2. Limitation of the multi-channel approach
A second limitation specifically concerns the multi-channel DIP approach. Although
exploiting correlations between multiple volumes can improve the quality of

reconstruction, this strategy might also introduce errors in some generated volumes. To
illustrate this, we simulated a porous core-shell structure with a single pore placed at
different positions within the shell. The simulated acquisition was performed with a
limited angular range of ±70° with 17.5° tilt increment, matching the acquisition
conditions of the experimental data presented in Chapter 5.

In the first case (first row), classical reconstruction techniques recover the pore

These errors seem to arise from the way the multi-channel reconstruction is
formulated. Because all channels are reconstructed simultaneously using the same
network weights and the same input noise, the optimization tends to favor
representations that are common to the different volumes. As a result, the network
might focus mainly on the overall shared structure of the channels, with less
consideration to channel-specific structure. In such situations, small details present in
one volume only might be attenuated or misinterpreted.
Overall, these observations indicate that the single-channel DIP approach is more
reliable than the multi-channel formulation for preserving the structure of the
reconstructed volumes. While the multi-channel DIP can be highly effective to produce
clean reconstructions, especially when strong correlation exists between channels, it
must be applied with caution. When the structure of the object is largely unknown, and
the correlation between channels is not certain, the single-channel approach might
provide more trustworthy reconstructions.
4.4. Conclusion
In this chapter, several approaches were investigated for the reconstruction and
restoration of simulated ET data acquired under simulated degraded conditions. A
supervised DL-based restoration method was introduced for post-processing classical
reconstruction when training data are accessible. In parallel, an unsupervised
reconstruction approach based on the DIP framework was proposed to directly
reconstruct volumes from their sinograms, without the need for training data. The DIP
approach was further extended to multi-channel reconstructions, as commonly
encountered in spectroscopic ET acquisitions.
These methods were evaluated on multiple simulated phantom data sets with
varying geometries, noise levels, angular ranges, and tilt increments. The results
demonstrated that both DL-based approaches perform robustly across a wide range of
challenging scenarios. In addition, the DIP-based methods performed well for the
reconstruction of correlated multidimensional data sets.
While supervised restoration approaches offer rapid restoration for known feature
distributions, DIP provides greater flexibility for unknown geometry, at the price of a
higher computational cost and with increased sensitivity to hyperparameter fine tuning,
but without requiring any prior data or assumptions about the specimen, making it of
great use for challenging reconstructions of specimens in ET. Nevertheless, caution is
required as neural networks-based priors can introduce over-smoothing or potential
suppression of fine-scale features.
However, the results obtained in this chapter must be interpreted with caution,
given the simplicity of the simulated data. The particles and nanocubes considered here
were based on relatively simple piecewise-constant shapes, with simple geometries or
100
internal structures, and no intensity gradients or overlapping phases. Such simplified
objects provide a useful way for comparison of the methods and quantitative evaluation,
but they do not fully reflect the complexity of real specimens. Therefore, the strong
performance observed here should be regarded as a first validation of the proposed
approaches rather than as a guarantee of their ability to generalize to any experimental
data. In particular, real specimens may contain more complex morphologies,
heterogeneous structures and additional sources of degradation that are not fully
represented in the simulations.
In the next chapter, both DL-based approaches will be applied to real experimental
data sets, including EDX and EELS data acquired under challenging conditions, to further
asses their performance in real experimental conditions.

5.1.5. Discussion
As expected, and consistently with the observations made on simulated particle
data, the DL-based approaches investigated in this work outperform the conventional
SIRT and CS-based reconstruction techniques in all examined acquisition scenarios. Both
DL methods provide reconstructions that are visually cleaner and quantitatively more
accurate according to the measurements presented above. The differences become
more particularly noticeable in the second and third scenarios, where the classical
approaches generated reconstructions affected by strong MW artefacts or shape
distortions. Such degradations can prevent reliable segmentation or quantitative
analysis of the reconstructed nanoparticles.
The reconstructions obtained with the DL methods appear significantly cleaner and
more reliable. Both the supervised restoration technique and the unsupervised DIPbased reconstruction produce volumes of comparable image quality. Nevertheless, both
approaches show some small limitations when reconstructing nanoparticles that are
very close to each other in the hardest acquisition cases. The supervised approach shows
reduced performance in the sparse acquisition scenario (10° tilt increment), where some
inaccuracies and slightly larger equivalent diameter are observed. This behavior likely
originates from the fact that the network is trained and applied on SIRT reconstructions,
which might already contain strong degradations. One possible improvement would be
to train the network using CS-based reconstructions exhibiting less artefacts as inputs.
In addition, the training data are simulated and may not perfectly reproduce the
characteristics of the experimental data, particularly regarding the noise distribution,
which was not modeled during data generation. For the DIP-based reconstructions,
limitations appear mainly in the most restricted angular range scenario (±30° angular
range), where neighboring nanoparticles are sometimes merged due to the large MW.
Although the MW artefacts are globally reduced, the lack of information in the
projections might be the reason that makes it difficult for the network to fully recover
accurately these nanoparticles
Overall, conclusions similar to those obtained on the simulated data can be drawn.
For this relatively simple application, involving nanoparticles with piecewise constant
shapes and low noise levels, both DL methods achieve comparable performance. The
supervised restoration approach can be easily reused for similar data sets once the
network has been trained, and retraining the model for similar scenarios or data is
feasible because the simulation data can be generated. However, this approach would
be less reliable when applied to data that differ significantly from the training
distribution.
112
In contrast, the DIP approach offers great flexibility because it does not require
additional training data. This makes it possibly more suitable for a wide variety of data
sets with different structures, shapes and acquisition conditions, including specimens
observed through analytical ET. However, it is important to note that DIP-based
techniques in their current implementation remain sensitive to the choice of
hyperparameters and require careful fine-tuning as well as appropriate early stopping
criteria during the optimization process.
This section focused on the application of DL methods, including the single-channel
DIP approach presented in Chapter 4, for the reconstruction and restoration of platinum
nanoparticles acquired by HAADF-STEM tomography. The studied samples remain
relatively simple, with limited artefacts, simple geometries and negligible noise in the
acquisition.
In the following section, we focus on the application of the more versatile DIP
reconstruction technique, in both its single-channel and muti-channel versions, for the
reconstruction of spectroscopic tomographic data.
5.2. 3D chemical analysis of PCRAM devices by STEM-EDX tomography
In the previous section, DL-based approaches were applied to the reconstruction
and restoration of platinum nanoparticles using STEM-HAADF tomography under
different acquisition scenarios.
The application of AET remains particularly challenging because spectroscopic
signals are often weak and noisy. These difficulties are especially pronounced in STEMEDX tomography of FIB-prepared lamellae, where large MW artefacts are commonly
combined with the limited signal-to-noise ratio. In addition, the geometry of the lamella
itself can lead to shadowing effect during tilting, degrading the projections acquired at
higher angles. In this context, DIP-based reconstruction techniques are particularly
attracting because they enable high-quality unsupervised reconstruction and allow the
simultaneous reconstruction of multiple volumes using the multi-channel approach
presented previously.
In this section, we apply this approach to the reconstruction of STEM-EDX
tomographic data. We demonstrate that the proposed method can generate highquality reconstructions, even when the electron dose or the number of projections is
reduced. Finally, we perform a quantitative analysis of the chemical elements present in
the reconstructions.
This result section focuses on the reconstruction of phase-change RAM (PCRAM)
devices, which are commonly studied in the field of microelectronics. We being by
describing the specimen and the acquisition conditions, followed by the technical details
of the reconstruction methods and finally the reconstruction results.
113
5.2.1. Presentation of the sample and acquisition conditions
In this section, we study the reconstruction of phase-change materials using STEMEDX. Typical phase-change materials usually consist of combinations of germanium,
antimony and tellurium (Ge-Sb-Te), commonly referred to as GST. These materials are
widely used because of the large resistivity difference between their amorphous and
crystalline phases, as well as their fast transition between the two phases. Several
compositions are possible for this type of device, although GST-based materials are the
most commonly used (197).
During the fabrication process, the material is exposed to a wide range of
temperatures, which can lead to separation of the different phases. In addition, several
compositions exist, and ongoing research focuses on the incorporation of additional
elements to modify the behavior of the PCRAM materials. For these reasons, PCRAM
devices are commonly studied in the TEM to observe the distribution and behavior of
the different elements using analytical techniques such as EDX or EELS.
The sample studied in this work represents a typical example where 3D imaging
techniques are required. Such specimens are often studied using conventional 2D
imaging in the TEM by acquiring a single projection of the object. However, these 2D
observations are insufficient because the different elements within the GST material are
superimposed, which makes their analysis ambiguous and unreliable. Figure 5:6 shows
two projections of the sample acquired at 0° and 40°. In these images, the GST material
appears as a horizontal strip, and no accurate information about the spatial distribution
of the Ge, Sb and Te elements can be obtained from STEM-HAADF images alone. This
illustrates the needs for 3D AET imaging techniques. In addition, the projections
acquired at higher tilt angle show signs of shadowing, leading to superposition of
features of interest and possible variations in intensity.
Figure 5:6: HAADF projections acquired at 0° and 40°. The distribution of Ge, Sb and Te cannot be
distinguished in the STEM-HAADF images, highlighting the need for a tomographic analysis to better
observe the elemental distribution. At 40°, shadowing effects being to appear, leading to a loss of

Ideally, such specimens would be prepared as needle-shaped samples to facilitate
the 3D analysis and prevent MW during the acquisition. This is not always possible and

the lamella, which leads to a relatively large MW, and because AET signals are
intrinsically weak, the data are typically noisy and limited in angular range.
Consequently, the reconstruction of such specimens represents a particularly
challenging scenario in which DIP-based reconstruction techniques may enable
improved reconstruction quality and more reliable chemical quantification.
To provide more details about the specimen studied in the work, in addition to the
GST material, the heater of the PCRAM device, visible as a vertical structure in the

Both STEM-HAADF and STEM-EDX projections of the specimen were acquired
simultaneously over a ±40° angular range with a 5° tilt increment. For the STEM-EDX
acquisition, a spectrum is obtained for each projection and must be further processed.
At each tilt angle, the acquisition consists of 50 consecutive fast low-dose acquisitions,
referred to as frames. These frames are summed to obtain spectra with improved signal
quality. The acquisitions were performed with a pixel size of 1.067 nm. The details of the
spectral processing are discussed in the next section.
5.2.2. Spectral processing
In an EDX tomographic acquisition, the data consist of a 1D spectrum at each pixel
position of the image for every tilt angle. These spectra must be processed in order to
obtain 2D elemental maps (i.e., the projections) for each element, from which 3D
reconstructions can be computed. Several preprocessing steps are required to obtain
reliable elemental maps. An example of the spectrum obtained for our specimen is
shown in Figure 5:7.

As briefly introduced in Chapter 3, EDX spectra consist of several peaks at
characteristic energies corresponding to specific electronic transitions. Their processing
can be performed semi-automatically using Python libraries such as HyperSpy.
Alternatively, it can be done almost automatically with minimal user input using
dedicated software such as Velox (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
We chose to use Velox to extract the 2D elemental maps because of its simplicity
and efficiency. The software provides an automated tool for elemental map extraction
based on automatic background estimation and curve fitting of the characteristic peaks
for each element present in the sample. Thermo Fisher does not provide detailed
information about the internal algorithms used in the software. Nevertheless, the tool
has proven to be reliable to recover elemental maps in a fast and user-friendly manner.

5.2.3. General processing of the projections
After obtaining the elemental maps of the specimen, several preprocessing steps are
required before performing the 3D reconstructions.
Due to an unexpected configuration error during the acquisition, one projection was
acquired with an incorrect pixel size and had to be removed. The projection at +5° was
therefore excluded from the data set.
The main processing step required to perform a high-quality reconstruction is the
alignment of the projections. The software Guidar was used for the alignement
procedure. Because the EDX elemental maps are very noisy, they are difficult to align
directly. Instead, the HAADF projections were used to guide the alignment. In a first step,
the HAADF projections were automatically aligned using Guidar, and the corresponding
alignement matrice was saved. The same alignement matrix was then applied to all EDX
projections. Because the HAADF projections contains significantly less noise and exhibit
117
clear structural features, the software is able to align them reliably, whereas the direct
alignment of the EDX maps is less trustable.
The second step concerns the correction of the tilt axis position. Guidar provides a
manual tilt axis tool in which intermediate reconstructions can be visualized while
shifting the tilt axis pixel by pixel. In our case, the tilt axis had to be manually shifted by
approximately 20 pixels because it was originally misaligned.
To reduce both reconstruction size and the noise level, the projections were
downsampled by a factor of two, reducing their size from 300 × 400 pixels to 150 × 200
pixels. The 0° projections for each element are shown in Figure 5:9 for the different
number of frames considered. In this specimen, the three elements are quite clearly
separated and three different phases are visible. Reducing the number of frames makes
it increasingly difficult to distinguish true elemental signals from noise. When only 10
frames are used, the elemental signals are barely distinct from the noise, making it
difficult to identify the spatial distribution of the elements within the GST material.

5.2.4. Reconstruction process and implementation details
In this section, we describe the reconstruction parameters of the different methods,
followed by the corresponding results. The reconstructions are performed with classical
SIRT and CS-TV approaches as well as both the DIP single-channel and multi-channel
variants. In addition, the impact of the conditions of acquisition is investigated by
performing reconstructions from different numbers of available frames (50, 25 and 10),
as well as by removing half of the projections in order to simulate an increase of the tilt

The reconstruction quality is initially assessed visually on 2D slices in order to
evaluate the efficiency of the different approaches. This is followed by several
quantitative analyses adapted to AET data. In particular, we examine the impact and
correction of MW artefacts in the different acquisition scenarios (frame reduction and
sparse acquisition).
The DIP single-channel and multi-channel reconstruction approaches are applied
and compared with conventional SIRT and CS-TV techniques. The SIRT reconstructions
for each elemental volume are performed with 20 iterations and a positivity constraint.
The CS-TV reconstructions are performed with 50 iterations and a regularization weight
for the TV of around 0.1. The exact values were manually fine-tuned by computing

5.2.5.2.
Elemental quantification of the Ge, Sb and Te elements
Beyond visual inspection, spectroscopic tomography makes possible the
quantitative analysis of elemental distributions.
To quantify the elemental composition and relative proportions of the GST
elements, the Cliff-Lorimer (CL) quantification method was applied using the
implementation available in the HyperSpy library. This method computes the elemental
concentration ratios from the measured x-ray intensities using k-factors (198). The
algorithm receives the reconstructed Ge, Sb and Te volumes as input and it outputs
normalized volumes in which voxel values represent the elemental concentrations
expressed as percentages. The sum of the three elemental concentrations at each voxel
equals 100%.
To restrict the analysis to the quantification of the GST region, a mask was generated
before computing the CL quantifications. To do so, the three elemental volumes
126
generated with the multi-channel DIP approach were summed and an Otsu thresholding
was applied to generate the mask (Figure 5:17). The mask is then applied to every
reconstruction before performing the CL quantification.
Figure 5:17: Mask generated to perform the CL quantifications. The masked in created from the multichannel DIP-TV reconstructions.
Because the mask isolates the GST region, MW artefacts are not visible in the
quantification results. Therefore, the quantification focuses only on the elemental

and rescaled to the original intensity range of the projections. This step ensures that the

Because the elemental distributions in this specimen are relatively well separated,
the differences between the reconstruction techniques remain moderate. Small
variations are observed between the different profiles, but without reference data it is
not possible to determine which reconstruction is the closest to the real elemental
concentrations.
Overall, these results show that the DIP-based reconstructions enable stable and
consistent elemental quantification, even when the acquisition conditions are degraded.
They also demonstrate that DIP methods are reliable to perform quantitative analysis
on the sample while preserving relative intensities between the different elemental
phases.
5.2.6. Discussion
This section presented the reconstruction of STEM-EDX tomographic data using DIPbased approaches. Because no ground-truth reference is available for these data, the
evaluation was mainly performed through qualitative observation of the
reconstructions and by comparing different degraded acquisition scenarios.
Overall, both the single-channel and multi-channel DIP variants outperform classical
techniques. In particular, they are very efficient to prevent MW artefact and reduce the
noise that was visible in the projections. As a result, DIP reconstructions appear more
reliable for interpreting the elemental distribution within such a sample.
Between the two approaches, the multi-channel DIP reconstruction generally
produces smoother volumes, while the single-channel reconstruction tends to retain
slightly more noise, as well as finer details. The influence of acquisition conditions was
also investigated by reducing the number of frames or increasing the tilt increment. The
results indicate that heavily reducing the number of frames seem to have a quite
stronger impact on reconstruction quality than increasing the tilt increment. In
particular, using only 10 frames leads to noticeable degradation in the reconstructions.
Line profile analyses show that both DIP methods produce very similar elemental
distributions and allow consistent quantification of the chemical elements.
Overall, these results demonstrate the potential of DIP-based methods for the
reconstruction of STEM-EDX tomographic data. In particular, they show that it is possible
to reduce the electron dose during acquisition, either by reducing the number of frames
or increasing the tilt increment, while still maintaining reliable reconstruction quality.
130
However, it remains difficult to determine whether the single-channel or multichannel approach is generally superior, as their performance may depend on the
specimen as well as on the objective of the analysis, such as analyzing finer details or
accurately recovering the overall structure of the specimen.
5.3. 3D visualization of oxidation states in an iron oxide core-shell
nanocube by STEM-EELS tomography
In this section, we present reconstruction results obtained with the DIP approach for
STEM-EELS tomographic data. Ten years ago, the first combined ET and EELS acquisition
was performed to study the oxidation states in a FeO / Fe3O4 core-shell nanocube with
nanometric resolution (52). In that work, a tilt series of 36 spectrum images was
acquired from -69° to +67° with a 4° tilt increment. Because severe beam damage
affected the second half of the acquired data and because the specimen had an
approximately cubic symmetry, only the subset of the projections from -69° to 0° was
retained and mirror symmetry was exploited for the reconstruction. The core and shell
volumes were reconstructed using CS-TV-based methods. The 3D corresponding
reconstructions from the original work are reproduced in Figure 5:22. Despite the
limited and noisy data, the reconstructions using CS-TV provided very convincing results.

More recently, projections from the same specimen were reacquired with a more

particularly challenging. In the present work, we investigate the application of DIP-based
methods introduced in the previous sections for the reconstruction of these very noisy

5.4. Perspectives: application to ptychography
Chapter 5 presented several applications of DL-based approaches for ET
reconstruction in conventional imaging modes, with a particular focus on challenges
related to limited angular range and noise. The DIP strategy can also be quite easily
extended and applied to recent techniques in electron microcopy, such as ptychography.
Electron ptychography is a TEM technique that reconstructs complex-valued images,
providing both amplitude and phase information, unlike conventional STEM imaging. It
relies on the acquisition of 4D-STEM data sets, in which a 2D diffraction pattern is
recorded at every probe position. Because adjacent probe positions overlap spatially,
there is redundancy in the acquisition, which enables quantitative, aberration-corrected
imaging with a spatial resolution down to the sub-ångström scale. More details on the
technique and its reconstruction principles can be found in (10).
The interest of ptychography lies in several key features. First, it provides a
quantitative projection of the specimen, which is advantageous for weakly scattering
materials such as biological specimens or light elements. Second, the redundancy
induced by the overlap between adjacent diffraction patterns enhances the achievable
spatial resolution. In addition, ptychography is compatible with ET and is therefore
considered a promising method toward higher resolution ET.
135
However, ptychographic ET also raises several challenges. Because a full diffraction
pattern is recorded at each scan position, the amount of data is already very large for a
single ptychographic acquisition. When combined with multi-angle acquisition in
tomography, the total data volume rapidly becomes enormous. In this context, DIPbased reconstruction may offer a way to reduce the number of projections required,
while preserving reconstruction quality. We propose to illustrate this on both simulated
and experimental ptychography tomographic data sets.
In a first scenario, a ptychography tomographic acquisition was simulated and
processed using the abTEM and py4DSTEM Python libraries. The simulated specimen
resembles a 3D single-wall carbon nanotube, a material of strong current interest for its
electrical and optical properties. The reference dataset was simulated over a complete
±90° angular range with a 2° tilt increment, while a degraded dataset was generated
using a restricted angular range of ±30°. The corresponding SIRT and DIP-TV
reconstruction are shown in Figure 5:27.
Figure 5:27: Reconstruction of the simulated ptychographic data set using SIRT and DIP-TV, with a
complete angular range (±90°:2°) and limited angular range (±30°:2°).
In the reference reconstruction, the atomic columns are clearly resolved. Under
limited angular range conditions, the SIRT reconstructions exhibit strong MW artefacts:
the atomic columns are elongated and blurred, and several atoms disappear entirely.
The DIP-TV reconstructions strongly reduce these artefacts. All atoms are not retrieved,
but the overall structure is recovered more faithfully. It suggests that such DIP-based
136
technique could help to reduce the number of projections required in ptychography
tomographic acquisition, while preserving the structural information at the atomic scale.
We also applied the DIP approach to the reconstruction of a real ptychographic
tomographic data set. In the work of Pelz et al. (55), a double-wall carbon nanotube was
imaged using ptychographic ET. The experiment was performed over an angular range
of -54° to +38° with a 4° tilt increment and it required more than 34 million diffraction
patterns. Before reconstruction, an extensive processing pipeline was required to
recover usable projections from the raw data. The resulting projections and
reconstructions are shown in Figure 5:28.
Figure 5:28: Reconstruction of the real carbon nanotube with the SIRT and DIP approaches.
This sample is particularly challenging, because the atomic columns are strongly
entangled and the MW artefacts, along the (Z) direction, severely deteriorate the
reconstruction quality. The SIRT reconstruction remains blurry, and the atomic columns
are only partially distinct. With the DIP reconstruction, the MW artefacts are partially
corrected and the overall morphology of the nanotube is recovered more faithfully. In
the (ZX) view, the atoms are slightly better resolved than in the SIRT reconstruction.
Overall, these preliminary results indicate that the DIP method is promising for
advanced electron microscopy modalities such as ptychography. Reconstructing such
highly complex samples at the atomic scale remains challenging, and DIP improves the
interpretability of the reconstructions, even if it does not completely remove the MW
artefacts. It appears as a promising method to reduce the amount of data required for
ptychography tomographic acquisitions.
5.5. Conclusion
This chapter presented the application of supervised and unsupervised DL-based
reconstruction and restoration techniques to several experimental ET scenarios, while
highlighting the main challenges encountered in ET reconstruction. This work focused in
137
particular on the use of the DIP technique for ET reconstruction acquired under
degraded conditions, where noise and MW artefacts strongly affect the quality of
conventional reconstructions.
In the first part, the DL approaches demonstrated their ability to recover the
morphology of nanoparticles mainly affected by MW artefacts. For such samples, where
representative simulated training data are available, both supervised and unsupervised
DL methods can be successfully applied. In the continuation of the chapter, the potential
of DIP for AET reconstruction was demonstrated. The unsupervised DIP approach
proved to be particularly well suited for the reconstruction of chemical maps, even when
the experimental projections are very degraded or when the geometry of the acquisition
is restricted.
By taking advantage of the multidimensional nature of spectroscopic data, the multichannel DIP approach was shown to further improve the reconstruction quality in some
specific cases. More generally, this work demonstrates that the acquisition time and the
electron dose delivered to the sample can be reduced while maintaining a high
reconstruction quality. One limitation of this method, however, remains the uncertainty
and variability of the optimization process. The choice of the network architecture, the
convergence behavior, and the selection reconstruction parameters such as the number
of iterations remain important questions for future work.
138
General conclusion and perspectives
In the course of this work, we first reviewed the principles and applications of ET for
materials science, detailing both the acquisition process and the associated
reconstruction challenges. Chapter 4 presented two DL-based approaches for
tomographic reconstruction and restoration. A supervised restoration strategy requiring
the generation of training data sets was first introduced, followed by the presentation
of the DIP framework, which can be applied to previously unseen data without any prior
training. Both methods were evaluated for the processing of simulated nanoparticle
data, while the DIP approach was further tested on simulated nanocubes phantoms
representative of spectroscopic acquisitions.
The results obtained on simulated nanoparticles showed that both approaches
performed well on relatively simple samples, with each method showing advantages
depending on the type of degradation. The supervised approach was particularly
effective when the angular range was limited, whereas the DIP method showed better
results in sparse-view conditions with large tilt increments. DIP was then applied to
simulated nanocubes under various scenarios combining limited number of projections
and noise. In these conditions, it clearly outperformed classical reconstruction methods.
In addition, a multi-channel implementation was proposed and shown to further
improve reconstruction quality, especially in terms of recovering the overall shape of
the volumes.
Chapter 5 focused on the application of these methods to real experimental ET data.
Both methods were applied for the reconstruction of nanoparticles data acquired in
STEM-HAADF. The DIP technique was then used for the reconstructions of volumes from
STEM-EDX and STEM-EELS acquisitions, which represent particularly challenging
scenarios because of the combined effect of noise, limited angular coverage, and
reduced numbers of projections.
The application on the nanoparticles confirmed the behavior previously observed
with each method showing strengths in different scenarios. However, these samples
remain relatively simple compared with the complexity of AET data.
In the second part of the chapter, the application of DIP to STEM-EDX data
demonstrated the strong potential of this approach for such acquisitions. In particular,
DIP proved highly effective at recovering the overall shape the elemental maps, strongly
reducing MW artefacts, and significantly suppressing noise. Even when the number of
frames or number of projections was strongly reduced, the method produced
reconstructions that remained trustworthy, with the elemental phases remaining clearly
visible, and conserving relative intensity ratios. The benefits of the multi-channel DIP
implementation were also confirmed, as the joint reconstruction of several correlated
volumes helped recover edges, boundaries and overall geometry.
139
Similar conclusions were reached for the reconstruction of iron oxide nanocubes
from STEM-EELS acquisition. In that case as well, the multi-channel approach improved
the overall reconstruction quality, even under highly degraded acquisition conditions.
These observations support several conclusions and perspectives.
The supervised approach appears particularly well suited to repeated application on
data sets containing similar structures, making the training of a single restoration model
worthwhile in such cases. In addition, the use of more advanced and realistic simulation
of the data could further improve the quality of the restoration and generalization
capability of the model therefore helping to extend the applicability of supervised
restoration to more challenging data sets.
The DIP approach appears to be a highly effective and flexible approach for
tomographic reconstruction, especially easily applicable for relatively small volumes,
such as those encountered in spectroscopic acquisitions. It performs well at correcting
typical MW and sparsity-related artefacts and requires no training data. However,
several limitations were identified. The convergence of the method is not guaranteed
and depends on several factors, including the number of iterations, the choice of the
network architecture, and the input noise. The optimization process also remains
sensitive to the random initialization, which contributes to the practical instability of the
method. In addition, DIP lacks interpretability, fine-tuning its parameters remains
difficult and it is not straightforward to identify the origin of possible reconstruction
errors.
This work opens several perspectives for future developments. Recent related
approaches, such as implicit neural representations (INRs) or coordinate-based
networks briefly introduced in Chapter 2, could be explore further, as they operate in a
conceptually similar way to DIP while representing signals differently (102,104). These
methods may improve flexibility, memory efficiency, and possibly stability. Other
extensions of DIP have also been proposed recently, such as the Deep Spectral Prior
(199), or a method that focuses on optimizing the input noise distribution instead of the
network weights (200).
From an electron tomography perspective, the method could also be extended to
multimodal reconstruction by jointly exploiting the complementary information
provided by different imaging modes such as HAADF, EELS and EDX in a simultaneous
reconstruction process in a similar manner to previous proposed multimodal
approaches (78,201). Such strategies have the potential to both improve the overall
quality of the reconstructions, as well as enabling reconstructions of the volumes from
more strongly degraded projections data sets. Additionally, the multidimensional nature
of the data could be further exploited, for example by incorporating HAADF information
into the DIP multi-channel framework in order to provide complementary structural
information.
140
Overall, this work demonstrates the strong potential of selected DL approaches for
electron tomography reconstruction under challenging acquisition conditions. It shows
that each of the DL approaches have their own domain of relevance, and that the DIP
framework especially is particularly well suited for the reconstruction of analytical and
multidimensional data.
