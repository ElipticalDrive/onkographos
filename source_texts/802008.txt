Reconstructing a three-dimensional object or environment from multiple photographs is a central problem in computer vision. The resulting models support applications such as robotics, augmented and virtual reality, digital twins, and cultural heritage preservation. An effective reconstruction system must represent both the visual appearance of a scene and the geometry of its surfaces. Achieving these goals simultaneously, however, remains challenging.
Recent methods based on 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) can render complex scenes in real time by representing them as collections of Gaussian primitives. These primitives can be optimized efficiently to reproduce the colors and appearances observed in input photographs.
Although this approach produces high-quality images from new viewpoints, it often prioritizes visual appearance over geometric accuracy. Reconstructed surfaces may contain blurred boundaries, floating artifacts, incomplete regions, or distortions around thin and sharply defined structures.
Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) offer a different approach. An SDF represents a surface as the set of three-dimensional points whose signed distance from that surface is zero. This continuous representation supports accurate geometry and watertight mesh extraction.
Nevertheless, many SDF-based reconstruction methods depend on dense sampling and volumetric ray marching, which increases their computational cost and limits their suitability for real-time rendering.
Blending speed with surface accuracy
Researchers at Sejong University have developed GauSDF, a framework designed to combine the rendering efficiency of GS with the geometric precision of signed distance representations. The work, titled "GauSDF: Signed Distance Embedded Gaussian Surfels for 3D Reconstruction," was published in CVPR Findings 2026.
GauSDF represents a scene using two-dimensional Gaussian surfels, which are flat, oriented primitives that approximate local surface elements. Its main distinction is that each surfel is augmented with an independently learnable signed-distance parameter.
This local parameter gives individual Gaussian primitives information about their relationship to the reconstructed surface instead of deriving every surfel's opacity directly from an imperfect global distance field.
A differentiable, bell-shaped mapping converts each signed-distance value into opacity. Surfels near the implied surface receive higher opacity, while those farther away contribute less to the rendered image. The mapping therefore connects surface geometry with image formation and allows appearance and geometric parameters to be optimized together through GS.
Two branches, shared constraints
The framework contains two complementary branches. The Gaussian branch provides efficient differentiable rendering and learns properties including surfel position, orientation, color, scale and opacity.
A neural SDF branch learns a continuous implicit surface from three-dimensional query points. Three distance-based objectives connect the branches: a pulling loss moves sampled points toward nearby Gaussian centers, a surfel-consistency loss aligns Gaussian centers with the SDF surface, and a flat-region loss supports reconstruction in smooth, low-curvature areas. Normal-consistency and depth-distortion constraints further improve surface orientation and spatial coherence.
After optimization, the neural SDF defines a continuous zero-level surface from which a mesh is extracted using the Marching Cubes algorithm. This design avoids volumetric ray marching during rendering while retaining an implicit representation for mesh reconstruction.
Benchmark results across scene types
GauSDF was evaluated on three established benchmarks covering different reconstruction conditions. The DTU dataset contains object-centered scenes and is commonly used to assess geometric accuracy.
Mip-NeRF360 contains indoor and outdoor environments and was used to evaluate novel-view rendering. DeepFashion3D includes garments with open surfaces and complex topology, providing a test beyond conventional watertight objects.
On DTU, GauSDF achieved a mean Chamfer Distance of 0.64 across 15 scenes, the lowest value among the methods reported in the study. Chamfer Distance measures the discrepancy between reconstructed and reference geometry, with lower values indicating closer surface agreement.
The method also obtained a structural similarity score of 0.949 and a learned perceptual image patch similarity score of 0.128, demonstrating that its geometric improvements remained compatible with high-quality rendering.
On Mip-NeRF360, GauSDF achieved an average peak signal-to-noise ratio of 31.06 decibels for indoor scenes and 27.81 decibels for outdoor scenes. It also recorded structural similarity scores of 0.946 and 0.890, respectively. On DeepFashion3D, it produced a mean Chamfer Distance of 1.69 × 10⁻³.
This result was competitive with unsigned-distance approaches and substantially better than the other signed-distance and Gaussian baselines included in that evaluation. All experiments were conducted for 30,000 optimization iterations on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.
Limits in curvature and occlusion
The current framework also has limitations. Its per-Gaussian distance value is a first-order representation and may not fully capture complex local curvature. Anisotropic or multiscale distance embeddings could provide richer geometric information. The SDF branch can also struggle in heavily occluded or visually ambiguous regions, suggesting that stronger interaction between the Gaussian and implicit branches may improve future reconstructions.
The broader significance of GauSDF lies in connecting two previously complementary representation strategies at the level of individual surface primitives. Its results indicate that real-time, appearance-oriented Gaussian rendering can incorporate explicit geometric awareness without returning to computationally intensive volumetric rendering.
This combination could support future systems that require both realistic visualization and usable surface geometry, including immersive environments, robotic perception, digital twins, and the digitization of physical objects.
