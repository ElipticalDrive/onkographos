Abstract
Objective. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive imaging technique that provides high soft tissue contrast, playing a vital role in disease diagnosis and treatment planning. However, due to limitations in imaging hardware, scan time, and patient compliance, the resolution of MRI images is often insufficient. Super-resolution (SR) techniques can enhance MRI resolution, reveal more detailed anatomical information, and improve the identification of complex structures, while also reducing scan time and patient discomfort. However, traditional population-based models trained on large datasets may introduce artifacts or hallucinated structures, which compromise their reliability in clinical applications. Approach. To address these challenges, we propose a patient-specific knowledge transfer implicit neural representation (KT-INR) SR model. The KT-INR model integrates a dual-head INR with a pre-trained generative adversarial network (GAN) model trained on a large-scale dataset. Anatomical information from different MRI sequences of the same patient, combined with the SR mappings learned by the GAN model on a population-based dataset, is transferred as prior knowledge to the INR. This integration enhances both the performance and reliability of the SR model. Main results. We validated the effectiveness of the KT-INR model across three distinct clinical SR tasks on the brain tumor segmentation dataset. For task 1, KT-INR achieved an average structural similarity index, peak signal-to-noise ratio, and learned perceptual image patch similarity of 0.9813, 36.845, and 0.0186, respectively. In comparison, a state-of-the-art SR technique, ArSSR, attained average values of 0.9689, 33.4557, and 0.0309 for the same metrics. The experimental results demonstrate that KT-INR outperforms all other methods across all tasks and evaluation metrics, with particularly remarkable performance in resolving fine anatomical details. Significance. The KT-INR model significantly enhances the reliability of SR results, effectively addressing the hallucination effects commonly seen in traditional models. It provides a robust solution for patient-specific MRI SR.

1. Introduction
As a non-invasive imaging modality, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) excels in providing high-contrast visuals, making it indispensable for diagnosing organ conditions, pinpointing tumors for radiotherapy, and tracking disease progression (Schenck 1996, Khoo et al 1997, Frayne et al 2003, Villeirs and De Meerleer 2007, Katti et al 2011, Jadvar and Colletti 2014). However, due to limitations in imaging equipment, scanning time, and patient compliance, the spatial resolution of MRI often falls short in meeting the clinical demand of high precision (Kilborn and Labbé 1990, Hartwig et al 2009, Tsao and Kozerke 2012, Shearrer et al 2016). For instance, many MRI scans employ anisotropic 2D imaging protocols, with excellent in-plane resolution while sub-optimal resolution in the slice direction (out-of-plane) (Alkadi et al 2018, Hu et al 2018). This low resolution not only hinders the detection of small lesions but limits the accuracy of 3D volume analysis and structure localization. In selected fast imaging modes or under specific scanning conditions, MRI images may exhibit isotopically low resolution in all directions (Carmi et al 2006, Marques et al 2019). Although resolution consistency is maintained across axes, the overall low resolution prevents the clear visualization of fine anatomical details, such as complex tissue boundaries, reducing diagnostic precision and reliability. To overcome these resolution limitations, MRI super-resolution (SR) techniques have been extensively studied (Van Reeth et al 2012, Chaudhari et al 2018).
Early SR methods primarily relied on traditional analytical approaches, with Cubic interpolation (Fritsch and Carlson 1980), Lagrange interpolation (Berrut and Trefethen 2004), and block-matching and 3D Filtering (BM3D) being representative algorithms (Dabov et al 2006). Cubic interpolation generates smooth high-resolution (HR) images by leveraging neighboring pixel information through polynomial fitting. However, this method often results in blurring, particularly at image boundaries and regions of high-frequency details. Lagrange interpolation improves accuracy by employing more complex polynomial models to fit local image features. Compared to Cubic interpolation, Lagrange interpolation performs better in smooth regions but struggles with complex structures and high-frequency variations. BM3D, initially developed for image denoising, was later extended to SR tasks. By leveraging block-matching and 3D filtering techniques, BM3D identifies repeating patterns in local and non-local image regions, enhancing detail preservation in SR results. However, BM3D’s performance is highly dependent on image content and may falter in noisy or structurally complex cases.
In general, traditional methods are inherently limited in modeling the complex spatial structures of MRI data, particularly when dealing with highly non-linear features. In recent years, deep learning methods have emerged as the dominant approach in the SR field, leveraging their end-to-end learning capability and proficiency in capturing intricate patterns to significantly improve the accuracy and visual quality of SR results (Dong et al 2015). For instance, the residual dense network (RDN) introduces Residual Dense Blocks (RDBs) to fully exploit hierarchical features and achieve adaptive fusion of global and local features, significantly enhancing detail recovery (Zhang et al 2018). Similarly, ResCNN uses residual learning with long and short skip connections to mitigate gradient vanishing issues of deep networks while effectively restoring high-frequency details in MR images (Du et al 2020). SRResNet incorporates perceptual loss by combining residual networks (ResNet) with a perceptual loss function, including adversarial and content losses, to generate super-resolved images that are perceptually closer to real HR images (Ledig et al 2017). Beyond CNN-based models, SuperFormer applies a 3D visual transformer architecture with self-attention mechanisms, outperforming traditional convolutional networks in 3D MRI SR tasks (Forigua et al 2022). Recently, implicit neural representations (INRs) offer a novel approach to SR tasks by learning a continuous mapping from coordinates to image signals, rather than relying on explicit pixel grids (Sitzmann et al 2020). Wu et al proposed the ArSSR method, which leverages implicit voxel representations to achieve arbitrary-scale SR, enabling efficient reconstruction across varying resolutions (2022).
However, the aforementioned deep learning models, mostly trained on population-based datasets, often risk introducing artifacts or ‘hallucinated’ structures, raising concerns about their clinical reliability. Patient-specific models trained on individual patient data address these concerns by fitting a network to specific patient traits. Multi-contrast INRs (MCINR) exemplifies such patient-specific SR models, relying solely on data from a single patient (McGinnis et al 2023). While MCINR promotes greater clinical reliability, its performance remains constrained by the limited data available from a single patient and fails to fully leverage existing large-scale datasets. To address these limitations, in this study we propose the knowledge transfer INRs (KT-INR) model, focusing on patient-specific SR tasks. KT-INR combines a dual-head INR structure with a pre-trained, population-based generative adversarial network (GAN) (Goodfellow et al 2020). By leveraging complementary anatomical information across MRI sequences and transferring population-based knowledge learned from large datasets into the INR framework, our model achieves significant improvements in SR fidelity and reliability. The contributions of this work are threefold:
To tackle the performance limitations of existing patient-specific SR models, we introduce the first approach combining dual-head INR with a pre-trained population-based GAN model, effectively transferring population-level knowledge into patient-specific SR tasks while maintaining personalized predictions.
The KT-INR model trains sparse coordinates from the low-resolution MRI for SR while incorporating priors from concurrently acquired sequences of the same patient, which maintains anatomical consistency across sequences.
We comprehensively validated KT-INR across three different clinical SR tasks using the brain tumor segmentation (BRATS) dataset. The experimental results demonstrate that KT-INR achieved superior performance across all tasks and metrics, with particularly significant improvements in SR accuracy for fine anatomical structures.

2.3. Population-based model
To provide population-level priors in the KT-INR framework, a 3D GAN model is first trained to super-resolve LR T2w images into HR T2w images. 3. Experiment and discussion
3.1. Datasets & preprocessing
This study evaluates the effectiveness of the KT-INR model on the BRATS 2021 dataset (Baid et al 2021), which includes multi-contrast brain MRI scans. Specifically, we selected the T1w and T2w sequences, as these scans have HR and well-aligned 3D volumes, making them ideal for SR validation. Since KT-INR is a patient-specific model that requires a separate model for each patient, we randomly selected ten patients from the BRATS 2021 dataset as the testing set. Additionally, another ten patients were randomly selected as the validation set, which was used for selecting the weights of our 3D GAN model and other baseline models. The remaining 1231 patients in the dataset were used as the training set. To mitigate the impact of scanner variability on model training, all MRI intensities were normalized to the [0, 1] range. In the experiments, HR images were downsampled using the nearest-neighbor interpolation method to generate ALR and ILR data, as required by the task settings. This approach ensured that the simulated low-resolution data closely matched the characteristics of real data. Furthermore, the input patches for our 3D GAN and other comparison models were randomly cropped to a uniform volume size of . Since all BRATS data were already well-registered, no additional registration steps were performed. This preprocessing pipeline ensured consistency across different methods while maintaining the integrity of the experimental setup.
In addition, we also include a public knee dataset. The knee dataset was acquired using a clinical 3T MRI scanner with the coronal proton density (Coronal-PD) sequence (Duan and Ren 2024). It includes data from 20 patients, out of which we randomly selected 17 patients for training, 1 for validation, and 2 for the test set. The dataset provides raw k-space data, and we applied Fourier transform to reconstruct the images. The reconstructed HR images have dimensions of . To simulate realistic low-resolution images, we retained only the central 1/4 of the data in the vertical axis of the original k-space and reconstructed low-resolution images with dimensions of . To mitigate the impact of scanner variability on model training, all MRI intensities were also normalized to the [0, 1] range.
3.2. Implementation details
The training and testing of the models were conducted on an RTX 4090 GPU. Both our 3D GAN and other baseline models were trained for a total of 100 epochs with a batch size of 4 and a learning rate of 0.001. For our INR model, the batch size was set to 1000, with a learning rate of 0.0004, and the training also ran for 100 epochs. The Adam optimizer was utilized for all training processes. The KT-INR model architecture for task 1 and task 3 remained identical, while for task 2, the 3D GAN’s output heads were increased from one to two, as both ALR T1w and T2w images require SR. Additionally, due to the higher level of difficulty in super-resolving isotropic low-resolution images compared to anisotropic ones (where only one direction has low resolution), the scaling factor k for anisotropic low-resolution images in tasks 1 and 2 was set to 4, whereas the scaling factor for isotropic low-resolution images in task 3 was set to 2. Considering that the background regions of the images consist entirely of clinically meaningless zero values, we first extract a foreground mask based on the image during model training. Training is then restricted to points within this mask, making the training process more efficient.
3.3. Evaluation metrics
To evaluate the performance of the model, we utilized several standardized metrics, including peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) (Korhonen and You 2012) to quantify the signal-to-noise ratio between the super-resolved images and the HR ‘ground-truth’ images, structural similarity index (SSIM) (Wang et al 2004) to assess the structural similarity, and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) (Zhang et al 2018) to measure perceptual differences. Since SSIM and LPIPS are designed for 2D images, we sliced the 3D images into 2D layers for evaluation, computed the metrics for each layer, and then calculated the average across all layers as the final result. In addition, we performed image segmentation as a downstream task on the super-resolved images to evaluate their accuracy. For the evaluation of segmentation results, we adopted the commonly used dice similarity coefficient (DICE) and average surface distance (ASD) metrics.
3.4. Compared methods
To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the KT-INR model, we selected a variety of methods for comparison, including traditional interpolation techniques, deep learning-based models, and INR approaches. For the deep learning-based models, we made modifications to enable them to jointly process both the MRI priors and the low-resolution MR images as inputs. Cubic interpolation and BM3D were chosen as representative interpolation and block-matching methods, respectively. While these methods are simple and efficient, they struggle with handling complex structures and high-frequency details. Among the deep learning approaches, RDN and SRResNet leverage residual learning and perceptual losses, leading to significant improvements in SR quality. SuperFormer, with its attention mechanism, demonstrated superior performance in 3D MRI SR tasks. Additionally, we compared the KT-INR model with ArSSR, an INR-based approach that relies on large-scale population-level training. Lastly, we also included MCINR, a patient-specific INR model trained on single-patient data, in our comparisons. However, since MCINR is designed specifically for complementary view SR tasks, its performance was evaluated exclusively in task 2.
All the comparison methods, except MCINR, are population-based models trained on large-scale training datasets. In contrast, MCINR only utilizes single-patient data for training. For our KT-INR model, the 3D GAN is trained on the large-scale training dataset, while the second-stage INR is trained exclusively on individual patient data.
3.5. Performance on Task 1
In task 1, we comprehensively evaluated the performance of KT-INR across ten patients using SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS metrics. As shown in figure 4, KT-INR outperformed the other methods across all metrics, achieving an average SSIM of 0.985, PSNR of 37.786, and LPIPS of 0.013. Additionally, the visual comparisons shown in figure 5 further validate the superiority of KT-INR on task 1. Traditional methods and some deep learning models, such as RDN and SRResNet, exhibit blurriness when performing SR on complex anatomical structures. In contrast, KT-INR not only achieves higher accuracy in detail SR but also avoids the generation of spurious structures. In figure 5, the ResCNN results for case II show certain spurious structures, with the sulci appearing significantly larger compared to the ‘ground truth’. Among deep learning-based models, SuperFormer and ArSSR stood out, with SuperFormer leveraging a transformer-based architecture to achieve relatively high performance. ArSSR employed INR as well, but its reliance on large-scale population-level data limited its ability to handle individual patient variability, resulting in some blurriness or structural omissions in the visualized results. In comparison, the strength of KT-INR is particularly evident in high-frequency regions and boundary structures, where it demonstrates closer consistency with the ‘ground-truth’ HR images.

3.7. Performance on task 3
In task 3, we evaluated the SR performance of the KT-INR model on isotropic low-resolution T2w images, with results shown in figure 8. It is evident that KT-INR can more accurately super-resolve anatomical details and boundary regions, showing very high consistency with the ‘ground-truth’ HR images. Overall, the results of task 3 further validate the outstanding performance of the KT-INR model in handling complex SR tasks.

3.8. Ablation study
To further validate the effectiveness of each module in the KT-INR model, we conducted an ablation study. The experimental results are presented in table 1, and the visual results for different configurations are shown in figure 10. First, when the INR module was removed (with 3D GAN only), the model’s SSIM dropped to 0.974, PSNR decreased to 34.797, and LPIPS increased to 0.027. As observed in figure 10, removing the INR module led to blurry boundaries and a lack of anatomical details in the generated images. This highlights that the INR module plays a critical role in enhancing fine detail reconstruction and anatomical consistency, as it effectively captures the continuous features of the image. Next, removing the prior MRI information (Head 1) resulted in an SSIM of 0.983, PSNR of 37.526, and LPIPS of 0.016. Figure 10 reveals that without prior cross-sequence MRI information, the SR results struggled to restore small structures and high-frequency details. This indicates that prior MRI information provides additional anatomical guidance, improving the reliability and accuracy of the SR results. When the knowledge transfer module was removed, the SSIM dropped to 0.980, and the PSNR decreased to 36.484. This demonstrates that knowledge transfer module enhances the precision of SR results by incorporating population-level knowledge. In the case where both the knowledge transfer module and the prior MRI information were removed, the model’s performance deteriorated further. Figure 10 clearly shows that under this configuration, the generated images exhibited significantly degraded quality. The complete KT-INR model achieved the best performance across all metrics, with an SSIM of 0.985, PSNR of 37.786, and LPIPS of 0.013. In summary, the ablation study results indicate that the INR module, the prior MRI information, and the knowledge transfer module each play a vital role in enhancing KT-INR’s performance. Their combination significantly improves the model’s overall performance, delivering superior SR results.

3.12. Analysis of the hallucinations of different models
To further analyze the advantages of the patient-specific INR model, we designed a hallucination effect comparison experiment. The hallucination effect typically becomes more noticeable when there is a significant time gap between the acquisitions of prior MRI and low-resolution MRI scans, during which some anatomical structures (such as tumor regions) may change. Since the prior MRI provides the anatomical prior information, population-based models may simplify the SR task into a modality conversion task, thereby introducing hallucinations during the prediction of missing details in low-resolution images. However, in the BRATS dataset used in our experiments, different MRI sequences were acquired at the same time point, meaning no significant changes occurred in anatomy. To simulate a hallucination scenario, we utilized the segmentation labels from the BRATS dataset, combining the non-enhancing tumor core and enhancing tumor regions to define the tumor boundaries. We then generated a deformation field that expanded the tumor boundary outward by 2 mm and applied this deformation field to the prior MRI (T1 sequence). This approach simulates a scenario where a patient’s prior MRI was acquired with a larger tumor which shrank after treatment. We conducted experiments on task 1, where the low-resolution MRI was the T2 sequence, and the prior MRI was the deformed T1 sequence. To emphasize the hallucination effect, we set the downsampling factor k to 6, as traditional models would rely more heavily on prior MRI under heavier undersampling. We selected ResCNN as a baseline traditional SR model and compared its hallucination effect performance against our KT-INR model. In addition to ResCNN, we also compared different configurations of KT-INR to analyze their hallucination effects. The experimental results are shown in table 5, and the visual comparison results are presented in figure 12. From the visualization results of figure 12 I, it can be observed that both ResCNN and KT-INR without the INR component exhibited noticeable hallucination effects in the tumor region, generating tumors similar in size to those in the prior MRI, which appear larger than the actual tumor. In contrast, the KT-INR model trained without the knowledge transfer module, using only INR, did not produce hallucinations in the tumor region. However, as shown in figure 12 II, the model without the knowledge transfer module still underperforms the full KT-INR model, as it fails to effectively utilize prior knowledge. Through a weighted loss defined on the GAN outputs and the original low-resolution MR images, KT-INR effectively incorporated the knowledge from the GAN outputs while suppressing the hallucinations with low-resolution MRI supervision.

3.14. Subjective image quality evaluation
To compare SR images from a clinical perspective, we conducted a subjective image quality evaluation experiment. We invited two board-certified medical physicists to assess the overall visual quality of the SR images. To reduce the evaluation workload, we selected SuperFormer as the comparison model. The images generated by KT-INR and SuperFormer on the test set were randomly shuffled and presented to two experienced physicists for evaluation. During the assessment, they were asked to subjectively select the images they believed to have better overall image quality. The evaluation results showed that KT-INR received a total of 19 votes, while SuperFormer received only 1 vote. This result indicates that the KT-INR model achieved higher recognition in terms of subjective visual quality.
3.15. Analysis of the patient-specific INR training process
Figure 14 illustrates the quantitative performance curves of the model and the visual results at different stages of INR training. The line plots show a steady improvement in SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS metrics as the number of training epochs increases, with performance stabilizing around the 20th epoch. This indicates that the quality of the generated images consistently improves and progressively approximates the true HR images. The visual results in the figure further validate the trends observed in the quantitative metrics. As training progresses, the details in the generated images become increasingly refined, particularly in complex anatomical regions such as gyri. In the early stages (e.g. Epoch 5), the generated images exhibit blurry edges and lack detailed features. During the middle stages, the model starts to capture more detailed information. By the later stages (e.g. Epochs 20 to 25), the generated images are nearly indistinguishable from the true HR images, especially in terms of detail representation in critical regions. While early stages focus on improving overall performance, the later stages refine the model to capture finer anatomical details. The stability of the training curves and the consistency of the visual results confirm the effectiveness and convergence of the INR training process. Based on these observations, we selected 25 epochs as the training duration for all patient-specific INR models to ensure sufficient training and optimal performance.

4. Limitation
Despite its outstanding performance in experiments, KT-INR has several limitations that require attention in future studies. First, the dual-head INR structure and knowledge transfer mechanism introduce a relatively high computational cost, necessitating significant hardware resources and training time. This could limit its applicability in resource-constrained clinical environments. Second, KT-INR’s reliance on additional MRI sequences as prior information may restrict its use in scenarios where only single-sequence data is available. Developing more flexible model architectures could address this limitation. In addition, potential mis-alignments between the MR sequences may reduce the accuracy of KT-INR. Additional registration modules or mis-registration-robust designs can be added into future KT-INR frameworks to enhance its clinical utility. Lastly, this study evaluated KT-INR solely on the BRATS dataset, leaving its generalizability and robustness to other data distributions unverified. Future research should focus on reducing computational complexity, broadening its applicability to diverse clinical scenarios, and conducting extensive validation on varied datasets to ensure its practicality and scalability across different clinical settings.
5. Conclusion
This study proposed a patient-specific KT-INR model for SR, effectively addressing the hallucination effects often introduced by traditional population-based SR models in clinical applications. The KT-INR model significantly enhances the fidelity and anatomical consistency of SR results by integrating a dual-head INR structure with a pre-trained population-based GAN model. By leveraging both patient-specific anatomical features and population-level knowledge, KT-INR demonstrated exceptional performance in personalized SR tasks. Experimental results showed that KT-INR consistently outperformed existing baseline models across three SR tasks on the BRATS dataset, excelling in key metrics such as SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS. Moreover, the visual results confirmed its ability to accurately reconstruct complex anatomical structures. These findings underscore the significant potential of KT-INR in improving MRI resolution and enhancing the reliability and precision of clinical diagnosis. In the future, the knowledge transfer framework of KT-INR can be further optimized and extended to other medical imaging applications, providing a novel solution for personalized medical image analysis.
