6D pose estimation of novel objects without CAD models
remains an open challenge: model-free methods must build
the object representation from scratch during onboarding
and have long trailed their model-based counterparts. We
present NovaSplat, a model-free pipeline that closes this
gap. From a short smartphone video captured at onboarding, we train a 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) model that
serves both as a template source for detection and coarse
pose estimation and as a differentiable backbone for pose
refinement. Our refiner optimizes the object-to-camera pose
with combined photometric and geometric losses under a
continuous rotation parametrization free of gradient singularities. On a household-object benchmark, NovaSplat surpasses all model-based methods. On a challenging industrial benchmark with textureless and specular surfaces, it
outperforms all model-free competitors while substantially
narrowing the gap to model-based ones. We further introduce NovaSplat-Lite, an inference variant that is 3.1×
faster at negligible accuracy cost. Code will be made available at https://github.com/POSE-Lab/novasplat.

Model-free methods face a fundamental reconstruction
problem. Where model-based methods receive the object
geometry by definition, model-free methods must recover
it from video, a noisier and less complete substitute that
degrades every downstream stage, from template rendering
to correspondence matching. As illustrated in Figure 1, this
has historically resulted in a substantial performance gap
between the two paradigms, limiting the practical utility of
model-free approaches.
We propose NovaSplat, a complete model-free pipeline
that closes this gap and in some cases reverses it. During
onboarding, we reconstruct a dense 3D representation of the
object from video and train a 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)
model. This representation serves two roles simultaneously:

1. Introduction
6D object pose estimation, recovering the full position and
orientation of an object relative to a camera, is a core capability for robotic manipulation, augmented reality, and industrial inspection. The highest-performing methods rely
on a 3D CAD model of the object: they render templates at
many viewpoints and extract geometric point clouds, which
are then matched against the query image using powerful
frozen foundation models [5, 35]. In practice, however,
CAD models are rarely available outside controlled environments, making these approaches impractical for realworld deployment. This motivates the model-free setting:
given only a short video of a novel object captured during
an onboarding phase, estimate its 6D pose in new scenes

2.3. Model-Free Novel Object Methods
Model-free methods can be organized into three categories.
Visual localization approaches [11, 44] apply Structurefrom-Motion (SfM) during onboarding to build a sparse 3D
point cloud, recovering pose via 2D-3D correspondences.
They struggle, however, with low-texture objects and large
viewpoint changes. Template-based methods [25] use viewpoint retrieval over posed reference images for a coarse hypothesis before refinement but are sensitive to detection and
bounding box quality. Few-shot methods operate from only
one or a handful of RGB or RGB-D images: [18] generate
a geometry-aware encoding from a few RGB views, [24]
compress a single RGB-D reference into a pose embedding
via latent diffusion, and [21] combine 2D-3D alignment
with render-and-compare refinement from a single RGB-D
anchor. Despite their practical appeal, these methods rely
on strong assumptions, such as symmetry priors or scale
constraints, to compensate for limited onboarding data, restricting generalization across object geometries.
2. Related Work
2.1. Taxonomy of 6D Object Pose Estimation
Existing 6D object pose estimation methods can be broadly
categorized by the level of prior object knowledge they require. Instance-based methods [15, 43, 46] leverage 3D
object models during training to achieve high accuracy but
are restricted to previously seen objects. Novel object
methods [4, 5, 11, 18, 21, 23, 25, 29, 32, 35, 49, 55, 56]
generalize to unseen objects and can be further divided
into category-based [45, 50] and unseen object approaches.
Within the unseen object paradigm, model-based methods [5, 28, 29, 32, 35] require a CAD model at inference
time, exploiting geometric priors from rendered templates
and 3D point clouds, while model-free methods [44, 49]
build an object representation from a short video sequence
during an onboarding phase, without any 3D model. This
work focuses on the model-free unseen object setting.
2.4. Neural Rendering for Object Pose Estimation
Neural rendering approaches encode objects into differentiable implicit representations for CAD-free novel-view
synthesis. Early NeRF-based methods such as LatentFusion [38] and NeRF-Pose [23] use latent voxel grids and
radiance fields, respectively. BundleSDF [55] builds a Neural Object Field on-the-fly from an RGB-D stream, concurrently optimizing pose and a 3D representation. FoundationPose [56] extends this into a unified foundation model
supporting both model-based and model-free setups via
large-scale synthetic training. More recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [19] has largely displaced NeRFbased representations owing to superior rendering quality and real-time speed. GS-Pose [3], iG-6DoF [4], and

During inference, given an RGB-D query image containing instances of onboarded objects, the goal is to detect each object and estimate its 6D pose. We first run a
modified CNOS [31] detection and segmentation module
(Section 3.3) to localize each object and produce its segmentation mask. We then employ the coarse pose estimation module of FreeZeV2 [5, 6] (Section 3.4), adapted to
use templates rendered from the 3DGS representation and a
3D point cloud sampled from the mesh model, to obtain an
initial 6D pose estimate. Finally, each coarse pose is passed
to our 3DGS-based hybrid refiner (Section 3.5), which produces the final 6D pose. Implementation details of the onboarding and inference stages are provided in Section 4.3.

Structure-from-Motion. The raw frames are passed to a
SfM pipeline [37, 41] to estimate camera poses and a sparse
3D point cloud. The object masks are then applied to isolate
the object point cloud from the background.
3D Gaussian Splatting. The masked frames, camera
poses, and object point cloud are used to train a 3DGS
model [19]. The 3DGS representation consists of a set of
3D Gaussians, each parameterized by its mean, covariance,
opacity, and view-dependent color encoded as Spherical
Harmonics (SH) coefficients. The model is optimized by
differentiably rendering the Gaussians onto the image plane
and minimizing the photometric error against the masked
training frames.
Multi-View Stereo. The SfM point cloud is often too
sparse to initialize stereo depth estimation reliably, particularly over untextured object regions where few or no feature
correspondences can be established. Since 3DGS densification fills in these regions during optimization, we exploit
the Gaussian centers as a semi-dense point cloud. Equipped
with camera visibility information, this denser prior serves
as input to our MVS pipeline. We run PatchMatchStereo [1]
to estimate per-view depth maps from neighboring views,
which are then fused into a dense point cloud. From this
point cloud we recover a 3D mesh by following [16], which
discretizes space via Delaunay tetrahedralization and formulates surface extraction as a graph optimization problem
that incorporates visibility constraints.
3.5. 3DGS-Based Pose Refinement
The coarse pose Pcoarse is further refined in the final stage
of our pipeline through a hybrid optimization scheme that
jointly considers appearance and geometric cues. The backbone of this optimization is the differentiable 3DGS representation learned during onboarding. During refinement,
the Gaussian parameters are frozen, and only the object-tocamera pose is optimized.
We parameterize the rotational component using the continuous 6D representation of Zhou et al. [59], which is defined over SO(3) without singularities or discontinuities,
allowing gradients to flow freely throughout the optimization. The translational component t is parameterized as a
standard 3D vector. Both are initialized from Pcoarse .

4.1. Datasets
We evaluate NovaSplat on two complementary benchmarks
spanning household and industrial objects.
HOPEv2 [47] is the BOP version of NVIDIA’s Household
Objects for Pose Estimation dataset, comprising 28 toy grocery objects across 50 cluttered scenes from 10 household
and office environments. For the BOP Challenge 2024 [33],
the dataset was extended with multi-view test images captured by a moving camera and provides a standardized
leaderboard for direct comparison with both model-free and
model-based methods under identical conditions. We note
that we encountered some inconsistencies in this dataset,
which are detailed in Sec. G.
4.2. Evaluation Metrics
For each of the benchmarks, we follow the proposed evaluation protocol to ensure fair comparison with the reported baselines. In particular, for HOPEv2, we adopt the
BOP detection evaluation protocol [33], computing Average Precision (AP or mAP) for the Maximum Symmetryaware Surface Distance (MSSD) APMSSD and the Maximum
Symmetry-aware Projection Distance (MSPD) APMSPD .
For IndustryShapes, we adopt the BOP localization protocol, computing the Average Recall (AR) for MSSD
(ARMSSD ), for MSPD (ARMSPD ) and for the Visible Surface Discrepancy (VSD) ARVSD . The overall accuracy is
measured for both datasets as an average over the different
metrics. We refer to Sec. 3.4 of [33] for details.
IndustryShapes [40] is an RGB-D benchmark designed
for industrial robotics, with two splits: classic and extended.
It features five object categories with properties that are particularly challenging for pose estimation: textureless surfaces, specular reflections, thin geometries, and small scale.
Scenes are captured in realistic industrial assembly settings
with multiple instances per object and inter-object occlusions. The extended split provides onboarding sequences,
directly matching our model-free paradigm.

The distinction between APMSSD and APMSPD is meaningful here: as noted by Hodaň et al. [14], MSSD measures 3D surface distance and is most relevant for robotic
manipulation, whereas MSPD measures 2D projection error and is more relevant for AR. Since our target application is manipulation, APMSSD is our primary metric. We
attribute this to NovaSplat’s end-to-end geometric grounding: GeDi descriptors drive the coarse stage and Lgeo constrains refinement, an integration absent in all competing
model-free methods that rely on appearance cues, which explains the disproportionately large gains on APMSSD relative
to APMSPD .

IndustryShapes Results. Table 2 evaluates NovaSplat

textured household objects, IndustryShapes consists of
texture-less, black industrial parts with specular surfaces,

Model-based methods, however, retain a clear advantage
here, with FoundationPose [56] achieving AR scores of 0.67
and 0.69 on the classic and extended splits, respectively,
compared to NovaSplat’s 0.36 and 0.40. That said, the gap
narrows considerably on ARMSSD : model-free FoundationPose trails the best model-based result by 0.43 on the classic split, whereas NovaSplat reduces this gap to 0.23, nearly
halving it. The same trend holds on the extended split (gap
reduced from 0.37 to 0.27). This is consistent with our findings on HOPEv2 and reinforces that NovaSplat’s geometric
grounding is the key driver of progress, as ARMSSD is the
metric most sensitive to 3D accuracy.
The overall performance gap relative to model-based
methods on this dataset is larger than on HOPEv2, and we
attribute this to the fundamental difficulty of the objects’
nature. Texture-less, uniformly black surfaces with specular highlights provide little signal for appearance-based feature matching and yield degraded 3D reconstructions that
fail to capture fine geometric detail. This explanation is
further supported by the qualitative results reported in the
appendix. Improving NovaSplat’s robustness to such objects, for instance, through geometry-only reconstruction
pipelines, is a natural direction for future work.

4.5. Ablation Study
Table 3 isolates the contribution of each component of our
refinement module on the HOPEv2 validation set. The
coarse pose estimation (CPE) stage alone achieves a strong
baseline of 0.724 AP and 0.703 APMSSD . Refining this initialization with standard Iterative Closest Point (ICP), as
employed by FreeZe [5], severely degrades performance
across all metrics: APMSSD drops by 45.4% (0.703 to 0.384)
and AP by 34.9% (0.724 to 0.471). This highlights the unreliability of zero-shot geometry extracted from monocular
video for standard point-to-point registration: the resulting
point clouds are too noisy for ICP, which is highly susceptible to local minima under such conditions.
Our 3DGS-based refinement takes a fundamentally different approach by directly optimizing the pose against the
captured scene rather than relying on point cloud alignment.
Using only the photometric loss Lphoto yields a marginal improvement over CPE (APMSSD : 0.703 to 0.706), as texture
ambiguities can still trap the optimizer in poor local minima. Isolating the geometric term, CPE + 3DGS (only Lgeo )
degrades performance below CPE (APMSSD : 0.703 to 0.651;
AP: 0.724 to 0.666), mirroring the ICP result: without the
complementary photometric signal to anchor it, geometric
alignment alone inherits the same sensitivity to noisy zeroshot geometry. Introducing the full dual loss, combining
photometric and geometric (depth) terms, effectively constrains the optimization and outperforms both individual
components, achieving the best performance across all metrics (APMSSD : 0.718, AP: 0.736). Figure 4 illustrates the
qualitative improvement. Together, these results validate
our design choice to use the 3DGS representation not only
for template rendering but also as a differentiable backbone
for pose refinement.

5. Conclusions
We presented NovaSplat, a model-free pipeline for 6D object pose estimation that replaces the CAD model requirement with a compact onboarding procedure: SfM and MVS
recover object geometry from a short smartphone video,
which is then used to train a 3D Gaussian Splatting representation for both template rendering and differentiable
pose refinement. NovaSplat is, to our knowledge, the first
model-free method to surpass model-based competitors on
a BOP benchmark, exceeding the best model-based baseline
on HOPEv2 by 2.9% on AP and 17.0% on APMSSD , and outperforms the best model-free competitor on IndustryShapes
while narrowing the gap to model-based methods. We additionally provide NovaSplat-Lite, an inference variant that
is 3.1× faster at a cost of 0.001 AP.
Our primary limitation is sensitivity to object appearance: on IndustryShapes, textureless, specular surfaces degrade both reconstruction quality and feature matching,
leaving a gap to model-based methods. Future work includes improving robustness to such objects, exploring our
refiner as a tracker, and replacing our separate MVS meshing step with direct mesh extraction from 3DGS, as in recent
approaches like MeshSplatting [12].

B. Runtime Analysis
Coarse pose estimation. We adapt FreeZe’s coarse pose
estimation module to our template-based setting, and omit
its point filtering and PCA dimensionality reduction, as we
found them to have no effect on system performance. NovaSplat computes the GeDi local reference frame at two
scales. NovaSplat-Lite uses a single scale (rlrf = 0.15).
NovaSplat-Lite additionally replaces the Open3D pointcloud conversions preceding descriptor extraction with native PyTorch operations (Sec. B.4). This is an implementation change only and leaves the descriptor formulation and
its numeric parameters unchanged.
B.1. Measurement Protocol
All timings are measured on a workstation with an Intel(R)
Core(TM) i7-14700K processor (28 cores) and a single
NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Inference timings are reported
on the HOPEv2 validation set, which comprises 457 images
containing 9,873 annotated object instances, an average of
21.6 instances per image. Onboarding timings are per object and are a one-time cost. We report wall-clock time with
all model weights already resident in GPU memory, excluding dataset I/O and one-time process startup.
Refinement. In the refiner, the alpha channel is thresholded at τα = 0.001, and the point cloud is filtered using
a distance threshold of drange = 30 mm. Pose optimization
uses two interleaved AdamW [26] optimizers with learning rates of 0.01 for rotation and 5.0 for translation. Rotation and translation updates alternate every Nalt = 10
steps. Per-iteration corrections are clamped to αthresh = 10◦
for rotation and tthresh = ±10 mm for lateral translation.
The geometric loss weight is set to wgeo = 0.001, reflecting the scale difference between the SSIM-based Lphoto and
the Chamfer-based Lgeo . NovaSplat runs the optimizer for
at most Nref = 200 iterations with early stopping after
Nhalt = 30 iterations without improvement. NovaSplat-Lite
reduces these to Nref = 20 and Nhalt = 3. All other refiner
settings are shared.
Because HOPEv2 images are densely populated, the perimage figure aggregates 21.6 instance-level pose estimates
on average. We therefore report both per-image and perinstance timings, as the latter is the quantity that scales with
scene complexity in a deployment setting.

E.1. Protocol
We discard the provided poses entirely and re-estimate the
onboarding trajectory from the RGB frames with COLMAP
features and matches [41] followed by the GLOMAP global
mapper [37]. Every onboarding frame is used (no temporal
subsampling), images are not masked to the object, loop
detection is disabled, and the camera model is PINHOLE
with the intrinsics shipped with each dataset, held fixed (no
intrinsic refinement).
We run this on two objects chosen to bracket the difficulty range of the benchmark: obj 000001 of HOPEv2,
a textured grocery item, and obj 000005 of IndustryShapes, the most textureless and specular object in that
dataset.
The estimated trajectory lives in an arbitrary gauge (the
global mapper fixes the world frame and the overall scale),
so it cannot be compared to the reference poses directly.
We recover the similarity transform between the two sets
of camera centers in closed form with the Umeyama algorithm, apply it to the estimated poses, and report the following:
• Absolute rotation error: the geodesic angle between the
reference camera orientation and the aligned estimate.
• Absolute position error: the Euclidean distance between
the reference and aligned camera centers, in millimeters.
• Relative rotation error: the same angle computed on the
relative pose between consecutive frames. This quantity
is invariant to the alignment and measures the local multiview consistency on which the MVS and 3DGS stages

E.2. Reconstruction
Table 9 reports the reconstruction itself, before any comparison to ground truth. Both sequences register completely:
every selected frame is placed into a single model, with no
sub-model fragmentation and sub-pixel mean reprojection
error. Registration is therefore not the failure mode on textureless objects.
What changes dramatically is the density of the sparse
point cloud. The IndustryShapes reconstruction recovers
5,802 points from 522 images (11 points per image) against
78,973 points from 178 images (444 points per image) on
HOPEv2, a fortyfold drop in points yield per frame, which
is precisely the signature of a surface that offers no repeatable features.
To locate those points, we transform the sparse cloud into
the object frame with the similarity transform of Sec. E.1
and test each point against the object’s bounding box. Only
1,117 of the 5,802 points (19.3%) fall inside it, and 1,481
(25.5%) fall within a 25 mm dilation of it. 80% of the reconstruction is the surrounding scene.
This is the mechanism that makes the onboarding stage
robust here, and it is worth stating explicitly: the camera
trajectory is not recovered from the object. Onboarding sequences image the object together with its supporting surface, the calibration target, and the surrounding workspace,
and those features (not the object’s own appearance) constrain the poses. The object’s geometry is then recovered
by the MVS and 3DGS stages, which are driven by the estimated cameras rather than by sparse keypoints on the object. A textureless object, consequently, yields a thin, sparse
cloud without yielding a poor camera trajectory.
E.3. Pose Accuracy
Table 10 compares the estimated trajectories against the
poses provided with the datasets. Ground truth is available
for 482 of the 522 registered frames, and the comparison is
restricted to those.
On the textured object the agreement is close to the annotation noise floor: 0.32◦ and 1.09 mm median error. On
the textureless object, the error grows to 1.36◦ and 9.47 mm
at a mean viewing distance of 447 mm. It is roughly 4×
worse in rotation and 9× worse in position, and it is consistent with the limitation stated in the main paper. What
matters is the form the degradation takes:
• No frame fails. The maximum position error over 482
frames is 22.9 mm, and the maximum rotation error is
3.77◦ . There is no heavy tail and no diverging segment.
• No drift. Figures 8 and 7 show the error varying
smoothly along the sequence and returning to its baseline, rather than accumulating, the signature of a globally
consistent reconstruction rather than a drifting one.
• Local consistency is preserved. The frame-to-frame relative rotation error stays at 0.19◦ (median), an order of
magnitude below the absolute error. Neighboring views,
which is what MVS matching and 3DGS training consume, remain mutually consistent even where the trajectory as a whole is slightly deformed.

G. HOPEv2 Onboarding Data Inconsistencies
The HOPEv2 dataset contains object instances whose physical configuration differs between the onboarding and query
images. In particular, articulated or loosely attached components are not always in the same relative pose across the
two splits. Figure 10 shows an example in which the cap
orientation relative to the printed label differs between the
images from the onboarding and validation sets. In the onboarding image, the cap is rotated sideways, whereas in the
image from the validation set, it is aligned above the label.
This inconsistency creates a mismatch between the object geometry reconstructed from the onboarding sequence
and the object in the query images. For methods that build
an explicit 3D reference representation from the onboarding
data, such as 3DGS-based refinement approaches, this mismatch can affect pose refinement. The rendered reference
model may contain local geometric structures whose relative arrangement does not correspond to the query observation, causing the refinement objective to optimize against
inconsistent geometry.
Methods that rely primarily on global learned features or
2D image-space cues may be less affected by these local
3D discrepancies. Consequently, results on such instances
should be interpreted with care, as the object in the query
images may not exactly match the onboarding-derived ref-

H. Qualitative Comparisons
Figure 11 shows additional qualitative comparisons on the
HOPEv2 test set, complementing the results presented in
the main paper. The examples include challenging scenes
with multiple objects, partial occlusions, clutter, and visually similar packaging, where accurate 6D pose estimation
requires both reliable object localization and precise pose
refinement.
Across these examples, NovaSplat produces predictions
that are better aligned with the visible object extent and orientation than the competing model-free baselines. By optimizing the pose against the reconstructed 3D representation, NovaSplat yields tighter alignment between the projected object geometry and the image evidence.
The comparison also highlights common failure modes
of the baseline methods. GFreeDet2, OPFormer, and
NeMO can produce plausible detections, but their predicted
poses are often less consistent with the object boundaries,
especially under occlusion or when only a limited portion
of the object is visible. In cluttered scenes, these methods
may also exhibit larger orientation errors or less stable box
projections. In contrast, NovaSplat maintains more geometrically consistent predictions, suggesting that the 3DGSbased reference representation provides useful constraints
during refinement.
Like all evaluated methods, NovaSplat may occasionally
produce false positive predictions. These predictions are
primarily inherited from misdetections made by the initial
object detection. Our method attempts to align these erroneous proposals, ultimately preserving them in the final
scene prediction. While NovaSplat mitigates many of the
failure modes seen in competing methods, explicitly filtering out these inherited misdetections remains an interesting
direction for future work.
