Perception‑based, Nonuniform Color Mapping for CT Reading

Abstract
In Brief
Background:
With modern CT technology offering faster acquisition, lower radiation exposure, higher resolution, and advanced detector designs, routine image interpretation still relies largely on gray‑scale windowing, which by design requires frequent manual adjustment. Existing color mappings rely on uniform mathematical gradients and offer no clear advantage over gray‑scale display for tissue or structure perception.
Objective:
To develop and evaluate a software framework supporting interactive exploration of perception‑based color mapping.
Materials and Methods:
A custom research DICOM viewer enabling interactive nonuniform color display was developed. Verification included a contrast‑agent dilution series compared with a clinical viewer and liver phantom CT scans with predefined lesions acquired at varying dose levels and reconstruction settings. Differentiation of tissues and structures in the liver phantom was examined by visual rating after application of different color mappings.
Results:
Quantitative verification using a contrast-agent dilution series confirmed correct mapping of CT values to color. In a phantom-based reader evaluation using liver phantom CT scans, nonuniform color mapping produced stable visual representations and was significantly preferred in subjective visual analysis.
Conclusions:
Unlike uniform color mappings, the proposed approach uses nonuniform color ranges to better match human perception. In CT, this may facilitate visual separation of tissues and structures and was preferred in phantom‑based visual assessment compared with standard uniform display methods.
This study developed and tested a custom DICOM CT viewer that lets users interactively apply nonuniform, perception-based color mappings instead of relying only on traditional gray-scale windowing or uniform color gradients. The software was verified using a contrast-agent dilution series, confirming correct mapping of CT values to color. It was then evaluated with liver phantom CT scans containing predefined lesions acquired at different dose levels and reconstruction settings. Visual ratings showed that nonuniform color mapping produced stable images and was significantly preferred over standard uniform color or gray-scale displays, suggesting it can improve visual separation of tissues and structures in CT.

CT imaging is inherently quantitative. Each voxel represents x‑ray attenuation expressed in Hounsfield units (HU), a standardized scale referenced to water and air.1 This quantitative nature enables tissue characterization, comparison across scanners, and longitudinal follow‑up. Modern CT scanners have become increasingly sophisticated, offering improved spatial resolution, noise reduction, spectral information, and stable HU values.2 Despite these advances, CT images are still viewed primarily in grayscale, using a visualization concept that has changed little since the early development of tomography. Because the wide HU range (approximately −1000 to >3000 HU) exceeds what the human eye can distinguish in grayscale and what medical displays can show as separate levels, radiologists adjust window width and level, often using predefined presets (eg, lung window), to focus on a limited HU range at a time.3 This approach is essential for routine interpretation and is well established in clinical workflows.4–6
Grayscale windowing has a fundamental limitation: only a single HU range can be optimally displayed with a given setting, while structures outside this range appear uniformly black or white despite containing diagnostically relevant additional information. Radiologists, therefore, frequently rewindow images and mentally integrate information from multiple views, particularly when assessing subtle lesions or heterogeneous tissues. Human vision, however, is not equally sensitive to all visual signals. Discrimination of small luminance differences is limited—especially in noisy images—whereas differences in color are detected more reliably and with less cognitive effort.7 One reason for this advantage is that color differences can remain visually distinguishable even when adjacent regions have similar luminance. In particular, complementary colors produce strong perceptual contrast because they stimulate different chromatic processing channels of the visual system. As a result, neighboring structures may remain readily distinguishable despite appearing similar in grayscale. The effectiveness of such color contrasts depends primarily on their separation within the color space rather than on luminance differences alone.8 This perceptual advantage of color is widely used in maps, warning systems, and medical parametric images. In quantitative imaging, carefully designed color scales can improve consistency of interpretation, while poorly chosen color maps may introduce artifacts.9 Nevertheless, current DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) viewers provide limited support for visualization approaches that go beyond uniform display methods.10
To address this gap, an interactive framework was developed for perception-based color mapping, a procedure we termed interactive nonuniform color mapping (INCM). The goal is to make image information visible that remains difficult to perceive with uniform display methods.
Materials and Methods
This technical note was primarily based on phantom experiments. Example images from fully anonymized clinical CT data sets were included. The project did not fall within the scope of the Swiss Federal Act on Research involving Human Beings (Human Research Act, HRA) and therefore was waived by the responsible ethics committee.
Software Implementation
A custom research DICOM viewer was developed by the first author (A.W.L.). The system uses a hybrid design in which all DICOM image data remains on the user’s machine. The browser frontend communicates with a small local service written in Python that handles DICOM access and streams image slices directly through local WebSockets, without any online data transfer. Color mapping and volume rendering are executed inside the browser using its built‑in graphics engine and GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) acceleration, enabling immediate visual updates. This approach combines the speed of local GPU rendering with the accessibility of a web‑based interface, while ensuring that medical image data never leaves the local environment. For metadata and session management, a remote Python server acts as a backend controller.
Color Mapping Workflow
The INCM process was implemented to enable fast, interactive perception-based color mapping with immediate feedback across the full image volume. Users could adjust colors and their positions along the value axis and directly observe how these changes affected the perceptual appearance of tissues and structures throughout the entire image stack in real time (see screenshot, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/RLI/B122). The resulting LUT (lookup table) could also be applied to other image series acquired under comparable conditions, enabling a consistent color impression across studies unless the user intentionally modified the colors or associated HU ranges again. Rather than relying on predefined color maps, the framework itself functions as an interactive LUT editor, enabling users to construct, refine, and evaluate nonuniform color mappings by adjusting both color selection and HU positioning according to the visualization task.
To illustrate how nonuniform color mapping can support visual separation in typical clinical data sets, the perception‑based technique was applied to anonymized clinical CT images during interactive exploration (Fig. 1) and compared with commonly used uniform color mappings (Fig. 2). All renderings were performed on a standard clinical radiology workstation (Dell Precision 3680) using a calibrated medical‑grade display (EIZO RX350).

Evaluation and Verification
Contrast‑agent Dilution Series
To verify that the color mapping reflected measured CT values, a dilution series of iodinated contrast agent (Xenetix 350, Guerbet, Zurich, Switzerland) with 12 concentration levels ranging from 0 to 17.5 mg iodine/mL was scanned on a photon‑counting detector CT system (NAEOTOM Alpha, Siemens Healthineers) using fixed scan settings [Quantum Plus mode, 140 kVp, 5 mGy, quantum iterative reconstruction at a strength level of 4 (QIR‑4)]. The individual tubes were first evaluated in a commercial clinical DICOM viewer (IDS7 Version 25.2, Sectra, Linköping, Sweden), where mean HU attenuation values were measured using ROI (region of interest). These measured values were subsequently used to define the corresponding color settings in the research INCM viewer.
Liver Phantom Reference Standard
A custom liver phantom (QRM GmbH, Möhrendorf, Germany) with predefined lesion locations and nominal HU values was used for further verification. Manufacturer‑provided schematics served as the reference standard for lesion identification and comparison (Fig. 3A).

To assess whether the same color mapping produced consistent visualization under different acquisition conditions, phantom images were acquired on three CT systems from the same vendor: (1) dual-source photon-counting detector CT (NAEOTOM Alpha), (2) dual-source dual-energy CT (SOMATOM Drive), and (3) single-source dual-energy CT (SOMATOM X.ceed). Visual Rating
Visual rating was performed using liver phantom images acquired at 2 radiation dose levels (1.25 and 5 mGy) with the photon-counting detector CT and QIR‑4 reconstruction settings as listed in Table 1. For each dose level, 9 corresponding slice positions were selected. From each slice, 6 image versions were generated: one conventional grayscale windowed image, 3 commonly used uniform color mappings (Hot‑iron, Jet rainbow, and Viridis), originally developed for general scientific and mathematical visualization rather than medical imaging, and 2 perception‑based nonuniform color‑mapped images (Fig. 3B). For the uniform display methods, window width and level were adjusted to cover the attenuation range of the phantom tissues and structures (−300 to 300 HU), whereas nonuniform color mapping was applied without additional windowing. For each image set, all display variants were generated from the same CT dataset and differed only in the visualization method applied.
For each slice position, the 6 images were displayed simultaneously in a 3×2 grid. Slice order and image position within the grid were randomized. Five readers were asked to select the image that provided the best visual separation of tissues and structures. All ratings were performed by radiologists not involved in the project and unfamiliar with the color‑mapping approach. All readers had fewer than 3 years of radiology experience and no subspecialty training.
RESULTS
Quantitative Verification Using Dilution Series
Mean HU values showed a strong linear relationship with iodine concentration (R²=0.9838), confirming the expected behavior of the dilution series. Based on these reference values, the color display reflected CT attenuation as intended (Fig. 4), with a consistent and ordered change in color across tubes.

Reader Preference for Visual Differentiation in Liver Phantom
Across all ratings (5 readers; 18 image sets per reader; n=90 selections), preferences differed markedly between display methods (Table 2). Perception‑based nonuniform color mappings were selected most frequently (78/90, 86.7%), followed by the windowed grayscale display (8/90, 8.9%) and the Jet rainbow color mapping (4/90, 4.4%). The Hot‑iron and Viridis color mappings were not selected (0/90, 0% each).

A binomial test against the category‑proportional null hypothesis (nonuniform vs. uniform) confirmed a highly significant dominance of nonuniform mappings (78 vs. 12 selections, P<0.001).
When stratified by radiation dose (n=45 selections per dose level), nonuniform color mapping accounted for the largest share of selections at both dose levels, with 43/45 selections (95.6%) at 5 mGy and 35/45 selections (77.8%) at 1.25 mGy. Majority preference (≥3 of 5 readers selecting the same display method) was observed in 15 of 18 image sets.
DISCUSSION
The results confirm that the benefit of perception‑based color visualization does not arise from the use of color alone, but from how perceptual contrast is distributed across image values. Uniform display methods, including gray‑scale windowing and commonly used color mappings, do not introduce intended, tissue‑specific contrast; instead, perceptually relevant differences become visible or remain hidden depending on how their values fall within the chosen display mapping. As a result, subtle differences between tissues or structures are visible incidentally rather than systematically.
In contrast, the proposed nonuniform approach allows perceptual contrast to be placed intentionally at ranges of interest through interactive exploration. This enables subtle changes in tissues and structures to be visually separated in a controlled and reproducible manner, with reduced dependence on overall image appearance. Once defined, the mapping remains stable across the image volume and comparable acquisitions, supporting consistent perception when navigating large data sets or comparing scans. From a conceptual perspective, this differs from established medical color visualization techniques such as spectral CT iodine maps, dual-energy overlays, or perfusion maps. These approaches typically display derived quantitative information or fuse additional parameters with the underlying image data. In contrast, the present method does not generate or add new information. Instead, it modifies the visualization of the original CT attenuation values to improve perceptual differentiation of existing image information. The underlying quantitative image data remain unchanged, while perceptual contrast can be redistributed according to the visualization task.
An additional consideration is color vision deficiency. Because the proposed framework allows free selection and placement of colors, the visualization is not restricted to a single color palette. Color sets specifically designed for accessibility, such as Viridis, may be incorporated while maintaining the principle of nonuniform placement of color transitions across selected attenuation ranges. Future work should evaluate optimized color schemes for readers with different forms of color vision deficiency.
Several limitations should be noted. Verification was performed using dilution series and phantom data rather than large patient data sets, which is appropriate for demonstrating technical accuracy and perceptual consistency but does not permit conclusions regarding diagnostic performance. Furthermore, the reader evaluation assessed subjective preference rather than diagnostic performance. Complete blinding was not possible because the visualization methods were visually recognizable. Consequently, reader selections may have been influenced by novelty effects or visual salience. In addition, verification was limited to CT systems from a single manufacturer; however, the approach operates on standard HU values and is therefore not vendor‑specific by design. Further evaluation in clinical data sets is needed to assess the impact on visual separation of tissues and structures, reader confidence, and agreement.
CONCLUSIONS
Nonuniform color mapping enables deliberate placement of perceptual contrast and was preferred by readers in phantom-based visual assessment compared with uniform display methods. While verified here for CT, the underlying principle is transferable to other quantitative imaging modalities.
