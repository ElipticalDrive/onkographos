Before any segmentation model, registration pipeline, or radiomics extraction is applied, medical images usually undergo a series of preprocessing steps. One of the most fundamental among them is resampling. It is a step that shapes how reliably images can be compared, analyzed, and interpreted. Yet because it often happens quietly in the background, its importance can be underestimated.
Resampling is, at its core, an operation that changes the voxel grid of an image: its voxel spacing, its matrix size, or the coordinate system onto which it is mapped. Although this may sound simple, these geometric decisions influence almost every downstream computation. A radiomics feature computed on anisotropic MRI slices is not the same as one computed on an isotropically resampled volume. A deep-learning model trained on 1 mm CT voxels will not behave as expected if given 3 mm slices. And images from different modalities cannot meaningfully be fused or subtracted unless they live in the same coordinate frame. To find out what matrix (Shape) and voxel sizes (VoxelSize) your specific image is, highlight the image in the process and look at the Properties panel.
Because of this, most practical workflows begin with resampling. Sometimes we are standardizing voxel sizes across a dataset, sometimes we are preparing images for a neural network that expects a fixed input shape and sometimes we are aligning different images of the same patient. Hero can help you accomplish all these aims.
Resampling to a specific voxel size
One of the most common motivations for resampling is the need for consistent voxel spacing. Clinical images are often highly variable in this respect. CT scans may have fine in-plane resolution but thick slices, MRIs may have excellent soft-tissue contrast but anisotropic voxels, and datasets across patients may differ widely.
Standardizing voxel size creates a predictable geometric environment for further analysis. Radiomics pipelines benefit from consistent voxel spacing because several texture descriptors are sensitive to anisotropy. Three-dimensional deep learning models require isotropic voxels to interpret structures without unintended distortions. Even simpler operations can behave more predictably when voxel spacing is uniform.
In Hero, resampling to a chosen voxel size becomes a straightforward operation. After loading an image, you connect the output of the image to the node Resample: Voxel Size and specify the desired spacing as [x, y, z] in mm and interpolation method in the Node Settings panel. Once you’ve run the process, you can see the results of the resampling the Preview Panel.
Resampling to a specific matrix size
While voxel size is important for geometric consistency, certain workflows care more about the final matrix dimensions. Deep neural networks, for example, usually require a fixed input size: often 128×128×128 or 256×256×256, depending on the architecture. In these cases, the physical size of the image is less important than ensuring that every input volume is shaped identically.
Resampling to a target matrix size can be understood as defining a fixed grid and asking the image to conform to it, with Hero automatically determining the voxel spacing needed to fill that grid. This is especially useful when preparing large collections of images for training or inference, where uniformity is essential. In practice, the node Resampling: Matrix Size is connected to the image output and you input the desired output matrix dimension as [i, j, k] voxels in the Node Settings panel.
Resampling into a reference image coordinate system
A third and equally important use case arises when combining or comparing two images. Two scans of the same patient—perhaps a CT and an MRI, or a baseline and follow-up MRI—may contain the same anatomy, but their voxel grids do not align. Origins differ, orientations may not match, and even minor geometric discrepancies can undermine voxelwise comparisons.
Resampling one image into the coordinate system of another ensures that they share the same spatial frame. The reference image defines the geometry: its voxel size, its matrix dimensions, its orientation, and its physical origin. The source image is then interpolated into this grid.
This step is essential in multimodal fusion, atlas propagation, longitudinal studies, dose mapping, and many other applications. In Hero, you input the image that you want to resample to the input port in the Resample to Reference node, and the reference image in the reference port and run the workflow.
The value of making resampling visible
Although resampling is sometimes treated as a routine housekeeping step, it has real consequences for the analyses that follow. Using linear interpolation on label maps, for example, will blur boundaries and invalidate segmentations. Misaligned orientations can quietly introduce systematic errors that are difficult to detect after the fact. And undocumented resampling decisions make it harder to compare results across studies or reproduce published workflows.
One of the advantages of doing this work in Hero is that the entire process becomes visible. Every parameter is represented as a node with every choice recorded in the graph. This explicitness encourages good habits, reduces ambiguity, and allows users to build pipelines that can be shared, audited, and reused.
Summary
Resampling is fundamental to medical image analysis because it establishes the geometric foundation on which every subsequent computation rests. Hero provides a way to carry out this step clearly and consistently, whether the goal is harmonizing voxel sizes, standardizing matrix dimensions, or aligning images across modalities.
As you expand your workflows—into segmentation, registration, or more advanced processing—resampling is often a prerequisite. By understanding its motivations and implementing it carefully in Hero, you set the stage for analyses that are both technically sound and reproducible.
You can try running the included Hero process to see the different types of resampling in action. Make sure to play around with the settings to see differences in interpolation – but be aware that some interpolation types are computationally very demanding (especially all of the Windowed Sinc methods).
