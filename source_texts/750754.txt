Abstract
We propose Squeeze3D, a novel framework that leverages implicit prior knowledge learnt by
existing pre-trained encoders and decoders to compress 3D data at extremely high compression
1

ratios. Our approach bridges the latent spaces between a pre-trained encoder and a pretrained decoder model through trainable mapping networks. Any 3D asset represented as
a mesh, point cloud, or radiance field is first encoded by the pre-trained encoder and then
transformed (i.e. compressed) into a highly compact latent code by a mapping network. This
latent code can effectively be used as an extremely compressed representation of the mesh,
point cloud, or radiance field. A mapping network transforms the compressed latent code
into the latent space of a powerful generative model; the decoder of this generative model
then recreates the original 3D asset (i.e. decompression). Squeeze3D is trained entirely on
generated synthetic data and does not require any 3D datasets. The Squeeze3D architecture
can be flexibly used with existing pre-trained 3D encoders and existing generative models. It
can flexibly support different formats, including meshes, point clouds, and radiance fields.
Our experiments demonstrate that Squeeze3D achieves compression ratios of up to 2187×
for textured meshes, 58.5× for point clouds, and more than 650× for radiance fields while
maintaining visual quality comparable to many existing methods. Squeeze3D only incurs a
small compression and decompression latency since it does not involve training object-specific
networks to compress an object.

Introduction
The rapid advancement of 3D data acquisition and representation technologies over the past decade has
significantly expanded the availability and generation of high-resolution 3D content across various domains
in different formats, including meshes, point clouds, and radiance fields (which could be extracted from a
NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2021) or a 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023)). The widespread use of 3D data necessitates
the development of techniques that enable efficient transmission, storage, and processing of large-scale 3D
representations. To this end, compression and the use of compressed representations for 3D data are of utmost
importance, e.g., in streaming, autonomous navigation, digital twins, remote sensing.
A large body of work proposes techniques to compress meshes, point clouds, neural radiance fields
(NeRFs) (Mildenhall et al., 2021), and 3D Gaussian Splats (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 2023). These approaches
aim to maximize the compression ratio while retaining reconstruction quality. For example, traditional mesh
decimation techniques (Schroeder et al., 1992; Garland & Heckbert, 1997; Li et al., 2014; Lescoat et al.,
2020) remain foundational for polygon reduction, but their reliance on handcrafted simplification rules limits
their ability to preserve fine geometric details at extreme compression ratios. MPEG’s G-PCC and V-PCC
standards (Schwarz et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019) use projection-based methods for point cloud compression;
however, these approaches incur overheads for representing fine details and packing. Prior works also propose
a range of compression methods for NeRFs (Li et al., 2024; Edavamadathil Sivaram et al., 2024; Takikawa
et al., 2022; Müller et al., 2022) or 3DGS models (Fan et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024). Several works propose
autoencoder-style networks that compress 3D models into small latent vectors (Zhang et al., 2025a; Hahner
& Garcke, 2022; Zhou et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2025a). The maximum compression ratios achieved by these
methods are of the order of 100× for meshes and of the order of 10× for point clouds, but typically much
lower.
Our goal is to develop a framework for extreme compression of 3D data stored in any format while retaining
high visual quality. We emphasize that Squeeze3D is designed to compress arbitrary, real-world 3D assets
(e.g., artist-created meshes and 3D scans). Such extreme ratios are most valuable at scale: streaming or
archiving collections of millions of 3D assets, delivering content to bandwidth-constrained or on-device
clients, and settings where the decoder is a shared, one-time asset that is amortized across all compressed
objects (Section 4.7). Recent years have seen significant and continued advances and development of powerful
generative models. In this work, we aim to leverage the implicit prior knowledge learnt by the decoders of
powerful 3D generative models (Xu et al., 2024; Jun & Nichol, 2023) to enable extreme compression ratios.
Recent works also propose techniques to leverage generative models for 3D compression (Zhang et al., 2024a;
Cui et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; van den Oord et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2025b; Chang et al., 2025;
Roessle et al., 2024), and one achieves extreme compression for meshes (Zhang et al., 2024a). However, these
approaches require training specialized encoders and generative models for a single 3D format. In contrast, our

goal is to flexibly use existing encoders and decoder models that provide adaptability as encoders/generative
models evolve and flexibility across 3D formats.
We propose Squeeze3D, a compression framework that generates a highly compressed latent vector that
can be used to recreate the original 3D data using an existing pre-trained decoder model. Squeeze3D
comprises three key components: (1) the input 3D data is encoded with a pre-trained encoder. This allows
us to extend Squeeze3D to other 3D encoders. (2) We train two small neural networks that we call the
forward mapping network and the reverse mapping network. The forward mapping network maps the encoded
representations into an extremely compressed latent space. The reverse mapping network converts the code
from the compressed latent space to the latent space of the generative model. (3) We use a pre-trained
decoder of a generation model to generate the original 3D data using the code generated by the reverse
mapping network. Squeeze3D can be flexibly implemented with any pre-trained encoder and decoder of a
generative model.
The forward and reverse mapping networks are trained for any given encoder-generator pair. We first
synthetically generate a 3D dataset via random prompts to the generator model. This 3D dataset is encoded
using the pre-trained encoder. The set of latents produced from the pre-trained encoders (training inputs)
and their corresponding latents from the pre-trained generator (ground truth) are used to train the forward
and reverse mapping networks. We propose a loss function that minimizes redundant information in the
compressed latent space.
Squeeze3D can be flexibly applied to 3D data in different formats. We implement and evaluate our method
for mesh, point cloud, and radiance field compression using 3 existing encoders and 5 existing generative
models. We demonstrate that our method achieves compression ratios significantly higher than or on par with
those of existing compression techniques for meshes, point clouds, and radiance fields, with reconstruction
quality that is on par with many prior approaches. We demonstrate a compression ratio of 2187× for a
subset of the Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023) dataset, 58.5× on a subset of ShapeNet (Chang et al., 2015),
and more than 650× across our radiance-field evaluations (Irshad et al., 2024). While Squeeze3D expectedly
cannot achieve state-of-the-art reconstruction quality, we qualitatively show that it is able to retain high
visual quality in many settings.
Contributions. (1) To the best of our knowledge, this is the first framework that leverages pre-existing,
pre-trained generative-model decoders to enable extreme compression of 3D data.
(2) We demonstrate the feasibility of aligning different latent spaces originating from neural networks with
different structures, optimization objectives, and training distributions. To make this bridging robust at
extreme compression, we introduce a Gram loss that prevents the compressed latent from collapsing onto a few
dominant directions (which we show is essential in our ablations), together with a synthetic data-generation
recipe that trains the mapping networks without requiring any 3D datasets.
(3) We evaluate Squeeze3D for mesh, point cloud, and radiance field compression and demonstrate that
generative models are a promising approach for extreme compression of 3D models. Squeeze3D can be
flexibly extended to different encoders, generative models, and 3D formats.

Related Works
2.1
Neural Graphic Primitives
Neural networks are increasingly being used to represent 2D images (Sitzmann et al., 2020; Tancik et al.,
2020; Gao & Jaiman, 2024), 3D objects and scenes (Sitzmann et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2020;
Tancik et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2022; Davies et al., 2021; Mildenhall et al., 2021; Martel et al., 2021), surface
representations (Takikawa et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023; Rakotosaona et al., 2024; Edavamadathil Sivaram
et al., 2024), occupancy networks (Mescheder et al., 2019; Chen & Zhang, 2019), and signed distance
fields (Michalkiewicz et al., 2019; Park et al., 2019; Atzmon & Lipman, 2020). These methods, out of the
box, can also be used to compress 3D models in some format since the learned neural network weights are
often already significantly smaller. Many NGP compression methods often employ standard neural network

techniques to compress MLPs by knowledge distillation (Shi & Guillemot, 2022), pruning (Isik, 2021; Xie
et al., 2023; Jung et al., 2024), quantization (Shen & McClinton, 2023; Zhong et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023;
Gordon et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b), factorizing tensor grids (Gao et al., 2023; Obukhov et al., 2024),
low-rank approximation (Shi & Guillemot, 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2025), and
using codebooks (Li et al., 2023b;a) for quantization. Another approach is to compress feature grids or
learnable embeddings (Shin & Park, 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Pham & Mandt, 2024) or extracted voxels (Wang
et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022), rather than compressing the MLP. Another set of approaches combines many
of these orthogonal improvements to compressing NeRFs (Li et al., 2024). However, these methods often
require training networks per scene or object, incurring significant compression latencies.
2.2
3D Autoencoders and Generators
Autoencoders encode inputs into a latent space and then decode them back into the original input space
using an encoder-decoder pair; thus, they could be used as compression algorithms. Early approaches relied
on volumetric representations, discretizing 3D shapes into voxel grids to leverage ConvNets for encoding and
decoding (Liu et al., 2020b; Hess et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023c; Brock et al., 2016; Molnár & Tamás, 2024).
While these approaches are effective for regular grid structures, these methods faced scalability challenges due
to cubic memory growth with resolution increases. Subsequent advancements focused on spectral methods for
encoding 3D shapes in frequency domains, offering compact latent representations but requiring precomputed
basis functions that limited generalizability across shape categories (Zamorski et al., 2020; Park et al., 2019).
Several works (Zhao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025d; Wu et al., 2024; Lan et al., 2024) use a VAE (Kingma &
Welling, 2022) to compress 3D data into a compact latent which could be used as a compressed representation.
There also exist many approaches that train generators to reconstruct radiance fields (Kosiorek et al., 2021;
Irshad et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2025b), Gaussians (Wewer et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025b),
or voxels (Ren et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2025b). Some recent works also pose the problem as learning in a
token-space (Chang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025c; Siddiqui et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a; 2022; Yang
et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023; Liang & Liang, 2022; Huo et al., 2025; Marques & Da Silva Cruz, 2022; Cui
et al., 2023), or with diffusion models (Zhang et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2024; Roessle et al., 2024; Galvis et al.,
2024; Ramirez-Jaime et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024). One such generative model (Zhang et al., 2024a) is able
to compress meshes with high compression ratios. A rapidly growing line of recent shape VAEs and native
3D generators learns increasingly compact latents with high-quality reconstruction (Zhang et al., 2023; Li
et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025b; Cho et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Xiang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025d).
Auto-decoder approaches such as ROAD (Zakharov et al., 2023) likewise encode shapes into compact codes,
but require a per-shape optimization at encoding time. Compared to these methods that use autoencoders
or generative models for compression, Squeeze3D does not require training specialized encoder-generators
for each representation, nor any per-shape optimization at encode time. Instead, Squeeze3D aims to use
existing encoders and generative models, and is in fact complementary to this line of work: any of these
stronger generators can be adopted as the decoder within our framework. This enables flexibly adapting the
approach as encoders and generative models evolve and supporting different 3D formats.
The work closest to our method is Generative Latent Coding (GLC) (Jia et al., 2024), which trains an
autoencoder-style generative model to compress images, particularly compressing the latent representations
for an image obtained through VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017). However, this method is not designed for
3D data, requires access to an encoder and decoder trained jointly, and only tests one encoder-decoder pair.
3
Method
In this work, we introduce Squeeze3D, a technique to generate highly compressed representations of 3D
models by leveraging the implicit prior knowledge learnt by decoders of 3D generative models. We also
leverage the availability of many 3D encoders to support an extensible set of 3D formats.
The Squeeze3D architecture is summarized in Figure 2. The key ideas of Squeeze3D are as follows. (1)
We leverage existing 3D encoders to generate encoded representations for a given 3D format. Thus, the
Squeeze3D architecture can be extended to support different 3D formats by using new or existing encoders.

Mapping networks offer two major benefits over autoencoders and encoder-generator pairs, such as MeshAnything (Chen et al., 2025c): (1) the mapping networks typically provide significantly higher compression ratios
than existing VAE approaches; and (2) they provide more flexibility in the choice of 3D format. For instance,
InstantMesh and LRM require multi-view images as input rather than a mesh, so they cannot be used directly
as 3D-to-3D autoencoders, whereas the mapping-network design lets us pair them with any 3D encoder.
3.1
Bridging Latent Spaces
Squeeze3D comprises two pre-trained models: (1) a 3D encoder E that maps 3D representations to a latent
space, and (2) a 3D generator G that synthesizes 3D models of the same initial representation. We use the
term “generator” to refer to the model whose weights and learnt priors we leverage; at decompression time we
only use its decoder component, G, which maps a latent code to a 3D asset. For latent-diffusion generators
such as LION (Zeng et al., 2022), this means we use the decoder that maps a latent to a point cloud and
bypass the diffusion sampler entirely. Consequently, decompression is a deterministic feed-forward pass: a
given compressed code always reconstructs the same 3D asset. For a given 3D representation (mesh, point

3.2
Training Squeeze3D
In order to train the Squeeze3D architecture, we need to train the mapping networks for any given pair of
pre-trained 3D encoders and pre-trained 3D generator models. To train these mapping networks, we need
training samples from the encoder’s latent space and the corresponding latents in the generator’s latent space.
These latents in the generator’s latent space serve as “ground truth” samples during the training process.
We summarize our training process in Figure 3. We now describe how to generate a training dataset with
samples from both of these latent spaces.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
We train Squeeze3D to compress three 3D formats: textured 3D meshes, point clouds, and radiance fields,
i.e., grids of (rgbσ). For 3D meshes, we train our approach with MeshAnything (Chen et al., 2025c) as the
encoder and train mapping networks for three 3D generators: Shap-E (Jun & Nichol, 2023), OpenLRM (Hong
et al., 2024; He & Wang, 2023), and InstantMesh (Xu et al., 2024). For point clouds, we train mapping
networks for PointNet++ (Qi et al., 2017) as the encoder and LION (Zeng et al., 2022) as the decoder. For
radiance fields, we train mapping networks for NeRF-MAE (Irshad et al., 2024) as both the encoder and the
generator. We present additional implementation details in Section A and the mapping-network architecture
in Section A.1.

4.3
Compression Ratio and Reconstruction Quality Trade-off
To better understand the trade-off between compression ratio and reconstruction quality, we analyze the
performance of Squeeze3D against Draco (Galligan et al., 2018) across different compression settings. While
many neural compression methods do not offer tunable compression ratios, Squeeze3D allows for different
compression levels by varying the size of the compressed latent representation. Similarly, Draco offers tunable
compression through its quantization parameters. In Figure 6, we plot the Pareto frontier for both methods,
showing the relationship between compression ratio (x-axis) and reconstruction quality (y-axis, measured
by LPIPS). We observe that Draco (Galligan et al., 2018) can only achieve compression ratios which are
orders of magnitude lower than Squeeze3D, and as its compression ratio increases, its quality degrades
significantly. In contrast, Squeeze3D maintains high visual quality. We further analyze the effect of the
compressed-latent size on quality in Appendix C.5 (Table 18).
For applications where fine geometric details are critical, such as CAD and cultural heritage, Squeeze3D may
be less suitable; for applications prioritizing storage or bandwidth, such as streaming and asset libraries, it
offers a compelling option. We also vary dC for point clouds and report bits per input point versus PointSSIM
in Appendix C.2 (Figure 10). Each point on the Squeeze3D curves uses its own trained mapping pair. We
do not report an analogous radiance-field sweep: increasing dC substantially enlarges the 3D convolutional
mapping networks, and training enough larger operating points was computationally infeasible in our setup.
Point-cloud mapping networks are smaller, making this sweep practical. Beyond storage, mapping into the
generator’s semantic latent space yields a representation that supports operations unavailable to standard
codecs such as Draco: as shown in Appendix C.1 (Figure 9), the compressed codes of two objects can be
linearly interpolated to produce smooth, plausible intermediate shapes, suggesting the learnt compressed
space is well-structured and could enable downstream tasks such as 3D asset mixing.

We perform an additional experiment to compare Squeeze3D against a baseline that uses the native encoder
and decoder of the generative models themselves for compression. This baseline effectively uses the latent

proxy for the quality of the “uncompressed” generator latent: it quantifies how much quality Squeeze3D
gives up to obtain its much smaller code. (Note there is no single uncompressed latent to compare against

4.7
Decoder Cost and Amortization
Following standard compression conventions, our reported ratios do not include the size of the pre-trained
decoder, just as a codec is not counted toward the size of each compressed file. The decoder in Squeeze3D
is a general, object-agnostic asset, stored once and reused to decompress every object and usable for other
tasks such as generation, so we do not count it toward the compressed size. We list the total decoder sizes
in Table 8. These one-time costs can be amortized after hundreds of objects for meshes, tens for radiance
fields, and thousands for point clouds.
5
Discussion and Limitations
The most significant limitation of our approach is its inherent dependency on the quality and expressiveness
of the underlying 3D generative model. The decompressed outputs from Squeeze3D fundamentally cannot
exceed the quality of what the generator can produce, as the generator serves as both a prior knowledge base
and a quality ceiling. In our evaluation, we observed that reconstruction fidelity is directly correlated with
the generative capabilities of the chosen generator model.

Since Squeeze3D is designed as a compression-decompression framework that should not require per-scene
retraining, the generalization capability of our mapping networks to unseen 3D models is crucial. We
evaluated this aspect by testing on a dataset of 500 3D meshes and 227 radiance fields, which served as
out-of-distribution samples, and found that our approach maintains consistent performance (see Section C).
Nevertheless, for certain outlier cases where the input 3D model contains features far from the distribution
seen during training, compression quality may degrade. Encoder-limited failures may benefit from multiscale
encoders (Dagli et al., 2026) or higher-resolution input sampling, while generator-limited failures require
a broader prior or a conventional fallback codec. A practical deployment could use the native encoder’s
reconstruction error or an OOD detector to identify risky inputs and route them to a standard codec.
6
Conclusion
In this work, we answer: How can we bridge the latent spaces of different models? We do so with losses
in the latent space, without any reconstruction-based losses. As an application, we apply this to 3D. We
introduce Squeeze3D, a framework for 3D compression that leverages the rich priors in existing 3D generation
models, bridging arbitrary latent spaces between different models to enable extreme compression ratios while
maintaining high visual fidelity. By mapping between latent spaces, we avoid the per-scene optimization
that plagues many neural compression methods, yielding faster compression and decompression than learned
approaches that train a network per object (e.g., NGF), at competitive visual quality, though the wall-clock
time remains dominated by the chosen encoder and decoder. We hope this work inspires more research on
using generative models for compressing 3D data.
Broader Impact Statement
Compression with generative priors can reconstruct geometry that is perceptually plausible but not faithful
to the original: fine details, text, and structures outside the generator’s prior may be smoothed, altered, or
hallucinated. Consequently, Squeeze3D should not be used where exact geometry is safety-critical without
fidelity checks and a fallback to a conventional codec or the original asset.

We use two skip-connection schemes: (i) the global residual is re-added immediately after the first hidden
layer, and (ii) every fourth hidden layer receives a local residual that adds its own input to its output, creating
short four-layer paths. A final linear projection transforms the resulting representation into the target latent

Radiance Fields. The mapping networks together consist of a custom LayerNorm that first permutes
tensors so that LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) can operate across channels before restoring the usual NCDHW
layout. We then build ResidualBlocks, which mirror a bottleneck ResNet-V2 design (He et al., 2016): a
3 × 3 × 3 conv–LN–GELU (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2023) stem, a 1 × 1 × 1 → 3 × 3 × 3 bottleneck branch that
doubles the channel count, and two skip paths: (i) projection shortcut for stride/width mismatches and (ii)
“within-block” residual that re-adds the pre-bottleneck activation just before the final GELU. The encoder
begins with a 96-channel input, expands to 192 channels, and applies two strided ResidualBlock units to
down-sample from 403 latents to 203 and then 103 . Thus we have a narrow bottleneck of 24 channels that
serves as the latent code. The symmetric decoder inverts this pathway with a 3 × 3 × 3 convolution, residual
processing, and two transposed-conv up-sampling stages that restore the original resolution, all regularised
by spatial Dropout in 3D. U-Net–style (Ronneberger et al., 2015) skip connections add encoder feature maps
to decoder activations whenever spatial dimensions match.
In all cases the reverse mapping network produces a single flat vector whose dimensionality equals the
flattened size of the target decoder’s latent, and a fixed reshape maps it to the structure the decoder expects.
Thus, we use no decoder-specific architectures. For InstantMesh and LRM, the decoder takes a triplane, so
the final linear layer produces 3 × 80 × 64 × 64 values that are reshaped into the (3, 80, 64, 64) triplane tensor
before being passed to the frozen triplane decoder. For LION, whose latent is an unordered set rather than a

Our results are collected on a machine with either 1×NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU or 1 − 4× H100 GPUs. We
list the choices we make during training for each encoder-generator pair.
For all the models we train with MeshAnything (Chen et al., 2025c) as the encoder, we use the 350-millionparameter version of MeshAnything and use the features created by MeshAnything as the encoded inputs.
These encoded inputs (257, 1024) are relatively larger compared to our desired compression size.
MeshAnything (Chen et al., 2025c)-InstantMesh (Xu et al., 2024). While generating the dataset,
we ensure that the input condition images are in the RGBA format and have no background. We experiment

when generating triplane representations; thus, we report results for this setting.
MeshAnything (Chen et al., 2025c)-Open LRM (Hong et al., 2024; He & Wang, 2023). While

MeshAnything (Chen et al., 2025c)-Shap-E (Jun & Nichol, 2023). We experiment with Shap-E in

Evaluation Details
Squeeze3D leverages pre-trained generative priors strictly as a decoding mechanism for compressing arbitrary
3D data, distinct from compressing the native outputs of generative models. Direct native encoder-decoder
comparisons are possible for Shap-E, LION, and NeRF-MAE, as reported in Section 4.6. They are not
available for feed-forward image-to-3D models such as InstantMesh and LRM, which lack native 3D encoders
and would require a lossy intermediate rendering step.
B.1
Metrics
For all mesh and radiance-field experiments, the image-quality metrics (PSNR, MS-SSIM, LPIPS) are
computed on rendered views: we render both the reference and the decompressed asset from 36 viewpoints
placed on a sphere using a Fibonacci lattice, at a resolution of 512 × 512 with a perspective camera and
Phong shading, and average each metric over all views and all objects. The geometric distortion metrics
(Chamfer Distance, EMD) are computed on 2048 points sampled from each surface, with both point sets
normalized into the unit cube [0, 1]3 .

We compare Squeeze3D against established compression methods for each representation, described below.
For radiance fields we compare against codecs applicable to arbitrary (rgbσ) grids, as most NeRF and 3D
Gaussian Splat (3DGS)-specific methods do not apply to our setting.
Draco. Draco (Galligan et al., 2018) is a non-learned codec that we apply to both meshes and point clouds.
We always use a compression level of 10 and report several quantization settings, given as the number of bits
for the position / texture-coordinate / normal / generic attributes: § uses 14/14/14/14, ‡ uses 11/10/8/8, †
uses 7/7/7/7, and ∗ uses 4/4/4/4. For meshes we report all four settings; for point clouds we report the ‡
and § settings.
Corto. We compress meshes with Corto (Lab, 2025) using 4-bit quantization for both the normal and
texture-coordinate attributes, with the texture image stored separately. The compressed size is read directly
from the encoder output.

Neural Subdivision. Neural Subdivision (Liu et al., 2020a) learns to refine a coarse mesh. We train the
per-object network for 700 epochs (learning rate 2 × 10−3 ) and apply 2 subdivision levels at inference; its
three sub-networks (initialization, edge, and vertex) each use two hidden layers of width 32.
DeepSDF. DeepSDF (Park et al., 2019) represents a shape as a latent code decoded by an 8-layer, width-512
MLP (the code is injected at the middle layer, with dropout 0.2), trained with code regularization for 2001
epochs. The compressed representation is the per-shape 256-dimensional FP-32 latent code (1 KB).
Neural Geometry Fields (NGF). NGF (Edavamadathil Sivaram et al., 2024) fits a neural displacement
field over a coarse base mesh. We use 1000 patches with 20-dimensional feature vectors and optimize each
object over base resolutions {4, 8, 12, 16} for 100 iterations each (Adam, learning rate 10−3 ); the compressed
size is the serialized feature stream.
3DShape2VecSet. We use the pre-trained shape autoencoder of 3DShape2VecSet (Zhang et al., 2023) (the
kl_d512_m512_l8 variant) as a learned mesh-compression baseline. To compress a mesh, we sample 2048
points on its surface and encode them into a set of 512 latent vectors of dimension 8; this 512 × 8 latent is the
compressed geometry. To decompress, we evaluate the decoder’s occupancy field on a 1283 grid and extract
the surface using Marching Cubes.
G-PCC. G-PCC (Schwarz et al., 2018) is the MPEG geometry-based point-cloud coding standard, which we
evaluate on point clouds using the TMC13 reference software.
V-PCC. V-PCC (Liu et al., 2019) is the MPEG video-based point-cloud coding standard, evaluated with
the TMC2 reference software. We encode with a 3D coordinate bit depth of 10 and geometry QP 32, a single
8-bit colour attribute at QP 28, occupancy-map precision 4, and a maximum depth of 12.
SparsePCGC. SparsePCGC (Wang et al., 2023) is a multiscale sparse-convolution codec. For point clouds,
we evaluate the released checkpoints and report the resulting operating point in Tables 3 and 14. For
radiance-field (rgbσ) grids, we use its 8-stage model with kernel size 5 and obtain different rate points by
varying the position quantization scale.
VQRF. VQRF (Li et al., 2023a) compresses radiance-field grids through voxel pruning and vector quantization.
We use a codebook of 4096 entries together with importance-based voxel pruning (inclusion and pruning
thresholds of 0.6 and 0.999).

Additional Related Works
While we are interested in learning-based methods for compression, we include classical compression techniques,
which are only tangentially related to our work, for completeness. These include approaches that reorder the
structure of triangles and faces in the mesh to enable compressed encodings of elements based on their local
structure and perform quantization (Deering, 1995; Taubin & Rossignac, 1998; Rossignac, 1999; Touma &
Gotsman, 1998; Galligan et al., 2018). Most of these techniques are lossless and thus achieve high fidelity;
however, they are inherently limited in their ability to significantly reduce file sizes since they preserve all
details of the mesh representation. To overcome the limitations of lossless compression, lossy techniques have
emerged as popular alternatives. Geometry simplification methods aim to reduce the number of polygons in a
mesh while retaining as much of the original structure as possible (Lescoat et al., 2020; Garland & Heckbert,
1997; Surazhsky & Gotsman, 2003; Szymczak et al., 2002). Extensions of these methods have incorporated
surface intrinsic properties, such as the mesh Laplacian, as a basis for simplification (Lescoat et al., 2020) or
error metrics (Garland & Heckbert, 1997; Cohen et al., 1998) and coupled this with entropy coding (Lab,
2025). Recently, there have also been approaches that couple these with learned models (Potamias et al.,
2022).
Traditional subdivision algorithms (Zorin et al., 1996; Catmull & Clark, 1998; Loop, 1987) refine coarse
meshes by splitting polygonal faces into finer elements, often paired with displacement mapping (Hoppe
et al., 1994) to enhance detail. However, these methods rely on hard-coded priors and fixed polynomial
