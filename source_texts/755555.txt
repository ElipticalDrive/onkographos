With the introduction of the RTX 20 series in 2018, programming specialized shaders and API [23] integration has become
widespread among developers. Dedicated ray tracing cores
paved the way for realistic reflections and effects that were
not attainable before when it comes to interactive media [24].
Nowadays, artificial intelligence aided denoisers and image
reconstruction algorithms swayed the focus towards hardware
integration of detailed AI components in GPUs [25]. While
hardware acceleration and AI denoising have made real-time
ray tracing computationally feasible, this milestone alone
does not guarantee a usable interactive experience. Frame rate
is frequently treated as the sole indicator of real-time viability,
yet recent studies show that consistency of frame delivery
affects user experience independently of average throughput
[20].
This paper presents an efficient and scalable ray tracing system which is optimized for rendering complex scenes and
does not present high f/s variance. Its performant, hybrid
essence emanates from dividing the workflow into separate
stages - geometry, ray tracing, image reconstruction, composition - that communicate and are intrinsically entire pipelines
[12]. The ray tracing stage focuses on modeling the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF), omitting
transmittance and non-opaque objects addressed by the bidirectional scattering distribution function (BSDF) or the bidirectional scattering surface reflectance distribution function
(BSSRDF). As 1 sample per pixel (spp) ray tracing is inherently noisy, this system adopts Schied’s et al. [29] spatiotemporal variance guided filter and modifies it to support separate
diffuse and specular lighting filtering.
This architecture is evaluated not just on theoretical visual
fidelity, but also on strict performance scaling and end-user
usability. By rigorously profiling hardware execution times
across discrete pipeline stages, identifying geometric bottlenecks, and measuring temporal variance to account for microstuttering, it is demonstrated a resilient rendering application.
ABSTRACT
Ray Tracing (RT) is a widely used technique in cinema, architecture, and engineering due to its elegant nature. It simulates
the behavior of light in a simple and precise way. Path tracing
had a breakthrough in the media industry with the release
of Monsters University. Real-time ray tracing has not been
viable before the introduction of the Turing architecture in
2018. Until that point, wholly photorealistic images were only
possible using offline approaches. In this day and age, developing a high-performing, consistent ray tracing system with
a denoiser that does not take a great toll on performance is
crucial in order to deliver a qualitative user experience. The
system described within this paper provides an overview of a
low sample per pixel ray tracing integration, a practice that
facilitates performance and stability, but introduces variance,
which translates to visual noise, mitigated by SVGF. The usability of the application, expressed in terms of low-stuttering
sequence frames and below or equal to 16 milliseconds processing image times, will be discussed, as well.

Introduction
Real-time rendering applications are held to a strict standard
of producing a new image, on average, every 16.6 milliseconds to sustain a 60 frames per second experience considered
acceptable for interactive use. Until recently, ray tracing was
mainly used in offline rendering, for a single frame consumed
hours of processing across thousands of CPU cores [8]. Interactive applications were restricted to using rasterization,
because it was not feasible to render realistic images due
to the lack of computation power and dedicated ray tracing
hardware components. The first documented iteration of ray
tracing is Appel’s [3] introduction of point-by-point Lambertian shading. Nevertheless, to achieve realistic light in
different mediums, a recursive approach is required [35]. The
difficulty of implementing accurate illumination algorithms
stems from the computational hurdles a system endures during execution. Infinite recursion is an insurmountable idea in
development, and, despite this limitation, Kajiya’s early path
tracer [16] still serves as the reference formulation of future
proceedings. Path Tracing, by industry’s standards, implies
several light bounces need to be optimized and deliberately
selected as a way to avoid spending time on unproductive
beams.

Noise
Importance sampling helps reducing noise by converging
quicker, when ray budgets are prohibitive in real time ray
tracing (RTRT), thus low-discrepancy sequences are vital for
making the noise less apparent and visually appealing [21].
In 1960, J. H. Halton [13] established an advancement in
sampling distributions, reducing Monte Carlo error bounds.
Its high-dimensional correlation flaws are apparent, but it laid
the groundwork for subsequent Quasi-Monte Carlo rendering
techniques. I. M. Sobol in 1967 [30] revolutionized the field
of sampling by introducing an algorithm that was reliant on
base 2 numbers, maintaining strict spatial uniformity even in
high dimensions and exceptional computation times.
Blue noise can be considered as a low-discrepancy distribution, for it aims to spread the points uniformly in the domain,
as well. Mitchell’s best-candidate algorithm [22] generates
samples using the toroidal distance and assumes that candidates are able to be extracted with the same probability
density. Although its rate of convergence is complementary to
white noise, its starting error is lower [21], and compared to
Halton [13] and Sobol [30] it struggles to reach small absolute
error results after several data points extracted for a Gaussian
function, but performs nearly as efficient as Halton’s sequence
given a diagonal step function. Blue noise mitigates visible
error by distributing samples in a pattern that closely mimics
the spatial arrangement of human retinal photoreceptors, as
noted by Lanaro et al [19].
Ahmed and Wonka [2] developed an algorithm which circumvents traditional per-pixel optimization by approaching sample generation as a global, screen-space operation. It achieves
a blue noise pattern distribution of samples by leveraging
Sobol sequences, applying Morton ordering and wrapping
these steps in a stochastic scrambling. Later, Ahmed et al.
[1] introduced Gaussian Blue Noise, shifting the focus from
screen-space distribution to the fundamental generation of
optimal blue noise point sets, minimizing the variance of a
sum of Gaussian kernels.
Monte Carlo Integration
Careful sampling and practices are the core ideas of reducing
variance and it is favorable to make more of a limited number of samples than to increase the amount [27]. Importance
sampling is a powerful tool to enable an integral to converge
quicker and is widely adopted and regarded as the predominant practice for variance reduction, compared to stratified
sampling which performs inadequately given a large numbers
of dimensions to sample from [27].
In his PhD thesis, Eric Veach [32] expanded on the idea of
Multiple Importance Sampling and came up with various
heuristics, concerning optimally drawing samples and weighting them for closely matching the shape of the integrand. The
balance and power heuristics are most notable, with the latter
reducing the variance further for an exponent V = 2.
Although the most ubiquitous for sampling specular lobes
was the GGX model for its long-tailed shape blur effect of
specular highlights, it was replaced by Heitz’s VNDF in 2014
[14] for its inclusion of the viewing angle in the probability
density function, while Walter et al.’s [34] microfacet distribution blindly evaluated half vectors and was burdening
the computations with wasteful rays. However, VNDF was
not pristine either with respect to performance, because the
algebra was cumbersome and was later revisited and optimized [15] to bypass the computations by stretching the GGX
distribution into a uniform hemisphere.

Performance
Geris et al. [11] outline the importance of frames per second
(f/s), framing this concept as one of the determining factors
in efficacy. Although the study focuses on the Virtual Reality
(VR) experience, they discuss the experience disruption of
the user when they interact with a low f/s application, which
is relevant for this paper’s scope. Dossena and Trentini [9]
look at latency as another factor in a system’s usability. High
latency, as mentioned in the paper, renders the application
as barely usable, and will be a certain consideration for the
future development of the presented implementation. Liu et al.
[20] emphasizes on the importance and effects of frame rate
variance. They noted that, in an interactive environment, such
as video games, lower frame rates have a greater impact on
the user’s experience compared to passively watching a video,
over and above frame time variance proved to be a powerful
indicator that may dictate the enjoyment of engagement with
the product on user end.

Conclusions
The main target of this thesis was to engineer a highly optimized, stable, and efficient ray tracing engine, which produces superior quality images under a strict 16.6 millisecond
prohibition. Clear communication between several rendering passes, such as the high speed geometric processing of
rasterization and physical accuracy of ray tracing, has been
ingrained in the classic methods of graphics development for
years. However, the difficulty always presents itself as the
endless choices and trade offs required to balance realism
with performance. Utilizing Liu’s et al. [20] frame rate floor
evaluation, clear data results regarding stability prove this
framework’s usability across different scenarios.

Further Development and Limitations
Light Scaling. The most critical architectural limitation ex-
posed by the testing phase is the linear $ (# ) scaling of the
light evaluation loop inside the ray tracing stage. The natural
evolution of the current system would be the adoption of ReSTIR [4]. Another choice for solving this issue would be a
simpler algorithm, based on a uniform grid structure, namely
ReGIR [21], necessitating an additional pass that builds the
grid before evaluation.
