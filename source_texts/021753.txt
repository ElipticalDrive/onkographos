# Abstract

Embodied artificial intelligence requires agents that can perceive, navigate, understand, and act upon user-defined tasks within complex environments. Achieving this demands a perceptual system that can bridge the gap between the reconstruction precision and the semantic richness respectively required for control and reasoning. This thesis proposes that Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) can serve as the foundation for such a system, evolving from their original role as a view-synthesis tool into a robust, queryable scene map that anchors a modular embodied ecosystem.

We first address the foundational need for a reliable, in-the-wild geometric mapping, introducing RING-NeRF. This architecture integrates structural inductive biases to solve the robustness gaps of standard NeRFs, enabling stable, incremental and adaptive reconstruction from sparse or imperfect multi-view imagery. Building upon this, we enable the agent's self-functioning capabilities by developing direct interpretation methods. We propose architectural enhancements and generalization techniques to perform semantic segmentation directly within the NeRF model, providing the fixed-category understanding essential for navigation and object manipulation.

Finally, we address the challenge of open-ended, user-driven scene understanding. We first introduce DiSCO-3D, an open-vocabulary NeRF segmentation framework that allows the agent to focus locally and decompose specific concepts based on arbitrary text queries. Complementing this local understanding, we develop LLaVA³, a global reasoning pipeline that interfaces the 3D NeRF representation with Large Vision-Language Models, enabling the agent to interpret the environment holistically and plan complex tasks without fine-tuning of the foundation model.

Through these contributions, the thesis establishes a comprehensive framework where a robust, semantically grounded NeRF serves as the central state representation, supporting specifically designed modules for both the functional reflexes and the cognitive reasoning of an embodied agent.

Keywords: 3D Scene understanding, NeRF, Embodied AI, 3D Reconstruction

# Résumé

L'intelligence artificielle incarnée nécessite des agents capables de percevoir, de comprendre et d'agir au sein d'environnements complexes. Cela requiert un système de perception conciliant la précision de reconstruction pour l'action physique et la richesse sémantique pour le raisonnement. Cette thèse propose d'utiliser les champs de radiance neuronaux (NeRF) comme fondation, les faisant évoluer de la synthèse de vues vers une représentation de scène robuste et sémantique, socle d'un écosystème incarné modulaire.

Nous abordons d'abord le besoin fondamental d'une cartographie géométrique fiable en conditions réelles en introduisant RING-NeRF. Cette architecture intègre des biais inductifs structurels pour combler les lacunes de robustesse et d'adaptabilité des NeRF standards, permettant une reconstruction stable et incrémentale à partir d'images éparses ou imparfaites. Sur cette base, nous permettons à l'agent d'assurer son auto-fonctionnement en développant des méthodes d'interprétation sémantique intrinsèques. Nous proposons des améliorations architecturales et des techniques de généralisation pour effectuer une segmentation sémantique au cœur même du modèle NeRF, offrant ainsi l'identification de catégories prédéfinies indispensable à la navigation et à la manipulation d'objets.

Enfin, nous relevons le défi d'une compréhension de scène interactive, pilotée par les tâches données par l'utilisateur. Nous introduisons d'abord DiSCO-3D, un module de segmentation NeRF à vocabulaire ouvert permettant à l'agent de focaliser son attention sur des concepts en réponse à des requêtes textuelles libres et de les décomposer. En complément, nous développons LLaVA³, un système de raisonnement global qui connecte la représentation NeRF à de grands modèles de vision-langage (VLM). Cette interface permet à l'agent d'appréhender l'environnement de manière holistique et de planifier des tâches complexes sans nécessiter d'affinage (fine-tuning) du modèle de fondation.

À travers ces contributions, cette thèse établit un cadre complet où une représentation NeRF robuste et sémantique constitue le pivot de l'écosystème, soutenant des modules désignés pour les réflexes fonctionnels et pour le raisonnement cognitif d'un agent incarné.

Mots-clés : Compréhension de scènes 3D, NeRF, IA Incarnée, Reconstruction 3D

# Introduction

# 1.1 From Digital Algorithms to Embodied AI: Confronting the Physical World

The last decade has witnessed a paradigm shift in Artificial Intelligence. We have entered the era of Generative AI, where systems operating in the digital realm can now synthesize complex code [175], converse fluently [1, 4, 165, 169], and essentially "dream" entire photorealistic worlds [7] from simple text prompts. In stark contrast, the physical realm has witnessed a relative stagnation in the capabilities of autonomous robots [42]. While software agents demonstrate near-human capabilities, physical agents still struggle to perform basic tasks in unstructured environments. Transferring intelligence from the error-forgiving world of bits to the unconstrained and noisy world of atoms remains a grand challenge [152, 182, 107]. Unlike Generative AI, which focuses on synthesis, the physical world requires grounding, i.e. the ability to truthfully map sensory inputs into a coherent, actionable understanding of the environment. This is the domain of Embodied AI [37, 190, 96]: the quest to create autonomous agents that do not merely process data, but perceive, reason, and act within the physical world we inhabit.

To operate effectively in the real world, an embodied agent requires more than just mechanical actuators; it relies on a sophisticated control architecture capable of both lowlevel sensorimotor reflexes and high-level reasoning. This duality mirrors the cognitive framework proposed by psychologist Daniel Kahneman [71], who distinguishes between two modes of human thought: "System 1", which governs fast, automatic, and intuitive operations, and "System 2", which handles slow, effortful, and analytical reasoning.

The foundation of this control hierarchy corresponds to the agent's Functional Core (System 1). This layer acts as the robot's "reflexes", handling the self-functioning and immediate safety of the machine. It executes tasks that are known at design time: predefined routines such as incremental environment mapping, collision-free navigation, and precise object manipulation, which must operate reliably and automatically regardless of the situation and the user input.

Models [1, 4, 165, 112]. This high-level planner is responsible for the unknown, i.e. tasks that cannot be anticipated before deployment. It interprets ambiguous, live user intent, breaks down long-term goals (e.g., "Clean the living room"), and reasons about openended concepts that were not explicitly programmed. While the functional core ensures the robot can move and act safely, the cognitive core determines why and where it should do so based on the user's immediate needs.

While these two systems operate at different levels of abstraction, they share a fundamental requirement: they both rely on a shared understanding of the robot's environment. While a growing body of research advocates for "model-free" or end-to-end approaches which attempt to map sensor inputs directly to actions without an explicit intermediate representation of the scene [113], deploying such systems for general-purpose tasks remains difficult. Crucially, these reactive policies lack persistence: they struggle to retain information about regions currently out of view or recall past observations necessary for long-term planning. We argue that maintaining a persistent internal scene model is preferable. Such a model acts as a shared memory that enables the robot to remember unseen areas, reason about past states, and ensure geometric constraints that black-box policies may violate.

The challenge, however, lies in the nature of this representation. The functional core demands one capable of efficient and incremental reconstruction to maintain a real-time map of a dynamic world, while providing the precise geometry and basic object-level semantics required for self-functioning physical tasks like navigation, object grasping and manipulation. Simultaneously, the cognitive core requires a representation that can act as a connective interface between the physical world and the agent's "brain", capable of grounding abstract language concepts into specific spatial regions. Consequently, the central bottleneck in embodied AI is perception: constructing a shared 3D representation that is robust and precise enough for the body's reflexes, yet semantically rich and queryable enough for the mind's reasoning.

Historically, robotics has relied on discrete geometric representations such as point clouds, voxel grids, or explicit meshes. While effective for specific sub-tasks, these classical formats struggle to serve as an effective backbone for modern embodied intelligence

Point clouds [94, 126] are unstructured and sparse. While sensor-native (e.g. from LiDAR) and thus easy to capture, they remain a collection of discrete samples rather than a defined shape, leaving the space between points geometrically ambiguous. Voxel grids [180] offer structure but suffer from cubic memory growth, strictly limiting their resolution in large environments. Explicit meshes [156] also provide surfaces but are rigid and difficult to update incrementally as new data arrives. Beyond these structural limitations, another primary challenge lies in constructing these representations from the raw sensor data (like point clouds, depth maps, or multi-view images). Converting noisy, partial sensor streams into clean, watertight meshes or dense voxel grids often requires complex, heuristic fusion pipelines that are prone to failure in cluttered real-world scenes. Finally, these discrete structures are ill-suited for the rich semantic understanding required

This sparsity prevents the system from building a comprehensive, dense semantic map, forcing it to rely on interpolation or heuristics to fill in the gaps. Ideally, the cognitive core requires a representation where any point in the continuous scene volume can be queried for its semantic identity rather just a subset of discrete surface points.

These limitations highlight the need for a paradigm shift: to bridge the perceptual gap, we require a representation that can be derived directly and incrementally from raw sensors into an adequate scene model capable of natively fusing dense geometry and rich semantics.

# 1.2 Neural Radiance Fields: An Adapted Novel Perceptual 3D Representation

To address the limitations of classical explicit representations, recent research has converged toward a powerful novel paradigm: optimization-based representations [73]. Unlike classical pipelines that explicitly construct geometry via depth fusion, meshing, or feed-forward large generative AI models, which are all prone to noise and integration errors, these methods define the scene itself as a learnable model, parameterized either by a continuous neural network [120] or a set of discrete primitives [75, 52, 114]. Crucially, the geometry is not manually assembled but is discovered via gradient descent, optimizing the model parameters to reproduce sensor observations (typically RGB images) through differentiable rendering. This shift moves the burden of reconstruction from heuristic geometric algorithms to gradient-based optimization, allowing for more robust handling of noisy sensor data and complex scene topologies.

Among these approaches, the Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) [120] has emerged as the paradigm-defining example. Fundamentally, a NeRF represents a 3D scene as a continuous volumetric function typically parameterized by a neural network that maps spatial coordinates to density and color. By utilizing differentiable volume rendering to optimize this function against a set of 2D multi-view input images, it learns to synthesize novel views with photorealistic fidelity while implicitly reconstructing the underlying scene geometry, as illustrated in Figure 1.1.

This unique formulation stands out as a very promising representation for our unified agent architecture, addressing the specific requirements of both intelligence cores.

For the functional core, NeRFs simplify the mapping process: they can be optimized directly from standard, unconstrained video streams and inherently support incremental updates by constantly refining the function as new data arrives. Furthermore, their spatial continuity provides infinite spatial resolution. This offers not only the precision required for physics-aware tasks like navigation and object manipulation, but also a dense foundation for basic semantic understanding. By associating a semantic class to every point in the continuous volume, the agent can reliably distinguish traversable terrain from obstacles or identify object boundaries for precise manipulation.

Crucially for the cognitive core, NeRFs are fully differentiable. This property is key to bridging the gap between geometry and meaning, as it allows us to easily backpropagate high-dimensional semantic signals directly into the 3D structure, usually from 2D segmentation masks or language embeddings. This transforms the representation from a purely geometric map into a multi-modal backbone capable of fusing appearance, geometry, and semantics into a unified, queryable scene memory.

However, adapting Neural Radiance Fields from a computer graphics tool into a robust representation adapted for robotics is non-trivial. Much like the Generative AI models discussed at the opening of this chapter, standard NeRFs were originally conceived as generative models for the digital realm, specifically for the task of Novel View Synthesis, i.e., generating photo-realistic views of a scene from viewpoints not present in the input. Their primary utility lies in applications such as cinematic rendering, virtual reality asset creation, or photorealistic product visualization. In these contexts, the data capture is perfectly planned, with a static environment and carefully curated camera trajectories, and the ultimate metric of success is visual fidelity for a human observer. This goal stands in stark contrast to the requirements of an Embodied AI agent operating within real-life, uncontrolled environments. This misalignment creates three critical gaps that this thesis aims to bridge:

- The Robustness and Adaptability Gap: Unlike a studio setup where dense camera coverage is available simultaneously, a robot incrementally explores "in-the-wild" via sparse and noisy sensor streams. Standard NeRFs fail here in two critical ways. First, they are fragile: optimization is prone to severe geometric artifacts like "floaters" or geometric collapse when faced with unconstrained data. Second, they lack structural extensibility: standard models are typically trained on a fixed number of images with a fixed resolution across the whole scene. They suffer from catastrophic forgetting (as the robot explores new areas, the network tends to overwrite its memory of previously visited regions) during sequential updates and cannot dynamically adjust their level of detail to match the varying complexity

- The Semantic Gap: While geometrically dense, standard NeRFs are semantically "blind." They represent the world strictly as a continuous field of density and color, lacking any inherent concept of object instances or categories. For a robot, a reconstructed table is indistinguishable from the floor it stands on. To serve the Functional Core, the representation must be actively enriched to distinguish traversable surfaces from obstacles and identify manipulable objects.
- The Interaction Gap: Finally, a standard NeRF is a passive data structure; it can be rendered, but not easily semantically queried from a user query. This presents two distinct hurdles for an intelligent agent. First, the representation lacks semantic queryability: a NeRF can render an image, but it cannot search for scene content like "Where is the mug?" or "How many chairs are there?" because it lacks the ability to perform keyword-based search. Second, it lacks a cognitive interface: there is no direct mechanism to feed a NeRF model into the "brain" of the agent. Unlike 2D images that can be tokenized and given as inputs to LLMs, the implicit Neural Field representation is opaque to high-level reasoning engines. To support the Cognitive Core, we must bridge this divide, transforming the NeRF into a representation that is both searchable by the agent and interpretable by its brain model.

# 1.3 Thesis Statement: A Unified Ecosystem for Embodied Perception

This thesis argues that Neural Radiance Fields can be fundamentally evolved from a passive visual representation, originally designed for novel view synthesis, into the foundational 3D representation for a comprehensive Embodied AI perception system. We propose that this evolution requires systematically addressing the critical gaps identified above, specifically the lack of robustness and extensibility, semantic meaning, and interactive capabilities. By bridging these divides, we argue that a NeRF can serve as the central scene memory of an autonomous agent navigating in real-life environments. This representation is uniquely positioned to bridge the perceptual bottleneck: it provides a single, continuous structure that simultaneously satisfies the rigorous mapping requirements of the robot's functional core and the rich, open-ended semantic requirements of its cognitive core.

Crucially, we position our work not as one single "end-to-end" model where a network attempts to map pixels directly to actions, but rather as a modular ecosystem anchored by the NeRF mapping. This architectural choice effectively decouples the construction of the scene model from its usage. By maintaining the NeRF as a persistent, shared "source of truth," we allow different downstream processes to access the same underlying scene map at the specific level of abstraction they require.

In this framework, the NeRF does not attempt to be all things at once. Instead, it serves as a stable and precise representation upon which specialized interpretive modules operate. This allows the system to seamlessly switch modes: relying on efficient, fixedcategory segmentation for high-frequency control loops, while reserving computationally intensive, open-ended reasoning tools for complex user interactions. This ecosystem approach ensures that the agent's perception is scalable and adaptable, providing a foundational memory that remains valid regardless of the specific task at hand.

Chapter 2 establishes the theoretical background of this work. # Background

Achieving comprehensive embodied AI requires a scene representation that supports both accurate, robust geometric reconstruction and deep semantic understanding of the environment. This background chapter establishes the conceptual and technical foundations upon which our contributions toward robust, semantic, and open-ended 3D scene understanding are built.

To situate our contributions within this context, we first review the evolution of 3D scene representations, from classical discrete models such as meshes, voxels, and point clouds to modern differentiable representations, with particular emphasis on Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs). We then shift our focus from reconstruction to interpretation, first tracing the development of 2D visual understanding and then extending it to 3D NeRF semantics, ultimately paving the way toward a representation capable of supporting high-level reasoning and embodied spatial intelligence.

## 2.1 Foundations of 3D Scene Representations

### 2.1.1 Goals of 3D Reconstruction and Scene Understanding in Embodied AI

Reconstructing and understanding the three-dimensional world has long been a central pursuit in computer vision. 3D reconstruction seeks to capture the geometry of a scene, while scene understanding enriches this representation with semantic and contextual knowledge about objects, their attributes, and their relations. Together, these capabilities form the foundation for systems that not only perceive but can also reason about and interact with their surroundings.

The importance of 3D reconstruction and scene understanding spans a wide range of applications. In autonomous vehicles [211, 59, 97], 3D geometry enables robust navigation and localization, while semantic understanding allows systems to identify signs, obstacles, and dynamic objects. In augmented and virtual reality [174], accurate and semantic reconstructions provide the foundation for immersive and physically consistent experiences. In medical imaging [138, 194, 147], semantic volumetric reconstruction of anatomy supports diagnostics, treatment planning, and surgical guidance. Similar technologies also enable



Figure 2.1: Examples of Explicit 3D Representations of the Stanford Bunny model. Figure is from [60].

the exploration and preservation of real-world sites at unprecedented scale and fidelity, from historical landmarks to industrial environments designed for safer and more efficient workflows [51, 27].

In recent years, these advances have converged toward the emerging field of embodied AI [137, 190, 96, 44], mostly within autonomous robotics [173], where perception, understanding, and action are integrated within interactive agents. Here, 3D representations serve as the bridge between sensory observations and decision-making. An embodied agent must reconstruct its surrounding environment from raw sensory inputs (e.g., RGB or RGB-D streams), understand the objects and affordances it contains, and reason about how to act to fulfill user-specified goals. This requires not only accurate and robust 3D reconstruction, but also semantic grounding and high-level reasoning capabilities, ultimately linking 3D perception with language and task planning.

This dual objective has driven the evolution of 3D representations, from discrete geometric primitives such as voxels, meshes, and point clouds to differentiable fields that can encode jointly geometry and semantics. In the following, we review these representation families, outlining their core principles, strengths, and limitations.

## 2.1.2 Classical Discrete Representations: Meshes, Voxels, and Point Clouds

Since the beginning of 3D vision, 3D scenes were traditionally modeled using discrete or geometric data structures such as voxels, meshes, and point clouds. These classical representations, illustrated in Figure 2.1, have been the foundation of computer graphics, computer vision, and robotics pipelines for decades, and remain widely used in practical systems. However, they also come with fundamental limitations that motivated the emergence of novel 3D scene models.

Voxels. Voxel-based representations extend the notion of pixels within 2D images and discretize the 3D scene space into a regular grid, where each cell encodes occupancy, color, or signed distance values [26, 183, 184]. They are conceptually simple and have been used extensively for many AI-related tasks notably due to their compatibility with many neural networks architectures (e.g. 3D convolutions for 3D object detection and occupancy completion) [191, 57]. Their main drawback is their scalability: memory requirements grow

cubically with resolution, making it impractical to represent large or detailed environments. Coarse voxel grids have poor geometric fidelity, while fine grids are prohibitively expensive to store and process [5]. Although solutions to improve the resolution without additional cost exists (e.g. hashgrids [166] or efficient space partitioning [28, 56, 148]), they still struggle to describe precisely detailed scenes. Moreover, in contrast to pixels, voxel representations are not directly sensed but instead reconstructed through complex pipelines, such as multi-view stereo or deep learning depth estimation, that approximate rather than capture reality. As a result, they inherit both the computational overhead and the inaccuracies of these upstream models.

Meshes. Meshes describe surfaces as collections of vertices connected by edges and faces, most commonly triangles [180, 72]. They are compact, efficient for rendering, and can capture accurate geometric detail. As a result, they form the backbone of many computer graphics pipelines. However, they require an explicitly defined topology, meaning that the connectivity between vertices must be specified and consistently maintained. This makes meshes difficult to generate directly from real-world objects or 3D scenes, where data is often noisy, incomplete, or unstructured, also requiring heavy pipelines. Furthermore, their reliance on handcrafted connectivity reduces their compatibility with neural representations, limiting their usefulness for learning-based or semantic scene understanding [156].

Point Clouds. Point clouds represent a scene as an unstructured set of 3D points, typically captured directly by LiDAR or depth sensors. They are simple, flexible, and widely used in robotics for mapping, navigation, and perception tasks, particularly in autonomous vehicles [94]. Contrary to voxels and meshes, point clouds are sensor-native and can directly encode rich geometric information. However, they are inherently sparse and lack explicit surface connectivity, which complicates downstream processing and surface reconstruction [126]. While many neural architectures [139, 140] have been developed to process point clouds effectively, the absence of inherent topology still poses challenges for tasks requiring dense surface understanding or fine-grained geometric reasoning. Consequently, tasks such as semantic scene understanding, surface completion, and point denoising remain active areas of research [187, 15, 208].

Discussion. While voxels, meshes, and point clouds each provide effective ways to represent 3D geometry, they also illustrate the trade-offs inherent in classical representations. Voxels offer a structured format that facilitates interpretation, but they struggle with scalability, fine detail and generation. Meshes efficiently capture continuous surfaces and details, yet require explicitly defined connectivity, which complicates reconstruction from raw data. Point clouds provide flexible, sensor-native access to geometry, but their sparsity and lack of inherent surface structure can limit semantic understanding.

These limitations highlight a persistent gap: classical representations excel at encoding geometry, but they all do so at the cost of crucial limitations for modern embodied AI scene understanding applications. They either lack the ability to represent details or large-scale scene; the flexibility to be easily derived from accessible captors (i.e. cameras); or are limited in semantic understanding applicability.

The Evolution of Explicit Methods: Feed-Forward Point Maps. It is worth noting that very recent advances in deep learning aim to overcome those discrete representations limits. Methods like MAST3R [178, 88], VGGT [176], or MapAnything [74] treat 3D reconstruction as a direct regression problem, training massive neural networks to predict dense 3D point maps directly from multi-view images in a single feed-forward pass. A "point map" is a dense 2D image where each pixel encodes the 3D coordinate (��, ��, ��) corresponding to the image's pixel's projection in 3D space, rather than a color, treating geometry as a multi-channel image generated by the network. These approaches achieve impressive speed and robustness, effectively bypassing traditional photogrammetry. However, despite their modern architecture, they inherit the fundamental limitations of the point cloud format: the output remains a collection of discrete, unorganized points without connectivity or a continuous surface definition. Moreover, their feed-forward nature prevents the maintenance of a persistent scene map, as they essentially regress a new, independent reconstruction at every inference step rather than incrementally updating a shared internal state.

Obtaining easily reconstructible and interpretable large-scale 3D scenes has motivated the rise of novel 3D representations. Specifically, recent representations focus on recovering high-fidelity geometry and appearance directly from images while supporting richer semantic and task-driven interpretation. Such advances are particularly relevant for embodied AI, where agents must not only perceive their surroundings but also reason about and act within them, relying on 3D scene representations that are both accurate and semantically meaningful.

### 2.1.3 The Rise of Differentiable 3D Scene Representations

While classical representations such as voxels, meshes, and point clouds have enabled a wide range of robotic and computer vision applications, they remain fundamentally constrained by various limitations. Those have spurred the emergence of differentiable, learned continuous representations, which reformulate the 3D reconstruction paradigm as an optimization problem where a model is overfitted to a specific scene's observations.

Early works on differentiable 3D modeling introduced implicit neural representations, where a neural network parameterizes a continuous function defining the surface of a scene or object. Examples include DeepSDF [133], which represents geometry as a signed distance function, and Occupancy Networks [118], which learn to predict the likelihood

of occupancy at any spatial location. However, these methods typically rely on explicit 3D supervision such as meshes or point clouds, complicating the reconstruction of in-the-wild scenes.

To overcome the need for 3D supervision, the field shifted toward optimizing representations directly from 2D images via differentiable rendering, where gradients flow straight from the image loss back to the 3D parameters. Within this paradigm, two dominant families of approaches have emerged: optimization of implicit volumetric functions and optimization of explicit primitives.

Implicit Volumetric Functions: Neural Radiance Fields. The first realization of this paradigm is the Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) [120], which forms the basis of this thesis. NeRFs introduce the notion of learning continuous volumetric functions as neural networks parameters directly from multi-view imagery. In this framework, a neural network takes a 3D spatial position as input and outputs the corresponding density and radiance. By jointly modeling them both within a fully differentiable volumetric rendering process, NeRF parameters are optimized end-to-end by comparing rendered views with ground-truth observed images. This produces continuous, photo-realistic and compact reconstructions without requiring explicit 3D supervision or depth data. Crucially, the differentiability of this pipeline allows integration with modern semantics frameworks, while the continuity of the representation enables spatial querying at any resolution.

Explicit Differentiable Primitives: 3D Gaussian Splatting. More recently, alternatives have sought to improve the computational efficiency of this process by returning to explicit primitives. The leading approach in this direction is 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [75]. Moving away from continuous fields, 3DGS represents the scene as a cloud of 3D anisotropic Gaussians, each optimized with explicit attributes for position, covariance, opacity, and color. The key innovation is a fully differentiable rasterizer that projects these 3D Gaussians into 2D "splats" on the image plane, allowing for optimization of explicit 3D parameters. This approach has revolutionized the field notably by enabling real-time rendering. However, fundamentally, 3DGS remains a collection of discrete primitives, mirroring an advanced unstructured point cloud, with the same limitations. Some recent works like Radiant Foam [52] and EVER [114] have emerged, intending to improve the quality, speed and functionalities of primitives-based differentiable 3D representations.

While 3DGS offers superior rendering speed, we argue that NeRFs remain a more suitable representation for scene understanding in robotics for various reasons:

- Continuity: NeRF defines a continuous volumetric field, effectively functioning as a dense 3D map. An agent can query geometric and semantic information at any spatial coordinate to resolve collisions or plan grasps. In contrast, 3DGS consists of discrete primitives with undefined empty space in between, acting fundamentally as

This transition toward differentiable representations marks a major conceptual shift in 3D modeling. Instead of reconstructing geometry explicitly, the representation itself is optimized to fit the real world through continuous and differentiable processes. This reformulation not only unifies the traditionally separate steps of perception and reconstruction but also enables gradients to flow across them, allowing models to be trained jointly for both geometry and semantics. In the context of embodied AI, such differentiable pipelines are particularly appealing, as they provide a unified representation through which agents can perceive, reason, and act within a coherent, continuously learned 3D scene model derived directly from 2D visual observations.

#### 2.2.1 Core Formulation

Overview. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [120], illustrated in Figure 2.3 represent a 3D scene as a continuous volumetric function. The key idea is to encode a radiance field that jointly describes color and density at every point in space. Formally, the model defines a mapping:



| $F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \rightarrow (\mathbf{c}, \sigma),$ | (2.1) |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------|
|------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------|

$$F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \longrightarrow (\mathbf{c}, \sigma), \quad (2.1)$$

where Θ are the parameters needed to describe the field, x = (��, ��, ��) is a 3D location, d = (��, ��) is the viewing direction, c = (��, ��, ��) is the emitted color, and �� is the volume density. This model can then be sampled at any 3D location to obtain the corresponding radiance values. NeRF are called implicit 3D representations as they do not store the scene in an explicit manner but rather allows arbitrary querying of the implicit model. In practice, in the original work, ��Θ is approximated by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), also called artificial neural network, optimized and overfit specifically for each scene through gradient descent using posed multi-view images of the 3D scene.

While NeRF are dense 3D representations, one very important aspect of neural fields is their ability to synthesise photo-realistic renders (i.e. 2D images) of its scene from any user-defined point of view (both camera position and view direction) using volume rendering.

Novel View Synthesis. From a NeRF, we are able to produce a 2D image given the corresponding camera parameters (extrinsics and intrinsics) by using ray casting and differentiable volume rendering techniques. Let's say we want to synthesize an image from a novel viewpoint defined by known camera parameters. Each pixel in the image corresponds to a camera ray  $r(t)$  emitted from the camera center through the pixel in the camera viewing direction and parameterized by depth  $t$ . The color of this pixel is obtained by integrating the radiance accumulated along that ray through the continuous volumetric field predicted by the NeRF model. For a camera ray  $r(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ , with origin  $\mathbf{o}$  and direction  $\mathbf{d}$ , the predicted color is computed as

$$C(r) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t) \sigma(r(t)) c(r(t), \mathbf{d}) dt \quad (2.2)$$

where  $\sigma(r(t))$  and  $c(r(t), \mathbf{d})$  are the density and color at point  $r(t)$ , respectively. The term  $T(t)$  is the transmittance, i.e., the probability that a ray travels from  $t_n$  to  $t$  without encountering occlusion:

$$T(t) = \exp \left( - \int_{t_n}^t \sigma(r(s)) ds \right) \quad (2.3)$$

In practise, since these integrals cannot be computed in closed form, they are approximated by discrete sampling along each ray. Using the quadrature rule [116], the rendered color is thus estimated as

$$C(r) = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) \mathbf{c}_i \quad (2.4)$$

where  $c_i$  and  $\sigma_i$  denote the predicted color and density at the  $i$ -th sample,  $\delta_i$  is the distance between consecutive samples, and  $T_i$  represents accumulated transmittance :

$$T_i = \exp \left( - \sum_{j < i} \sigma_j \delta_j \right) \quad (2.5)$$

The accuracy of this approximation depends heavily on the sampling strategy used along each ray. The original NeRF formulation employs a two-stage hierarchical sampling scheme: a coarse network first samples points uniformly through the ray to estimate a rough density distribution, and a fine network then resamples additional points according to the learned importance distribution.

Subsequent works have introduced more efficient and stable sampling strategies for large or unbounded scenes. Mip-NeRF 360 [10] notably replaces fixed hierarchical sampling with multiple smaller per-scene proposal networks that predict per-ray density distributions, also enabling adaptive sample allocation where it is most needed.

Finally, the full image is obtained by aggregating the predicted colors of all pixels, each computed from its corresponding ray. This rendering process not only enables novel-view synthesis but also serves as the basis for training NeRF models by comparing synthesized and ground-truth images as discussed next.

Training. NeRFs are scene-specific, meaning that each model is trained to represent a single scene and must be optimized individually for it. As mentioned earlier, this optimization relies on multi-view images of the scene and their associated camera poses, leveraging the same differentiable rendering process used for novel-view synthesis. During training, rays are randomly cast from all input images using their known camera parameters (see Figure 2.3\). For each pixel, the rendered color ��(��) obtained from volume rendering is compared to the ground-truth observed RGB value. The network parameters Θ are optimized by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) across batches of sampled rays. Over time, this supervision encourages the synthesized views to reproduce the captured images, progressively encoding both the scene geometry (through densities) and appearance (through radiance) into the network weights.

View-Dependency. The original NeRF formulation explicitly models view-dependent appearance to reproduce non-Lambertian effects such as specular reflections and glossy surfaces. The radiance field is decomposed into two components: a view-independent density ��(x), which encodes the scene geometry, and a color term ��(x, d) that depends on both the spatial position x and the per-camera viewing direction d:

$$F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) = (\sigma(\mathbf{x}), \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})). \quad (2.6)$$

As shown in Figure 2.4, this conditioning on the direction d enables the model to synthesize viewpoint-dependent effects such as reflections, refractions, or shading variations. In contrast, a purely view-independent color prediction would only capture Lambertian surfaces, leading to flat and unrealistic renderings. By incorporating viewing direction, NeRF produces photorealistic images that better match the complex appearance changes observed in real-world scenes.





Figure 2.5: Overview of the Grid-Based NeRF pipeline. Rather than using a large MLP, Instant-NGP introduces multi-resolution learnable hashgrids which provide for each 3D point a feature as the concatenation of each grid. This feature is then fed to a small MLP called decoder to predict both density and color. Figure is from [124].

Positional encoding. A standard MLP has limited ability to capture high-frequency variations, which for a NeRF results in overly smooth reconstructions with loss of fine detail. To overcome this, NeRF employs positional encodings that map input coordinates to a higher-dimensional feature space. For the spatial coordinates x, Fourier feature mapping is used:

$$\gamma(\mathbf{x}) = (\sin(2^0 \pi \mathbf{x}), \cos(2^0 \pi \mathbf{x}), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1} \pi \mathbf{x}), \cos(2^{L-1} \pi \mathbf{x})), \quad (2.7)$$

where L controls the number of frequency bands.

For the viewing direction d, low-order spherical harmonics [122] are used to encode angular dependencies. Spherical harmonics form an orthonormal basis over the sphere and provide a compact way to represent smooth angular functions. This makes them well-suited for capturing view-dependent effects, such as specular reflections.

The radiance field can then be expressed as:

$$F_\Theta = F'_\Theta \circ (\gamma(\mathbf{x}), \text{SH}(\mathbf{d})), \quad (2.8)$$

where �� ′ Θ is the MLP function, ��(x) encodes the 3D spatial coordinates, and ����(d) encodes the view direction.

This dual encoding allows the network to capture both fine geometric details and smooth view-dependent variations, mitigating the low-pass bias of MLPs and enabling high-quality, photorealistic renderings, as illustrated in Figure 2.4.

### 2.2.2 Grid-Based NeRF Representations

To represent complex 3D scenes, original Vanilla NeRFs rely on a large and deep multilayer perceptron (MLP) to approximate the radiance field function defined in equation \(2.1\). This design is highly expressive but comes at a significant computation cost. First, training and rendering are expensive, as each forward pass cost suffers from the large size of the MLP. More importantly, during optimization specifically, any MLP's weights parameters

update impacts the whole reconstruction, which slows convergence and may even lead to instabilities during training depending on the spatial distribution of sampled points.

To overcome these issues, Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (Instant-NGP) [124], illustrated in Figure 2.5, proposed a more efficient encoding scheme based on multiresolution learnable hashgrids. The key idea is to replace the large MLP with a compact structure that explicitly encodes spatial information. Concretely, the scene is partitioned into a hierarchy of �� 3D grids ���� of increasing resolutions. Each vertex of a grid is assigned to a trainable embedding vector, which is optimized directly during training via gradient descent. For any 3D query point, features are retrieved from each grid by trilinear interpolation of the surrounding embeddings. The concatenated features across all resolutions are then passed through a much shallower MLP called decoder, which predicts color and density.

Although this scheme shares some similarity with voxel-based representations (since both discretize space into a regular grid), it differs in two crucial ways. First, the interpolation across grid vertices (combined with the decoder) enables a continuous representation, avoiding the blocky artifacts typical of voxel grids. Second, the learned embeddings act as a compressed latent code rather than storing explicit occupancy or color values, which enables the representation to be significantly more detailed and expressive. This is why, although Instant-NGP adds explicit partitioning of the scene, it is still considered an implicit representation.

To further address the memory bottleneck of storing multiple high-resolution feature grids, Instant-NGP employs a multi-resolution hash encoding. Instead of assigning a unique feature vector to every grid vertex, a per-resolution fixed-size hashing table is used, and vertex indices are mapped to this table via a hash function. This strategy allows embeddings to be shared across vertices, drastically reducing memory consumption while still preserving the ability to capture fine detail.

Compared to the original Neural Radiance Fields, this design provides two major efficiency benefits. First, each forward pass is much faster using this architecture in contrast to the very deep MLP. More importantly, the representation becomes more locally constrained: apart from the shallow decoder, only the embeddings associated with the neighborhood of a sampled point are updated during training, in contrast to global weight updates in vanilla NeRF. Together, these factors yield significant improvements in both training time (reducing convergence from several hours to a few minutes per scene) and rendering speed, making NeRFs far more practical for large-scale or real-time applications.

Other existing variants were introduced later on, with various types of scene decompositions (e.g. tri-planes, ...). However, those methods received less interest from the community and will not be further mentioned in this thesis.

#### 2.2.3 SDF-based Neural Fields

Due to the purely 2D supervision, one disadvantage of NeRF representations is that their geometry estimation can be rather inaccurate, especially regarding details on object surfaces. While such imprecision may have limited impact on view-synthesis quality (as the rendering process inherently projects these geometric ambiguities back into the 2D domain), the 3D reconstruction itself may often contain many holes and surface errors. This is problematic for most 3D downstream applications that require accurate surfaces. In particular, modern industrial reconstruction pipelines often convert NeRF into explicit 3D representations such as meshes, where surface inaccuracies translate directly into geometric errors that harm precision-sensitive applications. Additionally, in embodied AI, accurate surface mapping is crucial. From navigation to semantic scene interpretation, the agent's ability to function relies on geometric fidelity: if the reconstruction of a chair is fragmented, the agent can neither reliably recognize it nor interact with it safely.

Several strategies have been proposed to improve surface quality, ranging from regularization terms to depth-guided supervision. A prominent research direction [130, 196, 177, 95, 179] has focused on representing geometry by combining NeRF with Signed Distance Functions (SDFs), which provide a more explicit and smooth formulation of surfaces.

Formally, a signed distance function is a 3D representation based on a continuous scalar field

$$s : \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R},$$

where for each 3D point x ∈ R 3 , the function value ��(x) gives the distance to the closest surface, with the sign conventionally negative inside the object and positive outside. The surface itself is thus defined as the zero-level set {x | ��(x) = 0}.

In SDF-based neural fields, instead of predicting a density ��(x) directly, the network predicts an SDF value ��(x), which is then converted into a density to enable volumetric rendering. Different conversion functions have been proposed. For instance, VolSDF [196] converts SDF values into densities via a Laplace cumulative distribution function, while NeuS [177, 179] uses the logistic distribution to better approximate surface rendering. These mappings provide a differentiable way to concentrate densities near the zero-level surface, leading to sharper reconstructions.

Because NeRF do not supervise empty areas by design (samples in empty areas have null contributions in the volume rendering formula), training SDF-based fields requires additional supervision to enforce valid distance-function properties everywhere in the scene. The most common approach is the Eikonal loss, which enforces the gradient of the SDF to have unit norm for every sample:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{eik}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}} \left[ (\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} s(\mathbf{x})\|_2 - 1)^2 \right].$$

This regularization ensures that the predicted SDF behaves consistently as a true distance field, thus improving the geometry and stabilizing training.

Beyond the basic formulations, a variety of improvements have been proposed to further enhance reconstruction quality and training stability. Some contributions focus on smoothing the reconstructed surfaces through regularization. For example, Neuralangelo [95] introduces a curvature-based ℒ2 regularization loss and leverages analytic derivatives of the SDF for finer gradient control when computing the Eikonal loss. Others focus on improving supervision or optimization strategies, such as coarse-to-fine training schemes [95] or auxiliary depth and normal supervision [199].

Overall, SDF-based NeRFs constitute a powerful line of research for achieving accurate and consistent surface reconstructions, offering a stronger geometric prior than densitybased formulations while maintaining compatibility with neural volumetric rendering frameworks. Nevertheless, SDF-based NeRFs are generally harder to train and more prone to instabilities than density-based formulations, which explains why most NeRF variants continue to rely on density prediction despite the geometric benefits of SDFs. In practise, SDF NeRFs are mostly used for specific use cases which needs precise surface reconstruction. In the following, density NeRF will be used unless specified otherwise; however, the concepts discussed throughout this thesis also apply in principle to SDFbased variants.

#### 2.2.4 Common Challenges in Real-World Scenarios

The original NeRF formulation was mostly demonstrated on isolated objects or restricted indoor scenes under controlled conditions. Extending these methods to real-world scenarios introduces several challenges that must be explicitly addressed, ranging from adapting to large-scale scenes to handling imperfect data. We highlight below the main issues and representative research directions.

Large-Scale and Outdoor Scenes. Neural fields are well suited for restricted indoor and object-centric scenes, but they struggle to represent large environments such as unbounded outdoor settings. Since the radiance field is usually defined over a limited volume, naively applying the formulation to large areas by scaling the scene to fit this volume leads to poor reconstructions and distortions, not to mention issues with representing infinitely far areas (e.g., the sky).

To address this, most methods separate the 3D scene into two parts, the region of interest (ROI) at center of the scene which needs to be reconstructed with great care, and the background part. To perform this separation, several methods propose alternative space parameterizations that compactify the unbounded background 3D space into a manageable domain adjacent to the ROI. NeRF++ [204], for example, introduces an inside/outside split by combining a boundedNeRF for the near fieldwith a second radiance field defined over the exterior of a unit sphere (which is contracted into a finite space). MipNeRF360 [10] further improves large-scale reconstruction by applying smoother scene contraction, making it possible to represent unbounded outdoor environments while maintaining continuity and resolution inside a single NeRF.

However, when ROI of the scenes become extremely large (such as city blocks or very long trajectories), single-field representations might become impractical. Block-NeRF [163] addresses this by partitioning the environment into spatially localized NeRF, which are trained and rendered independently but combined for global consistency. This strategy significantly reduces memory and computational demands while scaling to kilometerscale reconstructions. Another family of solution [201, 47] relies on proposing dynamically allocating model capacity for grid-based solutions by using octrees and expendables grids around task-relevant areas, improving both efficiency and reconstruction quality.

Overall, these advances extend NeRF from controlled, object-centric scenes to largescale in-the-wild environments, paving the way for real-life applications.

Distance of Observation and Aliasing. The original NeRF formulation implicitly assumes a constant observation distance from the scene. In practice, however, the apparent footprint of a pixel on a surface increases with distance, which is not reflected in the standard rendering process. Since NeRF estimates color and density at discrete sample points along each ray, without accounting for the varying spatial extent represented by each pixel, renderings often suffer from aliasing artifacts when synthesizing views at scales or distances different from those seen during training. This limitation, highlighted in Mip-NeRF [11], arises from the absence of a mechanism that adapts the image synthesis to varying observation distances.

To address this issue, several works have introduced Level of Detail (LOD) mechanisms into NeRF representations, allowing a scene to be represented at multiple spatial resolutions simultaneously. From those representations, aliasing artifacts are removed by sampling coarser LODs as the camera distance increases. These methods build LOD-aware latent spaces by explicitly conditioning the NeRF input on the desired scale, ensuring that the network retrieves features appropriate for the sampling resolution [11, 188, 171, 12, 62, 213]. While such formulations improve visual consistency and robustness across scales,

they often require explicit supervision for each LOD, making generalization to unseen observation distances challenging.

A complementary line of work foregoes explicit LOD representations and instead focuses on improving the sampling process itself by modeling volumes rather than points during ray integration, thus aggregating latent features over continuous regions of space [11, 62, 12]. By accounting for the continuous nature of pixel footprints, these techniques effectively mitigate aliasing, albeit at the cost of increased computational and memory overhead.

Few-View Reconstruction. By construction, NeRF is subject to the shape–radiance ambiguity [204]. When supervision from multiple viewpoints is sparse, the optimization process can easily overfit the observed views while failing to produce geometrically consistent reconstructions or generalize to unseen ones. This challenge is particularly critical in real-world settings where dense multi-view captures are impractical or impossible.

We can distinguish two types of solutions. A first family of approaches seeks to mitigate this issue through regularization terms that guide the optimization toward geometrically plausible solutions. Geometric priors such as smoothness or normal consistency constraints help stabilize the learned density field and reduce overfitting [129, 199, 185]. Other works instead leverage semantic regularization to enforce cross-view consistency [65], promoting reconstructions that align with high-level scene structure.

The second family of solutions relies on a progressive reconstruction of the details of the scene, as introduced in FreeNeRF [193] and Nerfies [135]. Restraining the ability of the model to reconstruct a complex scene at early stages enforces the consistency of the reconstruction over the different supervision viewpoints. This presents the advantage of bringing stability while keeping a fairly simple training process.

Imperfect Camera Poses. Accurate camera parameters are a strong requirement for NeRF training, regarding both intrinsics (e.g., focal length, principal point) and extrinsics (i.e., 6DoF poses). In standard offline workflows, datasets often rely on Structure-from-Motion (SfM) pipelines like COLMAP [153, 154] to estimate parameters a posteriori. Similarly, in online embodied AI scenarios, agents typically track their pose incrementally via Visual Odometry or SLAM, which inherently accumulate drift and noise. Regardless of the source (SfM or SLAM), these imperfect estimates can break multi-view consistency and lead to severe reconstruction artifacts. To mitigate this, several methods jointly refine camera parameters and radiance field weights during training. For instance, BARF [98] optimizes extrinsics only, while NeRF– [181], SC-NeRF [67], NoPE-NeRF [16], and CamP [134] also refine intrinsics, achieving improved robustness to noisy or incomplete camera calibration.

Dynamic Lighting and Appearance Variability. Real-world image collections often exhibit inconsistent lighting, transient objects, or even weather variations between captures. These factors violate the assumptions of the original NeRF, which presumes static geometry

and constant appearance. NeRF-in-the-wild [115] and its successors [24, 155, 119] address this by disentangling scene content from appearance factors, enabling reconstructions that are robust to variable illumination and transient occluders common in unstructured image collections. In practice, they introduce learnable per-image latent appearance codes, which are taken as input of the network alongside the samples, to explain perview variability such as lighting, tone, or transient content. This formulation allows the core geometry and consistent aspects of the scene to be reconstructed, while residual factors are absorbed by the appearance codes. An interesting consequence of this is the possibility to render views of the scene with varying lighting and/or weather by exploring the corresponding learnt appearance latent spaces. Extensions such as GRAF [155], Ha-NeRF [24], or RawNeRF [119] further refine this idea, for instance by explicitly modeling shadows, exposure, or weather conditions.

Incremental Reconstruction. In many real-world NeRF reconstruction scenarios, the input images are provided sequentially. For instance, in embodied AI, autonomous agents reconstruct their environment progressively as they navigate and capture the scene. Moreover, such scenarios often involve exploring large environments, resulting in a high number of captured images with high memory usage, thus requiring to discard older frames. In this online incremental reconstruction paradigm, the model must integrate new viewpoints as they arrive, while avoiding the important issue of catastrophic forgetting, i.e., the tendency of neural networks to overwrite previously learned information when updated with new data.

With vanilla NeRF, this issue is severe: every optimization step updates the shared MLP weights globally, which can cause previously reconstructed regions which are not supervised anymore to degrade as we supervise novel views within new areas. Gridbased representations partially mitigate this issue thanks to their spatially localized feature storage. Nevertheless, forgetting still occurs both due to the decoder and the hashgrid dictionary. A common strategy to partly address this limitation is to employ pre-trained, scene-agnostic decoders that remain frozen during the feature grids optimization [212]. This design enables new grid cells to represent unseen areas while preserving the integrity of already reconstructed regions.

In summary, adapting NeRF to real-world scenarios requires tackling inherent challenges of imperfect data, large-scale scenes and incremental reconstruction. These research directions collectively aim to close the gap between the ideal conditions of benchmarks and the complexity of in-the-wild visual data.

In the previous sections, we discussed Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) from a conceptual and methodological perspective. In this section, we adopt a more practical view, detailing the key implementation components, evaluation protocols, and datasets that form the

Frameworks. Nerfstudio [164] is a modular and open-source Python framework built upon the PyTorch library, designed for the development, training, and evaluation of NeRF-based methods. To combine flexibility with high computational efficiency, it integrates tiny-cuda-nn [123], a highly optimized C++/CUDA library used to accelerate the core components of the architecture, specifically the feature hash grids, MLPs, and various positional encodings. Nerfstudio provides a unified interface for data preprocessing, model configuration, optimization, and rendering, supporting a wide range of architectures and datasets. Its modular design enables rapid prototyping and systematic comparison between different approaches, while ensuring reproducibility through standardized evaluation protocols. All NeRF models and extensions proposed in this thesis were implemented within Nerfstudio. In particular, all experiments involving SDF–based NeRFs were developed and evaluated using SDFStudio [200], an extension of Nerfstudio focused on implementing several SDF methods, which follows the same modular and reproducible pipeline.

Nerfacto. Nerfacto, the default baseline model in Nerfstudio, integrates several advances from recent NeRF literature into a single, efficient pipeline for in-the-wild reconstruction. As illustrated in Fig. 2.7, it combines a hash-grid encoding [124] for compact and expressive scene representation, Mip-NeRF 360 [10] proposal sampling for efficient ray sampling, and several secondary improvements. These include the distortion loss from Mip-NeRF 360 to stabilize convergence, scene contraction to handle unbounded scenes, camera poses refinement and appearance embeddings to account for illumination variations across input images. Its robustness, efficiency, and ease of use make it an ideal baseline for the methods developed in this thesis.

Evaluation Metrics. Following standard NeRF evaluation protocols, most reconstruction and interpretation metrics are computed by comparing rendered views with ground-truth images from held-out test viewpoints. Regarding novel view synthesis (reconstruction

evaluation), we report PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio) for photometric accuracy, defined as:

$$\text{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{MAX_I^2}{\text{MSE}} \right), \quad (2.9)$$

where  $MAX_I$  is the maximum possible pixel value and  $\text{MSE}$  is the mean squared error between the rendered and ground-truth images.

The SSIM (Structural Similarity Index) measures perceptual fidelity through local luminance, contrast, and structure comparisons:

$$\text{SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + \eta_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + \eta_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + \eta_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + \eta_2)}, \quad (2.10)$$

where  $\mu$  is the pixel sample mean,  $\sigma^2$  is the sample variance, and  $\eta_1$  and  $\eta_2$  are two variables to stabilize the division with weak denominator.

The LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity) metric estimates perceptual similarity by comparing deep features extracted from pretrained networks (AlexNet [84] in this thesis):

$$\text{LPIPS}(x, y) = \sum_l w_l \|f_l(x) - f_l(y)\|_2^2, \quad (2.11)$$

where  $f_l$  denotes feature activations at layer  $l$  and  $w_l$  are learned weights.

Segmentation evaluation also follows standard 2D metrics, specifically mIoU (mean Intersection over Union):

$$\text{IoU}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c + FN_c}, \quad \text{mIoU} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{c=1}^N c \text{IoU}_c, \quad (2.12)$$

and mAcc (mean Accuracy):

$$\text{mAcc} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^C \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c}, \quad (2.13)$$

where  $N_c$  is the number of classes, and TP, FP and FN are respectively true positives, false positives and false negatives samples.

For SDF-based NeRFs, which explicitly model 3D surfaces, we additionally report geometric metrics. The Chamfer Distance (CD) measures the average closest-point distance between predicted ( $PC_{pred}$ ) and ground-truth ( $PC_{GT}$ ) point clouds:

$$\text{CD}(PC_{pred}, PC_{GT}) = \frac{1}{|PC_{pred}|} \sum_{p \in PC_{pred}} \min_{q \in PC_{GT}} \|p - q\|_2 + \frac{1}{|PC_{GT}|} \sum_{q \in PC_{GT}} \min_{p \in PC_{pred}} \|q - p\|_2. \quad (2.14)$$

Finally, the F-score evaluates geometric consistency under a distance threshold:

$$\text{F-score} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}, \quad (2.15)$$

where Precision and Recall are computed based on the fraction of predicted and ground-truth points lying within the fixed threshold of each other.

Datasets. Our experiments leverage a variety of established benchmarks to evaluate reconstruction quality and generalization. The Mip-NeRF 360 [10] and Tanks and Temples [81] datasets provide high-resolution, densely covered, unbounded outdoor and indoor scenes for view-synthesis evaluation. ScanNet [29] and Replica [160] offer richly semantically annotated indoor environments from natural cameras trajectories, making them ideal for reconstruction and segmentation tasks in embodied AI. Finally, DTU [66] proposes multi-view front-facing captures of various real-world object. While unused in this thesis, there exists many other existing newer large-scale datasets such as Scan-Net++ [198], DL3DV-10K [99], and the Nerfstudio dataset collection [164]. Together, these datasets provide a comprehensive basis for studying both geometric and semantic aspects of NeRF representations across diverse embodied scenarios.

# 2.3 Interpretation in 2D Vision

Although embodied agents ultimately require semantic understanding of full 3D environments, we first consider the 2D setting, where many of the core ideas of 3D NeRF interpretation originated. Interpreting 2D visual information has been a central challenge of computer vision since its inception. At its core, interpretation in 2D vision consists of transforming raw pixel arrays (i.e. images) into meaningful abstractions that can support object understanding and high-level reasoning. This process has almost always relied on the ability to extract and represent features: numerical descriptors that capture relevant aspects of the visual world, such as texture, shape, or semantics. The history of 2D vision can therefore be read as the story of evolving feature representations, from handcrafted descriptors to deep learned embeddings, and more recently to foundation models trained at unprecedented scale. In this section, we first trace this trajectory of representations, before turning to the major families of interpretation tasks they enable. Finally, we analyze the shortcomings of the 2D vision paradigm to interpret complex 3D scenes notably for embodied AI applications.

#### 2.3.1 Foundations of Visual Representations: Encoding Images into Features

The history of 2D vision can be understood as a continuous effort to design or learn increasingly powerful features that enable machines to interpret images. Features are descriptors which represent visual information of an image. Those can be of varying scales (i.e. dense per-pixel features, per-group of pixels, also called patches, or per-image) and can be obtained in many different ways. Specifically, the features gradually became more complex and abstract as the methods and models to extract those descriptors also grew more complex.

First, early computer vision relied on handcrafted descriptors such as SIFT [111], SURF [13], or HOG [31], which performed pre-defined computations on neighboring pixels of an image. These features captured local geometric and photometric cues like corners, edges, and textures, and were specifically engineered to be robust to specific transformations such as scale or rotation. While unexpectedly effective compared to their simplicity, their utility was limited for low-level shape recognition and inter-images correspondence tasks, as they lacked semantic meaning and context, making them impossible to scale toward a more complex or open-ended interpretation.

The rise of deep learning marked a turning point in the interpretation of images, opening the way to true semantic downstream tasks. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [84] introduced the idea of learning optimal features from annotated image datasets by optimizing the parameters of local pixel-processing operations, rather than relying on hand-engineered, sub-optimal formulas. By stacking and combining these learnable layers into deeper architectures known as backbones [84, 58, 151, 162], they emerged as powerful models for extracting informative features from images. Trained on large-scale image datasets [32, 41, 14], these networks learn hierarchical representations that progressively capture low-level cues such as edges or textures to high-level semantic concepts such as object categories. Their output typically consists of high-dimensional features grouped into feature maps (2D grids whose resolutions are lower than that of the input images due to the convolutions) or into global per-image embeddings. This shift enabled breakthroughs in more complex semantic tasks such as classification, detection, and segmentation, laying the foundation for modern computer vision.

Building on this success, Vision Transformers (ViTs) [35] demonstrated that attentionbased mechanisms could model long-range dependencies and global context more effectively than CNNs, leading to more performant backbones [144, 110] and more expressive and flexible visual embeddings.

The scaling of those deep architectures, combinedwith the appearance ofmany massive and diverse datasets, gave birth to very large, powerful and generic models, which we call foundation models [19, 132, 70, 159, 80, 146, 142, 25, 202, 50, 145, 150]. Those models can encode images into very complex and high-dimensional embeddings, each with their own specificities and downstream applications. For instance, DINO and its follow-ups [19, 132, 159] propose ways to scale up the number of used images without any semantic annotations (self-supervised paradigm) for multi-tasks feature maps while SAM [80, 146] specializes on encoding segmentation-focused features.

Another line of works studied multi-modal foundation models and especially imagetext combined modalities. Notably, CLIP (and its following works) [142, 25, 202, 50, 145] learned to align images and text encodings in a shared embedding space using datasets of image-textual caption pairs. By bridging these modalities, such models have unlocked open-vocabulary understanding, enabling systems to interpret and respond to arbitrary language queries about visual content.

These foundation models illustrate how large-scale pretraining can produce versatile, reusable features that generalize beyond any single benchmark, fundamentally changing the way visual representations can be applied.

#### 2.3.2 From Features to Interpretation: Addressing Known and Unknown Tasks

Having discussed how visual representations evolved from handcrafted features to deep multimodal embeddings, we now turn to how these representations are used to interpret images. In computer vision, interpretation refers to the process of extracting structured meaning from pixels in order to understand what objects are present, where they are located, and how they relate to one another or to a broader context. Historically, following the increase of the features complexity, this field has progressed from rigid, fixed-label systems to flexible, open-ended reasoning engines. This evolution aligns perfectly with the dual requirements of an embodied agent: the need for reliable perception of tasks explicitly defined at design time (for self-functioning), i.e. Known Tasks, and the adaptability to handle user-driven requests that remain unknown until deployment (Unknown Tasks). Some downstream tasks example are illustrated in Figure 2.8.

Interpretation for Known Tasks: Fixed-Category Perception. At the foundational level, interpretation focuses on recognizing a pre-defined set of concepts essential for the system's routine operation. In these "closed-set" paradigms, deep learning models are trained to classify, detect, or segment objects from a fixed vocabulary defined during training (e.g., the 80 classes of the COCO dataset or the standard categories of CIFAR-10) [121, 69], without the need to adapt to dynamic, user-specified queries. The pipeline is generally consistent across those tasks: feature maps extracted from the backbone, as the foundation to interpret an image, serve as the input for a lightweight task-specific neural network, usually called "decoder" or "head", and learn on a small amount of annotated data to map those features to the class labels.

For an embodied agent, this form of interpretation serves the Functional Core. It provides passive perception: a constant, reliable stream of semantic information required for self-functioning tasks such as navigation (distinguishing "floor" from "obstacle"), affordance detection, or basic object manipulation. Because the categories are known a priori, these models can be highly optimized for speed and stability, acting as the agent's semantic reflexes.

Interpretation for Unknown Tasks: Query-Driven Focus. However, a robot operating in human environments must also respond to requests that cannot be anticipated during training. This moves interpretation into the domain of Unknown Tasks, where the target concepts are open-vocabulary. The first tier of this flexibility is query-driven focus. Instead of predicting all possible labels, models leverage multi-modal alignment to compare visual features against arbitrary text queries (e.g., CLIP [142], OpenSeg [25]). This enables many open-vocabulary downstream tasks, such as detection, segmentation, image retrieval and classification. Similarly, promptable segmentation models like SAM [80] allow users to define a target by pointing at them (e.g. clicking) for instance segmentation. SAM2 [146], its follow-up, extends the idea to videos, enabling long-term interactive object tracking.

In the context of Embodied AI, this capability acts as a local cognitive tool. It shifts perception from passive pre-defined monitoring to active task execution conditioned on user intent. When a user asks "Find my keys," the agent uses these open-vocabulary tools to dynamically filter the scene, focusing its attention solely on the regions relevant to the immediate user query.

Interpretation for Unknown Tasks: Global Reasoning. Finally, addressing unknown tasks often requires more than just localizing specific objects based on keywords; it demands a holistic understanding of the scene to answer open-ended questions, explain visual content, or reason about complex spatial and semantic relationships. This capability stems from the development of Vision–Language Models (VLMs) [92, 91, 2, 103, 89], which align visual and textual representations in a shared embedding space to be processed by Large Language Models (LLMs). In such architectures, visual features, typically obtained from pre-trained 2D foundation models like CLIP, are projected as "visual tokens" into the same latent domain as encoded textual inputs. Recent systems such as BLIP-2 [92, 91], Flamingo [2], and LLaVA [103, 89] demonstrate this capability, integrating frozen visual backbones with pre-trained LLMs to achieve zero-shot understanding and multi-modal conversation.

For an embodied agent, this serves as the global cognitive core. It enables the robot to answer abstract questions ("Is this room safe for a baby?") or plan long-horizon tasks ("Clean up the table") by interpreting the visual scene holistically. This layer bridges the gap between seeing the world and thinking about it, allowing the agent to formulate plans based on a deep, contextual understanding of its surroundings.

#### 2.3.3 Limitations of 2D-Only Representations for Embodied AI

Despite the growing interest in 3D perception, many embodied AI systems still rely primarily on two-dimensional vision. Using 2D sensors, typically RGB cameras, remains the most accessible and cost-effective way to perceive the world. This simplicity has made 2D-based approaches a natural foundation for many interactive agents, especially when combined with recent advances in deep learning and large multimodal models. They allow robots to benefit directly from the progress of image-based perception and reasoning models, without requiring expensive 3D sensors or intermediary geometric reconstruction.

These 2D-based strategies span multiple levels of embodied intelligence. Some approaches [39] infer task-relevant representations of the visual environment, delegating decision-making to a separate cognitive or planning module. Others operate in a fully end-to-end manner, predicting decisions directly from raw RGB inputs. Depending on the framework, these outputs may take the form of high-level textual instructions for a robot [17] or even low-level motor commands [18, 68], particularly with the recent emergence of vision-language-action (VLA) models [113]. Together, these examples illustrate that 2D perception remains a powerful and versatile interface connecting vision, language, and action.

However, despite their versatility, 2D representations remain fundamentally constrained by two intrinsic limitations when applied to embodied agents: the lack of explicit geometry and, crucially, the lack of structured temporal persistency.

The Geometry Gap: Ambiguity and Viewpoint Dependency. First, a single 2D view is merely a projection of a three-dimensional scene. Multiple distinct 3D configurations can produce the same 2D image, introducing inherent geometric ambiguity regarding depth, occlusion, and object volumes. This makes it difficult for agents to reason about physical interactions or spatial layout. Furthermore, features extracted from one viewpoint rarely generalize across novel perspectives. As an agent moves, the 2D appearance of an object changes drastically, leading to inconsistent semantic interpretations unless grounded in a unified 3D structure [168, 149].

The Persistence Gap: On-the-fly Processing vs. Map-Based Reasoning. Second, and perhaps more critical for long-term autonomy, is the issue of memory. Pure 2D approaches typically process the environment as a continuous data stream of observations. The agent reacts to the current frame or a finite context window of recent frames. This streambased paradigm struggles with global context: if a user queries the agent about a room visited ten minutes ago (e.g., "Where did you leave the keys?"), the model must either search through its entire video history, which scales poorly in terms of compute and latency, or risk catastrophic forgetting. In contrast, a 3D reconstruction acts as a persistent, structured memory. By integrating observations into a spatial map, the agent aggregates

information over time, resolving inconsistencies and occlusion. This allows the agent to query the representation at any moment regarding any location without needing to retain or re-process the raw sensory stream [209].

These challenges motivate the development of methods that reason directly in 3D space. Yet, while 3D data promises richer geometric and semantic understanding, it also introduces new costs: acquiring dense geometry or semantic annotations is often expensive and impractical. In this context, image-based 3D representations such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) offer a promising middle ground. NeRFs reconstruct 3D structure directly from 2D observations, alleviating the limitations of purely 2D models while retaining compatibility with 2D annotations and pretrained vision–language representations. In the following, we explore how NeRF-based 3D representations enable richer scene understanding and serve as a natural bridge between 2D perception and embodied 3D reasoning.

# 2.4 3D NeRF Interpretation

While 2D vision provides powerful tools for image understanding, it remains inherently limited for interpreting three-dimensional scenes. These limitations directly affect how embodied agents can perceive, reason, and act within real-world environments.

Among existing 3D paradigms, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) offer a particularly compelling foundation. Learned directly from multi-view imagery, they encode a continuous volumetric representation of a scene that can be either interpreted directly or enriched with semantic information.

In the context of embodied AI, interpreting NeRFs enables agents to maintain a coherent and semantically grounded model of their surroundings, supporting both passive scene understanding and active task execution. In this section, we review the main approaches to 3D NeRF interpretation, first focusing on direct neural field interpretation and then on the integration of 2D feature representations within the radiance field. Finally, we conclude this background section by discussing how interpreting Neural Radiance Fields connects to Embodied AI and Spatial AI.

#### 2.4.1 Interpreting Radiance Fields as 3D Representations

While originally designed for photorealistic novel-view synthesis, a NeRF is fundamentally a 3D representation that implicitly encodes both geometry and appearance. Beyond rendering, its continuous volumetric formulation offers compelling properties for 3D scene understanding. Consequently, a growing body of work explores how to interpret NeRFs directly as 3D representations without taking advantage of NeRF differentiability for scene-specific fine-tuning. In this context, we refer to generalizable semantic NeRF any method capable of extracting semantic information from any pre-trained NeRF using a single forward pass.

One line of work leverages hypernetwork-based approaches, which process raw Vanilla NeRF network weights to function as backbones and produce holistic scene-level embeddings useful for interpretation. For instance, NeRF2Vec [143, 8] encodes a NeRF's MLP parameters into a compact vector representation usable for downstream tasks like scene classification or retrieval. LLaNA [3] extends this idea by aligning those embeddings with language representations for LLM processing, enabling open-ended questions over 3D scenes. More recently, weight2space [9] produces spatial tokens from NeRF weights to be interpreted by a fine-tuned LLaNA. However, all these hypernetworks methods remain limited to Vanilla NeRF processing, thus operating on single-object or very small-scale scenes, and limiting fine-grained spatial reasoning.

A second family of approaches instead learns generalizable semantic fields, where the goal is to produce novel segmentation view synthesis from a pre-trained RGB NeRF without any scene-specific ground-truth segmentation. Rather than relying on scenespecific optimization, the methods learn their models on annotated NeRF datasets to infer consistent semantic segmentation on novel environments. Those methods are quite varied, using various tricks to obtain this scene segmentation. Notably, NeSF [172] learns to predict a semantic field from an explicit regular density grid, thus needing an expensive grid decoding of the NeRF to interpret the 3D geometry. GNeSF [22] and SemanticRay [100] on the other hand use an explicit generalization mechanism that computes 2D multiview features from the observed images, fuses those into per-point features, decodes the features into semantics and projects those into the novel view.

However, no methods truly interpret scene-level NeRF models with fine-grained spatial understanding, e.g. directly from the grid features. This is because implicit neural representations are inherently scene-specific: each NeRF defines its own latent space, making specific regions of the representations non-reproducible and difficult to generalize across models. This necessitates either limiting the scope to a holistic understanding of object-NeRFs (as in hypernetworks) or learning semantic fields from the geometry or the multi-view images rather than the NeRF models themselves. Furthermore, existing approaches are often tailored to narrow downstream tasks such as classification or retrieval, limiting their versatility for broader semantic understanding.

A successful alternative builds on the dual 2D–3D nature of NeRFs, lifting semantically rich 2D features (usually from pre-trained foundation models) into the 3D domain, forming the basis of most recent advances in NeRF 3D scene understanding.

## 2.4.2 From 2D to 3D : Uplifting 2D Semantics into 3D Feature Fields

Neural Radiance Fields are not limited to learning RGB colors; in principle, they can learn any quantity that can be projected and supervised from 2D observations. Building on this idea, feature fields generalize the NeRF formulation to predict arbitrary per-point feature signals rather than only color. Formally, the mapping

$$F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \rightarrow (\mathbf{c}, \sigma), \quad (2.16)$$

is extended to

$$F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \longrightarrow (\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{c}, \sigma), \quad (2.17)$$

where f ∈ R �� represents one (or multiple) vector that may encode any type of data, whether it is color, semantics (e.g. per-point semantic class or instance), physical properties (e.g. temperature, sound, magnetic waves, ...), or any other latent descriptor (e.g. 2D Foundation Models embeddings). Supervision is achieved by reprojecting the predicted features onto the image plane and comparing them to their corresponding 2D feature maps, mirroring the color reconstruction process used in the original NeRF formulation.

Although NeRF can learn fields of infinite different natures, we focus in this thesis on semantic-related fields. We can separate semantic fields into two types.

A first line of research focuses on learning explicit scene semantics. Semantic-NeRF [207], notably, was the first to introduce semantic fields within the NeRF framework. Using ground-truth semantic segmentation maps, it augments the radiance field with per-point semantic logits, enabling dense 3D semantic understanding. The extension to instance-level fields [108] follows naturally when appropriate 2D annotations are available, allowing each point to encode instance-specific information. These early approaches laid the foundation for integrating semantics directly into NeRF representations, inspiring subsequent works [49, 85] that explore richer and more general forms of 3D semantic reasoning. However, the need for scene-specific 2D segmentation maps to interpret the scene is very limiting for most real-life applications.

Another direction for enriching NeRF representations with semantics leverages 2D feature encoders, transferring their knowledge into the 3D domain by learning feature fields from pre-trained foundation models. This strategy provides richer, more general, and implicitly structured semantic information, while benefiting from the robustness and scalability of mature 2D encoders. As 2D vision models evolved from self-supervised visual encoders to multimodal vision–language systems, feature fields inherited their capabilities, progressively enabling more expressive and interactive forms of 3D interpretation.

A wide variety of approaches [82, 76, 40, 197, 170, 46, 109, 83] follow this paradigm, each distilling different types of encoders depending on the target downstream task. For instance, DINO features have been distilled for semantic segmentation [82], while CLIP and related multimodal encoders such as OpenSeg provide open-vocabulary understanding in methods like LeRF [76] and OpenNeRF [40]. SAM features enable instance segmentation and mask propagation across viewpoints, as demonstrated in SAM-NeRF [46] and related frameworks [109]. More recently, RelationField [83] learns a Bert [33] (i.e. language-only encoder) feature field from LLM-generated textual descriptions of the objects relationships. Together, these methods illustrate the versatility of feature distillation for aligning NeRFs with a growing ecosystem of powerful 2D foundation models.



Figure 2.9: 2D CLIP vs LERF. The left visualizes similarity interpolated over patchwise CLIP embeddings, and the right rendered from LERF. Because volumetric language rendering incorporates information from multiple views, 3D relevancy activation maps have better alignment with the underlying scene geometry. Figure is from [76].

Beyond distillation of 2D encoders, several approaches learn self-supervised feature fields directly within the NeRF framework notably using contrastive learning: a paradigm that trains models by pulling representations of similar items together while pushing dissimilar ones apart. For example, Garfield [78] applies this principle over multi-view SAM-derived masks to build hierarchical 3D groupings, while NeRF-SOS [43] leverages DINO and density with contrastive objectives to segment objects without explicit labels. These methods treat per-point embeddings analogously to 2D backbone features, promoting 3D semantic consistency through these similarity signals.

Using feature fields to interpret 3D scenes represents a major shift from direct NeRF interpretation methods. Unlike those approaches, feature fields augment the radiance field with semantic information learned from 2D features. This means that geometry, appearance, and semantics are co-encoded within the same 3D representation, allowing each point in space to carry both physical and conceptual meaning. For embodied AI, this joint representation enables agents not only to perceive the structure of their surroundings but also to understand it inside one single unified map of their environment.

Additionally, a key advantage of this formulation lies in its multi-view consistency. By aggregating 2D features extracted from multiple viewpoints, the field reconciles per-image embeddings into a unified coherent 3D space. Because individual 2D feature maps are often low-resolution, this aggregation effectively increases spatial density, enabling the rendering of fine-grained feature maps, as illustrated with open-vocabulary segmentation in Figure 2.9. The fusion process also enhances robustness to changes in viewpoint, illumination, and occlusion, yielding spatially consistent and invariant semantics that surpass their 2D counterparts.

Nevertheless, training and exploiting feature fields introduces notable challenges. The high dimensionality of feature embeddings substantially increases memory usage and computational cost. Moreover, the process of lifting 2D features into 3D remains fundamentally 2D-driven and lacks the intrinsic geometric reasoning that explicit 3D encoders could provide. Finally, unlike direct NeRF interpretation methods that infer semantics in a single forward pass, feature fields require per-scene optimization before

their semantics can be exploited, limiting scalability for large datasets or real-time use.

Despite these limitations, feature fields constitute a powerful middle ground between reconstruction and interpretation. They transform NeRFs into a unified representation where meaning and structure coexist. By leveraging the dual 2D–3D nature of NeRFs and the expressive capacity of pre-trained 2D foundation models, they enable embodied agents to operate with spatially grounded semantics, bridging the gap between perception and scene understanding.

#### 2.4.3 Towards Embodied AI with Neural Radiance Fields

Building semantically enriched 3D representations marks a key milestone toward embodied artificial intelligence. For embodied agents, perception must not only capture an explicit understanding of the scene's structure, i.e. where objects are, how they relate, and what interactions they afford, but also support reasoning and action planning based on user-given tasks. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), when augmented with semantic feature fields, are adapted for all these requirements: they encode a coherent 3D scene model that can be interpreted by reasoning systems to plan and act, while maintaining the geometric and semantic consistency required for spatial understanding.

In modern embodied systems, large language models (LLMs) [1, 4, 165, 112] and Vision-Language models (VLMs) [103, 101, 102, 89] often serve as the cognitive core of the agent: interpreting user queries, reasoning over contextual information, and producing actionable plans [44]. Connecting NeRF-based scene models to such LLMs, however, remains a challenging problem. Early approaches such as LLaNA [3] and Spatial LLaNA [9] demonstrated that Vanilla NeRF embeddings and spatial tokens can be aligned with language representations through fine-tuning, allowing LLMs to interpret object-NeRF scenes. Yet, enabling LLMs to process full, complex 3D NeRF environments as direct inputs remains unexplored.

However, beyond NeRFs, related research has successfully connected LLMs with other 3D modalities such as point clouds (e.g., LEO [63, 64], Scene-LLM [48], ...) and Gaussian splatting (e.g., ChatSplat [21], SplatTalk [167]), highlighting a growing trend toward unifying 3D perception and language-driven reasoning. These advances (especially the methods from the differentiable Gaussian splatting representation) suggest that NeRFs could similarly serve as a foundation for integrating 3D understanding into multimodal reasoning systems.

While direct 3D-LLM connections allow holistic scene interpretation and high-level reasoning, they often lack fine-grained spatial control. The reasoning model can describe or analyze the global scene but struggles to selectively focus on specific objects or subregions of the scene. Recent approaches [195] address this limitation by coupling LLM-based reasoning with local perception tools that provide detailed visual or semantic grounding which can be called upon by the LLM. For instance, Chat-With-NeRF [192] combines the LLM with a LeRF [76] model, enabling the agent to query local semantics and focus

its attention across the scene. This framework reflects a natural evolution of embodied intelligence: high-level cognition guided by adaptable, localized sensory queries.

Together, these advances outline a clear trajectory toward agents capable of spatially grounded reasoning. By coupling NeRF-based 3D representations with language-driven reasoning and adaptive perception modules, embodied systems gain both a global understanding of their environment and the ability to act locally with precision. In this sense, neural feature fields not only enrich 3D reconstruction with semantics but also lay the conceptual and technical foundations for embodied AI, where seeing, understanding, and acting merge into a unified process.

# A Foundation Architecture for Robust and Extensible NeRF Map Reconstruction in Real-World Settings

Establishing a reliable 3D map is the prerequisite for any downstream understanding task. However, standard Neural Fields often struggle with the unconstrained nature of in-the-wild robotic data, such as varying observation distances and sparse viewpoints. This chapter introduces RING-NeRF, an architecture designed to bridge these robustness gaps through two structural inductive biases: a continuous multi-scale representation of the scene and a strictly enforced invariance of the latent space across spatial and scale domains. Leveraging these priors, we design a unified reconstruction process that achieves state-of-the-art quality on challenging tasks including anti-aliasing, few-view reconstruction, and initialization-free SDF reconstruction without requiring complex task-specific supervision. Furthermore, RING-NeRF allows for a fully scalable reconstruction process: it supports both dynamic adaptive resolution and spatial increments, paving the way for truly extensible reconstruction in unbounded environments.

# 3.1 Context and Motivation

For embodied AI agents to operate effectively in real-world environments, they must be able to build and maintain an accurate 3D model of their surroundings as part of their Functional Core activities. Such a representation forms the foundation for perception, navigation, and interaction, enabling the agent to reason about its environment and execute user-specified tasks. Neural Radiance Fields have rapidly become a central paradigm for high-fidelity scene reconstruction. Their ability to reconstruct detailed and consistent 3D representations directly from a simple RGB camera makes them particularly suitable for robotics-based environment 3D mapping.

However, current NeRF formulations still face important limitations that restrict their deployment in real-world scenarios, especially regarding embodied AI. Most recent associated research therefore concentrates on addressing challenges related to:

#### CHAPTER 3. A FOUNDATION ARCHITECTURE FOR ROBUST AND EXTENSIBLE NERF MAP RECONSTRUCTION IN REAL-WORLD SETTINGS

- Nature of scene by transitioning from object-centric scenes to large open unbounded ones, using mostly space contraction [10];
- Robustness notably by managing free motion trajectories with variations of the observation distance while avoiding aliasing artefacts (mostly through the integration of Level of Detail (LOD) in the model [11, 12]); or by reducing drastically the number of supervised views through different kinds of regularization [193, 65, 129];
- Extensibility by shifting from a holistic and fixed reconstruction process to an incremental (extensibility in the number of views) and adaptive (extensibility in resolution) process, mainly through the use of a frozen decoder [212, 106].

Much of the recent research in NeRF has focused on overcoming only one of these challenges at a time and introduce specialized and often complex mechanisms that increase computational cost and reduce composability. In practice, comprehensive mapping systems for embodied AI require all three properties to coexist within a single, efficient framework. Achieving this joint capability is crucial for deploying NeRF-based perception models in dynamic, unbounded, and uncertain environments.

Rather than introducing yet another heavy and task-specific solution, we propose in this chapter RING-NeRF, a versatile and simple NeRF architecture by rethinking usual grid-based models to introduce two inductive biases. We first represent a 3D scene as a continuous multi-scale representation. Secondly, we also make the decoder's latent space invariant in position and scale. Together, these two priors enable the production of intrinsic continuous LOD of the scene without explicit supervision. We demonstrate experimentally that, when combined with adapted cone casting and coarse-to-fine optimization, the resulting architecture is able to compete on several tasks with on-par quality performances with dedicated state-of-the-art solutions while improving speed, robustness and extensibility. The overall process is also simple, easy to implement and generic enough to be coupled with other specific solutions. Our contributions in this chapter can be summarized as follows:

- 1. an architecture that, by construction, represents the scene with a continuous and unbounded level of detail without the need for LOD-specific supervision and which permits extensible reconstruction in both resolution and space;
- 2. a distance-aware forward mapping compatible with scene contraction, that takes benefits of the continuous multi-scale representation with an adapted cone casting process to avoid aliasing artefacts when varying the observation distance.
- 3. a continuous coarse-to-fine reconstruction process that improves the convergence and stability (especially in challenging setups such as supervision with few viewpoints or no scene-specific initialization for SDF reconstruction).

### 3.2 Related Works

From its original iteration [120], a majority of current research focuses on overtaking limitations of NeRF-based reconstruction in terms of adaptability to various natures of scene, robustness (to varying observation distances or limited amount of viewpoints) and reconstruction extensibility.

Adaptability to various natures of the scene. The ability of Neural Fields to reconstruct various natures of scene depends on three factors. The first one is related to its architecture itself. Tri-plane architectures [62, 213, 20] are mostly designed for object-centric reconstruction (as they provide a higher density of information in the center of the scene) while vanilla and 3D grid-based NeRF are able to cover a wider variety of scenes, though they initially were still constrained to a limited volume. A second factor is related to the representation of the 3D space of the scene, especially to represent distant elements in open scenes. As mentioned before, some approaches use two different NeRF models to reconstruct separately the foreground and the background [204], while some apply space contraction to the 3D scene coordinates [204, 10, 12] to map the infinite scene volume into a bounded one. The last factor is related to the initialization of the Neural Fields. While the random initialization of density-based NeRF can adjust to almost any nature of scenes, the convergence of SDF-based (Signed Distance Function) Neural Fields is extremely sensitive to their parameters' initialization, as stated in [6]. Current solutions rely on a scene-specific initialization (using an SDF field representing a sphere for outdoor scenes or an inverted sphere for indoor scenes), making them unable to adapt automatically to any scene. We

#### CHAPTER 3. A FOUNDATION ARCHITECTURE FOR ROBUST AND EXTENSIBLE NERF MAP RECONSTRUCTION IN REAL-WORLD SETTINGS

built RING-NeRF on top of a grid-based NeRF with space contraction to support large, unbounded scene reconstruction. The method is also designed to remain robust in practice, mitigating initialization sensitivity and avoiding any need for scene-specific heuristics.

Robustness to observation distance variations. The initial NeRF model, as well as most of the subsequent works, relies implicitly on the hypothesis of a constant distance of observation to the scene.

Indeed, the NeRF model provides a per-3D-point scene density representation and a rendering process which does not take into account the increasing volume covered by a pixel with respect to the distance to the cameras. As underlined in Mip-NeRF [11], this discrepancy induces artefacts such as over-contrasted images or aliasing phenomenon when the distance of observation differs from the ones used at reconstruction time. To avoid these artefacts, the rendering process needs to assess the density and color for a volume instead of a point. In the current state-of-the-art, two main approaches are used (or their combination, as in Zip-NeRF [12]). The first solution consists of representing the scene with different levels of detail (LOD), in order to vary the precision of the reconstruction based on the observation distance. This is usually done by using a LOD-aware latent space [36, 161, 171, 11, 12, 188], meaning that the LOD information is already encoded in the inputs of the MLP. This can be achieved by using a per-LOD decoder as in PyNeRF [171] or by incorporating LOD information in the latent feature as done in Zip-NeRF [12] and VR-NeRF [188]. One main flaw of these solutions is that they require the supervision of every used LOD which makes it impossible to have different observation distances between the train views and novel synthesised views.

A second approach consists of defining the latent representation of a volume as the mean of the latent features of the points included in the volume. It requires integrating the features over the volume, which can be achieved through convolutions for tri-plan representation [62, 213], or through super-sampling of the latent space for 3D grids as also done in Zip-NeRF [12]. However, these approaches lengthen the training and rendering processes as they increase the number of computations.

In contrast to these approaches, RING-NeRF decouples LOD from the decoder through an LOD-invariant latent space, allowing the model to naturally adapt to varying observation distances without supervising multiple LODs or incurring the heavy computation typical of volume-integrating methods.

Robustness to limited amount of viewpoints. As already stated, NeRF is subject by construction to the shape-radiance ambiguity [204]. If not enough supervision viewpoints are available, the optimization might overfit them while not providing consistent 3D reconstruction nor generalization to non-supervised viewpoints. A first family of solutions to overcome this limitation consists of regularizing the reconstruction process through additional losses, whether via geometric [129, 199, 185] or semantic [65] regularization.

The second family of solutions relies on a progressive reconstruction of the details of the scene, as introduced in FreeNeRF [193] and Nerfies [135]. Restraining the ability of the model to reconstruct a complex scene at early stages enforces the consistency of the reconstruction over the different supervision viewpoints. This presents the advantage of bringing stability while keeping a fairly simple training process. However, these latter solutions mostly rely on Vanilla models for stability purposes and require long training duration.

RING-NeRF follows this progressive philosophy through a continuous coarse-to-fine scheme, extending it to grid-based neural fields to provide the same stabilization benefits without relying on vanilla architectures or incurring long training times.

Reconstruction extensibility. Two kinds of extensibility should be distinguished: extensibility with respect to the number of views or the resolution. The first one is related to reconstructing new scene areas while keeping the previously reconstructed ones unchanged, notably by avoiding catastrophic forgetting of previous areas. The usual solution consists of using grid-based approaches with a pre-trained position-invariant decoder that is frozen during the reconstruction[212].

On the other side, the extensibility of the resolution consists in dynamically increasing the level of detail of a previously reconstructed scene. This problem is intractable for classic architectures such as vanilla NeRF and I-NGP since the number of layers (respectively grids) of those approaches cannot increase during the reconstruction. Some rare solutions, such as Neural Sparse Voxel Fields [106], combine a pre-trained decoder with a data structure allowing to dynamically allocate voxels to increase the reconstruction resolution. This is however a crucial stake, notably for embodied AI, as being able to adapt the precision of the representation based on the complexity of the scene permits to optimize the computational efficiency (both in speed and memory requirements) with minimal loss in quality.

With its scale- and position-invariant decoder, RING-NeRF is perfectly adapted to be the pre-trained backbone of both view and resolution extensibility.

#### 3.3.2 NeRF Representation with Inductive Biases

Classic grid-based representations, such as the one introduced by I-NGP[124], rely on two key elements: a unique MLP decoder which transforms a latent space into an output space (color, density or SDF), and a 3D (hierarchical) grid of latent features that implicitly defines a unique mapping function from the scene space onto the decoder latent space.

We can distinguish two different approaches used to extend the mono-scale representation to a multi-scale representation: conditioning with scale information the decoding process [12, 171] and/or defining separate per-LOD mapping functions [171].

In both cases, the training becomes more complex, leading to potential convergence issues as it relies solely on additional specific supervision.

Instead, we propose to condition the mapping function itself with scale information and introduce inductive biases to guide the convergence.

$$(dc)^3 \det(J(\mathbf{x})) = \left(\frac{1}{g^{Lb}}\right)^3 \iff L = -\frac{\log\left(dcb\sqrt[3]{\det(J(\mathbf{x}))}\right)}{\log(g)} \quad (3.4)$$

Based on the contraction function defined in 3.3, this becomes :

$$L = \begin{cases} -\frac{\log(d.c.b)}{\log(g)} & \text{if } \|\mathbf{x}\| \leq 1 \\ -\frac{\log(d.c.b)+2\log(2-\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{x}\|})-4\log(\|\mathbf{x}\|)}{\log(g)} & \text{if } \|\mathbf{x}\| > 1 \end{cases} \quad (3.5)$$

With this formula, we can first notice that, in the non-contracted space (inside the cube of size 1), the LOD �� does not depend on the contraction function, and its value decreases with the distance as expected.

However, further in the scene, inside the contracted space, we notice that the LOD L might increase with the distance (due to ���� ��(||x||4 )), as illustrated in figure 3.6. While this can seem pretty non-intuitive at first, this has a logical explanation. Because of the contraction, the fixed volume of a grid cell will represent an increasing volume of the scene as the distance to the scene's center increases, up to an infinite volume. This clashes with the idea that the further our sample, the lower our chosen LOD needs to be. This duality is represented in the equation by the ���� ��(||x||4 ) − ���� ��(��) part, and it shows that, at some point, the contraction function will always take the advantage on the distance term as our sample grow further from the center. Hence, whatever the position of the camera, if a ray continues far enough, the chosen LOD will increase at some point because of the contraction function. This also implies that the more resolute grids are used both for near objects and further areas, which would not have been the case if we did not consider the contraction function and which provides a more optimal use of the grid hierarchy with less unused parameters.

We can note that our contracted cone casting process is similar to ZipNeRF [12] and VR-NeRF [188] propositions. However, the first one rather derives a contracted scale factor from its Gaussian samples while the latter directly computes the LOD in the contracted space, which can be considered an approximation of our computation.

Forward mapping. As illustrated in Figure 3.2, we use the determined LOD to compute a distance-dependent weighted sum of the features, which is fed to our decoder and transformed into density (or SDF) and radiance.

#### 3.3.4 Continuous Coarse-To-Fine and Resolution Extensibility

Recent works proposed to use coarse-to-fine optimization to improve the stability of NeRF models, especially when facing more challenging setups, including with few images [193] and surface-based models [95]. It consists of optimizing progressively the different LOD of the representation, from coarse levels to the most precise ones. The goal of this progressive optimization is to avoid the shape-radiance ambiguity [204] by introducing a strong regularization through LOD restriction, then relaxing progressively this regularization once the coarse geometry of the scene is reconstructed to recover the details of the scene.

The coarse-to-fine reconstruction process of RING-NeRF consists of estimating progressively the LOD of the mapping function from the coarsest to the finest ones. In practice, it implies, during the cone casting, to clamp the samples' LOD up to a maximal LOD ��, the grids of level �� + 1 and above being set to zero and not optimized. Moreover, since our architecture provides continuous LOD, the coarse-to-fine optimization can be achieved continuously in the LOD space by using a linear scheduler �� = (��0 + ������ �� ) ∈ R + with �� the current epoch, ������ �� a hyperparameter describing the speed of the process and ��0 defining the number of used grids at initialization; up to a specified maximum resolution.

Furthermore, the RING-NeRF architecture is more adapted than I-NGP-based architectures [95] for coarse-to-fine training. Indeed, for solutions based on the concatenation of features, keeping grid values to zero implies that some dimensions of the decoder's latent space are not supervised. When a grid starts to be optimized, those unsupervised dimensions are suddenly used. The weights of the decoder thus need to be refined, with a global effect on the whole scene. On the opposite, our solution keeps supervising all the dimensions of the decoder latent space, and when a new grid gets optimized, it only implies more degrees of freedom to define the mapping function between the scene space and the decoder latent space. This also means that our scene reconstruction can be refined by adding dynamically new grid levels without modifying the decoder's weights or previously trained grids, as we demonstrate experimentally in section 3.4.3. This resolution extensibility property opens the path to adaptive resolution models, where the precision used to describe an area depends on the details needed, to optimize efficiency both in memory consumption and training duration.

#### 3.4.1 Novel View Synthesis and Anti-Aliasing

This experiment aims to evaluate the reconstruction quality through the ability to synthesize viewpoints that are not supervised during the reconstruction. These new viewpoints differ from the angle of observation, but also from the distance of observation. The latter is particularly important since a reconstruction or a rendering process that does not take correctly into account the distance of observation leads to artefacts, from over-contrasted rendering to aliasing phenomenon. The challenge is to avoid these artefacts while keeping the reconstruction and rendering process as fast as possible.

Dataset. The evaluation relies on the dataset introduced by Mip-NeRF-360 [10]. This dataset is composed of 9 scenes, each containing both a central area and complex background in both inside and outside setups. Since the trajectory keeps a constant distance to the central part, each viewpoint is represented with a pyramid of 4 different image resolutions to simulate a variation of the distance of observation combined with a change of the camera FOV, following the Mip-NeRF [11] original anti-aliasing evaluation pipeline.

Algorithms. We compare our solution against several grid-based NeRF baselines, both with (PyNeRF [171] and Zip-NeRF [12]) and without anti-aliasing processing (Nerfacto), using their Nerfstudio implementations.

Hyperparameters Our Novel View Synthesis and Anti-Aliasing experiments on the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset are done on one unique configuration for all our baselines and setups. We mostly use the configuration provided in the article of ZipNeRF [12] with few differences. We trained each baselines for 25k iterations with a batch size of 2 16 rays. We use the RAdam Optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10−2 , and a cosine-decayed scheduler over the whole training to 10−3 .

Regarding the model itself, we use a hash grid pyramid of 10 grids with a growth factor of 2, with grid resolutions ranging from 16 to 8192. Contrary to the original Zip-NeRF, we use 8 features per level rather than 4, but with a similar hashmap size of 1283 = 2 21 (however note that our experiments with ZipNeRF were done also with a feature size of 8). We chose this parameter to maximize the quality of every baseline, as it is compatible with our GPU.
