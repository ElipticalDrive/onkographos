ABSTRACT
Article history:
The growing need to solve radiation protection and shielding problems in
reasonable computational times has led to an increasing interest in the use
of deterministic approaches. Codes of this type commonly use the discreteordinates method. In cases of propagation of point sources in low scattering
media, which are typical of shielding studies, the approximation leads to a
set of numerical artefacts known as ray effect. One of the most commonly
used techniques to mitigate this effect is the calculation of the uncollided flux,
which is the most affected flux component, with a fine transport operator. In
this paper we propose four different approaches: the method of characteristics (MOC), the trajectory-splitting MOC (TS-MOC), the Monte Carlo MOC
(MC-MOC) and the method based on quadrature points (QP). They have been
implemented inside the Cartesian solver IDT. This paper contains the formal
description of the four numerical methods and their verification over a set of
benchmark problems. An analysis of the accuracy and the computation performances of the methods is also given. Finally, a comparison of the four
methods on the Kobayashi benchmark problem is proposed.

1. Introduction
Transport problems in nuclear physics can be mainly grouped into two big families: critically and shielding
calculations. To the second category belongs the problems in which the radioactive source is particularly localized
in space. In these cases, deterministic methods relying on the discrete-ordinates approximation tend to provide
very poor results in term of accuracy, being not able to represent the effective particle propagation. In the discreteordinates methods particles are forced to travel along a finite number of directions which are, in general, not sufficient

The uncollided flux emitted from a point source presents two singularities: the first is a spatial singularity related
to the point-like nature of the source, while the second is directional, meaning that uncollided particles are directed
only along the direction joining the source to the target point [6].
In order to reduce the ray effect and, at the same time, propagate external point-sources, First-Collision Source
(FCS) methods are generally employed. These techniques are based on the decomposition of the flux in two different
components: the collided flux, generated by the particles that have collided at least once, and the uncollided one. By
doing so, the two contributions are evaluated separately using different approaches. Since the uncollided component is
generally the most affected by ray effect, it is solved using an ad-hoc transport discretization. This discretization must
be able to properly handle the spatial singularity of the source and, furthermore, to remove the ray effect. Secondly,
the first-collided source is calculated by applying the collision kernel to the uncollided flux. A second calculation is
then used for evaluating the collided component of the flux. In general, this is done using a standard discrete-ordinates
method.
We can find examples of FCS methods in several general-purpose deterministic transport codes. These techniques
generally differ from each other by the transport method used for the calculation of the uncollided flux. Historically,
one of the first FCS methods is the semi-analytic technique developed by Alcouffe et al. within the 2D transport code
TWODANT [7]. The method uses a ray-tracing technique for evaluating the flux attenuation at the vertices of the
spatial mesh of each region. By means of semi-analytical relations, it evaluates the currents at the interfaces of each
spatial region and finally, it calculates the region average fluxes by solving an angle-integrated balance equation. The
same code presents also a second First-Collision Source method based on a stochastic approach [5]. This second
algorithm relies on a Monte Carlo estimator for integrating the region average uncollided fluxes. The method operates
the ray tracing along randomly sampled trajectories crossing the entire domain. Each of them is generated by sampling
both the source point and the propagation direction. A different FCS strategy has been implemented in the transport

code ATTILA [8]: it makes use of a 3D quadrature formula for the computation of the angular moments of the
uncollided flux in each spatial region. More precisely, a ray-tracing strategy is used for the evaluation of the local
value of the uncollided flux on the quadrature points. Then, the code uses a linear mapping for reconstructing the flux
moments on the nodes of the tetrahedral finite-element mesh used by the code. A similar approach consists in using
a spatial (2D or 3D) quadrature rule for directly integrating the uncollided flux on the spatial mesh regions. This has
been applied to transport codes such as DENOVO [9] and Rattlesnake [10]. In a similar manner, FNSUNCL3, the
FCS algorithm in TORT [11], uses a single point quadrature (constant approximation) or uniform spatial quadrature
rules. The code JSNT-S also relies on an analogous strategy, but this time reconstructing the region average uncollided
flux using the uncollided flux point-wise values evaluated on the corners of the spatial mesh [12].
The IDT solver [13] presents a different technique based on the Method of Characteristics (MOC) in spherical
coordinates. This method consists of generating trajectories from the point source and propagate them all-over the
domain by using a properly chosen angular quadrature. The spatial integration is performed by using the spatial
discretization resulting from the intersection of these trajectories with the calculation mesh.
Another application of the MOC can be found in the FCS developed by Hanuš et al. for spatially distributed
sources [14]. Their approach is based on a double-MOC integration: the first one, similarly to IDT, is applied to the
uncollided flux spatial integration, and a second one is used for accounting the spatial dimensionality of the emitting
source.
This article focuses on the spatial integration of the uncollided flux moments emitted by point sources. We propose
and discuss different FCS strategies. A comparative study has been performed in order to assess their qualities and
features. The present work has been done within a mock-up version of the IDT XYZ transport solver [15].
More in detail, we have devoted Section 2 to an introduction on first collision techniques. In Section 3, the MOC
integration of constant and linear spatial moments is discussed. We hereby extend previous work by Zmijarevic et al.
[13]. Their implementation was limited to step-approximation, while the one we propose is able to account for linear
spatial order. In the same section, we will discuss newly developed angular quadratures for the MOC. The first one is
obtained using a uniform discretization of polar and azymuthal angles. The second one, instead, relies on a 2D mesh
over the domain boundaries [13].
For obvious geometrical reasons, the MOC trajectories, being generated from a single point in space have the
characteristic of spreading up throughout the domain as the distance from the source point increases. According to
this, the spatial regions which are closer to the source, being crossed by large numbers of trajectories, will present
a higher integration quality than the further ones. In addition, requiring an high number of trajectories will entail a
considerable computational cost. In order to mitigate this effect, we present in Section 4 a second version of the MOC
based on a trajectory splitting algorithm applied to a spherical mesh. This takes the name of Trajectory Splitting MOC
(TS-MOC). By splitting trajectories at a certain radial distance from the source, this technique is able to mitigate the
computational overhead involved by the ray-tracing algorithm for large numbers of trajectories.
In the third method we have removed the approximation of constant flux attenuation within the solid angle involved

2. The First Collision Source (FCS)
The FCS relies on the decomposition of the solution ψ of the transport equation into two components, namely the
uncollided and the collided flux,
ψ = ψu + ψc .
(2)
Let us consider the steady-state one-group neutral-particles linear Boltzmann equation with zero incoming flux as
boundary condition, symbolically represented as
Lψ = Cψ + Q,
(3)
where L is the leakage+removal operator, C is the scattering kernel and Q the source. By definition, the uncollided
flux is the solution of the equation
ψu = L−1 Q,
(4)
where the materials are considered as pure absorbing. Because of the decomposition of the flux in (2), the linear
Boltzmann transport equation (3) can be rewritten for the collided flux ψc as
Lψc = Cψc + QFCS .
(5)
Equation (5) is a classical transport problem whose external source is the so-called First Collision Source (FCS)
QFCS = Cψu .
(6)
Since most of the ray effect derives from the propagation of localized sources in pure absorbing media, Eq. (4)
is solved by taking particular care of the angular representation in each spatial region. The resulting FCS is better
distributed in space by minimizing errors from undesirable ray effect. Therefore, the FCS mitigates ray effect in the
second problem by removing the geometrical singularity of the flux. Eq. (5) is thus solved with the classical discrete
ordinates method [14].

3. The Method of Characteristics (MOC)
The MOC performs the integration of Eq. (16) thanks to a coordinate transformation to a spherical reference
frame centered in the point source. The angular variable is discretized into spherical surfaces according to a given
angular quadrature formula. For each angular surface, a continuous trajectory is then tracked starting from the source

geometry. Each mesh region is so decomposed into a set of homogeneous spherical-shaped slices (cones) having the
spherical incoming/outgoing transverse surfaces as bases, and the chord-length as radial height (see Figure 2 for a
graphical representation). Remark that, by using such representation, we implicitly neglect the angular dependence

3.1.1. Boundary quadrature (BQ)
The boundary quadrature is obtained by building a 2D Cartesian mesh on the boundaries of the domain, and by
projecting it to the unit sphere centered on the point-source. The 2D-Cartesian boundary mesh is obtained by fixing
a uniform step and adjusted to guarantee a user-defined minimum number of trajectories per boundary surface of the
calculation mesh. By connecting the source point to the nodes of this 2D Cartesian mesh, we identify a set of oblique
rectangular pyramids having as bases the Cartesian boundary mesh and as apex the source point. The solid angle
associated to each pyramid is the projection of the base of the pyramid on the unit sphere built around the source. The
reference direction of the trajectory is obtained by connecting the centroid of the base of the pyramid with the source
point. Figure 3 depicts in 2D the angular discretization strategy, where the “pyramids” take the shape of a triangle.
The trajectory weights Γ are integrated numerically by subdividing the solid angle ∆Ωt into smaller solid angles,
∆Ωt,k , such that ∆Ωt ≡ ∪k⊂t ∆Ωt,k . A step approximation is used for the spherical harmonics and the angular cosine
within ∆Ωt,k . The weights in Eq. (30) and (31) are thus the measures of each solid angle ∆Ωt,k , while the quadrature
point is the direction pointing to its centroid.

4. The Trajectory-Splitting MOC (TS-MOC)
The tests in [13] have shown results on the effectiveness and the robustness of the MOC. The accuracy of the
spatial integration of the flux moments and of the mass matrices depends on the number of trajectories passing through
each region. By construction, the geometrical factor s2 affects the distribution of trajectories in the space, leading to
trajectories that tend to rarefy as the distance from the source increases. More precisely, by resorting to the conediscretization shown in Figure 2, the cross sectional area associated to a cone with measure of the solid angle ∆ωt
is given by s2 ∆ωt . Thus, the regions close to the source are intersected by an higher number of chords than the
peripheral ones. This causes a consequent non-uniform accuracy of the volume integration that tends to decrease in
target regions that are far from the source. In other words, given a goal accuracy, the number of trajectories in the

The TS-MOC approaches the problem by splitting the trajectories after a certain distance from the source (splitting

4.2. The TS-MOC algorithm
As for standard MOC, the TS-MOC requires to sequentially solve the chords belonging to each trajectory because
of the continuity condition given by Eq. (38). The difference with respect to MOC is that, at splitting radii, a set of
ν child trajectories is generated. The children depend on the parent trajectory because of Eq. (38) which provides
the “entering flux”, ψ+c,ℓ−1,m , required to start the sweep. As it can be seen, this value depends on the single parent
trajectory, which means that a naive implementation may lead to a memory footprint of order O(νNℓ ). To avoid such
explosion, we implemented the sweeping strategy displayed in Algorithm 1, thus implicitly using a depth first search
ordering of the tree of sub-trajectories (see Figure 5 for an example of such ordering). In this configuration, one can
notice that the storage can be reduced to O(Nℓ ). In fact, as the tree is explored in depth, the parent trajectory updates
the value of the flux that will be used right after by one of its children. Once the deepest level is reached, the sweep
starts back from level Nℓ−1 going towards the deepest level and so on for the ν children of the same parent. The
entering flux for the ν children is the same given by the shared parent and, once all children are swept, it is needed no
more. The process continues in the same manner for all levels. Therefore, the entering flux can be stored in a single
buffer of the size of the largest Nl (c).
4.2.1. Need for an efficient weight integration

required to achieve the same accuracy. However, because of the splitting of solid angles, the number of Γ weights
that need to be evaluated in Eq. (23) increases considerably as compared to the MOC. This is because the solid angles
∆Ωc,ℓ,t , that defines the support of integrals in Eq. (25) and Eq. (26), changes for each child trajectory. Therefore,
computing Eq. (25) and Eq. (26) needs particular care in the TS-MOC implementation.

6.1. The single quadrature mode
The method requires the number of quadrature points N to be fixed in each target steps. This fixes the order of
the integration error residual. No error estimation is performed, the algorithms evaluates the integrand functions on
each quadrature points and return the approximation of the integral. In general, the method is more accurate when the
function in the region is sufficiently smooth. The accuracy of the approximation is expected to increase with the order
of the quadrature rule [19].
6.2. The automated integration mode

estimation, is met. Two options are available to refine the quadrature rule. It can be done by either increasing the
order of the quadrature (p-refinement), or by splitting the target region in sub-regions (h-refinement).

6.3. Considerations regarding the QP First-Collision Source method
The QP method requires to evaluate the flux attenuation along each trajectory, corresponding to the quadrature
points in each region. Its evaluation, as compared to the MOC, does not involve any solid angle and any angular
integration, but just the calculation of the exponential and geometrical attenuation of the flux. The mean number of
floating-point operations per chord is significantly lower than the one required by the MOC. On the other hand, the
cost of the algorithm scales with the number of quadrature points used per region, and inevitably with the number of
target regions.

7.1.2. Assessment on the effect of the Γ weight integration in MOC based methods
As seen in previous sections, the MOC and TS-MOC methods require to evaluate, for each trajectory, the trajectory weights Γ. These take the form of integrals of spherical harmonics over portions of solid angles. By their
nature, these terms are related to the projection operator of the uncollided flux on the spatial-angular moments. It is
therefore important to asses how much the accuracy of the Γ integration affects the accuracy of the result. This is even
more important in highly anisotropic problems since higher order spherical harmonics involved in the uncollided flux
expansion are in principle more difficult to integrate. We remind that the angular weights are specific to the MOC,
while they are not present in the QP method and MC-MOC.
Case 1 is studied by changing the parameters for computing Γ and by monitoring the errors of the uncollided flux
components. In this test, a P9 anisotropy level is used, corresponding to an angular flux expansion over a 100 spherical
harmonics base. The analysis is performed focussing on target regions (1, 1, 1) and (3, 3, 3). On each of them, we
have monitored the constant flux component (h = 1, m = 1) and the higher order flux component identified by the
indexes h = 99, m = 3. This last component correspond to the linear moment along the Y axis of the angular moment
k = 9, l = 9. The analysis consists in monitoring the variation of the relative error of the flux components with respect
to the number of chord-lengths. We have performed this study by using:
• the MOC method with the boundary-based quadrature (MOC-BQ). The accuracy of the method has been progressively increased by running simulations with decreasing values of the boundary surfaces steps, i.e. the
trajectory spacing δt,x and δt,y . For MOC-BQ, the angular weights are evaluated according to Eqs. (30) and (31)
by subdividing each solid angle in 2N × 2N smaller solid angles. Using the symbol Γ-N to specify the order of
the quadrature for Γ, we have tested from Γ-0, i.e. the step-approximation, up to Γ-3, i.e. a 64 sub solid angle
decomposition.
• the MOC method with the uniform quadrature (MOC-UQ). For the MOC-UQ, the accuracy has been progressively increased by running simulations with increasing number of trajectories. In this case, the integration
of the angular weights is performed by using the product type quadrature rule defined in Section 3.1.2 and
denoting with the symbol Γ-N the quadrature order corresponding to a solid angle decomposition into 2N × 2N
elements.
• the TS-MOC method with ν, i.e. the number of trajectories generated per splitting event, equal to 4. The
trajectory refinement is operated by progressively reducing the parameter δ2 . The starting quadrature of the
TS-MOC has been fixed to the coarser uniform quadrature requiring 4φ × 2θ angular directions at the pointsource. We run several simulations using tabulated orders for the Γ’s quadrature. These tabulated orders have
been obtained computing the Γ weights up to P9 moments and by requiring a decreasing error tollerance for the

8. Conclusion
The first collision source method is a very powerful technique, able to mitigate and, sometimes, completely
remove the ray effect produced by the propagation of localized sources in deterministic discrete-ordinates method. In
this paper we have studied and compared four different FCS techniques for point source propagation:
- the MOC, based on the uncollided flux integration by characteristic lines in spherical coordinates and on the
use of a properly chosen angular quadrature rule. It has been implemented at first in [13], and now it has been
extended up to the linear spatial order;
- the TS-MOC, which is the main novelty introduced by this research, comes as a variation of MOC. It relies on
a trajectory splitting algorithm in order to ensure a more uniform trajectory distribution over the domain, and
to reduce the overall computational cost of the MOC algorithm;
- the QP method, which preforms a region-by-region integration of the uncollided flux by means of a spatial
quadrature rule. It is able to progressively refine the quadrature formula in order to converge the accuracy of
the estimation up to the achievement of an a error related stopping criterion;
Matteo Falabino et al. / Journal of Computational Physics (2022)
35
- the MC-MOC, which uses a characteristic line integration and a random sampling of the propagation directions.
The tests have highlighted the robustness and effectiveness of MOC and TS-MOC methods in providing accurate
uncollided flux estimations in a limited computing time. TS-MOC appears as a very promising technique able to
guarantee an approximatively uniform trajectory-based spatial discretization of the domain. The method has shown
an increasing effectiveness with the increase of the problem sizes. QP method showed good performances only for
simulations with a limited number of regions. This method might be used whenever the user requires an estimate
of the uncollided flux on a circumscribed portion of the domain. Even if we focused only on isotropic sources, all
methods can be easily extended to anisotropic ones. However, particular care is needed in the case of TS-MOC for
correctly reconstructing the source along the directions of the child trajectories.
