Abstract
We define a Delaunay quadrangulation over a packing of two classes (colors) of disks. Each disk has two radii, a
small one preventing opposite-colored disks from being too close, and a 2.5 times larger one that prevents nearby
same-colored disks. The Delaunay quadrilateral edges are a subset of the edges of a Delaunay triangulation of
the disk centers. We retain Delaunay edges connecting opposite-colored disks, and discard those between samecolored disks. The retained Delaunay edges produce even-sided polygons. Any polygon with six or more sides
contains a forbidden monochromatic Delaunay triangle. Delaunay refinement, introducing the monochromatic
triangle circumcenter as an opposite-colored point, converts these to quads, but reduces the quality guarantees
provided by the packing radii. The ability to produce this quadrangulation, and achieve good quality, depends on
how the disks are arranged in the packing.
We have two algorithms for placing the disks. The first, “random,” generates everywhere-random quadrilateral
meshes with blue noise. It is based on Poisson-disk sampling. Disks are generated with random positions and
colors. The second algorithm, “biased,” produces large patches of boundary-aligned square tilings, connected by
small patches of random quads. It is advancing front. We place disks on the domain boundary, then deterministically extend a structured disk tiling into the interior. Where fronts collide, we fill in the remaining space with
the random algorithm and refine as needed. For both algorithms, the even-sided polygons are mostly quadrilaterals, with a few isolated many-sided polygons. Both efficiently produce high-quality meshes on non-convex planar
domains as well as curved surfaces. We may produce graded meshes by varying the disk radii over space.

For simple domains, structured quadrilateral grids are
usually generated using classical isoparametric mapping [ZP71] and transfinite mapping [GH73]. These methods produce elements with high quality. However, they
are difficult to robustly automate, especially for complex
geometries. Unstructured quadrilateral meshes are typically generated by one of two approaches: direct or indirect [Owe98]. An indirect algorithm starts with a triangular
or quad-dominant mesh and then converts all the triangles
into quads [IS02,EKMD10,SAD12]. A direct approach produces quads without any intermediate form of triangulation.
The main direct approaches are domain decomposition, advancing front and grid-based.
In decomposition methods, the domain is divided into
simpler convex or mappable regions. Then we solve interval assignment, the global problem of determining the number of mesh edges to put on each subregion side. The goal
is to be able to mesh each region independently, get good
element quality, and adhere to the input sizing function.
Finally, template-based, mapping or geometric algorithms
are utilized to generate the mesh for each of these regions.
There are many domain decomposition techniques. Tam and
Armstrong [TA91] introduced medial axis decomposition.
Joe [Joe91] decomposed the domain based on geometric algorithms. Quadros et al. [QRPG04] introduced an algorithm
that couples medial axis decomposition with an advancing
front method. Interval assignment is often solved as an optimization problem with linear constraints [TA93, Mit13];
Bommes et al. [BCE∗ 13] uses quadratic-objective optimization to perform both the decomposition and the interval assignment as a coupled problem, producing very high quality
quads over the curved surfaces typical of graphics character models. In general, decomposition methods produce high
quality meshes but they may require user interaction, especially if the domain has non-manifold boundaries.
Advancing front techniques start with placing nodes on
the domain boundaries. These represent the “initial front."
Quadrilateral elements are then formed by projecting each
edge on the front towards the interior. A new front is formed
using the edges on the new boundary of the quadrilateral
mesh. This process is repeated recursively until the domain
is completely covered with quadrilaterals. Zhu [ZZHW91] is
among the first to propose a quadrilateral advancing front algorithm. In his approach, two triangles are created using the
traditional advancing front methods then combined to form
a single quadrilateral. Blacker and Stephenson [BS91] introduced the Paving algorithm in which they place a complete row of quads between two sharp angles. White and
Kinney [WK97] enhanced the robustness of the paving algorithm through creating individual quadrilaterals rather than
a complete row. These methods generate near-boundary elements with high quality. However, the closure algorithms
for the colliding fronts in the interior are still unstable, especially if two overlapping elements have a large difference in
size. These cases are resolved by heuristics that sometimes
result in elements with poor quality. Moreover, the detection and resolution of the closure regions can be very time
consuming and sensitive to floating point errors. Bern and
Eppstein [BE00] used an advancing-front circle packing to
subdivide the domain directly into quadrilaterals with angles
less than or equal to 120◦ . It is advancing from in the sense
that new disks are placed tangent to prior disks.
Grid-based methods generates a uniform Cartesian background grid, or some quadtree refinement of it based on
the local feature size. Quadrilaterals, conforming to the domain boundaries, are then fitted into that grid. Baehmann
et al. [BWS∗ 87] modified a balanced quadtree to generate a quadrilateral mesh for an arbitrary domain. The dual
contouring method [JLSW02] uses an octree to extract a
quadrilateral mesh for an isosurface of a volumetric function. Zhang et al. extended this method to generate adaptive
quadrilateral and hexahedral meshes [ZB06]. Schneiders et
al. [SSW96] used an isomorphism technique to conform an
adaptive tree structure to the object boundaries. Atalay et
al. [AR08] utilized a balanced quadtree to generate a quad
mesh with angles between 18.43◦ and 180◦ . Grid-based algorithms are robust but often generate poor quality elements
and short edges at the boundary. Two-sided boundaries are
even more challenging. Liang et al. [LEZ09, LZ10] utilized
buffer layers and a hexagonal grid to improve the angles at
the boundary.
Disk packing methods create a set of disk center points,
such that the disks are forbidden to overlap in some way. The
packing is maximal when there is no room to add another
disk without creating a forbidden conflict. The maximality
ensures that no domain point is farther from a sample than
the disk radius, else we could insert a disk center there. In a
maximal packing where the disk radius is constant, or varies
slowly, the points are well-space. This means that there is a
maximum bound on the domain-point to nearest disk point
distance, a minimum bound on the distance between nearby
disk points, and a maximum bound on the ratio of the two,
all of which holds locally. Well-spaced points have Delaunay triangulations with good angles [Tal97, Che89]. Maximal Poisson-disk Sampling (MPS) is defined in terms of a
process that sequentially creates disks and keeps a disk only
if it is outside the prior disks. In the definition, the center
of each disk is chosen uniformly at random, but many algorithms change the placement process slightly for efficiency,
or to make it easier to check for maximality. MPS creates
well-spaced random packings, with good blue noise spectra
for graphics applications.
There are many algorithms for maximal and nearmaximal Poisson-disk samplings from graphics.

Each point has disks of multiple radii surrounding it, one
for each category. Each category of point has a different set

categories of points, and each have the same two radii disks
surrounding them; and our procedure for placing disks is
very different.
Simple MPS [EMP∗ 12] is perhaps the simplest and fastest
MPS algorithm. It is based on a quadtree that is refined in a
particular lock-step order that avoids bookkeeping. One nice
thing about this approach is that it is fairly ambivalent about
the primitives used for deciding whether to discard a square,
refine it, or accept a sample point in it. This paper uses the
Simple MPS framework, with radically modified disk conflict and cell discard checks. Each disk has two radii, a small
one defining conflict between different-category disks, and a
large one defining conflict between same-category disks. A
single category of disks with two radii have been used before [MREB12], but there the large radius is used to define
domain coverage and maximality.
The famous “two-color theorem” shows that the edges
of any quadrangulation are a bichromatic graph [Soi08].
That is, it is possible to color the vertices with two colors,
such that no edge connects two vertices of the same color.
(This always holds for topologically “planar” surfaces, including meshes on geometrically curved surfaces that can
be deformed into the plane, such as a sphere by picking one
quadrilateral to contain the point at infinity. For surfaces with
topological handles, such as a torus, some quadrangulations
are bichromatic, some not.) The two-color principle inspires
our use of two categories (colors) of disks. A Delaunay triangulation connects two vertices if there is a circle through
them that contains no other vertex in its interior; such vertices tend to be nearby compared to the other vertices in
about the same direction. This empty circle is related to the
domain coverage distance, which in turn is related to the disk
radius. Our two radii encourage disks of different colors to
be close enough to one another to be connected by a Delaunay edge, and same-colored disks to be far apart and unconnected. Further, we enforce this by discarding same-colored
Delaunay edges. To our knowledge, we are the first to define
this bi-chromatic Delaunay criteria.
2. Algorithm Details
We have two algorithms: random, with a classical Poissondisk sampling style of generating disks; and biased, with a
classical advancing-front style of generating disks. Both algorithms are based on sampling two classes of disks. Each
disk is associated with two radii; rs defines the minimum distance between two disks of different classes and rb defines
the minimum distance between two disks of the same class.

try to reach a maximal packing.

We find two overlapping big disks at the same level and of

two disk radii, and reject it if necessary. It is also rejected if

2.3. Monochromatic Triangle Refinement for All-quads
We take as input a maximal set of colored disks, generated by the random algorithm, the biased algorithm, or some
other source. To generate an all-quad mesh, we first construct the Delaunay triangulation with no special constraints.
Then we reduce the connectivity by discarding edges connecting two samples of the same color. Right away, this
produces quadrilaterals almost everywhere. The remaining
cells have an even number of sides, because the colors of the
vertices must alternate. (It turns out that cells have a single
connected boundary with no twice-used edges.) We started
with a triangulation; such many-sided cells contain a triangle
of discarded edges, hence a triangle with vertices all of the
same color. In this case we add an opposite-colored sample
in the center of the triangle’s circumcircle and update the triangulation. This may adversely change the angles and edge
lengths, but is required to get quadrilaterals. Figure 7 shows
the output Delaunay mesh and the final quad mesh.

To understand why a monochromatic triangle always exists inside a many-sided polygon, consider the colors of the
vertices of any triangle. Either they are monochromatic, say
blue, or have two blue and one red vertex. This means we
either discard one or three edges of a triangle, never two. No
triangulation of a cell with six, eight, or more sides is composed entirely of triangles with two edges on the boundary
and one in the interior. Hence there is at least one triangle
with at least two interior (discarded) edges. Since we never
discard two edges of a triangle, we must have discarded all
three, and the triangle is monochromatic.
Monochromatic triangles tend to occur in isolation. For
α = 2.5 we have never observed a case where a refinement
triggers the need for a second refinement. A cascade of refinements could possibly result in very short edges and bad
angles, so it is good that this does not happen.

4. Conclusions
We have introduced a novel concept, a Delaunay quadrangulation, based on assigning points one of two colors, and
only retaining the Delaunay edges between opposite-colored
points. In spatially-isolated cases, Delaunay refinement is
needed to produce all quads, without any six-or-more sided
cells. We have demonstrated both a random, and an advancing front algorithm, that can generate distributions of points
that produce good Delaunay quadrangulations. These distributions are generated by defining two radii around each

We have demonstrated these algorithms on a variety of
models, and studied the effects of tuning the ratio of the two
radii. We have an initial demonstration of variable sizing
functions on curved surfaces; we would like to understand
the limits of how fast the sizing function can vary and still
