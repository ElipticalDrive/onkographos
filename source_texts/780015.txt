Introduction
Scuba diving requires precise control of buoyancy to ensure both safety and an
enjoyable underwater experience. A critical factor in achieving this balance is proper
weighting, which allows divers to descend, maintain neutral buoyancy, and ascend safely.
Errors in weighting can have serious consequences: carrying excessive weights may result in
uncontrolled descents, rapid air consumption, and difficulty managing ascents, while
insufficient weight may prevent divers from descending or maintaining depth, causing
frustration and potentially unsafe situations.
Despite the importance of correct weighting, determining the appropriate amount of
weight remains largely a trial-and-error process, often performed under instructor supervision
during training dives. This approach can be stressful for new divers and leaves room for error,
potentially affecting both safety and learning. Although some basic guidelines and online
calculators exist to estimate starting weights, these are broad approximations that fail to
account for individual differences, particularly in body composition, suit thickness, and gear
selection. As a result, divers frequently arrive at the dive site only to discover inaccuracies in
their weighting, leading to repeated adjustments at the surface, disruptions to dive plans, and
added stress.
To address these limitations, this paper proposes a theoretical concept for a virtual
simulation system that would allow divers to create a personalised three-dimensional visual
model incorporating their specific body measurements and equipment configurations. The
system would simulate buoyancy outcomes in a virtual environment, visually representing
whether the diver would float, sink, or achieve neutral buoyancy under different configurations
and conditions. This system is designed to reduce the need for iterative adjustments, bolster

diver confidence, and encourage safer diving practices through its enhanced visual and intuitive
weight estimation capabilities. While it is not intended to replace essential in-water buoyancy
checks that remain critical to diver safety, the system aims to minimise initial trial-and-error
processes, allowing divers to approach in-water sessions and dives with greater confidence and
preparedness.
Background and Related Work
1. Current Approaches to Scuba Diver Weighting
Proper weighting is essential for safe and effective buoyancy control during scuba diving.
However, current methods for determining the appropriate amount of weight rely heavily on
estimation and in-water trial and error. Divers are typically advised to start with a rough
estimate based on body weight and exposure suit thickness, often expressed as a percentage of
the body weight. For example, a commonly cited guideline is to add approximately 10% of a
diver’s body weight when wearing a 5mm wetsuit (Professional Association of Diving
Instructors (PADI), 2011). These tables provide a starting point but do not account for
individual variation, such as body fat percentage, muscle mass or specific gear choices.
In practice, numerous divers adjust their weights during initial training dives under
instructor supervision. While this process is standard in diver education, it can be stressful,
especially for beginners, and does not eliminate the possibility of errors. Divers would have to
surface repeatedly to adjust weights or switch configurations, which would disrupt the learning
process and introduce safety risks during early training.
2. Existing Buoyancy Calculators and Dive Simulators
Several online buoyancy calculators are available to assist divers in estimating required
weights. These calculators typically allow users to input basic data, such as body weight,
exposure suit thickness and type of water (salt or fresh water depending on diving location) to
produce a recommended starting weight. However, these tools provide only static numerical
outputs and lack interactive elements or visual feedback. They do not simulate how a diver’s
body and gear would behave underwater, nor do they account for variations in body
composition or the buoyancy characteristics of specific gear configurations.
Furthermore, these calculators do not enable divers to explore the effects of adjusting
different components of their setup, such as switching to a different type of buoyancy control
devices (BCD), fins and the use of a weight belt over integrated weights, in addition to
switching from using an aluminium to steel cylinders. As such, divers are left to interpret these
results without any real sense of how those numbers translate to buoyancy control in a dive
scenario.
The Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) has developed a virtual reality scuba
simulation called “Amphibian” that goes beyond visual and auditory displays to create an
immersive diving experience wherein users lie face down on a supported, water-filled, motiondetecting platform whilst being suspended by elastic harnesses (Schneier, 2016). Whilst the
system creates an immersive diving experience by simulating buoyancy, drag and even
temperature through sensors (Jain et al., 2016), it is more of a virtual reality simulation that
involves a VR headset, and does not involve manipulating a virtual gear setup that demonstrates
the effect of placing weights differently on a diver.
Diving simulators that mirror the purpose of flight simulators – such as practicing
procedures and increasing confidence underwater – exist, but they do not include or offer the
ability to interactively calculate the correct weights (SSI Partners With VDI Gaming App Diving Simulator, 2021).
3. Weighting Errors in Scuba Diving: Incidents and Systemic
Issues
3.1.
Case Studies and Fatal Incidents
In a study of safe diving practices, Ranapurwala et al. (2017) found that weighting errors,
including under-weighting and over-weighting, were among the most frequently reported
unsafe conditions. While precise numerical data on the total number of weight-related incidents
globally between 2015 to 2025 is not comprehensively documented, there is substantial
evidence that demonstrates that both overweighting and the inability to ditch weights (thus
pointing at incompatible or unsuitable equipment) in emergency situations have proved to be
persistent and deadly problems.
The death of Linnea Rose Mills in 2020 is one such case that should be considered.
Mills drowned during a training dive in lake McDonald, Montana. Investigations found that
she was using unfamiliar and defective equipment which held excessive weights (about 20
kilograms), had no functional dry suit inflation mechanism and also wore a buoyancy
compensator that was too small to support the weights (Mills v. Snow, et al., 2020). The weights
were also loaded into her dry suit and BCD zipper pockets, rather than in a quick-release
system. During the dive, her dry suit was compressed by ambient pressure and Mills became
negatively buoyant, thereby inhibiting her ability to swim upwards. This case highlights the
importance of being correctly weighted and being comfortable with the equipment that a diver
is issued and uses.
More recently, a case study performed in 2025 in Sport Diver details the tragic demise of a
divemaster named Abe, who, despite his substantial experience, fell victim to the consequences
of being overweighted while ascending from a deep wreck, struggling to do so. Even when he
released his weight belt, his problems were not remedied (Douglas, 2015). Abe’s body was
only recovered ten months later. This report further supports that being overweighted in the
context of diving (in addition to being in less-than-optimal physical condition) can have fatal
consequences, even for experienced divers.
3.2.
Systemic Issues and Everyday Overweighting
In addition to fatal incidents, chronic overweighting is a widespread and persistent issue
faced by divers and instructors alike. Russell (2023) notes in Dive Magazine that “many divers
continue to rely on excessive lead as a way to ‘ensure’ that they descend, often without fully
understanding how this compromises their buoyancy and safety throughout the dive”.
Overweighted divers are then forced to overcompensate by over-inflating their BCDs (Russell,
2023), which then leads to unstable buoyancy control, poor trim and increased drag, which
often results in a vertical position in the water. In addition to increased effort and air
consumption, there is also the risk of uncontrolled and extremely dangerous feet-first ascents
if the air within the BCD is not properly vented. It is also further highlighted that overweighting
is frequently misunderstood as a “safety” measure, when it can potentially exacerbate the very
problems that it was to prevent in the first place. Divers who are properly weighted swim more
comfortably and maintain a more stable horizontal trim and are less likely to experience
buoyancy-related incidents.
4. The Critical Importance of Weight Placement in Scuba Diving
While determining the correct amount of lead weight is essential for safe diving, placing
those weights around a diver’s body is equally important, yet it is a concept that is frequently
overlooked. Proper weight placement directly affects a diver’s trim, buoyancy control, and
overall comfort underwater. When weights are positioned strategically, divers can achieve
neutral buoyancy and a horizontal trim, reducing drag and thereby minimising effort during a
dive. Contrastingly, poor weight distribution can lead to excessive air consumption, unstable
buoyancy, and dangerous situations, such as uncontrolled ascents or descents (Smith, 2021).
Risks include mask squeeze, ear barotrauma or descending deeper than planned (Smith, 2021)
because of a speedy descent.
Additionally, optimal weight placement is not universal and depends on a range of factors,
including individual body composition, exposure protection and equipment configurations.
Divers wearing thick wetsuits or drysuits may require different weight positioning compared
to those using thinner wetsuits. The buoyancy characteristics of cylinders (such as aluminium
as opposed to steel) also play a critical role in how the weights should be distributed.
Furthermore, factors like body fat distribution and shape affect whether weights should be
positioned higher or lower along the torso to maintain a proper balance in the water.
However, major diving organisations such as the Professional Association of Diving
Instructors (PADI), Scuba Diving International (SDI) and Technical Diving International
(TDI), and the Divers Alert Network (DAN) clearly establish that in-water testing and
adjustment is the predominant method of calculating the correct amount of weight required for
the correct buoyancy for each individual diver (Professional Association of Diving Instructors
(PADI), 2011; Scuba Diving International (SDI), 2021). PADI’s Five-Step Buoyancy Check
involves:
1. Entering water deep enough that a diver cannot stand, while fully equipped in diving
gear and the BCD fully inflated.
2. Completely deflating the BCD (opening the suit exhaust valve if using a dry suit).
3. Floating vertically while holding a normal breath.
4. Adjusting weight until one floats at eye level while holding said normal breath.
5. Making final adjustments with small weights as needed.
(Professional Association of Diving Instructors (PADI), 2011)
This approach has been the standard procedure taught in PADI courses worldwide.
However, the Scuba Diving International (SDI) and Technical Diving International (TDI)’s
weight check procedures closely mirror PADI’s approach, with the added specific emphasis on
tests being done at the beginning and end of each dive, highlighting the importance of
considering cylinder buoyancy changes throughout a dive (Smith, 2021).
Research Gap
Despite basic buoyancy calculators and standardised weighting procedures being
available, tools that allow divers to visually simulate and personalise buoyancy outcomes
before a dive remain significantly lacking. Existing buoyancy calculators are limited to static,
numerical estimates that fail to account for the complex interactions between body
composition, equipment selection and dynamic in-water behaviour. These limitations leave
divers reliant on the trial-and-error approach, which can lead to safety risks, inefficient
training and heightened stress and anxiety, particularly for beginners.
A virtual simulation system that enables divers to model their individual body
characteristics, equipment configurations and environmental conditions would offer
substantial advantages over the current methods. Such a system would provide an interactive
and visual platform where users can adjust variables and observe real-time buoyancy
outcomes, supporting more accurate weighting decisions and therefore better preparation.
Unlike traditional spreadsheet-based or tabular estimations, a fully visualised system would
offer a dynamic learning experience, helping divers to understand the relationship between
gear configuration and buoyancy in a way that abstract calculations cannot convey.
Furthermore, by providing a safe virtual environment for experimentation, this system
could reduce dependence on in-water-trial-and-error processes, enhancing diver confidence
and reducing the incidence of weight-related safety issues. The development of such a system
would thus bridge a crucial gap between theoretical buoyancy principles and practical diving
preparation, offering benefits for both novice divers and experienced professionals seeking to
optimise their configurations.
Methodology/Theoretical Framework
The proposed 3D virtual simulation system is designed to calculate and visualise diver
buoyancy before a dive, addressing the current reliance on static calculators and in-water
trial-and-error methods that are prone to errors and mistakes, which would result in
potentially unsafe situations. This system would offer a dynamic, interactive and personalised
visual approach to determining the correct dive weights, functioning as both an educational
resource and pre-dive planning tool for divers.
To create a personalised simulation, users will input individual data including height,
weight, estimated body fat percentage, muscle mass and lung capacity. Fat, being less dense
than water, contributes to positive buoyancy, while muscle and bone, which are denser,
contribute to negative buoyancy. Incorporating these body composition elements will allow
for a more accurate prediction of a diver’s in-water behaviour than existing generalised
estimations.
Users will also specify detailed equipment configurations, such as the type and
thickness of exposure suits, buoyancy control device (BCD) type, cylinder material and size,
as well as accessories like fins and weights. Environmental factors such as salinity (fresh or
salt water) and temperature will be included since both affect water density (Bosco et al.,
2018) and exposure suit compression at depth (Zhang & Song, 2013).

data, reflecting the combined effect of fat, muscle, and bone on buoyancy. The system will
visually represent whether the model floats, sinks, or achieves neutral buoyancy under
specific configurations.
All data will be visualised through a fully customisable 3D diver model that reflects
the user’s input of the aforementioned variables, and equipment will be shown as 3D objects
that can be added, adjusted or removed in real time. This system will simulate changes in
trim, showing how weight placements affect posture and balance underwater, helping divers
avoid common issues such as poor horizontal positioning.
Technical Development Considerations
Although this paper focuses on establishing the theoretical framework, several
technical approaches have been identified to realise the system. Core components include, but
are not limited to:
1. A customisable 3D diver model, adjustable to reflect user body dimensions and
composition.
2. An equipment and environment configuration panel which enables users to add and
adjust dive gear and environmental parameters.
3. A main canvas, which encompasses the diver model and all its customisations that
would reflect changes in buoyancy in real-time as changes are made.
Various 3D modelling tools and physics engines can be adapted to build the system,
provided that they provide support for real-time interaction, physics-based buoyancy
simulation and high customisability. Scalability, flexibility and performance are the main
criteria for deciding the development tools used during subsequent design, prototyping
and development phases.
Future Work
Further research and development prove necessary to bring this system into practical
use. The next step would involve developing a working prototype capable of accepting user
input, generating a customisable 3D diver model and simulating buoyancy outcomes based
on body composition, equipment configurations and environmental factors. An essential part
of work in the future would be to validate the system’s predictions against real-world diving
data and to ensure accuracy and reliability. This would likely involve collaboration with
diving centres, instructors and experienced divers to gather empirical data on weighting
practices and in-water behaviour.
In addition, user testing and feedback would be crucial to refine the system’s usability,
interface design and effectiveness as a planning and educational tool. Over time, there is
potential for the system to expand to include more advanced features such as but not limited
to including artificial intelligence (AI), dynamic weighting scenarios for different dive types.
There is also an eventual potential for inclusion with diver training programmes and elearning platforms, which can make this tool part of formal diving education to help reduce
weighting errors from the beginning of a diver’s experience with the sport.
Limitations
As a theoretical framework, the proposed system has inherent limitations that would
need to be addressed in future stages. Estimating body composition accurately without direct
measurement tools (for example, hydrostatic weighting or dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry
(DEXA)) presents a challenge, as user-reported data may introduce variability. Although the
system accounts for body composition factors like fat, muscle and bone, variations in factors
like fat distribution and body shape may still affect buoyancy in ways difficult to model.
Additionally, environmental factors such as water salinity, temperature and exposure
suit compression are complex and may not behave uniformly across all conditions,
potentially affecting the accuracy of simulations. It is also crucial to note that this system is
not intended to replace diver discretion, safety checks, and careful adjustments. Divers should
continue to exercise due diligence in performing proper buoyancy checks as part of safe
diving practices. Rather, the goal of this system is to minimise the amount of trial and error
initially required, thereby reducing the time, effort and stress involved, particularly for new
divers, by offering a personalised, visual starting point for weighting decisions.
Finally, the system’s ability to reflect true in-water behaviour will depend on the
accuracy of its physics engine and modelling and will require continuous testing and iterative
refinement. These limitations highlight the importance of ongoing development and
validation as part of the process to transforming this theoretical model into a reliable and
practical tool.
Conclusion
Accurately determining scuba diving weights remains a critical yet challenging aspect
of safe diving practice. Current methods relying on trial and error are potentially inefficient
and stressful, in addition to being prone to mistakes. This paper has proposed a theoretical 3D
virtual simulation that would allow divers to create personalised models and visualise
buoyancy outcomes based on body composition, gear, and environmental factors. This aims
to reduce repeated adjustments, improve safety and contribute to an enjoyable experience
within the activity, in addition to supporting diver education. Although still theoretical, this
concept establishes a foundation for future development of a practical tool that could enhance
diver training and practices, making dives safer, more fun, and more efficient from the outset.
