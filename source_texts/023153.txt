We propose an efficient method for differentiable rendering of parametric
surfaces and curves, which enables their use in inverse graphics problems.
Our central observation is that a representative triangle mesh can be extracted from a continuous parametric object in a differentiable and efficient
way. We derive differentiable meshing operators for surfaces and curves that
provide varying levels of approximation granularity. With triangle mesh
approximations, we can readily leverage existing machinery for differentiable mesh rendering to handle parametric geometry. Naively combining
differentiable tessellation with inverse graphics settings lacks robustness
and is prone to reaching undesirable local minima. To this end, we draw a
connection between our setting and the optimization of triangle meshes in
inverse graphics and present a set of optimization techniques, including regularizations and coarse-to-fine schemes. We show the viability and efficiency
of our method in a set of image-based computer-aided design applications.

INTRODUCTION
Differentiable rendering has become a powerful tool for solving
problems in computer graphics and vision. Various geometry representations currently permit differentiable rendering, including
point clouds [Müller et al. 2022; Rückert et al. 2022; Yifan et al. 2019],
triangle meshes [Chen et al. 2019; de La Gorce et al. 2008; Jakob
et al. 2022; Kato et al. 2018; Li et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2019; Loper and
Black 2014], implicit surfaces [Bangaru et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2020;
Liu et al. 2020; Mehta et al. 2022; Niemeyer et al. 2020; Vicini et al.
2022; Zhang et al. 2022], and (radiance) volumes [Mildenhall et al.
2020; Nimier-David et al. 2022].
Parametric curves and surfaces, in particular splines, are still the
most widely used representations in engineering disciplines and applications of computer-aided geometric design (CAGD). They offer
a compact and intuitive description of smooth curve segments and
surface patches. Despite their importance, they have only been sparingly investigated in the context of differentiable rendering [Cole
et al. 2021]. Clearly, gradients w.r.t. the image of parametric geometry would benefit a variety of applications, including reverse
engineering of CAD models [Benkő et al. 2001; Ren et al. 2022; Uy
et al. 2022; Varady et al. 1997], 3D surface reconstruction [Hasselgren et al. 2022; Munkberg et al. 2022] reconstruction of hairs or
strands [Luo et al. 2012, 2013; Nam et al. 2019; Rosu et al. 2022],
generative art and art digitization [Hsiao et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2017],
design [Li et al. 2020b; Schwartzburg et al. 2014], or quality control
in manufacturing [Martinsson et al. 2007a,b]. In contrast to directly
optimizing vertices of a triangle mesh, parametric representations
exhibit intrinsic smoothness, represent curved surfaces compactly,
can be re-sampled at arbitrary density after the optimization, and
bridge the gap to a wide variety of computer-aided design tasks.

While it is possible to scan-convert parametric curves and surfaces, i.e., evaluate them on the vertex or fragment level, this is
rarely done. Rather, most popular constructions such as (rational) Bsplines afford elegant tessellation strategies that are used to convert
them to triangle meshes dynamically. This approach to rendering
offers a trade-off between accuracy in the depiction and computational complexity. In traditional rendering, it also better exploits
optimized hardware for the rendering of triangles. We suggest that
similar arguments speak in favor of this strategy for differentiable
rendering of parametric geometry: rendering a triangulation of the
smooth geometry allows control over complexity and makes use
of the extensive development already invested in existing differentiable renderers. Using piecewise linear approximations has been
a promising avenue for differentiable programming in the context
of CAGD [Cascaval et al. 2022] as well as for differentiable rendering of continuous surface representations [Cai et al. 2022; Mehta
et al. 2022]. In Section 3, we explain how to extract piecewise linear
approximations from the commonly used, smooth surface representations in a differentiable way, so that gradients w.r.t. the parameters
defining the geometry (such as control points) can be automatically
generated by the chain rule. Additionally, we introduce a set of regularization and coarse-to-fine techniques that improve the robustness
of control point optimization with gradient descent. In Section 4 we
provide details about the implementation and investigate individual
components of our framework. We demonstrate the versatility of
optimizing parametric geometry at several examples in Section 5.

RELATED WORK: DIFFERENTIABLE RENDERING
Differentiable renderers are designed for specific geometry and
scene representations. Differentiable rendering of triangle meshes
has been approached using rasterization [Chen et al. 2019, 2021;
de La Gorce et al. 2008; Kato et al. 2018; Laine et al. 2020; Liu et al.
2019; Loper and Black 2014; Petersen et al. 2022; Ravi et al. 2020;
Takimoto et al. 2022] or ray tracing [Jakob et al. 2022; Li et al. 2018;
Nimier-David et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020b]. Differentiable renderers for point clouds are usually based on point splatting [Müller
et al. 2022; Rückert et al. 2022; Yifan et al. 2019], whereas implicit
surfaces are rendered using differentiable variants of ray marching
or sphere tracing [Bangaru et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2020; Liu et al.
2020; Niemeyer et al. 2020; Vicini et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022].
Similarly, differentiable rendering has been studied for volumetric representations including radiance fields [Fridovich-Keil et al.
2022; Gkioulekas et al. 2013; Mildenhall et al. 2020; Nimier-David
et al. 2022], and even for unconventional scene representations like
spheres [Lassner and Zollhöfer 2021] and metaballs [Keselman and
Hebert 2022].
By targeting specific scene representations, most renderers implement custom techniques to enable differentiable rendering of
their target representation. Recently, Cole et al. [2021] proposed a
general approach to differentiable rendering of different classes of
geometry, by performing a non-differentiable sampling of the surface, followed by differentiable splatting. Their work also includes
preliminary experiments with differentiable rendering of Bézier

The gradient preconditioning by Nicolet et al. [2021] offers a
practical solution for triangle meshes. Intuitively, the method is not
a regularizer as it does not alter the objective, but rather diffuses
gradients over a mesh, which makes their influence less local. The
requirement is a domain that permits a Laplacian, which makes
it applicable to control meshes. Some care has to be taken when
computing the Laplacian for the control mesh of a parametric surface. For example, naively triangulating the control mesh, therefore
reducing the situation to the one handled by Nicolet et al. [2021],
introduces unwanted asymmetries (see Figure 4). Generally, the
properties of the Laplacian for diffusion should be related to the
surface defined by the control mesh, and not the control mesh itself.
Under the “diffusion reparametrization” view of Nicolet et al.’s
method [2021], the Laplacian appears in the form of the Dirichlet
energy x⊤ Lx. For triangle meshes, using the cotangent Laplacian
for L leads to an exact expression of Dirichlet energy. For polynomial patches however, except for linear elements, Dirichlet energy
cannot be exactly represented by an expression of the form x⊤ Lx
in general. Note that polygon Laplacians [Bunge et al. 2021, 2020]
of the quadrilateral control mesh would approximate the Dirichlet
energy of surface elements defined by quads, which is different from
the surface being represented by the control net.
Monterde [2004] discusses several linear approximations of the
Dirichlet functional for Bézier-patches and compares them based
on their performance for minimal surface approximation. They find
that the graph or uniform Laplacian of the (quadrilateral) control
mesh, suggested by Farin and Hansford [1999], performs similar to
other, more principled discretizations. We therefore use the graph
Laplacian as a plausible linear approximation of the higher-order operator. As the graph Laplacian is independent of the geometry of the
patches, it can be pre-computed for fixed control mesh connectivity,
which improves the efficiency of the gradient preconditioning.

3.4
Coarse-to-Fine Schemes
In the solution of inverse rendering problems, it is common to
progressively increase the degrees of freedom instead of making
all optimization variables visible from the start. For example, in 3D
reconstruction a coarse-to-fine procedure first approximates the
target shape with a coarse surface, which is then refined to expose
variables with more localized influence. For triangle meshes, this
is facilitated by subdividing individual simplices or remeshing the
underlying surface [Luan et al. 2021; Nicolet et al. 2021].
An analogue in the context of spline curves and surfaces is the
subdivision of the control mesh: a single curve can be subdivided
into two curves and similarly a patch can be subdivided into four
patches, without changing the underlying geometry or its polynomial degree. For Bézier primitives, De Casteljau’s algorithm provides
an elegant way to compute the new control point positions; the more
general De Boor’s algorithm can be used to split B-Splines and knot
insertion to split NURBS. In the subdivided control mesh, the influence of each control point is localized to a smaller part of the surface.
We have experimented with coarse-to-fine schemes based on subdivision and found that it is a suitable alternative to remeshing,
significantly improving the robustness of the optimization.
For completeness, we note that subdividing the control mesh is
only one possible way of increasing the degrees of freedom. Most
popular spline constructions are polynomials, so degree elevation,
that is increasing the degree of the curve or surface, is another option. The combination of subdivision (changing the element size)
and degree elevation (changing polynomial degree) has some resemblance of hp-refinement in finite element methods [Hughes et al.
2005] and could be an interesting avenue to explore in future work.

IMPLEMENTATION AND EXPLORATION
We have implemented our framework and its components in PyTorch [Paszke et al. 2019], making it directly compatible with differentiable renderers like Mitsuba 3 [Jakob et al. 2022], nvdiffrast [Laine
et al. 2020], PyTorch3D [Liu et al. 2019; Ravi et al. 2020], and DIBR [Chen et al. 2019, 2021; Fuji Tsang et al. 2022] (see Figure 1). We
use a custom implementation for Bézier (spline) curves and tensor
product surfaces that is optimized for batched processing and uses a
mixture of analytic derivatives and automatic differentiation. For the
more general case of B-Spline and NURBs curves and surfaces, we
use the implementation of Deva Prasad et al. [2022] (NURBS-Diff).
In these settings, NURBS-Diff acts as a replacement for the Bézier
module. If not stated otherwise, we use a custom renderer based on
nvdiffrast [Laine et al. 2020] that uses local shading and supports
different kinds of light sources as well as reflection mapping. We
run all experiments on a workstation with an Intel i7-13700 CPU
and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM).

Optimizing Tessellations of Parametric Surfaces
So far, we have considered meshes of parametric surfaces that originate from fixed samples in the parameter domain. We make the
following observation: given a surface 𝑆 (𝑢, 𝑣) and a set of parameter
values (𝑢𝑖 , 𝑣𝑖 ) that define the vertices, we can optimize an object
function L in terms of the vertex positions on the surface, i.e.,
compute 𝜕L/𝜕{𝑢𝑖 , 𝑣𝑖 }. This requires no modifications because the
additional terms 𝜕𝑆/𝜕𝑢, 𝜕𝑆/𝜕𝑣 are already computed for the surface
tangents. Optimizing the tessellation of a (parametric) surface under
image constraints is useful in architectural design, where structures
are expected to produce a defined visual pattern when the surface
is faceted [Pellis et al. 2019]. We experiment with a similar setting:
given an image that shows a surface with high resolution, we optimize a low resolution tessellation with faceted look to reproduce
this target image (Figure 27). We do not optimize the vertex positions directly but the underlying parameter values (𝑢𝑖 , 𝑣𝑖 ) of each
vertex, effectively moving the vertices along the parametric surface.
We have also tested the tessellation optimization in a more involved
setting with architecture-like surfaces composed of multiple connected patches under different illumination and material models (see
Figure 28). The optimized surfaces, while having the same number
of facets, visually appear closer to the target image than the initial
tessellation with equal vertex distribution. We assume fixed connectivity so an interesting avenue would be the combination with
differentiable triangulation techniques [Rakotosaona et al. 2021].

CONCLUSION
We have presented efficient differentiable rendering of parametric (spline) surfaces by differentiable conversion of the continuous
parametric representations into a piecewise linear approximation.
The approximation permits rendering with existing differentiable
mesh renderers, building on extensive development in this area of
research: efficient differentiable rasterizers and advanced differentiable light simulations can be directly applied to parametric geometry. Inspired by recent work on inverse rendering with triangle
meshes, we have derived a set of regularization and coarse-to-fine

techniques for control meshes of tensor product surfaces, which
improve the robustness of gradient-based optimizations.
We are excited to see the applications that will be unlocked by
efficient differentiable rendering of parametric surfaces. Most notably, differentiable rendering has been extensively used for weakly
or self-supervised learning, which resulted in powerful generative
models for 3D geometry [Gao et al. 2022; Pavllo et al. 2021; Zeng
et al. 2022].
Limitations and Future Work. We have so far only considered a
subset of parametric constructions used in computer-aided design.
For example, NURBS are often used in a trimmed form, where one or
multiple trimming curves in the parameter domain mask out parts
of the surface. It might be possible to combine suitable tessellation
techniques with the differentiable setting [Kumar and Manocha
1995]. Investigating other popular constructions like subdivision
surfaces (e.g. Catmull-Clark [Catmull and Clark 1978]) in the context
of differentiable rendering could be an interesting avenue.
In addition to the regularizations proposed in this paper, there
are various energies in CAGD that put constraints on the optimized
surface and could be interesting to test in the differentiable setting.
Smooth surfaces can be favored by minimizing surface fairness
terms, for example based on the thin plate energy functional [Eck
and Hoppe 1996].
So far we have not considered tracking connectivity changes in
the mesh, which certainly is useful when optimizing the tessellation
itself (as in Section 5.3). We see potential in combining our setting
with existing methods for differentiable triangulation [Rakotosaona
et al. 2021].
Lastly, while differentiable tessellation is one option for differentiable rendering of spline surfaces, it might be promising to explore
specialized differentiable rendering tools without surface approximation, for example based on ray tracing [Kajiya 1982].

De Casteljau’s algorithm permits a numerically stable evaluation of Bézier curves. More interestingly, the recursive evaluation
scheme of this algorithm allows constructing the curve velocity and
acceleration vectors from its intermediate results. In other words,
evaluating a Bézier curve with De Casteljau’s algorithm evaluates
the velocity and acceleration at the same time. In the view of reversemode differentiation, the forward pass pre-computes terms required
in the backward pass (specifically for differentiating with respect to
parameter values). We use this property and implement a differentiable curve evaluation module based on analytic derivatives (see
Algorithm 1).
The gradient of an objective function L with respect to parameter
values is therefore practically “free”. What is left are the gradients
for each control point c𝑖 . We plug in the explicit derivatives from
Equations (27) and (28) and compute all Bernstein polynomials occurring in these terms in a single pass by exploiting their recursive
definition. Automatic differentiation would require a full backward
pass through De Casteljau’s algorithm, which is much more data
intensive because the computations involve the control point arrays. As a final remark, our formulation allows further optimization
