Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [22] has emerged as a powerful technique for
novel view synthesis, offering real-time and fully differentiable rendering. While
the rendered output is intended for human viewers, most 3DGS methods predominantly rely on ad-hoc combinations of pixel-level losses, such as the original
L1 + SSIM introduced by [22], which often lead to overly smooth or blurry textures. It is also common to use more recent distortion metrics such as LPIPS [44]
for evaluation, but their use for optimization has not been rigorously investigated. Importantly, the various perceptual optimization techniques have also
not been validated through human preference studies. This lack of emphasis
on human vision can lead to inefficient utilization of representational capacity,
affecting rendering speed and real-time performance. This matters even more
when a 3DGS representation must be compressed for storage or transmission,
where every bit spent on imperceptible detail yields no perceptual benefit.
Several prior works study this gap, but mainly through partial or indirect
strategies; e.g. by enhancing edges [46], or by manipulating the strategy of pruning and splitting Gaussians during training [13, 45]. In this work, we take a
principled approach: we make the perceptual optimization loss itself the central
design choice. Decoupling the modeling of human vision in the distortion loss
from the underlying 3DGS algorithms—such as initialization, densification, and
pruning—also simplifies the overall system design and enables broader generalization.
Building on this principle, in this work we take a systematic look at perceptual optimization for 3DGS, demonstrating that significant perceptual improve-

ments can be achieved with improved loss formulation alone, without relying on
any heuristics or changes to the underlying 3DGS algorithms themselves:
1. We study three families of distortion losses with the goal of maximizing
perceptual quality for 3DGS: (i) L1 + SSIM originally proposed in [22] and
widely used across the literature, (ii) a composite loss L1 + L2 + MS-SSIM +
LPIPS including common perceptual metrics [32, 33], and (iii) Wasserstein
Distortion (WD) [2, 34], a recently proposed distortion metric comparing
local statistics in a deep feature space, which has shown promise in the
learned compression literature [2].
2. To assess the perceptual quality achieved by these training objectives, we
conduct a large-scale human subjective study comprising 39,320 pairwise
ratings across 4 datasets and 3 distinct 3DGS frameworks—to our knowledge,
the first-of-its-kind for 3DGS.
3. Out of our analysis emerges a new loss as a top performer for 3DGS perceptual optimization. It is a regularized version of WD, which we refer to as
WD-R, that consistently outperforms all other losses. WD-R is preferred by
raters more than 2.3× over the original 3DGS loss and 1.5× over PerceptualGS, the current best perceptual 3DGS scheme. WD-R also achieves state-ofthe-art scores on common perceptual metrics such as FID [15], CMMD [20],
DISTS [11], and LPIPS [44] across diverse datasets. We further show that
WD-R generalizes to anti-aliasing and structured methods, as well as scene
compression (i.e. fitting 3DGS representations with a constraint on storage size rather than splat count), where it yields ≈ 50% bitrate savings at
comparable perceptual metric performance.
Our contribution is realized through a simple yet effective modification—replacing
the 2D distortion loss function used during optimization, without introducing
any 3D-specific constraints or heuristics. As such, other extensions of the 3DGS
framework (such as [28,42,43,45]) are largely orthogonal to and compatible with
our work. Our project page can be found at https://apple.github.io/mlperceptual-3dgs.

such as anchor-based Scaffold-GS [28].

The above-mentioned advances often apply modifications or constraints to
the model, the optimization procedure, and the loss at the same time, in order

in the loss function. We additionally corroborate these gains using a large-scale
human preference study, which, to the best of our knowledge, has never been
conducted in any prior 3DGS work.
2.2
3DGS optimization procedure
3DGS represents scenes as a collection of 3D Gaussian primitives that are differentiably rendered to produce 2D images. Each Gaussian primitive Gi is parameterized by Gi (µi , Σ i , ci , αi ) where µi is the center, Σ i is the covariance matrix,
ci is color, and αi is opacity [22]. During rendering, 3D Gaussians are projected
to two dimensions and alpha-blended in depth order to produce the final pixel
values of the reconstructed 2D view. As all the rendering operations are differentiable, this allows gradient-based optimization of the Gaussian parameters using
just a distortion loss imposed on the rendered views.
The number of Gaussians is modulated dynamically during training via adaptive densification, which introduces new primitives based on gradient magnitudes
and removes low-opacity ones through pruning. These decisions are driven by
gradients from the distortion loss, meaning the optimization objective influences
not only parameter updates but also the evolution of the representation itself.
Lower thresholds allow increased growth and more complex detail and texture
reconstruction at the cost of increased runtime and memory, while higher thresholds favor compact, faster real-time renderings.

rendered views x̂ are compared against ground-truth images using one of our
proposed distortion losses (described in Sec. 3.1), and the resulting gradients

We consider two settings with different optimization objectives: (i) evaluate perceptual training objectives in the standard 3DGS representation framework [22], and (ii) examine whether the gains obtained by the best-performing
perceptual strategy generalize to alternative 3DGS frameworks, including antialiasing rendering methods [42] and structured Gaussian representations [28], as
well as to a scene compression framework [27].
Representation For the representation task, training minimizes a distortion
loss between the ground-truth image x and the rendered image x̂:
\min _{\bmcG } \gamma \, D(\bx , \bhx ),\label {eq:2D_distortion}
(1)
where G is the collection of Gaussian parameters, D(·) denotes distortion computed on the rendered 2D image space, and γ is a scalar weighting factor. Because adaptive densification relies on gradient magnitude, the scale of the loss
indirectly affects the trade-off between reconstruction quality and the number
of Gaussians in the 3DGS representation. To ensure a fair comparison under
similar representation budgets, we allow γ to vary across datasets while keeping
all other hyperparameters fixed, in order to align the resulting Gaussian counts
across methods without modifying the underlying framework.
Variable-rate compression In the task of scene compression [27, 37], distortion and model size are balanced through a rate–distortion objective, as in
learned image compression [1, 3, 35]:
\label {eq:RD} \min _{\bmcG , \boldsymbol {\theta }} D_{\boldsymbol {\theta }}(\bx , \bhx ) + \lambda R_{\boldsymbol {\theta }}(\bmcG ),
(2)
where Rθ (G) estimates the storage cost of Gaussian parameters G, given the
compression network parameters θ. Concretely, we use the common rate term
used in neural compression Rθ (G) = E[− log2 pŷ (ŷ)], the Shannon cross-entropy
between the quantized latent encoding ŷ of G and a learned parametric entropy
model pŷ [1]. The trade-off between reconstruction quality and storage cost is
controlled by λ. The storage size of a 3DGS scene is not determined solely by
the number of Gaussians, but also depends on parameter redundancy and the
probabilistic modeling used for entropy coding.

Methodology
3.1
Alternatives for the distortion loss D
The original 3DGS formulation [22] optimizes a linear combination of L1 and
SSIM [39]; this is the canonical choice for 3DGS loss [27,28,42,45,46], and we refer
to it as the original loss. While computationally efficient, these pixel-level losses
often poorly correlate with human perception [38]. To explore alternatives better
aligned with perceptual quality, we evaluate three additional loss formulations.

Composite loss Since the introduction of SSIM as a model of human contrast
perception [39], the field of human perceptual modeling has made significant
advances. As a stronger baseline, we therefore consider a generalization of the
original loss, employing a weighted sum of several more recent and commonly
used distortion metrics:
\resizebox {.65\hsize }{!}{$\mathcal {L}_{\textrm {composite}} = \omega _{1}\mathcal {L}_{\textrm {L1}} + \omega _{2}\mathcal {L}_{\textrm {L2}} + \omega _{3}\mathcal {L}_{\textrm {MS-SSIM}} + \omega _{4}\mathcal {L}_{\textrm {LPIPS}}$}, \label {eq:composite_loss}
(3)
where LL1 and LL2 are per-pixel L1 and L2 norms, LMS-SSIM is the multi-scale
structural similarity loss [38], LLPIPS [44] measures perceptual distance using
features extracted from a pretrained deep neural network, and ω1 –ω4 control
the relative importance of each term.
For LPIPS, we train with LPIPS-AlexNet while evaluating with LPIPS-VGG
to avoid overfitting to the training metric and remain consistent with common
evaluation practices in the 3DGS literature [22, 45, 46]. The weights of the composite loss components are selected through ablation experiments (Sec. B), and
the configuration ω1 = 0.05, ω2 = 0.30, ω3 = 0.60, ω4 = 0.10 is used for all
the experiments for its favorable trade-off between reconstruction quality and
representation efficiency.
Wasserstein distortion (WD) LPIPS [44] was the first metric to demonstrate that deep feature representations can approximate aspects of human visual perception [41]. However, it is a pointwise metric, which does not account
for the way humans perceive texture. Fig. 3 illustrates this limitation in 1D: two
textures with a 180◦ phase shift exhibit large pointwise differences despite appearing visually similar. WD [34] is a feature-space agnostic distortion metric
that compares spatially local estimates of statistics, allowing textures that differ
substantially pointwise to be considered perceptually similar when their local
statistics match. This formulation is motivated by models of the human visual
system suggesting that peripheral vision encodes images using summary statistics over local pooling regions, rather than precise pointwise pixel values [12,36].
In this work, we use a version of WD where the spatially local statistics are computed in the VGG feature space [2]. For each feature map and spatial location,
WD computes the RMSE between the local mean µ and standard deviation ν:
d_{\textrm {WD}} = \sqrt {(\mu - \hat {\mu })^2 + (\nu - \hat {\nu })^2}, \label {eq:WD}
(4)
where ˆ indicates the local statistics computed on the reconstructed image rather
than the ground truth. These local differences are then aggregated over feature
maps and pixel locations.
The area over which the local statistics are computed is determined by a
pooling kernel of size σ, which can vary across locations. Larger σ-values capture
texture realism by aggregating information over broader regions, while σ → 0
converges to a pointwise distance. This can be used to make the metric more
permissive to texture resampling in certain regions such as ones that are less
visually salient. In this work, we use a constant σ = 4, selected empirically
(Sec. C.1) at the resolutions we benchmarked; we therefore interpret it as a

WD-regularized (WD-R) In practice, we found that including a small amount
of pixel-level fidelity loss along with WD helps to avoid artifacts in regions where
the number of splats is constrained, and the number of training views is small
(see Sec. 4.1). We therefore introduce WD-Regularized (WD-R), which augments
WD with a lightly weighted version of the original loss. In all the experiments,
WD still remains the primary objective, and incorporating the original loss serves
only as modest regularization. Concretely, we optimize LWD = γ dWD for the
WD setting and LWD-R = γ (dWD + β Lorig ) for WD-R, where Lorig is the original 3DGS pixel-level loss [22], γ is a dataset-specific global scale (Eq. (1)) used
to align Gaussian counts across methods, and β is chosen so that Lorig acts as a
mild pixel fidelity regularizer while WD remains the dominant perceptual term.
More details are discussed in Sec. A.2.
3.2
Training and evaluation setup
Implementation details We follow the experimental setup of prior work [22]
and train all models for 30k iterations, adopting the published configurations of
the respective baselines to ensure faithful reproduction. For WD-based training,
similar to the warm-up strategies used in perceptual and adversarial training [19,
25], we first optimize the model using the standard 3DGS loss for 3k–5k iterations
before introducing the perceptual objective.
For the representation task, we compare against Pixel-GS [45] and PerceptualGS [46], two recent methods that improve perceptual quality by modifying gradient accumulation and densification strategies. These methods are particularly
relevant baselines as they aim to improve perceptual sharpness while explicitly
controlling Gaussian growth in order to maintain fewer splats—a constraint not
8
Özyılkan and Chen et al.
always prioritized in the literature. To validate whether the perceptual improvements observed with WD-based losses generalize to other 3DGS frameworks, we
additionally integrate WD losses into Mip-Splatting [42] and Scaffold-GS [28],
as well as into the Comp-GS [27] compression scheme.
For fair comparisons, we align the representation budget of WD/WD-R across
all compared methods and ensure comparisons are made under similar or lower
budgets (i.e. fewer splat counts or smaller model size). This is achieved by adjusting the global scale γ in Eq. (1) while keeping all other hyperparameters
fixed. This procedure ensures that differences in performance arise from the optimization objective alone, rather than increased representation capacity.
More details about the training schedules, hyperparameters, and runtime are
discussed in Sec. A. The WD implementation we adopt [2] is unoptimized and incurs a ≈4.5× training overhead over original 3DGS; preliminary tuning (caching
ground-truth VGG features and pruning zero-weight VGG pyramid levels) reduces the WD per-iteration time by ≈48% with bit-exact loss and gradients,
bringing the relative training overhead down to ≈2.8×.
Datasets Following the methodology of [46], we evaluate the effectiveness of our
perceptual training across 21 scenes drawn from 4 widely used novel view synthesis datasets: Mip-NeRF 360 [4] (9 scenes), Deep Blending [14] (2 scenes), Tanks
& Temples [24] (2 scenes), and BungeeNeRF [40] (8 scenes). For Mip-NeRF 360,
we distinguish between indoor scenes (Counter, Room, Kitchen, Bonsai) and
outdoor scenes (the remaining 5), as these categories involve significantly different numbers of splats and memory footprints. For the same reason, for both our
human preference and compression studies, we also report results separately for
indoor and outdoor datasets.
Perceptual metrics We evaluate the rendering quality using several widely
adopted perceptual metrics: LPIPS [44] and DISTS [11], Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) [15], and CLIP-based Maximum Mean Discrepancy (CMMD) [20].
Additional implementation details of perceptual metrics are provided in Sec. A.4.
For completeness, PSNR and SSIM [38, 39] are also reported in Sec. E.1.
Human preference study In addition to providing quantitative perceptual
metrics, we also conduct a large-scale user study to directly assess perceptual
alignment with human preference. The study is carried out on Mabyduck [30],
an independent platform for user preference studies. Following the methodology
of the CLIC compression challenge [10], each trial presents participants with
a blind A/B comparison of the same 704 × 704 random crop of two rendered
images from different methods, compared against the ground-truth reference.
Participants are asked to choose which rendered image appears closer to the
reference. A total of 428 participants completed 39,320 pairwise trials across all
the datasets and three frameworks. Responses are aggregated using Bayesian Elo
rating based on the Bradley–Terry model [9,31], producing perceptual preference
scores for all evaluated methods. More details can be found in Sec. D.

Results
To understand the effectiveness of different perceptual objectives under realistic efficiency constraints, we conduct a comprehensive study across multiple 3DGS rendering settings. We first compare the original loss, the composite loss, WD, and WD-R against recent perceptual 3DGS variants—including
Pixel-GS [45] and the recent state-of-the-art splat-efficient perceptual method,
Perceptual-GS [46]—on standard reconstruction benchmarks under controlled
representation budgets. WD-based objectives consistently achieve the best perceptual performance. We then verify that these improvements align with human
visual perception through a large-scale human preference study, and provide
visualizations for how perceptual optimization affects the learned 3DGS representations. Finally, we demonstrate that WD-based optimization generalizes
across methodologically different frameworks—including anti-aliased rendering
(Mip-Splatting [42]), structured Gaussian representations (Scaffold-GS [28]), and
entropy-constrained compression (Comp-GS [27]).
4.1
3DGS representation
Quantitative results Tab. 1 provides a comprehensive evaluation of the studied perceptual loss families across indoor and outdoor datasets. We report both
perceptual quality metrics and the average splat count per dataset (#G), allowing methods to be compared under similar representation budgets. We observe
10
Özyılkan and Chen et al.
that the WD-based losses (WD and WD-R) consistently and substantially outperform the original loss, while also yielding more compact representations (e.g.
reducing the Gaussian count on the BungeeNeRF dataset from 6.92M to 4.89M).
Notably, they also outperform Perceptual-GS [46], the state-of-the-art in terms
of perceptual metrics to date, across the vast majority of perceptual metrics and
datasets that we evaluate.
Human preference study To verify that improvements in perceptual metrics
translate to perceived visual quality, we conduct a large-scale human preference
study as described in Sec. 3.2. In this study, a total of 320 participants completed 30,720 pairwise trials across all the datasets. We evaluate WD, WD-R,
Perceptual-GS [46], Pixel-GS [45], and the original 3DGS loss [22] across both
indoor and outdoor scenes. The composite loss is excluded from the human preference study, as it incurs significantly higher Gaussian counts on several datasets,
making direct comparison less meaningful.
As shown in Fig. 4, WD-R achieves significantly better Elo scores across all
scenes. The difference in Elo of more than 150 as compared with the original
loss implies that the WD-R reconstructions were chosen by raters 2.3× as often.
Compared with the state-of-the-art method, Perceptual-GS, both WD and WDR achieve better Elo scores (within the 95% error margin). Specifically, based on
an Elo difference of 72, WD-R was preferred more than 1.5× over Perceptual-GS.
Overall, the WD-R loss is consistently preferred by participants over all other
methods. These results align with the metrics reported in Tab. 1, confirming that
WD-based optimization produces the most perceptually appealing novel views
for both indoor and outdoor scenes.
Visual results Figs. 1 and 6 provide a visual comparison of different methods
on challenging real-world scenes. The WD loss demonstrates a clear advantage in
recovering fine-grained textures and structural details. In the Barcelona scene
(Fig. 1), WD achieves sharper reconstructions of intricate details on the Sagrada
Família cathedral, faithfully capturing the small circular patterns on the tower,
whereas Perceptual-GS tends to overemphasize linear structures (likely due to
its traditional edge detection mechanism derived from Sobel operator). We see
similar observations across various datasets as shown in Fig. 6. Additional visual
results are provided in Sec. F.1.

Fig. 9 shows the erank histograms for the Barcelona scene (more details
on the other scenes in the BungeeNeRF dataset are in Sec. G). WD-based objectives (WD and WD-R) produce densities shifted towards lower erank values, indicating a stronger tendency to form anisotropic Gaussians compared to
pixel-level losses. This allocation pattern reflects how perceptual optimization
exploits directional primitives to represent local structures. Specifically, the increased anisotropy allows splats to better represent high-frequency details and
fine textures, as their elongated shapes conform to local geometry and reduce
rendering blur [16, 22]. Visual inspection (Fig. 6) and the user study (Fig. 4)
confirm that such anisotropy indeed helps with representing texture and regular
structure, for instance in the large-scale city scene example shown in Fig. 1. Beyond Gaussian shape, WD-R also shifts where capacity is allocated across the
view: Fig. 10 compares splat density heatmaps of original 3DGS and WD-R on
a representative view, showing that WD-R concentrates splats on texture-rich
regions (a larger version and additional details are provided in Sec. H).
However, we notice that this tendency can also lead to undesirable “web-like”
artifacts in some scenarios as shown in Fig. 7, particularly in regions containing
dense high-frequency detail but with a low budget of splats. In this situation, WD
may allocate a few highly elongated Gaussians to match local statistics (mean
and standard deviation in a deep feature space; see Eq. (4)). This leads to weblike structures that crudely (but economically!) imitate texture. Pixel-level losses
avoid this behavior, but instead produce overly smoothed reconstructions.

Discussion
Our results demonstrate that a perceptual loss function defined on 2D rendered
images can serve as the main objective for reconstructing 3DGS representations
from multi-view images. WD, a recently introduced distortion metric focusing on
texture perception, and specifically our proposed WD-R variant, achieve stateof-the-art perceptual results in novel view synthesis, as confirmed by LPIPS,
DISTS, FID, and CMMD scores, as well as our large-scale human preference
study, which to our knowledge is the first-of-its-kind for 3DGS.
It is interesting that WD alone is perfectly suitable as a distortion loss for 2D
images within the context of learned image compression and causes no noticeable
training instabilities [2], but this appears not to be entirely the case for 3D scene
optimization. It can lead to artifacts such as those in Fig. 7, particularly (i) in
regions of 3D space with few training samples, and (ii) when splat count is highly
constrained. Regularizing WD with a modest amount of the original loss (WD-R)
suppresses these artifacts and produces substantially better human ratings than
all other methods we are aware of. Understanding and addressing the root cause
remains an open question.
We also explored spatially varying pooling kernel sizes (i.e. σ) for WD
(Sec. C.2). While these adaptive variants can occasionally improve the reconstruction of structured textures (e.g. English text), they overall yield similar
perceptual metrics to the σ = 4 case and introduce additional complexity. Future work may further investigate alternative spatially varying σ strategies—
potentially offering a more targeted remedy for WD artifacts—as well as multiresolution evaluation with σ adapted accordingly.
Additional promising directions include adversarial losses—though their computational complexity would exceed even that of computing classifier features
for WD—as well as fully end-to-end optimized approaches to 3DGS where the
splat count is explicitly incorporated into the optimization objective.

Implementation details
We follow the experimental setup of prior work [22, 46]. All experiments are
conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80 GB of memory.
A.1
Runtime comparison of WD and original loss
We analyze the computational overhead introduced by WD during training.
At a rendered resolution of 1063 × 1600 and with the Gaussian count fixed at
4.79M on the Bicycle scene from the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset [4], the forward
and backward pass time per iteration is 61.1 ms for the original L1 + SSIM
objective and 273.9 ms when using WD. In this work, we prioritize perceptual
fidelity over raw training efficiency, trading additional training cost for improved
visual quality. This 4.5× increase mainly stems from the additional feature extraction and statistical computation in the VGG space (see Eq. (4)), following
the WD implementation in [2]. The reference WD implementation is unoptimized, but two simple changes already remove a substantial fraction of this
overhead while preserving the loss value and its gradients bit-exactly relative to
the reference implementation: (i) caching ground-truth VGG features and their
pyramid statistics, since the ground-truth view does not change across training
iterations; and (ii) pruning zero-weight VGG pyramid levels, since the per-level
weight ReLU(1 − | log2 σ − i|) is zero for most levels under our constant σ = 4—in
our setup only 20 of 96 pyramid levels across the VGG feature stack contribute
to the loss. Together, these two changes reduce the WD per-iteration time by
≈48%, bringing the relative training overhead down from ≈4.5× to ≈2.8× over
original 3DGS. Further VGG-side optimizations (e.g. lower-resolution feature
extraction, parallelization, or mixed-precision arithmetic) are likely to yield additional speedups and are left to future work. Moreover, WD-based optimization
often converges to fewer splats at comparable perceptual quality, which can reduce rendering cost and partially offset the increased training time for the loss
computation.

A.4
Perceptual metrics
LPIPS [44] and DISTS [11] are computed per image and averaged across validation views. In contrast, FID [15] and CMMD [20] measure differences between
feature distributions of rendered and ground-truth images. Specifically, FID uses
Inception-v3 features, while CMMD is computed using CLIP-ViT-L/14-336 embeddings. As FID and CMMD require sufficiently large sample sets for stable
estimation, we augment the evaluation data by extracting five spatial crops per
view and also adding their horizontal flips. The metrics are then computed per
scene and averaged across all scenes to ensure statistical stability while accounting for inter-scene variation.
It is worth noting that FID and CMMD operate at the distribution level,
quantifying the divergence between the reconstructed and ground-truth data.
As such, they serve as indicators of realism rather than perceptual fidelity to
a specific reference image. However, it is common practice in perceptual image
processing and compression research to report these distributional metrics alongside per-image metrics like DISTS and LPIPS (see [32, 33]). Since each dataset
we evaluate on only contains a small handful of individual scenes, and hence
does not represent a larger ensemble of scenes well, we chose to compare distributions per scene (i.e. the FID or CMMD value is computed by comparing
the distribution of original views from each scene to the distribution of novel
rendered views from the model fitted to the same scene).

C
Ablation on σ choices for Wasserstein Distortion
C.1
Constant σ map
As discussed in Sec. 3.1, σ controls the permissiveness to texture resampling,
where lower values allow less resampling [2, 34]. The measurements in Tab. 6
show that there is a trade-off: decreasing σ, at a constant loss weight γ, improves
pointwise perceptual metrics, as σ = 0 corresponds to pointwise comparisons
between feature maps (or pixels). At the same time, this comes at the cost
of an increased splat count. For our main results, we selected σ = 4, which
would appear to be a suboptimal choice according to the metrics listed here.
However, the fact that we largely achieve better human ratings with WD/WD-R
than optimizing for combinations of such pointwise metrics, as evidenced by the
human preference study in Fig. 4, suggests that pointwise metrics simply are
not accurately aligned with human texture perception. Although the original
WD implementation paper [2] highlights a saliency-guided σ-map, we note that
a fixed σ for the WD loss remains a strong baseline itself, as demonstrated
in [2, Sec. 3, Fig. 5].

Generalization across alternative 3DGS representations
Tabs. 9 and 10 report results when integrating WD and WD-R into the other
two 3DGS frameworks studied, Mip-Splatting [42] and Scaffold-GS [28], across
additional datasets beyond those in Sec. 4.2. For most datasets, WD-based objectives consistently improve perceptual metrics such as LPIPS, DISTS, FID,
and CMMD relative to the original methods. In particular, WD-R provides the
most stable improvements, achieving the best or near-best perceptual scores
across the majority of datasets while maintaining comparable resource usage
(Gaussian count for Mip-Splatting and model size for Scaffold-GS). As expected
for perceptual optimization, these gains are sometimes accompanied by small
decreases in PSNR and SSIM, reflecting the known trade-off between pixel-level
fidelity and perceptual quality [7]. Overall, the results confirm that the benefits
of WD-based training generalize across multiple datasets and extend beyond the
original 3DGS algorithm [22] to alternative rendering pipelines.

H
Splat density heatmaps
To complement the erank analysis (Sec. G), which characterizes how perceptual optimization shapes individual Gaussians, we also visualize how WD-R distributes splat capacity spatially across a rendered view.
Computation. For a given trained model and target camera view, we project
each Gaussian center µi onto the image plane using the camera’s intrinsic and
extrinsic parameters; only Gaussians whose projected centers fall within the image are kept, and we refer to these as the visible splats for that view. For each
remaining splat Gi , we add its opacity αi to the 2×2-pixel bin containing the
projected center, yielding a 2D density map. Because the map is computed from
splat centers—not from the alpha-blended renderer—it captures where representation capacity is allocated.
