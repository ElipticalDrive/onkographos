The particle filter's cost was not the sampled points but the searches behind
them. Three of them were avoidable, and a fourth change fixes the instant the
measurement is referenced to, which the same investigation turned up.
Take the sampler's normals from the map
PointCloudSamplerWithNormal built a kd-tree over the whole cloud and estimated
a normal for every point each time it was asked to sample, and both measurement
models asked, so it ran twice a cycle. That estimation, most of it the tree
construction, was where the filter spent its time.
The models clipped the same cloud in different ways and handed over a copy of
what survived, which is why the sampler could not tell it was the same cloud
twice. They now name a subset of one cloud instead, and the sampler prepares
that cloud once per cycle.
The normals need not come from the scan at all. A scan point lies on a map
surface, so the map knows which way that surface faces. They are now estimated
per map point when the map arrives and looked up per scan point, which removes
the per-cycle tree entirely. Weighing every point would mean a map query per
point, so a pool of num * pool_ratio points is drawn uniformly first and only
those are weighed. pool_ratio is a new parameter, read on both ROS 1 and
ROS 2, and held to at least 2 where it is used: a pool exactly as long as the
draw leaves the weights nothing to decide.
This trades a dependence the old path did not have. The lookup goes through
the particle mean, so once the estimate is further out than
normal_search_range every lookup comes back empty, every normal is a NaN and
the weighting goes uniform without saying so -- through a global localization or
just after a kidnap, the draw is the uniform one. That is the upstream default
behaviour rather than a regression, but it is worth knowing about, and it is
written down next to the lookup.
Answer nearest-point queries from a grid
The likelihood model wants only the distance to the nearest map point -- the
index goes unused and the value is clipped at match_dist_min -- and it was
asking a kd-tree for it, once per sampled point per particle.
NearestPointGrid answers that from a grid of cells, walking shells outward
until the next one is farther off than the best distance in hand. Distances are
measured against the map points themselves, so the answer is the exact nearest
distance rather than a quantised one, which a test against brute force covers.
Two things it needs to be quick: each cell records how many shells away the
nearest occupied cell is, or the points that hit nothing mapped become the worst
case rather than the best; and cells are about half the match radius, which a
sweep put the optimum at -- the map's own resolution, the intuitive choice, is
slower.
It is not the chunked kd-tree's answer. That search is approximate
(setEpsilon) and its chunks overlap in unweighted metres, so the two disagree
near the cut-off, with the grid the stricter of the two. Measured on a chunk
boundary over 200k queries, the grid matched points the tree let go and never
the other way round. Scores move by that much, and with them the instant
match_ratio_thresh trips an expansion reset.
Reference the measurement to the instant the particles are at
The clouds are accumulated in the odom frame, each at its own stamp, so the
motion between them is already out of the scans and any instant can be chosen to
bring them back into the robot frame. It was the last scan's, and that is not
the instant the particles are at: the odom callback predicts them forward as the
odometry comes in, and a cloud arrives after the odometry that covers it. So the
measurement compared a scan from one instant against poses from a later one, and
through a turn that tilts the whole scan by the yaw between the two -- the
further the point, the further it lands from the map, until the far half of a
corridor wall stops matching at all.
The transform still falls back to the current time when there is no odometry to
date it from.
Cost
Localization rounds take substantially less CPU time, and position error is
unchanged. The map side pays for it: building the grid costs about four to five
times what the kd-tree build costs, with a comparable increase in resident
memory, and it happens on every map update. Estimating the map normals is
heavier still, and only happens when use_random_sampler_with_normal is set.
Cell coordinates are cut in the dist_weight_-scaled space, so an axis with a
large weight gets proportionally finer cells.
Tests
test_nearest_point_grid is new and registered on both the catkin and ament
sides. It compares the grid against brute force over weighted and unweighted
metrics, non-integer cell/radius ratios, negative cell indices, map replacement
and non-finite points.
test_point_cloud_random_sampler_with_normal gains the map-normal path, the
pool_ratio bound and the empty cases. The map there sits a fixed offset from
the scan rather than exactly under it, so the search range actually has to reach
-- a range shorter than that offset is asked for as well and has to produce the
even draw.
Not covered: use_random_sampler_with_normal defaults to false and no config or
launch test in the package turns it on, so the engine-side wiring of the map
normals runs in unit tests only. The reference instant is likewise only checked
by the bag-replay tests behind MCL_3DL_EXTRA_TESTS.

```
the draw keeps, and a draw that takes each point once has to take all of
them whatever the weights say. Two is the least that leaves the weights
anything to decide. ROS 1 reads the parameter as well; without that the
engine took the header's initialiser whatever the launch said.

defaults to false and no config, launch test or unit test in the package
turns it on. The new test asserts the weighting itself, on two walls:
the draw comes almost entirely from the wall across the particle spread,
and evenly from both when the parameters say the weights are equal. That
pair is what separates a working lookup from one that returns nothing,
since the draw hands back the right number of points either way.
```

```
The map in MapNormals was laid exactly under the scan, so the lookup
distance was float rounding and the search range could shrink by any
factor without the test noticing -- 0.4 to 0.08, the shrink the note
beside it describes, left it passing. The map now sits a fixed offset

weights still ask for the strongest of the three. Both sides of that
boundary are now pinned.

PoolRatioTooSmallToWeigh had the floor and not the ceiling, so the note
that a ratio of 1 weights more weakly than the default rested on
nothing; raising kMinPoolRatio now fails it.

IgnoresNonFinitePoints tried one NaN on one axis. A cell index is taken
from all three, so the other two and the infinities are tried too. This
does not change which point is returned -- a non-finite coordinate loses
every comparison in the walk and its cell lands outside any query's
reach -- but it holds the guard that keeps those cells from being
allocated at all.
```

Work out the cell and the offset within it to the same precision
…
86d21da
```
cellOf() divided in float and visitRing() multiplied the index back out
in float to find where the query sits inside its cell. The two do not
agree: a float cell index multiplied by the cell size lands up to an ulp
of the coordinate from the cell's real edge, and at map coordinates in
the tens of thousands that ulp is a sizeable fraction of a cell. The
offset then came out wrong, the gap visitRing() prunes a shell by came
out wider than the distance it stands in for, and a map point well
inside the radius was passed over.

Both now go through double. Measured over 200k single-point queries per
magnitude, the disagreements with a brute force go from 1 at 1e4, 61 at
1e5 and 7781 at 1e6 to none anywhere. They were all misses that should
have been hits, so the effect was to lower likelihood scores. Build and
query times are unchanged within the noise of repeated runs, and on a
map around the origin the answers are identical.

The brute-force comparison takes an origin, and runs again with the map
and the queries 1e5 out. Unit weights there, so the grid and the brute
force subtract the same two numbers in the same order; the existing
cases keep covering the rescaled metric near the origin.
```
