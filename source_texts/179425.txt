Abstract and Figures
Holographic displays require submicron pixel pitches to achieve wide viewing angles, resulting in extremely large data sizes. Inspired by foveated rendering, we propose a method that selectively reduces the resolution of peripheral regions by controlling the resolving power between two point light sources based on the Rayleigh criterion. This approach allows resolution-controlled sub-holograms to be generated using a single-panel spatial light modulator without hardware modifications. The characteristics of hologram data generated by this method were analyzed, and five lossless compression algorithms were evaluated. As the resolution decreased, the dynamic range and high-frequency components of the interference pattern were reduced, leading to increased spatial correlation and improved compressibility. Consequently, compression rates of 40%–70% were achieved in the peripheral regions without noticeable degradation in the reconstructed images. The compression efficiency was found to be consistent regardless of the number of point light sources, viewing positions, or object shapes. These results demonstrate that incorporating foveated rendering principles into hologram generation provides an effective strategy for reducing data size while maintaining image quality in the foveal region.

1. INTRODUCTION
Holography is a technique that records the brightness, phase,
and direction of light to reconstruct the wavefront of an object’s
light in space. It has been recognized as providing a complete
sense of depth perception by satisfying both the psychological
and physiological cues required for three-dimensional imaging
[1]. Hologram recording methods are classiﬁed into analog
techniques, which obtain interference patterns using two coher-
ent light sources, and digital techniques, which generate and
acquire interference patterns through digital processing.
Among digital techniques, the computer-generated holo-
gram (CGH) method exists, which mathematically simulates
the propagation and interference of light by arranging point
light sources on objects of arbitrary shapes [2]. In this case, a
specialized display called a spatial light modulator (SLM) is
required to observe holograms generated using CGHs. The
reference beam incident on the slit of the SLM is diffracted and
interferes to form a reconstructed image of an object, and the
viewing angle of the reconstructed image depends on the pixel
pitch of the SLM. In other words, as the pixel pitch increases,
the diffraction angle of light decreases, leading to limitations in
the viewer’s ﬁeld of view. Due to this characteristic, the devel-
opment of ultra-high-resolution SLMs with a pixel pitch below
1µm is required to achieve a wider viewing angle.
However, if an SLM is manufactured at the size of a typ-
ical mobile phone while maintaining a small pixel pitch,
the amount of holographic data required for reconstruc-
tion becomes tremendously vast. For example, if the SLM
is assumed to have the size of a 4 in. 16:9 mobile display and
the pixel pitch is set to 1 µm, the diagonal length would
become 4 ×25.4 (mm)×1000 =101,600 µm. Given the
16:9 aspect ratio, the width would be approximately 88,547
µm, and the height would be about 49,807 µm. Assuming
a pixel pitch of 1 µm, the total number of pixels would be
88,547 ×49,807 =4,408,572,329 pixels. Since an image
consists of three channels, the total data size would become
4,408,572,329 ×24 (bits)=105,805,735,896 bits, which
is approximately 13.2 GB. Since a single high-resolution holo-
gram data amounts to approximately 13 GB, compression
is essential for the efﬁcient transmission and storage of data.
However, because holograms have different data characteristics
compared with natural 2D images, achieving a high compres-
sion performance using conventional algorithms developed for
2D image compression is very challenging.
The reason is that a hologram generates an interference pat-
tern by superimposing fringe patterns from multiple object
light sources. Each fringe pattern consists of alternating con-
structive and destructive interference, and since these patterns
overlap irregularly, the spatial frequency variations within the
interference pattern become extremely complex. As a result of

this generation process, holographic data exhibit a signiﬁcantly
large dynamic range between neighboring pixels. Furthermore,
whereas high-frequency components in conventional 2D
images tend to be concentrated in boundary regions, holograms
spread a mixture of high- and low-frequency components
throughout the entire region due to the random superposition
of fringe patterns. Therefore, lossless compression algorithms
based on the statistical characteristics of data do not perform
effectively on holographic data [3]. Additionally, when using
the JPEG algorithm based on the DCT transform, if high-
frequency components distributed throughout the interference
pattern are removed, detailed patterns depicting the object are
lost, resulting in the loss of critical information on the original
object in the reconstructed image. For this reason, a different
approach is needed to effectively compress high-resolution
holograms containing large amounts of data.
The human eye can perceive a vertical ﬁeld of view of approx-
imately 130◦and a horizontal ﬁeld of view of about 165◦[4].
Research [5] has shown that the visual latency tolerance thresh-
old in the human visual system (HVS) is approximately 13 ms. If
real-time rendering is delayed, the user’s immersion may be dis-
rupted, resulting in inconsistent interaction with the content. In
practice, generating high-quality, artifact-free rendering images
in real time within 13 ms with constrained computational
resources is challenging.
To address this limitation, understanding the perceptual
characteristics of the HVS is crucial. The HVS exhibits nonuni-
form spatial resolution due to the distribution of photoreceptors
in the retina. The retina contains two types of photoreceptor
cells: cone and rod cells. Cones are responsible for color per-
ception and high spatial resolution under photopic conditions,
whereas rods are highly sensitive to brightness and motion under
scotopic conditions but provide relatively low spatial resolu-
tion [6,7]. Cone cells are densely concentrated in the foveal
region, while rods are distributed across the peripheral retina
[7]. According to the Tyler model [8], the distribution of cones
varies with the incident angle of incoming light, and light enter-
ing near the optical axis is focused on the fovea. Consequently,
objects illuminated within this central region are perceived with
high clarity, whereas peripheral objects appear less distinct.
Building upon these perceptual characteristics, the foveated
rendering technique has been introduced as an effective solution
that utilizes the properties of the HVS. In HVS perceptual
models, an eccentricity range of approximately 5◦from the
center of the retina, according to the angular distance, is deﬁned
as the foveal region. Objects within this range are perceived
clearly, whereas objects outside the 5◦range in the peripheral
region appear blurry. By exploiting this characteristic, com-
puting resources can be efﬁciently allocated by generating a
high-resolution rendering image for the foveated region and a
low-resolution image for the peripheral vision. Therefore, real-
time rendering has become feasible within the 13 ms latency
threshold allowed by foveated rendering.
Applying foveated rendering to CGH is effective in speeding
up the calculation of hologram data. However, CGH requires
a method for freely controlling the resolution of the peripheral
vision. As a preliminary step, we give an overview of prior studies
on foveated rendering in the ﬁeld of holography.
Lee et al. [9] addressed the resolution degradation issue
caused by pupil swim in conventional optimization methods
without eye tracking. To mitigate this, they applied a weighting
matrix to the foveal region based on foveated rendering, restrict-
ing the range of resolution reduction to the peripheral region to
minimize image quality loss. Through this approach, they suc-
cessfully implemented an OLED-based two-layer see-through
HMD.
Hakan et al. [10] designed an SLM such that the high-
resolution hologram remains aligned with the rotation center
of the eyeball, located approximately 13 mm behind the pupil.
Using a pupil-tracking system to detect the user’s eye move-
ments, the system dynamically updates the high-resolution
holographic scene on the basis of gaze direction. As a result,
it provides a visual experience in which the high-resolution
hologram appears to remain consistently aligned with the
foveal region. In this context, Refs. [9,10] utilize a dual-display
setup to render high-resolution and low-resolution holograms
simultaneously.
Ju and Park [11] proposed a progressive update method
using triangular mesh-based CGHs. They ﬁrst generated a
low-resolution hologram rapidly using large triangular meshes
and then increased the mesh density around the gaze point using
smaller meshes to generate a high-resolution hologram.
Kang et al. [12] implemented a real-time CGH generation
method for head-mounted displays (HMDs) by applying a
foveated rendering technique. The system ﬁrst tracks the user’s
gaze to distinguish between foveal and peripheral regions. A
higher number of point sources are used in the foveal region to
generate high-resolution holograms, while fewer point sources
are used in the peripheral region for lower resolution. As a result,
the overall computational load is reduced, enabling real-time
hologram generation.
Kim et al. [13] also proposed a method for reducing render-
ing resources by introducing personalized parameters, on the
basis of the observation that the size of the foveal region varies
among individuals.
The Luxembourg-based company SeeReal technologies was
the ﬁrst to propose a technique that enables real-time high-speed
calculations in holography, although it is not a foveated render-
ing technique [14,15]. SeeReal created a virtual, small viewing
window corresponding to the observer’s pupil and generated
holograms only for the object point sources that fall within this
window. As a result, the computational load was substantially
reduced compared with generating holograms for all object
point sources, enabling real-time generation of holographic
images. This technique of applying a virtual small viewing
window is referred to as a sub-hologram.
Sakamoto et al . [16,17] proposed a CGH calculation method
based on the Fourier transform optical system (FTOS). In this
method, the sub-hologram size is smaller than that proposed
by SeeReal, and changing the resolution of the reconstructed
image is controlled by the size of the sub-hologram data. This
effectively differentiates the spatial resolution between the
central and peripheral areas within the Fresnel zone. As a result,
the reconstructed image exhibits a resolution difference between
the foveal and peripheral regions, and the Fresnel zone also func-
tions as a spatial frequency ﬁlter within the FTOS. However,

Finally, a foveated rendering-based lossy compression
method has also been proposed. Yikai et al. [18] reported that,
by applying a low compression rate to the foveal region and a
high compression rate to the peripheral region on the basis of
JPEG lossy compression, the overall ﬁle size could be reduced
to approximately 1/40 of the original. However, this method
applies a nearest down-sampling technique to reduce the reso-
lution in the peripheral regions of the generated hologram. This
approach is limited in that it only allows rectangular regions to
be speciﬁed for down-sampling, which may lead to the object
becoming distorted during the resolution reduction process.
Furthermore, it offers limited precision in controlling the
resolution. Thus far, research on foveated rendering in holog-
raphy has begun; however, studies related to data compression
remain largely unexplored. Considering the massive volume of
holographic data, it is necessary to conduct an in-depth analy-
sis and discussion on the compression performance and data
characteristics of foveated rendering-based holograms.
In this study, we propose a resolution control method based
on the Rayleigh criterion to reduce the resolution of peripheral
regions in foveated rendering. The proposed method selectively
adjusts the spatial resolution of holograms in peripheral regions
by controlling the resolving power between two point light
sources, thereby modifying the sub-hologram size. Notably,
this method requires only a single-panel SLM, allowing it to
be implemented on conventional SLM devices without hard-
ware modiﬁcations. In addition, it offers the advantage that the
resolution of the reconstructed image can be efﬁciently con-
trolled solely by calculating CGHs to adjust the sub-hologram
size, without varying the number of point light sources for
generating high-resolution and low-resolution holograms. It
should be noted that the proposed method does not model
visual perception itself but only draws conceptual motivation
from the foveated rendering principle. The aim of this study
is to characterize how reducing peripheral resolution through
sub-hologram sizing alters the interference-pattern data—
speciﬁcally the pixel-level dynamic range, spatial correlation,
and frequency distribution—and how these inherent character-
istics affect compression performance. To this end, we apply the
proposed resolution-control method to CGHs and evaluate the
achievable lossless compression rates under varying conditions.
Lossless compression evaluation is used deliberately: because
lossy compression inevitably removes data, it would obscure
how the intrinsic data characteristics evolve with resolution.
In contrast, lossless compression preserves all information,
allowing the compression performance to directly reﬂect the
underlying data properties.

the proposed method. Section 3presents the experimental
setup. Section 4provides the experimental results and analysis.
Section 5concludes the study.
2. PROPOSED RESOLUTION ADJUSTMENT
ALGORITHM
We propose a method for adjusting the resolving power of
the peripheral regions in foveated rendering. In Section 2.A,
we derive an equation to adjust the resolving power between
two point sources based on the Rayleigh criterion. Then, in
Section 2.B, we describe an algorithm that adjusts the resolving
power by applying the formula deﬁned in Section 2.A.
A. Dependence of Resolving Power on
Sub-Hologram Size

SLM (setting z=0) is placed between the viewpoint and the
point light source. When a straight line is drawn connecting the

only within this rectangle. This arbitrary rectangular region

the resolution distance between two point light sources can be
controlled. A diagram illustrating the concept of sub-holograms

Pixels outside this region are excluded from the interference

Overlap between sub-holograms is conditional. When the
sub-hologram size is small, overlap tends to occur between
adjacent object points but is unlikely for distant ones. As the
sub-hologram size increases, its spatial extent on the SLM
becomes larger, and overlap may also occur between more
widely separated object points. In the extreme case of a single-
pixel sub-hologram, the occurrence of overlap depends on the
geometric relationship between the viewpoint and the object
points: two intersection points may coincide within the same
pixel, resulting in overlap, or remain distinct, with each pixel
contributing to only one sub-hologram.
Step 4. Apply steps 1–3 to all point light sources.
Here, the object wave overlaps and forms a Fresnel zone as the
wavefronts become aligned. In the proposed method, due to the
constrained sub-hologram size, the Fresnel zone is segmented
into pieces corresponding to the sub-hologram area. At this
point, since the region of the Fresnel zone that the viewpoint
passes through is segmented depending on the viewing direc-
tion, the position of each segment is affected by the viewpoint.
For intuitive understanding, the interference pattern is illus-
trated in Fig. 3. Therefore, the resolution is controlled through
this mechanism.
3. EXPERIMENTS
The objects used to generate the holograms consisted of four
types: dragon, bunny, teapot, and a mixed model. The CGHs
were generated using a point-based method [19]. All holo-
grams had a resolution of 1920 ×1080 pixels, were full color
(8 bits ×3), and had a ﬁle size of 5.93 MB. The conditions
for hologram generation are illustrated in Table 2, and in this
experiment, the reconstructed image was obtained using the
angular spectrum method [20]. Five types of lossless compres-
sion algorithms were used for evaluation: JPEG2000 (JP2K),
JPEG-LS (lossless mode), the Lempel–Ziv–Markov chain algo-
rithm (LZMA), Deﬂate (LZ77 + Huffman coding), and linear
predictive coding (LPC). Five experiments were conducted
under different conditions. Except for the speciﬁc condition
being evaluated in each experiment, all holograms were basically
generated using the dragon object with 10,000 point sources, a
ﬁxed viewpoint at (0, 0, 170 mm), and a depth of 66.5 mm.

4. RESULTS
The compression results are shown in Fig. 8for each sub-
hologram size. When the sub-hologram was set to 1920,
since interference patterns were generated over the entire
dimension, there was no reduction in resolution, and it was
equivalent to conventional hologram generation. As the sub-
hologram decreased, the ﬁle size also reduced, and among the
ﬁve algorithms, JP2K and JP-LS exhibited the best compression
performance. In Fig. 9, the surface plot, frequency distribution,
and frequency entropy maps are presented alongside the recon-
structed image based on sub-holograms to analyze the effect of
ﬁle size reduction. Since the R, G, and B wavelengths exhibited
the same trend, Fig. 9displays the results for R as a representative
example. In the reconstructed images from Fig. 9(a)—1to 9,
except for sub-holograms 150 and 290, there was a slight change
in the reconstructed quality due to the sub-hologram size, but
no severe degradation was observed. This can be explained
by the observation that, as the sub-hologram size decreases,
the overlap among all object point sources at each SLM pixel
is reduced, while the localization of each part of the object is
enhanced. Consequently, even with smaller sub-holograms, the
point sources within each region locally generate interference
patterns corresponding to speciﬁc parts of the object. Since
this process is continuously performed for all point sources, the
overall quality of the reconstructed image does not signiﬁcantly
degrade.
Figure 9(b)—1to 9presents the surface plot of the inter-
ference patterns. It can be seen that as the size decreased, the
dynamic range of pixel values decreased, and the area where
the interference pattern was formed also decreased. From this
result, the excellent compression performance of JP-LS can be
explained as follows: due to the effect of the reduced dynamic
range in the pixels, the correlation between the target pixel
being predicted and its adjacent left and upper pixels increased
signiﬁcantly. As a result, as the spatial correlation increased,
the prediction error decreased. This, in turn, led to an increase
in redundant data during the encoding process, resulting in a
substantial improvement in compression efﬁciency. Thus, the
reduction in dynamic range implies decreased variability in the
interference pattern.
As shown in the frequency component distributions in
Fig. 9(c)—1to 9, the high-frequency components (represented
in red) nearly disappeared as the sub-hologram size decreased,
with mid-frequency components taking their place. From this
observation, the superior compression performance of JP2K
can be interpreted as follows: as the sub-hologram became
smaller, low-frequency components dominated, while some
mid-frequency components still remained. This change in
the frequency component distribution caused the wavelet
coefﬁcients in the high-frequency region of the JP2K wavelet
transform to become very small or approach zero. Consequently,
during encoding, the wavelet coefﬁcients were organized into
bit planes, where the high-frequency region contained many

5. CONCLUSION
In this study, we proposed a method for reducing the resolution
of the peripheral region by adjusting the resolving power and
investigating the lossless compression rate and its properties for
Research Article Vol. 64, No. 36 / 20 December 2025 / Applied Optics 10687
holograms with reduced resolution. As the resolution decreases,
the dynamic range of the hologram data narrows, resulting in
increased spatial correlation, and the frequency components
shift from high to low frequencies. As a result, we conﬁrmed
that the lossless compression rate reached approximately 70%.
Moreover, it was observed that viewpoint position, object shape,
and number of point sources had negligible inﬂuence on the
compression results for different sub-hologram sizes. In addi-
tion, by analyzing the compression trends with respect to depth,
a practical reference was established for determining the optimal
depth setting in sub-hologram-based hologram generation.
Additionally, noticeable image quality degradation was not
observed for sub-hologram sizes except for 150 and 290 in the
reconstructed hologram. Speciﬁcally, while the compression
rate was around 20% for a size of 1920, a size of 600 achieved
a compression efﬁciency of 60%. Furthermore, the proposed
method applies lossless compression to interference patterns
that include both amplitude and phase. When sub-holograms
are calculated as phase-only holograms, additional compression
effects are expected.
Based on these results, our approach can be further extended
by taking advantage of the characteristics of the peripheral
region. While the peripheral region in foveated rendering is less
sensitive to resolution degradation, the perceptual threshold for
acceptable quality loss has not yet been quantitatively investi-
gated. Future work will focus on determining this perceptual
threshold and applying it to evaluate the feasible extent of lossy
hologram compression.
The proposed method allows selective resolution adjust-
ment according to the user environment, and it is expected that
real-time transmission of large-volume hologram data will be
feasible within a restricted bandwidth by reducing the data in
the peripheral region, except for the region the user observes.
Resolution adjustment therefore is expected to facilitate as
an effective strategy for real-time hologram streaming under
limited communication bandwidths.

Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) are the bridging neurons that connect the retinal input to the visual processing centres within the central nervous system. There is a remarkable diversity of RGCs and the various subtypes have unique morphological features, distinct functions, and characteristic pathways linking the inner retina to the relevant brain areas. A number of psychophysical and electrophysiological tests have been refined to investigate this large and varied population of RGCs. Technological advances, such as high-resolution optical coherence tomography imaging, have provided additional tools to define the pattern of RGC involvement and the chronological sequence of events in both inherited and acquired optic neuropathies. The mechanistic insights gained from these studies, in particular the selective vulnerability and relative resilience of particular RGC subtypes, are of fundamental importance as they are directly relevant to the development of targeted therapies for these invariably progressive blinding diseases. This review provides a comprehensive description of the various types of RGCs, the developments in proposed methods of classification, and the current gaps in our knowledge of how these RGCs are differentially affected depending on the underlying aetiology. The synthesis of the current body of knowledge on the diversity of RGCs and the pathways that are potentially amenable to therapeutic modulation will hopefully lead to much needed effective treatments for patients with optic neuropathies.

This paper proposes lossless linear predictive coding based on the directionality of the interference patterns of a hologram. We approached this study from two aspects. First, to determine the directionality of the interference patterns, we performed differential pulse coding modulation (DPCM), segmenting interference patterns into blocks and scanning the pixels in eight directions for each block. Then, we determined the direction that had minimum entropy, calculating entropy in each direction, and encoded the difference by DPCM in the determined direction. In the second approach, we attempted linear prediction using the prediction coefficients for the determined direction based on the first process. In this case, the DPCM was utilized only to determine the direction in which to progress prediction about the original pixel. Then, we calculated the difference between the predicted and the original, and encoded it. Through the above procedure, we derived an appropriate compression scheme for the hologram by comparing DPCM with the linear predictive coding. Experimental results showed that the compression rate of 26.7% could be obtained through the first process.

Fast calculation method with foveated rendering for computer-generated holograms using an angle-changeable ray-tracing method
Article
Full-text available
Feb 2019
Wei Lingjie
Yuji Sakamoto
The computer-generated hologram (CGH) technique is a technique that simulates the recording of holography. Although the CGH technique has a lot of advantages, it also has some disadvantages; one of them is the long calculation time. Much research on the human eye has established that humans see 135° vertically and 160° horizontally, but can see fine detail within an only 5° central circle. Foveated rendering uses this characteristic of the human eye to reduce image resolution in the peripheral area and achieve a high calculation speed. In this paper, a new method for CGH fast calculation with foveated rendering using an angle-changeable ray-tracing method is introduced. The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness and high-speed calculation of this method.

Digital Image Steganography Survey and Investigation (Goal, Assessment, Method, Development, and Dataset)
Article
Dec 2022
De Rosal Ignatius Moses Setiadi
Supriadi Rustad
Pulung Nurtantio Andono
Guruh Fajar Shidik
Digital steganography has a long history, starting to be developed in the 90s until now. The main aspects of early steganography are security, imperceptibility, and payload. Security is an essential aspect of steganography, where its development competes with the steganalysis method. The development of steganographic methods is not only based on statistics but also involves adaptive methods, machine learning, artificial intelligence, generative adversarial networks (GAN), convolution neural networks (CNN), coverless techniques, etc. Several new methods add robustness as an important aspect. This makes the development of steganographic methods more varied and generally focuses the goal of the method on one or two aspects. Nowadays, steganography articles, especially surveys, rarely explicitly classify steganography based on its goals. So this survey contributes to classifying steganographic research based on goals and its assessments. This paper also reviews the use of assessment tools in depth because it is closely related to the goal of steganography. Challenges or issues, methods, developments, and popular datasets are also reviewed. Steganalysis literature was also added to improve the quality of the study and analysis. Finally, this survey provides discussion, investigation, critical analysis, and explicit summary so novice researchers can more easily understand image steganography development.
