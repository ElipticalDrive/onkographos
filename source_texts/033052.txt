Abstract Gaming consoles, whether stationary or handheld, are designed to provide a reasonably high level
of computing power to run contemporary video games at an attractive price point, a compact form factor and
modest energy consumption. While consoles have traditionally been closed-off systems, recent versions of
the Xbox allow the development of apps for the Universal Windows Platform (UWP) on retail devices,
making it potentially a suitable platform for bringing scientific visualisation (SciVis) applications to the
masses. We describe how to run such applications, namely volume rendering and ray casting of spherical
glyphs, on commodity gaming systems, not only on the Xbox Series X/S, but also on handheld devices like
the Steam Deck. We detail the challenges and limitations we encountered during the implementation and
provide the results of an extensive study of rendering performance, not only proving the viability of the
approach but also allowing for a cost and benefit evaluation compared to standard desktop computers.

PlayStation 3 had a very particular hardware architecture and were more of an embedded system from a
programmer’s point of view, the Microsoft Xbox One and its successors are effectively PCs and even run a

Although almost a PC in many aspects, the console remains a walled garden, which includes the need to
have access to the Game Development Kit (GDK) when developing native games. By ‘‘native games’’, we
refer to game packages with access to all of the functions the platform provides. However, leveraging the
PC-like architecture, Xbox One and Xbox Series X/S allow running any application based on the Universal
Windows Platform (UWP), initially designed for developing smartphone-style apps. In contrast to the GDK,

restricted set of application programming interfaces (APIs), and there are stricter resource limits. Nevertheless, these Universal Windows Apps can be deployed to retail consoles without the need for certification
if the developer mode has been unlocked on the device. Mobile gaming consoles like the Steam Deck, the
ASUS ROG Ally and the Lenovo Legion Go are tiny PCs running on PC operating systems.
All of that begs the question of whether we can not only re-purpose the software tools of the gaming
industry for visualisation purposes but also run our applications on gaming hardware that is potentially more
money-efficient than traditional workstations or has the benefit of being mobile. In this paper, we try to
answer this question by porting two visualisation algorithms, namely volume rendering and ray casting of
spherical particles, to Xbox Series X/S (Fig. 1) and comparing their performance with traditional PC
hardware, a laptop and mobile game consoles.
Bringing scientific visualisation to gaming console ecosystems, which provide a large and well-established install base among the general public, is a promising pathway to bring visualisation to the living room
and the previously mostly untapped user group of the couch scientist. Readily available access to a moderately high level of computing power in a home setting might support future endeavours in citizen
science (Gura 2013; Vohland 2021) and help to expand on previous efforts in using scientific visualisations
and visual storytelling with data for a general audience (Weiskopf et al. 2006; Ynnerman et al. 2016).
Moreover, we also see the opportunity to provide expert users with easier access to visualisations in a home
office environment without investing in a workstation PC.
In the following, we first describe how we ported existing Direct3D implementations of visualisation
algorithms to run as UWP apps on the latest generations of the Xbox. We detail the challenges and
limitations we encountered during this task, and second, provide the results of an extensive study of
rendering performance, comparing the performance of the two Xbox variants, the Steam Deck and the ROG
Ally with desktop graphics processing units (GPUs) as a baseline. This way, we not only prove the principal
viability of the approach but also have a solid foundation for reasoning about its costs and benefits. Along
the way, we provide data on the performance impact of rendering visualisation as a Universal Windows App
compared to a C?? implementation with access to all-native APIs.
2 Related work
Scientific rendering, including sphere ray casting and volume rendering, has been extensively studied and
several visualisation frameworks are available to provide easy access to many rendering algorithms (Gralka
et al. 2019). Bruder et al. (2020) introduced a tool specialised for running performance benchmarks for
sphere ray casting and volume rendering. We adopted and extended their software for our purpose of
running benchmarks on Xbox consoles, as discussed in greater detail in Sect. 3.
In the past, the use of gaming console hardware for science was mostly limited to simulations, most
notably using the PlayStation 3, which was of particular interest to the simulation community due to its Cell
processor architecture (Kim and Carlson 2007; Stürmer et al. 2009). However, Sony eventually removed the
OtherOS feature that allowed the use of user-installed software on PlayStation 3 by citing security concerns (PS3 Firmware, 2023). Meanwhile, Xbox consoles offered the option to develop applications using
Microsoft’s XNA framework, albeit with some limitations (Shen 2009). Starting with Xbox One registered
Windows developers could create applications for Xbox using the UWP without the need to have the native
Game Development Kit. In part, this became possible because the system architecture of the recent consoles
has moved to the common x86 PC architecture. Detailed information on the hardware specification and
internal layout of Xbox consoles were described by Andrews and Baker (2006) for Xbox 360 (which used a
PowerPC-based design by IBM), by Sell and O’Connor (2014) for Xbox One, and by Grossman and

Andrews (2021) for Xbox Series consoles. In a recent interview (Plummer 2023), long-term Microsoft
employee Dave Cutler provided details about the software infrastructure of the new Xbox consoles, which
uses a hypervisor to isolate the user interface and each individual game. Additionally, Dempsey (2021)
reported his findings of a hardware teardown of the Xbox Series X and of the Steam Deck (Dempsey 2022),

Steam Deck’s SteamOS. As we focus on the UWP for Xbox support, we excluded this additional option in

Besides using the core console hardware, video game console peripherals have also been of interest for
scientific applications. Especially Microsoft Kinect (Sell and O’Connor 2014) received attention for its use
in computer vision and as an input device for human–computer interaction research. Han et al. (2013)
provided a review of using Kinect to address various computer vision problems, while Francese et al. (2012)
investigated the use of Wiimote and Kinect as low-cost motion capture and gesture input solutions.
With the widespread adoption of smartphones in the past fifteen years and the increasing interest in
ubiquitous computing, scientific visualisation has already been pushed onto a new hardware ecosystem with
a large install base among the general public. Especially with the support for graphics APIs such as
OpenGL ES, porting scientific visualisation rendering algorithms to mobile devices and smartphones
became possible (Moser and Weiskopf 2008). However, small mobile devices come with limitations due to
their compact form factor, their use of battery power and the use of different operating systems. Most
notably, rendering performance and support for advanced graphics API features is often limited compared to
traditional desktop systems but also compared to gaming consoles that were specifically designed around the
idea of providing good rendering performance.
Unlike video game console hardware, which has generally seen little use for scientific purposes in recent
years, software and frameworks originally designed for video game development, namely game engines
such as Unity 3D and Unreal Engine, have seen a large increase in interest by the scientific community (Lv
et al. 2013). Among other factors, this was pushed mainly by wide-ranging support for Virtual and Augmented Reality devices that would otherwise require much effort to integrate into custom visualisation
frameworks and applications. However, game development using an engine does not allow for bypassing the
need to have the native development kit when developing for consoles.
3 Benchmark tool
Our implementation is based on the one by Bruder et al. (2020); in fact, the code running on traditional PCs
(later referred to as the desktop setup) is their benchmarking framework, which we extended for our
purposes.1 To run on Xbox consoles, we had to port the software to the UWP, as the native GDK is only
available to game studios via a developer agreement or to independent creators via the ID@Xbox programme. Even to get access to the GDK via ID@Xbox, one would need to provide a detailed pitch of a
game to Microsoft, which reflects the alleged business model of selling the consoles at heavily subsidised
prices or even at a loss while making money via the commission on the revenue of games (Robertson 2021).
As researchers building scientific visualisation applications, we cannot pitch a game. Hence, using the UWP
is, albeit probably not the best way to unlock the full graphics potential of the console, the only way to
achieve our goal, as deploying UWP apps to Xbox only requires a Windows developer account. Such an
account allows for unlocking developer mode on a limited number of retail consoles and deploying apps for
testing without prior certification by Microsoft.
UWP apps are typically written using the.NET framework—typically in C# – or in C??. When using
C??, developers have direct access to Direct3D as their rendering API, which is no different from
applications using the complete set of Windows APIs. This makes the UWP port of the graphics code look
straightforward at first sight: the shaders for both Direct3D 11 and the newly added Direct3D 12 implementations are identical on the desktop and UWP. The only notable differences regarding Direct3D APIs
used in our application are all related to the interface between Direct3D and the windowing interface.
While the desktop and UWP applications are eventually compiled into executables and accompanying
libraries and resource files, the UWP variant is linked against a more restricted runtime than the desktop

3.1 Dealing with the UWP
The APIs of the UWP have their foundation in Microsoft’s Windows Phone endeavour and are more or less
a clean-sheet design aiming at a very responsive user interface. This is mainly achieved by only providing
asynchronous interfaces (supported by the async/await pattern in the C# language), thus preventing
applications from making blocking I/O calls or alike in the UI thread. Another effect of this legacy is that
UWP applications are heavily sandboxed and can, for instance, only freely access files within their designated application folders. Access to other locations in the file system is only possible on user request via
an OS-controlled file picker. These are obstacles to porting a benchmarking framework that synchronously
processes the test cases one after the other, which users provide via the command line. This issue should be
less severe for actual interactive visualisation applications that are already based on processing events in a
message loop. To overcome the issue of accessing the benchmarking scripts to run, we switched from
reading the path to the file from the command line to showing a file picker, exploiting the aforementioned
circumstance that apps are allowed to open any file as long as the user explicitly selects it. The files with the
benchmark results could be handled similarly in theory, but we opted for a different solution for two main
reasons. First, operating a file picker with a controller is cumbersome, and second, there is a non-negligible
chance of accidentally clicking the dialogue away and discarding the results. We therefore request the app
permission to access removable storage, which allows for freely writing to USB thumb drives attached to the
console without user interaction. Similarly, we do not want to manually select the data sets for all our
benchmarks during each run. The set of test files used in our experiment is, therefore, embedded in the
application package and installed with the app itself, which also allows our code to access the files without
any additional user interaction. This approach is actually very reminiscent of most game engines, which
usually package all the resources they need instead of asking the user to pick a file.
For the file picker to work, the benchmarking framework required retrofitting a message loop, which it
did not need before and consequently did not have. We, therefore, promoted the main thread to be the
message pump and run the benchmark in one of the threads used by the UWP to process I/O asynchronously—that is, a benchmark runs in the completion callback of the I/O request for reading its configuration file. Traditionally, interactive applications would run 3D rendering in the idle time of the message
pump, which, however, would have put our UWP port of the benchmarks at a disadvantage compared to the
original implementation, as the original benchmarks do not have to interrupt their work for processing UI
events.
In contrast to conventional desktop applications on Windows, the programmer is not responsible for
creating the window of a UWP app. Instead, the so-called core window is provided by the framework on
startup, and the swap chain used for presenting the results of Direct3D rendering must be associated with
this given window. As a direct consequence, a UWP application cannot destroy this window to start all over,

One particularity of the Xbox—in contrast to running UWP apps on desktop machines—is that applications can either run as an app or in so-called game mode. Running in app mode will limit the memory
available to the application to 1 GB and will also limit the use of the GPU, while running in game mode
allows applications to use 5 GB of memory and grants full access to the available GPU cycles UWP (2023).
We always ran our application in game mode because a large part of the data sets we used for our
benchmarks did not run at all in the limited app mode.
3.2 Direct3D volume renderer
The benchmarks by Bruder et al. (2020) use an OpenCL implementation for volume rendering. As OpenCL
is unavailable on Xbox, we had to port this part to use Direct3D like the ray caster for spherical glyphs
already did. Staying with a compute-based solution, we have implemented the volume renderer as a singlepass DirectCompute shader. This compute shader determines the intersection of a single ray with the
bounding box of the volume in each invocation and subsequently traverses the bounding box while sampling
the volume texture and evaluating the volume rendering integral (Max 1995; Engel et al. 2006).
3.3 Direct3D 12 benchmarks
The newly added Direct3D 12 variants of the benchmarks have been ported as directly as possible. Most
notably, they use the same shaders. The only difference is that we embedded the shader root signature into
the HLSL code, which could be avoided at the expense of more complex C?? code. On the host side, we
used double buffering, i.e. a pipeline depth of two. As the benchmark repeatedly renders the same frame for
a benchmark run, our code prepares a command list for each of the frames once and submits it in an
alternating manner, which theoretically slightly reduces the CPU overhead compared to what the driver is
likely doing to avoid resource conflicts on Direct3D 11. Likewise, the constant buffers holding the camera
parameters and the vertex buffer or structured resource buffers holding the per-particle data do not change
and are, therefore, shared between the frames. This was implemented using the same descriptor tables for
both frames of the pipeline, which is only possible as these tables never change.
4 Experiment
For evaluating the runtime performance of our port, we ran a series of automated benchmarks on the
following test platforms (see also Table 1): (i) an ASUS ROG Ally, (ii) a PC (AMD Ryzen 9 5900X, 64 GB
RAM) with several discrete GPUs (AMD Radeon RX 6700 XT, AMD Radeon RX 7900 XTX, Intel Arc
A770, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090), (iii) a first generation Steam Deck, (iv) a Lenovo ThinkPad X13 G3
AMD (AMD Ryzen 7 6850U, 32 GB RAM, AMD Radeon 680M), (v) an Xbox Series S and (vi) an Xbox
Series X. On these systems, we ran all of the benchmarks, comprising two qualitatively different families of
tests: ray casting of spherical glyphs and volume rendering. For display resolutions, we mainly tested Full
HD (1920  1080), WQHD (2560  1440) and UHD (3840  2160), because these are the typical resolutions used for the latest generations of gaming consoles. For the ROG Ally, the Steam Deck and the
ThinkPad, we focussed on the respective native display resolution of Full HD, 1280  800 and
2560  1600, because these mobile devices are typically only used at their native resolution. For the camera
positions, we followed the recommendation by Bruder et al. (2020) and used random camera positions. We
computed 16 positions that put the camera around the data set while looking at it, i.e. it was guaranteed that
the camera always sees at least a part of the data set (see Fig. 2).
For each configuration, i.e. combination of hardware, data set and other parameters, we performed
multiple passes to obtain measurements for different aspects, as detailed in subsection 4.3. During the
execution of the benchmarks, we rendered into an off-screen render target without displaying the result. This
is mainly motivated by the UWP app running in fullscreen mode on Xbox, which limits our ability to switch
the output resolution without side effects on performance (see Sect. 6). As the mobile devices and the AMD

Overall, according to the conceptual model of scalability in visualisation by Richer et al. (2022), we
characterise scalability by providing detailed computational performance measurements for varying input
parameters related to problem size (such as image resolution) and computing resources.
4.1 Sphere ray casting
The sphere ray casting benchmark uses different methods to draw spherical glyphs in 3D space
(Table 2). Most of these use some proxy geometry on which we subsequently ray cast pixel-perfect
spheres (Gumhold 2003). Our benchmarks also support using tessellation shaders, both for creating
spherical geometry on the fly and for generating the flat proxy geometry for later ray casting. Unfortunately,
we could not get any tessellation shader running on the UWP on the Xbox (see Sect. 5). Therefore we
measured ray casting on polygons with an adaptive number of vertices and tessellation of actual sphere and
hemisphere geometry only on desktop machines. The adaptive sphere and hemisphere techniques compute
the number of vertices being created for each particle based on the distance to the camera. They differ in that
the hemisphere technique distributes these vertices on the hemisphere facing the camera; whereas, the
sphere technique distributes them over the whole surface. The main focus of the results will, however, be the
following methods: ray casting on instanced triangles, ray casting on instanced quads and ray casting on
geometry-shader-generated quads and polygons.
For our benchmarks, we used two real-world data sets from molecular dynamics simulations: a time step
from the formation of a liquid layer and a time step from a laser ablation simulation (Table 3 and Fig. 3).
The majority of the data sets, however, contained uniformly distributed particles at random positions,
allowing for better investigation of the scaling behaviour of the devices than real-world data. The random
spheres were positioned in a domain of 103 units, i.e. an increase in either the number of spheres or the radii
of the spheres increased the density of the data and subsequently also the overdraw.
All of the benchmarks work effectively the same on the CPU side, regardless of whether they use
Direct3D 11 or Direct3D 12: all of the data points are emitted at once in a single draw call. Performance
should therefore be dominated by the transformation and pixel-filling capabilities of the graphics hardware.
We therefore hypothesised that (i) there is little to no difference between the frame times obtained using
Direct3D 11 and Direct3D 12 and (ii) there is little to no difference between the frame times obtained using
the UWP build and the native Win32 build.

4.2 Volume ray casting
For the volume rendering benchmarks, the most important controlled parameters were the data sets (Fig. 3),
the sampling distance scaling factor, which we modified between 0.5, 1 and 2, and the screen resolution. The
base sampling distance was computed based on the data set such that a diagonal ray would sample each
voxel on its path. Early ray termination was disabled for all test runs and the number of samples per ray was
not capped, i.e. the ray always traversed the whole bounding box from the entry point to the exit point.
As for the sphere ray casting, the volume rendering as we have it implemented is dominated by what the
GPU can achieve in terms of compute shader and texture throughput, because each frame is rendered by
emitting one compute shader on the whole grid of output pixels. We therefore again expected that there
would be no significant difference between Direct3D 11 and Direct3D 12 and between UWP and Win32,
respectively.

5.1 Impact of API and platform
Since the AMD Radeon RX 6700 XT has hardware specifications closest to the Xboxes, we chose it as
our reference platform on which we tested the UWP port against the Win32 desktop build. As stated before,
we expected that neither the choice of the Direct3D API version nor the choice of platform would have a
significant impact on rendering performance. Our measurements show that this is roughly the case for the
platform (Fig. 4a) but clearly not for the graphics API (Fig. 4b), in particular not for sphere rendering. Since
we cannot assume normal distribution of our measurements due to the way how the benchmark configurations are constructed, we computed pairwise relative performance numbers for each configuration and
inspected these visually. Figure 5 shows this relative performance comparing desktop Win32 with the UWP.
In general, we see a slight tendency of the UWP to perform better (which is somewhat unexpected),
although this is just above 1 % in the most extreme case, which largely confirms our expectations. At least,
we can infer that a poorer performance on the Xbox consoles than the AMD Radeon RX 6700 XT is not
primarily caused by the need to use the UWP on this system.
As illustrated by Fig. 6, we found noticeable differences between the Direct3D 11 and 12 implementations. The behaviour differs depending on the hardware, and only the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090
exhibits the expected similar performance. We therefore reckon that these differences are caused by how the
software interacts with the hardware, mainly due to the higher level of abstraction of Direct3D 11. AMD
devices, which include the Xbox and all mobile gaming devices tested, overwhelmingly perform better
under Direct3D 12, in some volume rendering cases up to almost 1.5 times—with the notable exception of
small data sets in volume rendering. For the Intel Arc A770, in contrast, Direct3D 11 seems to be the better
choice, which is unexpected because Intel allegedly focussed on Direct3D 12 when implementing their
drivers.

5.4 Summary
To summarise our results and to provide a high-level overview across all benchmark scenarios, Fig. 12
shows mean frame time for all systems plotted against the manufacturer’s suggested retail price (MSRP) in
U. S. dollar. Note that prices do not include additional PC parts and peripherals necessary to build a
complete and usable system, as this would introduce too many additional variables2. Instead, prices express
the minimum amount of money needed to acquire a GPU at launch, placing desktop graphics cards
seemingly more favourably in the chart. Nevertheless, we observe that the Xbox Series X provides similar
sphere rendering performance to the AMD Radeon RX 6700 XT at a similar price but without the need to
additionally invest in a gaming PC. Furthermore, additional performance gains for sphere rendering need a
substantially higher investment. The results for volume rendering show a noticeable performance gap
between Xbox consoles and the desktop GPU, which is primarily caused by the anomalous performance of
both Xboxes for large volume data sets.
Mobile systems generally come at a higher price, considering their relatively weak performance.
However, this is to be expected, as they provide a complete package with no further investments required for
usage. Also, a compact form factor and the intended purpose of running on battery power while on the move
provide stricter limitations on their performance.

6 Discussion
Bringing the core visualisation algorithms to the Xbox seems to be almost trivial at first sight, even without
access to the GDK—provided the existing implementation uses Direct3D. Shaders and the overwhelming
majority of Direct3D host code runs without the need to modify anything. However, things are getting more
complicated the closer one gets to the interface with the windowing system. There, the implementation must
adapt to whatever the UWP allows. Moreover, almost all interfacing with the operating system needs to be
rewritten to use the asynchronous UWP programming model, something that might be non-trivial when
trying to retrofit an existing application based on the traditional message loop paradigm and making heavy
use of synchronous POSIX-style APIs.
The analysis in subsection 5.2 reveals that the performance of the Xbox consoles meets our initial
assumption. Except for two scenarios, the Xbox Series X performance lines up closely to a desktop GPU
with similar theoretical computing power. The use of geometry shaders, however, results in an unexpected
drop in rendering performance. More importantly, volume rendering performance does not show the
expected scaling in relation to the size of the volumetric data set. This presents a noteworthy limitation and
warrants further investigation to determine if this behaviour could be remedied by optimising the shaders
and rendering implementation specifically for Xbox or whether it truly is a hardware limitation.
We also do not want to withhold that we could not get any tessellation-based technique to work on the
Xbox. We assume this to be a specific problem of the UWP on Xbox as there are obviously games that use
tessellation, but even Microsoft’s own UWP samples for tessellation did not work on the console. After
pointing out this issue on GitHub, it was forwarded to the graphics team, but the repository was temporarily
archived shortly after, effectively closing all unresolved issue as well. At the time of writing, the UWP
samples for tessellation are still not working on the consoles, which gives us reason to believe that the
problem is rooted in the platform and non-trivial to resolve.
During development, we also noticed that we could not use the Direct3D 12 debug layers on the Xbox,
whereas the desktop UWP app was running with debug layers enabled, as was the native Win32 build.

While the debug layers are a quality-of-life improvement in general, they are particularly important for
Direct3D 12, where the framework places enormous responsibilities on the programmer and counters many
violations of invariants simply with a crash. Especially in cases where a problem only occurs on the Xbox
and is not reproducible on PC—as for the aforementioned issue with tessellation—the lack of the debug
layers makes fixing the issues a difficult endeavour. A similar problem arises when encountering unexpected
performance behaviour, such as for the large volume data sets on the Xbox. As profiling tools like PIX are
only available to native Xbox developers with access to the GDK, it is almost impossible to diagnose such
an issue as a UWP developer without tooling support. The enormous visual fidelity of console games is
founded on developers’ ability to tailor their software to this single, specific architecture using the profiler.
Unfortunately, this path is blocked for developers of visualisation tools based on the UWP. We also want to
point out that developing for Xbox with the UWP still requires registering as a Windows developer, which
involves paying a small one-time fee. Furthermore, the number of dev mode activations is limited to three
Xbox consoles per account. It is necessary to publish an application in the Microsoft app store to use it on a
larger number of consoles and make it available to other users.
Finally, we encountered an oddity while running our benchmarks, which might only be relevant for
benchmarking use cases, but we deem still noteworthy: While the Xbox accepts back buffer sizes that do not
match the native resolution of the display attached, this native resolution still seems to be somewhat special.
In order to not limit the rendering performance to the refresh rate of the display, we performed all
benchmarks with a present interval of zero, i.e. instructing the framework to ignore v-sync. If, however, the
size of the back buffer matched the resolution of the display attached, the Xbox would ignore that and still
limit the frame rate to the frame rate of the display. For our benchmark, we therefore opted for only
rendering to off-screen buffers. However, for actual visualisation applications, this is a no-issue as the user
experience is not diminished by restricting the frame rate to the refresh rate of the display.
Employing a game engine that supports consoles is an obvious alternative to our efforts of porting an
existing visualisation framework to a console. However, implementing specialised visualisation algorithms
within a game engine can also be non-trivial. Game engines are primarily designed for rendering heterogeneous scenes with only moderate amounts of data loaded per individual scene entity, while scientific
visualisation scenes are typically much more homogeneous—often consisting of a single entity—with large
amounts of data loaded to update the scene. Fitting the requirements for loading and rendering such a scene
into the architecture of a game engine can be tedious and often requires creative solutions to bypass
limitations of interfaces that were designed for different scenarios. Additionally, the use case we present
here was not the development of an actual application but to evaluate the viability of the approach from a
technical and specifically performance point of view, which has different requirements like being able to
measure at the lowest level and to work around issues like the ones described above. Using an engine and all
of its infrastructure might, however, be a sensible approach for end-user applications visualising data that
can be easily transformed into or are already in formats the engine natively supports. However, building a
native Xbox game using an engine also requires the GDK for development and packaging, which requires at
least membership in the ID@Xbox independent developer programme, as it would be the case for a C??
application written against Direct3D and other native APIs. An engine that allows building universal app
packages like Unreal Engine works around this issue similarly to our approach—and it would largely hide
the particularities of the UWP from the developer.
In general, we consider the Xbox a viable platform for visualisation provided that (i) existing code is
written against the Direct3D API or uses an abstraction layer that can work on top of Direct3D, that (ii) it—
at least at the moment—does not need tessellation and (iii) has either a very small existing code base or
already uses solely asynchronous APIs for I/O and alike. The latter is obviously not a must, but switching
the paradigm will incur a non-negligible overhead for refactoring. All of these issues do not apply to the
other platforms running standard versions of Windows and providing all of its well-known functions.
Whether they are viable for running interactive scientific visualisation algorithms therefore mostly boils
down to the question of the rendering performance they provide.
Performance on mobile gaming systems meets our expectations. Small and medium-sized data sets
achieve interactive frame rates, but all systems struggle to maintain interactivity as volume and particle data
sets grow larger or for data sets that generate many fragments and much overdraw. One of our initial
assumptions was that choosing an appropriate native display resolution to match the available computing
power of a mobile gaming device could compensate for fragment bound scenarios. Comparing the results
for Steam Deck rendering at a resolution of 1920  1080 and at its native resolution of 1280  800 confirms
this.

7 Conclusion
We have investigated the use of PC-like dedicated gaming hardware for scientific visualisation. Most
importantly, we have ported two commonly used visualisation techniques—volume ray casting and ray
casting of spherical glyphs—to the UWP, which enables them to run on the most recent versions of the
Xbox consoles without the need of having access to the native Game Development Kit. Using this
implementation, we ran a series of benchmarks to assess the viability of using dedicated gaming hardware as
a cost-efficient replacement of laptops, gaming PCs or visualisation workstations typically used for this
application case. Our results show that the Xbox Series X can compete with desktop GPUs of similar
computing power but at a lower price than is typically needed to assemble a complete desktop PC system.
Therefore we could demonstrate that the Xbox Series consoles are a viable low-cost platform for scientific
visualisations, which is an important step towards future developments in researching and providing
visualisation software for the Xbox consoles. Nevertheless, we also identified performance problems for two
specific benchmark configurations on the Xbox Series consoles that will require further investigations into
possible optimisation, which are, however, difficult while being limited to the UWP. The mobile gaming
consoles we tested did not exhibit this kind of unexpected effects but behaved similarly to a PC system,
albeit with lower computing power than a typical desktop GPU.
To extend our work in the future, we plan to perform additional benchmarks to asses and compare power
consumption and to investigate existing performance bottlenecks. Furthermore we want to investigate how
visualisation applications with user interaction should be designed to accommodate typical gaming console
input devices such as gaming controllers and the restricted I/O capabilities of the console platform.
