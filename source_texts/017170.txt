CT angiography
is a promising new technique
for noninvasive
vascular imaging [1 , 2]. Most investigators
have used maximum
intensity
projection
(MIP) and/or surface rendering
to create
three-dimensional
(3D) angiogram-like
images from the CT
data. Both of these postprocessing
techniques
have significant
shortcomings,
however,
and at least one study has reported
superior results with standard axial and multiplanar
images for
imaging renal artery stenoses
[2]. The shortcomings
of surface
rendering
and MIP, coupled with the excellent results that have
been obtained with volume rendering
in other 3D applications
[3, 4], prompted
us to apply volume rendering to CT angiography. Unlike MIP and surface rendering,
which display only a
small fraction of the available
data, volume rendering
can display a volume of data in its entirety. This property allows volume-rendered
CT angiograms
to provide
vessel
“depth”
information
and to display multiple overlapping
vessels much
like conventional
angiograms.
Our preliminary
experience
has
shown that CT angiography
with volume rendering can have an
important
impact on patients’ care in several clinical applications, including
vascular
imaging,
oncologic
evaluation,
and
planning
of surgical
and minimally
invasive
therapy.
In this
essay, we illustrate applications
and imaging findings of volume
rendering for CT angiography.

Applications
The Aorta
Although we have found volume rendering to be effective
for most CT angiographic
applications,
volume rendering is
particularly
useful for diagnosing
vascular abnormalities
in
larger vessels such as the aorta. When applied to large yessels, MIP and surface rendering display only a small fraction
of the available intravascular
data, and even large intravascular defects may not be well visualized.
By displaying data
from the entire volume, volume rendering accurately portrays
the lumen of large vessels, clearly showing such abnormalities as thrombi and intimal dissections
(Figs. 2 and 3). Low
levels of vascular opacity simulate the appearance
of catheter angiograms
and allow the viewer to see “through” contrast columns,
providing
information
about vessel depth
similar to that found on conventional
angiograms,
which can
be important in diagnosing subtle intravascular
filling defects.

Conclusion
Volume rendering is a flexible method for generating 3D angiographic images from helical CT data that allows the usento generate images with a range of opacities for optimal display of surface
or internal detail as needed for the specific clinical problem. It is
particularly
useful for visualizing
larger vessels, multiple overlapping vessels, and the location of vessels relative to tumor and
othertissues.
