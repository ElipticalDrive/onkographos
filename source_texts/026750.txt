# DIGS: Distribution-Informed Gaussian Splatting for Training-Free Open-Vocabulary 3D Segmentation

Zhifan Zeng1 Wenbo Xiao1 Arcot Sowmya1 Changming Sun2 1UNSW Sydney 2CSIRO Technology zhifan.zeng@unsw.edu.au wenbo.xiao@unsw.edu.au a.sowmya@unsw.edu.au changming.sun@csiro.au

# Abstract

Open-vocabulary 3D Gaussian segmentation typically binds semantic features to the per-Gaussian state via per-scene gradient training (taking 1–4 h) or the more recent closed-form distillation. Both commit each primitive to a single feature; therefore, boundary Gaussians spanning multiple 2D instances are forced into a hard decision that degrades retrieval accuracy and yields unstable mask boundaries at render time. We present DIGS, a training-free framework with two stages, class-agnostic mapping and multi-modal encoding, that decouples instance discovery from linguistic naming. At the Gaussian level, our distribution-informed representation maintains an explicit top-M label distribution per primitive, updated by a tempered Dirichlet rule; preservation of multi-peaked label distributions at boundary primitives allows a late-argmax renderer to recover clean instance boundaries. A bidirectional state machine aggregates these distributions into a persistent class-agnostic instance ontology. The instance-level encoding stage then fuses the CLIP visual embedding of each instance's isolated 3D footprint with the CLIP-text embedding of an LLM-generated attribute description, disambiguating visually similar instances. On a single A100, DIGS completes mapping and instance encoding on LERF-OVS in 1.0–3.2 min per scene, depending on whether LLM fusion is enabled, and serves native 3D queries in 5.4 ms. It reaches 71.95% mean mIoU on LERF-OVS, outperforming both the training-free SFS and trainingbased LaGa baselines. DIGS also achieves the highest mean mIoU on LERF-Mask and 3D-OVS and surpasses Gaga and ObjectGS on Replica under their respective evaluation protocols. Code is available at [https://github.com/ZephyrZos/](https://github.com/ZephyrZos/DIGS) [DIGS](https://github.com/ZephyrZos/DIGS).

# 1 Introduction

Semantic understanding of a 3D environment from sequential observations is essential for embodied agents [1], virtual reality [2], and language-driven scene editing [3]. Open-vocabulary segmentation on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [4] requires a per-Gaussian semantic representation that supports text queries. Prior methods bind a single semantic feature (a continuous CLIP embedding or a clustered instance ID) to each Gaussian via per-scene gradient training [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. This design couples two failures. First, boundary Gaussians spanning multiple 2D instances are forced to one feature, blurring rendered edges. Second, the per-scene optimisation (taking 1–4 h) bakes the language signal into the per-Gaussian state, locking each reconstructed asset to one language model. Recent work eases one symptom at a time. The closed-form Splat Feature Solver [11] reduces training to minutes but inherits the boundary blur. ObjectGS [12] sharpens boundaries by splitting cross-instance Gaussians under joint photometric and cross-entropy optimisation, which can hurt RGB fidelity when the 2D mask supervision is noisy. The Bayesian alternative [13] treats each query as a binary Beta–Bernoulli problem. No prior design, however, resolves the coupling between training cost and boundary fidelity within a single per-Gaussian representation.

The two failures are not independent symptoms but consequences of what each Gaussian stores as the semantic state, as Figure 1a illustrates. A single one-hot label (e.g., ObjectGS [12]) yields sharp argmax-composited boundaries, but the discrete cross-entropy loss forces boundary Gaussians to split during training, degrading the reconstructed RGB whenever the 2D mask supervision is noisy or viewinconsistent. A continuous CLIP-style feature (e.g., LangSplat [5]) tolerates approximate geometry, but linear blending of adjacent-instance embeddings at boundary pixels produces semantically ambiguous mixtures that may match neither class at query time. Both choices commit each primitive to a single semantic mode: the discrete one pays in geometry, the continuous one in semantics. Neither breaks the boundary–cost coupling.

We present DIGS, a training-free framework whose distribution-informed per-Gaussian representation stores an explicit top-M label distribution, updated by a tempered Dirichlet rule. Targeting the boundary failure, the distribution is composited at the pixel level via late-argmax rendering; boundary Gaussians contribute proportionally to adjacent instances and the final label is decided by the majority of unambiguous neighbours, lifting LERF-Mask mean mIoU to 91.71 (+3.3 over the previous best ObjectGS at 88.4) and adding +8.84 mean mIoU in the LERF-OVS distribution-preserving ablation (§4.3\). Targeting the cost-coupling failure, the tempered Dirichlet update is closed-form with a provably bounded per-frame injection (Proposition 1\) and executes as a single parallel pass per frame, completing class-agnostic mapping in 0.91 min on a single A100—versus 1–4 h of per-scene gradient training for distillation baselines, a 60–250× reduction, as Figure 1b visualises.

On LERF-OVS, these choices place DIGS at 71.95 mean mIoU, surpassing the strongest training-free baseline SFS and the strongest training-based baseline LaGa, defining a new accuracy/wall-time frontier. The advantage sharpens on adversarial prompts: as Figure 1c shows on yellow desk in Waldo\_kitchen, where LaGa, LangSplatV2, and SFS all leak the mask into surrounding fixtures, DIGS returns a clean boundary that covers more of the object than even the human-annotated ground truth. These outcomes follow from three contributions: a distribution-informed per-Gaussian representation (top-M label distribution) that resolves the boundary failure via late-argmax rendering; a closed-form tempered Dirichlet voting rule with a provably bounded per-frame injection that yields a class-agnostic instance ontology in a single parallel pass without per-scene gradient training; and a post-hoc multi-modal encoding that fuses each instance's CLIP visual embedding with the CLIP-text embedding of an LLM-generated attribute description, keeping the mapping output reusable across language backbones.

# 2 Related Work

3D scene semantic segmentation. Since 3D Gaussian Splatting [4] became a mainstream scene representation, a growing body of work has addressed how to assign semantic or instance information to its primitives. The dominant recipe uses a 2D class-agnostic mask generator, typically SAM [14], and lifts its outputs onto the Gaussians through per-scene optimisation [9, 15, 16, 17, 18]. This inherits the 2D-to-3D lifting tradition from neural-field predecessors: Semantic-NeRF [19] and Panoptic Lifting [20] embed per-frame labels into implicit fields, while Contrastive Lift [21] and SA3D [22] lift instance or SAM masks; all are bottlenecked by the slow volumetric rendering of their underlying representation. Earlier point-cloud methods [23, 24] train from scratch on annotated 3D data, and open-vocabulary point-cloud systems [25, 26, 27] extend this to CLIP-driven text queries. A complementary line of work casts the lifting problem as a one-shot assignment: FlashSplat [28] solves it using linear programming with an optimal closed-form solution but operates on a single target mask per query; B3 -Seg [13] formulates segmentation as sequential per-query Beta–Bernoulli Bayesian updates, without a persistent multi-instance ontology; Splat Feature Solver [11] casts feature lifting as a closed-form sparse linear inverse problem, achieving CLIP feature fields at minute-scale per-scene but committing each Gaussian to a single continuous feature without per-primitive label uncertainty; and Occam's LGS [29] formulates language-feature assignment as a MAP-based weighted multi-view aggregation, training-free but again binding each Gaussian to a single CLIP feature. Most closely related to our two-stage design, DCSEG [30] and ExtrinSplat [31] also decouple class-agnostic 3D grouping from downstream language assignment via a 2D foundation model, but require per-scene optimisation. DIGS departs from these paradigms by performing multi-class instance lifting with an explicit per-Gaussian top-M distribution in a single streaming pass, without gradient computation.

Per-Gaussian semantic representation. What each Gaussian stores as semantic state is a decisive design axis. A first family distils a continuous vision–language feature into every Gaussian, extending the feature-field distillation idea of DFF [32], N3F [33], and LERF [34] to Gaussian primitives: LangSplat [5], LangSplatV2 [6], LEGaussians [7], and 3D-VLGS [35] all bind each Gaussian to a CLIP embedding through tens of thousands of training iterations, producing a feature field tied to the training-time language model. A second family supervises a per-Gaussian identity feature through dense 2D masks in three forms: InfoNCE-style contrastive losses (OpenGaussian [8], Unified-Lift [17], and OpenSplat3D [16]), cross-entropy classification over 2D mask IDs (Gaussian Grouping [9] and ObjectGS [12]) and 3D-aware memory-bank grouping (Gaga [15]). All of them commit each Gaussian to a single identity mode, enforced across frames by the training loss. A third option uses a binary Bayesian posterior [13], but only for one target at a time; this is itself the binary special case of a longer lineage of per-primitive Bayesian semantic aggregation on surfels [36] and voxels [37, 38], whose multi-class Dirichlet–Categorical form we adopt as the starting point of our voting rule. DIGS introduces a fourth option that none of the above provides: an explicit multi-peaked discrete distribution over instance identities, maintained per Gaussian, updated by a closed-form tempered Bayesian rule, and persistent across an arbitrary number of subsequent text queries.

Open-vocabulary querying and foundation models. Recent 3DGS open-vocabulary systems rely on vision–language foundation models such as CLIP [39] and open-set detectors [40] to connect text prompts with image content; methods that bind these models into the 3D representation at training time inherit a tight coupling, since the choice of language model becomes part of the scene asset itself. DIGS instead decouples lifting from language entirely: the mapping stage is class-agnostic, and foundation models enter only at the downstream per-instance encoding step, so the same map serves any text-driven query interface without re-running the lifting.

# 3 Method

# 3.1 Overview

DIGS operates in two decoupled stages on a pre-reconstructed 3DGS scene (Figure 2\). Stage 1 (classagnostic mapping, §3.2–§3.5\) builds a language-free instance ontology by distribution-informed per-Gaussian voting (top-M state under a tempered Dirichlet rule). Stage 2 (multi-modal instance encoding, §3.6\) attaches a fused CLIP-visual / LLM-text embedding to each stable instance.

Top-K geometry-aware rendering. We extend the CUDA rasteriser of 3DGS [4], following recent 3DGS segmentation pipelines that expose per-pixel Gaussian contributions during rendering [8, 9, 28], to output alongside the rendered RGB image the top-K contributing Gaussian indices and their alphacompositing weights for every pixel:

Gp = (gp,k, wp,k) K k=1, wp,k = op,k Q k′<k(1 − op,k′ ), (1)

where op,k is the effective opacity of gp,k at pixel p and wp,k is the resulting alpha-compositing weight. This top-K correspondence acts as both the back-projection operator for voting and the forward operator for rendering, with no learned matching.

Class-agnostic 2D observation. For each frame, SAM is applied in automatic mask generation mode to produce class-agnostic 2D instance masks S (t) = {Ωi} ns i=1. A mask-NMS post-processing step removes redundant highly-overlapping masks. These masks carry no category information; class attribution is deferred to the query stage (§3.6\).

### 3.3 Tempered Dirichlet Foundation

Both the state machine (§3.4\) and the voting pipeline (§3.5\) read from a per-Gaussian evidence accumulator Hi ∈ R J ≥0 updated from rendered SAM masks across frames (J = {0, . . . , J −1} is the label registry, with 0 reserved for background; J is distinct from the per-pixel top-K count of Eq. \(1\)). Naive additive accumulation Hi += W(t) i gives high-weight observations disproportionate influence and permits arbitrarily large evidence increments in one frame. In a closed loop, these increments can reinforce early association errors. We cap each frame's contribution with a closed-form tempered update.

Tempered update. Motivated by a Dirichlet–Categorical evidence model and generalised Bayesian tempering [41], we use a per-Gaussian per-frame learning rate

η (t) i = τ Si + W (t) i + τ , Si = P j Hi,j , W(t) i = P j W (t) i,j (2)

yielding the hit-accumulator evolution

Hi,j ← Hi,j + η (t) i W (t) i,j (3)

which executes in a single parallel pass per frame. The rate depends on τ and the current evidence, without a hand-designed η schedule.

Injection bound. The structural property that underwrites the rest of the method is below:

Proposition 1 (Injection bound). For τ > 0*,* Si ≥ 0*, and* W (t) i ≥ 0*, we have* η (t) i · W (t) i < τ .

One-step properties. Proposition 1 bounds the evidence injected by each voting step; it does not establish correct association or closed-loop convergence. At zero stored evidence, the injected mass approaches τ as the vote grows (Proposition 2\). When stored evidence dominates the current vote and τ , the learning rate decreases approximately as τ /Si (Proposition 3\). Proofs and implementationdependent qualifications are in Appendix A.2.

# 3.4 Bidirectional Two-Level State Machine

DIGS maintains a class-agnostic instance ontology at two bidirectionally-coupled levels: each Gaussian gi carries a top-M label distribution (DI) derived from Hi , and each non-background registry slot j ∈ J \ {0} carries a lifecycle record. Boundary straddling and occlusion fragmentation can split a Gaussian's evidence across multiple instance labels, motivating top-M evidence retention over argmax.

Gaussian-level semantic state. Let ji,1, . . . , ji,M denote the indices of the M largest entries of Hi in descending order and let Ti = {ji,m}M m=1. Each gi stores a hard top-1 label ℓi (set to background 0 before any evidence is received) and a normalised top-M evidence distribution supported on Ti ,

πˆi(j) = Hi,j1j ∈ Ti P j ′∈Ti Hi,j′ , P j ′∈Ti Hi,j′ > 0, 0, otherwise, (4)

which sums to one when the Gaussian has positive evidence; an uninitialised Gaussian contributes no instance vote. The distribution retains relative support for multiple labels when evidence is split. We write πˆgp,k (j) := ˆπi(j) with i = gp,k as the pixel-contributor shorthand used by the Late-Argmax renderer (Eq. [\(6\)). After each voting step we sparsify Hi to its M-support; computing η (t) i from this sparse state preserves the voting-increment bound (Appendix B.2\).

Instance lifecycle and footprint. Each discovered instance j is characterised by a Gaussian-space footprint

Gj = { gi ∈ G : ℓi = j } (5)

(the subset of primitives committing to j), and a lifecycle record with two counters: matchj (incremented on a successful Hungarian assignment) and missj (incremented when j is visible but unmatched, reset on a match). When missj > θmiss, instance j becomes a zombie; as the registry approaches capacity J, zombies are recycled first (longest-missing preferred), keeping the ID registry within a fixed maximum capacity J independent of sequence length.

# 3.5 Distribution-Preserving Voting Pipeline

The voting pipeline drives the state machine (§3.4\) forward under streaming observations, preserving the top-M distribution end-to-end.

Predict step. Before observing the current frame, the state machine renders a predicted label map Lˆ(t) (Eq. \(6\)) with predicted regions {Rj}. SAM masks {Ωi} are associated with global instance IDs by Hungarian bipartite matching on an IoU cost; an IoU threshold θiou decides new-ID allocation versus match, and a greedy fallback permits many-to-one matching under occlusioninduced fragmentation. The result is the global label map L (t) ∈ Z H×W consumed by the update step.

Update step. Given L (t) , the tempered Dirichlet update (Eq. \(3\)) refreshes H in a single parallel pass. Before voting, a visibility-aware leaky integrator with decay rate γ ∈ (0, 1) decays only substantially-visible Gaussians (W (t) i > wmin), so occluded or out-of-view primitives retain their evidence across long traversals.

Late-Argmax rendering. Rather than committing each Gaussian to a hard label before rendering (Appendix B.2\), we fuse the full top-M distribution at the pixel level:

ˆℓ(p) = arg max j>0 PK k=1 wp,k πˆgp,k (j). (6)

When every instance score is zero, the output label is background (0). Boundary Gaussians, which typically hold comparable evidence for adjacent labels, contribute proportionally to both sides and the final label is decided by the majority vote of neighbouring non-ambiguous Gaussians; this suppresses the staircase boundary artefacts that characterise hard-label rendering. The output Lˆ(t) feeds back into the next frame's predict step, closing the loop.

#### 3.6 Multi-Modal Spatial Instance Representation

Stage 2 attaches a fixed multi-modal embedding to each stable instance: the CLIP visual embedding of its isolated 3D footprint Gj (Eq. \(5\)), fused with the CLIP-text embedding of an LLM-generated attribute description. Computed once post-mapping, the embeddings support arbitrary downstream retrieval without re-running the voting pipeline.

CLIP visual retrieval. We retain the stable subset Istable = {j : matchj ≥ θmatch ∧ missj ≤ θmiss} to discard short-lived SAM fragments and zombie slots. For each j ∈ Istable we render Gj alone (all other Gaussians at zero opacity) from its top-B training views Vj (ranked by projected area), crop to the instance bounding box, and compute a CLIP visual embedding

ej = c¯j ∥c¯j∥2 , c¯j = 1 |Vj | P t∈Vj CLIPimg Crop(IsoRender(Gj , camt)) (7)

Isolated rendering prevents neighbouring-instance leakage into the embedding. At query time, the prompt is matched by cosine similarity, with j ∗ = arg maxj∈Istable cos(ej , CLIPtext(q)), in O(|Istable|) cosines per prompt; the predicted mask is then obtained by indexing the Late-Argmax label map Lˆ test rendered at the test viewpoint Mˆ q = 1[Lˆ test = j ∗ ].

LLM-enhanced fusion. A multi-modal LLM provides an attribute-rich text description per instance, CLIP-text-encoded into a complementary language channel that attaches post hoc to the language-free ontology. The same isolated crops as in Eq. \(7\) are passed to the LLM with a fixed prompt, producing description dj encoded into e txt j ; the fused embedding is

e fused j = λ ej + (1 − λ) e txt j , λ ∈ [0, 1] (8)

Retrieval uses e fused j in place of ej ; no second voting pass or re-optimisation is required (§4.3 quantifies the gain).

Detection-bridging (optional). When the evaluation protocol supplies the test-frame RGB, an open-vocabulary 2D detector can resolve the what, while the 3D instance field supplies the where via IoU matching against Lˆ test. The full specification is in Appendix B.4.

# 4 Experiments

#### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets. We evaluated on four open-vocabulary 3D segmentation benchmarks chosen to probe complementary aspects of the mapping pipeline. LERF-OVS [8] (4 scenes) follows the OpenGaussian 3D-native protocol with fine-grained, attribute-rich queries on cluttered scenes; it emphasizes 3D open-vocabulary retrieval, requiring that the correct instance be both localised and fully segmented, with mIoU and mAcc@0.25 as metrics. LERF-Mask [9] (3 scenes) follows the Gaussian-Grouping protocol with test-frame binary masks for short-noun queries; it stresses 2D mask boundary precision,



Figure 3: Qualitative comparison on LERF-OVS against LangSplat, LangSplatV2, LaGa, and the training-free comparator SFS. Two prompts per scene across the four LERF-OVS scenes.

with mIoU and the boundary-sensitive mBIoU as metrics. 3D-OVS [42] (5 scenes) provides 6–7 free-form language queries per scene on simple, spatially separated objects; we follow the original protocol and report mIoU only. Replica [43] (8 scenes) provides long indoor trajectories with revisits and many repeated, near-identical objects; we follow the protocols of Gaga [15] and ObjectGS [12]. Across all benchmarks, we follow the corresponding evaluation protocols and match evaluation settings across methods to ensure fair comparisons.

Implementation details. The 3DGS reconstruction was a fixed Mip-Splatting [44] model trained for 30,000 iterations to fit the RGB scene; the resulting Gaussians were frozen for the rest of the pipeline. We used SAM ViT-H [14] for class-agnostic masks, Open CLIP ViT-B/16 [39] for visual/textual embeddings, GroundingDINO Swin-B [40] for detection-bridging box prompting, and GPT-4.1 for per-instance LLM descriptions. Stage 1 (mapping) ran once per scene with a single hyperparameter set shared across all benchmarks (τ=5, M=3, γ=0.95, θiou=0.4 (0.15 on Replica's longer trajectories), and λ=0.7), producing a reusable instance ontology. All experiments ran on one NVIDIA A100.

# 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we present DIGS, a training-free framework for open-vocabulary 3D Gaussian segmentation. Existing methods bind a single semantic feature to each Gaussian via per-scene gradient training, blurring boundary Gaussians and locking each scene to one language model. We addressed this with two designs. First, a distribution-informed per-Gaussian representation: each primitive stores a top-M label distribution updated under a tempered Dirichlet rule with a provably bounded per-frame injection, allowing a late-argmax renderer to recover clean instance boundaries. Second, a streaming voting pipeline that updates a bidirectional state machine into a class-agnostic instance ontology in one closed-form Dirichlet pass per frame, removing per-scene gradient training. Openvocabulary querying is a lightweight post-hoc step fusing each instance's CLIP visual embedding with an LLM-generated attribute description. On LERF-OVS, DIGS maps a scene in 1–3 minutes on a single A100 and serves native 3D queries at 5.4 ms latency. It achieves state-of-the-art results on LERF-OVS, LERF-Mask, 3D-OVS, and Replica, while its CLIP-only configuration maps LERF-OVS scenes 225× faster than the strongest training-based baseline.

Limitations and perspectives. DIGS depends on its foundation-model stack: SAM's mask granularity caps the finest discoverable instance, while GroundingDINO, CLIP, and LLM jointly bound linguistic discrimination. Fusing multi-granularity SAM masks into a hierarchical ontology under the same voting machinery is a natural next step. A second limitation is that the pre-reconstructed Gaussian geometry is fixed throughout the semantic pipeline; jointly refining geometry and instance evidence may further improve boundary fidelity on transparent or thin structures.

# A Theoretical Derivations

This appendix supplies the proofs and derivations omitted from §3.3.

### A.1 Tempered Dirichlet Update

We use a Dirichlet–Categorical model to motivate the evidence update. Let θi ∼ Dir(αi), α (0) i = τ J 1J , τ > 0. In an untempered, unpruned count model, αi,j = Hi,j + τ /J relates additive counts to the hit accumulator Hi . In DIGS, however, the "observations" are not discrete counts but continuous rendering weights W (t) i,j = P p: gi∈Gp, L(t)(p)=j wp,i, and direct addition is unstable in two ways: scale dominance—a large-scale Gaussian can acquire a disproportionately large increment in one frame—and closed-loop feedback, where the rendered label map drives the next-frame association and can reinforce early errors.

Following the framework of generalised Bayesian inference [41], in the spirit of safe-Bayesian tempering of misspecified likelihoods [46], we introduce a per-Gaussian per-frame learning rate η (t) i . Within a voting step, after any visibility-aware decay and before top-M pruning, the corresponding tempered pseudo-count update is

α (t) i,j = α (t−1) i,j + η (t) i · W (t) i,j . (9)

Two design goals motivate our choice of η (t) i : (D1) cap the total evidence injected by one frame below τ ; (D2) decrease the learning rate as accumulated evidence grows, without a manual schedule. Both are satisfied by Eq. \(2\), equivalently τ /(Si + τ + W (t) i ), yielding the hit-accumulator update in Eq. \(3\). The deployed state also undergoes visibility-aware decay and top-M pruning, so its normalised evidence distribution is not an exact Dirichlet posterior.

#### A.2 Proofs of Propositions 1–3

Proof of Proposition 1 (Injection bound). Since Si+τ > 0, we have 0 ≤ W (t) i /(Si+W (t) i +τ ) < 1 for every W (t) i ≥ 0, including W (t) i = 0. Multiplying by τ gives the result.

Scope and conditional total-state bound. The one-step bound applies to non-negative votes even from erroneous associations and to retained top-M evidence (Si ≥ 0); it says nothing about association quality or the net state change after decay and pruning. Let S (t) i denote retained evidence. If S (0) i = 0 and every positive vote triggers decay, then S (t) i < γS(t−1) i + τ on positive-vote frames, while zero-vote frames cannot increase the state; hence S (t) i ≤ τ /(1 − γ). The implementation omits decay when 0 < W(t) i ≤ wmin, so no uniform total-state bound holds for arbitrary streams: a fixed one-label vote W (t) i = c ∈ (0, wmin] gives S (t) i = S (t−1) i + τ c/(S (t−1) i + c + τ ), which grows without bound.

Proposition 2 (Scale suppression). For fixed τ > 0 and Si = 0*, let* mi(W) = η (t) i W with W (t) i = W*. Then* mi(W)/W → 1 as W/τ → 0 for W > 0*, and* mi(W)/τ → 1 as W/τ → ∞.

Proof. With Si = 0, the injected mass is τW(t) i /(W (t) i + τ ). Its ratio to W (t) i tends to one as W (t) i /τ → 0, while its ratio to τ tends to one as W (t) i /τ → ∞.

Proposition 3 (Evidence inertia). For fixed τ > 0*, as* Si → ∞ with W (t) i /Si → 0*,* η (t) i = (τ /Si)(1 + o(1)) and η (t) i W (t) i = (τW(t) i /Si)(1 + o(1)). The rate remains positive at every finite state.

Proof. Dividing the denominator of Eq. \(2\) by Si gives η (t) i = (τ /Si)/(1 + (W (t) i + τ )/Si). The denominator tends to one under the stated conditions. At finite Si and W (t) i , it is positive, so η (t) i > 0.

Together, Propositions 1–3 characterise bounded injection, suppression of large votes at zero evidence, and attenuation under accumulated evidence. A positive learning rate at finite evidence permits later revision, but does not guarantee correction, top-1 stability, correct association, or closed-loop convergence.

Stage 1 builds the class-agnostic instance ontology under the tempered Dirichlet voting rule; Stage 2 computes per-instance multimodal embeddings once, post-mapping. The returned assets {e fused j , Gj , ℓi} are reusable across arbitrary downstream prompts.

### B.2 Top-M State, Sparsification, and Rendering

Top-M state. Let ji,1, . . . , ji,M index the M largest entries of Hi in descending order. Define

Ti = {ji,1, . . . , ji,M} ⊆ J , (10)

ℓi = ji,1, Si > 0, 0, Si = 0, (11)

πi,m = Hi, ji,mP j∈TiHi,j , m = 1, . . . , M, Si > 0, (12)

Hˆ i(j) = Hi,j 1[j ∈ Ti ], (13)

For Si > 0, πˆi(j) = Hˆ i(j)/ P j ′ Hˆ i(j ′ ); otherwise πˆi(j) = 0, as in Eq. \(4\). When M ≥ 2 and Si > 0, the gap πi,1 − πi,2 is a heuristic measure of ambiguity among retained labels, not calibrated posterior uncertainty. After each voting step we zero entries outside Ti and refresh ℓi according to Eq. \(11\). Top-M pruning is a sparsity–fidelity heuristic, not a Bayesian operation: it breaks conjugacy on truncated coordinates, and a label leaving Ti loses its stored evidence. The per-frame injection bound remains valid when η (t) i is computed from the truncated Si , because that sum is non-negative. The bound concerns the injected vote, not state changes due to decay or pruning. M=3 covers the dominant multi-peaked cases (boundary Gaussians with two adjacent labels; over-segmentation transitions). The implementation uses a dense N × J tensor for scatter updates but retains at most M nonzero evidence entries per Gaussian; M controls evidence support rather than the allocated tensor size.

Visibility-aware decay. A uniform decay H ← γH would erode occluded primitives whose evidence cannot be refreshed. We therefore decay only sufficiently visible Gaussians:

Hi,j ← ( γ · Hi,j W (t) i > wmin, Hi,j otherwise, γ ∈ (0, 1), γ=0.95, wmin > 0. (14)

Hard-label rendering. Given discrete {ℓi} (no top-M distribution), the rendered label at pixel p is

ˆℓ(p) = arg max j>0 X K k=1 wp,k 1[ℓgp,k = j], (15)

### B.3 Association and Lifecycle

Hungarian association. The IoU cost matrix Ci,j = |Ωi ∩ Rj |/|Ωi ∪ Rj | between SAM masks {Ωi} and predicted regions {Rj} is computed as a single batched tensor operation; the Hungarian algorithm solves σ ∗ = arg minσ P i (1 − Ci,σ(i)) with

ϕ(i) = ID(Rσ∗(i)) if Ci,σ∗(i) ≥ θiou, NewID() otherwise. (16)

A greedy fallback inherits the best overlapping global ID if maxj Ci,j ≥ θiou, permitting many-to-one matching under occlusion-induced fragmentation. We use θiou=0.4.

Lifecycle and zombie recycling. Each instance traverses four states: nascent (just created), active (matched each frame), missing (unmatched within θmiss window), and zombie (missj > θmiss). When the registry approaches capacity J, zombies are recycled first (longest-missing preferred), followed by all-zero columns; recycling zeroes the column and returns the ID to the pool, so |I| ≤ J regardless of sequence length, unlike B3 -Seg [13] (single query object per run) or Gaussian Grouping [9] (single batched optimisation, no streaming).

### B.4 Detection-Bridging Query

When the test-frame RGB is available, we localise the prompt q with GroundingDINO [40], obtain a 2D mask Mdet via SAM with box prompting, and match to the active registry by IoU:

j ∗ = arg max j∈I IoU Mdet, {Lˆ test = j} , Mˆ q = 1[Lˆ test = j ∗ ], (17)

with rejection threshold θbridge guarding against detector misfires or off-scene prompts. No retraining or embedding precomputation is required.

# B.5 LLM Description Cache

On the first mapping run for a scene, the optional LLM-fusion stage issues exactly one GPT-4.1 vision-language API call per stable instance j ∈ Istable with paired context / isolated view crops as input. The model returns a single line of text with three pipe separators (LABEL | CATEGORY | IDENTITY | DETAILS), which is written to a per-scene description cache keyed by integer instance ID. For all subsequent evaluation runs on the same scene we load this cache directly—no further API calls are issued—so that ablations, hyperparameter sweeps, and re-evaluations share identical per-instance descriptions and remain reproducible. At fusion time the cached line is CLIP-text-encoded into e txt j for use in Eq. \(8\). We release the description cache alongside the code so that all numerical results in the paper can be reproduced without new API calls.

Prompt template. The text passed to GPT-4.1 alongside the rendered crops is reproduced verbatim below.

You are given rendered images of ONE 3D instance extracted from a reconstructed scene. The images come in pairs from multiple viewpoints: - Odd-numbered images (1st, 3rd, 5th, ...): scene crops centered on the target object, rendered from the full 3D reconstruction. Other objects may appear at the edges. - Even-numbered images (2nd, 4th, 6th, ...): the same viewpoints but with ONLY the target object rendered on a black background. This shows the exact boundary and shape of the target.

Use the scene crops to understand context, recognize characters or brands. Use the black-background images to determine the object's true shape, color, and boundary. Describe ONLY the target object visible in the black-background images.

Output exactly ONE line with three pipe separators:

LABEL | CATEGORY | IDENTITY | DETAILS

LABEL -- one of: - OBJECT: a complete, well-formed item visible across all views. - FRAGMENT: partial, blurry, cut-off, or too distorted to name confidently. - BACKGROUND: wall, floor, table, pillar, or other large flat surface. - AMBIGUOUS: multiple unrelated objects merged, or unrecognizable. CATEGORY -- 1-2 bare nouns, no color, no adjectives (e.g. duck, chair, bag, camera). IDENTITY -- exactly 3-4 words: [color] [object name] or [color] [proper name] [object type]. Use the simplest, most common noun. If you recognize a specific character or brand, include its name. Do NOT add extra modifiers, materials, or adjectives beyond color and name. DETAILS -- one sentence, 10-20 words. Only factual visual features: shape, markings, text, accessories. No guessing, no function, no subjective words. Calibration examples (NOT from the actual scene): OBJECT | plush toy | orange tabby plush toy

| Green glass eyes, stitched nose, red bow on neck. OBJECT | teapot | blue ceramic teapot | White floral pattern, curved bamboo handle, round lid. FRAGMENT | edge | brown wooden edge | Only one corner visible, rest cut off by frame. BACKGROUND| surface | white countertop surface | Smooth matte finish with light scratches near edge. AMBIGUOUS | shapes | mixed colorful shapes | Multiple objects merged with heavy blur, no clear boundary.

- Rules:
- 1. IDENTITY must be exactly 3-4 words. No more. Start with color, end with object name.
- 2. Use the simplest, most common everyday noun. Prefer short familiar words over technical or formal synonyms.
- 3. Only describe the object in the black-background images. Ignore everything else in scene crops.
- 4. When uncertain, choose FRAGMENT over OBJECT.
- 5. BACKGROUND is only for flat structural surfaces with no distinct 3D shape. If it has depth, edges, or a recognizable form, use OBJECT.
- 6. One line only. No line breaks, no markdown, no JSON, no quotes. Exactly three pipes.

### E.3 Failure Cases

Despite leading mean mIoU on all three benchmarks, DIGS exhibits two residual failure modes that trace to upstream rendering and segmentation limits; representative examples are in Figures 8 and 9.

F1. Transparency couples voting weight to background. Our per-Gaussian voting weight W (t) i is the alpha-compositing weight from rendering, so transparent and translucent objects (glass / plastic surfaces) propagate non-trivial weight through the foreground surface to background Gaussians at the same pixel. The closed-loop association then assigns the foreground SAM mask partly to background Gaussians, "smearing" the instance footprint past the transparent surface and inflating its 3D extent. This is visible on the transparent box in Figurines and the transparent cup in Ramen.

F2. SAM-granularity caps part-level prompts. SAM's automatic-mask generator commits to a single granularity per scene; when a prompt names a sub-region of a larger SAM mask, our reconstructed semantic map has no slot for the part and retrieval returns the whole parent instance. Affected queries on LERF-OVS include waldo (a printed character on the cabinet), pirate hat (part of a figurine), and hooves together with dall-e brand (a body region and a printed logo on the same figurine); in each case the relevant SAM mask covers the whole parent, capping the achievable IoU on the part-level query. This reflects the SAM-granularity ceiling discussed in §5 (Limitations and outlook).
