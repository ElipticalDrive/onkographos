Abstract
With the ability to manufacture complex functional structures, Additive Manufacturing (AM) enables new advanced applications which could not be realized before. An example of such structures is open porous structures. In the presented approach,
the open porous structures are a result of the selected manufacturing parameters, instead of being defined in CAD models.
Thereby, various porosities can be achieved flexibly without design adjustments. However, to comprehensively understand
the correlation of manufacturing parameters and different properties, various tests are required. As an alternative, properties
can be determined by means of simulation. This study presents 3D reconstructions of complex open porous structures, which
are based on μ-CT imaging. The influence of the downsampling during voxelization on the 3D reconstructed geometry is
studied. Further, different polyhedral mesh settings have been tested in order to find a mesh that minimizes the deviation
between the CFD simulation results and the laboratory test results for flow resistance. A guideline for the creation of a
calibrated CFD model based on μ-CT imaging is proposed. As it is acknowledged by the authors that the data availability to
reconstruct shown cases is crucial, data for a reference case have been made freely available online.
Keywords Open porosity · Additive manufacturing (AM) · μ-CT imaging · Geometry reconstruction · CFD simulation
1 Introduction
In recent years, the development of additive manufacturing (AM) has led to faster prototyping, manufacturing of
high-performance materials, and the realization of complex
designs. Laser-based powder bed fusion (PBF-LB) has been
established as an important process for the manufacturing of
metal parts. Usually, the functionality of AM parts is determined by their topology, which is defined in a CAD model.
Parts are then manufactured as solid bodies during the AM
process. The resulting bodies can generate a certain function
like fluid guidance, transmission of forces, or heat conduction. However, by adjusting the manufacturing parameters,

variation, the microstructure of the material or the surface
texture can be controlled [1, 2]. Additionally, the adjustment of manufacturing parameters can be utilized to create
meso-scale porous structures without relying on detailed
CAD models. For example, parts can be built with open
porosities, allowing a flow through the material [3–5].
Without a predefined CAD geometry, the resulting properties cannot be simulated before manufacturing. Consequently, physical samples must be manufactured, which are
used for the evaluation of properties through testing and
simulations.
For simulations, a digital representation of the parts is
needed. For shapes designed by CAD software, the geometry can be imported, for example, as surface mesh, like
the STL format. Alternatively, the geometry of a physical
sample can be determined by computed tomography (CT).
This is especially useful if no CAD model is available or the
information about deviations from the 3D model needs to be
evaluated. Therefore, CT has also become an increasingly
applied method for the characterization of parts made by
additive manufacturing [6]. Studies have been performed for
the comparison of manufactured parts to the original CAD

µ-CT analysis procedure, which lays the groundwork for

Based on μ-CT imaging, parts without a CAD model can
be represented within a three-dimensional grid. Each cell in
this grid is called a voxel and is assigned a specific property,
i.e., their density value which is normally visualized as gray
value. The voxel grid can also be used for the generation
of meshes for the simulation of functional properties [17].
Additionally, voxel-based geometries have been applied
in the context of additive manufacturing. Ueng et al. [18]
described how the AM process can be previewed and emulated by a voxel-based simulation. Vega et al. [19] studied
the possibilities to model and simulate scaffold designs prior
to manufacturing. Although the obtained models were not
considered to be representative, the voxel-based approach
could be proven to be a valuable complement to conventional modelling.
The focus of the present study is the 3D reconstruction
and simulation of flow thorough open porous structures
made by AM. The presented 3D reconstructions are based
on voxel models derived from μ-CT imaging. In particular,
the influence of the downsampling on the 3D reconstructed
geometry is studied. Further, different polyhedral mesh
settings have been tested in order to find a mesh that minimizes the deviation between the CFD simulation results
and the laboratory test results.
An example of a voxel-based rendering of an open
porous structure is shown in Fig. 1. The surface meshes of
the voxel models have been extracted in a CAD Software
(i.e., Siemens Simcenter 3D). The surface mesh has been
imported to the CFD simulation software (i.e., Simcenter
STAR-CCM +) and subsequently remeshed into a volume
mesh, i.e., a polyhedral mesh. A direct import of the voxel
model, which is a volume mesh, is not possible, as the file
format is not supported.
The flow resistance of the open porous structures has
been simulated. The simulation results were compared
with the results of the laboratory testing. From the flow
analysis, the permeability and non-Darcy coefficient values [20, 21] can be found, which are two basic properties of porous materials. As the flow resistance, i.e., the
pressure drop, is strongly depending on the geometry of
the pores, it is seen as a suitable first case to study the
representativeness of the applied 3D reconstructions of
the geometry.
The reproducibility of the additively manufactured porous
structures, whose geometries are dependent on the used
manufacturing parameters and are not predefined by CAD,
is not considered in this work.
The study aims to increase the awareness of the possibilities for application designs enabled by AM. Results might be
accessible and applied by a broader community, e.g., for the
utilization of complex functional structures, such as porosities [22].
Amongst many possible applications, use cases in thermal management or acoustic dampening [23–26] have
already been published. Further, with this understanding,

2 Material and methods
2.1 Samples of open porous structures
The samples in this study have been produced by laser-based
powder bed fusion (PBF-LB). By choosing different sets
of manufacturing parameters, different porous structures
are produced. The coordinate system in the present work is
according to the definition in ISO/ASTM 52900:2021. The
XY-plane represents the plane parallel to the build layers and
the positive z-direction is the direction from the first layer to
the subsequent layers.
Three samples have been analyzed to validate the 3D
reconstruction and the simulation models presented in this
study. Their manufacturing parameters are based on previous studies by the authors [5, 12] and are summarized in
Table 1. As the hatch spacing, i.e., the distance of consecutively fused laser tracks, could be identified as a major factor
influencing the resulting porosity, the manufacturing sets
differ in this parameter. The selected hatch spacings were
chosen to cover a wide range of porosities, with a maximum
difference between the applied hatch spacings of 300 μm.
The samples have been produced in an EOS M290
equipped with a 400 W Yb:YAG fiber laser of 1070 nm
wavelength. All samples have been made from Inconel 625
in Argon atmosphere The powder particle size was 15–45
μm, and a brush powder recoater was used. The samples
have been cut off the build plate by wire electrical discharge
machining and cleaned in an ultrasonic bath. No heat treatment was applied.
Two different sample designs were applied. The samples
used for the μ-CT imaging were designed as cuboid porous
structures above a solid base, with a 5 × 5 mm footprint.
The second sample design for the laboratory testing of the
flow resistance was designed as cylindrical samples with an
open porous cylinder of 20 mm diameter and 25 mm height
and a solid shell of 2.5 mm thickness around the porous

2.2 CT‑based imaging
The geometry of the porous structures was acquired by
high-resolution synchrotron radiation μ-CT, using the beamline ID 17 of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility
(ESRF; Grenoble, France). The beam energy was 80 keV.
Images with an isotropic pixel size of 3.07 μm were realized. A total of 2500 radiographs over an angle of 360° were
captured.

voxel matrix, respectively.
As for the presented open porous structures, the indirect
approach is not sufficient, as resulting surface meshes had
large numbers of defects (e.g., gaps and overlaps), complicating a representative geometry reconstruction. These
defects might be explained by numerous complex structures, i.e., attached particles as shown in Fig. 1, which cannot easily be handled by standard approaches. Repairing
the geometry requires significant effort. Further, it is difficult to differentiate between an intended pore and a defect,
e.g., a gap, during the automated surface repair for the 3D
reconstruction.
For the direct approach, the volume mesh is directly
derived from the voxel-based μ-CT data, as shown in the
lower path in Fig. 5. This approach is applied in the present
study. In particular, the voxel-based 3D reconstruction, i.e.,
the voxelization and polyhedral meshing of the data, is studied. The voxel model is remeshed into a polyhedral mesh.
The polyhedral mesh settings and their adaption to increase
the agreement of simulations and laboratory test results
will be discussed. In a preceding study, Young et al. [32]
analyzed different mesh generation techniques, highlighting
the strength of voxel grid meshing in regard to robustness
and efficiency. The advantage of the obtained voxel model
is a closed surface without defects, i.e., gaps or overlaps.
A disadvantage might be the “staircase effect” of the surface, through which the surfaces might get a step-like shape.
Depending on the size of the voxel steps, this staircase effect
results in deviations from the original μ-CT voxels.

The software VoxTex [33], developed by the department
of Materials Engineering of KU Leuven (Belgium), was
applied to create a voxel model out of the image stacks.
In VoxTex, the original μ-CT data have been downsampled to the so-called super-voxels. This step is called voxelization in the present study. Resulting super-voxels combine
several original μ-CT voxels and thus have the potential
to reduce the computational power needed for the simulations. Figure 7 shows the definition of the super-voxels for
an example setup [34]. In general, the size of a super-voxel
is defined by the mesh density h, which is effectively the
distance between the middle point of two adjacent super
voxels. As for the shown example h is 12, which means that
this distance is 12 original μ-CT voxels. This results in a
super-voxel size of 36.84 μm with an original voxel size of
3.07 μm. As several original μ-CT voxels are combined in
a super-voxel, the gray value of the super-voxel needs to be
redetermined. This happens over a so-called window. The
size of the window is defined as two times of the window
radius w, which is placed on both sides of a center voxel. In
the shown example w is 4, which means a window size of 9.

Simulations on the flow resistance of the open porous structures were performed. Due to different pore sizes, Reynolds
numbers are expected in the range of 1 to 1000, meaning
an air flow in the transition regime between laminar and
turbulent conditions. The air was set as an ideal gas and
the k-ε Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) model
was chosen for the transport equations. The RANS model
can be seen as an engineering approximation, which averages the unsteadiness of a turbulent flow [37]. The boundary conditions, i.e., the fluid, mass flow, flow direction, and
temperature are set as the same as in the laboratory tests.
It was assumed that the simulated flow is adiabatic and the
relative fluid velocity tangential to the wall is zero (non-slip
shear stress specification). Additionally, the segregated flow
solver for conservation equations of mass and momentum,
in combination with the steady simulation model and the
SIMPLE implicit scheme, was used. The results of the simulations have been compared with the results of the laboratory
testing to verify the suitability of the 3D reconstructions.

For the PDS D10-based start settings of the polyhedral
meshes, the relative deviation from laboratory test to simulation for sample 1 increases from 9 to 28% with decreasing velocity. For sample 2, the deviation stays around 12%.
Sample 3 shows an increasing error from 23 to 35% with
increasing velocity.
The start settings give relatively good agreement between
the simulation and laboratory testing, with deviations under
35%. Considering the fact that a 9% difference in porosity
(50% of sample 2 to 59% of sample 3) leads to a difference
of 175% in pressure drop (550 to 200 Pa at the laboratory
test for 0.8 m/s air flow), this is seen as a good result.
The adaption of the polyhedral mesh settings to lower the
deviations from the simulation results to the laboratory tests
was studied for sample 2 and 3. Deviations from 1 up to 3%
for sample 2 and 8 up to 18% for sample 3 could be achieved.
As sample 3 is the sample with the highest porosity and
thus lowest pressure resistance, absolute deviations are very
low. The adaption of the settings has shown that if the pressure drop in the simulation is higher than for the laboratory
test result, the polyhedral mesh should be coarser, as this
will smoothen the geometry. The reverse approach applies
if the pressure drop from laboratory testing is higher than
the simulation result. To consider as many pores as possible,
the maximal mesh size should be smaller than the mean pore
size, and the minimal mesh size should be smaller than the
minimal pore size.
However, the start settings for sample 1 could not be
adapted. Here, the pore sizes and the staircase effect have
comparable orders of magnitude. Therefore, the surface
partly keeps the step form even after the polyhedral remeshing. A coarser polyhedral mesh is not possible, as the mesh
size would be larger than the pores and as a result blur them.
Finer mesh settings could not be applied, due to computational limitations. As a result, the deviation from laboratory
test to simulation for sample 1 is significantly higher than
the deviations for samples 2 and 3.
The results have shown that simulations on complex polyhedral meshes of voxel models can be done.
A guideline for the creation of a calibrated CFD model
based on μ-CT imaging is proposed. Generally, the adaption of the polyhedral mesh settings is a good way to reduce
the deviation of simulation results to laboratory testing. Yet
additional work must be carried out to derive general rules to
identify adapted mesh parameters, especially for small pores.
4 Conclusion and outlook
The present study focuses on the direct voxel-based 3D
reconstruction of open porous structures, which are made
by PBF-LB. CFD simulations on the flow resistance of such
structures, that are not based on CAD models, are conducted.
The effect of voxelization on the 3D reconstructed geometry
is studied. Further, different polyhedral mesh settings have
been tested in order to minimize the deviation between the
CFD simulation results and the laboratory test results.
The following can be concluded:
• The direct 3D reconstruction of the geometry from voxel
information is beneficial regarding the extraction of
defect free surfaces.
• With the appropriate voxelization combination, the geometrical features, i.e., calculated porosity, of the downsampled voxel model can correlate well to the original
μ-CT data.
• Polyhedral mesh settings, which are oriented on the pore
sizes of the open porous structures, can lead to reasonable results.
The International Journal of Advanced Manufacturing Technology (2024) 134:841–852
• Under certain circumstances, the polyhedral mesh set-
tings can be adapted further. A guide on how to minimize
the deviation between the simulation results and laboratory testing is given.
The presented results indicate the complexity of the 3D
reconstruction of open porous structures and the simulation
of their properties. For the structures presented in this study,
the voxelization combination and volume mesh settings will
significantly influence the simulation results, especially for low
porosities with fine details.
It was shown that the direct voxel-based 3D reconstruction
of the open porous structures can be successfully used in CFD
software. For the simulation of the flow resistance, the 3D
reconstruction can lead to two opposite phenomena: higher
detail level will include more channels that will increase the
permeability. Yet, at the same time, the resulting “staircase
effects” of voxelization can lead to additional turbulences
which will reduce the permeability.
Future studies shall focus on the extension of the presented
approach for the simulation of other functional properties, e.g.,
heat transfer and fluid–structure interaction. With a deeper
understanding of the correlation between 3D reconstruction
and the simulation results, a general guide for the 3D reconstruction of open porous structures could be created.
