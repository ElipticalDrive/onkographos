Abstract
The interaction of light and matter define what we see. This interaction can be
characterized by a function which relates incoming light to the distribution of out­
going or reflected light. The Bidirectional Reflection Distribution Function (BRDF)
is just such a function. Computer graphics scientists attempting to model this func­
tion have made simplifying assumptions about the reflecting surfaces. These simpler
models have produced adequate results but only handle very uniform or isotropic
surface characteristics. Reality on the other hand is filled with a variety of sur­
face textures which result in a variety of BRDFs. This thesis broadens the class
of BRDFs which can be used in computer graphics to render objects more realis­
tically. It presents two enhancements to previous work done by the author. First,
it describes a method for the approximation of surface absorption of light through
the use of a computed geometric attenuation factor. This factor is computed by
calculating the parts of the surface which are visible in the incident and reflecting
directions. Specifically, it describes the algorithms and theory behind the visibility
calculations and how it affects the reflection properties of the surface. Second, it de­
scribes a method for computing Fresnel’s Law for conducting and dielectric surface
materials and how Fresnel’s Law affects surface reflectivity. This work also presents
a method for rendering objects using the computed BRDFs.

Introduction
Image synthesis in computer graphics is the science of rendering two dimensional
images of reality. The ultimate goal of image synthesis research is to develop image
rendering algorithms that produce images which are indistinguishable from their
photographic counterparts. Achieving this goal requires very accurate modeling
of light, matter, and their interaction. This thesis will be concerned with modeling this interaction at the microscopic level. Although small with respect to the
objects being rendered, the microcscopic levelJs surface frequency is far above the
wave length of light. In particular this thesis will be interested in studying the farfield reflection properties of surfaces with arbitrary micro-surface geometries. The
characterization of the reflection properties is completely embodied in the Bidirec­
tional Reflection Distribution Function (BRDF)[27]. This function describes the
distribution of reflected light as a function of the incident or incoming direction
and the reflected direction. Intuitively this function describes how incoming light
is reflected or distributed after it strikes the surface. Most of the light reaching our
eyes is reflected light. Therefore, modeling this phenomenon is critical to generating
photo-realistic images.
All previous rendering algorithms included some type of BRDF. Most rendering
algorithms used very simple empirical approximations to BRDFs. The artificially
smooth images which resulted from these simple BRDFs produced images that were
noticeably computer generated. Increased image complexity has to a certain degree
made computer generated images more realistic. However, these images still suffer
from a certain “flatness” which comes from using the same simplified BRDF. Our
environment is filled with a variety of surface types. It is the premise of this thesis
that we need to model more accurately the interaction of light with surface textures
if we are to create more realistic images.
This thesis will examine the far field scattering of visible light from a small
sample surface whose peak-to-peak surface characteristics are on the order of 1001000 microns. This represents a surface frequency much greater than that of visible
1
2
light which has a wave length range of 300 to 700 nanometers. This thesis assumes
the light source is infinitely distant from the micro-surface. Thus, light wave phe­
nomenon can be approximated with geometric optics. Spectral phenonmenon can
also be modeled at the microscopic level. The most notable of these is Fresnel’s Law.
This law relates the amplitude and spectral composition of the reflected radiant flux
to the incident direction and wave length.
This study will describe an algorithm which will compute a discrete BRDF by
simulating the far field scattering off of an arbitrary sample surface. The BRDF
will then be used to render images of objects whose surface textures are that of the
corresponding BRDFs. The ultimate goal of this approach is to build a collection
of BRDFs which can be used in combination with one another. While the initial
cost of building the BRDFs may be great, the reuseability and increased flexibility
will help make this method long term cost effective.
Another important aspect of this method is that it allows blending into or from
bump map techniques and full surface renderings. Many people have discussed
the advantages of blending renderings of different levels of detail depending on the
distance away from the object. Objects which were far away can be rendered using
an inexpensive technique. As the observer moves closer to the object, one can blend
a more detailed rendering with the previous less detailed one. In the past this type
of hybrid rendering was impossible because reflection models were not based on
the specific geometry of the surface being rendered. Unless the surface geometry
corresponds to the reflection function, blending can produce noticeably incorrect
results. The method described here is based on actual surface geometries. This
does allow for smooth transitions between rendering techniques.
Chapter 1
Background
Physicists, optical engineers, and more recently computer graphics scientists, have
described how light reflects and scatters from various types of surfaces. Each of these
disciplines have had different reasons for modeling light. Physicists are interested
in understanding the phenomenon and are not necessarily interested in applications
of their models. Optical engineers are interested in using light models to design
optical instruments and surfaces. Comp titer graphics scientists are not interested
in the most general model but one which can be used to render images. The
diverse needs of these disciplines have led to different notations and approaches
when describing the same phenomenon. This thesis will use and explain notation
which has been standardized by optical engineers. This will provide a foundation
for all the notation used in this thesis and provide the basis for a discussion on
previous work.

1.2
Bidirectional Reflection Models
Torrance and Sparrow [35] developed a geometric lighting model for specular re­
flection which was applied to computer graphics by Blinn [4], and modified by
Cook and Torrance [12], to account for colored light and surface media color spec­
tra. It has been used to create spectacularly realistic shaded renderings of surfaces
such as brushed metal. This work was originally based on work by Beckmann and
Spizzichino [2],
The Torrance-Sparrow model assumes that an infinitely long, symmetrical, wedgeshaped groove has been cut into the surface. The normal N' to one of the two flat

sides of this groove is used, together with the lighting direction and the viewing
direction, to compute the fraction of the incoming light which is neither shadowed
on the way in nor obscured on the way out by the opposite side of the groove.
Random grooves, with some specific distribution of normals N! about the surface
normal N, generate a distribution of reflected rays from each incoming direction.
One can thus compute a bidirectional reflection coefficient. However, on any real
surface the grooves will interfere with each other, so they cannot be both randomly
oriented and infinitely long.
A real surface can be more explicitly modeled by a bump table, which gives the
surface height at a two dimensional array of sample points. Blinn [5] has shown how
to perturb the surface normal for use in a lighting model which takes account of
the bumps. The method has become known as “bump mapping.” It gives realistic
light reflections for a level of detail at which the bumps are visible, but need not be
modeled in full 3-D perspective. Max [25] has shown how to account for the shadows
from the bumps, by using a table of horizon angles. This might appropriately be
called “horizon mapping.” The method described in this thesis divides the surface
defined by the bump table into triangular facets. The reflection direction is then
computed for each facet and each tabulated lighting direction. The horizon table is
used to determine the portion of the incident light and reflected light which is not
obscured on the way into or out of the surface.
This method accumulates the radiant intensity into a tabulated bidirectional
reflection distribution function, using the tabulated reflection direction closest to
the actual reflected beam. Because it is derived from the bump table, the resulting
lighting model will be consistent with the area average of the light from an image
rendered with bump mapping and horizon mapping. This should permit smooth
transitions of detail between these two methods of representing surface roughness.
This is a major justification of the method: consistency with explicitly modeled sur­
face structure, including anisotropic reflections. Kajiya [21] has proposed smooth
transitions between three roughness representations: lighting models, bump map­
ping, and lull 3-D visible-surface calculations which include the bumps in the data
base. However Ins method uses the wrave theory of light, and is not computation­
ally practical. If bump mapped surfaces are rendered with horizon mapping, they
should in addition allow smooth transitions to full 3-D renderings including cast
shadows.
Perlin [29] has also proposed a smooth transition of surface roughness com­
ponents, between lighting models and normal perturbations. However his normal
perturbations do not come from an explicitly specified bump table, and therefore

The preceding chapter introduced the bidirectional reflection distribution function
and many techniques for computing the BRDF. This chapter refines the definition

2.1
An Explanation of BRDF
The BRDF is a directional form of surface reflectance. Reflectance, denoted as
p1, is the fraction of incident radiance that is reflected. This is the amount of light
which does not get absorbed by the surface or transmitted through the surface. The
BRDF is a continuous function of four variables 0tni
8ref, 0re/ or two directions,
an incident (incoming) direction and a reflected (outgoing) direction2. The BRDF
describes how much of the light incident on the surface from a particular direction is
reflected in another particular direction. This quantity is the ratio of the outgoing
light and the incoming light, and therefore must be less than or equal to unity.
However, the BRDF is more than a ratio. It is a directional, differentia! quantity
and when taken over the entire hemisphere it represents a distribution. This is
a statistical distribution function which describes the behavior of an independent
variable with respect to a dependent one (e.g. test scores over a student population).
The BRDF describes the distribution of a light with respect to solid angle and
surface area. It is very interesting to note that the BRDF equations that both Cook

2.2
Outline of the Algorithm
This section presents an algorithm which builds a discrete 4 dimensional BRDF in
the form of a 4 dimensional lookup table. The algorithm uses a surface approxi­
mated by a collection of triangular facets built from a height map. The height map
is a discrete function of two independent variables, e.g. z = f(x:y). The algorithm
is independent of any given surface. Thus the originating height map could be ar­
tificially created as in this thesis, or come from actual surface measurements. The
algorithm simulates the light-surface interaction by tracing rays onto the surface.
Rays which hit a facet without first being obscured by an intervening facet are re­
flected. If the reflected ray is not obscured by another facet it contributes a “delta
function” peak to the BRDF. The delta function is then weighted by the reflecting
facet’s area projected normal to the reflected beam. Thus, the discrete BRDF is a
weighted sum of the delta functions and is not continuous. Any light rays which
get obscured on the way out are assumed to be absorbed or transmitted and do not
contribute to the BRDF. Light which is discarded on the way in will contribute to
the reflection of another facet.
A brute force ray tracing algorithm, while potentially more accurate, would
be very costly for even a modest grid of 256x256. A ray tracing algorithm would
have to check all the triangles in the path of the ray to determine which of the
facets intersected the ray. This would have to be done for all of the rays over
the hemisphere. The algorithm presented uses a precomputed horizon for each grid
location in a number of discrete directions. It uses the horizon informat ion to quickly
determine whether the ray is obscured by the horizon in any given direction. The
horizon directions can be chosen to align with directions of incident light. Reflected

Building Horizon Maps
Precomputing the horizon is critical to the efficiency of the BRDF algorithm. If the
horizon is not precomputed and a complete visibility calculation is done for each
ray the temporal complexity would grow by an enormous constant. Implicit in the
horizon approach to the visibility calculation is that the surface be a bump map.
This does place a constraint on the generality of the surfaces handled by this ap­
proach but is not overly restrictive since many surfaces can be closely approximated
by bump maps [4].
A horizon map is a three dimensional map which stores the horizon in a finite
set of predetermined azimuthal directions for each point on a surface. Thus every
point on the surface has a horizon map. The horizon map is only a sampled approx­
imation to the actual horizon. The method for computing the horizon map, while
conceptually simple, is in many ways more complicated than the BRDF algorithm.
Up to now the bump map has been described as a height function with geometric
properties. It was implied in the description of the BRDF algorithm that some sort
of grid tessellation (tiling) existed so that the geometric pseudoptical phenomenon
could be modeled. The BRDF algorithm does not need to know the specific geom­
etry of the grid to perform its computations. It only needs to know the area and
the normal direction of tiles associated with a particular grid location. The horizon
mapping algorithm on the other hand must have a complete knowledge of the tiling
so that it can approximate the horizon along a line of sight which passes between
grid locations.
The tessellation pattern shown in Figure 2.2 groups four triangles about grid
locations whose indices3 when added together are even4.Triangles must be used
since the vertices of the tile must be coplanar. However, other types of triangular
tessellations would work equally well. This particular one was chosen for its asthetic qualities and because it has four-fold radial symmetry. Teselations which are
asymmetric can introduce unwanted biases in the reflection function which make
the surface appear anisotropic even when the surface is isotropic. Even though
the tessellation chosen is radially symmetric it does introduce some bias into the
reflection function (see Chapter 4, Figure 4.3). However, it does so in a radially
symmetric manner which is more desirable, especially for isotropic surfaces.

incident light direction6. For every ray, the ray grid line intersection is calculated
for a finite distance. Figures 2.3 and 2.4 illustrate the results of this process for the
even and odd grid locations respectively7. Once any given intersection is calculated
the fraction of the distance of the intersection point and the nearest grid location is
computed. These values are stored for each intersection for each ray. They are later
used in approximating the horizon for those points which lie between grid points.
Figure 2.7 outlines the line intersection algorithm. Note that since the angle ol the
triangles are either 0, 45, or 90 degrees that the intersection calculation is simplified.
An important but subtle part of this algorithm is the amount of space required
to store the computed fractions along the ray. Assuming unit distance between
grid locations the horizontal and vertical rays would require a number equal* to
the maximum distance of the ray. However, for arbitrarily oriented directions it is
not obvious how many would be needed. A fairly straight-forward maximization
approach can be taken to determine the worst case space requirement.

in a three dimensional table called the horizon map. It is indexed by grid location

Using Horizon Maps
The simplest usage of the horizon table is to directly render images as described
by Max [25]. A brief explanation of how to use the horizon map is given here to
illustrate the effectiveness of the technique.
A simple rendering algorithm was developed for debugging purposes and will
act as an example of rendering using the horizon map. The algorithm renders a
shadowed image of a bump map from a viewpoint normal to the bump map surface.
The algorithm reads a horizon map and takes as an argument the direction of a
light source. The algorithm then renders a pixel image representing shadows cast
from the light source. There is one pixel for each of the original grid locations.
The algorithm simply loops through the grid locations. For each grid location it
interpolates the horizon location based on the angular fraction between the nearest
horizon direction and the light source direction. If the horizon is below the light
source then the pixel is colored white. If the horizon is above the light source the
pixel s colored black. Figure 2.8 is a series of renderings in which the light source
acts like the sun, rising proceeding above the image and then setting. A height
map of the original surface is shown in Figure 2,8. In this image height is displayed
as intensity where black is minimum and white is maximum. As is evident by the
image the surface is much like a moon crater. Twenty-four horizon directions were
used for this and all the examples in this thesis9.
The horizon map is used by the BRDF algorithm in much the same manner as
by the simple rendering algorithm. The only difference is that the light direction
is always aligned with the horizon directions. As was mentioned before this is not
the case with the reflecting directions in which the horizon is interpolated as in the
rendering algorithm. It is worth noting that using interpolation to approximate
the horizon works well as seen in Figure 2.8. However, for surfaces which are
not as well behaved as the moon crater, the interpolation scheme could result in
some disastrous results. In particular, surfaces which have large features far off in
the horizon are candidates for severe horizon aliasing. Since the horizon directions
emanate radially from a grid location, features close to the grid location are sampled
at a finer level of detail than are objects farther away. Tins implies that sampling
is done non linearly as a function of distance from the location. Surfaces which are
statistically irregular, such a flat surface with a mountain, can produce horizon
maps which alias these features from one grid location to another. This would

There are several possible improvements of the BRDF table building algorithm pre­

This chapter will describe two improvements to the algorithm. The algorithm as

Fresnel’s Equations
The amount of incident flux reflected is not only a function of geometric effects
of the micro-faceted surface, but also the electromagnetic (EM) properties of the
facets themselves. When light interacts with a micro-facet the wave length of the
light, the incident angle, and the surface properties of the facet all play a role in
how much of the incident light gets transmitted, absorbed and reflected. KirchhofTs
Law of energy conservation states that the sum of the transmitted, absorbed and
reflected light must equal unity. This means energy must Ire conserved. Computing
the amount of light that is reflected as a function of surface properties, wave length,
and incident angle is completely described by Fresnel’s Law.

Conductors are surfaces which do conduct such as metals. The magnetic perme­
ability of a most dielectrics is near unity. This is certainly the case for the materials
discussed in this thesis. Furthermore, if we assume the incident media is a vac­
uum then its magnetic permeability will be exactly unity (for air p ^ 1.0)G. The
dielectric constant of a dielectric may be quite high depending on how much of an
insulator it is. The dielectric constant is the ratio of the conductivity in a vacuum
to the conductivity of the material. This quantity is a function of wave length of the
electric field. For instance, a red piece of glass has a lowrer dielectric constant for the
wave length of light corresponding to red. Thus, for dielectrics, Fresnel’s equations
can be readily computed if the index of refraction or the material constants are
known.
Fresnel’s equations for conductors are the same ones used for dielectrics. How­
ever, the interaction of the incident plane with the surface requires taking into
consideration the phase of the electric field. The phase information of the elec­
tric field is maintained by treating the electric field as a complex wave, where the
imaginary component of the wave represents the phase. The wave is attenuated
exponentially as a function of depth as it propagates into the medium. This results
in a phase change in the transmitted and reflected wave. The formal description
of this behavior is given in Born and Wolf [16] on pages 6T1-613*6. It turns out

4.1
Creating Faceted Surfaces
The BRDF algorithm creates a horizon map and micro-facets based on a height
map or bump map. There are two ways of creating bump maps as was pointed
out in Chapter 1, either by measuring actual surfaces or by synthesizing surfaces.
While there is equipment to perform surface measurements available at Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory1 it is an extremely time consuming task to acquire
such measurements. The National Bureau of Standards (NBS) is another source
of surface measurements. The NBS has also made reflection measurements from
these surfaces. Clearly for this thesis to be comprehensive it should compare the
results of the BRDF algorithm with the results acquired by the NBS. However, given
the limited scope of this thesis it was decided that synthesized surfaces should be
used, and that actual surface reflectance measurements be incorporated as a part
of further research. It is worth noting that the structure of the BRDF software
system (see section 4.4) is such that measured BRDF’s could be direc fly rendered
and visually compared with photographs of these surfaces.

The BRDF tables are created using algorithms in Figures 2.1, 2.6, and 3.2. There
are, however, three implementation issues which need to be discussed because they
play an important role in the quality of the BRDFs produced by the method de­

of the BRDF are spherical quadrilaterals formed on a hemisphere by taking equal
steps in polar and azimuthal coordinates as shown in Figure 4.5. It is obvious that
the bins near the pole are much smaller than those near the equator. When the
values in the bins are normalized they are divided by the solid angle of the bin. This
means that the bins near the north pole are in effect multiplied by a larger number
than those near the equator. In a statistical sense this is because the smaller bins
will receive a smaller amount of reflected flux than the larger bins if the surface was
a Lambertian surface2 and thus must be multiplied by a large number. The nature
of the spherical coordinate system creates a statistical problem.

with one of the surface generating programs, such as “noise.” Next “buildJjrdf” is

resulting BRDF table or tables can be used by the sphere renderer to draw images
or can be averaged with other BRDF tables to help improve the statistical quality
of the resulting images.
Helmholtz’s Law of reciprocity [19] can be used to further average the tables.
The values of the BRDF for any two directions should be the same if the directions
are swapped. Given the statistical nature of the table the two will not be the same
even if they should be. The table values for the two complementary directions
can be averaged to stored in the two complementary table locations. This insures
that Helmholtz’s Law of Reciprocity is maintained and at the same time improves
the table statistics by a factor of two. For isotropic surfaces the difference of the
incident and reflected azimuthal directions over the entire table can also be averaged
to improve the statistics of the table. This is possible because tables resulting from
an isotropic surface is a function of the incident and reflected polar directions and
the difference of the incident and reflected azimuthal directions.

5.1
Space and Time Costs
Perhaps the most obvious observation about the BRDF algorithm described is the
enormous amount of time and space that the algorithm uses. The size of the tables
used for the examples used in this thesis were 244 - 4 = 1,327,104 bytes long. Tables
of this size are very large, but manageable considering that it is becoming common
to have desk top machines with between 4 and 10 megabytes of main memory
storage. The horizon table, and grid geometry table require much more space than
the BRDF table itself. The horizon table requires 256-256-24-4 — 6,291,456 bytes
of storage. And, the grid geometry table, which stores the area and normal of the
triangle, requires 256 • 256 * 2 ■ 16 = 2,097,152 bytes. This results in a total space
requirement of about 10 megabytes. Unless the computer has significantly more
than lOmb of memory all virtual memory systems will have to page memory to
and from disk. Unfortunately the memory access pattern of the BRDF algorithm
is at best random and at worst sequential. The latter case implies that a least
recently used (LRU) paging algorithm1 will fail miserably. Fortunately machines
with main memories of 16-32mb are becoming more common and will allow the
BRDF data strcutures to become completely memory resident. It is worth noting
iMost operating systems use some approximation to the LRU algorithm based on the principle
of “locality of reference”. This principle states that a memory location recently referenced will be
reference in the near future. Thus pages which have not been recently used should be the first ones
to be paged back out onto disk if a new page needs to be brought into main memory and there is
no free pages in main memory. The BRDF algorithm violates this premise because it does not have
locality of reference with respect to accessing any of its main data structures |14],

the BRDF algorithm running on a VAX with lOmb was spending 20-40% of its time
page faulting. Thus it is imperative that this algorithm be run on a machine with
adequate main memory resources.
The BRDF takes considerable CPU resources. Building one BRDF table using
the full BRDF algorithm requires 6 hours of VAX 8600 CPU time. This time
could have been improved upon if the system used for this thesis had more main
memory. Unfortunately as was discussed in the last chapter a 256x256 microsurface might result in poor statistical distribution of the reflected flux into the
BRDF table. The solutions to this problem as described in Chapter 4 require
either running the BRDF alogorithm many more times with several surfaces with
similar surface characteristics and averaging the resulting BRDF tables or running
the BRDF algorithm with much larger sample surfaces. This can be very costly
since a linear increase in quality requires a quadratic increase in the size of the
grid. Another solution is to fit a polynomial surface in spherical coordinates to the
BRDF. Such a method was described in [10] and is also costly.
The sphere ray tracer performs nearly as fast as a ray tracer which does not
use the BRDF tables. A mere 10%-20% performance degradation is experienced by
the BRDF ray tracer. Since the table builder is not invoked for every image the
cost of using the BRDF tables is negligible. And given today’s memory costs the
memory requirement is not extreme. However, if one was rendering a scene which
required many different BRDF’s the memory requirements could rapidly become
extreme. Increased memory requirements will cause increased page faulting which
would degrade rendering performance. It, however, would be very interesting to
render a scene with many different surface textures rendered using BRDF tables.
Even if the BRDF is not created using methods described here, the table based
approach has the advantage of decoupling the BRDF creation from the algorithm
which solves equation (1.2). Thus the renderer which uses the BRDF tables can be
used to test out other BRDF creation algorithms.
5.2
Conclusions
A method for modeling the interaction of light with a micro-surface was pre­
sented. The method included techniques to handle problems of partial surface
self-shadowing and surface attenuation attributed to the electromagnetic proper­
ties of the surface. The result was an algorithm which constructed a table based
Bidirectional Reflection Distribution Function which more accurately describes the
5.2. CONCLUSIONS
61
scattering and nature of the reflected light over a hemisphere above the surface.
This work is different from the work done in [10] in two ways. First, in the previ­
ous work, reflections were computed without considering partial shadowing of one
facet by other facets. Instead, the facet was assumed either entirely shadowed or
illuminated. This is not as accurate as the method presented in this thesis. Sec­
ond, the previous work did not consider any electromagnetic interaction of the light
with the surface, such as was done in this thesis using Fresnel’s Law. The premise
behind both works is that tables for a variety of surfaces could be constructed once
and repeatedly used in subsequent renderings. The technique also has the impor­
tant advantage that the BRDF table comes directly from a bump mapped surface.
The renderers using BRDF tables created by this method could blend the BRDF
table values with other rendering techniques, providing smooth transitions from
renderings of one level of detail to the next.
The methods described here do not completely model the interaction of an inci­
dent plane wave with the micro-facets surface. Diffract ion and multiple reflections
need to be modeled. These two phenomenon are the major contributors to the
diffuse component of the BRDF. Previous micro-facet based models most notably
Cook [12] could ignore this fact and later add a Lambertian diffusion term to make
up for the energy which was destroyed by the geometrical attenuation calculation
and should have been re-reflected and diffracted. This technique works for these
models because the micro-facets where randomly oriented which meant that the
diffuse light would also be randomly oriented. In fact the diffuse light term used
by Cook can actually be analytically calculated by remembering how much energy,
radiant flux, was discarded by the geometric attenuation calculation. A lambertian
BRDF could then be created whose volume is equal to the amount of remembered
energy. This BRDF can be added to the BRDF calculated by the Cook algorithm
resulting in a correct BRDF.
The BRDF described here cannot use the previous diffuse light calculation,
because the distribution of the micro-facets do not necessarily result in a diffuse
Lambertian BRDF. The only alternative is to actually model the two elements
which contribute to the diffuse component of the BRDF. Diffraction and multiple
reflections can be modeled with a ray tracing approach to building the BRDF table.
Ray tracing can model light surface interactions since the frequency of light is so
much higher than that of the micro-surface. Ray tracing techniques developed
for high frequency electromagnetic physics can be readily applied here.[36] These
techniques could take into account diffraction from the edges of the micro-facets.
Normal ray tracing techniques could be used to model the multiple reflections. The
CHAPTER 5. RESULTS
62
obvious problem with this approach is computational expense. However, it merits
further exploration.
Further research into actual surface properties needs to be done to verify how
the methods described here compare with measured BRDFs taken from reflections
off of particular surfaces. The National Bureau of Standards has done much work
in this area and could be tapped as a source of information for future research.
Both the surface and BRDF measurements exist for a wide variety of surfaces. In
fact the rendering methods described in this paper could use the actual measured
BRDFs instead of computed BRDFs.
The application of the electromagnetic physics, photometry, color met ry, and
light engineering represent a great wealth of scientific knowledge which can be ex­
ploited by the computer graphcis research community. In turn computer graph­
ics techniques could have a profound impact on light design and electromagnetic
physics because computer graphics allows the scientist and engineer to visualize the
interaction of light and matter.
