Displaying in space
The alternative to simulating three dimensions on a two-dimensional surface is actually to create the displayed image within a
three-dimensional space. Multiplanar displays create an x-y-z addressable display
volume by physically creating multiple
planes. They have the advantages of not
requiring binocular or sequential presentation and of not requiring the viewer to
fuse virtual images. Ideally, multiplanar
displays supply parallax and motion parallax cues in real time.
Several of these displays have been developed. The most recent commercial effort, the SpaceGraph ™ 3D Display System, uses a varifocal mirror - a vibrating
flexible mirror - to provide the z-axis displacement. This system creates a maximum display volume 25 em on a side with
4096 x 4096 addressability in the x and y
directions and a 30-Hz refresh rate. The
display requires directional viewing of a
CRT display that is reflected onto the mirror surface.!
Writing on a disc
The SpaceGraph ™ is still available from
the BBN COrp.3 We thought we could improve upon two basic limitations of that
technology: the viewing-angle limitations
enforced by the placement of the CRT
and the limited volume in which the system produces its display. We began experiments to examine various mechanical approaches for displacement in the z
dimension. The resulting proof-of-concept
display successfully demonstrates a simple
yet flexible multiplanar 3D technology."
In this approach, the display surface
consists of a disc that is mounted on a
motor shaft at an angle. As the angled disc
rotates, it fills the display volume, as does
any image that is appropriately projected
onto it. The angle of the disc to the motor
can be selected to provide the optimum
display volume for a particular application
[Fig. 1].
The display system comprises a laser
beam that is modulated between 100 and
10,000 Hz and synchronized with the zdimension displacement of the rotating
disc. The beam is scanned in x and y, and
the synchronization of the modulation
with the rotating disc provides the z
dimension. The display can be as large as
the application requires, with as many
laser sources as needed to generate the
display elements in real time.
The lasers can address the display surface from either side (it's translucent) with
a typical resolution of 500 x 500 pixels for
each scanning system. Three lasers of
different colors can provide a three-color
image or they can be mixed to provide a
full-color palate. This is accomplished by
scanning the lasers independently and
overlapping the beams on the display
surface.
What the system can do
Rotating-disc displays can be large. A 36in-diameter version has been built, and
we see nothing that would prevent scaling
the technology up to a lO-ft. diameter.
The excursion in the z dimension depends
upon the angle of the disc to the motor
shaft, so cylinders of differing height-todiameter ratios can be created. The display can be viewed from all angles simultaneously, so it is suitable for a large
number of viewers.
The display's resolution can be changed
to any desired value by redirecting the
scanning system. The use of overlapping
scanning systems can effectively multiply
the resolution of an area. Areas of the display that require more resolution can be
dynamically addressed by the laser scanners to provide the needed detail. The
varifocal vibrating-mirror approach limited
the refresh rate to about 30 Hz because of
the mirror's mass, but the rotating plate
has much higher limits.

A natural application is the control and
management of the air traffic in a volume
of airspace [Fig. 2]. Multiple operators at
various levels could view the real-time
position of the aircraft. Altitude and protected airspace could be eolor coded. The
controller could use a laser pointing
device to select an aircraft of interest and
request identification. A somewhat related
application would be a combat pilot's situation display [Fig. 3].
Representations of molecular or submolecular structures could be displayed, as
could complex interactive fluid dynamics
models (see artist's rendering on the cover
of this issue). Other computationally
intensive problems, such as finite-element
analysis, could benefit from volumetric
displays.
In fact, most tasks that require a supercomputer to calculate data could use a 3D
volume display to view the results. Among
these are weather analysis and training, in
which 3D patterns and interactions of
wind, clouds, pressure systems, and force
vectors could be animated. In medicine,
the diagnostic viewing of sonograms,
x-rays, CAT scans, and nuclear magnetic
resonance imagery could be greatly
enhanced [Fig. 4].
Remote manipulation of objects in a
volume, such as the docking of the space
shuttle to the space station, could be
facilitated by such a display, as could the
interpretation of complex data. And we
don't think we've even begun to imagine
all the possibilities. We're open to
suggestions.
