Vector Ordering Derived from Binary Partition Tree
Leaf Reordering for Mathematical Morphology
Olivier Lézoray
Université Caen Normandie, ENSICAEN, CNRS, Normandie Univ, GREYC UMR 6072, 14000 Caen, France
Abstract—We introduce a new vector-ordering method for
multivariate mathematical morphology based on Binary Partition
Tree (BPT) leaf reordering. The proposed framework formulates
the construction of an h-ordering as an optimization problem
over all leaf permutations compatible with the hierarchical
structure of the BPT. We investigate several reordering criteria
and propose UltraMixed, a new objective combining ultrametric
tree information with feature-space similarities. The resulting
orderings are evaluated through palette smoothness and a novel
index irregularity measure reflecting neighborhood preservation.
Experiments on the CIFAR dataset show that UltraMixed consistently provides the best trade-off between smoothness and
regularity, yielding more coherent morphological operations than
existing BPT-based orderings. The approach is generic and
remains applicable to high-dimensional feature representations.
Index Terms—Mathematical Morphology, Complete Lattice,
Binary Partition Tree, Leaf Reordering.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Mathematical morphology (MM) is a nonlinear framework
for image analysis that plays a central role in tasks such as
filtering, segmentation, and classification [1]. The formulation
of morphological operators is based on the theory of complete
lattices [2], which requires an ordering to be defined among the
elements being processed. A complete lattice (T , ≤) is a nonempty set of vectors equipped with a total order, guaranteeing
that every non-empty subset P ⊂ T admits both a lower
bound ∧P and an upper bound ∨P. In this setting, an image
is modeled as a function that maps its spatial domain Ω
into the lattice T . Although MM is well established for
grayscale images, no generally accepted extension exists for
vector-valued images, as vectors do not possess an inherent
total ordering [3]. To address this well-known issue, several
approaches have been proposed, particularly for color images
[4]. As they rely on variations of the lexicographic total order,
they cannot be used for higher dimensions. More recent work
has also extended MM to structured non-Euclidean data, such
as directional data [5], further illustrating the need for ordering
strategies beyond the standard vector case. Therefore, the most
widely used proposed approaches define an ordering relation
between the vectors of a set T with the use of h-orderings
[6], [7]. This corresponds to defining a surjective transform
h from T to L where L is a complete lattice equipped with
the conditional total ordering. Supervised [8] and unsupervised
[9] orderings have been proposed within this formalism with
the use of distances between vectors. Recent approaches have
considered the combination of several (weak) orders using

either Borda [10] or Condorcet rules [11], but require a high
computational cost.
In this paper we propose to build an ordering from a hierarchical clustering performed on an image (a Binary Partition
Tree). This line of research has been explored in [12], where
the ordering induced by the hierarchical BPT construction was
obtained by a simple similarity-based traversal of the leaves.
In contrast, as an efficient permutation of the tree leaves might
provide a more meaningful order, we propose to consider leaf
reordering methods that can be formalized as the minimisation
of a cost function. Unlike the recursive ordering strategy of
[12], which is entirely driven by local decisions on the BPT,
our method explicitly optimizes a global objective function
over all admissible leaf orderings. The paper is organized as
follows. First we recall how MM can be formulated within the
h-ordering framework. Second, after representing an image by
its Binary Partition Tree, we present several approaches that
can be used to reorder its leaves and define an h-ordering.
Third, we discuss how the obtained orders can be evaluated.
Finally we provide some results and conclude.

vectors associated to all the vertices of the graph (with a rowmajor numbering of the image graph vertices). We will use

III. L EAF R EORDERING M ETHODS ON B INARY PARTITION
T REES
The Binary Partition Tree (BPT) follows the principle of
hierarchical agglomerative clustering applied to the set of initial image regions. Let H0 = {π1 , . . . , πm } denote the initial
partition, where each πi is a leaf region of the image (i.e.,
a vertex vi ). A dissimilarity measure, or linkage, is defined
both between individual leaf regions and recursively between
any two clusters formed during the hierarchy construction. The
BPT is then constructed by iteratively generating a sequence
of nested partitions H0 ≻ H1 ≻ · · · ≻ Hm−1 , where
Ht+1 is obtained from Ht by merging exactly one pair of
adjacent regions. Each merge creates an internal node whose
region is the union of its two children, ensuring that every
region in the hierarchy corresponds to a connected subtree.
After m − 1 iterations, the process yields a root region and
a full binary tree whose leaves represent the initial regions
π1 , . . . , πm and whose internal nodes encode progressively
coarser partitions of the image domain. An example of BPT
is presented in Figure 1. Once the BPT has been constructed
(with a complexity of O(m log m) with the efficient data
structures of the Higra software [15]), the linear ordering of the
leaves in the dendrogram (from left to right) gives a sorted permutation of the image vertices and this can be used to define
a complete lattice, as proposed in [12]. However, in the BPT,
each merging step implies an ordering choice of its leaves,
giving 2m − 1 different possible permutations (or equivalently
complete lattices). Several methods exist to reorder the leaves
of a dendrogram while respecting the hierarchical structure.
[16], [17], [18] These methods are typically defined by a cost
function that the reordering seeks to minimize. We propose
to consider such leaf reorderings to define complete lattices
from BPTs. Let us introduce different possible leaf reordering
choices.

Once the reordering of the leaves has been obtained, this
defines the h-ordering and the image f can be represented
by (I, P), its index and palette. The palette being an ordered
set, it corresponds to a Hamiltonian path on the set T . It is
plausible to expect that the obtained permutation has a small
Total Variation (TV) and yields more regular morphological
operators. The smoothness of the ordered set P is expressed
by the Total Variation of its elements [10]:

computationally demanding, even if some nearest neighbors
heuristics have been considered for MM [10]. In addition, TSP
solutions are Hamiltonian cycles and to transform it into a
path, the starting element has to be defined. This shows the
interest of using the reordering of the BPT leaves to construct
the complete lattice.
However, the topology induced by a total order on a multidimensional space cannot fully reproduce the natural topology
of the vector space. This is known as the irregularity issue in
MM [20], [21]. Because the irregularity issue follows from a
disparity between the partial order and the vector space metric,
an ordering scheme that preserves the spatial neighborhoods
as far as possible should result in fewer irregularities. This is
the motivation of considering leaf reordering from BPTs, as
the ordering depends on the tree structure that is constrained
by the spatial pixel neighborhoods. In addition, having the
permutation of the smallest TV does not necessarily solve the
irregularity issue [22].
A measure of the irregularity has been proposed in [21] but
it operates on the results of morphological operators and is
very costly to compute. In contrast, we propose to measure
the spatial neighborhood preservation on the index image I.
As pointed out in [21], the sum of pixel-wise distances on
the original image f is not an appropriate measure for the
irregularity. However, the index image directly reflects the
spatial organisation of the pixels with respect to the complete
lattice. Our proposed irregularity index is therefore defined as
the TV of the index image gradient:

leaf reordering, as in [22], [11], [21], we consider the training
set of the CIFAR dataset. It consists of 30000 color images of
32×32 size. For each image, f we compute its BPT with average linkage, and after reordering its leaves, we obtain its index
and palette (I, P). Then, we compute the palette smoothness
∥P∥T V and index irregularity ∥∇I∥T V . Figure 2 shows such
values for the image of Figure 1. We consider the order
induced by the BPT, (corresponds to a DFS of the tree [12])
and after its leaves reordering (with OLO, UltraMixed, and
GPL). We also include the classical Lexicographic ordering.
As can be seen, the lexicographic ordering gives the smoothest
order, but also one of the most irregular, which confirms
the findings of [22]. But our proposed irregularity index can
measure this directly without computing any morphological
operator, in contrast to [21]. Our UltraMixed leaf reordering
leads to the lowest irregularity while being still smooth. This
is confirmed when studied on the whole CIFAR training
dataset, as shown in Figure 4. The UltraMixed reordering is the
smoothest (among leaf reordering methods) and least irregular
order. For the smoothness, we do not show the values of the

To assess the sensitivity of UltraMixed to the trade-off
parameter λ, we varied it from 0.1 to 0.9 with a step size of
0.05 on the image of Figure 1 and measured the resulting path
length ∥P∥T V ) and index irregularity ∥∇I∥T V , as reported in
Figure 3. Both quantities remain within a narrow range across
the whole interval (path length: [0.758, 0.827]; index variation:
[0.070, 0.083]), indicating that the method is reasonably robust
to the choice of λ. We observe a non-monotonic pattern.
The value λ = 0.75 used throughout this paper, while not
strictly optimal for this single image, lies close to the bestperforming region and was retained for its stability across
the preliminary experiments we conducted. This plateau-like,
non-monotonic pattern follows from the discrete nature of the
underlying optimization: at each tree node, the two candidate
child orderings share the same ultrametric term h(LCA), so
the choice between them is governed solely by λ-weighted
feature distances. As λ increases, this local choice remains
unchanged until a competing ordering becomes equally attractive, at which point the selected ordering switches abruptly,
producing plateaus separated by sharp transitions rather than
a smooth drift.

exhibit less irregularity, confirming its potential as a vector
ordering approach. Overall it performs better than previously
proposed morphological processing based on BPT [12]. In
addition, it can be easily used with high dimensional vectors.

VI. C ONCLUSION
We demonstrated that BPT leaf reordering is an effective
way to build vector orderings suited for multivariate morphology. Among the tested methods, our proposed UltraMixed
leaf reordering achieves the best balance between smoothness
and low irregularity, producing more consistent morphological
results. Unlike [12], which orders BPT leaves through a
recursive, locally-driven traversal, our formulation casts leaf
reordering as an optimization problem over all admissible
permutations consistent with the tree structure, enabling UltraMixed to explicitly balance hierarchical consistency with feature similarity through λ. Future work will focus on improving
the scalability of the approach, as leaf reordering methods are
computationally demanding for large images.
