SECTION I
INTRODUCTION
Data and information are not equivalent. More to the point, data tends to
grow much faster than the information that it represents. (Witness the
Congressional Record.) Data compression comprises a collection of methods that
have been devised to reduce this discrepancy in a number of differing applica­
tions. Host data files can in fact be compressed, to a greater or lesser
degree, with only negligible loss of information by taking advantage of the
structure and redundancy of their contents. The term itself, "data compres­
sion" is a misnomer; better to have called it ^information compression via
data reduction." The picture that comes to mind is that of a gas being
compressed by a piston. One speaks of the gas as being compressed via r;duction of the volume in which it is contained. Similarly one should speak of
information being compressed via reduction of the data that expresses it.
However, the term "data reduction" already possesses a definite meaning that
encompasses only a part of what we want to imply. Hence the compromise term:
data compression.
By either rigorous definition or heuristic understanding, information
depends on the effect that data has on a receiver or observer, and this in
turn depends on its prior state. A trivial illustration: Almost everyone, if
presented with the last four digits of his neighbor's phone number, could
complete the number by supplying the first three. Those digits contain
practically no information for that person, they may contain significant
information for another, who might even need the area code to complete a
call. Thus, while it is possible to speak of data per se, it is not possible
to speak of its information content without imbedding the whole issue into a
larger context. Two inferences can be drawn from this simple observation.
First, it is impossible to assess a data compression method without paying
attention to the data's characteristics and ultimate usage. Second, a com­
plete data compression strategy will require several adaptive iterations after
its implementation, as experience is gained with the actual signals.
1-1
/
The human interactions that will be involved in this adaptive iteration
deserve careful attention. The experimentalists, who after all have ultimate
responsibility for their data, should be the ones who have the final word on
how it is to be compressed and on what manner of distortion would be accepta­
ble as a trade-off for the increased information density. Only they have the
authority to decide these questions and, ultimately, to rewrite files. They
•, must therefore play a major role in designing the final system, but this
should be done in such a way that they can spend their time studying the
effects of compression algorithms c.i data of interest, not working out the
computer implementation of those algorithms.
In short, it would be convenient to have a library of data compression
routines to draw from. Apart form their effectiveness, these routines should
be easy to understand, simple to use, and fast to operate. They constitute
the necessary first step, the nucleus around which a data compression system
can crystallize. The main thrust of work on this contract was directed toward
constructing at least the beginnings of such a library. The rationale behind
this choice of action was the conviction that the MFTF data compression system
will go through a number of developmental stages before attaining its final
version, that decisions made at each stage will be based on experience gained
from compressing actual data, and that at this point it would be better to
have a few concrete programs ready for immediate use than a comprehensive
theoretical analysis of some postulated system.
This report is organized as follows. Section 2 is devoted to discussions
of three of the most important data compression methods that have been devel­
oped and studied over the years: coding, transforms, and redundancy reduc­
tion. (A brief discussion of how to combine and synthesize these ideas, and
others, into a system is given in Section 2.4). Specific ideas for compres­
sing MFTF diagnostics and control data are developed in Section 3. Listings
and instructions for using FORTRAN programs th'it were compiled on the Livermore MrTF computers during the course of the study are collected together in
Section 4.

METHODS OF DATA COMPRESSION
The study of data compression canie into being during the 1960's in re­
sponse to the problem of maximizing the rate at which information could be
transmitted across communication channels. The problem for HFTF data base
management ~ reduction of the computer memory required to store a given
amount of information — is somewhat different, but the same techniques should
prove to be useful. A goodly number of these have been introduced and, in
varying degrees, developed over the years so that there now exists a fairly
diversified repertoire from which to choose.
Devising objective measures by which to gauge the performance of data
compression methods is difficult, primarily because information is in the eye
of the beholder: e.g., a file of sinusoidal data can be reduced to three
numbers — amplitude, frequency, and zero-phase -- jf one knows a priori its
trigonometric character; a file of pure, white noise data can be characterized
by only two numbers -- tne data's average value and standard deviation — jf
one knows that it arose from noise. There is, accordingly, no complete,
objective theory of data compression, as yet.* Instead, what exists is a
collection of ideas and algorithms that have been used in a number of contexts
with varying, but respectable, degrees of success.

means unique — categories is shown in Figure 2-1. In this report, we will

2.2 TRANSFORMS: GLOBAL ERROR CONTROL
He have presented Huffman coding methods as a way of compressing data in a
noiseless and error free environment with no information loss. Suppose now
that one is willing to tolerate "small" errors and distortions in order to
increase the compression ratio beyond the bound imposed by the coding theorem,
that methods are available? One would, of course, want to control the errors
incurred to remain within some prespecified tolerance. There are two ways to
proceed: global control — specifying a limit on a measure of the total error
« or local control — specifying bounds for individual errors. The arche­
typal forms for accomplishing locally controlled compression are the redunaancy reduction routines that will be discussed in Sec. 2.3. In this section we
will direct our attention to global control methods, as typified by the taking
of transforms.
All physicists are intimately familiar with the use of Fourier transforms
in spectral analysis: the theory of spectral decomposition permeates practi­
cally every nook and cranny of the curriculum. Nothing need be said here
about its usefulness. There are, however, other transforms of lesser, but
rapidly gaining, fame: Walsh, Hilbert, Haar, Hotelling, and so forth. We
shall discuss the application of some of these, notably the Walsh transform,
to data compression.
If we think of a discrete (sampled) signal as a function mapping a finite
set of integers into the real axis (i.e., a sequence), then a general trans­
form is a functional, a mapping of functions onto functions. Generally, the
only class of transforms that have been extensively studied and used comprise
the linear transforms, in which the transform of the sum of two signals is
equal to the sum of their transforms.

There are other transforms, e.g., the Haar transform, and Slant trans­
forms. (The latter were specifically designed to compress the highly corre­
lated information present in the transmission of televideo signals.) we shall
not treat them in detail: they tend to be more computationally involved than
the Walsh transform, and their compression performance would most likely be
comparable to it for the kinds of signals of interest here. The interested
reader is referred to material cited in the bibliography for information
on these other methods. Because of its special nature, we shall make one
exception and briefly discuss the use of the Karhunen-Loeve/Hotelling trans­
form.
Is there an optimum transform? The answer depends both on one's defini­
tion of optimality and on the data to which the transform will be applied:
e.g., a Fourier transform is clearly best when applied to data that is in
actuality a superposition of a few sinusoidal functions. On the other hand,
the Walsh transform is clearly optimal from the standpoint of speed. Its
practicality and popularity is due both to that and to the fact that it is
near optimal in compression performance for a large class of signals that
arise in practical applications.
The Karhunen-Loeve transformation arose from an attempt to find the
(compression performance) optimum linear transformation for a given, finite,
fixed ensemble of signals, or other numerical data. Its motivation is easily
understood by returning to the geometric picture that was touched upon earli­
er. Each signal is treated as a vector in a high dimensional space; the
entire ensemble becomes a reasonably densely populated "cloud" of such
vectors. As a first approximation, one can replace the ensemble with its
average value. The ensemble is now replaced by a new, "difference ensemble"
by subtracting this average from each vector in the original. The average
combined with this new ensemble contains all the information of the original
set, but we have expanded rather than compressed the data to be stored. To do
the compression, the difference vectors are projected into a subspace, reduc­
ing their dimensionality. This action, compression by projection, is essen­
tially the same as what was done with the Walsh transforms; clipping the
2-30
transformed signals amounted to zeroing a subset of their components. That is
really what a projection does. The difference is that here we shall search
for the optimum sub-spaces in which 'to project, rather than being confined to
the fixed subspaces provided by the Walsh basis vectors.

2.3 REDUNDANCY REDUCTION: LOCAL CONTROL
Of the various categories of available data compression methods, redundan­
cy reduction — as typified by interpolation and prediction Tut'ines — is by
far the conceptually simplest. Unlike the clipping of transformed data,
redundancy reduction seeks to control errors locally. That is, after data
compression is accomplished, it should be possible to reproduce the original
signal so that it is nowhere in error by more than a prespecified tolerance.
Redundancy reduction refers to a class of algorithms that eliminate
"redundant" data samples based on a comparison with neighboring samples and/or
a set of reference patterns. An example of the linear interpolator will
serve to illustrate this particular class of methods. Consider the set of
data shown in Figure 2-15. Each sample is imbedded in a fiducial interval,
and the criterioii is established that after compression the original signal
should be reproducible to within the given error. The procedure is to inter­
polate between the data points with a simple piecewise linear function that
passes through all the intervals. To store the interpolation, it is necessary
to store only the endpJnts of each linear segment: in the example, only ten
numbers need be stored -- five to record sample numbers (abscissas ) and five
to record their values (ordinates). The samples that are retained are called
the "knots" of the interpolation.
Figure 2-16 illustrates the application of redundancy reduction to the
first of the simulated signals drawn in Figure 2-9. Results are shown for
absolute error tolerances of 0.02 and 0.06; 65 and 39 points, respectively,
were retained — corresponding to compression ratios of 256/130 = 1.97 and
256/78 = 3,28. (In computing compression ratios, we must store double the
number of data points retained, since both x and y coordinates must now be
saved.) The main important features of the data were retained at the lower
error tolerance, but they were lost, in the middle region, at the higher.

Spatial transforms will undoubtedly prove useful in the storage of
photon or neutron imaging data. Although these methods are straightforward in
themselves, it ii important to use them with an open mind. Ue have already
stressed in Sec. 2.2, the utility of the Walsh transform , which is somewhat
less familiar than the Fourier transform to the physics community. There
is no reason to think in terms of using one or the other exclusively: both
may be employed p ofitably in different applications. Indeed, they even can
be combined in transforming a single image, a Walsh transform being performed
in one dimension and a Fourier transform in another. (We are not necessarily
advocating that such a thing be done but merely pointing out that biases that
restrict possibilities should be avoided.)
Because transforms take advantage of correlations between individual
signals, they can be used to compress data from sets of diagnostics in which
such correlations are to be expected. (The signals from the neutral beam
attenuation sensors form a natural set of candidates.) They can also be
applied to sets of similar signals of the same type collected from a number of
data shots. The Karhunen - Loeve transform may be especially useful for
archival storage of image data by transforming across the "ensemble" di­
rection. This potentially powerful method is similar to the Walsh transform
of correlated signals that was done in Section 2.2. Its discussion in the
present context is postponed until Section 3, where diagnostics will be
addressed individually.
Other ideas will present themselves to the user as he becomes more fa­
miliar with the processes involved and with his data. For example, seg­
mentation of the sampling period will be a very natural trick. Accuracy
requirements may be vastly different within different intervals of the total
2-47
sampling period. In such cases, it would be advantageous to employ different
compression methods in the different subdowains, e.g., a first order interpo­
lator with fairly large error tolerance near the beginning and end of a
shot, and Huffman codes for the data from the middle of the shot.
Along these same lines, the program for first order interpolation can be
modified reasonably easily so that the fiducial error is not assigned an
absolute, global value but can depend on the value of the signal itself.
This would allow a user to set a percent error criterion should he wish to
do so.
Multiple level, or sequential compression will also prove to be useful.
It frequently happens, for example, that Huffman coding produces a coded
signal containing long strings of a single code word, or even a single charac­
ter, repeated a large number of times. The sensible thing to do would be to
recode the signal, using run length coding to indicate these repeat strings,
thus producing a further compression of the data. (The discussion in Sec. l . l
on how to store the information in a clipped Walsh transform is another
example of this sort of two-step compression.)
Bit-plane encoding is a possible technique for compressing certain classes
of highly autocorrelated signals. The basic idea here is to treat each bit of
the digital signal separately. Advantages accrue by taking advantage of the
greater correlation that exists among the higher order bits than among the
lower order ones. (That is, higher order bits generally tend to change much
less frequently.) Each bit level in the strean. of data is then separately
encoded, using run-length coding, for example, to compress the string of high
order bits. Although this approach seems intuitively appealing, achievable
compression ratios tend to be small for most practical signals. Consider:
compressing the data by only 50% would require completely deleting half of the
bit streams. Further, it seems likely that other, more straightforward
methods would hork better in almost all cases.
2-48
Finally, we mention in passing that methods other than simply clipping
(i.e., projection) can be employed to compress transformed data. For example,
it may happen that the transformed data be smoother than the original data and
thus amenable to compression via redundancy reduction or Huffman coding.
There is no guarantee about this, but it certainly wuuld be worth investigat­
ing in individual cases and could lead to simultaneously increasing Jjoth the
compression ratio and the information retained.

Neutral Beam Attenuation
Attenuation of the neutral deuterium beam will be measured directly by
forty-one detectors arranged in the shape of a cross: ten in each arm and one
in the center. Unless the typical coherence length for plasma fluctuations
is smaller than the spacing between these sensors, we can expect that their
signals will be significantly correlated. They will all tend to rise and fall
more or less in concert. This will make the neutral beam attenuation data
also a natural candidate for compression by the transform methods discussed
in Section 2.2. It will be useful to scale the signals before transforming so
that each individually represents the same amount of signal energy. Both the
Walsh and Fourier transforms should be experimented with to see if they
produce comparable compression. If so, the Walsh is to be preferred because
of its faster speed of execution. If speed is not a consideration, then the
Karhunen-Loeve idea should also be attempted to see if it significantly
increases the compression ratio. Most likely, this will not be the case;
that is, any improvement probably will be only marginal.

Background Gas Pressure
The background gas pressure is more a control variable than a diagnostic

of a relatively stable vacuum environtaent. The first order interpolator
should provide a highly satisfactory compression of these data.

four samples spaced 0.1 msec apart, are shown in Figure 3-12. Corresponding
time histories of the total scattered flux and average energy are illustrated

All of tnese curves represent data derived from other, more fundamental
signals; energy distributions are obtained by cutting through this family at
specified time instants; the flux, by evaluating the total area under the
distributions; and the average energy, by evaluating their first moments.
Because the number of required computations is small, such data should be
generated upon request rather than stored separately. Alternatively, as the
sequence of shots continues to unfold and the data from any one shot becomes
"older", instead of purging it all at once to make room for more recent data,
it might be useful to establish a degradation schedule whereby data can be
sequentially processed and restored in increasingly reduced forms before
finally being dropped. Each processing step would reduce the available
information, but it might be possible in this way to retain at least some
information on old shots beyond the time when it otherwise would be gone.

CONCLUDING REMARKS
We have compiled (in both senses of the word) an initial library of
programs necessary for the compression of MFTF diagnostics and control data.
These programs have been loaded and run on the MFTF computer itself. Several
of them, notably the first order interpolator and the Huffman code routines,
were applied to data from a single 2XIIB shot, supplied by L.L.L. personnel.
Extremely large compression ratios were achieved with jio distortion, primarily
due to the enormous redundancy in most of the data on the file.
We have also sketched out how one should view these programs in the
larger context of an overall data compression system and postulated a nun^ar
of stages through which it must pass before achieving its final form. Host
importantly, emphasis has been placed on the need to experiment with various
methods and ideas, to try using them in different combinations, and in general
to tailor the technology to suit the data's characteristics and one's own
needs.
The guiding rationale behind the direction taken in the work reported here
was that a complete MFTF data compression system will not come into being full
blown but rather evolve from a nucleus of a few simple programs. That first
step now has been completed; the next will be to gain experience with thes^
programs by using them to compress numerical data files from previous experi­
ments at Livermore. By the time MFTF data is available, tht analysts should
have a good empirical understanding of the programs' characteristics, of how
to use them most effectively. Undoubtedly, new ideas will develop in the
course of this practice; new programs should be incorporated into the library
as they come into being.
Before irreversible changes are made in existing data files, the supervi­
sory level u." the data compression system must be carefully programmed. It
must be capable of recreating data in its original form from the compressed
5-1
files to within error tolercxes consistent with the compression method.
For each diagnostic, those errors and distortions that will be considered
tolerable, as well as intolerable, must be documented. This will take some
doing. The decisions will have to be made by physicists responsible for the
experiment, but they will need prompting by computer services personnel on the
types of distortions that can be expected.
Finally, some protocol has to be established for performing the actual
compression. Final der isions regarding which programs should be used on
individual sets of data must be carefully documented before implementation;
further, quality control procedures should be instituted whereby the system's
actions on selected subsets of data are periodically monitored and evaluated.
Once the components of the compression system have been compiled, however,
these actions will be the responsibility of the experimentalists, not computer
services.
