Abstract. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) represents scenes using Gaussian primitives and enables real-time novel view synthesis. Adaptive Density Control (ADC), a key part of the pipeline, governs when to densify these primitives to balance reconstruction quality and efficiency. In
the original 3DGS pipeline, densification is triggered by a thresholded
positional-gradient criterion. However, this criterion frequently selects
already well-covered regions, leading to redundant primitives and providing weak control over the balance between reconstruction quality and
compactness (i.e., fidelity versus primitive count). In CAFe-GS, we propose a new densification criterion based on a per-Gaussian score obtained
by mapping per-pixel rendering errors back to the contributing primitives, using their effective-opacity under front-to-back alpha compositing as weights. The score is then modulated by frequency guidance de-

Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [10] has recently set a new standard for real-time
novel view synthesis, achieving photorealistic reconstructions at interactive frame
rates from multi-view images. It represents scenes as collections of anisotropic
3D Gaussians rather than meshes or voxels. Each primitive encodes position,
shape, opacity, and view-dependent color, and is projected onto the image plane
through efficient alpha compositing. This continuous and differentiable representation enables direct optimization of scene appearance, producing high-quality
reconstructions with low latency. Owing to its balance between fidelity and performance, 3DGS has rapidly become a widely adopted baseline for real-time
scene capture and rendering.
A crucial mechanism in this pipeline is Adaptive Density Control (ADC),
which governs how the set of Gaussian kernels evolves during training. At regular intervals, Gaussians with high average positional gradient magnitudes are
densified via cloning or splitting. This strategy assumes that large gradients indicate poor reconstruction, either due to gaps in coverage (under-reconstruction)
or overly broad primitives (over-reconstruction). In both cases, duplication increases the model’s representational capacity and improves scene coverage.
However, this gradient-based approach often sacrifices compactness. Gaussians may be redundantly duplicated in already well-reconstructed regions, since
positional gradients remain high near edges, occlusions, or view inconsistencies.
As a result, the model can grow unnecessarily, inflating memory, storage, and rendering costs. This unbounded expansion limits scalability, especially for larger
scenes or higher resolutions. Moreover, the positional-gradient threshold lacks
perceptual interpretability, making it difficult for users to control the trade-off
between reconstruction quality and model size.
CAFe-GS reorients the ADC mechanism toward compactness as a primary
goal, aiming to reduce the number of primitives while preserving image quality. In doing so, the resulting representations become easier to store, transmit,
and render. We expose explicit control over the quality–compactness trade-off
through a single, interpretable threshold τ : lower values favor compact models,
while higher values yield finer reconstructions. Instead of relying on positional
gradients, CAFe-GS introduces a densification criterion based on a per-Gaussian

score derived from reconstruction error and modulated by local frequency cues.
This directs new capacity toward detail-rich, underfitted regions while suppressing unnecessary duplication elsewhere. Extensive evaluations across real-world

– We replace the positional-gradient criterion in ADC with a per-Gaussian error score Eg , computed by redistributing pixel-wise distortion using effective

– We introduce frequency guidance based on Laplacian-of-Gaussian responses

Scene optimization follows an analysis-by-synthesis loop. Given calibrated
input views, structure-from-motion initializes camera parameters and a sparse
set of Gaussians. Primitives are projected and rasterized using differentiable
splatting, and all parameters {µg , Rg , sg , αg , agk } are optimized to minimize a
photometric distortion loss between rendered and ground-truth images.
Since the initial set is typically too sparse to adequately cover the scene, additional primitives are introduced during training via Adaptive Density Control
(ADC). Every T iterations (T =100 over 15,000 iterations in our experiments),
candidate Gaussians are evaluated for duplication. A primitive is selected for
densification if its average positional-gradient magnitude exceeds a predefined
threshold. If the Gaussian is small in all dimensions (i.e., all components of
sg fall below a scale threshold), it is cloned: its attributes are copied and the
child is shifted along the dominant axis. Otherwise, it is split: the attributes
are duplicated, the scale is halved along all axes, and the child is slightly displaced. This periodic duplication compensates for the sparse initialization and
progressively refines scene coverage. Optimization typically proceeds for 30,000
iterations, with ADC active for the first 15,000.
2.2
3DGS Densification
Several approaches have been proposed to improve the densification process in
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), aiming to reduce model size while preserving
reconstruction fidelity.
Deng et al. [4] introduce an enhanced densification pipeline that uses an EdgeAware Score (EAS) for candidate selection and a deterministic Long-Axis Split
(LAS), which aligns child Gaussians along the parent’s dominant axis while adjusting opacity to minimize geometric artifacts. Their method also incorporates
several stabilization techniques, including Recovery-Aware Pruning, Multi-step
Update, and Growth Control.
Geometry-focused strategies include Mini-Splatting [7], which reorganizes
spatial layouts via blur splitting and depth reinitialization, using dense midpointbased depth maps to mitigate blur artifacts and improve coverage. Taming
3DGS [13] introduces a budget-constrained optimization framework with a constructive, score-based densification schedule that enables fine-grained control
over model size and computational cost.
Color-aware approaches also enhance densification decisions. Color-Cued Efficient Densification [11] uses gradients of the zeroth SH coefficient to capture local
color variation, augmenting positional gradients to improve perceptual quality
and reduce redundancy.

Other recent methods target both optimization and density control. BoGausS [15] combines precision-aware updates with an unbiased Sparse Adam
variant and introduces rate–distortion–inspired splitting and pruning strategies.
Bulò et al. [2] propose a per-Gaussian error metric derived from pixel distortions and opacity-weighted contributions, offering a more perceptually aligned

2.3
Pruning and Compaction Strategies for 3DGS
To mitigate the substantial memory and storage demands of 3DGS models, a
significant body of research has focused on pruning redundant Gaussians to
produce more compact scene representations.
LightGaussian [6] computes a global importance score for each Gaussian and
prunes those deemed less relevant, often combining this with vector quantization
and knowledge distillation to achieve compression. PUP 3D-GS (Principled Uncertainty Pruning) [8] introduces a mathematically principled pruning strategy
using sensitivity scores derived from a second-order approximation of the reconstruction error via the Fisher Information matrix, effectively preserving fidelity
and salient details at high compression ratios.
Reduced3DGS [14] proposes a resolution-aware pruning approach based on
spatial redundancy observed during optimization. The method further reduces
memory by selectively deactivating unused spherical harmonic bands and compressing Gaussian attributes via codebook-based quantization. TrimGS [5] introduces a trimming mechanism that evaluates Gaussian contributions using
alpha-blending weights (rather than raw opacity) and selectively removes inaccurate geometry. It promotes for small-scale Gaussians to retain fine details and
enforces normal consistency through regularization.
Other approaches combine pruning with densification-aware strategies. MiniSplatting [7], during its simplification phase, uses intersection preserving to
retain Gaussians that maximally affect the rendered image and importanceweighted sampling to maintain geometric fidelity. LP-3DGS (Learning to Prune
3DGS) [18] adopts a learning-based approach, applying a trainable mask via
a differentiable Gumbel-Sigmoid function to the importance scores. This allows
the model to learn an optimal pruning ratio per scene in a single training session,
avoiding manual threshold tuning.
However, pruning methods are inherently limited by their reliance on postoptimization redundancy detection: they can only remove excess capacity after
training, often at the cost of reconstruction accuracy in complex or fine-grained
regions. Unlike adaptive densification, they do not dynamically allocate new
primitives where needed, which restricts their ability to balance compactness
and fidelity during optimization.

Method
3.1
Overview
In the original 3DGS pipeline [10], Adaptive Density Control (ADC) periodically
duplicates Gaussians during training to compensate for the sparse initialization.
Candidate primitives are evaluated every T iterations (T =100 across 15,000
iterations in our experiments), using the average magnitude of the positional
gradient as a selection criterion. Primitives with gradient magnitudes exceeding
a threshold are selected for densification and either cloned or split based on their
spatial scale.
However, the gradient criterion often densifies already well-reconstructed areas, leading to redundant primitives and a bloated model size. To address this
limitation, CAFe-GS replaces the gradient trigger with a per-Gaussian error
score modulated by local frequency guidance. As illustrated in Fig. 2, this strategy prioritizes Gaussians that contribute most to the reconstruction error and
steers densification toward detail-rich, underfit regions.

3.3
Frequency Guidance
Error-only selection Eg can misallocate Gaussian primitives. For instance, lowfrequency color or exposure mismatches may cause nonzero distortion dv (u)
across smooth regions, leading large-footprint Gaussians to accumulate error
and be densified, even when they already provide sufficient coverage. Similarly,
persistent edge gradients, slight misregistrations, or occlusions can elevate dv (u)
near well-modeled structures, encouraging redundant duplication.
To address these challenging situations, we incorporate local frequency cues
that align densification with spatial detail. High-frequency content often corresponds to thin structures, edges, and view-dependent regions that benefit from
increased capacity, while smooth areas are deprioritized. This behavior is illustrated in Fig. 3, where a Laplacian-of-Gaussian (LoG) response peaks on both
sides of a step edge, indicating where additional capacity should be allocated.

3.4
Densification Criterion
Finally, the positional-gradient criterion is replaced by a per-Gaussian densification score:
Sg = Eg · Mg∆ .
(12)
At each densification step, all Gaussians with Sg ≥ τ are selected. The threshold
τ provides a single, interpretable control over the quality–compactness trade-off:
higher values favor compactness by reducing duplications, while lower values
increase capacity and reconstruction fidelity. The modulation term Mg∆ focuses
new capacity on underfit details and suppresses redundancy in well-modeled
regions.
Densification is performed on the same schedule as the original 3DGS pipeline
(e.g., every 100 iterations), requiring no changes to the training loop.
Clone vs. Split. Let the per-axis scales of g be sg = (sg,x , sg,y , sg,z ). A predefined threshold sth determines whether a primitive is cloned or split:
– Clone: If sg,i ≤ sth for all i ∈ {x, y, z}, the primitive is duplicated by copying
all attributes (µg , Rg , sg , αg , SH). The child’s position is shifted along the
dominant scale axis to preserve coverage while maintaining footprint.
– Split: Otherwise, the primitive is split. Its attributes are duplicated, the scale
is halved along all axes (sg,i ← 12 sg,i ), and the child is slightly displaced to
prevent overlap. Orientation and color parameters remain unchanged.
We use the same threshold sth as in the original 3DGS strategy to ensure consistent behavior across scenes.

4
Experiments
We follow the standard 3DGS evaluation protocol. Thirteen real-world scenes are
drawn from Mip-NeRF 360 [1], Tanks and Temples [12], and Deep Blending [9].
For each scene, every 8th image is held out for testing, with the remainder used
for training. Reconstruction quality is evaluated using PSNR (dB), SSIM, and
LPIPS. Model compactness is measured by the total number of Gaussians, which
we use as the x-axis in all rate–distortion plots.
All experiments are implemented in PyTorch using gsplat v1.5.3 [17] and
trained on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU. We preserve all original 3DGS hyperparameters, including learning rates, optimizer schedules, differentiable rendering
settings, and the photometric loss (L1 + DSSIM). Camera poses and sparse
point clouds are initialized using COLMAP [16], as provided with the datasets.
The only modification lies in the densification criterion. We adopt CAFe-GS’s
frequency-guided per-Gaussian score, using a clone–split threshold of sth = 0.01.
Densification begins after a 500-iteration warm-up and is applied every 100 iterations, ending after 15k iterations. Training continues to 30k iterations in total.
To explore the quality–compactness trade-off, we report results for five threshold
values: τ ∈ {10, 25, 50, 75, 100}.
We first compare against the original 3DGS gradient-based densification
strategy [10] using the same gsplat framework [17], training schedule, and hyperparameters for a controlled evaluation. For a broader context, we also include results reported in prior work, Taming 3DGS [13], BoGausS [15], ColorCued [11], and Reduced-3DGS [14] as summarized in Figure 5 and Table 1.
Quantitative results on Deep Blending are reported in the supplementary material as they exhibit the same behavior.
To ensure transparency, we report results from both the gsplat implementation used in this work and values copied from the original gaussian-splatting
codebase [10]. Because the two pipelines differ in rasterization precision, alpha
compositing order, and loss scaling, their metrics are not numerically aligned.
For instance, on Mip-NeRF 360 [1] the same scene yields 28.93 dB with 4.06 M
Gaussians in gsplat, versus 27.48 dB with 3.33 M in the original codebase. As
re-implementing prior methods within gsplat would risk introducing unverified
discrepancies and unfairly shifting performance, we therefore make all quantitative claims exclusively within the gsplat framework, using the gradient strategy
as the reference baseline.

5
Conclusion
We have proposed a compactness-aware densification strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting [10], replacing the traditional gradient-based criterion with a perGaussian error score modulated by local frequency cues. This approach provides
simple, interpretable control over the quality–compactness trade-off, achieving
comparable PSNR with approximately 2–4× fewer Gaussians, and up to 12–15×
fewer in compactness-focused settings. Frequency guidance improves capacity allocation without altering the rendering pipeline. While its impact diminishes in
low-resolution or low-frequency scenes, the method consistently produces sharper
reconstructions with significantly reduced model size.
