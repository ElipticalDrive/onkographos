Introduction
Realistic rendering of hair and fur has long been a challenging
problem in computer graphics, primarily due to the complex light
transport caused by their fine-scale fiber geometry. In dense hair
and fur, multiple scattering among individual fibers dominates the
overall appearance: it not only significantly increases the perceived
brightness but also produces soft shading variations and subtle color
shifts. This effect is especially pronounced for light-colored hair and
fur. However, accurately simulating such high-order scattering typically requires tracing a huge number of light paths and scattering
events. This is computationally expensive, making physically based
global illumination challenging to apply in practice for production
or interactive applications.
As a result, approximate methods are widely adopted in practice.
Among them, the dual scattering technique has gained popularity
for its simplicity and real-time performance, achieved by imposing strong constraints on scattering paths to reduce computational
cost. However, the assumptions underlying dual scattering [Zinke
et al. 2008] often break down under complex volumetric geometries and lighting conditions. It does not generalize robustly to fur,
which tends to exhibit thicker fiber layers and stronger volumetric
effects. Furthermore, even for hair, it often yields a visually “dry”
appearance, lacking the soft volumetric glow characteristic of multiple scattering. Another class of approaches [Yan et al. 2017b] uses
BSSRDF-like subsurface scattering formulations to approximate
multiple scattering and achieve smoother energy diffusion. However, such methods frequently suffer from excessive blurring and
systematic bias.
In this work, we propose an efficient rendering framework for
multiple scattering in hair and fur. Our key observation is that at the
scale of multiple scattering, the aggregate behavior of dense hair can
be approximated as an anisotropic participating medium. Based on
this insight, we adopt a hybrid geometric–volumetric representation:
explicit fiber geometry is used to accurately compute high-frequency
direct illumination, while an equivalent anisotropic volumetric representation efficiently approximates the low-frequency, soft contributions of multiple scattering. Specifically, we first accumulate perpixel visibility and hair density in screen space using a rasterization
pass. During shading, we perform a screen-space single-scattering
pass with stochastic sampling. In this pass, a scattering point is
sampled along the view ray using delta tracking [Woodcock et al.
1965], and we then march along a single sampled light direction to
evaluate transmittance and direct illumination at that point. Finally,
we re-weight the single-scattering estimate using a modified singleto-multiple scattering formulation to approximate higher-order scattering, while preserving the characteristic spectral behavior and
angular scattering properties of hair. Our results show that the proposed method closely matches the appearance of high-quality path
tracing for both hair and fur, while achieving approximately a 7–10×
speedup over brute-force path tracing. Code and data for this paper
are at https://github.com/NJUCG/efficient-fur-hair-ms-vol-approx.

2 Related Work and Background
2.1 Hair/Fur Models for Single Fibers
Hair and fur fibers share a similar layered structure from outer to
inner regions: an outer cuticle that reflects light, a cortex beneath
it that absorbs light, and a central medulla that scatters light. In
human hair, the medulla is usually very small, so hair models often ignore its influence. Early on, Kajiya and Kay [1989] presented
an empirical hair shading model with two lobes (one diffuse and
one specular), analogous to the Phong model, to approximate fur
appearance, which is overly oily. Marschner et al. [2003] introduced
the first physically-based model for human hair reflectance by treating a hair fiber as a rough dielectric cylinder and decomposing
its scattering into longitudinal and azimuthal components. Their
model identifies three main scattering paths 𝑅, 𝑇𝑇 , and 𝑇 𝑅𝑇 where
𝑅 denotes light that reflects off the surface and 𝑇 denotes light that
transmits into or out of the fiber. Built on top of the Marschner
model, Sadeghi et al. [2010] proposed an artist-friendly hair shading
system that reparameterizes the scattering lobes for intuitive control while preserving plausible appearance. The Marschner model
assumes perfectly smooth fiber cross-sections in the azimuthal direction, which is an idealization and not strictly physically accurate.
To address this, d’Eon et al. [2011] extended the Marschner model
to include azimuthal roughness in the fiber cross-section.
Compared to human hair, animal fur often has a larger medulla
relative to the fiber size, the effect of which cannot be neglected.
More recent research has pursued physically-based models: Yan et
al. [2015] introduced a double-cylinder fur fiber model that explicitly
simulates all three layers (cuticle, cortex, medulla) for accurate light
scattering. Yan et al. [2017a] further simplified the representation
of scattering paths by extending the Marschner model with just
two additional lobes (denoted 𝑇𝑇𝑠 and 𝑇 𝑅𝑇𝑠 ) to account for medulla
effects. Chiang et al. [2016] proposed a near-field formulation that
accounts for the exact azimuthal entry point of light on a fiber.
They employed an intentionally exaggerated azimuthal roughness
to mimic internal scattering effects; while not physically accurate,
this yielded improved visual realism. Xia et al. [2020] developed a
fur reflectance model based on wave optics rather than geometric
optics. In this paper, we adopt the Yan et al. [2017a] model as the
base for single-fiber fur reflectance.
2.2
Hair/Fur Multiple Scattering Methods
Simulating multiple scattering of light among many hair or fur
fibers is notoriously expensive, because a single light ray can bounce
through a dense fiber volume dozens of times. For diffuse surface
scenes, 3–5 bounces usually suffice for realism, but in a hair/fur volume the number of significant bounces can easily reach 30–100 [Zhu
et al. 2022], greatly increasing computation. Several approaches have
been explored to make this tractable. One strategy is to improve the
importance sampling of hair scattering events so that Monte Carlo
simulations converge faster. For example, Hery et al. [2012] and
d’Eon et al. [2013] both developed specialized sampling techniques
for hair fibers that preferentially sample important scattering directions. These advances made unbiased path tracing of complex
hair geometry feasible in practice; indeed, our implementation uses

the importance sampling scheme of d’Eon et al. [2013] to accelerate
convergence.
Another line of work exploits the similarity between densely
packed fibers and participating media. Moon et al. [2006] applied
photon mapping in a hair volume, shooting photons into the hair and
storing their multiple-scattering contributions, but this approach
proved inefficient especially given newer sampling methods. Moon
et al. [2008] went further by replacing explicit hair geometry with a
volumetric grid: they precomputed a 3D grid of spherical harmonic
coefficients to store the directional distribution of scattered light
within the hair volume, enabling faster lookups of multi-scatter
illumination. However, their method needs heavy precomputation
and can not fit to different light conditions. To our knowledge, Yan
et al. [2017a] is the only prior work targeting multiple scattering
in fur specifically. They proposed a conversion of the fur volume
into an equivalent surface problem by treating inter-fiber scattering
as a form of BSSRDF (bidirectional surface scattering reflectance
distribution function). This method avoids storing large volumes of
data; however, this tends to introduce some bias or blurring in the
final render.
In addition to physically-based simulations, researchers have also
developed heuristic approximations to fake the effect of multiple
scattering for efficiency. A widely used example is the dual scattering approximation [Zinke et al. 2008]. This model assumes light
propagation can be split into a simple two-part process: globally,
light travels along a straight “main path” through the hair volume
to the point of interest, while locally, light is scattered forward and
backward by neighboring fibers assumed to have similar properties.
The dual scattering approach is fast and is adopted in real-time
and interactive hair rendering systems. [Epic Games 2021; Tafuri
2019] However, due to its aggressive simplifications (assuming a
single dominant path and locally uniform fiber properties), consistency and energy conservation are not guaranteed. Furthermore,
classic dual scattering only considers specular unscattered lobes.
To support the highly diffusive scattered lobes introduced by the
animal fur medulla, Yan et al. [2017b] proposed an Extended Dual
Scattering approximation. It incorporates these scattered lobes by
averaging the attenuation and spread across all lobes to preserve energy. However, it still sometimes yields overly uniform appearances
and unstable brightness.
2.3
Volumetric and Hybrid Representations
To balance accuracy with performance, an alternative line of research has explored volumetric representations and hybrid strategies. Petrovic et al. [2005], for example, treated a bundle of hair
fibers as a participating medium, capturing the aggregate scattering behavior without simulating each strand individually. Similarly,
Moon et al. [2008] (as mentioned above) employed a two-pass volumetric method that caches outgoing radiance in a grid to accelerate
the computation of higher-order scattering.
A rigorous foundation for modeling anisotropic media was microflake theory [Jakob et al. 2010], which represented volumes using
statistical distributions of oriented particles. Heitz et al. [2015] introduced the SGGX model, enabling closed-form phase function evaluation, deterministic sampling, and multi-scale prefiltering. However,

a fundamental limitation of these frameworks is the assumption

and approximated multiple scattering via neural-network-predicted

normalizes the hair/fur Bidirectional Curve Scattering Distribution

phase function. This ensures that intricate light-fiber interactions
are accurately preserved within our volumetric formulation.
Other works used volumetric representations mainly to simulate primary-ray intersections or level-of-detail, typically combined
with empirical Kajiya-Kay shading that encompassed fake multiple
scattering effects without actually tracing rays between hair strands.
Early interactive methods, such as Lengyel et al. [2001], approximated these volumes using concentric textured shells and silhouette
fins mapped over arbitrary meshes. Later, Andersen et al. [2016]
rendered coarse guard hairs using explicit camera-facing geometry
while modeling undercoat as a ray-marched prismatic shell volume.
Focusing on scalable performance, Jansson et al. [2019] utilized a
voxelized representation of hair density and tangents as a dynamic
level-of-detail mechanism for distant views. In contrast, our method
also leverages a volumetric representation, but for a fundamentally
different purpose: rather than simplifying geometry or accelerating
primary visibility, we use it to approximate multiple-scattering light
transport in hair.
Beyond hair modeling, the idea of approximating multiple scattering within complex discrete assets using a volumetric representation
has been explored in general surface rendering. Blumer et al. [2016]
precomputed aggregate scattering operators to efficiently evaluate
multi-bounce light transport inside complex geometries, such as foliage or translucent objects. Similarly, Loubet et al. [2017] proposed
a hybrid level-of-detail framework that retains explicit meshes for
macroscopic surfaces but replaces sub-resolution micro-geometry
with an equivalent microflake volume to preserve appearance and
occlusion. These approaches share our underlying motivation: utilizing volumetric abstractions to bypass the cost of tracing intersurface bounces.
To mitigate the cost of many scattering bounces, especially in
performance-constrained scenarios, researchers have explored approximating the effect of multiple scattering by re-weighting singlescattering contributions. For instance, Wrenninge et al. [2013] described a technique to progressively broaden and dim the singlescattering component to mimic the look of added multiple scattering,
achieving a plausible result much more efficiently, as was demonstrated for cloth fibers in woven fabrics [Xu et al. 2021].
Our approach builds upon these insights with a hybrid method.
We calculate direct illumination and first-bounce interactions on
explicit fur strands to retain high-frequency detail, and then approximate the more diffuse, multi-bounce component by treating the fur
volume as an equivalent participating medium. This hybrid strategy
preserves the visual fidelity of strand-based rendering for primary
effects while gaining the efficiency of a volumetric treatment for the
soft inter-fiber glow. The idea is conceptually similar to multi-scale

3 Our Method
3.1 Motivation and Overview
Accurately rendering multiple scattering in hair remains challenging due to the extreme geometric and optical complexity of fiber
assemblies. Light within hair often undergoes many bounces. As a
result, standard path tracing struggles to capture these high-order
contributions at an acceptable computational cost [Xu et al. 2011].
This problem is especially pronounced in dense fiber structures.
Individual fiber scattering is strongly forward-directed, and the
visibility structure within a hair bundle is highly complex. Consequently, high-order scattering exhibits high variance and converges
slowly [Moon and Marschner 2006]. This issue is further exacerbated for animal fur, where internal fiber structures (e.g., a medulla)
introduce additional volumetric scattering effects and a mixture of
specular- and diffuse-like transport [Yan et al. 2017a].
Therefore, many practical solutions aggressively approximate or
truncate high-order light transport to suppress noise, then heuristically compensate for the missing contributions. However, these
methods often fail to reproduce the characteristic soft, low-frequency
“glow” appearance produced by true high-order scattering in dense
fibers [Zinke et al. 2008]. Treating hair as a participating medium is
therefore a natural alternative: once multiple scattering dominates,
the fine geometric arrangement becomes less important than local
fiber density and orientation statistics [Moon and Marschner 2006].
However, direct volumetric path tracing remains prohibitively expensive, as convincing results require very high scattering orders
[Wrenninge 2015].
These observations reveal a fundamental mismatch: direct illumination contains high-frequency details (such as highlights and
fine shadowing micro-structures) that require explicit geometry,
while high-order multiple scattering mainly contributes smooth,
low-frequency energy but converges slowly when simulated explicitly.
Based on this insight, we propose a hybrid framework that combines geometric and volumetric representations. At the first visible
interaction, we use the explicit hair geometry to compute direct
illumination and preserve high-frequency details. We then transfer
the remaining indirect transport into an equivalent anisotropic participating medium. Within this volumetric medium, we explicitly
compute only the single-scattering component and approximate
the higher-order scattering with a lightweight “single-to-multiple”
scattering method. We further adapt this approximation to better
capture the spectral and angular distribution characteristics of hair
scattering.
In this section, we address multiple scattering in both human
hair and animal fur, using the term hair to refer to both and distinguishing between them only in the results. We first voxelize the
hair geometry to compute local fiber densities and average orientations, yielding an equivalent volumetric medium with directionally
varying extinction, absorption, and scattering coefficients, as well
as an anisotropic phase function. A hybrid light transport scheme is

3.2
Volumetric Representation
We model hair as a participating medium that supports standard
volumetric path tracing with extinction, absorption, scattering, and
an anisotropic phase function (Fig. 2). This modeling choice is physically reasonable because densely packed hair fibers collectively
behave like a continuous scattering medium, so volumetric attenuation and scattering effectively capture their aggregate optical effects.
We achieve this by deriving effective volumetric absorption and
scattering coefficients and an anisotropic phase function from the
hair fiber distribution. Our method is inspired by Moon et al. [2008]
which only considers the directional extinction in hair. We extend
this idea into a full volumetric representation that supports absorption, scattering, and anisotropic phase functions for rendering.
We assume that within a voxel-sized neighborhood, hair fibers
are locally coherent such that their collective interaction with light
can be represented by averaged geometric statistics. Under this assumption, the explicit geometry of individual fibers can be replaced
by an anisotropic medium whose properties depend on the local
distribution of fiber orientations.

Our rendering algorithm combines explicit surface-based light transport on hair geometry with a restricted volumetric transport in the
equivalent medium (Figs. 4 and 5). We define a hybrid light transport estimator that explicitly samples a limited set of light paths,
while approximating the aggregate contribution of higher-order
volumetric scattering statistically.
First Geometric Interaction. We trace camera rays and evaluate

Transition from Geometry to Medium. After the first geometric

Beyond this point, light transport is handled using the equivalent

We explicitly restrict light paths to contain at most one interaction with the explicit hair geometry. All subsequent light transport
is handled exclusively in the equivalent participating medium. Restricting geometric interactions to the first hit allows the remaining
transport to be expressed entirely in the volumetric domain, which
naturally aligns with rasterization-based rendering pipelines.
Single Scattering in the Equivalent Medium. Within the equivalent
medium, we explicitly sample at most one volumetric scattering
event. The scattering location is sampled using delta tracking [Woodcock et al. 1965] in the heterogeneous medium.
At the sampled medium interaction point, we estimate direct
illumination by connecting the point to a randomly selected light
source. The transmittance along this shadow ray is evaluated using
ray marching through the medium, and the resulting contribution
corresponds to a single-scattering estimate.

where 𝑐 ∈ [0, 1] controls the rate at which higher-order scattering
becomes more isotropic. This interpolation models the progressive
angular decorrelation introduced by multiple scattering while remaining compatible with arbitrary hair phase functions. In practice,
the summation over octaves can be evaluated either deterministically by accumulating all terms, or stochastically by sampling a single octave according to its relative contribution. Both strategies produce unbiased estimates and offer different variance–performance
trade-offs. In this work, we adopt the stochastic formulation and
evaluate the summation by sampling a single octave.
All parameters used in this approximation are obtained by numerical optimization in a controlled lighting setup consisting of
three area lights arranged in a studio-style configuration. The optimization is performed using the Ceres Solver [Agarwal et al. 2023]
with an ℓ1 loss, on a scene containing only hair geometry; the same
lighting setup is described in detail in Section 4.

controls a specific aspect of the multiple scattering behavior, leading

Results
In this section, we show the results of our volumetric hair multiple
scattering method. A path-traced Monte Carlo render serves as
ground truth. We evaluate under equal SPP (same samples-perpixel for all methods, comparing MRE) and equal quality (each
method adjusted to similar output error/quality, then comparing
required samples/time). We report mean relative error (MRE) as
our primary metric, as it is scale-invariant and provides a balanced
evaluation across bright and dark regions, which is crucial for highdynamic-range hair scattering. All results are generated using our
implementation in Mitsuba 0.6 [Jakob 2010]. All tests were run on
an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X at 1024 × 1024 resolution.
We evaluate our method on four representative lighting setups:
• Studio lighting: A configuration with three spherical area
lights, positioned at the front-left-above, back-right-above,
and front-right-below of the hair volume, illuminating the
camera-facing side of the hair.
• Environment lighting: Illumination provided by a highdynamic-range environment map.
• Directional lighting: A single directional light shining from
the upper-right of the image toward the scene.
• Spot lighting: A single spot light placed above the scene,
illuminating the hair from the upper-left direction.
Effect. In terms of visual quality, our method closely matches the
path-traced reference across a wide range of hairstyles and lighting
conditions. As shown in Fig. 10, it reproduces the soft volumetric
appearance of dense straight hair while maintaining consistent
brightness and color balance throughout the hair volume.
Our method remains stable on curly, wavy, and Afro hairstyles,
where strong self-occlusion and high-order multiple scattering dominate the appearance. It preserves the gradual color accumulation
and low-frequency energy distribution induced by inter-fiber interactions.
Under both localized (Fig. 12) and environment lighting (Fig. 10),
our results exhibit coherent shading transitions and smooth energy
falloff, recovering subtle global color tinting and avoiding unnatural
darkening near silhouettes. This leads to a more natural appearance
compared to existing approximate models.
However, highly scattering and tightly coiled hair, such as the
white Afro, presents a limit case for our method. As highlighted
by the white inset box in Fig. 10, under studio lighting, the pathtraced ground truth exhibits a localized bright spot resulting from
intense multiple backscattering. Our method approximates multiple
scattering by scaling single-scattering contributions, which makes
it difficult to reproduce such concentrated highlights, while still
capturing the overall energy distribution at a macroscopic level.
We further evaluate our method on fur scenes with both shortand long-coat configurations (Fig. 11). On short fur, our method
preserves a compact yet volumetric appearance with consistent
energy and color variation, while on long-coat fur it captures gradual
color buildup and low-frequency volumetric effects over extended
path lengths.

5
Conclusion
In conclusion, we have presented a hybrid rendering framework
that efficiently captures multiple scattering effects in hair and fur.
Our approach treats dense fiber assemblies as an anisotropic participating medium at the multiple-scattering scale, while preserving
explicit fiber geometry for the first scattering event. By combining
geometric direct illumination with a volumetric single-scattering
simulation (augmented by single-to-multiple scattering approximation), our method bridges the gap between accurate path tracing
and practical computational cost. This hybrid strategy enables us to
reproduce the characteristic soft volumetric glow and subtle color
bleeding of high-order scattering, even in challenging scenes with
thick fur, where previous techniques often yield an overly “dry” or
overblurred appearance (Fig. 10).
The proposed method closely matches the appearance of highquality path tracing for both hair and fur, while running approximately 8.53 times faster than brute-force solutions. We demonstrate
that our framework robustly handles a wide range of fiber densities and lighting conditions, significantly improving visual fidelity
over prior efficient rendering techniques. The ability to capture rich
multiple-scattering effects at a reduced computational cost makes
our solution attractive for production rendering workflows. We

Lower-resolution volumetric approximation. Our approach relies
on an aggregated volumetric representation to approximate the
low-frequency multiple-scattering component. This approximation
will lose fidelity when the effective volume is too low in resolution
(Fig. 9): thin structures, sparse regions, sharp density gradients, or
small-scale clumping may be overly smoothed, leading to loss of
local contrast or incorrect energy redistribution. In the future, we
plan to improve the volumetric representation (e.g., adaptive grids
or sparse voxel structures) that preserves fine density variations
while keeping a lower-resolution volumetric representation.
Automatic Parameter Mapping. A key assumption of our method
is that dense hair/fur behavior at the multiple-scattering scale can
be approximated as an anisotropic participating medium. In our implementation, the volumetric parameters are not manually chosen;
instead, we employ an optimizer to fit a set of effective medium
parameters to best reproduce the target appearance. However, we
still need to manually optimize for each kind of hair/fur. In the
future, we aim to leverage a data-driven neural network to provide
strong initialization or amortized inference that generalizes across
diverse grooms and species.
