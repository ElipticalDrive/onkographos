Abstract
The paper introduces a new method to describe the surfaces of voxelised solids. It operates in three stages: a hierarchical linked list of chain code sequences is created first; the linked lists are pruned; and, finally, the content of the data structure is stored. The method uses chain codes from either a three- or nine-symbols alphabet. In the first case, two chain code symbols are needed to access the next face, while, in the second case, this is done by one symbol. The pair of chain codes from the three-symbols alphabet, or the individual symbol from the nine-symbols alphabet are considered as tokens. The sets of tokens are, in both cases, extended by two tokens, indicating the beginning and ending of the list. The method processes solids of any shape, including those containing holes, cavities, or multiple components existing in the same voxel space. Edge-connectivity is permitted. The method was compared against the method proposed by Lemus et al., which is designed for solids without holes. Although supporting a broader set of voxelised solids, the proposed method generates sequences of tokens that are, on average, up to 10% shorter. Since the information entropy of the sequences of tokens produced by the proposed method is also smaller, the obtained sequences are more compressible, as confirmed by applying gzip and bzip2 data compressors.

1. Introduction
To introduce computers into engineering, it was necessary to develop methods for describing geometric solids unambiguously in the computer’s memory. This is why a new scientific discipline, Geometric Modelling, was born in the last quarter of the twentieth century [1], [2]. It introduced five unambiguous representation methods, among which three have been used widely [3]: Boundary Representation (BR), Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG), and Spatial Enumeration (SE). Spatial Enumeration is based on discretising the space of interest into a grid of cells. Initially, it was challenging, due to the memory requirements [4] and the expensive and difficult acquisition of the data. Today, due to the availability of computer memory and 3D scanning devices, it has become popular and widely used [5], [6], [7].
In Computer Science, a three-dimensional grid of equally sized cells is commonly referred to as the voxel space. The voxel space can be realised trivially from the implementation point of view as a three-dimensional array. However, this representation does not offer any high-level information about the contained geometric solid [3], such as the number of surfaces, faces, edges, holes and their spatial relationships. The solid can be converted from SE into its BR to address this limitation [8]. However, this conversion includes pitfalls related with the finite arithmetic of a digital computer and possible non-unique representations [9], [10].
For some applications an alternative possibility, based on the 3D chain codes, is better [7], [11], [12], [13], [14]. In this case, all the boundary voxels of the solid should be visited, to obtain a sequence of chain code symbols. This allows the application of various grammatical techniques on such a sequence, which opens new possibilities for querying the solids’ characteristics [15].
A new algorithm for describing the voxelised surface of solids is presented in this paper. It may use a 2D or 3D relative chain code alphabet [15], [16], [17], extended with two additional symbols necessary for reconstructing the solid. The proposed approach can handle any geometric solids in the voxel space, including those with holes and cavities. It operates in three steps: (1) Generating the sequences of the boundary voxels’ faces and storing them in a hierarchical data structure, (2) Removing the redundancy partially from the data structure, and (3) Storing the resulting sequences in a file.
The paper consists of five Sections. Section two provides an overview of previous chain codes, with an emphasis on 3D chain codes. Section 3 introduces the proposed algorithm, while Section 4 presents the results and comparisons with state-of-the-art methods. Finally, Section 5 summarises the paper.
2. Background
Chain codes are a set of commands that describe how to move through the rasterised space, usually along the boundary of the considered geometric object. Initially, chain codes were introduced in 2D, starting with the Freeman chain codes in eight (F8) and four directions (F4) [18]. The alphabet of the first contains 8 codes, while the second four. Later, the Vertex chain code (VCC) was proposed by Bribiesca [19], and Three-orthogonal chain code (3OT) by Sánchez-Cruz and Rodríguez-Diaz [20], both using three symbols in their alphabets. The unsigned Manhattan chain code [21] and the Mid-Crack code [22], [23] have also been proposed. The first one has only two symbols in the alphabet, while the second has eight. Despite the fact that chain codes are already memory-efficient, they can be compressed further [17], [24], [25], [26].
The first 3D chain code was also proposed by Freeman [27], extending F8 to 3D and denoting all 26 possible absolute movements from the considered voxel, thereby covering face, edge and vertex connectivity. Bribiesca developed an interesting encoding of digitalised 3D curves using just two symbols, i.e., bits [28]. Later, the same author introduced a five commands chain code (5OT) for 3D curves based on face-connectivity [29]. This code was later used for representing and analysing tree-like geometric objects [30]. Another method for representing trees with support for Level-of-Detail visualisation was proposed recently by Strnad et al. [7]. Their so-called differential chain code consists of 9 symbols. A three-dimensional relative code (3DRC) for rasterised solids was proposed by Sánchez-Cruz [31]. Face-, edge-, and vertex-connectivity were described with an alphabet consisting of 25 symbols. The solid 𝒢, homeomorphic to a sphere, can be considered as a four-connected graph when only face-connectivity is considered. The Hamiltonian cycle can be constructed in linear time on such a graph [32]. Lemus et al. used the obtained Hamiltonian cycle and described it with a 3D chain code consisting of nine symbols [15].
Recently, new approaches have emerged to representing volume grids based on machine learning techniques. The HyperCube method [33] introduces an implicit field representation for voxelised solids, leveraging interval arithmetic and hypernetwork-based processing to achieve efficient, high-resolution 3D reconstructions. By encoding shapes as continuous field functions rather than discrete voxel grids, HyperCube enables smoother surface approximations and reduces memory overhead. However, despite these advantages, the method faces several challenges. Computational complexity is a key concern, as the implicit representation requires additional processing to extract explicit geometric features, making real-time applications more demanding. Data retrieval efficiency is another limitation—since HyperCube does not store discrete voxel information explicitly, operations like neighbour connectivity, topology queries, and local feature extraction can be computationally expensive. Furthermore, while the approach is highly flexible, it may struggle with preserving fine details when converting from discrete voxel structures, particularly in noisy or incomplete datasets. These issues highlight the trade-offs between implicit and explicit representations of voxelised solids, particularly in applications requiring direct access to structural and boundary information.
Similarly, The Vox-Surf method [34] presents a novel voxel-based implicit surface representation, where each voxel encodes geometric and appearance information at its vertices. This approach allows for continuous surface reconstruction while maintaining the structured nature of the voxel grids, making it particularly well-suited for multi-view learning and high-fidelity rendering. Unlike traditional voxel representations, Vox-Surf optimises memory usage by focusing on surface elements rather than full volumetric occupancy, improving efficiency in data storage and processing. However, despite its advantages, the method faces several challenges. Computational overhead remains a significant issue, as reconstructing detailed surfaces from voxel-based fields requires additional interpolation and sampling operations. Furthermore, since Vox-Surf prioritises surface detail, it may be less efficient in handling topological queries and discrete structural information, particularly for objects with complex connectivity or internal features. The reliance on vertex-based encoding also introduces potential data loss in highly detailed or irregular geometries, where finer structural elements may not be captured accurately without increasing the computational cost. These factors highlight the limitations of Vox-Surf in scenarios where efficient, structured, and hierarchical representations of voxelised data are required for direct processing and analysis.
The Fast Compressed Segmentation Volumes for Scientific Visualisation method [35] introduces an advanced lossless compression technique designed to store, transmit, and visualise large-scale voxel-based segmentation data efficiently. By leveraging high-throughput encoding and optimised memory structures, this approach reduces storage requirements significantly while maintaining precise segmentation boundaries, making it particularly useful for scientific and medical imaging applications. The method achieves its efficiency through specialised compression schemes that exploit redundancies in segmented volumetric datasets, enabling fast decompression and real-time visualisation. However, despite these advantages, certain limitations remain. Compression-induced latency can be a concern, as retrieving specific voxel information requires decompression operations, which may introduce delays in interactive applications. Additionally, while the technique reduces memory footprint effectively, it prioritises segmentation accuracy over direct geometric processing, making it less suited for applications requiring explicit spatial relationships, topology queries, or boundary-based feature extraction. Furthermore, the reliance on global compression patterns may lead to varying efficiency depending on the complexity and heterogeneity of the segmented structures, potentially affecting performance in datasets with irregular or highly detailed features. These factors highlight the trade-offs between efficient storage and computational accessibility, particularly in applications requiring direct manipulation of the voxel data.

3. The method for representing voxelised solids using 3D chain code
Let 𝒱={𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘} denote the voxel space, where 0≤𝑖<𝑋, 0≤𝑗<𝑌, 0≤𝑘<𝑍. Here, 𝑋, 𝑌, and 𝑍 define the resolution of 𝒱. Each cell 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 is in the form of a cuboid and is considered a voxel. Each voxel 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 is associated with a scalar value 𝑠, which corresponds to the material existing in it. In the simplest case, 𝑠 has only two states, indicating whether the voxel is occupied by the material of interest (𝑠=1) or not (𝑠=0); it is referred to as an empty voxel in the latter case. Each 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘, excluding the frontier voxels of 𝒱, has 26 neighbours, classified into three groups, shown in Fig. 1:
•
6 face-connected voxels: {𝑣𝑖±1,𝑗,𝑘,𝑣𝑖,𝑗±1,𝑘,𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘±1},
•
12 edge-connected voxels: {𝑣𝑖±1,𝑗±1,𝑘,𝑣𝑖±1,𝑗,𝑘±1,𝑣𝑖,𝑗±1,𝑘±1}, and
•
8 vertex-connected voxels: {𝑣𝑖±1,𝑗±1,𝑘±1}.
Let us assume that a geometric solid, denoted as 𝒢, has already been inserted into 𝒱 by setting the corresponding voxels 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘=1. Additionally, it can be assumed that none of the voxels 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘∈𝒢 are frontier voxels of 𝒱. A voxel 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘=1 is considered a boundary voxel when not all its face-connected voxels have 𝑠=1. The face of the boundary voxel with the face-connected empty voxel is considered as a boundary face. All boundary faces form a surface ℱ of 𝒢. Each solid has exactly one outer surface and zero or a finite number of inner surfaces, which are considered as cavities.
The proposed method for representing ℱ by means of chain codes consists of three steps:
•
Formation of a Hierarchical List of Chains,
•
Pruning the Hierarchical List of Chains, and
•
Storing the Hierarchical List of Chains in a file.
3.1. An algorithm for constructing hierarchical list of chains
The algorithm selects an arbitrary boundary face 𝑓∈ℱ. The 𝑓 is marked as visited, while all the remaining faces from ℱ are regarded as non-visited. An arbitrary edge 𝑒 is selected in 𝑓. Its midpoint is calculated, and marked as 𝐴 in Fig. 2. Subsequently, the midpoint 𝐶 of 𝑓 is determined, and vector ⃗𝐴⁢𝐶 is obtained in this manner. Three edge midpoints 𝐵𝑖, 0≤𝑖<3, can be reached from 𝐴 through 𝐶 using vectors ⃗𝐶⁢𝐵𝑖. As seen in Fig. 2, they form angles of 90°, 180°, and 270° in the mathematical positive sense with respect to vector ⃗𝐴⁢𝐶.
The neighbouring faces of 𝑓, which share midpoints 𝐵𝑖, are evaluated based on two criteria:

The sequences of chain code symbols 𝒮𝑠 are stored in the Hierarchical List of Chains HLoC, which is shown schematically in Fig. 4. The HLoC contains at least one sequence. However, in the majority of cases, more of them exist. Each record in the sequence contains two indices that refer to the branches, possibly originating from the considered face. When entering the boundary face 𝑓, one edge of 𝑓 serves as the entry edge, another is chosen for entering the next face, and the two remaining edges are retained as candidates for initiating new chain code sequences (see Fig. 2). These indices are not set if the branching has not occurred.
Let us consider the example shown in Fig. 4. The first chain code sequence 𝒮0 contains three branching pointers that point to 𝒮1, 𝒮3, and 𝒮4. 𝒮1 also contains a branching pointer pointing to 𝒮2, which does not have any branching pointer set. 𝒮4 has one branching pointer, pointing to 𝒮5, which does not have any branching pointers. Each record in 𝒮𝑠 also contains information about the chain code symbols needed to move from face 𝑓 to the next boundary face. If 𝛴2⁢𝐷 is used, then the pairs (𝑖,𝑗) are stored; otherwise, the records contain 𝜎𝑖∈𝛴3⁢𝐷.
After the backtracking process has terminated, the algorithm checks whether there still exist non-visited boundary faces from ℱ. If they exist, the algorithm selects one of them and executes the procedure already described. Another HLoC is obtained, representing another solid in 𝒱 or a cavity within 𝒢. Its type is determined by a classical ray-casting point-in-polyhedron test [36]. Their meaning is utilised in the final step of this method, where information from HLoCs is stored in the file (see Section 3.3).

3.2. Pruning HLoC
Only one boundary face of each boundary voxel is enough to reconstruct 𝒢. However, the algorithm from Section 3.1 and the algorithm proposed by Lemus et al. [15] inspect all boundary faces of every boundary voxel. Consequently, the obtained representation may be considerably redundant. The amount of redundancy depends on the characteristics of 𝒢, with the axes-aligned rectangular cuboid representing the best-case scenario. Lemus’s method constructs only one chain code sequence (i.e., the Hamiltonian cycle), and thus, the redundancy cannot be reduced. However, this is achievable with the proposed method, where the overhead can be reduced considerably. The proposed pruning of HLoC consists of two parts: the first one does not introduce new branches, while the second one does so only under predefined conditions.
3.2.1. Pruning HLoC without introducing new branches
Removing the chain code symbols from HLoC without introducing new branches is only possible in the last part of each sequence 𝒮𝑠, i.e., behind the last branching node. The procedure consists of the following steps:
1.
Sort all chain code sequences 𝒮𝑠∈ HLoC, 0≤𝑠<𝑤, in an increasing order of their lengths.
2.
Select the first sequence, i.e., the shortest one, and denote it as 𝒮𝑎.
3.
Start at the end of 𝒮𝑎.
4.
Get the voxel 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 whose face is encoded by the last chain code in 𝒮𝑎.
5.
If 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 is located in the branching element, move to the next shortest sequence, denote it as 𝒮𝑎, and go to step 3..
6.
Otherwise, check whether 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 exists in another sequence 𝒮𝑡, 𝑡≠𝑠.
7.
If 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 is not found, move to the next shortest sequence, denote it as 𝒮𝑎, and proceed to step 3..
8.
If 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 is found, remove the last element from 𝒮𝑠 and return to step 3..
9.
If all elements have been removed from 𝒮𝑠, delete this sequence from HLoC.
10.
Select the next shortest sequence, denote it as 𝒮𝑎, and proceed to step 3. until the longest sequence is encountered.
The algorithm can be implemented efficiently by using a hash-table of already considered voxels.
3.2.2. Pruning HLoC by inserting new branching nodes
This process also consists of several steps as outlined below:
1.
All boundary voxel faces, identified by the remaining chain codes in HLoC, are marked as unvisited.
2.
A voxel 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘 is identified, with more than one unvisited face, belonging to different chain code sequences 𝒮𝑠∈ HLoC, 0≤𝑠<𝑤. If such a voxel does not exist, the pruning process terminates. Otherwise, the sequences found are stored in a temporary list 𝑄, where 2≤|𝑄|≤4.
3.
The search for the longest match of sequences 𝒮𝑞∈𝑄, 0≤𝑞<|𝑄| is then initiated by moving along those sequences forward and backward, starting at 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘. Pairs of sequences 𝒮𝑖∈𝑄 and 𝒮𝑗∈𝑄 are formed, where 0≤𝑖,𝑗<|𝑄| and 𝑖≠𝑗. Their faces are checked to determine whether they share an edge and whether the corresponding nodes in 𝒮𝑖 and 𝒮𝑗 are not branching nodes. The matched part of 𝒮𝑖∈𝑄 is considered as a subsequence. At most four pairs of subsequences are obtained in this way.
4.
The longest pair of subsequences is selected (an arbitrary pair is chosen if multiple pairs of the same length exist). The selected subsequences must be longer than the user-specified minimum length, which is set to 3 in our experiments. If no sufficiently long pair of subsequences is found the process returns to Step 2..
5.
Let the selected pair of subsequences be denoted as 𝒜 and ℬ. They can be oriented either in the same or in the opposite direction as shown in Figs. 5a and 6a.
6.
If the orientation of 𝒜 and ℬ is the same (as shown in Fig. 5), the algorithm performs the following tasks:
•
A new branching node is inserted at the end of one of the subsequences (the subsequence 𝒜 in the case of Fig. 5b).
•
Two connections are established from this branching node:
–
The first one points to the continuation of the sequence 𝒮𝑎.
–
The second one connects the sequence 𝒮𝑏 by the new chain code symbol (or the token of symbols in the case of alphabet 𝛴2⁢𝐷), plotted in green in Fig. 5b.
–
Sequence 𝒮𝑏 is terminated at the symbol just before the beginning of ℬ.
–
It is verified through backtracking whether these reconnections result in a cycle. If a cycle is identified, then:
*
all the reconnections are annulled,
*
𝒜 and ℬ are swapped, and
*
the branching procedure is repeated exactly once more.
If the cycle is also identified in the second case, all faces identified by 𝒜 and ℬ are marked as visited, while 𝒜 and ℬ are removed from 𝑄. If 𝑄 is not empty, the procedure returns to Step 4.; otherwise, it returns to Step 2..
•
However, if the cycle has not been detected, then:
–
all faces identified by 𝒜 and ℬ are marked as visited,
–
the subsequence ℬ is removed from the HLoC,
–
the procedure then returns to Step 2..
A similar procedure is applied in the case of the opposite orientation, as illustrated in Fig. 6b.

3.3. Storing of the HLoC in a file
The storage format, considered as a 3D Solid Chain Code storage format (3SCC), consists of a header and the data from the HLoC. The header consists of the following entries:
•
The resolution of 𝒱, i.e., 𝑋, 𝑌, and 𝑍, is stored at the beginning as unsigned integers.
•
The number 𝑢 of existing surfaces, i.e., the number of HLoC ∈𝒱, is stored as an unsigned integer.
•
The 𝑢 flags 𝑐, indicating whether the considered HLoC represent a cavity (𝑐=0) or not (𝑐=1); these flags are collected in bytes, and the required number of bytes is calculated as ⌈𝑢/8⌉.
•
The flag 𝑡⁢𝑦⁢𝑝⁢𝑒={0,1}, determining the type of chain codes existing in the HLoC, is written as a byte; if 𝑡⁢𝑦⁢𝑝⁢𝑒=0 then 𝛴2⁢𝐷 is used, otherwise 𝛴3⁢𝐷 is applied.
Each HLoC (i.e. a surface) is encoded with its own header, which consists of:
•
The position of the starting voxel 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘, where each component is written with 2 bytes.
•
The index of the starting boundary face 𝑓 on voxel 𝑣𝑖,𝑗,𝑘. There are 6 possible starting faces, and one byte is used to indicate the desired one.
•
The index of the starting edge on 𝑓. There are four edges on 𝑓 and one byte is used to identify the starting edge.
Altogether, the HLoC header consists of 8 bytes.
Finally, the chain codes from the HLoC are stored using a depth-first search (DFS) strategy [37]. The chain code symbols start to be inserted into the file from the beginning of 𝒮0. When a branching node is encountered, the algorithm turns into the branch, which is indicated in the file by the symbol {. The procedure is repeated recursively if the considered branch contains branching points. When the sequence has no branches, it is written in its entirety, and the symbol } is appended at the end. This symbol informs the decoder that the information follows from the calling sequence of chain codes. The alphabet is extended with two symbols in this way, i.e., 𝛴2⁢𝐷={0,1,2,{,}} and 𝛴3⁢𝐷={0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,{,}}. Finally, the sequence of tokens produced by our and the referenced method was compressed using the popular, free open-source gzip [44] and bzip2 [45]. They employ considerably different compression techniques. gzip is based on the DEFLATE algorithm [46], which combines a dictionary-based algorithm LZ77 [47] and the Huffman coding [48]. As in our cases the alphabets are small, LZ77 may find long matches of the same tokens in the dictionary. This results in some cases in an even smaller number of bytes than expected theoretically. A different strategy is employed by the bzip2 [45], which utilises the Burrows–Wheeler transform [49], [50]. The Burrows–Wheeler transform tends to arrange the same tokens close together, and is followed by the Move-To-Front transform [51]. It transforms the same tokens into runs of zeros, which may reduce the information entropy further. Huffman coding is applied at the end. This combination of transformations turns out to be very efficient, especially with the small alphabets [25] as it is in our case. bzip2 outperformed gzip consistently, and compressed below the theoretical lower boundary in all cases except two (see the result for solid Tree in Table 5). Also, in the case of bzip2, the pattern was consistent: the sequences of tokens from 𝛴3⁢𝐷 were compressed the best, the sequences encoded by tokens from 𝛴2⁢𝐷 followed, while the reference method [15] required the most storage space.

5. Conclusion
A new algorithm for describing the surface of solids, placed in the voxel space, by means of chain codes, is presented in this paper. The algorithm first selects an arbitrary face of a solid’s surface. Such a face is called a boundary face. The movement is initiated from this boundary face. The algorithm then selects the next boundary face that has not yet been visited. If such a face does not exist, the algorithm performs backtracking until the next non-visited boundary face is found. A branching node is introduced in such a case. The algorithm continues until the initial boundary face is encountered. The branches and the branching nodes are stored in the Hierarchical List of Chains (HLoC). If there still exist non-visited boundary faces, the algorithm selects one of them and describes another surface existing in the voxel space. Since many voxels have more than one boundary face, this description may be redundant. Therefore, the process of pruning the HLoC is started after all boundary faces have been visited.
The movement from one boundary voxel to another is determined by two vectors, whose three possible relative directions are described by three symbols. These three symbols can be used in pairs. However, these pairs determine 9 possible combinations, resulting in a 3D chain code, the same as proposed by Lemus et al. [15]. To unify the vocabulary, the pairs of chain codes or their unified 3D counterparts are considered as tokens in this paper. In addition to the symbols used to encode the movement through the boundary faces, two symbols for denoting the beginning and ending of each branch are added to the alphabet.
The approach is not limited by the shape of the solids. It can process solids with holes, cavities and even edge-connected objects. The pruning contributed considerably to the compact representation. In general, 60% of branches are removed through the pruning process. This results in a reduction of the number of tokens by almost 20%. The method also generates slightly fewer tokens than Lemus’s method (by about 10%); however, their approach cannot process solids containing holes. The proposed method generates sequences of tokens, which have also smaller information entropy. As a result, these sequences are more compressible, which was confirmed by the extensive experiments using the popular gzip and bzip2 data compression algorithms.
We will attempt to adopt the method for time-varying voxelised shapes as part of future work.
