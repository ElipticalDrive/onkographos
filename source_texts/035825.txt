Abstract
Astrophysical and cosmological (AC) simulations generate enormous data sets, frequently amounting to terabytes or petabytes,
which necessitate efficient processing and advanced visualization to extract scientific insights. Despite the substantial advancements in scientific visualization (SciVis) frameworks, processing and rendering large AC data continues to pose significant
challenges for numerous researchers. To address this need, we present SpaceConverter, a versatile tool designed to transform raw AC simulation outputs into standardized formats suitable for visualization. SpaceConverter utilizes high-performance
computing resources for scalable parallel data processing and supports key astrophysical methods, such as Smoothed Particle
Hydrodynamics. The tool generates data compatible with widely used visualization platforms such as Blender, ParaView, and
Houdini, enabling highly accurate rendering. In accordance with the outputs provided by SpaceConverter, the integration of our
proprietary Blender add-on, designated as BSpace, has been implemented. This integration facilitates interactive exploration
and rendering in cinematic quality through the utilization of Blender’s Cycles Engine. The pipeline’s architecture is a hybrid
system that integrates remote high-performance computing (HPC) with local control and management. This integration enables
researchers to efficiently process and visualize complex, large-scale AC datasets. A comprehensive workflow is presented, employing data from various AC simulation codes. This facilitates interactive data exploration, high-resolution rendering, and
intuitive user interaction, enabling the publication of scientific findings and the discovery of new insights.

1. Introduction
Astrophysical and cosmological simulations (AC) [KHW92;
VGS*14; DPW*14; CSB*15; DAN*19; NSP*19; FQF*23] require
significant computational power and typically produce extremely
large datasets ranging from terabytes to petabytes. Efficient processing and visualization workflows are essential to derive meaningful scientific insights from such simulations. Among the available visualization techniques, volume rendering has emerged as the
leading method for visualizing complex physical phenomena. Enabling researchers to visualize the entire simulation domain, representing trillions of calculated values through an intuitive and informative visual framework while maintaining spatial and temporal
relationships.
Price [Pri07] introduced SPLASH, a dedicated visualization tool
tailored for SPH simulations. This tool addresses the limitations of
generic visualization software when applied to particle-based data.
In contrast to conventional grid-based methods, SPLASH employs
SPH-specific interpolation algorithms to directly map irregular particle distributions to pixel arrays, thereby providing more accurate
and continuous visualizations of fluid properties.
The Splotch [DGK*21; JKR*10; DRGI08] software has
emerged as a powerful and flexible tool for the high-performance
visualization of astrophysical datasets, particularly those derived
from large-scale particle-based simulations such as N-body and
SPH models [DGK*21; JKR*10]. Initially developed for raytracing visualizations, Splotch has been demonstrated to handle
point-like particle distributions and to apply physically motivated
radiative transfer equations to simulate emission and absorption
effects, enabling photorealistic renderings of cosmic structures
[DRGI08].
A significant focus has been placed on the processing and rendering of SPH data. In this context, accurate kernel-based interpolation
is imperative for ensuring scientific precision.
Griffin and Raskin [GR16] address a common limitation in SPH
visualization by proposing a Reproducing Kernel (RK)-enhanced
SPH resampling operator that achieves first-order consistency in
the rendering of non-uniform particle data. Conventional SPH visualization tools, such as SPLASH and YT, typically use zeroth-order
interpolation schemes. However, when the particle distribution is
irregular, this approach can lead to artifacts and reduced accuracy.
Petkova et al. [PLB18] present an analytic method for mapping
SPH data onto a Voronoi grid, offering a fast and accurate alternative to traditional numerical integration approaches. Their work
addresses the challenge of deriving cell-averaged densities from
particle-based representations, particularly when post-processing is
required for radiative transfer or visualization.
Morrical et al. [MZS*24] introduce Attribute-Aware RBFs
(AARBFs), a novel visualization framework that enables interactive rendering of large-scale, time-series particle volumes through
the use of hardware-accelerated ray tracing cores and radial basis
function (RBF) field reconstruction. This approach addresses the
key limitations in existing SPH visualization methods, particularly
the inability to blend color-mapped particle attributes accurately
and efficiently during interactive exploration.
Existing solutions demonstrate a broad spectrum of capabilities,
including maximum data file size, parallel scalability, rendering
strategies (e.g., scientific volume rendering vs. film-style rendering), SPH kernel support, and interactive exploration features. table 1 provides a concise overview of the key features of several
representative tools and frameworks that have been utilized for the
visualization of SPH data and cosmological data.
Existing solutions therefore demonstrate important progress in
SPH reconstruction, large-scale rendering, and astrophysical visualization. However, they often address individual workflow stages
rather than the full path from raw simulation outputs to reusable
visual analysis. For many astrophysical users, the main barrier is
not rendering performance alone, but a reproducible workflow that
connects raw data, HPC-side preprocessing, transferable intermediate representations, and familiar end-user visualization tools. This
motivates SpaceConverter as both a data-processing tool and a case
study in making visualization research software more adoptable in
astrophysical practice.
1.1. Contribution
To address this gap, we present SpaceConverter and the BSpace
Blender add-on as an end-to-end workflow for large-scale astrophysical visualization. The workflow combines scalable HPC-side
processing with local user interaction and rendering, thereby connecting simulation outputs to practical visual analysis environments. Specifically, this paper contributes: a) an HPC-enabled conversion workflow for raw outputs from HACC, OpenGADGET3,
and ChaNGa simulations; b) support for transferable intermediate
representations, including OpenVDB, NanoVDB, dense grids, and
particle exports; c) an integration between remote preprocessing
and local exploration through Blender and the BSpace add-on; d)
a scalability evaluation on production-scale datasets containing up
to 67 billion particles across multiple HPC systems; e) a discussion of the workflow as a step toward reproducible and adoptable
visualization research software for astrophysical users.
Much progress has been made in scientific visualization (SciVis)
[Joh04; BCWW20] with the introduction of frameworks like VTK
[MSU*16], LibSim (VisIt) [CBW*11], and recently with the introduction of standardized in-situ frameworks. However, SciVis, especially large-scale SciVis, remains out of reach of the masses. To
bridge that gap and provide a solid foundation for those who use
standard data formats and transformations in the AC community,
we propose SpaceConverter.
SpaceConverter provides a flexible solution for astrophysical
data visualization, converting raw simulation output into formats
suitable for rendering with tools such as Blender/BSpace. The tool
is designed to leverage high-performance computing (HPC) resources to process data in multiple chunks—often unstructured and
implementation-dependent—and supports online/offline modes. It
implements transformations that can be used to convert AC data to
a volumetric representation from, e.g., Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) [Pri12]; its architecture allows for the future integration of in-transit [MOM*11] and in situ techniques. Moreover,
this approach provides provenance and reproducibility.

Even though SpaceConverter condenses raw data into manageable, well-formatted structures for visualization, these structures
are still too large for a workstation to handle. We aim to enable high-fidelity interactive scientific visualization, allowing users

The pipeline is implemented with a local and a remote component: the most computation-intensive tasks are performed on remote HPC resources, while users interactively explore the data locally in Blender. As mentioned, our custom BSpace add-on extends
Blender’s capabilities for loading and rendering these simulation
datasets.
We demonstrate its capabilities by implementing a complete
pipeline that spans from the raw data of several AC applications
(ChaNGa [MWZ*15], OpenGADGET3 [Spr05; VHS10], HACC
[FEB*23; HMF*16]), through user-driven data queries in our
Blender/BSpace interface, to rendering with Cycles. The process
includes effective camera control, lighting, and data selection to
create high-fidelity scientific visualizations for data exploration and
publication, almost in real time.

2.1. BSpace
The BSpace [IT425a] add-on (see fig. 2) provides a user-friendly
interface within Blender for working with simulation data processed by the SpaceConverter (see section 2.2). It enables users to
load, configure, and render volumetric datasets, such as OpenVDB
files, directly in Blender.
The interface enables users to configure paths for temporary
data storage, ensuring efficient handling of cached and intermediate files. It supports connections to both local and remote servers,
enabling data conversion on external compute resources when
needed. Users can define a 3D region of interest by specifying its
size and resolution (see fig. 1), which determines the spatial bounds
for data extraction and visualization. The system facilitates the selection and extraction of volumetric data from the server, including
parameter adjustments such as particle size and density interpretation. Extracted data can then be integrated into the Blender scene,
where users can assign materials, reposition objects, and render
or inspect volume datasets. For animations, standard Blender tools
such as the Timeline editor can be used to visualize datasets with
multiple timesteps.

2.2. SpaceConverter
SpaceConverter [IT425b] is a software application for processing
simulation data in astronomy and cosmology. It provides users with
a range of tools to efficiently convert and prepare large-scale simulation outputs for visualization and rendering.
A key feature of SpaceConverter is its support for simulation data formats such as ChaNGa [MWZ*15], OpenGADGET3
[Spr05; VHS10], and HACC [FEB*23; HMF*16]. It includes specialized importers that allow seamless handling of these cosmological formats. When working with OpenGADGET3 data, users can
provide snapshot file in FORMAT2 format (OpenGADGET3’s format). For ChaNGa simulations, the application supports both the
TIPSY format, requiring the path to the input file, and the NChilada [N-B25] format, which requires the path to the corresponding data directory. For HACC simulations, the application supports
GENERICIO [Eif25] and HACCBIN (simple binary format) formats.
The application is compatible with multiple operating systems,
including Linux, macOS, and Windows. It primarily operates via a
command-line interface, enabling users to convert simulation data
into volumetric formats for further analysis and visualization. Users
can customize the output resolution by setting grid dimensions and
specifying the output location as a file or directory. Additionally,
remote operations are supported by configuring a server address
and communication port. The application can also display general
information about the simulation data being processed. Users may
choose to convert the data into NanoVDB format as an alternative
to the default OpenVDB format. Dense data (dense grid structures)
can be written directly to files, and specific datasets and particle
types can be exported with options for either sparse or dense data
representation. For greater efficiency, a simplified density calculation method is available.
Another important capability of SpaceConverter is its ability to
perform high-performance conversions of SPH simulation data into
volumetric VDB files, enabling more advanced and detailed visualization techniques (see section 2.3). The application is also fully
integrated with Blender, supporting the Cycles rendering engine.
This allows users to work with complex volumetric datasets directly within Blender’s interface. SpaceConverter is designed for
high-performance computing (HPC) environments and supports
MPI-based parallelization, enabling effective scaling across multiple CPU nodes (see section 2.4). The output generated by SpaceConverter is of high quality, and the application can quickly produce detailed, high-resolution volumetric datasets that are immediately ready for rendering.
SpaceConverter includes several modes of operation. In offline
mode, users can specify export parameters directly via the command line. Alternatively, the application offers an interactive mode
in which parameters are set through a graphical interface integrated
into Blender.
The workflow (see fig. 3) begins on an HPC (High-Performance
Computing) Cluster, where raw simulation data, typically in the
OpenGADGET3, ChaNGa or HACC particle formats, is first
loaded across multiple compute nodes. This stage is referred to as
the Loading (particles) step, in which each node handles a por-
tion of the input data in parallel (MPI) to optimize performance for
large-scale datasets.
Once the particle data is loaded, it proceeds to the Voxelization stage. In this step, particle data is converted into volumetric
grid representations, also known as voxels, on each compute node.
The voxelized output can be produced in several formats, including
NanoVDB, OpenVDB, and dense grid structures, depending on the
intended downstream usage.
Following voxelization, the data enters the Reducing stage,
where it is aggregated or consolidated across nodes. This reduction
step ensures that the voxel data from different nodes is merged efficiently, resulting in a unified volume dataset that remains in either
NanoVDB, OpenVDB, or dense format.
The next step is Conversion, which takes place on a single (root)
node. Here, the reduced volume data is transformed into a final format suitable for transfer and visualization, specifically NanoVDB
or OpenVDB. After this conversion, the data is sent via TCP to the
user’s local machine for further use.
On the User PC, the workflow continues with optional additional Conversion, allowing users to switch between NanoVDB and
OpenVDB formats based on their preferred visualization tools (e.g.
Blender requires OpenVDB). The data can then be imported into a
visualization environment using the OpenVDB format.
For interactive exploration, the user can utilize the BSpace GUI
(see section 2.1), which provides a graphical interface for viewing
and manipulating volumetric data. For high-quality visual output,
the user can initiate Visualization (rendering) via Cycles, Blender’s
integrated rendering engine. This rendering step specifically uses
the NanoVDB format to achieve optimized performance and quality.
Throughout the process, the system ensures efficient handling
of large-scale volumetric simulation data, from initial loading and
voxelization on an HPC cluster to final visualization and rendering
on a user’s local machine.
This pipeline sends the processed data directly to Blender, allowing the user to further manipulate it in their discretion. For example,
it is possible to configure the system to send a list of particles along
with their positions, calculated radii, and a selected value (e.g., velocity). The user can then process the data further using Geometry
Nodes directly in Blender to visualize, for example, particle trajectories or velocity directions.
SpaceConverter allows to calculate particle radii and density using a kd-tree [Ben75]. For density calculations, it offers the option
to use specialized SPH kernels, described in the next section.

3. Results
We evaluated the performance of the SpaceConverter component in
our workflow using three datasets: cosmological boxes generated
by HACC (labeled as Farpoint), ChaNGa (labeled as Romulus),
and OpenGADGET3 (labeled as CosmoG).
The first dataset, Farpoint, is the latest simulation in the Hardware/Hybrid Accelerated Cosmology Code (HACC) gravity-only
simulation family, introduced in [FHR*22]. This dataset contains
18 billion dark matter particles distributed across 64 files, with an
additional file containing header information.
The second dataset, Romulus, is derived from a simulation
conducted with the ChaNGa cosmological code. It represents a
(25 Mpc)3 volume and includes approximately 24 billion gas and
dark matter particles distributed across 4 files. This simulation is
based on the initial conditions of Romulus25 [TKG*17], but was
run at higher resolution and without subgrid physics. In this test,
we voxelized 12 billion gas particles.
The third dataset, CosmoG, is a cosmological box simulation
generated using OpenGADGET3, comprising 67 billion dark matter particles across 256 files.
Each dataset was processed on three computing clusters: the
Karolina cluster at the IT4Innovations (IT4I) National Supercomputing Center in the Czech Republic, and the Polaris and Aurora clusters at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) in the United
States. For detailed specifications of each cluster, see table 3

4. Conclusion and Discussion
References
In this paper, we presented SpaceConverter and the BSpace Blender
add-on as an end-to-end workflow for transforming large-scale astrophysical and cosmological simulation outputs into reproducible
visual representations. Beyond scalable data processing, the contribution lies in connecting raw simulation data, HPC-side voxelization and reduction, transferable intermediate formats, and local exploration in familiar visualization environments.
[AGL05] A HRENS, J., G EVECI, B ERK, and L AW, C HARLES. “ParaView:
An End-User Tool for Large Data Visualization”. Visualization Handbook (Jan. 2005) 3.
We demonstrated the workflow on datasets produced by HACC,
OpenGADGET3, and ChaNGa, including data with up to 67 billion
particles. The results show that the most demanding stages can be
executed on remote HPC resources, while users can interact with
the processed data locally through Blender and BSpace. This separation lowers the practical barrier for domain scientists, who can
process data close to where it is stored while using workstationbased tools for inspection, parameter adjustment, composition, and
rendering. This supports tasks such as rapid hypothesis validation
and preparation of publication-quality figures without repeated access to HPC resources.
[Ben75] B ENTLEY, J ON L OUIS. “Multidimensional binary search trees
used for associative searching”. Communications of the ACM 18.9
(1975), 509–517 4.
Another important aspect is accessibility for users who
are not visualization specialists. By integrating with Blender
through BSpace, the workflow leverages a widely adopted, welldocumented environment familiar to many users beyond the astrophysics community. This makes it suitable not only for expert analysis but also for collaborative scenarios, where visualization tasks
may be shared with students, visualization experts, or interdisciplinary partners. For example, simulation outputs can be prepared
once on the HPC system and then distributed as reusable assets for
teaching, outreach, or visual storytelling.
Although the presented workflow is centered on Blender, it is
not limited to it. SpaceConverter exports standardized formats such
as OpenVDB and NanoVDB, enabling integration into existing visualization ecosystems. This is particularly relevant in heterogeneous environments, where different teams rely on different tools
(e.g., ParaView for analysis, custom ANARI-based [WZA*24] renderers, or production rendering pipelines). The ability to reuse the
same processed data across tools supports reproducibility and reduces duplication of effort in multi-stage workflows.
