Knowledge Graph Components
Light Vector (L)
The Light Vector (L) represents the specific direction from the point on the surface being shaded directly back to the origin of the light source. It is purely directional and, in standard models, is usually normalized to a length of 1.
In both Lambertian and Phong shading models, calculating the relationship between the Light Vector and the Surface Normal determines the core intensity of the light hitting the surface. The smaller the angle (theta) between L and the Normal, the more direct light energy that point absorbs.

Surface Normal (N)
The Surface Normal (N) is an invisible mathematical arrow that points perfectly outward, exactly perpendicular to the surface at any given point. It represents the “orientation” or “facing direction” of the geometry.
Normals are the foundational reference line for lighting. Without knowing which way a surface faces, a graphics renderer cannot determine if a polygon is facing toward a light (bright) or facing away from it (in shadow). Every other vector in Lambertian and Phong physics is compared against the Normal.

Lambertian Reflectance (Diffuse)
Lambertian reflectance simulates ideal matte, or perfectly “rough”, surfaces (like chalk, paper, or unpolished wood). According to Lambert’s cosine law, when light hits such a surface, it scatters uniformly in all outward directions.
Because the light scatters equally everywhere, the surface’s brightness relies entirely on the angle of the incoming light, evaluated as the dot product between the Normal (N) and Light (L). Noticeably, it completely ignores the observer’s position—a Lambertian surface looks exactly the same from any viewing angle.

Phong Reflection (Specular)
The Phong reflection model adds “specular highlights”—the bright, tight dots of light seen on shiny materials like plastics, apples, or metals. To do this, the algorithm calculates the ideal Reflection vector (R), predicting exactly where the light would bounce if the surface were a perfect mirror.
Unlike Lambertian diffuse light, specular light is highly dependent on where the observer is standing. The brightness of the highlight is determined by the angle (alpha) between the Reflection vector (R) and the camera’s View vector (V). This value is raised to an exponent (the “shininess” power) to make the highlight tightly focused.

View Vector (V)
The View Vector (V) points directly from the surface hit-point to the observer’s eye or the virtual camera.
While purely matte surfaces don’t need to know where the camera is to calculate their color, shiny objects absolutely do. In the Phong shading model, the View vector is compared against the light’s Reflection vector to determine if the camera is correctly aligned to catch the blinding glare of the specular highlight.

Ambient Light
In classic rendering algorithms, the Ambient term is a “hack” used to fake the complex reality of global illumination. In the real world, light bounces off walls, floors, and dust, meaning shadows are rarely pitch black.
The ambient term is a constant, low-level amount of light added uniformly to every surface in the scene. It ensures that even sides facing entirely away from the main light source (where Lambertian and Phong values drop to zero) remain visible.
