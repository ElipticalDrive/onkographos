Demonstration of improvement in 2D tomographic reconstructions
of filtered cameras in the JET ITER-like wall divertor due to
corrected interpretation of reflections

Abstract: Improved localization of emission and reduction of artefacts have been achieved in
tomographic reconstructions of the tangentially viewing spectroscopic divertor cameras in JET
by introducing the contribution of reflections from the metallic wall surfaces in the geometry
definition of the tomography process. The reconstructions thus describe the plasma emission
only without features arising from misinterpreted reflected light. Benchmarks with synthetic
emission distributions showed accurate reproduction within 10% and 1–2% of the reference
emission distributions and camera images, respectively, when the reflection correction was
applied. Without the correction, the reconstructed emission was observed to concentrate from the
divertor volume towards the wall surfaces together with bright emission artefacts appearing in
front of the surfaces to reproduce the reflections seen in the camera images.
Keywords: Nuclear instruments and methods for hot plasma diagnostics; Plasma diagnostics high speed photography; Plasma diagnostics - interferometry, spectroscopy and imaging

Introduction
Tomographic reconstructions of tangentially viewing spectroscopic cameras have been
established as a standard method for determining the 2D characteristics of divertor radiation in
different tokamaks [1–7]. In JET, tomography is applied on the KL11 camera system [8–10],
which observes the divertor with four tangential cameras with changeable filters for
simultaneous investigation of the visible emission of hydrogen isotopes and different impurities.
With the all-metallic ITER-like wall (ILW) of JET [11], analysis of camera data —
including tomographic reconstructions — is complicated by reflections from the wall surfaces, as
previously assessed for the metallic wall configurations of the ASDEX Upgrade [6] and
COMPASS [7] tokamaks. This work demonstrates the improvements observed in tomographic
reconstructions of divertor camera images following the introduction of a recently developed
reflection correction method [12]. More thorough assessment of the issue, including analysis of
experimental camera images and the effect of reflections on line-integrated divertor
spectroscopy, is presented in [13].

the camera view as the path lengths of the poloidal projections of the camera sight lines,
corresponding to each camera pixel, within each cell of the reconstruction grid. Reflections,
however, complicate this description, as the pixels record also reflected emission, whose origin
does not lie along the corresponding sight lines. This challenges the validity of equation (2.1)
and decreases the accuracy of tomographic reconstructions, as the reflected light is interpreted as
plasma emission. Consequently, the role of reflections in the details of tomographic
reconstructions of the divertor camera data during the ILW era of JET has previously been left
open for speculations.
As a recent improvement in the tomography tools for the JET divertor cameras, the effect
of reflections has been integrated in W as an addition to the geometry definition on top of the
viewing geometry of the camera. The contributions of every fj on the camera image via
reflections have been determined by ray-tracing and a model for material-specific surface
reflectivity and roughness properties, as thoroughly described and benchmarked in [12].
Consequently, resolving f from equation (2.1) yields the plasma emission distribution alone
without misinterpreted reflected light. The solution is obtained iteratively using the Simultaneous
Adaptive Algebraic Reconstruction Technique (SAART), which is an extension of the
established Simultaneous Algebraic Reconstruction Technique (SART) [14] with adaptive
weighing [15] for improved localization of the emission. To avoid unphysical emission
intensities in the solutions, negative values are restricted to zero during each iteration step of the
algorithm. Due to the significantly increased density of non-zero values in the geometry matrix,
the time required for generating a reconstruction is increased from a few seconds to 5–10
minutes per frame with the introduction of the reflection correction.

3
Demonstration of improvements in tomography due to
reflection correction
The effect of the reflection correction on the tomographic reconstructions was demonstrated with
the help of two synthetic emission distributions based on magnetic equilibria of JET in verticalhorizontal (VH) and vertical-vertical (VV) divertor configurations, as shown in figures 1a and
1b. In both cases, the low-field side (LFS) emission patterns extend from the inner and outer
strike points towards the X-point. On the high-field side (HFS), where the strike point location is
approximately the same in both configurations, the emission patterns were made to deviate from
each other by concentrating the emission in front of the inner target in the VH configuration.
Consequently, the test cases provide emission in different parts of the divertor on the LFS and
HFS and allow investigation of the effect of misinterpreted reflections both close to and away
from the wall surfaces. The corresponding synthetic camera images in figures 1c and 1d were
obtained from the 2D emission distributions by equation (2.1), using the reflection-corrected
geometry matrix.
Synthetic data was used for the demonstrations to provide a well-defined reference for
the comparisons and to eliminate the effect of non-reflection-related factors, such as
measurement noise and misalignment between the camera view and the geometry calibration
used for generating the geometry matrix. While acknowledging these sources of uncertainty in
experimental data, discarding them in this analysis allows restricting the investigation to the
effect of reflections only. The analysis is extended to experimental camera data in [13].

Reproduction of 2D emission distributions
The effect of misinterpreted reflections on tomographywas visualized by generating the

without the reflection correction using the same camera view geometry, corresponding to the

benchmarking the functionality of the tomography algorithm in reproducing the reference
distributions with well-defined camera view calibration, while possible degradations in this
reproduction appearing in the latter case demonstrate the improvements gained through correct
interpretation of the reflections.
With the reflection correction, the reference distributions are accurately reproduced by
the tomographic reconstructions in both VH and VV configurations in figures 2a and 2d,
respectively. This is supported by the investigation of linear correspondence between the cell
intensities of the reconstructions, Irec, and the reference distributions, Iref, in figures 3a and 3b. In
both configurations, the reflection-corrected data points follow the Irec = Iref curve closely with

deviations within 10% at 𝐼ref > 2 × 1020m3s sr, corresponding to the main emission regions,
where the emission is stronger than 20% of the peak value. Significant deviations are observed
only in regions where Iref is below 2% of the peak value, indicating widened spreading of the
tails of the emission regions.
Without the correction, the localization of the emission in the tomographic
reconstructions in figures 2b and 2e is degraded, as the emission in the brightest regions is
concentrated closer to the wall surface — most notably at the inner target in the VH
configuration and in both strike-point regions in the VV configuration. Moreover, bright
emission artefacts appear in front of the wall surfaces, as highlighted by the differences between
the uncorrected and corrected reconstructions in figures 2c and 2f. Both of these phenomena are
symptoms of the algorithm attempting to re-create the light reflected from the tile surfaces in the
reference images in figures 1c and 1d. The deviations from the references are seen also in figures

3a and 3b as wider scattering of the data than in the reflection-corrected cases. The concentration
of the emission from the divertor volume towards the walls in figures 2b and 2e is seen as

especially in the VV configuration. The significant overestimation of the emission by 1–2 orders
of magnitude at lower Iref levels is due to the near-wall artefacts and increased background halos
above the X-point and in the inner divertor leg.
3.2
Reproduction of camera images
The reflection-corrected synthetic images presented in figures 4a and 4d show accurate
reproductions of the reference images in figures 1c and 1d. The synthetic images were generated
from the reflection-corrected tomographic reconstructions in figures 2a and 2d using equation
(2.1) and the reflection-corrected geometry matrix. Similarly as for the tomographic
reconstructions, figures 3c and 3d show the linear correspondence between the synthetic images
and the references, suggesting that deviations larger than 2% are observed only in regions, where
the image intensity is lower than 1% of the peak value. Such an accuracy benchmarks the
functionality of the SAART algorithm, which seeks the solution for the 2D emission distribution
by matching the synthetic image with the reference image, with well-defined geometry
calibration. On the other hand, retaining simultaneously deviations within 10% between the
tomographic reconstructions and the reference distributions indicates that the algorithm can
successfully converge into a near-identical match between the synthetic and reference images
even without equally good description of the details of the 2D emission distribution.
Without the reflection correction, the algorithm attempts to reproduce the reflections by
concentrating emission near the wall surfaces, as discussed in the previous section. As a result,
the primary difference between the synthetic images in figures 4b and 4e—obtained by applying
6
equation (2.1) on the tomographic reconstructions in figures 2b and 2e with the uncorrected
geometry matrix—and the reflection-corrected images in figures 4a and 4d is the lack of the wall
tile features in the former, as highlighted by the differences between the uncorrected and the
corrected cases in figures 4c and 4f. However, as indicated by figures 3c and 3d, match within
10% of the reference images is achieved only in the brightest regions, where the image intensity
is above 70% of its peak value and consists predominantly of the plasma emission with only
slight amplification due to reflections. Outside these narrow regions, deviations by a factor of 5–
10 and, in the darkest regions of the images, 1–2 orders of magnitude are observed. Together
with the results in figures 2b and 2e, this emphasizes that neglecting the contribution of
reflections in the tomography process and interpreting it as plasma emission distorts the solution
of the 2D emission distributions, while still not adequately reproducing the camera image.
4
Conclusions
Integration of the contribution of reflections from metallic wall surfaces into the geometry
definition of tomographic reconstructions of the tangentially viewing spectroscopic divertor
cameras at JET was demonstrated to improve the quality of the reconstructions by avoiding
artefacts and distortions due to misinterpreted reflections. The reflection correction allows thus
resolving the 2D distribution of the plasma emission alone. Consequently, the representativity of
the tomographic reconstructions and their comparability to results of divertor modelling are
improved from the earlier states of the tomography tool. While the demonstrations in this work
rely on synthetic data, qualitatively similar observations have been made also experimentally in
[13].
7
Benchmarks with synthetic emission distributions showed accurate reproduction mostly
within 10% of the reference distributions by the tomographic reconstructions, when the
reflection-correction was applied. Simultaneously, the synthetic camera images generated from
the reconstructions agreed with the reference images within 1–2%. Without the correction, the
symptoms of the reconstruction algorithm attempting to reproduce the reflections contributions
in the synthetic images were observed in the tomographic reconstructions as concentration of the
emission from the divertor volume towards the wall surfaces and appearance of bright emission
artefacts.
