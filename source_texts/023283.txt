Abstract
Deep learning has demonstrated an excellent capacity to capture prior information about image classes. This has driven advances in image formation to break through traditional limits of data fidelity in denoising, reconstruction, and processing of undersampled data. Diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) is one approach that combines a generative prior with an analytic model for the measurements to both enforce consistency with measurements and also integrate sophisticated prior information. In such approaches, performance gains can be limited by the quality of the deep learning prior, which, in turn, is limited by the data used to train that network model. In many cases, like tomographic reconstruction, training data is assembled from standard clinical protocols. Specialized, high-fidelity datasets (e.g. very high spatial resolution scans) are often limited in number and/or only target regional anatomy. We propose a novel DPS framework for image reconstruction that uses mixed prior models to enhance regional spatial resolution while maintaining global information for stability and consistency. Specifically, the method integrates a global diffusion model, trained on (untruncated) normal-resolution data, with a regional patch-based diffusion model, trained on high-resolution patches. The prior models are combined using frequency-domain methods, where low-frequency components are extracted from the global model and high-frequency components come from the patch-based model. To address boundary discontinuities inherent to patch-based diffusion model, we adopt a shifted patch division mechanism, which dynamically moves patch boundaries across sampling steps. This strategy removes the stitching artifacts by dispersing them as stochastic noise, while the diffusion prior and posterior constraints gradually eliminate residual inconsistencies. Furthermore, a resampling step is applied after each likelihood update, ensuring stability and preventing error accumulation across iterations. Finally, we introduce a regional sampling scheme, where a binary mask ensures the regional prior is applied within the appropriate anatomy, while the global prior is applied in the background. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves superior reconstruction quality by preserving fine-grained details in regions of interest without sacrificing global structural coherence. This work highlights the potential of combining multi-scale diffusion priors for high-fidelity and efficient posterior sampling in inverse imaging problems.
1. INTRODUCTION
Diffusion models1–3 have demonstrated remarkable performance in generative tasks. Recent research has shown that diffusion models can also serve as powerful priors for solving inverse problems through diffusion posterior sampling (DPS).4 One advantage of DPS is that a one-time unsupervised training defines the prior model which can then be applied to arbitrary data measurement models. This property makes DPS particularly well-suited for x-ray computed tomography (CT) reconstruction where imaging protocols including different fluence settings (tube current) and geometry (axial extent, pitch, etc.) can vary significantly and patient-to-patient on a single device; in addition to differences between devices, models, and manufacturers. Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of DPS in CT reconstruction across imaging protocols5 and efficient methods have been developed6,7 to apply such methods to enable processing of clinical data sizes. These studies highlight the potential of DPS as a flexible and data-efficient reconstruction method.
Although DPS has demonstrated image quality advantages for low-dose and undersampled data, a major limitation of DPS performance is the availability and quality of training data. For example, high-resolution large-scale CT data sets in clinical applications are difficult to obtain. Moreover, the limited supply of high-resolution CT scans are often noisy, making them undesirable for training diffusion models. Current diffusion priors are typically trained on CT data captured from standard clinical protocols, which provides a nominal spatial resolution, but such data serves as a poor source for methods seeking to improve spatial resolution over current clinical standard. Possible strategies to address this deficit include the use of super-resolution approaches to pre-sharpen training data or integration of prior models trained on more general image datasets. Both of these approaches have an indirect connection to the actual underlying features that might exist in a true high-resolution dataset.
To address this challenge, we envision an alternative approach in which the nominal spatial resolution clinically derived anatomical prior is maintained, but additional high-resolution prior information is added based on another dataset. For example, public databases provide ample training data for training based on thoracic CT scans, which could be augmented by ex vivo high-resolution scans of organs or pathological samples (e.g., microCT of lung tissue, etc.). This has the potential to provide a good global prior for reconstruction and a regional, high-resolution prior information for specific organs or regions.
Towards this goal, we propose a mixed prior DPS framework that leverages both global and local diffusion priors. The method combines a global diffusion model trained on full-size nominal-resolution data with a patch-based diffusion model trained on high-resolution local regions. The information from the two models is fused using a frequency-domain combination strategy. In this work, we develop the mathematical underpinnings of the strategy and present an algorithm to apply the DPS approach with these mixed priors. We show preliminary simulation data that illustrate that the proposed method is stable and can leverage information from the two priors, and present results suggesting that the method enables the regional higher resolution reconstruction while maintaining consistent imaging across the full image volume.
2. METHODS
We propose a DPS framework that leverages mixed prior models. Specifically, the approach integrates two diffusion priors: (i) a global, nominal-resolution diffusion model that captures broad structural information across the entire image, and (ii) a local, high-resolution patch-based diffusion model that preserves fine-grained details within regions-of-interest (ROIs). Fig. 1 summarizes our strategy for the mixed prior DPS. In essence, within the ROI, the posterior mean is estimated by the two diffusion priors together during the reverse sampling process, thereby allowing the local model to improve structural details while the global model enforces overall consistency. For regions outside the ROI, we use the standard diffusion posterior sampling guided solely by the global diffusion model.

2.1. Priors Based on Diffusion Models
Diffusion models generate samples from a target distribution by gradually transforming the sample distribution from pure noise to the target distribution. The diffusion models can be characterized by a stochastic differential equation(SDE),3 including one forward process which moves the data distribution from the target distribution to the standard Gaussian distribution and one reverse process that moves the standard Gaussian distribution to the data distribution. In practice, a widely adopted version is the variance preserve(VP) diffusion model, also known as DDPM.1 Let x be a stochastic process, such that x0~pdata(x) and t∈[0,T]. 2.2. Patch Based Diffusion
A patch-based diffusion model offers a practical advantage in terms of training efficiency and computational cost. However, the limitation of patch-based diffusion lies in their inability to capture global spatial context. Unlike full-size trained diffusion models that learn the global distribution, patch-trained models do not capture the spatial correlation across the entire image. One straightforward method is to divide the image into patches and apply diffusion on each patch individually. However, stitching artifacts will appear at the boundary of patches. These discontinuities arise because each patch is processed in isolation, without sufficient coordination to enforce global consistency.
To solve this issue, we adopt a shifted-patch division strategy, inspired by our previous work.7 Specifically, at each sampling step, the spatial partition of the image into patches is shifted relative to the previous step. This shifting method forces the location of boundary artifacts to change dynamically across iterations. Consequently, the stitching artifacts do not accumulate in a fixed location but instead are dispersed across the image during sampling. As the artifacts shift, their influence becomes comparable to stochastic noise rather than structured error in the mean.

Mixed Priors and Resampling:
We employ two diffusion models to estimate prior distributions: (i) a global diffusion model trained on full-size images obtained via Fourier-domain upsampling of nominal-resolution datasets, and (ii) a patch-based diffusion model, trained on localized high-resolution patches extracted from ROIs. The intuition is that the global model captures low-frequency structural consistency across the entire image, while the patch-based model provides high-frequency detail refinement within localized regions. The image size of the nominal-resolution dataset may differ from that of the high-resolution reconstruction. Therefore, the global diffusion model is trained on a Fourier-upsampled dataset to ensure compatibility with the reconstruction size, while retaining only low-frequency content. During posterior sampling, both models independently predict posterior means. However, since they are trained on datasets with different spatial resolution, their outputs emphasize different frequency components of the image distribution. Hence, we adopt a frequency-domain fusion strategy. Specifically, we compute the Fourier transforms of the posterior means generated by the two models. A low-pass filter is then applied to the global models output to preserve large-scale structural information, while a high-pass filter is applied to the patch-based models output to emphasize fine-grained details. Finally, the filtered frequencies are combined by simple addition in the Fourier domain, and the mixed posterior mean is obtained via the inverse Fourier transform. This process can be formalized as:
xˆ0,mixed=ℱ-1ℒℱxˆ0,global+ℋℱxˆ0,patch
(5)
where xˆ0,global and xˆ0,patch denote the posterior means predicted by the global and patch-based diffusion models, respectively; ℱ and ℱ-1 denote the Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms; and, ℒ(⋅) and ℋ(⋅) represent the low-pass and high-pass filters, respectively.
To correct potential errors introduced by prior mixing and the likelihood update, we include a resampling step after each likelihood update during posterior sampling. By adapting resampling at every iteration, the method samples from the marginal distribution whose mean is the posterior mean, thereby ensuring that accumulated errors do not propagate across sampling steps and making the sampling stable.
Regional Sampling:
Since the mixed prior is only applied within an ROI, the prior model becomes region-dependent during the posterior sampling process. Specifically, the global diffusion model governs the background regions outside the ROI, while the mixed prior — combining the global and patch-based diffusion models — is applied within the ROI. Following Lugmayr et al.,8 we employ a masking scheme to enforce this regional dependency. A binary mask M∈{0,1}H×W is constructed to separate the ROI from the rest of the image, where M(i,j)=1 if pixel (i,j) lies inside the ROI and 0 otherwise. At each posterior sampling step t, the posterior mean of the entire image is given by combining the ROI update and the global update as follows:
xˆ0=M⊙xˆ0,mixedROI+(1-M)⊙xˆ0global
(6)
where xˆ0,mixedROI is obtained using the mixed prior, xˆ0global is obtained using the global diffusion model, and ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication.
This formulation ensures that the ROI is selectively enhanced using the high-frequency details provided by the patch-based model, while the global model maintains overall structural consistency across the background. By preventing interference between the two regions, the mask-based update mechanism improves both computational efficiency and stability of the posterior sampling process. 3. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
We trained the diffusion models on the CT Lymph Nodes dataset.9 The high resolution patch-based model was trained on 64×64 lung patches from 512×512 images with the native voxel size 0.75 mm. The global prior dataset is constructed by Fourier upsampling a low-resolution 256×256 dataset with a voxel size of 1.5 mm to 512×512. The global prior model is trained on the resulting dataset with an effective voxel size of 0.75 mm, although it contains only low-frequency information from the original data. We apply a U-Net10 to estimate the score with an attention layer at the bottom of the U-Net. The diffusion model follows the DDPM1 with the time scheduler β1=0.0001,βT=0.02.
For our simulation study, we adopt a standard monoenergetic CT system model: y=I0exp(-Ax) where A is linear forward projection matrix, I0 is incident photon intensity and y are the measurements. To simulate a low-dose CT reconstruction experiment, we set incident photon intensity I0=5×104. The CT system geometry used a source-to-axis-distance of 800 mm, source-to-detector distance of 1200 mm with the detector pixel size of 1.5 mm and 800 views covering 360°. We compared our proposed mixed prior DPS with the Filtered back projection (FBP) and standard (single-resolution) DPS. The posterior sampling is initialized by jumpstart6 from a FBP image. The hyper-parameters of both methods were fine tuned, as follows: (i) Standard DPS: T′=30,η=0.0015,N=3 (ii) Mixed Prior DPS: T′=30,η=0.0017,N=10.
Fig. 2 summarizes the CT reconstruction results. The proposed mixed prior DPS achieves visibly improved resolution in anatomical structures, as highlighted by the red arrows. We further evaluated reconstruction quality using root mean squared error (RMSE) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) within zoomed-in regions. The quantitative results are: RMSE: FBP = 0.00087, DPS = 0.00090, Mixed Prior DPS = 0.00078. PSNR: FBP = 35.24, DPS = 34.92, Mixed Prior DPS = 36.10. These results demonstrate that the proposed mixed prior DPS can improve image quality and resolution compared with standard DPS approaches.

4. CONCLUSION
We have presented a mixed prior DPS framework for high-resolution CT reconstruction. By combining a global diffusion model trained on full-size nominal-resolution data with a patch-based diffusion model trained on localized high-resolution regions, the method achieves a balance between global structural coherence and local detail fidelity. Frequency-domain prior fusion, shifted patch division, iterative resampling, and regional mask-based sampling were introduced to ensure stability and suppress artifacts. Experimental results on low-dose CT reconstruction demonstrated that the proposed approach outperforms both FBP and standard DPS in terms of resolution and quantitative metrics.
There are a number of limitations in these initial studies including the training data where we only emulated datasets of differing resolution by intentionally downsampling a public dataset. Similarly, only a factor-of-two resolution difference was studied, limiting potential gains in for the dual prior approach. Similarly, these methods require more sophisticated evaluations across additional patients, additional image quality metrics, and investigations on the limits of high-resolution enhancement/potential for hallucination. We are addressing these issues in ongoing work. However, these preliminary findings highlight the potential of multi-scale and multi-resolution diffusion priors for advancing reconstruction quality in CT applications.
