Optimizing 3D Voxel Engine?
Hey!
Since I'm developing a 3D voxel engine in Scratch, I'm constantly looking for ways to improve performance. So far I've gotten a lot of the basic optimizations done, such as only rendering faces of cubes that aren't neighbouring another cube or that isn't visible by the player. Cubes won't render if they exceed the FOV range of the camera or go beyond the farplane/nearplane distances. However, that's sort of where my knowledge of optimizations takes a halt. I've heard of meshing and occlusion culling, but the check for occlusion seems almost more performance draining than not having one at all, since most worlds are going to be flat planes (the culling doesn't change anything for the camera as I use a z-buffer to draw stuff furthest away from the camera first) and meshing, while logical and surely effective on render times, is surely beyond my level of knowledge and skills and I suspect would need a alot of research.
Here's my pipeline:
Setup (only goes once, at start or when the world gets updated)
1. Check all faces on all cubes for neighbours (and put in a list)
2. Load in textures
Update (goes every frame) =
1. Culling
1. Get cube position
1. Check if cube is a block (not air) and that atleast one face on cube touches “air”
2. View frustum culling
3. Check if inside nearplane and farplane
4. Goes over all faces of cube with no neighouring cells and checks if the camera can see that face (due to the angle) (Backface-culling)
5. Adds cubes to camera into an array based on distance (z-buffer)
2. Render
1. Get cube position
2. Repeat for all drawable faces:
1. Transform points on face and add into array + Rotation matrix
2. Fill area with texture
This is the basic pipeline for the engine I have skipped the small steps as that would just be clutter.
The only direct optimization I can think of at the moment is to load all cubes coordinates into an array on start or update of world, so it doesn't have to get checked twice every single frame. However this check isn't very performance heavy either way, so I doubt it will speed up the engine much at all. Here's the link to a project with a pretty similar filling technique for filling faces. It's not the most effective (that project is just a test so I would'n read too far into the code) technique, I'm sure, but I can't see it being optimised much in any way.
The performance of my voxel engine from testing does better than most others I've found (if not a whole lot better) at times, but I don't want to stop here. Do you have any ideas for optimizations, either techiques or changes to my pipeline or in general? Any common practices used for other engines that I've missed? Also, if you have experience with meshing / occlusion culling and know a cheap /effective method please comment, I appreciate any help I can get! Thank you!
note: I haven't shared the project since I'm dealing with some bugs when it comes to rendering, which would likely lead to confusion. Rest assured, this pipeline is the same and the performance aswell.

Optimizing 3D Voxel Engine?
I made this https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/843330023/ so I can help you out. I recommend if you're not super clear on how to implement any of the things I've discussed you should look at the code of this, it might help you understand a bit better. This text will be a bit mathsy but I'll try to explain in a way that makes sense to the average person in case you're not super mathsy. It isn't too complex but this might be a bit of a wall of text but stick with it. You seem to know your way around 3D to some extent, so I have faith.
Grid/block worlds are nice because every block has a defined relationship to its neighbours, and each grid cell/block has defined attributes which we can use to let us optimise things in many ways.
Also, don't use that triangle filler. If you want speed, use a normal tri-filler (like @-rex- 's). if you want textures, use my triangle filler : https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/860911845/ (or any other that is z-buffer compatible)
Just to clarify a few terms and assumptions before we begin:
-Worldspace is just, the coordinates of each thing, doesnt depend on camera position, rotation, etc.
-Viewspace is the rotated and translated coordinates of an object so that its viewspace coordinates are relative to the camera (z is directly in, etc. The transform function that you out your worldspace coordinates into gives out viewspace coordinates)
-block coordinates are measured FROM THE CENTRE of the block. unless specificed otherwise, I'm talking about the centrepoint of the block as the block.
-any block here gets culled immediately if it is air, I'm only talking about solid blocks here.
-transform means the transform function for getting viewspace coordinates THIS INVOLVES TRANSLATING THE POINTS
-rotating the points means the same rotation as the transform function, but not translated to account for camera position. Rotating a vector gives you a Viewspace vector (a vector that represents the original vector in viewspace. For example, adding the vector (1,1,1) to (2,2,2) gives (3,3,3) in world space, so adding the viewspace vector that represents (1,1,1) to the viewspace coordinate of (2,2,2) gives the viewspace coordinate of (3,3,3))
Viewspace is what you use to find the position on screen of the point, by doing (vx * constant / vz), so knowing this and being able to manipulate viewspace effectively gives us a way of finding the on-screen positions of points while doing less of the expensive transform function.
First point:
you do not need to check each face for if its visible. You can immediately know which faces are visible based on where the block is relative to the camera (in world space). For example, if the x-coordinate of the block is greater than the x-coordinate of the camera (to the nearest block) then the -x face must be the only visible face, if its less than the camera x-coordinate then the +x face is visible, and if its the same then no face is visible (applies to all axes). Only one of the faces on any axis of the cube is visivle at one time (-x and +x are never both visible, its one or the other or neither)
-You do not need to do any looping over anything, you can just look at the coordinate and determine which face to render.
Second point:
DO NOT DO YOUR ARRAY THING!!!! This is bad, and slow, and gives imprecise sorting. Assuming that you're not using a z-buffer, then you do need to render your blocks in a certain way, but you don't need to load them all into an array or anything, you can just loop through them in a certain order in which you know that each cube doesn't overlap with any cube rendered after it. It turns out using an order like this: https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/867404961, does exactly that, so just loop through all those blocks in the right order (for 3D do the lowest y and the highest y and work inwards from there, doing one of those layers for each layer). Then for each block check if its visible and do the block render, etc. Now, heres where things get JAZZY with maths. I'm gonna assume you don't know what a linear transform is, and explain without that knowledge, but if you wanna know why it works, look into linear transforms. Basically, it turns out that if you rotate a vector (giving a vector in viewspace) and add it to another transformed point in viewspace, you get the same result as transforming the unrotated vector added to the untransformed point in space. This turns out to be very very useful in grid worlds where there are only a few relationships to keep track of. This isnt any faster in normal use but in grid worlds it has some uses.
The first of these uses is that since in your block rendering order, each block will have a defined relationship to the next block, you can rotate those vectors that define the relationships between each block (1,0,1 for going across 1 in x and 1 in z for example, the step used in the link I sent you). Then you can find the position of your starting block, render that block, then successively add the correct rotated vector and render the positon with the added vector. Hopefully https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/867410849/ helps you understand this better, its good to have a visual aid. Remember, this is all in viewspace. Its hard to represent it properly, but YOU SHOULD NOT BE TRANSFORMING EVERY POINT!!! If you want an example of it in code that isn't too complex, see https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/853184875/editor/ for a 2D implementation. Notice how there are no transforms in the chunk code. The result of those transforms can be obtained by adding vectors that have been rotated to transformed points. (sorry for harping on about this, i want to hammer home that youre adding vectors that have been rotated, and using that as your viewspace coordinates, not transforming each point)
Obviously cull the block if its visible faces are obscured too.
Third point:
Once you have the viewspace coordinate of the centre point of the block, you can use this to efficiently frustum and backface cull without having to check multiple things. For z-culling its easy, if the point is greater than sqrt(3)/2 (roughly 0.86602540378) plus your near clip distance, then it doesnt need to be culled or clipped. If it is less than (near clip subtract sqrt(3)/2) then it can be culled. between these values you should render, but youll need to z-clip some of your faces. For frustum cuilling, it is possible to calculate a frustum “gradient” (how much it gets wider for each unit of z you get further from the camera) for both x and y, as well as a “frustum depth” (a little bit of padding on either side of the frustum to increase its width by a constant amount, as some blocks whose centres are out of the frustum can still poke into it slightly). i won't go into how to calculate or use them here, all the details on that are in the beginning of the “render” section and “block” section of the minecraft project i linked all the way at the start. The variables are called “frustum X” (or Y) and “frustum X depth” (or y depth). Render stretch is defined in “new frame”. From that information its super easy to find and figure out that snippet of the code, its a handful of blocks.
Fourth point:
After you have the centre of the block in viewspace, you can use the same technique from point 2 to find the vertices of the block corners. You rotate (find a viewspace vector for) the vectors that go from the centre to the corners of a block (8 vectors, (0.5 , 0.5 , 0.5) and (0.5 , 0.5 , -0.5) and so on). If you wanted the coordinate of the +x +y +z vector in viewspace, youd add the viewspace vector representing (0.5 , 0.5 , 0.5) to the viewspace coordinate of the block. This gives you the exact same result with less computation.
If you decide to use a z-buffer, you should somehow keep a track of all of the blocks that are visible (preferably in chunks which you can easily cull too) and only render them (you can render them in any order using a z-buffer), but thats slightly too detailed for a forum post. I hope you found this information helpful.
It looks a lot more intimidating than it is. You should be able to figure it out by reading it a few times and looking at the links i sent you in depth. Good luck

I've also mean looking into optimizing the transform part of the code. Before I did a horrible 4 transforms per face, which at most could mean 12 transforms for one voxel. I quickly fixed that by just transforming all the points that were to be transformed and adding them into an array, which would then be referenced for every point. It's not very elegant, but was just something quick I did for the 30 minutes I had free. This just stops the same point from being transformed twice or even three times, which also lowers the maximum transforms per voxel to 7, as not all 8 points on a cube can be seen at once.
