Abstract—Robotic simulation, navigation, and inspection need
photorealistic and geometrically accurate maps. Radiance fields
achieve photorealism, but building them from robot data raises
three challenges. A robot’s trajectory serves its task, not reconstruction, so images from few viewpoints leave the geometry
under-constrained. Residual errors in camera poses and intrinsics
blur the map. The map’s GPU memory grows with scene size,
which limits its level of detail in large scenes. We present
SpaRV, a Sparse Radiance Voxel map rendered by differentiable
ray marching, which lets irregular LiDAR scans supervise it
alongside images. LiDAR depth and occupancy anchor the underconstrained geometry, and normal and smoothness regularizers
keep it smooth between LiDAR points. To correct camera errors,
SpaRV alternates map training with photometric optimization of
the camera poses and focal lengths. To bound GPU memory
in training and rendering, SpaRV holds one submap at a
time at full resolution and the rest at coarser octree levels.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Digital twins of real environments support robot simulation,
navigation, and inspection [1], and simulators train robot
policies before deployment [2]. Such applications need one
map that supports novel view synthesis, surface extraction,
and sensor simulation. It must therefore be both photorealistic
and geometrically accurate. Cameras and LiDAR supply both
properties: images record appearance, and LiDAR measures
metric range. LiDAR-visual SLAM [3], [4] already fuses them
into accurate colored point clouds, but point clouds do not
render photorealistic novel views.
Radiance fields such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [5]
and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [6] render photorealistic
novel views by fitting a scene representation to posed images.
Fitted to robot data, however, a radiance field can reproduce
the captured images and still fail once the viewpoint leaves the
captured trajectory. At an extrapolated view, the image-only
methods break into noise and holes (Fig. 2). This paper asks
how to build a radiance map from robot data that stays reliable
at viewpoints away from the captured trajectory. Radiance
fields are usually built from curated captures with dense views,
accurate camera parameters, and a scene of limited extent.
Robot data breaks each of these conditions, which raises
three challenges to reliability: under-constrained geometry,
inaccurate camera parameters, and limited GPU memory.
The first challenge is under-constrained geometry. A robot’s
trajectory follows its task rather than the needs of reconstruction, so each region is observed from few and uneven
viewpoints. Photometry constrains depth only through the
parallax between views, and a short baseline gives little
parallax. A radiance field then overfits the captured images
by placing geometry at the wrong depth, and the misplaced
geometry appears as floating artifacts away from the trajectory.
LiDAR measures depth directly, and prior work supervises the
rendered depth with LiDAR [7], [8] or builds a geometric prior
from it [9], [10]. Depth supervision, however, constrains only
the rays that LiDAR measures, and camera rays between the
sparse LiDAR points can still terminate at floating artifacts.
This motivates us to ground the geometry along the LiDAR
rays and also on the camera rays that LiDAR does not measure.
Correct geometry must also render consistently as the
viewpoint moves, which depends on the representation. 3DGS
sorts its Gaussians by their centers for each view, and a
change of viewpoint can invert this order, so the rendered
surface pops [11]. Popping persists even when LiDAR places
the Gaussians on the true surface, as for LetsGo and GSSDF in Fig. 2. NeRF avoids popping by compositing samples
along each ray in the order the ray meets them, but its
capacity is fixed by the network parameters. Explicit spatial structures [12]–[15] attach parameters to locations, so
capacity can grow where the scene needs it. Among them,
SVRaster [16] uses non-overlapping adaptive sparse voxels,
which keep the compositing order exact and add detail where
required. SVRaster renders these voxels by rasterization, however, which assumes a pinhole image grid and does not directly
apply to LiDAR rays or non-pinhole cameras. We therefore
render the sparse voxels with a differentiable ray marcher that
evaluates each ray independently, so the map can be supervised
directly along the LiDAR rays.
The second challenge is inaccurate camera parameters.
Camera poses and intrinsics are difficult to obtain exactly in
practice, whether they come from calibration [17], Structurefrom-Motion [18], SLAM [3], or LiDAR-visual bundle adjustment [19]. A small error projects the same surface point
to different pixels in different images. A map fitted to these
misaligned images tends to have blurred textures, even where
LiDAR constrains its geometry well. Prior work optimizes the
cameras jointly with the field from little or no prior [20], [21],
or holds the trajectory fixed and refines only the LiDAR-

The third challenge is limited GPU memory. The GPU
memory of a map grows with the extent of the scene. Within
a fixed GPU memory budget, a large scene can then be
trained and rendered only at a coarser level of detail. A
single view, however, needs fine detail only near the camera,
since distant regions project to few pixels. Divide-and-conquer
methods [24], [25] train parts of the scene separately, and
level-of-detail methods [26] select coarser primitives for distant regions at render time. Hierarchical 3DGS [27] combines
the two, but it needs extra stages after training: it merges
the Gaussians of each part into a hierarchy, optimizes the
merged nodes, and consolidates the parts into one hierarchy.
This motivates us to simplify the pipeline: the octree keeps
every coarser level that training passes through, and each
coarser voxel was already trained at its own level before it
was subdivided, so it serves as that level of detail without
further optimization. One cut rule that selects voxels by their
projected size then serves training as well as rendering.
To address these challenges, we present SpaRV, a Sparse
Radiance Voxel map for scalable, geometrically consistent
LiDAR-visual mapping and rendering. Our contributions are
as follows:
1) Ray-marching rendering of sparse radiance voxels: A differentiable ray marcher lets camera pixels, LiDAR rays,
and rays of non-pinhole cameras supervise the map through
a unified renderer (Secs. III–IV).
2) Geometry-grounded map training: LiDAR depth supervision, occupied voxel guidance, and normal and smoothness
regularizers keep the map geometrically consistent beyond
the captured trajectory (Sec. V).
3) Map-driven camera optimization: SpaRV alternates map
training with photometric optimization of the camera poses
and focal lengths, which reduces the texture blur caused by
residual camera errors (Sec. VI).
4) Large-scale reconstruction under bounded GPU memory:
One octree and one granularity-driven cut serve training
and rendering, holding the submap in focus at the finest
available level and the rest of the scene at coarser levels
(Sec. VII).
We evaluate SpaRV on synthetic indoor scenes, two outdoor
LiDAR-visual datasets, an omnidirectional benchmark, and
two large scenes we collected. At viewpoints away from the
captured trajectory, SpaRV keeps the geometry consistent,
while image-only baselines degrade and Gaussian-based baselines pop. Camera optimization sharpens textures on real data,
and the optimized cameras also improve the baselines retrained
on them. On a single GPU, SpaRV reconstructs and renders
a kilometer-scale university campus. The trained maps also
support surface extraction and the simulation of other camera
and LiDAR models.
II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Radiance Field Representations
Radiance-field representations store scene parameters either
implicitly in network weights or explicitly at spatial locations.
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [5] established continuous
volumetric modeling for photorealistic novel view synthesis. A
multilayer perceptron maps a position and viewing direction to
density and color. Methods that store learned features in hash
grids [13] or factorized tensors [28] retain a network only as a
decoder, which reduces training cost. A shared decoder, however, entangles those features: an update in one region perturbs
weights used throughout the field, so previously reconstructed
regions may drift as new observations arrive [29], [30]. The
parameter is also fixed for the whole field, so capacity cannot
readily be increased locally where additional detail is required.
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [6] makes the parameters
explicit and discrete. The scene becomes a set of anisotropic
Gaussian primitives, each with its own position, shape, opacity,
and appearance. A highly efficient differentiable rasterizer
sorts the primitives and blends them into the image. The
primitives remain independent, so the field they assemble has
no intrinsic compositing order: the per-view sort on their
centers can invert under viewpoint change and the rendered
surface pops [11]. Voxel-based radiance fields store parameters
explicitly on a voxel grid. Queries are answered by interpolating vertex values, without a decoder network. NSVF [31]
attached learned features to a sparse voxel octree, DVGO [15]
optimized an explicit density grid, and Plenoxels [14] removed
the network altogether, reducing optimization from hours to
minutes. SVRaster [16] further makes those voxels adaptive:
non-overlapping sparse voxels across levels of detail, with efficient rasterization and an exact compositing order that avoids
popping. Its rasterizer, however, assumes a pinhole image grid
and does not directly apply to LiDAR rays or non-pinhole
cameras. In contrast, our method shares the adaptive sparsevoxel representation of SVRaster but differs from it in three
designs. A differentiable ray marcher replaces the rasterizer, so
camera pixels, LiDAR rays, and rays of non-pinhole cameras
supervise the map through the unified renderer (Sec. IV).
LiDAR measurements ground the geometry (Sec. V), and a
granularity-driven octree cut keeps GPU memory bounded in
large scenes (Sec. VII).
B. Geometric Grounding in Radiance Fields
A photometric loss alone leaves geometry underconstrained. 2DGS [32] collapses each Gaussian primitive
to an oriented disk and aligns the normals derived from
the rendered depth to the disk normals. PGSR [33] further
introduces unbiased depth rendering with multi-view
photometric regularization to preserve global geometric
accuracy. They require the rendered geometry to be selfconsistent, which improves the quality of the surface
reconstruction. DS-NeRF [34] reuses the sparse 3D points
that Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [18] has already produced,
which anchors the scene geometry to the observation. This
supervision inherits what SfM has produced, so it thins out
exactly where correspondence fails, which is where geometry
is hardest to recover. Monocular depth predictors such as
Depth Anything [35] provide dense supervision [36], but
their depth is not guaranteed to be accurate or metrically
consistent across images.
Range sensors measure depth directly: each range measurement gives the metric distance to a surface along its own
ray, independent of texture. The most common approach is to
penalize the difference between rendered depth and measured
depth. This loss is used wherever such measurements are
available, across implicit fields [7], [25], [29], [30], [37], [38]
and 3D Gaussian representations [8], [39]. The alternative
is to reconstruct an explicit or implicit geometry from the
depth measurements and use it to constrain the radiance
field. LetsGo [9] uses such a geometry as a starting point.
It meshes the LiDAR point cloud by Poisson reconstruction
and initializes Gaussians from that mesh. A depth term on
the measurements then constrains the rendered depth during
training. LIV-GaussMap [40] and LI-GS [8] constrain splat
orientation and distribution with LiDAR-derived planar priors
and Gaussian mixtures. M2Mapping [41] and GS-SDF [10]
instead build a neural SDF from the range measurements. GSSDF further fits the Gaussians to its isosurface to improve
the geometric consistency of 3DGS. In contrast, our method
grounds the sparse-voxel field itself: LiDAR depth supervision
acts directly along the LiDAR rays, while occupied voxel guidance and the normal and smoothness regularizers constrain the
field where LiDAR does not measure.
C. Camera Optimization in Radiance Fields
Radiance-field reconstruction depends on accurate camera
parameters. SfM [18] is the common source, but it is computationally expensive and brittle in textureless or repetitive scenes.
SLAM systems [3], [42] produce poses efficiently in these
environments, yet their residual error still exceeds the pixellevel accuracy a radiance field requires. Joint photometric
optimization against the field corrects such errors, and existing
methods differ mainly in what they start from. Some recover
the cameras with little or no prior. Starting from rough or

method refines every camera of the input trajectory, such as
one from LiDAR-inertial-visual odometry [3], whose poses are

It optimizes the cameras against a map whose geometry is
anchored by LiDAR. The cameras and the map are updated
in alternation. Each update corrects the pose and focal length
of a camera by an error-state step around its initialization.
D. Large-Scale Radiance Field Reconstruction
Scaling radiance fields to large scenes raises two distinct
issues: representing unbounded outdoor extents, and keeping
active GPU memory bounded during training and rendering.
NeRF++ [50] and Mip-NeRF 360 [51] address unbounded
extents by wrapping the whole space with a single non-linear
parameterization into a bounded domain, so the field can be
queried anywhere. 3DGS does not require this contraction:
Gaussian positions live in Euclidean world space, so distant
content is represented by placing primitives farther away
rather than by warping the domain. Active GPU memory
is kept bounded by divide-and-conquer training and levelof-detail (LoD) rendering. Divide-and-conquer methods such
as Block-NeRF [24] and SiLVR [25] decompose a largescale capture into individually trained NeRFs, which decouples
rendering time from scene size and lets one block be updated
independently. LoD rendering methods such as Octree-GS [26]
organize the Gaussians into multi-resolution anchors and select
a level per view, so the number of primitives inside the viewing
frustum stays stable as the viewpoint zooms out. Hierarchical
3DGS (H3DGS) [27] combines the two. It partitions the
capture into parts trained independently, merges the Gaussians
of each part into coarser intermediate nodes, and consolidates
the parts into one hierarchy that is optimized further. Rendering selects Gaussian nodes through this hierarchy by the
projected size of its Gaussians and interpolates between levels
for smooth transitions. In contrast, our method addresses both
issues. For unbounded extents, it follows the NeRF line and
queries its bounded voxel domain in contracted space. For
GPU memory, a granularity-driven cut of one octree holds the
submap in focus at the finest available level and the rest of
the scene at coarser levels, in training as well as in rendering.

III. S PARSE R ADIANCE VOXEL R EPRESENTATION
Given RGB images and LiDAR points, together with initial
camera parameters, SpaRV reconstructs a sparse radiance
voxel map for geometrically consistent rendering (Fig. 3).
A granularity-driven cut selects the resident voxels from the
octree by their projected size, so GPU memory stays bounded
as the scene grows (Sec. VII). The selected voxels form an
adaptive sparse-voxel map, with finer voxels where additional
detail is required (Sec. III-A). A differentiable ray marcher
traverses these resident voxels along each ray independently,
and therefore renders camera images and irregular LiDAR
scans in their native sampling patterns (Sec. IV). Both then
supervise the map through the same renderer. SpaRV alternates
between geometry-grounded map training (Sec. V) and camera
optimization against the map (Sec. VI). The trained map
supports novel view synthesis, surface extraction, and LiDAR
simulation.

C. Octree Query
LiDAR supervision and camera optimization query the field
along independent rays in arbitrary directions. Most of a ray
crosses free space where no voxel is materialized, so empty
space must be skipped in large steps. SpaRV therefore queries
the field by an octree lookup, and rendering marches over the
resulting voxel segments (Sec. IV). Since only occupied leaves
are stored in a flat Morton-indexed array, the renderer does
not perform a pointer-based hierarchical octree walk. Instead,
Alg. 1 maps each query point to its finest-level Morton code
and locates it in the sorted leaf set by binary search (lines 2–
3). If the queried code matches a stored leaf, or falls inside the
finest-level Morton interval covered by the predecessor leaf,
the ray enters that occupied voxel directly (lines 5 and 8).
Otherwise, the predecessor and successor occupied leaves
bound the query in Morton order, and the largest empty voxel
between them is skipped (line 13). The marcher can therefore
skip free space without traversing unoccupied fine voxels
individually. The lookup thus returns either an occupied voxel
to be evaluated or an inferred empty skip voxel that advances
the ray to the next relevant event. The event-driven ray marcher
of Sec. IV runs entirely on this occupied-or-empty interface.
IV. S PARSE R ADIANCE VOXEL R ENDERING
SpaRV renders the sparse voxels with a differentiable ray
marcher that traverses them along each ray independently.
Camera pixels, LiDAR rays, and rays of non-pinhole cameras
then supervise the map through the same renderer. In contrast,
the rasterizer of SVRaster [16] batches a pinhole pixel lattice
and does not directly apply to LiDAR rays or non-pinhole
cameras (Fig. 6). Rendering converts the occupied-or-empty
lookup of Sec. III-C into pixel values through event-driven
ray marching and volumetric alpha blending.

V. G EOMETRY-G ROUNDED M AP T RAINING
This section trains the sparse radiance voxel map from RGB
images, LiDAR points, and the initial camera parameters, and
adapts its voxels to the scene during training (Sec. V-A).
The goal is a map that renders the captured views photorealistically and keeps correct geometry in regions that the
images alone under-constrain, so that it stays geometrically
consistent beyond the captured trajectory. To this end, photo-
metric supervision fits the images, LiDAR depth supervision
and occupied voxel guidance anchor the density field at the
measured surfaces, and normal and smoothness regularizers
keep the surface smooth between LiDAR points (Sec. V-B).
A. Voxel Initialization and Adaptation
SpaRV inherits the voxel initialization and adaptation
scheme of SVRaster [16]. Initialization uses the same
foreground-background layout, with voxel maximum sampling
rate as the guiding quantity for image-space coverage. The
implementation starts from uniform dense voxels and iteratively subdivides the voxels with the highest sampling rate
until the initial target voxel budget is reached. Voxels with zero
camera visibility are then removed. This initialization provides
a coarse scene-covering layout that is subsequently refined by
adaptive subdivision and pruning during training.
During training, we adopt the same adaptive structural
update criteria. At each adaptation, voxels whose maximum
blending weight 𝑇𝑖,𝑘 𝛼𝑖,𝑘 in Eq. (8) across training views falls
below a threshold are pruned. Voxels are thus kept only where
the rendering needs them, which makes the map sparse, as
shown in Fig. 4 (a). For subdivision, each leaf voxel is ranked
by the accumulated alpha-weighted loss gradient

VII. L ARGE -S CALE R ECONSTRUCTION
The GPU memory required by an explicit voxel map grows
with both the extent of the scene and the requested level of
detail. Consequently, the map of a large scene fits within a
fixed GPU memory budget only at a coarser level of detail.
A voxel’s projected size decreases with its distance from the
camera, so a view needs fine voxels only near the camera.
SpaRV therefore keeps the full octree in host memory and
places only a selection of its voxels, called a granularity-driven
cut, on the GPU. The cut selects voxels by their projected size:
fine voxels inside a box of interest, and progressively coarser
voxels farther away. Throughout this section, 𝑁max denotes the
maximum number of voxels resident on the GPU at one time.
During training, voxel subdivision stops when the number of
resident voxels reaches this budget. During deployment, the
cut is loaded subject to the same budget. GPU memory then
stays bounded as the scene grows.
Fig. 11 summarizes the pipeline. Coarse training first builds
the coarse map of the whole scene and initializes the octree.
Submap training then visits the disjoint submaps one at a time
and refines each within its own cut. The box of each cut
bounds the submap in focus, so the cut holds that submap
at the finest available level and the rest of the scene at coarser
levels. Deployment applies the same cut rule to the refined
octree, with the box placed around the moving camera. We
first describe the octree and the cut, then detail their roles in
training and rendering.
A. Level-of-Detail Data Structure
1) Structure and Growth: The octree O stores both the current leaves and the coarser internal nodes created during subdivision (Fig. 11 (1)), so every level that training passes through
stays available to the cut. It uses the flat (octpath, octlevel)
indexing of Sec. III-A. When a voxel subdivides according
to Sec. V-A, its trained parameters are frozen and retained
as the parent node, while its eight children become trainable
leaves. The parent is not trained further because a voxel is split
only after it has been sufficiently trained at its current level.
Its parameters therefore remain a valid approximation at that
coarser level. Training continues on the children, which add
finer detail while the parent stays a snapshot of the preceding
level. Fig. 12 (a)–(d) illustrates the detail represented at
different levels of the same octree.

Deployment applies the same cut rule around the camera,
so rendering preserves local detail without making all fineresolution voxels of the entire scene resident on the GPU at
once. It selects the cut from the refined octree O𝑑 , using a
granularity target 𝜏𝑑 and a deploy box 𝐵 𝑑 of target size 𝑠 𝑑 :
C𝑑 = C(𝜏𝑑 , 𝐵 𝑑 , O𝑑 ). The deploy box contains the camera and
aligns with the octree partition at the level determined by 𝑠 𝑑 ,
following the same sizing rule as in training (Fig. 11 (3)).
The deploy box is smaller than a training box (𝑠 𝑑 < 𝑠𝑡 ),
so the finest voxels cover a smaller region and more of the
memory budget remains for the exterior. The granularity target
𝜏𝑑 sets how coarse the exterior is and is increased when the cut
exceeds 𝑁max . The cut is recomputed only when the camera
moves beyond the current deploy box by a small margin,
preventing repeated switching near the box boundary. The
resident voxels are then updated by incremental host-device
transfer. Training and rendering thus share one octree and one
cut rule, and both keep the resident voxels within 𝑁max as the
scene grows.
VIII. E XPERIMENTS
This section tests the four contributions of the paper on
five datasets. Radiance-field methods fit their training views
closely, so differences in geometry appear mainly at viewpoints away from the captured trajectory. To evaluate geometric consistency, we compare the methods at interpolated
viewpoints along the trajectory and at extrapolated viewpoints
away from it, on the synthetic Replica dataset (Sec. VIII-B)
and the real FAST-LIVO2 dataset (Sec. VIII-C). Replica
provides ground-truth poses, so its extrapolated views are
evaluated against ground truth, while those of FAST-LIVO2
are compared qualitatively. Since the cameras of FAST-LIVO2
come from odometry and carry residual errors, we also use
this dataset to evaluate camera optimization (Sec. VIII-C1).
We further compare the methods with accurate cameras on the
Oxford Spires benchmark (Sec. VIII-D), and ablate occupied
voxel guidance on it. To evaluate large-scale reconstruction under bounded GPU memory, we use two kilometer-scale scenes
that we captured, Garden and Campus (Secs. VIII-E–VIII-F).
To evaluate the ray-marching renderer beyond pinhole cameras, we train SpaRV on the EgoNeRF dataset (Sec. VIII-G),
where an omnidirectional camera supervises the map through
the same renderer. Finally, Sec. VIII-H uses the trained maps
for surface extraction and, through the same renderer, for the
simulation of other camera and LiDAR models.

A. Experimental Setup
1) Baselines and Metrics: We compare SpaRV against
the following methods: InstantNGP [13]: hash-grid NeRF;
3DGS [6]: image-only 3D Gaussian splatting; SVRaster [16]:
sparse-voxel radiance field whose representation SpaRV
shares; LetsGo [9]: 3DGS initialized from a LiDAR Poisson
mesh and regularized by LiDAR depth; GS-SDF [10]: Gaussians coupled to a LiDAR-built neural SDF; EgoNeRF [55]:
NeRF on a balanced spherical grid for egocentric omnidirectional views (Sec. VIII-G); OmniGS [56]: omnidirectional
3DGS (Sec. VIII-G); H3DGS [27]: hierarchical 3DGS with
divide-and-conquer training and LoD rendering (Sec. VIII-E).
We report PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS [57]. PSNR measures
per-pixel fidelity and tolerates blur, whereas SSIM and LPIPS
respond to lost structure and perceptual detail.
2) Implementation Details: SpaRV is implemented in PyTorch with custom CUDA kernels. Every SpaRV result in this
paper is produced on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 with 24 GB
of device memory, an Intel Core i7-13700K CPU, and 64 GB
of host memory. On the benchmark scenes of Secs. VIII-B–
VIII-G, training runs 20,000 iterations of sparse Adam [16]:
voxels are subdivided every 1,000 iterations and pruned on
each coverage epoch, and camera optimization alternates with
map training on the same interval. The large-scale scenes
of Sec. VIII-E use the coarse-to-fine training of Sec. VII-B.
Coarse training stretches the benchmark schedule by a factor
that adapts to the dataset size. Here the factor is 5.0, so
coarse training runs 100,000 iterations and every milestone
above scales with it. Each submap is trained as an ordinary
map-training run on the 20,000-iteration schedule. The target
submap size is 𝑠𝑡 = 100 m, and the granularity target is 𝜏 = 1.
Deployment uses a target deploy-box size of 𝑠 𝑑 = 10 m at the
same granularity.
B. Geometric Consistency on Replica
Replica [58] is a synthetic indoor dataset with groundtruth camera poses, which remove camera error from the
comparison. We evaluate on 8 scenes at both interpolated
and extrapolated viewpoints, the latter generated by uniformly
sampling positions and orientations in each scene [41]. In the
extrapolation evaluation, pixels that are not seen in the training
views are excluded.
Interpolation evaluates how well each method fits the observed views; extrapolation evaluates whether the map stays
geometrically consistent at viewpoints away from the captured
trajectory. Table I reports the extrapolated results per scene and
the averages of both, and Fig. 14 shows one interpolated and

G. Omnidirectional Cameras on the EgoNeRF Dataset
The EgoNeRF dataset [55] is an egocentric view-synthesis
benchmark of 22 equirectangular sequences, 11 synthetic
scenes in OmniBlender and 11 real captures in Ricoh360,
each recorded along a short orbit around a fixed center.
The benchmark carries no range data, so SpaRV is trained
from images alone in this setting. The only change from the
benchmark experiments of Secs. VIII-B–VIII-D is the ray
generator: each pixel of an equirectangular image is mapped to
a ray direction by its longitude and latitude, instead of through
the pinhole intrinsics. The map, the renderer, and the training
schedule are unchanged. Following the OmniGS [56] protocol,
we reconstruct from the OpenMVG poses shipped with the
benchmark, train EgoNeRF [55] on the same poses and split,
and evaluate all methods on the same test frames.
SpaRV outperforms OmniGS on all three metrics on both
splits (Table VII). The PSNR margin is small, 0.34 dB on
the synthetic OmniBlender scenes and 0.15 dB on the real
Ricoh360 captures, while LPIPS drops by 26% and 11%.

H. Applications
The trained map also supports the simulation of other
camera and LiDAR models and surface extraction. The field
answers arbitrary ray queries, so it can simulate sensors it was
never trained on (Sec. VIII-H1). Its field can be polygonized
into an explicit surface (Sec. VIII-H2).
1) Sensor Simulation: After training, SpaRV renders a different sensor by changing only the ray generator; the geometry
and appearance stay fixed. Fig. 20 shows three camera models
(pinhole, fisheye, and omnidirectional) and three LiDAR scan
patterns (AVIA, MID360, and OS128), all from one trained
map. The Livox-style AVIA and MID360 patterns use timeordered scan tables from the official Livox scan-mode assets.
OS128 is a wide-field ring-azimuth grid approximation [60].
For each preset, SpaRV ray-marches those rays and converts
the rendered median depth into per-frame point clouds.

IX. C ONCLUSION AND F UTURE W ORK
This paper presented SpaRV, a sparse radiance voxel map
that fuses LiDAR and cameras for geometrically consistent
mapping and rendering. A differentiable ray marcher renders
the sparse voxels along each ray, so LiDAR rays supervise
the map alongside images. LiDAR depth supervision and occupied voxel guidance ground the geometry, so the map stays
consistent at viewpoints away from the captured trajectory.
Map training alternates with photometric optimization of the
camera poses and focal lengths, which sharpens the textures of
the map. A granularity-driven cut of the octree bounds GPU
memory in training and rendering, so SpaRV reconstructs and
renders the kilometer-scale scene on a single 24 GB GPU. The
trained maps also support surface extraction and the simulation
of other camera and LiDAR models.
SpaRV stores geometry and appearance in the same voxels,
but texture is often high-frequency where the geometry is not:
a flat poster needs small voxels for its texture, not for its
shape. Keeping geometry in the sparse voxels and moving
appearance to a more compact representation would let the
map resolve finer detail within the same GPU memory. SpaRV
also assumes a static scene and carries no semantic labels.
We leave the decoupled representation, dynamic objects, and
semantics to future work.
