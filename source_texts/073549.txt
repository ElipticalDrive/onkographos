Background: Cinematic rendering (CR) a new method of 3D computed tomography (CT) volumetric visualization
that produces photorealistic images. As with traditional 3D visualization methods, CR may prove to be of value
in providing important information when evaluating regions of complex anatomy such as the heart.
Methods: The gated, IV contrast-enhanced chest CT angiogram data from three recent patients were evaluated
with CR. Image comparision demonstrates the diﬀerence between CR and traditional volume rendering (VR),
owing to a more complex lighting model that enhances surface detail and produces realistic shadows to add
depth to 3D visualizations.
Results: Representative examples of normal cardiac anatomy, a coronary artery stenosis, and an intracardiac
malignant neoplasm are presented with 2D multiplanar reconstruction, traditional VR and CR. A potential pitfall
in CR utilization, namely the possibility of obscuring important pathology, is demonstrated and discussed.
Conclusions: CR is a promising method to enhance display volumetric CT data and should prove useful in diagnosis, treatment planning, surgical navigation, trainee education, and patient engagement. However, further
study is needed to establish the advantaged and disadvantages of CR in comparison to other 3D methods.

Advances in multidetector CT (MDCT) hardware and 3D rendering
post-processing tools have made MDCT a pivotal diagnostic tool for
cardiac and cardiovascular imaging.1 Both maximum intensity projection (MIP) and volume rendering (VR) are used for 3D rendering of
cardiovascular MDCT datasets,2 and provide a global overview of volumetric data as well as detailed anatomic and pathologic information
that can be diﬃcult to appreciate on 2D images. Recently, a new rendering method has become available that is similar to VR, but incorporates a more complex lighting model to create photorealistic
images. This method, known as cinematic rendering (CR),3–5 has not
yet been widely studied but has shown initial promise in the depiction
of complex anatomy and pathology in a range of diﬀerent body regions.6,7 As with traditional VR, this new technique provides particularly detailed visualizations of high density objects, including contrastenhanced vessels and highly vascularized structures, as well as soft
tissues, organs and musculoskeletal structures. In this manuscript, we
describe the technical principles involved in the creation of CR visualizations, compare the image display of available 3D rendering tools,

The creation of the CR visualizations parallels traditional VR in several
aspects. First, the thin-slice reconstructed images are stacked to create a
volume composed of isotropic voxels. Each of these voxels is assigned a
color and transparency that is based on the attenuation (i.e. the tissue
composition) within the voxel. However, at this stage of image creation
the technique diverges from VR. While VR makes use of a local lighting
model based on ray casting in which the pixels that are displayed are a
result of a combination of the color and transparency of all of the voxels
that intercede between the light source and the viewer, CR is based on a
global lighting model that takes into account all of the propagating light
rays that pass through the volumetric data as well as eﬀects on those
light rays beyond attenuation (i.e. scatter and the impact of diﬀerent
tissue types in adjacent voxels). The global lighting model is the basis
for the photorealism in CR images.5
Additional considerations that reﬁne CR photorealism include the
incorporation of shadowing eﬀects that derive from assuming that the
light source can be obscured by objects within the visualized volume.
Such shadowing is not typically included in traditional VR, although it
is possible to incorporate.8 Furthermore, while CR can use one or more
unidirectional light sources as is done in VR, more typically an environment map is used (i.e. texture maps that are related to the
brightnesses of surfaces within a virtual scene and more accurately
reﬂect realistic lighting scenarios).5

workstation is needed to optimize CR visualizations, just as is true with
any 3D methodology. Appropriate selection of window presets using
either a ramp (for tissues diﬀering markedly in attenuation such as bone

4. Discussion
Representative 2D MPR, VR and CR visualizations are shown in
Figs. 1–3. In Fig. 1, an example of normal left atrial appendage (red
arrow) anatomy from an 83-year-old female patient is demonstrated
with CR. The shadowing eﬀects that are produced by the global lighting
model and the assumption that the lighting source can be obscured
allow for the relative positions of the structures within the visualized
volume to be appreciated on this single view. In particular, there is
obscuration of the objects inferior to the lesser curvature of the aortic
arch as well as between the main pulmonary artery and the left atrial
appendage but well-detailed visualization of the left pulmonary arterial
and venous vasculature and branches of the left main coronary artery.
Fig. 2 demonstrates the visualization of a stenosis (red arrows in
Fig. 2A–C) in the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery in a 50year-old male patient on both a curved multi-planar reformatted image
(Fig. 2A) as well as on multiple diﬀerent CR images (Fig. 2B–D). In this
patient, the contrast-ﬁlled lumen of the vessel is easily delineated from
the soft plaque of the stenosis. For potential surgical planning, the CR
images provide a global overview of the relative positions of other
cardiac structures including the nearby left atrial appendage. Note in
Fig. 2D that a change in windowing settings leads to visualization of
partially opaciﬁed blood in the main pulmonary artery (red arrowheads). This partially opaciﬁed blood as well as the associated shadowing prevent adequate evaluation of the LAD stenosis.

As demonstrated in these cases, the CR technique can be used to
create photorealistic visualizations from standard volumetric MDCT
data. The displays provide high levels of anatomic detail that may prove
to be of value in regions of complex anatomy and pathology. The
multiple, closely situated cardiovascular structures in the mediastinum
are one such example and should be an area of active investigation into
the clinical role of CR images. Clearly, studies that evaluate the ﬁndings
on CR visualizations relative to MIP and VR images and that correlate
those ﬁndings to surgical and pathologic gold standards are needed in
order to assess the clinical utility of this novel technique. Ultimately, CR
images will almost certainly play a role in patient engagement and
trainee education, and in some instances may obviate the need for 3D
printing for pre-surgical planning. Whether there are additional potential advantages over standard 3D methods remains to be determined.
An apparent limitation of CR is illustrated in the 2nd case. While the
shadowing eﬀects that arise from the global lighting model that is used
contribute to the photorealistic quality of the images, shadowing can
also potentially obscure important pathology. Therefore, as with any 3D
rendering technique, it is important for the user to examine CR visualizations from multiple diﬀerent views and to interactively alter the
windowing settings to increase and decrease the transparency of
overlapping anatomic structures, and to use CR in conjunction with
