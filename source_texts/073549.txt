### ABSTRACT

Background: Cinematic rendering (CR) a new method of 3D computed tomography (CT) volumetric visualization that produces photorealistic images. As with traditional 3D visualization methods, CR may prove to be of value in providing important information when evaluating regions of complex anatomy such as the heart. Methods: The gated, IV contrast-enhanced chest CT angiogram data from three recent patients were evaluated with CR. Image comparision demonstrates the difference between CR and traditional volume rendering (VR), owing to a more complex lighting model that enhances surface detail and produces realistic shadows to add depth to 3D visualizations.

Results: Representative examples of normal cardiac anatomy, a coronary artery stenosis, and an intracardiac malignant neoplasm are presented with 2D multiplanar reconstruction, traditional VR and CR. A potential pitfall in CR utilization, namely the possibility of obscuring important pathology, is demonstrated and discussed. Conclusions: CR is a promising method to enhance display volumetric CT data and should prove useful in diagnosis, treatment planning, surgical navigation, trainee education, and patient engagement. However, further study is needed to establish the advantaged and disadvantages of CR in comparison to other 3D methods.

### 1. Introduction

Advances in multidetector CT (MDCT) hardware and 3D rendering post-processing tools have made MDCT a pivotal diagnostic tool for cardiac and cardiovascular imaging.1 Both maximum intensity projection (MIP) and volume rendering (VR) are used for 3D rendering of cardiovascular MDCT datasets,2 and provide a global overview of volumetric data as well as detailed anatomic and pathologic information that can be difficult to appreciate on 2D images. Recently, a new rendering method has become available that is similar to VR, but incorporates a more complex lighting model to create photorealistic images. This method, known as cinematic rendering (CR),3–5 has not yet been widely studied but has shown initial promise in the depiction of complex anatomy and pathology in a range of different body regions.6,7 As with traditional VR, this new technique provides particularly detailed visualizations of high density objects, including contrastenhanced vessels and highly vascularized structures, as well as soft tissues, organs and musculoskeletal structures. In this manuscript, we describe the technical principles involved in the creation of CR visualizations, compare the image display of available 3D rendering tools,

and discuss potential clinical applications in cardiovascular imaging.

### 2. Methods

### 2.1. Imaging protocol

MDCT datasets depicting normal cardiac anatomy (patient in Fig. 1\), a coronary artery stenosis (patient in Fig. 2\), and a primary cardiac spindle cell tumor (patient in Fig. 3\) were selected as representative examples for creation of CR visualizations. All patients were scanned on a Siemens SOMATOM Force 384-slice CT scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using our institution's standard clinical gated chest CT protocol (Care kV with reference 120 kVp and with CareDose with 320 reference mAs and 128 × 0.6 mm collimation) following the administration of 150 cc Omnipaque 350 via power injection into peripheral intravenous line at a rate of 5 cc/second.

The visualizations were created on a stand-alone SyngoVia VB20 workstation (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The creation of the CR visualizations parallels traditional VR in several aspects. First, the thin-slice reconstructed images are stacked to create a volume composed of isotropic voxels. Each of these voxels is assigned a color and transparency that is based on the attenuation (i.e. the tissue composition) within the voxel. However, at this stage of image creation the technique diverges from VR. While VR makes use of a local lighting model based on ray casting in which the pixels that are displayed are a result of a combination of the color and transparency of all of the voxels that intercede between the light source and the viewer, CR is based on a global lighting model that takes into account all of the propagating light rays that pass through the volumetric data as well as effects on those light rays beyond attenuation (i.e. scatter and the impact of different tissue types in adjacent voxels). The global lighting model is the basis for the photorealism in CR images.5

Additional considerations that refine CR photorealism include the incorporation of shadowing effects that derive from assuming that the light source can be obscured by objects within the visualized volume. Such shadowing is not typically included in traditional VR, although it is possible to incorporate.8 Furthermore, while CR can use one or more unidirectional light sources as is done in VR, more typically an environment map is used (i.e. texture maps that are related to the brightnesses of surfaces within a virtual scene and more accurately reflect realistic lighting scenarios).5

Ultimately, real-time interaction between the radiologist and the workstation is needed to optimize CR visualizations, just as is true with any 3D methodology. #### 3. Results

Representative 2D MPR, VR and CR visualizations are shown in Figs. 1–3. In Fig. 1, an example of normal left atrial appendage (red arrow) anatomy from an 83-year-old female patient is demonstrated with CR. The shadowing effects that are produced by the global lighting model and the assumption that the lighting source can be obscured allow for the relative positions of the structures within the visualized volume to be appreciated on this single view. In particular, there is obscuration of the objects inferior to the lesser curvature of the aortic arch as well as between the main pulmonary artery and the left atrial appendage but well-detailed visualization of the left pulmonary arterial and venous vasculature and branches of the left main coronary artery.

Fig. 2 demonstrates the visualization of a stenosis (red arrows in Fig. 2A–C) in the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery in a 50 year-old male patient on both a curved multi-planar reformatted image (Fig. 2A) as well as on multiple different CR images (Fig. 2B–D). In this patient, the contrast-filled lumen of the vessel is easily delineated from the soft plaque of the stenosis. For potential surgical planning, the CR images provide a global overview of the relative positions of other cardiac structures including the nearby left atrial appendage. Note in Fig. 2D that a change in windowing settings leads to visualization of partially opacified blood in the main pulmonary artery (red arrowheads). This partially opacified blood as well as the associated shadowing prevent adequate evaluation of the LAD stenosis.

Fig. 3 shows a right ventricular malignant spindle cell neoplasm

(red arrows) in a 64-year-old male patient who presented with shortness of breath depicted with axial CT (A), VR (B), and CR (C,D). The CR images (C,D) provide high contrast resolution for the differentiation of the mass from the surrounding contrast-opacified blood and, similar to the VR image in (B), allow for the discernment of the portion of the lobulated mass that extends into the right ventricular outflow tract.

#### 4. Discussion

As demonstrated in these cases, the CR technique can be used to create photorealistic visualizations from standard volumetric MDCT data. The displays provide high levels of anatomic detail that may prove to be of value in regions of complex anatomy and pathology. The multiple, closely situated cardiovascular structures in the mediastinum are one such example and should be an area of active investigation into the clinical role of CR images. Clearly, studies that evaluate the findings on CR visualizations relative to MIP and VR images and that correlate those findings to surgical and pathologic gold standards are needed in order to assess the clinical utility of this novel technique. Ultimately, CR images will almost certainly play a role in patient engagement and trainee education, and in some instances may obviate the need for 3D printing for pre-surgical planning. Whether there are additional potential advantages over standard 3D methods remains to be determined.

An apparent limitation of CR is illustrated in the 2nd case. While the shadowing effects that arise from the global lighting model that is used contribute to the photorealistic quality of the images, shadowing can also potentially obscure important pathology.
