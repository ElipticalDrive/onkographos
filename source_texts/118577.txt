Summary
Physical inspections in various contexts (industrial, operational, medical, etc.) often require
surface analysis, but how is it possible to review or improve past inspections retrospectively, even
when the object under investigation is no longer available? Classical photogrammetry enables 3D
reconstructions but often struggles with incomplete photo sets or noise. This study integrates
photogrammetry with Gaussian Splatting, a recent technique enhancing visualisation via colourbased 3D rendering; virtual images obtained from Gaussian-Splatting renders enable the
retrospective revisiting of photogrammetry-based reconstructions, improving their quality and
accuracy. Two case studies in aerospace maintenance and medical imaging suggest that this
approach can enhance ex-post surface inspections, allowing for a closer examination of details that
were initially overlooked. The novelty of this contribution lies in the use of virtual renderings not
only for visualisation purposes, but also as supplementary data to enrich 3D reconstruction,
combining the visual strength of Gaussian Splatting with the geometric reliability of
photogrammetry.

Introduction
How can surface inspections for objects such as industrial equipment, operating vehicles or
medical screening results be facilitated? How can past inspections be documented and what
opportunities exist to revisit them in light of newly emerged or previously overlooked details?
Photogrammetry (P) is a well-established technique for reconstructing three-dimensional (3D)
geometry from a set of two-dimensional (2D) photos of an object of interest, taken from different
angles [1,2]. Modern P broadens the scope, offering a range of image-based measurement and 3D
reconstruction methods, through which point clouds representing the surfaces of the object can be
generated by combining standard photogrammetric principles with computer-vision methods, most
notably Structure from Motion (SfM) and Multi-View Stereo (MVS). SfM estimates camera poses
and yields a sparse 3D reconstruction from overlapping images, while MVS densifies the
reconstruction by matching image content across multiple views, thus producing a dense point
cloud and, if needed, a surface model such as a textured mesh or an orthophoto [11,12].
A key advantage of P is the relative simplicity and speed of the input image-acquisition stage,
facilitated by the widespread availability of smartphone cameras and advanced processing
techniques [3]. In this sense, P can avoid the need for specialised and expensive acquisition
devices, such as laser scanners or LiDAR, and is therefore widely applied in various inspection
fields, including engineering, architecture and geotechnics [4,5]. This consideration, however,
mainly refers to the acquisition of the input photos and should not be extended to the whole

measurement process. In applications requiring dimensional metrological traceability, particular
care must be devoted to the definition of scale and, when needed, of a reference coordinate system
[1,2]. Unlike LiDAR acquisitions, photogrammetric reconstructions do not inherently contain this
information and may therefore require additional operations, such as surveys with other
dimensional-measurement instruments (e.g., total stations) or the placement of pre-calibrated
references/scale bars in the scene.
P primarily focuses on geometric reconstruction while often overlooking colour information.
Although colour information is used in the image composition process, it is rarely prominent in
the resulting point cloud, especially when point density is low. Additionally, P can be sensitive to
external factors – such as lighting conditions, shadows and background elements – and to optically
challenging surfaces, such as reflective ones, which may hinder the 3D reconstruction process [6].
Gaussian Splatting (G) is a recent technique that starts from a set of 2D photos – which can be the
same as those used in P – but, after an initial camera-pose estimation step comparable to the SfM
stage used in P, follows a different, predominantly colour/radiance-based representation.
Specifically, G represents the scene through a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian ellipsoids with
optimised positions, colours and opacities. These ellipsoids are projected and blended – i.e.,
“splatted” onto the observation plane – to generate visually consistent 2D renderings of the object
of interest when viewed from specific angles, even those not originally captured in the real
photographs [7,15]. In a somewhat unconventional interpretation, this approach resembles an
"impressionist" technique, where colour blending creates a perceptually accurate representation of
the scene. G offers two main advantages: (i) rapid and effective processing, even with limited input
data, and (ii) robustness to background noise and environmental inconsistencies. While it does not
provide an explicit 3D geometric reconstruction, it enables high-fidelity virtual/visual navigation
around the object.
This study proposes a method that integrates traditional P, based on real photos of the object, with
virtual images derived from G renders. The core idea is to synthesise targeted G-derived virtual
views that can be used, when needed, to improve the P reconstruction. The aim is to define a
retrospective inspection methodology in which G serves to enhance the P reconstruction, not only
as a visualisation tool but also as an intermediate source of additional information. In particular,
the visual strengths and robustness of G can be used to improve the quality of the P reconstruction,
especially in critical regions. Importantly, this synergy enables a form of “back-in-time” (or “backon-purpose”) analysis, allowing inspectors to virtually revisit a scene when needed and supplement
their investigations, even if the original object is no longer available.

Methodology and case studies
The proposed method can be applied to any scenario requiring surface analysis of a given object
at specific moments in time, based on photographs. It consists of the following steps, schematised
in Fig. 1:
• Initial photo acquisition: At time t0, a collection of real photographs of an object is captured
(steps a, b and c at t0). For example, in Fig. 1 a hypothetical object consists of three joined
elements: a yellow semi-cylinder, a green elongated parallelepiped with a pyramid top and a
red squat parallelepiped. If the object remains ideally static, photos can be acquired by a single
moving camera, recording a video that is later split into individual frames. If the object is in
motion, multiple synchronised cameras can capture simultaneous photographs, ensuring
coverage from different angles.

• Processing of P and G: The acquired photos are processed to generate a point cloud (P0) via P
and a navigable 3D rendering (G0) via G (steps d and e at t0). These processing stages can be
carried out using widely adopted software platforms. For instance, P reconstruction may be
performed in Agisoft Metashape Professional, while the G scene and the corresponding virtual
renders may be generated within a Python-based framework and visualised through an
interactive viewer for image-based rendering. From a methodological standpoint, the initial
SfM stage – which estimates the camera parameters, including intrinsic parameters (such as
focal length and principal point) and extrinsic parameters (i.e., camera pose in terms of position
and orientation) within a common reference frame – can be shared by the two processes. To
ensure consistency between the P and G processes, the camera/scene calibration can be
performed once in the P process and then imported into the G process by transferring the camera
parameters associated with the individual views. This avoids redundant alignment or
registration steps, simplifies the overall procedure and limits possible inconsistencies due to
separate pose-estimation stages. The P reconstruction can then be densified through MVS,
leading to a dense point cloud and, if required, to a 3D surface model of the object [11,12].
In the present study, the main output considered from the P process is a (dense) point cloud [2].
Any subsequent mesh generation and texture projection are intentionally excluded, because the
point-cloud domain – prior to any surface interpolation or visual refinement – provides a more
conservative and transparent representation of local data support. In this domain, variations in
sampling density, gaps and uncertain regions remain directly visible, whereas meshing and
texturing add a surface-continuity layer that is highly valuable for visualisation but can make
local undersampling less evident [12].

• “Back-in-time” (or “back-on-purpose”) virtual image generation: If further investigation
is needed due to the incompleteness or imperfections of P0 (e.g., the reconstruction of the
pyramid top of the green element in Fig. 1 is not sufficiently accurate, as evidenced by the
sceptical emoji in the bottom-right corner), additional images of the object can be generated
from optimal angles using G0 (steps b and c at t1). More specifically, selected virtual renders
can be generated from the G scene and used to complement the original set of real photos.
Because the G scene shares the same camera/scene calibration as the P reconstruction, these
virtual renders are associated with camera poses that are already consistent with those of the
real photos. This retrospective in-depth analysis is entirely based on acquisitions of the object
made at t0 and is unrelated to the actual condition of the object at t1; in principle, it remains
feasible even if the object is no longer available at that later time (see step a at t1).

with the original ones (in black) to feed a new photogrammetric process, generating an enriched
point cloud (P1), with increased density and accuracy in critical regions (steps d and e at t1),
ultimately resulting in a satisfied emoji. It should be noted that the virtual images were captured
at t1, while the real photos were taken at t0, even though both depict (portions of) the physical
object as it appeared at t0.

The proposed method can be applied in operational contexts where the surface of an object of
interest (e.g., a vehicle component, machinery, structure or even a living being) needs to be
examined, to reconstruct its 3D geometric and colour characteristics for a specific purpose (e.g.,
scheduled maintenance, conformity verification, health assessment, etc.). It can be especially
relevant when the initial P reconstruction is locally weakened by limited coverage or
geometric/optical conditions that typically require denser image acquisition. Possible examples
include protruding features, recessed regions, undercuts and narrow cavities, where self-occlusions
reduce the effectiveness of conventional multi-view matching. A further potentially favourable
case concerns optically challenging surfaces – e.g., reflective, translucent or weakly textured areas
– for which the P process may become unstable, whereas the G representation may still provide
visually coherent virtual views [2,7]. In such situations, the renders are not intended to replace
missing physical evidence, but to make better use of the information already embedded in the
original image set and to support a more complete subsequent P reconstruction. Accordingly, the
objective is not to replace or outperform conventional P, but to provide a practical tool for
reinforcing existing P datasets when targeted retrospective analysis is required. Below is a
description of two case studies that illustrate the application of the proposed method in diverse
contexts.

1. Aerospace maintenance: At time t0, a series of photographs of disassembled aircraft
components, subject to inspection for scheduled maintenance, are captured by an operator using
the 12 MP main camera of an iPhone 14 Plus (26 mm equivalent lens, f/1.5 aperture), moving
around each component along an approximately circular path and from slightly different
elevations, so as to ensure substantial overlap between consecutive views and adequate
coverage of the main inspection areas [13]. The resulting dataset is then processed through P
and G. P reconstruction is performed in Agisoft Metashape Professional version 2.2.2 (build
21287). Image matching is carried out with a limit of 40,000 key points and 10,000 tie points
per image, using both generic and estimated-reference preselection, while depth maps are
generated at high quality with mild filtering. The camera poses estimated during the initial P
image-alignment stage (SfM) are then manually imported into the G process and used as a

common geometric reference for the corresponding views, avoiding the need for redundant

CUDA rasterisation routines. The 3D scene can be visualised through the open-source System
for Image-Based Rendering (SIBR) viewer, and the render generation procedure is used to

A preliminary examination of the point-cloud reconstruction resulting from the P process does
not reveal any critical issues. However, at time t1, an experienced technician re-examines it,
identifying a potential defect in the fuselage – previously overlooked but now considered
relevant due to the recent introduction of stricter inspection protocols (see Fig. 2.a). In
particular, the defect appears to be located in the upper part of a vertical slot, which seems to
present a form of superficial irregularity. Consequently, further investigation is required. In this
region, the initial P reconstruction appears locally weakened, possibly because of the reflective
metallic surface of the object and/or insufficient photographic coverage of the vertical slot area.

enhanced point-cloud reconstruction.
After the point cloud is reconstructed and examined by a dermatologist, no significant anomalies
are detected. However, several months later, at time t1, another dermatologist examines the
same patient and identifies a suspicious mole. The previously generated navigable 3D rendering
allows clearer visualisation of the original characteristics of the mole. New rendered images
corresponding to t0 can then be used as additional input data for a new P process, leading to an
enhanced point-cloud reconstruction. This facilitates a direct comparison with the current
appearance of the mole, supporting a more informed clinical assessment based on changes in
shape and colour observed between t0 and t1.
As shown in Fig. 3, the enhanced point cloud (d) at the suspected mole is substantially denser
and more detailed than the initial one (b). The reconstruction is enhanced not only in terms of
geometric accuracy but, importantly, in its colour fidelity, allowing for a more precise medical
evaluation. The case study also shows that the 3D rendering produced by the G process
represents a digital archive of previous inspections, which is readily accessible, centralised and
easier to review than the original set of individual photographs from which it was constructed.

Consistency check
This section includes a three-phase consistency check of the results obtained from the integration
of P with G. The aim is to assess (i) whether G-derived renders can serve as surrogates of real
photographic acquisitions, especially when a limited number of photos are available at the input
level; (ii) whether the inclusion of such renders degrades/distorts the general geometric accuracy
of the resulting P reconstruction; and (iii) whether the same render-enriched P reconstruction
remains locally consistent when the analysis is restricted to a specific critical region. The three
phases are described with reference to the first case study on the aircraft fuselage.

Phase 1: Photo-render similarity. Four sets of photo acquisitions are considered. Set A represents
the “complete” case (baseline) and consists of 260 real photos. Set B retains 221/260 photos
(85%), excluding the remaining 39 (15%) selected at random. These 221 photos are then used
to build a G scene, which is in turn used to generate 39 renders from the same poses/viewpoints
as the excluded photos; this yields a mixed P dataset of 221 photos + 39 renders = 260 images.
Set C follows the same procedure with 182 photos + 78 renders (70% versus 30%) and Set D
uses 143 photos + 117 renders (55% versus 45%). For Sets C and D, the G scenes are
reconstructed only from the corresponding subsets of 182 and 143 real photos; renders are then
generated from the poses/viewpoints corresponding to the (respectively) 78 and 117 excluded
photo acquisitions, so that each render is homologous (pose-matched) to a specific excluded
photo. As already noted, pose/viewpoint consistency is ensured by importing the SfM-based P
camera parameters into the G process, so that both processes share a common reference frame
and no independent alignment is required for render generation [2].
Similarity between each render and its corresponding excluded photo is quantitatively evaluated
using the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), which compares local neighbourhoods between
two images and combines three perceptual terms (luminance, contrast and structural
information) into local similarity scores that are then aggregated into a global index ranging
from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating higher similarity [10]. SSIM is often reported
alongside other metrics, such as Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Learned Perceptual
Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS), which are not considered here for simplicity [7,10].

excluded from the construction of the G scene (a) with a homologous render from the same
pose/viewpoint (b), with reference to Set C. The resulting SSIM is 0.69. When the comparison
is restricted to the dashed red region (which focuses on the fuselage structure), the SSIM value
increases to 0.84, indicating a high similarity for the object of interest in this specific case. This

evaluates the overall geometric accuracy of the 3D reconstruction, obtained through P,
considering the P reconstruction at a broader scene/object scale rather than focusing on a
specific critical region. This overall-scale verification is based on a set of reference distances
distributed around the object, namely distances between pairs of markers placed in the
surrounding scene. More specifically, 12 inter-marker distances involve markers mounted on
reference boards positioned on the ground near the fuselage, visible in Fig. 4.a,b. The other 13
reference distances involve markers located in proximity to the object, in particular on its
supporting structure, which is partially visible in the same images (in blue, under the fuselage).
Although these markers are not placed directly on the inspected surface of the fuselage, they
remain spatially close to it, with typical distances between the supporting structure and the
fuselage surfaces on the order of a few centimetres.

distances (on the boards placed on the ground and the fuselage supporting structure) were precalibrated using a high-accuracy coordinate measuring machine (CMM), DEA Iota-0101 with
MPE ≈ 5 µm, i.e., about two orders of magnitude smaller than typical P geometric accuracy [1,
16, 17]. Accordingly, a total of 25 inter-marker distances are considered, with lengths typically
on the order of a few hundred millimetres, thereby ensuring compatibility with the measuring
volume of the CMM used for their calibration.
For each distance, the marker-to-marker measurement obtained after P reconstruction is compared
with the corresponding reference “true” value. The geometric error for each distance is then
computed as: ε = (measured distance − reference value). None of the markers or distances
considered in this verification were used to define the scale and, consequently, the metrological
traceability of the P reconstruction to the unit of length; this was done deliberately to preserve
the independence of the verification process from the P reconstruction process itself [2]. The
procedure is repeated for each of the four sets already described in Phase 1 (A, B, C and D),
after the P reconstruction, using the available photos and (when applicable) renders.
Fig. 7 reports the results of this geometric consistency check, in terms of summary statistics of
the error (ε) distributions for the marker-to-marker distances. As expected, the range of
variability of errors (which can be positive or negative quantities, generally on a submillimetre
scale) tends to increase as a larger fraction of renders replaces real photos in the P process.
However, variations remain relatively limited and no significant differences are observed (as
also suggested by the overlapping boxplots). Mild heteroscedasticity is also observed, with a
slight increase in dispersion of ε as the render fraction increases [1,10].
Overall, the results provide preliminary evidence that render-enriched P processes can yield
geometric reconstructions with accuracy comparable to those obtained with larger sets of real
photos. Even under relatively extreme conditions – such as Set D, where only 55% of the images
are real photos – the inclusion of G renders enables a reconstruction that is comparable to what
would be obtained if an equivalent number of additional real photos were available (cf. Set A).
A complementary verification, focusing on the local geometric plausibility and consistency of
the render-enriched P reconstructions in a specific critical region, is introduced in Phase 3.

Fig. 8. Flowchart for checking the geometric consistency of the local P reconstruction. Starting from
260 real photos and a common SfM-based camera-pose estimation, a subset of only 40 photos covering a
critical region of the object is then identified; three reconstruction paths are then compared: a pure-P MVS
reconstruction from the subset of 40 photos; Case 0, in which a local G scene is reconstructed from the
subset of 40 photos and used to generate 10 “favourable” virtual renders to enrich the subsequent MVS
reconstruction, restricted to the critical region; and Cases 1 to 4, in which progressively depleted subsets of
the 40 local photos are used to reconstruct the corresponding G scenes and to generate 10 virtual renders
analogous to those of Case 0, again used to enrich the subsequent MVS reconstruction restricted to the
critical region. The resulting point clouds are compared locally through C2M distances, using as reference
a mesh extracted from the pure-P reconstruction.
The same 260 real photos previously considered as the complete set (cf. Set A in Phase 1) are
taken as input. A common SfM stage is first performed to estimate the camera poses, which are
then used as the shared reference frame for the subsequent reconstructions. In the pure-P path,
MVS is applied directly to a subset of 40 photos, all including the vertical-slot region already
shown in Fig. 2, yielding a dense point cloud; this localised densification is intended to obtain
a more detailed reconstruction of a potentially critical region, where further inspection is
specifically required. In Case 0, the same subset of 40 photos, together with the poses obtained
from the previous SfM stage, is imported into the G process to reconstruct a local G scene. This
local G scene is then manually navigated to generate 10 virtual renders from “favourable”
viewpoints, selected so that the vertical-slot region occupies most of the image and the view
directions remain relatively close to the local surface normal. Since these renders are generated
within the same reference frame as the P reconstruction, their virtual camera poses are already
aligned with the real-photo poses. The renders are then added to the subset of 40 real photos
and MVS is repeated, yielding a render-enriched dense point cloud. The number of virtual
renders was set to 10 because it provides a non-negligible enrichment relative to the 40 real
photos used to build the local G scene, while still allowing the effect of virtual renders to be
assessed under conditions of relatively limited real-photo support.
Cases 1 to 4 repeat the same procedure under progressively depleted local input conditions.
Starting from the same 40 photos covering the critical region, x images are removed before the
G reconstruction, with x = 18, 23, 27 and 31, respectively. The remaining (40 – x) photos are
used to build a local G scene, from which 10 virtual renders are generated at the same poses as
in Case 0. These renders are combined with the corresponding reduced real-photo set of (40 –
x) images and a new MVS reconstruction is performed. The four cases (Case 1 to 4) therefore
test the plausibility and stability of G-derived renders when the number of real photos used to
construct the local G scene becomes increasingly limited.
Since no nominal reference geometry is available for the vertical slot, the point-cloud portion
corresponding to the critical region in the pure-P reconstruction is converted into a triangular
mesh and used as a local reference geometry. This reference is not intended to represent an
absolute ground truth, but rather an internal baseline for detecting whether the addition of
virtual renders induces local geometric distortions relative to the original pure-P reconstruction.

and the cloud-to-mesh (C2M) distances to the reference mesh are computed. In this analysis,
signed C2M distances are considered: positive and negative values indicate that points lie on
opposite sides of the oriented reference mesh, rather than representing positive or negative
distances in an absolute sense. Therefore, values close to zero indicate local agreement with the
reference mesh, whereas a systematic shift towards either positive or negative values may reveal

The signed C2M distributions in Fig. 9 are broadly compatible across the pure-P baseline, Case
0 and Cases 1 to 4. Mean and median values remain on the order of a few tenths of a millimetre,
and the interquartile ranges and extreme values do not show a systematic divergence as the
number of real photos used to build the local G scene decreases. Although some individual
(signed) C2M distances, especially the extrema, may appear relatively large, they should be
interpreted as point-wise deviations computed over very dense point clouds, rather than as direct
indicators of the dimensional accuracy of the reconstructed surface as a whole. In dense pointcloud data, local scatter, edge effects and isolated points can contribute to relatively wide pointwise C2M distributions; however, when the reconstruction is interpreted at the surface level,
these deviations are averaged over thousands to tens of thousands of points. This is consistent
with the sub-millimetre accuracy observed in the inter-marker distance verification reported in
Fig. 7.

not introduce evident local distortions in the critical region. In addition, within the cropped
volume around the vertical slot, the signed C2M distances appear to be rather uniformly
distributed in space in all cases, without evident localised clusters of larger deviations. This

Discussion and conclusion
This paper shows the potential of the synergistic integration of P and G in surface-inspection
contexts such as maintenance, conformity verification and medical imaging. This approach may
also prove useful in other fields where the geometric and chromatic reconstruction of a scene, as
well as retrospective access to past conditions, may be required, for example in archaeological
research, cultural heritage conservation and forensic investigations; these broader applications will
be explored in future work. From a practical standpoint, a relevant advantage of the proposed
methodology is that it can support ex-post inspection decisions in situations where a new physical
acquisition is impossible, impractical or disproportionately expensive. This is particularly relevant
when the initial photo acquisition is sufficient for global reconstruction but not dense enough in
specific local regions, such as protruding edges, recessed details, undercuts or small cavities. In
these cases, G can facilitate the generation of additional targeted virtual views, which may improve
the completeness of the subsequent P reconstruction. However, the proposed use of G-derived
renders does not relax the basic acquisition requirements of P. Sufficient image overlap, suitable
angular distribution and adequate coverage of the regions of interest remain necessary
prerequisites, consistently with standard close-range P practice [13].
Moreover, this approach holds promise for the non-invasive study of difficult-to-replace objects,
for which “back-in-time” (or “back-on-purpose”) analysis can significantly support their
documentation and preservation. In general, G may serve a dual role: functioning both as an expost inspection tool and as a dynamic 3D archive, enabling investigations even when the object
under examination is no longer physically available or accessible.
A particularly noteworthy aspect is that even a relatively small set of photos – e.g., just a few dozen
images when the surface to be reconstructed is relatively limited in extent, as in the second case
study – may be sufficient to generate an acceptable G model within several minutes, thanks to the
overall efficiency of the G process in exploiting the available input data [8]. In addition, G
generally appears more robust than P in scenarios involving limited real-photo support or optically
challenging surfaces, such as transparent or reflective ones [8]. Additionally, the combination of
P and G enhances not only the geometric accuracy of the reconstructed object but also the colour
fidelity in the areas requiring closer analysis. Indeed, individual photos may convey locally altered
colour perceptions due to non-uniform lighting conditions, exposure variations or other acquisition

disturbances; in this respect, the G process seems more robust than P, as it exploits colour

particularly important, as colour variations can sometimes indicate degradation, wear or ageing
processes (e.g., corrosion, abrasion, etc.).
A somewhat counterintuitive yet crucial aspect of the proposed method is the improvement
observed in P reconstruction, despite no actual increase in the input data (i.e., real photographs
taken at t0). Notably, the (re)introduction of an intermediate construct – i.e., the virtual images
generated by a colour-driven and navigable G model – leads to an improvement in P
reconstruction. Preliminary tests reported in this paper suggest that the improvements in
densification and accuracy observed in the “enhanced” point cloud (i.e., P1) are comparable to
those obtained by adding new real photographs, taken from optimal angles [9]. This effect is
particularly evident in regions where the initial point cloud generated by P (i.e., P0) lacks sufficient
density. This enhancement is probably due to the more effective utilisation of the existing data –
i.e., real photographs – exploiting not only geometric contours but also colour information. In this
sense, G helps overcome some of the inherent limitations of P; the virtual images produced by G
can be seen as “filtered” or “clean” acquisitions, which focus exclusively on elements of interest,
while removing irrelevant/distracting elements such as background noise, reflections and shadows,
which may hinder the P-based reconstruction of the point cloud. As a result, the virtual images
generated by G seem to enhance the P reconstruction without introducing geometric distortions,
as shown by preliminary consistency checks.
The experiments reported in this study primarily assess the benefits of enriching conventional P
with G-derived renders. A complementary comparison can be made against standalone G: the
integrated approach retains realistic and intuitive scene navigation while coupling it with an
explicit, metrically assessable reconstruction of the inspected surface. A dedicated qualitative and
quantitative analysis is provided in the Supplementary Information.
Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the method does not create new physical
information beyond what is implicitly captured in the original photographs; therefore, if a region
was never observed or was observed too poorly at t0, the retrospective enrichment can only be
partial. Second, the quality of the virtual images depends on the quality, coverage and consistency
of the initial acquisitions and on the reliability of the G model itself. Third, although the
preliminary consistency checks are encouraging, the proposed methodology should currently be
regarded as a support tool for inspection and reconstruction enhancement, rather than as a
replacement for a purposely optimised metrological acquisition when strict geometric accuracy is
required. In this sense, the most appropriate use case is the targeted reinforcement of existing
image datasets, in critical regions that deserve further inspection. In future work, more structured
quantitative tests and related metrics will be carried out, including comparisons between renderenriched P reconstructions and reference surfaces obtained using higher-accuracy measurement
systems, in order to rigorously evaluate the reconstruction accuracy of the proposed methodology
and the benefits of the synergy between P and G across various application contexts. In parallel,
the initial acquisition stage will be analysed more systematically, with the aim of identifying
minimum acquisition conditions – in terms of image overlap, angular distribution and coverage of
the regions of interest – under which the combined use of P and G can be implemented reliably
and reproducibly.
