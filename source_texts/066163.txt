Photorealistic volume rendering suffers from slow convergence with traditional path tracing algorithms and poses significant challenges for realtime applications. In this paper, we present Multi-feature Radiance Baking
Neural Networks (MRBNN), a neural volumetric rendering method that
achieves real-time performance by leveraging analytic decomposition of
scattering with an efficient learned representation. Our method reformulates in-scattering integral in Volumetric Radiance Transfer Equation to
diagonal scaling operator in spherical harmonics spectrum. Building on this
formulation, we propose a compact factorized representation with low-rank
compression in latent space that replaces costly integration. We then introduce efficient dual-pattern feature sampling and a lightweight neural
decoder for fast radiance prediction. With these dedicated designs, MRBNN
achieves 4 ∼ 5× speedup over the state-of-the-art while maintaining high
visual fidelity. Moreover, our method addresses key limitations of prior
work, including over-illumination artifacts and inability to handle volumes
with spatially varying albedo or heterogeneous phase parameters. Extensive
experiments demonstrate that our neural baking method can synthesize
photorealistic images for complex volumes in a few milliseconds.

Introduction
Volumetric rendering has been thoroughly investigated to depict
unique properties of non-surface materials such as clouds, fogs,
and jade. Although certain materials like skin can be approximated

using subsurface scattering [Hanrahan and Krueger 1993], it is inadequate to model correct interaction of ray and particles in general
participating media. Since Kajiya and Von Herzen [1984] introduced
the Volumetric Radiance Transport Equation (VRTE) to computer
graphics, path tracing with Monte Carlo integration method has
been widely adopted. The estimation process is accelerated by generalizing traditional methods to volumetric scattering like better tracing method (e.g. bidirectional path tracing [Lafortune and Willems
1996]), more efficient sampling for path construction and transmittance estimation (e.g. volumetric scattering path guiding [Xu et al.
2024], progressive null-tracking [Misso et al. 2023]), more sophisticated sample-reuse techniques (e.g. volumetric ReSTIR [Lin et al.
2021]) and so on. VRTE is also further derived into other practical
forms [Miller et al. 2019]. However, complex volumetric path tracing is still too costly for real-time applications. Particularly, some
media, notably clouds, have high single-scattering albedo, leading
to visually perceptible radiance contribution even after hundreds of
bounces in a single volume. This makes practical post-processing
like real-time denoising with low samples per pixel (spp) [Chaitanya
et al. 2017] inapplicable, as the input is too far from the converged
result in some circumstances.
Recently, neural methods have been applied to volumetric rendering. Radiance-Predicting Neural Networks (RPNN) [Kallweit et al.
2017] opened up a new paradigm to estimate multi-scattering effects,
achieving speedup of up to three orders of magnitude over CPUbased path tracing. However, RPNN still requires several minutes to
produce a converged result. Multi-feature RPNN (MRPNN) [Hu et al.
2023] improves the algorithm further, reaching up to 40 FPS on an
NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU at 10242 resolution. MRPNN also supports
globally configurable albedo and phase function parameters.
Despite these advances, MRPNN still has some key limitations.
First, its performance remains insufficient in complex real-time
applications for consuming tens of milliseconds in a single pass. Second, it assumes fragmented volumes to be continuous low-density
volumes and thus systematically overestimates global illumination
for such scenes, as shown in Fig. 2. Finally, it requires consistent
albedo and phase function parameters throughout the scene, which
significantly restricts its ability to represent more complex media.

To address these challenges, we propose Multi-feature Radiance
Baking Neural Networks (MRBNN) that exploits the structure of
volumetric scattering. Inspired by Neural Radiosity [Hadadan et al.
2021], we observe that evaluating the integral is more accurate but
computationally expensive than direct prediction for in-scattering
SIGGRAPH Conference Papers ’26, July 19–23, 2026, Los Angeles, CA, USA.
field. By expanding the phase function in Legendre polynomials, we
show that the in-scattering integral forms a diagonal scaling operator in spherical harmonics basis, yielding an equivalent reweighting
that eliminates integration. While such spectral operator form is
analytically compact, its coefficients remain high-dimensional field
that is infeasible to store or evaluate explicitly under dynamic conditions. To bridge this gap, we introduce a compact latent-space
radiance baking that provides a finite-rank, factorized parameterization with practical memory cost. After querying features with
our dual-pattern sampling strategy, we employ a lightweight neural
decoder for interpreting the learned representation to scattering
radiance with substantially low latency. As shown in Fig. 1, MRBNN
achieves significant acceleration over prior work while maintaining
high visual fidelity. It also supports challenging configurations such
as spatially varying albedo and heterogeneous phase parameters
that cannot be handled previously.
In summary, our contributions include the following aspects:
• We reformulate in-scattering integral in VRTE as a diagonal
scaling operator in the spherical harmonics spectrum, which
eliminates explicit spherical integration in theory.
• We propose a compact latent-space neural baking that learns
a low-rank, factorized approximation of such scattering operator with practical space complexity, which can be decoded
by our dual-pattern feature query and lightweight network.
• We design a real-time volumetric renderer that achieves 4 ∼
5× speedup over the state-of-the-art while supporting spatially varying albedo, heterogeneous phase functions and
improved temporal stability.

Related Work
Stochastic Volumetric Ray Tracing. Photorealistic rendering of participating media typically relies on stochastic approaches like Monte
Carlo integration method to solve VRTE numerically. Sampling inside volumes has drawn researchers’ attention as a distinct problem.
To avoid explicit transmittance estimation, null-collision methods
apply delta tracking [Woodcock et al. 1965] to sample the next position; the resulting path-space PDF was analytically formulated by
Miller et al. [2019]. In other sampling methods that require transmittance calculation, Novák et al. [2018] provided a comprehensive
survey of related estimation methods. More recently, power-series
expansion [Georgiev et al. 2019] and unbiased ray marching [Kettunen et al. 2021] have been proposed to introduce a range of novel
transmittance estimators and reduce estimation variance further.
Alternatively, volumetric path tracing can adapt techniques originally developed for surface rendering. For instance, bidirectional
path tracing [Lafortune and Willems 1996] samples from both camera and light, and Metropolis methods [Pauly et al. 2000] focus on
sampling high-contribution paths through mutation and acceptance.
More recent advances include path guiding that performs product
sampling over different integrand components [Herholz et al. 2019;
Xu et al. 2024] or uses neural network to fit target distribution [Dong
et al. 2023], reservoir resampling [Lin et al. 2021], and extension to
kernel-based volumetric primitives [Condor et al. 2025].
Though capable of producing photorealistic images, complex volumetric path tracing is generally unable to converge to a satisfying

result in real time, even with advanced sampling or post-processing
techniques. Our goal is to accelerate this process by leveraging lightweight network that predicts multi-scattering radiance directly.
Neural Rendering. Deep learning has been actively explored to
model neural global illumination for its powerful approximation
capability. Representative approaches include mesh-based rendering with homogeneous translucent materials using DiffusionNet
and BSSRDF [An et al. 2025; Sharp et al. 2022], vertex-based feature
learning with refinement [Su et al. 2025], object-oriented representations for modeling object-environment interactions [Zheng et al.
2024], and transformer-based architectures for end-to-end image
synthesis [Zeng et al. 2025]. However, these methods are designed
for surface-based light transport and rely on the surface RTE, making them ill-suited for real-time volumetric rendering governed by
VRTE. Extending them to participating media either breaks their
core assumptions (e.g. surface vertices and normals) or leads to
unpromising results and cost.
Particularly, neural feature fields have played a key role in graphics in recent years, ranging from 3D reconstruction (e.g. neural
radiance fields [Mildenhall et al. 2021]) to rendering acceleration
(e.g. neural radiance cache [Müller et al. 2021], neural radiosity
[Hadadan et al. 2021; Su et al. 2024]). An important representation
for such field is feature grid, where features are spatially discretized
and interpolated when used. To minimize memory usage, various
strategies including octree [Takikawa et al. 2021], hashing [Müller
et al. 2022] and k-planes decomposition [Cao and Johnson 2023]
were proposed. In this paper, we also utilize feature grid as a baking
approach to handle dynamic material and light conditions.
Precomputed Radiance Transfer (PRT). PRT [Sloan et al. 2002]
approximates indirect illumination by computing transfer matrix
under angular basis, notably spherical harmonics basis. Such representation allows the rendering equation to be evaluated as simple
dot products at runtime, and spherical harmonics also enables handling of dynamic transport [Pan et al. 2007; Sloan et al. 2005]. Recent
advances include using neural network to substitute transport in
PRT [Rainer et al. 2022]. Building on this line of work, we exploit the
zonal convolution properties of spherical harmonics to construct a
latent-space feature grid for dynamic lighting modeling.
Despite its compelling optimization, MRPNN still exhibits several limitations that hinder efficient and robust prediction. First,
to capture surrounding volumetric context, MRPNN relies on multiple complex sampling strategies, leading to numerous irregular
texture lookups and remaining artifacts such as over-illumination in
fragmented or challenging scenes. Second, MRPNN does not model
spatially varying albedo or heterogeneous phase configurations,
and thus cannot handle volumes with scattering coefficient grids
or multiple phase parameters. Finally, its network architecture remains relatively complex and leaves room for further simplification
to better exploit GPU computational efficiency. In this paper, we
introduce a tiny network with compact latent-space baking that
targets to address these problems.

RPNN Series. Among existing methods for photorealistic volumetric rendering, the RPNN family stands out for its impressive
acceleration. Kallweit et al. [2017] pioneered this line by predicting
indirect illumination through layered density field to avoid explicit
simulation of multi-bounce scattering. This process was further
improved by Panin and Nikolenko [2019] through caching intermediate features that achieved 2 ∼ 3× speedup. Hu et al. [2023]
later identified that direct mapping from density field to radiance
field is overly complex, leading to a large model with millions of
parameters and minutes of inference time. To address this problem, MRPNN introduces auxiliary hints on albedo, transmittance
field and cumulative phase, substantially reducing model scale and
enabling real-time performance with globally configurable albedo
and phase. MRPNN also improves shadow boundaries prediction
through additional high-frequency feature sampling.

4 Latent-space Field Decoding
4.1 Sampling Pattern Design
In order to capture both low-frequency and high-frequency details
in the baking, we use dual-pattern sampling as displayed in Fig. 4.
All feature grids adopt a multi-resolution representation as different levels of details (LoD) to model scattering environment across
varying spatial scopes. Normally, given a position 𝑝, features are
sampled at 𝑝 exactly in different LoDs as local sampling. To depict
shadow boundary more accurately, some features are also sampled
towards light from 𝑝 by cone tracing. Although our sampling strategy resembles two-part approach in MRPNN, our implicit baking
enables a much simpler workload for avoiding extensive irregular
texel access.
Naïvely, radiance field feature L𝑙𝑚 could be sampled both locally
and towards light directly. However, as a single sample of SH grid
requires (𝐾 + 1) 2 grids to be sampled, such design will amplify feature extraction cost. Instead, we introduce another 3D grid called
base grid B indexed by position solely. Besides, a mipmapped transmittance map T with respect to incoming light direction is also
introduced as input to assist learning. B and T , instead of (𝐾 + 1) 2
grid components L𝑙𝑚 , adopt towards-light sampling. Our experiments in Sec. 6 show that this does not necessarily affect the final
quality, while it contributes to additional 10% performance gain.

5 Implementation
5.1 Feature Configuration
Practically, we choose 𝑀1 = 𝑀2 = 4 for Eq. 9 to reuse position
samples. The feature grids are sampled within [0, 1] 𝑁 in reality, so
directions and positions are represented by normalized spherical
coordinates and relative coordinate in volume bounding box respectively. cos 𝜃 ∈ [−1, 1] is mapped by (𝑥 +1)/2. Considering that media
with higher albedo scatter rays with increased frequency, finer grid
resolution is allocated by a non-linear mapping (𝑒 𝑥 − 1)/(𝑒 − 1).
And for volume used in production, scattering and extinction coefficients are stored as scalar voxels that are not affected by direction,
therefore we omit 𝜔 in albedo. Finally, the transmittance map stores
optical depth normalized by maximum density. Detailed grid configurations and error analysis on low-order compression are given
in the Supplementary Sec. A.
5.2
Network Implementation
In practice, we use tiny-cuda-nn [Müller 2021] with PyTorch [Paszke
et al. 2019] to implement the network prototype and do training
for convenient network adjustment. On completion of the training phase, we export the parameters to perform inference with
tailored C++ code. We implement our own feature grid module to
fuse sampling within the rendering procedures for avoiding possible
computation redundancy. Furthermore, to maximize GPU utilization, we use fully fused MLP [Müller et al. 2021] with just-in-time
compilation to execute neural network in a single kernel. Practically, it is roughly sufficient to equip each MLP with two 64-neuron
fully-connected layers. We also find it beneficial to use ReLU6 as
the final layer activation in MLPs for numeric stability.

Results and Analysis
We train our model on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU while testing
the performance on an NVIDIA RTX 4080 SUPER GPU. Training
completes within four hours in each scene. Our renderer uses delta
tracking to sample the first bounce to cancel 𝑇𝑟 (𝑝𝑖′ → 𝑝)/𝑓 (𝑝𝑖′ ) in
Eq. 1, which is aligned with implementation in MRPNN. All results
for MRPNN are produced by authors’ open-source implementation.
6.1
Rendering Result and Performance Comparison
Comparison with MRPNN. We first demonstrate our performance
improvement quantitatively in Tab. 1. Compared with MRPNN, our
sampling strategy yields ∼ 1.6× speedup, and our network inference achieves above 20× speedup by eliminating complex module
designs and using five times fewer matrices. Combined, these optimizations result in 4 ∼ 5× acceleration for the entire render loop. It
is worth noting that the performance boost in inference does not
mainly attribute to partitioned WMMA used in tiny-cuda-nn, as
MRPNN implementation also has corresponding option to enable
such feature while no performance gain is observed. This makes
it rather fair to illustrate the importance of our simplification on
network structure.
We further compare our method with MRPNN under the same
time budgets (30 FPS, as a common baseline for real time). As shown
in Fig. 5, our method exhibits comparable numerical accuracy to
MRPNN while producing less noisy results, since we accumulate
more samples but each sample is more biased. Fig. 11 shows comparison of FLIP error map [Andersson et al. 2020] in more scenes under
different light conditions and equal spp. Our method is competitive
with MRPNN in visual quality while achieving a substantial speedup.
See our supplementary video for performance in different scenarios.
Training
Ground Truth Generation. For usual cases, we use the path-tracing
renderer provided by MRPNN implementation to ensure a fair comparison. Mitsuba3 Renderer [Jakob et al. 2022] is used for scenes
that cannot be handled by MRPNN. Normally, 9k images of resolution 5122 with random configurations are used as ground truth.

bounce depth. However, for hard scenes that suffer from high variance, increased spp and depth can be used to generate better result
(Sec. 6.3).
Dataset Preference. To improve robustness in challenging configurations, we use a non-uniform sampling strategy over the parameter
space to prefer high albedo and strongly anisotropic phase function.
We also augment the training set in which only phase parameters
are altered while albedo is fixed with constant near 1. Practically,
the dataset is augmented in a 4:1 ratio.
Training Settings. We use Adam optimizer [Kingma and Ba 2017]
with an initial learning rate of 1e-3, exponentially decayed by 0.8
every epoch. All scenes are trained for 10 epochs using MSE as
loss function. Moreover, considering that radiance values in volume
rendering can span several orders of magnitude and thus harm the
training process, we compress ground truth 𝑆 by log(𝑥 + 1).

Ablation of Feature Components. We conduct an ablation study
to illustrate the contribution from each part of features. We make
a general dataset with random configurations and a challenging

Use SH for base grid . We introduce a base grid in Sec. 4.1 to sample
towards light for avoiding cost of sampling SH grid, and point out
that such simplification does not necessarily lead to worse model.
We test several scenes and find that trained models show basically
equivalent ability. The training plot (Fig. 8) and test error (Tab. 2)
support our claim that using SH for base grid yields no consistent
improvement in accuracy. However, the simplified design achieves

7
Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work
In this paper, we propose a novel lightning-fast neural baking
method for high-quality volumetric rendering. We derive a novel
diagonal scaling form of in-scattering integral in spherical harmonics basis through Legendre polynomials that eliminates explicit
integration, and propose practical methods to employ feature grids
accordingly. With our design for dual-pattern feature sampling and
compact network architecture, our system is four to five times faster
than the state-of-the-art and achieves hundreds of FPS. Beyond ordinary configurations supported by prior work, our method is also
capable of handling more complex scenes like ones with spatiallyvarying albedo and phase or high-frequency ambient lighting. We
validate our claims through extensive experiments.
Despite the effectiveness of our method, there still exist some limitations. First, our approach bakes volumes with fixed density field,
which covers many rendering tasks but cannot handle dynamic media (notably clouds generated by Worley-Perlin noise). A temporal
baking method could be used to mitigate this problem. Second, in
line with previous work in the RPNN series, we assume a directional
light as the light condition to represent time-of-day effects. This
restricts its applicability in more complex dynamic incident light
environment. Finally, the training cost in our framework is still
relatively high. Although the inference speed is remarkable, long
training time makes it inconvenient for users to manufacture baking
assets. As a neural method, some delicate approach for tuning with
few-shot ground truth may be designed.
