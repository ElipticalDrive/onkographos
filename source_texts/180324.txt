A BSTRACT
Photorealistic volume rendering of computed tomography (CT) may convey anatomy intuitively,
but it needs the CT data, dedicated software and a powerful graphics processor. Three-dimensional
Gaussian splatting (3DGS) displays photorealistic scenes in real time on less powerful devices, but
existing medical approaches train each scene for minutes to hours. We aimed to convert a CT volume
rendering directly into a 3DGS model without training. Splats were placed on an adaptive octree of
millimetre-sized cells. Their colour and opacity were computed on the graphics processor with the
renderer’s lighting, cell opacity was converted analytically into splat opacity, and faint splats were
retained stochastically above the 1/255 opacity floor of standard 3DGS rasterisers. Six public CT
scans (1.5-mm voxels) were converted with three transfer functions and compared with converged
references by peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity and a learned perceptual metric.
Two-million-splat models reached a PSNR of 32.6 +/- 0.9 dB for bone, 30.1 +/- 1.0 dB for contrastenhanced vessels and 32.3 +/- 1.5 dB for skin and bone. A conversion pass (usually two or three
per model) took 4-30 s on a current laptop and 9-60 s on a desktop with a 4-GB graphics card.
On the laptop, the models rendered at 68 frames per second at 800 x 800 pixels. Training-free
conversion produced real-time 3DGS models of photorealistic CT renderings in about a minute or
less per conversion pass on ordinary computers, with fidelity that we consider sufficient for interactive
viewing.

1
Background
Photorealistic volume rendering of computed tomography (CT) simulates light scattering in tissue and has the capacity
to depict anatomy with apparent lifelike depth and shading [1–4]. It speeds anatomical understanding compared with
conventional CT images and serves surgical planning, teaching, patient communication and documentation [5–8].
However, it needs the CT data, dedicated software and a powerful graphics processing unit (GPU), so results are usually
shared as still images or videos.
Three-dimensional Gaussian splatting (3DGS) represents a scene as millions of semi-transparent Gaussians rasterised
in real time [9]. Standard 3DGS viewers display such models directly, so a 3DGS model of a CT rendering could be
viewed, also in head-mounted displays, without the CT data or renderer. For medical and scientific volumes, most
methods optimise Gaussians iteratively against tens to over a thousand rendered views per scene, for minutes to over an
hour on a dedicated GPU [10–16]; one refines an analytic initialisation [17]. Feed-forward networks predict Gaussians
within seconds but must first be trained on about 100–1,000 volumes with rendered views [18, 19]. Most optimised
models must be rebuilt, or extended [15, 16], when the transfer function changes.

A volume, however, already defines what a Gaussian representation needs: where the material is, how opaque it is
and, once lit, its colour. Gaussian kernels have long served direct volume rendering, notably in elliptical weighted
average (EWA) splatting [20]. Instead of optimising or learning Gaussians from rendered images, we aimed to bake the
appearance of a lit CT rendering directly into Gaussians, so that conversion is immediate on ordinary hardware. The
contribution is not a new rendering model but the removal of the prerequisites of existing 3DGS pipelines: rendered
training views, a dedicated GPU and per-scene training time. We describe CTBakeGS and measure how faithfully it
reproduces the original rendering.
2
Methods
2.1
Conversion
Principle. A CT volume stores voxel-wise radiodensity values in Hounsfield units. A direct volume rendering is fully
defined by three inputs: the CT data, a transfer function that maps Hounsfield units to colour and opacity [21], and
the virtual lighting. A 3DGS model, in turn, is a set of Gaussian splats. Each splat is a small, “soft”, semi-transparent
blob in space, described by a position, a size and orientation (scale and rotation; a sphere or a stretched ellipsoid), an
opacity and a colour; its opacity is highest at the centre and fades smoothly with distance from it [9]. A splat differs
from a voxel in three respects. First, a voxel is one sample of a regular grid, so its position and size are fixed by the grid,
whereas a splat can be placed anywhere and have any size. Second, a voxel stores a measured quantity, in CT the X-ray
attenuation in Hounsfield units, and says nothing about appearance; a splat stores the appearance itself, that is, how that
piece of the scene looks. Third, a voxel volume becomes an image only through volume rendering (transfer function,
lighting and ray casting) [21], whereas splats are simply projected onto the screen and blended in depth order [9, 20],
which graphics processors do very quickly.
CTBakeGS derives splats directly from the volume: the volume is divided into small cells, each cell becomes one splat,
and each splat receives the colour and opacity that the lit volume rendering gives to its cell.
This takes five steps, described in turn below (Fig. 1): (1) virtual lighting is precomputed, so that it can be stored in
the splat colours; (2) the volume is divided into cells, which set the positions and sizes of the splats; (3) colour and
opacity are sampled within each cell; (4) cell opacity is converted into splat opacity, as neighbouring splats overlap
to generate a smooth realistic appearance; and (5) faint splats, which would vanish below the 1/255 opacity floor of
standard rasterisers, are retained stochastically with raised opacity. An anti-aliasing compensation is applied when the
splats are drawn. The result is written as a standard 3DGS file (.ply or .spz).

Step 1: lighting. In CTBakeGS, each splat has a single view-independent colour that does not change with the
viewing direction, whereas 3DGS models trained from images usually store view-dependent colour. Lighting and
shadows must therefore be computed before conversion and stored (baked) in that colour. CTBakeGS uses the lighting
of its own photorealistic volume renderer, which approximates global illumination with a precomputed light volume of
about 112 cells along the longest axis. For each light-volume cell, rays cast through the transfer-function opacity give
the visibility of a sky dome in 24 directions and the transmittance towards a key light, which produces soft shadows. A
sample’s colour is multiplied by the visibility-weighted sky light plus the transmittance-weighted key light and then
tone-mapped. The renderer has a real-time view (one pass per frame), which supplies the colours for conversion, and a
progressive view, which averages jittered frames until the image converges.
Step 2: cells. The cells determine where splats are placed and how large they are. Cells of one voxel each would spend
many splats on homogeneous regions and would tie the splat count to the voxel count. Cells are therefore organised as
an adaptive octree that is refined only where opacity changes. Starting from cells four voxels wide, a cell is split into
eight when its opacity varies strongly, that is, when the maximum transfer-function opacity over its Hounsfield-unit
range, αmax , exceeds the smaller of the opacities at the two ends of that range by more than αmax /2 (and αmax > 0.01).
Homogeneous regions thus keep one large splat, whereas boundaries receive small splats, down to sub-voxel size. The
split test extends 1.5 cell widths beyond the cell, so that large splats do not protrude through nearby surfaces. Cells are
cubes measured in millimetres (unit: the finest voxel spacing), so that thick-slice data do not produce elongated cells.
The number of splats follows from the finest permitted cell size. To reach a chosen target count, this size is adjusted,
assuming a power law between cell size and splat count, and the conversion is repeated until the count is within 8% of
the target, usually after two or three conversion passes. If the target falls between two octree levels, as happened once
(case 3, bone, one-million target), the count overshoots.
Step 3: colour and opacity. Each cell is now reduced to one colour and one opacity. The cell is sampled on the GPU
at 27 stratified points (3 × 3 × 3). At each point, the transfer function gives the extinction, and the colour is shaded with
the lighting model and light volume of the real-time view (Step 1). The point colours are averaged with weights for
extinction and for the transmittance towards the most open axis direction, so that points lying deep inside the cell do not
darken it. From the mean extinction σ̄ and the cell size h, the cell opacity is
αcell = 1 − exp(−σ̄h) .

Step 5: faint splats. Standard 3DGS rasterisers skip splats whose opacity is below 1/255, and compact formats such
as .spz store opacity with 8 bits, so a splat with αsplat below this floor vanishes. In a thin translucent layer, every cell is
below the floor, although many such cells along an oblique ray add up to a visible veil; discarding them removes the
whole layer. CTBakeGS therefore keeps a faint splat (αsplat < α0 , with α0 = 0.01, about 2.5/255, so that it stays above
the floor after the anti-aliasing compensation) with probability τsplat /τ0 , where τ0 = − ln(1 − α0 ), and assigns it the
opacity α0 . The expected optical depth of the lattice is unchanged, and fewer splats are needed. The choice uses a hash
of the cell index, so the conversion remains deterministic.

Implementation
CTBakeGS is a single HTML file (JavaScript, WebGL 2) that runs locally in a web browser. It is available at
https://github.com/BocchiSlice/CTBakeGS; version 1.0 was used for all measurements.
2.3
Evaluation
Data. We used six CT scans from two scanner vendors in the TotalSegmentator dataset v2 (CC BY 4.0) [23]: case 1
(s0289), head CT angiography; case 2 (s0138), heart and thoracic aorta; case 3 (s1340), thorax to pelvis; case 4 (s0970),
polytrauma; case 5 (s1121), pelvis-to-leg CT angiography; case 6 (s0931), neck-to-leg CT angiography (Supplementary
Table S1). All have 1.5-mm isotropic voxels (215–519 slices; 4.9–56.7 million voxels). For the thick-slice test, the
1.5-mm slices of case 1 were also averaged along the slice direction in groups spanning 5 mm, giving voxels of
1.5 × 1.5 × 5 mm.
Views and reference. Each scan was rendered with three preset transfer functions (bone; contrast-enhanced vessels;
skin and bone), giving 18 scenes plus three for the 5-mm data. For each scene, 48 wide views (16 azimuths at three
elevations) and 16 close-up views were rendered at 800 × 800 pixels on a black background. Close-up views were
centred on visible surface points chosen by farthest-point sampling, at 0.3 times the wide distance. The reference image
for each view was rendered by the progressive view of the renderer (Step 1), from the same volume, transfer function
and lighting, by averaging 128 jittered frames. The same view averaged over 256 frames differed from it by a peak
signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR; see Metrics) of 54.7–58.7 dB, a root-mean-square (RMS) difference below half an 8-bit
grey level and far smaller than the differences of the 3DGS models, so the reference was considered converged.
The 3DGS models were rasterised from the same cameras.
Metrics. Each 3DGS model was compared with the reference by PSNR, computed over the whole image and over the
foreground (pixels non-black in either image), the structural similarity index (SSIM) [24] and the learned perceptual
image patch similarity (LPIPS, VGG backbone) [25]. PSNR is 20 log10 of the full-scale value divided by the RMS
pixel difference; 20 dB more means a tenfold smaller RMS difference, and 32 dB corresponds to about 2.5% of full
scale, or 6 of 255 grey levels in an 8-bit image. We also recorded splat counts, conversion times and frame rates; frame
rates were measured in the built-in WebGL rasteriser of CTBakeGS.
Variants. To test how splat count and the individual conversion steps affect fidelity, we evaluated models with about
1, 2 (main setting) and 3 million splats, and five ablations, each of which removes or replaces one step:
• no opacity mapping (Step 4; cell opacity used as is);
• no anti-aliasing compensation;
• without faint splats (Step 5; splats below the 8-bit floor discarded);
• a uniform grid with one cell per voxel instead of the adaptive octree (Step 2), compared with the octree at the
same number of splats;
• voxel-unit instead of millimetre cells (Step 2; 5-mm data only).
Hardware. Measurements used Chrome 155 on a 14-inch Apple MacBook Pro (2024; M4 Max, 14-core CPU,
32-core GPU, 36 GB memory, macOS 26.6). Speed was also measured on a Windows desktop (Chrome 155; 2018;
Intel Core i7-8700, 16 GB memory, NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1050 Ti, 4 GB, Windows 11) and conversion time on a
13-inch Apple MacBook Air (Chrome 137; 2017; 1.8-GHz dual-core Intel Core i5, Intel HD Graphics 6000, 8 GB
memory, macOS 12.6).
Statistics. Metrics were averaged over the views of each scene, and the case was the unit of analysis (mean±standard
deviation over six cases). Variants were compared with the main setting by the two-sided exact Wilcoxon signed-rank
test; with six cases the smallest attainable p value is 0.031, and p values are exploratory and not adjusted for multiple
comparisons. Analyses used Python 3.9 with NumPy 2.0, SciPy 1.13, pandas 2.3 and lpips 0.1.4 (PyTorch 2.8).

3
Results
3.1
Fidelity
Splat counts were within 8% of the targets in every scene except one (case 3, bone, 1.37 million for the one-million
target). With two million splats, wide-view PSNR was 32.6±0.9 dB for bone, 30.1±1.0 dB for contrast-enhanced
vessels and 32.3±1.5 dB for skin and bone, with SSIM of 0.960–0.980 and LPIPS of 0.028–0.091 (Table 1; Fig. 2).
Close-up views were about 3 dB lower; the real-time photorealistic view reached 42–47 dB. Errors lay mainly along the
edges of dense structures for bone and vessels and were spread over the translucent layer for skin and bone (Fig. 2).
Fidelity was lowest in close-up views of case 4, the largest volume, for bone and vessels (27.1 and 24.0 dB), and in
wide views of case 2 for skin and bone (29.8 dB; other cases 32.0–34.5 dB; Table 2).
3.2
Splat count and speed
Compared with two million splats, one million lowered close-up PSNR in all cases for bone (median −1.2 dB), vessels
(−1.3 dB) and skin and bone (−0.9 dB), and three million raised it in all cases (+0.6, +0.8 and +0.4 dB); close-up LPIPS
followed the same pattern (Supplementary Table S2). A two-million-splat pass took 14.3 s on average (4.2–29.5 s),
and rendering ran at 68 frames per second (800 × 800 pixels; Table 3). A desktop with a 4-GB graphics card needed
29.4 s per pass and rendered 28 frames per second. The 2017 laptop needed 106 s (30–216 s) and rendered 7 frames per
second.

4
Discussion
CTBakeGS converted CT volume renderings into standard 3DGS models in about a minute or less per conversion
pass, usually two or three passes per model, on a current laptop and on a 2018 desktop with a 4-GB graphics card,
without training or per-scene optimisation. The idea is simple, but three steps made it work: the opacity mapping for
overlapping splats, the baked lighting and the retention of faint splats above the 8-bit opacity floor. It is deterministic as
every splat derives from its cell’s voxels; a new transfer function needs only another conversion; and, with the virtual
lighting baked in, standard viewers show the lit result. Because the viewer runs in a web browser, no software has to be
installed, and a converted model could be packaged with the viewer page and sent, for example, to patients as their CT
scan in a form that runs on ordinary devices. Because splats are a common format, a volume rendering could also be
combined with splat models of other origin, such as reconstructions of a crime scene.
The fidelity results may define the scope of use. Bone reached about 33 dB in wide and 30 dB in close-up views,
corresponding to an RMS pixel difference of about 2.3% and 3.3% of full scale, or 6 and 8 of 255 grey levels, and skin
and bone about 29 dB in close-up views (3.5%; 9 of 255), whereas the real-time volume renderer reached 42–47 dB
(0.45–0.8%; 1–2 of 255). Contrast-enhanced vessels were weakest in close-up views (27 dB; 4.7%; 12 of 255), where
the edges of dense bone and thin vessels were smoothed. In case 4, the largest volume, the same splat budget gave
coarser cells, and close-up fidelity rose with more splats (vessels 22.8, 24.0 and 24.8 dB with 1, 2 and 3 million splats),
so large volumes may need more splats.
Values reported for trained models (about 21–37 dB [10, 12, 18]) used different data, resolutions and references and
are not directly comparable. Optimisation [10–17], feed-forward networks [18, 19] and hybrid streaming [26] remain
preferable when the highest fidelity matters and their cost is acceptable. CTBakeGS does not compete with them on
fidelity but removes their prerequisites.
Faint splats were the main source of error in translucent layers. Standard 3DGS rasterisers discard opacities below
1/255, and the thin outer soft-tissue layer of the skin-and-bone look consists of such faint cells, which are visible in the
volume rendering only because many of them add up along oblique rays. Without the retention of Step 5, this layer
was largely missing in case 2 and the ribs showed through it (Fig. 3); its wide-view PSNR fell from 29.8 to 23.8 dB,

complex, spectrally varying reflectance with subsurface scattering in its upper layers [28], and easy-to-use, realistic
skin rendering from CT remains for future research.
At equal splat count, the octree was not more faithful than a uniform grid. Its benefit is that the splat count can be
chosen, for example two million for every volume, instead of being dictated by the number of voxels, which ranged
from 0.14 to 2.86 million here.
This study has limitations. Six cases from one public dataset were used, with 1.5-mm voxels, which are slightly coarser
than the maximum slice thickness of 1.25 mm proposed for three-dimensional reconstruction in forensic casework [29];
clinical thin-slice scans have up to ten times more voxels and convert more slowly. We also stopped at three million
splats; close-up fidelity was still rising at that count, so more splats may improve it further at the cost of file size and
frame rate, whereas wide-view fidelity had levelled off. The evaluation has two boundaries of its own. Fidelity was
measured against the converged view of the same renderer, whose lighting only approximates global illumination, so
agreement with other photorealistic renderers is unknown, and PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS do not establish anatomical
accuracy. Nor did we test the use of the models: whether they aid teaching or communication, or are suitable for
diagnostic reading, requires reader studies. Finally, two choices in the method were made rather than optimised. The
opacity floor of the retained faint splats (α0 = 0.01) is roughly the smallest value that stays above the 1/255 cut-off after
the anti-aliasing compensation, which reduces the drawn opacity of small splats, and the two interact; and lighting,
transfer function and clipping are fixed at conversion, so that view-independent colours lose specular highlights.
Several of these limitations point to the next steps. A larger series, including clinical thin-slice scans, should be tested,
and conversion of thin-slice and very large volumes should become faster, for example by caching the Hounsfield-unit
ranges of the volume for the octree. It also remains to be determined which computer is the minimum for displaying
(rather than converting) the models, how they compare with volume rendering and path tracing in this respect and in
compressibility. Whether fine structures such as hairline fractures are preserved also needs to be tested, and the error
between the model and the rendering could be used to place more splats where details are lost. Fidelity could rise further
with view-dependent colours (spherical harmonics), which would restore highlights, and with flat or anisotropic splats
for thin translucent layers [30]. The converted model could also be edited directly, cut with a clip plane or recoloured by
segmented organ, without reconverting. Above all, reader studies are needed to show whether the models aid teaching
and communication.
5
Conclusions
We could demonstrate that photorealistic CT volume renderings can be converted directly into standard 3D Gaussian
splats, without training, and displayed in a web browser. Conversion took about a minute or less per pass on a current
laptop and on a 2018 desktop with a 4-GB graphics card, and under 4 minutes per pass on a 2017 laptop with integrated
graphics. The models reproduce the rendering with root-mean-square errors of about 2.3–3.1% of full scale in wide
views. This approach could make complex CT data easier to share and view interactively without the CT data or a
volume renderer, with potential value for communication and teaching.
