Clonoxels: Reduce, Reuse, and Register for Efficient Voxel-Based
Radiance Fields
O. Camilleri1 , M. Volino1 and A. Hilton1
1 Centre for Vision, Speech and Signal Processing (CVSSP), University of Surrey, Guildford, Surrey, U.K.
Abstract
In recent years, machine learning has led to significant advances in computer graphics and 3D scene understanding, enabling
photorealistic novel view synthesis and accurate 3D reconstruction from 2D images. While implicit methods like neural radiance
fields (NeRFs) achieve impressive fidelity, explicit approaches such as voxel grids and 3D Gaussian Splatting offer faster
training and inference yet remain limited by memory footprint and sensitivity to occlusion. We introduce Cloning-Plenoxels
(Clonoxels), a voxel-based framework that automatically detects repeated objects within a scene and enables information
sharing between them, improving reconstruction quality and reducing storage requirements. Clonoxels leverages semantic and
instance segmentation, rigid alignment, and voxel-by-voxel matching with lighting-aware corrections, producing volumetric
representations that are robust to occlusion. Experiments using synthetic and real-world data demonstrate enhanced recovery
of hidden structures, lower-memory scene representation, and denoised 3D geometry reconstruction. Our framework offers a
practical path toward more efficient and accurate reconstruction of challenging, complex scenes.

1. Introduction
Machine Learning is rapidly becoming a staple of media production
pipelines. In particular, learned 3D representations have seen an
explosion of interest through methods like Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRFs) [MST∗ 20] which enable photorealistic novel view synthesis (NVS) from captured imagery. By reconstructing real-world
environments without handcrafted assets, these methods streamline
set digitization and the creation of immersive media.
NeRFs and related implicit methods have now been surpassed
in popularity by explicit techniques that include voxel-based approaches such as Plenoxels [YFT∗ 21] and point-based representations like 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [KKLD23]. Although
they reduce training and inference times considerably, these techniques are associated with large memory footprints and their NVS
capacity relies on extensive multi-view capture. Sparse coverage or
occlusion leads to under-constrained optimization, often producing
noisy artifacts and degraded visual quality. In this paper, we attempt to address these issues by pooling information from repeated
objects which are typically visible from a range of viewpoints. This
better constrains reconstruction and allows regions to share information, saving memory. Concurrent research has used this principle to enhance the NVS of 3DGS [VMG∗ 25] yet relies on manual
object selection and does not explicitly explore memory reduction.
In contrast, we propose Cloning-Plenoxels, or Clonoxels, a voxelbased framework that automatically detects and shares information
between repeat objects. We directly measure storage savings and
use an alternative, more memory-conscious approach to handling
lighting differences. Voxels avoid the overhead of a large neural
network yet provide a truly volumetric representation whose regularity makes defining and manipulating discrete objects practical.
Clonoxels builds on top of Plenoxels, using both instance segmentation and semantic labels to automatically detect repetitions, prioritize the most numerous, and isolate objects in 3D. After aligning these objects via rigid registration, grid features and poses are
jointly optimized through a voxel-by-voxel comparison that considers lighting differences and obeys photometric scene reconstruction. Our key contributions, demonstrated in this paper using both
real and synthetic data, are summarised below:
• Provide a framework that can recover information from obstructed views while also reducing memory usage.
• Allow repeated objects in a given scene to share information
while handling differences in lighting in a memory-conscious,
parameter-free way.
• Offer additional reconstruction constraints, which are demonstrated through improved 3D geometry recovery.

for NVS. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [MST∗ 20] quickly followed. This landmark approach models scenes as continuous volumetric functions parameterized by neural networks. NeRFs achieve
strikingly photorealistic NVS, but their reliance on neural architectures has posed challenges for practical use.
To address these issues, a shift has occurred toward explicit representations that avoid the overhead of large neural networks. One
prominent voxel-based approach is Plenoxels [YFT∗ 21], which
does away with deep learning in favor of feature grid optimization.
This accelerates both training and inference by orders of magnitude
while maintaining visual fidelity, though at the cost of large memory footprints that can scale poorly for high-resolution scenes.
More recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [KKLD23] has
emerged. This approach represents a scene as a set of Gaussian
primitives anchored to points derived from structure-from-motion
(SfM). Their non-volumetric nature provides fast rasterization and
strong visual results, but comes with limitations: they lack a dense,
consistent field, which makes structural manipulations difficult and
may produce “popping” artifacts under viewpoint changes.
Together, these learned 3D representations define the main pillars
of current NVS research. While each provides a unique balance of
fidelity, efficiency, and memory cost, they continue to face common
challenges related to scalability, memory demands, and robustness
under sparse or occluded input.
2.2. Reducing Memory Demand
Several studies have sought to lower the memory footprint associated with NeRFs by reducing neural network size. For example,
one work found no significant degradation in quality after pruning
up to 30% of the baseline model [Isi21].
Memory usage is a more pronounced concern in the case of
explicit representations. In the case of 3DGS, one paper found
success in reducing the number of Gaussian primitives using a frequency-modulated coarse-to-fine optimization strategy
[FGHV25]. Another compresses by pruning insignificant Gaussians, applying knowledge distillation, and utilizing vector quantization [FWW∗ 23].
In the case of voxels, a prominent step towards memory reduction
is TensoRF [CXG∗ 22]. This method significantly compresses feature grids into a low-rank tensor decomposition while maintaining
quality. On the other hand, this factorized representation makes visualization, editing, and geometric reasoning less straightforward.
2.3. Shared Representations
Another key avenue aims to decrease the number of training views
required to achieve high-quality results. Many previous works have
utilized some form of cross-scene training to build appearance and
structure priors. For example, PixelNeRF [YYTK20] learns a generalizable NeRF by conditioning on image features extracted by
a convolutional neural network. Extending this concept to 3DGS,
SVG3D [FZW∗ 25] introduces a method for single-view reconstruction using a U-Net network trained on the RealEstate10K
dataset [ZTF∗ 18]. While promising, such approaches struggle
when encountering unfamiliar objects or environments. Moreover,
cross-scene training can be expensive.
Intra-scene information exchange was recently explored in Splat
and Replace [VMG∗ 25], which improves NVS considerably by
forcing consistency across similar objects within 3DGS representations. While similar in spirit, our Clonoxels framework differs
from this work in several key ways. We build on voxel grids rather
than splats, as they allow everything to remain differentiable while
making object decomposition, pose registration, and 3D model extraction more straightforward. Clonoxels also proposes a method
for automatic object selection, handles lighting conflicts differently,
and directly investigates memory implications.
3. Method
Clonoxels, summarised in Fig. 1, automatically identifies repeated
objects and projects them into 3D to define object subgrids. Voxel
features are shared across instances using learnable rigid transformations, while a single-channel, view-dependent color correction
handles lighting differences efficiently. Features and transformations are jointly optimized in stages, yielding a cohesive volumetric representation that leverages repeated structures for memoryefficient, accurate reconstruction. Key details are described in the
following subsections.
3.1. Baseline Representation

regular grid structure and storing learned features at vertices. Our
simple, baseline implementation closely follows Plenoxels, which
represents a given scene as a voxel grid of opacity values, σ, and
view-dependent color, c, in the form of spherical harmonic coefficients (9 per color channel). These features are queried using trilinear interpolation and rendered into images that are compared to the
ground truth set.

3.2. Clonoxels
Instance Detection. Repetitions are identified in training images
using instance segmentation and semantic labels – the former allows every object to be extracted while the latter is used to detect class repetitions. While we employ Mask2Former [CMS∗ 21]
as it offers both instance and semantic segmentation, we have
found success prompting Segment Anything 2 (SAM2) [RGH∗ 24]
with labelled bounding boxes obtained from models like YOLOv10
[WCL∗ 24] . For efficiency, detected semantic classes are ranked by
instance count and only masks corresponding to the most numerous class are retained. Alternative criteria, such as prioritizing by
the physical size of the repetitions, could also be considered, but
are not explored here. Masks are projected into the scene to localize objects in 3D; voxels are assigned by multi-view consistency
and used to define axis-aligned bounding cubes. We refer to each
segmented region as an object subgrid.
Feature Sharing. Given an object’s feature subgrid, F, we apply
a learnable rigid transformation to each vertex, v. The transformation is parameterized by a rotation matrix R ∈ SO(3), derived from
a learnable quaternion, a center of rotation o ∈ R3 , and a translation
vector t ∈ R3 :
v′ = R(v − o) + o + t
(5)
The transformed grid coordinates are used to resample features, producing a transformed subgrid, F ′ . Resampling is
performed using PyTorch’s differentiable grid_sample with
padding_mode=’border’ to ensure valid sampling. The transformation parameters are optimized via backpropagation through a loss
that compares F ′ to the subgrid associated with another instance of
the same object, F̂:
L f eat = λ f eat ||F ′ − F̂||1 .
(6)
This feature comparison is weighted by a coefficient, λ f eat . L1 is
used over L2 here due to its relative outlier tolerance, increasing
robustness to noisy reconstructions.

Lighting Differences. Sharing information in this way does not tolerate differences in lighting. To allow for such variations, like shadows or bright, specular highlights, we introduce a view-dependent
color correction in the form of a scalar voxel feature, ∆c, which is
not subject to L f eat and can therefore differ between objects. We
do not allow for per-channel corrections and instead apply them
equally to all three color channels. This limitation helps maintain
the memory-saving advantage of our approach and also encourages
the sharing of as much appearance information as possible. The
color associated with a given subgrid voxel is then defined by:

Optimization. We employ our losses via the following steps:
1. At first, an initial representation of the entire scene is built by
optimizing Lrecon and LTV .
2. Just L f eat is used to update the transformation parameters only
in order to estimate poses until convergence.
3. Lrecon is reintroduced and features continue to be updated so
that instances may exchange information while pose refinement
can be informed by additional photometric cues.
On loss convergence, this process yields a cohesive, shared model
that can be distributed around the scene using learned poses and
color corrections. We would like to note that pose optimization
works best after culling low-density voxels.

4.2. Reconstruction
Reconstruction enhancements are illustrated within Fig. 2 which
shows the point clouds of two objects of the same class before
and after feature sharing. These were formed by keeping only those
vertices with σ > 1.0. By enforcing similarity between repetitions,
Clonoxels imposes additional constraints on both geometry and appearance. This process also allows gradients to propagate to 3D
regions unseen within training images. Together, these constraints
appear to reduce the appearance of noisy artifacts. Such denoising is particularly promising, as voxel-based representations lend
themselves well to 3D mesh model extraction.
The advantages of feature sharing are most clearly depicted in Fig.
3 which shows two cases of rendering a specific object subgrid from
a novel, test view that was intentionally obstructed by an obstacle
within training images. Clonoxels significantly improves upon the
baseline, drawing information from other objects to recover hidden
regions. In terms of MSE with respect to the ground truth subgrid
render, Clonoxels reduced the MSE by 21% here.

We have demonstrated that with our proposed Clonoxels framework, it is possible to enhance voxel-based representations by automatically identifying repeated objects and allowing information
exchange between them. This was illustrated through the successful
recovery of hidden structure and appearance information, denoising
of reconstructed geometry, and reduction in memory footprint. Enriching explicit, voxel-based approaches in this way carries practical implications beyond simply lowering storage demands; the
ability to recover hidden structure reduces reliance on perfectly
controlled capture, easing constraints on production workflows
and data acquisition. Furthermore, refined geometry enables highquality 3D assets to be extracted directly from captured scenes.
Techniques such as marching cubes [LC87] can be easily applied
to the optimized voxel feature grids, yielding 3D meshes and bridging the gap between reconstruction pipelines and downstream use
in visual effects, animation, or game engines.
There are several clear avenues for future work. As previously discussed, intelligently sharing information in the face of visual differences between semantically similar objects could widen the flexibility and application of this approach. Indeed, if objects can be
decomposed further, there are serious applications for large-scale,
high-resolution scenes; increasing the capacity to exploit repetition
could result in drastic memory savings. It may also be possible to
combine our methodology with generative in-filling approaches to
more faithfully reconstruct scenes from sparse/unseen viewpoints.
Finally, it would be interesting to explore the limits of our scalar
correction, which operates under a simple lighting assumption.
