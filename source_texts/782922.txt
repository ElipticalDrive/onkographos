Abstract An established radiative transfer model (RTM) is adapted for simulating all-sky infrared radiance
spectra from the Canadian Global Environmental Multiscale (GEM) model in order to validate its forecasts at
the radiance level against Atmospheric InfraRed Sounder (AIRS) observations. Synthetic spectra are
generated for 2 months from short-term (3–9 h) GEM forecasts. The RTM uses a monthly climatological land
surface emissivity/reﬂectivity atlas. An updated ice particle optical property library was introduced for
cloudy radiance calculations. Forward model brightness temperature (BT) biases are assessed to be of the
order of 1 K for both clear-sky and overcast conditions. To quantify GEM forecast meteorological variables
biases, spectral sensitivity kernels are generated and used to attribute radiance biases to surface and
atmospheric temperatures, atmospheric humidity, and clouds biases. The kernel method, supplemented
with retrieved proﬁles based on AIRS observations in collocation with a microwave sounder, achieves good
closure in explaining clear-sky radiance biases, which are attributed mostly to surface temperature and
upper tropospheric water vapor biases. Cloudy-sky radiance biases are dominated by cloud-induced
radiance biases. Prominent GEM biases are identiﬁed as: (1) too low surface temperature over land, causing
about 25 K bias in the atmospheric window region; (2) too high upper tropospheric water vapor, inducing
about 23 K bias in the water vapor absorption band; (3) too few high clouds in the convective regions,
generating about 110 K bias in window band and about 16 K bias in the water vapor band.
1. Introduction
Spectrally resolved Outgoing Longwave Radiation (OLR) measured by satellite instruments such as the
Cross-track Infrared Sounder (CrIS) onboard the JPSS [Wang et al., 2013], the Atmospheric Infrared Sounder
(AIRS) onboard Aqua [Chahine et al., 2006], and the IASI onboard MetOp [Blumstein et al., 2004] is beneﬁcial
in improving weather forecast via data assimilation [e.g., Hilton et al., 2009; McNally et al., 2006] and in validating global climate models (GCMs) [Haskins et al., 1999; Huang and Yung, 2005; Huang and Ramaswamy,
2008; Huang et al., 2007; Bani Shahabadi and Huang, 2014; Turner and Tett, 2014].
Different approaches have been used for validating weather forecasts and climate simulations against satellite observations. Most commonly, model simulated geophysical variables, such as temperature, humidity,
and cloud properties, can be compared to satellite-retrieved values of these variables. However, satellite
retrievals are often obtained using complex inversion algorithms based on measurements of incomplete
sampling patterns [Stephens and Kummerow, 2007], which makes it difﬁcult to determine the uncertainty in
the validation results.

Alternatively, model validation can be made at the level of the variable directly observed by the satellites,
i.e., the radiance. Using a forward model to generate synthetic radiances from model-simulated atmospheric
proﬁles and comparing them with satellite-measured radiances has the advantage of avoiding problems
inherent with satellite retrievals [Morcrette, 1991; Bodas-Salcedo et al., 2011; Garand et al., 2011]. The top of
the atmosphere (TOA) radiance at different frequencies is sensitive to different meteorological variables
(temperature, water vapor, clouds, etc.) at various vertical levels. Hence, comparison of observed and simulated radiance spectra provides rich information which is relevant for model validation. Huang et al. [2007]
used spectrally resolved radiances from the AIRS instrument to validate the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics
Laboratory GCM, disclosing compensating spectral errors in different frequency bands, which would be

Although radiance-level validation has its advantages and conceptually complements validation of the
meteorological variables, it has remained a challenge as to whether and how we can quantitatively relate
the validation results from the two approaches. Here in this paper, we propose the use of radiative sensitivity kernels, also referred to as Jacobians. These kernels, as explained and demonstrated below, allow testing
the ﬁrst-order consistency between the radiance bias and meteorological variable biases. For this exercise,
it is recognized that a proper forward model is critical for obtaining valid validation results. In this study, we
incorporate in the forward model a newly developed cloud property library of Yang et al. [2013] and apply
the kernel approach to evaluate the forecasts of the most recent version of GEM. We note that although
radiative Jacobians are used in variational data assimilation to produce numerical weather prediction analyses, the use of the kernels here is different, in which (1) the validation is with respect to the GEM forecast
instead of initial condition; (2) usually only a subset of the channels are used in data assimilation (e.g., only
87 AIRS channels that are not affected by clouds or sensitive to land surface are assimilated for the GEM
forecast [Garand et al., 2011]), while the validation here uses all available channels across the middleinfrared spectrum; and (3) the use is based on monthly mean kernels as opposed to instantaneous Jacobians used in data assimilation.
The paper is organized as follows: the methodology is presented in section 2. GEM model validation results
are given in section 3. Section 4 summarizes the study.
2. Methodology
2.1. Observational Data
Observational data used here are from the AIRS instrument. AIRS is a grating spectrometer with 2378 channels ranging from wavenumber 650–2665 cm21 and a nominal spectral resolution m/dm 5 1200, where m is
wavenumber. The instrument has a nadir footprint of 13.5 km, and the observed radiance has a precision of
0.5 K at 250 K [Chahine et al., 2006]. The AIRS scan angles range from 249 to 498. However, following Huang
et al. [2007], we only use angles within 628 and treat them as nadir views, which simpliﬁes the kernel diagnosis. Quality control ﬂags are used to eliminate spectra affected by random in-ﬂight noise. For the periods
of January 2013 and July 2014, there are three types of AIRS products used in this study: (1) L1B radiance
(AIRIBRAD.005, manual available online via http://airsl1.gesdisc.eosdis.nasa.gov/Aqua_AIRS_Level1/AIRIBRAD.005/doc/README.AIRIBRAD.pdf). One day of AIRS data is divided into 240 granules, each with 6 min
duration. Each granule, consists of 135 scan lines each containing 90 cross-track footprints (135 3 90 footprints per AIRS IR granule); (2) L2 AIRS-retrieved proﬁles (AIRX2RET, documented at http://disc.sci.gsfc.nasa.
gov/AIRS/documentation/v6_docs/v6releasedocs-1/V6_L2_Product_User_Guide.pdf) which is the combined
AMSU1AIRS Level 2 Standard Product Physical Retrieval (AMSU: Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit, a
microwave sounder). AMSU1AIRS retrievals do not necessarily represent the true atmosphere, as they are
the products of retrieval algorithms. Due to the lower resolution of AMSU-A, about three by three AIRS
cross-track/along-track footprints match one AMSU-A footprint, yielding 45 3 30 retrieval proﬁles per 6 min
granule. Twenty-eight pressure levels ranging from 1100 to 0.1 hPa are used for temperature retrieval. For
water vapor, 15 pressure levels are available from 1100 to 50 hPa. As for any retrieval product, L2 AIRSretrieved proﬁles are susceptible to retrieval errors. For this reason, each retrieved physical quantity has its
own quality indicator and error estimate. Sources of retrieval errors for different variables are diverse and
can extend from excessive residuals, insufﬁcient valid channels, and numerical errors in retrieval algorithm
to AMSU instrument issues. To minimize the impact of L2 AIRS retrieval errors in this analysis, only the proﬁles with the best quality control indicator are used. It is assumed that for relatively large datasets, mean

2.2. Synthetic Data
2.2.1. Forward Model
2.2.1.1. Radiative Transfer Codes
Synthetic radiances are generated using the Radiative Transfer for Television Operational Vertical Sounder
(RTTOV) model, v11.2 [Matricardi and Saunders, 1999; Hocking et al., 2014]. It is developed by the Numerical
Weather Prediction Satellite Application Facility, led by the U.K. Met Ofﬁce, with partners European Center
for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute, and Meteo France. It is
extensively used for radiance assimilation at numerous weather centers. The model allows rapid simulations
of radiances of satellite infrared or microwave nadir scanning radiometers from an atmospheric proﬁle of
temperature, variable gas concentrations, and cloud and surface properties. RTTOV uses instrument-speciﬁc
coefﬁcient ﬁles (in this case, the AIRS instrument), which allow optical depth calculations. Coefﬁcient ﬁles
are developed using a line-by-line radiative transfer model at U.K. Met Ofﬁce and are available online via
http://nwpsaf.eu/site/software/rttov/downloads/coefﬁcients. Radiances are computed at an effective central
wavelength which allows conversion into brightness temperature (BT), the unit used to present the results
in the following sections.
2.2.1.2. Cloud Treatment
RTTOV offers options for cloud calculations. Here we use the common assumption of maximum overlap for
adjacent cloud layers and random overlap for nonadjacent cloud layers. One of the advantages of RTTOV
v11 is the option to directly supply the vertical proﬁles of the cloud particle optical properties. This allows
ﬂexibility in the calculation of cloud optical depth, which can be an important source of error in a forward
model [Heilliette and Garand, 2007]. The ice cloud spectral library used here is obtained from Yang et al.
[2013]. We assume that cloud ice particles have nonspherical aggregate plate habitats. We ﬁnd that
although assuming different aggregate habitat shapes for ice particles might affect individual proﬁles in the
RT simulations, the assumed habitat does not affect the conclusions of this study in which the radiance difference introduced by a different habitat (1 K) is much smaller than the radiance bias caused by the cloud
fraction and cloud condensate concentration biases (10 K) concerned here. In the library, wavelength
ranges 0.2–15.25 mm (396 intervals) and 16.4–99.0 mm (49 intervals), and particle size ranges 2–104 mm (189
intervals). Spectral absorption (ka) and scattering (ks) coefﬁcients values and phase function (PH) values for
each particle size are extracted from the database and a gamma size distribution function is used to integrate optical properties over the particle size distribution. The integrated optical properties are derived for
three effective ice particle sizes (10, 50, and 80 lm), following the formulae in Baum et al. [2011, Appendix
B]. For cloud liquid particles, the Mie scattering routine of Bohren and Huffman [1983] is used to calculate
scattering and absorption by spherical water droplet. Similar to ice particles, a gamma size distribution function is used to integrate the calculated spectral liquid absorption, scattering, and phase function over an
interval of particle sizes from 0.02 to 50 mm. The integrated liquid particle optical properties are derived for
two effective liquid particle sizes: 10 and 13 mm, which are used as liquid particle radii over land and sea,
respectively, similar to Garand et al. [2011]. The Cloud Water Content (CWC) GEM model output is partitioned into liquid and ice water content (LWC, IWC) in each model layer, using the water probability and
the layer temperature, following Garand and Nadon [1997]:

2.2.1.3. Surface Emissivity/Reflectivity Treatment
RTTOV provides values for the surface emissivity/reﬂectivity, or alternatively allows the user to provide values. For IR instruments, RTTOV has a sea-surface emissivity/reﬂectivity model that depends on surface wind
ﬁeld [Hocking et al., 2014]. The land surface emissivity values supplied to RTTOV are monthly climatological
emissivity values, on the AIRS spectral grid, and are derived from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) retrievals [Seemann et al., 2008]. The monthly climatological land surface bidirectional
reﬂectance distribution function (BRDF) values are based on the MODIS BRDF kernel product [Hocking et al.,
2014].
2.2.1.4. Input Factors
Since input proﬁles are on different pressure levels from those used internally by RTTOV, these are interpolated onto the RTTOV coefﬁcient ﬁle levels as a ﬁrst step. After the optical depth has been calculated on the
RTTOV levels, resulting optical depths are then interpolated back onto the input levels for conducting vertical integration of the radiative transfer equation. All input proﬁles use global monthly O3 climatology [Paul
et al., 1998] and historical values (single global values with no vertical variations) for well-mixed greenhouse
gases (CO2, N2O, CH4, and CO). The effect of solar radiation is turned off in our RTTOV simulations. This is justiﬁed as we focus on the spectral range 600–1650 cm21, excluding the near-infrared channels, in our
analysis.

using an L2 AIRS retrieval proﬁle as input and the corresponding L1B AIRS-observed BTs are shown in
Figures 1a and 1b for 50 randomly selected clear and cloudy proﬁles, respectively. The mean BT biases, as
well as the standard deviation of the BT differences, are generally within 1 K in all the channels. This error
estimation here suggests the GEM BT biases exceeding 1 K would be meaningful biases that can be related
to GEM biases in meteorological variables. It is important to note that the errors in the L2 AIRS retrievals

2.2.2. GEM Input
Two months (January 2013 and July 2014) of global surface and atmospheric temperatures, water vapor,
and cloud proﬁles generated by the GEM model are used in our diagnosis. The GEM forecast is initialized
every 6 h and the outputs are generated at 18 min intervals (model time step). The data used are from
hours 3–9 of each forecast. Outputs are generated on 30 3 30 km2 horizontal resolution grids and on 81level hybrid vertical coordinate levels, with the top level at 0.1 hPa. The output proﬁles are then supplied to
the RTTOV radiative transfer model.
To ensure that simulated spectra have the same spatial and temporal sampling pattern as the AIRS spectra,
only those GEM grid points overpassed by the Aqua satellite are selected in the RTTOV BT simulations. The
AIRS spectra falling into each GEM grid box are averaged to represent the corresponding observational
spectra for the simulated counterparts. This ensures that the simulated spectra have a spatial and temporal
sampling pattern similar to the AIRS spectra, a necessary condition for model evaluation [Huang et al.,
2007]. In total, there are about 800,000 simulated proﬁles in each month. For demonstration, the globe is
divided into 38 3 38 grid boxes, each containing 100–150 spectra over a month.

3. GEM Model Validation
Following the methods outlined above, we validate the GEM simulation by comparing synthetic BT spectra
to the actual AIRS-observed L1B spectra. We make comparisons with regard to (1) global monthly mean BT
spectra, which provide an overview of the biases across the spectrum, and (2) geographical distribution of
the BT bias in selected individual channels, which discloses the spatial pattern of the BT biases and their
connections to the meteorological variable biases.
3.1. Global Monthly Mean Radiance Biases
First we compare the globally and monthly averaged observed and simulated spectra. The corresponding
clear-sky proﬁle kernels are used to decompose the overall BT biases into contributions from surface temperature, atmospheric temperature, and water vapor. A proﬁle is tagged as a clear-sky proﬁle if there is no
cloud in either the GEM or L2 AIRS retrieval data sets. The globally averaged clear and all-sky spectra from
AIRS-observed L1B spectra and GEM forecast proﬁles (simulated using RTTOV, labeled as RT in the ﬁgures
from now on), and their difference (RT minus AIRS) for January 2013 are shown in Figure 2 (the results for
July 2014 have similar patterns as in January 2013 and thus are not shown for brevity).
From Figure 2b, the negative BT biases (RT minus AIRS) in the window band (800–1200 cm21) indicate that
the GEM-forecast surface temperature is too low and the negative BT bias in the water vapor vibrationalrotational band (1200–1650 cm21) suggests there may be an excess of tropospheric water vapor. The globally averaged all-sky AIRS-retrieved and GEM-simulated temperature and water vapor proﬁles, and their differences are shown in Figure 3 (the globally averaged clear-sky proﬁles are similar and thus are not shown),
which are qualitatively in agreement with the inference made from the BT comparison.
In Figure 4, the overall monthly averaged clear-sky BT bias as shown in Figure 2b is decomposed into contributions from the GEM biases in surface temperature, atmospheric temperature, and water vapor, using the
spectral kernels. The overall clear-sky BT biases can be well reproduced from all the contributions measured

4. Conclusions and Discussion
In this paper, a forward model, RTTOV, is used for simulating AIRS L1B-observed spectra from the atmospheric proﬁles of a weather forecast model, GEM. RTTOV is modiﬁed for cloudy BT calculations. Spectral
optical properties of the clouds are explicitly simulated using liquid and ice optical property data calculated
from the Mie scattering code and the Yang ice particle library, respectively. Monthly climatological surface
emissivity and reﬂectivity atlases are used for land emissivity and reﬂectivity, respectively. This forward
model is validated using the AIRS L1B BT measurements and L2-retrieved atmospheric proﬁles, which shows
that the forward model BT error plus the error embedded in AIRS L2 retrievals is within 1 K across the AIRS
spectral grids.
The forward model is then used to validate GEM simulations at the radiance level. A radiative spectral kernel
method is implemented to test the ﬁrst-order consistency between the BT biases and meteorological variable
biases, and also to quantify the contribution from each meteorological variable to the overall BT bias. Decomposition of the clear-sky radiance biases shows that good radiative closure is achieved by the kernel method.
The all-sky decomposition further discloses the magnitude of cloud-induced biases in GEM simulations.
