Approximate signed distance field for deformable bodies
Augment reference configuration deformable geometry with an approximate signed distance field. We precompute the signed distance at each vertex of the deformable mesh at reference configuration and approximate the signed distance at the contact point in the deformed configuration by linearly interpolating the precomputed values at the vertices.
The domain of the approximated signed distance field is strictly in the interior of the deformable mesh and the value is non-positive.
Collect this approximated signed distance information with DeformableContactData.

multibody/fixed_fem/dev/reference_deformable_geometry.h, line 35 at r2 (raw file):
Previously, xuchenhan-tri wrote…
IMO all three properties you listed should be satisfied (really 2 instead of 3 because the third includes the second). But like we discussed in the softsim meeting, there's no known/easy way to achieve them and keep the mesh suitable for FEM.
So my temporary far-from-perfect solution is something easy to satisfy part of the requirements and leave it to the geometry experts to come up with the perfect mesh.
There are two issues here: one is the input and the other is how it is used.
Relative to the mesh in its reference configuration, the signed distance field can be a very good approximation of signed distance. (Depending on the quality of the tessellation and the topology of the surface, of course). However, having a tet with the same values on every vertex indicates a poor tessellation, period. If you consider the cube in your test, there exists a simple tesselation which would perfectly reproduce the signed distance. However, with no interior vertices, every point inside the cube reports to be on the cube.
The second issue is how we use the distance. As the mesh deforms, we know that the mechanism for computing signed distance in the deformed mesh actively degrades. The greater the deformation, the worst the approximation becomes. That is a fundamental property of the trick being played here.
Philosophically, if I have a process that introduces (regrettable) error, I would want to start with a clean slate to minimize the total error. That said, the process that introduces the error can introduce, in some sense, unbounded error. So, clean start may not matter. Or, more particularly, the cost of enforcing a clean start may not be justified in the putative benefit at runtime.
