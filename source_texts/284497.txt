Glossary
59
Multiplanar mode – Ultrasound modality for volumetric imaging which displays and allows to navigate
60
the ultrasound volume. Multiplanar mode allows images to be created from the original plane of
61
acquisition (i.e. the axial plane) in any other plane of the space (either the coronal, sagittal, or oblique
62
one).
63
Post processing – Consists in the manipulation of the raw data after acquisition and includes all the
64
adjustments in terms of transparency, contrast, light source and rendering modality performed in order
65
to optimize image quality from the original volume.
66
Rendering – Rendering technologies consist in dedicated software which can be applied to the stored
67
3D volumes in order to obtain the desired mixture of contrast, light, and transparency and retrieve
68
additional information such as the surface of the index volume or the skeleton which is underlying the
69
fetal skin.
70
HD Volume Imaging™ (HDVI™) – HDVI encompasses a subsets of organ/tissue specific presets available
71
on the Samsung WS 80 ultrasound machine which aims to deliver clearer contrast by enhancing tissue
72
differentiation and edge depiction.
73
Crystal Vue™ – Context preserving post processing rendering technology for three-dimensional
74
ultrasound volumes introduced in 2015 by Samsung Medison and based on image-contrast
75
enhancement.
76
Realistic Vue™ – Post processing rendering technique for three-dimensional volumes developed by
77
Samsung Medison which can be compared to HDlive Silhouette, TrueVue and Luminance.
78
Inversion mode – Rendering technique which inverts anechogenic structures that are displayed as black
79
on the customary two-dimensional (2D) gray-scale ultrasound image into a white (or colorized), cast-
80
like appearance.
81
Region of Interest (ROI) – The ROI represents a subset of the 3D volume dataset which is identified for
82
volume rendering by means of a dedicated volume box which can be adjusted on the ultrasound
83
machine.

Abstract
87
The antenatal evaluation of the fetal central nervous system (CNS) is among the most difficult tasks of
88
prenatal ultrasound (US) requiring technical skills in relation to ultrasound and image acquisition and
89
knowledge of CNS anatomy and how this changes with gestation. According to the International
90
Guidelines, the basic assessment of fetal CNS is most frequently performed on the axial planes, while
91
the coronal and sagittal planes are required for the multiplanar evaluation of the CNS within the context
92
of fetal neurosonology. It can be even more technically challenging to obtain “non-axial” views with
93
two-dimensional (2D) US. The modality of three-dimensional (3D) US has been suggested as a panacea
94
to overcome the technical difficulties of achieving non-axial views. The lack of familiarity of most
95
sonologists in the use of 3D US and its related processing techniques may preclude its use even where
96
it could play an important role in complementing antenatal 2D US assessment. Furthermore, once a 3D
97
volume has been acquired, proprietary software allows it to be processed in different ways, leading to
98
multiple ways of displaying and analyzing the same anatomical imaging or plane. These are difficult to
99
learn and time consuming in the absence of specific training. In this manuscript we describe the key
100
steps in volume acquisition of a 3D US volume, manipulation and processing with reference to images
101
of the fetal CNS using a newly developed context-preserving rendering technique.
102
103
Introduction
104
Three-dimensional (3D) ultrasound (US) has the potential to improve the confidence of an anatomic
105
diagnosis suspected on 2D imaging. Enhanced resolution of newly developed US probes has
106
dramatically improved the diagnostic potential of 2D US, however it ultimately consists in a
107
tomographic technique of a 3D object. On the other hand, the offline multiplanar navigation of 3D
108
volumes can provide better visualization and recognition of normal fetal anatomy and more accurate
109
definition of fetal abnormalities compared to 2D US (1-6). For these reasons, this technology has been
110
shown to be helpful for the evaluation of the fetal central nervous system (CNS) (7-9) and particularly
111
of the posterior fossa (10-13), the cortical layer (14-17), midline structures (18-20) and the optic chiasm
112
(21,22). Additionally, 3D US may provide advantages for the assessment of the face (23,24). Several
113
Authors have focused on 3D US as a complementary tool in the evaluation of the secondary palate (25-
114
32) or the fetal mandible (24,33), and recent reports have suggested that the subjective (34-40) or semi-
115
automated (41) detection of facial dysmorphisms using 3D US can contribute in the identification of
116
highly suggestive features of genetic syndromes. Also skeletal (42-44), cardiac (45-53) and thoraco-
117
abdominal (54,55) imaging may benefit from 3D US assessment, while more recent advances in 3D
118
imaging have focused on the implementation of intelligent software for the automated data extraction
119
from 3D volume datasets for clinical purposes. Within this scenario, fetal intelligent navigation (56-59)
120
and the 5D CNS+ (60) software have been designed with the aim to provide the clinicians valuable and
121
reliable tools capable to simplify the ultrasound assessment of the fetal heart and brain, respectively.
122
Although the role of 3D US as a potential complementary tool in the advanced assessment of the fetal
123
CNS has been demonstrated (2-4,6-8) and is acknowledged in the International Guidelines for fetal
124
neurosonology (61), 3D US has not found yet a niche in fetal or general obstetric imaging and is not
125
considered to be as necessary investigation. This is partially because of the multiplicity of different
126
technologies, all of which impact on the cost of an ultrasound machine, and the time needed to process
127
the 3D volume. Also because there is little consensus as to the ideal method of volume acquisition,
128
settings and analysis of the volume, which may explain why 3D ultrasound has arguably not had the
129
impact in imaging in prenatal diagnosis that might have been expected. Furthermore, while
130
performance and interpretation of anatomic structures with 2D ultrasound is the subject of increasingly
131
rigorous training requirements (62-64) this has currently not been established for 3D US . Finally, after
132
the 3D volume has been acquired, commercial systems will allow it to be processed in different ways
133
(31,32,42,43,65-68), leading to many ways of displaying and analyzing the same anatomical imaging or
134
plane, and the lack of familiarity with post-processing of most practitioners may preclude the extraction
135
of information from the volume dataset even in those cases where 3D US can complement the
136
antenatal US assessment.
137
There remains little consensus among experts as to the value of different post processing settings.
138
Crystal Vue™ is a context preserving post processing technique for 3D volumes which is not dissimilar
139
to HDlive Silhouette (68-71) and allows easier differentiation between tissues with different
140
echogenicity by enhancing contrast. In this Expert Review we detail how 3D volumes are optimally
141
obtained, which settings are used to optimize the anatomical sections and how post processing
142
techniques – in this case Crystal Vue™ (32,36) – are used.
143
Technique of Crystal Vue™ imaging: from volume acquisition to post-processing.
144
Volume acquisition
145
3D US volumes consist of a high number of 2D frames (or slices), which are acquired with volumetric
146
transducers. Any scanning plane – axial, coronal and sagittal – is potentially suitable for the volume
147
acquisition, depending upon the fetal lie and position and the features of the structure which is being
148
evaluated. For example, volumes for the evaluation of the posterior fossa are usually acquired
149
transabdominally on an axial plane through the lamboid suture but may be obtained also transvaginally
150
on a coronal plane through the anterior or the posterior fontanelle or on a midsagittal plane through
151
the sagittal suture depending upon the fetal head position (10,11). The fundamental basis underlying
152
3D US is that whatever the plane of acquisition, the image can be orientated to show the fetal structures
153
in a standardized way.
154
Post processing: multiplanar mode
155
After acquisition, 3D volumes are usually displayed in three windows in the three orthogonal planes x,
156
y and z. Using the cursors, the examiner is able to move back and forth on one plane and evaluate the
157
corresponding changes on the other two planes. Furthermore, the examiner can move the fetal volume
158
into a position that is often not achievable with live scanning using conventional ultrasound.
159
Post processing: rendering
160
Rendering refers to the use of dedicated software which is applied to the stored 3D volumes in order
161
to obtain the desired mixture of contrast, light, and transparency and retrieves additional information.
162
This may include data on the surface or the skeleton of the index volume, allowing principally for the
163
translucency and smoothing of the image to be modified.
164
Factors impacting on the quality of the acquired volume
165
The quality of the volume acquired depends on the limitations inherent to the physics of ultrasound,
166
for example maternal obesity, fetal lie, depth of imaging, which apply both to 2D and 3D imaging,
167
further, the presence of fetal movements or movements of the transducer will affect the integrity of
168
the volume acquired. The volume resolution is also depending upon the number of slices which
169
constitute the index volume. Their overall number varies depending upon the “Scan Angle”. For a fixed
170
“Scan Angle” of 65 degrees, the number of slices ranges between 70 and 150 for “Scan Quality Low”
171
and “Extreme”, respectively. The “Scan Quality” is related to the acquisition speed. Slow speeds result
172
in more scanned “slices” and higher resolution volumes (up to 150 frames for “Scan Angle” of 65
173
degrees) usually used for nonmoving organs, higher speeds result in fewer “slices”.
174
Finally, the width of the “Scan Angle” is another parameter which needs to be adjusted before the
175
volume acquisition. This will affect the length of the volume acquisition but has no impact on the quality
176
of the acquired volume.
177
3D ultrasound and Crystal Vue™ rendering technology: step-by-step guide
178
This manuscript is intended to describe a step by step approach for Crystal Vue™ imaging providing
179
examples from unpublished images from normal and abnormal US brain volumes. These were collected
180
during scans performed routinely and based on clinical indication and all acquired transabdominally on
181
the axial, coronal and/or sagittal plane using a 4-8 MHz probe equipped Samsung WS-80 (Samsung
182
Medison Co Ltd, South Korea). The quality of the volume acquisition was set to “extreme” and the angle
183
of the volume sweep was adjusted in order to encompass the index structure.
184
Once the US volumes were acquired (Figure 1), they underwent offline postprocessing on the
185
ultrasound system. This can also be performed using dedicated “5D viewer software” by Samsung
186
Medison. Similar software has been developed also by other companies and are available also for other
187
ultrasound systems, for example “4D view” by GE. Post-processing with multiplanar mode is then
188
applied in order to orientate the volume along the X, Y and Z axis (Figure 2). This enables the
189
sonographer to be aware at any time of the position of the views within the volume. The Region of
190
interest (ROI) is then adjusted in order to obtain the thinnest slice (Figures 3-4). The “Crystal Vue™”,
191
together with the “Realistic Vue” icon is selected from the control touch screen (Figure 5-6). “Realistic
192
Vue” is an additional rendering software available on the Samsung WS-80 ultrasound machine. The
193
combination of Crystal Vue™ and Realistic Vue™ is possible, but not always needed.
194
Volumes are navigated by means of the scrolling function in each plane of the space. The icon “Render
195
setup” (Figure 7) is on the second page of the control panel of the Samsung WS-80 and allows the
196
rendering plane to changed (Figure 8). As an alternative, the rendering plane can be adjusted by manual
197
rotation in the x, y and z planes.
198
Optimization of the rendered views
199
The rendered views can then optimized by means of further options available for Crystal Vue™
200
software. HD Volume Imaging™ (HDVI™) delivers clearer contrast by enhancing tissue differentiation
201
and edge depiction; the different presets available (brain, early OB, heart) allow a straightforward
202
selection of the most appropriate HDVI setting for the structure which is being examined (Figures 9-
203
11). By selecting “Crystal Vue™ Setup” or “Realistic Vue™ Setup” (Figure 12) the control panel allows
204
the direction of the light source to be changed, either automatically or manually (Figure 13), ultimately
205
obtaining different views of the same diagnostic plane (Figures 14,15). Additionally, on the same panel
206
the inversion mode, which demonstrates hypoechoic structures such as ventricles and cystic lesions,
207
may be selected through the icon “invert” (Figures 16,17). To adjust the HDVI transparency, which is
208
labelled as “HDVI index” (Figure 18), the optimal compromise between transparency and contrast
209
enhancement is achieved, in pour experience, using the setting “3”. In Table 1, in Figures 1-18 in the
210
powerpoint slides and in the embedded videos we summarize the recommended steps for the 3D
211
volume post-processing using Crystal Vue™, which usually can be completed in a few minutes for those
212
familiar with 3D volume analysis.

Qualitative evaluation of Crystal Vue™ imaging of the normal and abnormal fetal brain
214
For the qualitative evaluation of the Crystal Vue™ rendering technology and the comparison with MRI
215
imaging, normal brain images and four abnormal cases were submitted for offline postprocessing and
216
qualitative evaluation by three fetal medicine experts (CL, GP, AD) and a paediatric neurologist (NSB).
217
As per decision of the Consultant responsible for the antenatal care, the abnormal cases were
218
submitted to antenatal MRI. The images were reviewed by the clinician and expert opinion was then
219
obtained from radiologist specializing in fetal imaging (EW) and a paediatric neurosurgeon (ZT).
220
Independent Fetal Medicine Experts (see Collaborating Authors) were emailed the same pictures and
221
asked to comment on the quality of imaging of anatomical landmarks for the same sections of the fetal
222
brain using multiplanar imaging, Crystal Vue™ and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Discussion
249
There are several proprietary post processing 3D techniques including HDlive Silhouette (68-71),
250
TrueVue (72), Luminance (73) and others, all of which have particular and specific attributes to different
251
imaging applications over which we have deferred to the manufacturers’ descriptions in the absence
252
“head to head” comparison in this area. In this manuscript we do not compare the techniques but
253
describe how to process a 3D volume and optimize its rendering using Samsung Crystal Vue™
254
technology.
255
Rendering optimization
256
Manipulation in the multiplanar mode is a prerequisite for any 3D post processing technique, hence
257
the quality of the acquired volumes, in common with any other 3D rendering technique, depends on
258
the time spent on obtaining the ideal plane. Using Crystal Vue™, several steps or ‘clicks’ are needed on
259
the ultrasound machine keyboard and the user interface requires time and experience for
260
familiarization of all the features although many are common to conventional multiplanar imaging.
261
Qualitative assessment of rendered views obtained with Crystal Vue™ and comparison with MRI
262
The qualitative evaluation of Crystal Vue™ in the assessment of the fetal CNS and its comparison to
263
multiplanar and MRI highlights different properties for each technique. Multiplanar imaging is a useful
264
modality for evaluation of the fetal CNS (7-22,63). Crystal Vue™ appears to give high resolution imaging
265
in the assessment of the developing cortical gyri and sulci in the coronal and sagittal planes and for the
266
delineation of the wall of cystic lesions. MRI imaging seems to provide more information particularly in
267
the axial plane, while the assessment of the cortical folding may be performed with 3D US and Crystal
268
Vue™.
269
Even though a very recent Randomized Controlled Trial suggests that MRI does add to prenatal
270
ultrasound (74), the genuine contribution of MRI compared to US in fetal CNS imaging has been subject
271
of controversies among experts in Neurosonology (75-78). Furthermore, costs and limited availability
272
represent major disadvantages of MRI, which can also be negatively affected by fetal movements (79).
273
On the other hand, multiplanar imaging has high reproducibility among operators and is able to identify
274
small lesions (<4 mm) which cannot be detected at MRI and provide the simultaneous visualization of
275
the same structure in the three planes (7,8,14).
276
Crystal Vue™ rendering in the evaluation of the fetal CNS: which perspectives
277
We do not envisage that Crystal Vue™ can substitute MRI as a second level examination for the
278
assessment of the fetal CNS especially, though it appears capable of providing additional information
279
compared to 2D US (80-82). Very recent evidence has shown that advanced ultrasound and MRI
280
assessment of the fetal CNS may yield information which may not be obtainable through other
281
investigations (80,83), and within this scenario Crystal Vue™ could be complementary to other imaging
282
techniques in the advanced neuroimaging of the fetus.
283
Cortical folding becomes more complex with advancing gestational age (15,16,84), hence we have
284
included only third trimester fetuses as we believe this represents the ideal timeframe in which Crystal
285
Vue™ imaging should be undertaken. It is worth noting that structures that may be suboptimally
286
visualized on 3D US could be clearly seen using Crystal Vue™, whose resolution appeared greater for
287
subtle brain structures such as sulci, fissures and cyst walls, the latter being difficult, if not impossible,
288
sometimes to see on MRI. This may suggest a use in the longitudinal assessment of fetuses where
289
disorders of the neuronal migration are suspected.
290
Conclusions
291
In conclusion, we describe how to obtain Crystal Vue™ rendered views by focusing on CNS imaging and
292
compare our images with conventional multiplanar mode and MRI. We previously reported the features
293
of Crystal Vue™ imaging of the skeletal system (43) and the oropalatal structures (32) and how this may
294
provide additional diagnostic information. By showing views of the normal and the abnormal fetal brain
295
we suggest that Crystal Vue™ rendering may similarly play a role in complementing the antenatal
296
imaging of different CNS structures. We believe that an important message for neurosonologists and
297
Fetal Medicine specialists is not that one technique is better than another but the combination of
298
several techniques – in this case Crystal Vue™ with multiplanar imaging and MRI – could be
299
complementary in the antenatal assessment of the fetal anatomy, thus providing a better diagnostic
300
performance than each technique alone.
