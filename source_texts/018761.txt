#### Abstract

Froxel-based rendering is widely used in modern graphics pipelines to render participating media such as fog and atmospheric lighting. Due to the low resolution of the froxel grid, required for real-time rendering feasibility, these techniques suffer from temporal instability, including flickering, shimmering and ghosting artifacts. Existing temporal accumulation methods that use an exponential moving average provide stable images in static scenes but perform poorly under dynamic volumetric lighting conditions.

This thesis investigates how temporal anti-aliasing techniques originally developed for surface-based rendering can be extended into froxel space for volumetric rendering of participating media. Several history validation, blending, jittering, filtering, and spatial reconstruction methods were implemented and evaluated in Godot and Frostbite. The methods were compared quantitatively against a supersampled volumetric ground truth and qualitatively in dynamic lighting conditions.

The results show that variance clipping applied directly to volumetric froxel data, together with heuristic anti-flicker techniques, greatly reduces ghosting artifacts while introducing only a small amount of flickering. By considering the six nearest froxels when computing current-frame local variance estimates, the best trade-off between temporal stability, spatial accuracy and performance cost is achieved. Depthonly low-discrepancy jittering and weighting samples based on their distance to the froxel center further helps reduce flickering artifacts. The proposed volumetric TAA algorithm was successfully implemented in Frostbite, showing clear visual improvements and acceptable runtime performance on modern console hardware.

Keywords: volumetric rendering, froxel rendering, participating media, temporal anti-aliasing, TAA, temporal accumulation, variance clipping, adaptive blending, real-time rendering, computer graphics, game engines, volumetric lighting, temporal stability, ghosting, flickering, Frostbite, Godot, thesis.

# 1 Introduction

Participating media effects such as volumetric fog, smoke, clouds and atmospheric light scattering are important tools in modern real-time rendering pipelines. In the context of video games, these effects are integral for visual realism and artistic direction, while also helping the player gauge distances and immerse themselves in the world [1]. Volumetric rendering of participating media is, however, complex and computationally expensive, as light transport must be evaluated through a volume rather than only at visible surfaces [2]. To achieve acceptable performance, modern game engines commonly use froxel-based volumetric rendering, where the camera frustum is discretized into a low-resolution three-dimensional grid of frustum-aligned voxels, called froxels [1], [3].

The froxel grid effectively divides a traditional ray marching algorithm into two separate shader passes. First, volumetric radiance is evaluated at the discrete froxel positions within the grid. Then, the volumetric light transport is integrated through the grid along the view direction in a second pass [1]. Separating these stages makes it possible to parallelize radiance evaluation on the GPU, resulting in massive performance gains.

While efficient to compute, the low resolution of the froxel grid introduces severe undersampling artifacts. A single froxel may span a large region, possibly more than a cubic meter, making the reconstruction of sharp volumetric features difficult. This manifests as flickering, shimmering, and noisy volumetric lighting, particularly in scenes with a moving camera, dynamic light sources, animated density textures, or participating media with high frequency spatial variance [3]. Increasing the froxel grid resolution, or taking multiple samples per froxel per frame, naturally reduces these artifacts. However, as the computational cost increases cubically with the resolution, this quickly becomes prohibitively expensive for real-time applications.

To mitigate undersampling artifacts, modern volumetric rendering pipelines commonly utilize temporal accumulation. By blending the current-frame sample with information from previous frames, an approximation of higher-quality volumetric lighting can be achieved at a very little additional computational cost [1], [3]. Over time, the averaging of multiple samples with different sub-froxel jitter offsets has the additional advantage of acting as a form of volumetric anti-aliasing. However, naïve temporal accumulation using an exponential moving average introduces new temporal blurring artifacts known as ghosting, where it takes a long time for the system to react to dynamic changes in the participating media. Depending on the chosen blend

factor, it may take multiple seconds for changes to fade. This fundamental trade-off between reactivity and temporal instability is an issue across modern volumetric rendering pipelines.

Temporal Anti-Aliasing (TAA) methods for surface-based rendering have been extensively explored in previous research and production real-time rendering engines. Modern pixel-based TAA algorithms utilize methods such as history validation and adaptive blending to reduce ghosting artifacts while maintaining temporal stability [4]. In contrast, very little work has explored how these techniques can be applied to volumetric rendering. The fundamental differences between pixel data and froxel data makes the adaptation of these TAA methods into a froxel-based volumetric rendering context non-trivial.

This thesis investigates how temporal anti-aliasing techniques originally developed for surface rendering can be adapted to froxel-based volumetric rendering. The goal is to reduce ghosting and flickering artifacts in dynamically changing participating media, while remaining within the real-time performance constraints of modern game engines.

#### 1.1 Research Questions

This work aims to answer the following questions:

- 1. How can temporal anti-aliasing techniques developed for pixel-space rendering be adapted to a froxel-based volumetric rendering context while preserving visual quality and temporal stability?
- 2. How do different history validation, jittering, blending, and reconstruction strategies affect visual quality, temporal stability, and performance in a froxelbased volumetric rendering pipeline?

# 2 Background

This chapter presents the theoretical background needed to understand temporal anti-aliasing in a froxel-based volumetric rendering context. First, the fundamentals of participating media and volumetric light transport are discussed. Then, real-time froxel-based rendering techniques are presented, focusing on temporal accumulation of froxel data and the volumetric rendering pipeline. Existing temporal anti-aliasing methods for surface rendering are presented next, and finally, the challenges of extending pixel-based TAA methods into froxel space are discussed.

#### 2.1 Volumetric Rendering of Participating Media



Figure 2.1: Comparison of an indoor scene rendered in Frostbite without participating media effects (left) and with participating media effects enabled (right).

Participating media rendering is widely used to achieve atmospheric scattering effects such as volumetric fog, clouds, god rays, light shafts, volumetric shadows, and physically based sky rendering [1]. These effects are important in modern video games for achieving realistic rendering, helping the player visually perceive distances, hiding LOD transitions and streaming of geometry [1]. They are also a crucial tool for art direction and setting the mood of an area or level, as well as being used as

special effects [1]. Figure 2.1 shows how such effects change the depth perception, appearance, and mood in a scene containing a lot of participating media effects.

Rendering participating media requires simulating how light interacts with a volumetric medium such as clouds, fog, or dust particles. It is too computationally heavy to simulate the interactions between individual photons and particles in a real-time rendering context, so approximations describing the behavior of a local region of particles are used instead. In particular, there are four photon-particle interactions, described using mathematical functions, that can affect the radiance that is propagated through a medium: absorption, emission, out-scattering, and in-scattering [2]. These interactions are visualized in Figure 2.2.



Figure 2.2: Illustration of the four fundamental photon-particle interactions at a particle P along direction d in participating media.

Absorption describes how photons are absorbed by the medium and transformed into other forms of energy such as heat. Conversely, emission describes how photons are emitted by the medium. These photon-particle interactions are not direction dependent, with photons being absorbed and emitted equally in all directions. Inand out-scattering, on the other hand, are dependent on the phase function, which describes, for a point in the medium, how much incoming light is scattered in a specific direction [2]. In particular, the phase function describes the proportion of forward- and back-scattering, i.e. how much of the light is scattered in the same direction as, or opposite direction of the incoming light. Together, absorption, emission, out-scattering and in-scattering define how light interacts with the medium as a whole, making real-time volumetric rendering of participating media feasible.

A common volumetric rendering method is ray marching, where for each pixel, the participating media is sampled along a ray at discrete steps [2]. At each step, the density of the participating media and in-scattering from light sources is evaluated, as shown in Figure 2.3. Some of the in-scattered light at the sample point will be transmitted towards the camera. This is evaluated using the phase function and the density of the medium [2]. The scattered light towards the camera is accumulated along the ray, and after marching, the participating media color contribution is blended with previously rendered geometry.

Sampling participating media is computationally expensive. All light sources and volumetric density textures must be iterated over to evaluate their contribution to in-scattering. This necessitates expensive shadow map lookups for light sources, and possibly also the evaluation of complex light-scattering equations. Furthermore, the complexity of ray marching grows by O(n 2 ) with the number of samples along the ray n, quickly making it prohibitively expensive for real-time rendering as sample



Figure 2.3: Illustration of ray marching in participating media, where in-scattered light from a light source s and medium density is evaluated at discrete sample points along a ray, and transmittance is accumulated along direction d towards camera c.

count increases [2]. For this reason, production pipelines commonly use workarounds like downsampling the participating media and using temporal dithered upscaling to hide artifacts [5].

#### 2.2 Frustum Aligned Voxels

In practice, modern video game engines often use frustum aligned voxels (froxels) for volumetric rendering of participating media [3], [6], [7], [8]. As the name implies, froxels are a 3D grid of voxels that are aligned to the camera frustum. Figure 2.4 visualizes how the froxel grid is constructed in the camera's frustum. They are used to discretize volumetric radiance data in the space directly in front of the camera, with each froxel storing local incoming radiance and participating media density.

As sampling participating media is computationally expensive, and the memory requirements for storing the froxel data scales cubically, a low-resolution froxel grid is used in real-time rendering. Wronski uses a froxel grid resolution of 160 × 90 × 64 or 160 × 90 × 128 depending on platform [1], with the depth precision halved on lower-end devices. In Red Dead Redemption 2, a resolution of 160 × 88 × 64 is used [9]. Unity's High Definition Render Pipeline (HDRP) utilizes a resolution of 240 × 135 × 64 by default [6]. These resolutions have an aspect ratio of ∼16:9 across width and height, meaning that each froxel spans an equal number of pixels in width and height in screen space on a standard 16:9 aspect screen. Due to the fixed froxel grid resolution, the performance and memory cost of volumetric calculations are not dependent on the resolution of the screen, making their cost more predictable than e.g. ray marching [1]. Modern game engines with a froxel system, such as Unreal Engine and Unity, expose the froxel resolution as a per-scene editor parameter [6], [7]. Some developers also expose the froxel resolution as a visual quality setting that



Figure 2.4: Visualization of a froxel grid [5].

the player can select in the final game build, based on their hardware and personal quality preference [5].

The froxel grid does not span the entire depth of the frustum as it would require an excessive number of froxels to accurately capture enough detail over the far-reaching draw distances used in modern video games. A volumetric effect range parameter is therefore exposed so that artists can select an appropriate per-scene depth [1], [10]. Wronski uses an effect range of 50 to 128 meters [1], whereas Bauer extends the effect range to around 160 meters in Red Dead Redemption 2 [9]. Kovalovs explains how certain effects, such as God Rays, might require a far draw distance and high resolution to look good [10]. Indoor scenes, on the other hand, may only need a short draw distance, necessitating the ability for artists to select resolution and volumetric effect range on a per-scene basis. As visualized in Figure 2.4, froxels are often non-linearly distributed across scene depth, being smaller closer to the camera. This is necessary because that is where the highest precision is needed as aliasing artifacts become most visible [1].

Local lighting information and participating media density is evaluated independently for each froxel in a compute shader. This allows the computation of in-scattered light within the discretized volumetric grid to be massively parallelized, enabling real-time rendering of high-quality volumetric lighting [1]. By including density in the stored froxel grid, it is trivial to perform the volumetric integration step towards the end of the volumetric rendering pipeline. Furthermore, storing volumetric lighting and density data on a per-froxel basis allows the re-use of previous results through a process called temporal accumulation. It also creates the opportunity to apply temporal anti-aliasing techniques directly on the volumetric data. In contrast, this is not possible in traditional ray marching as volumetric radiance data is stored per-pixel, and not in a world-aligned grid.

#### 2.3 Temporal Accumulation of Volumetric Data

The computationally heavy nature of evaluating participating media necessitates one sample per froxel per frame, which on its own results in a heavily aliased output. By re-using froxel data from the previous frame and blending it with new samples, it is possible to achieve an effectively supersampled volume at very little extra performance cost. This is known as temporal accumulation and is a key tool in modern real-time volumetric rendering.

Temporal accumulation is a form of Monte Carlo integration, using stochastic sampling of the participating media to estimate the value of the light transport integral over time. By averaging the result of stochastic sampling over several frames, it is possible to achieve an output that is statistically very close to the true answer [11].

Temporal accumulation uses, at its core, an exponential moving average (EMA) that, for each froxel, continuously accumulates radiance over multiple frames [4]. For each froxel in each frame, its newly evaluated radiance is blended with its historical value using linear interpolation, as described by Equation 2.1.


$$f(p) = \alpha \cdot s_t(p) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot f_{t-1}(\pi(p)) \quad (2.1)$$

where ft(p) is the blended radiance for froxel p at frame t, α ∈ [0*,* 1] is the blending factor, st(p) is a single current-frame sample of in-scattered radiance, and ft−1(π(p)) is the froxel's historical radiance value, reprojected using π(p).

The result is used to evaluate volumetric lighting contribution in the current frame and is stored in a history buffer to be used in the next frame. The accumulated radiance can be described using an exponential smoothing filter where older samples are progressively assigned lower weights [4].

Re-using historical froxel data is straightforward in a static scene. If the camera moves, however, the data must be reprojected into the current frame before it can be used. This is done using a reprojection vector that describes how the camera was moved and rotated from the previous frame into the current one. It does not describe any movement of the froxel itself, as the world-space position it represents is not associated with any movement.

In order to achieve the desired temporal supersampling effect, the sampling point must be offset from the center of the froxel each frame. This is known as sub-froxel jittering. Without jittering, no new information about the participating media is learned nor accumulated. A low-discrepancy sequence such as the Halton(2,3) sequence of length 8 or 16 is commonly used to obtain sub-pixel jitter points [4], with the latter shown in Figure 2.5. The Halton sequence can be extended into three dimensions to acquire sub-froxel jitter points.

Over time, temporal accumulation with sub-froxel jittering acts as a form of temporal anti-aliasing. The addition of history reprojection makes this a viable approach for high-quality real-time rendering of static participating media in video games, and is



Figure 2.5: The Halton(2,3) sequence of length 16, commonly used to obtain sub-pixel jitter points. The points are numbered by their index in the sequence.

the approach that is currently used in Frostbite. The method breaks down, however, when the scene changes dynamically. Moving lights, animated density textures and color changes all result in blurring and smearing, known as ghosting artifacts [4]. These artifacts, demonstrated in Figure 2.6, appear because the exponential moving average has no way of reacting to changes in the media, and stale or invalid history bleeds into the current frame [4], [12], [13].



Figure 2.6: Comparison of an indoor scene rendered in Frostbite showing no artifacts when the scene is static (left), and ghosting artifacts when the lamp is rotating (right).

Since such a small percentage of the newly sampled radiance values are used each frame (typically 5-10%), it takes a long time for ghosting artifacts to fade. This can be seen in Figure 2.7 where the response time (in frames) is plotted for common α values. With the game running at 60 FPS, it takes around 1, 2 and 4 seconds for ghosting artifacts to fade for an α of 10%, 5%, and 2%, respectively. Naturally, this issue is exacerbated at lower frame rates.

Kovalovs explains how during the implementation of froxel-based volumetric lighting in The Last of Us: Part Two, run time dynamic lights became so problematic that



Figure 2.7: The analytically derived response time (in frames) of the exponential moving average for common α values.

they could not utilize temporal compositing when rendering moving light sources such as flashlights [10]. They instead opted for the computationally expensive alternative of calculating volumetric lighting of dynamic light sources at a high quality every frame. An example of the typical temporal artifacts from a moving spot light can be seen in Figure 2.8. Ghosting is mentioned as a problem in the latest version of many modern game engines such as Unity's HDRP 17.6.0 [6], Unreal Engine 5.7 [7] and Godot 4.7 [8]. The documentation for these engines recommends entirely disabling volumetric contribution for dynamic lights to avoid ghosting.



Figure 2.8: Ghosting and temporal instability artifacts from a moving spot light in Frostbite.

In regions where the signal changes sharply, such as around the source of a spot light, or in a volumetric density texture with high-frequency noise, samples in consecutive frames might produce drastically different values. This manifests as flickering and shimmering artifacts, most noticeable in static scenes. While flickering might be obscured by movement in dynamic scenes, temporal artifacts may also manifest in

other ways, such as the distinct disjointed blobs visible in Figure 2.8. Although using frustum aligned voxels results in temporal aliasing and flickering, it is often preferable to storing volumetric radiance in a world-aligned grid [1]. This is because the ray integration step is trivial and fast to compute for froxels, and they always follow the camera [1].

#### 2.4 Volumetric Accumulation Rendering Pipeline

This section gives a short overview of the stages of a froxel-based volumetric rendering pipeline using temporal accumulation.

Each frame, a low-resolution froxel grid is created in the camera frustum. The local in-scattered radiance and participating media density is computed in parallel for each froxel in a compute shader on the GPU [1].

A per-frame sub-froxel jitter offset is acquired from a pre-defined set of points, with each froxel having the same per-frame offset. This allows the jitter offset to be pre-calculated and passed to the GPU as a shader parameter. The Halton sequence is commonly used to acquire jittered sampling points [4]. Some implementations only jitter the sampling point along the z-axis (depth) of the froxel [3].

The history is reprojected into the current-frame froxel grid using a reprojection vector. The same reprojection vector can be used for all froxels, offset in the shader by the froxel's position in the grid, since the vector only contains information about how the camera moved in between frames [1]. If reprojection fails, i.e. if the reprojection vector points to outside of the history buffer, the current-frame sample is immediately returned [3]. This effectively resets temporal accumulation for the froxel.

The current frame sample is blended together with the reprojected history using an exponential moving average. The resulting value is used as the output of the current frame and the historical input for the next frame [4].

After calculating in-scattered radiance for each froxel, the result can be modified using a low-pass filter to smooth out sharp changes between nearby froxels. This is performed in a separate shader pass. Godot 4.6, for instance, utilizes a gaussian blur with a radius of 3 [8]. Depending on the use-case this might not be ideal as it blurs the volumetric data, removing all sharp features. The documentation for Godot recommends disabling the volumetric fog filter when more detail is required [8].

Next, volumetric radiance and transmittance in the camera's direction is integrated through the froxel grid. The result is another 3D texture of the same size as the froxel grid, where each slice contains the accumulated volumetric transmittance up to that depth [1], [3]. In practice, this is done by stepping through the froxel grid depth-wise from the camera outward in a low-resolution 2D shader (corresponding to the x and y dimensions of the froxel grid) and accumulating the transmitted radiance into the output 3D texture at each step [1], [3].

Finally, when rendering the participating media onto the scene, the world-space position of each fragment is transformed into froxel space and the integrated 3D

texture sampled at that position to acquire the volumetric contribution [3]. The participating media texture is filtered to achieve a smooth result. Without filtering, the participating media appears jagged and pixelated. Wronski uses a quadrilinear filter [1]. The result is blended with previously rendered geometry similarly to pre-multiplied color/alpha [3].

#### 2.5 Pixel TAA History Validation Techniques

To mitigate ghosting artifacts caused by temporal accumulation, the EMA is modified using history validation techniques [4]. Of particular interest is how much of the history to discard (history rejection), and modifying the history to better match newly computed samples (history rectification). This chapter discusses existing history validation techniques that are used on pixel data, as there is not much research on history validation techniques for froxel data.

#### 2.5.1 History Rejection

Conceptually, the simplest way to eliminate stale or invalid historical pixel data is to discard it [4]. This can be done as a hard reject by setting α to 1 when stale or invalid history or drastic changes in light color or intensity is detected. A soft reject can be achieved by limiting α to a high minimum value, this gives a smoother transition as some history is always blended in with the new frame. As α is no longer constant across frames or pixels, this is called adaptive blending [4].

Rejecting history might initially seem straightforward, but detecting stale history or drastic changes in light color or intensity using only aliased current frame samples is a non-trivial problem. It can, for instance, be difficult to determine if a big change in intensity is due to a dynamically changing scene or the per-frame jittered sample offset. Due to jittering, the sampling point might jump from a high radiance/density region to a low radiance/density region very quickly, even if there is no camera or scene movement. This is an especially big issue where there is a lot of variance in the participating medium, such as around the source of a spot light or near the boundary of a participating media volume.

It is expected that current frame samples will inevitably be aliased. This means that creating an error estimate by directly comparing the history to the current frame sample results in both spatial and temporal instability [4]. Filtering the error estimate using a box filter has been proposed [14]. The error estimate can then be used to set a minimum limit on α, forcing the history to refresh by a minimum amount [4]. Other techniques that adjust α based on the color extent of a neighborhood of current frame samples have been developed [4].

Malan proposes utilizing the range of colors in the neighborhood around a pixel to adaptively increase or decrease the blend factor [15]. The idea is that if the color bounding box is small, the color is relatively flat, and the previous frame should have a small contribution. This results in large flat areas appearing more quickly, reducing ghosting [15]. If the color range is large, it instead means that the neighborhood

covers a sharp edge, undersampled feature or noisy region. In such cases, temporal blurring is introduced by reducing the blend factor [15]. This effectively makes it possible to create a parameter for the allowed maximum amount of flicker, that artists can use to balance ghosting with temporal stability on a per-scene basis.

#### 2.5.2 History Rectification

Most history rectification techniques are built upon the assumption that if the historical color value falls outside of the color extent of the current-frame neighborhood, it has become stale and should be corrected before blending [4]. Although any individual sample is assumed to be aliased, a neighborhood of current-frame samples, demonstrated in Figure 2.9 (a), can still be used as a spatial estimate of the range of values that are covered by each pixel [4]. Geometrically, the neighboring color samples define a region in color space that represents reasonable values for the current pixel. History rectification aims to restrict the reprojected history to this region.



Figure 2.9: Illustration of 3 × 3 × 3 neighborhood sampling (a), convex hull clipping (b), AABB clamping and AABB clipping (c), and variance clipping (d) [4].

Mathematically, it would be ideal to clip the historical value against the convex hull formed by neighboring color samples [4]. This hull, shown in Figure 2.9 (b), represents the smallest convex region containing all neighboring colors, providing the tightest possible color extent. It is, however, too computationally expensive

to construct and project the history onto the convex hull within the performance constraints of real-time rendering [4].

Instead, a fast approximation in the form of an Axis-Aligned Bounding Box (AABB) is utilized in practice [4]. The AABB is constructed by computing the minimum and maximum color values of the neighboring pixels for each channel. The range of values in each channel then represents a side of the AABB. This approximation is less accurate than the convex hull but is much cheaper to build and easy to clamp or intersect against.

The most simple rectification technique using the AABB is clamping. In AABB clamping, shown in Figure 2.9 (c), each channel of the color is individually clamped to its minimum and maximum as defined by the AABB. Formally, if ci ∈ R k denotes the set of N current frame samples within a local neighborhood, ch the reprojected historical color from the previous frame, and cmin and cmax the bounds of the AABB, the rectified color cr is obtained as:

$$c_r = \min(\max(c_h, c_{min}), c_{max}) \quad (2.2)$$

where all operations are performed component-wise. Clamping is computationally efficient and ensures that the history remains within the valid color extent. However, because each channel is individually clamped, values tend to collect in the corners of the AABB [16]. For gray-scale scenes this is not an issue, but may lead to strange color-related artifacts in scenes with color information.

A more advanced technique is AABB clipping [4], [16], shown in Figure 2.9 (c). Instead of restricting each color channel individually, the historical color value is projected onto the AABB, towards the current frame sample or the center of the box. The current frame sample is guaranteed to be within the AABB as it is included when computing the extent of the neighborhood. This better preserves the temporal color gradient, generally resulting in a reduction of color-related accumulation artifacts.

Variance clipping is an extension of the idea of clipping against the current frame color extent, but relies instead on the statistical distribution of the values in the neighborhood [4]. The extent bounds are estimated using the neighborhood mean and variance, producing a smaller clipping region [4], visualized in Figure 2.9 (d).

Formally, the first and second sample moments used for variance clipping are defined as follows:

$$m_1 = \sum_{i=1}^N c_i \quad (2.3)$$

$$m_2 = \sum_{i=1}^N c_i^2 \quad (2.4)$$

where the square and summation are taken component-wise. The sample mean µ and standard deviation σ are then estimated as:

$$\mu = \frac{1}{N}m_1 \quad (2.5)$$

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\left| \frac{1}{N} m_2 - \mu^2 \right|} \quad (2.6)$$

where all operations are performed component-wise. The absolute value is taken to avoid negative values caused by floating-point inaccuracies. Then, a min-max clipping interval is constructed around the mean as follows:

$$c_{min} = \mu - \gamma \sigma \quad (2.7)$$

| $c_{max} = \mu + \gamma\sigma$ | (2.8) |
|--------------------------------|-------|
|--------------------------------|-------|

where γ is a user-defined parameter that controls the size of the box, and therefore the strength of clipping.

A useful property of AABB clamping, AABB clipping, and variance clipping is that they operate entirely on current-frame data, making them easier to implement [4].

Performing history rectification directly in RGB color space can produce colorrelated artifacts since the RGB channels are commonly highly correlated. As seen in Figure 2.10 (a), the RGB-space AABB can become poorly aligned with the actual distribution of color samples. Transforming colors into a luminance-chrominance color space such as YCoCg or YCrCb orients the bounding box in the direction of luma [16]. This is preferable since luminance typically has high local contrast and chroma typically does not [16]. This is shown in Figure 2.10 (b), where the neighboring samples lie along the luma direction, resulting in a better fit using the YCoCg-space AABB, and ultimately fewer color-related ghosting artifacts [16].



Figure 2.10: Illustration of clipping against an RGB oriented box (a), and YCoCg oriented box (b).

In the YCoCg color space, visualized in Figure 2.11, the Y component represents luminance, the Co component the orange offset to Y , and the Cg component the green offset to Y [17].



Figure 2.11: The separation of image components into luminance Y , chrominance orange Co, and chrominance green Cg.

The RGB to YCoCg transform, and its inverse are given by [17]:

$$\begin{bmatrix} Y \\ Co \\ Cg \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{4} \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 & -\frac{1}{2} \\ -\frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{2} & -\frac{1}{4} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix} \iff \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} Y \\ Co \\ Cg \end{bmatrix} \quad (2.9)$$

The RGB to YCrCb transform, and its inverse are given by [17]:

$$\begin{bmatrix} Y \\ Cr \\ Cb \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.299 & 0.587 & 0.114 \\ -0.5 & -0.4187 & 0.0813 \\ -0.1687 & -0.3313 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix} \iff \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1.402 & 0 \\ 1 & -0.714 & -0.344 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.772 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} Y \\ Cr \\ Cb \end{bmatrix} \quad (2.10)$$

There exist many RGB-reversible color spaces where luminance and chrominance are decoupled [17]. Both YCoCg and YCrCb are explored in this work even though it has been shown that YCoCg can provide better decorrelation of luminance and chrominance than YCrCb and other similar transforms [18].

#### 2.6 Applying TAA to Froxel Data

In the context of TAA, there are a few key differences between pixel and froxel data. Most notably, froxel data is not associated with a surface, instead describing in-scattering at a point in space. This means that surface-related TAA techniques, such as using the geometry and depth buffer to detect disocclusions, are not viable.

Pixel data is also dependent on the position and rotation of the camera; history can only be re-used if the reprojected pixel represents the same point on the same surface. In between frames, the history might have become occluded by other geometry, and pixel TAA algorithms must reliably detect this change. In contrast, since froxels represent volumes in world space, no such disocclusion handling is necessary [1]. Reprojection is straightforward, with the reprojection vector containing only the motion of the camera, not geometric motion.

Reprojected froxels must only be discarded if they fall outside of the camera's frustum in the new frame. This occurs mainly near the sides of the frustum [1], although the entire history buffer may become unusable if the camera has moved enough between frames.

Performing history rectification on froxel-based radiance data has been proposed [3]. However, existing color-based temporal filtering techniques introduce artifacts when applied to very noisy inputs [19], and due to the low resolution of the froxel grid, a single froxel spans a much larger spatial extent than a single pixel. This inherently amplifies undersampling artifacts, also resulting in the neighborhood around a froxel more likely being aliased than the neighborhood around a pixel.

A subset of length 8 or 16 of the Halton(2,3) sequence is often used to acquire a sub-pixel jitter offset each frame [4]. Quincunx and rotated grid offsets can also be utilized [4]. The Halton sequence can easily be extended into three dimensions to acquire sub-froxel jitter points that span the entire froxel. However, as froxel-based volumetric rendering is, in essence, a massively parallelized form of ray marching, sampling across the entirety of the froxel is not necessarily the best choice for temporal stability as it introduces signal variance in the x and y directions. By instead centering the sampling point in the middle of the froxel and offsetting it only along the froxel's depth (in the camera forward direction), the spatial complexity of the jittering sequence is reduced to one dimension. This is the approach used in Frostbite [3].

The phase function introduces view-dependence for volumetric radiance data. This can become problematic for reprojected history data if a highly view dependent phase function is used [1]. However, this work does not explore how volumetric TAA is affected by highly view dependent phase functions due to scope.

# 3 Methods

In this chapter, implementation details and the methods used for gathering and analyzing data is discussed. First, it is described how the Godot volumetric rendering pipeline was modified to support temporal anti-aliasing algorithms beyond the already existing temporal accumulation technique. Then, the evaluation metrics are defined and explained. The overall methodology of the experiments is described next, and finally, the Frostbite implementation is discussed.

#### 3.1 Modified Godot Volumetric Rendering Pipeline

Initially, the volumetric TAA algorithms were implemented in the open source game engine Godot 4.6. Godot was chosen because it already had support for volumetric rendering of fog, light, and density textures using a froxel-based system. In addition, a basic version of volumetric temporal accumulation already existed in the form of a simple exponential moving average. This implementation exhibited the typical trade-off of temporal accumulation, with heavy flickering for high α values and lots of ghosting for low α values. In-between α values only resulted in both temporal artifacts being prevalent. As such, it was mostly useful for static scenes. The open source nature of, and extensive public documentation for Godot enabled quick prototyping, shortening the time before data could start being collected and analyzed.

Each frame, a froxel grid of resolution 160 × 90 × 128 is constructed within the camera frustum. The froxels are distributed non-linearly across scene depth, using the exponential distribution function shown in Equation 3.1.


$$z'(z) = z^s, \quad (3.1)$$

where z ∈ [0*,* 1] represents the normalized depth of the froxel in clip space, s is the exponential distribution factor, and z ′ is the transformed depth value.

This makes nearby froxels smaller, where volumetric detail is needed the most. We found that a distribution factor of s = 2*.*0 provided a reasonable quality trade-off between nearby and far-away froxels. The same froxel grid resolution and depth distribution factor was used in all our Godot tests.

Figure 3.1 presents the individual shader passes of the modified Godot volumetric rendering pipeline. In shader pass 1, volumetric radiance and participating media density are evaluated for each froxel. First, it is checked whether the history can be reprojected, If reprojection fails, i.e. if the reprojection vector points outside of the history buffer, no sub-froxel temporal jittering is applied, as jittering is only useful when current frame samples can be blended with the history [4]. For froxels that can be reprojected, a sub-froxel jitter offset is acquired from a pre-calculated list containing the Halton(2,3,5) sequence of length 16. If depth-only jittering is used, the jitter offset is applied only to the z-axis, remaining centered in the x and y directions.



Figure 3.1: Diagram of the relevant shader passes in the modified Godot volumetric rendering pipeline. Shader passes 1-4 are compute shaders run for each froxel, pass 5 is a compute shader run for each view ray, and pass 6 is a fragment shader run for each pixel.

At the jittered sample position, the participating media density and in-scattered lighting are evaluated. All relevant light sources are iterated over, and their radiance contribution is accumulated, taking shadow maps and attenuation into account. Ambient volumetric lighting and global illumination is also added. Local density is evaluated by iterating over all volumetric shape nodes and accumulating their density contribution, and combining the result with the global fog density value. The result is stored in a 16-bit RGBA 3D texture of the same size as the froxel grid, with the RGB channels containing wavelength-dependent radiance information, and the alpha channel containing participating media density. Storing density together with radiance makes it possible to later apply temporal accumulation and anti-aliasing methods on the volumetric medium directly, and also makes the volumetric depth integration step trivial to compute.

In the original Godot implementation, volumetric sampling and temporal blending were performed in the same compute shader pass. In order to implement more advanced TAA techniques that require a neighborhood of current frame samples, a

separate volumetric TAA resolve pass was introduced as shader pass 2 in Figure 3.1. In this pass, the newly sampled radiance and density is blended with the reprojected historical froxel value after performing history validation. Most of the volumetric TAA methods explored in this work exist within this pass, and as such, a more detailed breakdown is presented in section 3.1.1.

In shader pass 3, the TAA resolved data is copied to the history buffer to be used in the next frame. This is done in a separate pass to avoid reading and writing to the same buffer in the same pass.

In shader pass 4, a low-pass filter is optionally applied to the TAA resolved data in the x and y directions of the froxel grid. This shader pass is skipped entirely if the user disables volumetric fog filtering in the project settings. Volumetric low-pass filters are not explored further in this work, but this is where in the pipeline they would be applied.

In shader pass 5, the TAA resolved data is integrated along the camera view rays in the forward direction. This is done by iterating through the froxel slices in frontto-back order, accumulating in-scattered radiance and taking transmittance into account. Transmittance is calculated using the Beer-Lambert law, as per Equation 3.2. The accumulated radiance is stored in another 3D texture that is used later when sampling the participating media contribution at shaded surface fragments.


$$T(\Delta d) = e^{-\Delta d t}, \quad (3.2)$$

where T(∆d) is the transmittance after traveling distance ∆d between two consecutive depth slices in the medium, and t is the extinction coefficient describing absorption and out-scattering within the participating medium, which in our implementation corresponds directly to the local participating media density. Because of the nonlinear distribution of froxels across depth, ∆d is different for each pair of consecutive depth slices.

Finally, in shader pass 6, the integrated volumetric lighting is blended with the rendered scene color. The volumetric contribution for each pixel is retrieved by transforming the shaded fragment position into froxel space and trilinearly sampling the integrated volumetric 3D texture. When testing the volumetric TAA methods implemented in Godot, trilinear sampling was disabled, leaving froxels blocky and visually distinct. This would not be done in a production setting (other than perhaps as an artistic effect), but is useful for visualizing the resolution of the froxel grid and qualitatively assessing temporal artifacts.

#### 3.1.1 TAA Resolve Pass In More Detail

Most of the tested volumetric TAA methods are implemented in the TAA resolve pass (shader pass 2 in Figure 3.1\). Figure 3.2 provides a more detailed overview of this stage of the volumetric rendering pipeline.

The TAA resolve pass begins by reading the froxel's current frame volumetric radiance and density values (step 1 in Figure 3.2\) that were computed in the previous compute



Figure 3.2: Diagram of the steps of the volumetric TAA Resolve pass.

shader pass. These values can trivially be retrieved using an integer 3D texture lookup.

Next, the history buffer is sampled using the froxel's position and the reprojection vector (step 2 in Figure 3.2\). Because of floating-point precision limitations of the reprojection vector, an exact one-to-one mapping between froxels across frames is not guaranteed, and a trilinear sampler is therefore used. In practice, this leads to some blurring when the camera moves.

If the reprojected froxel position falls outside of the bounds of the history 3D texture, a historical value cannot be retrieved. In such cases the current frame in-scattering and density values are returned immediately, skipping the rest of the shader. Temporal accumulation is effectively reset for the froxel.

When both current frame values and reprojected historical values have been retrieved, they are transformed into the selected color space (step 3 in Figure 3.2\). Subsequent steps will operate on the transformed colors, with the density, stored in the alpha channel, remaining unchanged.

History validation is then applied (step 4 in Figure 3.2\). This involves using a neighborhood of current frame samples to estimate the color extent of the froxel, and then mapping the historical value onto that color extent. The details of the algorithm at this stage depends on the selected history validation method.

Next, the rectified history is blended with the current-frame sample using an exponential moving average (step 5 in Figure 3.2\). Depending on the selected blending method, the blend factor may either be constant across all froxels, or adapt dynamically based on the local neighborhood.

Finally, the blended result is transformed back into RGB space and stored in the output froxel texture (step 6 in Figure 3.2\). The TAA resolved froxel grid is then used in subsequent stages of the volumetric rendering pipeline.

#### 3.2 Experimental Variables in Godot

This section describes the temporal anti-aliasing methods and spatiotemporal reconstruction techniques that were implemented and evaluated in the modified Godot volumetric rendering pipeline.

#### 3.2.1 Exponential Moving Average

A baseline implementation of temporal accumulation using an exponential moving average (EMA) and a constant blend factor of α = 0*.*05 was first established. Currentframe volumetric radiance and participating media density is linearly blended with the reprojected history each frame.

#### 3.2.2 History Rectification Methods

Three history rectification methods were implemented and evaluated: AABB clamping, AABB clipping, and variance clipping. All of these methods work by constraining the reprojected historical value to a neighborhood of current-frame froxel samples. AABB clamping individually limits each channel of the history's RGBA to the minimum and maximum values of the neighborhood. AABB clipping instead projects the history onto the AABB defined by the minimum and maximum values in each color channel. Finally, variance clipping uses the statistical distribution of values in the neighborhood to create the bounds of the AABB used for clipping. The mathematical definition for each method remains unchanged from their original pixel-space TAA counterparts.

In addition to the core history rectification methods, a heuristic anti-flicker method inspired by the work of Karis [16] was implemented. The method biases the signal towards the frame where clipping occurs, essentially reducing the impact of quick impulses. Karis does not explain exactly how the blend factor is reduced. For simplicity, we therefore use a linear scaling of α based on the distance to clipping as shown in Equation 3.3.


$$\alpha_{i,t} = (1 - k)\alpha_{\min} + k\alpha_{\max} \quad (3.3)$$

$$k = \text{clamp} \left( \frac{d_{i,t} - d_{\min}}{d_{\max} - d_{\min}}, 0, 1 \right) \quad (3.4)$$

where αi,t is the blend factor for froxel i at frame t, αmin and αmax are user-defined minimum and maximum blend factors, k is the interpolation factor, and di,t is the distance in color space between the reprojected history and its clipped counterpart for froxel i at frame t. The parameters dmin and dmax define the range over which the linear scaling is applied.

Small distances indicate that the history was recently clipped, in which case a lower blend factor is used to favor slower temporal accumulation near the sharp discontinuity. Larger distances increase the blend factor, allowing the EMA to respond more quickly to changes in the signal. In practice, the distance is computed as the Euclidean length of the RGB difference vector between the reprojected history hreprojected and the clipped history hclipped:

$$d_{i,t} = |h_{reprojected} - h_{clipped}| \quad (3.5)$$

Across our test scenarios, we found that αmin = 0*.02 and *αmax = 0*.05, with *dmin = 0 and dmax = 0*.*1, produced stable results.

#### 3.2.3 Color Spaces

The RGB, YCoCg, and YCrCb color spaces were implemented and evaluated. After retrieving any sample, it is transformed into the selected color space. The final blended result is transformed back into RGB space before being stored in the output froxel texture.

#### 3.2.4 Adaptive Blending Method

An adaptive blending method inspired by the work of Malan [15] was implemented and evaluated. In contrast to the anti-flicker method described earlier, the adaptive blending does not modify the history whatsoever. Instead, only the blend factor is changed, adaptively adjusting to the color extent of the local neighborhood. Malan does not describe exactly how the α should be increased as the extent of the neighborhood becomes smaller, so the most conceptually simple method, a linear mapping shown in Equation 3.6, is used.


$$\alpha_{i,t} = (1 - k)\alpha_{\min} + k\alpha_{\max} \quad (3.6)$$

$$k = 1 - \text{clamp}\left(\frac{r_{i,t} - r_{\min}}{r_{\max} - r_{\min}}, 0, 1\right) \quad (3.7)$$

where αi,t is the blend factor for froxel i at frame t, αmin and αmax are user-defined minimum and maximum blend factors, and ri,t is the extent of the local color-space neighborhood. The parameters rmin and rmax define the range over which the linear scaling is applied.

In practice, the extent of the neighborhood is computed as the Euclidean length of the RGB difference vector:

$$r_{i,t} = |c_{max} - c_{min}| \quad (3.8)$$

where cmin and cmax are the minimum and maximum RGB values of the local neighborhood, respectively.

In our tested scenarios, we found that αmin = 0*.05 and *αmax = 0*.50, with *rmin = 0*.01 and *rmax = 0*.*40, produced stable results.

#### 3.2.5 Jitter Methods

Jittering and reconstruction filters change how the participating media is sampled without modifying the underlying TAA algorithm. Still, the chosen jittering and reconstruction methods greatly affect the spatial and temporal reconstruction quality of the output.

To generate jitter points, 3D Halton noise [20] was used. This type of noise was chosen since any subsequence is roughly equally evenly distributed in the pixel domain, allowing for fast convergence [4]. It was chosen to utilize a Halton sequence of length 16, as done in INSIDE [21], but extended to three dimensions using Halton(2,3,5), as visualized in Figure 3.3. For depth-only jittering, the sampling points are centered on the froxel and offset only along the z-axis.



Figure 3.3: Illustration of full 3D (left) and depth only (right) sub-froxel jitter points derived using the Halton(2,3,5) sequence of length 16. The points are numbered by their index in the sequence.

Weighing each jittered sub-froxel sample equally each frame effectively results in a spatiotemporal box filter [16]. This filter has poor edge quality and is not stable under motion due to the sharp edges of the box [16]. Karis applies a Gaussian filter fit to Blackman-Harris 3.3, plotted in Figure 3.4, to the jittered samples. We utilize the same filter, weighing samples by their distance to the center of the froxel:

| $W(x) = e^{-2.29x^2}$ | (3.9) |
|-----------------------|-------|
|-----------------------|-------|

where W(x) is the sample weight at distance x from the center of the froxel.

#### 3.2.6 Spatial Reconstruction Methods

When estimating current frame color extent in pixel-space TAA, a 3×3 neighborhood of pixels is commonly used. The most straightforward analogy in volumetric space is to use a 3 × 3 neighborhood of froxels in the current depth slice.

As shown in Figure 3.5, the 3 × 3 neighborhood contains no spatial information from nearby depth slices. By instead considering a 3×3×3 neighborhood of froxels, spatial information from nearby slices is included. This applies an additional smoothing of the signal across depth.



Figure 3.5: Illustration of the 3 × 3 (left), 3 × 3 × 3 (middle) and 6-tap (right) neighborhoods.

A 3 × 3 × 3 neighborhood requires 27 samples per-froxel. This is likely too many in a real-time rendering context. A 6-tap neighborhood is therefore also implemented and tested. Analogous to the 4-tap nearest pixels neighborhood that Pedersen uses in INSIDE [21], 6-tap utilizes samples from the six nearby froxels, as well as the center froxel, to compute the bounds of the neighborhood.

The 3 × 3, 3 × 3 × 3 and 6-tap neighborhoods were implemented in Godot as precomputed lists of sampling offsets. Each frame, the sampling index is provided as a shader uniform.

#### 3.3 Frostbite Implementation

The findings from the experiments in Godot were used to create a final proposed volumetric TAA algorithm. This algorithm was implemented in the Frostbite engine and uses variance clipping with anti-flicker applied. Anti-flicker uses minimum and maximum blend factors αmin = 0*.02 and *αmax = 0*.05, over the range defined by *dmin = 0 and dmax = 0*.*1. The bounds used for variance clipping are constructed using a 6-tap neighborhood. Initially, the YCoCg color space was also implemented, but it produced too much flickering to be feasible. The RGB color space is thus used instead.

In practice, we found that the region near the source of a spot light exhibits too great intensity variance to be temporally stable. This is due to the infinitesimally small point from which the light originates; the sampled value might be zero one frame, while jumping incredibly high the next simply due to sub-froxel jittering. This was mitigated by introducing a minimum distance threshold of 0.5m when calculating spot light intensity, with any sample closer than this using the minimum distance. This greatly reduces high-intensity fireflies, at the cost of the attenuation shape of the spot light being less visually distinct.

#### 3.4 Evaluation Methodology

When evaluating temporal anti-aliasing techniques, the important artifacts to consider are ghosting and flickering. Conceptually, these correspond to the classical statistics bias-variance trade-off of a biased estimator. We therefore treat the TAA algorithm as a biased estimator, defining its correctness using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) as per Equation \(3.10\).


$$\text{MSE}[F] = \mathbb{E}[(F - G)^2] = V[F] + \beta[F]^2 \quad (3.10)$$

where F is the output of the estimator, G is the true value (ground truth), V [F] is the variance of the estimator and β[F] is its bias [22].

What a volumetric TAA algorithm essentially tries to do is to perfectly reconstruct the ground truth while introducing as little visually perceptible temporal variance as possible. Constant reconstruction errors, in the form of ghosting, behave similarly to estimator bias, and temporal instability, in the form of flickering, corresponds to estimator variance. We therefore measure these two artifacts separately, making evaluating overall algorithm quality analogous to a classical bias-variance minimization problem.

The reconstruction error and temporal instability metrics described in this section are mostly useful for comparing different algorithms to each other. The values on their own are difficult to interpret as their scale does not directly correspond to perceptual image quality. Therefore, an additional image quality assessment is performed using the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM). SSIM produces normalized values

in the range of [0*,* 1] which are easier to interpret, as their meaning is well established in literature [23].

#### 3.4.1 Measuring Reconstruction Error

Reconstruction error measures how much a tested algorithm deviates from the ground truth. The difference is quantified using the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), where a higher RMSE value indicates a larger difference. Instead of comparing the pixels of the final image output, as is commonly done for pixel-based rendering, the froxel data is used directly when measuring algorithm quality. This allows for a more nuanced understanding of how the TAA algorithm operates. The reconstruction error E for froxel i at frame t is defined as:

$$E_i(t) = F_i(t) - G_i(t) \quad (3.11)$$

where Fi(t) is the output of the TAA algorithm and Gi(t) is the ground truth value. All operations are performed component-wise.

RMSE across an entire test sequence is calculated as:

$$RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{i \in \Omega} \frac{1}{T} \sum_t E_i(t)^2} \quad (3.12)$$

where Ω is the region of interest and T is the total number of frames in the sequence. All operations are performed component-wise and color channels averaged at the end to obtain the final RMSE score [24].

This metric captures constant reconstruction error, including ghosting artifacts, and can therefore be used as a measurement of estimator bias.

#### 3.4.2 Measuring Temporal Instability

While RMSE measures the overall accuracy of the algorithm, it does not capture temporal instability, or at least not in a very readable way. We define a temporal instability (TI) metric based on the frame-to-frame change in error:

$$TI = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{i \in \Omega} \frac{1}{T} \sum_t (E_i(t) - E_i(t-1))^2} \quad (3.13)$$

where Ω is the region of interest, T is the total number of frames in the sequence, Ei(t) is the error of froxel i at frame t. All operations are done component-wise and the color channels averaged at the very end to obtain the final flicker value.

The TI metric measures temporal variation in the reconstruction error, and can therefore be used as a measurement of signal variance for the tested TAA algorithm.

Large values indicate quick changes in the underlying signal, which are often perceived visually as flickering artifacts.

However, since the metric does not distinguish between rapid changes in the ground truth and slow responses in the reconstructed signal, the temporal instability metric does not correspond directly to visually perceived flicker. For instance, if a big change occurs between frames in the ground truth and the TAA algorithm reacts very slowly to the same change, a large spike in the TI metric is produced even if the visual output remains relatively stable. However, if there is a lot of visual flickering, it is reflected in the temporal instability metric, as flickering cannot occur without temporal oscillations in the signal. In other words, high visual flickering translates to high temporal instability, while high temporal instability does not necessarily manifest as visual flickering artifacts. The 95th percentile of the TI signal is therefore also sometimes presented as it omits big one-frame outliers, making it more aligned with visually perceived flicker in some scenarios.

#### 3.4.3 Measuring Structural Similarity

While the RMSE captures direct froxel-space numerical differences between the tested algorithm and the ground truth, it is not necessarily a good measurement of perceived visual quality in the final rendered output. To evaluate the perceptual similarity between the tested algorithm and the ground truth, we use the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) [25].

SSIM compares local luminance, contrast, and structural information between two images, producing a similarity score in the range of [0, 1], with a higher value indicating greater similarity to the ground truth [25]. SSIM is calculated for each frame and the values plotted to highlight how different algorithms compare across a sequence.

In addition to the SSIM score, an SSIM map can also be computed. The SSIM map visually displays local structural similarity between two images, making it possible to qualitatively assess regions where the reconstruction quality is poor. In the SSIM map, pixels with perfect similarity are white, becoming darker as the SSIM score is reduced. In our tests, this results in dark regions where there are a lot of ghosting and flickering artifacts.

SSIM score and SSIM maps are calculated using the structural\_similarity method provided by the scikit-image metrics package [26]. A gaussian kernel with weight 1*.*5 was used to match the implementation of Wang et al. [25].

#### 3.4.4 Ground Truth

In order to calculate our evaluation metrics, each frame requires a reference to compare the output of the TAA algorithm to. For each frame, two ground truth outputs are generated: the final rendered image and the corresponding volumetric 3D texture containing the froxel data directly after the TAA resolve pass.

The ground truth image is used to evaluate final screen-space image quality using SSIM. The 3D texture is used for calculating the RMSE and TI metrics.

The same froxel grid resolution is used for both the TAA algorithm and the ground truth. This makes it possible to directly compare the outputs. In the ground truth, each froxel is supersampled using a uniform 16 × 16 × 16 pattern and the samples averaged to produce the froxel's reference value. This process is similar to how the ground truth is often generated for pixels, with Schied et al. using 4096 samples per pixel as their ground truth [19]. While the froxels in our case span a larger spatial extent than pixels, the same supersampling resolution of 163 = 4096 is used.

#### 3.4.5 Region of Interest

In our test scenarios, most of the froxels are completely unaffected by in-scattering and therefore do not contribute with meaningful information when computing evaluation metrics. A region of interest (ROI) is therefore applied to the volumetric data, allowing us to effectively zoom in on froxels that participate in light transport calculations.

A froxel is included in the ROI if it is ever affected by radiance, be it from a light source or through emission. In practice, this is calculated as any of the RGB channels containing a non-zero value at any point during the test scenario. In effect, this means that only froxels that were visible at some point during the scenario are included in the ROI.

The ROI is computed separately for each tested algorithm since different methods may produce non-zero contributions in different regions of the froxel grid even in the same test scenario. In practice, this means that when analyzing an algorithm, the union of its ROI and the ROI of the ground truth is used:

$$ROI_{effective} = ROI_{scenario} \cup ROI_{truth} \quad (3.14)$$

This ensures that only the relevant froxels are included for each output while excluding completely empty regions.

#### 3.4.6 Measuring Performance

Performance measurements are done in the Frostbite engine, as the goal is to evaluate how the final proposed volumetric TAA algorithm fares in a production rendering environment. We measure GPU frame times over a one-minute interval, reporting the mean, minimum, and maximum time in milliseconds, as well as the Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) in percents.

We are interested in seeing how much the proposed algorithm increases the performance cost of the TAA resolve pass itself, and also how it compares in the larger context of the entire volumetric rendering pipeline.

Performance measurements are straightforward in our case, as the cost of the volumetric TAA methods explored in this work does not differ due to the volumetric effects used in the scene, or due to view dependence. In comparison, the performance of some pixel-based anti-aliasing methods, such as MSAA, depends on the sampled geometry, coverage, and visibility [2].

#### 3.5 Experiment Methodology

To measure the quality of a TAA algorithm, an appropriate test scenario is first selected. The scenario is run multiple times, first to generate the ground truth, and then once per tested TAA algorithm to acquire the test outputs. The number of output frames depends on the length of the test scenario.

The Godot volumetric rendering pipeline was modified to output the resulting 3D texture after the TAA resolve pass into a .raw file each frame. The final image output after rendering was also stored as a.png each frame. To make the process of testing and analysing data easier, the output was automatically organized in folders for each test scenario, as shown in Figure 3.6. Careful consideration was put into making the jittering sequence deterministic across all tests, ensuring that running the same test scenario multiple times always results in exactly the same output.



Figure 3.6: Diagram illustrating the output folder structure of a test scenario, showing one reference run (ground\_truth ) and one test run (test\_run\_1). Folders are shown in gray and files in green. The dotted line around the .png and .raw boxes indicate that there are multiple files in the folder, one for each of the N frames in the test scenario. The data.txt files contain configuration parameters for each run.

A python script was developed to compare the results between different algorithms. The script first loads the data from the ground truth run, and then all of the test runs within the scenario folder. It then iterates over all frames of the scenario, and for each frame, calculates the RMSE, Temporal Instability and SSIM values between the ground truth and each test run. The RMSE, TI and SSIM values can then be

plotted across the duration of the test scenario. When calculating RMSE and TI across the entire sequence, the sum of the squared error is accumulated across all frames, and the root is taken at the very end.

# 4 Results

In this chapter, the froxel TAA algorithms are quantitatively evaluated in terms of volumetric spatial accuracy (RMSE), temporal instability (TI), and structural similarity of the final image output (SSIM). Some qualitative assessments are also included where relevant. Initially, the behavior of a exponential moving average (EMA) is established. Then, different froxel TAA algorithms are evaluated across a variety of scenarios in Godot. All Godot tests were performed with the same froxel resolution of 160×90×128 and depth distribution factor of 2. Finally, output images and performance characteristics of the Frostbite implementation are presented.

### 4.1 Exponential Moving Average Behavior

To understand the limitations of temporal accumulation, the behavior of the exponential moving average is analyzed. The EMA, henceforth referred to as the baseline, establishes a reference point that subsequent techniques can be evaluated against. For simplicity, the baseline scenario uses depth-only jittering and a blend factor of α = 0*.*05.

#### 4.1.1 EMA Response to Abrupt Change

To evaluate how the baseline EMA behaves when a sharp discontinuity occurs, the step response scenario is used. The scenario, shown in Figure 4.1, contains a white spotlight in the center of the screen. The light is lit at the start and remains so for 100 frames, then it abruptly turns off to zero brightness for 100 frames, before finally turning back on for another 100 frames. Before data is collected, the history buffer is allowed to converge over 120 frames. The RMSE and Temporal Instability across the scenario are plotted in Figure 4.2.



Figure 4.1: Rendered image output of the ground truth in the step response scenario, showing the three distinct states of the light in sequential order.



Figure 4.2: RMSE (left) and Temporal Instability (right) over time for the baseline EMA in the step response scenario.

For the first 100 frames, both the RMSE and the Temporal Instability remain small as expected, confirming that EMA performs well under static conditions. There is a little temporal variance (TI) due to the jittering of sample points. There is also a small constant bias (RMSE) since the algorithm is never allowed to fully converge.

When the light turns off at frame 100, the RMSE jumps sharply. At this time, the history contains high intensity values while the ground truth instantly drops to zero. As the blending factor is fixed at α = 0*.*05, the EMA reacts to the change by only 5% each frame, resulting in a slow exponential decay towards zero. This takes around 100 frames, matching the analytically derived expectation for a 5% decay.

A similar spike in RMSE can be seen when the light turns on again at frame 200, this time for the opposite reason; the history buffer has converged to all zeroes and the EMA is unable to respond to the sudden increase in illumination. Visually, this manifests as the light slowly fading in before converging to fully lit again. Note how the measurements converge to the same values as in the beginning of the scenario.

The Temporal Instability value also spikes when the sharp discontinuities occurs. This is because the error between the baseline and the ground truth is very small before the discontinuity, and very large in the frame directly after. These spikes are not useful when attempting to measure overall visually perceptible flicker, as they do not accurately convey how much visual change occurs in the scene. Indeed, they exists precisely because the baseline algorithm did not react to the discontinuity fast enough.

The abrupt on/off transition is designed to clearly show temporal lag, but the convergence time of the baseline EMA is defined entirely by the blending factor α. Consequently, it takes equally long for all changes in the underlying signal to become fully accumulated in the history. This means that while a change of a big magnitude is more visually noticeable, all ghosting artifacts take equally long to fade, no matter the magnitude of the change.

#### 4.1.2 EMA Response to Gradual Change

The limitations of the baseline EMA are further evaluated in a scenario where the underlying signal changes slowly over time. While the step response scenario could realistically occur in a production setting, this experiment is designed to examine the behavior of the algorithm over an extended duration where the scene is constantly changing.

The gradual change scenario is based on the same spotlight configuration as before, but the light source instead moves slowly across the scene, changing direction every 50 frames. Between frames 350-400, the light stops moving and instead rotates 90 degrees. Between frames 400-450, the light color linearly fades from white to red, and between frames 450-500, the color linearly fades from red to blue. The RMSE and Temporal Instability across the scenario are plotted in Figure 4.3.



Figure 4.3: RMSE (left) and Temporal Instability (right) over time for the baseline EMA in the gradual change scenario.

The baseline EMA exhibits a high error throughout the entire sequence. As the light moves, new froxels become illuminated while other, previously lit froxels, constantly fall outside of the light cone. As the EMA reacts to the change at a fixed rate, it consistently lags behind, leading to a persistent bias which manifests visually as ghosting.

When the light changes direction, the RMSE temporarily decreases as previously lit froxels are revisited and their values become more consistent with the ground truth for a short while. When the light then continues into new regions, the error increases again. This explains the valleys seen around frames 50, 150, 200, and 250.

During constant motion, the RMSE does not converge down toward zero. It instead exhibits a high constant bias. For example, between frames 300-350, the light is moving from left to right at a constant speed, producing a relatively constant error.

The high Temporal Instability seen between frames 0-350 does not produce visual flickering artifacts. It instead arises from the baseline not reacting fast enough to sharp changes in the underlying signal; individual froxels are constantly becoming lit and unlit around the sides of the light cone, going from zero to high intensity and vice versa. This is captured perfectly in the ground truth but the baseline EMA cannot react to it fast enough, exactly as in the step response scenario, but at a smaller scale. This highlights a quirk of the Temporal Instability metric: it cannot distinguish between changes in the ground truth and the tested algorithm, making it somewhat difficult to parse for dynamic scenes.

Changes caused by motion and rotation of the light source impact more froxels simultaneously, and to a higher degree, leading to bigger errors.

#### 4.2 History Rectification

At this stage, the choice of neighborhood sampling technique becomes relevant, as all evaluated history rectification techniques utilize a neighborhood of current-frame samples. The 3 × 3 × 3 and 6-tap neighborhoods smooth out the signal across depth slices, essentially acting like a spatial stabilizer and reducing visible flickering. At this stage, we want these artifacts to remain exposed as it helps when evaluating the underlying TAA algorithm. Therefore, a 3 × 3 neighborhood is used, as it does not average the signal across depth. To make the results comparable with the baseline EMA, depth-only jittering and a constant blend factor of α = 0*.*05 is used.

#### 4.2.1 Response to Abrupt Change

Again, the step response scenario is used to test how the history rectification techniques react to abrupt change. While this scenario is incredibly rudimentary, it gives important insights in how these algorithms operate. The RMSE and Temporal Instability across the scenario are plotted in Figure 4.4.



Figure 4.4: RMSE (left) and Temporal Instability (right) over time for history rectification techniques and the baseline EMA in the step response scenario.

As shown previously, the baseline EMA performs well in fully static conditions (the first 100 frames in Figure 4.4\). When comparing it against the history rectification techniques, it actually performs the best, both in terms of accuracy and Temporal Instability. However, when the light turns off, the difference between the baseline EMA and the history rectification techniques becomes incredibly pronounced. The history rectification techniques all immediately drop to an error of exactly 0, reacting perfectly to the drop in intensity. This confirms that, while optimal for static scenes, the baseline EMA is completely unsuitable when quick changes occur.

The perfect drop in error displayed by each history rectification technique when the light turns off is a result of the current-frame neighborhood having an extent of 0 across all axes. This means that the history is clamped/clipped against, effectively, a point. It is clear that history rectification is excellent at reacting a drop in intensity, as the size of the neighborhood shrinks, and temporal lag is effectively eliminated entirely.

When the light turns on again (frame 200 in Figure 4.4\), the history rectification techniques react slightly differently. All techniques have substantially better accuracy than the baseline EMA, with variance clipping producing the lowest error spike, meaning that it is the best at reconstructing the signal when light is quickly introduced. The two method perform roughly equally well for the rest of the sequence. After frame 500, when the light becomes completely static in terms of both movement and color, AABB clipping actually outperforms variance clipping by a tiny margin, precisely as it did in the step response scenario. Still, variance clipping greatly outperforms AABB clipping overall, with the difference in RMSE after frame 500 being negligible in comparison to during movement.

AABB clamping again performs identically to AABB clipping when the light is white (frames 0-400). However, after frame 400 when the light stops moving and gradually starts changing color, AABB clamping starts performing worse. There is an especially big RMSE spike at frame 500 when the light finishes fading from red to blue and becomes a static blue color. AABB clamping struggles here, with the error constantly increasing as the light fades from red to blue (frames 450-500 in Figure 4.5\). It then takes around 50 frames for the error to converge back down to the level of AABB clipping. This delayed response highlights the limitations of clamping when color is introduced.

At frame 400, the light has completed a 90 degree turn. As shown in Figure 4.2, the previous orientation of the spot light can still be seen quite clearly in the baseline scenario as a large smeared region. In contrast, AABB and variance clipping are much more accurate, with the latter preserving the shape of the spot light better. AABB clamping is not shown as it produces the exact same result as AABB clipping for this frame.

#### 4.2.3 Comparing AABB Clamping and AABB Clipping

Earlier testing indicated that AABB clamping struggles to rectify the history when the color changes gradually. In this experiment, AABB clamping is compared to AABB clipping in the color overlap test scenario where the light color changes in a multitude of ways.

The color overlap test scenario consists of two static spot lights that overlap. The light on the left stays the same blue color throughout the scenario. From frames 0-200 the light source on the right abruptly changes color every 50 frames. At frame 200 the light abruptly turns blue and then linearly fades to green over 100 frames. From frames 300-350 the light linearly fades from green to red over 50 frames before abruptly turning magenta. Frame 200 and 350 produce the biggest differences between clipping and clamping. The image output at these frames can be visually compared in Figure 4.3, with the SSIM map highlighting differences to the ground truth. Note, for instance, how some green color bleeds into frame 200 at the intersection of the light sources.

As shown in Figure 4.6, clipping consistently achieves better accuracy than clamping. The difference is most pronounced when the color changes gradually (frames 200-350). The RMSE spike at frame 350 is especially large because the error has been building up from the gradual color change starting at frame 300, and when the color abruptly changes at frame 350, more error is quickly introduced. These errors compound, becoming especially visible for clamping.

#### 4.2.4 Impact of Color Space on History Rectification

As seen in section 4.2.3, clipping performs better than clamping but the RGB-space can still cause temporal color bleeding. This artifact is especially noticeable when the color changes quickly. Outdated color information in the history buffer is not sufficiently rectified to reflect the change in the scene, and stale color information is carried over into the next frame. This artifact is clearly visible in the RGB color space at frame 100 in Table 4.4, where a red outline remains around the edges of the spot light after it abruptly turns yellow. It takes several frames before the light converges to a uniformly yellow color, reflected in the exponential decay of the SSIM error between frames 100-150 in Figure 4.7.



Figure 4.7: SSIM score over time for the RGB, YCoCg, and YCrCb color spaces in the color overlap scenario.

Both YCoCg and YCrCb reduce this type of color bleeding artifact. Since chrominance is decoupled from luminance, outdated color information bleeds less into the reconstructed signal. The reduction of chrominance artifacts comes at the cost of increased luminance artifacts, however. This is most visible at frame 200, where the SSIM score for both YCoCg and YCrCb drastically drops before slowly converging again. At this time, the color goes from green to blue, and stale luminance information in the green channel bleeds into the next frame. This is most clearly visible for YCoCg, which clearly exhibits an excess of luminance around the source of the spot light in Table 4.4. The artifact is also prevalent for YCrCb, but to a lesser extent.

#### 4.2.5 Anti-Flicker Heuristics

As seen previously, variance clipping introduces Temporal Instability in the form of flicker. An anti-flicker technique based on the work by Karis [16] was tested in a scene containing a cube with a noise density texture and two spot lights. The lights and cube both remain static for the duration of the test.

In the static noise cube scenario, anti-flicker produces substantially less visual flicker than fixed α. This is most noticeable around the source of the spot light and around the edges of the cube where it is contrasted against the black background. This is illustrated in Figure 4.8, where the SSIM map for the fixed α is averaged across the entire scenario, showing flickering hotspots. It is also reflected in the Temporal Instability metric which is decreased by 56.21% as presented in Table 4.5. While the measured RMSE is also slightly lower, the difference is imperceptible.



Figure 4.8: Image output of the static noise cube scenario (left), and the averaged SSIM map across the entire scenario for fixed α (right), showing flicker hotspots.

The algorithms were also tested in a dynamic scenario containing the same volumetric noise cube, containing a spot light exhibiting movement and abrupt color change. In this scene, the fixed α performs slightly better than anti-flicker as can be seen in Table 4.5. However, the difference is negligible and visually imperceptible, with anti-flicker increasing Temporal Instability by only 1.41% compared to fixed α. The scenario was also run with a lower fixed α of 0.02 to compare anti-flicker to inherently less noisy data. This resulted in both worse accuracy and more Temporal Instability, showing that anti-flicker is better than simply lowering the blend factor globally.
