We present Radiance Cascade Path Guiding (RCPG), a real-time product
path guiding method that improves upon previous state-of-the-art realtime path guiding methods both in quality and performance. We derive
bounds for radiance intervals such that the direction of incoming light is
not misrepresented by radiance cascades. This allows us to take advantage
of the hierarchical nature of radiance cascades in a sampling process that
takes the product of the incoming radiance and the material’s BSDF into
account. It is also possible to compute the probability distribution function
of RCPG sampling for any given direction, making it readily compatible
with other sampling methods, such as multiple importance sampling (MIS)
and unbiased ReSTIR.
As a product sampling scheme, RCPG can use a single sampling method for
producing scattering directions, as opposed to needing separate importance
sampling schemes for incident radiance and the BSDF. Compared to prior
state-of-the-art real-time path guiding methods utilizing multi-sample MIS,
this allows RCPG to generally trace twice as many paths per pixel within a
real-time frame rate. Because the data structure is defined in world space,
it can be used to generate samples starting from off-screen path vertices,
which is necessary for effective multi-bounce path guiding.

Introduction
Monte Carlo Path Tracing is perhaps the most well-known photorealistic rendering method that can be used to compute an unbiased
estimate for the solution of the rendering equation [Kajiya 1986].
However, with a limited number of samples, the estimates are very
noisy. While this noise can be reduced by applying denoising techniques to the resulting images, such approaches are inherently lossy
and cause bias.
Another approach is to improve the Monte Carlo sampling itself.
There are various methods for this, many of which can be used together. Path Guiding methods are based on constructing or learning
a data structure based on the lighting of the scene being rendered,
which is then used to inform sampling [Vorba et al. 2019]. Traditionally, path guiding methods have focused on offline rendering,
but real-time methods are starting to emerge as well [Alber et al.
2025; Derevyannykh 2022; Lu et al. 2024]. Additionally, sample reuse
schemes such as ReSTIR [Bitterli et al. 2020] are also gaining popularity. The noise level of ReSTIR-based renderers can be lowered

with improved initial samples, especially in the worst-case situations where the number of samples usable for spatial and temporal
reuse is low.
Real-time realistic lighting methods that are not based on path
tracing are also still being researched [Majercik et al. 2019; Sannikov
2023]. Occasionally, these methods may represent lighting information of the scene in such a format that they may provide useful
information for path guiding as well. In this article we show that one
such method, “Radiance Cascades” [Osborne and Sannikov 2024],
is particularly suited to be used as a path guiding data structure.
By its hierarchical nature, the method makes it straightforward to
sample directions efficiently, taking into account both the surface
BSDF and indirect illumination. It can thus be used for real-time
product path guiding.
We build upon radiance cascades in order to allow for path guiding, and present the following novel contributions:
• Application of the data structure to path guiding.
• Derivation of radiance interval bounds for preserving directional information in radiance cascades.
• A product path guiding method that improves upon state-ofthe-art in real-time path guiding in both speed and quality.

Related work
Radiance Cascades (RC) were introduced in a draft paper [Sannikov 2023] for use primarily in two-dimensional global illumination, although they also propose generalizations to the third dimension. The first academically published description of the method is in
the context of astrophysics [Osborne and Sannikov 2024]. The core
idea of radiance cascades is to compute a hierarchical representation
of radiance in such a way that it is later possible to approximately
reconstruct the radiance field in an efficient manner. The method
organizes probes in multiple grids of different resolutions called
“cascades”, where the probes encode information from a limited
range of distances. Higher-density cascades contain shorter-range
probes. The method has recently seen interesting developments
with the introduction of “Holographic Radiance Cascades” [Freeman et al. 2025], although this work is not yet directly applicable to
path guiding due to the limitation to 4-directional fluence and poor
scaling to three dimensions. Compared to NERFs [Mildenhall et al.
2020], radiance cascades represent the radiance field with lower
quality, but can usually be constructed in just a few milliseconds or
less and are more straightforward to use for efficient importance
sampling.
Linearly Transformed Cosines (LTC) enable efficient analytical approximations of BSDFs. They were originally introduced for
analytical shading with polygonal area lights [Heitz et al. 2016] and
have also been applied to BRDF importance sampling of polygonal
lights [Peters 2021]. LTC approximations work by applying a linear
transformation matrix to a cosine lobe such that it approximates
a more complex BSDF, such as Trowbridge-Reitz [Trowbridge and
Reitz 1975; Walter et al. 2007]. As the optimal linear transformation
heavily depends on material roughness and viewing angle, the coefficients of these matrices are typically computed into a lookup
table in a pre-processing step. We utilize LTCs for computing BSDF

weights during our iterative sampling process and optionally also
for sampling from within a single texel of a light probe.
Path guiding methods are a class of sampling techniques for
path tracing that adapt to the scene content in order to select better
rays [Vorba et al. 2019]. They collect or learn scene-dependent
lighting information either during sampling or ahead of time, in
order to guide future samples to directions which are more likely
to contribute lighting. Much of the prior work in path guiding has
focused on offline rendering [Jensen 1995; Lafortune and Willems
1995; Müller et al. 2017]. While many of these methods are based
on 5D tree structures [Lafortune and Willems 1995; Müller et al.
2017] or neural representations [Dong et al. 2023; Müller et al. 2019]
that are currently relatively slow for sampling in real-time, some
offline approaches present concepts that are also applicable to realtime rendering. In particular, Gaussian mixture models [Vorba et al.
2014], von Mises–Fisher (vMF) distributions [Ruppert et al. 2020]
and voxelization [Rath et al. 2023] have found use in the real-time
domain as well [Alber et al. 2025; Derevyannykh 2022; Lu et al. 2024].
Practical Product Path Guiding [Diolatzis et al. 2020] proposes
an LTC-based product sampling scheme in the offline context, which
we adapt to implement product sampling with radiance cascades in
our method.
Neural methods typically aim to learn a continuous distribution,
thus sidestepping problems with discrete approaches. In Neural
Parametric Mixtures [Dong et al. 2023], vMF mixtures are used for
approximating the full product distribution, and optimized online
with stochastic gradient descent. While their OptiX-based implementation is able to perform a single inference and importance
sampling iteration in 3 milliseconds in 1280x720 image resolution,
they note that there is work left to do on making the method practical for interactive rendering and that the training process can take
seconds per scene.
The full product distribution can also be represented with Normalized Anisotropic Spherical Gaussians [Huang et al. 2024],
where each shading point is mapped onto a 5-parameter spherical distribution that is learned with a small multilayer perceptron,
using stochastic ray samples. Samples are drawn either from the
learned distribution or from the BSDF, based on the learned selection
probability. Their Clight-based implementation takes approximately
85–372 ms per sample (with naive scaling based on the reported
1024 spp results) on an Nvidia RTX 2080 Ti GPU, depending on the
scene and resolution. The method can therefore be suitable for interactive use, but is an order of magnitude away from high-framerate
real-time use.
Higher-dimensional distributions can also be broken down, as
is done with Distribution Factorization [Figueiredo et al. 2025],
which separates the 2D distribution into marginal and conditional
1D PDFs. Each PDF is then modeled with a separate neural network,
which in this case learns the distribution at a set of uniformly spaced
discrete coordinates. While the separation also allows for caching
the individual components for more efficiency, the method is not
targeted towards real-time usage; rendering the presented examples
takes tens of seconds or even minutes. With naive scaling based
on their equal-time results, the faster of their methods, “DF-N”,
takes 55–103 ms to compute one sample at an unreported image
resolution.

Neural resampling is also used for production rendering. In
Disney’s Hyperion CPU renderer [Rath et al. 2025], a GPU-trained
network can directly estimate the incident RGB radiance, instead of
a PDF. They also use resampled importance sampling (RIS) [Talbot
et al. 2005] to generate product samples (radiance times BSDF) from
a set of candidate samples, determining optimized candidate counts
through aggregated statistics. As the queries are performed on CPU,
the runtime is in the order of tens of seconds per sample. A later
modification to this method replaces the radiance encoding scheme
with a hash-grid-sphere encoding [Weier et al. 2026], improving
quality at the cost of about 40% slower performance.
Learning the light transport can also be formulated as a reinforcement learning problem, where updating the incident radiance
corresponds to the Q-learning algorithm [Dahm and Keller 2017].
The light sources are treated as rewards, and the reinforcement
policy decides the scattering directions, for example based on representing each surface sample point with a discretized hemisphere.
Their implementation takes 51 ms for 1 spp at a 720p resolution,
with an unspecified GPU.
In one early real-time path guiding publication [Dittebrandt et al.
2020], temporally- and sample-adaptive world-space octrees are
used to construct Visibility Light Caches (VLC) and Compressed
Directional Quadtrees (CDQ); the former are used for product importance sampling next event estimation rays, and the latter for
sampling indirect lighting. The combined VLC+CDQ method takes
about 17 ms for a 1 spp 1080p frame on an RTX 2080 Ti, although
the implementation is primarily evaluated with Quake 2 video game
levels that contain relatively simplistic 3D scenes with few polygons
by modern standards.
A real-time path guiding method based on learning a 2D Gaussian distribution on a 2D projection of the hemisphere has been
proposed [Derevyannykh 2022]. They store one such distribution
per pixel, making it a screen-space method. Hence, we call this
approach Screen-Space Path Guiding (SSPG) in this paper. The
method is primarily only useful for a single bounce of indirect light;
off-screen intersections do not have path guiding data. Our method
instead stores its distributions in world-space grids and is discretized
to texels instead of an analytical distribution.
Markov Chain Mixture Models [Dittebrandt et al. 2023] uses
screen-space vMF distributions to approximate the product of incident radiance and BSDF. Each pixel hosts its own vMF lobe, and
the Markov chains transition by exchanging vMF lobe parameters
between pixels. Their implementation runs in 15–16 ms for an 8 spp
1080p frame, on an RTX 2080 Ti. This method is primarily positioned
for direct light sampling, with indirect light left as future work.
This future work is then realized in Markov Chain Path Guiding (MCPG) [Alber et al. 2025], where the vMF distributions are
instead stored in a world-space hash grid data structure, which allows for handling indirect light and single scattering as well. This
work is omitted from comparisons in this paper due to difficulty
of replication with arbitrary scenes; their implementation is specific to the Quake game engine, and they do not compare to other
state-of-the-art real-time path guiding methods either. In the relatively low-poly Quake 2 levels measured in their paper, the method
reaches 2–4 spp in 15–19 ms with two bounces for a 1080p frame,
using an AMD Radeon RX 7900 XTX GPU.

Voxel Path Guiding (VXPG) [Lu et al. 2024] takes a different
approach by storing per-voxel importance in a voxelized version
of the scene. During training, rays inject light in the voxels they
intersect. This data is then used to sample brighter voxels more
often. While VXPG does not rely on temporal information, temporal flickering can be present, as the light injection is inherently
stochastic and may entirely miss voxels at random. In contrast, our
method does utilize temporal information in the form we propose,
leading to initial instability but being more temporally stable after
a few dozen frames.
Techniques suitable for path guiding can also be extended to
other applications related to path sampling control. Progressive
Spatio-temporal Filtering [Pantaleoni 2020] combines path tracing and radiosity-style finite element solvers for versatile filtering
of samples produced by a path sampler, for example for control
variates and biased estimation. They employ a 5D spatio-directional
hash grid, with an additional spherical harmonics representation
to describe normal variations within a grid cell. Combined with
spherical Gaussian mixture models and k-d trees, their method is
able to handle product distributions without computing the product of individual radiance and BSDF approximations. While their
extended applications are performant, the path guiding application
itself suffers from a large 70 ms overhead (for a 1 spp 1080p frame
on an RTX 2080 Ti) and high memory consumption.
Reservoir-based Spatiotemporal Importance Resampling
(ReSTIR) is a method that allows for biased and unbiased reuse
of samples in a ray tracer in a manner that drastically increases
overall sample quality. It was originally proposed for direct illumination [Bitterli et al. 2020], but has been extended for global illumination [Ouyang et al. 2021] and generalized to path tracing [Lin
et al. 2022]. ReSTIR is orthogonal to path guiding and can be used in
addition to it; path guiding methods can be used to generate highquality candidate samples for ReSTIR to reuse, which reduces the
resulting noise and artifacting. The quality improvement attained
with ReSTIR-based resampling methods is usually so drastic that
it is difficult to justify omitting it in real-time use. Concurrently
with our work, ReSTIR [Lin et al. 2022] has also been generalized
to handle path guiding: In ReSTIR PG [Zeng et al. 2025], the full
product distribution for path guiding is directly estimated from
ReSTIR samples, via a four-lobe vMF mixture that approximates a
marginal 2D distribution within each spatial hash grid cell. This
method produces high-quality 1 spp 1080p frames in 18–33 ms on
an RTX 6000 Ada GPU.

Construction of radiance cascades
We build and update the radiance cascades data structure in realtime, although the ray tracing involved with this is temporally
amortized across multiple frames. In Section 3.1, we outline radiance cascades, a hierarchical set of grids containing light probes
representing radiance from limited distance intervals. Next, in Section 3.2, we derive bounds related to those radiance intervals such
that radiance cascades can be used in path guiding. Then, we present
the rationale for selecting a spherical mapping for use in the light
probes in Section 3.3.

3.1 Cascades
In the context of radiance cascades, a cascade refers to a grid, where
each grid cell contains a probe. The grid is scaled to cover the entire
scene, has a power-of-two resolution and has equal extent along
each axis (is a cube). Each cascade covers the same volume but with
a different resolution. Each probe stores an image of the surrounding radiance originating from a limited distance range, [𝑡𝑚𝑖𝑛 , 𝑡𝑚𝑎𝑥 ].
This range is called the radiance interval and depends on the cascade.
Figure 2 visualizes a radiance interval. Each texel of a probe contains two values: Radiance and transparency. Transparency is the
proportion of light that can pass through the interval; if an opaque
object covers visibility through the interval, it is zero.
“Cascade 0” is the most spatially dense grid, where each probe has
the lowest angular resolution and shortest interval: [0, 𝑡 0 ]. Each step
to a higher cascade halves the spatial resolution (i.e., probe density),
doubles the angular resolution (i.e., probe’s image resolution), and
extends the radiance interval to [𝑡𝑛−1, 𝑡𝑛 ], where 𝑡𝑛 > 𝑡𝑛−1 and
𝑡𝑛+1 −𝑡𝑛 > 𝑡𝑛 −𝑡𝑛−1 , i.e., each interval covers a longer range further
out. Together, information from all cascades can be combined to
reconstruct radiance with high spatial resolution, at the expense of
low angular resolution with short-distance rays. In Section 3.2, we
derive a formula for 𝑡𝑛 that faithfully retains directional information,
which is crucial for path guiding.

Our approach diverges from prior work on radiance cascades in
a few key ways. For performance reasons, we always use nearestneighbor interpolation. This way, each probe of cascade 𝑛 has a
single corresponding parent probe in cascade 𝑛 + 1, and radiance
intervals are merged by looking up further intervals from the parent
probes in successive cascades.
To allow for this nearest-neighbor interpolation and to increase
reliability for path guiding, we assign the value of each texel by numerically integrating over its solid angle and the grid cell’s volume.
In practice, this means that the ray origins are jittered within the
probe’s grid cell and the ray directions are jittered within the texel’s
solid angle, and an exponential moving average of the results is
taken with ratio 𝛼 = 1/30. The approach from the original radiance
cascades paper [Sannikov 2023], tracing one deterministic ray per
texel, places a limit on the minimum size of representable scene
detail and can miss important lighting details that are smaller than
that minimum.
In comparison with SD-trees [Müller et al. 2017], radiance cascades also store an approximation of the surrounding 5D light field.
However, there are some key differences. Radiance cascades do not
include any adaptive splitting; the structure itself does not adapt
when updated, only the values contained are changed. In real-time
rendering on a GPU, this predictable and uniform structure is somewhat of a benefit, as “traversing” the structure for updating or sampling does not involve divergence in control flow.
SD-trees and other tree-like path guiding data structures usually
store an independent representation for the directional portion of
the data (quadtree [Müller et al. 2017] or vMF mixture [Ruppert
et al. 2020]) separately for each leaf node of the spatial tree, and
thereby decouple the spatial and directional domains. The hierarchical structure of radiance cascades intertwines the two domains by
storing short-distance lighting with high spatial resolution and low
directional resolution, and long-distance lighting with low spatial
resolution and high directional resolution. The spatial resolution
of each cascade is directly linked and inversely proportional to the
angular resolution on that cascade.

starting points inside the probe’s cell acts as a low-pass filter on the
probe’s image, as shown in Figure 3(c). The width of the passband
depends on the distance 𝑡 of a scene point 𝑝 to the probe. Effectively,
the contribution of 𝑝 is “smeared” across all the potential directions
it can be seen from inside the cell, and we denote this “parallax”
range with angle 𝜃 . This angle is equivalent to the angular diameter
of the “blurred” point in the probe’s image.

3.4 Culling probes by voxelization
3.3 Probe mapping
When extending radiance cascades to the third dimension, the number of probes in the volume can quickly become prohibitively large.
For example, using a resolution of 1283 for cascade 0 with 16 texels
for each probe, computing two rays (primary ray & shadow ray)
for each texel in 5 cascades would involve ∼ 130 million rays. The
performance cost of tracing this many rays is excessive.
Because probes are selected using nearest-neighbor sampling, if
a probe’s cell does not overlap with any geometry, it will be unused.
Therefore, we can skip updating those probes. To do this, we conservatively voxelize the scene in real time, resulting in a 3D lookup
texture that marks the cells occupied by any geometry. Rasterization
hardware is utilized to perform the voxelization efficiently [Crassin
et al. 2011; Dong et al. 2004].
We perform this voxelization at the resolution of cascade 0, i.e.,
the cascade with the highest spatial resolution. This can then be
used to produce lower-resolution voxelizations somewhat akin to
constructing mipmaps: A lower-resolution voxel is marked as occupied if any of the voxels in the corresponding 2x2x2 block of the
higher-resolution map is occupied. When a cell is not occupied, the
corresponding probe can be culled.
For each cell, we also track the total number of frames that it has
been occupied, so that we know if the probe has been visited in the
past and old data is available. This is useful in dynamic scenarios
where moving objects may return to a position they have previously
occupied, in which case we can retain some of the previous probe
data instead of restarting from scratch.
We stress that while we use a voxelization-based culling scheme
in this paper, it is not a core part of the algorithm and alternate
culling methods may be more suitable if implementing RCPG for a
production renderer.
In 3D, the probes in radiance cascades store spherical images. In order to discretize these into a square 2D bitmap, a spherical projection
mapping is needed. Initially, we explored equal-area mappings [Clarberg 2008; Mortari et al. 2011]. The benefit of equal-area projections
in this use case is that by definition, they allow for a uniform distribution of resolution per solid angle. Equal-area transforms also
have a constant Jacobian determinant across every point, which can
simplify PDF computation when sampling uniformly from a texel.
However, as this projection has to be done for each corner of
each texel that is considered during the iterative sampling process,
the octahedral projection [Meyer et al. 2010] is more suitable for
real-time use due to its very efficient implementation. Unlike most
equal-area mappings, computing the octahedral projection does not
involve transcendental functions. Figure 5 demonstrates how this
projection mapping is constructed.
As this projection does not preserve the equal-area property, it
slightly reduces the consistency of sampling, and introduces additional complexity by having a non-constant Jacobian determinant;
We trace one ray per probe texel. Ray origins and directions are
jittered with the 𝑅5 sequence [Roberts 2018] (3 dimensions for position within the probe’s cell, 2 for direction within the texel), and an
average color and visibility are computed via exponential moving
average. This allows the probe to represent all positions within its
cell, and each texel to represent all possible directions within its
solid angle. Unfortunately, the moving average also introduces some
temporal delay to updating the radiance cascades.
We suggest ignoring backfaces when casting rays for radiance
cascades. For cells near surfaces, a portion of the jittered positions of
the probes are likely inside geometry, in which case they generally
contribute no radiance. By ignoring backfaces, rays can often pass
through enclosing geometry. This way, even those samples can
contribute some useful radiance data.

3.6 Gathering
The final step in the building of the radiance cascades structure
is merging the radiance intervals of high-resolution probes back
into low-resolution probes, and computing the moving averages of
radiance and transparency for each probe texel.
Initially, if light is arriving from outside the range of a cascade, it
does not affect that cascade. For importance sampling, however, we
would like to know all of the light arriving from a direction, instead
of just light that is within a specific range. Hence, for each texel
in a probe, we gather the incoming light from the corresponding 4
texels in the next cascade. The coverage ratio of a texel is used to
alpha-blend the gathered light in the background of that texel. This
process is visualized in Figure 9. Because some texel values may
incorrectly be zero due to the value being computed stochastically,
we ensure that the sampling remains unbiased by adding a small

positive value to the luminance, 𝐿𝑎𝑣𝑔 · 𝛾. For cascade 0, 𝐿𝑎𝑣𝑔 is
the average of luminances over the entire probe, while for further
cascades the 2x2 neighborhood is used. 𝛾 is an arbitrary small value,
for which we use 𝛾 = 0.05 in this paper. Higher values cause higher
noise levels but fewer outlier samples.

4.3 Sampling from the chosen texel
Once the traversal stage has selected a texel from a probe, what
remains is the sampling of a ray from within that texel. We propose
two options for this.
The first approach is simple: Use a uniform distribution within
the texel’s solid angle. This method is very simple to implement,
but not necessarily optimal. A drawback is that it can pick samples
from below the horizon, and large texels can be poorly sampled
for narrow BSDF lobes. While it is not always ideal, we use this
method in the measurements of this paper due to its slightly better
performance. Note that even with this approach, LTCs are still used
to determine weighting for texel selection, as described in Section 4.2.
The uniform distribution is only utilized to sample a direction within
a texel.
The second approach is to treat the chosen texel as a polygonal
light, and use an LTC-based BSDF importance sampling scheme
with it [Peters 2021]. This method improves sampling quality when
the texels are large and lobes are narrow (e.g., grazing angles reflecting close-by objects), and never produces below-horizon samples.
We can also reuse the LTC fit from Section 4.1 here, but accurate
horizon clipping is needed instead of the approximate method used
in Section 4.2. This method can cause a performance impact by
introducing many more arithmetic operations. Whether this impact
is substantial or not depends on if the shader is bandwidth-limited;
in simple scenes with a single indirect light bounce, the impact can
be high, whereas complex scenes with multi-bounce lighting are
less likely to be severely affected.

causes visible hard edges in the noise levels between areas, but is
an 8x improvement for the bandwidth. These edges can be hidden
by denoising algorithms or ReSTIR. Together, these measures take
the theoretical memory bandwidth to roughly 15.4 gigabytes per
second, which is much more palatable.

5.3 Integration in a path tracer
It is possible to compute the PDFs of RCPG sampling for arbitrary
directions at arbitrary surfaces, and it is thus trivially compatible
with multiple importance sampling (MIS) schemes. The PDFs are
computed very similarly to sampling (Sections 4.2 and 4.3), by evaluating the probability of choosing the texel that covers the given
direction at each iteration.
While path guiding samples are usually combined with BSDF
sampling via MIS (one such approach is shown in Figure 10(a)),
we instead propose to fully rely on RCPG-guided samples in realtime use (as in Figure 10(b)). Due to RCPG implementing product
sampling, samples generated with it already take the BSDF into
account.
Single-sample MIS with BSDF sampling in particular appears to
usually not be worthwhile with RCPG. When using it, we generally
encounter increased noise levels by most metrics at equal sample
counts. Despite the ray count being equal, single-sample MIS also results in 40–80% higher frame times due to higher branch divergence
during sampling. Still, single-sample MIS can reduce variance and
can be a reasonable choice when using RCPG for unbiased offline
rendering.
We suggest using RCPG in a multiple importance sampling setup
with a next event estimation or light sampling scheme such as “light

may sample small area light sources poorly, and does not help with
sampling infinitesimal lights. Figure 8 shows how RCPG behaves

Experimental setup
We compare our method, Radiance Cascade Path Guiding (RCPG),
to unguided unidirectional path tracing (PT) including next event
estimation, Screen-Space Path Guiding (SSPG) [Derevyannykh 2022]
and Voxel Path Guiding (VXPG) [Lu et al. 2024]. All measurements
are run at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 on an Nvidia RTX 3090 GPU,
and 1000 warm-up frames are given for the path guiding methods
to train their path guiding data structures unless otherwise noted
for a specific measurement. The high number of warm-up frames
is chosen specifically to present the ideal image quality for SSPG,
which can take a very long time to stabilize in certain scenes. RCPG,
VXPG or ReSTIR do not need such long warm-up periods for the
image quality to stabilize, see Figure 11.
Performance measurements are taken from GPU timings by averaging over 250 frames. Reference images are rendered using unidirectional path tracing, with 262144 samples per pixel. Quality
measurements are done with four different metrics: MAE, PSNR,
SSIM [Wang et al. 2004] and FLIP [Andersson et al. 2020].
The MAE metric is computed from the images in linear color
space (i.e., before applying tone mapping or gamma correction),
while the other metrics operate on tone mapped [Reinhard et al.
2002] and gamma-corrected LDR images. MAE is specifically used
over MSE, as it is much less sensitive to outliers. All of the compared
methods, including the reference path tracer, are able to produce rare
but extreme outlier samples (“fireflies”), making the MSE of different
frames at real-time sample counts vary by orders of magnitude.
Some prior path guiding literature has made the observation that
path guiding methods may actually increase variance, even if quality
is improved by other metrics [Diolatzis et al. 2020]. Nevertheless,
as MSE is directly related to the estimator’s variance, we use this
metric specifically when showing the convergence behavior of the
compared methods.
Three different configurations are used:
• One bounce of indirect light (Figure 12).
• Two bounces of indirect light (Figure 13).
• Sampling in conjunction with ReSTIR GI [Ouyang et al. 2021],
with 1 canonical sample per pixel (Figure 14)
SSPG is omitted from the two-bounce case due to the method
only properly handling one bounce, and it is also omitted from
the ReSTIR comparison as its paper [Derevyannykh 2022] did not
propose this kind of usage. In non-ReSTIR cases, the sample counts
are chosen such that the frame rate is as close as possible to 30 Hz,
or 33.33 ms.

In the RCPG configuration used in the measurements, there are

VXPG is used with the same MIS strategy as in its publication,

introduces means that the sample count is not doubled in practice.
We found such a setup to consistently cause higher noise levels

paper. While it may be possible to develop a divergence-avoiding
single-sample MIS scheme for VXPG, as has been done for some
other path guiding methods [Dittebrandt et al. 2020], that would be
a non-trivial extension of the VXPG algorithm and is therefore not
done here.

The “Ajar” scene measurements are listed in Table 2. This scene is
effectively ideal for VXPG: The rectangular slit of light very cleanly
matches the voxel data structure, and visibility is very simple in
most of the scene. It’s possible to see some edges in the noise level
of RCPG sampling on the door in Figures 12 and 13. In non-ReSTIR
tests, RCPG is second in this scene, with a clear margin, and the
aforementioned noise edges are no longer visible in Figure 14. ReSTIR results are addressed collectively later.
Table 3 contains measurements in the “Breakfast” scene. Here,
the best quality in the one- and two-bounce cases is achieved using
RCPG. It is also apparent that RCPG is able to sample the caustic
reflection much better than the other methods, as seen in Figure 12.
In the two-bounce case, the unguided path tracer manages twice
the number of samples compared to RCPG. This underlines an
interesting performance issue in multi-bounce path tracing; after
one bounce, the intersection points are already highly divergent
between nearby pixels, causing divergent memory accesses to the
path guiding data structures. This significantly slows down both
VXPG and RCPG, both of which include significant amounts of
memory traffic during sampling.
In the “Classroom” (Table 4), “Rungholt” (Table 5), and “Staircase” (Table 6) scenes, RCPG dominates in all measurements. RCPG
manages to trace roughly 2–3 times as many samples compared
to SSPG and VXPG. The sample count is not directly comparable,
as the latter two methods utilize multi-sample MIS for quality and
unbiasedness, while RCPG only samples a single bounce ray per
intersection. There are also differences in the performance of the
sampling procedures. These scenes are somewhat difficult for VXPG;
they contain lots of important details that are smaller than a voxel,
and exhibit non-trivial visibility.
The many metallic reflective surfaces and multiple light sources
in the “Kitchen” scene (Table 7) pose a problem for the grid-like
structure of VXPG and RCPG. While RCPG can sample reflective
surfaces as well, the angular resolution of the radiance cascade
data is generally very limited, harming its usefulness with highly
directional lighting. VXPG stores irradiance for its voxels, limiting
its ability to effectively sample light reflected from shiny surfaces.
Additionally, it is not very effective with the mostly occluded light
sources present in this scene. Especially the pink light, which is
located inside the lampshade, causes a lot of temporal instability
with VXPG. In the end, the regular unguided path tracer is quite
strong in this scene, defeating RCPG by the PSNR and SSIM metrics.
Quality over the first 64 initial frames (without warm-up frames)
is separately measured in the “Staircase” scene in Figure 11. In this
figure, it becomes very apparent that the Gaussian lobes of SSPG
can be slow to converge. In this scene in particular, early frames
seem to cause the distribution to overfit to a non-ideal direction, and
recovering from this takes especially long. PT and VXPG have flat
curves, as they do not reuse temporal information. The construction
and updating of the radiance cascades is temporally amortized,
causing a slower stabilization for RCPG. This construction occurs
in world-space, and does not need to be restarted if the camera is
moved. ReSTIR by its nature relies on temporal reuse, so all ReSTIR
curves indicate that the quality improves quickly during the initial
dozen or so frames.
In the ReSTIR measurements, RCPG ends up with the best image
quality in all scenes by most metrics, although VXPG wins in “Ajar”,
“Breakfast”, and “Rungholt” by the MAE metric. Visually, VXPG
seems to suffer from temporal flickering in some scenes, which is
visible as instability in Figure 11 as well as in a similar graph in
the original paper [Lu et al. 2024]. This is likely caused by the light
injection step of VXPG, which includes stochastic steps that can
occasionally miss some voxels. The fluctuating probability distribution function caused by this could introduce instability in unbiased
ReSTIR’s temporal reuse, and thus raise the overall noise level with
ReSTIR.
RCPG also suffers from similar instability issues, particularly during its initial frames when the radiance cascades have not yet stabilized and when used with high 𝛼 values for the exponential moving
average. We assume this to be the reason causing the RCPG+ReSTIR
curve to stabilize later than PT+ReSTIR. The level of temporal instability appears to be highly scene-dependent in both VXPG and
RCPG, which is the likely reason for why they trade wins in the
MAE metric with ReSTIR.

SSPG is also often highly effective. However, its efficacy is tied to
screen-space; if the camera moves, SSPG can take some time to retrain its Gaussian lobes. Guiding information is also only available
for on-screen surfaces, making SSPG unsuitable for multi-bounce
rendering. This heavily limits its usefulness in typical real-time
rendering tasks, where the camera is nearly always animated or
interactive. It is not an issue for either RCPG or VXPG, whose data
structures operate in world space.
Highly local and directional lighting environments, such as those
induced by caustics, often reveal the limited spatial and angular
resolution of RCPG. In such circumstances, the grid-like structure
can be visible as edges between more and less noisy areas. An
example of this is shown in Figure 27.
The same figure also demonstrates another limitation with lowroughness materials: The ring itself appears much noisier due to
the LTC weighting being an increasingly poor approximation for
the actual BSDF towards very low roughnesses. For such materials,
one may entirely forgo RCPG and just use regular BSDF importance
sampling, although the additional divergence this can cause is likely
to somewhat reduce performance.

10 Future work
In many real-time scenarios, one may forgo path guiding data structures and instead focus on maximizing the number of samples per
pixel, especially as the core ray-casting operation is becoming increasingly cheap with each round of improvements to hardware
acceleration. Many interesting approaches to real-time path guiding
could become viable if ray tracing ceases to be the largest individual
performance bottleneck; e.g., VXPG could be improved by drawing
multiple samples and selecting one via RIS [Talbot et al. 2005], using
additional visibility rays as weights.
For world-space path guiding, it would be interesting to find
an effective representation with a much lower bandwidth cost, as
well as investigating methods to increase coherence, such as Shader
Execution Reordering [NVIDIA Corporation 2022].
While the method currently culls probes using a real-time voxelized version of the scene, one potential future improvement to
this would be available if hardware ray tracing APIs gained the
ability to perform distance queries on their acceleration structures.
That way, the probe culling could be implemented without a separate voxelization step. Alternatively, memory consumption with
the voxelized representation could be reduced by utilizing sparse
voxel octrees [Crassin et al. 2009].
Similarly, allocating memory for all probes (even the culled ones)
is wasteful, and may be an obstacle for applying this method for large
dynamic worlds such as in many types of video games. As shown in
Table 1, only ∼ 10% of the allocated memory is actually used during
probe updates or sampling. A more efficient data structure could
allow scaling the method to larger scenes, such as a sparse voxel
octree [Crassin et al. 2009] or spatial hash grids.
During the probe update step, it may be possible to share the
results of traced rays across probes of different cascades. This could
yield notable performance improvements by reducing the number
of rays traced, but is non-trivial to organize in a way that is efficient
in real-time rendering.

While the proposed method stores radiance information as luminance, one could also store full RGB colors. This could be made practical by storing two copies of the radiance cascades data structure;
one with single-precision floating point values per color channel,
and another with RGBE-encoded values. The former is necessary
to retain sufficient precision for the exponential moving average,
while the latter could be used during the sampling procedure. The
main drawback of such an approach is likely to be increased arithmetic pressure during sampling due to the decoding of the RGBE
format. This could still be an effective strategy in scenes with highly
saturated material colors.
Instead of stochastically accumulating rays in the radiance cascades, each texel could instead be computed using cone or beam
tracing. This could allow computing approximations of texel values
with a single sample and improve path guiding in dynamic scenes
by removing the need for temporal reuse via exponential moving
average. However, there is currently no hardware acceleration for
these methods. Still, for example Sparse Voxel Octrees could possibly
be used for this purpose [Crassin et al. 2011].
11 Conclusion
We presented a path guiding algorithm, RCPG, which leverages
the unique properties of radiance cascades to implement a product path guiding scheme that is suitable for real-time rendering on
contemporary hardware. Our method is both significantly faster
and higher quality in equal time than state-of-the-art methods. We
also developed the theory of radiance cascades further by deriving radiance interval bounds that preserve directional information.
This development may enable other use cases for radiance cascades
outside of just path guiding.
