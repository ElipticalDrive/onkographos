Chapter 5 — Alpha Compositing & Forward Rendering
Session goal: take many 2D screen-space Gaussians — each an ellipse with a color and an
opacity — and blend them into a full image with correct occlusion, so that near objects
properly cover far ones. By the end you'll have a from-scratch NumPy renderer that sorts splats
by depth and composites them front-to-back with a running transmittance, and you'll understand
exactly why the real 3DGS rasterizer does it per-tile on the GPU.
📐 Prerequisites: Chapter 4 (each Gaussian is now a 2D ellipse with center $\mathbf{x}_i$,
inverse covariance $\Sigma'^{-1}_i$, color $\mathbf{c}_i$, opacity $\alpha_i$, and a depth $Z_c$).
🎯 The one sentence to remember: a rendered pixel is a stack of semi-transparent colored glass
sheets, blended from front to back until no light gets through.
5.1 A cathedral window made of glass sheets
Stand inside a cathedral and look at a stained-glass window. Now imagine the window isn't one
pane but hundreds of small colored glass chips, each partly see-through, layered one behind
another. The chip nearest your eye tints the light first. Whatever light survives passes to the
next chip, which tints what's left, and so on. A deep-red chip in front will drown out a pale-blue
chip behind it — but a faint, barely-there chip in front lets most of the color behind shine
through.
That is exactly what rendering a pile of Gaussians is. Each splat is a glass chip: it has a
color (the tint) and an opacity $\alpha$ (how much it blocks). To find the final color of
one pixel, we look through the whole stack of chips that cover that pixel, ordered by distance,
and blend.
Two facts make this more than a metaphor. First, "how much this splat covers this particular
pixel" is not a single number per splat — it falls off with distance from the splat's center,
following its 2D Gaussian shape. Second, the order matters: blending front-to-back and
back-to-front give the same answer only if you do the algebra right, and one of the two orders
lets us stop early once the glass has gone fully opaque. 3DGS exploits that.
🌍 Real-world anchor. This is the same math Photoshop uses to stack layers, the same math
that composites a CGI dragon over a live-action plate, and the same math NeRF uses along a ray.
Alpha compositing is one of the most reused ideas in all of graphics. We're just applying it to
Gaussians.
5.2 From an ellipse to a per-pixel alpha
In Chapter 4 each Gaussian became a 2D ellipse in screen space: a center $\mathbf{x}_i = (u_i, v_i)$
and a $2\times2$ covariance $\Sigma'_i$ describing its stretch and tilt. But an image is made of
pixels, and we need a single number per pixel: how strongly does splat $i$ affect the pixel at
$\mathbf{x} = (u, v)$?
The Gaussian answers that. Evaluate the (unnormalized) 2D Gaussian at the offset from the center:
$$
G_i(\mathbf{x}) ;=; \exp!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2},(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i)^\top, \Sigma'^{-1}_i, (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i)\right)
$$
At the very center, $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_i$, the exponent is 0 and $G_i = 1$. As you walk away
from the center, the quadratic form in the exponent grows and $G_i$ decays smoothly toward 0. The
shape of that decay — circular, elongated, tilted — is dictated entirely by $\Sigma'^{-1}_i$. This
is the "tilted balloon" from the notation guide, now seen head-on as a soft blob on the screen.
That $G_i(\mathbf{x}) \in (0, 1]$ is a coverage factor. Multiply it by the splat's intrinsic
opacity $\alpha_i$ and you get the effective per-pixel alpha:
$$
\boxed{;\alpha_i(\mathbf{x}) ;=; \alpha_i \cdot G_i(\mathbf{x}) ;=; \alpha_i ,\exp!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2},(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i)^\top \Sigma'^{-1}_i (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i)\right);}
$$
So one splat does not contribute the same alpha everywhere. It's strong (near $\alpha_i$) at its
center and fades to nothing at its edges. A pixel far outside the ellipse sees $\alpha_i(\mathbf{x}) \approx 0$
— the splat may as well not be there.
⚠️ Notation collision, resolved. We use $\alpha_i$ for the splat's stored opacity (one
scalar, learned during training) and $\alpha_i(\mathbf{x})$ for the effective alpha at a
specific pixel (opacity times the Gaussian falloff). In compositing formulas below, whenever we
write $\alpha_i$ inside a per-pixel sum, we mean the effective per-pixel value $\alpha_i(\mathbf{x})$.
The paper does the same; keep the distinction in your head.
5.3 Why we must sort by depth
Blending is order-dependent. Consider two chips over one pixel: a fully opaque red chip and a
fully opaque blue chip. If red is in front, the pixel is red. If blue is in front, the pixel is
blue. Same two chips, opposite answers — the only thing that changed is the order. There is no way
to recover the right image without knowing which splat is nearer the camera.
"Nearer the camera" is precisely the depth $Z_c$ we computed back in Chapter 1: the splat's
center transformed into camera space, its $Z_c$ coordinate. Small $Z_c$ = close = drawn first when
we go front-to-back.
So before any blending, we sort all splats by $Z_c$. In a full renderer we do this per tile
(§5.7); in the toy renderer below we do it globally. Either way, sorting is not optional — it is
the step that turns a bag of splats into a scene with occlusion.
🐛 Popping artifacts. 3DGS sorts by the splat center's depth, treating each splat as if it
lived at a single depth even though it's really a 3D blob with extent. When two splats overlap in
depth and the camera moves, their sort order can suddenly flip, and a splat "pops" from behind to
in front. Those flickers you sometimes see in 3DGS videos are sort-order flips. Per-splat single
depth is a speed/quality trade-off, not a law of nature.
5.4 The "over" operator and where it comes from
Now the core algebra. Take a foreground color $\mathbf{c}_f$ with alpha $\alpha_f$ placed over a
background color $\mathbf{c}_b$. What color results?
Think of $\alpha_f$ as "the fraction of this pixel's light that the foreground emits/blocks." The
foreground contributes $\alpha_f \mathbf{c}_f$. The remaining fraction $(1 - \alpha_f)$ of the
light comes from behind, showing the background $\mathbf{c}_b$. Add them:
$$
\mathbf{c}_{\text{out}} ;=; \alpha_f \mathbf{c}_f ;+; (1 - \alpha_f),\mathbf{c}_b
$$
This is Porter & Duff's over operator, the atom of all compositing. It reads literally as
"foreground over background." Everything else in this chapter is the over operator applied
repeatedly down a sorted stack.
The key quantity hiding in $(1 - \alpha_f)$ is transmittance: the fraction of light that makes
it through the foreground to reach whatever is behind. One sheet transmits $(1 - \alpha_f)$. Two
sheets in a row transmit $(1 - \alpha_1)(1 - \alpha_2)$. The transmittances multiply, because
each sheet independently lets a fraction through. That product is the engine of the next section.
5.5 Front-to-back compositing with running transmittance
Line up the sorted splats $i = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ from nearest to farthest. Define the accumulated
transmittance $T_i$ = the fraction of light that survives all the splats in front of $i$:
$$
T_1 = 1, \qquad T_i ;=; \prod_{j < i},(1 - \alpha_j)
$$
$T_1 = 1$ because nothing is in front of the first splat — all the light is still available. Each
subsequent splat sees less light, because the ones ahead of it have absorbed some. The final pixel
color is every splat's color, weighted by both its own alpha and the light still reaching it:
$$
\boxed{;\mathbf{C} ;=; \sum_{i}, \mathbf{c}_i,\alpha_i, T_i, \qquad T_i = \prod_{j&lt;i}(1 - \alpha_j);}
$$
In practice we never build that product from scratch each time. We sweep once, front to back,
carrying a running $T$ that starts at 1 and gets multiplied down:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&T \leftarrow 1, \quad \mathbf{C} \leftarrow \mathbf{0} \\
&\textbf{for } i = 1, 2, 3, \dots \textbf{ (near} \to \textbf{far):} \\
&\qquad \mathbf{C} ;\mathrel{+}=; T \cdot \alpha_i \cdot \mathbf{c}_i \\
&\qquad T ;\mathrel{*}=; (1 - \alpha_i) \\
&\qquad \textbf{if } T < \epsilon:\ \textbf{break} \quad \text{(fully opaque — nothing behind matters)}
\end{aligned}
$$
Two things to notice. First, this is the boxed formula computed incrementally — after processing
splat $i$, the running $T$ has become exactly $T_{i+1}$, ready for the next splat. Second, the
early termination: once $T$ has dropped near 0 (say below $10^{-4}$), the glass has gone
effectively opaque and no splat behind it can change the pixel. We break. On a real scene with
dense splats this stops most pixels after only a handful of contributions — a huge speedup that is
only possible in the front-to-back order.
Here is transmittance decaying down a stack, drawn out:
The bar shrinks fast. Splat 4 contributes to only $0.04$ of the pixel — its color is nearly
invisible under the stack in front of it. That's occlusion, emerging naturally from the running
product.
🌍 Foggy layers. Picture driving into successive banks of tinted fog. The first bank you hit
colors most of your view; by the third or fourth bank so little light gets through that its tint
barely registers. $T$ is "how clear the air still is." When $T$ hits zero, the road ahead is
invisible no matter what's out there.

$$T \mathrel{*}= (1 - 0.6) = 0.5 \cdot 0.4 = 0.20$$
Running: $\mathbf{C} = (0.5,\ 0.30,\ 0)$, $T = 0.20$.
Splat 3 (blue, $\alpha = 0.8$).
$$\mathbf{C} \mathrel{+}= 0.20 \cdot 0.8 \cdot (0,0,1) = (0,\ 0,\ 0.16)$$
$$T \mathrel{*}= (1 - 0.8) = 0.20 \cdot 0.2 = 0.04$$
Final: $\mathbf{C} = (0.5,\ 0.30,\ 0.16)$, $T = 0.04$.
The pixel is a muddy red-leaning color. Red dominates because it was in front with the full
$T = 1$; blue barely registers ($0.16$) because only $20%$ of the light reached it. The leftover
$T = 0.04$ is how much background would still show through — if we composite onto a background
color $\mathbf{c}{\text{bg}}$, we add $T \cdot \mathbf{c}{\text{bg}}$ at the end.
Sanity check — flip the order. If blue were in front instead ($\alpha=0.8$ first), it would
grab $0.8$ of the pixel immediately and red/green would fight over the remaining $0.2$. The result
would be blue-dominant — a completely different color. Order matters; §5.3 was not kidding.
5.7 Tile-based rasterization (why 3DGS is fast)
The toy renderer in §5.8 sorts all splats once and, for every pixel, walks the whole sorted list.
That's correct but wasteful: a splat on the left of the image is tested against pixels on the right
that it never touches, and a global per-pixel sort would be brutally slow on millions of splats.
3DGS uses a smarter scheme built for GPUs — tile-based rasterization:
Split the image into 16×16-pixel tiles. A 1600×1000 image becomes a grid of tiles.
Assign each splat to the tiles its ellipse overlaps. A splat covering a $30\times30$ pixel
region touches only ~4 tiles; it's ignored everywhere else. (A splat straddling tiles is
duplicated into each — cheap.)
Sort once per tile. Within a tile, sort that tile's splats by depth $Z_c$. Crucially this is
one sort shared by all $16 \times 16 = 256$ pixels in the tile — not a sort per pixel.
🌍 How the real kernel pulls this off — one global radix sort. The CUDA implementation
doesn't run thousands of little per-tile sorts. Instead, each (splat, tile) pair gets a
64-bit key: the tile ID packed into the high bits, the splat's depth into the low bits.
One GPU radix sort over all pairs then does two jobs at once: keys sharing a tile ID
become contiguous (that's the binning), and within each tile ID they land in depth order
(that's the per-tile sort) — because with the tile in the high bits, the depth bits only ever
break ties inside a tile. One O(n) sort, both problems solved. That packed key is the
duplicateWithKeys / SortPairs step you'll meet in the rasterizer source
(Chapter 9).
Composite per pixel using the tile's sorted list. Every pixel in the tile runs the
front-to-back loop of §5.5 over the same ordered list, evaluating each splat's per-pixel alpha
$\alpha_i(\mathbf{x})$ and terminating early when its own $T$ drops.

Why is this so much faster than a global per-pixel sort?
One sort per tile, not per pixel. 256 pixels share a single sort — a 256× saving on sorting
work in that tile.
Locality. Each tile's splat list fits in fast on-chip shared memory; all 256 threads stream
the same list. GPUs love this.
Massive parallelism. Tiles are independent, so thousands of tiles composite simultaneously
across the GPU's cores.
Early termination compounds. In a dense scene most pixels go opaque after a few splats and
break, so the average pixel touches far fewer than the tile's full list.
🌍 Anchor. This tile design is the single biggest reason 3DGS renders in milliseconds where
NeRF takes seconds. The math (alpha compositing) is old; the tiled GPU scheduling is what made
it real-time. The reference CUDA rasterizer and libraries like
gsplat implement exactly this pipeline.
⚠️ Approximation alert. Per-tile sorting means a splat gets one depth order within a tile,
even if a huge splat spans a wide depth range. Combined with the per-center depth of §5.3, this
is why 3DGS occlusion is "mostly right" rather than exact. For photorealistic novel views it's
more than good enough.
5.8 A from-scratch NumPy renderer
Here is a complete, runnable renderer: ten hardcoded 2D Gaussians, each with a center, a $2\times2$
covariance $\Sigma'$, an RGB color, an opacity, and a depth. It sorts by depth and composites
front-to-back with an explicit running transmittance, producing correct occlusion (near covers
far). No graphics library — just NumPy.
```
import numpy as np

# ---- Scene: 10 hardcoded 2D Gaussians ----------------------------------
# Each splat: center (u,v), covariance Sigma' (2x2), color RGB, opacity, depth Zc.
H = W = 128

def splat(u, v, sx, sy, rot, color, opacity, depth):
    """Build one splat. sx,sy = ellipse radii (px); rot = tilt in radians."""
    c, s = np.cos(rot), np.sin(rot)
    Rm = np.array([[c, -s], [s, c]])
    Scov = np.diag([sx**2, sy**2])          # axis-aligned covariance
    Sigma = Rm @ Scov @ Rm.T                 # rotate it -> tilted ellipse
    return dict(center=np.array([u, v], float),
                inv=np.linalg.inv(Sigma),    # Sigma'^{-1}, precomputed
                color=np.array(color, float),
                opacity=float(opacity),
                depth=float(depth))

splats = [
    splat( 40,  40, 22, 12,  0.5, (1.0, 0.1, 0.1), 0.9,  2.0),  # red, near
    splat( 80,  55, 18, 18,  0.0, (0.1, 0.4, 1.0), 0.8,  4.0),  # blue
    splat( 60,  70, 30, 10, -0.6, (0.1, 1.0, 0.2), 0.7,  6.0),  # green
    splat( 95,  95, 15, 25,  0.3, (1.0, 0.8, 0.1), 0.85, 3.0),  # yellow
    splat( 30,  90, 20, 20,  0.0, (0.8, 0.2, 1.0), 0.6,  8.0),  # purple, far
    splat(105,  35, 14, 14,  0.0, (0.1, 1.0, 1.0), 0.75, 5.0),  # cyan
    splat( 55,  30, 25, 15,  1.0, (1.0, 0.5, 0.0), 0.65, 7.0),  # orange
    splat( 75, 100, 18, 12, -0.3, (1.0, 1.0, 1.0), 0.5,  9.0),  # white, far
    splat( 20,  55, 12, 22,  0.2, (0.4, 0.9, 0.4), 0.7,  3.5),  # pale green
    splat( 90,  75, 16, 16,  0.0, (0.9, 0.1, 0.6), 0.8,  4.5),  # magenta
]

# ---- Sort splats by depth: NEAR (small Zc) first -----------------------
splats.sort(key=lambda g: g["depth"])

# ---- Pixel grid --------------------------------------------------------
vv, uu = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W]                  # v = row, u = col
pix = np.stack([uu, vv], axis=-1).astype(float)   # (H, W, 2)

# ---- Front-to-back compositing with running transmittance --------------
background = np.array([0.05, 0.05, 0.08])    # dark navy
C = np.zeros((H, W, 3))                       # accumulated color
T = np.ones((H, W))                           # accumulated transmittance, starts at 1
EPS = 1e-4                                     # early-out threshold

for g in splats:                              # already near -> far
    d = pix - g["center"]                     # (H, W, 2) offset from center
    # quadratic form  d^T Sigma'^{-1} d   for every pixel
    q = np.einsum('...i,ij,...j->...', d, g["inv"], d)
    G = np.exp(-0.5 * q)                       # 2D Gaussian falloff in (0,1]
    alpha = g["opacity"] * G                   # effective per-pixel alpha
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0.0, 0.999)         # guard against alpha == 1

    w = alpha * T                              # weight = alpha * light still available
    C += w[..., None] * g["color"]             # emit this splat's color
    T *= (1.0 - alpha)                         # absorb: less light for splats behind
    # (a real renderer would break where T < EPS; here we vectorize over all pixels)

# ---- Composite the remaining transmittance onto the background ---------
C += T[..., None] * background

img = np.clip(C, 0, 1)

# ---- Show it -----------------------------------------------------------
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.figure(figsize=(5, 5))
plt.imshow(img)                                # v grows downward -> already correct
plt.title("10 Gaussians composited front-to-back (near covers far)")
plt.axis("off")
plt.show()
```
A few implementation notes tie back to the theory:
splats.sort(key=depth) is §5.3 — near ($\text{small } Z_c$) first, so the front-to-back
loop is correctly ordered.
G = np.exp(-0.5 * q) is the per-pixel Gaussian of §5.2; alpha = opacity * G is the
effective $\alpha_i(\mathbf{x})$.
w = alpha * T; C += w * color; T *= (1 - alpha) is the exact recurrence of §5.5, run over
the whole image at once via NumPy broadcasting.
The final C += T * background uses the leftover transmittance — the same $T = 0.04$ we saw in
the worked example — to blend in the backdrop.
🐛 np.clip(alpha, 0, 0.999). If any $\alpha$ hits exactly 1, then $(1-\alpha) = 0$ zeroes
$T$ forever after — fine for forward rendering, but it makes the gradient vanish in Chapter 6.
Clamping just under 1 keeps everything well-behaved. Real implementations do the same.
The real kernel also guards the low end: if a splat's effective per-pixel alpha comes out
below $1/255 \approx 0.004$, its contribution is skipped entirely — it couldn't change even
the last bit of an 8-bit pixel, so evaluating it is wasted work (and wasted gradient traffic in
the backward pass). Between min(0.999, …) above and the 1/255 skip below, a splat only
participates in a pixel when it can actually matter.
⚠️ We vectorized instead of break. The math wants an early-out when $T &lt; \epsilon$, but
that's a per-pixel decision and we're processing all pixels in lockstep with NumPy. On a GPU each
pixel-thread breaks independently — that's where the speedup lives. Our version does the same
arithmetic, just without skipping the finished pixels.
5.9 3DGS vs. NeRF: same math, different sampling
People often say "NeRF and 3DGS are totally different." At the rendering level, they are
surprisingly the same — both integrate color along the line of sight using transmittance. What
differs is what they sample.
NeRF represents the scene as a continuous field: a neural network (MLP) that, given a 3D point and
a view direction, returns a color and a density $\sigma$. To render a pixel, NeRF shoots a ray,
picks many sample points along it (often 64–256), and queries the MLP at each. It then composites
those samples with the identical transmittance formula:
$$
\mathbf{C} = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i,\alpha_i, T_i, \qquad \alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}, \quad T_i = \prod_{j&lt;i}(1-\alpha_j)
$$
Look at the structure — it is the boxed equation of §5.5. The transmittance $T_i$, the front-to-back
product, the weighting by $\alpha_i$: all shared. This is the classic volume rendering equation,
and both methods inherit it.
The difference is entirely in the source of the $(\mathbf{c}_i, \alpha_i)$ list:

So the takeaway: the compositing math is shared heritage; the speed gap is about sampling a
heavy continuous field versus blending a light discrete one. 3DGS trades the MLP's implicit,
flexible field for millions of explicit, cheap primitives you can sort and rasterize — and wins
orders of magnitude in render time.
Summary
Each 2D Gaussian becomes a per-pixel alpha $\alpha_i(\mathbf{x}) = \alpha_i,G_i(\mathbf{x})$:
its stored opacity times the Gaussian falloff $\exp(-\tfrac12 d^\top \Sigma'^{-1} d)$. Strong at
the center, fading to zero at the edges.
Compositing is order-dependent, so splats must be sorted by depth $Z_c$ (near→far) first.
The over operator $\mathbf{c}_{\text{out}} = \alpha_f \mathbf{c}_f + (1-\alpha_f)\mathbf{c}_b$
is the atom; transmittances multiply down a stack.
Front-to-back compositing carries a running transmittance $T$ (start 1, multiply by
$(1-\alpha_i)$ each step): $\mathbf{C} = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i T_i$. It allows early
termination when $T \approx 0$.
Tile-based rasterization (16×16 tiles, assign splats to tiles, sort once per tile, composite
per pixel) is why 3DGS is real-time on GPUs — a per-tile sort instead of a per-pixel global sort.
NeRF vs 3DGS share the volume-rendering/transmittance math; NeRF samples a continuous MLP
(slow), 3DGS composites discrete sorted primitives (fast).

Why must Gaussians be sorted by depth before compositing?
answer
Because alpha compositing (the over operator) is order-dependent: two opaque splats over one
pixel give the color of whichever is in front, so swapping their order changes the result.
Correct occlusion — near objects covering far ones — only emerges if we blend in true
near-to-far order, which is exactly the depth $Z_c$ ordering.

answer
The final pixel color $\mathbf{C} = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i T_i$: a weighted sum of every
overlapping splat's color, where each weight is that splat's *effective* per-pixel alpha
$\alpha_i$ times the transmittance $T_i = \prod_{j
What happens if two Gaussians have very different opacities at the same pixel?
answer
Whichever is in front sets the tone. A high-opacity front splat absorbs most of the light
($T$ drops sharply), so the low-opacity splat behind it contributes very little — it's occluded.
If the low-opacity splat is in front instead, it tints slightly but passes most light through, so
the high-opacity splat behind still shows strongly. Opacity and order together decide the mix;
see the §5.6 example where front-red (α=0.5) dominates over back-blue (α=0.8) because blue only
received $T=0.2$ of the light.
Why is per-tile rasterization used instead of per-pixel global sorting?
answer
Speed on GPUs. A global per-pixel depth sort of millions of splats is prohibitively slow.
Splitting the image into 16×16 tiles lets us sort each tile's splats *once* and share that single
sorted list across all 256 pixels in the tile. Tiles are independent (massive parallelism), each
tile's list fits in fast shared memory (locality), and per-pixel early-out compounds the savings.
This is the core reason 3DGS renders in real time.
What's the difference between this and standard NeRF volume rendering?
answer
The compositing math is the same volume-rendering equation ($\mathbf{C} = \sum \mathbf{c}_i
\alpha_i T_i$ with multiplicative transmittance). The difference is the source of the list: NeRF
*samples a continuous MLP field* at many points along each ray (hundreds of network evaluations
per pixel → slow), while 3DGS *composites a discrete, sorted set of explicit Gaussian primitives*
that overlap the pixel (a handful of splats with early-out → fast). Same integral, different (and
much cheaper) sampling.
Exercises
(Core) Run the renderer in §5.8. Confirm the near red/yellow splats visibly cover the far
purple/white ones. Then reverse the sort (reverse=True, far→near) without fixing the
compositing math and watch the occlusion invert — far splats now wrongly paint over near ones.
(Worked example in code) Reproduce §5.6: composite the three splats (red α=0.5, green α=0.6,
blue α=0.8) at a single pixel with a Python loop carrying $T$, and check you get
$\mathbf{C} \approx (0.5, 0.30, 0.16)$ with leftover $T = 0.04$.
(Early-out) Add a per-pixel break: track T as a scalar in a double loop over one pixel's
sorted splats and stop when T < 1e-4. Count how many splats you skip on the most-occluded
pixel — that's the speedup the vectorized version hides.
(Opacity sweep) Take two overlapping splats and sweep the front one's opacity from 0 to 1.
Plot how much the back splat's color survives in the final pixel. You should see it fall off as
$(1 - \alpha_{\text{front}})$ — the transmittance curve, felt directly.
(Tile sketch) Split the 128×128 image into 16×16 tiles. For each tile, compute which splats'
ellipses overlap it (a cheap bounding-box test on center ± a few std-devs) and print the count.
Notice most tiles touch only 2–4 splats — the property that makes tiling fast.
What's next
You can now render a full, correctly-occluded image from a pile of Gaussians — a complete
forward pass. But rendering is only half of 3DGS. The whole point is training: nudging every
splat's position, shape, color, and opacity until the render matches a photograph. That requires
running this compositing pipeline backwards — computing how the final pixel color changes when
you wiggle each $\alpha_i$, $\mathbf{c}_i$, or $\Sigma'_i$.
Chapter 6 — Differentiability & Loss makes this exact renderer
differentiable: we'll define the loss $\mathcal{L}$ between the render and the target image, then
push gradients back through the transmittance recurrence to every Gaussian parameter. The break
and the clip(alpha, 0, 0.999) you saw here will suddenly matter a great deal.
