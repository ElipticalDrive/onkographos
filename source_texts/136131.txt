GPU Pro 6 - Realistic Volumetric Explosions in Games
This article presents an extension of well known ray-marching techniques for volume rendering ﬁt for modern GPU’s, in an
attempt to modernise the emulation of explosions in games.
Realism
massively effects the users level of immersion within a game, and
previous methods for rendering explosions have always lagged behind that
of production quality. Traditionally,
explosions in games are rendered using mass amounts of particles, and
while this method can look good from a static perspective, the effect
starts to break down in dynamic scenes with free roaming cameras.
Particles are camera facing bill-boards, and by nature, always facing
the screen; there is no real concept of rotation or multiple view
angles, just the same texture projected onto the screen with no regard
for view direction. By switching to a volumetric system, explosions look
good from all view angles as they no longer depend on camera facing
billboards. Furthermore, a single volumetric explosion can have the same
visual quality as thousands of individual particles, thus, removing the
strain of updating, sorting and rendering them all - as is the case
with particle systems.
By harnessing the power of the GPU, and the
DirectX 11 tessellation pipeline, I will show you that single pass,
fully volumetric, production quality explosions are possible now in the
current generation of video games. We will be exploring volumetric
rendering techniques, such as, ray-marching and sphere tracing, as well
as utilising the tessellation pipeline to optimise these techniques.
A sample project including full source and media is available on -github-.

GPU Pro 6 - Rendering Vector Displacement Mapped Surface in a GPU Ray Tracer
Introduction
Ray tracing is an elegant solution to render high quality images. By combining Monte Carlo integration with ray tracing, we can solve the rendering equation. However, a disadvantage of using ray tracing is its high computational cost which makes render time long. To improve the performance, GPUs have been used. However, GPU ray tracers typically do not have as many features as CPU ray tracers. Vector displacement mapping is one of the features, which we do not see much in GPU ray tracers. When vector displacement mapping is evaluated on the fly (i.e., without creating a large number of polygons in the preprocess and storing them in the memory), it allows us to render a highly geometric detailed scene from a simple mesh. Since geometric detail is an important factor for realism, vector displacement mapping is an important technique in ray tracing. In this chapter, we describe a method to render vector displacement mapped surfaces in a GPU ray tracer.
Conclusion
In this chapter, we have presented a method to ray trace vector displacement mapped surfaces on the GPU. Our experiments show that direct ray tracing requires a small memory footprint, and ray tracing performance is competitive or faster than ray tracing with pre-tessellation. The advantage gets stronger as there are more VD patches in the scene.

GPU Pro 6 - Real-time Rendering of Physics-Based Clouds using Precomputed Scattering
Rendering realistic clouds has always been a desired feature for a variety of applications, from computer games to flight simulators. Clouds consist of innumerable tiny water droplets that scatter the light entering the cloud. Rendering clouds is challenging because photons are typically scattered multiple times before they leave the cloud. Despite the impressive performance of today's GPUs, accurately modeling multiple scattering effects is prohibitively expensive, even for off-line renderers. Thus, real-time methods rely on greatly simplified models.
We present a new physics-based method to efficiently render realistic animated clouds. The clouds are comprised of scaled and rotated copies of a single particle called reference particle. During the pre-processing, we precompute optical depth, single and multiple scattering integrals describing the light transport in the reference particle for all possible camera positions, and view directions and store the results in look-up tables. During the run-time, we load the data from the look-up tables to approximate the light transport in the cloud in order to avoid costly ray marching or slicing. We also present a new method for blending particles that, in contrast to traditional alpha-blending, accounts for volumetric intersection and produces more accurate visual results.

GPU Pro 6 - Tile-based Omnidirectional Shadows
Though
rendering efficiently a large amount of local light sources is actually solved
by techniques as Tiled or Clustered Shading, generating in real-time
appropriate shadows is still an ongoing topic. This article will demonstrate
how to efficiently generate soft shadows for a high number of omnidirectional
light sources while each light casts individual shadows.
In the
proposed technique the shadow maps of all light sources are rendered in a
single draw call and packed as tiles into a single 2D texture atlas with the
help of a hierarchical quad-tree while the resolution of each tile is adapted
to the screen area that the corresponding light influences. For this the
already known concepts of Programmable Draw Dispatch and Tetrahedron Shadow
Mapping are combined with a novel usage of the GPU's programmable clipping
unit. In comparison with a traditional cube map-based solution this system not
only significantly reduces the CPU and GPU times required for generating the
shadows, but also allows to store shadow maps for considerably more light
sources within the same amount of texture memory, while providing quality-wise
competitive results.
"Crytek
Sponza" scene rendered with 128 medium-sized moving omnidirectional light
sources that all cast individual soft shadows using the proposed technique.
The demo
with the full source code can be downloaded here from my website.

GPU Pro 6 - Hybrid Ray Tracing on a PowerVR GPU
Ray tracing and rasterization are often presented as a dichotomy: ray tracing is the gold standard for visual realism by allowing physically accurate light simulation, it produces extremely high quality images, while rasterization dominates real-time rendering because it can be accelerated by efficient, commonly available, GPUs and has mature standardized programming interfaces.
This article describes how these two techniques can be combined by taking advantage of the deferred shading architecture used by many modern game engine renderers. The G buffer that is output from the initial pass of the deferred shading algorithm is a description of all the visible surfaces in the scene. This G buffer can be used as input to the ray tracer, which then emits rays based on the world space position, normal and other information stored in the G Buffer.
In this way ray tracing becomes another tool for an existing raster-based game engine to use, just like texture filtering or programmable shading. The power of ray tracing can be used to implement sophisticated light transport effects like physically accurate hard and soft shadows, reflection, refraction, and transparency, while rasterization remains the primary rendering method.

GPU Pro 6 - Practical screen space soft shadows
Shadows are important to establish spatial coherency, relationship between objects, enhance composition, add contrast and indicate off-screen space that is there to be explored. As a gameplay element, they are used to project objects onto walls with the intent to create new images and signs that may tell a story. Shadows are often used to either lead the viewers' eye, or obscure non-important parts of the scene.
Yellow light casts soft shadows onto the walls
In computer graphics, light emitters are often represented as a single point with no definite volume. These kinds of mathematical lights cast only hard-edged shadows called umbra. However in the real world lights usually have volume, and therefore they cast soft-edged shadows that consist of umbra, penumbra and antumbra.
This article describes novel techniques that extend the the original Screen Space Soft Shadows algorithm (from GPU Pro 1) in order to make sure that the speed of rendering is optimal and we take into consideration overlapping (cast from multiple light sources) and translucent shadows. We introduce layers, an essential component to filtering overlapping shadows in screen space.

We present a fast, robust method for rendering real-time overlapping shadows with variable penumbra.
