Abstract
This paper presents a novel virtual reality (VR) system for real-time foveated rendering of large-scale 3D meshes composed of nearly 100 million triangles. As VR applications demand exceptionally high frame rates and stereo rendering
for immersive experiences, existing desktop-based rendering techniques face significant computational challenges when
adapted for VR. We incorporate foveal focus into the level-of-detail (LOD) selection process, prioritizing high geometric
detail in the foveal region while reducing detail in peripheral areas. The foveated LOD selection method is seamlessly
integrated with spatial factors, supported by a hierarchical patch-based mesh structure that enables precise rendering load
estimation and adaptive LOD adjustments. Our system offers constrained optimization (fixed total load budget) executed
in a parallel fashion on the GPU to ensure real-time performance. It is evaluated against state-of-the-art VR rendering
solutions. The results demonstrate that our VR rendering system significantly outperforms these existing solutions in
maintaining high visual fidelity while delivering the performance required for real-time VR rendering.
Keywords Foveated VR rendering · Level of detail · Massive model rendering · Hierarchical mesh structure

by the growing demand for realistic, immersive, and interactive virtual experiences, the rendering of complex 3D
meshes in VR headsets has gained attention from both the
research community and industry. Applications range from
digital heritage visualization and architectural design to scientific visualization and mathematical simulations. These
applications often involve 3D scenes containing tens or
even hundreds of millions of vertices and triangles, which
provide highly detailed representations of objects and environments. In VR, rendering complex 3D meshes in real-time

explored software solutions to enhance GPU programming

While these approaches have achieved success in desktop applications for rendering large meshes, rendering such
meshes through VR headsets introduces additional computational challenges. To ensure a seamless user experience
and avoid discomfort or nausea, VR applications demand
very high frame rates, around 90 frames per second, which
is significantly higher than the 30 fps typically considered
sufficient for desktop environments. Furthermore, VR systems require stereo rendering, which means computing the
scene twice–once for each eye–to produce the stereoscopic
effect. This stereo rendering requirement doubles the rendering workload and intensifies the computational demands.
Additionally, VR headsets feature a much wider field of
view (FOV), typically exceeding 100 degrees, compared
to 45-60 degree FOV in desktop displays. This broader
FOV requires rendering more of the scene at high detail,
further complicating the challenge of meeting performance
requirements.
Overview of Our System In this work, we aim to achieve
high frame rates for rendering large 3D meshes in VR headsets, specifically targeting a scene composed of nearly 100
million triangles. To achieve this, we define the maximum
number of geometric primitives that the VR system can handle based on its real-time rendering capabilities. The viewdependently selected vertices and triangles that form the
adaptive version of the original 3D scenes must not exceed
this limit.
To maintain rendering quality under high-performance
constraints, our work integrates a foveated computational
approach into the LOD selection process. This method
ensures that the foveal region of the VR display, where the
user’s gaze is focused, retains high geometric detail, while
peripheral areas are rendered with progressively lower
detail to reduce the computational load. Although foveated
techniques are widely used in shading and imaging stages
of VR rendering (e.g., Franke et al. 2021; Meng et al. 2020;
Patney et al. 2016), their application to real-time continuous
geometry processing of large 3D meshes remains largely
unexplored.
To support this LOD selection process, we employ a
hierarchical patch-based mesh structure, where patches are
nearly equal in size, allowing for accurate estimation of rendering load. Each patch contains continuous LOD adjustments and represents a topological region of the mesh, with
patches at higher levels of the hierarchy representing larger
regions with coarser details.

2 Related work
2.1 LOD selection for massive model rendering
In a complex 3D scene, the large amount of geometric
primitives like vertices and triangles requires expensive
computational costs and therefore makes real-time rendering challenging. This complexity leads to lower frame
rates, which can disrupt the smoothness of the visual experience. An important optimization strategy to speed up the
rendering performance of massive 3D polygonal meshes
is to reduce the number of triangles based on the viewer’s
perspective while preserving visual quality. This involves
determining which portions of the mesh should retain high
detail and which can be simplified, a process known as LOD
selection.

The adaptive display algorithm presented by Funkhouser
and Séquin (1993) is an early work that dynamically adjusts
the detail level of objects based on the viewer’s perspective,
prioritizing critical objects to ensure consistent frame rates.
It optimizes rendering for 3D scenes that consist of multiple
objects. Similar LOD selection strategies for rendering complex multi-object scenes have been incorporated into later
GPU-based rendering frameworks. For instance, Peng and Cao
(2012) introduced an object-level parallel method that ensures
each object is rendered at an appropriate level of detail based
on criteria such as distance, screen region, and the original
complexity of the object, while keeping the total number of
selected vertices and triangles within the GPU memory budget. Derzapf and Guthe (2012) developed a neighborhood
dependency-free progressive mesh data structure for parallel view-dependent refinement. Additionally, they used a per
operation compression method that supports parallel randomaccess decompression, ensuring efficient out-of-core memory
management. Dong and Peng (2023) presented a multi-GPU
rendering framework, where the detail levels of visible objects
are determined by a single adaptive LOD selection algorithm.
The selected vertices and triangles are then distributed across
multiple GPUs, maintaining cross-GPU load balancing. In a
multi-object scene, each object is a self-contained unit, often
composed of a few to tens of thousands of triangles. Since
these objects are independent of each other, they can be parallelized at the object level.
Another type of complex 3D scene involves large surface
models made up of numerous interconnected triangles, forming intricate mesh topology and detailed geometric features.
These scenes require spatial or topological data structures to
support LOD selection. De Floriani et al. (2005) reviewed several data structures for encoding different levels of geometry
details of models. Geometric primitives in a surface model can
be represented in a hierarchy, where each node in the hierarchy represents a region of the surface at a specific geometric
detail level. For example, the root node represents the entire
model at the coarsest level, and as the selection process moves
down the hierarchy, the nodes represent models with progressively higher details. Yoon et al. (2004) decomposed the 3D
scene into a cluster hierarchy, where each cluster contains progressive meshes. Cignoni et al. (2003) used a binary tree for
mesh partitioning, and they constructed multi-resolution and
per-node LOD. Gobbetti and Marton (2005) represented data
with a hierarchy mixed with voxel and triangle nodes. More
recently, Karis et al. (2021), a virtualized geometry system in
Unreal Engine, demonstrates a promising hierarchical representation for large models. Nanite employs a Directed Acyclic
Graph (DAG) as its hierarchical structure, where meshes are
decomposed into clusters of triangles that preserve surface
locality. To generate the DAG, 2N clusters are merged into
a group, with each cluster connected to all corresponding
parent clusters. However, LOD decisions are made at the group
level rather than at the individual cluster level. Li (2023) focused
on popping and cracking issues in Hierarchical LOD implementations for large surface model rendering. They employed
an octree-based hierarchy and vertex interpolation, ensuring
smoother transitions between levels.
In this work, we employ hierarchical data representations
based on a binary tree structure, which can be suitable for
complex scenes that consist of both multi-object and surface
meshes. Nodes of the hierarchy feature with the preservation
of surface locality, uniform depth levels for leaf nodes, and balanced node sizes.
2.2 Foveated rendering in VR
Foveated rendering is a key performance optimization technique in VR, designed to reduce graphical detail in peripheral
vision while maintaining high detail in the fovea region—the
center of the visual field where the human eye is most sensitive
to detail (Patney et al. 2016). This approach leverages the natural characteristics of human vision: as the eye moves away from
the center, visual acuity decreases significantly, making peripheral vision less detailed (Wang et al. 2023; Zhan et al. 2020). By
focusing resources on where visual quality matters most, foveated rendering balances performance and visual fidelity.
Mohanto et al. (2022) provided a comprehensive survey of
foveated rendering techniques that adapt image synthesis based
on the user’s gaze. The authors categorize foveated rendering
into four primary techniques: adaptive resolution, geometric
simplification, shading simplification and chromatic degradation, and spatio-temporal deterioration. Each of these methods
is designed to reduce computational loads by focusing detail in
the foveal region while lowering the quality in peripheral vision,
where human sensitivity is reduced. Jabbireddy et al. (2022)
offered a classification and comparison of foveated techniques,
discussed their efforts to mitigate aliasing artifacts, and identified open challenges for future research. Early work studies the
methods to reduce the resolution in peripheral vision. Watson
et al. (1997) evaluated the impact of peripheral LOD degradation on visual search performance in VR headsets. Their findings indicate that peripheral LOD degradation can significantly
reduce visual complexity and without substantially affecting
search performance in terms of accuracy or speed. This suggests that peripheral detail reduction is a promising method for
optimizing system performance in VR without compromising
user experience.
Guenter et al. (2012) introduced a computational method
for supporting foveation during the shading stage of the graphics pipeline. Their approach incorporates an antialiasing algorithm that generates a smoothly composited, non-uniform
resolution, reducing computational costs compared to fullresolution rendering. Meng et al. (2020) enhanced foveated
13
82 Page 4 of 19
rendering performance in VR by leveraging the human visual
system’s ocular dominance. Unlike traditional foveated rendering, their method adjusts the shading detail levels based on the
dominant and non-dominant eyes. The dominant eye receives
higher detail, while the non-dominant eye, which is less sensitive, receives lower detail. Their method achieved up to 1.47×
speedup in rendering performance. Ye et al. (2022) proposed
a rectangular mapping-based foveated rendering that supports
resolution scaling based on eccentricity and the scene complexity, demonstrating improved quality-cost tradeoffs compared
to conventional foveated rendering methods. Fan et al. (2024)
integrated scene awareness, visual acuity, and gaze motion
into foveated rendering. Their system uses a pixel-size control
map and temporal refinement to improve perceptual quality.
Recently, Tariq and Didyk (2024) explored a limitation of foveated rendering through user experiments, finding that it impairs
motion detection in peripheral vision, leading to inaccurate perception of motion cues like velocity. They proposed a real-time
technique to compensate for this by synthesizing controlled
spatio-temporal motion energy inspired by visual illusions.
Some work has been done to reduce geometric complexity
for foveated rendering. Ohshima et al. (1996) introduced a rendering method for VR systems that leverages gaze detection to
optimize interaction within virtual spaces. Their method adapts
LOD based on the visual acuity derived from the viewer’s gaze,
simplifying models in areas of lower visual importance. However, their method was tested only on a few isolated objects
without continuous LOD across an object’s surface, proving
the concept but limiting its application. Schütz et al. (2019)
introduced a real-time continuous LOD rendering technique
for point clouds that minimizes popping artifacts common in
traditional methods. By down-sampling the geometry points
based on camera position and orientation in a point-wise rather
than chunk-wise manner, the approach enables smoother transitions between LODs. Murphy et al. (2009) proposed a hybrid
technique that combines image-based and model-based rendering for gaze-contingent displays using ray tracing. This method
leverages GPU-based ray casting to achieve spatial peripheral
degradation, where sampling rates are guided by visual acuity. While the technique applies to geometric LOD, it does not
improve rendering performance. Surace et al. (2023) recently
conducted user experiments to study perceptual models and
applied these models to reduce the geometric complexity in
peripheral vision. Their work demonstrates the feasibility of
using foveated rendering for geometric LOD processing in
multi-object scenes while maintaining visual fidelity. However,
their models have not yet fully addressed the need for continuous LOD across the surface of large 3D meshes.
In this work, we focus on a LOD selection technique that
considers both spatial factors and foveal focus. This technique enables performance-constrained rendering in VR

while ensuring that quality is preserved in perceptually sensitive regions for foveated rendering.
3 Methods
In this section, we present a dual-factor LOD selection
method, inspired by foveated rendering techniques (Surace
et al. 2023; Meng et al. 2020; Ohshima et al. 1996) used in
VR environments. Our method leverages a hierarchical data
structure to optimize the rendering of complex 3D scenes
in VR, ensuring that computational resources are focused
where they are most perceptually significant.
3.1 Hierarchical mesh structure
We developed a hierarchical mesh data structure inspired
by concepts from HLODs (Erikson et al. 2001) and Nanite
(Karis et al. 2021). To construct this data structure as a perfect binary tree, we employed a hybrid approach combining
METIS (Karypis and Kumar 1997) and spatial partitioning
(Karras and Aila 2013). The construction process begins
by recursively bipartitioning the original mesh until each
resulting patch satisfies a predefined triangle budget. These
patches serve as the leaf nodes of the hierarchy. Then, the
hierarchy is built upward by iteratively merging each pair
of sibling nodes to form their parent node, continuing this
process until a single root node is obtained. During merging,
we ensure that each parent node contains approximately the
same number of triangles as its children by coarsening the
children nodes. To maintain boundary integrity across the
hierarchy, we preserve all boundary vertices throughout the
coarsening process without removing or collapsing them.
This approach ensures that the resulting hierarchy effectively organizes the mesh into manageable components.
Figure 2 illustrates the structure of this hierarchy, where
each node represents a distinct part of the original mesh at
varying levels of detail. Throughout the construction process, we maintained uniformity in the size of the nodes.
This uniformity allows for a straightforward estimation of
the number of potentially rendered triangles based on the
number of selected nodes, enabling dynamic adjustments
based on the visual importance of each node.
In addition, we incorporated the Progressive Meshes
technique (Hoppe 2023) during the construction phase. Specifically, we applied a Quadric Error Metrics (QEM) (Garland
and Heckbert 1997) based edge collapse algorithm to each
patch in order to generate a continuous simplification sequence.
This allows each node to support smooth, fine-grained simplification within its local geometry range, independently of
the global hierarchy. This technique allows each parent node
to represent a simplified version of its child nodes, typically

3.2 Dual-criteria LOD selection
This section presents a computational model for LOD selection. With a fixed number of nodes determined by the VR system’s real-time rendering capabilities, the computational model
selects a subset of nodes that maintain high geometric detail in
visually important areas of the model’s surface, while reducing
detail in less critical regions. To achieve the best possible visual
quality while ensuring real-time performance, we consider the
criteria including both spatial factors and foveal focus.
3.2.1 Criteria
In a 3D environment, shapes closer to the camera and those
projected onto a larger region of the screen are considered
more important to the viewer’s perception than shapes that
are farther away or appear smaller on the screen (Dong and
Peng 2019; Funkhouser and Séquin 1993; Reddy 1998).
These spatial factors enable LOD selection to estimate the
shapes’ contribution to the viewing perception. However,
this often results in peripheral regions receiving more detail
than needed by the human visual system, leading to a wasteful use of computational resources.
In VR systems where the rendering frame rate must be
higher and rendering capabilities are generally lower than
in desktop systems, such wasteful use of resources can critically impact overall performance. For performance-constrained VR applications, such as those with a fixed load
budget for real-time rendering, allocating excessive detail to
peripheral regions can lead to a loss of quality in the foveal
regions. Therefore, integrating spatial factors with considerations of foveal focus during LOD selection is essential
to ensure optimal visual quality and efficient performance.
As described in Sect. 3.1, the hierarchical mesh structure
balances the number of geometric primitives within nodes,
but their spatial properties, like bounding volume size, can vary
significantly. In this work, we use bounding spheres to estimate
the spatial size of nodes on the screen. These bounding sphere
sizes can differ across hierarchies of various objects in multiobject scenes due to differences in geometry complexity, spatial size, and hierarchy depth. Like shown in Fig. 4, even within
a single hierarchy, nodes at the same level may have different

The foveal region is a small, central portion of the retina. It is represented by small values of eccentricity, which
is defined as the angular distance away from the ventral
gaze direction. In the human visual system, as eccentricity increases, visual acuity falls off, making the transition
from the foveal region to the peripheral regions. When measuring the eccentricity, we look for equalizing the effect of
changes across different bounding volume sizes, so that they
can be evaluated under a common threshold. To do this, for
each node, we identify the point on its bounding sphere that

3.2.2 LOD fidelity field
We utilize ξ and θ to define a parametric space for LOD selection, which we term the LOD fidelity field. In this field, each
node ni , with its parameters (ξi , θi ), is assessed to determine
whether it should be selected or adjusted to a coarser or finer
level of detail. The LOD fidelity field has two properties.
First, it supports the process of view-frustum culling, where
any node outside the viewing frustum is not selected. Second,
it is formed for individual hierarchies. As an example shown
in Fig. 5, depending on the depth of a hierarchy, the fidelity
field is divided into ranges in both dimensions of spatial distance and angular distance. Equation 2 defines the boundary
values of these ranges in the LOD fidelity field. spa(h) is the
boundary value in the field on the dimension of spatial distance for the level of nodes with the height h. For instance,
when h = 0, it corresponds to the leaf nodes. As h increases,
it moves up the hierarchy, with the root node having the greatest height. ecc(h) is the boundary value on the dimension of
angular distance for the level of nodes with the height h.
eccbase and spabase are the base values determined according to the finest level perceived by the human visual system.
tecc and tspa are scaling factors, determining the overall magnitude of the expressions by setting the baseline values when
h is varied. k is an index factor, influencing the rate of growth
as h changes. To determine these base values, we referenced
established perceptual studies (Surace et al. 2023; Mohanto
et al. 2022) on visual acuity and foveated rendering, and conducted internal visual tests. These tests involved inspecting
the perceptual boundary where LOD degradation becomes
noticeable at various eccentricities and distances, ensuring
that the base thresholds align with the limits of human sensitivity. The resulting fidelity field, based on node positions and
their depths in the hierarchy, is constructed once and reused
during runtime for node selection.

foveal focus. However, this process does not limit the number of nodes selected. In performance-constrained rendering scenarios, where the total number of selectable nodes is
fixed and the GPU memory allocation for rendering depends
on this number, the process may exceed the total load budget, potentially causing a memory overflow. To prevent this,
we implement a pruning strategy (Algorithm 2): sibling

The indices of a triangle’s vertices may exceed the bounds
of the selected vertex subset, making the triangle non-rasterizable due to missing vertex coordinates. To address this, we
employ a mesh reformation technique. Similar techniques
have been utilized in prior studies (e.g., Hu et al. (2009);
Peng and Cao (2012)), although they may be referred to
by different names in those publications. This technique
reforms triangle shapes, connects them, and therefore reconstructs the mesh topology within the node using pre-existing
edge-collapsing data.
This reformation process is crucial during implementation, where each GPU independently processes triangles
using triangle-level parallelization. If a vertex index exceeds
the count of selected vertices, it is substituted with the index
to which it collapsed during pre-processing (Fig. 3). This
substitution is continuously verified and adjusted by GPU
threads, referencing the edge-collapsing data associated
with each node. The process repeats until a valid, selected
vertex index is identified, ensuring the integrity and rasterizability of the mesh surface.
To further optimize performance, we implemented a parallelized culling process at the node level. For rasterization,
we adopted the strategy that uses the single buffer object
with multiple draw calls, rather than issuing separate buffer
objects for each node. This approach significantly reduces
CPU overhead associated with buffer switching, thereby
enhancing overall rendering efficiency.

4 Evaluation
We conducted experiments focusing on three aspects: the
influence of LOD fidelity field boundary value distribution,
the influence of node budget, and the overall performance
of our approach compared to existing rendering solutions.
Our test scene comprises 3D meshes from Stanford’s
Digital Michelangelo Project (Levoy et al. 2000), the 3D
Scanning Repository, and Disney’s Moana Island dataset
(Disney Animation Studio 2018). We implemented our VR
rendering system using OpenVR (Valve Software 2023),
OpenGL, and CUDA 11.6. To facilitate the exchange of
triangle and vertex data between OpenGL and CUDA,
we employed CUDA-OpenGL interoperability (NVIDIA
2024). Parallel processing for culling, LOD selection, and
triangle reformation was then executed using CUDA kernel
functions. The output is two arrays of triangles and vertices,
rendered using a single call to glDrawElementsBaseVertex
function.
Run-time computations are executed on a workstation
and the real-time rendering result is displayed on the Oculus
Quest Pro headset in the resolution of 4128*2112 pixels.
The workstation was powered by an Intel Core i9-10980XE
CPU running at 3.00 GHz and supported by 128 GB of
RAM. It was equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090
GPU with 24 GB of memory. The system runs on 64-bit
Windows 10, with NVIDIA driver version 546.01.
For testing, we created a complex scene that includes
high-detail statue models including David, Lucy, and a
section of Moana Island, nearly 100 million triangles in
total. The specific details of these models are summarized
in Table 1. To preprocess these models into our hierarchical mesh structure, the construction takes up to 18 minutes
per model, depending on mesh complexity. All hierarchical
mesh structures are fully preprocessed offline and cached
on disk. At runtime, the system only loads this data into the
CPU memory and performs no additional hierarchy construction, so preprocessing does not affect real-time performance. We designed a walk-through camera path with 9,566
frames to test rendering performance and quality.

quality with regions of low eccentricity and high spatial

these high-priority areas outward, balancing the preservation of essential visual detail with rendering efficiency.

to changes in eccentricity and spatial importance, which
reduces the LOD strategy’s effectiveness in tailoring rendering quality to varying visual priorities.

4.3 Performance evaluation
We compared the performance of our system with several
widely-used rendering solutions, including the OpenVR
OpenGL framework, Unity URP (Universal Render Pipeline), Unity HDRP (High Definition Render Pipeline), and
Unreal Engine’s Nanite. The Unity tests were conducted
using version 2022.3.7f1, while Unreal Engine tests were
performed on version 5.3.2. All methods were executed
on the same hardware configuration described in Sect. 4,
and rendered using a render scale of 1.0. We disabled all
shadows, MSAA, and post-processing effects across Unity,
Unreal, and OpenVR to ensure that the comparison focuses
solely on geometric processing performance. For Unreal
Engine, Nanite was enabled with default cluster sizes and
default streaming settings. For our system, we chose k = 1
and set the node budget to 2,000. Unreal Engine’s Nanite
utilizes a hierarchical level-of-detail system, which dynamically selects and streams the necessary details based on
the viewer’s perspective. Nanite is highly effective in VR
rendering, where it optimizes performance by reducing the
load of high-detail geometry while maintaining visual fidelity in areas closest to the viewer. Unity URP is designed for
scalability and real-time performance across a wide range
of platforms including VR, while Unity HDRP focuses on
Page 13 of 19 82
delivering high-fidelity visuals with slightly higher performance costs. Both URP and HDRP support VR applications
very well.
Figure 9 illustrates the performance comparison result,
showcasing the average rendering times per frame. Our
system achieves an average rendering time of 8.85 ms per
frame, significantly outperforming the other solutions. This
is 2.63 times faster than Unreal Engine’s Nanite, 3.06 times
faster than URP, and 3.13 times faster than HDRP. Additionally, our method demonstrates the least fluctuation in
performance. The standard OpenVR framework exhibits

geometric processing stage.

5 Conclusion
The paper presents a data structure and algorithm for
foveated rendering of complex 3D meshes, achieving significantly higher frame rates compared to state-of-the-art
solutions. In this work, meshes are processed to be represented as hierarchical patches, ensuring the adaptability of
our method to handle diverse scenarios. In the future, our
method can be altered for specific use cases. For example,
extending our approach to support instance-based rendering could significantly improve GPU memory utilization,
enabling more efficient handling of repetitive geometry
while maintaining the computational advantages demonstrated in this work.
While our experiments were conducted using the Oculus Quest Pro, the proposed rendering framework is not
restricted to a single device. Our system is built on the
OpenVR SDK, which offers broad compatibility with various VR headsets. Key device-specific parameters such as
field of view (FOV) and display resolution are incorporated
into the LOD selection pipeline as configurable inputs,
enabling the method to adapt to different headsets. Our
method supports eye-tracking for accurately detecting the
user’s focal point and estimating the foveated area. However, it can also operate on VR headsets without eye-tracking by using head gaze direction as an alternative, ensuring
broader applicability. In future work, we plan to validate the
portability and robustness of our approach across additional
VR platforms and datasets to further demonstrate its hardware adaptability.
The basic Lambert shader is used in the shading process
of our approach, which is the same as those used in the rendering solutions we have compared. The basic shader sufficiently demonstrates the differences in visual changes. In
the future, we plan to employ advanced shaders to enhance
realism, including effects such as soft shadows, screen-space
reflections, and ray tracing. These effects will be integrated
during the shading stage, so they do not impose additional
costs on the geometry processing stages. They will increase
the time required for rasterization or ray tracing. However,
we anticipate that the time spent on LOD selection and mesh
reformation will remain unaffected, as these processes scale
with the node and primitive counts.
