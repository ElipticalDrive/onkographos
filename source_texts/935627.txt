Abstract:
This paper presents a unified framework for real-time deformation of three-dimensional Signed Distance Fields (SDFs). To address the lack of local control in conventional SDF deformation methods, we propose an effective local editing technique coupled with an inverse transformation pipeline. Each query point in the deformed field is traced back to its pre-image in the original field via an operator-specific inverse transform, and a per-point compositing rule confines the deformation to the selected Region of Interest (ROI). The deformed distance field is efficiently reconstructed using the Fast Sweeping Method (FSM) to restore the Eikonal condition, and a smooth weighting function, applied iteratively, compensates for volume changes induced by the deformation. By integrating the pipeline into a GPU-accelerated ray-marching renderer, our framework provides immediate visual feedback during editing. Experimental results demonstrate that our framework enables effective local editing while preserving both distance-field validity and volumetric fidelity.

SECTION I.
Introduction
In recent years, the demand for precise 3D object representation and control has increased substantially across various industrial sectors. Traditionally, 3D modeling systems have predominantly relied on polygonal mesh structures. Mesh-based deformation employs forward mapping, which directly displaces an object’s vertices, offering simple and fast computation [1]. However, because a polygon mesh encodes only the surface boundary with no representation of the interior volume, mesh-based modeling has no awareness of the object’s topology. Under large or extreme deformations, this can produce self-intersection and tearing artifacts that are difficult to resolve within a purely boundary-based representation (see Fig. 1).

To overcome these geometric limitations, implicit surface modeling techniques have emerged [2], representing the object as a continuous volumetric field rather than a boundary alone so that overlapping geometry merges smoothly instead of tearing. Implicit surfaces perform deformations through inverse-mapping-based domain warping rather than by directly manipulating vertices [3], thereby deforming the entire embedding space. Because domain warping acts on the entire embedding space, conventional implicit deformations remain largely restricted to global edits. Local deformations, which control only specific regions, remain at the level of simple Constructive Solid Geometry operations, making flexible and complex shape control difficult.
With the rapid advancement of 3D computer vision and generative AI, Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) have been widely adopted as a core data structure for neural implicit representations [4]. However, applying local deformations to SDFs is particularly challenging: nonlinear domain warping violates the Eikonal condition ($\lVert \nabla \phi \rVert =1$
) and produces geometric discontinuities at region boundaries. Despite the growing industrial demand to edit SDFs generated by deep learning models intuitively and precisely, manipulating such SDFs in real time remains an open problem.
Naively applying conventional global domain-warping formulas [3], [5] only to a specific Region of Interest (ROI) within an SDF introduces geometric discontinuities between the deformed and undeformed regions during the inverse mapping, leading to unintended shape distortions and artifacts. Furthermore, domain warping violates the Eikonal condition, making subsequent shading and rendering computations unreliable. Therefore, an SDF-specialized deformation system is needed—one that overcomes the limitations of global deformation and allows users to perform intuitive, smooth, and complex local shape control while maintaining the mathematical integrity of the SDFs.
To this end, we propose a real-time framework (see Fig. 2) capable of applying multiple local deformations to SDFs without introducing significant computational overhead or geometric discontinuities. We design a hybrid GPU–CPU architecture that immediately reflects user edits. We further introduce a distance-field reconstruction pipeline that resolves boundary artifacts and distance-field distortion caused when domain warping is restricted to a local ROI.

The main contributions of our study are summarized as follows:
A Unified Deformation Framework Specialized for SDFs: We mathematically formulate an inverse mapping approach tailored to the geometric characteristics of SDFs. By employing a generalized deformation model that traces each query point back to its origin in the source field, our framework consistently handles both global and local deformations while suppressing the shape distortion artifacts inherent in conventional implicit deformations.
A Diverse Set of Deformation Operators for SDFs: We propose a diverse set of deformation operators—including rigid transformation, scaling, twisting, tapering, stretching, and bending—optimized for real-time SDF editing under the unified inverse-mapping formulation.
Real-Time Validity Restoration via Volume Preservation and FSM-based Reconstruction: We introduce an iterative volume-preservation scheme that compensates for geometric distortion during linear and nonlinear deformations, together with a deferred reconstruction pipeline that integrates the Fast Sweeping Method (FSM) [6]—an established Eikonal solver—enabling immediate restoration of the signed-distance property after each deformation without interrupting real-time interaction.
SECTION II.
Related Work
In geometric modeling and processing, the deformation of 3D objects has been predominantly studied using polygon mesh structures. While all mesh-based methods share the forward-mapping paradigm—displacing geometry from source to target positions—they differ substantially in their control interface. Free-Form Deformation, proposed by Sederberg and Parry [7], embeds the object’s vertices within a trivariate Bernstein volume defined by a control lattice; displacing the lattice control points propagates smooth deformation to the embedded vertices through polynomial interpolation. Handle-based differential methods such as As-Rigid-As-Possible modeling by Sorkine and Alexa [8] directly manipulate user-specified surface handles and propagate the edit by minimizing a linearized rigidity energy, preserving geometric details during deformation. Further extensions include meshless shape matching [9], deformation transfer between triangle meshes [10], and cage-based methods—such as Mean Value Coordinates [11] and Bounded Biharmonic Weights [12]—which propagate edits through generalized barycentric coordinates; Botsch and Sorkine [1] provide a comprehensive taxonomy of these approaches. These methods offer computational efficiency and simple control.
However, because these methods operate purely on the surface boundary and encode no information about the object’s interior volume, they cannot enforce any global constraint on the object’s topology. Consequently, extreme edits such as bending or twisting can drive separate surface regions into contact, producing self-intersections that are geometrically invalid and difficult to detect or correct within a purely surface-based representation.
To overcome these limitations, implicit surface techniques [2] define a scalar field over the entire volume, allowing topology changes to occur naturally without self-intersection. As a foundational study, Barr [3] introduced domain warping and inverse mapping to establish mathematical formulations for global deformations such as bending, twisting, and tapering, providing an important theoretical basis for space-based deformation. While Barr also described spatially restricted variants, these are implemented by simply masking the warp to a fixed interval along an axis rather than through ROI-aware compositing; they do not constitute true local editing in the sense of arbitrarily bounded, artifact-free regional control. These analytic formulations were subsequently extended to real-time rendering through sphere tracing [13], which advances rays according to SDF distance values and makes implicit surfaces particularly suitable for GPU ray-marching pipelines. Building on this, Reiner et al. [5] applied domain warping to SDF modeling integrated with sphere tracing to construct a real-time rendering pipeline. However, both Barr’s formulation and Reiner et al.’s method [5] share a fundamental constraint: the inverse mapping is designed to act on the entire spatial domain. Consequently, confining the warp to a local ROI breaks down at the boundary: the distance field is no longer valid there, and visual artifacts appear.
To address the dual challenge of supporting arbitrary geometric shapes and enabling true local control—limitations inherent to analytic domain warping—active attempts have been made to manipulate SDFs in discrete volume environments. Early foundational works introduced basic surface editing operators [14]. As highly efficient volume data structures such as OpenVDB [15] became the industry standard, grid-based SDF editing techniques [16] were proposed to directly store SDF values in 3D discrete grids and interactively deform them. While this approach freely accommodates arbitrary geometric shapes, existing pipelines apply nonlinear inverse transformation formulas across the entire grid, significantly increasing computational cost. Moreover, restricting edits to local areas causes structural disconnections during backtracking, leading to boundary artifacts.
As an alternative to hand-crafted discrete grids, neural implicit representations have emerged by leveraging deep learning to encode geometry directly into network weights. DeepSDF [4] and Occupancy Networks [17] introduced learned continuous implicit functions for high-quality 3D shape representation, while hierarchical approaches such as neural geometric level of detail [18] extended these to real-time rendering via octree-based acceleration. Following the paradigm shift brought by NeRF [19] and its extension to SDF-based geometry through neural volume rendering approaches such as NeuS [20], attention turned to editing within these learned representations. Frameworks such as DualSDF [21] and NeuMesh [22] enable semantic shape manipulation, while more recent efforts tackle geometry editing within radiance fields [23] and cage-based deformations of neural implicit models [24]. However, these approaches face fundamental limitations for interactive local editing: each deformation requires costly network inference or latent-space optimization, making per-frame real-time response impractical. Moreover, neural latent spaces do not expose explicit operator-level controls—such as twist, taper, or bend—making precise, geometrically interpretable local edits difficult without retraining or full network re-evaluation.
Each of these paradigms addresses part of the problem but leaves a critical gap that none resolves alone. To address these challenges, we propose a unified, SDF-specific real-time deformation framework combining ROI-based control, inverse-mapping operators, and a GPU ray-marching engine. By compositing the original and deformed fields per voxel, the method confines each edit to its ROI and avoids the boundary artifacts that nonlinear domain warping otherwise produces. FSM [6] then restores the Eikonal condition across the updated field, and an iterative weighting scheme compensates for discretization-induced volume fluctuations—together ensuring both geometric validity and volumetric fidelity after each edit.
SECTION III.
Unified Deformation Framework
This section presents a unified framework for real-time local deformation of SDFs (see Fig. 3). The framework integrates ROI-based domain definition, inverse-mapping-based deformation, FSM reconstruction, and volume preservation into a coherent pipeline applicable to both global and local editing.

A. Domain Initialization
The proposed framework begins with a standard polygonal mesh, which is converted into a discrete SDF $\phi $
on a uniform grid: the system first computes the unsigned distance to the closest surface triangle for each grid point using an Axis-Aligned Bounding Box (AABB) tree-based spatial acceleration structure. The sign of the distance is then determined by a pseudo-normal weighting scheme [26] to distinguish between the interior and exterior regions, resulting in a mathematically valid discrete SDF. Once the implicit volume has been initialized, the deformation domain is explicitly defined according to the intended editing scope. The framework supports both global deformation, which affects the entire object, and local deformation, which is restricted to a user-specified ROI. For local editing, the ROI is formally defined as a compact region $\Omega \subset \mathbb {R}^{3}$
, interactively specified through a bounding-box interface that enables intuitive selection of the deformation target. For global deformation, $\Omega $
is taken to be the entire object domain, so the compositing rule of Eq. (2) reduces to directly evaluating the deformed field everywhere.
B. Inverse-Mapping Formulation
Within the proposed framework, geometric manipulation is formulated as an implicit deformation evaluated through inverse mapping. Let $T$

This inverse formulation enables deformation to be performed without explicitly transporting scalar values over the grid. Furthermore, for localized nonlinear editing, the inverse map can be constructed over spatially separated subregions, allowing multiple deformation operators to be composed within a unified framework while avoiding abrupt discontinuities near ROI boundaries.
C. ROI-Based Field Compositing
While the inverse map defined above specifies the deformed value at each query point, restricting an edit to an ROI requires deciding, at every point, whether the original field, the deformed field, or a combination of the two should be used. We resolve this with a per-point compositing operator that depends on the ROI membership of both the query point p and its source pre-image $\mathbf {q}=T^{-1}(\mathbf {p})$
. Let $\Omega $
denote the ROI, $\phi $
the original field, and $\phi '(\mathbf {p})$
the deformed sample obtained by evaluating the operator’s inverse mapping at q (Eq. (1)). The composited field is computed by the following update rule\begin{align*} \phi (\mathbf {p}) & \leftarrow \phi '(\mathbf {p}), \quad \mathbf {p}\in \Omega,\ \mathbf {q}\in \Omega, \tag {2a}\\ \phi (\mathbf {p}) & \leftarrow d_{\infty }, \quad \mathbf {p}\in \Omega,\ \mathbf {q}\notin \Omega, \tag {2b}\\ \phi (\mathbf {p}) & \leftarrow \min \!\bigl (\phi (\mathbf {p}),\phi '(\mathbf {p})\bigr ), \quad \mathbf {p}\notin \Omega,\ \mathbf {q}\in \Omega, \tag {2c}\\ \phi (\mathbf {p}) & \leftarrow \phi (\mathbf {p}), \quad \mathbf {p}\notin \Omega,\ \mathbf {q}\notin \Omega . \tag {2d}\end{align*}

where $d_{\infty }$

Each case carries a direct geometric meaning. Case (2a) retains geometry inside the ROI whose source also originates within $\Omega $
. Case (2b) treats as empty any interior point whose pre-image lies outside $\Omega $
, preventing geometry from the untouched exterior from being pulled in by the inverse map. Case (2c) fuses geometry that the deformation sweeps beyond the ROI boundary into the surrounding field via the signed-distance union ($\min $
), a standard approximation for the union of implicit surfaces [2], effectively forming the swept volume of the moving region. Case (2d) leaves unaffected points unchanged. This compositing scheme mitigates the tearing artifacts that arise when domain warping is applied to a local region, as the union operator along $\partial \Omega $
blends the deformed and original fields rather than introducing an abrupt discontinuity. The corresponding jump to $d_{\infty } $
in Case (2b) is likewise confined to points swept far from the actual surface, so it does not introduce visible artifacts during rendering. In practice, an additional boundary constraint fixes points below the pivot to their original values, stabilizing the base of the edited region.
D. Fast Sweeping Method for Distance-Field Reconstruction
Although inverse mapping provides a flexible means of geometric manipulation, nonlinear deformation generally distorts the local metric structure of the field and violates the Eikonal condition, $\lVert \nabla \phi \rVert =1$
. Consequently, the deformed field may no longer satisfy the signed-distance property, leading to reduced accuracy in subsequent geometric operations and visible rendering artifacts. To address this, the proposed framework incorporates a distance-field reconstruction stage based on FSM [6].
To effectively restore the Eikonal condition without altering the newly deformed geometry, FSM computes the distance field by solving the discretized Eikonal equation using upwind differences. The algorithm begins by identifying the grid points immediately enclosing the deformed zero level set (the object’s surface). Exact signed distances for these narrow-band grid points are computed geometrically and fixed as Dirichlet boundary conditions, while all other unknown grid nodes are initialized to a sufficiently large positive (outside) or negative (inside) value.
Following initialization, the entire 3D discrete grid is traversed sequentially in eight alternating directions. For a grid of size $I \times J \times K$
, the sweeps cover all combinations of forward and backward traversals (e.g., $i=1 \to I, j=1 \to J, k=1 \to K$
; followed by $i=I \to 1, j=1 \to J, k=1 \to K$
, and so forth). This systematic traversal efficiently propagates the distance information in all directions from the boundary surface along the characteristic lines. During each sweep, the distance value $\phi _{i,j,k}$
at the current grid point is updated using the Godunov [25] upwind difference scheme to locally satisfy the Eikonal equation. The new value is determined by solving the following nonlinear equation:\begin{align*} & \hspace {-1pc}\max \!\left ({ \frac {\phi _{i,j,k} - \phi _{x,\min }}{h_{x}}, 0 }\right )^{2} + \max \!\left ({ \frac {\phi _{i,j,k} - \phi _{y,\min }}{h_{y}}, 0 }\right )^{2} \\ & \quad + \max \!\left ({ \frac {\phi _{i,j,k} - \phi _{z,\min }}{h_{z}}, 0 }\right )^{2} = 1, \tag {3}\end{align*}

Because of the alternating sweeping directions, the characteristic lines of the distance field are covered comprehensively, and the SDF values converge within a small, fixed number of passes. Through this process, the newly formed geometry is strictly preserved while the numerical validity of the field is immediately restored for stable rendering and further editing.
E. Iterative Volume Preservation
While domain warping enables flexible shape control, it inherently induces unintended local volume fluctuations due to grid discretization and non-uniform spatial scaling. To alleviate this, the proposed framework incorporates an iterative volume preservation scheme tailored to discrete SDF environments. Rather than relying on a single-pass linear approximation, the method estimates a physically interpretable distance offset from the discrepancy between the source and deformed volumes, applying the correction in a numerically stable iterative manner.
At each iteration, the local volume error $\Delta V$
and the local surface area $A$
within the ROI are measured. Given a uniform grid with a spatial resolution of $h$
, the physical volume and area are calculated by scaling the voxel counts. In practice, $\Delta V$
is estimated as the difference in the number of interior voxels ($\phi \le 0$
) before and after deformation multiplied by $h^{3}$
. Similarly, $A$
is estimated as the number of boundary voxels—voxels whose sign of $\phi $
differs from that of a face-adjacent neighbor—multiplied by $h^{2}$
. Based on these geometrically scaled quantities, a base compensation distance $\Delta d$
is computed as\begin{equation*} \Delta d = \eta {\,}\frac {\Delta V}{A}, \tag {4}\end{equation*}

where $\eta \in (0,1$
] is a damping factor introduced to ensure stable convergence. In practice, $\eta = 0.5$
provides a conservative and robust choice. Because $\Delta d$
is a first-order estimate of the offset required to eliminate the volume discrepancy, an undamped step ($\eta =1$
) can overshoot due to the nonlinear, discretization-dependent relationship between the offset and the resulting volume change; damping with $\eta \lt 1$
under-relaxes this step, and we empirically observe monotonic convergence within a few iterations across all tested configurations for $\eta \le 0.5$

and $A$

SECTION IV.
Instantiations of the Framework
Based on the unified framework described in the previous section, this section presents the mathematical formulation of specific geometric deformation operators for discrete SDFs. To maintain consistency across operators, deformations are evaluated through a common inverse-mapping procedure in Eq. (1). In particular, nonlinear deformations are described under the assumption that the deformation axis is aligned with the $x$
-axis, without loss of generality. Deformations along arbitrary directions can be handled flexibly through coordinate-frame alignment.
Notably, the framework is designed to be highly extensible. While we present specific linear and nonlinear operators in this section, the system is not restricted to these predefined functions. Users can define and integrate any custom transformation into the pipeline, provided that a valid inverse mapping $T^{-1}(\mathbf {p})$
exists; the new operator will then automatically benefit from the FSM reconstruction and volume preservation steps.
A. Linear and Affine Deformations
Rigid and scaling transformations provide the most fundamental forms of shape manipulation within the proposed framework. These operators directly manipulate the underlying coordinate frame, globally or locally affecting the spatial configuration of the object.
A rigid transformation changes only the position and orientation of the object while strictly preserving its intrinsic geometry. Let $R \in SO(3)$
denote a rotation matrix and $\mathbf {t} \in \mathbb {R}^{3}$
a translation vector. Then, for a query point p in the deformed field, the original field value is evaluated by mapping the point p back to the source field:\begin{equation*} \phi '(\mathbf {p}) = \phi \bigl (R^{T}(\mathbf {p} - \mathbf {t})\bigr ). \tag {9}\end{equation*}

Since rigid motion preserves Euclidean distance without scaling or shearing the space, the signed-distance property ($\lVert \nabla \phi \rVert = 1$

with a scale factor $s \gt 0$

Under uniform scaling, both spatial coordinates and the evaluated signed distances change proportionally. Therefore, scaling the sampled field value by $s$

In the anisotropic case, however, the scaling matrix distorts the local metric structure of the field, so the signed-distance property is no longer exact across the volume and the FSM reconstruction step of Section III-D is required.
B. Nonlinear Deformations
While linear transformations apply uniform changes across the domain, nonlinear deformations provide flexible and expressive control over local shape variations by warping the spatial metric in a non-uniform manner. to the inverse-mapped field:\begin{equation*} \phi '(\mathbf {p}) = \lambda {\,}\phi \bigl (T^{-1}(\mathbf {p})\bigr ). \tag {13}\end{equation*}

3) Stretching
Stretching extends the object along the deformation axis while simultaneously shrinking the perpendicular directions in a compensatory manner, as illustrated in Fig. 8. Unlike simple non-uniform scaling, which inflates or deflates the apparent volume of the object, this compensation keeps the volume constant, reproducing the Poisson effect observed in real-world elastic materials and thereby preserving visual plausibility. 4) Bending
Bending transforms a linear object into a curved configuration by angularly displacing its cross-sections based on their axial progression. SECTION V.
Implementation and Results
A. Implementation
The proposed framework was implemented using a hybrid GPU–CPU architecture to support real-time deformation and accurate SDF reconstruction. The mesh-to-SDF conversion pipeline used to initialize the volume data is described in Section III-A.
During user interaction, the inverse-mapping deformation is evaluated on the fly inside the GPU ray-marching shader, providing immediate visual feedback without modifying the underlying volume data. When the edit is committed, the same inverse mapping is evaluated for every voxel by a GPU compute pass that writes the deformed grid, which is then read back to the CPU for FSM-based reconstruction and volume-preserving correction.
1) Region-of-Interest Selection
The ROI introduced in the framework overview is specified interactively through two complementary tools. In box mode, the user drags a screen-space rectangle that is unprojected onto a view-aligned plane to form a three-dimensional selection box; its extent along the view direction is adjusted with the scroll wheel, and the pivot is placed at the bottom-center of the box. In lasso mode, the user draws a closed freehand contour. The system casts a ray through each interior sample of the contour, intersects it with the volume bounding box using a slab test, and marches the ray to collect the first connected band of interior voxels ($\phi \le 0$
) while discarding occluded back-facing material. Surface hits sampled along the contour boundary yield a cut line whose two most distant endpoints define a local frame: the view direction as the $z$
-axis, the screen-projected cut line as the $x$
-axis, and their cross product as the $y$
-axis, with the sign chosen so that the captured material lies on the positive side. Projecting the captured voxels onto this frame produces a tight oriented bounding box aligned with the selected feature rather than the world axes. The box may be further translated, rotated, or scaled with an on-screen gizmo, and its orientation is forwarded to the deformation stage as the rigid pre- and post-transformation that wraps the inverse mapping, so that every operator can be applied along an arbitrary direction.
2) GPU Inverse-Mapping Pipeline
The inverse-mapping deformation is evaluated on the GPU by a compute shader operating directly on the discrete SDF volume. The source field is uploaded to a single-channel 32-bit floating-point 3D texture, and a second texture of identical format receives the deformed output; both are allocated once and reused across edits as a ping-pong pair. The shader is dispatched over the grid in $8\times 8\times 8$
workgroups, with one invocation per destination voxel. Each invocation first maps its voxel into the ROI’s local frame by applying the inverse rigid transform about the pivot, evaluates the operator-specific inverse transform $T^{-1}$
in that aligned frame, and then maps the resulting coordinate back through the forward rigid transform so that the field is sampled in world space. During this pass, the original SDF value is retrieved via an exact integer fetch to maintain consistency with CPU-side sampling, while the deformed field is sampled at the inverse-mapped coordinate using hardware trilinear interpolation under a normalized-to-texel-center mapping.
For non-isometric operators such as non-uniform scaling, tapering, and stretching, the sampled distance is scaled by the conservative factor $\lambda $
(Section IV) to keep the field a valid lower bound for safe sphere tracing. The compositing operator Eq. (2) then merges the original and deformed fields per voxel, returning the original field outside the ROI, the deformed field inside it (or empty space $d_{\infty } $
where the pre-image falls outside the ROI), and their signed-distance union ($\min $
) where the deformation sweeps across the boundary; this localizes the deformation to the target region while fusing displaced geometry into the unedited exterior. Voxels far outside the ROI bypass this evaluation and retain their original value. Finally, a memory barrier ensures all GPU writes are visible before the deformed texture is read back to host memory for the subsequent FSM and volume-preservation passes.

C. FSM-Based Eikonal Reconstruction
We compared the distance-field properties before and after applying FSM. Nonlinear deformations reposition the zero level set directly through the inverse mapping, so the resulting surface geometry itself remains valid and artifact-free. The more pronounced degradation appears in the field around the surface: because the mapping warps the spatial metric, the sampled distances away from the boundary no longer satisfy the Eikonal condition ($\lVert \nabla \phi \rVert = 1$
), and these off-surface errors grow with distance from the surface. The FSM addresses exactly this off-surface degradation. Holding the edited zero level set fixed as a boundary condition, it re-solves the distance field outward so that the Eikonal condition is restored across the grid without moving the surface. As shown in Fig. 25, the dotted isocontours (before reconstruction) coincide with the solid ones (after reconstruction) right at the zero level set but diverge increasingly with distance, confirming that the distortion is confined to the surrounding field rather than the surface. After sweeping, the isocontours become uniformly spaced around the boundary, yielding a visually stable and mathematically valid signed-distance field.

E. Performance Evaluation
We evaluated the system’s performance in terms of processing time and frame rate on a PC equipped with an Intel Core Ultra 7 265K CPU, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Ti GPU (16GB VRAM), and 32GB of DDR5 RAM. During the interactive editing phase, the GPU-based ray-marching shader evaluates the composite inverse mapping formulas on the fly, consistently achieving real-time interaction at approximately 60 FPS on a $256^{3}$

This decoupled hybrid architecture ensures that the computational overhead of updating the high-resolution discrete grid does not interrupt the fluid and immediate visual feedback required for intuitive 3D modeling.
SECTION VI.
Conclusion
This paper presented a unified framework for real-time deformation of three-dimensional SDFs via inverse mapping. The framework integrates four tightly coupled stages—domain initialization, ROI-based inverse-mapping deformation, FSM-based distance-field reconstruction, and weighted iterative volume preservation—into a single coherent pipeline that consistently handles both global and local editing within the same formulation.
The central idea is to evaluate every edit as an inverse map and to localize it through a per-point composite operator that decides, per voxel, whether the original field, the deformed field, or their signed-distance union should be retained. This compositing guarantees continuity across the ROI boundary and thereby eliminates the artifacts that arise when domain warping is applied to a local region—the principal failure mode of conventional implicit deformation. Because nonlinear warping violates the Eikonal condition, FSM [6] is then applied to reconstruct the field while strictly preserving the edited zero level set, and a compactly supported weighting field iteratively compensates for discretization-induced volume fluctuations.
Built on this formulation, the framework supports a broad set of deformation operators—rigid transformation, uniform and non-uniform scaling, twisting, tapering, volume-preserving stretching, and bending—all evaluated through the same inverse-mapping procedure and made arbitrarily orientable through the ROI’s rigid pre- and post-transformation. A hybrid GPU–CPU architecture sustains real-time visual feedback at approximately 60 FPS on $256^{3}$
volumes via a ray-marching compute shader, while the exact FSM reconstruction and iterative volume correction run as a deferred CPU pass that does not interrupt interaction. The FSM reconstruction restored the Eikonal condition across the entire grid—recovering the uniformly spaced isocontours shown in Fig. 25—while, quantitatively, the iterative volume-preservation correction reduced the relative volume error to below 1% across all tested operators (Table 1); for a capsule subjected to combined twisting, stretching, and bending, the error decreased from roughly 0.96% to about 0.14% over ten iterations (Fig. 26).
Despite these contributions, several directions remain open. First, validating generalizability across diverse SDF sources—including 3D scanning, photogrammetry, and neural implicit models—would extend applicability beyond mesh-converted volumes. Second, adaptive grid-refinement strategies such as octree-based hierarchies could improve memory efficiency and reconstruction accuracy in regions of high geometric detail. Third, blending and conflict-resolution strategies for multiple overlapping ROIs would enable richer editing scenarios in which adjacent deformation regions interact. Finally, a formal user study assessing the usability of the bounding-box interface would inform the interaction design for practical 3D modeling workflows.
