Abstract
This study proposes a robust algorithm for tomographic reconstruction of
infrared radiance from an axisymmetric fuel spray in a hot environment by
directly solving the radiative transfer equation. The proposed algorithm combines differential infrared thermography (DIT) with multi-parameter regularized
inversion. First, the DIT emissivity is used to reconstruct the local extinction
coefficient, and the local radiative source term is then recovered by solving the
attenuated line-of-sight integral. To stabilize the ill-posed inversion, a global
smoothness penalty is combined with an additional center-axis constraint that
imposes the zero-gradient condition required by axisymmetry. A numerical phantom study, including configurations relevant to evaporating cold sprays and
flashing jets, is conducted to evaluate the reconstruction accuracy under welldefined conditions and noisy measurement scenarios. The results demonstrate
that the proposed method extends conventional DIT beyond the equivalent-slab
approximation by resolving the line-of-sight-integrated emissivity into a local
extinction field and accounting for self-attenuation within the spatially resolved
axisymmetric spray structure. This approach allows reconstruction of the local
radiative source term and local radiance with good agreement with the ground
truth in the main spray region. Consequently, this establishes an IR-tomographic
framework for recovering the internal radiance and potentially the temperature field of axisymmetric sprays with appropriate radiometric calibration. An
additional noise-suppression strategy is introduced to improve reconstruction
robustness at higher noise levels.

1 Introduction
Modern combustion engines are designed to operate at high chamber pressures and
temperatures to improve combustion efficiency. In modern liquid rocket and internal combustion engines, the chamber pressure can exceed the critical pressure of the
fuel, while the fuel temperature often remains low or close to room temperature as it
exits the injection nozzle. The injected fuel is subsequently heated by the hot combustion gases and surrounding chamber structures, and its temperature may exceed the
critical temperature during the injection process. This regime is commonly referred
to as transcritical injection (Segal and Polikhov 2009) and involves complex thermodynamic spray behavior, including phase transition (Crua et al. 2017) and phase
separation (Traxinger et al. 2019).
At the opposite pressure extreme, propellant sprays in upper-stage rocket engines
may undergo substantial phase change before ignition because the chamber pressure can be close to vacuum. Under such conditions, the injected propellant may
become highly superheated relative to the local saturation temperature and undergo
rapid vaporization, a phenomenon known as flash boiling (Luo and Haidn 2016).
Similar to transcritical injection, the rapid phase change and highly transient spray
structure make quantitative measurements under flash-boiling conditions particularly
challenging (Rees et al. 2020).
Therefore, quantitative investigations of spray behavior under extreme thermodynamic conditions, including both transcritical injection and flash boiling, are
particularly relevant to high-performance combustion systems, in which rapid mixing
and stable combustion are directly linked to system performance and stability (Liu
et al. 2017b; Oschwald et al. 2006; Son et al. 2025).
To investigate sprays in hot environments, optical and laser-optical diagnostics
have been widely used. Light extinction measurements (LEM) or diffuse-background
illumination (DBI), often called simply “shadowgraphy”, is one of the most common
techniques for observing the overall spray structure (Linne et al. 2010; Knorsch et al.
2013), and the liquid phase can be distinguished by combining it with Mie-scattering
signals (Baab et al. 2018). Several studies have attempted to quantify spray characteristics from optical images, such as spray angle, penetration (Jeyaseelan et al. 2023),
and liquid-air volume fraction (Weiss et al. 2020; Lazzaro et al. 2024). However, optical
diagnostics are limited in dense sprays because extinction and multiple light interactions make quantitative interpretation difficult. Optical extinction tomography has
been applied to reconstruct the internal structure of dense fuel sprays, although large
optical depths require careful treatment to suppress reconstruction artifacts (Lazzaro
et al. 2024). Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) has also been employed to quantify spray
characteristics, such as vapor temperature (Trost et al. 2013) and droplet size (Mishra
et al. 2020). Nevertheless, LIF usually requires fluorescent tracers, which are typically
added to the fuel, and its calibration becomes complicated under extreme conditions
2
due to strong phase changes, pyrolysis and chemical reaction of tracers, and low LIF
signals. Rayleigh scattering can partially provide gas-phase temperature information,
but it is generally restricted to diluted regions to avoid contributions of elastic scattering from droplets (Allison et al. 2016), which excludes the dense spray region of
primary interest.
Infrared (IR) imaging is a non-intrusive two-dimensional diagnostic technique that
has been widely applied to quantitative measurements in thermo-fluid systems (Carlomagno and Cardone 2010). IR thermography has been applied to characterize thermal
distributions of sprays (Lamanna et al. 2013; Golzke et al. 2016) and droplets (Akafuah et al. 2010; Saha et al. 2010). However, IR thermography for sprays and droplets
has typically been used to obtain surface temperature or effective temperature, rather
than tomographic temperature inside dense sprays. The fundamental challenge of IR
tomography originates from the radiative transfer equation (RTE), which describes
the coupling between local emission and attenuation, as expressed in Eq. 1.

Solving the RTE includes two major challenges, self-attenuation of radiation within
the spray and the ill-posed inversion of the integral source term. Traditional tomographic studies based on optical diagnostics often simplify the inversion by assuming
a transparent medium (Klinner and Willert 2012; Weiss et al. 2020; Cai and Kaminski 2017). However, in dense sprays or soot-laden flames, signal attenuation cannot be
neglected (Yu et al. 2019). Li et al. (2018) solved the RTE for sooting flame tomography using a multi-wavelength technique to estimate the attenuation. However, this
method relies on iterative estimation loops that may accumulate errors and assumes
that attenuation is caused exclusively by soot particles (nanometer-scale), which is not
directly applicable to spray studies since sprays contain micrometer-scale droplets.
Differential infrared thermography (DIT) has been used in spray studies to avoid
direct inversion of the ill-posed integral term by estimating the spray transmittance
e−δmax from Eq. 1 (Lamanna et al. 2013; Golzke et al. 2016). Although DIT can provide
temperature fields of sprays, the DIT temperature cannot represent the local spray
temperature because the 3-D spray structure is simplified as a thin slab layer rather
than being spatially resolved along the line of sight. The DIT temperature instead
represents an effective apparent temperature associated with the region where the LOD
is of the order of unity according to the Eddington–Barbier approximation (Rutten
2003), which has also been applied in spray studies (Golzke et al. 2016). Therefore,
DIT alone is insufficient to recover the internal radiance or temperature distribution
of optically thick sprays. In the present study, the integrated slab emissivity obtained
from DIT is decomposed to estimate the local extinction coefficient, and the local
radiative source term is subsequently reconstructed by directly solving the RTE.
3
The remaining difficulty is the ill-posedness of the inverse problem. Iterative techniques such as the algebraic reconstruction technique (ART) have historically been
used because direct inversion is unstable. However, iterative methods have been
reported to converge slowly as the solution approaches the ground truth (Wan et al.
2015). They are also affected by the semi-convergence phenomenon, in which the
solution initially approaches the correct profile but later diverges as measurement
noise is amplified (Cai and Kaminski 2017; Liu et al. 2017a). To stabilize direct
inversion, regularization techniques such as Tikhonov regularization (Tikhonov 1975)
and total variation regularization (Bachmayr and Burger 2009) have been employed.
Tikhonov regularization promotes smooth solutions, whereas total variation regularization favors piecewise-constant solutions and can introduce stair-casing effects.
However, a single global regularization term is not always sufficient for axisymmetric spray tomography, particularly near the symmetry axis where numerical
artifacts can occur due to the Abel-type inversion. The reconstruction of axisymmetric fields from line-of-sight measurements is particularly sensitive to the choice
of inversion algorithm and measurement noise, as already revealed for axisymmetric
background-oriented-shadowgraphy measurements (Xiong et al. 2020).
To overcome the limitations of single-penalty regularization, multi-penalty models
have been investigated in inverse problems (Bortolotti et al. 2025). Ito et al. (2011)
showed that multi-parameter models are useful when the reconstructed solution has
multiple distinct characteristics. Gazzola and Reichel (2016) demonstrated that combining regularization matrices with different null spaces can preserve various physical
features of the target. Chen et al. (2008) further showed that individual penalties
tailored to different scales can improve the stability of inversion. Based on this concept, we introduce a multi-parameter regularization framework that combines a global
smoothness penalty with an additional center-axis constraint for axisymmetric IR
tomography. This method provides a new regularization-based strategy for analyzing
axisymmetric sprays.
This study presents a robust framework for tomographic reconstruction of IR
radiance from an axisymmetric spray in a hot environment by directly solving the
RTE. The DIT technique is used to obtain the integrated emissivity, from which the
local attenuation information is reconstructed. The local source term is then recovered using a multi-parameter regularized inversion. A numerical phantom study is
conducted to demonstrate that the proposed algorithm can reconstruct the radiative source term even in the presence of measurement noise. Finally, an additional
noise-suppression strategy is introduced to improve robustness under severely noisy
measurement conditions.

To maximize the contribution of spray radiance, a bandpass filter is typically selected
in a spectral region with strong absorption, such as the C–H stretching band for
hydrocarbon fuels (Clark et al. 2009). In addition, the ambient gas should preferably
be non-radiative and transparent within the selected spectral band. As a homonuclear diatomic molecule, nitrogen is a suitable choice due to the absence of strong
dipole-active vibrational transitions in the mid-IR range, minimizing radiative interference (Bernath 2025). Additionally, nitrogen is chemically inert with respect to the
fuel, avoiding additional radiative contributions from chemical reactions.
Based on the assumptions summarized in the previous section, the measured
line-of-sight signal can be formulated as an axisymmetric radiative transfer problem. Radiative transfer through the optical system must account for local attenuation
within the non-uniform spray structure (Chandrasekhar 2013). Since the fuel is

injected through a narrow nozzle and spreads into the combustion chamber, the spray

absorption, such as the C–H stretching band, attenuation is assumed to be dominated
by absorption. Under this assumption, the same local absorption coefficient that determines the LOD also contributes to the local radiative source term through Kirchhoff’s

on fuel concentration but also on thermodynamic conditions such as temperature and
pressure. For IR imaging in a narrow spectral band, the radiance described by the
RTE can be related to the camera digital signal through radiometric calibration using
a black-body calibrator (Golzke et al. 2016).
The inverse reconstruction proposed in this study starts from several assumptions
that are required to reduce the radiative transfer problem to a tractable axisymmetric tomographic formulation. These assumptions define the range of validity of the
reconstructed attenuation coefficient, source term, and local radiance.

Second, scattering is not included explicitly in the inverse reconstruction. This
approximation follows the practical treatment of DIT thermography, where the radiative signal is mainly interpreted in terms of emission and attenuation (Lamanna et al.
2013; Golzke et al. 2016). However, in dense liquid regions close to the nozzle, radiation originating from chamber structures (e.g. wall and windows) or heaters may
be scattered by droplets and may contribute to the measured IR signal, as discussed
by Golzke et al. Therefore, the reconstructed attenuation coefficient and source term
should be interpreted as effective radiative quantities within the selected IR band. In
practice, an appropriate bandpass filter, e.g., the C–H band for hydrocarbon fuels,
should be used to enhance the relative contribution of spray emission and reduce background radiation from chamber structures or heaters, thereby reducing their potential
scattering contribution to the measured signal.
Third, local thermodynamic equilibrium is assumed for the radiative emission of
the spray. Under this assumption, the local emission can be directly related to the
local temperature through a calibration-based black-body radiance in the selected
wavelength band. In this study, the local radiance I (r) does not represent an absolute physical spectral radiance, but rather an effective black-body radiometric signal
obtained from the camera calibration. This assumption allows the reconstructed
source term to be converted into an effective local radiance and, subsequently, into a
temperature field using a calibration curve.

2.2 Reconstruction of Extinction Coefficient
The Differential Infrared Thermography (DIT) technique allows for the temperature measurement of a transmissive target with two different radiating backgrounds,
assuming that the temperature or radiance of the target remains constant while
the background changes (Sharkov 2003; Lamanna et al. 2013; Golzke et al. 2016).
This means that the spray field remains statistically consistent between the two DIT
measurements used to evaluate the effective slab emissivity. Therefore, the local attenuation coefficient and source term are considered identical for the two background
conditions. If the spray structure or chamber radiation changes significantly between
the two measurements, the estimated emissivity and the subsequent reconstruction
contain additional uncertainty.

3.1 Phantom configuration

are known. The purpose of this study is to evaluate whether the inverse algorithm
can recover the local extinction coefficient α(r) and the local radiative source term
j (r) from synthetic line-of-sight measurements affected by measurement noise. Since
the ground-truth fields are prescribed analytically, the reconstructed results can be
compared directly with the known reference profiles.

6 Full Reconstruction Results and Discussion
Based on 0.3 % noisy measurement data, the radiative source term jrec is reconstructed
together with the extinction coefficient αrec , as shown in Figure 19 (left). Using jrec
and αrec , the reconstructed local radiance Irec is then calculated using Eq. 25, and
the result is shown in Figure 19 (right). The profiles of jrec and Irec agree well with
the ground truth, even in the presence of noise, within the range where r or x equals
1.0. Near the outer boundary, because the radiance is defined as jrec divided by α,
small perturbations in low jrec cause large fluctuations that cannot be avoided. Wei
et al. (2021) successfully reduced the boundary fluctuation through external masking
or thresholding (Wei et al. 2021). Similar to the approach used by Wei et al., the lowintensity region can be removed by applying a mask with a threshold derived from
the εDIT profile, thereby excluding areas with low emission, as shown in Figure 19
(bottom); radiance values for which εDIT < 0.05 are omitted in this work.
When we compare Irec with the radiance obtained by the DIT method as defined in
Eq. 15, IDIT approximately captures the outer region, but its profile is displaced toward
the centerline, and the DIT radiance is overestimated near the axis. Therefore, it is
clearly demonstrated that the proposed multi-regularized inversion approach significantly outperforms the direct DIT method for reconstructing axisymmetric radiance
profiles of evaporating sprays from infrared thermography data.

It should be emphasized that the proposed method does not always yield a perfect
reconstruction of the local radiance, as shown in Figure 19. To understand this, we
return to Figure 17. Comparing the reconstructed j and α in Figure 17, we observe
that α is slightly underestimated, while j is slightly overestimated near the axis. These
deviations appear to be associated with the different shapes of the underlying radial
profiles in the present phantom case. The Gaussian-like α profile, with its maximum at
the axis, is slightly underestimated near the centerline, whereas the bimodal j profile
shows a slight overestimation in the same region. Since the local radiance is obtained
from the ratio Irec = jrec /αrec , both reconstruction biases propagate into the resulting
radiance profile. Nevertheless, Irec shows good agreement with the ground truth for
the present phantom configuration.
Although more accurate estimates could, in principle, be obtained by carefully
tuning µ2 , this is only feasible when the ground truth is already known. In practice, however, the ground truth is unknown and µ2 has to be chosen according to
a consistent criterion, such as selecting the corner of the L-curve. Consequently, the
observed tendency toward underestimation and overestimation near the axis represents
an inherent limitation of the proposed method.
Another important aspect is the sensitivity to noise. In this study, a noise level of
0.3 % is primarily used because the other inversion methods are already unstable at
this level. To further assess the robustness of the proposed method, noise levels up to
3.0 % are added to the measured phantom data, as illustrated in Figure 20. For noise
levels up to 1.0 %, the spray radiance profiles are reconstructed in a stable manner,
whereas the reconstruction at 3.0 % noise is unacceptably degraded. As anticipated
from Figure 9 and Eq.12b, the TOD is highly sensitive to measurement noise due to its
logarithmic nature. Consequently, at 3.0 % noise, δmax becomes extremely unstable,
as shown in Figure 21.
To reduce noise propagation, three smoothing strategies are evaluated. In the first
strategy, the original ε profile is kept unchanged while δmax is directly smoothed. In
the second approach, ε is smoothed first, and δmax is then implicitly smoothed via the
relation δmax (x) = − ln [1 − ε(x)]. In the third method, the measured data Imeas,1 and
Imeas,2 are initially smoothed, after which ε and δmax are successively obtained and

smoothed using Eq. 14. As shown in Fig. 21, all three strategies effectively suppress the

significantly distort the values after the logarithm is taken, making it challenging to
accurately determine δmax even with smoothing. Therefore, smoothing ε seems to be
a reasonable approach, as it preserves most of the information in the ε domain while
still generating a result that closely matches the ground truth.

7 Conclusion
In this study, we introduced a robust framework for tomographic reconstruction of
infrared (IR) radiance from an axisymmetric spray in a hot environment by directly
solving the radiative transfer equation (RTE). The main novelty of the proposed
approach is that the effective slab spray emissivity obtained from differential infrared
thermography (DIT) is not used solely for apparent thermometry, as in conventional
DIT, but is further decomposed to reconstruct the local extinction coefficient. The
reconstructed attenuation field is then incorporated into the attenuated line-of-sight
integral to reconstruct the local radiative source term. In this way, the proposed
method extends conventional DIT from an apparent thermography technique to a
tomographic reconstruction framework for optically attenuating sprays.
A numerical phantom study was conducted to validate the proposed two-step
reconstruction procedure under predefined conditions, primarily representing an evaporating cold spray in a hot environment. An additional source configuration was
also considered to investigate applicability of the suggested method to a flashing-jetlike radiative distribution. The results showed that conventional DIT can evaluate
the outer spray region relatively well but cannot recover the internal radiance of
28
an optically thick spray. In particular, the DIT radiance was displaced relative to
the ground-truth profile and showed significant deviations in the dense spray region
near the axis. This confirms that the DIT radiance should be interpreted as an
effective apparent quantity rather than a local tomographic radiance when strong
self-attenuation is present.
The inverse reconstruction of both the extinction coefficient and the local radiative
source term was found to be highly sensitive to measurement noise. Direct inversion
accurately reproduced the ground-truth profiles in the noise-free case but became
unstable even under a noise level of 0.3 %. Iterative algorithms improved numerical
stability compared with direct inversion but still showed high sensitivity to noise and
non-physical fluctuations near the axis. These results indicate that a stable regularized
inversion is essential for IR tomography of dense sprays in hot environments.
To address this issue, multi-parameter regularization was introduced. The proposed formulation combines a global smoothness penalty based on second-order
Tikhonov regularization with an additional center-axis constraint that imposes
the zero-gradient condition required by axisymmetry. Tikhonov regularization suppressed high-frequency oscillations caused by measurement noise, while the center-axis
constraint enforced a zero gradient at the axis. Compared with single Tikhonov regularization, the multi-regularized inversion improved the reconstruction near the axis
without significantly influencing the overall radial distribution. This shows that the
proposed center-axis constraint effectively suppresses non-physical artifacts near the
symmetry axis while preserving the overall radial distribution.
As a result, the local radiance was successfully recovered in the phantom study. The
reconstructed radiance Irec agreed well with the ground truth within the main spray
region. Remaining fluctuations appeared near the outer boundary, where the extinction
coefficient becomes small, because the local radiance is obtained from the ratio of the
radiative source term to the extinction coefficient. These fluctuations can be reduced by
applying a mask based on the DIT emissivity εDIT , excluding weakly radiating regions
below a prescribed threshold. For higher noise levels similar to practical applications,
additional smoothing techniques further improved the robustness of the reconstruction
by suppressing noise amplification in the total optical depth.
Consequently, the proposed method provides a promising framework for quantitative IR tomography of axisymmetric sprays under high-pressure and high-temperature
conditions. The key contribution is the combination of DIT-derived attenuation,
RTE-based source-term reconstruction, and multi-parameter regularization with a
physics-informed constraint. Although the reconstruction is demonstrated in this study
only for spray thermography, the suggested framework may also be extended to other
axisymmetric radiating and attenuating media, such as flames, as long as the assumptions behind the radiative transfer model remain valid. Future work will focus on
applying the proposed framework to experimental spray measurements with precise
calibration.
