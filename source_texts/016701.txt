Abstract
The rapid advancement of digital technologies has led to an exponential increase in the volume of image
data, creating significant challenges for efficient storage, transmission and retrieval. Image compression has
therefore become an essential technique for reducing storage requirements while preserving image quality.
Wavelet transforms provide an effective mathematical framework for representing image information at
multiple levels of resolution, enabling efficient approximation and compression with reduced data
redundancy. This study presents a two-dimensional (2D) image compression method based on the Haar
wavelet, the most computationally efficient wavelet transform. The proposed approach employs the twodimensional Discrete Wavelet Transform (2D-DWT) to decompose the input image into approximation and
detail coefficients. The compressed image is subsequently reconstructed using the retained approximation
and estimated detail coefficients. The performance of the compression method is evaluated using standard
image quality metrics, including Compression Ratio (CR), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Picture
Quality Scale (PQS). The proposed method demonstrates effective compression while maintaining
acceptable reconstructed image quality. Furthermore, the study highlights the relevance of wavelet-based
compression in medical imaging, where efficient storage and transmission must be achieved without
compromising significant image information. Although lossy compression techniques generally provide
higher compression ratios, their adoption in medical imaging remains limited because of diagnostic
concerns. Consequently, the primary objective of the study was to maximize compression efficiency while
preserving clinical relevance and ensuring diagnostic reliability. Hence, the Haar wavelet becomes a
promising solution for resource-constrained environments because of its ease of implementation and
computational efficiency.

1. Introduction to the study
The rapid growth of digital technologies and web-based services has significantly increased the volume of
digital image data generated, stored and transmitted across various applications. Consequently, efficient
image compression has become essential for reducing storage requirements, accelerating data transmission
over bandwidth-limited networks, and improving the efficiency of image retrieval systems. These
requirements are particularly important in applications such as medical imaging, remote sensing,
multimedia systems, and cloud-based image repositories, where large image datasets are routinely
processed [1,2].
Image compression techniques aim to reduce data redundancy while preserving the visual quality and
essential information contained in the original image. In addition to minimizing storage space and
transmission time, effective compression techniques provide progressive or incremental transmission
capabilities, allowing coarse image representations to be transmitted first, followed by finer details as
additional data become available. This feature is particularly valuable in web-based applications and realtime image communication systems [2,3].
Among the various image compression approaches, wavelet-based methods are one of the most effective
techniques because of their ability to represent images at multiple resolutions. Unlike the Discrete Cosine
Transform (DCT), which processes images in independent blocks and may introduce blocking artifacts at
high compression ratios, wavelet transforms analyze the entire image using multiresolution decomposition.
This property enables superior energy compaction, reduces visual artifacts, and preserves important image
features more effectively [4,5].
Wavelet compression is based on multiresolution analysis, where an image is decomposed into
approximation and detail components at different frequency levels. Similar to conventional JPEG
compression, the transformed image is represented by a set of coefficients. However, most wavelet
coefficients are either very small or close to zero, allowing insignificant coefficients to be discarded while
retaining only the most informative components. As a result, high compression ratios can be achieved with
minimal degradation in reconstructed image quality [4,5].
Among the available wavelet families, the Haar wavelet is computationally most efficient transform. Its
low computational complexity, ease of implementation, and fast decomposition and reconstruction
processes make it particularly suitable for real-time image compression applications and systems with
limited computational resources. Despite its simplicity, the Haar wavelet provides satisfactory compression
performance while preserving important image characteristics [5,6].
This study investigates a two-dimensional (2D) image compression approach based on the Haar Discrete
Wavelet Transform (DWT). The proposed method performs multiresolution image decomposition,
reconstructs the compressed image using the approximation and selected detail coefficients, and evaluates
2
compression performance using widely accepted quality metrics, including Compression Ratio (CR), Peak
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Picture Quality Scale (PQS). The findings demonstrate the
effectiveness of Haar wavelet-based compression as a low-complexity and reliable solution for efficient
image storage and transmission while maintaining acceptable reconstructed image quality.
2. State of the art in the field
Wavelet transform has become one of the most influential techniques in modern image processing because
of its ability to represent image information at multiple spatial resolutions. Unlike conventional transformbased methods, wavelet analysis decomposes an image into approximation and detail components, enabling
efficient representation of both low- and high-frequency information. This multiresolution property has
made wavelet transforms a fundamental tool in image compression, denoising, feature extraction,
segmentation, and image enhancement [7-9].
Among the numerous transform-based image compression techniques, wavelet-based methods have
demonstrated acceptable performance, particularly at medium and high compression ratios. Earlier image
coding standards, such as JPEG, employ the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), which processes images in
independent blocks and often introduces blocking artifacts when high compression ratios are applied. In
contrast, wavelet-based compression analyzes the entire image without block partitioning, thereby reducing
visual artifacts and preserving structural image information more effectively. These advantages led to the
adoption of wavelet technology in the JPEG image compression standard [8-10].
Wavelet-based image compression offers several important advantages. First, multiresolution
decomposition enables progressive image transmission, allowing low-resolution representations to be
reconstructed before finer details are received. Second, wavelet coding produces fewer visual artifacts than
block-based methods, resulting in improved subjective image quality. Third, wavelet transforms exhibit
excellent energy compaction, where most of the signal energy is concentrated in a relatively small number
of coefficients, facilitating efficient compression. Furthermore, wavelet-based methods are more robust to
transmission errors and provide flexible control over compression quality and bit rate, making them
suitable for web-based multimedia applications, remote sensing, and medical imaging systems [8-11].
The effectiveness of wavelet compression largely depends on the selected wavelet family. Among the
available wavelets, the Haar wavelet remains one of the most computationally efficient transforms.
Although more sophisticated wavelets, such as Daubechies, Symlets, and Coiflets, generally provide higher
energy compaction, the Haar wavelet offers significant advantages in terms of computational simplicity,
rapid decomposition and reconstruction, and ease of implementation. Consequently, it remains an attractive
choice for real-time applications and systems with limited computational resources [11,12] of the low
income countries.

2.1 Digital images maintaining acceptable visual fidelity
A digital image is a two-dimensional function, (f(x,y)), where (x) and (y) denote spatial coordinates and the
function value represents the intensity or gray level at each location. Following image acquisition, the
continuous image is converted into a discrete representation through two fundamental processes; spatial
sampling and gray-level quantization. Sampling divides the image into a finite number of pixels, while
quantization assigns each pixel an integer intensity value. The resulting image can therefore be represented
as a two-dimensional matrix whose elements correspond to individual pixel intensities [13].
The quality of a digital image depends on the sampling resolution and the number of quantization levels.
Higher spatial resolution and finer quantization provide a more accurate representation of the original scene
but also increase storage requirements. Consequently, efficient image compression techniques are essential
for reducing data redundancy while maintaining acceptable visual quality [8,13].
2.2 Digital image processing algorithms
Digital image processing involves manipulating digital images using computational algorithms to improve
image quality, extract useful information, or reduce data redundancy. Since computers operate on
numerical data, continuous images must first undergo digitization before further processing. The digitized
image is represented as a matrix of pixel intensity values, enabling mathematical operations such as
enhancement, restoration, segmentation, feature extraction, and compression [13,14].
Image compression is one of the most important applications of digital image processing. By exploiting
spatial and statistical redundancy within image data, compression algorithms significantly reduce storage
space and transmission bandwidth while preserving the visual and structural characteristics required by the
target application. Wavelet compression has become particularly attractive because of its multiresolution
representation, high compression efficiency, and ability to reconstruct images with minimal perceptual
distortion [8-11].
3. Methodology and system framework
This study implements a two-dimensional (2D) image compression framework based on the Haar Discrete
Wavelet Transform (DWT). The methodology consists of image decomposition, coefficient quantization,
entropy coding, and image reconstruction. The proposed framework was designed to reduce image
redundancy while preserving the visual information required for accurate image reconstruction [17].
3.1 Image compression system framework
The implemented lossy image compression system comprises three major components (i) transform coding,
(ii) quantization, and (iii) entropy encoding [17,18]. The transform stage converts the original image into
4
another domain in which most of the image energy is concentrated in a relatively small number of
coefficients. The transformed coefficients are subsequently quantized to reduce data precision and achieve
compression. Finally, entropy coding was applied to eliminate statistical redundancy and further reduce the
compressed bitstream size without introducing additional information loss. Fig 1 illustrates the general
architecture of the proposed image compression framework.
Fig.1 the image compression framework
Various transform techniques have been developed for image compression, including the Discrete Fourier
Transform (DFT), Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), and Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT). Among
these techniques, the DWT provides multiresolution representation and generally produces better image
quality at higher compression ratios [18,19].
3.2 Quantization process
Quantization is the primary source of compression in a lossy image coding system [18,20]. During this
stage, the precision of the transformed coefficients is reduced by mapping a range of coefficient values to a
smaller set of representative values. Since multiple input values are represented by a single output value,
quantization introduces irreversible information loss but significantly decreases the number of bits required
for storage.
Quantization methods are generally classified into scalar quantization (SQ) and vector quantization (VQ)
[20]. Scalar quantization processes each transform coefficient independently, whereas vector quantization
operates on groups of coefficients simultaneously. Depending on the application requirements, either
uniform or non-uniform quantization strategies may be employed to balance compression efficiency and
reconstructed image quality.
3.3 Entropy encoding for representation methods
After quantization, entropy encoding was performed to remove the remaining statistical redundancy [18,21]
from the compressed data without further degrading image quality. Entropy encoders assign shorter
codewords to frequently occurring symbols and longer codewords to less frequent symbols, thereby
minimizing the average number of bits required to represent the compressed data.
5
Common entropy coding techniques include Huffman coding, arithmetic coding, and Run-Length Encoding
(RLE). Huffman coding and arithmetic coding provide high compression efficiency, while RLE offers
lower computational complexity and is particularly effective when long sequences of identical coefficients
occur [21]. An efficient entropy encoder, together with an appropriate transform and quantization strategy,
plays a significant role in maximizing the overall compression performance.

recursively combining the approximation coefficients with the corresponding detail coefficients through

represented as a sequence of wavelet coefficients. Alternatively, a one-dimensional image may be viewed
8
as a piecewise-constant function defined on the half-open interval [0,1). This interpretation provides the
mathematical foundation for multiresolution analysis through the concept of vector spaces.
A one-pixel image corresponds to a function that is constant over the entire interval [0,1). Let V0 denote
the vector space containing all such functions. Similarly, a two-pixel image consists of two constant
segments defined over the intervals [0,1/2) and [1/2,1), and the corresponding vector space is denoted by
V1. Extending this construction recursively, the vector space Vj contains all piecewise-constant functions
defined on the interval [0,1), where each function is constant over each of the 2j equal subintervals.
Consequently, every one-dimensional image containing 2j pixels can be regarded as an element (vector) of
the space Vj. Since every function in Vj is also a piecewise-constant function in the finer-resolution space
Vj+1, the vector spaces satisfy the nested relationship as follows:
V0⊂V1⊂V2⊂⋯ .
This hierarchy of nested vector spaces forms the basis of multiresolution analysis (MRA), which is
fundamental to wavelet theory. It guarantees that every function represented in a lower-resolution space can
be represented exactly in any higher-resolution space. The converse, however, is generally not true. A
function G(x) belonging to Vj+1cannot, in general, be represented exactly in the lower-resolution space Vj,
since the finer-scale details are lost during approximation.

The transformation of a two-dimensional (2D) image is a natural extension of the one-dimensional (1D)
Haar wavelet transform described previously. In the first stage, the 1D Haar wavelet transform was applied
independently to each row of the image matrix. This operation generates a set of approximation (average)
coefficients together with the corresponding detail coefficients for every row [19].
10
The transformed rows are subsequently treated as a new image, and the 1D Haar transform is applied to
each column [22]. This second transformation produces detail coefficients in both the horizontal and
vertical directions, leaving a single approximation coefficient that represents the overall average of the
image. To obtain a multiresolution representation, the decomposition process is recursively applied only to
the sub-band containing the approximation coefficients until the desired decomposition level is reached.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the final transformed matrix contains a large number of zero-valued entries. From
this transformed matrix, the original matrix can be easily reconstructed by applying the inverse operations
of averaging and differencing. Therefore, the original image can be recovered from the transformed image
without any loss of information. This process results in a lossless compression of the image.
However, to achieve a higher compression ratio, lossy compression must be applied. In this approach, a
non-negative threshold value, denoted by ε, is selected. Any detail coefficient in the transformed data
whose magnitude is less than or equal to ε is replaced with zero. This increases the number of zero entries
in the transformed matrix, thereby improving the compression level. Hence, when ε = 0, the compression is
lossless. When lossy compression is applied, an approximation of the original image can be reconstructed.

4.2 Discussion
The experimental results demonstrate that the performance of Haar wavelet-based image compression is
strongly influenced by the decomposition level and the selected threshold value. Increasing the number of
wavelet decomposition levels improves the ability of the transform [24,28] to separate significant image
information from less important details. Consequently, more insignificant wavelet coefficients can be
discarded while preserving the essential visual characteristics of the reconstructed image. This
multiresolution property enables efficient image compression with acceptable reconstruction quality.
Compression in the proposed method is achieved through wavelet coefficient thresholding [24]. Three
thresholding techniques; hard thresholding, soft thresholding, and universal thresholding, were investigated
to determine their influence on compression performance. Coefficients with magnitudes below a predefined
threshold are removed, thereby reducing the amount of information required to represent the image. As the
threshold increases, the compression ratio improves because more coefficients are discarded. However,
17
excessive thresholding [24,28] also removes useful image information, resulting in reduced reconstruction
quality.
The proposed compression algorithm was evaluated under two operating conditions. In the first mode, the
compression ratio (CR) was specified, and the threshold value was adjusted until the desired compression
ratio was achieved. The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) of the reconstructed image was then calculated
to assess image quality. In the second mode, the desired PSNR was fixed, and the threshold value was
varied until the required image quality was obtained, after which the corresponding compression ratio was
determined. These two evaluation modes provide insight into the trade-off between compression efficiency
and reconstructed image quality.
The experimental observations indicate that increasing the decomposition level generally improves image
quality [28] because the wavelet transform provides a more effective separation of low-frequency image
content from high-frequency details. Nevertheless, additional decomposition levels increase computational
complexity and reduce coding efficiency. Furthermore, PSNR tends to reach a saturation point beyond a
certain decomposition level, indicating that further decomposition provides only marginal improvements in
reconstructed image quality. For each compression ratio, an optimal decomposition level exists at which
PSNR reaches its maximum value. Beyond this point, image quality gradually deteriorates due to the loss
of important wavelet coefficients. These findings suggest that adaptive selection of the decomposition level
is essential for achieving an appropriate balance between compression performance and computational cost.
Compression performance was primarily evaluated using objective quality metrics, including Compression
Ratio (CR), Mean Square Error (MSE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Picture Quality Scale
(PQS). Although MSE and PSNR remain the most widely used quantitative measures because of their
computational simplicity, they do not always correlate well with perceived visual quality [26,27]. Human
observers evaluate reconstructed images [27] based on characteristics such as edge preservation, texture
continuity, smoothness, and the visibility of compression artifacts rather than on pixel-wise differences
alone. Consequently, perceptual quality assessment methods based on characteristics of the Human Visual
System (HVS), including Mean Opinion Score (MOS) and Picture Quality Scale (PQS), provide
complementary information regarding the visual fidelity of compressed images. The encouraging PQS
results obtained in this study indicate that the proposed Haar wavelet compression method preserves
perceptually important image information while achieving satisfactory compression performance.
The proposed framework can also be extended to color image compression [29]. Instead of processing
RGB components directly, the image may first be transformed into the YIQ or a similar luminance–
chrominance color space. Since the Human Visual System is considerably more sensitive to luminance
variations [29,30] than chrominance information, larger compression errors can be tolerated in the
chrominance channels without noticeably affecting perceived image quality. This approach can further
improve compression efficiency while maintaining satisfactory visual appearance.
18
So, the experimental results confirm that the Haar wavelet provides an effective and computationally
efficient solution for two-dimensional image compression. The proposed method achieves a favorable
balance between compression ratio and reconstructed image quality while maintaining relatively low
computational complexity. This makes the approach suitable for applications requiring efficient image
storage and transmission, including medical imaging, multimedia systems, and web-based image services.
Despite these promising results, several opportunities exist for further improvement. Future research may
investigate adaptive threshold selection strategies that automatically determine the optimal threshold value
according to image characteristics. In addition, adaptive decomposition level selection could improve the
balance between computational complexity and compression performance.
5. Conclusions
This study presented a two-dimensional (2D) image compression method based on the Haar Discrete
Wavelet Transform (DWT). The proposed approach exploits the multiresolution characteristics of the Haar
wavelet to decompose an image into approximation and detail coefficients, enabling efficient data
reduction while preserving important visual information. The reconstructed images were evaluated using
widely accepted performance metrics, demonstrating that the proposed method achieves effective
compression while maintaining acceptable reconstructed image quality.
The experimental analysis indicates that compression performance is strongly influenced by the
decomposition level and the selected threshold value. Higher decomposition levels generally improve the
separation of significant and insignificant wavelet coefficients, resulting in better image quality. However,
increasing the decomposition level also raises computational complexity and eventually yields diminishing
improvements in reconstruction quality. These observations highlight the importance of selecting
appropriate decomposition levels and threshold values to achieve a practical balance between compression
efficiency and visual fidelity.
The computational complexity and efficient reconstruction capability of the Haar wavelet make it a suitable
choice for applications requiring fast image compression, including multimedia systems, web-based image
transmission, and medical image storage. Although more advanced wavelet families may achieve higher
compression efficiency, the Haar wavelet remains an attractive solution for resource-constrained
environments because of its ease of implementation and computational efficiency.
Additional research should investigate adaptive decomposition level selection, comparative evaluations
with other wavelet families and the incorporation of more advanced perceptual image quality assessment
metrics. Furthermore, validating the proposed method on larger benchmark datasets and applicationspecific image collections, particularly in medical imaging, will provide a more comprehensive assessment
of its effectiveness and practical applicability.
