Quantitative photoacoustic tomography faces challenges arising from ill-posedness caused by acoustic heterogeneity and multi-parameter coupling. We propose the forward-backward splitting diffusion network (FBS-DiffNet), to simultaneously reconstruct optical absorption coefficient and sound speed distributions. This framework unfolds the classical forward-backward splitting algorithm into a cascaded architecture. Each level integrates a data consistency module driven by a residual gradient step network and a generative joint diffusion module. This configuration ensures physical fidelity to measured signals while exploiting spatial correlations between parameters at anatomical boundaries to provide synergistic regularization. Experimental results on simulation, phantom, and in vivo datasets demonstrate that FBS-DiffNet surpasses uniform sound speed methods and prominent sound speed estimation techniques such as K-means-GMM or NF-APACT concerning both visual quality and quantitative metrics. Ablation studies verify the contribution of each key component to these results. By combining physical interpretability with deep learning representational capacity, FBS-DiffNet offers a promising approach for high-precision quantitative photoacoustic tomography.

Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) integrates the high contrast of optical imaging with the high spatial resolution of ultrasound [1]. Short-pulsed laser illumination induces thermoelastic expansion in biological tissues, generating broadband photoacoustic waves. Reconstruction algorithms process these signals to resolve tissue properties, revealing morphological and functional characteristics [2].
The PAT inverse problem comprises acoustic and optical components. The acoustic component reconstructs the initial pressure or absorbed energy distribution [3] using techniques such as time reversal [4] or backprojection [5]. The optical component, known as quantitative PAT (qPAT), aims to recover functional parameters like the optical absorption coefficient (OAC), scattering coefficient, and chromophore concentration [6,7].
Model-based reconstruction is the standard approach for qPAT [8]. It employs a coupled optical-acoustic forward model and iteratively minimizes the discrepancy between measured and simulated signals [9,10]. However, this approach faces challenges regarding model fidelity, discretization, ill-posedness, and computational efficiency. A common simplification assumes spatially homogeneous acoustic properties [11], yet biological tissues are inherently heterogeneous. This heterogeneity perturbs wave propagation, causing phase mismatches and structural artifacts when a constant speed of sound (SoS) is assumed [12,13]. A more effective approach is to reconstruct spatially varying SoS distributions. For example, K-means-GMM optimizes the SoS via gradient-based algorithms after region segmentation [14]. Neural fields for adaptive photoacoustic computed tomography (NF-APACT) use multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) to represent the SoS field implicitly, optimizing it through a differentiable forward physical model in a self-supervised framework [15].
Conventional regularization has limitations. Tikhonov regularization (ℓ2 norm) suppresses noise but blurs details [16], while total variation (TV) preserves edges but often over-smooths weak gradients [9]. Deep implicit priors, such as plug-and-play alternating direction method of multipliers (PnP-ADMM) [17], decouple the forward model from the prior using deep denoisers.
Multiparameter joint reconstruction addresses ill-posedness [18,19] but introduces non-linear, non-convex optimization challenges [20,21]. Early alternating iterative frameworks were sensitive to initialization and prone to local optima [22,23]. While constraints such as support or boundary markers improve stability [24], they impose high computational costs. Recent iterative learning frameworks embed physical models into neural networks to alternate between data consistency and parameter updates [25]. However, they often struggle with parameter coupling and reliance on accurate priors. Other robust frameworks incorporate uncertainty modeling [26], but their performance degrades when actual error patterns deviate from assumptions.
To address these limitations, we propose the forward-backward splitting diffusion network (FBS-DiffNet) for joint OAC and SoS reconstruction. This framework is designed to overcome the rigid step sizes of conventional iterative algorithms, the lack of physical constraints in purely data-driven methods, and the systematic biases inherent in uniform SoS assumptions. FBS-DiffNet integrates a deep unfolding architecture to enable physically interpretable, end-to-end learning, while incorporating a residual gradient step network (RGSN) for adaptive step-size adjustment. Furthermore, the method employs a multi-channel joint diffusion model as a generative implicit prior, exploiting spatial correlations between OAC and SoS to achieve synergistic regularization.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 details the FBS-DiffNet formulation. Section 3 describes the experimental design. Section 4 presents the results. Section 5 discusses physical implications and limitations, and Section 6 concludes the paper.

2.1.2.FBS solution
The FBS algorithm is a classical method for composite optimization [27] that alternates between a forward gradient descent step on the smooth data fidelity term and a backward proximal mapping step on the non-smooth regularization term. Although originally established for convex problems, we adapt the FBS framework to the non-convex joint OAC-SoS reconstruction in Eq. (1). This approach is selected for its physical interpretability and modularity, with its effectiveness in this non-convex setting empirically validated in Section 4.
In our framework, the regularization term is implicitly defined by a deep generative model, which serves as an implicit prior replacing hand-crafted functions. By providing updates analogous to proximal mapping, the generative model overcomes the representational limits of explicit priors and achieves a synergy between physics and learning. Furthermore, the FBS framework naturally handles multi-parameter coupling. The forward step calculates gradients with parameter-specific step sizes, while the backward step employs a multi-channel joint model to impose coordinated constraints, allowing OAC and SoS to guide each other iteratively. This transparent structure ensures data consistency and prior adherence, facilitating the integration of complex physical models.

2.1.3.Nonlinear unrolling and mapping of generative priors
Traditional FBS algorithms require many iterations and are sensitive to manual step-size tuning and proximal operator design [28]. These fixed strategies lack adaptability and preclude end-to-end optimization [29]. We address this by unfolding the iterations into a K-layer network, which truncates steps to K and replaces manual parameters with learnable components [30] for end-to-end joint optimization.
The forward step is parameterized as a data consistency module. To handle the high dynamic range and numerical oscillations inherent in photoacoustic inverse problems, we replace the fixed step size with a RGSN. The RGSN adaptively predicts the update magnitude based on the current estimate

From a Bayesian perspective, the proximal update is equivalent to maximum a posteriori (MAP) denoising under Gaussian noise assumption, where

Therefore, we replace the manual proximal operator with a pre-trained multi-channel joint diffusion model. Its single-step denoising output functions analogously to a proximal operator, with the diffusion timestep acting as the regularization parameter. This correspondence holds provided the diffusion model accurately represents the data manifold, the noise is approximately Gaussian, and the timestep matches the actual noise level. By pre-training on biological tissue data, the diffusion model captures complex, high-dimensional features that hand-crafted priors cannot and the intrinsic spatial correlations between OAC and SoS, thereby enforcing structured regularization without explicit functional forms.

2.2.Network architecture

Each layer integrates a data consistency module for adaptive gradient updates and a joint diffusion module for generative regularization.

2.2.1.Data consistency module
This module computes the physical gradient update. It accepts a four-channel tensor formed by concatenating the current estimate
uk
and the data fidelity gradient
∇D(uk)
. It outputs an intermediate variable
vk=uk+Δuk
, where
Δuk
is a two-channel residual update. This variable is then passed to the joint diffusion module. The architecture follows an encoder-decoder design to extract multi-scale features and recover spatial details.
The encoder comprises an initial 3 × 3 convolution (mapping to 32 channels) and three downsampling blocks. Each block utilizes dual 3 × 3 convolutions with group normalization (GN) and ReLU activation, followed by 2 × 2 max-pooling with stride 2. This progressively halves spatial resolution while increasing channels (32 to 256) to capture global semantics. The decoder restores resolution using bilinear interpolation followed by 3 × 3 convolutions, avoiding the checkerboard artifacts associated with transposed convolutions (TC). Skip connections fuse high-frequency encoder details with decoder semantics. A final 1 × 1 convolution yields a two-channel residual update.
The module employs a residual learning strategy, predicting the correction term rather than the absolute value. This ensures the update remains consistent with the physical gradient direction, reducing optimization difficulty and providing a physically guided initialization for the subsequent diffusion module.
2.2.2.Joint diffusion module
This module performs single-step joint denoising and regularization on the intermediate variable from the data consistency module. A single step per layer instead of full iterative sampling suffices because the intermediate variable is already a structured estimate close to the data manifold and requires only refinement. In addition, the cascaded K layers provide cumulative refinement across iterations. The module utilizes a time-conditioned U-Net architecture comprising timestep embedding, encoder, bottleneck, and decoder, with adaptive group normalization (AdaGN) and self-attention mechanisms for multi-parameter feature representation.
The timestep embedding maps the scalar timestep to deep features that encode noise intensity. Because noise levels vary across the diffusion process, the model adapts its denoising behavior based on the current timestep. Sinusoidal position embeddings first map the timestep to a high-dimensional continuous vector. This vector then passes through a MLP with linear layers and Sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activations, expanding its dimension to four times the base channel count to produce the timestep embedding vector
temb
. This vector is injected into all levels of the encoder, bottleneck, and decoder to guide the model during different denoising stages.
The encoder extracts multi-scale semantic features through progressive downsampling. The input passes through a 3 × 3 convolution to map the channel count to a base dimension of 128. Four downsampling levels are built with channel multipliers of 1, 2, 4, and 8. Each level first applies an AdaGN residual block, which dynamically adjusts scaling and shift parameters based on
temb
, fusing temporal and spatial features. In deeper layers (512 or 1024 channels), self-attention blocks capture global structural correlations, facilitating feature decoupling between OAC and SoS. Downsampling is performed by a 3 × 3 convolution with stride 2, halving the spatial resolution. Before each downsampling operation, the current feature maps are stored in a skip-connection stack for later reuse.
The bottleneck processes the compressed semantic features using a three-stage structure: AdaGN residual block, self-attention block, and AdaGN residual block. This preserves resolution and channel count while adding global context and reinforcing timestep constraints.
The decoder uses a symmetric structure, performing upsampling in the reverse order of the channel multipliers to restore spatial resolution and structural details. Each upsampling layer applies bilinear interpolation followed by a 3 × 3 convolution, expanding spatial size while halving the channel count. The upsampled features are concatenated with encoder skip connections, combining shallow high-frequency details with deep low-frequency semantics. The concatenated features pass through an AdaGN residual block for nonlinear mapping. In high-channel layers, self-attention is re-applied to strengthen global modeling, and timestep conditions are re-injected via
temb
. After four upsampling levels, the feature maps return to the original input resolution.
The final layer applies GN and SiLU activation, followed by a 3 × 3 convolution to output the noise components for the given timestep.
3.Experimental design and datasets3.1.Datasets
We constructed three complementary datasets. The simulation dataset provides ground-truth reference for quantitative validation. The phantom dataset offers a transitional platform with known material properties. Separate models were trained on simulation and phantom data with identical architectures but independent parameters. The in vivo dataset, lacking ground truth, was used solely for testing generalization.
3.1.1.Simulation dataset
Numerical mouse phantoms were sliced at 0.3 mm intervals, with optical and acoustic properties assigned based on histological references [33,34] (Table S1). Photon propagation was modeled using Monte Carlo simulations with 107 photons per pulse to derive optical fluence, followed by the k-space pseudo-spectral method [35] to solve the wave equation.
The resulting 200 original samples (256 × 256 pixels) represent independent anatomies from different numerical mouse phantoms and slice positions rather than adjacent slices from the same phantom. The samples were randomly shuffled and partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio. To prevent overfitting, the training set was augmented to 2,000 samples via random rotation, flipping, and scaling. Augmentation operations were applied exclusively to the training set, ensuring that no augmented version of any original sample appeared in the validation or test sets.
The ground-truth OAC and SoS distributions in each slice, denoted as
u=[μa,c]T
, serve as the target for pre-training the joint diffusion module. For end-to-end training, 500 augmented samples were randomly selected per epoch. The network input consisted of initial uniform estimates (SoS: 1500 m/s, OAC: 0.01 cm–1) concatenated with physical gradients computed via the adjoint-state method. The network was trained to map these inputs to the ground-truth distributions, learning the convergence path through multi-layer unrolling.
3.1.2.Experimental phantom dataset
Cylindrical phantoms (20 mm diameter, 80 mm height) were fabricated from agar, gelatin, and emulsifiers. Inclusions with varying quantities, positions, and materials were embedded to simulate absorbers with varying optical properties. The phantoms were scanned along the longitudinal axis using an MSOT inVision 256 system (iThera Medical GmbH, Munich, Germany). Data acquisition employed a tunable Nd:YAG laser (680–900 nm, 7 ns pulse) and a 256-element circular ultrasound array (5 MHz center frequency).
A total of 412 slices from 14 phantoms were collected. To ensure rigorous evaluation and prevent data leakage, we employed leave-one-phantom-out cross-validation. In each fold, slices from one phantom were held out as the test set, and the remaining 13 phantoms were used for training and validation. This process was repeated 14 times, with each phantom serving as the test set once. All reported results are the average over the 14 folds. Ground-truth maps were established based on nominal design values and material formulations [36–38] (Table S2) rather than absolute physical measurements. Consistent with the simulation protocol, data augmentation was applied solely to the training folds. Inputs for FBS-DiffNet were constructed by concatenating uniform initial property estimates with their corresponding data fidelity gradients.
3.1.3.In vivo dataset
In vivo data were acquired from six male BALB/c nude mice (age: 6–8 weeks, weight: 18–22 g) using an MSOT inVision 128 system (730 wavelength, 0.3 mm step size). Animal protocols adhered to national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Experiment Management and Supervision Committee of the Chinese Ministry of Health. During imaging, mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane in oxygen (flow rate 1 L/min). Vital signs were monitored, and body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C throughout the imaging of 394 slices.
Given the absence of ground-truth OAC and SoS maps in live tissues, this dataset was used exclusively to evaluate the reconstruction fidelity and generalization capability of the trained FBS-DiffNet model against baseline methods.

3.2.2.Baseline methods and ablation study design
To evaluate FBS-DiffNet, we compared it against state-of-the-art algorithms: PnP-ADMM [17] for OAC reconstruction, and K-means-GMM [14] and NF-APACT [15] for SoS reconstruction. All methods utilized identical datasets. We derived training configurations from the original studies for PnP-ADMM and NF-APACT. K-means-GMM hyperparameters were optimized via grid search on the validation set.
Eight ablation studies assessed architectural design, prior modeling, optimization strategies, loss functions, and the joint reconstruction strategy. Each variant modifies only the target component:
Cascaded Layers. Compared the default K = 3 with K = 2 and K = 4 to balance precision, convergence speed, and computational cost.
Up-sampling Strategy. Compared the default bilinear interpolation with convolution (BI+Conv) against TC to evaluate impacts on resolution and artifact suppression.
RGSN. Replaced the RGSN module with a fixed scalar step size to evaluate its capability in handling numerical oscillations in physical gradients.
Gradient Input. Modified the data consistency module to rely solely on parameter estimates, excluding explicit gradients, to clarify the importance of optimization direction.
Generative Prior. Removed the multi-channel joint diffusion model to assess its role in noise suppression, structural fidelity, and constraining the solution space.
Joint Parameter Modeling. Replaced the joint diffusion module with two independent single-channel models to assess the benefit of cross-parameter spatial correlations.
Loss Function. Compared hybrid loss against ℓ1-only and SSIM-only to evaluate the trade-off between pixel-wise accuracy and structural fidelity.
Joint Reconstruction Strategy. Conducted an oracle-SoS experiment with true SoS inputs to isolate the impact of SoS estimation and quantify performance degradation.
3.3.Performance evaluation3.3.1.Evaluation metrics
Reconstruction accuracy for simulation and phantom data was assessed using PSNR, SSIM [39], and mean relative error (MRE). For in vivo data lacking ground truth, we used gray-level-difference contrast (CR) [40] and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) to evaluate image quality. Mathematical definitions are provided in Section S1 of the
Supplement 1.
Statistical significance was assessed via a Friedman test followed by a Nemenyi post-hoc test. For each dataset, 20 test samples were randomly selected (fixed seed 2024). Under a fixed pairing strategy, methods were ranked per sample to calculate average ranks, testing the null hypothesis of equivalent performance at
α=0.05
. The Nemenyi test controlled the family-wise error rate via critical difference (CD). For the in vivo dataset, a hierarchical analysis with the animal subject as a random effect accounted for inter-slice correlations.
3.3.2.Experimental environment configuration
Simulations and K-means-GMM were implemented in MATLAB R2024a with Parallel Computing Toolbox and Intel MKL. Deep learning models (FBS-DiffNet, NF-APACT, and PnP-ADMM) were developed in Python 3.9 using PyTorch 1.12.1. Experiments ran on an Ubuntu 16.04 LTS workstation with an NVIDIA Tesla P100-SXM2 GPU. FBS-DiffNet has approximately 40.9 million parameters (joint diffusion: 32.8 M; data consistency: 2.7 M). Inference time excluded pre- and post-processing; stopping criteria followed the original literature for each method.

As shown in Fig. 7(a), acoustic heterogeneity causes phase distortions that blur tissue boundaries and introduce artifacts in initial pressure maps. By simultaneously estimating the SoS distribution, FBS-DiffNet compensates for these propagation path distortions, yielding clearer anatomical structures in the reconstructed OAC maps. 4.5.2.Generative prior and joint modeling
Generative Prior. Removing the diffusion module causes noise residue and structural distortion (Fig. 10(c)). The full architecture achieves superior noise suppression and clarity. Without the prior, PSNR drops by 13.4% in OAC and 14.6% in SoS (Table 4), confirming its necessity for high-fidelity reconstruction.
Joint Modeling. The independent configuration processes parameters separately, leading to boundary misalignment (Fig. 10(c)). The joint diffusion model enforces spatial consistency via inter-parameter correlations, outperforming the independent model by 13.9% in OAC PSNR and 17.1% in SoS PSNR (Table 4), demonstrating the efficacy of joint modeling.
4.5.3.Loss functions
ℓ1 loss ensures numerical precision but smooths fine textures; conversely, SSIM loss sharpens boundaries but introduces local numerical deviations (Fig. 10(c)). The hybrid loss integrates both strengths, preserving structural clarity without sacrificing pixel-level accuracy. It improves OAC PSNR by 11.9% over ℓ1-only and 20.2% over SSIM-only (Table 4), validating their complementarity.
4.5.4.Joint reconstruction strategy
To isolate the contribution of SoS correction, we conducted an oracle-SoS experiment on the simulation test set, reconstructing OAC only with the true SoS as input. As shown in Table 4, oracle-SoS improves OAC PSNR by 4.5%, SSIM by 0.7%, and reduces MRE by 10.0% relative to joint reconstruction. The gap reflects the cost of estimating SoS from data, while the gap between joint reconstruction and PnP-ADMM highlights the combined benefits of the network architecture and diffusion prior. The oracle-SoS result serves as a diagnostic reference, not a practical upper bound.

5.Discussion5.1.Superiority of dual parameter joint reconstruction
FBS-DiffNet achieves high-fidelity
joint reconstruction of OAC and SoS. In acoustically heterogeneous regions, it outperforms PnP-ADMM which assumes uniform SoS by iteratively updating the SoS field. This ensures accurate wave propagation modeling, reduces systematic biases, and improves phase alignment for OAC inversion. For SoS reconstruction, FBS-DiffNet overcomes the blocky artifacts of K-means-GMM and the global softening of NF-APACT, producing maps with natural boundaries and precise anatomical alignment. These results confirm that joint reconstruction effectively decouples acoustic heterogeneity interference and ensures physical consistency.
5.2.Advantages of the diffusion based generative prior
Integrating a pre-trained multi-channel joint diffusion model as a generative prior eliminates the oversmoothing associated with hand-crafted regularization. Ablation studies confirm its critical role in noise suppression and structural fidelity. This prior offers two key advantages: joint modeling enables shared feature representations for synergistic denoising and edge preservation, yielding higher contrast and fewer artifacts in vivo; and it effectively removes noise deviating from learned tissue distributions, balancing noise suppression with detail retention.
5.3.Role of deep unfolding and adaptive optimization
FBS-DiffNet employs a deep unfolding architecture that maps FBS iterations into cascaded layers, preserving interpretability through explicit data consistency and proximal steps. The RGSN module replaces fixed step sizes with an adaptive, data-driven strategy, resolving numerical oscillations and accelerating convergence. Ablation confirms that incorporating physical gradients is essential for valid solutions. Computationally, limiting diffusion to a single denoising step per layer (with
K=3
) achieves an optimal balance between fidelity and cost, yielding an average inference time of 42.6 seconds per slice.
5.4.Comparison with existing joint reconstruction and deep unfolding methods
FBS-DiffNet integrates FBS-based deep unrolling and joint reconstruction to address physical complexities in heterogeneous tissues. Unlike PnP-ADMM [17], which relies on uniform SoS assumptions and single-channel denoising, our method employs a multi-channel joint diffusion prior to capture OAC and SoS spatial co-occurrence, enabling synergistic error correction. While recent joint estimation methods [23,24] improve stability, they remain constrained by hand-crafted regularizers and fixed step sizes within classical iterative optimization paradigms. FBS-DiffNet overcomes these limitations through a learnable gradient update network and a generative diffusion prior, facilitating end-to-end data-driven adaptation.
This work advances beyond our previous physics-constrained framework [25] by incorporating SoS as an optimization variable, rather than assuming uniformity. Unlike learned iterative methods focused on measurement noise [26], our approach targets systematic structural errors caused by acoustic heterogeneity. Furthermore, we replace the manually designed proximal operators of classical FBS [27] with a learnable diffusion prior. Collectively, FBS-DiffNet combines physics-preserving unfolding, adaptive gradient bias compensation, and joint generative priors to address challenges where existing approaches fall short.
5.5.Limitations and future work
Despite promising results, several limitations warrant future investigation. Extending this framework to 3D volumetric reconstruction is currently hindered by high computational costs. Future work will explore model compression techniques, such as knowledge distillation and pruning, to address these bottlenecks. Additionally, while the current method focuses on single-wavelength data, integrating multi-spectral excitation is a priority to enable quantitative functional imaging (e.g., blood oxygen saturation). Inference speed also requires optimization; incorporating accelerated sampling mechanisms like denoising diffusion implicit models could facilitate near real-time imaging.
Furthermore, the current framework assumes constant optical scattering coefficients and Grüneisen parameters. While common in current studies [44,45], this simplification may introduce biases in heterogeneous or temperature-dependent tissues. Future work should incorporate wavelength-dependent scattering models and temperature-aware Grüneisen priors. Regarding phantom experiments, ground-truth values were derived from material formulations and literature references rather than independent measurements. Fabrication tolerances, temperature variations, material homogeneity, and wavelength-dependent effects may introduce deviations from the nominal values. Without additional measurements, a quantitative uncertainty analysis cannot be performed. Consequently, reported metrics should be interpreted relative to design values rather than as absolute physical validations. Future studies must employ independent validation techniques, such as photoacoustic spectroscopy or ultrasonic transmission, to ensure reliable ground-truth characterization.
Finally, the generalization capability of FBS-DiffNet requires validation across diverse clinical scenarios. The lack of ground-truth OAC and SoS maps in in vivo experiments currently restricts conclusions to qualitative plausibility, and the diffusion prior may bias estimates toward learned distributions. Future work will prioritize independent validation methods, including calibrated phantoms, ultrasound transmission, or controlled ex vivo measurements, to quantify in vivo reconstruction accuracy. Clinical translation will necessitate large-scale validation on independent in vivo cohorts, systematic comparison with histology, and adaptation to clinical imaging systems.
6.Conclusions
This paper proposes FBS-DiffNet, a physics-informed deep unfolding framework for the joint reconstruction of OAC and SoS distributions in PAT. By integrating the FBS algorithm with a joint diffusion model, this method improves the reconstruction accuracy of OAC in acoustically heterogeneous media. Compared to independent diffusion models, the proposed multi-channel joint diffusion prior leverages shared feature representations to achieve superior noise suppression and edge preservation. Furthermore, the RGSN adaptively optimizes physical inversion convergence, yielding higher reconstruction precision than fixed-step iterative methods. Validated on synthetic, phantom, and in vivo datasets, FBS-DiffNet demonstrates consistent generalization and competitive quantitative performance. These results highlight its potential for quantitative PAT, with future work focusing on 3D volumetric reconstruction and multi-spectral excitation to advance functional imaging and clinical translation.
