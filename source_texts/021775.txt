# Splat Regression Models

Mara Daniels MIT Mathematics maradan@mit.edu

Philippe Rigollet MIT Mathematics rigollet@mit.edu

#### Abstract

We introduce a highly expressive class of function approximators called Splat Regression Models. Model outputs are mixtures of heterogeneous and anisotropic bump functions, termed splats, each weighted by an output vector. The power of splat modeling lies in its ability to locally adjust the scale and direction of each splat, achieving both high interpretability and accuracy. Fitting splat models reduces to optimization over the space of mixing measures, which can be implemented using Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient flows. As a byproduct, we recover the popular Gaussian Splatting methodology as a special case, providing a unified theoretical framework for this state-of-the-art technique that clearly disambiguates the inverse problem, the model, and the optimization algorithm. Through numerical experiments, we demonstrate that the resulting models and algorithms constitute a flexible and promising approach for solving diverse approximation, estimation, and inverse problems involving low-dimensional data.

# 1 Introduction

In the recent history of the deep machine learning discipline, certain problem areas have enjoyed "inflection points" wherein the attainable performance and scale have rapidly improved by many orders of magnitude. These inflection points are often directly coupled to the discovery of the right model architecture for the problem at hand. To name a few examples: deep convolutional networks and ResNets for image classification [KSH12, HZRS16]; the U-Net architecture for image segmentation and generation [RFB15, SE19]; and the transformer architecture for language modeling [VSP+17]. This motivates the search for new parsimonious architectures for different problem domains, and in this work, we target low-dimensional modeling problems such as those lying at the intersection of computational science and machine learning.

We introduce a new candidate that we call the 'Splat Regression Model.' In its simplest form, the model can be written as,

| $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^k v_i \mathcal{N}(x; b_i, A_i A_i^T)$ | $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^p,$ | $b_i \in \mathbb{R}^d,$ | $A_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ |
|----------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------|-------------------------|-----------------------------------|
|                                                          |                         |                         |                                   |

where N (x; µ, Σ) is the Gaussian density function. This rather simple architecture can be seen as a two-layer neural network with an atypical activation function, or alternatively, as a generalization of the classical Nadaraya-Watson estimator for nonparametric regression to use heterogeneous mixture weights. Toward the goal of developing a general theory for these heterogeneous mixture models, we abstract them into the form fµ(x) := Ev∼µx [v], where µx are conditionals of a probability distribution (x, v) ∼ µ. We develop a theoretical framework for optimizing these functions and understanding their expressivity. We further demonstrate that the splat regression model is a performant architecture

for low dimensional approximation, regression, and physics-informed fitting problems, often vastly outperforming Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) [LWV+25] and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) models in architecture size, generalization error, and convergence speed during training.

Another major success story of large-scale machine learning is in the computer graphics literature on solving Novel View Synthesis, where the goal is to learn a 3D scene from 2D snapshots annotated by camera position. A major breakthrough came with the introduction of Neural Radiance Fields [MST+21a]. A few years later, the so-called "3D Gaussian Splatting" methodology took over as a premier architecture for graphics and reconstruction problems. There are parallels between developments in Novel View Synthesis and recent progress in physics-informed learning, which has arguably not yet enjoyed its inflection point. An essential component in NeRF modeling is the use of sinusoidal positional encoding, which drastically improves the modeling capabilities across different 'scales' of variation in the target modeling problem, and which is paralleled by the use of positional encoding schemes in physics informed PDE-fitting [TSM+20, ZBG24], to moderate success. In both cases, rendering solutions by pointwise evaluation of an MLP across the spatial domain can be prohibitively slow. Recognizing that Novel View Synthesis is inherently a spatial inverse problem, a major goal of our work is to replicate the successes of 3D Gaussian Splatting across a wide variety of physical modeling and inverse problems. We intend for this work to provide an instruction manual for deploying and training splat regression models in these settings. Our main contributions are the following.

- 1. We introduce the Splat Regression Model. We prove some preliminary structural theorems about the it and we show that it is a universal approximator.
- 2. We develop principled optimization algorithms for gradient-based training, which are crucial to our empirical success. To do this, we leverage an interpretation of the splat model parameters as a hierarchical 'distribution over distributions' and we invoke the theory of Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient flows to compute gradient updates in parameter space.
- 3. We recover 3D Gaussian Splatting as an instance of splat regression modeling. In Example 2, we detail a clean formulation of it, where different aspects of the pipeline are split into clear, 'modular' parts.
- 4. We test the performance of splat modeling in a few representative modeling problems including low-dimensional regression (Figure 2\) and physics-informed fitting.

## 2 Related Work

Our work lies at the intersection of a few different areas, such as computer graphics, deep machine learning, particle methods in optimization, and the theory of gradient flows on measure spaces. We discuss some of the connections here.

Novel View Synthesis. The Novel View Synthesis problem in computer graphics has a long history. Input data is typically sourced using Structure From Motion (SfM) methods [Ull79, OVBS17]. In the idealized setting, scenes are rendered using the Radiative Transfer Equation [Cha13], which governs how light reaches a camera as it is emitted from sources in a scene. Much work has gone into developing fast approximations to the RTE, such as the widely used α-blending technique introduced in [PD84, Car84]. For 3D scene representation, [ZPBG02a] introduced the "elliptical weighted average" method, although these ideas were not applied at scale for about two decades, with the Gaussian Splatting methodology being introduced by [KKLD23a]. Physics-informed Modeling. Following the introduction of PINNs [RPK19], much work has gone into developing ML methods for solving computational science problems. As documented by [KGZ+21], PINN training can have surprising failure modes and requires careful tuning and hand-crafted architectures. More recently, adding positional encoding schemes [TSM+20, HAS21] through either RBF or sinusoidal encoding was shown to improve performance. Alternatively, the Kolmogorov-Arnold Network architecture [LWV+25, RP25] was introduced as a competitor, and some comparisons between KAN and RBF interpolation methods are explored in [Li24]. One way to view splat regression modeling is as learning an 'adaptive interpolation grid,' and our experimental results suggest that 'smart positional encoding is all you need' for low-dimensional modeling problems.

Mean-field Theory of Two-layer Networks. Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient flows can be used to study the optimization of two-layer ReLU neural networks. This approach was pioneered by [CB18], who prove a conditional global convergence result for two-layer network optimization. Further work in this direction includes [MMM19a, MMM19b]. The main difference between optimizing ReLU networks and Splat models is that our model has a fundamentally different geometry of its parameter space, since first-layer output neurons are represented as elements of a Bures-Wasserstein manifold. This is also a major difference between our work and [CRY25], which introduces a modified ReLU

network architecture whose neurons can be 'superpositions' of ReLU functions.

### 3 Splat Regression Models

We now state a few essential definitions and basic properties of splat regression models. Readers who are uninterested in the abstract formulation may skip to \(1\), which is the concrete object that appears in algorithms. In Example 1, we discuss how to fit these models my minimizing loss on a training dataset.

We write L s π (R d ; R p ) to denote the functions f : R d → R p for which R ∥f(x)∥ s s π(dx) < ∞, where π is a measure on R d . When s = 2, we endow it with the inner product ⟨f, g⟩L2 π = R ⟨f(x), g(x)⟩ π(dx). We write C s b (R d ) to denote the real-valued bounded s-times continuously differentiable functions on R d . For a function ϕ : R d → R, we write ∇ϕ(x) its gradient, ∇2 P ϕ(x) its Hessian, and ∆ϕ(x) = d i=1 ∂ 2 xi ϕ(x) its Laplacian. For ψ : R d → R p we write Dψ(x) its Jacobian and (when p = d) we write div ψ(x) = Pd i=1 ∂xiψ (i) (x) its divergence. We denote by P(Ω) the space of probability distributions on Ω, by Ps(Ω) the distributions with s ≥ 1 finite moments, and by Pac(Ω) the distributions with a density. For µ ∈ P(Ω) we write EX∼µ[f(x)] = R f(x) µ(dx) and, if µ has a density, we denote it as µ(x) : Ω → R. Last, we denote by T#µ the pushforward of µ, that is, the distribution of T(X) when X ∼ µ. We write id(x) = x the identity map, id#µ = µ.

We begin by defining the family of splat models that we consider in this work. We first define the family of splats and then explain how they are used in splat modeling.

Proposition 1 (Splats are a generalized Bures-Wasserstein manifold). Let ρ ∈ P(R d ) be a centered isotropic mother splat. We denote the set of all splats as,

$$\text{BW}_\rho(\mathbb{R}^d) := \left\{ (A(\cdot) + b)_\# \rho : A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}, b \in \mathbb{R}^d \right\}.$$

Then BWρ(R d ) is a geodesically convex subset of W2(R d ), and on this space the Wasserstein metric reduces to the Bures-Wasserstein metric [Mod16, BJL19],

$$W_2^2(\rho_{A,b}, \rho_{R,s}) = \|b - s\|_2^2 + \|A\|_F^2 + \|R\|_F^2 - 2\|A^T R\|_*$$

where ∥ · ∥F is the Frobenius norm and ∥ · ∥∗ is the nuclear norm.

We give a short, self-contained proof of this proposition in Appendix B.2. We occasionally write ρA,b := (A(·) + b)#ρ when we wish to call attention to the parameters (A, b) of the splat. These parameters can be thought of as a global coordinatization of BWρ(R d ). We discuss further the Wasserstein distance and its associated geometry in Section 3.2.

We envision ρ ∈ BWρ(R d ) as an isotropic 'bump' function and call each individual copy a splat in homage to the computer graphics literature where an instance of this model was first widely popularized. We call ρ the mother splat in analogy to the mother wavelets used in wavelet theory [Mal08]. Splat models are defined as follows.

Definition 1 (Splat Measures and Splat Models). Let ρ ∈ Pac(R d ) have zero mean and identity covariance, and let ρA,b be the distribution of AX +b when X ∼ ρ. We say that µ ∈ P(R p×BWρ(R d )) is a splat measure and the associated splat model fµ(x) is,

$$f_\mu(x) := \mathbb{E}[v\rho_{A,b}(x)] \quad v, \rho_{A,b} \sim \mu.$$

If the support of µ has k < ∞ elements, we say µ is a k-splat measure, fµ is a k-splat model, and we write


$$\begin{aligned} f_{\mu}(x) &= \sum_{i=1}^k v_i \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) |\det A_i^{-1}| \mu(v_i, \rho_{A_i, b_i}) \quad \{(v_i, \rho_{A_i, b_i})\}_{i=1}^k = \text{supp } \mu. \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^k v_i \rho(A_i^{-1}(x - b_i)) |\det A_i^{-1}| \mu(v_i, \rho_{A_i, b_i}) \end{aligned} \quad (1)$$

We write Sp,d := P(R p × BWρ(R d )) (resp. S (k) p,d ) to denote the set of splat measures (resp. k-splat measures).

As we later discuss, Sp,d may be endowed with a natural metric structure, allowing us to perform gradient-based training of k-splat models.

### 3.1 Regularity and Universal Approximation

We note the following basic condition for fµ(x) to be continuosly differentiable.

Proposition 2 (Sufficient conditions for regularity). Let µ ∈ Sp,d and suppose v bbvthat the mother splat ρ has s ≥ 0 bounded derivatives. Then fµ(x) also has s bounded derivatives.

It is also helpful to understand the expressiveness of the class of splat models, and in particular, to understand what kinds of functions can be approximated by k-splat models for finite k. As we show in Appendix A, the finite splat model \(1\) is already in the scope of Cybenko's early result on universal approximation of real-valued functions by two-layer neural networks.

Proposition 3 (Corollary of [Cyb89], Definition 1 and Theorem 1). Let Ω be a compact subset of R d and suppose ρ is a continuous density with marginal ρ1(x1) = R Rd−1 ρ(x1, x2 . . . xd) dx. Then for any f ∈ C 0 b (Ω; R) and ε > 0, there exists a k-splat measure µ (with mother splat ρ) such that

$$\sup_{x \in \Omega} |f(x) - f_\mu(x)| < \varepsilon.$$

We also prove in Appendix A a quantitative bound on the number of splats required to approximate any Lipschitz function on a 'nice' domain using any 'nice' mother splat.

Theorem 3 (Informal – quantitative universal approximation) . For any bounded, 'nice' Ω ⊆ R d , any bounded, Lipschitz f : Ω → R p , and any bounded, Lipschitz mother splat with finite first moment, there exists for each ε > 0 a k-splat measure µ with k ≲ ε −2(d+2) and,

$$\sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f(x)\|_2 < \varepsilon.$$

We find in our experiments that in a variety of low-dimensional modeling problems, one can attain very high quality fits from k ≪ ε −2(d+2) by optimizing the splat parameters, which can be thought of as interpolating the observed data on a learned non-uniform grid.

### 3.2 Geometry of Splat Models

Ignoring some technical caveats, the space P(R d ) of probability distributions can be viewed as a manifold by assigning it a distance metric such as W2(µ, ν), the 2-Wasserstein distance, or H(µ, ν), the Hellinger distance. In both cases the distance induces a metric in the geometric sense, and a tangent space structure, which together can be used to construct gradient-descent-like algorithms over the space of probability distributions. In Wasserstein space, moving between points µ0, µ1 ∈ P2(R d ) is accomplished by applying "infinitesimal" transport maps and the geodesics follow the straight line interpolations of optimal transport maps, µt = (t Tµ0→µ1 + (1 − t)id)#µ0. Under the geometry of the Hellinger metric, known as the Fisher-Rao or Information geometry [Ama83], moving between points is accomplished by "mass teleportation," which means directly scaling the density up or down, pointwise over the domain, in a mass-preserving way. Its geodesics are µt(x) = (αt √µ0(x) + βt √µ1(x))2 , where αt , βt are spherical linear interpolation coordinates, as can be seen from the L 2 form of H2 (µ0, µ1) = R ( √µ0(x)− √µ1(x))2 dx for densities µ0, µ1 ∈ Pac(R d ). Finally, it is also possible to consider the geometry that arises when allowing movements by transport and teleportation at the same time, which is the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao geometry [CNWR25]. We provide a self-contained overview of this theory in Appendix B.

The Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao geometric perspectives are widely used in the design and analysis of probabilistic algorithms, such as for dynamical sampling [CNWR25], variational inference [LCB+22], barycentric interpolation [GPRS19, CMRS20], and lineage tracing [SST+19]. We apply both to design a principled gradient-based training method for splat regression. As a byproduct, we recover the heuristic methods used to optimize Gaussian Splat models, which has a clean interpretation as regularized risk minimization via Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient descent.

Relative to particle methods, lifting to Sp,d allows one to implement 'smoothed particles' on P(R p × R d ), thus providing a computationally tractable way to flow measures that are everywhere positive and absolutely continuous. We comment in Appendix B on the relationship between gradient flows in Sp,d and in P(R p × R d ). This generalizes the concept of 'Gaussian particles' introduced in [LCB+22], because particles are represented by any affine pushforward of an arbitrary density ρ. Such a particle representation was also hinted at in the computer graphics literature on 'volume splatting' [ZPBG02b], and has also appeared in early work on hydrodynamics simulations [GM77].

We endow Sp,d with the Wasserstein and/or Fisher-Rao metric, similar to the 'Wasserstein over Wasserstein' approach taken in [LCB+22, BVK25]. After stating our results, we give Examples 1, 2 that illustrate these objects concretely in two practical settings.

Definition 2 (First variation). Let F : L 2 π (R d ; R p ) → R be a functional. Its first variation (when it exists) is the function δF[f](x) which satisfies for every f, ξ ∈ L2 (R d ; R p ),

$$\partial_\varepsilon \mathcal{F}(f + \varepsilon \xi) \mid_{\varepsilon=0} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \langle \xi(x), \delta \mathcal{F}[f](x) \rangle \pi(dx).$$

It is convenient to express the Wasserstein gradient ∇∇µF(fµ) : R p × BWρ(R d ) → R p × BWρ(R d ) in the global coordinate system (v, A, b) ∼= (v, ρA,b). Written in coordinates, the gradient has the expression ∇∇µF(fµ) = (∇∇v, ∇∇A, ∇∇b)F(fµ), so that the particle dynamics

$$i_t = -\mathbb{W}_{\mathcal{F}}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) \quad \dot{A}_t = -\mathbb{W}_A \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) \quad \dot{b}_t = -\mathbb{W}_B \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b).$$

implement a Wasserstein gradient flow in Sp,d. Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1 (Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient of µ 7→ F(fµ)). Let µ ∈ Sp,d and let F : L 2 π (R d ; R p ) → R be a functional. Assume that ρ has a sub-exponential density. Then the Fisher-Rao gradient is given by,

$$\nabla_{\mu}^{\text{FR}} \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v, A, b) = \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) - \mathbb{E}_{v, A, b \sim \mu} \left[ \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \right].$$

and the Wasserstein gradient is given by,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{W}_v \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= \int \delta \mathcal{F}(x) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \\ \mathbb{W}_A \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle (I_d + \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x)(x - b)^T) A^{-T} \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \\ \mathbb{W}_b \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= - \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \end{aligned}$$

where the argument of δF(·) = δF[f](·) was suppressed.

The condition on ρ is required to apply integration by parts without a boundary term, but when this does not hold (such as when ρ(x) = 1{∥x∥2 ≤ 1}) one can derive the appropriate corrections on a case-by-case basis. We conclude with a few concrete examples of F which are relevant to applications.

Example 1 (Empirical risk minimization). Suppose x1, x2, . . . , xn ∼ U(Ω) are i.i.d. samples, where Ω = [0, 1]d for simplicity, and set yi = f ∗ (xi) with

$$\mathcal{F}(f) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n L(f(x_i), y_i)$$

for a loss function L(y, y ˆ ). The abstract situation is that π = 1 n Pn i=1 δXi and F(f) = ∥f − f ∗∥ 2 L2 π . To calculate the first variation:

$$\partial_{\varepsilon=0}\mathcal{F}(f + \varepsilon\xi) = \partial_{\varepsilon=0} \left\{ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n L(f(x_i) + \varepsilon\xi(x_i), x_i) \right\} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \langle \xi(x_i), \nabla_{\hat{y}} L(f(x_i), x_i) \rangle$$

and so δF[f](xi) = 2 n∇yˆL(f(xi), xi). Note that, in a standard deep learning pipeline, this vector is precisely the error signal vector that is propagated to model parameters through backpropagation. The difference with our setup is that, since we treat model parameters as probability distributions in a (pseudo-)Riemannian manifold, rather than vectors in Euclidean space, the chain rule appears in an unfamiliar form.

To compute the parameter gradients, we plug into the formulas in Theorem 1 to see that the gradients are,

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_{\mu}^{\text{FR}} \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= \frac{2}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \langle \nabla_{\hat{y}} L(f(x_j), x_j), v_i \rangle \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x_j) - \frac{2}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \sum_{l=1}^k \langle \nabla_{\hat{y}} L(f(x_j), x_j), v_l \rangle \rho_{A_l, b_l}(x_j) \\ \mathbb{W}_v \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= \frac{2}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \nabla_{\hat{y}} L(f(x_j), x_j) \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x_j) \\ \mathbb{W}_A \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= \frac{2}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \langle \nabla_{\hat{y}} L(f(x_j), x_j), v_i \rangle (I_d + \nabla_x \log \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x_j)(x_j - b_i)^T) A_i^{-T} \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) \\ \mathbb{W}_b \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= -\frac{2}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x_j), v_i \rangle \nabla_x \log \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x_j) \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x_j).\end{aligned}$$

These formulas can then be input to off-the-shelf gradient update mechanisms like batched SGD or ADAM [KB15].

Example 2 (Inverse problems and physics-informed training). Using a different selection of π, we can also target general purpose spatial estimation problems, such as PDE fitting and modeling for spatial inverse problems. For simplicity, take the domain to be Ω = [0, 1]d , π = U(Ω), and set


$$\mathcal{F}(f) = \frac{1}{2} \|\mathcal{A}[f](x) - g(x)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \quad (2)$$

where A is a known integro-differential operator and g is a known forcing function. In this case the variation is,

$$\begin{aligned} \partial_{\varepsilon=0} \mathcal{F}(f + \varepsilon\xi) &= \partial_{\varepsilon=0} \left\{ \frac{1}{2} \int \|\mathcal{A}[f + \varepsilon\xi](x) - g(x)\|_2^2 \right\} \\ &= \int \langle \mathcal{A}[f](x) - g(x), D_f \mathcal{A}[\xi](x) \rangle dx \\ &= \int \langle (D_f \mathcal{A})^* \mathcal{A}[f](x) - (D_f \mathcal{A})^* [g](x), \xi(x) \rangle dx. \end{aligned}$$

where DfA is the linearization of A at f and (DfA) ∗ is its adjoint. So δF[f] = (DfA) ∗A[f](x) − (DfA) ∗ [g](x). Two illustrative instances of this setup are,

- 1. A 'Physics-informed' splat-based Poisson solver. Take p = 1, A[fµ] = ∆fµ. The gradients are,

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_{\mu}^{\text{FR}} \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= \int_{\Omega} v_i (\Delta f(x) - g(x)) \Delta \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) dx, \\ \mathbb{W}_v \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= \int_{\Omega} (\Delta f(x) - g(x)) \Delta \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) dx, \\ \mathbb{W}_A \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= \int v_i (\Delta f_{\mu}(x) - g(x)) \Delta \{ \nabla \log \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) (x - b_i)^T \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) \} dx, \\ \mathbb{W}_B \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v_i, A_i, b_i) &= - \int_{\Omega} (\Delta f(x) - g(x)) \Delta \{ \nabla \log \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) \Delta \rho_{A_i, b_i}(x) \} dx\end{aligned}$$

In the same way, the coordinates of the Wasserstein gradient can be expressed as integrals with respect to ∆ρ(x) and ∇(∆ρ)(x). For simple ρ, these functions can be precomputed to accelerate gradient computations. When ρ is a member of an exponential family (so that its derivatives are proportional to itself) samples from ρA,b can be used to form a Monte-Carlo gradient estimator.

- 2. Splat regression modeling for Novel View Synthesis (NVS). NVS can be viewed as an inverse problem whose forward operator A is the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) [Cha13], [ZPBG02b, Equation (7)], whose unknown parameters are: the 'emission function,' s : R 3 × S 2 → R, and the 'extinction function' σ : R 3 → R. The emission s(x, v) is the amount of light radiating from x ∈ R d outwards in direction v, and the extinction σ(x) represents the degree to which point x 'occludes' points behind it. Following [KKLD23a], we parametrize splat models σ(x) = gν(x) and we parameterize s(x, v) as,

$$s(x, \cdot) = \sum_{i=1}^p f_{\mu}^{(i)}(x) \phi_i(v)$$

where {ϕi} p i=1 are spherical harmonic basis functions and fµ : R 3 → R p is another splat model (p ≈ 20). Given these two fields, rendering the scene involves evaluating the Radiative Transfer Equation.

$$\mathcal{A}[s, \sigma](x, v) = \int_0^\infty s(x + tv, v) \sigma(x + tv) \exp\left(-\int_0^t \sigma(x + sv) ds\right) dt. \quad (3)$$

The RTE is typically evaluated using a discrete approximation called α-blending [ZPBG02a, KKLD23a]. In this setting, gradient formulas depend on the specific approximations used in practice and can be computed using autodifferentiation.

Finally, we remark that the performance of Gaussian splatting in large-scale Novel View Synthesis depends heavily on the use of many training heuristics, such as initialization of splat locations [KKLD23b], selective noising of splat locations during training [KRS+24], and on 'pruning strategies' to move spurious splats either by deleting them or teleporting them elsewhere in space [KKLD23b, HTS+25]. By providing a principled optimization perspective on fitting splat models, our results pave the way to interpret these heuristics through the lens of regularized risk minimization. For instance, in a sampling context, the stochastic Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm can be interpreted as Wasserstein gradient descent of the functional F(µ) = KL(µ | µtarget) divergence, which enjoys strong convexity whenever µtarget is strongly log concave. We anticipate that the selective noising heuristic introduced by [KRS+24] can be interpreted as adding a convex entropic regularizer to the objective \(2\), improving convergence. Similarly, particle birth-death dynamics are a well-known discretization algorithm for Fisher-Rao gradient flows [LSW23]. With an appropriate discretization, Theorem 1 thus prescribes a pruning criterion that is guaranteed (in the continuous-time limit) to decrease the loss. We hope to initiate through this work a cross-pollination between the literatures on Gaussian Splatting, particle methods, and algorithms for gradient flow on measure spaces.

### 4 Experiments

In this section, we demonstrate the power of the splat regression modeling by directly comparing it on approximation, regression, and physics-informed modeling problems.

#### 4.1 Multiscale Approximation

First, we show in Figure 1 a representative 1D example of training a splat regression model, which we compare to two standard function approximation algorithms: chebyshev polynomial interpolation and wavelet basis projection.

We compare in Figure 2 the performance of splat regression modeling to Kolmogorov-Arnold networks [LWV+25] and to fully connected MLP architectures on a 2D regression task. We sample xi ∼ U([0, 1]2 ) i.i.d. and set yi = f(xi) + εi where εi ∼ N (0, 0.01), i = 1, . . . , 1000,

$$f(x, y) = \sin(3\pi\sqrt{x}) \cos(3\pi y).$$

Each model is trained using 104 Adam iterations using Adam with learning rate 10−4 and momentum parameters β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.99.

For splat modeling, we use ρ = N (0, I). We initialize each vi = 0, bi ∼ U([0, 1]), and Ai = 0.1 I2. For multilayer perceptron, we use two and three layer ReLU networks with LeCunn initialization. #### 4.3 Physics-informed Modeling

We further test the splat regression model in a two dimensional physics-informed learning problem where the goal is to estimate the solution of the Allen Cahn equation,

$$\varepsilon^2 \Delta u(x) + u(x) - u^3(x) = 0 \quad x \in [0, 1]^d.$$



Figure 3: We compare splat regression, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks [LWV+25], and fully connected Multi-layer Perceptron in a physics informed regression task. Models are fit to solve the Allen-Cahn equation on [0, 1]2 . (Left). True solution under this parameter regime. (Middle). Validation error for each model class as a function of the number of training iterations. (Right). Validation error relative to total number of model parameters. Among the test pool, a k = 50 splat model outperforms all KAN and MLP architectures by an order of magnitude while using significantly fewer parameters.

This equation is a well known example of a PDE whose solutions can have 'boundary interfaces' that converge to discontinuities as ε → 0. We take ε = 0.1 and train each model to minimize a weighted sum of losses,

$$\mathcal{L}(u_\theta) = \frac{1}{n_{\text{int}}} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{int}}} \|\mathcal{D}u_\theta(x_i) - f(x_i)\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{n_{\text{bdy}}} \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{bdy}}} \|u_\theta(z_j) - u^*(z_j)\|_2^2$$

where xi ∼ U([0, 1]d ) and where zj ∼ U(∂[0, 1]d ) both i.i.d. We fit each model with i = 1 . . . 105 and j = 1 . . . 5 · 104 samples respectively. We observe that the splat equation converges very quickly and to a highly accurate solution, even when it is limited to use orders-of-magnitude fewer parameters the KAN and MLP models.

### 5 Conclusion

We have shown that splat models are highly effective in solving a variety of representative low dimensional modeling problems. We believe that these results are extremely promising from a practical perspective. Furthermore, we have drawn a novel connection between 3D Gaussian Splatting and Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient flows, which we hope will lead to many symbiotic interactions between the optimal transport, computer graphics, and statistics communities.

There is much future work to be done. First, as we discuss in Section 3.2, splat models are highly expressive and therefore susceptible to overfitting, warranting a larger scale computational study beyond the scope of our work. The splatting community has developed many heuristics for regularizing and pruning splat models, which we expect will be instrumental to large-scale splat regression modeling. As a second direction, we remark that the proposed model can be interpreted as a new neural network layer, opening the door to compositions of splat models ('deep splat networks') and/or integrations into existing deep architectures. We look forward to investigating these directions in followup work.

### A Well-posedness, Regularity, and Universal Approximation

Here we consolidate the proofs that are related to understanding structural properties of splat measures µ ∈ P(R p × BWρ(R d )) and splat functions. This includes 2 on the well-posedness and differentiability of splat functions and also the proofs of two universal approximation theorems, Proposition 3 and Theorem 3. We restate these claims for reading convenience.

Proposition 2 (Sufficient conditions for regularity). Let µ ∈ Sp,d and that the mother splat ρ has s ≥ 0 bounded derivatives. Then fµ(x) also has s bounded derivatives.

Proof. Since ρ ∈ C s b (R d ) and for any A ∈ R d×d , b ∈ R d , x 7→ Ax + b is C∞ b (R d ), the function (v, x) 7→ v [(A(·) +b)#ρ](x) has s bounded and continuous derivatives in x and is dominated by the νintegrable function v 7→ Csv∥∂sρ∥∞ supv∈supp(ν) ∥A∥ s op where Cs is a universal constant. Derivatives of fµ(·) are therefore given by differentiation under the integral.

Next, we show that under a wide range of ρ, splat models satisfy the conditions of Cybenko's theorem on universal approximation by two-layer neural networks. We restate the universal approximation theorem and check that its conditions hold.

Theorem 2 (Definition 1 and Theorem 1, [Cyb89]). We say that σ is discriminatory if for any measure µ ∈ In := [0, 1]n ,

$$\int_{I_n} \sigma(y^T x + \theta) \, d\mu(x) = 0$$

for all y ∈ R n and θ ∈ R implies that µ = 0. If σ is any continuous discriminatory function, then the finite sums of the form

$$G(x) = \sum_{j=1}^N \alpha_j \sigma(y_j^T x + \theta_j)$$

are dense in C 0 b (In).

Corollary 1. Let Ω ⊆ R d compact and suppose ρ is a continuous density with marginal ρ1(x1) = R Rd−1 ρ(x1, x2 . . . xd) dx. Then for any f ∈ C 0 b (Ω; R) and ε > 0, there exists a k-splat measure µ (with mother splat ρ) such that

$$\sup_{x \in \Omega} |f(x) - f_\mu(x)| < \varepsilon.$$

Proof. By scaling we may assume Ω ⊆ [0, 1]d . We claim that ρ1 is a discriminatory function and that the approximator G(x) with σ = ρ1 and with parameters {(αj , yj , θj ) : j = 1 . . . k}, which is guaranteed by Theorem 2 for some k ≫ 1, can be realized as a splat model. It is easy to see the latter: to realize G(x) as a splat, take Avj = e1y T j , bvj = e1θj , and vj = αj .

Let µ be a nonzero measure on [0, 1]d and let s ∈ supp(µ). Assume for contradiction that for all y ∈ R d , θ ∈ R,

$$0 = \int_{[0,1]^d} \rho_1(y^T x + \theta) \mu(dx) \geq t P_{x \sim \mu}(\rho_1(y^T x + \theta) > t).$$

Thus µ(By,θ(t)) = 0 for all sets By,θ(t) := {x ∈ [0, 1]d : ρ1(y T x + θ) > t} and all t > 0. By continuity, the set ρ −1 1 ((t, ∞)) is open, so that by affine rescaling we can construct from the basis {By,θ(t) : y ∈ R d , t, θ ∈ R} the entire Borel σ-algebra on [0, 1]d , which would imply µ = 0.

Finally, we give a more constructive proof of a universal approximation theorem on sets Ω ⊂ R d whose uniform measure U(Ω) has a finite Poincaré constant. This constant is the smallest CΩ > 0 for which any f ∈ H1 (Ω) satisfies the inequality

$$\text{var}_{X \sim \mathcal{U}(\Omega)}(f(X)) \leq C \|\nabla f\|_{\mathcal{L}^2(\Omega)}^2.$$

This is satisfied by most 'nice' sets, namely whenever Ω is an open connected set with a Lipschitz boundary [Eva10, §5.8 Theorem 1].

Theorem 3. Let Ω ⊆ R d compact and suppose f ∈ C 1 b (Ω; R p ). Suppose further that the measure ω = Unif(Ω) has finite Poincaré constant CΩ < ∞. For ε > 0 there exists a k-splat measure µ at most k ≤ CΩ 2 ε −2(d+2) (LfBρε + BfLρ) 2 splats and for which

$$\sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f(x) - f_\mu(x)\| < (1 + L_f M_\rho) \varepsilon.$$

Where the constants are:

$$B_\rho = \|\rho\|_\infty \quad B_f = \|f\|_\infty$$

$$L_\rho = \sup_{x,y \in \mathbb{R}^d} \frac{|\rho(x) - \rho(y)|}{\|x - y\|_2} \quad L_f = \sup_{x,y \in \Omega} \frac{\|f(x) - f(y)\|_2}{\|x - y\|_2}$$

$$M_\rho = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \rho}[\|z\|_2]$$

Proof. Set f ε (x) = Ez∼ρ[f(x + εz)], then

$$\|f(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)\|_2 \leq \int \|f(x) - f(x + \varepsilon z)\|_2 \rho(dz) \leq L_f M_\rho \varepsilon$$

We now show that, for k ≫ 1 also to be determined, there exist a k-splat measure µ so that

$$\sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)\|_2 \leq \varepsilon.$$

Take random x1, . . . , xk ∼ ω ⊗k and take ν = 1 k Pk i=1 δf(xi) , k(v, x) = ε −dρ(x/ε), and µ(dv, x)dx = ν(dv)k(v, x). Then we have

$$\begin{aligned} P\left(x_1 \dots x_k \mapsto \sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)\|_2 > \varepsilon\right) &\leq \frac{\text{Var}(x_1 \dots x_k \mapsto \sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)\|_2)}{\varepsilon^2} \\ &\leq \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2} \sum_{i=1}^k \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}_i(x_1 \dots x_k \mapsto \sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)\|_2)] \\ &= \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2} \sum_{i=1}^k \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}_i(x_1 \dots x_k \mapsto \sup_{\substack{x \in \Omega \\ r \in S^{p-1}}} \langle r, f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x) \rangle)] \end{aligned}$$

where Vari(f(x1, . . . , xk)) is the variance of f(·) holding x1, . . . , xi−1, xi+1, . . . , xk fixed and resampling xi ∼ ω independently. In advance of applying Poincaré's inequality, invoke the envelope theorem to see,

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_{x_i} \left\{ x_1 \dots x_k \mapsto \sup_{\substack{x \in \Omega \\ r \in S^{p-1}}} \langle r, f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x) \rangle \right\} &= (r^)^T D_{x_i} \left\{ \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k f(x_i) \varepsilon^{-d} \rho((x - x_i)/\varepsilon) \right\} \\ &= \frac{1}{k \varepsilon^d} (r^)^T D f(x_i) \rho((x - x_i)/\varepsilon) \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{k \varepsilon^{d+1}} \langle r^*, f(x_i) \rangle (\nabla \rho)((x - x_i)/\varepsilon)\end{aligned}$$

where x ∗ , r∗ attain the supremum. Its norm is bounded by

$$\| \dots \| \leq \frac{1}{k\varepsilon^{d+1}} (L_f B_\rho \varepsilon + B_f L_\rho)$$

where ∥f∥∞ := supx∈Ω ∥f(x)∥2, and Cρ := supx,y∈Rd |ρ(x) − ρ(y)|/∥x − y∥2, so that by Poincaré inequality,

$$P(x_1, \dots, x_k \mapsto \sup_{x \in \Omega} |f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)| > \varepsilon) \leq \frac{C_\Omega}{\varepsilon^{2(d+2)} k} (L_f B_\rho \varepsilon + B_f L_\rho)^2.$$

Consequently, for k = 1 2CΩ ε −2(d+2) (LfBρε + BfLρ) 2 , it occurs with probability at least one half (over x1, . . . , xk ∼ ω ⊗k ) that supx∈Ω ∥fµ(x) − f ε (x)∥2 ≤ ε. By the triangle inequality

$$\sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f(x)\|_2 \leq \sup_{x \in \Omega} \|f_\mu(x) - f^\varepsilon(x)\|_2 + \|f^\varepsilon(x) - f(x)\|_2 \leq (1 + L_f M_\mu) \varepsilon.$$

### B Gradient flows on measure spaces

We provide in this section some of the mathematical background on Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao calculus that is required to state our main results. We then give the proofs of Theorem 1, Proposition 1, and some helpful calculation rules for computing gradients with respect to splat measures.

### B.1 Review of Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao calculus

First, we define the Hellinger and the 'static' Wasserstein distances.

Definition 3 (Wasserstein Distance [Vil03]). Let M be a Riemannian manifold (endowed with the canonical volume measure) and let µ, ν ∈ Pp(M) be measures with finite p-th moments. The p-Wasserstein distance is,

$$W_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Gamma(\mu, \nu)} \int d_{\mathcal{M}}(X, Y)^p \pi(dX, dY)$$

where dM(·, ·) is the geodesic distance on M and Γ(µ, ν) is the set of all joint couplings whose marginals are πX = µ, πY = ν. We denote by Wp(M) the space Pp(M) endowed with the Wp metric.

Definition 4 (Hellinger Distance [Ama83]). Let M be as in 3 and let µ, ν ∈ P(M) be measures with finite second moments. Setting λ = 1 2 (µ + ν), the Hellinger distance is

$$H^2(\mu, \nu) = \int \left( \sqrt{\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}}(x) - \sqrt{\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}}(x) \right)^2 \lambda(dx)$$

where dµ dλ (x) is the Radon-Nikodym derivative. Here, λ can be replaced by any measure λ ′ ≫ µ, ν. We denote by H(M) the space P(M) endowed with the H metric.

For our purposes we only need to consider rather two rather simple manifolds: the Euclidean space (with the canonical ∥ · ∥2 metric) and the Bures-Wasserstein space BWρ(R d ). We postpone the proof of Proposition 1, where we formally define the Bures-Wasserstein manifold, and proceed with the review. For further reference and for proofs of the background theorems stated here, see wonderful reference [San15] as well as the more contemporary [CNWR25], which emphasizes a statistical perspective.

In their seminal 1998 paper, Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto [JKO98] introduced an iterative 'minimizing movements' scheme as a variational approach to solving the Fokker-Plank equation in fluid dynamics, leading them (largely by accident) to discover the geometric perspective on the so-called 'static' Wasserstein distance. Shortly afterwards, [BB00] developed the following elegant formulation.

Theorem 4 (Benamou–Brenier [BB00]). Given two probability measures µ0, µ1 ∈ W2(M), it holds that

We turn our attention to the Fisher-Rao geometry, which is more straightforward to explain. A standard reference for this material is the book [Ama16]. The 'admissible' tangent vectors in this geometry are those belonging to the absolutely continuous curves,

Definition 7 (Fisher-Rao absolute continuity). A curve (µt)t∈[0,1] is absolutely continuous at t ∈ [0, 1] if its Hellinger metric derivative is finite:

$$|\dot{\mu}|(t) := \lim_{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{H(\mu_{t+\varepsilon}, \mu_t)}{\varepsilon} < \infty.$$

Theorem 7. If (µt)t∈[0,1] ∈ H(W) is absolutely continuous at t ∈ [0, 1], then there exists αt : M → R such that ∂tµt = αtµt, and where EX∼µt [αt(X)] = 0.

The Fisher-Rao geometry is essentially based on the fact that for any probability density µ(x) : M → R, the square root density √µ(x) is an element of the unit sphere S(L 2 (M)) := {v ∈ L 2 (M) : ∥v∥ = 1}, since it has R ( √µ(x))2 dx = 1 by definition. We endow it with the pullback metric on P(M) induced by the map ι : µ 7→ √µ ∈ S(L 2 (M)). Hence:

- The geodesic connecting µ0, µ1 is the spherical linear interpolant connecting √µ0, √µ1:

$$\sqrt{\mu_t} = \left( \frac{\sin((1-t)\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} \right) \sqrt{\mu_0} + \left( \frac{\sin(t\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} \right) \sqrt{\mu_1}, \quad \cos(\theta) = \langle \sqrt{\mu_0}, \sqrt{\mu_1} \rangle$$

- Each tangent space TµH(M) is by definition isomorphic to T√µH(M). The isomorphism map is the pushforward or 'differential' dι, which is given by

$$d\iota(\xi) := \partial_\varepsilon \iota(\mu + \varepsilon\xi) \Big|_{\varepsilon=0} = \frac{\xi}{2\sqrt{\mu}}.$$

and so the inner product on TµH(M) is

$$\langle \xi, \psi \rangle_\mu = \langle d\nu(\xi), d\nu(\psi) \rangle_{\sqrt{\mu}} = \frac{1}{4} \int \frac{\xi \psi}{\mu}(x) dx.$$

- By absolute continuity we may write ξ = αµ, ψ = βµ for densities α, β : M → R, and the Fisher-Rao metric can be rewritten as

$$\langle \alpha \mu, \beta \mu \rangle = \frac{1}{4} \int \alpha(x) \beta(x) \mu(dx).$$

It is customary to identify the tangent vectors with the coefficients and to write

$$T_\mu \mathcal{H}(\mathcal{M}) = \{\alpha \in C_c^\infty(\mathcal{M}) : \mathbb{E}_\mu[\alpha(X)] = 0\}_{\mathcal{L}^2(\mu)}$$

with metric ⟨·, ·⟩µ = 1 4 ⟨·, ·⟩L2 µ(M) . It is also common to drop the prefactor 1/4.

- The Fisher-Rao gradient of a functional F is (when it exists) the function ∇FRF ∈ TµH(M) that satisfies, for α ∈ TµH(M),

$$\partial_\varepsilon \mathcal{F}(\mu + \varepsilon \alpha \mu) \Big|_{\varepsilon=0} = \langle \nabla^{\text{FR}} \mathcal{F}(\mu), \alpha \rangle_\mu.$$

Finally, the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao geometry is the one whose tangent spaces are direct sums of the Wasserstein and the Fisher-Rao tangent spaces. In other words, Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao tangent vectors have the form

$$\partial_t \mu_t + \operatorname{div}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu_t \nabla \phi_t) = \alpha_t \mu_t, \quad \nabla \phi_t \in T_{\mu} \mathcal{W}(\mathcal{M}), \quad \alpha_t \in T_{\mu} \mathcal{H}(\mathcal{M}).$$

and as usual we identify each tangent vector with its coefficients (∇ϕt , αt). The Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao metric is

$$\langle (\nabla \phi_t, \alpha_t), (\nabla \psi_t, \beta_t) \rangle_\mu = \frac{1}{4} \int \alpha_t(x) \beta_t(x) \mu(dx) + \int \langle \nabla \phi_t(x), \nabla \psi_t(x) \rangle \mu(dx),$$

and its tangent spaces are

$$T_\mu \mathcal{W}(\mathcal{M}) \oplus T_\mu \mathcal{H}(\mathcal{M}) = \overline{\{(a, v) : \exists \alpha, \phi \in C^\infty(\mathcal{M}) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, v = \nabla \phi\}_{\mathcal{L}^2(\mu)}},$$

and the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient of F is ∇WFRF(µ) = (∇∇F(µ), ∇FRF(µ)).

Finally, to highlight the connection between particle methods and measure-valued gradient flows, we recall the well-known Fokker-Plank Equation.

Theorem 8 (Fokker-Plank Equation, [CNWR25]). Suppose the process (Xt)t∈[0,1] solves the SDE,


$$dX_t = v_t(X_t)dt + \sqrt{2\varepsilon}dW_t \quad (7)$$

for some vector field vt on M, with initial condition X0 ∼ µ0. Then µt = law(Xt) solves the equation,

$$\partial_t \mu_t + \operatorname{div}_{\mathcal{M}}(v_t \mu_t) = \varepsilon \Delta \mu_t.$$

As a consequence, we can approximate the continuity equation dynamics (Theorem 6\) by simulating a particle (Xt)t∈[0,1] undergoing the dynamics \(7\) with vt = ∇ϕt and with ε = 0.

#### B.2 Proofs of Proposition 1 and Theorem 1

The proof of Proposition 1 follows exactly the same steps as the case ρ = N (0, Id).

Proposition 1 (Splats are a generalized Bures-Wasserstein manifold) . Let ρ ∈ Pac(R d ) be a centered isotropic mother splat. We denote the set of all splats as,

$$\text{BW}_\rho(\mathbb{R}^d) := \left\{ (A(\cdot) + b)_\# \rho : A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}, b \in \mathbb{R}^d \right\}.$$

Then BWρ(R d ) is a geodesically convex subset of W2(R d ), and on this space the Wasserstein metric reduces to the Bures-Wasserstein metric [Mod16, BJL19],

$$W_2^2(\rho_{A,b}, \rho_{R,s}) = \|b - s\|_2 + \|A\|_F^2 + \|R\|_F^2 - 2\|A^T R\|_*$$

where ∥ · ∥F is the Frobenius norm and ∥ · ∥∗ is the nuclear norm.

Proof. Fix ρA0,b0 = (A0(·) + b0)#ρ and ρA1,b1 = (A1(·) + b1)#. By Brenier's uniqueness theorem [CNWR25, Theorem 1.16], the map

$$T(x) = b_1 + A_0^{-1}(A_0A_1A_1^TA_0)^{1/2}A_0^{-1}(x - b_0)$$

is the optimal transport map T#ρA0,b0 = ρA1,b1 as it is the gradient of a convex function. It follows that the geodesic connecting ρ0 to ρ1 is ρt = ((1 − t)id + tT)#ρ0 ∈ BWρ(R d ). As ρ is centered and isotropic, the distance W2(ρ0, ρ1) is

$$\begin{aligned} W_2^2(\rho_0, \rho_1) &= \mathbb{E}_{\rho} \left[ \|(A_0 X + b_0) - T(A_0 X + b_0)\|^2 \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\rho} \left[ \|b_0 - b_1 + (A_0 - A_0^{-1}(A_0 A_1 A_1^T A_0)^{1/2}) X \| \right] \\ &= \|b_0 - b_1\|^2 + \|A_0 - A_0^{-1}(A_0 A_1 A_1^T A_0)^{1/2} \Sigma_X^{1/2}\|_F^2 \end{aligned}$$

where ΣX = Covρ(X) = Id. Thus W2(ρ0, ρ1) = W2(N (b0, A0AT 0 ), N (b1, A1AT 1 )) is the Bures-Wasserstein metric [BJL19].

We now proceed to the proof of Theorem 1. We develop these calculations at a formal level – the reader who wishes to understand an entirely rigorous proof may compare to [AGS08, Chapter 11].

Theorem 1 (Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient of µ 7→ F(fµ)). Let µ ∈ Sp,d and let F : L 2 π (R d ; R p ) → R be a functional. Assume that ρ has a sub-exponential density. Then the Fisher-Rao gradient is given by,

$$\nabla_{\mu}^{\text{FR}} \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu})(v, A, b) = \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) - \mathbb{E}_{v, A, b \sim \mu} \left[ \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \right].$$

and the Wasserstein gradient is given by,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{W}_v\mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= \int \delta\mathcal{F}(x) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \\ \mathbb{W}_A\mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= \int \langle \delta\mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle (I_d + \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x)(x - b)^T) A^{-T} \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \\ \mathbb{W}_b\mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= - \int \langle \delta\mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \end{aligned}$$

Proof. We begin by calculating the Fisher-Rao gradient. Along the way, we develop a 'chain rule,' which may aid in future calculations. Specifically we view µ 7→ fµ as a mapping between manifolds, f(·) : H(Sp,d) → L2 π (R d ; R p ), and we calculate its differential dfF R µ : TµH(Sp,d) → L2 π (R d ; R p ). For α ∈ TµH(Sp,d),

$$d^{FR} f_\mu[\alpha](x) = \partial_\varepsilon f_{\mu+\varepsilon\alpha\mu} \Big|_{\varepsilon=0} = \int_{S_{p,d}} v \alpha(v, A, b) \rho_{A,b}(x) \mu(dv, dA, db).$$

Invoking the definition of the first variation, our chain rule takes the form

$$\begin{aligned} \partial_{\varepsilon} \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu+\varepsilon\alpha}) &= \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_{\mu}], d^{FR} f_{\mu}[\alpha] \rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{\pi}^2(\mathbb{R}^d; \mathbb{R}^p)} \\ &= \iint \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_{\mu}](x), v \rangle \alpha(v, A, b) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \mu(dv, dA, db) \\ &= \int \alpha(v, A, b) \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_{\mu}](x), v \rangle \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \mu(dv, dA, db). \end{aligned}$$

We identify the Fisher-Rao gradient by matching the integrand to the definition.

Computing the Wasserstein gradient follows largely the same steps. One checks the following auxiliary calculations.

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_A \{ \rho \left( A^{-1}(x - b) \mid \det A \right)^{-1} \} &= - |\det A|^{-1} A^{-T} (\nabla \rho)(A^{-1}(x - b))(x - b)^T A^{-T} \\ &\quad - \rho \left( A^{-1}(x - b) \mid \det A \right)^{-1} A^{-T} \\ &= -\rho_{A,b}(x) \left( I + \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x)(x - b)^T \right) A^{-T} \\ \nabla_b \{ \rho \left( A^{-1}(x - b) \mid \det A \right)^{-1} \} &= -(\nabla \rho)(A^{-1}(x - b)) \mid \det A \mid^{-1} \\ &= -\nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x) \rho_{A,b}(x).\end{aligned}$$

Now fix u ∈ TµW2(Sp,d). We calculate the differential dW fµ[u] as,

$$\begin{aligned} d^W f_\mu[u](x) &= - \int_{S_{p,d}} v \rho_{A,b}(x) \operatorname{div} s_{p,d}(u\mu)(dv, dA, db) \\ &= \int \langle \nabla_{v,A,b} \{v \rho_{A,b}(x)\}, u(v, A, b) \rangle \mu(dv, dA, db) \\ &= \int u_v(v, A, b) \rho_{A,b}(x) \mu(dv, dA, db) \\ &\quad + \int v \langle \nabla_A \{\rho_{A,b}\}(x), u_A(v, A, b) \rangle \mu(dv, dA, db) \\ &\quad + \int v \langle \nabla_b \{\rho_{A,b}\}(x), u_b(v, A, b) \rangle \mu(dv, dA, db) \end{aligned}$$

where u = (uv, uA, ub) are the coordinate function representations of u. Thus, setting ξ = − divSp,d (uµ),

$$\begin{aligned} \partial_\varepsilon \mathcal{F}(f_{\mu+\varepsilon\xi}) &= \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu], d^W f_\mu[u] \rangle_{\mathcal{L}^2(\mathbb{R}^d; \mathbb{R}^p)} \\ &= \iint \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu](x), u_v(v, A, b) \rangle_{\rho_{A,b}}(x) \pi(dx) \mu(dv, dA, db) \\ &\quad + \iint \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu](x), v \rangle \langle \nabla_A \{ \rho_{A,b} \}(x), u_A(v, A, b) \rangle \pi(dx) \mu(dv, dA, db) \\ &\quad + \iint \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu](x), v \rangle \langle \nabla_b \{ \rho_{A,b} \}(x), u_b(v, A, b) \rangle \pi(dx) \mu(dv, dA, db). \end{aligned}$$

From this we see,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{W}_v \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= \int \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu](x) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \\ \mathbb{W}_A \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= - \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu](x), v \rangle (I + \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x)(x-b)^T) A^{-T} \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx) \\ \mathbb{W}_b \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, A, b) &= - \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu](x), v \rangle \nabla_x \log \rho_{A,b}(x) \rho_{A,b}(x) \pi(dx). \end{aligned}$$

### C.2 Smoothed Particles and Heterogeneous Mixtures

In this appendix, we discuss an alternative formulation of splat regression modeling in terms of heterogeneous mixtures. The general philosophy is to investigate the functions f ∈ Ls (R p ; R d ) that can be represented by a probabilistic coupling µ ∈ P(R p×R d ) which dictates, at each (v, x) ∈ R p×R d , how much the vector v contributes to the value of f(x). This perspective gives some useful intuition about how splats can be viewed as 'smoothed particles.' We define a heterogeneous mixture as follows.

Proposition 4 (Heterogeneous Mixtures). Suppose that µ ∈ P(R p × R d ) is of the form µ(dv, dx) = µ(dv, x) dx and where E(v,x)∼µ[∥v∥ s s ] < ∞, for s ≥ 1. Then the function fµ(x) := Ev∼µ(·,x) [v] belongs to L s (R d ; R p ), and furthermore, there exists a measure ν ∈ P(R p ) (the 'mixture components') and a density k(v, x) > 0 (the 'heterogeneous mixture weights') so that

$$\mu(dv, x) = \nu(dv) k(v, x) = f_\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}_{v \sim \nu}[vk(v, x)].$$

Having a density in x is required to be able to make sense of fµ(x) pointwise. For each x, k(·, x) determines which v ∈ supp(ν) (and in what proportions) contribute to the value of fµ(x). The splat regression model defined in Proposition 1 is realized by parametrizing k(v, ·) as a v-dependent mixture model density.

Proof of Proposition 4. If E(v,x)∼µ[∥v∥ s s ] then by Jensen's inequality,

$$\|f_\mu\|_{\mathcal{L}^s(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^p)}^s = \int \left\| \int v \mu(dv, x) \right\|_s^s dx \leq \iint \|v\|_s^s \mu(dv, x) dx < \infty$$

so fµ ∈ L2 (R d ; R p ). Now take ν to be the v-marginal ((v, x) 7→ v)#µ. By the disintegration theorem [AGS08] there exists a family of measures kv ∈ P(R d ) so that µ(dv, x)dx = kv(dx)ν(dv). We must check that each kv has a density, and for this it is sufficient to check that for any ϕ ∈ L1 b (R p ) and

any sequence ψk ∈ L1 (R d ) that converges ϕk k→∞ −→ 0 in L 1 ,

$$\begin{aligned} \int \phi(v) \left( \int \psi_k(x) k_v(dx) \right) \nu(dv) &= \iint \phi(v) \psi_k(x) \mu(dv, dx) \\ &\leq \sup_{v \in \text{supp}(\nu)} \phi(v) \cdot \|\psi_k\|_{L^1(\mathbb{R}^d)} \\ &\xrightarrow{k \rightarrow \infty} 0. \end{aligned}$$

by Hölder's inequality. We have shown that for ν-almost every v, any sequence converging to zero in L 1 (R d ) also converges to zero in L 1 (R d ; kv). The proof follows by identifying k(v, x) as the density of kv(dx).

□

Next, we compute the Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao gradients on P(R p × R d ).

Theorem 9 (Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient of µ 7→ F(fµ) for µ ∈ P(R p×R d )). Let µ ∈ P(R p×R d ) and let F : L 2 (R d ; R p ) → R be a functional. Assume that ρ has a sub-exponential density. Then the Fisher-Rao gradient is given (if it exists) by the formula,

$$\nabla_\mu^{\text{FR}} \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, x) = \langle \delta \mathcal{F}(x), v \rangle - \mathbb{E}_{X, V \sim \mu}[\langle \delta \mathcal{F}(X), V \rangle].$$

and the Wasserstein gradient is given (if it exists) by the formula,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{W}_v \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, x) &= \delta \mathcal{F}(x) \\ \mathbb{W}_x \mathcal{F}(f_\mu)(v, x) &= -v^T D_x \mathcal{F}(x) \end{aligned}$$

where the argument of δF(·) = δF[f](·) was suppressed.

These formulas follow from calculating dW fµ, d F Rfµ, which can be derived by the same steps as in Theorem 1.

$$\langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu], d^W f_\mu(u) \rangle := \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu] \otimes v^T D_x \delta \mathcal{F}(f_\mu), u(v, x) \rangle \mu(dv, dx) \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu], d^{FR} f_\mu(\alpha) \rangle := \int \langle \delta \mathcal{F}[f_\mu], v \rangle \alpha(v, x) \mu(dv, dx).$$

Integrating by parts, the expression for ∇∇bF is equivalent to,

$$\mathbb{W}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{F}_{\mu})(v, A, b) = -\mathbb{E}_{X \sim \rho_{A, b}}[\langle v, \delta\mathcal{F}(X) \rangle \nabla_x \log \rho_{A, b}(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \rho_{A, b}}[v^T D_x \delta\mathcal{F}(X)].$$

By comparison to the expressions in Theorem 1, we see that the gradients have the same form as Theorem 9, but they are averaged with respect to the splat density. This is the precise sense in which splat measures behave as smoothed particle systems.

We conclude this appendix with a sketch of a discretization scheme for the Fisher-Rao dynamics in H(R p × R d ). It is not a-priori clear how to simulate these dynamics, since a traditional particle method involves simulating particle measures that do not satisfy the "density in x" condition required by 4. Consider µ0 ∈ P(R p × BWρ(R d )), which induces the measure µ˜0 ∈ P(R p × R d ) given by µ˜0(dv, x)dx = R ρA,b(x) µ0(dv, ρA,b). Suppose we would like to simulate the Fisher-Rao dynamics ∂tµ˜t = αµ˜t where α(v, x) : R p × R d → R is a tangent vector in Tµ˜0H(R p × R d ). For 0 < ε ≪ 1,

$$\tilde{\mu}_\varepsilon \approx (1 + \varepsilon \alpha) \tilde{\mu}_0.$$

One can approximately sample the right hand side using a generic accept-reject scheme, since the density ratio α(v, x) = dµ˜ε dµ˜0 (v, x) is available in closed form. Then, given access to samples with approximate distribution x1, . . . , xn ∼ µ˜ε, one can approximate µ˜ε(dv, x)dx by the following KDE-inspired estimator:

$$\tilde{\mu}_\varepsilon(v, x) \approx \hat{\mu}_\varepsilon(v, x) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{v_i} \rho(h_n(x - x_i)).$$

where hn ≥ 0 is a user-selected bandwidth parameter. This formulation is a natural choice for density estimation, and it is also an induced heterogeneous mixture measure corresponding to P(R p × BWρ(R d )), given by

$$\hat{\mu}_\varepsilon = \mathcal{U}(\{(v_1, \rho_{x_1, hI_d}), \dots, (v_n, \rho_{x_n, hI_d})\}) \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^p \times \text{BW}_\rho(\mathbb{R}^d)).$$

which is to be taken as the output of one discretization step. While this sketch is highly heuristic, we believe that it suggests an interesting family of 'smoothed particle methods' based on approximating the H(R p × R d ) gradient flow.
