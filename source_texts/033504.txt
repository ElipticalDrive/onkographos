Abstract
Visualization of volumetric data has been widely used for exploration of data from scientific simulations and biomedical imaging. Despite advances of GPU-assisted rendering, which has become the state-of-art in direct volume rendering, still many volumetric data sets are too large to be visualized interactively. Therefore, compression-domain rendering approaches are used in visualization processes in order to reduce the amount of data sent to the GPU and thus speed up interactive visualization. Hence, reliable tools to assess the quality of the reconstructed 3D data are of great importance, influencing the effectiveness of the visualization. However, numerical error analysis approaches such as mean-squared-based metrics are often inconsistent with perceived visual quality. We propose a structural volume quality assessment approach for 3D scalar volume based on the human visual system (HVS). Our approach consists of two stages: First, we provide an interactive tool for extracting significant volume features via isosurfaces from a 3D scalar field using an isovalue classification process. Second, we propose a structural volume quality assessment (VQA) metric that employs representative isosurfaces as benchmark structures. For this purpose, we use a recently developed perceptual-based mesh quality metric [1] to assess the visual quality of compressed 3D scalar fields. Our experiments on a number of benchmark data sets suggest that, compared to existing methods, the proposed isovalue classification approach provides a more distinct set of isosurfaces that are more representative of the complexity of the data sets. We examine a number of widely used compression approaches, namely, discrete wavelet transform, discrete cosine transform, and tensor approximation, to establish the utility of our volume quality assessment approach.

Introduction
Volume visualization has become an indispensable tool in volumetric data analysis and continues to facilitate discovery in a broad range of applications from scientific computing (e.g., simulations) to biomedical imaging (e.g., CT and MR). Direct visualization algorithms make use of a user-defined transfer function to assign color and opacity to the scalar field data while indirect visualization requires the user to provide specific data values for extracting and rendering an isosurface. In both paradigms, the user’s input directly impacts the features selected for visualization.
Direct volume rendering (DVR) [2] approaches allow scientists to visually inspect and explore a volume by simulating emission, absorption, and scattering of light as it travels through the volume rather than explicitly extract geometric surfaces from the data. In visualization of large-scale data sets, DVR approaches are often limited by the memory and computing resources needed for rendering; hence, compression approaches are commonly integrated to the visualization process [3].
Compression approaches use compact data representations, which require fewer bits for encoding than what is needed for the original volume. To generate a compact representation, a routine decomposes a data set through a mathematical framework or learned model into a set of bases and coefficients, which are usually less error-prone to data reduction. Lossless algorithms are those that ensure maximum accuracy in the reconstruction and guarantee zero error. Lossy algorithms produce an approximation of the reconstructed volume up to a tolerated approximation error. Compact representations are often initially lossless and dense. However, higher compression ratios can be achieved by thresholding or encoding the coefficients. A recent example for an advanced encoding strategy has been proposed with fixed-rate compression of floating point coefficients [4]. Since data reduction becomes an essential step in large-scale data visualization, as recently shown with lossy compression of seismic tomography [5], reliable tools to assess the quality of the reconstructed 3D data are of great importance. The quality assessment of the compressed data directly affects the chosen compression rates and influences the usability and effectiveness of the visualization.
The volumetric approximation quality is either measured in the reconstructed data set, i.e., in the data domain, or in the aggregated final 2D visualization, i.e., in the image domain. In case of volume visualization, the approximation quality is usually measured numerically in the data domain in terms of rate-distortion, which is a ratio between the achieved approximation quality and the data reduction level. Frequently, the approximation quality is measured using the mean-squared error (MSE) between the original and the approximation. However, the MSE measures a distortion rather than the perceived visual quality. The limitations of the MSE have been noted in image processing [6], mesh processing [7], as well as for comparing 3D compression approaches [8].
One important characteristic of volume rendering is that only subsets of the volumetric data, i.e., features of interest, are selected and visualized in the final image [9]. A volumetric data set contains a large number of isosurfaces at different isovalues of the scalar field. In contrast, the volumetric structure is only characterized by a finite number of representative isosurfaces that segment the data into important structures. The reconstruction quality of these representative isosurfaces directly portrays the visualization of compressed data. For a similar reason, Ljung et al. [10] proposed to evaluate the distortion of compressed data in the rendered images since the distortion of the reconstructed (whole) volume has a reduced correspondence to the final image. That is, large subsets of the volume will give little or no contribution to the rendering.
In our approach, we propose to directly assess the quality of the reconstructed volume in the data-domain. Instead of conducting quality assessment on the whole volume, we leverage the characteristics of volume rendering and use representative isosurfaces as benchmark structures to evaluate the visual quality of compressed 3D scalar fields. Specifically, we propose a two-stage approach to conduct volume quality assessment (VQA). First, we cluster isovalues of a given data set and identify its representative isosurfaces (see Fig. 1(a)). Second, we propose a structural VQA metric that examines the quality of compressed volumetric data by evaluating its representative isosurfaces using the tensor-based perceptual distance measure (TPDM) [1] that simulates human visual system (HVS) (see Fig. 1(b)).
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 3.1, we discuss the choices of candidate isosurface similarity metrics for our proposed isovalue classification algorithm, presented in Section 3.2. In Section 3.3, a set of experiments analyzing our proposed approach on simulation and real data sets is conducted. Finally, in Section 4, we demonstrate a perceptual VQA metric using representative isosurfaces to assess the visual quality of compressed 3D scalar fields.

Show abstract
Isosurfaces are an important visual representation of volumetric data sets and isosurface extraction and rendering remains one of the most popular methods for volume visualization. Previous works identify a small set of representative isosurfaces from a set of sample ones, providing a concise description of the underlying volume. However, these methods do not lend themselves to equally spaced isosurfaces, i.e., keeping the same distance between neighboring isosurfaces, which can be advantageous from the user’s perspective in terms of visual summarization and interactive exploration. In this paper, we present a new solution that efficiently identifies a set of nearly equally spaced isosurfaces for a given volume data set. Our approach includes an estimation stage of linear interpolation and a refinement stage of binary search in order to balance the tradeoff between quality and performance. The refinement stage can incorporate spike and/or jump treatments to possibly improve the convergence. Experimenting with multiple data sets of different sizes and characteristics, we perform both quantitative and qualitative studies, demonstrate the efficiency and effectiveness of our approach, and summarize our findings.
