Abstract
Volumetric displays render true 3D graphics without forcing users
to wear headsets or glasses. However, the optical diffusers that
volumetric displays employ are rigid and thus do not allow for
direct interaction. We showcase FlexiVol, a volumetric display that
employs elastic diffusers, allowing users to reach inside the display volume and have direct interaction with true-3D content. We
plan to demo two playful come-and-interact applications involving FlexiVol, enabling participants to explore its interaction design
space, without having to wear any devices. Participants will be able

1
Introduction
We are used to seeing and interacting in a 3D world. Our vision
uses cues to interpret depth, and our hands directly grab and manipulate the objects within. From the different 3D technologies,
only volumetric displays and holograms provide all the depth cues
[16]. Volumetric displays render true-3D and can provide most of
the visual cues that we perceive from the real world. Nonetheless,
with current technologies, users cannot reach inside the display
volume to directly interact with virtual objects as they would do
in the real world. Display diffusers are usually rigid, which causes
safety hazards when reaching through; and interaction techniques
are thus performed indirectly using a 3D mouse or a keyboard.
While several interaction techniques have been developed for
3D environments [5, 11], mostly indirect approaches have been
adapted for volumetric displays [8] such as rays [7] or cursors.
Other alternatives use hand gestures [2] or the index finger as a
pointing device [9]. Commercial volumetric displays such as the
Voxon VX1 [15] use traditional mouse and keyboard techniques,
or 3D mice [1] as an input modality. Voxon displays also support
a hand-tracking device (Leap Motion, Ultraleap Ltd.) enabling the
use of the hands to manipulate virtual objects, but the real hands
have to remain outside of the display volume. Methods based on
touching the display boundary (i.e., protective case) [13] try to get
closer to the display volume, yet the interaction remains indirect.
In our previous paper, “FlexiVol: a Volumetric Display with an
Elastic Diffuser´´ [4], we investigated how to enable reach-through
interaction to facilitate the selection and manipulation of true-3D
content inspired from the definition of direct interaction from [10]:
allowing the user to literally get their hands into the virtual display
and to directly interact with a spatially aligned 3D virtual world. We
proposed the use of elastic diffusers on swept volumetric displays
to enable the user to reach inside the display volume. We drew
inspiration from the use of elastic diffusers and their taxonomy of
interaction techniques [18] to enable novel interaction techniques
within a volumetric display with elastic diffusers.
We now propose to showcase FlexiVol, and enable participants
to insert their hand within a volumetric display with an elastic
optical diffuser and directly interact with spatially overlapped true3D graphics. Using FlexiVol, the true-3D graphics and user hand
provide coherent focus accommodation (Figure 1.Left), which allows for enhanced depth perception. The interaction with the rendered graphics is direct, the user can grab an object to move and
rotate it (Figure 1.Right). We developed two mini-games, where
users can explore freely and interact with all the elements within
the scene; without having to follow a given scenario. These minigames demonstrate FlexiVol’s interaction design space, previously
discussed in FlexiVol’s paper [4].

Envisioned Interaction
In this section, we first depict FlexiVol’s interaction design space,
and then propose two mini-games to showcase the interactions. The
games can work with up to two hands including both handedness.
Playing the two mini-games last less than 5 minutes. Mini-games
were designed using Unity 2021.3.22.

Interactional Design Space
We first envisioned a set of interaction techniques to be used in
reach-through direct interaction with volumetric displays. We selected the interaction techniques that are closer to real-life ones:
techniques that use metaphors such as rays, clutching with the
thumb or micro-gestures were discarded. We illustrate the interaction techniques as combinations of actions and gestures (Figure 2).
FlexiVol’s interaction techniques are inspired from the literature involving touchscreens, multi-touch displays, and augmented/virtual
reality 3D object manipulation [12, 17, 20].

2.2
The Village
As a representation of our interaction design space, we propose
a first mini-game, consisting in walking through a village. The
participant first sees a terrain, a plain with grass and trees. They
then need to perform an alternate swiping gesture to navigate
through it and discover its interaction opportunities. The terrain
contains four main areas to explore: the town center (with houses,
a church, and a cemetery), a mountain (where hot air balloons float
around), a lake (where a campfire is set), and a plain (with cows
and trees).

Navigation. The navigation follows the hand orientation when
alternate swiping on top of the oscillating bands, and starts with a
threshold of four index and middle finger switches. The participant
can thus explore the four corners of the virtual environment. When
getting closer to its limit, the environment duplicates from its other
corner, making it wrap-around and enabling infinite exploration in
any direction.
Touching. Touching elements triggers events: touching the
church starts the bells ringing, while touching the hot-air balloons
makes them pop and re-appear further away, and touching the logs
at the campsite starts or stops a bonfire (see Figure 3 - The Village).
If the participant touches the water, they will hear the sound of
their fall into it.
Rotating. Other elements, such as the cows or the windmill can
be rotated. The user first grasps the object, using their thumb and
index and/or middle finger; and change their orientation.
Editing. While navigating through the plain, the participant
can find different types of mushrooms: ceps, morels, etc. They can
touch and deform them by pushing on them. Up to a point where
they will pop and split into multiple small mushrooms that spread
around, so the participant can grow more if they want.

Come Fishy Fishy!
We also propose a second mini-game, where the participant can
interact inside a fish tank (see Figure 3 - Come Fishy Fishy!). Participants will see different fish randomly moving around; and will
be able to put their hands inside. The fish will avoid them; and
the closer they get to a fish, the faster they go away. However, if
a participant manages to touch a fish, it will start an animation. It
is when the participant stops moving their hands around, that the
fish will come and interact with them.

System Description
In this section, we describe the four parts needed for demoing
FlexiVol. Beyond the visual display, other components are required
to enable come-and-interact scenarios with true-3D content (see
Figure 4).
3.1
Swept Volumetric Display
3.1.1
Hardware.
Projector: A high frame rate is needed to render into the display
volume: if 100 slices are projected at a frame rate of 30 Hz, the
projector has to operate at 3000 Hz. Usually, DLP projectors with
time-multiplexation for the color are employed. The projected images are reduced to binary patterns and thus the original frame rate
of the projector can be multiplied by the number of color bitplanes.
For example, the Voxon VX1 projects 192 slices at 15 Hz (2880 Hz)
with a resolution of 980×720px. When operating at 120 Hz with
24-bit color depth, it projects binary patterns at 2880 Hz with a
resolution of up to 1280×800px.
Actuators: the typical actuators for a planar oscillating SVD are
voicecoils (e.g., Voxon VX1 [15] and Dan Maloney’s [14]) either
custom designed or from a modified speaker.
Mechanical amplifier: the actuators may not oscillate with sufficient amplitude for the diffuser to provide a large enough display
volume. That is why some designs (e.g., Voxon VX1 and Dan Maloney’s) use mechanical amplifiers. A mechanical amplifier can be
just a spring or a lever mechanism.
Diffuser: it is usually a sheet of an optically diffusing material,
which catches the projected light and diffuses it towards the viewers.
The display volume is usually protected with an acrylic case to avoid
users touching the solid diffuser. For FlexiVol, we replaced this solid
diffuser with an array of elastic strips. We tried different materials:
projection screens, elastane, latex and silicone; and found the elastic
bands to present the best compromise of mechanical, optical and
oscillatory properties.
3.1.2
Software.
Rendering: The virtual scene needs to be sliced along planes,
those slices are projected at high-speed on the oscillating diffuser.
Several slices are combined into a color image to be sent to the
projector. For example, 24 slices with binary color are packed into a
regular 24-bit RGB image that the projector expects from the HDMI
or Display Port input.
Synchronization: In general, even if the actuators are excited
with the same frequency as the projector, there will be drift and
synchronization is required. If the actuator signal is used to synchronize the display, the rendering system has to sample the actuation
signal before the start of each frame. That is, the rendering system
checks the phase of the actuation signal and extrapolates the diffuser position for the next 24 slices, renders the slices and sends
them packed as a color image to the projector. If the projector is
the synchronization source, the vertical sync signal can be used to
keep in a phased-locked-loop the actuation signal. The actuation
signal is required to be a divider of the vsync signal. For example,
in the Voxon VX1 the actuation signal is a 15 Hz sinusoidal and the
vsync of the projector is a 120 Hz signal.

3.2
Hand Capture (Webcams)
To enable interactive scenarios where the user can actively perform interaction techniques, we need to capture and track its hand
positions and movements. As the diffuser is oscillating at a high
frequency, occlusion can occur, blocking the cameras views and
hand capture. We thus chose to use two cameras (Papalook Web
Camera HD 1080p); which we placed perpendicular to each other.
One is oriented towards the volumetric display on the lateral side of
the diffuser; while the other one is oriented towards its longitudinal
side. They are placed higher than the display volume, to enable
hand tracking above the volumetric display and a clear vision of
the palm or wrist joints.
3.3
Computer with Mediapipe
The two cameras are connected to a computer; which runs a Python
3.8.20 terminal. We tried different hand tracking systems (Meta
Oculus Quest, Leap motion), but chose a Mediapipe solution [19].
We triangulated hands points using the two cameras through Batpurev’s real-time 3D hand pose estimation [3], and transformed
the coordinates from cameras space to volumetric display space.
3.4
Communication (UDP)
Finally, the hand joint coordinates are sent through UDP (User
Datagram Protocol). We programmed an ESP32 to work as a hotspot
and UDP server, enabling the communication between the computer
and the volumetric display. The computer sends “computed 3D
coordinates´´ as packets of 63 values (x, y, z for 21 hand joints; 4
per finger + palm) in the new ordered basis to the server, which
subsequently sends it to the display. These received coordinates are
displayed onto the diffuser, giving the user a visual feedback of its
hand 3D position in the volume. We allow for up to two hands to
be present simultaneously within the volume.
4
Conclusion
We propose to showcase a modified swept volumetric display (FlexiVol) with an elastic diffuser to allow users to reach inside the
display volume for interaction. This simple yet significant modification on volumetric displays opens up new opportunities for
exploring the advantages of volumetric displays and direct reachthrough interaction; we do so through two mini-games. Participants
can navigate, select, rotate or edit true-3D elements following a
come-and-interact approach, without having to wear any device
(e.g., headset, controllers).
