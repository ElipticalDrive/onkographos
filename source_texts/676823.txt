METHODS OF CONTAINERIZING THE RENDERING
OF THREE-DIMENSIONAL GRAPHIC IMAGES
Modern systems for generating three-dimensional graphic images are
characterized by a simultaneous increase in scene complexity, photorealism
requirements, and the need to reduce frame generation time. Rendering
is increasingly performed not on a single workstation but in a distributed
environment that combines local graphics processors, server nodes, and cloud
resources. Under these conditions, not only the choice of a ray-tracing algorithm
is important, but also the method of deploying the software environment,
managing dependencies, providing GPU access, and scaling computations.
Rendering containerization is one practical means of addressing these tasks.
Rendering containerization can be understood as the organization of the
image-generation process in which rendering software components, libraries,
shaders, auxiliary utilities, and part of the runtime environment are packaged
into an isolated container image. Unlike full virtualization, a container does
not require a separate guest operating system for each instance; therefore, it
introduces lower overhead and starts faster. This is important for computer
graphics tasks because the number of rendering nodes can be scaled within a
single frame, a sequence of frames, or a batch of independent tasks.
A distinctive feature of graphics computing is that a container must obtain
controlled access to a hardware accelerator. NVIDIA Container Toolkit provides
a mechanism for attaching GPUs to OCI-compatible containers and makes it
possible to specify which graphics devices and driver capabilities are visible
inside the container1. This provides a basis for reproducible deployment of

Studies devoted to the parallel generation of three-dimensional scenes
emphasize that rendering efficiency is determined not only by the aggregate
performance of computing resources but also by the method of task decomposition
and workload distribution among executors2. Containerization does not replace
parallelization algorithms; rather, it creates a standardized software layer on
which these algorithms can be implemented for heterogeneous nodes. It is
therefore appropriate to regard containerization as an infrastructural method
for organizing distributed rendering.
1. Architectural Principles of Containerized Rendering
A typical architecture of a containerized rendering system includes
a control component, scene and resource storage, a set of containerized
executors, and a mechanism for aggregating results. The control component
accepts jobs, determines available resources, and forms units of work. An
executor receives a scene description or a reference to shared storage, camera
parameters, a frame range, or an image region, and then starts rendering inside
the container. The result may be a completed frame, a tile, a set of samples, or
an intermediate buffer.

to be coordinated with the container life cycle.
2. Main Methods of Rendering Containerization
Depending on which part of the image-generation process is isolated and
scaled, several containerization methods can be distinguished. They differ
in the unit of work distribution, memory and network requirements, and the
mechanism used to assemble the result.

2.1. Frame-Based Containerization
The frame-based method is the simplest: each container receives one frame
or a sequence of frames. The scene, materials, and rendering configuration
remain the same, while primarily the time and camera parameters change. This
scheme is well suited to offline animation rendering because there is almost no
synchronization between containers. After computation is completed, frames

are sent directly to the result storage. A disadvantage is that the complexity of
adjacent frames may differ substantially. A container assigned a complex frame
becomes the “long tail” of the batch while other GPUs remain idle.
2.2. Spatial or Tile-Based Containerization
With the tile-based method, a frame is divided into rectangular regions. Each
container renders one or more tiles, and the final image is produced by composing
them. This approach is particularly useful for high resolutions and nonuniform
fragment-processing costs. Tile size determines the trade-off between balancing
accuracy and overhead. Small tiles provide better workload equalization but
increase the number of tasks, input/output operations, and scheduler messages.
Large tiles reduce infrastructure overhead but decrease adaptability.
2.3. Sample- and Pass-Based Containerization
For ray tracing and stochastic methods, an image can be generated as the
sum of independent or partially independent samples. Containers are assigned
to different sample ranges, layers, lighting passes, or ray sets. The results are
accumulated after computation. The method scales well when the composition
operation is less expensive than the tracing itself. At the same time, deterministic
control of random-number generators and compatibility of intermediate buffer
formats must be ensured.
2.4. Pipeline Containerization
The pipeline method places individual stages in specialized containers:
geometry preparation, construction of acceleration structures, rasterization,
tracing, denoising, and post-processing. Its advantage is the ability to use
different types of resources. For example, CPU nodes can prepare data, GPU
nodes can perform the main rendering, and dedicated accelerators can perform
neural-network denoising. However, transferring large buffers between stages
can negate performance gain; therefore, pipeline containerization should be
used when stage boundaries involve relatively compact data or when shared
high-speed storage is available.
2.5. Service-Based Containerization
In the service-based approach, the renderer operates as a long-running
containerized service. The client sends scene parameters or an image-generation
request through an API. This approach is convenient for remote visualization
systems, CAD/CAE, e-commerce, preview generation, and interactive cloud
applications. Unlike a one-time container launch, a long-running service can

3. GPU Access and Resource Management
A key distinction between containerizing graphics tasks and conventional
microservices is the need for direct GPU use. A container does not emulate a
graphics processor; instead, it receives access to specified host devices. NVIDIA
Container Toolkit includes runtime components, a hook, and the libnvidiacontainer library, which configures the container and attaches the required GPUs
and driver capabilities4. This approach allows the driver to remain on the host
system while the versions of user-space libraries and the renderer itself are fixed
in the image.
From a scheduling perspective, it is appropriate to distinguish between
exclusive and shared GPU use. In the exclusive model, one container receives
the entire device for the duration of execution. This simplifies performance
prediction and reduces mutual interference between tasks. In the shared
model, several containers compete for compute units, memory, and bandwidth.
This scheme increases GPU utilization for small tasks but makes rendering
time less stable.
Video-memory capacity is critical for rendering. If geometry, textures, BVH
structures, and intermediate buffers exceed the available memory, additional
transfers are required or the task cannot be executed. Therefore, the scheduler
must take into account not only computational performance pj but also resource
constraints: VRAMj ≥ VRAMi, supported graphics APIs, driver version, and the
set of hardware capabilities.
Studies of high-performance computer graphics system architecture
demonstrate the need to coordinate algorithmic and hardware resources and
to select an appropriate structure for parallel execution5. Containerization
additionally provides environmental reproducibility: the same image can be
deployed on different nodes provided that the driver and GPU are compatible.
This reduces the number of errors associated with different versions of libraries,
shader compilers, and auxiliary components.

4. Load Balancing in a Containerized Environment
Static distribution, in which each container is assigned the same number of
frames or tiles in advance, is effective only when task complexities are similar.
In three-dimensional graphics, this condition is often not satisfied. Even equally
sized tiles may differ in the number of visible polygons, overlaps, transparent
surfaces, light sources, and complex materials. Therefore, a dynamic task queue
is appropriate for containerized rendering.
After completing the current tile, a container requests the next queue item.
This scheme automatically directs more work to faster GPUs. It can be improved
by predictive estimation of tile cost based on statistics from previous frames.
Bobko and Romanyuk proposed considering the history of computational
complexity and renderer characteristics to reduce imbalance6. In a containerized
implementation, the prediction can be used by the orchestrator when selecting
not only the executor but also the size of the next portion of work.

5. Methods for Storing and Transferring Graphics Data
A container image should not contain large sets of scenes and textures if
they change independently of the software code. It is more rational to separate the immutable runtime environment from the input data. Scenes can be
mounted as volumes, retrieved from object storage, or cached locally on a node.
This reduces image size and accelerates deployment.

The network component becomes critical during distributed rendering of

and effective network bandwidth by B, the lower-bound estimate of transfer time
is (Din+Dout)/B, excluding latency and contention. For complex scenes, this time
may exceed the benefit of adding another GPU. Therefore, containerization should
be combined with locality-aware scheduling, in which a task is directed, whenever
possible, to a node where the required resources are already cached.
6. Reproducibility, Isolation, and Scaling
An important advantage of containerization is the reproducibility of the
software environment. The renderer version, libraries, configuration files, and
auxiliary programs are fixed in the image. This simplifies the repetition of experiments, comparison of algorithms, and deployment of the same software
version on several computing nodes. For scientific research, this property is particularly valuable because it makes it possible to separate algorithmic changes
from incidental changes in the software environment.
Container isolation also makes it possible to run different renderer versions
in parallel. For example, a reference implementation and an experimental modification can be executed on the same cluster without dependency conflicts. The
results should nevertheless be compared to identical or normalized hardware
configurations because the container itself does not eliminate differences between GPUs.
Horizontal scaling is implemented by adding executor containers. However,
linear speedup can be expected only when the share of independent work is sufficiently large and the network, storage, and composition stage do not become
bottlenecks. In the general case, speedup S(m)=T1/Tm is limited by sequential
operations and orchestration overhead. Therefore, increasing the number of
containers should be accompanied by measuring efficiency E=S(m)/m.
Containerization is well suited to heterogeneous environments but requires
resource classes to be described. The scheduler must know whether a node supports the required API, memory capacity, and hardware ray-tracing features. For
different GPU classes, it is advisable to maintain a performance profile that can

Practical Rendering Containerization Scheme
Practical implementation should be based on the principle of an immutable
image renderer and an external job description. The image contains the executable module, the required user-space GPU libraries, base-configuration shaders, and an executor agent. At startup, the container receives a task identifier,
rendering parameters, a storage address, and an available GPU. Large scene resources are mounted or downloaded separately.
The workflow can be organized as follows: 1) analyze the scene and form a
set of tasks; 2) estimate their complexity; 3) determine the available GPU nodes;
4) launch or reuse containers; 5) dynamically assign frames, tiles, or samples;
6) collect timing and utilization metrics; 7) compose the results; 8) update the
historical complexity model. This scheme directly integrates the container infrastructure with renderer load-balancing methods.
For interactive applications, it is appropriate to use a pool of pre-started
containers. This minimizes first-frame latency. For batch offline rendering, by
contrast, containers can be created on demand and terminated after a group of
frames has been processed. For tile-based rendering, a hybrid approach is effective: a container remains alive for longer than a single tile and sequentially receives tasks from the queue while retaining a local scene cache.
Thus, a container should be regarded not as the smallest unit of parallelism
but as an isolated executor within which the GPU performs massively parallel
operations, while the orchestrator distributes larger units of work among executors. This avoids excessive container-creation overhead while preserving the
flexibility of the distributed system.
Conclusions
Containerization of three-dimensional graphics rendering is a method for
organizing a software and computing environment that provides isolation of
rendering nodes, reproducibility of dependencies, controlled GPU access, and
horizontal scaling. Its efficiency is determined not only by container technology
but primarily by the appropriate choice of the graphics-task decomposition unit.
For animation sequences, frame-based containerization is appropriate; for
images with non-uniform complexity, tile-based containerization is suitable;

METHODS FOR ACCELERATING THE RENDERING
OF THREE-DIMENSIONAL SCENES
Rendering of three-dimensional scenes is the final and at the same time
one of the most resource-intensive stages of computer graphics. At this stage,
geometric representations of objects, material parameters, light sources,
camera settings, textures, and auxiliary data structures are transformed into
a two-dimensional image. Modern software systems impose contradictory
requirements on rendering performance: realism must be increased, geometric
complexity must grow, high image resolutions must be supported, and
interactive frame rates must be maintained. Rendering acceleration should
therefore be considered not as the optimization of one isolated algorithm, but
as the coordinated design of an efficient graphics pipeline.
