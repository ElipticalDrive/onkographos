Abstract
As the amount of global data is growing exponentially, lossy compression technology has become an inevitable trend in artificial intelligence and big data
applications. However, existing compression algorithms are difficult to further
improve the compression ratio while ensuring compression performance. Therefore, this study proposes a data prediction compression framework based on
a lightweight perceptron model. Our work mainly includes three aspects of
innovation: (1) introducing positive and negative factors to achieve effective conversion from relative domain to absolute domain and eliminate additional symbol
storage;(2) building a lightweight four-layer perceptron network to improve prediction accuracy;(3) modifying the data selection rules of the prediction surface
to achieve parallel compression within the compression block and improve the
compression speed. In the comparative experiment with SZ2.1’s PW REL, our
method achieved a compression ratio improvement of up to 17.78% on multiple
test sets.

1 Introduction
With the exponential growth in data generation, data storage and retrieval face significant challenges. Early computers were primarily used for scientific calculations, but
as the scope of applications expanded, the volume of data to be processed increased
1
significantly. This data is not only diverse and complex but has also grown markedly
with advancements in AI and big data. According to IDC’s ”Data Age 2025” [1] report,
global data production is expected to rise from 33ZB in 2018 to 175ZB by 2025. In this
digital era, effective and accurate data processing becomes crucial. However, the size of
retrieval indexes grows with large datasets, increasing load times from disk to memory
and affecting user search experience. Furthermore, big data presents new challenges to
existing data management technologies. While parallel database technology uses multiple processors to enhance processing speed, it also incurs high hardware costs. Data
compression offers an attractive solution to the growing data volume by significantly
reducing data size while retaining key information to maintain analytical accuracy.
Smaller data sizes also effectively increase the density of transmitted information without changing hardware transmission speeds. Lossless compression preserves 100% of
the information but offers limited compression ratios, insufficient for large-scale data
demands. Therefore, lossy compression with user-defined error control is necessary to
meet the requirements for data accuracy and large-scale execution.
In response, this paper proposes a lossy compression framework based on a
perceptron model, aiming to enhance compression performance while increasing
the compression ratio. By improving prediction accuracy, the framework achieves
consistently high compression quality. Specifically, our contributions are as follows:
• Introducing positive and negative factors based on the conventional relativeto-absolute domain transformation algorithm, thereby eliminating the need for
additional symbol storage.
• Employing a four-layer perceptron as the predictor for improved predictive accuracy.
• Modifying the data selection rules for predictive surfaces to enable parallelized
compression within compression blocks, enhancing compression speed.
• Compared to SZ2.1’s PW REL mode, we achieved a maximum compression ratio
increment of 17.78%.

2 Related Work
As an abundance of data continues to flood into society, the challenge of efficiently
processing massive datasets in an information-driven society becomes increasingly
pertinent. During this data processing, limitations in memory capacity can hinder
the ability to store all the extensive data for subsequent computations. Additionally,
the relatively slow data transfer speed between hard drives and memory can lead to
significant supplementary overheads resulting from multiple data transfers.
Data compression presents a viable solution to the aforementioned issues. By compressing data, it becomes possible to store more information in memory, thereby
reducing the additional costs associated with data swapping and enhancing memory
2
utilization. Furthermore, when compressed data streams are transmitted, it can to
some extent boost I/O speed, as a greater amount of information can be transmitted
within a unit of time. Thus, compression technology offers a positive solution for vast
datasets processing in addressing both data volume and data swapping speed.
To address these challenges, a series of lossless compression techniques have been
developed, such as Gzip [2], FPC [3], BlosC [4], and various other lossless compressors
[5] [6] [7]. Despite their widespread application, these lossless compression techniques
do not yield significant compression gains for scientific data due to their reliance on
repeated byte streams. Scientific data is typically composed of diverse floating-point
arrays and is characterized by a massive scale, necessitating higher compression ratios
with some tolerance for precision loss. Therefore, research into lossy compression for
scientific data has been ongoing for several years [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16].
Many existing lossy compression techniques lack the capability to perform compression based on user-defined error bounds, which is a stringent requirement for
numerous scientific applications. Scientific applications necessitate the establishment
of an error threshold to ensure the acquisition of accurate simulations. Error-controlled
lossy compression is aimed at significantly reducing data volume while keeping the
loss of precision in the decompressed data within acceptable limits. However, such
compressors are typically designed and optimized with the objective of maximizing
compression ratios under given absolute error bounds or PSNR constraints. In the
section 3, we will delve into the per-point relative error bounds of state-of-the-art
lossy compressors, and in the following section 4, we will introduce our modification
approach.
Presently, the leading lossy compressors for scientific data are primarily ZFP [8]
and SZ3 [17]. In our work, we have chosen a prediction-based model because SZ is
widely recognized as a leading compressor in the scientific data compression domain.
In fact, research on improving compression quality using SZ has been ongoing for
more than five years. SZ originated from the Lorenzo predictor and introduced a comprehensive compression and decompression process [18]. Subsequently, Di and others
proposed a technique for mapping multidimensional data to one dimension and made
predictions through optimal curve fitting [19]. Tao and colleagues expanded Lorenzo’s
one-dimensional prediction to the multidimensional case, broadening the applicability
of SZ [20]. They also proposed combining SZ and ZFP to achieve better compression
results [21]. Liang and others introduced three prediction techniques: the classical
Lorenzo predictor, the average Lorenzo predictor, and linear regression prediction,
aiming to estimate which predictor is more effective and thus select the most suitable
one [22]. Zhao and team introduced second-order regression prediction and parameter
selection methods to enhance prediction accuracy [23], and later proposed dynamic
cubic spline interpolation for prediction [24]. Inspired by the field of artificial intelligence, people are trying to utilize neural networks for lossy compression. AE-SZ
based on autoencoder [25] and CoordNet based on coordinate grid [26] and SRN-SZ
combining super-resolution and grid [27]. Liang also proposed a data transformation
approach [28], converting the point-wise relative error estimation compression problem into an absolute error estimation compression problem, thus making relative error
an optional criterion for compression.

4 Overall Framework
Within this section, our initial focus lies in retrospectively examining the existing
architecture of SZ. Subsequently, we conduct a thorough analysis of the challenges
intrinsic to the SZ compression mechanism. An in-depth understanding of these challenges is of paramount importance for appreciating the profound significance of our
proposed resolution in substantially augmenting compression efficiency.
4.1 A Review of the SZ Lossy Compression Framework
Based on the research and application efforts of numerous scholars, the SZ compressor
[22] has been widely acknowledged as an exemplary lossy compression tool.
The SZ compressor encompasses four distinct stages:
7
• Prediction: In the prediction phase, SZ forecasts the current data by utilizing
neighboring points, employing various prediction methods in different versions.
• Linear Scale Quantization: In this phase, SZ computes the prediction error between
the forecasted outcome from the previous stage and the actual data. This prediction
error is then linearly quantized into integers based on the absolute error bound,
producing quantization codes.
• Huffman Coding: SZ subsequently transforms the quantization codes into Huffman
codes using a tailored Huffman coding scheme.
• Lossless Compression: Lastly, SZ employs a lossless compression algorithm to
further enhance the compression ratio of the SZ compressor.
Firstly, SZ is a highly flexible compression framework, where data prediction is
the most critical step. More accurate prediction will lead to the concentration of
prediction errors of data tending to zero, which can be quantified in a smaller range
and compressed with Huffman coding using fewer bits, resulting in higher compression
ratio. Therefore, how to optimize the prediction process so that any sequence can be
transformed into a small-range sequence after prediction will be the focus of the next
step.
At the heart of SZ’s prediction mechanism lies the utilization of previously forecasted neighboring points to predict the current target point. Its core formula is

4.3 Training
4.3.1 Selection of Model Parameters
In this section, we begin by partitioning the data to be compressed into sub-blocks
according to the computational requirements. Subsequently, we aggregate neighboring sub-blocks into larger blocks, aiming to maximize the utilization of nearby
data to enhance predictive capabilities. During the compression process, we operate
on the aggregated data blocks. Following this, we uniformly sample from the data
9
blocks to form a training set, allowing the perceptron to achieve improved predictive performance. Experimental findings indicate that a uniform sampling rate of
10% is relatively stable, significantly reducing the size of the training set without
compromising data features.
Subsequently, we partition the training set data into training and validation sets
in a ratio of 8:2. During the training phase, we add L2 regularization to enhance
the model’s generalization capability [36]. Upon the three hyperparameters, we focus
on the selection of learning rate and regularization parameter while disregard the
number of quantization bits in this context. To streamline hyperparameter tuning
during training, we concurrently set multiple groups of hyperparameters for parallel
training, as shown in Fig.4. The final selection involves choosing the results from the
group with the best predictive performance, i.e., the group exhibiting the smallest
range of prediction errors.

4.3.2 Selection of Quantization Bit Depth
During the training process, model parameters need to be trained only once, as they
primarily influence the predictive capability of the model. In situations where data
features remain relatively constant, changing the relative error threshold does not
necessitate retraining model parameters. However, the selection of the quantization
bit depth requires adjustment for different relative error thresholds. Thus, it is imperative to fix the model parameters obtained in Section 4.3.1 and validate the optimal
quantization bit depth under the best relative error conditions on the dataset.

a predetermined value for m, we can initially determine the maximum value for

Generally, a smaller coverage leads to a higher compression ratio because more

Additionally, determining what constitutes the best effect requires simulating a real
compression process on the dataset. We simulate the compression process by sequentially undergoing prediction, quantization, Huffman coding, and writing to bitstream.
We calculate the compression ratio at this point and perform decompression to calculate the relative error. In practice, a model with a higher compression ratio and a
relative error within the user-defined range is considered to have a better compression
effect.
Once we have obtained a suitable model and determined the value of m, a validation stage is necessary. In this stage, we need to fix the previously obtained
best-performing model parameters and the corresponding m value. We simulate the
entire compression process on the validation set, obtaining the quantization code frequency for each data block. Subsequently, we calculate the total frequency of each
quantization code across all data blocks, obtaining the average frequency of the quantization code for each data block. Based on this average value, we construct a Huffman
tree to derive the corresponding Huffman code, which is recorded for direct retrieval
during the compression phase. During training, we construct the Huffman tree each
time to obtain the optimal Huffman code, aiming for the best compression effect.
However, during actual compression, our Huffman code is not the optimal solution
for each compressed block but rather a suboptimal solution based on the validation
set samples. Nevertheless, this approach saves a significant amount of time spent on
constructing the Huffman tree.
4.4 Compressed Stage
Within the compression stage, the initial data is employed as the input and subjected
to prediction through the trained model, yielding prediction deviations. These deviations are sequentially subjected to quantization coding and Huffman coding processes,
culminating in the encoding being written into a bitstream.
11
4.4.1 Compression Process
During the compression process, we have made minimal alterations, which continue
to involve four distinct steps, as follows:
• Prediction Process: In this phase we follow the rules for prediction-dependent data
selection in the Lorenzo predictor [23]. Initially, we select an appropriate number
of prediction layers, which determine the selection of data points on the prediction
surface. Subsequently, we pass the data points on the prediction surface through
the trained perceptron, obtaining the perceptron’s predictive outputs.
• Quantization Process: In this stage, we calculate the prediction error, defined as the
difference between the predicted value and the true value. Subsequently, we quantize
the prediction errors. During quantization, we assess whether the prediction error
falls within the quantization range. If it falls within the quantization range, we
obtain the corresponding quantization code for that prediction error. If it exceeds
the quantization range, we assign a quantization code of 0, which serves as a flag
indicating uncompressed data. Simultaneously, we include the original value of the
data point in an uncompressed data set.
• Encoding Process: In this stage, we exclusively process data points that fall within
the quantization range. Specifically, we encode the quantization codes obtained
in the previous step using the Huffman coding table derived during the training process. Similarly, the flag 0 representing uncompressed data is encoded as 0.
Consequently, in the Huffman coding table, 0 is encoded as 0. Subsequently, we
sequentially write all the Huffman codes obtained into a bitstream.
• Decompression Stage: In this stage, we first reconstruct the Huffman tree based
on the Huffman coding table. Subsequently, we decode the bitstream to recover
the quantization codes. If the decoded result is 0, it signifies that the data point
exists in the uncompressed data set, and we retrieve a value from the uncompressed
data set. Otherwise, we employ the inverse process of quantization to restore the
quantization code to a prediction error. Additionally, we perform prediction once
more to obtain the predictive value for each data point. The restored data point
value before compression is achieved by adding the decompressed prediction error
to the predictive value.
4.4.2 Selection of Predictive Surface Data
During the prediction phase, it is noted that the SZ compressor utilizes prior prediction
data as the input for subsequent predictions. This methodology effectively maintains
consistency in the decompression stage, thereby ensuring that data remains entirely
within the scope of the error range. However, it has been observed that such predictions often progressively deviate from the original data. We postulate that employing
authentic values of the data slated for compression as input could yield enhanced
predictive outcomes, consequently boosting the overarching compression ratio. However, this paradigm introduces a quandary during the decompression phase, wherein
the decompressed data must similarly serve as prediction input. Yet, the compressed
data is inevitably tainted by errors, and the persistent utilization of flawed data
12
as model input would inexorably culminate in error accrual, potentially leading to
unmanageable ramifications.
To circumvent the predicament of cumulative errors, we propose two methodologies.
Suitable regularization parameters
Throughout the training phase, we engage in parallelized training featuring multiple
regularization parameters, aiming to acquire models showcasing divergent generalization capacities. By subjecting the models to straightforward decompression
assessments on the validation dataset, we identify the minimal regularization parameter that forestalls excessive error accumulation while simultaneously aligning with the
stipulated user-defined error thresholds. This course of action ensures that predictive
accuracy is preserved to a reasonable extent while adhering to the user-prescribed
error tolerance.
Balance Points
During the compression phase, instances of data that extend beyond the quantization
range are frequently generated, subsequently conserved as uncompressed data. In the
course of decompression, whenever a marker signifying uncompressed data is encountered, data is directly retrieved from the uncompressed data stream. Consequently,
this uncompressed data remains unblemished by errors and is denoted as balance
points. The presence of balance point mechanisms fosters a tendency toward error
convergence during the prediction phase of decompression, specifically when balance
points are manifest on the predictive surface. Thus, by harnessing balance points, the
compressor can exercise control over errors within a delimited span, either adhering
to or marginally surpassing the user-specified relative error prerequisites.
Given that a certain quantity of balance points inherently exists within the compression phase, our primary strategy involves adjusting regularization parameters to
manage errors while optimizing the compression ratio. We would resort to deliberately
introducing a defined number of balance points into naturally sparse regions of balance
points to achieve targeted error control only when identifying suitable regularization
parameters becomes infeasible.
4.4.3 Parallelized Prediction
In prior research, due to the necessity of knowing the predictive values of nearby
points for data prediction, it was not possible to parallelize prediction simultaneously.
However, to enhance predictive accuracy, we ensured that the data on the prediction
surface were derived from the actual values of previously predicted data rather than
their predictive values. This adjustment enabled parallel prediction. During the compression stage, only the actual values of nearby points for the data to be compressed
are required for prediction. However, during the decompression stage, we must first
restore the decompressed values of nearby points to proceed with the prediction of the
current data point. After prediction, we add the decompressed prediction error to predict the next data point. Consequently, the decompression stage can only be executed
sequentially. In contrast, during the compression stage, all data points to be predicted
13
depend on actual values, allowing for the direct retrieval of these values. This enables
us to initiate predictions from any data point without affecting the overall predictive
results. Thus, we have established the reliability of parallel prediction methods.
We allocate the data blocks to be compressed to each thread according to a static
task assignment to facilitate concurrent prediction. In consideration of load balancing
and locality principles, we employ a static row-based partitioning when dividing data
within a data block. Since boundary points of the data consistently exhibit larger
prediction errors than points within the data block, due to their reduced available data
for reference, we place the starting task points for each thread at the boundary points
of the data block, as shown in Fig.5. These boundary points are designated as balance
points to ensure that during decompression, boundary points introduce fewer errors,
thereby controlling the overall error. This approach requires fewer balance points in
comparison to initiating parallel tasks from points within the center of the data block,
thus further enhancing the compression performance of the compressor.
Fig. 5 Multi-threaded simultaneous prediction from different starting points.
Certainly, if users prioritize precision and do not overly concern themselves with
compression speed, we have included the option for parallelization. Users can choose
whether to enable the parallelization process. When users disable the parallelization
process, the system reverts to the SZ selection procedure. In this mode, during the
compression stage, the predictive values of nearby data points are taken as the basis for
prediction, and during the decompression stage, these predictive values are similarly
used as inputs for predicting the next data point. This approach ensures that the
entire compression process occurs within the user-defined predictive error bounds,
thereby maximizing data accuracy.

For the training phase, we start by sampling the original data to form a training

prediction performance of the corresponding trained perceptron, thereby enhancing
the overall compression ratio. Subsequently, we input the training dataset into the
initialized perceptron and conduct parallel training with multiple sets of hyperparameters. During the training process, we also obtain the m-value mentioned in Section
4.3. Following this, from the multiple sets of models, we choose the group of models
with the most effective performance and the corresponding Huffman codes. These two
components will be applied in the actual compression process.
As for the compression process, we begin by passing the data to be compressed to
the pre-trained model to obtain its predicted value. Next, we calculate the difference
between the predicted value and the original value, forming the prediction error, which
is then quantized. The range of quantization error should be calculated based on the
m-value obtained during the training process. Data beyond the quantization range
is referred to as balance points, and we save the original information of these data
points and write a flag for balance point data in the bitstream. For data within the
quantization range, we encode it into Huffman codes using the Huffman code keyvalue pairs obtained during the training phase and finally write it into the bitstream.
The result of this process is the compressed data stream, i.e., the bitstream, along
15
with the balance point dataset, and the associated f actorpos , f actorneg for this data
block. These data will be utilized in the decompression process.
During the decompression process, we reconstruct the Huffman tree based on the
Huffman code key-value pairs. Then, we read bit by bit from the bitstream and decode
accordingly using the Huffman tree to obtain the corresponding quantization code.
If the read quantization code is a flag for balance point data, we directly retrieve
the corresponding data from the balance point set. If it is not a flag for balance
point data, we restore its prediction error in accordance with the inverse process
of the quantization process. Simultaneously, our perceptron will traverse from the
beginning of the data and use the prediction error for correction to ensure the accuracy
of the current predicted data and the accuracy of predicting the next data bit.The

However, at relative error levels exceeding 20%, due to the requirement of a certain

to prevent excessive error accumulation, our solution’s compression ratio is likewise
lower than SZ. It is noteworthy that within the 0.2% to 20% relative error range,
our method consistently outperforms SZ, achieving a maximum compression ratio

larger tolerances allowing the use of fewer quantization codes, reducing the bit length
of the constructed Huffman codes and ultimately resulting in a smaller size of the
compressed bitstream. Similar trends are observable in other datasets.
We conducted comprehensive tests on datasets such as ISABEL, NYX, and USGS,
and Fig.12 illustrates the variation trends of compression ratios for the two methods at
different scales. We observed that, at certain scales, our solution exhibits higher compression ratios compared to SZ. This can be attributed to the presence of our training
process and the deeper neural network, which enhances the precision of the prediction process. Consequently, in the quantization process, we require fewer quantization
codes to cover the majority of prediction errors, effectively improving compression
efficiency.
In this regard, our approach, facilitated by more refined training and a deeper network structure, brings about enhanced adaptability for data compression, especially
when dealing with specific scales or datasets. This advantage contributes to the higher
efficiency of our solution in practical applications of data compression.

7 Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we have modified the existing mapping formula from relative domain
to absolute domain and introduced an innovative approach. We utilized a neural
network framework as a predictor and introduced a block-wise parallel structure by
modifying the selection of points on the prediction surface. Comparative analysis with
the PW REL mode of the SZ 2.1 version was conducted to evaluate the compression
ratio and performance of our solution. The primary research findings are as follows:
• Our analysis indicates that the structure of the neural network predictor is complex,
and the additional time overhead introduced cannot be neglected.
• In comparison with SZ’s relative error mode, employing our solution improves compression ratios at commonly used relative error scales, with an increase of up to
17.78% in optimal scenarios.
24
• Our solution demonstrates compression performance similar to SZ 2.1 but incorporates a block-wise parallel structure, providing higher performance improvement
potential compared to SZ.
In future work, we plan to enhance compression quality further by optimizing the
neural network prediction model, designing appropriate network structures, adjusting
relevant model hyperparameters, and introducing suitable algorithms. Meanwhile, we
will also conduct further research to further reduce the relative error generated by
compression while keeping the compression speed and compression ratio constant.
Finally, efforts will be directed towards optimizing the code implementation to increase
practical feasibility and efficiency for real-world applications.
