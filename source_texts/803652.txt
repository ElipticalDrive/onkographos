Abstract: Clinical imaging resolution is often insufficient for accurate ultrasound simulation in personalised treatment planning. Naive image upsampling algorithms introduce artifacts that reduce simulation accuracy. We develop a Gaussian smoothing and mesh-based upsampling method and evaluate upsampled image fidelity and ultrasound simulation accuracy using an
ex vivo high resolution human cervical vertebra volume obtained with hierarchical phase-contrast tomography, against linear
and nearest neighbour interpolation. The mesh-based method significantly and consistently reduced L2 and L1 errors and
reduced focal errors with inconsistent significance. Medical image upsampling for ultrasound simulation should be completed
with edge-aware methods to improve accuracy. CV 2026 Author(s). All article content, except where otherwise noted, is licensed under a

Therapeutic ultrasound applications extend from targeted cancer treatment to non-invasive deep brain neuromodulation
(Martin et al., 2025; Vidal-Jove et al., 2022). The development of new therapeutic ultrasound applications is often enabled
by the adoption of advanced simulation methods (Aubry et al., 2022). For example, ultrasound neuromodulation is a
developing tool in personalized medicine. Subject-specific simulations can be used to estimate treatment pressures and
heating, giving confidence in the safety and efficacy of the treatment (Martin et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024). Subject-specific
simulations may also be used to calculate transducer element phase and amplitude corrections (Martin et al., 2025) or to
design acoustic holograms (Jimenez-Gambõn et al., 2019) for focusing through aberrating media such as the skull.
One limitation in patient-specific ultrasound simulation is medical image resolution. The resolution of a clinical
magnetic resonance image is typically 1 mm (isotropic), while clinical x-ray computed tomography resolution is typically
0.5 mm in plane and 1–2 mm between planes. Accurate grid-based ultrasound simulations typically require 6–12 grid
points per wavelength, which at the commonly utilized ultrasound frequency of 500 kHz and in water or most soft tissues,
corresponds to an isotropic resolution of 0.5 to 0.25 mm (Robertson et al., 2017). Prior to ultrasound simulation, these
images are typically segmented into labels, with each label representing a different type of tissue or material. Simple image
segmentation can be completed manually or semi-automatically using threshold-based methods (Yushkevich et al., 2016).
Complex tasks, such as identifying specific tissue types such as vertebral number, can now be performed using deeplearning approaches (Saillard et al., 2024). The resulting label-map is then used to define the acoustic and thermal properties throughout the simulation grid. The resolution of these segmented images and the accuracy of the segmentation tends
to limit simulation accuracy (Robertson et al., 2017).
An “upsampling” process is required to bridge the gap between image resolution and the required simulation resolution. However, naive interpolation-based upsampling methods, such as nearest neighbour or linear interpolation, may
lead to “staircasing” effects at a detriment to simulation accuracy (Drainville et al., 2019; Robertson et al., 2017). A challenge lies in developing an upsampling method that can increase the resolution of segmented medical images smoothly,
without introducing staircasing effects or other artifacts. The upsampling method should also be able to partially smooth
staircasing artifacts introduced by poor image segmentation.

Finally, the surface mesh is rasterized back onto the target grid at the required resolution, generating an upsampled voxelized image suitable for ultrasound simulation.
The workflow was implemented in Python using publicly available libraries for image and mesh processing.
Gaussian smoothing is performed with scipy.ndimage (kernel size 4) (Keerthi and Lin, 2003; Virtanen et al., 2020).
Then, a surface mesh is generated with the vtkFlyingEdges3D algorithm (Schroeder et al., 2006; Schroeder et al.,
2015). Beyond approximating anatomical boundaries, the smoothing step improves the distinction between surface voxels
near the isovalue and voxels well inside or outside the object. This distinction is exploited during rasterization, where a
voxel-wise distance filter (vtkDistanceFilter) only needs to identify voxels close to the surface (Quammen et al.,
2011; Schroeder et al., 2006) to ensure accurate surface representation while reducing computational overhead. The
Gaussian smoothing value r and the isovalue I can be selected automatically using the segmentation sphericity or optimised against a ground truth. While other functions including multi-label applications were not tested in this paper, the
method has been extended for such uses. Complete function documentation and implementation details are available in
the linked GitHub repository.

A well-defined ground truth is needed to quantitatively evaluate upsampled image accuracy. Simple geometric objects such
as spheres or cubes can be code-generated at arbitrary resolutions, but their relevance to anatomically realistic structures is
limited. To ensure our evaluation remained biologically relevant, hierarchical phase-contrast tomography (Mirone et al.,
2025; Walsh et al., 2021) images of an ex vivo human vertebra were used as the ground truth. These images were obtained
by the Human Organ Atlas Collaboration using the high-energy fourth-generation synchrotron source of the European
Synchrotron Radiation Facility, which is capable of imaging at up to cell-level resolution (Mirone et al., 2025; Walsh et al.,
2021). Simulations of ultrasound propagation through the human spine are relevant to ultrasonic spinal cord neuromodulation (Hou and Lei, 2025; Xu et al., 2024) and ultrasound and microbubble-enabled blood-spinal cord barrier opening for
drug delivery (Frizado and O’Reilly, 2023; Martin et al., 2024). Simulations of ultrasound propagation through bony interfaces including the skull and the spine remain an active area of research, as the acoustic impedance mismatch, speed of
sound, and irregular geometries can severely aberrate the focus of a therapeutic ultrasound beam. The vertebra and the vertebral canal is a particularly challenging geometry as the canal may act as a reverberant chamber, where errors in the gridbased bone interface amplify reflection errors in ultrasound pressure field simulations.
A 907  886  478 image of a fourth cervical vertebra (isotropic voxel spacing: 50 lm) was used to obtain a highly
detailed vertebral mask using intensity-based thresholding. The initially porous vertebral mask was filled using a semiautomatic gap filling process in 3D Slicer to produce a continuous vertebral body segmentation. The vertebra segmentation
was downsampled using the nearest-neighbour algorithm to generate a reference image of size ð454; 443; 239Þ with an isotropic resolution of 0:1 mm, which served as the ground truth. This downsampling was performed to obtain a tractable resolution
for 3D acoustic simulations. Two lower-resolution versions were obtained via further nearest-neighbour downsampling to isotropic resolution of 0.2 and 0.5 mm. The low-resolution images were then upsampled back to the reference resolution using
nearest neighbour, linear interpolation, and the proposed mesh-based method. Figure 2 depicts the experiment pipeline.
Nearest neighbour and linear interpolation are often used in biomedical ultrasound simulation for upsampling. The mesh
upsampling parameters for  2 and  5 upsampling were ðr ¼ 0:61; I ¼ 0:3Þ and ðr ¼ 0:56; I ¼ 0:43Þ, respectively.
Rounding during downsampling then upsampling back to ground truth resolution can introduce small differences in grid size
between the ground truth and upsampled volumes. The upsampled grids were cropped to the ground truth grid size using an
automated alignment algorithm that maximised the upsampled–ground truth Dice scores.
Acoustic simulation domains were defined with the upsampled and ground truth segmentation volumes. The vertebra was modelled as homogeneous; the objective of the analysis was to isolate differences in the acoustic field resulting
from errors in the upsampled vertebra interface position, not vertebral heterogeneity. Accordingly, the vertebra was given a

Ultrasound simulations were completed using the j-Wave compressional wave simulator in PYTHON (Stanziola et al., 2023).
Simulations were completed in 2D using transverse image slices and in 3D using the full upsampled and ground truth segmentation volumes. A flat acoustic aperture was placed along the superior boundary of the transverse plane (a 44.3 mm
line in 2D and a 45:4  44:3 mm plane in 3D), generating a downward (within image) pulse through the tissue domain.
The excitation signal was a 1 MHz sinusoid (monochromatic) of unitary magnitude. The measure of 1 MHz is in the frequency range of interest for improved trans-spine targeting resolution, and for simulation-based beamforming of spinal
microbubble emissions (Frizado and O’Reilly, 2025).
The field of ultrasound metrology currently lacks methods for directly measuring ultrasound pressure distributions within bone. To produce simulations with tentatively experimentally measurable outputs and to reduce our computer
memory requirements, only the vertebral canal was considered as the region of interest (ROI) for the ultrasound simulations. The ROI was extracted from 36 slices ([:, :, 86:121]) that correspond to the vertebral canal in the ground truth
image. To accommodate slight morphological variations across upsampling methods, the ROI mask was eroded and manually corrected to exclude bony voxels. 2D simulations were repeated with the 36 image slices, each upsampling method,
upsampling scale (5: 0.5 to 0.1 mm, and 2: 0.2 to 0.1 mm), source (planar, focused), and source signal (short pulse,
continuous wave). The shortest wavelength in the simulation domain was in water, which at 1 MHz is 1.5 mm. In points
per wavelength (PPW), here 5 upsampling corresponded to upsampling from 3 PPW to 15 PPW, and 2 upsampling
was from 7.5 to 15 PPW. 3D simulations were repeated in the same manner, but once per volume instead of by slice. Each
2D simulation required 1–2 min using an Intel Core i7-11800H @2.30 GHz processor, and the 3D simulations required
approximately 130 min using a 64 gigabyte, 8-core AMD EPYC 7742 node.
The error metrics employed to evaluate image-based and simulation-based upsampling performance are described
in Table 1. Common image and surface comparison metrics (Dice similarity coefficient, Jaccard index, and Average

Symmetric Surface Distance, ASSD) were used to evaluate the image upsampling accuracy. The Dice and Jaccard coefficients quantify volumetric similarity and misalignment, normalized by the total labelled volume. The average symmetric
surface distance (ASSD) is used as a surface comparison metric in anatomical image processing (Kim et al., 2024), and
measures the mean distance between corresponding surface voxels in the upsampled and ground-truth images. Common
pressure field comparison metrics (Aubry et al., 2022) were used to evaluate the errors between the ground truth simulated
pressure field and the pressure field obtained from the upsampled images.
In the short-pulse simulations, a three-cycle pulse was propagated for the duration required for the acoustic wave
to travel from top to bottom of the domain. For the continuous-wave simulations, the source was driven continuously for
a simulation duration corresponding to 1.5 times the top-to-bottom travel time. The Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) number was set to 0.3, giving a temporal discretisation of dt ¼ 10 ns for the spatial discretisation dm ¼ 0:1 mm. The CFL number was determined through a convergence test, benchmarked with a 2D cross-sectional simulation using a CFL of 0.05.
Both planar and focused source configurations were simulated: the planar source replicated the input signal uniformly
across all source voxels, while the focused source used time delays to achieve central focusing at a depth of 60 mm.
For the short-pulse simulations, the peak pressure field was extracted for comparison. For the continuous-wave simulations, the steady-state wave amplitude was extracted from the last three cycles. The L2 and L1 norms were computed
from the upsampled results and the ground truth to quantify global and maximum local errors, respectively. The focal position
and focal magnitude were also analysed; these are crucial parameters for treatment planning. In the continuous-wave simulations, the L2 and L1 norms were again used to assess errors against the ground-truth pressure fields. Paired statistical comparisons were completed for each 2D simulation condition and upsampling method. The normality of the paired differences
between the mesh-based method and the naive interpolation methods were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Then, paired
t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied accordingly, and the Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Only one 3D volume was available, which precluded the statistical comparison of the upsampling method errors in the
3D simulations. For brevity, the 3D errors are listed in the linked GitHub repository.

The 2D ultrasound simulations were compared to evaluate the importance of upsampled image fidelity for simulation
accuracy, and the resultant utility of our mesh-based image upsampling algorithm. The 2D simulation error analysis is
summarized in Fig. 4. The mesh-based method consistently yielded significantly (p < 0:001) lower L2 and L1 errors than
linear and nearest neighbour interpolation, and the effect size (Cohen’s d) often exceeded 2. In some cases, the mesh-based
method significantly improved the predictions of focal characteristics, but these improvements were not as consistent as
the L2 and L1 improvements. In several cases, there was no significance to the focal improvements. This indicates that L2
and L1 are more sensitive to the staircasing artifacts that impact the entire ROI, while the focus is less sensitive to errors
in the grid-based representation of the bone interface.
The mesh-based method improves L2 and L1 across all simulation configurations, including source field (planar
or focused), excitation mode (short-pulse or continuous-wave), and upsampling scale. Errors with dx ¼ 5 (3 to 15 PPW)
are larger than at dx ¼ 2 (7.5 to 15 PPW) using all upsampling methods; fine details are lost in excessively coarse grids and
these details cannot be retrieved with the tested methods. The magnitudes of the ultrasound simulation errors dwarf the
image similarity errors, particularly for the continuous wave simulations. There is an approximately tenfold increase in L2

normalized by the sum of voxel volumes, and a 1% deviation corresponds here to approximately 0.1  106 erroneous voxels

The presented mesh-based upsampling method produces visibly smoother surfaces, as shown in Fig. 3, and demonstrates small but consistent quantitative improvements over the conventional interpolation approaches, evaluated using
the Table 1 image similarity metrics, and reported in Table 2. Figure 3 displays the source configuration and an example
set of focused short pulse peak pressure fields in the vertebral canal. The single aperture trans-laminar approach generates
a focus near the centre of the canal. These waves are then reflected by the anterior edge of the canal into the lateral “corner” of the canal, where further reflections create a secondary higher-pressure focus. Therapeutic approaches to the spinal
cord often use dual apertures and aberration correction methods (Martin et al., 2024; Frizado and O’Reilly, 2025) to
improve focusing. These approaches are not investigated here to maintain the generalizability of our simulations to other
geometries but are worth considering for therapeutic ultrasound therapies in the spinal cord.

5. Summary
There are many potential sources of error in subject-specific ultrasound simulation, including registration errors and errors
in acoustic properties. This work shows that naive approaches to image upsampling can also generate substantial errors,
particularly in continuous wave simulations and in constrained and acoustically reverberant geometries like the vertebral
canal. Mesh-based upsampling improves the smoothness of upsampled segmentation interfaces, when compared to traditional upsampling methods. This smoothness reduces staircasing artifacts and significantly improves global metrics of simulation accuracy, evaluated here with simulations of transvertebral ultrasound propagation with a high resolution spine
