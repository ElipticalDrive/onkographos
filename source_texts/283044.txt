Abstract
Implicit neural representations (INRs) have been applied
to deformable image registration, where a lightweight network maps a spatial coordinate to a deformation vector.
Because an INR encodes one specific signal in its parameters, a fresh network must be optimized for every image
pair at test time, which is slow. Existing methods reduce
this cost by meta-learning an initialization across a population of image pairs, and condition the weights of the INR
globally, so the same conditioning applies at every coordinate while the deformation field it initializes varies across
the domain. We propose LUMINR, a meta-learning framework that accelerates this per-pair fitting and in which the
conditioning is local instead. LUMINR uses an INR that
maps a coordinate, together with local intensity and gradient features, to a deformation vector, so the conditioning
varies over the domain at the resolution of the field being
modelled. Its objective is to find an initialization of the INR
parameters from which the network can be fine-tuned to register any unseen pair in only a few optimization iterations.
We evaluate LUMINR on 3D deformable registration of
brain MRI from the OASIS dataset, where local conditioning and the meta-learned initialization prove complementary: early in fitting they reach a markedly higher accuracy than either does alone. Fine-tuned from that initialization, LUMINR matches the accuracy of the same conditioned INR fit from scratch in roughly a tenth of the gradient
steps and a tenth of the wall-clock time, while the deformation fields stay smooth with negligible folding.
1. Introduction
Implicit neural representations (INRs) parameterize complex signals as continuous functions, using a neural network to map input coordinates directly to signal values.
They are a compact, resolution-independent alternative to
discrete representations such as voxel grids. Because of
their versatile and continuous nature, INRs have been applied across many areas of medical imaging, including image reconstruction [13, 16, 22], dataset-level representation
learning [6], and predictive tasks such as segmentation [20].
Deformable image registration is another such task. An INR
can represent the coordinate transformation between an image pair directly: a lightweight MLP takes a continuous spatial coordinate as input and returns a deformation vector,
which is added back to the coordinate through a residual
connection to form the transformation [23].
For deformable registration, fitting an INR to each pair
has been reported to be more accurate than predicting the
transformation with a trained network [23]. Such feedforward encoder-decoder models are trained on large annotated collections and return a deformation field for an unseen pair directly [2, 9, 14]; once trained they run faster
than conventional iterative methods, but at reduced accuracy [23]. An INR is instead fit to the pair at hand
rather than amortized over a population, so registration
remains an optimization problem rather than a prediction
problem, and it is this per-pair optimization that accounts
for the accuracy.
These properties come with an important limitation. Because a learned INR is tied so closely to the signal it was
fit to, representing a new signal requires fitting a fresh network through iterative optimization. This is slow, and the
cost is more acute in deformable image registration, where
a separate network must be optimized for every fixed and
moving image pair at test time. Several methods aim to reduce this per-instance cost. The shared idea is to replace
a generic random initialization with one learned across
a population of signals, so that an INR fits an unseen signal in far fewer gradient steps [4, 18, 21]. Such learned
initializations have been studied for image fitting and reconstruction, and also for deformable image registration, where
the initialization is meta-learned over image pairs and then

REINDIR [19] goes one step further and conditions that
initialization on the pair to be registered. A convolutional
encoder embeds the fixed and moving images into a single vector, and per-layer heads turn it into modulation vectors, one repeated to fill each hidden weight matrix and one
multiplying the corresponding bias. These vectors specialize meta-learned template INRs before test-time optimization begins. The network itself still receives a bare spatial
coordinate as input, and because one vector describes the
whole pair, the same conditioning signal applies at every
coordinate, while the deformation field it initializes varies
across the domain.
That gap between a conditioning signal held constant
over the domain and a field that varies across it limits what
the initialization can hold. Vyas et al. [21] motivate their
work by observing that an INR partitions its input coordinate space finely around the spatial gradients of the one signal it was fit to, so that its features do not readily transfer
to a second signal even of similar content, and that the partition induced by a meta-learned initialization is closer to
random. A bare coordinate says nothing about which pair is
being registered, so an initialization shared across a population can encode only what all pairs have in common, and
the pair-specific structure is left to gradient descent at test
time. Sampling the two intensity fields and their spatial gradients at the query coordinate supplies that structure at the
input instead, where it varies from point to point, and the
initialization then has only to encode how to use it.
We propose LUMINR, a meta-learning [5, 12] framework for accelerated medical image registration in
which the conditioning is local.
The INR maps
a spatial coordinate together with the image intensities and gradient features sampled at that coordinate,
x̃ = [x, F (x), M (x), ∇F (x), ∇M (x)], to a deformation
vector, so the conditioning varies over the domain at the
resolution of the field being modelled. LUMINR then seeks
an initialization for that INR from which the network can
be fine-tuned quickly to any fixed and moving image pair
(Fig. 1). An inner loop adapts the parameters to a single sampled pair over several gradient steps by minimizing
the registration loss for that pair, and an outer loop moves
the shared initialization toward these adapted values [12].
Repeated across many pairs, this produces an initialization
from which fine-tuning converges to an accurate deformation field for an unseen pair in far fewer iterations than fitting from scratch.
We evaluate LUMINR on 3D deformable registration
of brain MRI from the OASIS dataset [8, 11], against
coordinate-only INR baselines fitted from random and from
meta-learned weights. Fine-tuned from the meta-learned
initialization, the network reaches the registration accuracy
of the same INR fit from scratch in roughly a tenth of the
gradient steps and a tenth of the wall-clock time. The acceleration holds under both a coarse and a fine anatomical
segmentation, and it grows as the labels become finer and
small boundary displacements matter more. The deformation fields stay smooth throughout fitting, with the fraction
of folding voxels below 0.001% at every checkpoint.
Contribution. Our contributions can be summarized
as follows:
• We introduce LUMINR, an INR framework for 3D
deformable registration whose conditioning is local.
Each coordinate is presented with the fixed and moving intensities and their gradients sampled at that point,
so the pair-specific signal varies over the domain,
whereas existing meta-learned INR registration specializes shared weights from one global embedding
of the pair.
• We meta-learn the initialization of this conditioned
network with Reptile, so that an unseen pair is fitted
from weights adapted across the training population
rather than from random ones.
• We show on OASIS brain MRI that local conditioning
and the meta-learned initialization are complementary,
and that together they shorten the test-time fit by
roughly an order of magnitude at no cost in accuracy
or deformation regularity.
2. Method
We consider the pairwise registration task for a given
dataset D = {(Fj , Mj )}N
j=1 of N three-dimensional fixed
and moving image pairs, where the goal is to recover a spatial transformation that aligns each moving image with its
fixed counterpart. Both images of a pair share a normalized domain, F, M : Ω → R with Ω ⊂ [−1, 1]3 . For
every image we precompute the spatial gradients ∇F and
∇M offline, so that at any continuous coordinate x ∈ Ω
we can sample the intensities F (x), M (x) ∈ R together
with their gradients ∇F (x), ∇M (x) ∈ R3 . Concatenating these with the coordinate yields the input feature vector
x̃ = [x, F (x), M (x), ∇F (x), ∇M (x)] ∈ Rd (d = 11), so
the network has access to local appearance context as well
as position.
LUMINR represents the deformation with an L-layer
MLP fθ : Rd → R3 that maps x̃ to a displacement
u(x̃). A residual connection adds the original coordinate
back to form the transformation Φ(x) = u(x̃) + x. The
warped moving image is obtained by sampling M at the
transformed coordinates Φ(x), with subvoxel intensities resolved through trilinear interpolation [23]. We seek the
transformation that maps each fixed-image coordinate to its
anatomically corresponding location in the moving image,
so that (M ◦ Φ)(x) = F (x) for all x ∈ Ω. LUMINR’s objective is to find an initialization θ∗ of the INR parameters
from which fθ (·) can be fine-tuned to satisfy this correspondence for any unseen pair in only a few optimization iterations [12, 23]. In the following Sec. 2.1, we describe how
Reptile meta-learning is used to obtain this initialization.
2.1. LUMINR: Initialization for Accelerated Medical Image Registration
LUMINR uses the Reptile meta-learning strategy [12] to
learn an initialization θ∗ from which the network adapts
quickly to a new fixed and moving image pair. We chose
Reptile over MAML [5] because MAML differentiates
through the inner-loop update and so requires the Hessian,
whose similarity term reaches the parameters through the
trilinear resampler that warps the moving image. Trilinear
interpolation is only C 0 : within a voxel cell the interpolant
is multilinear, so its pure second derivatives vanish identically, and the mixed terms that remain are in general discontinuous across cell faces. At those faces the true second
derivative is a singular measure that automatic differentiation cannot access. This path therefore contributes degenerate curvature. Reptile’s update depends only on the finetuned parameters, so it needs no second derivatives, and its
memory cost does not grow with the number of inner steps.
Training is a nested optimization with an inner and an
outer routine. The inner routine treats each pair as a separate task: given a sampled pair (Fj , Mj ), it adapts the current parameters θt by running Ti Adam steps [10] on the
registration loss of fθ (·), which yields the adapted weights
θ̃j [12, 23]. The outer routine begins from random values
θ0 at t = 0 and runs for To meta-iterations. At each iteration it calls the inner routine on a freshly sampled pair and
moves the shared parameters toward the adapted weights,
so that after training a few gradient steps on an unseen pair
are enough to reach a low registration loss.

2.2. Experimental setup
Dataset and preprocessing. We evaluated LUMINR on
aligned 3D brain MRI volumes from the OASIS dataset
[7, 8, 11]. We took 414 T1-weighted scans and split them
randomly into 280 training, 34 validation, and 100 test volumes. Each volume comes with segmentations at two granularities: a coarse set of 4 labels (cortex, subcortical gray
matter, white matter, and CSF) and a fine set of 35 labels for
individual anatomical regions. We normalized all volumes
to the intensity range [0,1] and kept the original resolution
of 160 x 192 x 224. In addition, we computed the image
gradients with a Sobel operator and used the raw gradient
magnitudes to build input feature vector.
OASIS contains unpaired images, so we built registration pairs explicitly. For training, each subject is paired
once with every other subject, which gives every unordered
fixed-moving combination. At each outer optimization step
we drew a new batch of 300 pairs at random from this pool,
and for each pair we evaluated the registration loss on a random minibatch of 10,000 spatial coordinates rather than the
full image grid. For evaluation we instead pair the 100 test
volumes consecutively, which gives 99 test pairs.
Implementation. For all experiments we used a SIREN
INR with L = 5 hidden layers, width h = 512, and
ω0 = 30. We optimized the network with Adam [10], using
a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 for the inner loop and 1 × 10−3
for the outer meta-learning loop. We set Ti = 100 inner
steps and ran the outer loop for To = 100 iterations, applying an α-weighted bending-energy regularizer with α =

Baseline. We compared our method against a baseline INR that receives only the spatial coordinate as input,
without the sampled intensity and gradient features, so that
fθ : R3 → R3 . Apart from the input, this baseline is identical to LUMINR: the same SIREN backbone, the same registration objective (2), and the same optimization settings
described above. The comparison therefore isolates the contribution of the enriched input representation.
We ran both models under two initialization regimes.
In the first, the network starts from randomly initialized
weights following the standard SIREN scheme, and is fit to
each unseen test pair for Tf = 500 iterations with the Adam
optimizer, as in the classical INR approach. In the second,
the same fitting procedure starts from the meta-learned parameters θ∗ . The meta-learning stage of the baseline used
the settings reported above for LUMINR. Together the four
configurations separate the effect of the intensity features
from the effect of the initialization, and show how each of
them affects registration speed and accuracy.
Grid-searched baseline. We also compared LUMINR
against a coordinate-only INR whose hyperparameters we
selected by grid search. It is a SIREN with two layers and
256 hidden features, optimized at a learning rate of 6.6 ×
10−5 . We evaluated this baseline in the same two regimes,
from scratch and from a meta-learned initialization obtained
with the same meta-learning parameters as described above.
We describe the search space and the selection procedure in
detail in the supplementary material (Sec. A).

3.1. Effect of the enriched input representation
Fitting from scratch. We began by analyzing the effect
of the enriched input representation on network fitting. We
observed that conditioning on local intensity features improves registration accuracy beyond the initial fitting iterations. LUMINR (random) attains a higher mean Dice score
than both coordinate-only baselines from iteration 50 onwards on the coarse labelling and from iteration 150 onwards on the fine one, and this ordering is preserved at every subsequent checkpoint (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Over the interval from iteration 50 to iteration 500 the conditioned model
gains more Dice score than either INR or INRGS on both labellings, its margin over each baseline attains its maximum
in the second half of the fitting schedule, and it remains
superior at the terminal iteration. We attribute this to the
input representation: because the fixed and moving intensities and their gradients are sampled at the query coordinate,
the network observes the local appearance mismatch that
the displacement must resolve rather than inferring it from
position alone.
Fitting from the meta-learned initialization. Since this
advantage needed several tens of iterations to appear, we
next asked whether a better starting point removes the delay. Starting from the meta-learned parameters, LUMINR
(meta) attains a higher mean Dice score than both INR
(meta) and INRGS (meta) at every evaluated iteration and
on both labellings (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). No early crossover of
the kind observed under random initialization occurs: the
+15.1%

advantage is present at the second iteration and persists after all models have plateaued. It is more pronounced on
the coarse labelling, where the margin over either baseline

that improve accuracy only from iteration 50 onwards on
the coarse labelling and from iteration 150 onwards on the

3.2. Accelerated medical image registration
We then measured what the combination of the two saves
in practice, by reading off the iteration count and the wallclock time at which each model reaches a matched Dice.
Coarse labelling. For the 4-classes and background setting (Table 1), LUMINR (meta) reaches a Dice of 0.843
after 50 gradient steps (6.6 s), a score the same network fitted from random weights attains only after the full 500 steps
(0.845, 64.8 s); the meta-learned initialization matches the
same registration quality with roughly a tenth of the iterations and a tenth of the wall-clock time. The gap is largest
early in fitting: within its first ten steps (1.3 s) the metainitialized fit is already level with what the random start
needs about 250 steps (32.4 s) to reach, and a two-step
fit (0.3 s) already surpasses the 500-step plateaus of both
coordinate-only baselines (0.734 at 31.8 s for INRGS , 0.708
at 91.0 s for INR) as well as the random start at 50 steps.
Fine labelling. The 35-classes-and-background setting
follows the same pattern. Within 50 steps (6.6 s) metalearned LUMINR reaches the Dice that LUMINR (random)
recovers only at 500 steps (64.8 s), again about a tenfold reduction in both iterations and time. The early advantage is
larger here: a two-step fit already exceeds what LUMINR
(random) reaches after 50 steps, and a ten-step fit (1.3 s)
is above the plateau INRGS (random) reaches after its full
500-step schedule (31.8 s). The finer segmentation penal-
izes small boundary displacements, so fitting from scratch
needs many more steps to resolve them, and the acceleration
from the meta-learned initialization is larger.
Folding. We finally verified that the acceleration does
not come at the cost of deformation regularity. Across
all six configurations the fraction of voxels with a negative Jacobian determinant stays below 0.001% at every
checkpoint; because folding is a property of the deformation field, it is identical for the 4- and 35-class evaluations. Neither coordinate-only baseline folds a single voxel
in either regime. Only the conditioned models fold at all,
and from iteration 100 onwards the meta-learned initialization is the better behaved of the two: its peak folding
fraction, 2.07 × 10−5 % at iteration 100, is roughly seven
times lower than the peak of the same architecture fitted
from scratch, 14.50 × 10−5 % at iteration 150. As Fig. 2
illustrates, LUMINR enables the generation of a viable segmentation mask close to the ground truth in just 2 - 10
fitting iterations.

4. Discussion
We have shown that conditioning the INR locally and
meta-learning its initialization are complementary, together
accelerating INR-based deformable image registration.
Fine-tuned from the meta-learned initialization, the network
reaches the accuracy of the same INR fit from scratch in
roughly a tenth of the gradient steps and a tenth of the
wall-clock time, under both a coarse and a fine segmentation, while folding stays negligible. This is a step toward INR-based registration that is fast enough for interactive clinical use. The remaining cost is dominated by
the regularizer, not the data term. Total fitting time scales
with the number of backward passes per epoch, and regularizers that we are using are computed by analytically dif-
ferentiating the transformation Φ with respect to the input
coordinates through autograd, rather than by finite differences on a grid [23]. Within the regularization term, the
bending energy penalty is the most expensive component,
because it requires second-order derivatives of the network
output. Reducing this cost is what would bring INR-based
registration close to real time and make it usable during live
clinical procedures.
Our method is not immune to folding. The enrichedinput models introduce a small, non-zero fraction of folded
voxels, and the meta-learned initialization inherits this
rather than removing it. This motivates further work on
INR regularization. One direction is to design INRs that
need less regularization in the first place, for example by
addressing the frequency behavior that makes SIREN-based
fields prone to high-frequency artifacts.
A second direction concerns how the INR is conditioned
on the image pair. Here we condition on local intensity and
gradient features at each coordinate. The present work tests
this local-conditioning strategy for meta-learned INR registration rather than benchmarking it against established registration methods; we leave that comparison to future work.
These hand-chosen features could instead be replaced by
a learned feature vector from a foundation model, which
would carry more context about the anatomy being registered. Taken further, the same idea points toward an INRbased foundation model for image registration, in which
a single meta-learned representation adapts to a wide range
of image pairs and, we expect, surpasses the registration
performance of current methods.

A. Baseline hyperparameter search
We selected the hyperparameters of the coordinate-only
baseline on the validation split of the OASIS dataset
(Sec. 2.2). The search was run with the Optuna framework [1] for 15 trials over three parameters: the learning
rate, drawn log-uniformly from [1 × 10−6 , 1 × 10−3 ]; the
number of hidden layers, drawn from 2 to 6; and the layer
width, drawn from {64, 128, 256, 512}. All remaining settings, including the NCC data term and the bending-energy
regularizer, were kept unchanged.
Each trial was scored on the validation pairs with the
Dice score. For every pair we fit an INR from scratch to the
full-resolution 3D volumes for 1,000 iterations with Adam,
using coordinate batches of 10,000 points. We then applied the fitted transformation to the moving segmentation
with nearest-neighbour interpolation and measured the Dice
score against the fixed segmentation. The score of a trial is
the mean Dice over all validation pairs, and Optuna maximizes this value. The best trial gave two hidden layers,
a width of 256, and a learning rate of 6.6 × 10−5 , which are
the settings we report for the grid-searched baseline.
