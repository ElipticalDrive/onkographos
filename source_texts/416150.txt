Abstract
We present an implementation of moment of fluid (MOF) multi-material
interface reconstruction that employs a combinatorial sequencing of
materials in determining the optimal ordering of immiscible materials in the
mixed zones of a hydro simulation model. Our combinatorial-based
implementation achieves significant reduction in operation count in terms of
the required number of evaluations for the optimizing cost functions,
compared to N factorial (N!) operation count for an exhaustive permutationbased ordering of materials. The proposed approach is implemented in KULL
hydro code to demonstrate improved interface reconstruction fidelity,
without trading off execution efficiency.
1. Introduction
A key component in a multi-material hydrodynamics code is an algorithmic
formulation and implementation for reconstructing and tracking the
interfaces of non-intersecting, immiscible materials. Sample industrial and
environmental fluid dynamics problems where accurate tracking of material
volumes and interfacial boundaries are useful include the tracking of
deflagrations and detonations, shock waves in coupled gas dynamics-solid
mechanics applications, shock refraction in gases, free surface flows, and
cloud mixing in geophysical flows. A popular interface reconstruction
procedure for the handling of interfacial phenomena is the volume-of-fluid
(VOF) algorithm. One of the earliest works for this approach dates back to the
SLIC (simple line interface calculation) method of Noh and Woodward [5],
and the VOF procedure that was developed by Hirt and Nichols [3].

SLIC-based algorithms assume piecewise constant, or staircase profile for
interface geometry within a mixed cell, and horizontal or vertical interface
profiles, which partly accounts for their greater susceptibility to flotsam and
jetsam for flows with strong interfacial shear, and vorticity [11].
Improvements to the early incarnations of volume-of-fluid interface
reconstruction algorithms in the form PLIC (piecewise linear interface
calculation) were later developed by Youngs’ and co-workers [6,7]. The PLIC
procedure, which admits sloped-line interface segments compared to vertical
or horizontal or staircase profile in SLIC, is similar to a much earlier work by
Debar [14], and to some of the more recent high-order reconstruction
methods such as LVIRA [11], ELVIRA [11], and Swartz [13].
A common feature in volume-of-fluid PLIC-based approach is the use of
stencil-based volume fractions for the estimation of the interface normal.
However, this non-local procedure does render their extension to
unstructured grid, and for reconstruction of multi-materials in mixed zones
non-trivial. The resulting algorithmic complexity stemming from the use of an
extended stencil is that parallelization of such routines requires more
bookkeeping, compared to methods that rely solely on local zonal data.
More recently, a new-generation interface reconstruction algorithm known as
Moment of Fluid (MOF), originally developed by Dyadechko and Shashkov
[1,16], has been demonstrated to be the most accurate in terms of interface
reconstruction fidelity amongst modern VOF-based interface reconstruction
algorithms. The MOF procedure is based on finding the volume-preserving
partitions within a mixed zone by solving an optimization problem, which
minimizes the cumulated distance error. MOF has all the desirable attributes
for an interface reconstruction scheme: like the popular stencil-based VOF
schemes, it is volume conservative, and has high interface reconstruction
fidelity; it is based on a local formulation, i.e., generating computational
stencils for computing volume fraction gradients is not formally required.
Locally formulated interface reconstruction schemes like MOF schemes are
naturally extendible to unstructured meshes, and amenable to multi-threaded
programming for high performance.
However, whilst MOF interface reconstruction technique has all of the abovementioned desirable properties, an aspect of its numerical procedure that
quickly renders it as a relatively expensive and complex method is its worstcase N factorial (N!) complexity for automatic ordering of materials in mixed
zones. For example, a worst-case scenario for as little as four materials in a
mixed zone using a serial nested dissection strategy [2], translates to 24
material combinations, or cost function evaluations in the determination of
the optimal material ordering. For five materials and higher in a mixed zone,
we quickly have an impractical number of cost function evaluations. In fact,
for 12 materials and above, the number of function evaluations already
exceeds the maximum value that can be stored in an integer data type on
some platforms, i.e., 12! The complexity is even higher, i.e., N!(N-1)! if the
reconstruction strategy is a B-Tree based group nested dissection technique
[2].
We note, however, that although multi-material zones with five or more
materials is quite conceivable in a given simulation model, however, such
highly packed zones will most likely be indicative of a rather poor mesh
resolution for the simulated problem. Fortunately, the proportion of mixed
zones in most multi-material simulation models is usually a small fraction of
the total zone count, which makes MOF a competitive option - especially if the
implementation is multi-threaded.
In this article, we propose the use of an alternative combinatorial algorithm
to avoid the worst-case N factorial complexity of a permutation-based
exploration procedure in the ordering of materials in mixed zones. In a
combinatorial procedure, we start-off by generating material sub-groups,
which form trial material subsets, or solution space from which an optimum
material ordering is determined. From the resulting precomputed optimal
sub-group of materials, material polygons are then generated in a fashion that
is similar to the group nested dissection method as earlier outlined by Ahn
and Shashkov [2]. The recursive resolution is terminated when the number of
materials in a sub-group falls to one.
This paper is structured as follows: in Section 2, we describe briefly the MOF
interface reconstruction method. In Section 3, we present the algorithmic
details for generating a combinatorial sequence, where the symbols in each
indexed item in a combinatorial sequence are the integer identifications for
the materials in a mixed zone. Preliminary numerical test results to
demonstrate proof of concept, as well as improvements in interface
reconstruction in a hydrodynamic setting using MOF are presented in Section
4. And finally, the outlook for future work is discussed in Section 5.

3. Combinatorial grouping of multi-materials
The combinatorial material grouping/ordering that is described in this article
is only required for mixed zones containing three or more immiscible
materials. The central idea of a combinatorial material sequencing is the
repeated use of the aggregation of materials in a mixed zone into two subgroups of pseudo-material composites, where two sub-groups are generated
from subsets of materials in a mixed zone. For example, in a zone containing
three materials, the two sub-groups will be comprised of two unique
materials, and one material respectively, and vice versa. A zone containing
four materials is again aggregated into sub-groups, where each sub-group is
the aggregation from two unique materials, i.e., each mixed zone is repeatedly
resolved in a binary partitioning fashion. Thus, each sub-group is generated
from a pre-determined subset of materials.
Whilst the generation of material sub-groups is in itself not a new idea, what
is different is the manner in which the sub-groups are generated. The existing
methods generate sub-groups of materials in an exhaustive fashion, or from a
precomputed permutation sequence, or via use of a B-Tree data structure,
whilst our method proposes the use of a precomputed combinatorial
sequence.
As earlier mentioned, the first step in a combinatorial procedure is the
generation of a sequence of a unique sets of material combinations that are
subsequently interrogated for cumulative distance errors for all terms in the
sequence. For illustration, choosing two material indices to form a sub-group
from the set of four materials in a mixed zone, where the material indices are:
{0,1,2,3} will result in the following combinatorial sequence of material

The interpretation of the above equations is as follows: in a combinatorial

and combine it with all other symbols in the set, up to the maximum specified

Beginning with a binary resolution procedure, and similar to the GND as
described in [2], and using Eqn. (1) for illustration purposes, we need only
test a maximum of six initial sub-groups of material combinations in the
initial step of an onion-skinning procedure. In an onion-skinning procedure,
each material, or sub-group of composite pseudo-material is separated from a
host zone one-at-a-time, and in a predetermined order. Note that the
optimum material sub-group that is identified in this first step is the material
sub-group that generates the least cumulative distance error in the form of
centroid residuals.
Upon determination of the material ordering with the least distance error in
the feasible set of pseudo-materials sub-group of two materials, each subgroup is further decomposed in a recursive fashion (if the number of
materials is still >= 3) to determine the interface separating the first material
entry from its neighbor within the sub-group. And in a separate parallel
operation, the materials forming the neighbor material group are also
resolved in an onion-skinning fashion by generating additional material
boundaries, thereby making a total of 8 operation count in terms of the
number of the invocations made to the optimization routine.

Our implementation of a combinatorial material in the KULL hydrodynamics
code provides for two modes of execution: i) using pre-ordered set of
materials, along with the matching volume fraction gradients for estimating
the normal direction, and ii) automatic material ordering using a
combinatorial sequence. The volume and distance residuals for each material
combination are evaluated using a combination of Brent non-linear solve [9],
and Levenberg-Marquardt non-linear optimization [10]; the optimization
solution determines the optimal orientation of the capping plane(s), whilst
the Brent solve determines the extent of the translation of the half-space
along the normal direction to satisfy volume conservation.
4. Numerical tests
4.1 Static reconstruction tests
The implementation of the combinatorial algorithm described in Section 3
was first applied to sample standalone static reconstruction test cases
comprising three, four, five and six materials in a zone to test its feasibility as
an alternative to a permutation-based ordering. In all cases, both zeroth and
first moments were resolved down to machine accuracy. Figure 1a shows the
resolution of the well-known filament or layered test case comprising of three
materials in a mixed zone as reported in [2]. Note that the host zone is
decomposed into triangular facets or sides, which enables the
implementation to naturally handle unstructured polygonal meshes. The
reconstruction of the well-known 3-material T-Junction configuration is
shown in Figure 1b.
The test results of additional junction configurations are shown in Figures 1c1f, beginning with a 4-material Cross-Junction test case. The results clearly
indicate the feasibility of using a combinatorial-based sub-grouping of
materials to resolve complicated interfaces in a mixed zone. In the case of five
materials in a mixed zone, using a permutation-based exhaustive search
requires the evaluation of 120 (5!) material combinations, or cost functions in
a worst-case scenario, whereas using a combinatorial ordering where we first
determine the optimal sub-group of two materials, and then repeating the
combinatorial sequence for the neighboring three materials, results in 5C2 +
3C2 + 2 + 2 = 20 cost function evaluations.
In particular, the resolution of five materials demonstrates the capability to
resolve multiple T-Junctions as previously reported in [2]. In the maximum
supported case of six materials in a mixed zone, a permutation-based
ordering requires exploring at least 720 material combinations, whereas a
combinatorial sequence incurs the cost of 6C3 + 3C2 + 3C2 + 2 + 2 = 70 material
combinations. For the case of six materials, we first determine the optimal
sub-group of three materials, and the corresponding neighbors of each threematerial sub-group; we next generate two sub-groups comprising of two
materials, and one material from the first parent sub-groups of three
materials. This is repeated for the second parent sub-group of three
materials.

4.2 Dynamic tests
Hydrodynamic test results using MOF interface reconstruction with a
combinatorial ordering of materials in the KULL hydro code is presented
next, where results are compared to those obtained using a donor-acceptor
based MFC (Monotonic Flux Corrected) interface reconstruction/tracking
procedure [16]. Note that the MFC procedure does not result in explicit
material polygons or boundaries. Rather, orientation of material interfaces in
mixed zones are inferred from volume fractions in neighboring upwind and
downwind zones to the target donor zone. It is a method that only typically
works well for layered material configurations – see [15] for additional
details.
4.2.1 Translation of an equal-arm cross in an oblique direction
The first test case in a hydrodynamic setting is the translation of an equal-arm
cross material in an oblique direction. The MOF output dataset as interpreted
and rendered by the Visit visualization toolkit [9] is shown in Figure 3b. A
corresponding interpretation by Visit using the MFC is shown in Figure 3a. As
expected, the MOF result is a more accurate rendition of an equal-arm cross.
This is a fairly useful initial test case for an interface reconstruction method
as it helps to demonstrate the capability of a reconstruction procedure to
handle materials with sharp corners. There is also the added complexity that
the material motion is not aligned to the Cartesian coordinate grid.

5. Remarks and future Work
We have demonstrated the feasibility of using a combinatorial-based material
ordering as an alternative to an exhaustive permutation-based grouping of
materials for automatic ordering of materials in mixed zones. The new
algorithm, avoids logical repetition of material ordering, and its
implementation in a hydrodynamics code has been shown to result in
improvements to the reconstructed interfacial boundaries in terms of
significant reduction in the associated artifacts of flotsam and jetsam
compared to the use of a donor-acceptor-based Monotonic Flux Corrected
scheme, which assumes either vertical or horizontal interface profile to the
flow direction, with no explicit material polygons. The current
implementation has so far only been tested for mixed zones with a maximum
of three materials in a full hydrodynamic context. We shall be reporting on its
extension to 3D, as well its application to the handling of more materials in
future publications.
Finally, we remark that though the combinatorial approach looks promising,
additional production tests will be conducted to further verify its robustness
in its use of only a subset of a permutation-based exploration space in
material grouping. However, the new algorithm can be combined with the
classic permutation-based material ordering in a hybrid approach for rare
cases, where a combinational approach fails to reconstruct the interfaces in a
multi-material mixed zone with acceptable centroid matching error.
