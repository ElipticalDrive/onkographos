## Summary

Adds Light Lab module 08, Scattering, on `/sky.html`. One porch looks out at the sky. Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter blue about five times more than red (Rayleigh, about 1/λ⁴). Lowering the sun lengthens the path, so the sun and the sky beside it turn orange and red while the sky overhead stays blue. The haze rail adds larger particles (Mie). The sky goes white or grey, and the clouds look white. A glass of slightly milky water on the rail glows blue from the side and orange on the card at the far end.

Stacked on `cursor/mirrors-reflection-616d`. Nothing is merged.

## Art direction

Techniques only, inside the existing Light Lab frame (paper spine, ticket, ember rails, Bricolage Grotesque, Instrument Sans, Space Mono).

- **penguin.music** — The sky is one weather. Sun height and haze repaint the whole dome, the porch light, and the beam in the glass together. A narrow view keeps that same picture with a lighter draw.
- **Persona City** — The lesson is a place you can look around. The teaching stays in the paper ticket over the scene.
- **Monogrid Pasticcino** — One continuous landscape. The sun travels from noon to the horizon in that same view.
- **Why Zero** — The sky gradient is the interaction. Moving a rail rewrites the color in the picture.
- **TILToooTILT** — The sun and the haze have weight. The sky eases toward the rail.
- **Bearplus** — The camera drift stays small. The change from noon to sunset is the event.

Aardvark Book Club, Loop Agency, Samuclima Beyond, nexstudio.tech, and rubenmarcus.dev were not used. Their techniques did not fit a sky, a haze, and a sunset gradient without leaving the Light Lab frame.

## Where you'll see this

A meteorologist, or an air-quality scientist, reads the color of the sky for particles. A milky white or grey veil often means larger bits in the air, such as haze, dust, or droplets. Those bits scatter every color about the same, so the blue washes out. A deep red sun means the direct beam has lost its short wavelengths. That happens on a long path through the air. Smoke can make the red deeper, because still more of the short wavelengths scatter aside before the light arrives.

A cinematographer, or a photographer, plans for golden hour. When the sun is low, its light travels through more air on the way to the set. Short wavelengths scatter out of that path. The light that remains is warm. Faces, walls, and dust in the beam pick up the orange.

A satellite remote-sensing scientist corrects each picture for the air in the way. Sunlight scatters in the atmosphere on the way down, and again on the way from the ground to the satellite. That scattered light adds a veil, and the veil is stronger in the blue. The correction takes the veil off, so the picture shows the land and the water.
