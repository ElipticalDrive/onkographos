Definition and scope
Holography as encoded reconstruction names a way of understanding holography in which the central artifact is not simply a three-dimensional image, but an encoded record capable of reconstructing an optical field. In classical optical holography, a hologram records an interference pattern formed by light from an object and a reference wave. When illuminated appropriately, that pattern diffracts light so that the observer receives a wavefront related to the one that originally came from the object. The apparent depth of a hologram is therefore not produced by drawing a perspective picture on a flat surface; it arises because the hologram stores and later replays information about the light field itself.[1]
This article is scoped around that encoding-and-reconstruction logic. It treats holography as a technical family of methods for recording, computing, modulating, and replaying optical wavefronts. That family includes silver-halide and photopolymer holograms, off-axis laser holography, computer-generated holography, holographic optical elements, and some display architectures that use diffraction to synthesize spatial imagery. It also includes a limited conceptual bridge to information-limit uses of the word “holographic,” such as the holographic principle in gravitational theory, but only as an analogy of boundary encoding and not as evidence that the universe is literally a display hologram.[2][3]
The word “encoded” is important because most holograms are visually unintuitive when inspected directly. A photographic hologram may look like scratches, grains, rings, or a cloudy surface; a computed hologram may look like a high-frequency phase map; a spatial light modulator may display a pattern that seems unrelated to the reconstructed scene. The apparent image is not painted onto the medium. Instead, the medium is a transformation device: it contains a pattern that redirects coherent or partially coherent light into a meaningful wavefront. This article therefore complements holography as wavefront reconstruction by emphasizing the representational status of the hologram: it is a code whose decoding operation is optical propagation.
This scope also separates literal holography from many casual or marketing uses of “hologram.” Pepper’s ghost stage illusions, transparent screen projections, lenticular postcards, volumetric displays, and light-field panels may be described as holographic in popular speech even when they do not reconstruct a coherent wavefront by diffraction. Some of these systems are valuable spatial interfaces, and some can provide binocular parallax or multi-view imagery, but they should not be treated as optical holography unless the actual mechanism uses holographic recording, holographic computation, or holographic optical elements. In display engineering, terminology can vary by community and product category, so the practical distinction is not whether a device looks spatial, but how it encodes and redirects light.
Historical context and evolution
Dennis Gabor introduced holography in 1948 in the context of microscopy. His paper “A New Microscopic Principle” proposed a lensless imaging method in which diffraction information could be recorded and later reconstructed, with the aim of improving microscopic resolution.[1] The original method predated the laser and depended on the coherence available from existing light sources, which limited early optical demonstrations. Gabor later received the 1971 Nobel Prize in Physics for the invention and development of the holographic method.[4]
The invention’s early promise was constrained by what is now known as the twin-image problem. In Gabor’s in-line arrangement, the desired reconstructed image and an unwanted conjugate image overlap, making clean three-dimensional reconstruction difficult. The method nevertheless introduced the essential idea: phase relationships, which are not directly recorded in conventional intensity imaging, could be indirectly preserved by interference with a known reference wave. That idea is the root of holography as encoded reconstruction: what is saved is not a conventional image but a record from which phase-dependent optical relationships can be recovered through reconstruction.[1]
The laser era transformed holography. After coherent laser sources became available, Emmett Leith and Juris Upatnieks developed off-axis holography, separating the desired reconstruction from unwanted terms and enabling practical three-dimensional holograms of diffuse objects.[5] In off-axis holography, the reference beam meets the object beam at an angle, shifting reconstructed image terms into different diffraction orders. That geometry made the encoded reconstruction cleaner and helped establish holography as both a scientific instrument and a display medium.
By the late twentieth century, holography had diversified into art, security printing, interferometric measurement, optical storage, head-up displays, and holographic optical elements. Stephen Benton’s rainbow hologram made white-light viewable holograms more practical by trading vertical parallax for viewability under ordinary illumination, an important example of engineering the code for a specific reconstruction environment.[6] Holographic stereograms and synthetic holography then linked holography to computer graphics by recording or computing many perspective views and encoding them into a holographic format. These developments foreshadowed modern computer-generated holography, where the hologram may never be optically recorded from a physical object at all.
Core mechanisms and processes
The core mechanism begins with interference. Light from an object carries amplitude and phase information. A normal camera records intensity at its sensor or film plane, but it does not directly retain the full phase structure of the arriving optical field. Holography mixes the object field with a reference field so that phase differences appear as intensity variations in the recording medium. The resulting pattern can later act as a diffraction grating with locally varying structure. When illuminated by a reconstruction beam, the hologram bends light into a wavefront related to the original object field.[7]
This process can be described as a chain of encoding, storage, and decoding. The encoding step converts a complex optical field into a physical or digital pattern. The storage step preserves that pattern in film, photopolymer, embossed foil, liquid crystal, metasurface, or numerical memory. The decoding step uses propagation and diffraction to reconstruct a field. If the reconstructed field supplies the eyes with appropriate directional and focus cues, the viewer perceives depth. Unlike stereoscopic displays, which typically present separate flat images to the two eyes, holographic reconstruction can in principle reproduce a continuous wavefront with parallax and accommodation cues, though practical devices often approximate this ideal.
The encoded aspect also explains why holography is sensitive to wavelength, geometry, and coherence. A hologram is not a universal picture but a matched optical object. Change the wavelength, reference angle, medium shrinkage, illumination coherence, or viewing geometry, and the reconstruction changes. This dependence is a strength when used intentionally: holographic optical elements can route, focus, disperse, or combine light in compact ways. It is also a constraint for display systems, where color, brightness, speckle, viewing angle, and eye-box size must be controlled simultaneously.
Computer-generated holography replaces optical recording with numerical calculation. Given a target scene or desired wavefront, algorithms compute a hologram pattern that will diffract light into the intended field. The computed pattern may specify amplitude, phase, or both, but many practical spatial light modulators primarily modulate phase, forcing algorithms to approximate the target field under device limits. Reviews of computer-generated holography describe it as a technique for obtaining a complex optical field by numerical calculation and then reconstructing it through diffraction, often using spatial light modulators.[8]
Modern computer-generated holography systems face severe computational and physical constraints. High-quality holographic display requires large spatio-angular information capacity: the system must control not only where light appears on a screen but also where it travels. Research on neural and optimization-based holography attempts to accelerate this calculation and improve perceived image quality, with one Nature study describing computer-generated holography as a route toward high spatio-angular-resolution three-dimensional projection by simulating diffraction and interference.[9] Even when algorithms improve, physical modulators impose limits on pixel pitch, refresh rate, phase depth, diffraction efficiency, and achievable field of view.
Representative examples
A classic transmission hologram is the simplest example of encoded reconstruction. A laser illuminates an object and a photographic plate while a reference beam also reaches the plate. The recorded interference fringes are too fine to read visually, but under suitable illumination the plate reconstructs a virtual image behind it. A viewer moving laterally sees parallax because the reconstructed wavefront changes with viewpoint. The plate functions less like a window painted with an image and more like a wavefront transformer.
Reflection holograms provide another example, often visible under white light. In a reflection hologram, the interference fringes are arranged through the thickness of the medium and selectively reflect wavelengths according to Bragg-like conditions. This makes some display holograms viewable without a laser, though the reconstructed color and brightness remain tied to the recording medium and illumination geometry. White-light viewability made holography more accessible for art, museum display, security labels, and public demonstrations.
Rainbow holograms show how encoded reconstruction can be deliberately reduced. Benton’s approach sacrificed vertical parallax so that the hologram could be viewed in white light while preserving horizontal parallax and a strong depth impression.[6] This is a key example for display taxonomy: a holographic experience may be produced by restricting the full optical field in a way that matches human perception and practical illumination. The reconstruction is real, but the encoded field is not the complete wavefront for every direction and wavelength.
Holographic optical elements represent a less image-centered example. A hologram can encode the function of a lens, mirror, grating, beam combiner, or waveguide coupler. In augmented-reality and head-up display systems, holographic elements can redirect light from a microdisplay into the user’s eye while remaining transparent to the outside world. Such elements may not be intended to show a freestanding three-dimensional object; they use holographic encoding to transform wavefronts compactly.
Computer-generated holographic displays are a contemporary frontier. A spatial light modulator displays a phase or amplitude pattern calculated from a target scene. A laser or narrowband source illuminates the modulator, and the diffracted output creates a perceived image at one or more depths. These systems can potentially support focus cues more naturally than ordinary stereoscopy, but they must manage speckle, diffraction artifacts, limited field of view, and computation. Speckle suppression research emphasizes the practical difficulty of producing clean holographic images with coherent illumination and spatial light modulators.[10]

The final representative case is the metaphorical or theoretical use of “holographic.” In black holes, gravity, and information limits, holographic language refers to the possibility that information associated with a region may be bounded by, or encoded on, a lower-dimensional boundary. Gerard ’t Hooft and Leonard Susskind developed this idea from entropy bounds and black-hole physics, not from display engineering.[2][3] The relevance to this article is conceptual: both optical holography and the holographic principle use the motif of information encoded in one structure and represented as something with higher-dimensional significance. The mechanisms, evidentiary standards, and domains are otherwise distinct.
Evidence and methods
Evidence for optical holography is experimental, mathematical, and perceptual. The experimental basis is direct: interference patterns can be recorded, and appropriate illumination reconstructs images with parallax and depth. Holographic interferometry further demonstrates the method’s phase sensitivity by comparing wavefronts before and after small deformations; tiny changes in an object can produce visible fringe shifts. This makes holography useful not only for display but also for measurement.
The mathematical basis rests on wave optics. A hologram can be modeled as a transmittance or reflectance function that multiplies an incident reconstruction field. Propagation from that plane to the viewer or sensor is then described by diffraction integrals or Fourier optics. Joseph Goodman’s Fourier-optics treatment is widely used for understanding how lenses, diffraction, spatial filtering, and holograms manipulate optical fields.[7] In this view, the hologram is an optical operator: it converts one input field into another field according to a physically constrained encoding.
Digital holography and computer-generated holography add computational methods. Algorithms may use point-source summation, Fresnel propagation, angular-spectrum propagation, Fourier holograms, iterative phase retrieval, gradient descent, camera-in-the-loop optimization, or neural networks. The goal is usually to find a pattern that produces an acceptable reconstructed image when the real optical system’s limitations are included. This has made holography a hybrid field: optical physics determines what can be reconstructed, while computation determines how efficiently the encoding can be found.
Perceptual evidence is equally important for displays. A physically exact wavefront may be unnecessary if the human visual system cannot distinguish the approximation, while a mathematically plausible reconstruction may fail if it produces speckle, eye strain, or incorrect focus cues. Holographic display research therefore measures image quality, depth cues, diffraction efficiency, viewing comfort, and task performance. Encoded reconstruction is not only a physical operation; it is also a design problem involving the visual system.
Debates and open questions
The first debate concerns terminology. Many devices advertised as holographic are actually stereoscopic, multiview, volumetric, or projection-based. These systems can be impressive and useful, but calling all spatial imagery “holography” obscures mechanism. A precise technical taxonomy should identify whether a system encodes and reconstructs wavefronts through interference, diffraction, computed holograms, or holographic optical elements. “Holographic-like” or “holographic display” may be acceptable in broader interface language, but the mechanism should be stated when comparing technologies.

A second debate concerns how much wavefront fidelity is necessary. Full-parallax, full-color, high-resolution holographic video over a large viewing zone requires control over vast optical information. Practical systems often reduce vertical parallax, constrain viewer position, use narrow depth ranges, or exploit perceptual tolerances. This raises a design question: should holographic displays aim for physical completeness, or should they encode only the cues needed for a given task? Benton’s rainbow holography and modern computer-generated holography both show that selective encoding can be more practical than total reconstruction.[6][9]
A third open question is computation. Real-time, high-resolution holography demands fast calculation of diffraction patterns, especially for dynamic scenes. Neural methods and hardware acceleration can reduce latency, but they must be evaluated against physical optics rather than only image-space metrics. A computed hologram that looks good in simulation may reconstruct poorly when displayed on a real modulator with finite pixel pitch, nonuniform phase response, optical aberrations, and coherent noise.[9]
A fourth issue is speckle. Coherent illumination enables clean interference but also produces granular noise from random phase relationships and scattering. Speckle reduction methods include temporal averaging, angular diversity, wavelength diversity, partial coherence, algorithmic optimization, and diffuser strategies. Speckle suppression remains a central obstacle for holographic display quality because reducing visible noise can conflict with preserving the coherence needed for reconstruction.[8]
A final debate concerns the philosophical extension of holographic language. The holographic principle in theoretical physics is not a display technology, and optical holography does not prove that physical reality is an illusion. The bridge is structural: in both cases, information associated with a higher-dimensional phenomenon can be related to a lower-dimensional encoding. Responsible use of the bridge requires stating where the analogy ends. Optical holography is experimentally reproducible wave optics; gravitational holography is a theoretical framework about entropy, boundaries, and quantum gravity.[3]
Significance and applications
Holography matters because it changes the unit of representation from image surfaces to wavefronts. Ordinary images encode brightness and color at points on a plane. Holograms encode how light should propagate. This shift supports depth, parallax, optical transformation, and phase-sensitive measurement. It also creates a bridge between display technology and information theory: the medium need not resemble the thing it reconstructs.
In display systems, encoded reconstruction points toward spatial interfaces that can occupy apparent depth rather than merely simulate it through binocular disparity. This is relevant to augmented reality, scientific visualization, telepresence, medical imaging, design review, and education. Holographic avatars as spatial interfaces depend on whether the system actually reconstructs wavefronts, reconstructs a sampled light field, or presents multiview imagery. The article therefore offers a vocabulary for distinguishing literal holographic avatars from broader spatial-display avatars.
In optics, holography supports compact components. Holographic optical elements can replace or supplement conventional lenses and mirrors, especially where weight, transparency, wavelength selectivity, or unusual beam shaping is important. These uses often matter more commercially than freestanding display holograms. The encoded pattern can perform an optical function that would otherwise require a bulky shaped surface.
In measurement, holography remains valuable because phase is sensitive. Digital holographic microscopy, holographic interferometry, and wavefront sensing use the same reconstruction logic to infer shape, displacement, refractive index, or vibration. In these cases the reconstructed field is evidence, not entertainment. The hologram becomes a scientific record of optical relationships that were not directly visible in intensity images.

A hologram is an encoded optical structure, and reconstruction is the event in which that structure becomes visible as a field of light.
