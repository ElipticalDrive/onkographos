Opening this at @jbms's suggestion after an email exchange (cc @seankmartin and the Allen folks from that thread).
We're the Cheng Lab at Penn State College of Medicine. We image whole organisms and tissues with synchrotron micro-CT at sub-micron resolution (X-ray histotomography). Our latest large volume is a 3-day-old Octopus bimaculoides hatchling: 12,200 × 12,200 × 9,349 voxels at 0.7 µm isotropic (about 2.8 TB at 16-bit), served from S3 as a precomputed pyramid (2×2×2 mean downsampling, six levels). It's the dataset of our eLife reviewed preprint (https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110201) and is public:
image: precomputed://https://cephalopod.team/assets/precomputed_repository/10mm/2BM/A3/Octo9_Merge_Pass4_Pass2_cleaned_16bit_precomputed_sharded_2
segmentation with meshes: precomputed://https://cephalopod.team/assets/precomputed_repository/10mm/2BM/A3/segmentations/Octo_9_Regions_1.25_10162025_replaced_segmentations_precomputed_sharded
The paper already uses a modified Neuroglancer to link figures to viewer states and to navigate our anatomical ontology.
Over about six months, working from a fork of 64c47bd (master as of mid-January 2026), we extended volume rendering so that the octopus, and several other test volumes (CT, STPT, LSFM), can be explored from the whole animal down to individual cells in 3D, while streaming from S3. No data formats changed: precomputed volumes and multi-resolution Draco meshes load as they are. Every new feature has its own setting, and settings left at their default don't appear in the saved JSON state. The larger features (sorted compositing, progressive loading, empty-space skipping, lighting) are off by default; a few smaller ones are on (jittered sampling, accumulation at rest, nearest-first downloading, mesh/volume depth compositing).
The main pieces, in the order we'd suggest discussing them:
Progressive multiscale loading. Today a volume layer draws at a single resolution level per view. Instead, each frame picks the right level for each region of the volume, aiming for about one voxel per screen pixel, so nearby regions get fine data and distant ones coarse. Coarse data fills the view first; finer data replaces it region by region as it arrives. A complete coarse view appears within a few seconds and then sharpens in place.
Sorted front-to-back compositing. Chunks are drawn nearest-first, so each ray can stop once it has passed through enough opaque material, both within a chunk and across chunks. When that isn't possible, rendering falls back to the existing unsorted blending. This only affects ordering within one volume; blending between separate volume layers (feat: Improve blending with multiple volume rendering layers #771) is left alone for now.
Nearest-first downloads and empty-space skipping. Chunks are requested in order of distance from the camera, and chunks whose brightest voxel is below a threshold are skipped.
Still images that refine at rest. When the camera stops, frames are averaged with tiny sub-pixel offsets so the image settles into a clean still. Also: jittered (randomised) sample positions along each ray, a crop box you can resize by dragging its faces, and a cut plane that can be pinned in place.
Meshes and volumes that occlude each other correctly, with per-segment opacity.
Separately, and not what we'd propose for core: experimental gradient lighting; keyframed guided tours (the viewer glides between saved states, and the user can take over mid-tour and let go, after which the camera glides back); frame-exact offline rendering to export those tours as video; and a WebXR mode with webcam hand tracking (relevant to #532).
Real-time screen capture (ShareX) streaming the octopus data from S3, on a 2024 Razer Blade 16 (mobile RTX 4090) in Chrome on Windows 11.
neuroglancer_issue_clip.mp4
Caveats.
Tuned and tested on micron-scale volumes with equal voxel sizes in all directions (octopus, zebrafish, mouse). Electron-microscopy data (e.g. 4×4×40 nm voxels, 64³ chunks, eight or more levels) is largely untested and will liekly exercise different parts of the code; we'd welcome a test dataset.
Needs float render targets and multiple render targets (WebGL2 features), and uses EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2 for GPU timing when the browser provides it.
It's a big change: our src/volume_rendering/ is about 83k lines, nearly half of it tests (including tests that run real WebGL). volume_render_layer.ts and the perspective panel have grown a lot, so splitting them up would be part of any upstream path.
Upstream has moved since January (for example perf(volume_rendering): faster volume rendering depth check in fragment shader #1078 reworked the same depth check we had been optimizing), so anything we propose would be rebased onto current master.
Development was AI-assisted: coding agents (Claude; planner, Codex; implementer, me; the middle guy with human eyes) working under my direction, with every change (painfully) gated on automated tests (synthetic data, real WebGL) and then checked by my eyeballs on the octopus or a zebrafish dataset. We plan to publish the design notes, per-change reports and commit notes with the code.
Status. we plan to make the code public (Apache-2.0) with the revised octopus manuscript; maintainers who'd like to look sooner are welcome email me. Three design notes (download priority, per-region resolution, sorted compositing) are already close to the shape of a formal design proposal.
A possible path. Neuroglancer's governance doc (docs/governance/governance.rst) asks for a written design proposal for big changes and ordinary pull requests for small ones. So, smallest first: nearest-first downloads, empty-space skipping, and jittered sampling as an opt-in, each as its own small pull request. In parallel, since progressive multiscale loading was mentioned, a design proposal covering it together with sorted compositing (our design notes are most of the text). Then accumulation at rest, mesh/volume compositing and the crop box; last, the controller that lowers resolution while the camera is moving. We'd be glad to help review and maintain whatever lands.
cheers,
Dan Vanselow

So again would be nice to see what's done as it sounds you've done something similar. Using scene analysis we could know when we need to switch back to OIT.

Thanks @seankmartin, really glad to hear it, and great to know we've been wandering down similar roads!
Progressive loading. "Which coarser levels, and how many" is something we spent... a lot of time on. Our rule ended up being something along the lines of: every cell at the target resolution is drawn exactly once, either from its own chunk if it's resident, or from exactly one resident coarser ancestor clipped to that cell, but not both, since a ray would otherwise integrate the same tissue twice. We started with the target plus the next coarser level only; the general form takes the finest resident ancestor, with the coarsest complete level as an always-present floor. With 2× levels, keeping the whole chain of coarser levels resident costs under 1/7 of the target's voxels (1/8 + 1/64 + …). Happy to send the design note on this, and the one on download prioritization, so you can compare with your two versions.
Sorting. That sounds pretty close to what we did: sort chunks front to back when there's a single volume, standard blending, empty-space skipping and early ray termination, with a fallback to OIT when the preconditions fail. There's a design note for that too.
Order of PRs. Agreed, jittered sampling is a good first one; of the small, self-contained pieces it makes the biggest visual difference. Averaging frames at rest appears to matter less than I first thought: at our sample counts jitter removes most of the banding, and averaging mainly cleans up the remaining grain at lower sample counts, so it could come later. Also, #1078 covers the depth-check change we'd made separately, so we'll take yours.
One more that pairs well with jitter: a matched sampling kernel, a small Gaussian prefilter along each ray sized to the sample step. It does a pretty good job of removing moiré where it shows up and makes a big visual difference in biological detail for us, especially at sample surfaces. On integer textures with manual trilinear it's expensive (about 3–4× the GPU time per frame in our measurements), so we paired it with hardware-filtered data textures: R8 for uint8, and R16 via EXT_texture_norm16 for uint16, falling back to the integer path when the extension is missing. Each lookup does one filtered fetch instead of eight and the kernel's cost mostly disappears. So far images match the integer path to within one 8-bit step. We're extending it to float32 next, since we often render at that depth, and the same approach should cover int8/int16; 32- and 64-bit integers would stay on the exact integer path. The filtered textures might be worth a look on their own, independent of the kernel.
And agreed on defaults and the JSON state: always-on or automatically managed is fine with us wherever it works out.

Here are the three design memos behind our fork's volume rendering: [gist link]. They are reconciled with the fork's current code (6fc29bd).
How many coarser levels, and which. With progressive LOD on, we fetch:
the screen-warranted target: the coarsest scale whose projected voxel is at least 1 px, coarsened to stay under 8192 cells and 60% of system memory;
exactly one coarser level (target + 1) as each cell's interim backing;
the coarsest level, over its full extent and pinned above everything, so a complete image exists immediately.
Intermediate levels are never requested, only used if already resident. When idle, the next-finer level is warmed into system memory. An experimental planner generalizes this to a multi-level cut, requesting each leaf plus its immediate parent. See 01 (Status items 3–5) and 03 (Policy 2).
Each target cell gets exactly one backing, so levels must nest: axis-aligned, integer voxel ratios, aligned origins, every cell inside one parent chunk. Otherwise the whole layer falls back to a coarse-complete-then-atomic swap. Your fill-tracking texture for arbitrary downsampling factors looks complementary: it could serve the layouts we reject. See 01 §2.2.
When sorted applies. It needs:
the per-layer toggle (off by default);
DVR mode, no wireframe;
exactly one visible volume layer;
required chunks ready (100% by default) for 10 consecutive frames. 750 ms dip tolerance.
Otherwise the frame uses stock WBOIT. There is no scene analysis: meshes don't force OIT, and the finished volume enters the scene's OIT pass once. Termination from Front-to-back: rays stop below 1/256 transmittance (RGB clamped to 1), and a stencil pass every 8–128 chunks (sparser later) culls saturated pixels. See 02 (Status, §5).
Accumulation at rest is on by default:
up to 64 jittered frames, running-mean into a half-float history;
each pixel's ray start offset by interleaved gradient noise, advanced by the golden ratio per frame;
restarts on camera motion, chunk arrival or LOD change;
once converged, the image is re-presented without re-rendering.
See 02 §6.
Next steps. On your suggestion of jittered sampling as the first PR: agreed. I've started the contributor license agreement process with Penn State and will open it once that's in place. Timing for the rest: our publication plans are apparently in flux. ’ll post here when the code goes public, and I’m still happy to share it privately before then.
