What if you could explore a real 3D scene smoothly in real time at more than 100 frames per second? All learned from ordinary photos. That is the promise of 3D gausian splatting. In this video, we will understand it step by step. In our previous videos, we explored light fields and knee RF. If you have not watched them yet, I recommend watching them first. N RF gave us beautiful new views but was slow. Training took many hours and rendering one image took several seconds. The reason is simple. For every pixel, a neural network must be evaluated at hundreds of points along array. Gausian splatting asks a different question. What if we skip the network and store the scene directly? Here is the key idea. The scene is represented by millions of tiny colored semi-transparent blobs floating in 3D space. Each blob is a 3D gausian. To make an image, we simply throw these blobs onto the screen like splashes of paint. This is called splattering. There is no neural network to query. So rendering becomes extremely fast. This method was introduced by Kerbal and colleagues at SIGRA 2023. So what exactly is a gausian? In one dimension, it is the familiar bell curve. It is highest at the center and fades smoothly to zero. In two dimensions, it becomes a soft ellipse and in three dimension, a soft ellypoid like a fuzzy rugby ball. Its shape is controlled by a covariance matrix which decides how stretched it is and in which direction it points. Every gausian in the scene stores just a few learnable values. A position which is the center of the blob in 3D space. A scale and a rotation which together define its car variance. The car variance is built as rs transpose ranspose which always keeps the shape valid. An opacity which says how transparent it is. and a color stored as spherical harmonics so that the color can change with the viewing direction just like shiny surfaces in real life. In total, each gausian has about 59 numbers and a full scene can use several million gausian. Where do these gausian come from? Just like n rf, we start with photos and camera poses from structure from motion such as kalma. But structure from motion also gives us something extra for free. A sparse cloud of 3D points. Each of these points becomes the starting position of one gausian. In our example, we use 40 small photos of a new toy scene with a mushroom, a cactus, and a party hat. Now, how do we render an image? First, we project every 3D gausian onto the camera image. A neat property of gausian is that a projected 3D gausian is again a 2D gausian. It becomes a soft ellipse on the screen. Its new 2D car variance is computed with the camera transformation and a local linear approximation of the projection. Next comes rasterization and this is where the speed comes from. The screen is divided into small tiles of 16x 16 pixel. Each gausian is assigned to the tiles it overlaps. Within each tile, the gausian are sorted by depth from front to back using a very fast GPU sort. Then for each pixel, the colors are blended front to back. This is exactly the same alpha compositing formula as in nef. But instead of evaluating hundreds of network samples, we only blend a handful of sorted ellipses. That is why it runs in real time. Let's build some intuition with a 2D example. Here we fit a picture using only 800 2D gausian. They start as random colored blobs. During optimization, each blob moves, stretches, rotates, and changes color until together they reproduce the image. 3D gausian splatting does exactly this but in 3D and seen from many cameras at once. Training works like this. We pick a training photo and render the gausian from the same camera. We compare the rendering with the real photo using an L1 loss combined with a structural similarity term. Because every step of the rasterizer is differentiable, the error flows back to every gausian updating its position, shape, opacity, and color. No neural network is involved. We directly optimize the gausian themselves with gradient descent. There is one more important trick called adaptive density control. Where region is under reconstructed and details are missing, small gausian are cloned. Where a single gausian is too large and covers too much detail, it is split into two smaller ones. And gausian that become almost transparent are pruned away. This way the number of gausian grows exactly where the scene needs it. For this video, we trained a real small gausian splatting model on an ordinary processor from 40 images. It started from a sparse point cloud and grew through cloning and splitting during training. Here is the result rendered from new viewpoints that were never photographed. And here you can see what the scene is really made of. Thousands of small colored ellipsoids. So how does gausian splatting compare with nref? N RF stores the scene implicitly inside a network. Gausian splatting stores it explicitly as a set of blobs. Training drops from many hours to tens of minutes and rendering jumps from seconds per image to more than 100 frames per second at high resolution. The price is memory. A scene can need hundreds of megabytes compared to just a few megabytes for a knee RF network. Gausian splattering also has limitations. It can produce floating artifacts and needle like gausian especially in regions seen by few cameras. Zooming in or out can cause aliasing and the blobs do not directly give a clean surface or mesh. Researchers are already addressing these. MIP splatting reduces aliasing. 2D gausian splatting recovers accurate surfaces and dynamic versions capture moving scenes. Gausian are also used for realtime mapping and slam and for compact compressed scenes. Today gausian splatting is used in virtual reality games and film to capture real places and walk through them in robotics and mapping to build realistic 3D maps very quickly and for digital humans ecommerce and cultural heritage where real time photo realistic 3D is essential. Let's recap. Gausian splatting represents a scene with millions of colored 3D gausian. Each gausian has a position, a car variance, an opacity, and a view dependent color. Rendering projects them to the screen, sorts them by depth, and blends them in real time. Training compares renders with photos and adapts the number of gausian where needed.
