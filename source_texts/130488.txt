We introduce two fundamental advances to the NanoVDB data structure:
dynamic GPU-native topological updates, and a voxel-sequential access
paradigm that dramatically accelerates point-wise and stencil-based operators on sparse grids. NanoVDB’s linearized memory layout has delivered
strong GPU performance, and the Index-Grid concept introduced in the
fVDB framework [Williams et al. 2024] provided topology/data separation
and optimal compactness of storage in sparse machine learning workloads.
However, all these prior iterations of NanoVDB assume a static topology that
has precluded use of this data structure in simulation and dynamic geometric
processes. We eliminate this limitation by introducing a suite of dynamic
topology operators – morphological dilation, grid merging, pruning, and
cross-resolution coarsening and refinement. These operators use bit-parallel
algorithms to reconstruct grids containing hundreds of millions of voxels in
just a few milliseconds.

Introduction
The need to store and manipulate high-resolution, sparse volumetric
data is ubiquitous across the spectrum of modern spatial computing. From high-fidelity fluid simulations in visual effects to massive datasets in medical imaging and neural radiance fields, a few
recurring technical challenges emerge prominently: efficiency in
memory utilization, scalability in computational throughput and
accommodation of larger domain sizes, and the necessity of sparsity
in representing irregular 3D domains. The OpenVDB data structure [Museth 2013] and its various derivatives were born out of
these challenges, emerging as the industry standard for encoding
sparse grid-embedded data. By providing a framework to represent virtually unbounded Cartesian domains with sparse occupancy,
OpenVDB enjoys a rich ecosystem of operators and broad adoption
in digital content creation tools. More recently, NanoVDB [Museth
2021] has extended this functionality to the GPU with emphasis on
implicit geometry modeling and real-time rendering tasks, while
the fVDB framework [Williams et al. 2024] further extended the
scope of NanoVDB and demonstrated its efficacy in sparse machine
learning workloads. NanoVDB has seen industry-wide adoption,
powering tools such as Blender’s Cycles rendering engine, Houdini’s Pyro, Vellum and MPM solvers, Autodesk’s Arnold, Chaos’
V-Ray, and Pixar’s RenderMan.
Despite its success, the VDB ecosystem – as originally materialized in OpenVDB – was fundamentally conceived for the CPU,
relying on fine-grained heap allocation and pointer-heavy nodes, accelerated by means of a software cache implemented in ValueAccessor objects. Although this design allows for the effortless topological
change required by dynamic simulation, it is not the most natural
fit for the massively parallel, throughput-oriented architecture of
the GPU. NanoVDB [Museth 2021] was introduced to facilitate the
transition of VDB grids to GPU. Originally intended for read-only
applications, e.g. random access tasks like ray-tracing and collision detection, NanoVDB forgoes heap allocation and pointer-based
bookkeeping by adopting a linearized memory layout with relative
addressing, specifically tailoring the structure for SIMT execution.
In a phenomenon that should not surprise us given the precedent
of other high-throughput systems, NanoVDB’s narrower scope –
sacrificing OpenVDB’s topological malleability for a compact, prebaked buffer – results in a structure that is not only more portable
but significantly faster for streaming, sampling, and rendering tasks.
Consequently, OpenVDB remains the tool of choice for dynamic,
evolving topologies at the cost of peak performance, while NanoVDB
serves as the expert subset for GPU-accelerated workloads, limited
by its static, read-only topology. So far, no published research has
seriously challenged this limitation, leaving developers with an
untenable choice: the dynamic topological versatility of the CPU
(OpenVDB) or the read-only speed of its GPU sibling (NanoVDB).
In this paper, we resolve this tension by introducing a framework
for the dynamic evolution of NanoVDB sparse volumes directly on
the GPU. We demonstrate that static topology is not an inherent
trait of the NanoVDB data structure, but rather a limitation of the
algorithms previously used to maintain it. Our work specifically
aims to be synergistic with NanoVDB index-grids [Williams et al.
2024], the latest feature iteration of the data structure that explicitly

separates the active voxel set topology from the data payload stored
in it, and also offers the highest compactness of storage of all VDB
variants. Our tools target the rapid reconstruction of the tree’s hierarchical backbone independently of the underlying data payload. We
adopt a bulk-synchronous manipulation paradigm where topological changes are enacted collectively in discrete epochs, and enable a
suite of operators – including morphological dilation, grid merging,
pruning, and multi-resolution refinement – that can reconstruct
trees with hundreds of millions of voxels in a few milliseconds.
With topological manipulation now handled efficiently on the
GPU, the remaining fundamental concern for sparse simulation
codes is the performance of streaming and stencil operations. Accessor objects – originating in OpenVDB and carried into NanoVDB
– are the standard paradigm for data access in the VDB ecosystem.
They act as software caches that hide the cost of tree traversal, if
the locations being accessed are spatially coherent. They are also
available on the GPU (in NanoVDB) although their performance
benefit may be hampered at times due to thread divergence when
an accessor needs to update its local cache after a miss. Another
challenge stems from the fact that on the GPU, the natural way to
dispatch parallel threads is to assign a threadblock (e.g. 512 CUDA
threads) to process all voxels of a leaf node in parallel, leading to load
imbalance if threads are wasted being assigned to inactive voxels.
Although accessor-based random access still has its place (especially
in ray tracing and collision processing workloads), we propose an
alternative data traversal paradigm, which we label voxel-sequential
access that is optimized for stencil and streaming operations on
the GPU. Within this paradigm, we stream active voxels as a flat
linear sequence, regardless of the leaf nodes they straddle along
this ordering. We facilitate this via a new VoxelBlockManager data
structure that opens the path to streaming and stencil operations at
optimal compute density, and can absorb the cost of tree traversal
via enhanced regularity and warp-synchronous operation.
Our specific contributions are:
• Bulk-Synchronous Topological Evolution: A paradigm
for GPU- and VDB-native topological changes—including
merging, pruning, and refinement—on linearized index-grids.
We argue this is the most fitting abstraction of dynamic topology evolution in GPU-focused workloads. We demonstrate
bit-parallel algorithms that reconstruct complex topologies
with hundred million voxels in milliseconds.
• Injection and Sidecar Operators: A suite of operators for
the efficient integration and evolution of auxiliary sidecar
payloads alongside the primary topology.
• The Voxel-Sequential Access Paradigm: The VoxelBlockManager (VBM) data structure, which replaces legacy hierarchical traversal with a linear streaming pattern. This eliminates thread divergence and load imbalance, significantly
boosting efficiency on stencil-oriented workloads.
• High-Performance Validation: We demonstrate the efficacy of our framework additions in microbenchmarks sourced
from real-world workloads, including 3D geometry reconstruction, interface tracking in fluids simulation, and highresolution 3D mesh voxelization.

Background and Related Work
We will start with an overview of the evolution of the data structures
in the VDB ecosystem, and the implications that the design decisions
in every iteration brought about. Figure 2 highlights some of the
key architectural differences, which are further detailed below.
OpenVDB [Museth 2013] introduced the core concept of the
VDB tree structure: a hierarchical tree-based representation with
fixed depth but aggressive and varying branching factors. At the
coarsest scale, the sparse 3D voxel domain is covered by aligned
macroscopic root tiles (nominally spanning 40963 voxels), which are
maintained by the root node in a linear list or a hashtable.
If a root tile requires refinement rather than carrying a constant
value, an upper node is associated with it, spanning the same area
and holding the branch of the tree that refines it. Upper nodes are
subdivided into voxel partitions of size 1283 voxels (so called lower
nodes) and those into smaller aligned partitions of 83 (leaf nodes).
A leaf node densely allocates a buffer (on the heap) sufficient
to store all 8 × 8 × 8 values within its span, and a bit-encoded
mask indicating an active/inactive state for each voxel in its domain.
OpenVDB strives to present a usage model that is as permissive as a
hypothetical, infinite-extent dense grid would be: random read/write
access is supported to arbitrary locations in the Cartesian domain,
with the data structure performing on-demand allocation to create
storage for any location that was not previously provisioned for.
The fundamental abstraction that OpenVDB uses to mitigate the
(substantial) worst-case performance cost of random access is the
concept of accessors, cf. ValueAccessor in [Museth 2013]. An accessor is a tool for read/write access at arbitrary coordinates, which
seeks to accelerate access cost if the read/write requests are made
for spatially coherent locations. Specifically, if repeated access requests are made for voxels that fall within the same leaf node, a
software cache that the accessor incorporates will completely bypass tree traversal and provide fast access to the requested value.
That is, an accessor allows for bottom-up vs top-down tree traversal,
optimally avoiding traversal altogether. However, the highly permissive use model of OpenVDB limits its potential for aggressive
optimization: the data structure is clearly targeting CPU-only usage,
as accessors are prone to warp divergence (they need to modify
their internal cache even if they access data for read purposes only),
and the allocate-on-insertion model for topology updates is very
challenging to reconcile with the typical GPU/CUDA programming
paradigm.
NanoVDB [Museth 2021] was introduced as a narrowly-scoped
subset of OpenVDB that was expressly aimed at maximizing portability, supporting use in both CPU and GPU, x86 and ARM platforms,
and even within shading languages. NanoVDB froze the geometric sizes of the tree nodes from being tunable at compile-time in
OpenVDB, to a standardized 4096/128/8 node size progression as
illustrated in Figure 3, and replaced the allocation-on-access model of
OpenVDB with a strict allocation-on-initialization without support
for changing the grid topology after it was created. Leaf nodes and
their data would be allocated in a contiguous, linear storage span,
rather than the fragmented heap-based allocation that OpenVDB
required. NanoVDB is more efficient in (spatially coherent) random
access than OpenVDB on the CPU, due to the fact that NanoVDB
uses linearized, cache- and prefetch-friendly storage. Of course, in
this initial iteration of NanoVDB, its linear memory model came at
the expense of no support for performing edits to the grid topology
(modifying the active voxel set) without requiring rebuilding the
grid from scratch.
Index-Grids [Williams et al. 2024] were a crucial addition to
NanoVDB, introduced to facilitate support for machine learning
workloads in the fVDB framework. In an Index-Grid, the value associated with each active voxel is not the actual data “payload”, but an
integer index into an auxiliary linear storage, referred to as a sidecar.
This topology/data separation provided a number of substantial benefits. First, it is trivial to store scalar fields corresponding to distinct

minimal storage footprint (typically less than 2 bits/voxel for grids
with approximately 50% leaf-level occupancy). Still, in this latest
iteration as presented in fVDB [Williams et al. 2024] NanoVDB
(including IndexGrids) remained a read-only structure after initialization. Our work closes exactly this feature gap, describing a set of
GPU-native dynamic topology manipulation operators.
At this point, we should clarify (and defend) the characterization
of such operators as “dynamic”, in contrast with the context that this
term is used elsewhere in the VDB ecosystem. OpenVDB supports a
truly laissez-faire paradigm of topology updates, where an individual
voxel can be introduced or removed from the active set at any
time. We argue that such an extremely permissive model of adhoc topology manipulation is not how typical high-throughput
(and at the same time thread-safe and deterministic) simulation
and geometry processing workloads ought to operate. OpenVDB
topology updates are not thread-safe, and must be guarded in usespecific ways to ensure that each execution thread doesn’t attempt
to consume voxels that were asynchronously created by another.
We advocate, especially in light of GPU-oriented workloads, for
a bulk-synchronous model of topological adaptation, where the
topological change is prescribed holistically for the entire domain
(e.g. a morphological dilation, or a topological union of two grids),
and enacted as one synchronous operation. Within this framework,
we defend the “dynamic” label for our update operators in the sense
that (a) they are GPU-native and highly optimized, and (b) they
operate natively within the VDB representation. The one concession
we commit to is the need to retain in memory both the “before”
and “after” version of the VDB topology, while the operation takes
place, although the absolute storage cost of this is small, due to the
topology/data separation of NanoVDB Index-Grids.
Sparse grids for level set methods and volumetric modeling. Sparse
volumetric grids have a long history in computer graphics, particularly in applications such as implicit modeling, fluid simulation, and
level set methods. Because this body of literature is too extensive to
cover in its entirety, we restrict our focus to data structures designed
for narrow-band level set simulations – a central application of the
current work – and exclude VDB-based approaches discussed above.
Existing methods generally fall into three categories: hierarchical,
blocked, or compressed representations. Hierarchical methods typically utilize height-balanced octrees [Losasso et al. 2004; Min 2004;
Strain 1999] to adaptively refine the grid near the interface; however,
these structures often suffer from the computational overhead associated with deep tree traversals and pointer chasing. To address cache
locality, blocked or tiled schemes, eg. [Lefohn et al. 2003], group voxels into contiguous memory blocks, leveraging hardware-friendly
layouts such as those found in SPGrids [Setaluri et al. 2014] and the
ACM Trans. Graph., Vol. 45, No. 6, Article 225. Publication date: December 2026.
more recent Multiresolution Sparse Block Grids (MSBG) [Braun et al.
2025]. Beyond its memory layout, SPGrid also demonstrates that
a pyramid of sparsely populated uniform grids can support adaptive, multi-resolution stencil work without explicit tree traversal
– the same coupling of resolution change and stencil computation
that our coarsening and refinement operators are intended to support. Conversely, compressed representations such as the Dynamic
Tubular Grid (DT-Grid) [Nielsen and Museth 2006] and Hierarchical Run-Length Encoding (H-RLE) [Houston et al. 2006] generalize
compressed-row-storage and RLE to 3D. While these formats provide exceptional memory efficiency for static volumes, they incur
significant re-structuring costs when the topology is dynamic and
requires frequent updates. Finally, out-of-core approaches [Nielsen
et al. 2007] enable streaming computing of extremely large level
sets, however their performance is fundamentally bottlenecked by
I/O bandwidth and the latency of disk-to-CPU data transfers and as
such cannot match the computational speeds of GPU approaches
like ours.
Block-sparse hash grids for volumetric reconstruction. Outside the
VDB ecosystem, the dominant sparse-volumetric representation in
real-time 3D reconstruction is the block-sparse hash grid: integer
block coordinates (typically 83 voxels per block) are inserted into a
parallel hash map, and each occupied block heap-allocates a dense
voxel payload. Newcombe et al. [2011]’s KinectFusion popularised
dense volumetric TSDF fusion from depth streams, and Nießner et al.
[2013] replaced the dense grid with a GPU hash map to remove the
bounded-domain restriction. This block-hash recipe has since been
adopted across the field: Voxblox [Oleynikova et al. 2017] extends it
to incremental Euclidean signed distance fields for MAV planning,
nvblox [Millane et al. 2024] ports the same recipe onto the GPU, and
Open3D [Zhou et al. 2018] ships a block-hash TSDF backend built
on ASH [Dong et al. 2023], a modern GPU spatial-hashing framework. Within the VDB family itself, VDBFusion [Vizzo et al. 2022]
demonstrates that an OpenVDB-backed TSDF pipeline reaches comparable quality without committing to a specific block resolution at
compile time. The octree alternative pioneered by OctoMap [Hornung et al. 2013] remains widely deployed in robotics for occupancy
mapping. All of these systems are built around a static fusion-andquery loop — they grow topology as new observations arrive but do
not support the kind of bulk topological rewriting (dilation, union,
pruning, refinement) that fluid simulation, dynamic-scene mapping,
and geometry processing demand. Section 5.1 compares directly
against nvblox, VDBFusion, and Open3D on the same reconstruction
benchmarks.
Sparse-voxel deep learning frameworks. A parallel line of work
targets sparse 3D learning rather than reconstruction. Minkowski
Engine [Choy et al. 2019] and spconv [Spconv Contributors 2022]
encode active coordinates in a hash table and run sparse convolutions over the resulting key-value structure; TorchSparse [Tang
et al. 2022] and its successor TorchSparse++ [Tang et al. 2023] extend this design with kernel-fusion and tile-sparsity optimisations.
O-CNN [Wang et al. 2017] and Kaolin [Jatavallabhula et al. 2019]
instead embed sparse data in an octree, while Taichi [Hu et al. 2019]
provides a more general DSL for spatially-sparse compute that has
been used both for simulation and for learning. NeuralVDB [Kim

et al. 2022] explores neural compression of VDB topology, complementary to the explicit dynamic operators we introduce here.
Compared with these flat hash-keyed representations, the VDB hierarchy used by fVDB [Williams et al. 2024] retains spatial locality at
every level of the tree, which our work exploits to make topological
updates — bit-parallel at the leaf, hash-based at the root — both
deterministic and fast.
Implicit and generative alternatives. Recent neural representations
sidestep explicit sparse grids by encoding geometry in network
weights and learned feature volumes. Instant-NGP [Müller et al.
2022] stores trainable features in a multiresolution hash table whose
collisions are disambiguated by the downstream MLP — an approach
that is highly efficient for fitting but does not expose an explicit
topology that downstream geometric operators can act on. Closer
to our setting, NKSR [Huang et al. 2023] demonstrates large-scale
neural surface reconstruction from sparse oriented points using a
VDB-backed kernel basis, and XCube [Ren et al. 2024] performs
voxel-hierarchy diffusion directly on VDB grids to generate 10243
scenes in a single feed-forward pass. Both rely on fVDB for their
voxel topology, and both would benefit directly from the dynamic
topology operators we develop here: NKSR’s basis support could
be incrementally refined as new observations arrive, and XCube’s
coarse-to-fine generation could write its progressively finer levels
through our refinement operator rather than rebuilding the grid
from scratch.
Resolution-selection criteria. A complementary line of work focuses on how resolution should adapt. In volumetric reconstruction
the criterion is typically derived from an observation model rather
than from geometry alone: Vespa et al. [2019] select the integration
scale within an octree from the effective sensor resolution at the
observed depth, while De Rebotti et al. [2025] adapt voxel size to the
local variance of SDF observations in a flat multi-resolution hash.
Both evaluate the criterion per block and apply it incrementally, as
observations arrive during fusion. Criteria of this kind are the natural drivers for the coarsening and refinement operators we introduce
in section 3, which supply the corresponding mechanism on the
GPU: such a predicate, evaluated over the active set, becomes the
input mask of a coarsening or refinement epoch, which the operator
then enacts as a single bulk rewrite of the topology. We deliberately
keep decision and mechanism separable – the same primitives serve
a sensor-driven, a variance-driven, or a purely geometric criterion
without modification.
3
Dynamic Topology Evolution
We propose a bulk-synchronous model for evolving the topological
domain of active voxels in a NanoVDB sparse grid. Topological
adaptation follows three steps: (a) topology changes (where and
by what rule) are decided collectively; (b) prescribed changes are
materialized in bulk, resulting in a new, valid topology and data
state; and (c) computation resumes on this new epoch of the grid.
We adopt this epoch-based paradigm as the foundation of our
dynamic topology updates, providing five operators for evolving
the hierarchical tree structure: (1) Morphological dilation expands
the active voxel set into its immediate neighborhood; (2) Pruning
225:5
removes voxels based on an input mask; (3) Merging computes a
Boolean union of two Index-Grids; (4) Coarsening downsamples by
a structured 2:1 ratio on each axis (8:1 volumetric reduction); and
(5) Refinement, the adjoint of coarsening, upsamples by the same
2:1 ratio.
These operators compose into macroscopic, domain-specific workflows. For example, a typical level set narrow-band update is recast
as three discrete steps: a speculative dilation to accommodate the anticipated motion of the interface; a fixed-topology numerical update
of the level set values; and a pruning pass to re-establish the narrowband limits and discard voxels that have moved beyond the region
of interest. These operators are designed specifically for Index-Grids
[Williams et al. 2024], which only store the topology structure of the
grid, leaving the data payload to a separate linearized sidecar. We
first describe the common skeleton (and individual details) for these
operators as they pertain to the rebuilding of the topology-only
grid itself; operators that update the sidecar storage in response to
topological changes are discussed in section 3.4. The applications
of section 5.1 additionally use the prior voxelsToGrid primitive of
Williams et al. [2024], which rasterizes a set of integer coordinates
into a fresh Index-Grid; we treat it as a black-box building block.
Figure 4 illustrates the conceptual pipeline that our topological
operators follow. Several elements of this pipeline are common to
all operators, while others are specialized per operation; we call
these out explicitly in the description of fig. 4.
3.1
Topology Enumeration and Allocation
Index-Grid reconstruction follows a multi-phase pipeline designed
to saturate GPU parallel resources while maintaining a linearized
memory layout. It begins with topology enumeration, where we
determine the hierarchical composition (node count per tree level)
of the new grid before any final memory is allocated.
Root Tile Table Pre-processing. We first construct a conservative
envelope of candidate root tiles for the target operation (fig. 4; Step
1). While a tight estimate is ideal, we prefer to over-provision and
speculatively create tiles that may go unused, as these are removed in
subsequent steps. The construction of this candidate set is operatorspecific: for merging, we take the union of all input root tiles; for
pruning, we retain the existing set (deferring removal of unused
ones). For dilation, coarsening, and refinement, we analyze the indexspace bounding box of each existing tile and instantiate candidate
neighbors wherever the operation may trigger spatial expansion.
These operations run on the CPU (e.g., via a hash table), as the root
table is a minuscule fraction of the tree’s total memory footprint.
Hierarchical Mask Marking. We next allocate two bit-encoded
scratchpad structures to enumerate the result tree nodes precisely
(fig. 4; Step 2). To visualize their encoding, consider as in fig. 5 a
conceptual dense NanoVDB tree that starts at the candidate root tiles
and branches out to a dense set of child nodes at every finer level,
down to leaf nodes. In NanoVDB, each non-leaf node maintains a
bitmap (childMask) marking all its geometric children that contain
active voxels. Our scratchpads store, sequentially, all the childMask
bitmaps at the two internal levels of the tree, as seen in fig. 5.

rarely exceed a few dozen tiles. Once a tile’s occupancy exceeds a
mere 0.01%, the footprint of a single scalar float channel already
outweighs these temporary bookkeeping structures.

3.2
Index-Grid Materialization and Voxel Population
With the destination buffer allocated and the hierarchical offset
arrays computed, we proceed to materialize the final Index-Grid in
three parallel stages: structural reconnection, voxel-level bit-mask
population, and topological post-processing.
Structural Reconnection and Root Pruning. The first stage “wires”
the parent-child relationships within the new buffer (fig. 4; Step
5). Each thread responsible for a node in the scratchpad uses its
corresponding prefix sum from the offsets arrays to resolve its
absolute address in the destination buffer. This phase also performs
the final pruning at the root level. While the CPU pre-processing
phase speculatively created a conservative envelope of candidate
root tiles, the upperOffsets array now acts as a contraction map:
a candidate tile 𝑖 is written to the final tileTable only if its entry
in the offset array indicates it is non-empty.
For every surviving tile and internal node, we update the relative
child pointers by sampling the starting offset of its children’s range
Operator-Specific Voxel Population. With the skeletal structure
established, we populate the leaf-level voxel masks (valueMask) in
an operation-specific manner (fig. 4; Step 6). Separating structural
reconnection from bit-mask filling is a key optimization, as the latter
can be performed in-place within the already-allocated hierarchy.
For operators such as merging and pruning, threads perform bitwise
OR or AND operations between input grids and masks, writing directly
to the new leaf nodes. For coarsening and refinement, we perform
structured 2:1 bit-permutations to downsample or upsample the
occupancy patterns. The logic for morphological dilation is more
complex and is detailed in section 3.3.
Topological Post-Processing. The final stage of the pipeline performs global bookkeeping. We compute a final prefix sum across
the newly populated leaf bit-masks to assign a unique voxel offset to every leaf node. This count provides the total active voxel
population of the tree and defines the size of the linearized data
sidecar discussed in section 4. Finally, a parallel reduction updates
the index-space bounding boxes and computes the grid checksum,
leaving the grid fully compliant with the NanoVDB specification.
3.3
Morphological Operators: Bit-Parallel Dilation
While merging and pruning follow a straightforward implementation path, morphological dilation presents a unique challenge: an
active voxel at the boundary of a leaf node may require materializing
adjacent neighbors to accommodate the dilation stencil’s footprint.
For a standard 3 × 3 × 3 stencil, a single boundary voxel may instigate the creation of up to seven neighboring leaf nodes (at a leaf
corner), while the collective occupancy of a leaf can influence all
26 neighbors. To resolve this without expensive tree traversals for
neighbor access, we implement a hierarchical bit-parallel dilation
that decouples topological expansion from voxel-level population.
Leaf-level Neighbor Influence Masks. Dilation begins with a parallel sweep in which each leaf node is processed by an individual
CUDA thread, determining whether the dilated footprint of the
leaf’s active voxels intersects any of its 26 leaf neighbors and thus
necessitates their materialization in the result tree.
We recall that the 8 × 8 × 8 voxel occupancy mask is bit-packed
into eight uint64_t variables (in Mask<3>). For a standard 3 × 3 × 3
box dilation, we infer whether each leaf neighbor must materialize
using simple bitwise operations on these packed words: combining
slices via bitwise OR and isolating the “faces” of the 83 volume via
bitwise AND with predetermined masks yields a 27-bit influence
mask per leaf, indicating whether each of its leaf neighbors needs
to be introduced. These masks accommodate even wider dilation
scenarios; for any stencil with a radius 𝑅 ≤ 8, the influence of an
83 leaf node is strictly confined to its immediate 33 cluster of leaf
neighbors. This leaf-level computation is the only step that inspects
the dilation stencil itself; all subsequent steps depend only on the
resulting mask of affected leaf neighbors.

Voxel-sequential access and the VoxelBlockManager
A fundamental feature of Index-Grids [Williams et al. 2024] is the
compactness of storage they enable: only values associated with
active voxels need to be stored in the auxiliary sidecar, in contrast
to prior iterations of NanoVDB, which quantize data allocation upwards to full 8×8×8 blocks corresponding to the domain of each leaf
node. It is natural to seek a matching compactness of computation,
especially for streaming and stencil-oriented workloads. Ideally, a
data-parallel operation such as applying a finite difference stencil
to all active voxels would iterate over the linear sequence of active
voxels, regardless of how they are distributed across leaf nodes or
how sparsely occupied those leaf nodes are in different parts of the
domain.
Implementing such an iteration paradigm, which we call voxelsequential access, is significantly more challenging on the GPU. On
the CPU, the typical parallelization pattern in both OpenVDB and
NanoVDB distributes leaf nodes across threads and processes their
active voxels in a single-threaded iteration. On the GPU, the canonical pattern launches kernels over leaf nodes, with an entire thread
block to process all 83 voxels of a leaf in parallel, active or not. The
load balance difficulty is clear: any warp mapped to a 32-tuple of

than its number of taps. The fifth-order Hamilton-Jacobi WENO

taps, at grid offsets (−3 . . . 3, 0, 0) , (0, −3 . . . 3, 0) , and (0, 0, −3 . . . 3),

and fetch them in bulk via NanoVDB’s probeLeaf; the indices of all
19 stencil taps are then computed with fully warp-synchronous, nondivergent operations. Similar optimizations apply to other stencil
types; for example a 3 × 3 × 3 convolution stencil for machine learning workloads touches at most 7 neighboring leaf nodes (typically
far fewer), to compute all 27 taps.

Applications
The dynamic topology operators (section 3) and the voxel-sequential
access paradigm (VBM, section 4) are general-purpose primitives
that compose into algorithms spanning a wide variety of real-world
domains. We demonstrate this with applications from 3D perception
and reality capture (section 5.1), fluid and gas simulation (section 5.2),
and large-scale shape voxelization and rendering (section 5.3). Furthermore, the algorithms built from these compositions are often
more memory-efficient and performant than their counterparts in
the literature. The details of the hardware used for our experiments
can be found in Appendix A.

volumetric field, and dilated outwards from the surface crossing voxels to create a narrow band of active voxels. The Eikonal equation is
iteratively solved over just this sparse narrow band using a stencil

for a total of 18 adjacent voxel visits in addition to the one central
voxel visit. The discretization of the Euler equation is achieved with
the optimally fifth-order accurate WENO scheme [Liu et al. 1994],
which is both computationally and arithmetically complex.
Level set tracking. The flame front is represented implicitly as the
zero contour of a signed-distance field 𝜙, stored on a narrow band
of active voxels around the interface. Each timestep advects 𝜙 by
the flame-front velocity (derived from first-order detonation shock
dynamics (DSD) [Hong et al. 2007]), then reinitializes it back to a
signed-distance function so the contour itself stays sharp while 𝜙
remains usable elsewhere as a smooth distance. Because the field is
sparse, the active set has to move with the interface: each step (i)
dilates the band so the next advect has source voxels to read from,
(ii) semi-Lagrangian advects 𝜙 along 𝑤, (iii) reinitializes 𝜙 inside the
band with a few iterations of a WENO-discretized Hamilton–Jacobi
update [Liu et al. 1994], and (iv) prunes voxels whose |𝜙 | has grown
past a band-width threshold. The microbenchmarks below isolate
three operators from this pipeline—reinit, dilate, and prune.

Reinitialization micro-benchmark. The reinit kernel implements
WENO5 on top of the new Voxel Block Manager (VBM) interface,
and is compared against an accessor-only baseline that represents
best-practice NanoVDB usage today. Each WENO5 update consumes
a 19-point cross stencil per active voxel (center, plus 𝑅=3 taps along
each of ±𝑥, ±𝑦, ±𝑧); the two implementations differ in how they
fetch those off-center taps. The sparse grid stores active voxels in
83 tiles, so an off-center tap usually lands in the same tile as the
center but occasionally crosses into a neighboring one. The accessor
variant launches one CUDA block per tile whose threads walk the
tree for every off-center tap, employing a small tree-walk helper
that caches the last-touched tile, so consecutive within-tile taps
are O(1) but every tile-boundary tap re-walks the tree. The VBM
variant amortizes that bookkeeping across a 128-thread cluster of
consecutive active voxels: the cluster cooperatively resolves its 128
tile addresses into shared memory once, and because 𝑅=3 is strictly
less than the 8-voxel tile width, a single neighboring-tile pointer per
axis covers every off-center tap any thread in the cluster can reach.
The 19 stencil reads then compile to direct array loads against four
resident tile pointers, with no per-tap tree walk. Figure 9 plots the
throughput of the VBM-based WENO5 iteration against the accessor
baseline as a function of mean leaf occupancy. VBM’s throughput is
essentially constant and up to 1.98× that of the accessor implementation, whose throughput decreases as occupancy decreases and a
growing fraction of its threads land on inactive slots.

5.3
Large-Scale Shape Voxelization
We demonstrate one further application of our topological pipeline:
transforming a triangle mesh into a rasterized, sparse Unsigned
Distance Field (UDF). Although distinct from the topological operators of section 3, it constructively interweaves with the pipeline of
fig. 4. Since our objective is a narrow-band UDF, we use intersection
between a triangle and a volume primitive to indicate intersection
with a thickness equal to the narrow band half-width.
We first stream through the triangle list, identifying how many
root tiles (40963 voxels each) each triangle interacts with. Deduplicating these root tiles seeds steps 1–2 of the topological pipeline.
A hierarchical broad-phase intersection progressively refines the
(triangle,rootTile) pairs into (triangle,leafNode) pairs, where each
leafNode spans 83 voxels. This list marks the lower and leaf node
bitmaps in Step 3; steps 4–5 then build the sparse tree that will hold
the voxelized representation, with no voxels yet marked active. Step
6 visits the 83 voxels of each leaf appearing in a (triangle,leafNode)
pair with a 512-thread CUDA block, marking active every voxel
within the narrow-band half-width of the triangle. With the topology constructed, we allocate the UDF sidecar at exactly the storage
the narrow band requires. Finally, we resolve the (triangle,leafNode)
list once more and perform a minimum reduction in the sidecar,
preserving at every voxel the smallest distance across all nearby
triangles.
We evaluate the construction on the intricate “hairball” triangle
mesh [McGuire 2017] shown in fig. 12, consisting of 2.8 million
triangles. The mesh is rasterized into a NanoVDB index grid with
UDF values stored in a separate sidecar array. At voxel size 0.003
and narrow-band half-width 2, OpenVDB’s meshToUDF produced
1.025 × 109 active voxels in 23.87 seconds, while our MeshToGrid
implementation produced 1.026 × 109 active voxels in 2.30 seconds—
a roughly 10× speedup at comparable resolution. At voxel size 0.001
with the same narrow-band width, our implementation generated
9.88 × 109 active voxels in 3.24 seconds; OpenVDB did not complete,
terminating on memory exhaustion. These results highlight the
ability of NanoVDB’s dynamic sparse-grid construction to capture
fine geometric detail at multi-billion-voxel scale while maintaining interactive construction times. For visualization, we render the
sparse grid directly with the hierarchical digital differential analyzer (HDDA) in the NanoVDB library[Museth 2014]. Rays traverse
the sparse index-grid topology, and surface hits are identified by
detecting UDF iso-crossings along the traversed active voxels, so
the multi-billion-voxel result is visualized without conversion to a
dense representation.

Limitations and Future Work
Topological Operations. Although our topological operation framework can easily accommodate larger dilation stencils (as long as
their radius does not exceed the size of leaf nodes, i.e 8 voxels, to
avoid modifications to other “generic” components of the pipeline),
stencils that have substantially more irregular (non-rectangular,
non-symmetric) shapes might offer fewer opportunities for the aggressive bit-parallel optimizations we employed. We note however
that box stencils of radius up to 8 can be trivially accommodated
with bit-parallel operations. Although we have included details on
the implementation of morphological dilation, we have yet to implement its inverse operator, namely, morphological erosion. We
plan to achieve this in terms of the existing prune operator. We
should mention that our scratchpad-based bookkeeping commits to
allocating approximately 16MB of storage for every 40963 root tile
that potentially overlaps with the output of our operation. Although
it is unlikely that this limitation will prevent us from scaling up (the
data payload would be substantially larger), it could potentially limit

Voxel-sequential processing. The key benefit of the Voxel-sequential
access paradigm (via VBM) is maximization of compute bandwidth,
by ensuring that data-parallel processors (e.g. GPUs) saturate execution units with regular work on active voxels. This benefit is
diminished in domains where leaf nodes are mostly dense, and
those are also examples of applications where “traditional” NanoVDB grids (leaf-level dense) might be more appropriate than sparse
Index-Grids. The benefit of maximizing compute density and saturation is also more prominent on compute bound, stencil-based
operations; the WENO stencil in our fluid simulation examples, or
sparse convolutions with deep vector-valued features (reducing to
GEMM operations) would be best examples of such compute-bound
workloads that stand to gain the most out of VBM. Memory-bound
workloads (e.g. very small stencils on scalar fields, like a gradient or
divergence Finite Difference operator) would struggle to see substantial benefits. Finally, workloads that access sparse data in spatially
coherent (but not streaming/sequential) patterns, would remain
closer to random-access tools (e.g. accessors) as the paradigm of
choice.
Cross-resolution operators. The coarsening and refinement operators share the same processing pipeline and building blocks as
dilation, merging, and pruning; nevertheless, they are not exercised end-to-end by any of the applications of section 5. We also
acknowledge that on an application-specific basis they may need to
be paired with additional sidecar manipulation tools, beyond what
we presented here. For example, one possible application would be
the construction of a pyramid of sparse grids for multiresolution
solvers, in the spirit of Setaluri et al. [2014]. Such a workflow would
also require a cross-resolution analogue of inject: whereas our
present sidecar injection relates voxels across two topologies by
coordinate identity, a 2:1 transfer is a reduction in one direction and

an interpolation in the other, calling for an upsample/downsample
(prolongation/restriction) operator optimized for sparse GPU computation. We defer these application-specific extensions to future

Parallelism strategies. The five algorithms considered in section 5.1
and this appendix use three distinct GPU parallelism strategies depending on which is best matched to the workload’s data flow.
(i) Per-leaf-slot dispatch with an active-voxel mask, used by the
depth-TSDF update kernel of algorithm 1: this is the simplest pattern and is competitive when leaf occupancy is high. (ii) Per-ray
hierarchical-DDA, used by the LiDAR-TSDF update of algorithm 1
and by algorithm 4: one thread per input point walks the active
voxels along its ray and writes via lock-free atomic accumulation.
(iii) The voxel-sequential VBM dispatch of section 4, used by the
ESDF sweep of algorithm 2: each active voxel is mapped to a single
thread independently of leaf occupancy, which is the right pattern
for low-occupancy stencil work. We quantify the VBM speedup over
per-leaf-slot dispatch on the ESDF sweep in section B.3.

B.2
Cross-library evaluation
Setup. We benchmark against nvblox [Millane et al. 2024] for
LiDAR/depth TSDF, ESDF, and occupancy; Open3D [Zhou et al.
2018] for depth TSDF; and VDBFusion [Vizzo et al. 2022] for LiDAR
TSDF. All measurements are on a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada
Generation GPU (48 GB) with PyTorch’s expandable_segments
allocator. All systems use truncation distance 𝜏 = 3 × voxel size for
TSDF and maximum ESDF distance 𝑅 = 10 × voxel size, applied uniformly across baselines so the comparison is on integrator behaviour
rather than parameter choices. nvblox in particular is configured
with projective_integrator_ truncation_distance_vox = 3.0
(matching 𝜏) and a value lidar_projective_integrator_max_
integration_distance_m of 60 m on LiDAR plus an 8 m equivalent
on Replica/7-Scenes depth, in place of nvblox’s upstream defaults
of 4.0× truncation and 10 m / 7 m integration ranges; the upstream
defaults clip nvblox’s input to a ±10 m strip on LiDAR and inflate
its truncation band by 33%, neither of which is consistent with the
workload fvdb processes.
Sensitivity to baseline defaults. As discussed, larger parameter
values were chosen in order to produce larger scale scenes, consistent with the size of workloads our system was designed to process,
rather than the upstream defaults assigned in nvblox. The voxelsize thresholds which produce out-of-memory errors for nvblox in
table 4 and table 6 which support our scale claims are insensitive to
using nvblox’s default or the 6× larger value we chose.
We repeated the table 4 and table 6 sweeps with nvblox at its upstream defaults: 10 m LiDAR max integration distance and 4× voxel
truncation (ProjectiveIntegratorParams defaults in nvblox version 0.0.10). In this experiment (table 2), nvblox’s OOM voxel size
moved by at most one voxel-size step in the sweep, and on Mai City
it did not move at all.
nvblox’s OOM is triggered by its GPU block-hash rebuild (hardcoded max_load_factor = 0.5, growth_factor = 2): when the
accumulated map crosses half of the current power-of-two capacity,
nvblox constructs a new table at twice the capacity while the old
one is still resident and re-inserts every entry, with the backing
voxel-block pool growing by the same ×2 policy. On Mai City at
5 cm, this rebuild transiently demands ~50 GB of device memory
to admit 854 new blocks into the map. Using a 96 GB device, we
measured that the run settles at 4.85 GB but peaks at 51.7 GB during
the rebuild which will OOM with the 48 GB budget of the RTX 6000
Ada used in the experiments. Because the trigger is the accumulated

and our update kernel touches only those.
Memory regime caveat. nvblox stores its state as fixed 8 × 8 × 8
voxel blocks, allocating every cell in a block whether the cell is
active or not; ours allocates exactly the active voxels with a pervoxel index. The two strategies trade places depending on how
densely each 83 block is occupied. On dense KITTI seq 02 / 20 cm
LiDAR TSDF, urban ground returns fill blocks nearly to capacity,
so nvblox’s per-block overhead amortises and our per-voxel index
has the larger footprint: our peak GPU memory (5.04 GB) exceeds
nvblox’s (3.08 GB) by 1.6× (table 3). The same regime appears at
40 cm KITTI where the gap is small (within ±20%). Conversely, on

allocation while our cost grows with the count of voxels actually
stored in the truncation shell. Open3D begins to fail on increasingly
many Replica scenes as the voxel size shrinks below 5 mm and
crashes on every scene at 3 mm (caption of table 5). Ours scales
monotonically through 3 mm at an 8-scene mean 44.50 ms/f over
∼109 M active TSDF voxels and an 8-scene-mean 1.61 s ESDF cold
sweep over 438 M ESDF voxels. Our claims pertain to scale and
breadth, not throughput parity on this single workload.

(2) Cold ESDF cost is 1.0–2.0× higher than nvblox’s at 20 cm
KITTI voxels (tied on seq 02, 1.5× slower on seq 05, 2.0×
slower on seq 00), and 1.5× higher at 40 cm Mai City. We
become uniformly faster from 20 cm on Mai City and from
5 cm on KITTI; nvblox OOMs from 5 cm KITTI seq 02 and
3 cm everywhere else.
(3) Our memory advantage over nvblox’s per-block storage
is regime-dependent. Dense urban LiDAR at coarse voxels
(KITTI seq 02 / 20 cm TSDF) flips the per-voxel comparison to
nvblox-favourable by 1.6× since its 83 blocks are nearly full;
sparse-state fine-voxel occupancy (KITTI 1.5 cm) flips it back
to a 3.5× advantage in our favour, and at 1 cm nvblox cannot
fit at all. The advantage holds asymptotically for sparselyoccupied applications (Memory regime caveat, above).
(4) The VBM speedup over per-leaf-slot dispatch on the
ESDF sweep is 3–6%, not the order-of-magnitude one
might expect from a hierarchical-dispatch-replacement story.
ESDF leaves end up densely occupied by the dilation step
(algorithm 2, line 1), so per-leaf-slot iteration wastes few
threads on inactive entries. VBM’s advantage is realised on
low-occupancy stencil work where active voxels are sparse
within each leaf.
A fifth caveat was present in early designs of algorithm 3: incremental ESDF warm-reuse was 400× slower than nvblox’s update_esdf.
The dirty-mask Python short-circuit of algorithm 3 reduces the nochange case to sub-400 𝜇s and matches nvblox’s sub-100 𝜇s blockcache hit on the typical sparse-update case.
