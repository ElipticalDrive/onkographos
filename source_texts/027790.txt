Abstract
This paper presents a hardware accelerated approach to the voxelization of a wide range of 3D objects, including curves/surfaces, solids, and volumetric CSG models. It allows 3D scenes to be modeled and manipulated in their own representations, and generates the volume representations of regions of interest on-the-fly for volumetric operations. The algorithms take advantages of graphics hardware features in modern graphics workstations, such as texture mapping and frame buffer blending functions, to generate slices of the volume representations for various 3D models. As a result, we are able to perform interactive object manipulations and Boolean operations in an intermixed environment of geometric and volumetric objects under a unified volume graphics framework.

Introduction
Volume visualization and volume graphics have attracted increasing research interest in recent years. Generally speaking, volume visualization is concerned with the abstraction, interpretation and rendering of volume data sets from sources such as medical imaging data acquisition (e.g. CT or MRI scans) and scientific simulations. Volume graphics [1], on the other hand, is concerned with the modeling, manipulation and display of synthetic geometric objects using volumetric representations and volume rendering techniques. This allows geometric objects to be intermixed with volume data sets under a uniform volume representation scheme. In this process, the 3D scene is pre-converted to a volume representation using voxelization algorithms before volumetric modeling and rendering techniques are applied. Thus, voxelization is mainly used as a pre-processing step in the current volume graphics paradigm. A major drawback of this paradigm is its limited support for dynamic scenes and interactive applications. A volume representation is often not suitable for or capable of providing many sophisticated object manipulation and modeling operations needed for applications such as computer-aided design, animation and surgical simulation. The modeling, animation and interaction techniques under each object's original representation (geometric or volumetric) are generally more convenient, accurate, and cost-effective. In a computer-aided design system, for instance, complex geometric modeling operations have to be interactively applied to the geometric representations. The voxelization process, therefore, needs to be done on-the-fly after each change of the model for volume rendering and other volume-related applications such as layered manufacturing and finite element analysis. The ability to work with both geometric and volumetric representations also allows some part of the 3D models to be directly defined from scanned volume data to produce alternative 3D models that are often more accurate and realistic. As another example, a surgical simulation system needs to combine medical scanning data sets, surgical tools, and synthetic models (e.g. artificial organs) in one operational procedure, and perform interactive surgical operations with both geometric and volumetric representations.
To support dynamic scenes and interactive applications, an interactive volume graphics paradigm can be employed. In this approach, 3D scenes are modeled and manipulated in their own representations, and the volume techniques are only applied to the desired regions (e.g. the viewing regions) of the scene when needed. This requires fast voxelization algorithms that are able to generate volume representations of regions of interest on-the-fly for complex 3D scenes involving both geometric and volumetric objects. Although there have been many discussions in the literatures on voxelization algorithms and related properties, the issue of speed has rarely been addressed, primarily due to the notion that voxelization is a pre-processing procedure. As a consequence, current voxelization algorithms are not able to meet the speed requirement for interactive volume graphics. Unlike volume rendering where special hardware can be designed to achieve real-time rendering from the volume buffer, special hardware for voxelization is very difficult to design and implement since voxelization algorithms are generally object type dependent, and do not offer a simple and uniform procedure suitable for efficient hardware implementation. This paper offers a more cost-effective solution, based on the fact that current 3D graphics systems have already been providing a uniform treatment of a wide range of 3D objects by using a set of standard graphics primitives and powerful 3D graphics processors to display the boundary surfaces of 3D objects. Using existing 3D graphics hardware systems, our voxelization approach is able to provide interactive voxelization for any region of interest with desired resolution. This enables a natural integration of volume-based techniques into a surface-based graphics system for interactive volume graphics applications.
Conceptually, voxelization is a set membership classification problem for all voxels in a 3D regular grid against the given 3D model. It can also be considered as a 3D extension of the 2D scan conversion process. In recent years, a number of curve and surface voxelization algorithms have been proposed [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Broadly speaking, these algorithms aim to provide efficient ways to extend 2D scan conversion methods to a volumetric domain. This requires additional sampling in the third dimension for each scanline. Although several important theoretical issues, such as accuracy, separability (or tunnel-free), and minimality, have been addressed in previous publications [2], [4], [5], [8], a central issue, namely the voxelization speed, has received very little research attention. In addition, existing algorithms are object-type specific, i.e. a different algorithm needs to be employed for a different type of object (e.g. lines, circles, polygons, quadratic surfaces, etc.), leading to implementation difficulties for general volume graphics tools.
Solid object voxelization, on the other hand, has not been sufficiently studied. Solid objects are normally represented by their boundary surfaces, commonly defined as graphics primitives such as OpenGL polygons. Since the interior of a solid object is not explicitly represented, solid voxelization is difficult, and in principle requires an inside test for each voxel involved. Thus, surface voxelization methods cannot be directly applied. The only available algorithm for solid objects is the one by Lee and Requicha [9] based on point classification. Unfortunately, this algorithm is too slow for interactive applications.
Another common solid representation is the constructive solid geometry (CSG) method [10]. Graphics techniques for CSG models have been studied by many researchers. Most of such efforts are focused on the direct display of CSG Objects [11], [12]. With volume graphics, the CSG method can also be extended to include volume data sets as CSG primitives. Such models are called volumetric CSG (or VCSG) models [13], and are potentially useful for combining geometric and volumetric objects in one common modeling environment. There are also a number of voxelization and volume rendering techniques for CSG or VCSG models. They include the beam-oriented voxelization algorithm [14], the volume sampling approach [15], the point sampling algorithm [16], the octree-based rendering algorithm [13], and the distance volume algorithm [17]. In general, these algorithms reconstruct a volume for the entire CSG or VCSG model in some form, which is then rendered by a volume rendering algorithm. Since these volume reconstruction algorithms for CSG or VCSG models are very slow, the VCSG technique has not yet provided effective support to interactive volumetric modeling.
For the convenience of presentation, we will use OpenGL as a representative surface graphics programming interface, and the algorithms have been indeed all implemented in OpenGL. But the approach, in principle, can also be used with any graphics API that providex ways to access the required hardware features. The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the basic curve and surface voxelization algorithm. The solid voxelization algorithm is described in Section 3. In Section 4, the volumetric-CSG (VCSG) model and its voxelization algorithm will be presented. Some implementation experience and results will be given in Section 5. Further discussions about the advantages and disadvantages of this approach compared to other approaches and alternatives are given in Section 6. We conclude this paper with a few more remarks and future work in Section 7.

Implementation

The program is written in C and OpenGL 1.1. Hardware system requirements include 3D texture mapping, frame buffer blending functions and a polygon-based graphics processor.

Discussion
Our approach aims to provide a practical and fast solution using existing graphics systems. But since the algorithms, to a certain extent, depend on the hardware implementations of the graphics API, certain properties of the voxelization results are difficult to assess. For instance, anti-aliasing is used to ensure that the voxelized surfaces are connected and tunnel-free. But the exact result may change slightly on different systems due to the different anti-aliasing implementations.
Conclusions
We have presented a hardware accelerated and region-based approach for fast voxelization of curves/surfaces, solid objects, and VCSG models. The algorithms use existing 3D graphics hardware, and are able to provide volume graphics support for interactive volume modeling applications involving both geometric and volumetric objects, with inter-object operations.
