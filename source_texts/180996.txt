How are people getting good results with dual contouring? (Especially when edges aren't aligned with grid)
Question
I'm working on a game where the main mechanic requires creating a mesh from signed distance fields, and naturally I was drawn to dual contouring because of its promise of retaining sharp edges and corners.
I have it working on a compute shader in Unity. Unfortunately, I've been having pretty mixed results. It seems really unpredictable, to the extent that I really feel I must be doing something wrong.
Vanilla dual contouring gives me crazy degenerate triangles and vertices everywhere, but that's ok because I know it can be unstable in flat regions. So I added a check to ensure that the result of the QEF solver stays inside the cell. If not, I fall back on the naive surface nets solution (average of the edge intersections.) This to me seems like a good compromise, because naive surface nets provides good results in flat regions. I find adding an additional margin before resorting to this also helps. I'm also only using a uniform grid, no octree.
So here's some dual contouring on a cuboid and torus with flat shading. It looks pretty decent until I start rotating the cuboid.
Here's rotating the cuboid. Note that I'm not actually rotating the mesh, rather the underlying sdf. Are the artifacts on the edges something it's possible to avoid?
If someone has figured out a technique to smooth out the edges, or even an alternate way of solving the QEF, it'd be much appreciated.
(I should note that the QEF stuff is kind of a mysterious black box for me. I get what it's doing but not how. I'm using a solution I found online.)

Hi!
These artifacts ("jagged/chamfered/cut sharp corners/edges") happen at least for the following reasons:
you clamp sharp vertices' positions to lie inside their cells;
if the object is "badly" aligned w.r.t. the grid, or the object has thin/small features (aliasing), in some grid cells the edges may miss the surface even if the cell actually intersects the surface (imagine a cone-shaped surface piercing a cell face). As a result, predicted vertex positions might lie outside their cells and would get clamped.
When you rotate the object w.r.t. the grid, the set of active (i.e. intersecting the object's surface) grid edges/cells changes, so vertex positions change/wobble.
3) you most likely are not splitting quads along the sharp diagonal (which connects quad vertices that lie on sharp corners/a sharp edge).
This is a fundamental limitation of all techniques that rely on grid edges vs surface intersections to reconstruct the surface (and its sharp features), like Dual Contouring (and Manifold DC, Dual Marching Cubes (with Hermite data, Gregory Nielson), Cubical Marching Squares and Extended Marching Cubes). I'm afraid, moving to octree won't help much.
You may also want to read papers on SHREC/MergeSharp algorithm and this post, they discuss the same problem:
https://www.reddit.com/r/dualcontouring/comments/4l6pfm/questiondual_contouring_sharp_features_are_lost/
How to solve it?
Give the vertices more freedom, use some slack/margin for clamping. But it will cause more intersecting quads/ugly folds in the resulting mesh.
Use another meshing algorithm, e.g. Dual Marching Cubes (Primal Contouring of Dual Grids) and its variants - it fits the grid to the "ridges" of the distance function, not to the surface features, but they don't support multiple materials out-of-the-box.
paniq's hybrid meshing algorithm or Direct/Closest-Point Contouring should also better preserve sharp corners, because they don't rely on grid edge intersections to construct quads (like DC), they build triangles by connecting vertices of 2x2x2 adjacent cells.
3) ???
I'm also interesting in solving this problem and building the "sharpest" polygonizer, so please let me know if you find something.

Hi, thanks for the response!
So, as I mentioned in my other reply in this thread, the clamping doesn't seem to be the culprit. That said, after messing with the settings of the QEF (epsilon size, number of sweeps) I no longer find the clamping nearly as necessary.
Additionally, I already have the quad splitting thing in there!
So over the weekend I did manage to find a method which, while not solving it entirely, does seem to improve sharp edges a lot. What's interesting is that I haven't seen it proposed anywhere.
In my implementation, I already have a final step which uses the underlying SDF to 'correct' every vertex back onto the surface. What this means is I'm slightly less worried about being totally accurate on the distance relative to the surface, it's the direction I'm worried about. The distance will always be forced back to near zero at the final step.
So I added one more step before this 'correction'. I take the average normal of each intersection point's normal in that cell, and add that (multiplied by some small scalar) to the vertex. This has the effect of exaggerating the influence of the direction at the expense of moving the vertex way off position.
This image shows the steps.
And here's the relevant bit of HLSL code. Note that 'massPoint' is the solution you would get from surface nets. The dot product bit mitigates the artifacting this produces with interior angles, and the max with 0 prevents the direction from pointing into the shape. The scalar is something you need to experiment with: I find 0.1 is good. Any more and you encounter the obvious drawback of this solution: if you exaggerate the surface position too much, the closest surface to that vertex may be totally wrong and you'll get weird spiky artifacts.
```
    float dotWithMassPoint = max(0, dot(sumNormal, normalize(surfacePoint - massPoint)));
    
    surfacePoint += sumNormal * _NudgeVerticesToAverageNormalScalar * dotWithMassPoint;
    
#ifdef APPLY_GRADIENT_DESCENT
    for (int gIterations = 0; gIterations < _GradientDescentIterations; gIterations++)
    {
        // add to the 'surface' point the vector which brings it to the closest point on the surface
        surfacePoint -= (MapNormal(surfacePoint) * Map(surfacePoint));
    }
#endif
```
Hope this helps anyone else who falls down the dual contouring rabbit hole in future. I definitely want to see if I can improve this further.

Hey,
I've also started to work on dual-contouring and I wanted to mention the following with respect to boxes: when you perform dual contouring it is assumed that you are given ideal edge intersection points and normals. In reality, however, you will use a grid to sample the SDF and then determine edge intersection points from sampled SDF values. For this to work, one usually makes another set of assumptions:
- the surface is smooth in the vicinity of an edge
- edges are small compared to the surface shape.
using the two assumptions, you may then find intersection points by approximating the intersection point along the edge using a linear equation (derived from the two sdf values at edge endpoints).
Now here is the issue: the SDF of a box is not smooth, which breaks the very first assumption and hence determining intersection points becomes unstable. Here is quick sketch of an edge interacting with the sdf of box. You can see that linear approximation will yield a misplaced vertex (hallow circle).
https://imgur.com/a/DXoYMUX
However, moving the vertex according to the SDF gradient will also be of no help, as it will move the vertex to the right instead of to the top. This will probably lead to self-intersecting faces in the result.
Update
I've added improved plots, explanations and mitigation strategies to the documention of my dual isoextraction library:
https://github.com/cheind/sdftoolbox/blob/main/doc/SDF.md
