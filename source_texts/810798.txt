Abstract
Voxel-based volumetric mapping is fundamental to 3D reconstruction, yet fixedresolution grids remain inherently inefficient – wasting memory in uniform regions and
losing detail in complex ones. Existing adaptive methods, such as MAP-ADAPT, partially address this by varying resolution based on geometry and user-defined semantic
class lists, but these heuristics require expert tuning, lack generalization to unseen objects, and provide no explicit mechanism to control memory usage. We propose an adaptive framework that refines voxels based on semantic entropy, which captures label uncertainty, together with geometric curvature and texture richness as scene complexity cues,
yielding principled resolution allocation without reliance on semantic taxonomies. To
make the accuracy-memory trade-off explicit and user-controlled, we further introduce a
reinforcement learning agent that learns voxel subdivision policies under a user-specified
target memory budget, replacing hand-tuned thresholds with a single intuitive control parameter. The resulting multi-resolution TSDF achieves higher geometric accuracy, better semantic consistency, and improved memory-accuracy trade-offs compared to MAPADAPT and fixed-resolution baselines on both synthetic and real-world datasets. Our
code and models are available at https://github.com/alpayozkan/UnRL.
Introduction
Autonomous systems rely on 3D maps that capture geometry, color, and semantics to perceive, navigate, and interact with their environment. Volumetric representations based on
Truncated Signed Distance Fields (TSDFs) [17, 18, 19] have become the standard for dense
reconstruction, offering accurate geometry and consistent integration over time. However,
conventional TSDF pipelines employ a fixed voxel resolution, which leads to redundant storage in large homogeneous regions (e.g., walls or floors) and loss of detail in complex areas.

Recent work has therefore shifted toward adaptive mapping, where voxel resolution is
adjusted according to scene complexity. MAP-ADAPT [31] represents an important step in
this direction, combining geometric cues and manually defined semantic classes to control
voxel subdivision. By allocating higher resolution to semantically meaningful regions, it
achieves compact multi-resolution 3D voxel maps while maintaining fidelity. However, its
refinement policy remains tied to user-specified semantic classes and fixed geometric rules,
requiring dataset- or task-specific design choices and providing no class-agnostic measure
of semantic ambiguity or texture complexity. Moreover, these deterministic rules provide no
principled mechanism to manage the trade-off between reconstruction quality and limited
storage capacity.
We address these limitations by reformulating adaptive mapping as a data-driven refinement problem guided by semantic uncertainty and geometric/texture complexity. Each voxel
maintains a semantic class distribution whose entropy quantifies label uncertainty, complemented by geometric curvature and texture richness scores that capture local scene complexity. Subdivision is triggered when semantic uncertainty is high or when scene complexity
indicates that coarse resolution cannot faithfully represent the local geometry or appearance,
ensuring that memory is devoted to informative regions rather than predefined object categories. This data-driven refinement produces adaptive maps that respond dynamically to
scene complexity, semantic ambiguity, and visual richness.
While these signals provide a principled refinement criterion, the deterministic variant

Our main contributions are as follows:
• Unified adaptive mapping framework: We integrate geometric, semantic, and texture complexity signals into a multi-resolution TSDF representation that generalizes
beyond class-based heuristics and scales with available memory.
• Uncertainty- and complexity-driven adaptivity: We trigger voxel refinement using semantic entropy as a measure of label uncertainty, complemented by geometric
curvature and texture richness as scene complexity signals, providing a unified and
data-driven subdivision criterion.
• Reinforcement-learned subdivision policy: We train an RL agent using Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO) [24] to decide when and where to subdivide, learning
budget-aware policies that trade map fidelity against storage cost.

Related Work
Volumetric Mapping. Volumetric 3D reconstruction based on Truncated Signed Distance
Fields (TSDFs) is widely used for dense scene mapping [17, 18, 19]. These methods integrate RGB-D observations into voxel grids, providing continuous surface representations
and free-space reasoning. To mitigate the memory footprint of dense grids, hierarchical data
structures such as voxel hashing [8, 18] and octrees [6, 27] have been proposed to allocate
voxels only near surfaces. While these systems achieve real-time performance and scalable
mapping, they typically rely on a fixed voxel resolution, which limits their ability to represent both fine local details and large-scale environments efficiently. Neural implicit methods
such as NeRF [15] and 3D Gaussian Splatting [9] target image rendering rather than explicit volumetric mapping, and do not produce TSDF grids, semantic labels, or free-space
representations required for planning and navigation.
Semantic Mapping. Integrating semantic understanding into 3D mapping has been central to advancing scene perception. Early works like SemanticFusion [12] fused per-frame
semantic predictions into surfel-based reconstructions, while Kimera [22] combined realtime metric reconstruction with semantic fusion into a unified volumetric mesh. Instanceand panoptic-level methods [5, 13, 16, 28] further extended these ideas by maintaining perobject volumes or combining instance labels. However, these pipelines generally operate at
uniform spatial resolution, distributing computational and memory resources equally across
all regions, independent of their semantic or geometric complexity.
Adaptive Mapping. To improve efficiency, adaptive techniques dynamically adjust voxel
resolution based on scene content or task relevance. Geometry-based adaptivity refines regions of high curvature, surface density, or structural complexity [25, 26, 27, 33]. These
approaches improve geometric fidelity near surfaces but lack semantic or visual awareness.

Uncertainty and Learning-Based Adaptivity. Several works have incorporated uncertainty
into mapping, active exploration, and reconstruction. SemanticFusion [12] and Kimera [22]
maintain probabilistic class distributions per voxel, though uncertainty is not used to influence map structure. Information-theoretic formulations [1, 2] employ entropy and mutual
information to guide exploration; notably, semantic entropy within a Bayesian Octree has
been used to drive active exploration via mutual information maximization [1]. Reinforcement learning has similarly been applied to active exploration and mapping [3, 21, 32],
demonstrating that learned policies can balance performance and efficiency. However, these
exploration-focused methods determine where to move next rather than how to allocate reconstruction resolution along a fixed trajectory. In contrast, we formulate voxel subdivision
as an uncertainty-driven refinement problem and leverage reinforcement learning to learn
adaptive, budget-aware subdivision policies that directly regulate the trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and storage capacity.

Proposed Method
We propose an adaptive 3D semantic mapping framework that refines voxel resolution by
explicitly targeting high-uncertainty regions. We build upon MAP-ADAPT [31], which op-

soft-constrained subdivision policy with reinforcement learning (RL) that replaces handtuned thresholds with a single intuitive parameter: the target storage budget.
Preliminaries. We consider a sequence of RGB-D frames {(Ik , Dk )}Kk=1 with camera poses
{Ck } and per-frame semantic predictions {Pk }, where Ik and Dk are the input image and

allocates fine resolution only where it meaningfully reduces uncertainty. We adopt a hierarchical voxel hashing structure in the spirit of MAP-ADAPT [31], with three resolution levels
(coarse: 8 cm, medium: 4 cm, fine: 1 cm voxels). Each voxel vi stores vi = {di , Si , gi ,ti },

from Si , while the combined uncertainty proxy hi used by the RL agent is computed from all

selection and provides no direct mechanism to target a desired global storage budget. Thus,
we cast adaptive subdivision as a sequential decision problem and train an RL agent to choose
complexity thresholds per region while penalizing budget overuse.

Training. We train the policy using PPO [24], following a reinforcement-learning formulation in which each episode corresponds to a complete scene and the agent sequentially selects
thresholds for blocks across frames. We train separate agents for different fixed budget ratios
ρbudget , each targeting a different memory regime.
Inspired by RL-VO [14], which uses cross-attention for variable-length RL observations,
we adopt a PerceiverIO architecture [7] for the variable number of sampled blocks N. Each
block’s (µb , σb ) pair is a key–value input; four learned latent queries compress all block
features into a fixed 256-dim representation processed by an MLP to produce the action
distribution over 2N threshold indices. The value network additionally receives the global
critic features (h̄, σ (h), ρused , ρbudget ), focusing the critic on budget context.
We train our reinforcement-learning agent on 50 scenes randomly sampled from the
ScanNet training split [4], disjoint from all evaluation scenes. Each scene provides a sequence of RGB-D frames with corresponding camera poses and precomputed 1 cm Voxblox
reference reconstructions used for reward computation. Training is performed for a total of
10 million environment steps. Each policy is trained separately for approximately 24 hours
on a single consumer-grade RTX 3080 (10 GB), using the precomputed 1 cm reference reconstructions for reward supervision. At inference, these references are no longer required:
the user simply selects a desired storage-budget regime, and the corresponding policy predicts subdivision thresholds directly from the observed block uncertainty statistics {µb , σb }.
Additional training details, including hardware and total training cost, are provided in Appendix C.

Experiments
We evaluate geometric reconstruction, semantic accuracy, texture fidelity, and efficiency
on both synthetic and real-world benchmarks, comparing fixed-resolution baselines, MAPADAPT, and our uncertainty- and RL-driven variants.
Datasets. Following MAP-ADAPT [31], we evaluate on 81 scenes (all disjoint from the 50
ScanNet scenes used for RL training): (i) HSSD [10] provides photorealistic synthetic indoor
scenes with noise-free ground-truth geometry, semantics, and textures. (ii) ScanNet [4] contains real-world RGB-D scans with measurement noise, missing depth, and partial semantic
annotations, testing robustness under realistic conditions.
Metrics. All metrics are computed on meshes extracted from the adaptive TSDF grids. (i)
Geometry: mean completion error and completion ratio within 5 cm. (ii) Semantics: vertex
labels from the fused voxel distribution are rendered into held-out test views via raycasting
and compared to ground-truth semantic projections, penalizing both label errors and geometric misalignment. (iii) Texture: MS-SSIM and LPIPS [30] on raycasted color renderings.

(iv) Efficiency: total voxel count, peak memory, and per-frame integration time. All adaptive
methods share the same TSDF integration and meshing pipelines [31].
Baselines and Ablations.
• Voxblox (8 cm, 4 cm, 1 cm) [19]: Fixed-resolution baselines at each of the three levels
that the adaptive methods select among during subdivision. The 1 cm grid provides a
quality upper bound at prohibitive memory cost.
• M AP -A DAPT [31]: Uses geometric CoC [20] and a manually selected semantic class
list, limiting generalization. We use the official HSSD configuration; for ScanNet, we
adopt the same list with unknown classes assigned to coarse resolution.
• M AP -A DAPT-S PLIT-AVG [31]: MAP-ADAPT averaged over 5 runs with randomly
sampled semantic class lists, where each list specifies which class labels trigger fineresolution subdivision. This evaluates sensitivity to class list selection when no datasetspecific configuration is available.
• U N -M AP -A DAPT (O URS ): Our method using semantic entropy, geometric complexity, and texture complexity as subdivision signals, without user-defined class lists.
• U N RL (O URS ): Budget-aware RL refinement trained at 25% and 75% target voxelbudget regimes, providing budget-aware refinement at inference.
We omit Multi-TSDF [23], which has been shown to underperform MAP-ADAPT. We further evaluate robustness to the semantic segmentation backbone by replacing RefineNet with
SegFormer; Table 5 shows consistent accuracy–memory behavior with the alternative backbone.
Results on ScanNet. Table 1 shows that our uncertainty-driven refinement outperforms
MAP-ADAPT on almost all metrics. U N -M AP -A DAPT (O URS ) achieves lower completion
error and higher Compl. <5cm than MAP-ADAPT, and also improves semantic accuracy
and photometric quality. These improvements primarily arise from automatically identifying informative or uncertain regions for refinement, rather than relying on a preselected class
list. Importantly, this enables generalization to scenes containing object categories not explicitly known during system design. Because our method additionally refines texture-rich
regions, which are ignored entirely by MAP-ADAPT, the unconstrained variant naturally allocates more voxels, as expected. This additional cost directly motivates the budget-aware
RL variant, which recovers the efficiency advantage while preserving the broader refinement
criteria.
The RL-based variant learns predictable trade-offs between memory and accuracy. At
75% of the voxel budget, the reconstruction quality is close to our deterministic model, while
memory is reduced by more than half. These results confirm that the agent learns to allocate
resources where they most reduce reconstruction loss. At extreme budgets (25%), the policy
prioritizes high uncertainty and complexity regions, maintaining key structural and semantic
details, and gracefully reducing resolution elsewhere.
Results on HSSD. Table 2 reports results on the synthetic HSSD scenes. Although the
RL agent and all hyperparameters were trained on ScanNet, our uncertainty-driven model
again surpasses MAP-ADAPT across geometric and texture metrics. This demonstrates a
key strength of our formulation: since voxel refinement is triggered by modality-specific
uncertainty and complexity signals rather than predefined semantic categories, the method
naturally generalizes to scenes containing previously unseen objects, materials, and textures.

Conclusion
This work tackles the absence of explicit, user-controllable accuracy–memory trade-offs in
current volumetric mapping pipelines. MAP-ADAPT offers limited control through manual
class lists and class-dependent subdivision levels, but this requires expert tuning, fails to generalize, and becomes impractical in scenes with many small or unseen objects. Our method
provides a principled and fully automatic alternative: uncertainty- and complexity-driven
refinement removes dependence on semantic taxonomies, while a reinforcement-learning
policy enables direct specification of a desired memory budget, with the system allocating
resolution accordingly. Experiments on real and synthetic datasets show that our approach
matches or surpasses MAP-ADAPT in geometric, semantic, and texture accuracy, while delivering predictable and stable performance under varying storage constraints. By making
the target memory budget the primary user-facing parameter, our framework provides a flexible and scalable approach to adaptive 3D mapping. Our evaluation is currently limited to
the indoor RGB-D setting of ScanNet and HSSD. Nevertheless, the semantic, geometric,
and texture cues exploited by our approach are not intrinsically tied to indoor scene categories. Extending and adapting the method to outdoor and larger-scale mapping scenarios is
an important direction for future work.

Experiments with SegFormer
This section provides additional experiments and visualizations. We evaluate our method
using SegFormer [29], a stronger but heavier segmentation model than RefineNet [11] used
in the main paper. We present extended qualitative comparisons, and analyze the behavior
and generalization of the reinforcement learning (RL) agents. Our goal is to demonstrate that
(i) the proposed entropy-driven framework is robust to changes in the semantic backbone, (ii)
the accuracy-memory trade-off remains consistent under different segmentation signals, and
(iii) methods relying on fixed semantic class heuristics, such as MAP-ADAPT, are inherently
brittle across datasets and segmentation models.

Qualitative Comparisons
Figure 4 and Figure 5 present visualizations on HSSD and ScanNet for RefineNet and SegFormer, respectively. We visualize textured reconstructions and semantic renderings from
multiple viewpoints and across all ablations.

Table 5 reports quantitative results on ScanNet with SegFormer. Reconstruction and texture

All experiments, including both 3D semantic scene reconstruction and RL policy training,
were conducted on a single workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-14700K processor
(20 cores, 28 threads) and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 GPU (10 GB VRAM, CUDA
12.2).
For RL training, we used PPO with 4 parallel environments via SubprocVecEnv,
training for 10M timesteps per experiment. Evaluation was performed every 50k steps on a
held-out validation set of 5 episodes, with model checkpoints saved every 250k steps. Each
training run took approximately 24 hours.
The 3D reconstruction pipeline, which generates the RL dataset by processing ScanNet
scenes into per-frame voxel observations and reconstruction losses, was executed on the
same machine.
