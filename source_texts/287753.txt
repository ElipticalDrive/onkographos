# Neural Implicit Surfaces with Frequency-Adaptive Positional Encoding

Yan Xing, Yongxin Wu, Cheng Yang, Xiaonan Luo\, Fang Li and Jiale Hong**

Abstract: As the Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have achieved significant success in view synthesis, some works attempt to apply volume rendering to 3D reconstruction task. However, these methods use positional encoding with uniform frequency for the whole scenario during training, ignoring different regions of the scene and different training stage. Therefore, we aim to design a frequencyadaptive positional encoding. The adaptivity here refers to two points: first, it is adaptive to scene regions, meaning different regions of each scene have different frequency of positional encoding. Second, it is adaptive to the training process, meaning frequency changes during training to adapt to the convergence of the network. To achieve this adaptivity, we use a neural network to learn the frequency field of scenes, that is, using the network to predict the required frequency for each point during the training process. The introduction of the frequency field allows each scene to obtain an adaptive positional encoding, enabling the neural network to learn the geometric information of scenes in a more flexible and efficient manner. Experimental results show that our approach produces more accurate surfaces compared to baseline methods, especially in scenes with complex geometry. Just Accepted

Key words: Volume rendering; signed distance function; positional encoding, surface reconstruction

In response to the aforementioned issues, we innovatively propose frequency-adaptive positional encoding (referred to as Adaptive Positional Encoding hereafter). The adaptability of this encoding enables the neural network to learn the signed distance field of the scene at an appropriate frequency throughout the training process, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of the learning. For example, in our experiments, we found that for regions of the scene characterized by high-frequency information, the frequency-adaptive positional encoding assigns higher frequencies to capture the intricate details of object surfaces. # 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Traditional Multi-View 3D Reconstruction

Image-based multi-view 3D reconstruction has been a pivotal task in the field of computer vision. Traditional methods for multi-view 3D reconstruction typically involve a multi-step process. Structure- from-Motion (SFM)[4,5] is employed to extract and match features across adjacent viewpoints, thereby estimating camera parameters and generatinig sparse point clouds. Subsequently, Multi-View Stereo (MVS)[6-9] is utilized to estimate depth maps for each viewpoint, which are then fused to produce a dense point cloud. From this dense point cloud, Surface reconstruction methods[10- 12] , such as screened Poisson surface reconstruction[13], can be applied to reconstruct the object's surface. Traditional surface reconstruction methods have demonstrated success in various reconstruction tasks, but they often suffer from issues such as surface omissions or artifacts, largely due to their heavy reliance on the quality of feature matching. The above methods can be classified as point-based reconstruction methods. There exists another category of voxel-based methods[14-18]. These methods partition space into a grid of voxels and determine whether voxel grids are occupied by objects from multiple viewpoint input images. However, the high grid resolution demands significant storage requirements, making it challenging for these methods to achieve high- precision reconstruction results.

# 2.2 Neural Implicit Surfaces

With the rapid development of deep learning, neural implicit fields have emerged as a novel geometric representation. Traditional methods for implicitly representing object surfaces include signed distance functions (SDFs) and occupancy functions. DVR [19] derives analytic gradients for the predicted depth map with respect to the network parameters of the implicit shape and texture representation, leveraging a differentiable rendering formulation to learn an object's occupancy network. IDR[20] introduces an end-to-end neural architecture that learns signed distance functions without the need for additional supervision, while also estimating camera parameters from masked 2D images. Following the significant strides made by NeRF [1] and its subsequent papers in the realm of view synthesis, some methods have attempted to combine neural implicit representation with volume rendering techniques. UFORecon[21] and SC-NeuS[22] explore generalized surface reconstruction from challenging sparse views. UNISURF [23] strikes the balance between surface rendering and volume rendering by replacing the opacity function in volume rendering with the occupancy function, thereby proposing a unified formula between implicit surfaces and volume Just Accepted

rendering. VolSDF [2] employs a transformation function to convert signed distance values into volume density values, enabling it to learn the implicit surface of objects within the volume rendering framework. NeuS[3] derives an unbiased density function for volume rendering using the sigmoid function, enabling the extraction of high-quality surfaces from the zero level set of the learned signed distance function. These methods learn a continuous geometric representation of the scene, resulting in more complete reconstruction results compared to traditional 3D reconstruction methods.

# 2.3 Positional Encoding

The paper[24] argues that MLPs are unsuitable for low-dimensional coordinate-based visual and graphics tasks. Specifically, MLPs struggle to learn highfrequency functions, which is referred to as "spectral bias" in some literature[25]. However, recent works[1,26], have found that applying a simple Fourier transform to spatial coordinates (i.e., positional encoding) can overcome this "spectral bias" and enable MLPs to learn high-frequency details. The positional encoding employed in NeRF significantly improves rendering quality, especially in high-frequency regions. In fewshot view synthesis tasks, literature [27] observed that MLPs learn low-frequency and high-frequency information at different rates. To improve the performance of few-shot neural radiance fields, it proposed frequency regularization. This technique reduces the failure of few-shot neural rendering by controlling the linear growth of positional encoding's frequency during training. In surface reconstruction tasks, literature [28] suggested that direct learning of high-frequency positional encoding can render neural networks vulnerable to noise, because incorrectly learned high-frequency information can impede the learning of low-frequency information. Consequently, they recommend a coarse-to-fine training strategy, gradually increasing the frequency of positional encoding during training using a specially designed function. We hold the belief that different scenes require distinct frequencies of positional encoding. By leveraging a network to learn the optimal frequency of positional encoding, we can tailor the adaptive positional encoding to the learning of scene geometry information.

# 4 Experiments

To evaluate our method, we chose two widelyused 3D reconstruction datasets: the DTU dataset [29] and the NeRF synthetic dataset [1]. For assessing the accuracy of the reconstructed meshes, we adopted the Chamfer distance metric, following previous methods. Theoretically, our adaptive positional encoding can be integrated as a component into any radiance field method that utilizes positional encoding. However, in this study, we focus primarily on its application in 3D reconstruction. Consequently, we chose NeRF, IDR, and NeuS as baseline methods for comparison.

### 4.1 Datasets

Following previous work, we select 15 scenes from the DTU dataset to evaluate our method. Each scene consists of 49 or 64 images with a resolution of 1600x1200 pixels. The scenes in the DTU dataset contain real objects with varying shapes and materials, enabling us to test our method's performance on realworld scenes. Since most scenes in the DTU dataset have relatively smooth surfaces, we further select six scenes from the NeRF synthetic dataset. Each scene in the NeRF synthetic dataset consists of 100 images with a resolution of 800x800 pixels. The scenes in the NeRF synthetic dataset exhibit more intricate geometries, allowing us to evaluate our method's capability in reconstructing high-frequency details. For both datasets, we utilize the provided camera poses.

Table 1 displays a quantitative comparison of our method with several baseline methods on the DTU dataset (15 scenes). We report the Chamfer distance as a metric for evaluating reconstruction surface accuracy, where lower values indicate better performance. The results demonstrate that our method outperforms the baseline methods (NeuS, VolSDF and HF-NeuS), achieving superior numerical results on most scenes and on average. It is worth noting that Geo-NeuS currently represents the state-of-the-art method in surface reconstruction quality. The performance gap between our method and Geo-NeuS primarily arises from their utilization of multiple additional constraints (e.g., photometric consistency and point cloud supervision). Since our work focuses on the innovation of adaptive frequency encoding, we did not incorporate these constraints, leading to slightly inferior performance in complex geometric regions compared to Geo-NeuS. However, our approach demonstrates clear advantages over HF-NeuS, which is the most directly comparable method aligned with our research direction (both leveraging frequency optimization approaches based on NeuS). This validates that our adaptive frequency prediction strategy better balances detail reconstruction and stability compared to fixedfrequency methods. ### 4.5 Limitation

Despite the overall improvements, our method has an inherent limitation in specific scenarios. Notably, on smooth object surfaces (e.g., Scene 63 in the DTU dataset), the reconstruction quality deteriorates compared to the baseline method. This issue arises because the learning framework misinterprets illumination-induced color variations on these surfaces as geometric changes. In such cases, our frequency



network inadvertently exacerbates the problem by assigning higher-frequency positional encoding to these regions. Consequently, the network learns spurious geometric fluctuations caused by lighting effects instead of the true smooth surface. A key focus for future work is to address the challenge of changes induced by illumination during adaptive frequency encoding. 5 Conclusion In this paper, we propose a frequency-adaptive positional encoding for multi-view neural surface reconstruction. Our method employs a neural network to predict the frequency of positional encoding for each point, enabling it to adapt to both the scene and the training process. This adaptability allows our reconstruction method to accurately represent both the high-frequency and low-frequency geometric features of the scene. Compared to methods that use positional encoding with a fixed frequency, our model produces more precise reconstructions in scenes with complex geometry. One limitation of our approach is that the introduction of the frequency network increases the training time and complexity. Furthermore, the optimization of the frequency network lacks additional constraints, which may lead to suboptimal frequency assignment for positional encoding in certain regions of the scene. A promising direction for future work is to integrate our adaptive positional encoding with fast reconstruction methods, aiming to enhance efficiency for real-world application.
