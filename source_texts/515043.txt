Abstract. In this paper, we are interested in an interface reconstruction method for 3D arbitrary convex cells that could be used in multi-material flow simulations for instance. We assume
that the interface is represented by a plane whose normal vector is known and we focus on the
volume-matching step that consists in finding the plane constant so that it splits the cell according to a given volume fraction. We follow the same approach as in the recent authors’ publication
for 2D arbitrary convex cells in planar and axisymmetrical geometries, namely we derive an analytical formula for the volume of the specific prismatoids obtained when decomposing the cell
using the planes that are parallel to the interface and passing through all the cell nodes. This
formula is used to bracket the interface plane constant such that the volume-matching problem
is rewritten in a single prismatoid in which the same formula is used to find the final solution.
The proposed method is tested against an important number of reproducible configurations and
shown to be at least five times faster.
Introduction
Interface reconstruction methods are intensively used in multi-material flow simulations codes
when the interfaces between two or more materials must remain sharp like when two fluids are
not miscible or when one material is a solid and the other a gas. In some numerical methods,
the interface is explicitly tracked by marker points [12, 24, 22, 8] while in others, the interface
is captured by the material volume fractions. The volume-tracking methods belong to the latter
class. They were pioneered by [7, 16, 17] and substantially improved since then [5, 14, 3]. In
these methods, only the volume fractions of the materials are known and the interfaces have
to be reconstructed at each iteration in order to geometrically solve the evolution equations for
each volume fraction.
In this work, as in most interface reconstruction methods [13, 11, 1], we assume a linear
interface representation given by the equation
nx x + ny y + nz z = d,
where ~n = (nx , ny , nz ) and d are the normal vector and plane constant of the interface, respectively. The reconstruction procedure consists of two steps: 1) the computation of the normal
vector to the interface ~n and 2) the computation of the interface constant d so that it splits the
cell according to a prescribed volume fraction. The focus of this work is on the latter step and
we assume that ~n has already been obtained using one of the many existing methods [26, 4, 18].
In the sequel, we refer to this problem as the volume-matching step of the interface reconstruction and propose an original technique based on an analytical formula to solve it in arbitrary
three-dimensional convex cells. Several other analytical methods exist for specific type of cells,
e.g. for tetrahedra [25] or right parallelepipeds [21], but up to our knowledge, only one analytical approach is valid for convex polyhedra [15] and is based on the formula for the volume
of a polyhedron that uses the nodes coordinates. We believe that our approach is conceptually
simpler and will show that it is computationally more efficient.
Classically, reconstructing an interface in a polyhedral cell is performed by an iterative
Brent’s-based method [20] intensively using plane-polyhedron intersections that may be slow
and not always robust due to float comparisons in particular. Another approach is to tetrahedrize
the polyhedral cell and to use the analytical formulae of [25] within an Brent’s-like iterative process. The main interest of this alternative method is to be valid for non-convex cells, however
we do not consider them in this work. Therefore, all our numerical comparisons will be carried
out against the Brent’s-based method of [20].
The method that we develop is based on the three following requirements. It has (1) to enforce volume-matching accuracy within machine precision, (2) to be significantly faster than the
Brent’s-based method and (3) to derive the analytical formula from simple geometrical calculations. More specifically, these requirements are the extension of the ones we used to develop the
volume-matching technique based on analytical formulae for arbitrary two-dimensional convex
cells in planar and axisymmetrical geometries in [9]. They can be summed up as: first decompose the cell in elementary elements (here, prismatoids) by slicing it with the planes parallel to
the interface and passing through all the nodes, and then derive an analytical formula for the
volume of these specific elements and use it to bracket and find the plane constant.
Finally we point out that in the present paper, we deal with the reconstruction of an interface between two materials only. However in multi-material flow simulations more than two
materials may be present in a given cell. In that case, the proposed technique would have to be
used within a multi-material reconstruction scheme, e.g. the onion-skin [5] or nested-dissection
[10] schemes, that consists in a series of interface reconstructions between two entities (i.e. one
material and the remaining others) given a specific materials order. We want to emphasize that
for the nested-dissection scheme, the reconstruction of more than two materials in a cell always
involves general convex polyhedra since each reconstructed material volume is subtracted from
the cell. Therefore, even on Cartesian meshes, the use of a volume-matching technique for general convex polyhedra as the one we propose is mandatory.
The paper is organized as follows: in the first section, we describe the cell decomposition
and the plane constant bracketing step. The second section is dedicated to the description of the
prismatoids obtained during the cell decomposition and more importantly to the derivation of the
analytical formula for their volumes. The technical details of the implementation are described
in the third section while numerical results are provided in the last section to demonstrate the
gain in computational efficiency of the method against a classical approach.

Notation. Let us first define the notation that we will use throughout the paper in an analoguous way to the one used in [9]. We consider an arbitrary convex polyhedral cell Ω with Ne edges,
Nf faces and Nn nodes. The nodes coordinates are denoted by Xl = (xl , yl , zl ). By definition
of a convex polyhedron, each face f has coplanar nodes, thus having a unique outward normal
vector denoted by ~nf = (nf,x , nf,y , nf,z ). The volume of the cell is denoted by VΩ and the material volume we want to match during the interface reconstruction is V̄ = αVΩ with α ∈ [0, 1] the
material volume fraction.
As mentioned in the introduction, we assume that the unit normal vector to the interface
~n = (nx , ny , nz ) is known and points from the material under consideration to the exterior. In
consequence, any plane parallel to the interface is defined by the equation nx x + ny y + nz z = d
and will be called a d-plane. Moreover the material volume corresponding to a d-plane will be
referred to as d-volume and denoted by Vd . Finally, the intersection between a d-plane and the
cell is a convex polygon called d-polygon.

Implementation details of the complete algorithm
This section is dedicated to the the details of the different steps of the overall volume-matching
algorithm. The main focus is on providing a data structure and related tools in order to be able
to evaluate the volume using formula (11)-(12). Though the formula in itself is not complex,
defining the proper data structure and updating it for the next prismatoid is not straightforward.
Consequently, we describe in the first part the necessary data structure attached to a polyhedron
and provide a way to ensure the right ordering for the sets of edges of each node. In the following
two paragraphs, the initialization of the d1 -polygon and the update from a dk -polygon to the
dk+1 -polygon are described. Finally the overall algorithm of the method is drawn in the last
part.1
Polyhedron representation. Several different ways to represent the convex polyhedral cell
Ω exist and using the minimal one is not always the best solution, particularly in terms on computational costs. In our implementation, we have chosen to represent a polyhedron as
• a list of nodes: for each node, its x-, y-, and z-coordinates are stored,
• a list of edges: for each edge, the indices of its two nodes are stored,
• a list of faces: for each face, the indices of its edges are stored in counter-clockwise order
when seen from the outside.
This representation is good enough to easily navigate through the different geometrical entities
of the polyhedron, but we need two more lists to simplify the rest of the algorithm:
• the two faces of Ω attached to each edge,
• the list of edges of Ω passing through each node,
note that the second list is not ordered at this point and that in our implementation, these two
extra lists are computed at the beginning of the algorithm.
As mentioned in subsection 2.1, the ordering of the list of edges passing through a node is
very important. Therefore we provide in Algorithm 1 a general procedure, based on the convex
nature of the polyhedron, to ensure the right ordering for each node n.

Numerical tests
This section is dedicated to a series of numerical tests carried out to demonstrate the computational interest of the proposed method over a classical approach for convex 3D cells. In the first

proposed volume-matching approach and compare the results to a classical iterative technique

In the proposed technique, the interface plane constant is the root of V (h) − V̄ for 0 < h <

that reducing it won’t change the accuracy of the results. We emphasize that increasing the
tolerance value does not significantly lower the computational times but importantly deteriorate
the final accuracy, as illustrated in [15].

Numerical results
In this subsection we provide the numerical results of the volume-matching procedure for the
sets of configurations described in previous subsection. The errors and computational times for
the sets S1 and S2 are gathered in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. As expected, both methods provide volume-matching errors within machine tolerance independently of the type of cell and of
the values of the volume fraction to match.
For the set of configurations S1 , the proposed method is between 4.7 (for the cube) and 5.9
(for the icosahedron) times faster than the classical Brent’s method, while these numbers increase to 7.2 (for the irregular polyhedron) and 9.0 for the set S2 . This is a very significant
speed-up that demonstrates the computational gain of our approach. Let us point out that in
comparison, the analytical approach of [15] was only between 2 and 3.5 times faster than the
Brent’s approach for 3D cells. Let us note that the speed of our implementation of the Brent’s
method has been indirectly compared with the one of the Brent’s method used in [15] and they
are comparable.
Moreover, we see that independently of the set of configurations the number of Brent’s iterations is about 1.5 larger than the one of the Newton’s method used in our approach. This
mainly explains the better speed-up over the set S2 , for which these numbers of iterations are
twice larger than for the set S1 , since the cost of a Newton’s iteration is negligible (one evaluation of a cubic polynomial and one of its derivative) compared to the cost of a Brent’s one (one
plane-polyhedron intersection and one polyhedron volume computation).
Finally, in order to compare the R.T.U. values of Tables 1 and 2, let us add that the computational time taken by our method to complete the set S1 on the tetrahedral cell is only 5% slower
than for the set S2 . Therefore comparisons of computational times for the two sets show that,
except on simple cells (tetrahedron, cube), our approach is about 25% faster over the set S2 than
16
over the set S1 . This is easily explained by its incremental nature that makes the bracketing step
much quicker for very small volume fractions.

Summary and conclusion
In this paper, we have proposed a volume-matching technique based on an analytical formula
for 3D arbitrary convex cells. This work can be seen as an extension of our recent work in 2D
[9] since the main principles are identical, namely a decomposition of the cell into elementary
volumes (prismatoids in 3D vs trapezoids in 2D) and the derivation of analytical formulae for the
volume of these specific elementary volumes using a sub-decomposition in simpler geometrical
elements.
The complexity inherent to the three-dimensional framework have led to theoretical and
computational difficulties for which we presented solutions and possible implementations in
section 3. For instance, the right ordering of the elements of the data structure of the polyhedral
cell is critical to ensure that the algebraic volumes used in the final formula for the prismatoidal
volumes have the right signs.
The proposed technique has been extensively validated against two sets of 259.2 millions reproducible volume-matching configurations in six different polyhedra and have always provided
errors within machine precision. Furthermore, comparisons with a classical Brent’s method have
shown that our approach is between 4.7 and 9.0 times faster and less sensitive to the values of
the volume fractions to match.
Finally, we remind that the method we have presented is valid for an interface between two
materials only, but that it could be used in cells with more than two materials by simply following a multi-material reconstruction scheme [5, 10].
In the future, we plan to extend this approach, along with its 2D version [9], to non-convex
cells such that this approach could be used in Arbitrary-Eulerian-Lagrangian methods in which
non-convex cells naturally arise, as noted in [1].
