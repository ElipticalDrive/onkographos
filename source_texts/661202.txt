ABSTRACT
We present a technique for very low bit rate encoding of
faces for videoconferencing applications over the Internet or mobile communication networks. The proposed
scheme represents each new face view as a linear combination of three basis face views of the same person. The
background area of the target face view is also encoded
by the same method using the same basis views as used
for the face area. Changes in the eyes and mouth regions are encoded using principal components analysis
with three training sets of eyes and mouth images of
the same person. The scheme results in a very compact
representation of the face video data, and good quality images are reconstructed with an estimated bit rate
from 1422 to 1620 bits/frame.
1 INTRODUCTION
Videoconferencing applications over the Internet or mobile communications networks require very low bit rate
encoding techniques of face video data since the available bandwidth is usually limited. By low bit rate, we
mean a compressed video stream whose bit rate does not
exceed 64 Kbits/sec. For CIF or QCIF size frames at
a typical rate of 30 frames/sec, the uncompressed video
stream requires 36.5 Mbits/sec or 10 Mbits/sec respectively. Hence, the desired compression ratios are from
600:1 to 160:1. Achieving these compression ratios is a
very dicult task.
In conventional DCT block{based compression
schemes, such as H.261 [1], the performance deteriorates signi cantly at bit rates below 64 Kbits/sec and
the decoded video stream su ers from signi cant coding artifacts. Most current implementations eliminate
these e ects by lowering the spatial (frame size) and
temporal (frame rate) resolution. Attempts to transmit
face video data using H.261 over low bandwidth Internet
links usually achieve 2-10 frames/sec at the lower QCIF
resolution and the quality of the decoded video stream
is poor. Furthermore, dropping the frame rate results
in synchronisation problems between audio and video.

In order to achieve higher compression ratios, it seems
that knowledge of the image content, for example that
the video data contains faces, needs to be used. Such
model-based image coding techniques have recently received a lot of attention for the encoding of face images
for videoconferencing applications [2, 3]. Although these
methods can achieve high compression ratios, they require complex 3 models of the face or explicit 3 information of the scene content to be used.
The proposed encoding scheme is based on the modelling of objects using linear combination of images [4].
Each novel face view of the person who uses the videoconferencing system is expressed as a linear combination
of three basis face views of the same person. The face
is encoded by either using the linear combination coecients or the coordinates of a few control points located
on the face. Using this information, the shape of the
novel face view is easily reconstructed. The texture of
the face is reconstructed using the texture of the basis views and a weighted interpolation technique. The
background area is also encoded by the same method
using as basis views the background areas of the corresponding face basis views. However, changes in the eyes
and mouth regions can not be encoded by the linear combination method, therefore, we decide to use principal
components analysis for encoding these facial features.

2.1 Geometry
The modelling of objects using linear combination of
images was initially proposed by Ullman and Basri for
object recognition [4]. They found that all the possible images of an object can, to a good approximation,
often be expressed as a linear combination of a small
number of \basis" 2 views. For objects with smooth
boundaries, such as human faces, undergoing rigid 3D
transformations and scaling under orthographic or weak
perspective projection [5], as in videoconferencing applications, three views of the object are sucient to synthesise any other novel view of the same object. Each novel
face view of the person who uses the videoconferencing

2.2 Texture Rendering
The texture of the new face view is reconstructed by
the texture of the three basis views using a weighted
interpolation technique. In particular, for each pixel
in the novel face view, the corresponding pixels in the
three basis views are found by using the \piecewise linear mapping" technique [7]. The control points divide
the face in triangular regions. In our experiments, the
triangulation of the face has been done manually for the
three basis views and remains the same for all novel face
views. For each triangle in the novel face view and its
corresponding triangle in each of the basis views, a local
linear mapping function is determined which maps every pixel of the triangle in the novel face view to a pixel
in the corresponding triangle in the basis view.

3 EXPERIMENTS ON FACE RECONSTRUCTION
In our experiments, we selected the three basis face
views to be one frontal view, one oriented to the left
and one to the right with almost 45 orientation change
around the vertical axis. With these particular basis
views we are able to synthesise novel face views of the
same person within a wide range of orientation about
the vertical axis and slight changes around the horizontal axis, as well as, of course, those produced by rotating or moving the face on the image plane. This set of
novel face views describes most of the face views that
usually occur in videoconferencing applications. Figure
1 show the basis face views used in our experiments together with an example of the the position of the control
points in the frontal basis view and the segmentation of
the face in triangular regions. A number of face images of the same person in di erent positions in the 3
space were selected as test face views. In each basis and
each test face view 19 and 30 control points were located
manually. The novel face views were encoded by the coordinates of the control points rather than by the linear
combination coecients, since we found that this leads
to better quality in the reconstructed image. Figures
2 and 3 show the actual and reconstructed images of a
nearly frontal face view when 30 and 19 control points
were used respectively.

The bit rate required to encode the overall face area
depends on the number of the control points used. If
the video data contains frames of CIF or QCIF resolution, we need 18 bits to represent each control point (9
bits/coordinate). Thus, the bit rate for the case of 19
points is 342 bits/frame and for the case of 30 points is
540 bits/frame.
4 ENCODING OF THE BACKGROUND
AND FACIAL FEATURES
The same linear combination method is applied for the
encoding of the background. A small number of control
points are placed at xed positions on the boundary of
each frame and of the basis views. Combined with the
control points at the face boundary, they de ne a triangular mesh that is used for the synthesis of the back-
ground texture. Since the background control points do
not change position, they do not need to be encoded.
As a result, the background area is e ectively encoded
with zero bits. Preliminary results show that uniform
areas of the background are synthesised very well, especially with a smaller number of control points, although
areas of the background revealed or occluded as a result
of face rotation and movement are not so good, as may
be seen in Figure 3.
The linear combination technique fails to encode the
eyes when the person talks, opens/closes their eyes or
changes facial expression, since such changes are not included in the manifold spanned by the basis face views.
However, we found that principal component analysis
(PCA) techniques [9] can successfully encode the eyes
and mouth of the face using a small number (around
10) of eigenvectors. We, therefore, combined both techniques and found by experiments that we can encode
the eyes and mouth in each frame of colour video data
with good quality using only 1080 bits. The total bit
rate for the whole face area becomes 1620 bits/frame
(with 30 control points) and 1422 bits/frame (with 19
changes of expression,may be encoded using principal
components analysis. As a subset of the control points
used in the linear combination of views are used in
the PCA, features reconstructed by PCA may easily be
merged into the face reconstructed from the basis views.
The linear combination method can achieve very high
compression ratios since for every new frame it does not
transmit any textural information; only shape information needs to be transmitted. Also, it is computationally
simple since only implicit 3 information derived from
the three basis 2 face views is used. Furthermore,
it preserves the temporal and spatial resolution of the
video data. However, sparse correspondence between
the basis and novel face views is needed, so as in other
knowledge-based coding approaches, a robust and fast
scheme for detecting faces and control points, along with
elimination of control points outliers, is required. The
quality of the reconstructed face images can be further
improved by eliminating self-occlusion e ects by hidden
surface removal [10].

5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we propose a simple encoding technique
that can be used for the compression of face video data
for low bit-rate videoconferencing applications. By using knowledge that the video data contains face images
against a fairly uniform and stationary background, the
proposed scheme encodes the face and background area
in each frame as a linear combination of three basis
views. Important facial features such as the eyes and
