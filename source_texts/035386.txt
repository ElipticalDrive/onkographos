the primary barrier for the effective use of a 3D visualization tool is its user interface. Developing user interfaces for
portraying and interacting with large quantities of information is rapidly becoming one of the most challenging areas in
human-computer interaction research [19, 3]. One example
of a complex and frequent operation in volume visualization
is the positioning of a clipping plane. Such a plane is for
instance used to cut through a three-dimensional (3D) data
set in order to explore its interior structure. The common
method of controlling the 5 (or 6) degrees of freedom (DOF)
of the virtual clipping plane, i.e., its position (3 DOF) and
orientation (2 DOF in case of a plane, or 3 DOF in case of
a window), is by means of a 2 DOF control device such as
a mouse. In order to accomplish this, the positioning task
needs to be decomposed in at least 3 subtasks that require
at most 2 DOF at a time. Despite the fact that such a twodimensional (2D) interface (in principle) allows to perform
the task, it is difficult for the user to obtain enough awareness of the spatial relationships and to manipulate data efficiently. This is due to the fact that the 2D interaction
is unrelated to the natural interaction process in 3D space.
This paper therefore proposes alternative interface designs
for the clipping plane task that make use of 3D (tangible)
intersection devices. After a discussion of related work, we
describe the proposed interface prototypes. Some usability
experiments that are planned with these prototypes, in order to address both technical and psychological issues of the
design, are also discussed.

Interacting with volumetric scalar data requires a user
with spatial reasoning and 3D perception skills. The dominant interface for 3D manipulation with volumetric data is
currently the computer desktop with a graphical user interface that is controlled by a mouse and keyboard. Recent
3D interfaces that are based on more advanced tracking
technologies potentially provide the possibility of improving the 3D interaction process, and several studies have already been undertaken to develop alternative interfaces for
3D interaction. Many of these studies have focused on more
generic 3D manipulation tasks, such as selection, positioning, etc. [9, 23, 4], but some have focused on the specific
task of creating 3D intersections.

the first 3D interfaces to support continuous clipping interaction in 3D space. The PassProps was developed to allow
Visualization via direct volume rendering is a potentially
powerful technique for exploring and interacting with large
amounts of scientific (i.e., volumetric) data. However, the
available two-dimensional (2D) interfaces make three-dimensional (3D) manipulation with such data difficult. The many
usability problems experienced during interaction in turn
discourage widespread use of volume rendering as a scientific tool. In this paper, we present a more in-depth investigation into one specific interface aspect, i.e., the positioning of a clipping plane within volume-rendered data.
More specifically, we propose three different interface prototypes that have been realized with the help of wireless
vision-based tracking. These three prototypes combine aspects of 2D graphical user interfaces with 3D tangible interaction devices in different ways. They allow to experience
and compare different user interface strategies for performing the clipping plane interaction task, and provide a basis
for carrying out user evaluations in the near future.

Inspired by the success of Tangible User Interfaces (TUIs)
[10, 21], De Guzman et al. presented two tangible devices
for navigating a slice through the human body [5]. Interface A consisted of a 30-inch 2D model of a human body,
together with a U-shaped fork at the end of an adjustable
arm that could be rotated 180 degrees along the device’s
baseboard. Interface B consisted of a transparent 3D model
of the human body and a free-moving hand-held fork. The
fork in each case represented the intersection plane (window), and its position and orientation was used to generate
an intersection image on a separate display.
The Cubic Mouse (CMouse) [6] was developed to support exploration of 3D geological data (seismic data) and
car crash analysis data. The CMouse allows users to specify three orthogonal cutting planes and to perform so-called
”chair cuts” through the data. The prop is a cube-shaped
case with three perpendicular rods passing approximately
through the centers of two parallel faces of the case. It is
usually held in the non-dominant hand. The rods are used to
control three orthogonal slices through the 3D data, i.e., by
pushing or pulling a rod, usually with the dominant hand,
the corresponding intersection plane moves back and forth.
The movement of a slice is hence constrained to the direction orthogonal to the slice. There is also a (wired) magnetic
tracker embedded in the cube-shaped case. The tracked six
DOF are used to translate and orient the data set in the
virtual world, relative to the observer. The 3D data set and
the orthogonal slices are visualized on a large stereo display
in front of the user.
There are some limitations in the above systems that are
likely to have an effect on the usability and the user acceptance. First, because of the active tracking technology
being used in these systems, the interaction elements need
to be wired. Such wires obviously will have an effect on the
freedom of movement, an effect that is seldom mentioned,
let alone evaluated. Alternative techniques such as optical
tracking allow for interaction elements that are passive and
unwired, and are therefore likely to ameliorate this problem.
Second, there is currently little insight into how the limited
accuracy of 3D tracking (of the order of millimeters), but
with 6 DOF, compares with the limited DOF of 2D inter-

3.
DESIGN OF THE USER INTERFACE
Our goal is to combine current knowledge and understanding of 3D user interface design [2] with new technical possibilities to create clipping interfaces for more demanding and
realistic tasks. The design principles that we adhere to in
the design of these interfaces are the following:
• Easy to use. The interface should not distract the user
from the actual clipping task.
• Easy to learn. The interaction should be natural and
intuitive, requiring little explanation and training.
• Adequate perceptual feedback. The interface should
provide (passive) tactile and visual cues that assist in
the interaction.
• Low-cost setup. The interface and system should be
created using of-the-shelf and inexpensive technology.
• Real-time interaction. The interface should work in
real-time despite of the use of volume rendering of realistic high-resolution data.

3.3
Tangible Pen for Clipping Plane
In the second interface, the clipping plane is controlled
by a pen-like device. The idea behind this choice is that
many people are used to a pen as an interaction device.
The pen contains a unique linear pattern constituted of four
stripes near its top, but can easily be grasped close to the
point at the bottom, without obscuring the pattern. This
pattern is required for the recognition of the movement of
the pen by the optical tracking system. It is very important
to keep the pattern visible for the stereo cameras in order to
get a continuous orientation report. Five DOF are reported
for the pen (the rotation of the pen around its axis cannot
be tracked). This interface requires the user to work with
two hands (unless a pedestal is used to fix the position of
the cube). Usually, the non-dominant hand holds the cube
that controls the volumetric data, while the dominant hand
holds the pen. The virtual clipping plane has an orientation
that is orthogonal to that of the pen, and a position that
is determined by the pen tip. The user task is to position
the pen relative to the wooden cube such that the virtual
plane cuts through the volumetric data as the user expects
(Figure 7).
An advantage of a pen-like interaction device is that such
a pen can potentially be used as both a 2D and a 3D interaction device. This might be especially useful in case of the
hybrid 2D/3D interaction that we have mentioned before.
Indeed, the available system combines a projector with a
digitizer tablet to create a 2D interaction space on a horizontal surface. If we equip the digital pen of the tablet with
infrared markers, then this pen may be used for both highresolution 2 DOF interaction and low-resolution five DOF
interaction. The main implementation (and user) issue is to
create a mechanism for switching between both interaction
modes. A straightforward solution would for instance be
to replace one of the infrared markers by an infrared LED
that can be controlled by a switch on the side of the pen.
An alternative way would be to use another modality, such
as a foot pedal or a speech command, to initiate the mode
switching.

3.4
Tangible Frame for Clipping Plane
Inspired by the second prototype, we designed a third prototype that uses a square-shaped metal frame to control the
clipping plane. The five infrared-reflecting stripes on three
of its sides form a unique planar pattern as the Figure 8
shows. The six DOF of the frame (three DOF for position
and three DOF for rotation) are monitored continuously by
the vision-based tracking algorithm (note that this is one
DOF more than in case of the pen device). The main difference between this interface and the second one is the shape
of the interaction device that controls the clipping plane (or
window). The appearance of this device makes its purpose
very obvious. While using this prototype, the user positions
the cube with his non-dominant hand, and grasps the frame
with his dominant hand on the side that has no dots on
it. The physical cube intersects with the physical frame in
a way that agrees one-to-one with the intersection of their
virtual counterparts on the screen. As a result, even though
all six DOF are enabled when moving the plane, the interface does not seem difficult to control. The offset between
both interaction devices, that is required in the previous
case, because the pen and cube cannot physically intersect,
is also avoided in this way. This is expected to result in an
interface that is more easy to learn and use. The cube can
again be placed on a small pedestal in case the user prefers
to perform clipping operations with a single hand.
4.
Comparison with Previous Work
There are some differences between what we present here
and previous studies. Firstly, our cube-shaped interaction
device is a generic (abstract) tactile device that can be used
in many diverse application. This is different from earlier
studies where a dedicated device was used to position and
rotate the data set. For example, Hinckley used of head prop
that resembled his anatomical data. This created some confusion with his subjects, since the position of the nose of
the head prop did for instance not necessarily coincide with
the position of the nose in the virtual data. In De Guzman’s prototypes, the physical models of the human body
may exercise similar problems. Secondly, our prototypes
can provide more depth cues to subjects because they can
work in stereoscopic mode. It remains to be evaluated experimentally how important this is for the intersection task.
Thirdly, the optical tracking used in our interfaces allows
for wireless interaction devices, hence avoiding possible interaction problems associated with the cables required in
the interafces of Hinckley and De Guzman. Fourthly, the
hardware-accelerated texture mapping allows for real-time
3D interaction with volumetric data.
We have asked several colleagues from our department to
try out the different prototypes and give informal feedback.
The mouse-driven clipping plane did not surprise most of
them. However, the tangible clipping interfaces, including
the second and third prototype, did receive many positive
feedbacks. These tangible interfaces have also been demonstrated at the Second European Symposium on Ambient
Intelligence (EUSAI 2004) [15]. The feedback also indicated
that most users share a preference for the tangible interfaces.
4.2
3D Clipping Task Analysis
A 3D clipping interaction needs spatial reasoning. Spatial
reasoning is a mental cognitive process that requires thinking about relationships between 3D objects. The analysis of
the intersection task shows how many and what kind of universal interaction tasks are required. Hinckley [9] recognized
that intersection operations consist of two sub-tasks: viewing and clipping. He thought viewing could be subdivided
into orienting and zooming. In total, ten separate parameters (yaw, pitch, roll, and zoom for the view on the data; x,
y, z, yaw, pitch, and roll for the clipping plane) need to be
specified. Of course, the orientation and position of the clipping plane is coupled to the orientation and position of the
object being clipped, since the relative spatial positioning
between both objects is obviously the most relevant one.

Traditional 2D interfaces require the user to reason mentally about the spatial relationships between the objects
in a scene, based solely on the rendered scene on the 2D
screen. Not all people are necessarily able to do this equally
well (many Intelligent Quotient (IQ) tests actually contain

Since a 2D interface for controlling the clipping interface
is very unlike human operation in 3D space, users with a
limited spatial ”ability” are likely to experience much more
problems with such an interface than with a real 3D interface. This remains to be tested experimentally.
Although the graphical feedback of the (volumetric) data
set with the clipping plane is crucial for performing the intersection task, additional information on spatial relationships and tactile feedback may be beneficial. Both tangible
interface prototypes work well in the way that they enrich
the user with more haptic feedback and position awareness.
Especially, the informal observation of the user performance
with the third prototype illustrates how the 3D tangible interface simplifies the apparent complexity of manipulating
a clipping-plane relative to an object. The intersection task
can be rapidly optimized with minimal user training effort,
because of the easy understanding of the shape and function
of the interaction elements. Using the tangible interface with
two hands also reduces the entire hierarchy of the task into a
single transaction that directly corresponds to the task that
the user has in mind.

Planned Experiments
The next logical step is to undertake more structured and
formalized experiments with the different prototypes. We
intend to adopt the task performed in an earlier paper [20]
as the starting point for such experiments. More specifically, the experimental task is to navigate an intersection
plane through a 3D model in order to visualize a dark-gray
disk hidden inside the model. By repeating this experiment,
we can establish how our prototypes compare in absolute
performance (time) with respect to this earlier prototype
that used other technology (i.e., acoustical tracking). We
can also verify whether or not the observed preference for
hybrid 2D/3D interaction also holds in our case. Moreover,
we can study the effect of two-handedness and of the form
factor of the interaction device (i.e., a pen, as in prototype
2, or a frame, as in prototype 3). In addition, we want
to see how performance relates to the spatial ability of our
individual subjects. Later on, we intend to also perform
experiments with more complex volumetric data sets and
alternative tasks.
