1. The Painterly Cel-Shading (The Terrain & Assets)
Standard toon shading often looks too harsh and plastic. For a soft, painted anime look, you want "stepped" lighting with slightly soft edges.
The Quick Way: Use Three's built-in MeshToonMaterial. You can pass a custom gradientMap (a tiny 1x3 or 1x5 pixel texture) to dictate exactly what colors the shadows should be. Instead of pure black, you use warm purples or cool blues for the shadow steps.
The Pro Way: Write a custom ShaderMaterial (or use onBeforeCompile on a standard material) to blend a watercolor texture pattern exclusively into the shaded areas, which creates that hand-painted illusion on the terrain.
2. The Ink Outlines
Getting those crisp, hand-drawn lines around the mountains and plane is crucial for this style. You have two main options in Three.js:
Inverted Hull Method (Best for objects like the plane): You duplicate the mesh, scale it up very slightly along its normals, flip the faces inside out (side: THREE.BackSide), and color it solid black or dark brown.
Post-Processing Edge Detection (Best for terrain): Use the EffectComposer with a custom edge-detection pass (like the SobelOperatorShader or a custom depth/normal-based outline shader). This draws lines wherever there is a sharp change in depth or angle, perfectly highlighting your mountain ridges.
3. The Painted Sky
In Ghibli films, the sky does most of the heavy lifting for the mood.
Instead of a dynamic procedural sky, map a high-resolution, hand-painted panoramic image (or a layered skybox) of anime clouds to your scene.background.
You can put the clouds on large, curved planes in the distance and slowly pan their UV coordinates to simulate wind without needing actual 3D volumetric clouds.
4. Post-Processing (The "Ghibli Filter")
The final 20% of the work creates 80% of the vibe. You will want to set up an EffectComposer with a few specific passes:
Color Grading / LUT Pass: Anime art uses very specific, nostalgic color palettes. You can create a Look-Up Table (LUT) in Photoshop to give the whole camera feed a warm, pastel color grade.
Kuwahara Filter (Optional but amazing): There are custom Three.js implementations of the "Kuwahara" filter, which takes 3D renders and clumps the pixels together to make them look like literal brushstrokes in real-time.
Subtle Bloom: A very light UnrealBloomPass to give the sunset that hazy, glowing light leak over the mountains.
Since you already have the low-poly geometry, the UI, and the flight mechanics working, you have a perfect canvas. Would you like to start by looking at how to set up the MeshToonMaterial with a custom color ramp, or how to implement the outline shader?
