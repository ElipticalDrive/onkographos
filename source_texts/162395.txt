Abstract
Hand-held volumetric images that users can move and orient in space can broaden educational, entertainment, and AR experiences without requiring head-worn devices. However, existing volumetric displays often rely on stationary bases equipped with precise motor control and sensing, limiting tangible and embodied interaction with the displayed imagery. We propose a mobile volumetric display that combines a multilayer hand-held fan with dynamic projection mapping, allowing users to manipulate a passive display object within an instrumented tracking and projection workspace. High-speed optical tracking estimates the object’s six-degree-of-freedom pose and rotation, and the renderer generates phase-synchronized frames in real time to keep the content registered to the rotating layers. A prototype that projects onto stacked fan-shaped disks supports phase-registered projection during handheld manipulation within the tested operating range. Technical measurements demonstrate that latency compensation improves projection alignment, and a preliminary user study suggests that the prototype can convey layered depth while users manipulate the fan. We demonstrate the approach on layered fan-shaped disks and spinning tops and discuss its potential extension to other rotating geometries.

1 Introduction
Flat panel displays that present images on two-dimensional surfaces have long been used as computer graphics display devices. In recent years, there has been an increasing demand for display systems capable of rendering more realistic and immersive visual experiences, particularly in entertainment, digital signage, and artistic applications. Among these, volumetric display systems [6, 15, 27, 31, 37] have been extensively explored for their ability to enhance viewer immersion and can support spatial understanding in specific visualization tasks. However, numerous research challenges persist in volumetric display systems, including limitations in visual expressiveness, simultaneous viewing by multiple users, and low interactivity due to not being hand-held. For instance, systems requiring special eyewear or equipment [5, 14, 22] often restrict visual content to only those wearing the devices and frequently cause visual fatigue.
As one of the volumetric display systems, the method of projecting optical patterns onto a rotating object has been explored [1, 6, 15, 20, 38, 41]. This approach enables the incorporation of physical properties such as the material and shape of the rotating object into the projected imagery, allowing viewers to see highly realistic visuals without requiring special equipment. It leverages the human visual system’s characteristics to perceive sufficiently rapid light modulation as continuous illumination, creating aerial images by projecting optical patterns when the rapidly rotating object’s depth and material properties are in the desired state. Consequently, these systems have been actively explored and proposed, particularly in the field of spatial augmented reality. Despite these advantages, many previously proposed systems require synchronization between motors or sensors attached to the rotating object and the projection. Additionally, they often become complex due to projector rotation mechanisms, making the projection surface difficult to manipulate.
To address these issues recent studies have explored ways to let users directly grasp, reposition, and re-orient the rotating structure itself [17, 18, 19]. Building on this idea, we present Mobile Volu-Me, a hand-held volumetric display that employs dynamic projection mapping on a rotating fan. Users physically grasp and reorient the fan, which simultaneously functions as the projection substrate, realizing a tangible, embodied form of interaction. In this system, we simplify the mechanism of the rotating object that serves as the projection surface for volumetric imagery and project images onto it from external sources using dynamic projection mapping techniques. This approach allows users to utilize various rotating objects as displays and freely rotate them to observe the projected imagery. Furthermore, by structuring the rotating object as a multi-layered fan assembly, we can express layered 3D imagery. The purpose of this research is to demonstrate the potential for converting various everyday rotating objects into volumetric displays through this method, and integrating haptic manipulation and spatial perception into a unified, embodied experience.
The main contributions of this research are as follows:
•
A method for enabling users to handle volumetric images as handheld objects via dynamic projection mapping.
•
A technical evaluation of projection alignment and latency compensation in the proposed system.
•
A preliminary perceptual evaluation and application examples that explore the potential of handheld volumetric interaction.
2 Related Work
2.1 Volumetric Display Technologies
Methods for displaying stereoscopic images have been studied for a long time. Commercially widespread techniques include shutter glasses [22] and visualization using Head-Mounted Displays (HMDs). However, these methods require users to wear special equipment, making simultaneous observation by an unspecified number of people difficult. Although simpler methods allowing for autostereoscopic viewing have been proposed [5, 14], they suffer from significant reductions in brightness and resolution compared to the original display. Furthermore, parallax-based methods can cause vergence-accommodation conflict, leading to eye strain. Holographic technology [10] does not have such issues, but computational holography for dynamically displaying CG still faces challenges for real-world applications.
2.2 Projection Mapping on Dynamic Objects
Projection mapping technology can alter the apparent material and shape of objects. This technique leverages the physical shape of real objects to render more immersive CG imagery. While commercially prevalent projection mapping typically targets static objects like buildings, there has been significant research into projecting onto dynamic objects. Techniques for dynamic projection include tracking the dynamic object using cameras [16, 24, 25, 36], as well as methods that project images by following the object using optical axis controllers [34], prediction based on motion models [21], and low-latency high-speed projection [26, 28]. Furthermore, expressive techniques that utilize dynamic projection to change the apparent properties of objects [7] have been explored.
2.3 Displays Using Periodically Moving and Rotating Objects
Prior studies have explored volumetric displays that reconstruct three-dimensional imagery by illuminating physical elements undergoing high-speed, periodic motion [31], or projecting light onto such moving elements [3, 37]. FLEXiVol [3] mitigates safety concerns by employing touch-safe materials, thereby broadening the range of interactive affordances available to users. BloomBeacon [40] further explores interaction with motion based displays by using a soft rotating line with integrated LEDs and touch sensing electrodes to form an on demand touchable display surface through persistence of vision motion. BloomBeacon demonstrates that rotating elements can support not only mid-air visualization but also direct touch interaction when safety, rotation disturbance, and transient touch sensing are carefully addressed.
When structured light or specialized imagery is projected onto an object undergoing periodic motion, particularly rotation, at a certain speed or higher, the afterimage effect integrates the projected content, enabling representations that leverage the object’s shape and material. Existing research includes techniques that project structured light onto periodically moving objects to deform their appearance [8, 9] and methods for displaying imagery with realistic material properties [1, 41]. Furthermore, projections onto elaborately shaped rotating objects [6, 15, 17, 18, 20, 38] and systems capable of displaying arbitrary shapes, such as those employing rotating projectors to project omnidirectional viewpoint imagery [27], also exist. Imagery projected using such methods can be simultaneously observed by multiple people with the naked eye and does not cause vergence-accommodation conflict. Additionally, the properties of the projected imagery can be made more realistic by carefully designing the shape and material of the rotating object. However, precise motor control and strict measurements are required, so the rotating object is typically fixed to a base. Consequently, interaction achieved by moving a volumetric display based on a rotating object has not been sufficiently discussed. In contrast to approaches that embed light emitting or sensing components into the rotating structure, our work externally projects phase synchronized imagery onto passive, hand held rotating objects to create layered volumetric images.
2.4 Spatial 3D Interface
In fields such as scientific analysis, data visualization, and entertainment, previewing methods for 3DCG using XR technologies have been actively researched in recent years. Augmented Reality (AR) interfaces using see-through HMDs and flat panel displays [2, 4, 13, 30, 35, 42] utilize physical controllers or hand tracking to synthesize CG into the real world, providing highly immersive interaction experiences. However, these require viewing the imagery through a screen. On the other hand, tabletop visualization methods using projectors [32, 33] and small projectors [12, 29, 33, 39] were actively studied in the past. However, these primarily utilize object surfaces or flat planes as interfaces, and displays that allow free manipulation of three-dimensional shapes have not been sufficiently explored.
3 System Design
The objective of this system is to realize a handheld volumetric display that supports direct manipulation of the display object. Related research has issues with the displayed volumetric images and the mobility of the system, thus not satisfying this requirement. Therefore, we propose a display utilizing a rotating object with fan-shaped disks and dynamic projection technology.
3.1 Design Requirements
The design of Mobile Volu-Me is guided by three requirements: naked-eye layered depth presentation, mobility of the passive display object, and tangible interaction with appropriate safety boundaries.
3.1.1 Naked-eye layered depth presentation.
The system should present visual content at multiple physical depths without requiring head-worn devices or special glasses. Rather than presenting separate images to the viewer’s left and right eyes, Mobile Volu-Me forms discrete physical image planes using multiple rotating disks positioned at different depths. This layered configuration allows the physical depth and material properties of the projection surfaces to contribute to the perceived imagery without relying on eye tracking or viewer-worn equipment.
3.1.2 Mobility of the passive display object.
Many existing displays based on rotating objects incorporate a motorized rotation mechanism and are fixed to a stationary base [1, 6, 15, 20, 27, 38, 41]. To support handheld manipulation, the display object should remain passive and should not require an embedded projector, light-emitting element, rotation sensor, or continuously powered motor. In Mobile Volu-Me, the position, orientation, and rotational phase of the passive display object are measured externally, and the projected imagery is updated accordingly. This design allows users to change the position and orientation of the display object within the calibrated tracking and projection workspace.
3.1.3 Tangible interaction and safety.
The display object should be directly graspable so that users can manipulate its position and orientation while observing the layered imagery. In the proposed system, the rotating fan therefore serves both as a projection surface and as a tangible object for interaction. However, direct interaction with a rotating structure introduces the risk of accidental contact with the blades. To reduce sustained mechanical force during such contact, the current prototype uses a manually operated hand-crank mechanism rather than a continuously powered motor. Users manipulate the display by holding its handle and are instructed not to touch the blades while they are rotating. The detailed mechanical design and remaining safety limitations are described in Sections 4 and 7, respectively.
3.2 Layered Volumetric Image Formation
A mechanism for displaying layered volumetric images. By projecting the image to be projected on each layer (Target image) in accordance with the fan shape, the user perceives the target image on each disk.
It is necessary to resolve the issues of volumetric imaging described in Section 3.1.1 and achieve natural image observation for the user. The proposed system uses three-dimensional imaging utilizing a rotating object. For the representation of volumetric images, a layered three-dimensional image is projected by projecting a specific optical pattern with a projector onto the surface of a rotating object composed of fan-shaped disks as shown in Figure 2. Compared with spiral swept-volume approaches [15, 20, 38], the proposed configuration provides discrete physical depth planes but has lower depth resolution.
The shape of each disk is such that fan shapes with an interior angle of 360nldeg are evenly arranged, where n is the number of blades and l is the number of layers, and each disk is attached with a phase shift of 360nldeg. This arrangement ensures that all blades do not overlap when the rotating object is viewed perpendicular to the axis of rotation, and the projector’s projection frame is most effectively projected onto the surface of the disk blades.
3.2.1 Image formation model.
Each disk forms a discrete physical image plane at a different depth. During one rotation cycle, the viewer temporally integrates the radiance reflected from the illuminated blade segments. The perceived image is therefore formed by the time-averaged combination of the visible contributions from the individual layers. The opaque PLA blades serve as diffuse projection surfaces.
3.3 External Tracking of Rotating Objects
To allow users to freely move and rotate the rotating object, as described in Sections 3.1.2 and 3.1.3, a method that does not embed a rotation mechanism, rotation detection mechanism, or light-emitting element in the rotating object is required. The proposed system applies dynamic projection mapping technology, which tracks the position, orientation, and rotation around the axis of the rotating object using cameras and projects images externally using a projector. A transformation matrix is calculated from the tracked pose and position, and a projection frame obtained by deforming the target image is projected by a high-speed projector, thereby projecting the image onto the surface of the rotating object.
This allows users to freely move and rotate the rotating object, enabling them to carry and rotate it. Furthermore, since the projector’s projection frame is deformed according to the position and orientation of the rotating object, users can observe images while manipulating the rotating object.
4 Implementation
To discuss the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed system, we implemented the proposed method using actual hardware.
4.1 Hardware
Hardware setup: Projection frames are generated based on tracking results from high-speed cameras and projected onto a rotating object by a high-speed projector.
To realize the proposed system, it is crucial to track objects at high speed and project images with minimal latency. Therefore, our system utilizes high-speed cameras to detect the object’s position, a computer for tracking and generating projection images, and a high-speed projector (Figure 1 (Left) and Figure 3). Three high-speed cameras (PrimeX 13W1) are mounted on a stand and connected to the computer via a switching hub. The computer is equipped with 32GB of RAM, an Intel Core i7-13700KF CPU, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX3080 GPU, running on Windows 11. A high-speed projector (DynaFlash2) is connected to the computer via an optical cable.
4.2 Detachable Rotating Fans
The detachable five-layer fan assembly used in the prototype. It consists of five stacked three-bladed disks and is attached to a common hand-crank base. Retroreflective markers are attached to the frontmost disk for external tracking.
Process of projection image generation. Masks the target image into a fan shape and transforms it to the disk location.
As projection surfaces, we use a fan-shaped layered assembly designed to be detachable from the hand-crank base. For the prototype and user study, we fabricated two interchangeable configurations using a 3D printer and PLA filament: a five-layer configuration and a three-layer configuration. In both configurations, each disk comprised three blades, each 50 mm in radius and 3 mm thick, and adjacent disks were spaced at 10 mm intervals (Figure 4). The disks were mounted with successive angular offsets of 360∘3×l, where l denotes the number of layers. Accordingly, the angular offset was 24° for the five-layer configuration and 40° for the three-layer configuration. This arrangement ensures that the blades of the disks do not overlap when viewed from the front, and the entire assembly rotates in unison. The disks are attached to the base of a hand-cranked fan, allowing users to rotate the fan by gripping and turning its handle. Because the rotor is driven manually rather than by a continuously powered motor, contact with an external object causes it to rapidly decelerate and stop. This passive stopping characteristic reduces the risk of sustained contact during accidental contact and supports supervised handheld operation. Retroreflective markers are attached to the surface disk for tracking by the high-speed cameras. To enable the recognition of the disk’s rotation angle and orientation (front/back), the markers are placed at the center point and at two points along the bisector of the central angle of two blades. These two markers on the bisector are positioned at distances of 47 mm and 23.5 mm from the center, resulting in a total of three markers.

4.3 Tracking and Projection
The software used in this system performs optical tracking of the rotating object, coordinate transformation, and projection image rendering.
4.3.1 Optical Tracking for Rotating Objects.
The images captured by the high-speed cameras are analyzed using OptiTrack’s Motive API3. Motive API allows for the pre-registration of the retroreflective marker positions on the rotating object and detects objects (the rotating fan) that match this configuration. From the detected object’s position and rotation information, the object’s model matrix M is calculated. Simultaneously, the rotation angle a[°] of the rotating object around its axis of rotation is computed.
4.3.2 Calculation of Coordinate Transformation.
Given the projector’s projection matrix P, obtained through prior calibration, the homogeneous coordinates pp on the projector’s frame where the image intended for a point pr on the rotating object should be drawn are calculated as follows:
pp=PM−1pr
After applying this coordinate transformation, the desired image is rendered onto the surface of the rotating object.
4.3.3 Rendering Projection Frame.
We prepare a target image Ti that we want the user to perceive at the position of the i-th layer. The image to be displayed on the surface of each layer’s disk is generated as shown in Figure 5. For an assembly with l layers and three blades per disk, the i-th layer is rotated by 360(i−1)3×l[∘] relative to the frontmost layer. Accordingly, the angular offset is 24(i − 1)[°] for the five-layer configuration and 40(i − 1)[°] for the three-layer configuration. Furthermore, although the rotating object rotates by a[°] around its axis of rotation, we want the perceived orientation of the target image to be independent of this rotation, with the upward direction of the image always perceived as constant. To achieve this, we rotate the original target image by − a[°] to counteract the rotation around the axis. By processing the target images for each layer according to the above rules, the projection frame is rendered.
4.4 Projection Result
The projection results of this system are shown in Figure 6. The image demonstrates that different visual content is displayed on each layer of the rotating fan as manipulated by the user. Furthermore, the visualization persists even when the user moves or rotates the volumetric display.
5 Experiment
We conducted an evaluation focusing on the accuracy and expressiveness of our prototype system that influence the video presentation experience.
5.1 System Evaluation
Ideally, the projected video should perfectly track the dynamic object. However, real-world hardware systems have performance limitations. Here, we describe a technical evaluation that specifically examines the latency occurring from camera tracking to video projection. In this evaluation, we measure the projection delay based on the discrepancy between the intended projection position and the actual projected position.
5.1.1 Setup and Procedure.

The overall measurement system is illustrated on the left side of Figure 7. Instead of using the fan-shaped disk described in the implementation section, we employ a laser-cut circular disk with a pattern that serves as a reference for the projection position, as shown on the right side of Figure 7. 5.2 Preliminary Perceptual Evaluation
Accurate depth representation is a crucial requirement for a volumetric display. In this study, we conducted a user experiment to evaluate the perceived quality of the prototype system’s displayed imagery and discuss the accuracy of its presentation. This user study was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics review board.
5.2.1 Experiment Design.
Projected images used in user experiments. Upper row: An image with depth (Cond. A) and an image that merely projects a flat surface (Cond. B). Lower row: Images with five colors displayed on each layer.
This experiment used the same tracking and projection setup described in Section 4. The layered fan assembly was detachable from the hand-crank base, allowing the number of physical depth layers to be changed without replacing the base or the external tracking and projection hardware. We mounted the three-layer configuration for Task 1 and the five-layer configuration for Task 2. The rendering masks were configured according to the number of layers used in each task. The video projection incorporated the latency compensation described in the previous section. We began by briefly explaining the objectives of our research and the experiment, followed by a demonstration of the system. During the experiment, participants were provided with a hand-cranked layered fan. The prototype uses the hand-crank mechanism of a commercially available handheld fan rather than a continuously powered motor, and the rotor readily stops when it comes into contact with an external object, thereby reducing the risk of injury from accidental contact. Nevertheless, as a safety precaution, participants were explicitly instructed not to touch the rotating blades. They were allowed to view the displayed imagery from any position and angle within the visible range. Participants were instructed to rotate the fan until the projected image appeared visually continuous without excessive rotational speed. The rotational speed was not recorded but was estimated not to exceed approximately 1,000 RPM.
To evaluate the quality of the prototype system’s displayed imagery, we conducted tasks to verify two key aspects: 1) whether a three-dimensional image was projected, and 2) whether the layers were displayed in the correct order.
Task 1. Using the three-layered disk shown in Figure 8, users were presented with two video conditions. One condition (Cond. A) displayed imagery where a 3D model was sliced along the depth axis and each slice was presented on a corresponding layer according to its depth. The other condition (Cond. B) displayed the same planar rendering of the 3D model on all three physical layers, with the image centered on each disk. Users were asked to identify which condition presented the 3D model with a sense of depth. The order in which Cond. A and Cond. B were presented was counterbalanced across users.
Task 2. As illustrated in Figure 8, an image intended to display squares of different colors on each of the five layers of the rotating five-layered disk was presented. Users observed the displayed imagery and reported which color appeared on which layer, starting from the frontmost layer.
Each condition was presented once, and participants were allowed to observe the display until they were ready to respond. No fixed viewing distance or observation time was imposed because participants were encouraged to move within the calibrated workspace and inspect the display from different directions. Although no time limit was imposed, all participants completed each task in less than five minutes.
Following these tasks, participants were asked to complete a form detailing their impressions of the system and their ideas for potential applications.
5.2.2 Participants.
We recruited twelve participants (eight males and four females) from a local university. All participants reported having normal color vision and were able to accurately distinguish the different colors in Task 2.
5.2.3 Result.
For Task 1, ten out of twelve participants reported that Cond. A better conveyed the depth of the model. In the task asking participants to identify the layer on which each colored square was displayed, all participants provided correct answers.
User feedback indicated enjoyment in how the projected imagery tracked the hand-cranked fan’s movement and tilting. Participants also noted that layering was a good approach for achieving clear imagery at a low cost. However, several participants mentioned that while different colors were discernible on each layer, there was some color bleeding from one layer to others. Some also pointed out that the image quality was not sufficient due to color bleeding to other layers and clipping of the imagery on the layers. In Task 1, the participants who preferred Cond. B mentioned that while they vaguely recognized that Cond. A displayed different images on each layer, the imagery in Cond. B was projected more cleanly, making the layered structure easier to understand.
Participants also proposed a range of potential applications. We descriptively grouped the nine open-ended responses into six overlapping categories: entertainment and immersive media (four responses), including volumetric trick art, a 3D fishing game, illusion-based games, and a mobile cinema; interactive interfaces (two responses), including a layer-selective laser pointer and interaction with imagery projected onto a fan; ambient and relaxation experiences (one response), combining natural scenery with music and airflow; visualization, education, and skill training (one response), such as inspecting layered architectural structures, learning the use of complex tools, and AR-assisted skill acquisition; signage and guidance (one response), including switchable advertisements and distance-dependent road warnings; and mobile deployment (one response), such as mounting the display on a drone. Because a single response could include ideas belonging to multiple categories, these category counts are not mutually exclusive.
5.3 Discussion
The results of the experiment indicate that our prototype system, utilizing rotating disk tracking and high speed projection, can display intended images on specific depth layers. This study was designed as a preliminary perceptual evaluation to confirm whether participants could perceive the layered images and depth ordering, rather than as a full quantitative assessment of perceptual accuracy or image quality. Therefore, we focused on participants’ responses and qualitative feedback, and did not conduct detailed numerical analyses of the displayed image quality or perceptual thresholds.
However, applying this approach to render complex 3D models reveals several challenges. One issue is the clarity of the imagery. Since the projection relies on persistence of vision, displaying different images on each layer can lead to a loss of brightness and detail. Furthermore, if the flickering rate of the projected light falls below the critical flicker fusion threshold (CFF [11]), it can be perceived as flickering or incomplete imagery. Another challenge is the accuracy of layer-specific projection. The five disks are distinguished by their angular offsets, and the intended layer is selected by synchronizing the projection mask with the tracked rotational phase. Tracking errors and residual phase errors can cause the projected mask to deviate from the actual blade position. As a result, light intended for one depth layer may illuminate a blade belonging to another layer, which is perceived as color bleeding across layers.
6 Potential Applications
This section discusses the potential applications of the proposed system through projection onto a broader range of rotating objects. To explore the application possibilities of our proposed system, we identified potential application examples by creating actual applications through modifications of the prototype system and by surveying user study participants regarding use cases achievable with the system. The augmented spinning-top application was implemented by the authors as a demonstration of projection onto an autonomously rotating object. The relaxation scenario corresponds to a participant suggestion involving natural scenery, music, and airflow. The volumetric mouse is an author-developed concept related to participant suggestions concerning layer-specific pointing and interaction with imagery projected onto a fan.
6.1 Entertainment - Augmented Spinning Top
One of the features of the proposed system is that volumetric images can be observed even when the rotating display is moved freely. Therefore, it is possible to project images not only onto objects operated by the user but also onto autonomously rotating objects. Similar to the system by Matoba et al. [23], displaying images on a spinning top allows its use as a tangible device (Figure 9). By utilizing the proposed system in this manner, existing forms of entertainment can be augmented by incorporating volumetric images, providing richer expressions.
6.2 Spatial Experience - Relaxation
Projection effects onto spinning tops. 3D character models are projected onto the tops, and sparks are displayed when they collide with each other.
Illustrations of example applications. Left: Relaxation by projecting 3D images on a tabletop fan. Right: Example of a game using three-dimensional volumetric pointing (goldfish scooping).
Although the proposed system displays layered volumetric images for simple 3D representation, in principle, it can also render volumetric images on spiral-shaped rotors used in existing research [20, 38] and commercially available curved blades. This allows for the transformation of everyday rotating objects into volumetric displays, enabling applications for creating ambiance in living spaces and for relaxation. Furthermore, using movable rotating objects such as electric fans allows for easy layout changes.
6.3 Interface - Volumetric Mouse
The proposed system allows for free movement and rotation of the display. Therefore, this system can be utilized for pointing in three-dimensional data. Beyond simply indicating a point in 3D space, it can simultaneously display the surrounding environment and enable volumetric range selection. Applying this characteristic, Figure 10 illustrates a concept for virtual goldfish scooping. Additionally, the proposed system allows multiple unspecified individuals to simultaneously observe 3D images without the need for special equipment such as glasses, which is particularly advantageous for collaborative tasks with others.
7 Discussion
We propose a volumetric display that allows users to freely move and rotate to observe the projected imagery within the calibrated tracking and projection workspace, leveraging a fan-shaped rotating mechanism and dynamic projection mapping techniques. In this paper, we present the implementation of a prototype system utilizing high-speed cameras and a high-speed projector. During the study, participants could grasp, turn, and tilt the fan while viewing the projected content. Ten of twelve participants selected the depth-sliced condition in Task 1, and all twelve correctly reported the layer order in Task 2. These observations provide preliminary evidence that discrete depth layers were perceptible in the tested setup. Because we did not include static or head-tracked baselines, the study does not establish that the proposed interaction is more intuitive than those alternatives. Based on these findings, this section examines the effectiveness and challenges of the proposed system, along with its potential applications.

7.2 Degrees of Freedom in Volumetric Image Display
The proposed method allows the user to change the position and orientation of the display within the calibrated workspace. Participants’ responses suggest that the discrete depth layers were perceptible under the tested conditions. However, we did not measure depth-estimation accuracy or compare the interaction with alternative display techniques.
This mechanism can also accommodate freehand spinning motions, suggesting potential applications to everyday rotating objects. By introducing a freely customizable rotating plate mechanism, as seen in DiskPlay [18], or a material-based rotating disk like Phyxel [41], more flexible modifications of the projected imagery can be achieved. However, there are challenges in implementing a real-world system. In our prototype system, the displayable angle was limited due to the dependency on the camera’s angle, the reflection direction of the retroreflective markers, and the projector’s projection direction. Employing a greater number of cameras and projectors [28] could potentially enhance the display’s degrees of freedom.
7.3 Projection Quality
The user experiments demonstrated that the system could represent images with depth in most cases. Furthermore, the tracking and rotational prediction enabled the projection of intended images onto the desired depth layers. However, due to the use of a projector, shadows could be cast on the disk by the rotating object itself or other obstacles. This issue could also be resolved by employing multiple projectors for projection [28]. Due to the mechanism of multi-layered projection using a projector, the luminous intensity may decrease compared to the original planar image, resulting in reduced clarity. Additionally, the color bleeding reported by participants is caused primarily by residual tracking and phase errors. When the estimated rotational phase differs from the actual blade position, the projection mask intended for one layer can partially overlap a blade on another layer, causing the wrong color or image to appear on that layer. Although the latency compensation reduced the mean apparent projection delay, instantaneous tracking and phase errors were not completely eliminated. Improving tracking accuracy, reducing end-to-end latency, and increasing the tracking and rendering rates would therefore improve layer separation and image quality.
7.4 Safety and Interaction Boundaries
The current rotor consists of rigid PLA blades, each 3 mm thick and 50 mm in radius. During the study, participants held the device only by its handle and were explicitly instructed not to touch the blades while the rotor was spinning. The fan was manually driven at approximately 600–1,000 RPM using a hand-crank mechanism. Unlike a continuously powered motor, the hand-crank mechanism stops supplying torque soon after the user stops cranking. Consequently, the rotor rapidly decelerates and stops when operation is interrupted or when it comes into contact with an external object. Although this behavior limits sustained contact with the rotating blades, it does not eliminate the possibility of injury. No injuries or other safety-related incidents occurred during the user study. Nevertheless, the exposed rigid blades may still pose risks of impact or abrasion, and the current prototype has not undergone formal mechanical safety testing or certification. We therefore restrict its use to supervised laboratory environments. Future versions should employ softer or touch-safe materials, rounded blade edges, a protective guard, mechanical speed limiting, and more secure blade attachment.
7.5 Technical Limitations
In our prototype, the tracking of the rotating object was performed at 240 FPS, which constituted a bottleneck. Furthermore, the program’s processing speed was insufficient, resulting in a projection rate of approximately 600 Hz. To achieve higher quality projected imagery, it is necessary to transition to faster hardware and software components.
Moreover, we evaluated single-user manipulation only. Although the content may be visible to multiple observers without eyewear, simultaneous handling was not studied and may increase occlusion and collision risks.
8 Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a novel hand-held volumetric display method that enables tangible, embodied interaction with three-dimensional imagery through dynamic projection mapping. We implemented a prototype system using high-speed camera tracking and high-speed projection onto a rotating object with shape-adapted imagery, and we discuss its feasibility. Users could observe the projected imagery from various positions and angles by manipulating a hand-cranked fan with the rotating object attached. This paper presents evaluation experiments on the prototype system, focusing on latency and the quality of the displayed imagery. Under the motorized latency-evaluation setup, compensation reduced the measured mean apparent delay from 12.5 ms to 0.147 ms. In the preliminary perceptual study, 10 of 12 participants selected the depth-sliced condition, and all participants correctly identified the five-layer order. These results support the feasibility of the approach within the tested tracking, projection, and viewing conditions; broader viewing-angle, image-quality, and comparative interaction evaluations remain future work.
