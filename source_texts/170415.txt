Abstract
We review holography techniques related to imaging and sensing. Holography has been actively researched as three-dimensional (3D) imaging and 3D display techniques. Because of the successive evolutions of electronic and optical devices, digital holographic and quantitative 3D measurements with high accuracy and realistic 3D motion-picture image display without glasses have been realized. Moreover, holography has led to breakthroughs in various applications in the fields of measurement and processing through the development of holographic light-wave modulation techniques. We briefly introduce various applications of holography and then review imaging and sensing techniques with holography, focusing on quantitative phase imaging with daily-use light, spatially incoherent digital holography, holographic display, and microscopy with holographic light modulation.

1. Introduction
Holography [1–5] is a three-dimensional (3D) imaging and sensing technique exploiting the interference and diffraction of light. A wavefront diffracted from an object is recorded on a two-dimensional (2D) recording medium, and the medium containing the wavefront information is called a ‘hologram.’ Both the amplitude and phase information of the object are obtained from the hologram. A 3D image of the object is reconstructed from the hologram on the basis of the theory of light diffraction. Holography has been researched towards a natural 3D display, a wavefront modulator, multiplexed imaging, and a lensless 3D image recorder. Remarkable features of holography are that the 3D motion-picture imaging of light-pulse propagation in a scattering medium is achieved with light-in-flight recording [5], and multidimensional information such as 3D coordinate, phase, wavelengths, and the polarization distribution of light is simultaneously recorded with a single exposure and no optical filter [3].
There have been continuous improvements in electronic devices such as image sensors, spatial light modulators (SLMs), and computers including processors, graphical processing units (GPUs), and field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs). Such improvements strongly contribute to research studies on holography. Quantitative 3D image sensing and quantitative phase imaging (QPI) are achieved with holography using an image sensor and a computer, which is termed digital holography (DH) [6–16]. High-speed holographic motion-picture recording with a high-speed camera [17–19] and multidimensional holographic image sensing with an advanced image sensor [20, 21] have been reported. With an SLM, 3D and 4D (3D + time) displays [22–28], dynamic wavefront modulation and compensation [29, 30], and multidimensional holographic imaging with a single-pixel detector [12, 31–34] have been realized. Real-time holographic measurement in DH is performed using a GPU or an FPGA [35–39]. Thanks to these improvements, holographic techniques such as DH, QPI [40–46], 3D displacement measurements [47, 48], phase tomography [49–60], adaptive optics [29, 30], holographic display [22–28], real-time holographic 3D imaging [31–34], holographic projection [61, 62], arbitrary wave generation [63–65], microscopy with holographic light modulation [66, 67], and light processing [68, 69] have been actively researched.
In this article, we review imaging and sensing techniques with holography, focusing on QPI with daily-use light, DH with spatially incoherent light termed incoherent DH (IDH), holographic display, and microscopy with holographic light modulation. Initially, we introduce DH briefly and multiplexed imaging with holography to explain the topics listed above.
2. DH

As indicated in equation (1), in the hologram, both the object wave Uo(x,y) and undesired images such as the 0th-order diffraction wave and the conjugate image, respectively, are contained. Signal processing for the object-wave extraction from the recorded hologram is important in DH. Two representative methods are the FT [8] method and PSI [10, 70]. Figure 2 shows the flows of these methods. In the FT method, a digital hologram is obtained with the off-axis geometry. A spatial carrier frequency is introduced by the off-axis geometry, and Uo(x,y) and undesired images are separated in the spatial frequency domain after the calculation of 2D FT. Undesired images are numerically filtered out, and then Uo(x,y) on the image sensor plane is obtained by applying 2D inverse FT to the filtered FT image. When conducting numerical refocusing, a diffraction integral is calculated using the filtered FT image. Single-shot DH without undesired images is realized by using an off-axis geometry with the FT method at the cost of the spatial bandwidth of an image sensor. In contrast, Uo(x,y) is extracted without the cost of the spatial bandwidth. In PSI, several holograms whose φr(x,y) are 0, π/2, π, and 3π/2 are recorded. Through the calculation of PSI, undesired light waves are numerically cancelled out and only Uo(x,y) is obtained. PSI requires multiple phase-shifted holograms and therefore, temporal resolution is partly sacrificed.

One of the unique characteristics of DH is multiplexed imaging without any optical filters. Multidimensional information is simultaneously recorded on an image sensor by exploiting the phase modulation of the recorded hologram(s) [3]. Three types of holographic multiplexing techniques have been reported: spatial frequency-division multiplexing [3, 47, 48, 72], temporal frequency-division multiplexing [73–75], and phase-division multiplexing or computational coherent superposition (CCS) [76–78]. Figure 3 illustrates their schematics. In spatial frequency-division multiplexing, different spatial carrier frequencies are introduced to different object waves by angular multiplexing and/or utilizing the difference in wavelength. Their holograms are multiplexed on the image sensor plane. These object waves are separated in the FT plane by the FT method. In temporal frequency-division multiplexing, different temporal heterodynes are introduced to different holograms to separate different object waves in the temporal FT domain. CCS is the multidimension-multiplexed PSI technique and is implemented with an in-line DH configuration. Multidimensional information is encoded by phase shifts with a phase modulator and selectively extracted from recorded multidimension-multiplexed phase-shifted holograms by the developed PSI technique [76–78]. These holographic multiplexing techniques have been applied to highly accurate 3D shape measurements, quantitative phase measurements with optical phase unwrapping [72, 73, 75, 79], and multiwavelength and hyperspectral holographic imaging [74, 76–78, 80, 81].

As described above, DH has attractive features and prospective applications. However, spatially and temporally coherent light is generally required to record a digital hologram; thus, it is regarded that the use of a laser light source is essential. Spatially coherent light generates speckle noise and affects the quality of the reconstructed image.
3. QPI by DH with daily-use light
With the invention of the Zernike phase-contrast microscope [83], transparent specimens have been visualized with incoherent light. However, only qualitative 2D imaging is conducted and it is difficult to realize quantitative analysis with it. Interferometry including DH has been researched to achieve QPI, but a laser light source is generally required, and a two-arm interferometer is not robust against external vibrations. To achieve QPI with daily-use light and high robustness, several approaches have been proposed as described in figure 5: the combination of a point light source, a self-reference interferometer with a 4f optical setup, and PSI, termed Fourier phase microscopy (FPM) or simply self-reference DH (SDH) [84–88] as shown in figures 5(a)–(c), quadriwave lateral shearing interferometry (QWLSI) [89] shown in figure 5(d), the combination of a phase-contrast microscope, a self-reference interferometer with a 4f optical setup, and PSI, termed spatial light interference microscopy (SLIM) or flat-fielding quantitative phase contrast microscopy (FF-QPCM) [90–95] as shown in figures 5(e)–(g), and the combination of a lateral shear and PSI, termed a QPI unit [96] shown in figure 5(h). FPM/SDH and SLIM/FF-QPCM adopt both a common-path self-reference interferometer and the principle of Gabor holography in which light diffracted by the object and light passing through the object without diffraction are regarded as object and reference waves, respectively. These waves are spatially separated on the FT plane, and phase shifts are introduced using an SLM. The reference wave is generated from the object-illumination light, and QPI is conducted with a spatially partial coherence and daily-use light source. FPM/SDH uses a quasi-plane or quasi-spherical wave, and SLIM/FF-QPCM uses ring-shaped illumination light. From the difference in the intensity distribution of the illumination light, SLIM/FF-QPCM can obtain a higher spatial resolution than FPM/SDH. However, SLIM/FF-QPCM has a measurement range limitation in the depth direction owing to the ring-shaped illumination light. Therefore, FPM/SDH is suitable for application to digital holographic QPI in 3D space with large depth. A QPI unit is composed of a polarimetric lateral shearing phase-shifting interferometer and set on the output port of a microscope. An object magnified by the microscope is sparsely distributed or small, and the object and the light not diffracted by the object interfere through the lateral shearing. A polarimetric lateral shearing interferometer is constructed with a polarizer, a calcite to generate a lateral shear, a liquid crystal phase modulator to conduct PSI, and an analyzer, and is more compact than FPM/SDH and SLIM/FF-QPCM. However, the field of view is limited. QWLSI exploits self-interference and differential interference. The object-wavefront information is retrieved by obtaining quantitative phase gradients along the x- and y-axis directions based on four-wave interference. Different from SLIM/FF-QPCM, FPM/SDH, and a QPI unit, QPI is conducted with a single exposure of an image sensor because of the off-axis configuration. Therefore, the temporal resolution is relatively high.

It is possible to realize single-shot QPI with PSI by exploiting single-shot PSI (SSPSI) [100–102] in QPI systems. SSPSI is implemented by combining a quarter-wave plate and a polarization-imaging camera [101] into a QPI system utilizing polarization. SSPSI has been adopted to SLIM/FF-QPCM [92, 94] and FPM/SDH [87] as shown in figures 5(b) and (f), and single-shot QPI has been performed similarly to QWLSI. As a unique method, QPI with SSPSI was also realized by exploiting two-step PSI, the phase distribution of a blazed grating, and artificial intelligence [95]. Figure 6 shows examples of the experimental results of SDH, a light-emitting diode (LED), and SSPSI [87]. Figures 6(a)–(e) indicate that the complex amplitude distributions of HeLa cells at different depths are reconstructed from a single self-reference digital hologram; thus, single-exposure digital holographic QPI with an LED is performed. Figures 6(f)–(h) show the motion-picture QPI of the Brownian movements of transparent polystyrene particles of 1 µm diameter at 10 fps. It is noted that a concave mirror was used to illuminate specimens with convergent light and generate light interference of object-wave components unmodulated by specimens. The reconstructed images in the areas in which specimens were not set were affected by random noise relatively although quantitative phase information was retrieved [87]. Coded phase masks [86, 88] will be an effective way to improve the image quality in such the area. However, these results indicate a solution for the drawback of QPI utilizing PSI. Furthermore, it should be noted that the SBWP available for recording an object wave is extended by applying SSPSI in comparison with an off-axis configuration [103, 104]. Roughly estimating, a QWLSI setup obtains a ninth of the SBWP of an image sensor [103]. In contrast, a QPI setup with SSPSI and the calculation of four-step PSI obtains up to a fourth of an image sensor theoretically [104].

Several research groups have adopted two-arm interferometers [97–99, 105–109] such as the Mach–Zehnder and Michelson interferometers shown in figure 5(i) and succeeded in highly accurate QPI with daily-use light while it is generally considered that a two-arm interferometer is not robust against external vibrations. It is worth noting that a research group in Korea achieved QPI for reflective 3D objects with micron- and submicron-order heights, exploiting the CCS scheme and multiwavelength phase unwrapping [97–99]. Multidimension-multiplexed PSI in CCS requires accurate phase shifts to extract object waves selectively. Therefore, an optical setup should be robust against external vibrations to guarantee accurate phase shifts. Experimental results in [97–99] indicate the applicability of a two-arm interferometer with CCS to highly accurate 3D surface shape measurement. The coherence length is limited when using a temporally low-coherence light; thus, an in-line configuration has the advantage of the measurement range in three dimensions.
4. IDH
Interferometry and DH have been implemented with not only a laser but also a daily-use light source. Interferometry and DH with daily-use light have been applied to QPI described in section 3 and spatially incoherent digital holographic imaging. The latter was developed from incoherent holography [110–112] and is termed IDH. IDH obtains a spatially incoherent hologram by exploiting the self-interference of light and has performed the monocular 3D imaging of self-luminous light such as fluorescence light [113, 114], sunlight [115], LED light [116], Raman scattering light [117], thermal light [118], and the light generated from discharge [119]. IDH was initially implemented using two-arm [74, 120, 121] and Sagnac interferometers [122, 123]. Then, novel common-path self-interference digital interferometers adopting a static birefringent material and a rotational phase shifter termed conoscopic holography [124], a grating to generate a quadriwave lateral shear termed QWLSI [125], and a phase-only SLM termed Fresnel incoherent correlation holography (FINCH) [118, 126] and coded aperture correlation holography (COACH) [126, 127] have been proposed. These interferometers are highly robust against external vibrations, make the optical systems compact, and enable rapid incoherent 3D imaging.
Single-shot IDH is highly important for high-speed incoherent 3D motion-picture imaging. PSI is frequently adopted in novel common-path self-interference IDH and has contributed to the improvement of image quality. However, PSI generally requires multiple exposures to obtain multiple phase-shifted incoherent digital holograms. To solve this problem, SSPSI [100–102] has been applied to IDH [128–134]. Moreover, CCS can be combined with SSPSI to achieve single-shot multidimension-multiplexed incoherent holographic imaging with a monochrome image sensor [135]. In this section, we will introduce the developments of SSPS-IDH and IDH with SSPS and CCS.
4.1. SSPS-IDH
The basic concept of SSPS-IDH was applied as a patent in 2013 in Japan [136]. Thanks to the evolutions of optical and electronic devices, SSPS-IDH was successfully and experimentally demonstrated using a FINCH system and published in 2017 in the Journal of Optics [128]. Then, various optical systems of SSPS-IDH have been proposed by many research groups [129–142]. Figure 7 shows the basic concept and developed optical systems of SSPS-IDH. As shown in figure 7(a), SSPS-IDH is usually implemented by introducing an SSPS system to an IDH system. The self-interference phenomenon is utilized to obtain an incoherent hologram in incoherent holography and IDH. Two object waves with different wavefront curvature radii are generated from an incoherent object wave to obtain a self-interference hologram, by introducing a birefringent lens, an SLM, diffractive optical element(s), and a self-interference optical setup. A birefringent phase plate is inserted to adjust the optical-path-length difference between the two object waves when a thick birefringent lens such as a crystal lens is used. Then, in SSPS-IDH, multiple phase-shifted incoherent digital holograms are simultaneously recorded with a single exposure of an image sensor, which is based on the space-division multiplexing of SSPS. With a recorded incoherent hologram, signal processing of PSI and diffraction integrals are conducted to reconstruct an incoherent 3D image of an object, based on the image-reconstruction algorithm of SSPS. Various optical systems of SSPS-IDH have been proposed as shown in figures 7(b)–(k). Until now, SLM(s), phase grating(s), geometric phase lens(es) (GPL(s)), a birefringent lens, and a two-arm interferometer with a concave mirror have been set as two-wave generators. In SSPS-IDH, pixelated/parallel PSI is adopted in figures 7(b), (d), (f) and (j) and diffraction-grating interferometry is adopted in figures 7(c), (e) and (k). In the former SSPS-IDH system, the light-use efficiency is a problem to be solved owing to the requirement of a polarization filter, and in the latter system, the wavelength dependence of the diffraction angle is a problem. In figure 7(k), it is numerically confirmed that both the 3D and polarimetric information of incoherent light are obtained simultaneously using TPP gratings.

4.1.1. Incoherent 3D motion-picture imaging of moving object(s).
By exploiting SSPS-IDH, the incoherent 3D motion-picture imaging of a moving object has been performed by many research groups. In 2019, the five-megapixel 3D motion-picture imaging of a moving object at 24 fps was performed using the system shown in figure 7(d) [130]. Then, in 2022, high-definition 3D motion-picture imaging with 16 megapixels at 2 fps was demonstrated using the combination of figures 7(e) and (g) [143]. A polarization high-speed camera was adopted to the system shown in figure 7(g), and in 2023, high-speed 3D motion-picture imaging was performed with 5 megapixels at 250 fps and 1 megapixel at 1000 fps [144]. After that, two research groups independently constructed the system shown in figure 7(j) [140, 141], and 3D motion-picture imaging at 60 fps was reported with that system in 2023. SSPS-IDH was also extended to real-time imaging, and real-time 3D motion-picture imaging with 5 megapixels at more than 20 fps was successfully demonstrated in 2024 [39]. In the application to holographic fluorescence microscopy, the 3D motion-picture imaging of 0.2 μm-diameter fluorescence particles was performed with 4 megapixels at more than 100 fps, using the system combined with figures 7(f) and (g), in 2022 [137].
4.1.2. Single-shot full-color incoherent 3D imaging.
It has been challenging that SSPS-IDH simultaneously achieves both downsizing to a chip-sized hologram recorder and single-shot full-color incoherent holographic 3D imaging without undesired diffraction waves. The following approaches for the theme were reported. Single-shot full-color incoherent 3D imaging was attempted with the system shown in figure 7(b), and the experimental results were initially reported in 2019 [132]. The size was not portable, and the exposure time was longer than 100 ms. Then, single-shot full-color incoherent 3D imaging was presented with the systems related to figure 7(d) [145] and 7(h) [134] in 2020. The system based on figure 5(d) was palm-sized and the recording speed was video rate. The latter IDH system was able to become portable and the recording speed was also video rate. The system was later integrated as shown in figure 7(h), and it was confirmed that an at least thumb-sized SSPS-IDH system was successfully constructed. As another way, the SSPS-IDH system adopting conoscopic holography and wave-plate array, which is related to figure 7(f) and based on CCS-IDH that is described in the next subsection, demonstrated single-shot color-multiplexed incoherent 3D imaging in 2020 [135]. By introducing the conoscopic holography system to the polarization-imaging camera-based SSPS-IDH, the system shown in figure 7(f) was conceptually proposed in 2020 [134] and then experimentally demonstrated since 2022 [146]. It has been confirmed that the size is portable by hand, and a solid and birefringent SSPS-IDH system can perform single-shot color incoherent holographic 3D imaging without undesired diffraction waves [147]. Figure 8 shows the experimental setup with the SSPS-IDH system of [146] termed holocamera, which is based on figures 7(a) and (g) and the experimental results. With a holocamera, both a photographic image on a plane shown in figures 8(c) and a holographically reconstructed image on another plane shown in figure 8(d) are obtained from incoherent hologram(s) and red-, green-, and blue-LED light sources with nominal wavelengths of 625, 530, and 455 nm, respectively.

A thumb-sized SSPS-IDH system has been constructed using SSPS-IDH with a diffractive optical element, and the construction of a chip-sized SSPS-IDH system is expected. In such a system, there is a problem that undesired diffraction waves appear in the reconstructed image of SSPS-IDH (e.g. the center of figure 5(d) in [134].) with a diffractive optical element owing to the wavelength dependences of phase modulation and diffraction efficiency. On the other hand, the downsizing of the system based on figure 7(g) is a problem. A thin birefringent lens such as a liquid crystal gradient refractive index lens [148] will be an effective way to make the IDH system compact.
4.1.3. Other remarks in single-shot IDH.
Not limited to SSPS-IDH, many single-shot IDH techniques have been proposed until now. As an example, single-shot incoherent holography using a Sagnac interferometer was already performed experimentally in 1966 [111]. Single-shot IDH with PSI and multiple image sensors were proposed by Lee’s group in 1997 [122]. The differences between Lee’s method and the SSPS-IDH systems in figure 7 are the wide SBWP and the requirement for the synchronization of image sensors in exposure. Considering the data-transfer bandwidth, Lee’s method has a merit of a wide SBWP and SSPS-IDH with an image sensor has the merit of high-speed motion-picture recording. As modern research evolutions, the following developments are reported. Single-shot FINCH without PSI has been actively researched, and the experimental demonstrations have been successfully performed using an iterative method [149] or a correlation method using a point-spread-function (PSF) library obtained in advance [150]. As a related work of FINCH, a single-shot single-channel off-axis IDH method termed Fourier incoherent single-channel holography was proposed and optical implementations were developed [151]. Another off-axis IDH system that adopts a Michelson interferometer and a concave mirror was also developed [152]. Single-shot COACH was developed by the inventors of COACH [153], and single-shot COACH is now implemented without the interference of two object waves through successive developments [154]. QWLSI-based single-shot IDH was developed by several research groups and applied to holographic fluorescence microscopy [125, 155] and spontaneous Raman scattering holography [117]. Highly accurate 3D microscopic measurements are seen in the publications. The use of the computational technique is also actively researched, and compressive sensing and deep learning were applied to single-shot IDH to remove the undesired-order diffraction waves in an incoherent hologram [156, 157].
4.2. CCS-IDH
CCS was initially proposed with a laser DH system [76–78]. The concepts of single-shot CCS-DH and single-shot CCS-IDH were described [158]. After the experimental demonstrations of CCS-DH and SSPS-IDH, CCS-IDH systems for multiwavelength-multiplexed full-color incoherent 3D imaging and polarization-multiplexed polarization incoherent 3D imaging were proposed and experimentally demonstrated [159–163]. Figure 9 shows the basic concept and developed optical systems of CCS-IDH. In the same manner as SSPS-IDH, CCS-IDH is usually implemented by introducing a CCS system to an IDH system as shown in figure 9(a). Physical-quantity-dependent phase shifts are introduced to an IDH system, and an incoherent object wave that contains multiple physical quantities such as amplitude, wavelength, and polarization distributions in 3D space is recorded as multidimension-multiplexed self-interference incoherent hologram(s). With the recorded phase-shifted incoherent holograms and designed physical-quantity-dependent phase shifts, the multidimensional incoherent image of an object is reconstructed through the calculation of the matrix equation of CCS and diffraction integrals to the retrieved multidimensional object waves. CCS-IDH was then extended to identify the object waves whose wavelengths are overlapped and whose wavelength bands are different. CCS-IDH that recognizes both multidimensional information and the variety of light whose wavelength bands are different is termed multidimension-multiplexed full-phase-encoding holography [164]. Various optical systems of CCS-IDH have been proposed as shown in figures 9(b)–(i). Until now, SLM(s) and a birefringent lens have been set as two-wave generators. The systems shown in figures 9(b)–(e) are designed for multiwavelength-multiplexed IDH and those in figures 9(f)–(i) are for polarization-multiplexed IDH. The wavelength-dependent polarization-sensitive phase-modulation (WPP) arrays shown in figures 9(d) and (e) have been specially designed to implement single-shot CCS-IDH. The systems shown in figures 9(f)–(i) adopt both CCS and spatial multiplexing, and are also useful for general multiple-exposure phase-shifting IDH because light-use efficiency is improved in comparison with other multiple-exposure phase-shifting IDH systems by removing a half mirror and/or polarization filters. Figure 10 shows the experimental results obtained with a full-color-multiplexed incoherent digital holographic microscopy system, which is based on figures 9(a) and (b), as an example of CCS-IDH. It is known that, by FINCH, self-interference IDH can improve the PSF in the in-plane direction in a certain depth range in incoherent 3D imaging and in general incoherent 2D imaging [126]. From the plots of figures 10(h)–(j), PSF improvement is seen in full-color IDH imaging.

5. Holographic display
3D display technologies include stereoscopic and ray-based light field methods; however, holographic displays can present the most natural 3D images because they can perform wavefront reproduction [170]. Holographic displays first prepare 3D data. This can be achieved using a 3D graphics library or a 3D camera (depth or light field camera). By simulating light waves emitted from 3D data, the complex amplitude of the object light in a hologram (complex hologram) can be calculated. SLMs for displaying holograms can generally modulate only the amplitude or phase of the incident light. Therefore, complex holograms must be converted to amplitude- or phase-only holograms. This process is known as complex amplitude encoding. SLMs include amplitude and phase modulations using liquid crystal displays (LCDs) and binary modulation SLMs such as digital mirror devices (DMDs). Several methods have been proposed for complex encoding depending on the type of SLM. In the early 90 s, Benton at MIT demonstrated the feasibility of electronic holographic displays using AOMs [171], and Hashimoto et al at Citizen demonstrated that holographic reproduction could be achieved using LCDs simultaneously [172].
Holographic displays are ideal but far from practical. A major factor hindering practical applications is the amount of information contained in holograms. 5.1. Spatial tiling techniques
The resolution of generally available SLMs is approximately 4 K, which is insufficient for holographic displays that require a large SBWP. The most intuitive way to increase the SBWP is to spatially align several SLMs. A schematic diagram of a typical spatial tiling technique is shown in figure 11. In [175], a 15-megapixel holographic display was designed by arranging five SLMs side by side, and the size of the 3D reproduced image in the horizontal parallax was only 50 mm 150 mm 50 mm, realizing an observation area of 65 mm. SLMs have unmodulated areas around them. Therefore, simply lining them up creates gaps between them. However, this should be avoided in future studies. In [175], the reproduced light can be laid down without gaps through half mirrors. A system capable of reproducing holograms of 74 600 000 pixels by arranging the SLMs in a 3 3 array was developed in 2014 [176]. The gap between SLMs can be successfully combined with several lenses to obtain a seamless color 3D reproduced image. The reproduced image size was 55.3 mm 31.1 mm and the viewing angle was 5.6°.

5.2. Time-division techniques
Increasing the SBPW through spatial tiling is effective; however, the number of SLMs that can be tiled is limited because of the complexity and size of the system. Increasing the SBPW using time-division techniques is useful in this context. Figure 12 shows a typical time-division technique.

The higher-order diffracted light generated by the periodic structure of SLM pixels is generally unwanted; however, a unique method that combines it with the time-division method to extend the viewing angle was proposed in 1999 [181]. Holographic displays using amplitude SLMs have been developed; however, conjugate light is generated in addition to the object light. The conjugate light can be successfully removed using a half-zone plate when calculating the hologram and by placing a single sideband filter on the Fourier plane of the lens when optically reproducing the hologram. However, the viewing angle is reduced. Mishina et al [181] proposed a method of alleviating the problem of reduced viewing angles in the single-sideband + half-zone plate method by time-division switching between the hologram and the spatial filter.
Time-division methods require fast SLMs, and DMDs are often used; in 2009, a method was proposed to increase the SBPW using DMDs [23]. In this method, the horizontal-only hologram is reduced horizontally to reduce the pixel pitch, and the mechanical scanner scans the reduced hologram horizontally to achieve an image size of 3.4 inches and a viewing angle of 15°. In 2014, a system was developed [182], in which the viewing zone of a hologram reproduced using a DMD was scanned laterally using a mechanical scanner. This method reduces the size of the reproduced image compared with that in [23]; however, it has been shown that the viewing angle can be expanded up to 40° [182]. A trade-off exists between the image size and the viewing angle as described in [23] and [182]. These studies require a mechanical scanner; a method was developed in 2020 to achieve a 41° viewing zone angle without the use of a mechanical scanning mirror by applying a pulsed laser while the DMD mirror is rotating [183].
In 2017, a method was developed to increase the SPBW by synchronizing the rotation of a special grating with the SLM [184]. In this study, a special grating was developed by dividing a disk into four parts and attaching vertical and horizontal gratings to each region. The size and viewing angle of horizontal-only color-reproduced images were 60 mm × 30 mm and ±18.5°, respectively. The reproduced images were observed at a refresh rate of 75 Hz.
In 2020, a viewing angle-enlarging method was proposed to synchronize multiple plane-wave light sources placed at different angles with holograms displayed on a DMD [185]. This technique exploited the fact that the Fourier spectrum shifts horizontally when holograms are illuminated by plane waves at different angles. A switchable polarization filter in the Fourier plane is used for single-sideband filtering. The filtered spectrum is converted to the spatial plane using a lens to obtain a reproduced image with an enlarged viewing angle. It was demonstrated that the viewing angle could be expanded to ±9.6 × ±6.4°. The viewing area can be further extended by introducing additional plane-wave light sources.
5.3. Combination techniques of spatial tiling and time-division methods
Approaches to efficiently increase the SBPW by combining spatial tiling and time-division methods have been proposed. In 2003, an active tiling technique was developed [186]. Active tiling comprises SLMs with fast switching, optically addressed SLMs, and replication optics systems that utilize diffractive optical elements. The holograms of the fast-switching SLM are reduced using a replication optics system and written onto a portion of the optically addressed SLM. This is repeated to realize a 20 k 5 k (100 M pixel) holographic display.
In the method proposed in 2012 [187], six SLMs are arranged in a curve, and the grating phase is displayed on another SLM. The light waves from the six SLMs are guided in different directions in a time-division manner using grating phases, resulting in a hologram equivalent to 12 2 SLMs. Each SLM must be controlled synchronously.
A holographic module, Holobrick, was developed in 2022 [188]. Holobrick can be used to construct seamless holographic displays. Holobrick comprises a DMD, a mechanical scanner, and a lens array. A DMD displays a hologram guided in a specific direction by a scanner at a high speed, and a lens is used to display a larger hologram. The combination of the time-division and spatial tiling methods enables the display of holograms with a large SPBW.
5.4. Scattering techniques
The spatial tiling and time-division methods described above reproduce large holograms spatially or temporally. Recently, holographic displays using scattering, which is based on a different principle from the spatial tiling and time-division methods, have been proposed. The first holographic display that uses scattering was proposed in 2006 [189]. It was shown that the combination of a general-resolution SLM and a high-resolution random phase plate could provide a viewing angle twice as large as that of the SLM alone. However, noise was superimposed on the reproduced image owing to the use of random phase plates. A method was introduced to reduce the noise to by the fast switching of holograms generated with different random phases. This method is computationally time-consuming because of the need to calculate the object light waves on random phase plates at high densities. In 2014, a scattering-based holographic display was proposed [190] using a wavefront recording method and scaled diffraction calculations to reduce the computational complexity, showing that a 3 cm 3 cm viewing angle of 30° could be achieved.
A wide-viewing-angle holographic display using a deformable mirror, a DMD, and a holographic diffuser for the scattering device was proposed in 2017 [191]. The modulation characteristics of the holographic diffuser need to be known in advance, and in this study, the transmission matrix [192] was used; it was shown that a reproduced image of approximately 1 cm 1 cm size could be obtained in a viewing range of 30°.
A photon sieve-based holographic display was proposed in 2019 [193]. Photon sieves are optical elements comprising multiple pinholes that are used to suppress the side lobes of a Fresnel zone plate. In [193], a special photon sieve mask was created, which corresponded one-to-one with a pinhole in each pixel of the SLM. This implies that the numbers of pinholes and pixels in the SLM are the same. By combining a 1024 768 pixel SLM with a photon sieve mask, an image size of 4 cm 3 cm and a viewing angle of 30° were achieved.
Holographic displays using scattering can increase the numerical aperture of the reproduced light. This has been shown to reduce the depth of the field and crosstalk of the 3D reproduced image in the depth direction [191, 194], and improve the resolution of the reproduced image [195].
Methods using scattering devices with random structures require the precise alignment of the SLM and scattering device and the measurement of the optical properties of the scattering device. In 2024, a holographic display using a scattering device (lens array) with a periodic structure was proposed [196]. This periodic structure facilitates alignment. This method could reproduce 3D images at a viewing angle of 30°.
5.5. Eye-tracking holographic displays
The holographic displays introduced thus far simultaneously reproduce an entire 3D scene. However, because the human pupil is small, if the number of viewers is limited, a 3D scene should only be reproduced in an area equal to the size of the human pupil. In 2009, Häussler et al [197] developed a holographic display in which the reproduced light entered only the human eye (figure 14). The pixel pitch of these holograms can be large, allowing SLMs with a pixel pitch of tens to 100 µm to be used. Eye tracking can reproduce the motion parallax. However, the SLM and the human eye must be separated by several meters. A holographic display using eye tracking was developed in 2017 with an SLM capable of displaying large complex holograms of 300 mm × 200 mm [198]. The SLM capable of displaying complex holograms was realized by using a sandwich configuration of liquid crystal layers with phase and amplitude modulations. To illuminate this large SLM, a lighting system using HOE was also developed.

5.6. 360° holographic display
A holographic display with an extremely wide viewing angle can be used to observe 360° 3D images. A 360° DMD-based holographic display was developed in 2014 [201]. In this system, the reproduced light is directed at a rotating mirror tilted at 45° to obtain a reproduced image that can be observed at 360°. In 2015, a holographic display that could be observed at 360° by rotating an off-axis Fresnel lens was developed [202]. While the system described in [201] rotates the mirror, that described in [202] rotates the Fresnel lens, facilitating rotation control. A holographic display using a conical holographic optical element was developed in 2022 [203]. Horizontal viewing angle of 140° and vertical viewing angle of 30° were experimentally confirmed. A holographic display using a concave mirror was developed in 2024 [204], achieving 360° horizontal angle and an 80° zenith angle range.
5.7. Others
Photorefractive polymers would be promising for realizing holographic displays with a large SBWP [205, 206]. Photorefractive polymers can write and erase holograms using nanosecond pulsed lasers. Blanche et al [206] successfully wrote 4 × 4 inch color holograms in 2 s. Magneto-optical SLMs (MO-SLMs) is promising SLM [207]. In 2022, a holographic display using MO-SLM with fs pulse laser was reported [208], and in 2023, a holographic display using MO-SLM with electric drive system was proposed [209]. This MO-SLM has a pixel pitch of 1 µm × 4 µm, 10 k × 5 k pixels, and a refresh rate of 0.2 Hz. Lithium niobate is also a promising recording medium for dynamic holography. In 2024, an SLM using lithium niobate was developed [210]. 200 nm pixel pitch holograms can be recorded on lithium niobate using a nonreciprocal femtosecond laser writing technique. The results demonstrated that holograms with a 200 nm pixel pitch can be recorded on lithium niobate and reproduced with a viewing angle of 120°.
A holographic display using air as a medium was proposed in 2016 [211]. Femtosecond-pulsed lasers are used to plasmaize the air and emit light. A 3D image of voxels can be reproduced in the air by generating several luminescent points in a hologram.
In holographic displays using SLMs, the SLMs and reproduced planes are generally parallel, and the depth of focus is large because the numerical aperture of the SLMs is small. Therefore, the depth spacing of a 3D image must be discretized to a large extent. The image reproduced in the depth direction is discrete. To overcome this drawback, a holographic display using frozen waves (a type of light sheet) was developed in 2023 [212]. In 2024, it was shown that image quality could be improved using neural networks [213].
5.8. Summary
Starting with the development of AOM-based holographic displays by Benton at MIT [171] and LCD-based holographic displays by Hashimoto et al at Citizen [172] in the early 1990s, efforts over the past 30 years have led to the development of methods that can physically or equivalently increase the SBWP. The image quality, reproduced image size, and viewing angle of the holographic displays were significantly improved. It may be possible for practical holographic displays to become commonplace in households in the next 30 years.
6. Microscopy with holographic light modulation
Holographic techniques have been applied to manipulate the spatial property of light waves for illumination and detection in biological fluorescence microscopy. The holographic light modulation allows us to design a PSF in light microscopy with a high degree of freedom, remarkably advancing various imaging capabilities. This approach is also referred to as ‘PSF engineering’, enabling the enhancement of spatial resolution, the selective excitation of fluorescence samples, the improvement of the accuracy of image positions, and the acceleration of the acquisition speed of 3D images (figure 15).

Most of the holographic approaches modify the intensity distribution of excitation or fluorescent light at the focus instead of using a single spot, i.e. an Airy pattern, formed in conventional microscopy. The formation of multiple focal spots at desired 3D locations inside a specimen is one of the typical examples that fully utilize a holographic technique [214–216]. An excitation beam, modulated by a hologram mask, is focused on multiple biological cells simultaneously, which can be applied to the rapid recording of calcium signals from multiple neurons in functional imaging [215] and to the on-demand stimulation of target cells in optogenetics on demand [216].
The spatial resolution in light microscopy can be enhanced with the aid of holographic techniques. In general, the spatial resolution in light microscopy largely depends on the size of the excitation PSF. Various techniques have been proposed to effectively reduce the PSF size. One of the representative and well-established approaches is stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy [217, 218], which exploits a doughnut-shaped beam (figure 15(a)), overlapped with a usual excitation focal spot. The irradiation of the doughnut-shaped STED beam with a wavelength slightly longer than that of fluorescence can induce stimulated emission for excited fluorophores, which prevents fluorescence emission in the doughnut-shaped region except for its center of the excitation spot. The co-irradiation of an STED beam with an excitation beam results in reducing the effective PSF size, which can remarkably increase the spatial resolution. The generation of a STED beam is achieved commonly by introducing a spiral phase variation on the beam cross section into an incident plane wave to create a phase singularity at the center, often referred to as a vortex beam with an orbital angular momentum [219, 220]. The doughnut-shaped STED beam enables the enhancement of the lateral spatial resolution down to the order of tens of nanometers even for excitation using a visible light source. Additionally, the enhancement of the axial spatial resolution is realized by a STED beam with a dark spot along the axial direction, produced by a concentric 0-π phase mask to cause the destructive interference at the focus (figure 15(b)). The appropriate combination of a spiral and 0-π phase mask allows super-resolution imaging for both lateral and axial directions [221]. Note that the concept of STED microscopy has now been further advanced by combining a sophisticated localization method to realize a technique termed MINSTED [222], approaching an Ångström localization accuracy [223].
Sharpening a PSF directly by modifying the amplitude, phase, and polarization of an incident excitation beam is a traditional but effective technique to readily improve the spatial resolution, particularly in laser scanning microscopy. A notable approach in this direction is the utilization of the small focal spot characteristics of a radially polarized beam under high numerical aperture (NA) conditions [67]. A radially polarized beam is capable of generating a strong longitudinal field at the focus owing to its axially symmetric polarization [224]. In principle, focusing a radially polarized beam with a thin annular-shaped amplitude mask enables reaching the central peak size of the focal spot as small as 0.36λ/NA in the limiting case of an ultimately thin annular mask [225]. Compared with conventional light microscopy, this spot size is sufficiently effective for improving spatial resolution because, in general, a conventional linearly or circularly polarized Gaussian beam produces a focal spot with a size of ∼0.6λ/NA under high NA conditions. However, a limitation of a thin annular amplitude mask is a severely low transmittance when it is inserted on the pupil plane of an objective lens. Alternatively, a multiple concentric 0-π phase mask with an appropriately designed radii can be used for a radially polarized beam (figure 15(d)) to produce a similarly small focal spot [226]. The small focal spot produced by a radially polarized beam has been applied to confocal laser scanning fluorescence microscopy [66] and two-photon excitation microscopy [227] to demonstrate the lateral spatial resolution. Importantly, the enhancement of the spatial resolution is achieved simply by focusing an excitation beam with spatially varying amplitude, phase, and polarization, implemented by various holographic light modulation techniques.
An engineered PSF based on holographic light modulation can modify not only the spatial pattern but also the propagation characteristics, which have been extensively applied to retrieve the axial information of fluorophores in localization microscopy. The intensity pattern or the lateral position of a point image modified by, for example, double-helix [228], corkscrew [229], and Airy beam [230] (see figure 15(b)) varies depending on the propagation. This propagation behavior is exploited to decode the axial position of each fluorophore. Owing to the propagation-dependent variation of the PSF pattern, the recorded image itself reflects the axial information of each fluorophore. Thus, localization microscopy with the engineered PSF attains 3D super-resolution imaging capability with a high degree of accuracy by analyzing the shape of point images [231].
The depth information based on holographic light modulation has been extracted even in laser scanning microscopy [232–234]. In general, the optical sectioning capability in confocal laser scanning or multi-photon excitation microscopy allows the acquisition of 3D volumetric images of fluorescence specimens. However, in these microscopy techniques, multiple 2D image acquisitions by changing the observation planes are necessary to construct 3D images, which, in principle, limits the acquisition speed for 3D imaging.
An approach proposed in [232] aims to overcome this limitation (figure 16). The proposed method is realized by combining two concepts, i.e. a needle-shaped excitation spot (excitation PSF) with an extended focal depth and the propagation-dependent, lateral shifting behavior for a detection PSF. Under the raster scanning of a needle excitation spot, fluorophores existing along the needle spot simultaneously emit fluorescence, whose axial information disappears when a single detector is employed. To retrieve axial information, the wavefront of the fluorescence emitted by the excited fluorophores is modulated so that the centroid of each point image on the image plane shifts laterally, according to its axial position in the objective space. For this purpose, a cubic phase modulation for fluorescence has been adopted, generating an Airy beam with a non-diffracting and self-bending propagation property [235] at the image plane. Owing to the self-bending propagation of an Airy beam, the image of the fluorescence signal converted to an Airy beam is formed at different lateral positions on the image plane depending on the axial position of an emitter. By placing an array detector oriented along the lateral-shifting direction of the Airy beam, each detection channel can distinguish the fluorescence signals emitted from the different axial positions. Thus, the conversion of fluorescence to an Airy beam, achieved through holographic light modulation, enables axially resolved fluorescence detection for needle scanning microscopy. The above-mentioned concept has been demonstrated in two-photon excitation laser scanning microscopy with a near-infrared femtosecond laser source as excitation [232]. In this demonstration, a needle-shaped spot with a focal depth of about 15 μm was used as a scanning excitation spot, and the emitted fluorescence was converted to an Airy beam using an SLM displaying a cubic phase. By this configuration, a volumetric region with a depth of about 10 μm has been successfully reconstructed as an axially resolved 3D image from a single raster scanning of the needle spot.

7. Conclusion
We have reviewed imaging and sensing techniques with holography, focusing on QPI with daily-use light, IDH, holographic display, and microscopy with holographic light modulation. As described in this review, the successive evolutions of electronic and optical devices raise the specifications of holographic measurements and display, lead to the variety of functions of holographic imaging and sensing, and extend the applicability of holography to other technologies. An SLM [237], a polarization image sensor [238], and a polarization-modulation array such as a WPP array [239] played important roles and led to significant advancements in imaging and sensing with holography. The synergy between holographic techniques and electronics and optical materials is essential for the broad use of holography in the near future. Indeed, the specifications of holographic techniques have been improved by the synergy. It is considered that developments of thin, compact, wavelength-insensitive, and strongly light-modulated optical devices, those of light and compact electronic devices with high specifications, and evolutions of the basic principle of holography techniques exploiting such optical and electronic devices are expected next for breakthroughs in holography applications.
