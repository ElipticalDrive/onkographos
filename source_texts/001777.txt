# Ray-Bundle Based X-ray Representation and Reconstruction: an Alternative to Classic Tomography on Voxelized Volumes

Yuanwei He, Dan Ruan

Abstract*—Tomography recovers internal volume from projection measurements. Formulated as inverse problems, classic computed tomography generally reconstructs attenuation property in a preset cartesian grid coordinate. While this is intuitive and convenient for digital display, such discretization leads to forward-backward projection inconsistency, and discrepancy between digital and effective resolution. We take a different perspective by considering the image volume as continuous and modelling forward projection as a hybrid continuous-todiscrete mapping from volume to detector elements, which we call "ray bundles". The ray bundle can be regarded as an unconventional heterogenous coordinate. Projections are modeled as line integrations along ray bundles in the continuous volume space and approximated by numerical integration using customized sample points. This modeling approach is conveniently supported with an implicit neural representation approach. By representing the volume as a function mapping spatial coordinates to attenuation properties and leveraging ray bundle projection, this approach reflects transmission physics and eliminates the need for explicit interpolation, intersection calculations, or matrix inversions. A novel sampling strategy is further developed to adaptively distribute points along the ray bundles, emphasizing high gradient regions to allocate computational resources to heterogenous structures and details. We call this system T-ReX to indicate Transmission Ray bundles for X-ray geometry. We validate T-ReX through comprehensive experiments across three scenarios: simulated full-fan projections with primary signal only, halffan setups with simulated scatter and noise, and an inhouse dataset with realistic acquisition conditions. These results highlight the effectiveness of T-ReX in sparse view X-ray tomography.** 

Index Terms*— X-ray imaging, implicit neural field, sparse view CT, projection synthesis, image reconstruction.** 

#### I. INTRODUCTION

-RAY is commonly used in medical imaging to penetrate a body part and capture attenuation properties to characterize anatomy [1-3]. X-ray images from sufficient number of projection angles can reconstruct the 3D spatially resolved voxel-wise attenuation property, known as Computed Tomography (CT) [4-6]. Formulated as an inverse problem, the spatial attenuation is typically parametrized with respect to a canonical cube basis (grid coordinate) and estimated by maximizing the agreement between predicted and measured X

photon at the detector [7-9]. X-ray imaging involves ionizing radiation, and dose reduction driven by As Low As Reasonably Achievable principle, which is often investigated by reducing either mAs or number of views [10, 11]. Using sparse views is also desirable in X-ray guided radiation therapy, where acquisition of concomitant monitoring X-ray images is limited by the therapy need and machine configuration.

Direct inversion-based reconstruction methods, such as filtered back projection (FBP) [12], require sufficient data. Insufficient projection views can lead to artifacts [13, 14], quantitative inaccuracy [15], and high noise [16] in reconstructed images. Optimization-based methods can incorporate regularizing priors and are solved either iteratively [17, 18] or using deep networks as optimizers [19]. While data sufficiency conditions are less clearly defined for statistical reconstruction than analytical methods, assumed data (in)consistency and the corresponding mismatched residual are mapped back to image domain via forward-backward projections [20-22]. With very sparse angular samples, there is a risk of overregularizing to compensate for the ill-posed problem. In recent years, deep learning techniques have been developed to reconstruct CT from sparse views, either with strong leant prior encoding, unrolled networks, or supervised learning with enhancements [23-25]. They typically require a significant amount of training data and are subject to the risk of creating structures not necessarily supported by data, a common phenomenon known as AI hallucination [26].

Recently, neural radiance field (NeRF) [27], a representative application of implicit neural representation (INR), offers a revolutionary way to reconstruct 3D scene from multi-view 2D images. INR parameterizes the scene in the continuous space with networks, and can be combined naturally with various imaging systems reflecting the acquisition physics. This advance in computer vision has motivated developments and applications in X-ray imaging with sparse view driven novel view synthesis [28, 29] and CT reconstruction [30-32]. However, these methods omit the sampling flexibility in the intrinsic continuous attenuation field or address sampling attention to improper regions, making the reconstructed results from sparse-view measurements degraded by artefacts.

The flexible sampling properties of a continuous attenuation field, along with the discretization error introduced by preset Cartesian grids remind us to consider two questions: (i) whether

In this study, we propose a physicsinspired X-ray bundle coordinate system to relate an INR model to measurements acquired at discrete detector units. In contrast to conventional ray tracing, where the image volume is first discretized into voxels and related to the attenuation path with fraction counting, the ray bundle works with rays shaped as slanted pyramid, with detector unit as its base. It is only during the implementation stage, that the attenuation along the ray is approximated numerically, accommodating adaptive spatial sampling. This flexibility enables us to further develop a novel adaptive sampling scheme to improve sensitivity to local heterogeneity. Building on this, both the forward projection matrix and the attenuation filed can be dynamically updated and optimized to approach the actual physical distribution, which is different from the classic tomographic reconstruction using a preset system matrix. Overall, T-ReX represents the reconstructed space as a completely continuous mapping, simultaneously refining the system matrix and the attenuation field during optimization. This setup fully leverages measurement information, enabling high-quality reconstruction from sparse views. Fig. 1 provides an overview of the proposed T-ReX framework.

#### II. RELATED WORKS

#### A.Traditional Reconstruction Method

Traditional CT reconstruction methods can be categorized into analytical and iterative algorithms. Analytical reconstruction methods like FBP [12] and its variant, Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) [33], are commonly used in commercial cone-beam scanners due to their computational efficiency and implementation simplicity. These methods reconstruct a discretized 3D volume by back-projecting filtered projection data along the original ray paths. The choice of filters and reconstruction kernels allows a balance between spatial resolution and noise suppression [34], making them effective

for reconstructions with complete projection data. However, they are inherently sensitive to artifacts and noise, resulting reconstruction inaccuracies in sparse-view acquisitions. To address these limitations, iterative reconstruction methods have been widely developed, utilizing likelihood-based modeling, prior knowledge, and optimization techniques to enhance image quality. Unlike analytical approaches, which perform backprojection with the filter operation, iterative methods refine the reconstructed volume by repeatedly updating voxel values to minimize the residual between measured and forward-projected synthetic data. Common techniques include algebraic reconstruction techniques [35-37] and statistical methods [38, 39], which can further incorporate noise models and sparsity priors as constraints to improve performance in sparse view reconstruction. Total variation [22, 40-42], low-rank [43, 44], dictionary learning [45, 46], and ℓ0 -norm [47, 48] are typical regularization terms to constrain the solution space and improve reconstruction accuracy. Nevertheless, achieving an optimal balance between regularization strength and image quality remains challenges, particularly under conditions of ultrasparse angular sampling.

#### B.Deep Learning based Reconstruction Method

Deep learning-based tomography reconstruction has rapidly evolved to improve the reconstruction performance, which falls into three categories: post-processing, iterative, and end to end reconstruction algorithms. Post-processing algorithms are widely used to map the sinogram/image to a high quality one. SinoConvNet utilizes convolutional neural network to interpolate and complete missing sinogram data, improving downstream reconstructions [49]. FBPConvNet [23], DenseNet deconvolution network [50], and residual encoder-decoder convolutional neural network [51] directly operate in image domain which is analytically reconstructed. More advanced architectures, including the dual-domain residual-based optimization network [19], hybrid-domain convolutional neural network [52], and multi-domain integrative swin transformer network [53], jointly optimize results in both the projection and image domains. However, these methods often fail to explicitly enforce consistency between measured projections and the

Learned experts' assessmentbased reconstruction network [24], iterative residual optimization network [54], multi-scale learn iterative approach [55], and cycle-domain geometric-integrated denoising diffusion probabilistic model [56] are the representative methods to simultaneously optimize both reconstruction quality and data consistency. End-to-end methods, such as learned invertible primal-dual iterative scheme [57] and deep efficient end-to-end reconstruction network [58], directly learn the mapping from projection domain to image domain. Despite their promise, these methods typically discretize the image space onto a fixed grid and rely on a preset system matrix for reconstruction.

#### C.Implicit Neural Representation based Reconstruction Method

Recent advances in INR have introduced a novel framework for CT reconstruction by learning a mapping from spatial coordinates to attenuation coefficients. This approach parameterizes the continuous attenuation field in neural networks, enabling more flexible and high-resolution reconstructions. Neural adaptive tomography (NeAT) [59] is a hybrid approach to bridge traditional voxel-based methods and continuous representations. NeAT represents the scene as octrees, embedding neural features in each leaf node to adaptively capture spatial detail. Other methods, such as implicit neural representation learning with prior embedding [30] and InTomo [60], utilize Fourier encoding to map coordinates into a high frequency space, enhancing the neural network's ability to capture fine structures. Both of these two frameworks built on the operator discretization library [61], employing preset sampling points along each ray. To improve the computational efficiency, SNAF [31], neural attenuation fields (NAF) [32], structure-aware X-ray neural radiodensity fields (SAX-NeRF) [62], and self-prior embedding neural representation (Spener) [63] incorporate hash encoding [64]. While effective for acceleration, hash encoding is not a mathematically continuous mapping, which introduces discontinuities and limits the model's ability to produce smooth outputs [65]. Additionally, potential hash collisions and the inherently localized nature of hash-based storage present challenges for handling missing or noisy data, and can compromise reconstruction quality to lower resolutions. In terms of sampling strategies, SNAF and Spener evenly sample points along each ray, whereas NAF and SAX-NeRF adopt stratified sampling. More recently, diffusion prior driven neural representation [66] is proposed by combining a pre-trained score-based diffusion [67] model with INR, but it still employs a fixed-interval point sampling strategy along each ray, inherently tied to a preset system matrix.

#### III. METHODS

# A.Classic Tomography and Room for Improvement

Under ideal conditions, the attenuation field of interest �� relates to the X-ray measurement via the Beer-Lambert Law [68]:

$$I_i = I_0 \exp\left(-\int_{r_i} \mu(\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{x}\right), i = 1, \dots, M, \quad (1)$$

where ��0 and ���� are the incidence and detected photon intensities at the ��th detector element, respectively. �� is the 3D spatial coordinate in the image domain. It is common to work with the problem in the log-transformed domain:

$$p_i := \log(I_0/I_i) = \int_{r_i} \mu(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}, \quad i = 1, \dots, M. \quad (2)$$

The operation of integrating along photon ray is known as "forward projection".

In classic tomography, the attenuation field is first parameterized with respect to a predetermined basis, typically the voxel-basis on a cartesian grid, and reconstruction is performed for the corresponding coefficients. With the voxel basis, it can be interpreted as a piecewise constant approximation to the underlying continuous field, where attenuation intensity within each voxel is constant. The corresponding vectorized coefficients �� are naturally compatible with digital display and are often used interchangeably with the continuous field ��. Correspondingly, the measurements are modeled as a discrete linear system:

$$p = A\mu, \quad (3)$$

where �� is the concatenation of ���� in (2) across different projection angles. �� is a predefined system matrix that describes the contribution of each voxel to the attenuation of the ray connecting source and the specific detector element.

This setup is the foundation for all conventional tomographic reconstruction, both analytical and statistical. However, there are intrinsic limitations with this formulation. First, the forward projection matrix needs to be defined with respect to preconfigured voxel resolution. It is quite common that a default resolution in the sub-mm to mm range is chosen, but then adjust kernel choice (e.g. in FDK [33]) or regularization strength to change the effective resolution to obtain a reasonable system conditioning. This subtle balance is nontrivial and can often leads to oversmoothing of details or insufficient noise suppression. Second, the voxel-wise constant assumption induces model mismatch and geometry inconsistency across different projection views, resulting in aliasing artefacts [69, 70]. Discontinuity at the voxel edge introduces artificial high-frequency components into the image [71]. Third, uniform grid is an ineffective use of representation for locally homogenous regions, causing a waste on computational source. Fourth, it is technically difficult to choose the best forward-backward projectors and interpolation operators [72-75]. Specifically, it is known that reconstruction with forward/back projector pair using voxel-based projectors produces ring artefacts [76, 77]. Unmatched pair with a raydriven forward projector and a pixel-driven back projector can alleviate this artefact but at the cost of a possible incoherent



solution by violating the adjoint relations of the projectors [71, 78, 79]. Finally, it is important to understand footprint and interaction patterns in these discrete models, which could be computationally expensive. While approximating footprint with separable functions can lead to speed up, footprint projector is typically nontrivial and could require additional interpolation when the coordinate is "nonstandard" [80, 81].

#### B.Proposed Ray Bundle System

These observations motivate us to consider an alternative representation using a novel coordinate system that (i) can naturally reflect the continuous attenuation distribution without artificial frequency change, (ii) offers flexibility to allocate intensity characterization budget efficiently, (iii) captures line integration with no or minimal interpolation, and (iv) is compatible to wide configurations of scanning geometry and views without complex footprint analysis.

We revisit the scanning geometry and define the x-ray bundle by casting rays from the X-ray source to the center of each detector element. The ray corresponding to the ��th detector element can be expressed as:

$$\mathbf{r}_i(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}_i, t \in [0, 1], \quad (4)$$

where �� is the location of X-ray source, ���� is the vector pointing from the X-ray source towards the ��th detector element, and �� is the traversing parameter.

Given the scanning position �� , all rays within that cone bundle are determined, and it is convenient to use �� to index the view and detector element �� to index each specific ray within the view. A ray bundle is the collection of rays:

$$\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{r}_{v,i}\}. \quad (5)$$

sampling strategies along the ray bundle and varying scanning geometries, allows it to dynamically adapt. Any sampling scheme on the ray bundle can be considered as having heterogenous trapezoid prisms at each point to form a basis group across all projection angles, depending on the ray geometry, the spatial location under analysis, and the sampling pattern. By design, projections of each basis group match the corresponding detector elements perfectly across different views, and completely circumvents the need for either rayvoxel tracing or footprint analysis. Besides, the overlapped basis groups densely cover the image space, offering possibilities to represent the reconstructed space in a more realistic manner. $$p = \mathcal{A}\mu. \quad (6)$$

This formulation dynamically updates both the system matrix and the attenuation coefficients, ensuring consistency between the reconstruction and the real anatomical distribution up to sampling-induced approximations along rays and enabling the optimization of a more realistic representation of the patient's internal structure.

### C. Optimization and Adaptive Sampling

Motivated by INR development that biased towards continuous and smooth neural reconstruction [82], we adopt a Multilayer Perception (MLP) [83] to model the continuous attenuation field. The forward model follows goodness-of-fit criterion:

$$\arg \min_{\Theta} \mathbb{E} \|P_{v,i} \circ \Phi_{\Theta} - p_{v,i}\|_2^2, \quad (9)$$

where expectation  $\mathbb{E}$  is taken over the ray bundle  $\mathcal{B}$ .

A differential Fourier feature positional encoding scheme is further employed prior to network input to enhance the

modeling of high-frequency components [84]. This setup maintains the continuous nature of the mapping from spatial coordinates to the attenuation field.

Once the network is trained by minimizing the loss function in (9), the continuous attenuation field is implicitly obtained. Tomographic images can be generated by querying the network at grid points for visualization, and projections at any arbitrary angle can be predicted by  $P_v \circ \Phi_{\Theta}$ .

Given the flexibility of both the ray bundle and the network, we propose to perform progressive sampling in two stages: an initial stage to “scout” the intensity variation in the image space, and a refinement stage where samples are generated adaptively with a distributional preference to heterogeneous regions. Fig. 3 shows the proposed two-step sampling process during each iterative optimization.

In each iteration, we first use a stratified sampling strategy, where we partition  $[t_n, t_f]$  into  $N_c$  even segments and then draw one sample uniformly at random within each segment:

$$T_c \sim \mathcal{U} \left[ t; t_n + \frac{i-1}{N_c} (t_f - t_n), t_n + \frac{i}{N_c} (t_f - t_n) \right]. \quad (10)$$

This stratified sampling strategy ensures balanced volume coverage to evolve the MLP mapping for volume characterization. We use this initial estimated attenuation to guide the distribution of  $N_f$  new samples along each ray:

$$T_f \sim \eta \mathcal{U}[t; t_n, t_f] + (1 - \eta) \mathcal{W}[t; t_n, t_f], \quad (11)$$

where  $0 \leq \eta \leq 1$  determines the mixing weight between the uniform distribution  $\mathcal{U}$  and heterogeneity preference distribution  $\mathcal{W}$  in interval  $[t_n, t_f]$ . Here, we design to be proportional to local gradient estimation, allocating denser sampling probability to locally heterogeneous regions:

$$\mathcal{W}[t; t_n, t_f] = \frac{\left\| \partial_{\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{v},i} \mu}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{v},i}(t)) \right\|}{\int_{t_n}^{t_f} \left\| (\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{v},i}(t)) \right\| dt}. \quad (12)$$

Note that the mixture distribution in (11) is different than that used in the NeRF scheme [27] where stratified sampling scheme via a union operation. The proposed mixture method uses a unified statistical approach to allocate computational resources to the heterogeneous while providing coverage guarantees with the uniform component. #### IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

We set the 0° azimuthal angle corresponding to the sagittal view at the left side of the body, increasing counter-clockwise according to the right-hand rule, with the axis along anteriorposterior direction. A 9-layer MLP network with a width of 256 neural nodes were used for continuous attenuation representation, where each fully connected layer was followed by the ReLU activation function. A skip connection concatenated the input to the fifth layer's activation. The network was trained by the Adam optimizer [85] with a learning rate that starts at 5 × 10−4 and steps down to 5 × 10−5 . The optimization for a single volume took 200k iterations for training. At each optimization iteration, we randomly sampled a batch of X-rays from the set of measurements, with a batch size 1024, and then followed the adaptive volume sampling strategy with ���� = 256 and ���� = 512 samples along each ray. For reconstructions from full-fan and half-fan settings, contribution weight �� for the uniform probability density function (PDF) was respectively set to be 0.2 and 0.4 in the mixture distribution model. Goodness of fit loss in (9) was optimized to drive the evolution of ΦΘ.

To evaluate the performance of T-ReX, we test it in three scenarios: reconstructions from simulated full-fan projections with only primary signal, half-fan setup with simulated scatter and noise, and our in-house dataset with realistic scatter and noise from calibrated open cone beam geometry. The ground truth (GT) for SPARE dataset is the reference FDK reconstruction from complete projections with only primary signal. For the simulated full-fan and the in-house data, the GT is the corresponding high-quality fan-beam data. The reconstructed results were compared across the following methods: GT, FDK [33], ASD-POCS [40], InTomo [60], NAF [32], SAX-NeRF [62], "T-ReX w/ E" using evenly sampling strategy along the ray as the ablation study, and the proposed T-ReX with adaptive sampling along the ray bundle named as "T-

ReX". FDK and ASD-POCS are classic voxel discretized reconstruction algorithms. FDK directly reconstructs the volume using the backward projection, while ASD-POCS employs an unmatched forward-backward projector pair. For the back projection, both methods back project detector values to voxels using FDK weights. The Siddon ray-tracing algorithm [86] with optimized operations [87] is implemented as the forward projector in ASD-POCS. Being same as our proposed T-ReX system, the rest compared models parameterize the continuous attenuation field with implicit neural representation, which utilize forward projection only without explicit back projection for reconstruction. NAF and SAX-NeRF use dynamic system matrix which adopt a stratified sampling strategy for the forward projector. InTomo utilizes predefined system matrices, only iteratively updating the attenuation coefficients. All these methods are different from our proposed T-ReX which progressively and jointly optimizes the system matrix and the continuous attenuation field approaching to the realistic physical distribution. Results from FDK and ASD-POCS are reconstructed using a preset discrete grid. The same grid is used to query the networks in InTomo, NAF, SAX-NeRF, T-ReX w/ E, and T-ReX for display and a fair comparison with conventional discretized reconstruction algorithms.

The experiments were performed in PyTorch framework on a single RTX 4090 GPU. Reconstructed CT volume and synthesized 360-view projections were quantitatively assessed with structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [88] and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR).

#### V.DISCUSSIONS AND CONCLUSION

In this work, we propose a physics-inspired ray-bundle based "coordinate" system that accommodates diverse scanning geometries and replaces the commonly used voxel basis. As a hybrid mapping that is continuous with respect to image space and discrete according to detector elements, it works seamlessly with INR to model the underlying continuous attenuating anatomy. Fourier positional encoding is incorporated to improve the detail sensitivity of the MLP without sacrificing its global smoothness.

We further introduce a novel adaptive sampling strategy on the ray bundle to progressively refine the sample points, prioritizing regions with greater heterogeneity. This sampling strategy is conceptually similar to entropy encoding, aiming for an efficient compressive representation of anatomical structure. It differs from importance sampling in NeRF, where denser samples are placed in visually prominent regions for numerical integration. This choice is based on the different physics of Xray versus visible light: the former is fully penetrating, with the attenuation value invariant to view angles, while the latter has a short penetration depth (long wavelength), with clear local obstruction/transmission, and dependency of appearance on view angles. This distinction also results in our simpler network structures without the conditional path.

The proposed ray bundle formulation naturally supports high quality novel projection synthesis and can provide implicit anatomical reconstruction from insufficient measurements on an as-needed basis for rendering. Besides, since the MLP is defined for any real valued spatial coordinate ��, reconstructed images of arbitrary resolution can be obtained by querying the network with a high-density grid. However, the effective resolution is constrained by the frequency content of the object being imaged, the band capacity of the reconstruction network, and the physical limitations of the imaging system (source, detector properties, the system magnification, and the sampling effects). In addition, the network allows querying spatial locations beyond the original training region, providing "interpolation" and "extrapolation" without additional effort.

The flexibility of sampling along ray bundles in a continuous image space allows dynamic updates of both the system matrix and the attenuation field to better match the actual anatomical geometry of each patient. This contrasts with the classic tomographic reconstruction methods, which rely on a fixed, predefined system matrix. This characteristic provides our system with the ability to minimize both deterministic and random errors, and decouple constraints on the system matrix and the attenuation field.



We have used an idealized formulation without explicit scatter/noise modeling and ignored the shape and finite source size, polychromatic X-ray, or its associated beam hardening. These simplifications, though present model mismatch, were consistently used across all benchmarks, ensuring a fair comparison. We made these choices to reveal the fundamental properties of the proposed methodology and components. Some of these limitations can be readily addressed by extending the current work. An example would be noise modeling, which can be accommodated with a different norm or a log-likelihood cost in (9).

To verify the performance of the proposed T-ReX system, we compared it against two classic spatial discretized reconstruction techniques, FDK and ASD-POCS, and three implicit representation methods, InTomo, NAF, SAX-NeRF on simulated full-fan data with only primary signals, half-fan SPARE data with simulated scatter and noise, and in-house collected data. Notably, InTomo, NAF, and SAX-NeRF originally evaluated in full-fan synthetic settings, which exhibit significant performance drops under the more challenging and realistic half-fan scanning geometry. In contrast, T-ReX

- consistently outperformed all baselines both qualitatively and quantitatively across all evaluation settings, and also demonstrated robustness to real-world imperfections. It is worth noting that there is neither explicit regularization in the loss function for structure representation, nor any morphological constraints imposed on the structures. All improvements in experimental results solely reflect the advantage of the intrinsic physics-enhanced continuous representation, the presented adaptive sampling strategy, and the feature encoding, in being consistent and flexible in scale. Despite its reconstruction quality, the current implementation of T-ReX incurs a high computational cost, requiring several hours to reconstruct a single volume. This overhead can be partly alleviated by employing alternating optimization between the system matrix and the attenuation field to accelerate convergence. Additional efficiency improvements remain an important direction for future work. At present, the model assumes a fixed number of partitions along each ray to represent the traversed tissues. In practice, the actual anatomical complexity varies across ray paths, resulting in different levels of information entropy. A more effective allocation of sample points could be achieved by adapting the number of samples per ray, refining sampling strategy prioritizing diagnostically relevant regions, such as the lung cavity, and incorporating more expressive representation kernels to better capture fine structures like pulmonary vessels.
