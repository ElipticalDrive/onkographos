ABSTRACT
A new measure of the accuracy of an isosurface produced
by marching isosurfacing algorithms (e.g., Marching Cubes)
is described. This metric has a low computational cost yet
well-approximates a computationally-expensive, common assessment of produced isosurface accuracy (i.e., the spatial
discrepancy between the produced isosurface and the isosurface of trilinear interpolation). Experiments of the method’s
quality and computational performance are also exhibited,
including comparisons against a competing approximation
metric previously described.
Keywords
Isosurfaces, Marching Cubes, Trilinear Interpolant, Volumetric Data
1.
INTRODUCTION
Isosurface extraction, which involves determining surfaces
of constant value in volumetric datasets, is an important visualization technique that can aid a variety of analyses. The
best known isosurfacing algorithm is the Marching Cubes
(MC) [8]. MC produces and renders a triangular mesh representation of an isosurface given a scalar, rectilinear volumetric dataset. (Our focus here is such data.) Over the
years, many improvements on MC’s topological correctness
and speed have been described [10] [1].
Trilinear interpolation is a common mathematical model
for data variation within scalar rectilinear dataset cells. As
such, a common way to assess accuracy of isosurfacing algorithms is by the closeness of the produced isosurface and the
isosurface given by trilinear interpolation. Estimating the
quantitative accuracy of marching isosurfacing algorithms
can aid researchers to develop new, improved isosurfacing
algorithms and help isosurface visualization users to choose
a suitable isosurfacing algorithm according to the requirements of their problem, such as the level of quality, computational cost, etc.

The quantitative accuracy of marching isosurfacing algorithms can be estimated by the Volumetric Divergence Metric [14]. This metric is related to the total volume between
the 3D regions that are enclosed by a produced mesh and
the 3D regions enclosed by the trilinear interpolation isosurface (i.e., it is related to the difference between these
two enclosures). A method in [14] describes how to calculate the volumetric divergence metric. The metric estimates
the volumetric divergence for the isosurface with high accuracy. However, since the method requires recursive interpolation until a condition is satisfied as well as many triangle
and cube intersection tests, its computational cost is very
high (i.e., its execution time is very long). For example,
the time for the method to compute the volumetric divergence metric with recursive depth 7 was about 10000 times
longer than the time to extract the isosurface by MC, when
a 256 × 256 × 44 volumetric dataset was used.
In this paper, we propose a new metric that can be determined with straightforward, fast calculation. It also works
well for topological cases of MC that occur frequently in
real datasets. The major advantage of the new metric over
the Volumetric Divergence Metric is its fast computational
speed. In fact, its computation time is less than the time
for MC to extract its standard isosurface. The new metric
focuses on detecting the cubes with high volumetric divergence. Those cubes are the ones needing additional refinement. The new metric is calculated before MC is applied
to a cube. If it predicts that the cube has high volumetric divergence, a refined description of the isosurface will be
used for the cube. One other advantage is that using the
metric to refine an isosurface does not increase the number
of triangles in the produced mesh by much, since only the
cubes with high volumetric divergence will be refined.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, background and related work are introduced. In Section 3, the
new metric is described. In Section 4, experimental results
are presented. Conclusions are in Section 5.
2.
BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
2.1
Trilinear Interpolation
A scalar rectilinear volume is a discrete representation of
a 3D region: data values are only given at grid points. The
data in the interior of the grid cells defined by the lattice
(i.e., the cubes) is commonly considered to be well-modeled
by trilinear interpolation function G on the 8 vertex values
of the cube, where G can be expressed as:
G(x, y, z) = G000 (1 − x)(1 − y)(1 − z)
+ G100 x(1 − y)(1 − z) + G010 (1 − x)y(1 − z)
+ G110 xy(1 − z) + G001 (1 − x)(1 − y)z
+ G101 x(1 − y)z + G011 (1 − x)yz + G111 xyz,
(1)
where x, y, and z represent a location in a cube in local
coordinates, 0 ≤ x, y, z ≤ 1, and Gijk denotes the value
at a cube corner in local coordinates, i, j, k. Since local
coordinates vary from 0 to 1, the i, j, and k values are 0 or
1.
It is also useful to write G in the standard monomial form
G(x, y, z) = axyz + bxy + cyz + dzx
+ ex + f y + gz + h,
(2)
with a = −G000 + G100 + G010 − G110 + G001 − G101 − G011 +
G111 , b = G000 − G100 − G010 + G110 , and so on, and to
define the isosurface of interest as
ST ≡ {(x, y, z) : G(x, y, z) = α},
(3)
where α is the constant value (isovalue) of the isosurface.
2.2
Marching Isosurfacing Algorithms
The original Marching Cubes processes one grid cell (cube)
in the volumetric dataset at a time in a scan-line (marching)
fashion. During marching, cube vertices are compared with
the isovalue. Vertices with values less than the isovalue are
labeled as outside; other vertices are labeled as inside. Since
there are 8 vertices in a cube and each vertex has 2 possible labelings, there are totally 256 possible cases of cube
labelings. With rotational and reflective symmetry, the 256
configurations can be reduced to a set of 15 base cases. The
isosurface intersects any cube edges that have two opposite
labelings. The set of intersection points is triangulated by
MC to yield an approximation to the isosurface within the
cube. The triangulations are defined by connection topologies set up in advance for the base cases.
Since MC was first developed in 1987, many efforts have
been made to improve its quality of surface representation
and its performance on large data sets. Nielson and Hamann
[12] pointed out that the original MC may produce an isosurface mesh that has “holes” due to its face ambiguity problem (i.e., the common face of adjoining cells can have mesh
vertices that are linked differently on one side than the
other). One way to solve the face ambiguity problem is to
use Marching Cubes with 23 base topologies (MC23). Nielson [11] has described these 23 base cases. In MC23, only rotational symmetry is exploited, which reduces the 256 cases
to 23 base cases. MC23 can produce a watertight isosurface without a “hole”. Figure 1 illustrates the connection
topologies for the MC23 base cases.
Another way to solve the problem of “holes” is to use the
asymptotic decider (AD), which was developed by Nielson
and Hamann [12]. AD compares the isovalue and saddle
point value of the bilinear interpolant on a cube face to dictate the edge connections on an ambiguous face. It modifies the original MC for the base cases that have ambiguous
faces. For each of those cases, additional triangulations are
added according to the connection patterns on the ambiguous faces determined by AD.
Figure 1: Illustration of connection topologies for
the 23 base cases of MC23 (adapted from [11])
Later, researchers recognized that the original MC also
has an internal ambiguity problem [9, 2]. In the interior of
the cube, the linking topologies produced by MC may differ
from the trilinear interpolation isosurface [2]. For example,
when the trilinear interpolation isosurface inside a cube is
a single “tunnel” piece, topologically equivalent to a cylinder, MC may produce two separate pieces using two triangles. Nielson [11] has proposed an improved version of MC
that can produce a triangular mesh isosurface that preserves
the same connectivity/separation of vertices as given by the
trilinear interpolation isosurface. These isosurface meshes
resolve both face and internal ambiguities. Nielson gave a
formal proof that the set of cases given in his paper are the
only ones possible and that they are topologically correct
[11]. Carr and Max [11] presented a more straightforward
proof of this situation based on a variation of the Dividing
Cubes algorithm.
Another approach that can resolve face and internal ambiguity is Lopes and Brodlie’s method [7]. It allows creation
of a topologically correct triangular mesh isosurface within
the cube. It uses some mesh vertices that are in the interior
of the cube and lie on the trilinear interpolation isosurface.
Using these mesh vertices also improves the accuracy of the
produced mesh isosurface. The drawback of this method is
that its produced mesh has an increased number of facets.
2.3
Accuracy Measurements
Due to the complex shape of a trilinear interpolation isosurface, measuring the accuracy of the isosurface mesh produced by an isosurfacing method is challenging. Many researchers use visual examination to check isosurface mesh
accuracy [5, 7]. Often an extracted mesh isosurface is compared against a very high resolution triangular mesh rather
than the actual trilinear interpolation isosurface (e.g., [5, 7]).
When a high resolution mesh is used as the benchmark, measures related to the closeness of two meshes can be applied
to determine the accuracy of the extracted mesh isosurface.
Using global geometric metrics, such as the surface area of
the isosurface or the volume inside the isosurface [13], are
other ways mesh closeness has been estimated. However,
such metrics don’t well-measure local deviation of meshes
[14]. Another common way to measure mesh closeness is to
use distance-based metrics, such as quadric error metrics [4],
Hausdorff distance [6], etc. However, such metrics usually

2.4
Volumetric Divergence Metric
The volumetric divergence is defined between two closed
surfaces. The regions inside and outside some surface A is
denoted as Ain and Aout , respectively. The volumetric divergence between two closed surfaces A and B is the volume
of region RD , defined as
RD = (Ain ∩ Bout ) ∪ (Aout ∩ Bin ) .
(4)
Figure 2 illustrates volumetric divergence using a 2D example. The first region is the yellow region in Figure 2 (a).
The second region is the blue region in Figure 2 (b). The 2D
equivalent of the volumetric divergence between the two is
the red region in Figure 2 (c). It is obvious that the smaller
the volumetric divergence is, the closer the two surfaces are.
Thus, volumetric divergence is one of the natural metrics to
measure the closeness between two surfaces.
The Volumetric Divergence Computation (VDC) method
was introduced in [14] to estimate volumetric divergence.
The VDC first recursively and evenly subdivides a cube into
8 subcubes, repeating until a certain subdivision depth is
reached. Then, each subcube’s contribution to the volumetric divergence is determined. The sum of the contributions
of all subcubes yields the volumetric divergence metric value.

There is little need to refine the mesh for it. We can also

we pick a new point along the median of the triangle from

the triangle and the trilinear interpolation isosurface meet
at three vertices of the triangle, we can always find an intersection point between some triangle normal half line and
the trilinear interpolation isosurface at some point of this

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
4.1
Study of MC23 Cases
A set of experiments was done to determine if the new
metric approximates the volumetric divergence metric well,
especially for the base cases of MC23 that occur frequently
in real datasets. We did this using Monte Carlo tests for
each base case of MC23. For each base case, we randomly
generated 100 instances. In these tests, since the datasets
were byte-formatted for our study, the “outside” labeled vertices were randomly generated in the range of [0, α); the “inside” labeled vertices were randomly generated in the range
of (α, 255]. For each instance, we computed its volumetric
divergence metric value, MD , and TVD predictor value, PT .
Then, we calculated the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient r of these two sets of values. If r is close to
+1, then it shows MD and PT have a strong positive linear
correlation. Positive values of r indicate that as values for
MD increase, values for PT also increase. Isovalues 50, 128,
and 180 were tested. In base cases 0 and 22 of MC23, all
vertices have the same labels, so the isosurface doesn’t intersect cubes that are either of these base cases. Thus, these
cases have a correlation coefficient of 0. The results for all
base cases are shown in Table 1.
From the results in Table 1, we can see that the MD and
PT have a strong positive linear correlation for some base
cases (close to +1 ), such as base cases 1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 17, 20,
21, etc. For some other base cases, such as cases 6, 11, and
14, the linear correlation between the two types of metrics is
positive but weak, at least for some isovalues. This is due to
the very complex shape of trilinear interpolation isosurface
and the internal ambiguity problem of MC23.

4.3
Synthetic Dataset Experiments
We also tested the new metric on a synthetic dataset with
dimensions 20 × 20 × 20. It used byte-formatted data whose
range is [0, 256). In the volume, the value of a spatial point is
proportional to its distance from the center of the volume.
Thus, any isosurface in the volume is a sphere. Since the
VDC method can calculate the highly accurate volumetric
divergence for the isosurface by using a high subdivision
depth, we used the subdivision depth 10 in the method to
generate standard volumetric divergence values. We called
the metric VD10. Then, we estimates the errors of the TVD
predictor and the volumetric divergence metric calculated
with a lower subdivision depth 7 (VD7) by comparing them
with VD10 values. Three isovalues, 40.5, 70.5, and 100.5,

4.4
Performance Experiments
We also did a set of experiments to compare the speed of
calculating the TVD predictor versus the volumetric divergence metric. As mentioned before, the method to compute
volumetric divergence metric recursively subdivides the cube
into 8 subcubes until a certain subdivision depth is reached.
The subdivision depth of the final cubes determines the execution time of the method. Here, we used the frog dataset
mentioned above. The time to calculate the volumetric divergence for the whole dataset with subdivision depths 3,
4, 5, 6, and 7 are 27.8, 76.5, 272.6, 1012.8, 3961.2 seconds,
respectively. On the other hand, the time to calculate the
TVD predictor is only 0.35 seconds. The time for MC23
to produce an isosurface mesh is 0.47 seconds. This set of
experiments shows that calculating the TVD predictor is
much faster than calculating volumetric divergence metric.
However, volumetric divergence time does include subdivision computation that would need to be done if TVD was
used in isosurfacing.
5.
CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This paper has introduced a new metric to measure the
accuracy of the isosurfaces extracted by marching isosurfacing algorithms. Compared with the existing volumetric
divergence metric, the new metric can well approximate the
volumetric divergence metric for base cases of marching isosurfacing algorithms with high frequency of occurrence. It
also works well for real volumetric datasets. The new metric also has a lower computational cost than the volumetric
divergence metric. Further research work could result in
new marching isosurfacing algorithms with the help of this
metric. Such algorithms could use this new metric to produce refined triangulations from the standard ones in certain
cubes needing refinement.
