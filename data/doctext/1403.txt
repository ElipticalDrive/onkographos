Abstract
The texture-based volume rendering is a memory-intensive algorithm. Its performance relies heavily on the performance of the texture cache. However, most existing texture-based volume rendering methods blindly map computational resources to texture memory and result in incoherent memory access patterns, causing low cache hit rates in certain cases. The distance between samples taken by threads of an atomic scheduling unit (e.g. a warp of 32 threads in CUDA) of the GPU is a crucial factor that affects the texture cache performance. Based on this fact, we present a new sampling strategy, called Warp Marching, for the ray-casting algorithm of texture-based volume rendering. The effects of different sample organizations and different thread-pixel mappings in the ray-casting algorithm are thoroughly analyzed. Also, a pipeline manner color blending approach is introduced and the power of warp-level GPU operations is leveraged to improve the efficiency of parallel executions on the GPU. In addition, the rendering performance of the Warp Marching is view-independent, and it outperforms existing empty space skipping techniques in scenarios that need to render large dynamic volumes in a low resolution image. Through a series of micro-benchmarking and real-life data experiments, we rigorously analyze our sampling strategies and demonstrate significant performance enhancements over existing sampling methods.

1. Introduction
Scientific simulation and medical imaging are two of the most common sources of volume datasets. With the increasing ambitions and the advancing data acquisition techniques in these domains, the size of volume datasets is growing at an unprecedented rate. Volume datasets are usually represented by numerous rigid grid cells (voxels) with scalar (e.g. density, temperature) or vector (e.g. velocity, gradient) values on the vertices of each cell. To visualize a volume, interpolation among values from the discrete vertices is the most frequent operation. The huge amount of interpolations make volume visualization a memory-intensive task.
Over the last decade, GPUs have become the most favorable hardware for volume rendering, due to their massively parallel architecture and increasingly programmable model (our explanations in this paper use NVIDIA GPUs and CUDA concepts NVIDIA, 2013a). The texture memory of a GPU is equipped with the fast hardware-accelerated interpolation feature. As a result, it becomes the best place to store volume datasets. However, the intensive access to texture memory makes it the performance bottleneck, especially when dealing with large volumes. Cache hierarchy has been designed to accelerate the data fetching operations. When requesting data from the texture memory, texture cache will be accessed first before L2 cache. In modern GPUs, a cache-hit can be hundreds of times faster than a cache-miss, as the actual device memory access is avoided. However, some texture memory access patterns are cache incoherent, thus leading to low rendering frame rates. We discuss these patterns in texture-based volume visualization algorithms in this paper and propose an optimized memory access algorithm to fully take advantage of the texture cache. Our optimization is limited to the texture cache. However, detailed L2 cache performance results are also reported to support our explanations and discussions.
The embarrassingly parallel nature of the ray-casting algorithm also makes the GPU a desirable hardware for it. On a modern GPU, tens of thousands of threads can be executed concurrently. Rays in the ray-casting algorithm can be flexibly mapped to these threads, and thus be calculated independently in parallel. Threads on a GPU are organized as grids of blocks, and blocks are further divided into atomic groups when being scheduled to the hardware components. In CUDA, 32 consecutive threads form such an atomic group, called warp. Threads in the same warp execute the same instructions and every single step of them is synchronized automatically. How warps of threads access the texture memory of GPUs has a significant consequence on the texture cache performance (NVIDIA, 2013a).
In this paper, we try to improve the texture cache performance by minimizing the memory stride inside an atomic warp of threads. To achieve this, we propose a new sampling strategy, called Warp Marching, in the ray-casting algorithm. Our strategy demonstrates a novel computation-to-core mapping and provides cache-friendly texture memory access patterns. In addition, a pipeline manner color blending, i.e. double buffer accumulation, for the new sampling strategy is proposed to enhance the GPUs’ resource utilization. The power of the advanced warp-level operations from the NVIDIA Kepler architecture is also leveraged. By changing the number of samples along different dimensions, our sampling strategy can minimize the performance variations from different viewing directions. Thus, it is a strong candidate for applications that need stable rendering frame rates during volume explorations. Empty space skipping and scalability issues of rendering large-scale static and dynamic volumes are also included in our discussions. In short, our contributions can be summarized as follows:
1.
We rigorously analyze the influence of the GPU texture cache on the ray-casting algorithm and demonstrate thorough performance results through a set of benchmarks (Sections 3 Motivation, 5 Warp shape and warp orientation, 6 3D Warp Marching).
2.
We introduce a new sampling strategy, called Warp Marching, to improve the texture cache performance in the ray-casting algorithm. A pipeline manner color blending (double buffer accumulation) and specific GPU operations (warp voting and warp shuffling) are leveraged to improve the performance (Section 4).
3.
Through thorough experimental studies, we establish that the Warp Marching algorithm can maintain a roughly constant rendering frame rate when rotating volumes around an arbitrary axis (Section 6, 7).
4.
We apply our sampling strategy to an existing high-quality volume rendering algorithm, and test with large-scale static and dynamic volumes. Empty space skipping and scalability issues have been exhaustively discussed (Section 7).
This paper is an extension of our preliminary work published in IEEE LDAV (Wang et al., 2014). We introduce GPU texture memory, warp scheduling mechanism and related work in Section 2. A benchmark is designed in Section 3 and it clearly demonstrates our motivations in developing cache-friendly sampling algorithms. The Warp Marching algorithm and its two hardware-oriented optimizations are presented in Section 4. Sections 5 Warp shape and warp orientation, 6 3D Warp Marching thoroughly analyze the performance of all possible 1D, 2D and 3D warp shapes and six warp orientations. These exhaustive analyses and detailed results have significantly extended and fulfilled the preliminary results in our previous work (Wang et al., 2014). The results of applying our sampling strategy to an existing high-quality volume rendering algorithm are shown in Section 7. This section includes another important extension to our previous work (Wang et al., 2014), which demonstrates the results when working with dynamic volumes. Empty space skipping (Parker et al., 2005) and scalability issues of large-scale volume rendering are included in the discussions. Advantages and disadvantages of different algorithms have been explicitly illustrated through comparisons in this section. Finally, we conclude the paper in Section 8.
2. Background and related work
The GPU hardware is built with multiple stream multiprocessors (SMs) and numerous GPU cores are embedded on each SM. For example, the NVIDIA GeForce GTX TITAN GPU consists of 14 SMs and each SM has 192 cores. In total, this GPU is equipped with 2688 (14 ×192) cores. The massively parallel architecture of GPUs allows tens of thousands of threads to be created and executed concurrently on the hardware. These threads are organized as grids of blocks and work in a single instruction multiple data (SIMD) mode. When mapping these threads to the GPU hardware, they are first mapped to SMs in the unit of blocks. A thread block on a SM will be further divided into thread warps. A thread warp is 32 consecutive GPU threads, and it is the atomic scheduling unit. During execution, the warp schedulers of a SM pick out available warps and map them to cores on the SM. This computation-to-core mapping affects the workload on SMs and therefore has an influence on the execution performance (Wang et al., 2015). After being launched to cores, threads in the same warp execute the same instructions in parallel and each single step of them is synchronized. If a branch happens inside the warp, some threads have to be idle to wait for others. This case wastes part of the hardware resources and leads to a low warp execution efficiency (also known as control flow efficiency NVIDIA (2013c), Wang et al. (2015)).
The texture memory of GPUs provides hardware-accelerated linear interpolations. For this reason, it has been used extensively in volume rendering. The 3D texture memory is constituted by many 2D slices and 𝑧-curves (Morton, 1966) are applied in each slice to enhance the spatial locality (Wilt, 2013), as shown in Fig. 1. If the 32 concurrent threads of a warp take samples on the same 2D slice, good texture cache performance is expected (NVIDIA, 2013a). On the contrary, taking samples across different slices will cover a big memory stride and result in low texture cache hit rates. Sugimoto et al. (2014) analyzed the 𝑧-curve patterns in 3D texture memory. Their study demonstrates that the distance between adjacent voxels along three dimensions of a 3D texture is different in memory space. The memory stride along the 𝑋 dimension is the smallest and the ratio among the 𝑋, 𝑌 and 𝑍 axis is about 1 :2 :6. To achieve better volume rendering performance, they proposed an approach to dynamically adjust the sampling direction of a warp to be parallel to the axis that has a smaller memory stride.
The first 3D texture-based implementation of the ray-casting algorithm (Levoy, 1988) dates back to the early 1990s, such as the work by Cullip and Neumann (1993) and Cabral et al. (1994). Two principal types of texture-based volume rendering methods are: (i) view-aligned approaches with 3D textures (Westermann and Ertl, 1998); and (ii) axis-aligned approaches with 2D textures (Rezk-Salama et al., 2000). The former is superior to the latter in three major aspects Engel et al. (2004), Weiskopf et al. (2004): (1) view-aligned approaches require only 1/3 the texture memory of the axis-aligned approaches; (2) axis-aligned approaches suffer from the artifacts due to image stacks switching; (3) view-aligned approaches provide more flexibilities in adjusting slice distance. However, a major drawback of the view-aligned approaches is the texture cache penalty (Engel et al., 2004), which results in view-dependent rendering performance. To address this problem, Weiskopf et al. (2004) proposed an approach that breaks 3D volumes into smaller bricks and reorganizes the bricks into different orientations. During rendering, the performance variations from different viewing angles were averaged out. Therefore, a roughly constant rendering frame rate was achieved. Their approach, however, has two major drawbacks. First, the entire volume has to be within the viewing frustum. Second, the approach requires a data pre-processing stage, which may not be feasible in certain scenarios, such as for in-situ visualizations (Ma, 2009). Deriving the best brick resolution and handling the overlap between neighboring bricks further complicate the data pre-processing.

The fundamental principle of the direct volume rendering is the volume rendering integral (Engel et al., 2004), which can be approximated with discrete algorithms that accumulate the color of a ray by samples or segments Engel et al. (2001), Lum et al. (2004). Color accumulation is done through the alpha blending operation (Porter and Duff, 1984). The associative property of this operation (Ma et al., 1993) has inspired and enabled much research in parallel algorithm designs, such as the binary swap (Ma et al., 1994) and the 2-3 swap image compositing (Yu et al., 2008). In this paper, we also use the associative property to composite samples along rays. Our composition, however, is inside a warp of threads. In addition, we propose a pipeline manner color blending, which feeds more work to each GPU thread and keeps more computing units active during the color blending.
For most volume datasets, a large portion of the volume is empty under certain transfer functions (iso-values) in semi-transparent (iso-surface) volume rendering. Empty space skipping/leaping is one of the most commonly applied optimizations to improve rendering performance Fogal et al. (2013), Scharsach (2005). The major research efforts of conducting empty space skipping rely on additional data structures, such as macrocell Parker et al. (2005), Parker et al. (1998), Wald et al. (2006), octree Ruijters and Vilanova (2006), Wilhelms and Van Gelder (1992) and implicit k d-tree Hughes and Lim (2009), Wald et al. (2005). These structures have been used extensively in both iso-surface and semi-transparent volume rendering. Numerous texture fetching operations are avoided by sacrificing some additional memory space for these structures. For static volumes, the acceleration structures only need to be constructed once before the rendering. However, for dynamic volumes, the structures have to be updated every frame.
3. Motivation
Our work is motivated by the fact that the performance of texture-based volume rendering is usually dominated by the texture cache performance. The critical factor impacting the texture cache performance is the distance between samples taken by GPU threads. Consequently, different sampling strategies with various thread organizations will lead to dramatically different rendering performances. Here, we design a simple benchmark to demonstrate the effects of two factors that influence the distance between samples.
3.1. Sampling distance related factors
Viewing direction (Volume orientation). In the existing GPU-based implementations of the ray-casting algorithm, samples taken by different threads are fitted into a plane, sampling plane, which is always perpendicular to the viewing direction. The distance between samples on this plane is affected by the viewing direction. When the direction is perpendicular to the 2D image stack, the sampling plane will be parallel to the image stack (Fig. 2, 𝑓⁡𝑎⁢𝑐⁢𝑖⁢𝑛⁢𝑔 ⁡𝑋⁢𝑌). In this case, neighboring threads take samples on the same slice and the samples are close to each other in the texture memory. On the contrary, when the viewing direction is parallel to the image stack (Fig. 2, 𝑓⁡𝑎⁢𝑐⁢𝑖⁢𝑛⁢𝑔 ⁡𝑍⁢𝑌), threads will take samples from different image slices and the samples’ memory locations will be far from each other.
Ray distance (Volume image ratio). The sampling distance on the sampling plane is also affected by the ratio between volume dimensions and projected image resolutions (Volume Image Ratio). This ratio decides the distance between neighboring rays in the ray-casting algorithm. For example, if the volume dimension is 10243 and the projected image resolution is 512 ×512, the distance between neighboring rays will be the length of two voxels (in orthographic projection). Varying projected image resolutions will result in different ray distances, as well as distinct distances between samples taken from these rays.

3.2. Benchmark design
A benchmark has been designed to demonstrate the effects of these two factors on the texture cache hit rate, as well as the rendering performance. The benchmark program is a common parallel implementation of the texture-based volume rendering on GPU, such as the NVIDIA CUDA code sample ‘volumeRender’. In the benchmark, one GPU thread is mapped to one ray of the ray-casting algorithm. The thread accumulates color and opacity values for one pixel of the final image. A warp of threads, in this case, is responsible for 32 pixels on the projected image. At any timestamp, samples taken by the warp form a rectangular plane (or a 1D line, depending on the thread block shape), i.e. a subregion of the sampling plane we mentioned in Section 3.1, which is always perpendicular to the viewing direction. The warp of threads goes through the volume from front to back to take samples and blend colors for the 32 pixels. We call this sampling strategy the Standard/Traditional sampling strategy throughout the paper and we will compare our new sampling strategies against it. To accurately collect the texture cache hit rate, several changes have been applied to the benchmark program:
1.
The early ray termination mechanism is disabled. All rays in the benchmark march a fixed number of steps, 512 in this case, to guarantee a constant amount of workload and rule out the effect of different transfer functions.
2.
Different from most of the GPU-based volume rendering implementations that put the transfer function into the texture memory, our benchmark uses the GPU constant memory to store the transfer function. This change assures that our texture cache hit rate values accurately reflect the texture cache performance when accessing the volume data.
3.
The benchmark program uses orthographic projection to keep a constant distance between neighboring rays during ray marching. Sampling distance along rays (in depth direction) is always fixed at 0.5 voxel length throughout the paper.
To eliminate the impact from different volume datasets, an 8-bit 10243 volume with random density values is generated. The ratio of spacings along three dimensions is 1 :1 :1. The projected image resolution is fixed at 512 ×512, so all executions trace the same number of rays (the same amount of workload for each execution). The distance between neighboring rays can be adjusted by tracing different portions of the volume. For example, if the 5122 rays cover the entire volume, the distance between neighboring rays will be 2 voxels length. However, if the viewing frustum only covers a quarter of the volume, the distance between neighboring rays will be 1 voxel length. If not explicitly mentioned, the thread block size is configured as 256 throughout this paper.
An NVIDIA GeForce GTX TITAN GPU with 6 GB device memory is used to run the benchmark and the collected results are shown in Fig. 3. When the viewing direction is parallel to the image stack (Fig. 2, facing
𝑍⁢𝑌), the texture cache hit rate drops dramatically as the ray distance (volume image ratio) increases. A larger distance between neighboring rays indicates that samples on these rays cover more slices. As a result, the texture cache performance, as well as the rendering performance, is poor. On the contrary, the decline is insignificant when the viewing direction is perpendicular to the image stack (Fig. 2, facing
𝑋⁢𝑌). Although samples are spatially getting further from each other when the ray distance is increasing, they are still on the same 2D slice and their memory locations are still close to each other in this viewing direction. The texture cache hit rates (metric tex_cache_hit_rate) and the L2 cache hit rates (metric l2_texture_read_hit_rate) reported in this paper are collected by the CUDA profiling tools interface (CUPTI) (NVIDIA, 2013b).

4. 1D Warp Marching
We introduce a new sampling strategy to address the cache-related performance issues in the Standard sampling mechanism (Fig. 4 (a)). The new strategy samples volumes along rays, as shown in Fig. 4 (b). A warp of threads in this strategy is responsible for only one ray in the ray-casting algorithm. Color blending for the corresponding pixel is performed inside the warp of threads in parallel. Rays march a warp size of samples each step, so we call the algorithm Warp Marching. The algorithm works along the same lines as (Ma et al., 1993), which breaks a ray into a series of segments and composites these ray segments. Since the warp of samples forms a sample line along the depth direction in this case, we call the sampling strategy 1D Warp Marching.

4.1. Algorithm design
The ray-casting algorithm has two major sequential steps: (1) intersection tests between rays and the bounding box of a volume; (2) marching rays through the volume to accumulate colors for corresponding pixels. We only focus on the second step when applying the new sampling strategy. Each thread is given a binary bit to depict its sample’s value. After taking a sample from the ray, each thread maps the sample’s density value to a color (RGBA) value through the transfer function. If the alpha component is zero, the binary bit is set to zero, otherwise it is set to one. The warp voting function, __ballot(), can then take these binary bits as input and output the state of the entire warp. If the function returns zero, the warp of samples will be skipped. Otherwise the color blending will be performed inside the warp of samples. The front to back color blending can be done in a reduction manner by virtue of its associative property (Ma et al., 1994).
Fig. 5 illustrates an example of the color blending in 1D Warp Marching. For simplicity, we assume eight threads per warp in this example. In each warp cycle, all threads of the warp take samples along the ray and map the densities to color values according to the transfer function (step 1). Reduction starts from step 2 and continues till step 4. The value derived in step 4 is the contribution of the warp of samples to the final pixel. It needs to be copied to a “safe” place (we use an extra memory unit to store this intermediate result) and accumulated as rays march further. In implementation, we use a float4 variable to store the RGBA color value of each sample, so nine such float4 variables (eight for eight threads of the warp and one for the intermediate result) are required in total, and they can be allocated from the fast on-chip memory of GPUs. We allocate them from the shared memory. Since each thread requires a single buffer from the shared memory in this approach, we call it Single Buffer Shared Warp Marching (SBShared).

4.2. Hardware-oriented optimization
One drawback of the SBShared approach is the number of active threads reduces by half after each step, as shown in Fig. 5. To mitigate this problem, we propose a pipeline manner reduction, i.e. double buffer color blending. Moreover, since the reduction operations are all inside warps, we take the efficient warp shuffling operations (among thread registers) as an alternative to allocating buffers from the shared memory.
4.2.1. Double buffer approach
The reason that the number of active threads decreases in SBShared is because the workload in the reduction is shrunk to half after each step. To alleviate this problem, we ask the warp to delay the blending process until all threads of the warp have received enough data to blend. Each thread of the warp now needs two buffers, since one blending operation needs two operands. These buffers are still allocated from the shared memory of a GPU. For these reasons, we call this approach Double Buffer Shared Warp Marching (DBShared).
Fig. 6 gives an example of this optimized approach. In the first cycle, all threads of the warp initialize their first buffer’s value to zero (cycle 1, step 1). Then, they take samples along the ray, derive colors of the samples through the transfer function and load them to their second buffer (cycle 1, step 2). Now, the number of buffer units with data to be blended is the double of the thread number. All threads of the warp perform one step reduction and write the results back to their first buffer (cycle 1, step 3). Before the second step reduction (cycle 2, step 2), the warp of threads load another warp of samples into their second buffer (cycle 2, step 1). In this way, every thread of the warp has enough data to blend and the entire warp is active during the reductions. All cycles from the third cycle onwards are similar to the second cycle. Thread-inactive case only happens in the last warp cycle (as shown in the red rectangle). Since there is no new data to load, some threads of the warp will be idle. If the number of marching steps for a warp is large, the percentage of the last cycle (threads inactive time) will be small. Compared with the SBShared (1 load, 3 steps reduction), DBShared (1 load, 1 step reduction) has a higher load-compute ratio, but a better warp execution efficiency.

The DBShared approach brings problems to the early ray termination mechanism, since the exact integrated value cannot be derived at each warp cycle. In other words, the DBShared approach may have to take 3 (log2⁡8, when the warp size is 8) more warps of samples (3 ×8 extra texture fetching operations) on each ray. Fortunately, the early ray termination only relies on the accumulated opacity value, so we can still do the delayed blending for color values, though the accumulated opacity will have to be derived at each warp cycle. The subfigure on the left of Fig. 6 depicts this repair. After the one step reduction in each cycle, the warp transfers the opacity components from buffer 1 to buffer 2. A complete reduction is performed there then. Compared to the SBShared that blends 8 float4, the DBShared blends 8 float in each cycle. Although DBShared involves more data movements, it reduces the amount of work that only part of the warp will be working on. Before the warp marches ahead, it uses the opacity reduction result in buffer 2 to decide whether the ray should be terminated or not.
4.2.2. Warp-level operation
In Section 4.1, we have shown how the warp voting function helps in reflecting the state of the entire warp. Here we are going to incorporate the efficient warp shuffling operations into the implementation of the Warp Marching. The warp voting functions are available since the Fermi architecture, whereas the warp shuffling operations are only available on the Kepler or newer generations of GPUs. With these shuffling operations, threads within the same warp can efficiently communicate their register values. Instead of allocating buffers from the shared memory, the shuffling approaches allocate buffers from the register space of each thread.

Single buffer shuffling (SBShuffle). Different from the SBShared that allocates 9 float4 from the shared memory, the SBShuffle allocates a float4 from the register space of each thread. The shuffling width, i.e. variable width, by default is the warp size. The intermediate result in single buffer approaches (Fig. 5) will also be allocated from the register space and broadcast ( __shfl()) to all threads of the warp in each iteration.
Double buffer shuffling (DBShuffle). Two float4 are created from the register space of each thread in the DBShuffle approach. In the reduction process of each cycle, all threads of the warp first shuffle their buffer 1 values to the first half warp of threads, and this half warp performs one step reduction there. Then all threads shuffle their buffer 2 values to the second half warp of threads, and this half warp conducts another reduction. Divergent branching happens here due to different shuffling sources. Consequently, this step is divided into two separate steps and a half warp is active in each.

Optimizations: Fig. 7 shows the performance speedup of four different implementations of the 1D Warp Marching algorithm, i.e. SBShared, DBShared, SBShuffle and DBShuffle. The results indicate that the shuffling approaches are always better than the shared memory approaches. The double buffer approaches perform better than the single buffer approaches when rays march by taking a larger number of steps. For double buffer approaches (Fig. 6), the number of active threads in the last cycle still decreases by half in each step. However, this only happens in the last warp cycle. When rays march more steps, the percentage of this part becomes small. 5. Warp shape and warp orientation
Until now, we have only discussed the Warp Marching algorithm that organizes a warp of threads as a 1D line, i.e. 1D Warp Marching. A warp of 32 threads can actually be organized as three possible shapes, i.e. 32 ×1, 16 ×2 and 8 ×4. We call Warp Marching with the latter two warp shapes as 2D Warp Marching. In addition, there are six possible orientations for each warp shape. The longer dimension of the warp can be called as the major sampling direction, and the shorter one as the minor direction. We name warp orientations using their major direction axis followed by their minor direction axis. For example, in 1D Warp Marching (warp shape 32 ×1), the warp orientation is 𝑧⁢𝑥, as a warp takes 32 samples in depth and 1 sample along the horizontal direction. For the Standard sampling strategy, thread block shape is 16 ×16, its warp shape in this setting is 16 ×2. Since each warp takes 16 samples along the horizontal direction and 2 samples along the vertical direction, the warp orientation is 𝑥⁢𝑦. Upper case characters 𝑋, 𝑌 and 𝑍 are used to represent the viewing directions, whereas lower case characters, 𝑥, 𝑦 and 𝑧 are for the warp orientations in this paper. Note that characters 𝑋, 𝑌 and 𝑍 (𝑋, 𝑌 and 𝑍 dimension of the volume) used in the viewing directions are from the object coordinates, whereas characters 𝑥, 𝑦 and 𝑧 (horizontal, vertical and depth direction) used in the name of warp orientations are from the view coordinates. Warp orientation 𝑥⁢𝑦 does not indicate that the warp takes samples along the 𝑋 and 𝑌 dimension of the volume, but indicates that it takes samples along the horizontal and vertical direction of the view.
All six warp orientations are demonstrated in Fig. 9 (A). Warp orientation 𝑧⁢𝑥 and 𝑥⁢𝑧 can be generated by changing the marching step of a warp in the Warp Marching (in the shuffling implementations, this can be done by changing the shuffling width, i.e. the variable width in Algorithm 1). For example, if the size of a warp is 32 and it marches 8 samples in the depth direction each cycle, the warp orientation will be 𝑧⁢𝑥, because the warp takes 8 samples in depth and 4 samples horizontally. On the contrary, if the warp marches 4 samples along the depth direction, the warp orientation will be 𝑥⁢𝑧. In this case, 8 samples are taken in the horizontal direction and 4 samples are taken along the view (in depth) by a warp. Warp orientation 𝑥⁢𝑦 can be generated using the Standard ray-casting algorithm. Here, we consider the Standard algorithm as a special case of the Warp Marching, in which, the marching step is 1 in the depth direction. In conclusion, we can derive three warp orientations (three blue ones in Fig. 9 (A)) by changing the marching step of the Warp Marching algorithm. The Standard and the Warp Marching methods by default are row-major sampling algorithms (Fig. 9 (B)). For example, if we set the marching step to 4 in the Warp Marching, the first 4 threads of the warp will work on the first pixel, row 0 column 0; and the second 4 threads will work on the second pixel on right, row 0 column 1, etc. We can easily change the row-major sampling to a column-major sampling (by modifying the thread-pixel mapping). Then, the rest of the three green warp orientations in Fig. 9 (A) can be generated.
There are six unique viewing directions, as shown in Fig. 10 (top row). The figure (middle row) also depicts results of the three warp shapes in combination with the six warp orientations in these six extreme viewing directions. The results suggest that the major sampling direction of a warp should always be aligned with the 𝑋 dimension of the volume to achieve a higher texture cache hit rate. For example, when the viewing direction is facing 𝑌⁢𝑋, the 𝑋 dimension of the volume is aligned with the vertical direction of the view. So, warp orientation with a major direction 𝑦, i.e. 𝑦⁢𝑥 and 𝑦⁢𝑧, achieves the best texture cache performance. Our conclusion here is consistent with the solution in the work of Sugimoto et al. (2014). Since the 𝑋 dimension of volumes mapped to the 3D texture has a smaller memory stride, samples should always be taken along that dimension.

6. 3D Warp Marching
We have shown that the texture cache performance can be optimized when facing certain viewing directions. However, there is not a simple approach that can maintain a good and roughly constant rendering frame rate when taking all viewing directions into consideration. Weiskopf et al. (2004) tried to address this problem by reorganizing volumes into small bricks oriented to different directions so that the cache performance variations can be averaged out. Instead of reorganizing data, we reorganize samples along different directions in the Warp Marching. The shape of thread warps in this case is a 3D volume. For this reason, we call the algorithm 3D Warp Marching.
Our explanation of constructing 3D warps will be based on our description of the 2D warps’ construction in Section 5. There are six possible 3D warp shapes: 4 ×4 ×2, 4 ×2 ×4, 2 ×4 ×4, 8 ×2 ×2, 2 ×8 ×2 and 2 ×2 ×8. Here we give an example of organizing the warp shape as 4 ×2 ×4, other 3D shapes can be derived in the same way. To organize the warp shape as 4 ×2 ×4, we first adjust the warp shape to 8 ×4 and the warp orientation to 𝑥⁢𝑧, i.e. the warp takes 8 samples along the horizontal direction and 4 samples along the depth direction. The 2D warp covers 8 pixels on the final image plane and these 8 pixels form a line along the horizontal direction. To upgrade the warp shape to a 3D volume, we only need to organize the line of pixels (8 ×1) into a rectangular plane (4 ×2). This can be done by carefully configuring the shape of the thread block. If we want the warp shape to be 4 ×2 ×4, the thread block shape needs to be 4 ×16 ×4 (256 threads/block).
Other than the number of samples along three directions, the distance between neighboring samples should also be taken into consideration when trying to cover the same number of voxels along three dimensions. In the benchmark settings, the distance between rays is 2 voxels length (the 10243 volume is projected to 512 ×512 pixels), and the distance between samples along rays is 0.5 voxel length. So, the sampling distance along the horizontal, vertical and depth direction is the length of 2 voxels, 2 voxels and 0.5 voxel respectively. Multiplying this distance with the number of samples taken along each direction is the number of voxels that one warp will access along that direction. For example, warp shape 4 ×2 ×4 covers 8, 4 and 2 voxels along three directions. Among the six 3D warp shapes, shape 2 ×2 ×8 covers the same number of voxels (4 voxels) along three directions. Therefore, its variance of texture cache hit rate in different viewing directions should be small.
We use the same benchmark to demonstrate the texture cache hit rate, L2 cache hit rate and overall rendering performance of all 3D warp shapes, in comparison with the Standard method (warp shape 16 ×2 ×1) and the 1D Warp Marching method (warp shape 1 ×1 ×32) in Fig. 12. Results are collected when rotating the experimental volume around the 𝑋, 𝑌 and 𝑍 axis 360 ° respectively. Due to symmetry, only 180 ° rotation results are shown. The Standard method demonstrates a big performance variation during these rotations. A deep trough appears in the results due to its poor texture cache and L2 cache performance when rotating around the 𝑌 axis. For 1D Warp Marching, the texture cache hit rate is high when rotating the volume around the 𝑌 axis, but it becomes low when rotating around 𝑋 or 𝑍 axis. Fortunately, L2 cache performs extremely well during these two rotations. As a result, the 1D Warp Marching maintains a roughly constant frame rate during all rotations. The texture cache hit rate of the 3D Warp Marching with warp shape 4 ×2 ×4, 2 ×4 ×4 or 2 ×2 ×8 only demonstrates slight variations during these rotations. Consequently, their overall rendering performances are roughly constant. When rotating around the 𝑌 axis, the texture cache hit rate collected from the 3D Warp Marching with warp shape 4 ×4 ×2, 2 ×8 ×2 or 8 ×2 ×2 shows a deep trough, which is similar to that of the Standard sampling strategy. A warp in any of these three shapes distributes 2 samples along the depth direction and the distance between samples in depth is 0.5 voxel length. Thus, the warp only covers one voxel along the view. With the benchmark settings, these three shapes cover 8 ×8 ×1, 4 ×16 ×1 and 16 ×4 ×1 voxels along three directions respectively. The big variation among the numbers of voxels covered along each dimension leads to the decrease of texture cache performance when rotating around 𝑌. However, their good L2 cache performances when rotating around 𝑌 compensate for the cache-misses from the texture cache. Therefore, these three 3D Warp Marching methods also achieve high and approximately constant frame rates. In conclusion, all Warp Marching methods can maintain a good and roughly constant rendering performance during these rotations. Performance of the Standard method, on the contrary, fluctuates significantly. It becomes dramatically poor when rotating around the 𝑌 axis.

7.2. Empty space skipping and scalability
The empty space skipping techniques can significantly reduce the amount of texture fetching operations. These techniques usually rely on additional data structures and require extra memory space. When working with static volumes and memory space is not limited, these structures are preferable, as they only need to be built once. For dynamic volumes, however, these structures have to be updated every frame. Updating empty space skipping structures can be costly, especially when the volume image ratio is large. When rendering a large volume into a small resolution image, the construction of an extra structure may replace the rendering part to become the major performance bottleneck. Here, we show that the Warp Marching can work as a data structure-free alternative to this case. We also include the scalability issue in the discussion by rendering dynamic volumes in different image resolutions.
We use a sequence of Jet-Flame simulation volumes (Attili et al., 2014) as our experimental dataset. The dataset contains 40 time steps in total, and the resolution of the volume in each time step is 1024 ×1024 ×512. The simulation generates several scalar and vector fields. Fig. 17 shows six time steps of the temperature field visualization. More information about the dataset and the simulation process can be found in Attili et al. (2014). Each time step of the Jet-Flame volume is around 512 MB (1024 ×1024 ×512 ×8 bits). In order to demonstrate the results of large dynamic volumes (in our benchmark scale), we duplicate the volume in each time step to generate a set of bigger volumes. Fig. 18 shows the result of the dynamic volumes with a resolution of 1024 ×1024 ×1024 (the volume in every time step of the Jet-Flame data is duplicated along the 𝑍 dimension). To demonstrate the effect of different volume image ratios, we render the dynamic volumes in three different resolutions respectively, i.e. 256 ×256, 512 ×512 and 1024 ×1024. When rendering large-scale volumes, the data transfer between CPU and GPU is often a performance bottleneck. To avoid the data transfer latency, we map several (3 volumes in Fig. 18) consecutive time steps of the volumes to different 3D textures of GPU before rendering. During execution, the GPU kernel takes different 3D textures to render in different frames.
The Macrocell solution Parker et al. (2005), Parker et al. (1998) in Fig. 18 divides volumes into smaller bricks. The size of each brick is 𝑑𝑥𝑛 ×𝑑𝑦𝑛 ×𝑑𝑧𝑛, where 𝑑𝑥, 𝑑𝑦 and 𝑑𝑧 represent the three dimensions of the volume. The structure represents the original volume in a coarser resolution and each brick maintains the minimum and maximum scalar values of the entire brick. We parallelized the macrocell construction on GPU and tested the parameter 𝑛 with values from 0.1 to 0.9. The best performance was achieved when 𝑛 =0.3. During rendering, the macrocell is first traversed with the 3DDDA algorithm (Amanatides and Woo, 1987). If the opacity value accumulated from the minimum to the maximum densities is 0, the entire brick will be skipped; otherwise, the Standard sampling strategy will be used inside the brick. The results shown in Fig. 18 lead to the following conclusions:

When rendering the volumes in the resolution of 1024 ×1024, the Macrocell method performs better than the Warp Marching algorithms. Though the macrocell structure is still built every frame, it contributes a lot to reducing the rendering cost.
3.
When rotating the volume around the 𝑍 axis, the result of the Standard method demonstrates a trough, which has already been found in Fig. 16. Consequently, curves representing active resolutions are very smooth.
Results of rotating the set of dynamic volumes around several arbitrary axes in different image resolutions are provided in Fig. 19 to further support our argument. In short, we suggest to use the Warp Marching algorithms when working with large-scale dynamic volumes and the volume image ratio is large. Empty space skipping structures are ideal for static volumes or for dynamic volumes when the rendered image resolution is large enough to hide the structure reconstruction overheads.

8. Conclusion
We demonstrate the significant impact of texture cache on the performance of texture-based volume visualization techniques, and propose a cache-friendly sampling strategy, i.e. Warp Marching, for the ray-casting algorithm. Effects of different computation-to-core mapping strategies in different viewing directions are thoroughly analyzed. Two hardware-oriented optimizations are introduced to maximally take advantage of the parallel architecture of GPUs. Both the 1D and 3D Warp Marching algorithms show slight performance variations during rotations. Therefore, they can be considered as acceleration structure-free solutions for rendering tasks that require a constant rendering frame rate. In addition, the Warp Marching algorithms are desirable when rendering large-scale dynamic volumes and the volume image ratio is large. They outperform the existing empty space skipping approaches by saving the memory space for additional data structures and avoiding the structure construction step every frame.

High-performance adaptive texture streaming for planetary-scale high-mobility information visualization
Masood Z., …, Irfan M.
Journal of King Saud University - Computer and Information Sciences • Volume 34 • 2022
Citation Excerpt:
High-performance planetary-scale landscape visualization requires high-speed texture streaming for high-fidelity display systems. Texture streaming is a computation, memory, and communication intensive task for real-time visualization of large-scale data sets (Wang et al., 2017). Streaming of multi-resolution textures as mipmap chains enables low-cost filtering of textures with reduced anti-aliasing (Backes et al., 2017).
Show abstract
Planetary-scale terrain requires streaming of world-scale high-resolution data sets in real time. High-performance streaming of world-scale data sets to achieve high mobility in terms of speed and view such as look-around, side-ways, or backward for high fidelity display systems is challenging. Moreover, streaming data sets without any preprocessing overhead require high-performance decompression and re-projection in real time. This work proposes a high-performance adaptive texture streaming system to capture high-speed scenes with view switching capability over a real-world terrain for high-fidelity display system. The system proposes multi-stage pipeline to stream multi-resolution data sets with a stable high-performance rendering rate. The proposed pipeline stages are asynchronously connected using request queues with a cache hierarchy. Efficient algorithms for resource management, prefetching and caching, prioritization of load requests, and hardware-accelerated processing are proposed for texture streaming without frame-rate stuttering. The proposed algorithms adapt to viewer navigation, display resolution, and dynamic execution environment. The proposed method is evaluated for complex scenes, mobility in terms of speed and view, and high-fidelity display. Our method achieved a ground-speed of 2.4x105,1.2x105,and9.0x104 km/h for HD (1024x720), FHD (1920x1080), and UHD (3840x2160) display resolutions respectively with a stable frame rate of 850, 590, and 420 frames per second.
