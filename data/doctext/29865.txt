NeRFifyMesh: Optimizing Neural Radiance Fields from Textured Meshes for
Robotics Scene Building
arXiv:2610.10387v1 [cs.RO] 7 Oct 2026
Nillan Nimal1 , Mahboubeh Asadi2 , and Sajad Saeedi3
Abstract— In robotics, scene representation plays a pivotal
role in understanding and interacting with the environment.
The advent of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and its variants,
as a novel representation, has opened a new frontier of research.
In applications such as semantic mapping and simulation,
roboticists aim to build scenes using multiple NeRF models,
each representing an object. While extensive datasets of 3D
mesh models already exist, there is an urgent need to develop
tools to convert these assets to NeRF models for rapid algorithm
development and testing. This paper presents a new pipeline
for converting existing mesh models to NeRF representations by
artificially generating a ground truth point-based radiance field
through sampling mesh geometry and texture. This approach
alleviates the need for camera-based sampling or rendering
multi-view images of the original mesh to train the NeRF model.
Extensive benchmarking demonstrates that our method yields
comparable rendering quality to the baselines. Additionally,
the application of this representation is shown by constructing
unified NeRF scenes and performing collision simulations with
extracted geometry.
I. I NTRODUCTION
In 3D digital graphics and computer vision, the accurate
and efficient representation of objects and scenes is paramount.
Popular methods for representing 3D structures vary based
on their characteristics and applications. Point clouds capture
geometric detail sparsely through individual data points.
Meshes define surface geometry by using interconnected
points, or vertices, which together form polygonal units like
triangles [1]. Voxels offer a distinct approach by discretizing
space into uniform, grid-like structures [2]. Signed Distance
Functions (SDFs) represent the distance of a point from the
surface of a 3D shape where the sign is negative inside the
surface and positive outside [3]. Recently, Neural Radiance
Fields (NeRFs) have emerged as a novel representation [4].
Optimized for visual consistency, NeRFs can achieve stateof-the-art results for novel view synthesis and image-based
rendering tasks.
The ability to transition between diverse model representations can be immensely valuable. Different fields
and applications often require or benefit from specific 3D
representations. For instance, while robotics might leverage
SDFs for object interaction, computer graphics might prefer
mesh models for detailed visual rendering. As such, the
interoperability between these representations can bridge the
gap between fields and facilitate a wider range of applications.

Converting between mesh and NeRF format is particularly
interesting given the prevalence of mesh models in computer
graphics and robotics. Textured mesh models are widely
available in databases such as Amazon Berkeley Objects [5],
Google Scanned Objects [6], ShapeNet [7], and Objaverse [8].
Developing an efficient and automated representation
conversion tool has many applications in robotic systems, such
as composing large-scale scenes out of many NeRF models,
where the NeRF models are built from existing mesh models.
However, traditional pipelines to convert representations entail
rendering images of the mesh model from known camera
poses that encompass the entire model to serve as the ground
truth for optimizing a NeRF. This approach has significant
drawbacks. Rendering high-quality images from mesh models
is computationally expensive and training images that omit
areas of the model can lead to poor results. Additionally, the
rendered images require storage, which can be problematic
when working with large-scale datasets such as Objaverse [8].
In this paper, we propose NeRFifyMesh, a pipeline that
removes the rendering optimization step from the training
process and alleviates the need for high-quality ground-truth
images and camera poses of the mesh model (see Fig. 1). We
achieve this by sampling the mesh to generate a discrete pointbased radiance field with the properties of color (cgt ), binary
opacity (αgt ), point normals (⃗ngt ), and their significance,
wc , wα , and w⃗n respectively. We then proceed to train
our neural representation directly on this discrete field. By
including point normals in our representation, we can relight
our rendered scene post-training, recover accurate geometry
through surface reconstruction, and facilitate applications that
rely on geometric information, such as robotics simulation.
The contributions of this work are: 1) A mesh-derived
Neural Radiance Field representation is proposed, where
neural supervision is performed on a discrete radiance field
generated from mesh geometry and texture. This method
alleviates the need for multi-view image supervision or
camera-pose information during training. 2) Surface normals
are learned by the representation, enabling normal-based
shading during rendering and supporting geometry-dependent
applications. 3) The effectiveness of the representation is
demonstrated through model compression, relighting, surface
extraction and collision simulation experiments in constructed
scenes. The project page and videos can be found at:
https://sites.google.com/view/nerfifymesh

II. R ELATED W ORK
This section presents the literature review for neural
representations, mesh-based NeRF pipelines, and NeRFs in
robotics applications.
A. Neural Implicit Representations
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [4] are a novel representation, mapping a 2D ray viewing direction (ϕ, θ) and
Cartesian coordinates for a 3D location (x, y, z) to volume
density (σ) and emitted view-dependent radiance (r, g, b).
Other neural implicit representations can also parameterize
the geometry and/or appearance of meshes in a continuous
and compact manner. Neural SDFs [9], [10] model surfaces
implicitly as the zero-level set of signed distance functions.
For example, Neural Geometric Level of Detail (NGLoD)
[11] represents a neural SDF as a collection of feature vectors
in a sparse voxel octree (SVO) where the levels of the SVO
define levels of detail. Occupancy Networks [12] employs
a neural network-based binary classifier to implicitly define
surfaces as a set of 3D points. Instant-NGP [13] addresses
the computational costs of utilizing fully connected neural
networks by introducing a multi-resolution hash encoding
that allows for accelerated training and rendering of neural
graphics primitives such as NeRF and Neural SDFs.
In addition, mesh autoencoders [14], [15] apply convolutional operations to meshes directly to create latent
representations for mesh reconstruction. Although these works
either directly utilize mesh data or derive point clouds from
sampled meshes, their focus is limited to mesh geometry
encoding and reconstruction.
Volume rendering-based methods [16], [17], [18] and
Scene Representation Networks (SRNs) [19] take a different
approach. They represent 3D scene geometry and appearance
derived from multi-view images. NeuS presents a volume
rendering method for surface reconstruction from multi-view
images that addresses the limitations of NeRF which lacks
surface optimization [16]. NeuMesh [20], which is supervised
with NeuS, utilizes a mesh-based scaffold with learnable
disentangled geometry and texture features to encode a
neural implicit field. These approaches require the synthetic
generation of images from the mesh model. This leads to
reconstruction inaccuracies for regions of the mesh that are
rarely visible or occluded in the generated images.
B. Mesh-based NeRF Pipelines
The extraction of accurate underlying 3D geometry from
NeRFs presents a challenging problem given that they are
not explicitly optimized for geometry. Consequently, current
works [21], [22], have developed methods for extracting
meshes from volumetric NeRF representations, enabling
integration with common computer graphics pipelines and
real-time rendering. NeRFMeshing [21] utilizes a signed
surface approximation network (SSAN) to represent a triangle
mesh of the scene and an appearance network to capture
view-dependent colours. NeRF2Mesh [22] extracts meshes
with view-independent diffuse textures and view-dependent
specular textures. Additional approaches incorporate a mesh
representation of the target scene to edit NeRFs [23], [24],
[25] or improve rendering efficiency [26]. For example, DENeRF [24] attaches learnable disentangled features to an
extracted triangle mesh of the scene to allow for editing the
model.
Mesh2NeRF [27] showed that training directly on meshes
significantly improves rendering quality. However, their
method samples from virtual cameras rather than mesh
geometry, does not learn surface normals, bakes in lighting,
and relies on off-the-shelf NeRF backbones. Our representation instead uses a custom architecture that leverages mesh
geometry directly, enabling relighting and accurate geometry
extraction through learned surface normals.
C. NeRFs in Robotics Applications
There is an increasing prevalence of NeRFs in robotics due
to their continuous scene representation and photorealistic
rendering capabilities. For robotics applications, NeRFs have
been leveraged for data generation, perception, mapping,
simulation, and multi-robot agents [28].
Neural Sim [29], presents a pipeline to generate synthetic
data with NeRFs to train vision models, enabling applications
such as object detection and scene understanding. Similarly,
Wang et al. [30] investigates the performance of a NeRF
model that uses only semantic data for tasks such as
segmentation and detection.
Additionally, NeRFs have demonstrated potential in robot
navigation and mapping. Adamkiewicz et al. [31] demonstrated vision-only navigation of robots in a learned radiance
field, with trajectory algorithms to avoid high-density regions
corresponding to obstacles.
In terms of physics-based simulations, Cleac’h et al. [32]
produce a differentiable neural object pipeline which includes
NeRFs for simulating the motion of objects. This allows
for the estimation of dynamics properties such as friction
and mass from visual data. NeRF2Real [33] synthesized
scenes composed from static NeRF environments and dynamic
objects and utilized a physics-engine to compute contact
dynamics for sim2real policy transfer.
Together these works demonstrate the advancing interest of
NeRFs in robotics. Consequently, a pipeline to convert meshes
to NeRFs will be useful by allowing existing mesh assets
to be integrated into NeRF-based simulation and perception
frameworks.
III. M ETHOD
NeRFifyMesh aims to leverage the explicit geometry and
texture information of a mesh model to encode a mesh within
a NeRF representation without the need for multi-view images.
Here, Sec. III-A provides an overview. Network architecture
and loss functions are covered in Sec. III-B and Sec. III-C,
respectively. Rendering is explained in Sec. III-D.
A. Overview: NeRFifyMesh Representation
The NeRFifyMesh representation makes two assumptions:
1) mesh appearance is view-independent, 2) meshes are
opaque with no translucent features. Consequently, 2D ray
view-direction parameters (ϕ, θ) are omitted, and opacity is
modeled as a binary function, as in Occupancy Networks
[12]. Since normals are learned in this representation, viewdependent shading can be incorporated during rendering
(Sec. IV-C.3).

radiance field by sampling points within the bounding box of

B. Network Architecture
The NeRFifyMesh architecture decouples predicted color c,
point normals ⃗n, and opacity α into three separate networks.
Each network is optimized with separate loss functions and
trained simultaneously for efficiency. An overview of the
network architecture is displayed in Fig. 2.
1) Color and Point Normal Networks: For color and
normals prediction, positional encoding transforms the input
point x into a higher-dimensional space using a series of sine
and cosine functions [4]. Positional encoding, as implemented
by Mildenhall et al. in the original NeRF [4], allows for the
representation of high-frequency details by capturing the fine
spatial variations in the scene. The positionally encoded input
is then fed into separate color and normal decoder networks,
fθ and fϕ respectively. Each network is composed of SIREN
(Sinusoidal Representation Networks) layers. SIREN layers
use sinusoidal activation functions which allows for the
effective modelling of complex continuous signals, including
those with high frequency components [10]. This structure
captures color and normal variations across the mesh.
2) Opacity Network: For opacity prediction, we introduce
a multi-resolution hash-encoding architecture that directly
leverages mesh data. The surface points sampled from
the mesh are utilized to initialize N surface voxel grids
G1 , G2 , ..., GN . Each grid Gl is set to a resolution of R = 2l
where l is the level of detail. Trainable latent vectors vl [i, j, k]
with 2 dimensions are assigned to each grid vertex where
i, j, k are the vertex indices. For a given input point x,
the point is queried at each of the surface voxel grids by
multiplying by the grid resolution. If x ∈ Gl , the indices of the
eight corners that the point x belongs to are used as hash keys
to retrieve the corresponding latent vectors from hash table Hl ,
i.e., vl [i, j, k] = Hl ([i, j, k]). Trilinear interpolation is utilized
to interpolate the latent vector according to its position in
the voxel grid producing vl′ (x). If the point x ̸∈ Gi , then
vl′ (x) = vl,def ault where vl,def ault is a predefined default
latent for Gl . These latent vectors for N grid levels are

The hash-encoding architecture employed for NeRFifyMesh is inspired by Instant-NGP [13]. However, as opposed
to initializing trainable latent vectors for each grid point at
a given resolution, NeRFifyMesh strategically assigns these
vectors solely to the grid points corresponding to the occupied
voxels within a surface voxel grid. Points external to this grid
are mapped to a default latent vector, effectively distinguishing
the mesh-contained regions from the surrounding empty space.
This approach not only leverages the inherent geometric
information provided by the mesh to minimize the number
of trainable parameters but also integrates this structural data
into the implicit representation. The default latent vector plays
a crucial role in assisting the decoder network to differentiate
between the mesh surface and void regions effectively.
Since the surface voxel grid is initialized from the mesh, to
avoid the necessity of retaining the original mesh to rebuild
the model, the indices I = [i, j, k] of the surface voxels at
each grid Gl are saved with the model.

IV. E XPERIMENTS
This section presents experimental results for NeRFifyMesh. Implementation details are first described, followed
by comparisons of mesh scene renderings against NeRF-based
methods trained from images. Additional applications and
simulation experiments are then presented, followed by a
discussion of limitations.
A. Implementation Details
For each mesh model used in the experiments, 2 million
samples were taken of points inside, outside and on the mesh
surface using the strategy proposed by Takikawa et al. [11] to
generate training data. NeRFifyMesh was trained to 300,000
iterations with a batch size of 4096 samples using a system
with 50 GB of RAM and an NVIDIA P100 GPU. Training
takes roughly 2 hours per model and the best weights for the
color, opacity, and normal networks are retained.
B. Mesh Scene Rendering
To evaluate the performance of NeRFifyMesh on mesh
scene rendering, we selected 13 objects from the Google
Scanned Objects (GSO) dataset [6], 7 objects from Poly
Haven [35], and 5 objects from the Objaverse dataset
[8]. The GSO dataset features scans of household objects,
represented as simplified and watertight meshes. The Poly
Haven dataset includes artist-created 3D mesh assets. The
Objaverse dataset comprises scans of museum and nature
artifacts with complex geometries and high-resolution textures.
The Objaverse dataset is particularly challenging due to
its high vertex counts and non-manifold geometry, which
complicates sampling.
NeRFifyMesh’s rendering performance is benchmarked
against two NeRF methods: the original NeRF [4], and InstantNGP [13]. While these methods require a set of input images
for training, NeRFifyMesh is trained directly from mesh data.
For comparison, we rendered 100 training images and 20
test images at 1024×1024 resolution of each mesh from
camera poses following a spherical coordinate system. These
rendered images were used to train the NeRF-based methods.
The quality of our renders for the test images is compared
to those generated by the other methods in terms of PSNR,
SSIM, and LPIPS. In Table I, our method achieves the lowest
(best) LPIPS score for all three datasets, indicating superior
perceptual similarity. In terms of the SSIM measure, our
method demonstrates competitive performance, with higher
scores than NeRF but slightly lower scores than InstantNGP. For the PSNR metric, ours is lower (worse) than
Instant-NGP and NeRF for all three datasets. This highlights
a methodological difference. Instant-NGP and NeRF are
optimized for photometric rendering loss from multi-view
images. In contrast, our representation is trained from meshderived samples without volume rendering optimization
during training. As a result, while our method does not
explicitly minimize pixel-level photometric error, it preserves
perceptually meaningful geometric and textural details shown
by our strong LPIPS performance.
A qualitative comparison of mesh scene rendering is shown
in Fig. 3. Our method represents fine mesh details with high
fidelity resulting in visually superior renders compared to the
baselines Instant-NGP and NeRF.

mesh datasets, size compression is a crucial application of
neural mesh representations. To analyze model compression,

compared to our representation, which includes the opacity,
color, and normal module weights, the hash table keys, and
the mesh bounding box used to reconstruct the model. Table II
shows that our representation achieves significant compression
ratios compared to the baselines. The compact nature of our
representation highlights the space efficiency of initializing
surface voxel grids at different levels of detail using the

Each NeRFifyMesh model is normalized to fit within a
unit cube and defined in a local coordinate frame. To render
a global scene from these models, rays are first cast from a
virtual camera in the global frame, then transformed into the
local coordinate frame of each NeRFifyMesh model. Let the
ray in the world frame be rw (t) = ow + td⃗w , where ow is
the ray origin and d⃗w is the ray direction. To evaluate this
ray in the local frame of model i, the inverse transformation,

Each local NeRF model returns RGB and depth values
for the transformed rays. These per-model renderings are
then composited in the 2D image plane using depth-based
ordering to resolve occlusions. Specifically, for each pixel,
the RGB outputs from different models are blended from
back to front using the corresponding depth values. This
pipeline also supports augmenting standard NeRF scenes

V. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduced NeRFifyMesh, a pipeline and
architecture that directly samples a mesh to generate a groundtruth radiance field with point normals, and subsequently
trains on this field to produce a NeRF. Our method achieved
impressive results in mesh scene rendering compared to stateof-the-art baselines. Additionally, NeRFifyMesh supports
composing multiple models into unified scenes, enabling applications in robotics simulations where constructing complex
environments from reusable assets is essential.
