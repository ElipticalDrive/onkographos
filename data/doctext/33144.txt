Abstract
OBJECTIVE. The purpose of this article is to present an overview of cinematic rendering, illustrating its potential advantages and applications.
CONCLUSION. Volume-rendered reconstruction, obtaining 3D visualization from original CT datasets, is increasingly used by physicians and medical educators in various clinical and educational scenarios. Cinematic rendering is a novel 3D rendering algorithm that simulates the propagation and interaction of light rays as they pass through the volumetric data, showing a more photorealistic representation of 3D images than achieved with standard volume rendering.
The introduction of MDCT has changed the field of medical imaging. The multiple rows of detectors allow faster acquisitions with higher temporal resolution and better patient coverage with higher spatial resolution [1]. This enables acquisition of volume datasets as opposed to the individual section data of conventional helical CT [2]. The result is isotropic imaging, by which data can be arranged in different planes without a reduction in spatial resolution, leading to accurate high-quality reformations and 3D rendered images [3].
Although 3D reconstructions were already available with conventional, single-slice helical CT, the higher-resolution display achieved with MDCT, along with ever increasing computing power, substantially enhanced the embrace of 3D rendered images in clinical practice. Three-dimensional reconstructions combine a series of axial sections to form an all-inclusive image, allowing the visualization of anatomic relations that would not be as intuitive viewed in axial sections alone. These techniques have allowed more comprehensive visualization, description, interpretation, and communication of many disorders [4–8]. Consequently, volume-rendering techniques are playing an increasingly important role in medical imaging practices.
The aims of this review are to provide an overview of the volume-rendered reconstruction technique and to introduce cinematic rendering (in development by Siemens Healthcare and not yet approved for clinical use), a novel 3D reconstruction algorithm, focusing on its potential future clinical applications.
Volume-Rendering Technique
In medical imaging, tissue information such as MRI signal intensity, ultrasound echogenicity, and CT attenuation is transformed into coded data. The data are then processed through mathematic algorithms to generate gray-scale or color-coded images. Different algorithms are used to create customizable images [9]. In 2D techniques, such as maximum intensity projection and minimal intensity projection, a single volumetric parameter from the original data is used to reconstruct images. More complex algorithms, however, can be used to generate 3D reconstructions that improve visualization of complex structures and anatomic relations. To date, volume rendering has been the most commonly used technique for 3D reconstructions in clinical practice.
Volume rendering is a visualization technique whereby acquired CT volumetric data are processed in their entirety, resulting in a 3D image that can be manipulated and customized in real time to display anatomic details from different perspectives [10]. It is based on the ray casting and local lighting model principles that state that for each pixel on a screen, one ray is cast through the volume and intersects a line of voxels. This technique is simple to compute with a Riemann integral but neglects complex light paths, such as scattering and light extinction, resulting in more artificial appearing images. As the light ray passes through the volume, specific steps must be applied by the algorithm before the final image is formed. These steps include sampling, classification, and compositing. All volume-rendering algorithms apply these steps, but the order can vary [11]. As a light ray passes through the volume, the volume contributions are accumulated and calculated on specific points along this ray by the process of sampling. Classification is the mechanism by which each sample point is given a color and an opacity value via transfer functions. This step allows the formation of a color representation from gray-scale sections. Compositing is the process by which the color and opacity values from each line of sample points are accumulated by means of a mathematic formula to generate the final projection on the corresponding pixel [11].
Volume rendering is a percentage-based technique [12]; each voxel contains one or more types of tissue. Each tissue is determined and represented by a percentage number ranging from 0 to 100 based on a predefined attenuation threshold level and is assigned a color and opacity value. The weighted sum of these values for tissues in one voxel is then calculated to determine the overall color and transparency of the voxel. This step is repeated for each sampled voxel along the light ray passing through the volume. The values of all the sampled points along one ray are accumulated and projected to contribute to the final image. The software analyzes the different combinations of Houns field unit ranges to show different tissue types and their location and then generates a 3D image based on observer and light source positions. The advantage of volume-rendered over classic CT reconstructions is the all-encompassing data analysis of volume-rendered images, which creates an accurate 3D representation of target tissues. This allows visualization of larger volumes on single images and contemporaneous evaluation of distant structures and their spatial relations.
Cinematic Rendering
Cinematic rendering is a recently introduced 3D reconstruction technique, currently not approved for clinical use, that can produce photorealistic 3D images from traditional CT and MRI data. This technique was inspired by the success of the animated movie industry, especially Pixar Animation Studios, in creating highly realistic looking characters, hence the name cinematic rendering.
Overall, cinematic rendering follows the same steps used for volume rendering in determining color and opacity: the gray values in each voxel of the original images are mapped to a color and opacity value by means of transfer functions. Different transfer functions can then be applied for rendering, depending on the characteristics of the case and the intended structures to highlight. However, instead of ray casting methods, in which each pixel is formed by one light ray, the algorithm used in cinematic rendering is based on path-tracing methods and the global illumination model—which simulate the different paths of billions of photons traveling from all possible directions through a volumetric dataset—and their interaction with the volume to form one pixel. As a result, the technique models the real-life physical propagation of light and generates a highly photorealistic 3D image based on the acquired data [13–15].
Given the infinite number of possibilities for light ray directions and the computational power required to simulate them, the Monte Carlo integration formula, which solves a multidimensional and noncontinuous rendering equation for each particle, is used to create a randomized finite number of light paths with an adequate and accurate distribution to imitate real-life light propagation [16, 17]. Then, by use of importance sampling (a parallelization and optimization algorithm), the modeling of scattering, reflection, and bouncing of light can be achieved. To attain photorealistic quality, high dynamic range rendering light maps are used to create a natural lighting environment by simulating realistic lighting effects from real-world environments, which are then applied to the rendering scene.
Using this technique, one can achieve complex lighting effects, such as soft shadows, depth of field, subsurface scattering, refraction, absorption, and ambient occlusion. Camera settings, such as aperture, exposure, shutter speed, and motion blur, can be modeled to create a natural lighting environment. By combining the accurate simulation of photon paths with the natural lighting simulated, the result remains a physically accurate 3D representation of the imaging data.
Advantages of Cinematic Rendering Over Volume Rendering
In cinematic rendering, billions of light rays are propagated through the voxels and interact with the volume to create one pixel. Volume rendering, however, operates on the one light ray per pixel principle. As a result, the final pixel in volume rendering depends only on the light properties of the voxels along the light ray leading to that pixel. In cinematic rendering, the lighting effects of neighboring voxels are also taken into account and create effects such as reflections and shadowing. Current 3D reconstructions are representations of volumes that are displayed as planar images. As such, the reflections and shadowing applied in the reconstruction algorithm are vital to conveying depth perception and relations in the through plane (relative to the monitor screen). Compared with the results of volume rendering, the more realistic light emulation of cinematic rendering—although it does not affect spatial resolution—enhances evaluation of spatial relations, particularly in the through plane.
Cinematic rendering entails the use of complex high dynamic range rendering maps to create a natural lighting environment, as opposed to the synthetic light source of volume rendering. The result of this difference in lighting functions available in cinematic rendering is an overall more lifelike and visually appealing 3D image, compared with a volume-rendered image, that has a focus on improving depth and shape perception.
Postprocessing
One of the most important features of cinematic rendering is its postprocessing tools, which can be applied to generate, manipulate, and refine the final image.
View Options
Multiple view options can be applied to the final rendered image. Light map affords multiple possibilities that simulate different lighting conditions in the environment of the rendering scene. A central component of postprocessing in cinematic rendering is the different transfer functions that can be applied to convert the intensity and opacity values of the original CT datasets into a wide range of different predefined color palettes, allowing better enhancement of certain tissue types. The exposure option simulates the exposure to light time, which would affect the brightness of an image. Aperture and focal plane options, as in photography, affect the sharpness of varying portions of the image. These parameters can be manipulated to maximize the depth of field and therefore the evaluable data and volume in a given image (e.g., with a small aperture or distant focal plane) or to focus on a particular area of interest, blurring the volumes in the foreground and background (e.g., with a wide aperture and closer focal plane). Specular reflection (the reflection of light off a surface at one angle) and diffuse reflection (the reflection of light off a surface at multiple angles) are two important principles of light ray propagation that can be simulated in the postprocessing of a rendered image. Diffuse and specular reflection options simulate the reflection of light at different angles and one angle, respectively. Finally, the window level and width option, just as in conventional CT reconstructions, allows setting a range of colors to be displayed in order to include or exclude certain anatomic structures.
Masks and Clip Planes
Masks and clip planes allow image editing to expose and display an ROI. Masks, such as the heart-isolation mask, which isolates the heart from all other structures, can be applied. Similarly, specific anatomic features can be isolated or removed, such as the coronary tree, calcium and bone, and the lungs and airways. A clip plane and a crop box can also be applied to cut into rendered volume. The plane can then be rotated, tilted, or shifted to better display the ROI. A punch tool allows removal of regions out of the final image.
Potential Applications
The potential applications of cinematic rendering are numerous and varied, including medical education, communication among physicians and between physicians and patients, presurgical and preinterventional planning, early disease detection, and better description and classification of lesions.
Anatomy Teaching
Basic human anatomy, a core aspect of medical education, has traditionally been taught by means of cadaver dissection and textbook study. Medical imaging, however, has become an increasingly important tool in anatomy teaching [18]. Students responds positively to the inclusion of traditional planar, cross-sectional, and 3D rendered images in their anatomy curriculum [19–21]. Cinematic rendering has the potential to further enhance virtual anatomy and may have additive value with the inclusion of more photorealistic images that more closely simulate actual cadaveric specimens. The increased realism of cinematically rendered images compared with volume-rendered images may enable a smoother transition from cadaver through 3D representations to traditional planar CT reconstructions. Real-time manipulations, including masks, clip planes, and crop boxes, can facilitate a form of repeatable virtual dissection. Although cadaver dissection will likely remain an integral part of the medical education experience, students will be able to interact with the gross anatomy of multiple patients and innumerable disease states at any time and place a cinematic rendering workstation is available.
Patient Education
Cinematic rendering can enhance physician-patient communication and relationships, leading to more informed decisions and possibly improved compliance. Medical imaging is an increasingly essential part of medical communication [22, 23], allowing clinicians and surgeons to illustrate for patients the underlying pathologic features of their disease process and to map out possible treatments. However, interpretation of traditional gray-scale planar and cross-sectional images is rarely intuitive for patients. Volume-rendered images, although more palatable, are also clearly computerized reconstructions. Cinematically rendered images, however, are more akin to a photograph of an actual 3D model of the volume of interest and are a much more consumable form than a traditional medical image and can improve patient understanding of and engagement in their illness and treatment plan.
Surgical Planning
Another beneficial application of cinematic rendering is presurgical and preinterventional planning. Routinely used in orthopedic surgery and neurosurgery [24], image-based treatment planning is becoming increasingly implemented in other surgical specialties, such as cardiothoracic surgery, genitourinary surgery, and minimally invasive cardiac and vascular interventions [25–27].
Although surgical planning is classically done by mentally integrating multiple images, possibly obtained with different modalities, cinematic rendering generates an all-inone highly realistic 3D representation of the structures of interest with a single modality. Image manipulation allows visualization of anatomic details from different perspectives. The more realistic representation of images than achieved with volume-rendered, cross-sectional, or planar imaging should more closely match what clinicians see in the operating suite, possibly reducing the impact of otherwise unexpected incidental findings and anatomic variants.
Three-Dimensional Printing
Three-dimensional printing is a rapidly evolving technology that has gained increased attention in the medical field [27, 28]. It is already being used both in medical education [29, 30] and for surgical planning in several specialties, including thoracic surgery, maxillofacial surgery, and interventional radiology [3, 25, 31–33]. The process is slow, however, and is not currently widely available. Because it depicts a photorealistic appearance of anatomic features with improved depth, shape, and structure delineation, cinematic rendering can be an alternative to 3D printing when the latter is not available.
Detection of Disease
Cardiovascular
Heart and coronary arteries—Owing to much improved image quality, sensitivity, and specificity, coronary CT angiography has become an increasingly important tool in coronary artery disease detection [34–38], congenital heart disease assessment [39, 40], treatment guidance [41, 42], and assessment before and after radiologic interventions [43–49]. The detection and grading of individual coronary artery stenosis will continue to rely predominantly on multiplanar 2D reconstructions; however, cinematic rendering can provide a detailed overview of the coronary artery tree, allowing rapid visualization of the extent and location of disease (Fig. 1). Cinematic rendering may also hold additive diagnostic value in identifying and classifying anatomically complex congenital anomalies and in the preprocedural and postprocedural assessment of proposed and previous interventions (e.g., coronary artery bypass grafting and valve replacement) (Fig. 2).

Intracranial and carotid arteries—CT angiography with volume rendering has had great accuracy in the detection of carotid artery disease and intracranial aneurysms and has been increasingly used for early disease detection and prevention of complications [50–52]. Given its complexity, carotid and intracranial vascular anatomy can be difficult to assess with 2D reconstructions. Cinematic rendering provides a more realistic representation owing to better resolution and increased depth perception, allowing all-in-one visualization and image manipulation of the carotid arteries and their branches (Fig. 3). As in the coronary arteries, stenosis detection and grading will likely remain within the purview of multiplanar reconstructions; however, cinematically rendered imaging may improve the detection of aneurysms. Furthermore, cinematic rendering will likely be useful in surgical and minimally invasive interventional planning, as in intravascular catheter selection and embolization planning.

Aortic and lower extremity runoff—The aortoiliac and lower extremity arteries are additional common sites of vascular diseases, such as atherosclerosis, aneurysms, dissection, and stenosis, which can lead to serious and sometimes fatal complications. Cinematic rendering may again be useful for determining disease location, extent, and severity and to help in follow-up and treatment (Figs. 4 and 5).

Pulmonary
Parenchymal disease—One of the most frequently encountered abnormalities of the lung parenchyma found with CT is pulmonary nodules [40]. Detection and characterization of nodules remain frustrating but important challenges, both in patients with known lung or metastatic cancer and in patients found to have incidental pulmonary nodules. Volume rendering has superior sensitivity in detecting lung nodules compared with maximum intensity projection (p < 0.001; sensitivity, 76.5–97.3%) [31]. Cinematic rendering, with its superior image quality and 3D representations, may serve as an additional tool for nodule detection and characterization (Fig. 6).

Tracheobronchial tree—Three-dimensional imaging has established utility in the evaluation of the tracheobronchial tree. Lesions of the airways can be difficult to analyze in classic CT reconstructions given the complex tracheobronchial anatomy that is rarely restricted to one plane. Virtual CT bronchoscopy (with volume rendering) is routinely used for the detection and characterization of tracheal stenosis and tracheomalacia. Cinematic rendering likewise will allow visualization of the entire airway volume in a variety of representations. This may afford better assessment of the relations of airway lesions to surrounding structures; establishment of lesion length, severity, and anatomic landmarks for preprocedural planning (e.g., stent placement); and generation of intraluminal views that will simulate actual bronchoscopic findings.
Abdominal
Three-dimensional reconstructions can be an important tool in liver and renal imaging, specifically in assessing tumor burden, vascularity, and anatomic relations to other structures, possibly improving tumor classification and staging and helping to determine resectability. Moreover, 3D imaging can be a helpful tool for preoperative and postoperative assessment of renal and liver grafts, particularly in establishing vascular anatomy. The complexity of gastrointestinal anatomy also lends itself to 3D visualization techniques, as already evidenced in virtual colonoscopy. Cinematic rendering may have an adjunct role in detecting gastrointestinal diseases such as occlusions, strictures, herniation, and tumors and aid physicians and surgeons in treatment planning (Figs. 7 and 8).

Musculoskeletal
Cinematic rendering may be helpful in several aspects of musculoskeletal imaging. Trauma (both osseous and soft tissue) is often complex, as in extremity and facial fractures that cross multiple planes and involve numerous fragments. Volume-rendered imaging has some utility in establishing the extent of abnormalities, and cinematic rendering may have additive value owing to its improved shadowing and depth perception. Cinematic rendering may be more sensitive than traditional postprocessing techniques in detecting small fractures and fracture fragments, because the entire volume is included in one view (Figs. 9 and 10). Cinematic rendering may also be a useful tool in the evaluation of congenital bone abnormalities, bone and soft-tissue tumors, and spinal lesions, providing more anatomic information to guide interventions and follow-up.

Limitations
As with other 3D reconstruction algorithms, the quality of the final image created by the cinematic rendering algorithm depends highly on the quality of the original dataset. As such, any reduction in image quality, as by physics-, patient-, or scanner-based artifacts, will be reflected in the 3D reconstruction, reducing its quality.
Cinematic rendering, being a more complex algorithm than volume rendering, requires higher computational power to generate the 3D images, and for every change made to the image, the software has to restart the calculations. As a result, postprocessing times are currently longer than in traditional methods, hindering the use of this technique in routine clinical practice, where time is a limited resource. In our experience, depending on the size of the dataset and the image desired, generating images can take anywhere from 3 minutes, when the data are simply loaded to cinematic rendering (Fig. 10B), to 5 minutes, as in Figure 7B, in which a mask and a clip plane were applied to generate the image. In the future, graphics processing unit–based rendering as opposed to the central processing unit–based rendering currently used could improve rendering speed.
Finally, because this software was only recently introduced, further research is needed to determine whether the more lifelike visualization will improve diagnostic precision and communication over those achieved with volume rendering and other reconstructions in clinical practice.
Conclusion
Three-dimensional reconstruction techniques such as volume rendering are playing an increasingly important role in different medical fields. Cinematic rendering, a new 3D reconstruction technique, provides a lifelike representation of imaging data and may have potential for enhancing diagnostic utility compared with volume rendering, particularly in terms of a more natural and physically accurate image with improved shape and depth perception. This novel technique has the potential to create a new paradigm in virtual anatomic visualization. Further studies are needed, however, to determine the possible diagnostic utility of cinematically rendered compared with volume-rendered and cross-sectional images alone.
