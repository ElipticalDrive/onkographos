#### Abstract

 Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has gained attention for its high fidelity and real-time rendering. However, adapting 3DGS to different camera models, particularly fisheye lenses, poses challenges due to the unique 3D to 2D projection calculation. Additionally, there are inefficiencies in the tile- based splatting, especially for the extreme curvature and wide field of view of fisheye lenses, which are crucial for its broader real-life applications. To address these challenges, we introduce Fisheye-GS, a novel method that recalculates the projection transformation for fisheye cameras, and then optimizes the rendering process with the reduced number of overlapped tiles. Our method reduces computational redundancy and memory usage, significantly improving rendering speed and enabling 3DGS to be effectively used with a wider range of camera perspectives. With our optimization, we achieved a maximum rendering efficiency of 570 FPS at a high-definition resolution of 1752 × 1168.

# 1 Introduction

 The advent of high-resolution fisheye cameras has transformed how we capture and interact with the world, providing a 180° or greater field of view that offers unique perspectives. These perspectives are essential for various real-world applications. In immersive virtual reality (VR), fisheye cameras enable the creation of panoramic environments that enhance the user experience by providing a more comprehensive and realistic field of vision [1]. This is crucial for applications such as VR gaming [2], virtual tourism [3], and training simulations [4], where a wide and uninterrupted view can significantly improve immersion and usability. In surveillance systems, fisheye cameras allow for broader area coverage with fewer cameras, reducing costs and complexity. They are particularly valuable in security and monitoring scenarios, such as in large public spaces[5], retail stores [6, 7], and traffic monitoring [8], where capturing every angle with high detail is vital for effective oversight and incident management.

 However, integrating fisheye imagery into novel view synthesis poses several challenges due to the inherent curvature and distortion. While Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [10] handles the distortions of fisheye lenses through its ray sampling approach, which can easily accommodate various camera models, the state-of-the-art 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [9] faces difficulties. The original 3DGS approach relies on perspective projections and is less adept at managing the extreme curvature and wide field of view of fisheye imagery, leading to inaccuracies and inefficiencies in rendering.

 In this work, we aim to integrate fisheye cameras with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) to fully unleash the potential of fisheye cameras in real-world applications that require real-time rendering performance. By combining the wide field of view provided by fisheye lenses with the efficient rendering capabilities of 3DGS, we seek to overcome the challenges associated with the curvature and distortion inherent in fisheye imagery. We identify the key challenge lies in accurately representing

To achieve this, we need to apply projection with geometric distortion for fisheye cameras while keeping their wide field of view and avoiding information loss from undistortion. Developing fast and memory- efficient methods is also vital to handle the computational demands of rendering high-resolution fisheye imagery within 3DGS without compromising quality.

 Our proposed method, Fisheye-GS, is the first work that seamlessly integrates fisheye camera data into 3D Gaussian Splatting, unlocking new possibilities for applications in virtual reality, gaming, security, and more. We achieve this by:

 Extensive experiments and comparisons with existing methods demonstrate the effectiveness of Fisheye-GS in producing high-quality, distortion-free imagery, making it ideal for various 3D graphics applications.

# 2 Related Work

# 2.1 Novel View Synthesis

 Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). NeRF[10] represents a breakthrough in the field of novel view synthesis, which aims to generate realistic images of a 3D scene from any viewpoint. NeRF employs a fully connected deep neural network to encode a continuous volumetric scene function. This function maps a 3D point and a viewing direction to the corresponding RGB color and volume density. To enhance rendering quality, some methods directly refine the point sampling strategy in ray marching, resulting in more accurate modeling of the volume rendering process [11]. Others enhance rendering by reparameterizing the scene, which creates a more compact scene representation and simplifies the learning process [12, 13]. Despite many optimizations, NeRF still consumes a lot of time and computational power during the rendering process. NeRFs are typically represented as global MLPs encoding the entire scene space. However, this approach can be inefficient and costly when reconstructing complex and large-scale scenes. Some works focus on innovations in scene representations. They propose voxel grids [14] and octree-based [15]. Although these methods  greatly enhance rendering speed, achieving real-time rendering remains difficult due to the inherent ray marching strategy in volume rendering.

 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). 3D Gaussian Splatting [9] models the scene with a set of Gaussian ellipsoids, allowing efficient rendering through rasterizing these Gaussian ellipsoids into images. While 3DGS produces high-quality reconstruction results, there is room for improvement in its rendering capabilities. [16, 17, 18, 19, 20] are designed to improve rendering performance under sparse views and challenging inputs. These methods enhance the robustness and accuracy of the rendered images in difficult scenarios.For anti-aliasing and improving both efficiency and quality, approaches like [21, 22, 23] are utilized. These techniques help in reducing aliasing artifacts and ensuring smooth transitions in the rendered images, providing a more visually appealing output.Anchor-based methods, such as [24, 25] focus on improving efficiency and quality by using hierarchical structures and anchor points. These methods enhance the computational efficiency and scalability of the rendering process, allowing for faster and more accurate scene reconstructions.

#### 2.2 Various Camera Models for Radiance Fields

 NeRF-based approaches. NeRF often assumes a pinhole camera model for simplicity, as it directly relates 3D points to their 2D projections without considering lens distortions. Specifically, NeRF uses rays cast from the camera center through the image plane to sample points in 3D space for volume rendering. NeRF's flexibility in handling various camera models, as also integrated in [26], enables the reconstruction of a wide range of scenes from different types of input images.

For fisheye cameras, the direction d can be derived using the fisheye projection model. Given a 2D pixel coordinate (u, v) on the image plane, the corresponding 3D direction

$$\mathbf{d} = [x, y, z]^T = [\sin(\theta) \cos(\gamma), \sin(\theta) \sin(\gamma), \cos(\theta)]^T,$$

 where θ is the angle from the optical axis, and γ is the azimuthal angle represented with spherical coordinate. These angles can be computed as with θ = π v h , γ = 2π u w , where h and w are the height and width of the image, respectively.

 Various advanced models have also been developed for NeRF-based methods. For example, (1) SC-NeRF [27] addresses camera self-calibration for non-linear distortions by jointly learning scene geometry and camera parameters without traditional calibration objects. (2) For 360-degree panorama rendering, 360FusionNeRF [28] uses geometric supervision and semantic consistency with re- projection techniques and depth map extraction to improve geometry and realism. Similarly, OmniNeRF [29] combines omnidirectional distance fields (ODF) with neural radiance fields to enhance 3D reconstruction, particularly at scene edges. PERF [30] synthesizes novel views from a single 360-degree panorama using collaborative RGBD inpainting and an inpainting-and-erasing strategy, allowing extensive 3D roaming and demonstrating superiority in real-world applications. (3) Neuro Lens [31] proposes end-to-end optimization of the image formation process, including lens distortion and vignetting, integrating seamlessly with NeRF to enhance fidelity and realism.

 3DGS-based approaches. 3D Gaussian Splatting primarily works with perspective projections. Adapting it to work with fisheye and panoramic camera models, instead, involves additional considerations to handle specific distortions and projections associated with these types of images involving various approximations in the rasterization process. [32] suggests pre-processing fisheye images to correct for radial distortion, effectively converting them to a more standard perspective view before applying Gaussian Splatting. Alternatively, one can modify the projection process within the Gaussian Splatting pipeline to approximate the non-linear mapping, ensuring correct positioning and scaling of splats to fix the distortion. For example, [33] projects 3D Gaussians onto the tangent plane of the unit sphere, addressing challenges in spherical projections and enhancing rendering quality by leveraging structural priors from panoramic layouts. [34] introduces an optimal projection strategy for Gaussian splatting by analyzing projection errors and minimizing artifacts through function optimization, refining the projection of Gaussians to improve the overall accuracy and visual quality of 3D scene representations.

#### 118 3.1 3D Gaussian Splatting

119 Definition. 3DGS represents a 3D model as a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian functions and renders 120 images via a differentiable tile-based rasterizer. Each Gaussian function is designated as the mean µ ∈ R 3 , anisotropic covariance Σ ∈ R 3×3 121 ,

$$C(\mathbf{x}) = e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}, \quad (1)$$

 where x is an arbitrary position in the world coordinate system. To constrain the positive semidefinite form, the covariance is defined as Σ = RSST RT , where R ∈ SO(3) is the rotation matrix represented by quaternions and S = diag(s1, s2, s3) is the scaling matrix.

125 Transformation. To project the Gaussian into the 2D plane, 3DGS applies the viewing transformation from the camera coordinate system φ ∈ R 3 → R 3 126 and the projective transformation ϕ ∈ R 3 → R 2 . We can model φ as the linear transformation φ(x) = Wx + b where W ∈ R 3×3 127 and b ∈ R 3 . Then the projected mean µp ∈ R 2 and covariance Σ ′ ∈ R 2×2 128 are given as follows:

$$\mu_p = \phi(\mu_c) = \phi(\mathbf{W}\mu + \mathbf{b}), \quad \Sigma' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\Sigma\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{J}^T, \quad (2)$$

where µc ∈ R 3 is the mean in the camera coordinate system and J ∈ R 2×3 129 is the Jacobian of ϕ. Notice that the Σ′ 130 is the affine approximation. Then the projected 2D Gaussian can be modeled as an 131 ellipse on the image plane.

Rendering. To render the color C ∈ R 3 132 of the pixel, 3DGS uses differentiable alpha blending from 133 ellipses of N Gaussians intersecting the pixel according to the depth order:

$$\mathbf{C} = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad (3)$$

 where α is evaluated by projected 2D Gaussians. To determine intersections between pixels and 2D Gaussians, 3DGS applies the technique of dividing the image plane into several 16 × 16 tiles. Firstly each ellipse determines its axis-aligned bounding box. Then 3DGS traverses each bounding box to store the intersections and sorts them by tile IDs and depths. Finally, each pixel in a certain tile performs alpha blending on intersected Gaussians to render its color.

#### 139 3.2 Equidistant Projection Model for Fisheye Cameras

 Fisheye cameras use wide-angle lenses with a large field of view, creating images with significant radial distortion to capture extensive scenes. These lenses project images with optical distortion at the edges, making fisheye cameras ideal for applications in target tracking and security. Unlike pinhole cameras, which form images on a plane, fisheye cameras form images on a spherical surface. Their projection models include equidistant, equisolid angle, orthogonal, and stereographic projections.

#### 158 4.1 The Projection and Gradients of the Means

Suppose the mean in camera space is µc = (xc, yc, zc) T 159 and the mean in pixel coordinates is µp = (xp, yp) T 160 , the projection of means is simply applying the equidistant projection model ϕ:

$$\mu_p = \phi(\mu_c). \quad (7)$$

Suppose the loss is L, then the gradients of projected mean ∂L 161 ∂µ′ can be calculated by vanilla 3DGS. To compute the mean in camera space ∂L ∂µc 162 , we can apply the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mu_c} = \frac{\partial \mu_p}{\partial \mu_c} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mu_p}. \quad (8)$$

Notice that ∂µp ∂µc 163 is the Jacobian matrix of equidistant projection ϕ:

$$\mathbf{J}_\phi = \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}_p}{\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}_c} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{f_x x_c^2 z_c}{l_z^2 l_2} + \frac{y_c^2 \theta}{l_z^3} & x_c y_c \left( \frac{f_x z_c}{l_z^2 l_2} - \frac{\theta}{l_z^3} \right) - \frac{f_x x_c \theta}{l_2} & -\frac{f_x x_c \theta}{l_2} \\ x_c y_c \left( \frac{f_y z_c}{l_z^2 l_2} - \frac{\theta}{l_z^3} \right) & \frac{f_y y_c^2 z_c}{l_z^2 l_2} + \frac{x_c^2 \theta}{l_z^3} & -\frac{f_y y_c \theta}{l_2} \end{bmatrix}, \quad (9)$$

where l2 is the square length of the vector µp , i.e., l2 = x 2 c + y 2 c + z 2 c 164 .

#### 178 4.3 Implementation Details

 Although the CUDA kernels of 3DGS efficiently parallelize the training and rendering of the pinhole camera model, there remains redundant computation in the tile-based splatting. Thus we have optimized CUDA kernels to reduce the number of overlapped tiles during rendering and expanded the original CUDA implementation to accommodate fisheye cameras.

 In 3DGS, we need to determine the tiles covered by the Gaussian ellipsoid projection for subsequent rendering. The original 3DGS implement calculates the bounding box of the ellipse based on the major axis after the Gaussian ellipsoid projection, and then all tiles within the bounding box participate in the key-value binding and alpha blending of this Gaussian ellipsoid. However, many tiles within the bounding box are not actually covered by the projected ellipse, which introduces a lot of computational redundancy for subsequent key-value binding and alpha blending.

 In the original 3DGS, the size of the ellipse is often calculated according to the 3σ principle (σ is the standard deviation), without considering the opacity of the Gaussian ellipsoid. In statistics, it is generally believed that in a Gaussian distribution, data points with an absolute difference from the mean greater than 3σ are extremely rare. Considering that the contribution to the final result is related to the opacity of the Gaussian ellipsoids, we use Eq. \(13\) as a substitute to narrow the definition of the ellipse size:


$$r = \sqrt{2 \ln(255\sigma) \max(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)}. \quad (13)$$

 As shown in Fig. 2\(b), the bounding box defined by the major axis of the ellipse in the original 3DGS is often not tangent to the ellipse, which may encompass more tiles that do not need to be rendered. We can directly calculate the axis-aligned bounding box that is tangent to the ellipse, with a height of 2h and a width of 2w, which is proved in the Appendix:

$$w = \sqrt{\Sigma'_{11}}, \quad h = \sqrt{\Sigma'_{22}}. \quad (14)$$

 Although eliminating redundant key-value pairs is the most effective method for optimizing compute workload and memory usage, we have also implemented additional optimizations to further reduce

 memory consumption and save memory bandwidth. Due to the significant size differences among the Gaussian ellipsoids, the original implementation of the second function exhibited severe load imbalance issues. We modified the thread organization in this section so that each warp is responsible for the computation of one Gaussian ellipsoid. Additionally, since the output order of key-value pairs is not important because they will be sorted, we replaced the original prefix sum with dynamic scheduling implemented by atomic addition, eliminating the array point\_offsets and the CUDA library call for computing the prefix sum. Finally, we fused the two CUDA kernels preprocessCUDA and duplicateWithKeys, eliminating the array radii, which stores the temporary information of bounding box size. Another significant advantage of kernel fusion is that it prevents all accesses to the array shs from being concentrated in the first CUDA kernel preprocessCUDA, thereby avoiding the reduction in computational efficiency caused by the bottleneck of memory bandwidth.

# 5 Experiments

# 5.1 Experimental Setup

 Dataset. We have adopted 6 indoor scenes from Scannet++[32] dataset as our real world dataset, where the images are captured by fisheye cameras and resized to the resolution of 1752 × 1168. The dataset also provides point cloud, camera intrinsic and poses of each view as COLMAP[36] SfM format. We have also generated a synthetic dataset from [10] with Blender 3.6 by equidistant fisheye camera models. We generate 200 frames with the resolution of 800 × 800 by rotating around the model for the Lego scene, and 100 frames for other scenes. For each scene, we render the dataset separately for the field of view (FOV) settings of 120◦ and 180◦ . We use 7/8 of each scene as the training set for optimization and the rest as the test set for evaluation.

 Optimization. We have initialized Gaussians from COLMAP point cloud on Scannet++ dataset and randomly initialized 100000 points for synthetic dataset. For each, we train our models for 30000 iterations and use the same hyperparameters, training strategy, and loss function as [9]. For the Scannet++ dataset, we randomize the background color for each iteration to model the walls of indoor scenes. Black backgrounds are used for training on synthetic datasets.

 Baseline and Evaluation. We compare our method against original 3DGS[9] as our baseline. Because 3DGS is applied for the pinhole camera model, we have undistorted the images from the Scannet++ dataset for training. The baseline shares the same training technique as our method. For fair comparison, we evaluate the baseline according to ground-truth images before undistortion, using our Fisheye-Gs on both training results.

#### 5.2 Evaluation

 Visual Quality. We use visual PSNR, SSIM[37] and LPIPS[38] to test our method and the baseline on 6 real indoor scenes captured by fisheye cameras. As is shown in table 1, our method outperforms the baseline on each metric and scene on average. Fig. 3 shows that the baseline performs well on the central area of the image. However, artifacts frequently appear on the edge. Besides the interpolation of the undistortion process can lead to the loss of information, the difference between the pinhole and fisheye camera models plays a key role. The fisheye camera has a larger field of view (FOV) than the pinhole camera, so the undistortion process will clip the scenes on the edge of the image plane. Then the 3DGS struggles to learn the edge area of the scene. In other words, the corners of the scene can be more frequently viewed by our Fisheye-GS, add more geometry constraints, and show fewer artifacts in novel views.

 Evaluation on Synthetic Dataset. As is shown in table 3 and fig 4, our Fisheye-GS can successfully model the scene from random initialization, and adapting different views. The results show our method can adapt to larger distortions like Lego in fig 4.

# 263 5.3 Limitaion

 Our method derives and implements the fisheye camera model into 3DGS, and optimizes the implementation to achieve better rendering performance. However, our method ignores the approximation error of the projection. And because we explicitly compute the gradients, our method struggles to be generalized to other distortion models. Due to the lack of fisheye camera datasets, our method has not be tested by various types of scenes, like unbounded scenes, city-scale scenes, etc.

# 269 6 Conclusion

 This paper introduces a new approach, Fisheye-GS, aimed at addressing the adaptability issues of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) across different camera models, particularly fisheye lenses. Fisheye-GS recalculates the projection transformation for fisheye cameras and optimizes the rendering process by reducing the number of overlapped tiles, effectively minimizing computational redundancy and memory usage. These optimizations significantly enhance rendering speed, achieving a maximum efficiency of 570 frames per second at a high-definition resolution of 1752 x 1168. This paper demonstrates that Fisheye-GS can effectively expand the application range of 3DGS technology to various camera perspectives, which is crucial for its broader real-life applications. We anticipate that the outcomes of this study will provide robust support and new perspectives for research and development in related fields.
