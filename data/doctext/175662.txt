## Abstract

N-dimensional splatting extends 3D Gaussian Splatting with view direction and time to model view-dependent and dynamic effects. Each primitive is lifted into a joint distribution over position and conditioning variables, then conditionally sliced at each rendering query. N-DGS and UBS share this step across Gaussian and Beta kernels: recovering the conditional mean, opacity, and covariance requires per-primitive matrix inversions and regression products in every forward pass. We introduce direct conditional parameterization, replacing this covariance-derived readout with explicit per-effect parameters: a Cholesky precision factor for opacity and a factorized query-to-position regression operator with learnable per-dimension coupling. Unrestricted conditional coordinates recover a valid joint covariance; our factorization adds spatial-scale and coupling priors. Applied to both kernels, the parameterization yields *d*GS (Gaussian) and *d*BS (Beta). Across five static and dynamic benchmarks, they match or exceed their covariance-derived counterparts on quality, with gains up to +1.26 dB in the main MCMC evaluation. Production slicing is 6.9–7.7× faster than on-the-fly covariance-derived conditioning; complete-render profiling gives 1.25–1.91× speedups on four trained scenes. Direct conditioning provides a structured, kernel-agnostic alternative for N-dimensional splatting. Project page: .

## 1 Introduction

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [Kerbl et al., 2023] achieves photorealistic novel-view synthesis at real-time speeds through explicit 3D Gaussian primitives. Static primitives, however, struggle with specular reflections, temporal dynamics, and view-dependent geometry.

Recent work extends 3DGS along two orthogonal axes. The first is kernel shape: alternatives to the Gaussian kernel include Beta kernels [Liu et al., 2025a, 2026], generalized exponentials [Hamdi et al., 2024], and Student-t distributions [Zhu et al., 2025]. The second is dimensionality: N-dimensional Gaussians (N-DG) [Diolatzis et al., 2024], refined as 6DGS [Gao et al., 2025a] and 7DGS [Gao et al., 2025b] (collectively N-DGS), lift each primitive into a joint distribution over 3D position and conditioning variables (view direction with C=3, view+time with C=4). At render time, the (3+C) dimensional primitive is conditionally sliced at the current query (a view direction or timestamp) using the conditional Gaussian formulas: the mean shifts, the opacity is modulated by a kernel evaluated at the query, and the spatial covariance receives a correction term. Universal Beta Splatting (UBS) [Liu et al., 2026] combines the two axes by replacing the Gaussian opacity decay with an anisotropic Beta kernel while inheriting the same conditional-slicing structure. N-DGS and UBS together represent the leading N-dimensional splatting formulations across Gaussian and Beta kernels.

Despite their different kernels, N-DGS and UBS share the same conditional-slicing step, and this is where the per-primitive cost concentrates. Read-out of the three effects requires inverting the query block, constructing a regression matrix, and applying a covariance-correction product (Table 1\). These operations run once per primitive in every forward pass, so the total cost grows linearly with primitive count, which on large scenes like Mip-NeRF 360 reaches millions. Their ambient Cholesky coordinates also entangle the optimization of opacity precision, position regression, and conditional covariance, and conditioning can become numerically fragile when the query block is ill-conditioned.

Our insight. N-DGS uses ambient joint-covariance coordinates, although rendering needs only conditional spatial covariance, query precision, and query-to-position regression. Recovering them requires inversion and Schur complementation. Appendix C proves that this unrestricted operational tuple is a smooth, globally bijective coordinate chart for joint Gaussian covariances, with block-triangular reconstruction, an explicit inverse, and a nonvanishing coordinate-volume Jacobian throughout the SPD interior. *d*GS directly parameterizes a structured factorization of these rendering-aligned coordinates. It therefore both removes expensive operations and separates position from opacity coupling.

We introduce direct conditional parameterization for N-dimensional splatting. Opacity uses a directly parameterized Cholesky precision factor, and position uses a factorized regression operator with learnable per-dimension coupling that decouples position from opacity by default and recovers full coupling on demand. Because the change touches only the conditioning step, the same parameterization is kernel-agnostic and carries across kernel families: we instantiate it as *d*GS (Gaussian) for the direct counterpart of N-DGS and *d*BS (Beta) for the direct counterpart of UBS. Pairing each direct method with its covariance-derived counterpart (*d*GS vs. N-DGS, *d*BS vs. UBS) isolates the contribution of direct conditioning from the choice of kernel.

Our contributions are:

- Direct conditional parameterization for N-dimensional splatting: a kernel-agnostic alternative that explicitly parameterizes opacity precision and a factorized query-to-position regression operator. We prove that the underlying unrestricted operational tuple forms a smooth, globally bijective coordinate chart for joint Gaussian covariances, with an explicit inverse and a nonvanishing coordinate-volume Jacobian.
- Two kernel instantiations and matched-pair evaluation. We develop the formulation for the Gaussian kernel (*d*GS, the direct counterpart of N-DGS) and show that the same conditioning extends to the Beta kernel via a Beta-kernel instantiation (*d*BS, the direct counterpart of UBS). Pairing each direct method with its covariance-derived counterpart isolates the gain from direct conditioning from the choice of kernel.
- Efficient CUDA implementation. Fused, register-level CUDA kernels for direct conditioning and the N-DGS/UBS baselines achieve 6.9–7.7× faster production slicing than on-the-fly covariance-derived conditioning. Inference caching controls and complete-render profiling quantify the resulting speedups. The N-DGS baseline was previously only available as a PyTorch implementation; we provide an optimized CUDA comparison.

## 2 Related Work

3D Gaussian Splatting. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [Kerbl et al., 2023] achieves real-time novel view synthesis through explicit 3D Gaussian primitives with spherical harmonic color encoding, and has inspired a wide range of extensions to anti-aliasing [Yu et al., 2024, Yan et al., 2024], densification [Kheradmand et al., 2024], compression [Lee et al., 2024, Morgenstern et al., 2024], and semantic modeling [Shi et al., 2024, Zhou et al., 2024]. The original formulation remains static, relying on spherical harmonics alone for view dependence and offering no mechanism for temporal dynamics or view-dependent geometry.

Alternative Kernel Designs. Several works replace the 3D Gaussian kernel with alternatives for better geometric fidelity: generalized exponentials [Hamdi et al., 2024], truncated [Liu et al., 2025b], second-order [Zhang et al., 2025], half-Gaussian [Li et al., 2025], and discontinuity-aware [Qu et al., 2024] variants; Beta [Liu et al., 2025a], Student-t [Zhu et al., 2025], and Gabor [Zhou et al., 2025] distributions; and alternative primitives such as convex shapes [Held et al., 2025], triangles [Held et al., 2026], and radiance meshes [Mai et al., 2026]. Orthogonal to kernel shape, Textured Gaussians [Chao et al., 2025] models spatially varying color and opacity, while Spherical Voronoi [Di Sario et al., 2026] provides a directional appearance representation. These works modify primitives or appearance models rather than replacing covariance-derived conditional slicing; UBS combines the Beta kernel with N-dimensional conditioning, discussed next.

N-Dimensional Splatting. To model view-dependent and temporal effects, recent methods lift primitives into higher-dimensional spaces, conditioning on view direction or time alongside position. 4DGS [Yang et al., 2024] adds a temporal dimension for dynamic scene reconstruction, and N-DG [Diolatzis et al., 2024] establishes a general framework for arbitrary-dimensional Gaussians. 6DGS [Gao et al., 2025a] jointly models spatial position and view direction; 7DGS [Gao et al., 2025b] adds time as well; we refer to the two collectively as N-DGS. Universal Beta Splatting (UBS) [Liu et al., 2026] extends the same paradigm to Beta kernels with per-dimension bandwidth control. N-DGS and UBS together cover the two kernel families currently used in N-dimensional splatting, and both inherit the covariance-derived conditioning step that our work replaces.

Dynamic Scene Modeling. NeRF-based methods model dynamics through deformations: D-NeRF [Pumarola et al., 2021] learns a canonical scene and a time-dependent deformation field, Nerfies [Park et al., 2021a] handles non-rigid motion, and HyperNeRF [Park et al., 2021b] captures topological changes. For Gaussian Splatting, 4DGS [Yang et al., 2024] slices native spatiotemporal primitives for real-time rendering. Dynamic 3D Gaussians [Luiten et al., 2024] optimizes persistent primitives' motion and rotation under local-rigidity constraints, while FreeTimeGS [Wang et al., 2025] allows Gaussians to appear at arbitrary times and locations with motion functions. Tracked and motion-based representations evolve 3D primitives explicitly; N-dimensional splatting instead obtains query-dependent primitives by conditional slicing.

## 3 Preliminary

3D Gaussian Splatting 3DGS [Kerbl et al., 2023] represents a scene as a collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians. Each primitive carries a mean µ ∈ R 3 , a covariance Σ ∈ R 3×3 factored as Σ = RSS⊤R⊤ with rotation R (quaternion) and diagonal scale S = diag(sx, sy, sz), an opacity α ∈ [0, 1], and view-dependent color via spherical harmonics. At render time, primitives are projected to 2D screen-space Gaussians, sorted by depth per tile, and alpha-composited front-to-back to produce the final pixel color. The per-pixel contribution of each primitive is α · KG(δ; Σ2D) = α · exp − δ ⊤Σ −1 2D δ , where δ is the offset from the pixel to the projected primitive center, Σ2D is the projected 2D covariance, and KG denotes the Gaussian kernel. The representation is static: it has no mechanism for temporal dynamics or view-dependent geometry.

N-Dimensional Splatting To capture view-dependent and temporal effects, N-DGS [Gao et al., 2025a,b] (Gaussian kernel) and UBS [Liu et al., 2026] (Beta kernel) parameterize each primitive as a joint distribution over position Xp ∈ R 3 and query variables Xq ∈ R C (view direction for C = 3, view+time for C = 4):

$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{X}_p \\ \mathbf{X}_q \end{pmatrix} \sim \left( \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_p \\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_q \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{pp} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{pq} \\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{pq}^\top & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{qq} \end{pmatrix} \right). \quad (1)$$

## 4 Our Approach

We replace the covariance-derived conditioning step with three explicit parameters per primitive: a Cholesky factor L for opacity precision, a spatial coupling factor Vpq for position, and a perdimension coupling vector Λ that ties the two. We develop the formulation for the Gaussian kernel below, yielding *d*GS, and show in Section 4.4 that swapping in the Beta opacity decay yields *d*BS.

#### 4.2 Opacity Conditioning

The Cholesky factor L is parameterized directly, with exponential activation on the diagonal so that Vqq = LL⊤ is symmetric positive-definite. Evaluating opacity reduces to a triangular matrix-vector product L ⊤δ followed by a squared norm, avoiding query-block inversion.

For C = 4 (view+time), all ten lower-triangular entries of L are learned. View–time off-diagonals start at zero but remain unconstrained. With δ = (δv, δt) ⊤, opacity is

$$\alpha_{\text{cond}} = \alpha \cdot \exp\left(-\lambda_0 \left( \delta_v^\top \mathbf{V}_{vv} \delta_v + 2\delta_t \delta_v^\top \mathbf{v}_{vt} + \delta_t^2 v_t \right) \right), \quad (7)$$

where Vvv, vvt, and vt are the view, cross, and time blocks of the full precision Vqq.

## 4.3 Position Conditioning

The position update \(6\) factors into the spatial coupling factor Vpq, the per-dimension coupling diag(Λ), and the precision Vqq shared with opacity:

$$\mu_{\text{cond}} = \mu_p + \underbrace{\bar{s} \Theta_{pq}}_{\mathbf{V}_{pq}} \underbrace{\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}) \mathbf{V}_{qq}}_{\tilde{\mathbf{V}}_{qq}} \delta.$$

The coupling Λ interpolates between two extremes per primitive: at Λ = 0, position is queryindependent (recovering *d*GS-O); at Λ = 1, position and opacity share the full precision Vqq, mirroring N-DGS's coupling structure. Precision-transformed query channels can learn different coupling strengths.

With Λ = 0, opacity conditioning remains active under Equation \(5\). A primitive can therefore be suppressed under an unfavorable view without moving its center; stronger coupling permits query-dependent motion with the same base spatial covariance.

Spatial scaling. We set Vpq = ¯s Θpq with ∥Θpq∥F = 1, giving ∥Vpq∥F = ¯s and ∥V (i) pq ∥2 ≤ s¯. This is an explicit spatial-scale prior on the coupling factor, not a bound on the final shift: the effective

regression is MdGS = Vpq diag(Λ)Vqq and the shift also depends on the query deviation. N-DGS instead obtains its regression operator indirectly from shared covariance blocks. Appendix D derives both forms and a basis-independent Gaussian bound; the ablation in Table 3 confirms the value of the prior, as removing s¯ degrades PSNR by 2.65 dB on NeRF Synthetic. The mean scale follows primitive size symmetrically across principal axes, while query precision and Λ retain separate control of opacity and position coupling.

#### 4.4 Extension to Beta Kernel: *d*BS

Direct conditioning touches only the slicing step (precision read-out, regression, and coupling), making it kernel-agnostic: any opacity decay that consumes the precision-Cholesky distance L ⊤δ can be substituted in. We instantiate this with a Beta opacity decay following UBS [Liu et al., 2026] to obtain *d*BS (direct Beta Splatting), the direct counterpart of UBS.

We follow the released UBS implementation used in our experiments. UBS evaluates opacity as α Q i (1 − tanh((Lˆ−1 q δ) i ))βi , with Lˆ qLˆ⊤ q = Σqq. *d*BS replaces this triangular solve with the direct product z = L ⊤δ while retaining the same bandwidth activation and coupling convention:


$$\alpha_{\text{cond}} = \alpha \cdot \prod_{i=1}^C (1 - \tanh(z_i^2))^{\beta_i}, \quad (8)$$

where βi = 4 exp(β raw i ) and Λβ = min(β/4, 1), as in UBS. The position update reuses the opacity bandwidth for coupling instead of a separate learned Λ. The precision factor, spatial factor, and directly stored conditional covariance transfer from *d*GS. Including the spatial Beta parameter, UBS and *d*BS each use 19/27 conditioning parameters for C = 3/4; N-DGS and *d*GS use 15/16 and 22/24, respectively. Appendix F gives the source-aligned UBS formulation.

The shared bandwidth gives opacity and position a consistent response to the query. Dividing β by four puts the coupling near unit magnitude when its raw parameter is near zero, while clipping prevents a larger bandwidth from increasing the coupling beyond one. Gaussian and Beta instances thus retain the same direct conditional structure, with a separate learned coupling in *d*GS and a bandwidth-derived coupling in *d*BS.

#### 4.5 CUDA Implementation

The original N-DGS methods (6DGS [Gao et al., 2025a], 7DGS [Gao et al., 2025b]) rely on PyTorch's autograd for the conditional Gaussian operations, so for a fair comparison we implement *d*GS and the N-DGS baseline with custom CUDA kernels. The kernel fuses the conditional position and opacity computation in a single pass (Algorithm 1 in Appendix B\), and *d*BS reuses the same pipeline with the opacity decay swapped (Algorithm 2\).

Kernel design. The kernel is templated on C (3 or 4) with compile-time loop unrolling, and each thread processes one primitive. The triangular product L ⊤δ uses only C(C + 1)/2 multiplications followed by a dot product, all in per-thread registers; the small 3 × C and C × C matrices fit entirely in registers, so the kernel allocates no intermediate tensors and avoids shared-memory traffic. The N-DGS baseline also fuses inversion, regression construction, and Schur correction, using a cofactor inverse for C = 3 and register-local Gauss–Jordan elimination for C = 4.

Kernel benchmarks. Table 1 reports production CUDA slicing at 1M primitives on an A100, excluding autograd and rasterization. Direct conditioning is 6.9–7.7× faster than optimized on-thefly conditioning. In a separate harness with materialized inputs, *d*GS remains 2.0–2.3× faster than inference-cached N-DGS and 1.8× faster than a factorized-query control (Appendix H\). Profiling four trained scenes gives 1.25–1.91× complete-render gains: shared rasterizer code still processes different learned footprints and tile workloads, limiting the benefit of faster slicing.

## 5 Experiments

## 5.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets. We evaluate the d-splatting family across five diverse datasets covering both static and dynamic scenes:

- Static scenes: (1) NeRF Synthetic [Mildenhall et al., 2020] with 8 synthetic scenes under controlled lighting for evaluating geometric reconstruction; (2) Mip-NeRF 360 [Barron et al., 2022] with 9 challenging unbounded real-world scenes for evaluating view synthesis; and (3) 6DGS-PBR [Gao et al., 2025a] featuring 7 physically-based scenes with complex view-dependent effects.
- Dynamic scenes: (4) D-NeRF [Pumarola et al., 2021] with 8 synthetic sequences featuring various deformation types; and (5) 7DGS-PBR [Gao et al., 2025b] containing 6 physically-based dynamic scenes with complex spatio-temporal-angular correlations.

Evaluation Metrics. We assess reconstruction quality using Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) [Wang et al., 2004], and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [Zhang et al., 2018]. We follow the 3DGS [Kerbl et al., 2023] evaluation protocol (joint-RGB PSNR, white background on NeRF Synthetic). We re-evaluate UBS [Liu et al., 2026] under this protocol; its released evaluator instead averages channel-wise PSNR and uses a black background on NeRF Synthetic. We also report training time (minutes), Gaussian count (K), and two rendering speeds: FPS uses a PyTorch reference implementation for slicing, while FPSCUDA uses the fused CUDA slicing kernels.

#### 5.3 Ablation Study

Table 3 ablates the two key design choices: the per-dimension coupling Λ and the spatial scaling s¯ in the coupling factor Vpq. All ablation runs use standard 3DGS densification [Kerbl et al., 2023] on NeRF Synthetic and 6DGS-PBR (the same protocol as the appendix Standard-Densification results). An unrestricted direct-regression control reaches 32.91/36.44 dB versus *d*GS's 33.91/37.75 dB, supporting the role of structure and optimization priors beyond direct readout (Appendix G\).

Learned Λ outperforms both fixed extremes. On NeRF Synthetic, learned Λ achieves 33.91 dB vs. 33.84 (Λ=0, opacity-only) and 33.78 (Λ=1, full coupling). The gap widens on 6DGS-PBR (viewdependent specular scenes): 37.75 dB vs. 36.54 (Λ=0, −1.21 dB) and 37.31 (Λ=1, −0.44 dB). These results suggest that the optimal coupling is scene-dependent and may be primitive-dependent: the diffuse NeRF Synthetic scenes prefer weak coupling (Λ=0 beats Λ=1), the specular 6DGS-PBR scenes prefer strong coupling (Λ=1 beats Λ=0 by +0.77 dB), and learned per-Gaussian Λ improves on both extremes on both datasets.

Spatial scaling is critical. Removing s¯ degrades PSNR by 2.65 dB on NeRF Synthetic (33.91 vs. 31.26) and 2.97 dB on 6DGS-PBR (37.75 vs. 34.78). Notably, an unscaled coupling factor is worse than no position regression at all (the Λ=0 baseline at 33.84 / 36.54 beats *d*GS w/o s¯ at 31.26 / 34.78). This supports the spatial-scale prior on Vpq analyzed in Appendix D; the effective regression and final shift additionally depend on diag(Λ)Vqq and the query deviation.

Scope and limitations. Our evaluation covers five static and dynamic benchmarks; other kernels and city-scale scenes require separate validation of memory use and visibility handling.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented direct conditional parameterization for N-dimensional splatting. Whereas N-DGS derives rendered quantities from a joint covariance through inversion and Schur complementation, *d*GS directly parameterizes conditional quantities with explicit spatial-scale and coupling priors. Unrestricted conditional coordinates preserve the joint Gaussian covariance family, while the structured factorization makes rendering parameters explicit and gives optimization direct control over their coupling. Across five benchmarks and two kernels, the resulting methods match or improve aggregate quality. Production slicing is 6.9–7.7× faster than on-the-fly conditioning, with smaller complete-render gains determined by downstream workload. Future work can extend the formulation to other primitive attributes, conditioning queries, and kernel families.

This establishes that the query precision, query-to-position regression operator, and rendered spatial covariance used by *d*GS are compatible with a valid joint Gaussian, even though *d*GS never constructs that covariance. The unrestricted coordinate map is a global diffeomorphism; *d*GS directly optimizes a structured factorization of these rendering-aligned coordinates.

$$\Sigma_{pp}^* = \Sigma_{\text{cond}} + \frac{1}{2\lambda_0} \mathbf{V}_{pq} \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}) \mathbf{V}_{qq} \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}) \mathbf{V}_{pq}^\top. \quad (26)$$

The resulting conditional mean and covariance are exactly

| $\mu_{\text{cond}} = \mu_p + \mathbf{V}_{pq} \text{diag}(\Lambda) \mathbf{V}_{qq}(\mathbf{q} - \mu_q),$ | $\Sigma_{p q} = \Sigma_{\text{cond}}.$ | (27) |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|------|
|                                                                                                         |                                        |      |

Proof. Apply Theorem C.1 with query precision Π and regression operator M. The query marginal has precision (Σ ⋆ qq) −1 = Π = 2λoVqq, so its conventional Gaussian exponent is


$$-\frac{1}{2}\delta^\top(\Sigma_{qq}^*)^{-1}\delta = -\lambda_o\delta^\top \mathbf{V}_{qq}\delta, \quad (28)$$

which is exactly the implemented opacity exponent. Moreover,

$$\Sigma_{pq}^(\Sigma_{qq}^)^{-1} = \frac{1}{2\lambda_o} \mathbf{V}_{pq} \text{diag}(\Lambda)(2\lambda_o \mathbf{V}_{qq}) = \mathbf{M}, \quad (29)$$

which is the implemented conditional-mean regression. The conditional covariance follows from Theorem C.1, and direct substitution gives the blocks in Equations \(25\) and \(26\).

As in standard Gaussian splatting, opacity uses an unnormalized Gaussian kernel rather than a normalized probability density. Consequently, the probabilistic interpretation concerns the quadratic form, conditional mean, and covariance structure; it does not require including the Gaussian density normalization factor in the renderer.

Corollary C.3 (Decoupling and full coupling). When Λ = 0*, Equation* \(24\) is block diagonal and position is query-independent although opacity remains query-dependent. When Λ = 1*, the full regression coupling is active,* M = VpqVqq*. Intermediate values continuously weight the precision-transformed query channels.*

## C.1 Determinant Structure

The outer factors in Equation \(15\) are unit block-triangular. Therefore

| $\det \Psi = \det(\Sigma_{\text{cond}}) \det(\mathbf{V}_{qq}^{-1}) = \frac{\det \Sigma_{\text{cond}}}{\det \mathbf{V}_{qq}}.$ | (30) |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|

The regression operator M does not appear. Geometrically, M acts as a block shear: it changes cross-covariance and the conditional mean without changing total covariance volume. The two determinant factors separately measure spatial conditional volume and query-space volume.

## C.2 Volume-Element Jacobian of the Coordinate Transformation

Use the standard Lebesgue coordinates on symmetric and rectangular matrices. The conditionalcoordinate map has a closed-form volume-element Jacobian.

Proposition C.4 (Coordinate-volume factor). For the map in Equation \(12\),


$$|\det D\Psi| = (\det \mathbf{V}_{qq})^{-(p+C+1)}. \quad (31)$$

For spatial dimension p = 3*, hence*

$$|\det D \Psi| = (\det \mathbf{V}_{qq})^{-(C^{(4)})}. \quad (32)$$

Proof. Factor the transformation into three block-triangular steps. First set Q = V−1 qq . Inversion on C ++ has Jacobian

$$\left| \det \frac{\partial \mathbf{Q}}{\partial \mathbf{V}_{qq}} \right| = (\det \mathbf{Q})^{C+1} = (\det \mathbf{V}_{qq})^{-(C+1)}. \quad (33)$$

Second, Σpq = MQ. Right multiplication of a p × C matrix by Q contributes

$$(\det \mathbf{Q})^p = (\det \mathbf{V}_{qq})^{-p}. \quad (34)$$

Finally, Σpp = Σcond + MQM⊤ is a translation in the Σcond coordinates and contributes determinant one. Cross derivatives between these steps lie below the corresponding diagonal Jacobian blocks and do not change the determinant. Multiplying the diagonal factors gives Equation \(31\).

For every Σcond ≻ 0 and Vqq ≻ 0, the coordinate-volume factor is finite and nonzero. Thus the conditional-coordinate map has no singularities in the interior of its SPD domain. The domain retains the usual boundary behavior when either SPD factor approaches its boundary, including when the conditional covariance or corresponding query covariance approaches singularity. The construction Vqq = LL⊤ with a strictly positive Cholesky diagonal keeps *d*GS in the interior with respect to query precision. This statement concerns the ambient (Σcond,M, Vqq) chart, not uniqueness of *d*GS's structured internal factorization of M.

Interpretation. The rendered spatial covariance, query precision, and regression operator are Gaussian regression coordinates. Direct conditioning optimizes these operational quantities rather than repeatedly recovering them from an ambient covariance by inversion and Schur complementation. The coupling is a block shear with an explicit inverse and determinant. Within the structured dGS factorization, Vpq supplies the spatial-scale prior, while diag(Λ)Vqq completes the regression operator and exposes learnable coupling.

## D Covariance-Derived versus Direct Regression Coordinates

The conditional mean depends on the regression operator ΣpqΣ −1 qq , not on the cross-covariance Σpq alone. This section derives that operator for N-DGS [Gao et al., 2025a,b] and contrasts its implicit covariance coordinates with the direct conditional coordinates of *d*GS. UBS [Liu et al., 2026] inherits the same covariance-derived structure.

Block-Cholesky derivation. Partition the joint Cholesky factor into spatial and query blocks:

$$\mathcal{L} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{D} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}, \quad \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times p}, \quad \mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}. \quad (35)$$

Here A and D are lower triangular. Multiplication gives

$$\Sigma = \mathcal{L}\mathcal{L}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top & \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top \\ \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^\top & \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^\top \end{pmatrix}, \quad (36)$$

and therefore

| $\Sigma_{pp} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T,$ | $\Sigma_{pq} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^T,$ | $\Sigma_{qq} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^T + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^T.$ | (37) |
|-----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
|-----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|------|

The actual N-DGS query-to-position regression operator is

| $M_{\text{N-DGS}} = \text{AB}^T (\text{BB}^T + \text{DD}^T)^{-1}$ | (38) |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
|-------------------------------------------------------------------|------|

Thus A affects both spatial covariance and cross-covariance; B affects both cross-covariance and query covariance; and D also affects query covariance. The operational regression is available only after forming and inverting the query block.

A valid cross-covariance bound. For spatial row i,

| $(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{pq})_{i:} = \mathbf{A}_{i:}\mathbf{B}^T$ , | $\ (\mathbf{\Sigma}_{pq})_{i:}\ _2 \leq \ \mathbf{A}_{i:}\ _2 \ \mathbf{B}\ _2$ . | (39) |
|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|

Moreover,

$$\|\mathbf{A}_{ii}\|_2^2 = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T)_{ii} = (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{pp})_{ii}. \quad (40)$$

In N-DGS, bounded Cholesky coefficients are multiplied by the query diagonal scales sq. Thus B = diag(sq)Be with |Bejk| ≤ 1, giving

$$\|\mathbf{B}\|_2 \leq \|\mathbf{B}\|_F \leq \sqrt{p} \|\mathbf{s}_q\|_2, \quad (41)$$

so

$$\|(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{pq})_{i,:}\|_2 \leq \sqrt{p} \|\mathbf{s}_q\|_2 \sqrt{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{pp})_{ii}}. \quad (42)$$

The off-diagonal entries of A contribute both to Σpq and to the actual marginal spatial extent (Σpp)ii. They should therefore not be interpreted as producing cross-covariance independently of spatial scale.

Cross-covariance is not displacement. The rendered N-DGS shift is

$$\Delta \mu_{\text{N-DGS}} = \Sigma_{pq} \Sigma_{qk}^{-1} \delta = M_{\text{N-DGS}} \delta. \quad (43)$$

A bound on Σpq alone therefore does not bound the shift: the inverse query covariance and the query deviation also enter. In Euclidean coordinates, a narrow or ill-conditioned query covariance can amplify the regression operator.

Invariant Gaussian bound. A basis-independent statement follows from the normalized crosscovariance

$$\mathbf{K} = \Sigma_{pp}^{-1/2} \Sigma_{pq} \Sigma_{qq}^{-1/2}. \quad (44)$$

Positive-definiteness of the joint covariance implies ∥K∥2 &lt; 1 by the Schur complement. With the whitened query deviation h = Σ −1/2 qq δ,

$$\Delta \mu_{\text{N-DGS}} = \Sigma_{pp}^{1/2} \text{Kh}, \quad (45)$$

and hence

$$\left\| \Sigma_{pp}^{-1/2} \Delta \mu_{\text{N-DGS}} \right\|_2 \leq \left\| \Sigma_{qq}^{-1/2} \delta \right\|_2. \quad (46)$$

Measured in marginal spatial standard-deviation units, the shift is bounded by the query deviation in query standard-deviation units, irrespective of basis or Cholesky ordering.

Direct regression coordinates in *d*GS. The direct formulation parameterizes

| $\mathbf{V}_{qq} = \text{LL}^\top$ | $\mathbf{M}_{\text{dGS}} = \mathbf{V}_{pq} \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}) \mathbf{V}_{qq}$ | $\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{cond}}$ independently. | (47) |
|------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|------|
|                                    |                                                                                               |                                                |      |

Its spatial coupling factor is normalized as

| $\mathbf{F}_{pq} = \bar{s} \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{pq}$ , | $\ \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{pq}\ _F = 1$ , | (48) |
|--------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|------|
|--------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|------|

which gives

| $\ \mathbf{V}_{pq}\ _F = \bar{s}$ , | $\ \mathbf{V}_{pq}^{(i)}\ _2 \leq \bar{s}$ . | (49) |
|-------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|------|
|                                     |                                              |      |

This is an explicit spatial-scale prior on the coupling factor, not a bound on the effective regression or rendered shift. Those additionally depend on diag(Λ)Vqq and δ.

The conditional-coordinate theorem in Appendix C makes the comparison precise. N-DGS indirectly parameterizes conditional covariance, regression, and query precision through the ambient joint Cholesky factor. *d*GS makes the corresponding operational quantities explicit and exposes a learnable coupling strength, without forming a query-block inverse during slicing. The benefit is this computational and parameterization separation, rather than an invalid geometric bound for N-DGS.

Empirically, removing the spatial factor s¯ degrades PSNR by 2.65 dB on NeRF Synthetic and 2.97 dB on 6DGS-PBR (Table 3\), supporting the spatial-scale prior on Vpq. On two trained MCMC scenes with six views each, opacity-weighted ∥∆µ∥/s¯ has median 0.29–0.30 and 99th percentile 4.8–7.0 for N-DGS, versus 2.9–4.8 at the 99th percentile for *d*GS. The ordering reverses when normalized by the smallest principal axis, supporting s¯ as an isotropic prior rather than a per-axis optimum.

## F UBS Formulation

The released UBS implementation used in our experiments evaluates βi = 4 exp(β raw i ) and Λβ = min(β/4, 1). Its covariance-derived slicing is

$$\mathbf{Z}^{\text{UBS}} = \hat{\mathbf{L}}_q^{-1} \boldsymbol{\delta}, \quad d_i = \tanh((z_i^{\text{UBS}})^2), \quad (50)$$

$$\alpha_{\text{cond}} = \alpha \prod_{i=1}^C (1 - d_i)^{\beta_i}, \quad (51)$$

$$\mu_{\text{cond}} = \mu_p + \Sigma_{pq} \Sigma_{qq}^{-1} \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_B) \boldsymbol{\delta}, \quad (52)$$

$$\Sigma_{\text{cond}} = \Sigma_{pp} - \Sigma_{pq} \Sigma_{qq}^{-1} \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\beta}) \Sigma_{qp}, \quad (53)$$

where Lˆ qLˆ⊤ q = Σqq. The tanh values are capped below one for finite opacity evaluation. *d*BS (Equations \(8\) and \(9\)) retains the same opacity decay, bandwidth activation, and clipped coupling. It replaces the triangular solve with L ⊤δ, replaces the covariance-derived regression with Vpq diag(Λβ)Vqq, and directly stores Σcond.

Frame-time decomposition. Slicing is a vertex-like preprocessing stage before projection, tile binning, sorting, and rasterization. If its baseline fraction is f and its speedup is s, an unchanged remainder would give the Amdahl speedup [(1 − f) + f /s] −1 . In Table 10, slicing accounts for 42–64% of baseline frame time. The downstream code is shared, but the learned primitives have different projected footprints and tile workloads: the measured remainder is 1.10–1.68× slower for *d*GS. The observed end-to-end gains are consequently 1.25–1.91×. These per-scene frame times differ from arithmetic-mean FPS across all nine Mip-NeRF 360 scenes (Table 2\).

## I Limitations and Societal Impact

Limitations. The evaluation covers Gaussian and Beta kernels. Larger-scale scenes may change memory, visibility, and partitioning bottlenecks; other kernels and domains require validation.

Societal impact. Faster neural rendering can benefit simulation, telepresence, robotics, and content creation, but can also facilitate unauthorized reconstruction of private spaces. This work introduces no new data sources or user-facing decision systems.

Existing assets. All datasets and baseline methods used in this work are public research assets and are cited in the paper. We use NeRF Synthetic, Mip-NeRF 360, D-NeRF, 6DGS-PBR, and 7DGS-PBR only for academic evaluation and do not redistribute the datasets or baseline code.
