Abstract Learning and inferring underlying motion
patterns of captured 2D scenes and then re-creating
dynamic evolution consistent with the real-world
natural phenomena have high appeal for graphics
and animation. To bridge the technical gap between
virtual and real environments, we focus on the inverse
modeling and reconstruction of visually consistent and
property-verifiable oceans, taking advantage of deep
learning and differentiable physics to learn geometry
and constitute waves in a self-supervised manner. First,
we infer hierarchical geometry using two networks,
which are optimized via the differentiable renderer.
We extract wave components from the sequence of
inferred geometry through a network equipped with a
differentiable ocean model. Then, ocean dynamics can
be evolved using the reconstructed wave components.
Through extensive experiments, we verify that our new
method yields satisfactory results for both geometry
reconstruction and wave estimation. Moreover, the
new framework has the inverse modeling potential to
facilitate a host of graphics applications, such as the
rapid production of physically accurate scene animation
and editing guided by real ocean scenes.
Keywords inverse modeling; surface reconstruction;
wave modeling; ocean waves; differentiable
rendering (DR)

Introduction and motivation
In recent years, realistic ocean simulation and its
photorealistic rendering have achieved great success
in simulators, movies, and video games. However,
they are insufficient for the most complex VR/AR
scenarios and the digital-twin modeling of natural
phenomena, which rely heavily on real-world scene
reconstruction, prediction, evolution, and physical
editing. For example, video or geometry-based
end-to-end fluid predictions contradict physics and
lack realism due to the fluid’s changeable geometry.
Hence, real-world ocean reproduction and physicsguided editing in virtual environments remain urgent
research priorities. In the real world, ocean surface
motion is always simulated via a sum of wave
trains. To perform model-based deformation rather
than directly deforming 3D shape, we would like
to reconstruct not only ocean geometry but also
component wave trains.

Various oceanographic studies have estimated wave

analyzed frequency from height data; these include

affect the normal of the macro-geometry, and smallamplitude waves determine the microfacet roughness.

both re-rendering and inferring wave trains.

• Inverse modeling of hierarchical ocean geometry
from video. We propose a self-supervised deep

2
Related work
We briefly review relevant work on ocean modeling
and rendering, differentiable rendering, and 3D
reconstruction below.
2.1
Ocean surface modeling and rendering
Ocean modeling approaches in computer graphics
are mainly based upon physical fluid simulation,
wave geometric modeling, and wave spectrum-based
modeling. The first kind of approach simulates the
sea surface based on the Navier–Stokes equations,
using them to calculate in detail the motion of water
particles. The ocean dynamics are clear and realistic,
but the computation is complex [5]. Wave shape
modeling methods consider the ocean surface moves
as a superposition of waves; the shape of each wave
can be described by a different mathematical equation.
The theory developed from early sinusoidal models [6]
to the later Peachy model [7] and Gerstner model, or
trochoids proposed by Fournier and Reeves [8]. The
Gerstner model solves the Euler fluid equations for
gravity waves in deep water exactly. The advantage
of this method is that the wave model is simple,
the solution of the mathematical function is easy
to obtain, and the ocean wave model is similar
to the real motion. However, due to use of a
simple mathematical function, the generated wave
models are solitary, weakly random, and only ideal
for calm sea surface simulation. In oceanography,
empirical wave spectrum approaches are based
on aggregating observed real ocean data into
mathematical distributions. The main wave spectra
include the Pierson–Moskowitz spectrum, Jonswap
spectrum, generalized A, B spectrum, Tessendorf
spectrum, and TMA spectrum.
The Pierson–
Moskowitz spectrum gives the energy distribution
of gravity waves as a function of their frequency.
Hasselmann et al. [4] proposed the Jonswap spectrum
by multiplying the Pierson–Moskowitz spectrum by
an extra peak enhancement factor to improve the
fit to various scenes, and then extended this model
with wave direction parameters [9]. The research
in Ref. [10] fitted the TMA spectral form to over
2800 wind-sea spectra to test its viability and to
determine if any parametric relationships could be
281
established linking the spectral parameters to the
external wind field. These methods are costly, but
do improve real-time results and realism. Bruneton
et al. [11] and Podee et al. [12] combined the ideas of
using wave spectra and wave geometry equations to
simulate the ocean surface for low amplitude motions.
Breakers or splashes generated by waves of higher
amplitude are usually simulated by physical methods
[13]. Research has focused on adding interaction
details to the large open ocean surface by combining
physical models. Huang et al. [14] simulated ships,
splashes, and waves on a vast ocean by a hybrid timestepping algorithm combining an FLIP domain and
an adaptively remeshed boundary element method
(BEM) domain for the incompressible Euler equations.
Xiong et al. [15] modeled complex vortex–interface
interaction problems on the free surface by combining
the expressiveness of the Clebsch wave function for
describing vortex structures with the capability of
level-set functions to follow interfacial dynamics.
Analyzing the above methods, we combine the
expanded three-dimensional trochoids wave and the
ocean wave spectrum to model an animated ocean
surface; we implement the process as a differentiable
ocean model.
Research into ocean rendering dates back to
the work of Fournier and Reeves. Tessendorf [16]
presented a sophisticated lighting model for realistic
reproduction of ocean waves, while Ashikhmin et
al. [17, 18] presented a light transport approach for
the complex lighting effects of the ocean. However,
traditional physically-based rendering is timeconsuming and impractical for real-time applications.
Hu et al. [19] and Schneider and Westermann [20]
studied real-time water surface rendering. Bruneton et
al. [11] accelerated rendering with high performance
hardware and incorporated more sophisticated
lighting models, including reflection, refraction, and
Fresnel terms. Podee et al. [12] approximated the
reflection direction distribution for the water surface
as an elliptical Gaussian distribution and integrated
the reflection contribution over the rendering interval
time to avoid reflection aliasing and flickering. While
these methods use a pipeline to render images, they do
not meet the requirement of differentiability. We aim
to combine differentiable rendering with sophisticated
lighting ocean rendering to realize our differentiable
ocean renderer.

Differentiable rendering
Differentiable rendering (DR) differentiates the
rendering operation, allowing it to be utilized
to optimize parameters or reconstruct scenes
from observations. Existing approaches can be
roughly divided into two categories based on mesh
rasterization and ray tracing. For example, OpenDR
[21], DiRT [22], and the Neural Mesh Renderer
(NMR) [23] manually define approximated gradients
of the rendering operation to alter the position
of the face. In contrast, SoftRasterizer [24] and
DIB-R [25] redefine rasterization as a continuous
and differentiable function, allowing gradients to be
computed automatically. Cole et al. [26] first sampled
the surface using non-differentiable rasterization,
and then applied differentiable, depth-aware point
splatting to produce the final image. For ray tracerbased or volume-based differentiable rendering, Li et
al. [27] represented each 3D point as a multivariate
Gaussian and performed occlusion reasoning with grid
discretization and ray tracing. Such methods require
an explicit volume to perform occlusion reasoning.
Mitsuba 2 [28] proposed differentiable rendering
for implicit surface representations with a focus on
reconstructing rigid objects. Inspired by this work, we
adopt the SoftRasterizer method to make our ocean
rendering model differentiable by representing the
ocean surface by a mesh.
2.3
3D reconstruction
The last few years have clearly shown the effectiveness
of neural networks for 2D and 3D reasoning [29, 30].
At first, most 3D reconstruction methods relied on
supervised training methods and costly annotations,
making it challenging to collect all properties of 3D
observations. Xie et al. [31] reconstructed the ocean
surface normal using an image-to-image translation
network to cycle translate images and 2D surface
normal maps. Recently, there has been a surge
in efforts to integrate graphics rendering processes
into neural network pipelines. Specifically, the
common 3D self-supervision pipeline is applied by
integrating the rendering layer with the predicted
scene parameters, and then computing a loss by
comparing the rendered and input images in various
ways. Liu et al. [24] in SoftRasterizer, and Chen
et al. [25] in DIB-R, predicted the meshes of objects
by differentiable rendering. Some ray tracing-based
DR are combined to reconstruct the volumes of
objects. Other research has considered a variety
of applications, such as image-based training
of 3D object reconstruction [32], human pose
estimation [33], hand pose estimation [34], and face
reconstruction. NeRF [35] reconstructs 3D geometry
using an implicit representation. For fluids, Thapa
et al. [36] reconstructed the dynamic fluid surface
using a deep neural network and a differentiable
renderer that renders the refractive distortion of an
underwater reference background image. Li et al. [37]
inversely modeled gas parameters to reconstruct highresolution scenes. Qiu et al. [38] used a conditional
generative model to rapidly reconstruct temporal
density and velocity fields for gaseous phenomena
based on the sequences of two projection views.
Inspired by this work, we reconstruct the ocean
surface using DR-based deep learning networks, using
ocean surface characteristics to anticipate surface
normal and roughness simultaneously.
3
Algorithmic overview
The goal of our method is to reconstruct dynamic
ocean models from videos for prediction or editing.
Because the dynamic ocean is moved by a
superposition of waves that comprise the ocean
wave spectrum, we estimate component waves from
the original data for dynamic ocean reconstruction.
However, direct estimation is infeasible.
Due
to the causality linking surface geometry and
component waves, we adopt a two-step framework,
first reconstructing the geometry of the ocean
surface and then estimating wave parameters. In
a wave spectrum, the wavelengths vary from a
few millimetres to several hundred meters, and
their frequencies also change accordingly. Some
high-amplitude waves affect surface displacement,
while very small-amplitude waves have a negligible
impact on the surface displacement, as seen at
a viewing distance. Some waves with high and
middle amplitude have an impact on the macrosurface
normal, while some minor waves affect the roughness
of the microfacets. Thus, we reconstruct ocean
surface geometry at two explicit and implicit levels,
the normal of the macrosurface, as well as the
roughness of the microfacets, and estimate the
whole component wave trains based on the twolayer geometry. Overall, to make realistic and
vivid animations of the ocean surface, we need to
Dynamic ocean inverse modeling based on differentiable rendering
recover accurate 3D geometry, including normal
and roughness, and estimate the implicit geometry
representation, i.e., the component wave parameters.
Given a sequence of monocular images {It }n ,
we use two encoder–decoder neural networks to
reconstruct the ocean hierarchical geometry Gt from
each image It . The hierarchical geometry includes its
surface normal Nt and roughness Rt , corresponding
to the two sub-networks respectively. Thus, we can
acquire a sequence of ocean geometry {Gt }n . We
then use a multilayer perceptron (MLP) network
to estimate the wave trains from these geometric
sequences. Each wave is represented by a set of
parameters, including the amplitude hi , the angular
frequency ωi , and the wave direction θi . Ocean
rendering can be regarded as the inverse of geometry
reconstruction, while ocean modeling by superposing
several wave trains to generate the geometry is the
inverse of component wave estimation. Hence, we
train our networks for geometry reconstruction and
wave estimation using a differentiable renderer and
a differentiable ocean model as loss functions in
a self-supervised way. In other words, we extract
the hierarchical geometry from the image, then
render it with known extrinsic variables into a new
image Iˆt , and ensure consistency between Iˆt and
the input image It . Similarly, we estimate wave
parameters using an MLP network and synthesize a
time series of surface geometry using a differentiable
ocean model. Then, we measure the consistency of
the generated and the input geometry of the MLP
network. This procedure is summarized in Fig. 1. Data
generated by networks is marked by “ˆ” to distinguish
it from ground truth, which is omitted without
ambiguity.

We simulate the ocean surface with discrete wave
spectra, where we sample N wave trains from the
spectrum and express the parameter set of each wave
as {hi , ωi , θi }. Then, we superpose these surface
waves. In practice, the details of surface geometry
that need to be modeled in ocean images depend
on the viewing distance. Waves with wavelengths
longer than the size of a pixel have an impact on
the macrosurface. Waves with a wavelength less
than the size of the pixel (i.e., sub-pixel waves), not
only have no effect on surface height but also cause
signal aliasing. Instead, sub-pixel waves affect the
microfacets rather than the macrosurface. Therefore,
we employ a Nyquist filter to calculate the weight
of each wave being used, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b).
The blue line represents the weights for filtering the
long waves that affect displacement. The red line
represents the weight for filtering medium to long
waves that affect the normal of the macrosurface.
Finally, the green line represents the weight for
filtering the short waves that affect the roughness
of the microsurface. Further details can be found
in Bruneton et al.’s work. We use the Nyquist filter
weights to superpose waves to generate the hierarchical
ocean geometry.
The wave spectrum determines the characteristics
of the ocean surface. In turn, given a sequence of
surface geometry, constituent waves can be deduced,
similar to wave spectrum estimation, which is the
inverse of ocean surface modeling. In our framework,
we use the model not only to simulate ocean datasets
but also to estimate constituent waves by making the
model differentiable, as described in Section 4.4.
4.2
Differentiable ocean rendering
The most recent ocean animation study portrays the
ocean surface using hierarchical geometry, including
displacement, surface normal, and roughness, which
achieves good results in both geometric simulation
and realism of rendering. We adopt three hierarchical
models for ocean geometry to provide a detailed
surface. Specifically, we model the surface using a
triangle mesh that is driven by large waves at the
grid level for geometry deformation. We next project
and interpolate the grid into image space. For give
detail, we add more medium-length waves at the
pixel level to the normal computed using the triangle
mesh. The roughness of the microfacet in each
pixel is estimated by measuring the slope variance
of these little waves. The image can be rendered
using the normal and roughness values for each
pixel. When implementing the algorithm, instead
of rasterizing in a traditional rendering pipeline, we
use the SoftRas method in Ref. [24], which provides
a continuous and differentiable rasterization function.
The preceding description gives all information
needed for computation of the entire hierarchical
geometry. We consider the reflected light from the
Sun and sky dome, as well as the refracted light
from the water. Overall, we compute displacements
to alter the geometry and compute normals and
roughness to render images using our differentiable
ocean renderer under given lighting conditions. The
renderer is used to both generate the realistic images
as a dataset and to determine the loss to optimize
the related networks. Given an ocean image and its
Dynamic ocean inverse modeling based on differentiable rendering
corresponding geometry, the differentiable renderer
can also be used to determine the camera and lighting
information for the image by iterative optimization.

Geometry reconstruction network

surface are closely related to the ocean image
during rendering, we reconstruct them from ocean
photographs by combining a deep learning network
with differentiable rendering. Specifically, given a

5
Experiments
5.1
Data preparation and network training
We generated the dataset with our differentiable
ocean modeling and ocean rendering algorithms,
which were implemented using the Taichi (https://
taichi.graphics/) framework due to its automatic
differentiation feature. Our dataset includes ocean
image sequences, normal, roughness, wave
parameters, and camera position and lighting
conditions. To ensure sufficient variation in physical
motions and dynamics, we randomly sampled the
wave trains for various wave spectra and used
random camera and lighting setups. The dataset has
2000 generate scenes with varying initial conditions;
each scene has 10 frames. In order to test the ability
to generalize to new scenes absent from the training
set, we randomly selected 200 completed scenes
as the test set. To monitor model overfitting and
to determine the number of training epochs, we
randomly divided the remainder into a training set
and a validation set in the ratio 9:1.
Our networks were implemented in Python 3.8
using PyTorch 1.11.0. Our networks were optimized
using Adam with ε = 10−8 , β1 = 0.9, and β2 = 0.999
on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 SUPER GPU.
We split training into two phases. Sub-net1 and
sub-net2 were trained together using loss function
LTotal for 200 epochs. We alternately trained the
four auxiliary discriminators and the generator, as

typically done for GAN training [42]. The wave
parameter estimation network was then trained for
100 epochs using the loss function LGeom on data
generated by sub-net1 and sub-net2.

Evaluation of geometry reconstruction
We evaluated our sub-net1 and sub-net2 for ocean

whereas we train our network using a differentiable
renderer as a loss function. In addition, we add
a further structure to reconstruct the roughness of
microfacets. Thanks to these improvements, the
reconstruction result is significantly improved, and
our framework supports more complicated editing
such as varying camera position and varying lighting.

Applications
6.1
Image-based parameter fitting
Our framework can extract imaging parameters
from observed images, such as illumination, camera
positions, and sea colors. Firstly, the surface
normal and roughness can be reconstructed using
the methods described above. Then the lighting
parameters can be optimized iteratively using the
differentiable renderer: we use our ocean renderer to
generate a new image with the reconstructed normal,
roughness, and a series of initial parameters, and then
match the rendered results to the input original image.
Due to our renderer’s differentiability, the gradient
of the lighting parameters can be calculated using
gradient propagation, allowing the parameters to be
optimized incrementally. The lighting parameters
include the color of sky light, the color of sunlight,
and the direction of the sunlight. An example of
such optimization was run for 400 iterations using
Adam; we plot the convergence of loss and various
parameters during iteration in Fig. 9. The loss
has reduced to 10−6 and all optimized parameters
converged to their ground truth values. A more

Limitations
Our networks for reconstructing geometry have high
generality for ocean scenes, while our proposed model
is based on the deep water waves model using Pierson–
Moskowitz and Jonswap spectra, which do not work
well near coasts and shores. Our algorithm shares the
spectral model’s limitations: restricted by the wave
model, the network for wave estimation only works
for calm deep oceans. For more complicated scenes,
e.g., rough oceans or large-scale interactions between
the ocean and objects immersed or floating in it, the
wave spectrum cannot be estimated. Furthermore,
the scenes may not contain white caps, which develop

We did not consider shadows because, for distant

8
Discussions and conclusions
To reproduce a dynamic ocean in a virtual
environment, based on real ocean video, this study
has detailed a revolutionary ocean inverse modeling
method using images. It takes advantage of deep
learning, differentiable rendering, and a differentiable
ocean model. To drive the ocean motion, we not only
reconstruct ocean surface hierarchical geometry from
images but also estimate the component waves of
the surface geometry. Specifically, we use two subnetworks to extract the normal of the macrosurface
and the roughness of the microfacets and train them
in a self-supervised manner with a differentiable
ocean renderer as a loss function. An MLP network
then estimates component wave parameters from
a sequence of hierarchical geometry, using the
differentiable ocean model as a loss function. The
dynamic ocean can be reconstructed and forecast
using approximated waves. Ocean scenes can be reedited flexibly, e.g., to change illumination and vary
views. Our differentiable renderer and ocean model
