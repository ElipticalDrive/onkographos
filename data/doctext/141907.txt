Sparse Voxels Rasterization: Real-time High-fidelity Radiance Field Rendering | alphaXiv

Abstract
We propose an efficient radiance field rendering algorithm that incorporates
a rasterization process on adaptive sparse voxels without neural networks or 3D
Gaussians. There are two key contributions coupled with the proposed system.
The first is to adaptively and explicitly allocate sparse voxels to different
levels of detail within scenes, faithfully reproducing scene details with
65536365536^3655363 grid resolution while achieving high rendering frame rates. Second,
we customize a rasterizer for efficient adaptive sparse voxels rendering. We
render voxels in the correct depth order by using ray direction-dependent
Morton ordering, which avoids the well-known popping artifact found in Gaussian
splatting. Our method improves the previous neural-free voxel model by over 4db
PSNR and more than 10x FPS speedup, achieving state-of-the-art comparable
novel-view synthesis results. Additionally, our voxel representation is
seamlessly compatible with grid-based 3D processing techniques such as Volume
Fusion, Voxel Pooling, and Marching Cubes, enabling a wide range of future
extensions and applications.

Reconstructing a place from photographs and then flying a virtual camera through it is the promise of novel-view synthesis: rendering a captured scene from viewpoints that were never photographed. The two leading approaches pull in opposite directions. Rasterized 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) is fast, but its overlapping primitives can be sorted in the wrong depth order and are hard to turn into clean geometry. [p1] Grid-based volumes make structure and ordering explicit, but ray casting through them makes rendering slower. [p1] SVRaster's move is to rasterize sparse voxels instead: use voxels whose size adapts to the scene, and order them according to the viewing ray's direction.
A fly-through of a bicycle scene rendered with SVRaster, from the authors' project page.
Drawing an explicit volume like a primitive
A radiance field is a representation from which colors can be rendered along viewing rays. SVRaster stores one explicitly, with no neural network, no 3D Gaussians, and no sparse-point prior from structure-from-motion. The scene is a set of voxels sitting at the leaves of an octree-style spatial partition, with different sizes in different places. Density varies trilinearly inside each voxel, interpolated from values at its eight shared corners. Spherical-harmonic coefficients encode view-dependent color, approximated as constant within a voxel for efficiency. [p3] [p3]
Rendering follows the rasterization recipe. Voxel corners are projected into the image, each voxel is assigned to the image tiles it covers, and its density, color, and normal are preprocessed. Voxels are then sorted per tile and composited front to back.
Sorting is where the trouble lies. Ordering voxels by center, or by nearest corner, can place a smaller voxel behind a larger one when it should be in front. The paper instead remaps Morton order, a space-filling traversal order for the octree, according to the signs of the viewing ray's direction components; in 3D there are eight such sign cases. [p4] [p5] This is the proposed ordering rule and a correctness argument, not a measured explanation for any speed or quality difference.
Schematic of the ordering problem. Panel (a) shows cases where sorting by center or nearest corner places a smaller voxel behind a larger one. Panel (b) shows how the Morton traversal in a 2D world changes with the quadrant the ray points toward. Panel (c) shows a multilevel Morton-code example for a ray directed up and right.
Spending detail only where it is needed
The second ingredient is adaptive allocation. During training, voxels whose maximum contribution across the training views is too small are removed. Voxels with higher loss-related priority are subdivided, while subdivision is suppressed where the training views sample a voxel too sparsely. [p6] The aim is fine voxels only where the photographs call for them, rather than a scene filled uniformly with tiny cells.
The ablation tests this on two scenes, the indoor Bonsai and the outdoor Bicycle. Adaptive SVRaster reaches LPIPS 0.200 and PSNR 28.01, against 0.326 and 25.37 for a uniform 512³ grid. LPIPS is a learned perceptual distance where lower is better; PSNR is peak signal-to-noise ratio in dB where higher is better. Speed is similar, at 171 FPS for adaptive and 190 FPS for uniform 512³. The uniform 1024³ setting ran out of memory on the authors' 24-GB GPU. The table caption does not say whether the scores are averaged over the two scenes. [p8] The result supports the argument that adaptive sizing improves quality without the memory blow-up of a finer uniform grid; it does not isolate every component of the system.
Adaptive voxel sizing against uniform foreground resolutions of 256³, 512³, and 1024³, with Plenoxels at 640³, reporting LPIPS, PSNR, and FPS. Uniform 1024³ is marked out of memory.
How it stacks up against 3DGS
On Mip-NeRF360, averaged over four indoor and five outdoor scenes, the base SVRaster setup scores LPIPS 0.219 versus 0.257 for 3DGS on the paper's corrected intensity scale, while PSNR is 27.33 versus 27.45 dB. [p19] Rendering speed, measured in frames per second on test-set images with a 3090 Ti, is 121 FPS versus 131, and the paper notes it varies substantially by scene. [p7] [p7] The 3DGS baseline uses COLMAP sparse points, which SVRaster does not. The fair reading is comparable quality and speed, not a general win.
The boundaries are visible. On Tanks&Temples, where the training views vary substantially in exposure, SVRaster shows brightness boundaries and floaters, and its PSNR and FPS are worse than 3DGS even though its LPIPS is better; the authors attribute this to the exposure variation. [p8] The method also uses more GPU memory and produces larger models than 3DGS. [p7] Its maximum grid resolution of 65536³ is a representational limit, not a dense allocation. And the paper says popping from incorrect ordering is not a major factor in its numerical results, so the benchmark scores should not be credited to the sorting rule. [p8]
Structure Is the Shortcut
The paper's bridge between the two camps is to keep explicit voxel structure, draw it with rasterization, and let training decide where detail goes. The adaptive-versus-uniform ablation is the clearest evidence that this allocation, rather than simply a finer grid, is what makes the voxel representation workable.
