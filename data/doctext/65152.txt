Volumetric Display
All of the three-dimensional imaging techniques described up to this point have been
two-dimensional representations with enhanced depth cues to create the illusion of a third spatial dimension. However,
as Wheatstone stated, no matter how complex they are, they are still two-dimensional and ultimately flawed. This is
because they lack the ability to appease the eyes need for occular accomodation in experiencing a three-dimensional world.
Spatial three-dimensional displays will allow the eye to focus at different depths throughout the display and truly
experience all three dimensions. The first method introduced is a volumetric display, in which colored voxels (volume + pixel)
are addressed and illuminated by two scanning laser beams to create a three-dimensional picture. This technique is
still in its infancy, but it is worth further investigation because it is rich not only in fundamental science from different
fields but also in skillful, detailed engineering.
Research groups had addressed the possibility of such an endeavor as early as the early 1970's, but lacked
the required exitation sources or display materials to make it feasible(1). The solution came from fiber optic industry in the
form of high quality rare-earth ion doped glasses. These glasses are transparent to visible light, except for when two infrared beams (invisible to the
naked eye) of appropriate wavelength intersect in the medium. Then a two photon absorption process occurs in which visible light is generated
or "upconverted" from the infrared (see figures 13a and b).
In this process, the active ion at energy level E1absorbs a photon from the first infrared beam of wavelength
l12, and is excited to an energy level E2,where it remains for a
lifetime t2. If the second infrared
beam illuminates this ion during this lifetime, it is further excited to an energy level E3 by a photon at
wavelength l23. It remains there for a lifetime t3, before
relaxing back down to level E1 through the emission of visible light (Downing, 1186). It is apparent from this process that
a voxel will be illuminated only where the two laser beams intersect and nowhere else. Therefore, by scanning the two beams throughout the
display volume allows for complicated three-dimensional patterns to be drawn.

In order to achieve full color displays, it is necessary to identify three different dopant materials
with three different upconversion processes for emission of the color primaries. Couple that with the need for the materials
to have suitable energy levels and flourescence lifetimes, and you have no easy task at hand. This is where this project was able to
benefit from the work in another field. Much research has been done on the absorption properties of the trivalent rare earth ions for
their use in fiber amplifiers, and it turns out that they are efficient at absorbing infrared radiation and reaching higher metastable
quantum states (Downing, 1186). They can then relax from these levels by emitting visible light. Praseodymium, erbium, and thulium
can be used to generate red, green, and blue light, respectively from infrared excitation. Figure 14 shows the
the emission spectra of three dopants.

With the general principles established, it is then possible to design a system with full color
three-dimensional display. Due to the possibility of cross-talk, thin layers of alternately doped glass are stacked rather than fully
doping one large volume of glass. Linear arrays of infrared diode lasers are used instead of a single pair of lasers not only because
of speed, but also because of power considerations. For a single pair of lasers to scan the entire volume at rates necessary for video,
the lasers can spend very little time exciting the individual voxels, meaning they must deposit more energy over that short time for
sufficient flourescence. The powers required to do this would be above the damage threshold of most glasses (Downing, 1188).
Therefore, it is preferrable to use many lasers, and refresh the voxels more frequently. Figure 15 shows a schematic diagram for a
volumetric display.

display consisting of stacks of rare earth doped glass and linear arrays of ifrared diode lasers (Downing, 1188).
This technique is capable of rendering dynamic three-dimensional scenes, with the advantages of
occular accomodation, and its ability to be viewed from any angle. Its does, however, have two
major drawbacks. Since the display medium is transparent, it lacks the ability to produce occlusion. Therefore, this display
system would not be ideal for video. However, there are applications where this characteristic would actually be an advantage. Those
include visualization of three-dimensional data (MRI, CAT), tracking of aircraft positions, and computer aided design of three-dimensional
structures (2). The other obvious drawback of this display technique is cost. Although this technique is still fairly new, a prototype
system was developed at Stanford in which three-dimensional color pictures were created in a one inch cube. The estimated cost
for a ten inch cube was estimated at $80,000. Sufficient interest and mass production may reduce this cost and make this display
type financially affordable in the future.
