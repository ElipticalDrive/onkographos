## Abstract

Most conventional swept volumetric displays do not allow for direct physical interaction because their rigid, high-speed sweep screens make touch dangerous or impractical. However, several existing approaches enable direct interaction with volumetric content through the use of devices such as re-imaging plates and flexible screens. A major challenge in implementing direct interaction systems lies in the high demand for latencies, but it is, in general, difficult to achieve low latencies with swept volumetric displays because their refresh rates are limited by the physical sweep periods.

This paper reports yet another type of interactive swept volumetric display using a half mirror to realize a truly 3D augmented reality mirror system. It enables a "pseudo-direct" interaction by aligning the volumetric content with the user's mirror image without occlusion problems inherent in the existing direct-interactive systems. This configuration, which presents the reflected real body and the displayed content closely together without occlusion, faces a more severe demand on latencies. To address this, we propose a new low-latency control method of a swept volumetric display to make the displayed content swiftly track the mirrored target movement. The proposed method dynamically updates each slice of

the volumetric content in response to the latest pose of the tracked target, without increasing the sweeping rate of the screen.

Experiments demonstrate that the proposed method effectively maintains image fidelity at a moderate speed of target movement while significantly reducing perceived latency, enabling smooth and natural pseudo-direct interaction with volumetric content.

## 1 Introduction

Swept volumetric displays project images onto a screen that sweeps through space, leveraging the persistence of vision to form true 3D images [21]. This technology generates light points in actual space, eliminating the need for special eyewear and resolving the vergence-accommodation conflict common in stereoscopic displays [32]. As



a result, users can experience highly immersive volumetric images from multiple viewpoints, with multiple users, and without the discomfort or fatigue often associated with conventional stereoscopic methods.

However, swept volumetric displays have a drawback: typical systems use rigid screens that pose a risk of injury if touched due to high-speed motion, making direct interaction with the volumetric images infeasible.

Several authors attempted to enable direct interaction. Bouzbib et al. [6] proposed a volumetric display using a flexible material as its diffuser, and Hunter et al. [15] proposed an aerial volumetric display using a re-imaging plate. However, even with these systems, dynamically interacting with displayed content remains a challenge because direct interaction systems require high responsiveness to user movement compared to indirect systems [26]. In general, it is difficult to achieve low latency with swept volumetric displays because their rendering refresh rates are limited by the physical sweep period, as shown in Fig. 2(a).

In this paper, we investigate a configuration in which a volumetric display is positioned behind a half-mirror. Through the half-mirror, the mirror image of the interacting object and the volumetric image appear co-located in space, allowing users to interact with the volumetric image in a “pseudo-direct” manner. This configuration not only offers higher usability than indirect interaction methods such as 3D mice or hand gestures [3] but also enables new forms of 3D visual expression by avoiding the occlusion problem, i.e., the content cannot be displayed where a real object or user body blocks projection, inherent in existing direct interaction methods [6, 15]. This occlusion-free nature of our system will make the requirement of responsiveness more severe.

A straightforward approach to reduce latency is to increase the rendering refresh rate of volumetric images, which can be achieved by speeding up the sweep rate by introducing, for example, an optical sweep mechanism [34]. However, it sacrifices the number of slices that make up a volumetric image under the condition that the projection frame rate is the same.

To tackle this difficulty, we propose a novel low-latency control method that dynamically updates the individual slices that constitute the volumetric image based on the tracked object’s latest pose, at a rate exceeding the rendering refresh rate, without increasing the physical sweep rate. Note that our method can be applied not only to the augmented mirror setup but also to other direct-interactive swept volumetric displays.

In summary, this paper presents a swept volumetric display system that integrates an augmented reality mirror and a low-latency control method. We also report the results of evaluation experiments that verify the effectiveness of our proposed method compared to a conventional control method that updates the entire volumetric image only after each sweep cycle is complete. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

- • An implementation of a new volumetric display system using a half-mirror,
- • A novel low-latency control method for volumetric displays without increasing the physical sweep speed,
- • Experimental results comparing the proposed method with the conventional update method.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Overview of Volumetric Displays

Many conventional 3D displays rely on binocular disparity, with Head-Mounted Displays (HMDs) and lenticular lenses being prime examples. While these methods create a sense of depth by presenting different images to each eye, allowing users to experience binocular disparity, they typically do not support vergence and focal accommodation. This often causes user fatigue and restricts the viewing area for stereoscopic images [32]. Although recent HMDs are beginning to address these issues using eye tracking [1] or holographic near-field displays [16, 23], users still have to wear a head-mounted display, which introduces various usability drawbacks.

In contrast, volumetric displays position light points directly in space, providing all depth cues without requiring users to wear special equipment to perceive true 3D content. Volumetric displays can be broadly categorized into three types: swept, solid, and free-space [14].

Swept volumetric displays form three-dimensional images by mechanically moving a projection screen [2, 11, 35, 36] or an LED array [10, 31] at high speed while synchronizing projected images or emitted light with the motion. By combining precise projection timing and well-controlled screen movement, this method enables relatively large display volumes with uniform voxel distribution and straightforward scalability using established projection and motion control technologies. However, mechanical motion inevitably introduces noise and requires careful design to ensure operational safety.

Solid volumetric displays generate visible voxels inside a fixed medium, such as rare-earth-doped glass [7] or stacked scattering panels [33]. On the one hand, they benefit from having no moving parts, which ensures silent operation and minimal mechanical devices. On the other hand, the displayable volume tends to be small due to low optical conversion efficiency or the limited number of layers.

Free-space volumetric displays directly create light points in the air, for example, by using laser-induced plasma [22] or trapping particles [4, 20, 29] in a field. These systems can produce truly floating points in free space and enable natural occlusion, but they typically face challenges with low brightness, limited voxel count, and the need for high-power lasers, which can raise safety concerns.

Considering the characteristics of each method, swept volumetric displays can be implemented with relatively simple mechanisms compared to other approaches and offer a wider displayable volume. In addition, the high-speed moving screen can be safely positioned behind a half-mirror, which minimizes the risk of accidental contact. For these reasons, this study adopts a swept volumetric display.

### 2.2 Interaction with Volumetric Displays

Various interaction methods have been proposed for 3D displays [17]. However, because the screens or diffusers in volumetric displays are typically rigid, almost all interaction methods are indirect, such as using a cursor [12], laser pointer [8], or hand gestures [36].

Hilliges et al. [13] reported that direct interaction is superior to indirect methods when engaging with 3D content. Therefore, enabling direct interaction for volumetric displays is highly desirable.



Figure 2: Comparison between the conventional method and the proposed method. (Note: The diagrams assume a planar screen for explanatory purposes, while our actual implementation uses a rotating helical screen.)

Hunter et al. [15] integrated a re-imaging plate into a swept volumetric display to present mid-air 3D content and enable direct interaction. To address the inherent occlusion problem, they proposed to remove a portion of the volumetric image to facilitate direct hand penetration. While this method partially mitigates occlusion, it is still not suitable for applications that require maintaining a stable volumetric shape at positions where an object blocks light rays, such as inside or in front of an object.

Bouzbib et al. [6] used a screen made of flexible material. This method allows users to touch 3D content directly. However, it has drawbacks, such as occlusion and distortion. Because the projector projects images onto the diffuser, users cannot touch the 3D content from angles that would block the projection. In addition, the diffuser flexes when touched, causing the volumetric image to distort.

### 2.3 Augmented Reality Mirrors

Augmented Reality (AR) mirrors have attracted significant attention due to their interactive nature. Previous research has mainly focused on superimposing virtual content onto mirror displays and seamlessly integrating it with reflections in 3D space.

Early AR mirror systems include "MR-Mirror" [30], which adjusts the display to the user's viewpoint, and "Holoflector" [25], which utilizes the Microsoft Kinect sensor's depth sensing and body tracking capabilities. These systems rely on view-dependent rendering of virtual objects and facilitate natural bodily interaction.

Applications of AR mirrors have been explored in fields such as education [5], entertainment [24], and training [37]. Examples include playing a virtual drum set behind the mirror or controlling virtual objects using body tracking.

Recent research extends AR interaction by combining an HMD with a smart mirror and merging the mirrored world with the real world [38].

## 3 Proposed Volumetric Display System

### 3.1 New Low-Latency Control Method for Volumetric Displays

As mentioned in Section 1, increasing the rendering refresh rate for low latency is technically challenging. Therefore, this study proposes a system that achieves low-latency projection of volumetric images without increasing the rendering refresh rate.

As shown in Fig. 2(b), our proposed control method generates and projects volume slice images immediately based on the pose of the tracked object estimated at a significantly higher rate than the sweep rate. This eliminates the latency caused by waiting for a full sweep to complete as in Fig. 2(a), thereby minimizing latency relative to the tracked object.

In the ideal setup where the measurement/calibration errors are negligible and zero latency is assumed, the proposed method always presents a fragment of the volume that is completely aligned to the tracked object.

Some of existing volumetric displays appear to have the ability to dynamically update the image during the sweep. Nevertheless, the updates are primarily based on the screen position, and not on the pose of the tracked target as in our method. For example, Bouzbib et al. [6] state that their system can update the image at 120 Hz. However, they do not mention that the updates respond to scene changes during the sweep, nor do they discuss the effects of such updates on latency perception.

On the one hand, our approach forms a distorted volumetric image in the stationary space because the slices that constitute the volumetric image are generated at slightly different timings. Specifically, the points within the volumetric image that intersect the screen later in the sweep period will appear distorted, shifting in the direction of the tracked object's movement. On the other hand, considering that it has been suggested that the human visual system utilizes motion information to integrate temporally-fragmented pattern information and perceive a coherent entire image [27], we expect that the perceived shape of the displayed image would be acceptably correct. This is examined in the experiment presented in the next section.

Nomoto et al. [28] realized low-latency projection of noise-reduced path-traced images by rapidly projecting intermediate path-traced images, which remained noisy due to insufficient sampling. This is an example of decomposing an image in the space domain and controlling each component timely as done in our proposed method.

### 3.2 Hardware/Software Implementations

The configuration of our proposed system is shown in Fig. 3. Users sit in front of the half-mirror and interact with the volumetric image through its reflection.

The system consists of a high-speed projector, a PC (CPU: Core i9-14900KS, GPU: RTX 4090, RAM: 64 GB), a DC motor (Maxon A-MAX32), a rotary encoder (Autonics E40S-6-3600-3-N-24), a helical

screen, a microcontroller (Waves ESP-WROOM-32 DevKitC), two high-speed cameras (Basler acA720-520um), and a half-mirror. The maximum frame rate of the cameras is 524 fps. The helical screen has three blades and rotates at 5 rps under PID control, sweeping the volumetric image at 15 Hz. The helix has a radius of 120 mm and a height of 160 mm.

We developed two configurations: one uses a binary-frame-warping projector [18] and the other uses a ViaLUX V-7001 projector.

The binary-frame-warping projector cannot project arbitrary images at high speed but can apply homography transformations to preloaded binary images at 2469 Hz, based on coordinate transformation parameters sent from the PC. We preload an image containing three bright circles representing intersection points and update it using an affine transformation to generate the desired image. This configuration offers high depth resolution and reduced latency because only affine parameters are computed and sent. The three light points are randomly selected from the intersection points of the volume and the screen in each slice image. Due to the limited number of light points, renderable volumetric shapes are restricted to sparse ones such as wireframe structures.

To allow more complex shapes, we also developed a configuration using the V-7001 projector, which projects arbitrary 1-bit images at 625 Hz in our implementation. While this configuration allows rendering of arbitrarily shaped volumetric images, it sacrifices depth resolution and increases latency due to the additional time required for image generation and transfer.

In both configurations, the PC runs two threads in parallel: an object tracking thread and an image generation thread, as shown in Fig. 4. The tracking thread estimates the pose of the tracked object from camera images, transforms it from camera coordinates to screen coordinates, and passes it to the image generation thread. The image generation thread holds the previous pose and performs linear extrapolation to compensate for camera-to-PC latency. Based on the estimated pose and synchronization signals from the microcontroller, intersection points are calculated, and either of the image or affine parameters are computed and transmitted to the projector via USB. ## 4 Evaluation Experiments

To evaluate the proposed control method, we conducted two user experiments. The same participants took part in both experiments: 14 adults (12 males and 2 females) aged 20 to 23, all with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. To eliminate delays caused by PC processing and inter-device communication as much as possible, we employed the configuration using the binary-frame-warping projector.

### 4.1 Experiment 1: Perceptual Distortion of Volumetric Images for Different Motion Speeds

4.1.1 Procedure. In Experiment 1, we assessed how the perceived distortion of a volumetric image changes with motion speed.

Such distortions were scarcely perceived by the authors in informal preliminary experiments with the conventional method for any motion speed.

Therefore, the aim of this experiment was not to compare the proposed method with the conventional method, but rather to examine whether distortions caused by the proposed method are

perceptible to users. Furthermore, if such distortions are perceived, the experiment aimed to investigate the relationship between perceived distortion and motion speed, as well as to determine whether there exists a range of motion speeds at which distortion is not perceived.

To facilitate quantitative evaluation of distortion by the participants, the experiment was conducted under a simple condition: linear motion of a cube instead of complex shapes or motions.

A volumetric image of a cube with side lengths of 30 mm was oscillated back and forth at a constant speed with a travel distance of 160 mm along a single direction on the screen, reversing direction instantaneously at each end. The participants observed its distortion at various motion speeds.

Ten motion speed conditions were tested: 0.27, 0.29, 0.32, 0.36, 0.40, 0.46, 0.53, 0.64, 0.80, and 1.07 m/s. The pose update rate for the volumetric image was fixed at 500 Hz. Here, the pose update rate refers to the frequency at which the pose used to calculate the projected image is updated. In other words, a pose update rate of 500 Hz means that pose changes can be reflected in the volumetric image 500 times per second. Note that the rendering rate of the volumetric image itself is 15 Hz.

The participants selected the reference image that best matched the perceived distortion from a set of 10 reference images given in advance showing shear distortion angles from  $0^\circ$  to  $18^\circ$  in  $2^\circ$  increments (numbered from 0 to 9), as shown in Fig. 6.

The motion speed conditions were presented in random order, where each condition was repeated three times. The average of the three responses was used in the analysis. The participants observed the volumetric image from various angles at a distance of approximately 40 cm from the screen.

4.1.2 Results. Fig. 7 shows the results: the vertical axis represents the perceived distortion angle, and the horizontal axis shows motion speed on a continuous scale.

The results of this study confirmed that the perceived distortion of a moving volumetric image varies depending on its motion speed. Specifically, when the speed was 0.29 m/s or lower, the perceived distortion remained roughly constant at about  $3^\circ$ . In the range from 0.32 to 0.64 m/s, the distortion increased almost proportionally with the increase in speed. Furthermore, in the range from 0.80

## 4.2 Experiment 2: Perception of Latency in Volumetric Images Following a Moving Target

4.2.1 Procedure. Experiment 2 aimed to evaluate differences in perceived latency between the conventional method and our proposed method.

In Experiment 2, we evaluated how users perceived latency in a volumetric image tracking a moving target. A cubic volumetric image was projected onto the mirror image of an acrylic cube attached to the tip of a moving robot arm. The participants chose whether they noticed a delay in the volumetric image for each combination of the robot’s motion speed and the pose update period.

To make latency easier to perceive, we used as the displayed volumetric shape a wireframe model identical in shape to the tracking target—an acrylic cube with tracking markers.

The motion speeds tested were 0.35, 0.26, 0.21, 0.18, 0.15, and 0.13 m/s, which represent the average speeds during the oscillatory motion. The pose update periods were 66.6, 33.3, 16.7, 8.3, 4.2, 2.8, and 2.0 ms. We used the pose update period instead of the pose update rate to simplify analysis. Note that the 66.6 ms condition coincides with the display with the conventional method.

The results showed that, compared to the conventional method with a pose update period of 66.6 ms, the probability of noticing delays shifted toward the unnoticed side at all pose update periods tested with the proposed method. This suggests that the proposed method has a certain effect in reducing perceived latency.

In particular, when the pose update period changed from 8.3 ms to 66.6 ms, the increase in the probability of noticing delays was much larger than when it changed from 2.0 ms to 8.3 ms. This result indicates that a pose update period of around 8.3 ms may be sufficient to achieve the desired latency reduction effect. When the pose update period becomes very short, each edge of the volumetric image is divided into finer segments for projection, which may cause the surface of the image to appear wavy. Therefore, if comparable latency reduction can be achieved with a relatively longer pose update period, it would be preferable to operate with longer periods to maintain the visual quality of the volumetric image.

Another notable point is that no significant differences in the probability of noticing delays were observed among the different motion speeds. The spatial offset between the volumetric image and the tracked object is generally proportional to the object's motion speed. Therefore, one may expect that the speed would affect the results. However, it should be noted that the present results reflect the probability of noticing delays, not the magnitude of perceived delay itself. If the magnitude of delay were measured, the amount may have been proportional to the motion speed and the results might have varied accordingly.

In summary, the proposed method is considered effective in realizing a volumetric image that follows a moving object with low perceived latency.

## 5 Conclusion and Future Work

### 5.1 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel interactive swept volumetric display system that integrates an augmented reality mirror using a half-mirror setup. Unlike previous direct interaction methods, our configuration enables pseudo-direct interaction with a volumetric image through its mirror image, eliminating the occlusion issues that occur with direct touch. This setup allows new types of 3D expressions that were previously impractical due to physical occlusion.

To address the increased perceptibility of latency caused by aligning real reflections and volumetric images, we developed a low-latency control method that dynamically updates each slice of the volumetric image based on the latest tracked pose, without increasing the rendering refresh rate.

The experimental results demonstrated that, compared to the conventional method, the proposed method is effective in reducing perceived latency. At the same time, it was also confirmed that the perceived shape distortion increases in proportion to the motion speed of the volumetric image. Although the amount of distortion would be acceptable when the motion is at a moderate speed, it is

essential to address how to compensate for this distortion, particularly when the motion speed is high.

### 5.2 Limitations

The present experimental results have potential limitations. The quantitative results have been obtained for a single type of shape model (cube) translating along a single line. Using different shapes and motion types could lead to different quantitative results, although we believe the overall trend would be similar. The visual tracking of the physical cube using 2D barcode markers and the calibration between the cameras, screen, and projector were not perfectly optimized, which may have adversely affected the results.

All experimental results relied on participants' visual observations, which may have introduced subjective bias and adversely affected the results. Moreover, in Experiment 2, our analysis was limited to comparing the probability of perceiving latency against the conventional method as a baseline. We did not sufficiently quantify the extent to which the proposed method reduces latency relative to the conventional method; this remains a limitation.

The evaluation of AR-mirror performance is also incomplete. While we propose applying a swept volumetric display to an AR-mirror setting, we have not conducted experiments to demonstrate its effectiveness. To quantitatively assess the usefulness of our method, experiments involving AR-mirror-specific tasks are necessary.

Another limitation of our proposed method is its reliance on specialized, high-cost laboratory equipment. This reliance represents a substantial barrier to immediate adoption in real applications.

### 5.3 Future Work

The most critical challenge for putting the proposed method into practical use is distortion compensation. Several approaches have been devised to address this issue.

One of those approaches is the distortion compensation model. In this method, the 3D model used for slice computation is replaced with a pre-deformed version that accounts for the distortions perceived by the user. As a result, users can perceive distortion-free volumetric images. However, since the deformed model does not perfectly match the actual tracked object, drawbacks such as increased perceptual latency are anticipated. Nevertheless, preliminary experimental results indicate that the use of this compensation model does not necessarily lead to increased perceptual latency, suggesting that it is a highly promising approach.

Another approach is to improve the screen geometry. One possible reason why distortions are perceived by the user is that, when the progression direction of the volumetric image remains constant, the image is always distorted in the same manner. This recurring distortion arises from the fact that the screen sweeps the volumetric image in the same sequence every time. If the sweeping order could be varied at a high rate, it would be possible to prevent the same distortion from consistently occurring, thereby potentially reducing perceptual distortion.

As future work, we plan to investigate distortion correction methods, such as improvements to the projection technique and modifications to the screen shape. Moreover, we intend to quantify the reduction in perceived latency achieved by the proposed method.

We also plan to conduct task-based experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed AR mirror system. In addition, we aim to explore application concepts that take advantage of the unique features of the proposed system.
