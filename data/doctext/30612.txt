Achieving high-quality novel view synthesis in 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS) often depends on effective point primitive
management. Adaptive Density Control (ADC) addresses this by
automating densification and pruning, yet its densification
strategy shows key shortcomings. In response, we introduce a
volume-driven density control method, where Ellipsoid’s
volumes dictate the refinement process. Furthermore, we
enhance the initialization pipeline by replacing traditional sparse
SfM methods with a deep-image-matching framework,
leveraging SuperPoint and LightGlue to construct a robust initial
point cloud. Evaluations on the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset reveal
that our approach not only surpasses existing methods but also
maintains efficiency, delivering state-of-the-art performance
across diverse scenes.

1. Introduction
High-quality Novel View Synthesis (NVS) is a fundamental task in computer vision and
computer graphics, with diverse applications in AR/VR/MR, robotics, and cinematography.
The goal is to generate photorealistic images of a scene from unseen viewpoints, given a set of
input images with known camera parameters. Early methods such as Structure-from-Motion
(SfM) [1] and image-based rendering (IBR) have gradually evolved towards more sophisticated
neural representations. A pivotal breakthrough came with Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [2] ,
a method that leverages a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to represent scenes as continuous fields
of color and density. NeRF-based methods optimize scene representations using volumetric
rendering and have demonstrated remarkable image quality, capturing intricate lighting and
geometric effects. However, they suffer from slow training and inference, limiting their
applicability in real-time scenarios [2], [3].
In response to these limitations, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [4] has emerged as a promising
alternative. Unlike NeRF’s implicit representation via MLPs, 3DGS uses an explicit set of 3D
Gaussian primitives. These Gaussians are characterized by position, covariance, opacity, and
color attributes. Splatting-based rasterization is then employed to project Gaussians into 2D
screen space, enabling high-resolution real-time rendering. Each Gaussian's appearance is
modeled with anisotropic spherical harmonics (SH), following established practices [5].
Importantly, 3DGS avoids computationally expensive ray marching and point sampling,
making it well-suited for fast rendering [4].
One of the challenges in 3DGS is the Adaptive Density Control (ADC) mechanism, which
manages the density of Gaussians by introducing or pruning primitives based on gradients and
opacity thresholds. However, this gradient-based approach can fail in high-frequency regions,
such as grass or complex wires in the industrial environments, where changes in positional
gradients may not trigger sufficient densification [6]. Moreover, ADC lacks control over the
maximum number of Gaussians, risking out-of-memory issues during training [6]. To address
these limitations, a more robust volume driven densification strategy can be adopted. By
calculating volume of each primitive, we define high-volume Gaussians by identifying points
within a specific volume range and creating additional samples around these points based on
their scaling and rotation parameters. It generates new points using random sampling from a
normal distribution, applies transformations, and computes new properties like scaling,
rotation, and features for the new points. Finally, it replaces the original high-volume points
with the newly generated points and prunes the unused ones.
Our contributions are as follow;
- We replace the traditional SfM process with a deep image matching framework to
generate the initial point cloud. In this framework, SuperPoint is used as the feature
extractor, and LightGlue serves as the feature matcher. [1], [7], [8], [9], [10].
-
We introduce a complementary mechanism to the ADC that facilitates the generation
of new points based on Ellipsoid’s volumes, building upon the existing splitting and
cloning methods.

2. Related works
Traditionally, the task of 3D reconstruction has been tackled using established methods such
as Structure from Motion (SfM) and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) [1], [11]. However, they have
limitations in robustness, efficiency, and scalability make them less competitive compared to
modern deep learning based methods. Sergey et al., in 2015 [12] proposed a novel approach to
learn a general similarity function for comparing image patches using Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs). Unlike traditional methods that rely on handcrafted features like SIFT, this
approach learns features directly from raw image data. This work is a significant contribution
to the field, demonstrating how deep learning can surpass traditional feature descriptors in tasks
like image retrieval, stereo matching, and object recognition. Kwang et al., introduced an endto-end pipeline that combined keypoint detection, descriptor extraction, and orientation
estimation in a unified pipeline [13]. Subsequently, SuperPoint, SuperGlue, and LightGlue
have introduced more improved approaches for keypoint detection and descriptor extraction,
improving computational efficiency while maintaining robustness [14], [15], [16].
Sitzmann et al., [17] presented Scene Representation Networks (SRNs) as a continuous, 3Dstructure-aware neural scene representation that encodes both geometry and appearance
without explicit 3D supervision. In 2020, Mildenhall et al, introduced NeRF (Neural Radiance
Fields) [2], a novel method for generating views of complex scenes by using a continuous
volumetric scene function and a small set of input images. However, it has limitations in terms
of slow training times and high computational costs. 3D Gaussian splatting has emerged as an
efficient technique for representing and rendering volumetric data by modeling scenes with a
sparse set of anisotropic Gaussian primitives [4]. Numerous applications leveraging 3DGS
have been proposed, including Gaussian-based avatars for realistic human representation [18],
[19], [20], autonomous driving for efficient scene understanding [21], [22], [23] and dynamic
scene reconstructions to capture temporal changes [24], [25], [26]. Recent advances focus on
improving its scalability, fidelity, and integration with neural radiance fields to enhance scene
representation [27].
However, only a limited number of studies emphasize the importance of addressing
fundamental improvements in 3D Gaussian Splatting methodologies. Yu et al. [28] introduce a
3D smoothing filter to regularize the frequency of 3D Gaussian primitives and replace the 2D
dilation filter with a 2D Mip filter to address aliasing and dilation artifacts, improving rendering
quality and addressing out-of-distribution issues. Lu et al. [29] propose a hierarchical and
region-aware scene representation by initializing anchor points from a sparse voxel grid to
guide the distribution of local 3D Gaussians. They further predict neural Gaussians on-the-fly
within the view frustum to accommodate varying perspectives and distances, enabling robust
novel view synthesis. To overcome the limitations of spherical harmonics in modeling specular
and anisotropic components, Yang et al. [30] introduced anisotropic spherical Gaussians and a
coarse-to-fine training strategy, significantly enhancing rendering quality for complex surfaces
without increasing Gaussian counts. Lee et al. [31] proposes a learnable masking mechanism
based on the volume and opacity of Gaussians. Hence, Gaussians with small volumes or low
opacity are identified as redundant and removed using binary masks. GaussianPro developed
by Cheng et al. [32] addresses densification challenges of shortcomings of SfM-based
initialization. Their method involves a sophisticated approach that progressively propagates
primitives along estimated planes, guided by patch-matching techniques and geometric
consistency. Huang et al., [33] contribute by analyzing the error in 3D Gaussian Splatting that
causes rendering artifacts, by identifying rendering errors that correlate with Gaussian
positions. They also derive the mathematical expectation of this error and determine its extrema
Page 3 of 10
using function optimization techniques. Bulò et al., [6] enhances 3D Gaussian Splatting’s
Adaptive Density Control by guiding densification using a per-pixel error function instead of
positional gradients and addressing biases in Gaussian cloning.

4. Volume Based Densification
The densification procedure has inherent limitations, particularly in regions with sparse point
cloud data. In regions with textures like grass, traditional SfM often fails to generate a sufficient
number of points in the point cloud due to the lack of distinct features. In such cases, the process
struggles to capture fine details effectively. Moreover, relying solely on gradient computations
to determine whether to clone or split primitives proves inadequate for addressing this issue.
3DGS often struggles in regions with sparse point data, primarily due to the uncontrolled
growth of Gaussian primitive volumes. This leads to the creation of excessively large Gaussian
volume primitives in these areas. The issue arises because the ADC mechanism considers only
the maximum scaling along a given direction when deciding whether to split or clone a
primitive, which can overlook sparsity-related constraints.

5. Experimental Setup and Evaluation
In this experiment, we utilized datasets from Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks and Temples, and Deep
Blending. For Mip-NeRF 360, we included all scenes, comprising five outdoor scenes and four
indoor scenes. From the Tanks and Temples dataset, we selected two specific scenes: Dr.
Johnson and Playroom. Similarly, for Deep Blending, we focused on two datasets: Truck and
Train. We evaluate performance using three metrics: peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR),
structural similarity index (SSIM), and the perceptual metric LPIPS. The experiments were
conducted using the Gaussian Splatting GitHub repository1. The experiments were performed
on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU. We set the volume threshold to 0.03, meaning that ellipsoids
with a volume exceeding this value were considered for splitting. Additionally, gradient-based
splitting was maintained, as we believe it to be a valuable criterion. The cloning mechanism
for densification was left unmodified.
To manage computational resources, we imposed a ceiling on the number of points generated
during training, capping this value at 34 million. This limit can be adjusted as needed, providing
flexibility in handling different datasets. We also restricted the number of images in each
category to under 200 to avoid memory-related issues. Although the MiP-NeRF 360 dataset
features image resolutions in the 2K to 3K range, we opted to use HD-ready resolutions. These
were derived from the 2x, 4x, and 8x downsampled versions of the original images obtained
through COLMAP, ensuring a balance between computational efficiency and visual quality.
