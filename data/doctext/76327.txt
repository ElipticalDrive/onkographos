Volumetric Fog Using
3D Textures in OpenGL
This article outlines how to implement real-time volumetric fog in
OpenGL by combining compute shaders with a 3D texture grid, commonly
referred to as a frustum grid or "froxel" volume. You will learn how the
frustum space is discretized, how lighting and density are injected into
the 3D volume, the mathematical accumulation of light along the view
ray, and the final compositing step with the rendered scene.
1. Allocating the
Frustum Volume (Froxels)
Rather than using an axis-aligned world-space grid, volumetric fog
typically relies on a view-frustum-aligned grid. In OpenGL, this
structure is stored as an immutable 3D texture allocated via
glTexStorage3D with a target of
GL_TEXTURE_3D.
The dimensions are typically set to a fraction of the screen
resolution (for instance, \(160 \times 90
\times 64\) or \(256 \times 144 \times
128\)) to balance performance and quality:
X and Y dimensions: Map linearly to normalized
device coordinates (screen space).
Z dimension (depth): Distributed exponentially
between the near and far planes. An exponential distribution places more
slices near the camera where visual fidelity is critical and fewer
slices in the distance.
The mapping from view-space depth \(z\) to a normalized texture coordinate
\(w\) typically follows the
formula:
\[w = \frac{\ln(z / z_{near})}{\ln(z_{far}
/ z_{near})}\]
2. Density and Phase
Function Evaluation
The implementation begins with a compute shader pass
(glDispatchCompute) that evaluates fog density and material
properties for every cell in the 3D texture.
Density Generation: For uniform fog, density is
constant. For dynamic, patchy fog, the compute shader samples an
auxiliary 3D noise texture (such as Perlin-Worley noise) using the
reconstructed world-space position of the froxel.
Scattering and Absorption: Medium parameters define
scattering (\(\sigma_s\)) and
absorption (\(\sigma_a\)) coefficients,
giving an extinction coefficient \(\sigma_t =
\sigma_s + \sigma_a\).
Phase Function: The angular distribution of
scattered light is modeled using the Henyey-Greenstein phase
function:
\[p(\theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \frac{1 -
g^2}{(1 + g^2 - 2g \cos\theta)^{3/2}}\]
where \(g \in (-1, 1)\) is the
asymmetry parameter controlling forward or backward scattering relative
to the view vector and light direction.
3. Light Injection Pass
In this compute pass, incoming radiance is calculated for each
froxel:
World Position Reconstruction: The shader maps the
3D grid index \((x, y, z)\) to clip
space, applies the inverse view-projection matrix, and divides by \(w\) to determine the world-space center of
the cell.
Shadow Map Sampling: The shader samples standard
shadow maps (e.g., cascaded shadow maps for directional lights or shadow
cubemaps for point lights) to check for occlusion.
In-Scattering Calculation: For all unshadowed
lights, the shader evaluates light color, attenuation, and the phase
function \(p(\theta)\), storing the
resulting in-scattered radiance alongside the extinction coefficient in
the 3D texture using image load/store
(glBindImageTexture).
4. Raymarching and
Accumulation
Once the lighting and extinction are evaluated per cell, a separate
compute shader integrates the volume along the view rays (the Z-axis of
the 3D texture) from near to far.
Using the Beer-Lambert law, light is accumulated through each cell
slice:
Transmittance Step: \[\Delta \tau = \exp(-\sigma_t \cdot \Delta
d)\] where \(\Delta d\) is the
distance between the near and far planes of the current slice.
Light Accumulation: \[L_{accum} = L_{accum} + L_{cell} \cdot
T_{current}\]
Transmittance Accumulation: \[T_{current} = T_{current} \cdot \Delta
\tau\]
This integration computes the total light scattered toward the camera
and the net transmittance up to each depth slice. The output is written
into a final filtered 3D texture.
5. Temporal Filtering and
Jittering
To hide spatial banding artifacts caused by coarse slice resolution,
depth jittering is applied. Each frame, a sub-voxel offset derived from
a pseudo-random sequence (such as blue noise or Halton sequence) offsets
the ray positions.
A temporal reprojection compute pass then blends the current frame’s
3D texture with the previous frame's 3D texture using camera motion
vectors. This effectively smooths out noise and improves perceived
volume resolution without extra raymarching steps.
6. Final Scene Composition
The accumulated volumetric data is applied to the scene during the
post-processing or forward-lighting pass:
The linear depth of the opaque scene fragment is retrieved from the
scene's depth buffer.
The fragment's screen coordinates and linear depth are converted
into 3D texture coordinates \((u, v,
w)\) using the exponential depth equation.
The integrated 3D texture is sampled using hardware trilinear
interpolation (GL_LINEAR).
The sampled in-scattered light is added to the scene color, and the
scene color is attenuated by the sampled transmittance:
\[\text{Final Color} = \text{Scene Color}
\times \text{Transmittance} + \text{InScattered Light}\]
