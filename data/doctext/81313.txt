### ABSTRACT DIRECT VOLUME RENDERING OF TREE-BASED TETRAHEDRAL ADAPTIVE MESH REFINEMENT DATA

Musa Ege Unalan ¨ MS in Computer Engineering Advisor: Ugur G ˘ ud¨ ukbay ¨ September 2025

Ray-tracing-based direct volume rendering (DVR) techniques often use data representations such as regular grids, unstructured meshes, and adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) data. A less-explored option for DVR is tree-based tetrahedral AMR data (Tet-AMR), which combines the benefits of unstructured meshes and AMR by having both a coarse unstructured tetrahedral mesh that can represent complex domains, on which acceleration structures can be constructed to perform efficient ray-triangle intersection tests, and a forest of refinement trees, each rooted at a coarse mesh element, increasing detail where needed. Tet-AMR data can be visualized by converting it to an unstructured mesh representation; however, this approach introduces new unstructured elements, increasing memory usage during rendering while decreasing the performance of acceleration structures. We propose leveraging the regularly subdivided nature of the tetrahedral refinement trees by only storing the coarse geometry during rendering. We construct a bounding volume hierarchy over the coarse mesh to efficiently identify the refinement trees from which to sample. Then, we generate the geometry of the finer level elements on the fly when traversing the refinement trees to find the actual elements to sample. We also show that the tree structure can be utilized to implement a dynamic view-dependent level of detail effect, improving performance by decreasing fidelity in regions that minimally affect the final image. It can be used to obtain a density range for each tree, enabling empty space skipping with ray marching renderers or used as local majorant extinctions with delta tracking renderers to improve performance. Our proposed method outperforms or performs comparably to rendering the Tet-AMR as an unstructured mesh, using less memory and enabling the described effect and optimizations on the various datasets we have tested with our GPU renderers.

Keywords: Direct volume rendering, unstructured volumetric mesh, ray tracing, acceleration structure, tetrahedralization, hardware-acceleration, Graphics Processing Unit, Adaptive Mesh Refinement, delta tracking.

## Chapter 1

## Introduction

Direct volume rendering for scientific visualization is a method that is used across many fields, from fluid simulations to medical imaging, to create visual representations of volume data and allow a correct and easy interpretation of the data, by providing a model that simulates the various properties of light inside a volume, and is also easily modifiable through the editing of transfer functions and camera parameters.

Volume data can come in various flavors, the most popular being structured grids, unstructured meshes, and Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) [1] data; each variety has its advantages for specific purposes. Tetrahedral AMR meshes created through tetrahedral grid refinement [2] combine the benefits of unstructured meshes and AMR. Tetrahedral AMR meshes contain a coarse unstructured mesh where each coarse element is associated with a refinement tree of recursively subdivided elements. The elements on these refinement trees are further subdivided, where more detail/fidelity is needed. There exist methods for rendering AMR meshes and unstructured tetrahedral meshes, but not for tetrahedral AMR meshes. Tetrahedral AMR meshes can be rendered by constructing an unstructured mesh from all leaf-level elements. However, converting AMR meshes into unstructured meshes increases the memory usage of the meshes.

We propose a structure to store Tetrahedral AMR data during volume rendering, called TET-AMR, which we accompany with two refinement tree traversal methods that take advantage of the regular subdivided nature of the tetrahedral elements to perform point location queries within the refinement trees. Additionally, we utilize hardwareaccelerated point location queries to locate the refinement trees efficiently using the coarse unstructured tetrahedral mesh. Using tree traversal and coarse mesh point location queries, we implement two renderers for rendering TET-AMR data: A ray marcher and a Woodcock tracker. We show that a macrocell grid can be constructed by treating each element of the coarse mesh as a macrocell element, storing the minimum and maximum post-classification density values contained in them, to perform empty space skipping and adaptive free-path sampling for Woodcock tracking. Lastly, we show that the TET-AMR traversal can be modified to implement a dynamic screen space level of detail (LOD) effect that adjusts the depth of the traversal according to the screen space size of the elements, allowing us to improve performance by decreasing fidelity in regions that minimally affect the final image. Our contributions are as follows.

- TET-AMR, a structure for storing tetrahedral refinement forests
- An efficient tetrahedral tree traversal method for TET-AMR
- Direct volume rendering of the TET-AMR structure
- The coarse mesh macrocell, and dynamic screen space LOD optimizations, to the rendering methods using TET-AMR

The organization of the thesis is as follows. Chapter 2 gives info on existing volume data organization approaches, DVR, and rendering techniques we used. Chapter 3 introduces the TET-AMR structure and the tetrahedral tree traversal operation, used to find the child element containing a point. Chapter 4 talks about rendering the TET-AMR structure and the specific optimizations it allows us to perform. Chapter 5 is about our experimental setup, results, and discussions on the results of the experiments. We conclude with Chapter 6 and discuss future research directions.

## Chapter 2

## Background and Related Work

This chapter provides an overview of the volume data formats commonly used for volume rendering, their structure, and the methods employed to sample and render them. We also briefly introduce volume rendering for scientific visualization and our two volume rendering methods in this work.

### 2.1 Volume Data Formats

Volume data refers to scalar functions defined over a three-dimensional spatial domain. Each point in 3D space is associated with a scalar value such as density, pressure, or temperature. The data used in volume rendering can be classified into several main approaches, such as structured grids, unstructured meshes, and adaptive mesh refinement (AMR). The method used is generally not an arbitrary choice, but a direct consequence of how data was acquired, such as medical imaging and microscopy producing structured grids, numerical simulations generating AMR meshes, and finite element methods with unstructured meshes [1]. A simplified visual summary of these volume formats is given in Figure 2.1.



Figure 2.1: An unstructured mesh, a structured grid, and a tree-based AMR mesh, shown in 2D for ease of visualization.

How these volumes store their scalar data can also differ; some store their data at the center of their elements as cell-centered scalars, while others may store it at the vertices of the cells as vertex-centered scalars. Generally, simulation and imaging sensors produce cell-centered data, while rendering methods use vertex-centered data. Usually, rendering vertex-centered data requires locating the element that contains the points of interest. However, rendering cell-centered data involves locating the element itself and its neighbors. Regardless of how we store the data, all volume structures are a mapping from R 3 → R, mapping the data coordinates to scalars. The goal is to sample data from these volumes to scalars, with different volume structures needing different sampling methods.

#### 2.1.1 Structured Grids

Structured grid volumes comprise cells on a Cartesian grid, usually cubes, with the same data resolution throughout the volume. Since the data is laid on a grid with known constant resolution, the grid structure is implicit, allowing easy random access and making it simpler to sample [3], without any external data structures. The data from the eight neighboring cells can be trilinearly interpolated to create the value at the sample point. The main drawback of this method is that empty regions of the volume still need to be stored. When we need to increase the resolution of the data in a particular region, we need to increase the resolution of the grid globally, which can result in needlessly large grids that carry mostly redundant data, increasing memory and storage requirements.

Recent work on structured grids [1] focuses on empty space skipping, which needs recomputation whenever the transfer function changes. Wald et al. [4] build a macrocell grid over the data, storing the min/max post-classification alpha, and use a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) that intersects only the faces for the non-empty grid cells. Or faster (re)construction of k-d trees used for skipping with sparse volumes, on the GPU ([5]). Moreover, recent work has focused on out-of-core rendering of huge datasets, where the whole dataset cannot fit into the device memory. Wang et al. [6] stream data in the form of a forest of multi-resolution bricks that are loaded on demand when requested by the renderer to memory. Sarton et al. [7] implement a memory paging and caching structure on the GPU with a parallel caching structure on the host side for efficient access to data bricks.

### 2.1.2 Unstructured Grids

Unstructured grids are usually comprised of tetrahedral elements, but can also be comprised of other polyhedral types such as hexahedra, pyramids, wedges, or any combination of them [1]. This approach makes them suitable for representing volumes with arbitrarily complex shapes.

Unlike structured grids, sampling unstructured grids is more challenging due to the lack of an implicit structure. Sampling unstructured meshes at a point involves finding the element that contains the location of the sample. There are two main approaches for finding the containing element: element-marching and point-location methods. Element-marching methods traverse the mesh one element at a time, using connectivity information to locate the containing element. On the other hand, point-location methods use spatial acceleration structures to query the element that contains the point directly.

Recent work on element marching methods focuses on improving element traversal efficiency. Maria et al. [8] propose a fast tetrahedral mesh traversal method that uses Plucker coordinates. Aman ¨ et al. [9] introduce an XOR-compacting scheme for tetrahedral mesh marching, improving traversal performance and reducing the memory required to store the mesh connectivity information. A limitation of traversal-based methods is the need for an initial element to begin marching. To address this, Aman et al. perform an additional traversal starting from an arbitrary element to locate the one containing the camera. Sahistan et al. [10] extend this idea with a shell-to-shell traversal strategy, where they construct a hardware-accelerated BVH over the boundary faces of the tetrahedral mesh, or the "shell", and initialize traversal from these shells, therefore avoiding the cost of an additional search. Sahistan et al. also adapted the work of Aman et al. for volume rendering. Later, Sahistan et al. [11] extended the XOR-compacting scheme to other polyhedral types: wedges, pyramids, and hexahedra.

Point-location methods use a spatial acceleration structure constructed over the elements. Modern methods utilize the ray tracing hardware in modern GPUs with accelerated BVH traversals. Wald et al. [12] first proposed using hardware-accelerated BVH traversal on tetrahedral meshes for point location. They use user-defined intersection routines for the tetrahedral intersection. Morrical et al. [13] later extended this to other element types.

### 2.1.3 Adaptive Mesh Refinement Data

Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) data, introduced by Berger et al. [14], is refined more in the sections where further detail and data precision are needed, or, on the contrary, the mesh is coarsened in the regions where the current level of detail is unnecessary. Since the mesh can be refined and coarsened in small regions, it does not suffer from the size drawbacks of structured grids while having a similar structure.

AMR data can come in different flavors: Block-structured AMR and tree-structured AMR. In block-structured AMR, the data at different refinement levels are stacked on top of each other, maintaining multiple levels of data for the same location. Treestructured AMR uses octrees, or a forest of octrees, to store the adaptive grid hierarchy, maintaining only one level of data for a location. Storing a region at a deeper level in the octree, containing more data points, increases the refinement level in the desired regions.

Most outputs from simulation codes are cell-centered grids. To efficiently sample these volumes, one approach is shifting each grid by half of its cell width, to create the "dual grid" of it, effectively shifting the data to the vertices. Sampling inside AMR regions using the dual grid is as simple as applying the structured grid sampling methods. However, the problem arises at level boundaries, where regions of two different refinement levels meet and cause a "T-junction". The data points on each side of the dual grid can not be used to trilinearly interpolate a sample at these boundaries, as the vertices are not equally spaced. Moreover, correctly sampling from these regions requires different approaches.

A straightforward solution is to use nearest-neighbor interpolation, which has been shown to cause artifacts, cracked isosurfaces, or seams where the levels meet when rendered [1]. Properly rendering the data at level boundaries requires converting the dual grid cells to hexahedral elements and "stitching" between them, using unstructured elements to create a connected mesh. However, it is a costly method that increases the data storage requirements, as the grid elements are now stored in an unstructured fashion. Zellmann et al. [15] introduce a memory-efficient way of rendering AMR data using dual meshes with stitching, by clustering and converting the hexahedral grid cells to "gridlets", which are small grids of neighboring hexahedra of the same size.

Wald et al. [16] use a tent-shaped basis function to compute the weighted average of the cell samples that fall inside the footprint of the sampled cell. Wang et al. [17] propose a method of sampling the level boundaries by interpolating the sample point inside octants, one-eighth-sized subdivisions of the cell, first interpolating the data for the octant vertices, using the data of the dual mesh and the cell centers, then the sample point itself. Unlike stitching, these works focus on better construction of the samples at the boundaries, without creating extra elements.

### 2.2 AMR Meshes with General Element Types

AMR data generally contains cube-shaped cells, which can limit the domains it can perfectly represent. AMR and unstructured grids can be combined to create a hybrid mesh, with an unstructured mesh that defines the data's coarse/top-level structure. However, each element in the coarse mesh can be further refined to create a tree of subdivided child elements to fit the nature of the volume better. However, it also inherits the problems of both structures, needing to find the enclosing coarse element to start sampling, from unstructured meshes, and the sampling problems that arise at the level boundaries, from AMR data.

t8code [18] is a library that supports the creation and adaptation (refinement and coarsening) of tree-based AMR meshes of hexahedra, tetrahedra, prisms, pyramids, or any combination of these elements [19, 20]. Some recent works focus on AMR meshes with general element types. Burstedde and Holke [21] present a method of partitioning the coarse mesh into multiple parts such that the leaf elements contained on each process/node hold a balanced number of elements, as well as the required metadata of the coarse neighbors. They also introduce a tetrahedral space-filling curve [22] with accompanying algorithms to perform various operations on red-refined tetrahedra for adaptive meshes.

### 2.2.1 Tetrahedral AMR Meshes

We focus on tetrahedral AMR meshes, one such application of general element AMR, where the coarse mesh is a tetrahedral mesh, and each coarse tetrahedron in the mesh can be refined to create a refinement tree of tetrahedral elements [2]. ### 2.3 Direct Volume Rendering

Direct volume rendering creates an image from volumetric data without creating an intermediary, such as isosurfaces or geometric primitives. As opposed to traditional rendering techniques, which are concerned with surfaces, volume rendering is instead concerned with volumes of media and how light interacts with them. These volumes can be rendered to exhibit different properties, such as light emission, absorption, and scattering [24].

The two main operations of volume rendering are sampling and classification. Sampling is the acquisition of data belonging to a point inside a volume, and classification is the mapping of the acquired data samples into color and opacity values to be used in the visualization, which is achieved through transfer functions. The appearance of the rendered volume depends on the transfer function. Transfer functions map scalars to color and opacity values, which allows the viewer to focus on specific parts of the data.

Different approaches to direct volume rendering exist; some approach the problem as a simple sampling problem and do stepwise sampling along the rays. Others approximate the physical interaction between the light photons and particles in the medium. We briefly summarize the two methods this work focuses on, ray marching and Woodcock (Delta) tracking.

### 2.3.1 Ray Marching

Volume ray marching, introduced by Levoy [25], works by taking fixed-sized steps along a ray, taking a sample of the volume at each step, and compositing the color values corresponding to these samples until the ray terminates, which means that either we have reached a certain opacity threshold in the composited result, or we have taken samples throughout all of the volume where the current location on the ray is outside the volume (cf. Figure 2.4\). Using the front-to-back alpha compositing formulation given in Equation 2.1, the color values obtained at each step are used to create the final color value for a specific pixel in the frame. The opacity used during the compositing step determines how opaque the volume will be rendered. The pseudo-code for ray marching is given in Algorithm 1.

The step size can be a fixed value or adjusted dynamically to better suit the different regions of a heterogeneous volume. Setting the step size too big causes undersampling, where high-frequency features of the volume might be missed during rendering. On the other hand, setting the step size low (oversampling) ensures accurate rendering; however, taking more samples reduces the rendering performance.

1: function RAYMARCH(volume, ray, stepSize, transferFunction) 2: color ← Color(0,0,0) 3: α ← 0 4: while volume.contains(ray.origin + ray.dir×ray.t) do 5: sampledScalar ← volume.sample(ray.origin + ray.dir×ray.t) 6: sampledColor ← transferFunction.mapColor(sampledScalar) 7: color ← composite(color,sampledColor,α) 8: ray.t ← ray.t+stepSize 9: end while 10: return color + backgroundColor ×(1 - α) 11: end function

#### 2.3.2 Woodcock Tracking

Tracking methods simulate the movement of photons inside a volume and the various collisions they may have by sampling a free path, or the distance without any scattering or absorption collision events, instead of directly sampling the data [26]. Closed-form tracking can perform free-path sampling in homogeneous volumes with constant extinctions. However, this is impossible if the volume is heterogeneous, like many volumes we would like to render.

Woodcock tracking (also known as delta tracking) [27] solves this by introducing the concept of fictitious particles. Woodcock homogenizes the density of the volume with fictitious particles to an upper bound, called the volume's majorant extinction, and simplifies the problem to one solvable with closed-form tracking. When the ray samples a fictitious particle, it is treated as a null-collision. A null-collision gets treated as another type of collision, in which the ray experiences no interruption in its path and continues as is. The traversal stops if a ray samples a real collision (see Figure 2.5\).



Figure 2.5: Woodcock particles and sampled collisions.

Woodcock tracking is a Monte Carlo method in which the rays are sampled probabilistically to estimate particle collisions. Over many rays, the Monte Carlo estimate converges to an unbiased, accurate rendering of the volume. Compared to raymarching, Woodcock tracking usually requires a smaller number of volume samples. However, it requires the results of many rays to accumulate. Algorithm 2 describes the Woodcock method for a ray.

#### Algorithm 2 Woodcock tracking.

1: function WOODCOCK(volume, ray, majorant, transferFunction) 2: while volume.contains(ray.origin + ray.dir×ray.t) do 3: ray.t ← ray.t −log(1−rand())/majorant //Sampling the free-path 4: sampledScalar ← volume.sample(ray.origin + ray.dir × ray.t) 5: sampledColor ← transferFunction.mapColor(sampledScalar) 6: if sampledColor.density ≥ rand()×majorant then 7: return sample 8: end if 9: end while 10: return backgroundColor 11: end function

The performance of Woodcock tracking is defined by the number of null-collisions sampled during the traversal of a ray, which depends on the majorant extinction used for the volume. Instead of using a single majorant extinction over the whole volume, we can calculate tighter majorant extinctions for smaller parts, reducing the number of null-collisions. Recent works have used coarse structured grid macrocells to obtain tighter majorants for parts of a volume [28, 29, 1].

### 2.3.3 View-dependent Rendering

View-dependent rendering in direct volume visualization modifies the rendering based on the camera location, which could be done in various ways. We can sample according to an importance function, highlighting specific/desired volume features, depending on the camera's location, as described in [30] and [31]. Alternatively, we could exploit Level-of-detail-based methods that stop traversing into higher refinement levels and use data at lower detail when a threshold like the size of elements on screen is crossed, improving performance with minimal visual fidelity loss, as described in Kahler ¨ et al. [32].

## TET-AMR Structure

This chapter introduces the TET-AMR data structure we use to store our data during rendering. We first discuss constructing the TET-AMR from an unstructured mesh representation of tetrahedral AMR, such as the vtu output from t8code. Then we introduce the two methods we used to efficiently perform point location queries within each refinement tree, locating the child element containing the query point.

We store the data for the tetrahedral elements in a structure we call TET-AMR. It consists of a forest of tetrahedral refinement trees. For each tree, we store the data for the elements at all refinement levels. The elements store child counts, direct face neighbors, and associated scalars. Using point location queries, we store the coarse mesh of elements to build a bounding volume hierarchy over it during rendering to identify the refinement trees to sample from efficiently. We also store the minimum and maximum densities of each coarse element, the details of which will be given in Section 4.2. The structure is given in Listing 3.1.

Listing 3.1: The TET-AMR structure.

struct TET\_AMR { uint64\_t num\_coarse\_elements ; uint64\_t num\_elements ; vec3i \* coarse\_index ; vec3f \* coarse\_vertex ; uint64\_t \* tree\_root\_ids ; vec2f \* macrocell\_values ; uint64\_t \* child\_counts ; uint64\_t \* neighbors ; float \* cell\_data ; float \* vertex\_data ; };

### 3.1 Tetrahedron representation

We use the tetrahedron subdivision rules from Bey's red-refinement rule [2, 23, 33], but modify the notation to fit our needs. Four unique vertices identify each tetrahedron, where T, L, R, and B refer to the top, left, right, and back vertices. We also define the midpoints of these four vertices as follows:

- TL = (T + L) / 2
- TR = (T + R) / 2
- TB = (T + B) / 2
- LB = (L + B) / 2
- LR = (L + R) / 2
- RB = (R + B) / 2

A tetrahedron is divided into eight child tetrahedra, four outer (those that have a vertex of the original parent tetrahedron) and four inner (those that do not have a vertex of the original parent tetrahedron), and they are defined with a combination of the original corner vertices and the previously defined midpoints. All of them have oneeighth of the volume of the parent element. The eight child tetrahedra can be seen in Figure 3.1, and we define them as follows:

- Top Outer = { T, TL, TR, TB}
- Top Inner = {LB, TB, TR, TL}
- Left Outer = { L, TL, LB, LR}
- • Left Inner = {TR, LR, LB, TL}
- Right Outer = { R, RB, TR, LR}
- Right Inner = {LB, LR, TR, RB}
- Back Outer = { B, RB, LB, TB}
- Back Inner = {TR, TB, LB, RB}



Figure 3.1: The child tetrahedra, colored according to their definitions.

### 3.2 Constructing the TET-AMR Structure

The construction process starts with unstructured leaf elements with cell-centered data, where all elements have been refined from a coarser level element according to Bey's red-refinement rule, or are themselves coarse elements. The process is divided into several steps. First, we de-duplicate the vertices. The elements are then processed to create the missing upper-level elements of each tree. Then we reorder the elements of each tree. After that, we sample vertex-centered data from the elements' cell-centered data. Finally, we create the face neighborhood information for cell-centered data sampling. The non-coarse tetrahedra vertex coordinates are discarded after this step, since the traversal process we introduce in Section 3.3 computes child tetrahedra vertex positions on-the-fly. However, the per-vertex scalars still need to be stored if they are to be used during rendering. After construction, we are left with a forest of refinement trees, each ordered predictably, allowing efficient traversal.

### 3.2.1 De-duplicating the Vertices

If the starting data has duplicate vertices, we remove them and store the shared unique vertices with an accompanying index array that stores the vertex IDs for each element, reducing the amount of data we store. Moreover, it allows us to work with the indices during the later steps of the data preparation process.

### 3.2.2 Creating Parent Elements

Since we start the process with leaf elements, we must create the missing parent elements for a complete refinement tree. This process is bottom-up. Starting from the leaf elements, we process every group of eight elements subdivided from the same parent element. The parent element gets its children's four outer corner vertices as its vertices. We calculate the cell data at the centroid of this new element using the cell data of its children. We also calculate the child counts for each element, which we use during the reordering and traversal processes. The child count for an element is the sum of the child counts of its direct children. Once we have calculated all the necessary values, the child elements are removed from our working set, and the new parent element takes their place. We continue the process until the root element of the current tree has been generated. Then the process continues with the next tree until all trees have been processed.

### 3.2.3 Reordering Elements

The elements in each refinement tree need to be ordered to allow easy access. We accomplish this by reordering the elements so that every element will have all its descendants before the next sibling element of the parent; this corresponds to elements being given IDs ordered according to the preorder traversal of each tree, an example of which can be seen in Figure 3.2

The first child of an element is the "Top Outer" child defined in the tetrahedron representation given in Section 3.1, and is given the ID parentID+1. The next child of the element is the "Top Inner" child, which is given the ID topOuterChildID+1+ childCounts[topOuterChildID], the rest of the siblings' IDs are similarly offset by the previous sibling's child count plus one. The sibling pairs follow the order Top → Le ft → Right → Back, and for each pair, the outer one is ordered before the inner.

During this process, we also reorder the vertices of each child with respect to its parent vertices, so that each child's order of vertices is consistent with the definitions given in Section 3.1, and has a positive signed volume. After all elements are reordered, we can use the child counts to find the IDs of the children or siblings of a given element.



Figure 3.2: TET-AMR element order with refinement tree preorder traversal.

### 3.2.4 Sampling Vertex-centered Data From Cell-centered Data

We calculate vertex-centered scalars for all vertices in the forest. For each vertex, we collect cell scalars from all leaf-level elements that share the vertex. We then use inverse distance weighting with the neighboring cells' scalars and centroid distances to calculate the final value of the vertex-centered scalar.

Since we will not store the non-coarse mesh vertices during rendering, we duplicate the vertex-centered data and store four vertex-centered scalars per element. If we were not to duplicate the data per element, we would need to store the indexing array for all vertices during rendering, increasing our storage requirements. Assuming that the vertex data can be represented by a data type smaller than or equal to the index data type, duplicating the vertex data results in 4*N* scalars being stored, versus 4*N* indices plus V many scalars being stored if we were to store the vertex data along with the indexing array, where N is the number of elements and V is the number of unique vertices.

### 3.2.5 Creating Neighborhood Info

We do not rely on connectivity information during rendering. However, if we want to sample cell-centered data during the rendering process, we need to store the face neighbors of each element. Since we have already ordered the elements of each tree in a previous step, we can use the refinement tree traversal logic that will be introduced in Section 3.3 to locate the element on the other side of a face.

Since a face can have an arbitrary number of direct neighbors, each having varying levels of refinement, we do not use neighbors of a higher refinement level than our element and only extract four direct face neighbors of equal or lower refinement level per element. This allows us to keep the cell sampling process predictable, limiting the impact on performance during rendering.

We use the most significant bit of the neighbor ID to indicate that the neighbor is in a different refinement tree. We also store the difference in neighbor refinement levels, since neighbors are guaranteed to be at the same or lower refinement levels (coarser); a value other than zero means the neighbor is coarser. This information is stored in the next seven most significant bits after the first. Lastly, if the face is a boundary face of the volume, we store the maximum value allowed by the neighbor ID data type as the neighbor ID for that face, indicating that no neighbor exists.

### 3.3 Tetrahedral Refinement Tree Traversal Process

The tetrahedral refinement tree traversal process starts with a query point, the ordered vertex set of the coarse tetrahedron, and its element ID. The process traverses the tetrahedral refinement tree from the coarse root element to the actual child tetrahedron containing the query point. It returns the element ID of the child and its four corner vertices.

At each step, we modify our vertex set to represent the current tetrahedron; because of this, we do not need to store the actual coordinates of the lower-level tetrahedra, as they can be calculated on the fly. We continue the traversal process until we have reached a leaf element, a preset maximum traversal depth, or if the current size of the traversed tetrahedron on the screen has fallen below a certain threshold with the method detailed in Section 4.4. We then return a sample from the last traversed level.

During rendering, the tetrahedral refinement trees will need to be repeatedly queried, so an efficient method of locating the actual child element containing the query point is needed. A naive way of doing the in-element lookup would be to check all of the eight elements at the current level to see if our query point is contained in any of them by doing containment tests until we find the one that contains it. This approach results in many checks, some of which are redundant. However, there is a better way to achieve better performance using the regular geometry of the red-refinement tetrahedra. To make the in-element lookup process faster, we have experimented with two alternative methods: point-plane tests and barycentric coordinates.

### 3.3.1 Point-plane Tests

The tests use planes that intersect the midpoints of the element's corner vertices, as the children of an element also receive these midpoints as vertices when subdivided, as described in Section 3.1.

We use half-space tests to see whether the query point lies before or behind the plane defined by the three points. The first two tests check which side the point resides on for the planes defined by the midpoints (T R,LB,T B) and (T R,LB,T L). If the point is to the front of the plane (T R,LB,T B), we know that it is either in the right or the back quadrant, and similarly for the plane (T R,LB,T L), the top and back quadrants(see Figure 3.3\). Since both tests omit the left quadrant, we can test if the value for both tests was the same, and if they were, we are either in the left or back quadrants. Moreover, from the first test, we know which half we are in, either top and left, or right and back. So we can identify which of the four quadrants we are in. Lastly, we perform the in-quadrant plane test to check whether the point is in the inner or outer child. We do not actually need to do all four in-quadrant tests given in Algorithm 3; once we know which quadrant the point is in, a single plane test is enough to identify the child tetrahedron, but for the sake of conciseness, the algorithm is expressed in this manner.

After the local child number has been found, the CalculateChildID function uses the child counts of the current element's children to find the element ID of the child with the given local child number (cf. Algorithm 4\). The CalculateTetrahedronVertices function uses the given set of vertices to create the vertices of the child element using the tetrahedron representation (cf. Algorithm 5\). The process repeats until one of the termination conditions is met, after which we return the ID of the located element and its ordered vertex set.

#### Algorithm 3 Point plane test method for TET-AMR refinement tree traversal.

1: function POINTPLANETRAVERSAL(tetID, childCounts, maxDepth, point) 2: rLevel ← 0 3: tetID ← coarseTetID 4: V ← GetCoarseVertices(tetID) 5: while childCounts[tetID] > 0 and rLevel ≤ maxDepth do 6: t, l, r, b ← V 7: RB half ← halfSpace(point, (t+r)/2, (l+b)/2, (t+b)/2) 8: TB half ← halfSpace(point, (t+r)/2, (l+b)/2, (t+l)/2) 9: inner ← halfSpace(point, (t+l)/2, (t+b)/2, (t+r)/2) & halfSpace(point, (l+t)/2, (l+r)/2, (l+b)/2) & halfSpace(point, (r+t)/2, (r+b)/2, (r+l)/2) & halfSpace(point, (b+t)/2, (b+l)/2, (b+r)/2) 10: localChildNo ← (RB×4) + ((RB == T B)×2) +inner 11: V ← CalculateTetrahedronVertices(V, localChildNo) 12: tetID ← CalculateChildID(tetID, localChildNo, childCounts) 13: ++rLevel 14: end while 15: return tetID, V 16: end function

1: function CALCULATETETRAHEDRONVERTICES(V, childNo) 2: t, l, r, b ← V 3: V ′ ← {} 4: if childNo == 0 then 5: V ′ ← {t, (t + l) / 2, (t + r) / 2, (t + b) / 2} 6: else if childNo == 1 then 7: V ′ ← {(l + b) / 2, (t + b) / 2, (t + r) / 2, (t + l) / 2} 8: else if childNo == 2 then 9: V ′ ← {l, (t + l) / 2, (l + b) / 2, (l + r) / 2} 10: else if childNo == 3 then 11: V ′ ← {(t + r) / 2, (l + r) / 2, (l + b) / 2, (t + l) / 2} 12: else if childNo == 4 then 13: V ′ ← {r, (r + b) / 2, (t + r) / 2, (l + r) / 2} 14: else if childNo == 5 then 15: V ′ ← {(l + b) / 2, (l + r) / 2, (t + r) / 2, (r + b) / 2} 16: else if childNo == 6 then 17: V ′ ← {b, (r + b) / 2, (l + b) / 2, (t + b) / 2} 18: else if childNo == 7 then 19: V ′ ← {(t + r) / 2, (t + b) / 2, (l + b) / 2, (r + b) / 2} 20: end if 21: return V ′ 22: end function

#### 3.3.2 Barycentric Coordinates

An alternative to the point-plane tests is to use the barycentric coordinates of our sample point calculated for the current element we are inside. The logic is similar to the point plane test traversal algorithm. However, instead of using planes explicitly, we can determine which child element we are in by checking different combinations of these barycentric coordinates. (u, v,w, x) correspond to the barycentric coordinates of the point associated with the currently traversed element's top, left, right, and back vertices, respectively. The first check (u+v) < 0.5 corresponds to the plane (T R,LB,T B), the vertices of which have the following barycentric coordinates.

- TR = (0.5, 0, 0.5, 0) LB = (0, 0.5, 0, 0.5) TB = (0.5, 0, 0, 0.5)

The plane defined by these three points is the u + v = 0.5 plane and divides the volume of the tetrahedron into half. Any point closer to the top or left vertices will have u > 0.5 or v > 0.5, moving away from the plane. The other check, (v + w) < 0.5, corresponds to the plane (T R,LB,T L) in the point-plane test version and works similarly. The plane tests that check whether the point is in an inner or outer element are also similarly translated to their barycentric coordinate versions; we also do not need to do all four tests here, as mentioned previously. Using these tests, we can identify which child element we are in of the current traversed element. Then we use CalculateChildID, and CalculateTetrahedronVertices to calculate the new ID and vertex values of the tetrahedron to continue the traversal. Since the volume of the child tetrahedra is one-eighth of the parent element, we divide the volume by eight after each iteration.

To optimize the algorithm, we can skip normalizing the barycentric coordinates, and instead of the volume, we can calculate the half volume at the beginning, so the comparisons against 0.5 become comparisons against the half volume. We know that any volume calculation will have a positive signed value, as the vertices of the elements have been ordered for that. So, we do not need to worry about the volume signs during the comparisons.

Algorithm 6 Barycentric coordinate method for TET-AMR refinement tree traversal.

1: function BARYCENTRICCOORDINATETRAVERSAL(tetID, childCounts, maxDepth, point) 2: rLevel ← 0 3: tetID ← coarseTetID 4: V ← GetCoarseVertices(tetID) 5: vol ← volume(t,l,r,b) 6: while childCounts[tetID] > 0 and rLevel ≤ maxDepth do 7: t, l, r, b ← V 8: u ← volume(point,l,r,b)/vol 9: v ← volume(point,t,b,r)/vol 10: w ← volume(point,t,l,b)/vol 11: x ← 1−(u+v+w) 12: RB half ← (u+v) < 0.5 13: TB half ← (v+w) < 0.5 14: inner ← (u < 0.5) & (v < 0.5) & (w < 0.5) & (x < 0.5) 15: tet ← (RB×4) + ((RB == T B)×2) +inner 16: V ← CalculateTetrahedronVertices(V, tet) 17: tet id ← CalculateChildID(tetID,tet, childCounts) 18: vol ← vol/8 19: + +rLevel 20: end while 21: return tetID,V 22: end function

### 3.4 Sampling the TET-AMR

During rendering, once the element containing the sample point has been found, the next step is to generate the actual sample from the associated scalars of the element. Depending on the scalar mode, we either sample cell-centered scalars from the face neighbors of the element or interpolate the vertex-centered scalars of the element.

#### 3.4.1 Vertex-centered Data Sampling

We sample vertex-centered data by interpolating the scalar data at the vertices associated with the current element, using the barycentric coordinates of the sample point inside the sampled element. Since we have stored the vertex-centered data duplicated per element during the TET-AMR construction process, they can be accessed directly with just the element ID.

### 3.4.2 Cell-centered Data Sampling

We can use the neighbor IDs of a given element to calculate a sample by averaging the cell-centered data of the element and its neighbors. We call this the "simple" cell sampling method. However, this will be a coarse approximation as we are not considering the distances to the neighbors' centroids, where the cell-centered data is sampled.

Since we do not store the vertices of the non-coarse elements during rendering, finding the cell neighbors' centroids is not trivial. We know the three vertices belonging to the neighbor if it has the same refinement level since we share a face, or if the neighbor is from a coarser level, we know that the neighboring face of our element is on the neighbor's larger corresponding face. Without traversing the refinement tree to which the neighbor belongs, we cannot find all the vertices of the neighbor.

So we approximate the location of the neighbor's centroid by treating the face as sitting in the middle of the coarse elements' face. If the neighbor is on another refinement tree, we can calculate the volume of our coarse element and the coarse element the neighbor is refined from, and take their ratio (which is one if the neighbor is on the same refinement tree). We can then use the refinement level difference between our element and the neighbor, which we had stored during TET-AMR construction, and multiply the volume ratio by 8*rLevelDi f f erence*, since each child element has one-eighth of its parent's volume. After getting this ratio of volumes, we can calculate the proportional face-to-centroid distance of the neighboring element, as we approximate the ratio of the centroid distance on the face to which they are neighboring. Using this value, we can approximate the neighbor's centroid location. The method is given in Algorithm 7. The descriptions of the helper functions used in the algorithm are as follows: ProcessNeighborID returns the data we stored in the high bits during TET-AMR construction. GetVolumeRatio returns the ratio of the coarse element volumes of both elements given. GetUnitFaceNormal returns the unit face normal pointing outside of our element. We refer to this method as the "approximate" cell sampling method.

Going one step further, we can also devise a method that uses the refinement tree traversal process, which depends on the neighborhood information of only the coarse mesh elements. Like the approximation method, we can find which coarse tetrahedron our neighbor is in, then use the traversal process to find the vertices of the element we want to sample from. We call this the "exact" cell sampling method.

## Chapter 4

## Rendering TET-AMR

As introduced in Chapter 3, the tetrahedral tree traversal process starts with a coarse tetrahedral element. So, during the rendering process, we need to find the coarse element ID in which the sample point is contained whenever we want to sample a point. To this end, we use a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) to locate each coarse element efficiently. Once we find a coarse element, it is processed by traversing the child elements of the associated refinement tree on the ray until we are out of the coarse element, using one of the two rendering algorithms we describe in this chapter. Then we repeat the coarse mesh traversal process and find the ID for the next coarse element.

### 4.1 Coarse Mesh Traversal

Coarse mesh traversal can be done using element marching, with a method like shellto-shell traversal [10], which uses a BVH for finding the (possibly multiple) entry and exit points of a mesh, handling holes and non-convexity, but uses an XOR-compacting scheme [9] to march across elements between the entry and exit points. Another option is using modern GPU hardware for accelerated BVH traversal and ray-triangle intersection tests to perform point location queries like Wald et al. [12]. Following the replicated faces method detailed by Wald et al., we use a triangle BVH to traverse the coarse tetrahedral mesh. The BVH is built over the coarse mesh by inserting four triangular faces facing outside, for every coarse element.

Unlike element marching-based approaches that use connectivity information to march across elements, point location querying allows us to directly find the element containing the point, allowing us to skip coarse elements that we were not going to be sampling from. Not relying on the connectivity information also allows us to insert coarse elements from varying refinement levels into the coarse mesh, without being limited to only the root elements of the TET-AMR. Neighboring tetrahedra in the coarse mesh can even be from different refinement levels, which would not be possible while using connectivity information to march across the elements, as one face could have many neighbors on the other side. Since the refinement tree traversal process is the same at each level, we can start the traversal process from the child tetrahedra in the coarse mesh, treating them as root elements. This approach can allow performance gains in certain situations where we can reduce the height of deep trees, at the cost of increasing the BVH element count.

We have used OptiX Wrapper Library (OWL) [34], a wrapper for OptiX [35] (NVIDIA's GPU Ray Casting API), to implement the coarse mesh traversal logic and the renderers. We have implemented hit and miss functions that return information about the ray when traced into the geometry stored in the BVH. The hit function returns the information about the hit point and, most importantly, the coarse tetrahedron ID, which we use to start the refinement tree traversal process. In contrast, the miss function reports that the ray did not intersect with any faces in the BVH.

We trace two rays into the BVH, one with front-face culling enabled, and the other with back-face culling enabled. If at least one of the rays has hit, there are three cases with the coarse mesh traversal algorithm, which can be seen in Figure 4.1. If the front culling ray has missed, we know that we are out of the coarse mesh, and there are no elements after the current one, as if there were any, they would have intersected with the ray. Then, if the back culling ray has missed, we know that we are inside the last element that will intersect with the ray on the coarse mesh (Case 3). If both rays intersect with the same tetrahedral element, we know that the ray is currently out of the coarse mesh, and the next element is a boundary element (Case 1). If both rays have intersected with different tetrahedral elements (Case 2), which is the case for any neighboring tetrahedra, or if there is a hole in the volume. Using the miss case and the three distinct cases, we can handle traversing coarse meshes that are non-convex or have holes.

If none of the rays hit, we return a value indicating that we are outside and beyond the coarse mesh. For any other case, we return the coarse element ID, the maximum t value for the element, and the t value for the next coarse element on the ray. The detailed pseudo-code of coarse mesh traversal is given in Algorithm 8. We use the nextafterf function to carry the t values to the next representable floating point number, just over the previous hit's t value to avoid hitting the same face in the next iteration.

Case 2: the ray is inside the coarse mesh and intersects another coarse mesh element. Case 3: the ray is inside a boundary element of the coarse mesh, and will exit the volume.

### 4.2 Coarse Mesh Macrocells

Since we already use the coarse mesh for the identification of the coarse elements, also using it as a macrocell [29] mesh can allow us to perform performance optimizations, such as empty space skipping by checking the maximum density value associated with the coarse element before we start processing it. If it is zero, we continue to the next coarse element, skipping the current one. It can also allow the Woodcock renderer to obtain a tighter majorant extinction for each coarse element, reducing the chances of a sample being rejected and allowing us to sample longer free-path distances for that region of the volume, reducing the number of samples taken, and improving performance.

#### Algorithm 8 Coarse mesh traversal.

1: function TRAVERSECOARSEMESH(ray) 2: rayRecord ← traceRay(ray, CULL FRONT FACING TRIANGLES*)* 3: if rayRecord.missed then 4: return -1, 0, 0 5: end if 6: tBack ← rayRecord.t 7: coarseID ← rayRecord.coarseTetID 8: traceRay(ray, CULL BACK FACING TRIANGLES*)* 9: tFront ← rayRecord.t 10: if !rayRecord.missed then 11: if coarseID == rayRecord.coarseTetID then 12: ray.t ← nextafterf(tFront, INFINITY*)* 13: tNextFace ← nextafterf(tBack, INFINITY*)* 14: else 15: tNextFace ← nextafterf(tFront, INFINITY*)* 16: end if 17: else 18: tNextFace ← nextafterf(tBack, INFINITY*)* 19: end if 20: return coarseID, tBack, tNextFace 21: end function

We treat each element of the coarse mesh as a macrocell, for which we store the minimum and maximum density values of each child element of the tree rooted at that coarse element. The densities are post-classification values obtained by applying the transfer function to each scalar (cell or vertex, depending on sampling method) of these child elements. So, the coarse mesh macrocell densities must be recalculated when the transfer function changes. We use a CUDA kernel to recompute the values whenever the transfer function changes. Since both the transfer function and the scalars already reside in GPU memory for rendering, the recalculation process can be done while rendering at interactive frame rates on the datasets we have tested.

### 4.3 Rendering Algorithms

We have implemented two volume renderers, one using ray marching and the other using Woodcock tracking. Only emission and absorption have been implemented in the renderers, without any secondary effects. Each renderer has an accompanying coarse mesh traversal method that calls the actual rendering method, which processes each coarse element traversed on the coarse mesh. The algorithms given for the renderers use the macrocell density optimizations, but we have also implemented modified versions that do not use the macrocells and use them for the experiments.

### 4.3.1 Ray Marching Algorithm

Our ray marching method starts by identifying which coarse element we will be sampling from. Once we identify the coarse element, we check if the maximum macrocell density for the coarse element is zero before processing it. If this is the case, we skip the element without processing it and move the ray to the start of the next coarse element. This process is given in Algorithm 9.

For each coarse element, we do a two-level nested march, where we march across the coarse element, and inside that, we march across each element on the ray path that is a descendant of the coarse element. This process is given in Algorithm 10. After the refinement tree traversal algorithm returns us the vertices and ID of the element we will be sampling from, we use the vertices of the element to find when our ray is going to exit the element's volume by intersecting the ray with every face of the element and taking the smallest t value that is in front of the ray. This saves us from calling the tree traversal methods again as long as we are in the same sample element. Then we march and take samples until we are out of this sample element. After we take each sample, we move the ray forward by the step size, then apply the transfer function to the scalar, and if it is in range, we use front-to-back compositing to accumulate the color and opacity. Then, when we are out of the coarse element, we return to the coarse mesh traversal method, trace new rays, and find the bounds of the next coarse element. We repeat the process until we are out of the volume or have passed the opacity threshold of 0.99.

The ray marcher uses a constant adjustable step size; we have not implemented any adaptive step size adjustments. The smaller the step size, the more samples will be taken, impacting the rendering performance.

#### Algorithm 9 Ray marching algorithm for the coarse mesh.

1: function RAYMARCHCOARSE(ray, macrocellValues, stepSize, tf) 2: color ← {0,0,0} 3: α ← 0 4: while α < 0.99 do 5: coarseID, tBack, tNextFace ← TraverseCoarseMesh(ray) 6: if coarseID == −1 then 7: break 8: end if 9: skipElement ← macrocellValues[coarseID] ≤ 0 10: if !skipElement then 11: color, alpha ← MarchElement(ray, color, α,tBack, coarseID,stepSize,tf) 12: end if 13: ray.t ← max(tNextFace, ray.t) 14: end while 15: return (1 - α*)* × bgColor + color 16: end function

#### Algorithm 10 Ray marching algorithm for every coarse element.

1: function RAYMARCHELEMENT(ray, color, alpha, tMax, coarseTetID, stepSize, tf) 2: tExit ← 0 3: sampleTetID ← 0 4: tetVertices ← {} 5: while ray.t < t max do 6: samplePoint ← ray.org + ray.t × ray.dir 7: if ray.t > tExit then 8: sampleTetID, tetVertices ← traverse(samplePoint, coarseTetID) 9: tExit ← intersectFaces(ray.t, samplePoint, ray.direction, tetVertices) 10: end if 11: scalar ← sampleData(samplePoint, sampleTetID, tetVertices 12: ray.t ← ray.t + stepSize 13: if !tf.inRange(scalar) then 14: continue 15: end if 16: tfColor ← tf.getColor(scalar) 17: tfColor.opacity = tfColor.opacity × tf.opacity 18: color ← color + (1 - α*)* × tfColor.opacity + tfColor 19: alpha ← α + tfColor.opacity × (1 - α*)* 20: end while 21: return color, α 22: end function

#### 4.3.2 Woodcock Algorithm

Like the ray marcher, we have two algorithms, the first one, Algorithm 11, to navigate the coarse mesh, and the other, Algorithm 12, to perform the actual Woodcock tracking inside each coarse element.

The method for processing each coarse element starts with obtaining a majorant extinction value for the volume contained inside the coarse element. We use the maximum macrocell density for the coarse element scaled with the opacity of the transfer function as the coarse element's majorant extinction to provide finer visualization control, and to obtain a tight majorant. If we do not use the macrocell values, we use the transfer function opacity as the majorant. If the majorant value is 0, we do not process the element and move to the next coarse element on the ray.

We then sample a free path and move our ray forward with the majorant we obtained. Suppose we have not stepped outside the coarse element's volume yet. In that case, we find the element to sample from by calling the refinement tree traversal function, and find when the ray will exit the sample element, like we have done in the ray marching algorithm, saving us from traversing the tree again while we are inside the same element. Then we apply the transfer function to our sampled scalar value and check if the sample is accepted by comparing the density of our sample with a randomly sampled value in the range [0,majorant). If the sample is accepted, we return the color; otherwise, we treat it as a null-collision and continue processing the same coarse element.

During Woodcock with macrocell majorant values, if we step over to the next coarse element at the end of the previous element, we might overstep if the previous volume was less dense than the volume we are stepping into, since the free-paths are sampled with respect to the density. So when we are done with a coarse element, we set ray.t to just after the next coarse element's minimum t value in front of the ray, effectively taking a step back to the beginning of the next coarse element. We do not need to do this if we are not using macrocell majorant values.

The coarse Woodcock algorithm continues until a sample is accepted or we have passed through the volume without accepting any samples, in which case we return the background color. At each render step, we wait until each launched ray returns a color, whether an accepted sample or the background color. We use a global accumulation count that we increase after each step to weigh and add the new sample to the existing image buffer. As the sample count increases, the rendered image becomes less noisy as the variance reduces.

Algorithm 11 Woodcock tracking algorithm for the coarse mesh.

1: function WOODCOCKCOARSE(ray, macrocellValues, tf) 2: while true do 3: coarseID, tBack, tNextFace ← TraverseCoarseMesh(ray) 4: if coarseID == −1 then 5: return bg color 6: end if 7: macrocellMajorant ← macrocellValues[coarseID] 8: if WoodcockElem(ray, color, tBack, coarseID, macrocellMajorant, tf) then 9: return color 10: end if 11: ray.t ← tNextFace 12: end while 13: end function

### 4.4 View-dependent Early Tree Traversal Return

We have implemented a view-dependent early return from the refinement tree traversal function. While we traverse each level of a refinement tree to find the element to sample, whether we go down another level is decided by comparing the normalized screen space area with a user-controlled refinement criteria variable. We have defined the variable as a threshold for normalized screen space size, so the same refinement criteria give the same results with different resolutions. However, it can be easily converted to a true screen space area by multiplying it by the total screen space.

The total normalized screen space area of a tetrahedron can be calculated by summing up the four triangles that can be created with the four vertices of the tetrahedron

1: function WOODCOCKELEM(ray, color, tMax, coarseTetID, macrocellMajorant, tf) 2: majorant ← tf.opacity × macrocellMajorant 3: if majorant ≤ 0 then 4: return false 5: end if 6: tExit ← nextafterf(ray.t, -INFINITY) 7: sampleTetID ← 0 8: tetVertices ← {} 9: while true do 10: ray.t ← ray.t - log(1 - rand()) / majorant 11: if ray.t ≥ tMax then 12: return false 13: end if 14: samplePoint ← ray.org + ray.t × ray.dir 15: if t > tExit then 16: sampleTetID, tetVertices ← traverse(samplePoint, coarseTetID) 17: tExit ← intersectFaces(ray.t, samplePoint, ray.direction, tetVertices) 18: end if 19: scalar ← sampleData(samplePoint, sampleTetID, tetVertices) 20: if !tf.inRange(scalar) then 21: continue 22: end if 23: color ← tf.getColor(scalar) 24: if color.opacity × tf.opacity ≥ rand() × majorant then 25: return color 26: end if 27: end while 28: end function

## Experimental Results

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

The experiments were run on a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti desktop GPU, using OWL and OptiX 7.7 to implement our renderer. The BVHs we use in our renderers are OptiX Geometry Acceleration Structures. We build them with the default build flags, which are as follows: OPTIX BUILD FLAG PREFER FAST TRACE and OP-TIX BUILD FLAG ALLOW COMPACTION.

We have implemented two TET-AMR renderers, a ray marcher, and a Woodcock tracker. To compare against them, we have also implemented the renderers' unstructured mesh versions that use the same coarse mesh traversal process, but all leaf-level elements are inserted into the BVH, removing the refinement tree traversal process. Unless otherwise stated, the tests were run with vertex scalars, using the barycentric traversal method for the TET-AMR renderers, and the images were rendered at a resolution of 1920 x 1080. The transfer functions, step sizes for the ray marcher, and the opacities were chosen per dataset, but were kept the same throughout the experiments. All renderers were warmed up before the benchmarks started, to avoid the overhead of cold starts. The render times are the mean time to render a frame, calculated over 100 frames for the ray marchers and 1000 frames for the Woodcock trackers.

In the tests, if the memory needed for the acceleration structure building process could fit into the GPU device memory, we kept it as a single bottom-level acceleration structure (BLAS). Otherwise, we constructed a top-level acceleration structure (TLAS) + BLAS hierarchy. If we group the elements into BLASs without any care about the geometry, this results in more TLAS tests per ray, thus decreasing the performance. The data must be carefully divided into smaller non-overlapping groups for the best performance. To this end, we have split the tetrahedra into four groups, with a split through the center x-value and another split through the center y-value of the bounding box of the geometry, trying to keep the bounding boxes of the chunks as separate as possible to minimize the performance loss and provide a fair comparison. We only had to do this for the unstructured renderer with the Engine dataset with 34M leaf elements.

### 5.2 Datasets

We have generated the datasets for the experiments using t8code to convert existing datasets to tetrahedral AMR data or to generate it directly. We then used the vtu output from t8code, containing tetrahedral elements consisting of only recursively subdivided leaf-level tetrahedral elements, and processed it into our own TET-AMR structure with the process we have detailed in Section 3.2.

The data generation process with t8code starts with a coarse tetrahedral mesh, optionally with the coarse mesh elements already refined to a certain level. At each step, t8code adapts the mesh, coarsening or refining all leaf-level elements, according to user-provided callback functions. We decided to refine elements whose difference of their element centroid sample and the average of their four corners' samples differs more than a certain threshold. The data is sampled from the input volume we are processing, a structured grid or an unstructured mesh.

The datasets we have converted or created are as follows:

- Engine, Carp, MRI, Heptane: These raw structured grids from Open Scientific Visualization Datasets [36] were processed with the above-mentioned method.

- Engine Uniform: The same Engine dataset as above, but refined to a uniform refinement level of 7 throughout the mesh, with 6×8 7 leaf elements.
- Torus: Data sampled from the implicit equation of a torus, refined to a uniform refinement level of 6, with 6×8 6 leaf elements.
- Advection: Comes from the t*8 *advection example on the t8code GitHub repository [18].
- Agulhas: Unlike the ones above, the Agulhas unstructured mesh was used as the input coarse mesh and was refined until the threshold was met up to a maximum refinement level of 3. Creating many refinement trees that are shallower than the other datasets.

### 5.7 Cell Sampling vs. Vertex Sampling

We have tested the three cell sampling methods and the vertex sampling method described in Section 3.4. Their rendering times are given in Tables 5.7 and5.8, and a visual comparison between them is given in Figure 5.2. The cell sampling methods result in worse visual fidelity, with varying degrees of visual fidelity between them. The vertex samples provide better visual fidelity.

The best performing method was the simple cell sampling method, as it does not perform any calculations other than averaging the data of the neighboring cells. Vertex sampling comes in second as it just performs barycentric interpolation with respect to the sample point inside the sampled element, since scalars at the vertices are already sampled from the neighbors of each vertex during the TET-AMR construction process. The approximate and exact cell sampling methods come last, requiring the most additional computation. The exact method calls the refinement tree traversal method up to four times per sample, totaling up to five traversal method calls per point sampled, with the initial call for the point itself. This results in the worst performance, taking an order of magnitude more time to sample than the simple method.
