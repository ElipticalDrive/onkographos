# 1 Introduction

Volumetric rendering has been thoroughly investigated to depict unique properties of non-surface materials such as clouds, fogs, and jade. Although certain materials like skin can be approximated

using subsurface scattering [Hanrahan and Krueger 1993], it is inadequate to model correct interaction of ray and particles in general participating media. Since Kajiya and Von Herzen [1984] introduced the Volumetric Radiance Transport Equation (VRTE) to computer graphics, path tracing with Monte Carlo integration method has been widely adopted. The estimation process is accelerated by generalizing traditional methods to volumetric scattering like better tracing method (e.g. bidirectional path tracing [Lafortune and Willems 1996]), more efficient sampling for path construction and transmittance estimation (e.g. volumetric scattering path guiding [Xu et al. 2024], progressive null-tracking [Misso et al. 2023]), more sophisticated sample-reuse techniques (e.g. volumetric ReSTIR [Lin et al. 2021]) and so on. VRTE is also further derived into other practical forms [Miller et al. 2019]. However, complex volumetric path tracing is still too costly for real-time applications. Particularly, some media, notably clouds, have high single-scattering albedo, leading to visually perceptible radiance contribution even after hundreds of bounces in a single volume. This makes practical post-processing like real-time denoising with low samples per pixel (spp) [Chaitanya et al. 2017] inapplicable, as the input is too far from the converged result in some circumstances.

Recently, neural methods have been applied to volumetric rendering. Radiance-Predicting Neural Networks (RPNN) [Kallweit et al. 2017] opened up a new paradigm to estimate multi-scattering effects, achieving speedup of up to three orders of magnitude over CPU-based path tracing. However, RPNN still requires several minutes to produce a converged result. Multi-feature RPNN (MRPNN) [Hu et al. 2023] improves the algorithm further, reaching up to 40 FPS on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU at  $1024^2$  resolution. MRPNN also supports globally configurable albedo and phase function parameters.

Despite these advances, MRPNN still has some key limitations. First, its performance remains insufficient in complex real-time applications for consuming tens of milliseconds in a single pass. Second, it assumes fragmented volumes to be continuous low-density volumes and thus systematically overestimates global illumination for such scenes, as shown in Fig. 2. Finally, it requires consistent albedo and phase function parameters throughout the scene, which significantly restricts its ability to represent more complex media.



Fig. 2. Fragmented-volume scenario as a failure case for MRPNN [Hu et al. 2023]. Our latent-space baking operates without low-density volume assumption and thus alleviates over-illumination artifact.

To address these challenges, we propose Multi-feature Radiance Baking Neural Networks (MRBNN) that exploits the structure of volumetric scattering. Inspired by Neural Radiosity [Hadadan et al. 2021], we observe that evaluating the integral is more accurate but computationally expensive than direct prediction for in-scattering

field. By expanding the phase function in Legendre polynomials, we show that the in-scattering integral forms a diagonal scaling operator in spherical harmonics basis, yielding an equivalent reweighting that eliminates integration. While such spectral operator form is analytically compact, its coefficients remain high-dimensional field that is infeasible to store or evaluate explicitly under dynamic conditions. To bridge this gap, we introduce a compact latent-space radiance baking that provides a finite-rank, factorized parameterization with practical memory cost. After querying features with our dual-pattern sampling strategy, we employ a lightweight neural decoder for interpreting the learned representation to scattering radiance with substantially low latency. As shown in Fig. 1, MRBNN achieves significant acceleration over prior work while maintaining high visual fidelity. It also supports challenging configurations such as spatially varying albedo and heterogeneous phase parameters that cannot be handled previously.

In summary, our contributions include the following aspects:

- • We reformulate in-scattering integral in VRTE as a diagonal scaling operator in the spherical harmonics spectrum, which eliminates explicit spherical integration in theory.
- • We propose a compact latent-space neural baking that learns a low-rank, factorized approximation of such scattering operator with practical space complexity, which can be decoded by our dual-pattern feature query and lightweight network.
- • We design a real-time volumetric renderer that achieves  $4 \sim 5 \times$  speedup over the state-of-the-art while supporting spatially varying albedo, heterogeneous phase functions and improved temporal stability.



## 2 Related Work

Stochastic Volumetric Ray Tracing. Photorealistic rendering of participating media typically relies on stochastic approaches like Monte Carlo integration method to solve VRTE numerically. Sampling in-side volumes has drawn researchers' attention as a distinct problem. To avoid explicit transmittance estimation, null-collision methods apply delta tracking [Woodcock et al. 1965] to sample the next position; the resulting path-space PDF was analytically formulated by Miller et al. [2019]. In other sampling methods that require transmittance calculation, Novák et al. [2018] provided a comprehensive survey of related estimation methods. More recently, power-series expansion [Georgiev et al. 2019] and unbiased ray marching [Kettunen et al. 2021] have been proposed to introduce a range of novel transmittance estimators and reduce estimation variance further.

Alternatively, volumetric path tracing can adapt techniques originally developed for surface rendering. For instance, bidirectional path tracing [Lafortune and Willems 1996] samples from both camera and light, and Metropolis methods [Pauly et al. 2000] focus on sampling high-contribution paths through mutation and acceptance. More recent advances include path guiding that performs product sampling over different integrand components [Herholz et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2024] or uses neural network to fit target distribution [Dong et al. 2023], reservoir resampling [Lin et al. 2021], and extension to kernel-based volumetric primitives [Condor et al. 2025].

Though capable of producing photorealistic images, complex volumetric path tracing is generally unable to converge to a satisfying

result in real time, even with advanced sampling or post-processing techniques. Our goal is to accelerate this process by leveraging light-weight network that predicts multi-scattering radiance directly.

Neural Rendering. Deep learning has been actively explored to model neural global illumination for its powerful approximation capability. Representative approaches include mesh-based rendering with homogeneous translucent materials using DiffusionNet and BSSRDF [An et al. 2025; Sharp et al. 2022], vertex-based feature learning with refinement [Su et al. 2025], object-oriented representations for modeling object-environment interactions [Zheng et al. 2024], and transformer-based architectures for end-to-end image synthesis [Zeng et al. 2025]. However, these methods are designed for surface-based light transport and rely on the surface RTE, making them ill-suited for real-time volumetric rendering governed by VRTE. Extending them to participating media either breaks their core assumptions (e.g. surface vertices and normals) or leads to unpromising results and cost.

Particularly, neural feature fields have played a key role in graphics in recent years, ranging from 3D reconstruction (e.g. neural radiance fields [Mildenhall et al. 2021]) to rendering acceleration (e.g. neural radiance cache [Müller et al. 2021], neural radiosity [Hadadan et al. 2021; Su et al. 2024]). An important representation for such field is feature grid, where features are spatially discretized and interpolated when used. To minimize memory usage, various strategies including octree [Takikawa et al. 2021], hashing [Müller et al. 2022] and k-planes decomposition [Cao and Johnson 2023] were proposed. In this paper, we also utilize feature grid as a baking approach to handle dynamic material and light conditions.

Precomputed Radiance Transfer (PRT). PRT [Sloan et al. 2002] approximates indirect illumination by computing transfer matrix under angular basis, notably spherical harmonics basis. Such representation allows the rendering equation to be evaluated as simple dot products at runtime, and spherical harmonics also enables handling of dynamic transport [Pan et al. 2007; Sloan et al. 2005]. Recent advances include using neural network to substitute transport in PRT [Rainer et al. 2022]. Building on this line of work, we exploit the zonal convolution properties of spherical harmonics to construct a latent-space feature grid for dynamic lighting modeling.

RPNN Series. Among existing methods for photorealistic volumetric rendering, the RPNN family stands out for its impressive acceleration. Kallweit et al. [2017] pioneered this line by predicting indirect illumination through layered density field to avoid explicit simulation of multi-bounce scattering. This process was further improved by Panin and Nikolenko [2019] through caching intermediate features that achieved  $2 \sim 3\times$  speedup. Hu et al. [2023] later identified that direct mapping from density field to radiance field is overly complex, leading to a large model with millions of parameters and minutes of inference time. To address this problem, MRPNN introduces auxiliary hints on albedo, transmittance field and cumulative phase, substantially reducing model scale and enabling real-time performance with globally configurable albedo and phase. MRPNN also improves shadow boundaries prediction through additional high-frequency feature sampling.

Despite its compelling optimization, MRPNN still exhibits several limitations that hinder efficient and robust prediction. First, to capture surrounding volumetric context, MRPNN relies on multiple complex sampling strategies, leading to numerous irregular texture lookups and remaining artifacts such as over-illumination in fragmented or challenging scenes. Second, MRPNN does not model spatially varying albedo or heterogeneous phase configurations, and thus cannot handle volumes with scattering coefficient grids or multiple phase parameters. Finally, its network architecture remains relatively complex and leaves room for further simplification to better exploit GPU computational efficiency. In this paper, we introduce a tiny network with compact latent-space baking that targets to address these problems.



### 3 Neural Baking

We adopt a common and effective strategy for volumetric rendering by approximating indirect illumination beyond the first scattering event. As illustrated in Fig. 3, we first draw volumetric samples along the camera ray using stochastic sampling techniques (see Sec. 2). For each sampled point, we compute direct illumination explicitly and predict high-order scattering effects via our model by approximating the in-scattering radiance. The contributions from all samples are then accumulated to estimate the radiance received by the camera. Formally, the whole process can be summarized as:


$$\hat{L}(p, \omega) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{T_r(p'_i \rightarrow p)}{f(p'_i)} \left( L_{\text{direct}}(p'_i, \omega) + \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{F}(p'_i, \omega)) \right) \quad (1)$$

where  $T_r(p_1 \rightarrow p_2)$  is transmittance from  $p_1$  to  $p_2$ ,  $p'_i$  is position sampled from distribution  $f$ , starting from camera position  $p$  and view direction  $\omega$ ;  $\mathcal{F}$  denotes multi-scale feature extraction from the baking, and  $\mathcal{S}$  denotes in-scattering prediction networks. Besides, as a basic assumption in RPNN series, we establish sampling pattern and predict contribution from a directional light. For ambient light environment, light direction can be drawn randomly to fulfill such assumption. Additionally, we emphasize that as a baking method, our latent-space representation needs a per-volume tuning to find suitable solution, while MRPNN operates as a general mapping with input extracted from volume directly. This is a trade-off in our method to achieve substantial acceleration.



Fig. 3. Rendering pipeline overview.

In Sec. 3, we first briefly review VRTE and present theoretical derivation to express in-scattering integral in spherical harmonics basis using Legendre polynomials. Next, we will illustrate how such

formulation is practically integrated in our feature baking. In Sec. 4, we will propose sampling patterns to extract features from latent space and a lightweight network for decoding input to radiance.



### 3.1 In-scattering Integral Transformation

VRTE expresses the radiance  $L(p, \omega)$  at position  $p$  and direction  $\omega$  in the form of an integro-differential equation. By solving ordinary differential equation, VRTE can be written in the classic form below:

$$\begin{aligned} L_s(p, \omega) &= \sigma_a(p, \omega)L_e(p, \omega) + \sigma_s(p, \omega) \int_{S^2} \phi(p, \omega_i, \omega) L(p, \omega_i) d\omega_i, \\ L(p, \omega) &= \int_0^\infty T_r(p' \rightarrow p) L_s(p', \omega) dt \end{aligned} \quad (2)$$

where  $\sigma_a(p, \omega)$  and  $\sigma_s(p, \omega)$  are absorption coefficient and scattering coefficient respectively, and  $\phi(p, \omega_1, \omega_2)$  is phase function between direction  $\omega_1$  and  $\omega_2$  at position  $p$ . Specifically, this equation can be interpreted as follows:

- •  $L_s(p, \omega)$  denotes radiance contribution from point  $p$  along direction  $\omega$ , which contains self-emission (i.e.  $L_e(p, \omega)$ ) and in-scattering from surrounding directions (i.e. the spherical integral). Phase function models radiance ratio scattered from one direction to another.
- • To get  $L(p, \omega)$ , all radiance contribution at point  $p'$  along direction  $\omega$  will be accumulated, weighted by transmittance to represent decay when travelling through media.

And finally let  $\sigma_t(p, \omega) = \sigma_a(p, \omega) + \sigma_s(p, \omega)$  be extinction coefficient (or density), albedo  $\alpha(p, \omega)$  is defined as  $\sigma_s(p, \omega)/\sigma_t(p, \omega)$  and transmittance is formulated as  $T_r(p_1 \rightarrow p_2) = e^{-\int_0^d \sigma_t(p, \omega) dt}$  where  $p = p_1 + \omega t$ ,  $d = \|p_1 - p_2\|_2$ . For notation brevity, the in-scattering integral  $\int_{S^2} \phi(p, \omega_i, \omega) L(p, \omega_i) d\omega_i$  is denoted by  $S(p, \omega)$  in following formulas.

In practice, phase function  $\phi(\omega_1, \omega_2)$  is rotationally symmetric and therefore a function of  $\cos \theta = \omega_1 \cdot \omega_2$ , even for the most complex form Lorenz-Mie. Thus essentially, the in-scattering integral in VRTE naturally implies an angular operator  $\mathcal{K}$ :

$$(\mathcal{K}h)(p, \omega) := \int_{S^2} \phi(p, (\omega_i \cdot \omega)) h(p, \omega_i) d\omega_i, \quad (3)$$

Applying such operator on the angular radiance distribution  $L(p, \cdot)$  results in the in-scattering field  $S(p, \cdot)$ , i.e.  $S = \mathcal{K}L$ . Our primary goal is to estimate  $S(p, \omega)$  so there are two approaches:

1. (1) Direct: decompose and estimate  $S(p, \omega)$  directly.
2. (2) Indirect: decompose and estimate  $L(p, \omega)$ , then apply  $\mathcal{K}$  to estimate  $S(p, \omega)$  by evaluating integral.

Though case (2) is more computationally expensive, it is likely to yield less error as similar conclusion in surface rendering is experimentally verified by Neural Radiosity [Hadadan et al. 2021]. However, we prove that  $\mathcal{K}$  forms a diagonal scaling operator analytically under spherical harmonics (SH) spectrum, which allows it to be evaluated without explicit integration. Formally:

THEOREM 3.1. Let radiance field SH decomposition be  $L(p, \omega) = \sum_{l=0}^\infty \sum_{m=-l}^l L_l^m(p) Y_l^m(\omega)$ , the in-scattering field can be expressed

as:

$$S(p, \omega) = \sum_{l=0}^\infty \eta_l(p) \sum_{m=-l}^l L_l^m(p) Y_l^m(\omega)$$

where  $\eta_l(p) = \frac{4\pi f_l(p)}{2l+1}$  and  $f_l(p)$  is the  $l$ -th order coefficients of Legendre polynomials  $P_l$  decomposed from phase function  $\phi$  at position  $p$ , and  $Y_l^m$  is the SH of degree  $l$  and order  $m$ .

By expanding SH components in two matrices,  $\mathcal{K}$  is essentially a diagonal matrix  $\text{diag}(\eta_0, \eta_1, \dots)$  (see Supplementary Sec. D.1 for details). When the whole volume shares the same phase parameter as MRPNN assumes, this matrix is globally consistent.

PROOF. Substituting the SH expansion of  $L(p, \omega)$  and the Legendre expansion of  $\phi$ , then applying the addition theorem of SH to  $P_l$ ,  $S(p, \omega)$  is thus equal to:

$$\int_{S^2} \left( \sum_{l=0}^\infty \frac{4\pi f_l(p)}{2l+1} \sum_{m=-l}^l Y_l^m(\omega) \bar{Y}_l^m(\omega_i) \right) \cdot \left( \sum_{l', m'} L_{l'}^{m'}(p) Y_{l'}^{m'}(\omega_i) \right) d\omega_i \quad (4)$$

Utilizing the orthogonality property  $\int_{S^2} Y_{l'}^{m'}(\omega_i) \bar{Y}_l^m(\omega_i) d\omega_i = \delta_{ll'} \delta_{mm'}$ , the integral is reduced to:

$$S(p, \omega) = \sum_{l, m} \frac{4\pi f_l(p)}{2l+1} L_l^m(p) Y_l^m(\omega) \quad (5)$$

Identifying  $\eta_l(p) = \frac{4\pi f_l(p)}{2l+1}$  yields the theorem.  $\square$ 

Detailed derivation, including illustration of summation reordering, is provided in Supplementary Sec. D.2 and D.3. In particular, Henyey-Greenstein phase function (HG) has an elegant decomposition form [Sansone 1959] as stated in Lemma 3.2.

LEMMA 3.2. Given  $\phi(g, \omega_1, \omega_2) = \frac{1-g^2}{4\pi(1+g^2-2g(\omega_1 \cdot \omega_2))^{3/2}}$ ,  $\eta_l = g^l$ .

It's also worth noting that decomposition of Lorenz-Mie function, as the most complex phase function practically used in rendering, is already derived by physicists [Fowler 1983].

### 3.2 Operator Approximation via Latent-space Baking

Theorem 3.1 reveals the fact that the in-scattering operator  $\mathcal{K}$  essentially acts as a diagonal scaling in SH space, which is an ideal estimator that eliminates explicit integration in  $L \rightarrow S$ . However, such formulation assumes full knowledge of  $L$  decomposition, which is infeasible under dynamic lighting or materials. Therefore, we reinterpret neural baking as an approximation of  $\mathcal{K}$  applied to a compressed latent representation of  $L$ .

For simplicity, now we might as well assume that only light direction  $\omega_s$  varies, so both the total radiance field and in-scattering field become functions of  $\omega_s$ :

$$\begin{aligned} L(p, \omega) &= \sum_{l=0}^\infty \sum_{m=-l}^l L_l^m(p, \omega_s) Y_l^m(\omega) \\ S(p, \omega) &= \sum_{l=0}^\infty \eta_l(p) \sum_{m=-l}^l L_l^m(p, \omega_s) Y_l^m(\omega) \end{aligned} \quad (6)$$

We align view and light direction (i.e.  $\omega = \omega_s$ ) first and introduce factorized encoding  $\mathcal{L}_l^m(p)Y_l^m(\omega_s)$  to express  $L_l^m(p, \omega_s)Y_l^m(\omega_s)$  in latent space, which results in  $\tilde{L}(p, \omega) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m=-l}^l \mathcal{L}_l^m(p)Y_l^m(\omega_s)$  as the latent-space representation of  $L(p, \omega)$ . Applying  $\mathcal{K}$  with low-rank compression, we assume  $\tilde{L}$  can be transformed into scattering field  $\tilde{S}$  in latent space:

$$\tilde{S}(p, \omega) = \sum_{l=0}^K \eta_l(p) \sum_{m=-l}^l \mathcal{L}_l^m(p)Y_l^m(\omega_s) \quad (7)$$

where  $K$  denotes truncation rank in SH.

This expression allows us to convey effects of  $\mathcal{K}$  by weighted sum of 3D feature grids  $\mathcal{L}_l^m$ , which avoids both explicit integration and  $O(N^5)$  space complexity before factorization. To account for view directions  $\omega$  that are distinct from  $\omega_s$ , we introduce view module  $\mathcal{V}$ , another 2D feature grid indexed by  $\omega$ , as condition in decoding. The neural networks are expected to decode  $\tilde{S}(p, \omega)$  with conditions to physical in-scattering radiance, or formally,

$$\mathcal{S} : \left( \sum_{l=0}^K \eta_l(p) \sum_{m=-l}^l \mathcal{L}_l^m(p)Y_l^m(\omega_s), \mathcal{V}(\omega) \right) \rightarrow \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \eta_l(p) \sum_{m=-l}^l L_l^m(p, \omega_s)Y_l^m(\omega) \quad (8)$$

Similarly, we also add corresponding modules for other parameters as network input (i.e. phase module  $\mathcal{P}$  and albedo module  $\mathcal{A}$ ); and to further eliminate effects of aligning  $\omega$  and  $\omega_s$ , both modules will condition on  $\cos \theta = \omega \cdot \omega_s$  as a new dimension. Specifically, in configuration with HG function, we introduce the phase grid  $\mathcal{G}$  indexed by  $(g, \cos \theta)$ .



## 4 Latent-space Field Decoding



### 4.1 Sampling Pattern Design

In order to capture both low-frequency and high-frequency details in the baking, we use dual-pattern sampling as displayed in Fig. 4. All feature grids adopt a multi-resolution representation as different levels of details (LoD) to model scattering environment across varying spatial scopes. Normally, given a position  $p$ , features are sampled at  $p$  exactly in different LoDs as local sampling. To depict shadow boundary more accurately, some features are also sampled towards light from  $p$  by cone tracing. Although our sampling strategy resembles two-part approach in MRPNN, our implicit baking enables a much simpler workload for avoiding extensive irregular texel access.

Naïvely, radiance field feature  $\mathcal{L}_l^m$  could be sampled both locally and towards light directly. However, as a single sample of SH grid requires  $(K+1)^2$  grids to be sampled, such design will amplify feature extraction cost. Instead, we introduce another 3D grid called base grid  $\mathcal{B}$  indexed by position solely. Besides, a mipmapped transmittance map  $\mathcal{T}$  with respect to incoming light direction is also introduced as input to assist learning.  $\mathcal{B}$  and  $\mathcal{T}$ , instead of  $(K+1)^2$  grid components  $\mathcal{L}_l^m$ , adopt towards-light sampling. Our experiments in Sec. 6 show that this does not necessarily affect the final quality, while it contributes to additional 10% performance gain.



Fig. 4. Dual-pattern sampling illustration, projected on 2D plane.  $\mathcal{B}$  and  $\mathcal{T}$  use (a) towards-light sampling like cone tracing, which increases step size exponentially as well as raising feature grid level; other features use (b) local sampling, which queries only at shading point across levels of feature grid. Circles with different colors represent features extraction in different LoDs, which will be input into network gradually later.

### 4.2 Feature Extraction and Network Architecture

To progressively describe the scattering environment, features are extracted level by level across different LoDs and concatenated. For instance, for volumes with HG phase function, feature extraction at  $i$ -th level  $\mathcal{F}_i$  is just concatenation of the following features:


$$\mathcal{F}_i(p, \omega) = \left( \mathcal{B}_i(p_i), \sum_{l=0}^K g(p)^l \sum_{m=-l}^l \mathcal{L}_{i,l}^m(p)Y_l^m(\omega_s), \mathcal{V}_i(\omega), \mathcal{G}_i(g(p), \cos \theta), \mathcal{A}_i(\alpha(p, \omega), \cos \theta), [\mathcal{T}_i(p_j, \omega_s)]_{j=1}^{M_2} \right) \quad (9)$$

where  $i \in \{1, \dots, M_1\}$  and  $M_1$  means both grid levels and total steps towards light to sample  $\mathcal{B}$ , so  $\mathcal{B}_i$  samples at the  $i$ -th towards-light point  $p_i$ ;  $M_2$  denotes steps to sample  $\mathcal{T}$ . Since  $\mathcal{T}$  does not lie in latent space, each level  $\mathcal{T}_i$  samples at all towards-light points  $p_j$  and concatenated as vector of  $M_2$  dimensions to depict the environment.

To fuse multi-scale features to capture both local and global scattering effects, we employ hierarchical residual blocks, where each level additionally incorporates features from the next scale. Formally, let  $\mathcal{F}_i(p, \omega) \in \mathbb{R}^D$  denote the feature at level  $i$ , the fusing process is:

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{O_i}(p, \omega) &= O_{i-1}(p, \omega) + \mathcal{F}_i(p, \omega), \\ O_i(p, \omega) &= \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{O_i}(p, \omega)) + \overline{O_i}(p, \omega), \end{aligned} \quad (10)$$

where  $O_i$  is the output latent feature,  $\mathcal{S}_i$  is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with input and output both in  $\mathbb{R}^D$ . The initial latent feature  $O_0$  is set to zero. After aggregating all features, a final MLP  $\mathcal{S}_{M_1+1}$  decodes the latent representation  $O_{M_1}$  into radiance. An illustration of the network architecture is provided in the Supplementary (Sec. B).

## 5 Implementation

### 5.1 Feature Configuration

Practically, we choose  $M_1 = M_2 = 4$  for Eq. 9 to reuse position samples. The feature grids are sampled within  $[0, 1]^N$  in reality, so directions and positions are represented by normalized spherical coordinates and relative coordinate in volume bounding box respectively.  $\cos \theta \in [-1, 1]$  is mapped by  $(x+1)/2$ . Considering that media with higher albedo scatter rays with increased frequency, finer grid resolution is allocated by a non-linear mapping  $(e^x - 1)/(e - 1)$ . And for volume used in production, scattering and extinction coefficients are stored as scalar voxels that are not affected by direction, therefore we omit  $\omega$  in albedo. Finally, the transmittance map stores optical depth normalized by maximum density. Detailed grid configurations and error analysis on low-order compression are given in the Supplementary Sec. A.

### 5.2 Network Implementation

In practice, we use tiny-cuda-nn [Müller 2021] with PyTorch [Paszke et al. 2019] to implement the network prototype and do training for convenient network adjustment. On completion of the training phase, we export the parameters to perform inference with tailored C++ code. We implement our own feature grid module to fuse sampling within the rendering procedures for avoiding possible computation redundancy. Furthermore, to maximize GPU utilization, we use fully fused MLP [Müller et al. 2021] with just-in-time compilation to execute neural network in a single kernel. Practically, it is roughly sufficient to equip each MLP with two 64-neuron fully-connected layers. We also find it beneficial to use ReLU6 as the final layer activation in MLPs for numeric stability.

### 5.3 Training

Ground Truth Generation. For usual cases, we use the path-tracing renderer provided by MRPNN implementation to ensure a fair comparison. Mitsuba3 Renderer [Jakob et al. 2022] is used for scenes that cannot be handled by MRPNN. Normally, 9k images of resolution  $512^2$  with random configurations are used as ground truth. Each pixel contains sampled position  $p$ , direction  $\omega$  and radiance  $S(p, \omega)$  generated by path tracing with 1024 spp and 1024 maximum bounce depth. However, for hard scenes that suffer from high variance, increased spp and depth can be used to generate better result (Sec. 6.3).

Dataset Preference. To improve robustness in challenging configurations, we use a non-uniform sampling strategy over the parameter space to prefer high albedo and strongly anisotropic phase function. We also augment the training set in which only phase parameters are altered while albedo is fixed with constant near 1. Practically, the dataset is augmented in a 4:1 ratio.

Training Settings. We use Adam optimizer [Kingma and Ba 2017] with an initial learning rate of 1e-3, exponentially decayed by 0.8 every epoch. All scenes are trained for 10 epochs using MSE as loss function. Moreover, considering that radiance values in volume rendering can span several orders of magnitude and thus harm the training process, we compress ground truth  $S$  by  $\log(x+1)$ .



## 6 Results and Analysis

We train our model on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU while testing the performance on an NVIDIA RTX 4080 SUPER GPU. Training completes within four hours in each scene. Our renderer uses delta tracking to sample the first bounce to cancel  $T_r(p'_i \rightarrow p)/f(p'_i)$  in Eq. 1, which is aligned with implementation in MRPNN. All results for MRPNN are produced by authors' open-source implementation.

### 7 Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work

In this paper, we propose a novel lightning-fast neural baking method for high-quality volumetric rendering. We derive a novel diagonal scaling form of in-scattering integral in spherical harmonics basis through Legendre polynomials that eliminates explicit integration, and propose practical methods to employ feature grids accordingly. With our design for dual-pattern feature sampling and compact network architecture, our system is four to five times faster than the state-of-the-art and achieves hundreds of FPS. Beyond ordinary configurations supported by prior work, our method is also capable of handling more complex scenes like ones with spatiallyvarying albedo and phase or high-frequency ambient lighting. We validate our claims through extensive experiments.

Despite the effectiveness of our method, there still exist some limitations. First, our approach bakes volumes with fixed density field, which covers many rendering tasks but cannot handle dynamic media (notably clouds generated by Worley-Perlin noise). A temporal baking method could be used to mitigate this problem. Second, in line with previous work in the RPNN series, we assume a directional light as the light condition to represent time-of-day effects. This restricts its applicability in more complex dynamic incident light environment. Finally, the training cost in our framework is still relatively high. Although the inference speed is remarkable, long training time makes it inconvenient for users to manufacture baking assets. As a neural method, some delicate approach for tuning with few-shot ground truth may be designed.
