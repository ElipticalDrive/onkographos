# I.2 – English summary

Creating full-scale interactive 3D experiences imposes three antagonistic constraints: fine detail, large worlds, and real-time frame rates. Discretizing virtual environments in hierarchical voxel grids presents some key advantages with regard to those constraints. Voxels can represent all types of geometry: they are known for volumetric content like clouds, but can just as easily be used for hard-surface objects like cars, and all the in-between world of aggregate geometries like foliage that have surfaces so complex that they are better represented by an average density field. Voxels are also easy to interpolate and average while preserving appearance, making them perfect for anti-aliased rendering and level of detail (LOD), ensuring both consistent performance and visual quality. Finally, thanks to their native spatial coherence and ordered structure, voxels can easily be ray-traced. Of course, voxels also have their drawbacks, the main one being the tremendous amount of memory they occupy if stored naively.

Previous work showed that by using an octree data structure combined with a cache to dynamically stream or procedurally generate only visible voxels at the appropriate resolution, it is possible to fit voxel scenes with pixel-size detail in GPU memory and reach acceptable frame rates. However, the solution proposed at the time relied on numerous back-and-forth synchronous exchanges between CPU and GPU to manage the two dependent tasks of rendering cached data and on-demand production of new data. This caused poor GPU utilization with many intervals of starvation per frame, spikes of irregular longer frames, and more generally perfectible performance.

In this thesis, we revisit this idea using modern GPU capabilities. We were able to greatly improve performance by adopting more GPU-friendly data structures and restructuring the rendering pipeline architecture to be fully GPU-driven. By letting the GPU itself dynamically schedule tasks at a micro level, GPU utilization was dramatically increased and the resulting performance was doubled. This research also led us to develop a GPU utilization profiling tool suited for dynamic thread scheduling. More generally, this journey led us to tackle the question of the real-life performance of various GPU features, and in particular GPU dynamic thread scheduling and hardware sparse textures, which we experimented with and evaluated.

# I.3 – Résumé en français

Créer des expériences 3D interactives complètes impose trois contraintes contradictoires : un haut niveau de détail, des mondes immenses, et une cadence de trame temps réel. Discrétiser les environnements virtuels en grille hiérarchique de voxels présente des avantages clés à l'égard de ces contraintes. Les voxels peuvent représenter n'importe quel type de géométrie : on les connaît pour les contenus volumiques comme les nuages, mais ils peuvent tout aussi bien être utilisés pour des objets à surfaces tels que les carrosseries de voitures, et aussi tout l'entre-deux des géométries agrégées comme les feuillages dont les surfaces sont si complexes qu'elles sont mieux représentées par un champ de densité moyenne. Les voxels sont également faciles à interpoler et à moyenner en préservant l'apparence, ce qui les rend parfaits pour le rendu anticrénelé et les niveaux de détail, garantissant ainsi une constance en termes de performance et de qualité visuelle. Enfin, grâce à leur cohérence spatiale native et leur structure ordonnée, les voxels se prêtent facilement au rendu par lancer de rayons. Bien sûr, les voxels ont aussi leurs inconvénients, le principal étant l'énorme quantité de mémoire qu'ils occupent lorsqu'ils sont stockés naïvement.

Des travaux antérieurs ont montré qu'en utilisant une structure de données d'arbre subdivisant l'espace récursivement combinée à un cache permettant de charger dynamiquement ou de générer procéduralement uniquement les voxels visibles à la résolution appropriée, il est possible de faire tenir des scènes voxelisées avec des détails à l'échelle du pixel dans la mémoire du processeur graphique tout en atteignant une cadence de trame acceptable. Cependant, la solution proposée à l'époque reposait sur de nombreux allers-retours synchrones entre le processeur principal et le processeur graphique pour gérer deux tâches codépendantes : le rendu des données en cache et la production à la demande de nouvelles données. Cela entraînait un faible taux d'utilisation du processeur graphique, avec de nombreux intervalles de famine à chaque trame, des pics de trames irrégulièrement longues, et plus généralement des performances perfectibles.

Dans cette thèse, nous revisitons cette idée en utilisant les capacités modernes des processeurs graphiques. Nous avons pu sensiblement améliorer les performances en adoptant des structures de données plus adaptées aux processeurs graphiques et en restructurant l'architecture du pipeline de rendu pour qu'elle soit entièrement pilotée par le processeur graphique. En laissant le processeur graphique luimême planifier dynamiquement les tâches à un niveau micro, le taux d'utilisation du processeur graphique a été fortement augmenté et les performances ont été doublées. Cette recherche nous a également amenés à développer un outil de profilage de l'utilisation du processeur graphique adapté à la planification dynamique des tâches. Plus généralement, ce travail nous a amenés à explorer la question des performances réelles de diverses fonctionnalités processeur graphique, en particulier la planification dynamique des tâches et les textures clairsemées matérielles, que nous avons expérimentées et évaluées.

# Introduction and problem statement

# II.1 – Rendering ultra-large and ultra-detailed virtual worlds in real-time

Whether you play them or not, it is undeniable that video games have always been a major driving force behind advancements in computing. The most salient example is the recent bursting of Nvidia, and GPUs more generally, from the niche gamer world onto the global scene with the explosion of "A.I.". Perhaps the reason video games continue to be at the forefront of computer development is that it is fundamentally a very hard task.

The goal of most video games is to offer players highly immersive experiences. This broad concept of immersive experience encompasses many aspects: low input latency and real-time frame rates (see Section II.2.2\), a high level of detail, large and seemingly infinite worlds, realistic (or at least pseudo-realistic) lighting, complex interactions between objects, and many more. Achieving all those aspects at the same time is tremendously challenging, so during this thesis we chose to focus on only three: real-time, infinite scenes, and high detail (see Figure 1).



Figure 1 The three goals of real-time frame rates, seemingly infinite world size, and pixel-sized detail pull in opposite directions. Our own diagram.

# II.2 – Real-time

Real-time rendering matters for games, but also for interactive simulation and preview workflows where fast feedback accelerates iteration (see Figure 2\). Different applications tolerate different levels of frame-time variability, but all benefit from low latency and stable timings, as we detail below.

#### II.2.1 – Frame time is often a better metric than frame rate



Figure 3 Frame rate is the reciprocal of frame time. A delta in fps is not an absolute measurement because it depends on the reference fps; see the orange and purple arrows. Our own diagram.

Rendering performance is often discussed in terms of frame rate, measured in frames per second (fps) or Hz, but its reciprocal frame time, measured in milliseconds, is often easier to work with. For example, when adding a new effect to a rendering pipeline, that effect has a cost that is constant in terms of milliseconds. So if the pipeline previously had a render time of �� ms and the effect has a cost of �� ms, the new pipeline has a cost of �� + �� ms. This simple addition does not work with frame rate: if the pipeline without the effect ran at 1 �� fps, and the effect alone runs at 1 �� fps, the pipeline with the effect runs at 1 1 ��+ 1 �� fps, which is different from 1 �� + 1 �� .

In interactive scenarios, frame rate is also a crucial component of input latency, the delay between a user input (such as a mouse movement) and the display of the first image that reflects it. Low input latency is critical to avoid motion sickness and to permit accurate control. Input latency depends on many factors, such as the internal latency of the input and output peripherals, but frame rate puts a lower bound on it: a game running at �� fps imposes a minimum latency of 1 �� second, as an input that happens at the very beginning of a frame will only be displayed on the next frame, 1 �� second later. As with the pipeline effect above, a change of frame rate from 1 �� fps to 1 ��′ fps changes latency by �� − �� ′ ms, showing that frame time is the more natural metric.

In conclusion, talking of gains or losses of fps is always problematic because it depends on the reference point (see Figure 3), so frame time should always be used when talking about a change. When talking about absolute values, however, frame rate is fine.

#### II.2.2 – What is the threshold of real-timeness?



Figure 4 Illustration of motion perception as a function of frame rate.

When we see an animation with a long delay between each frame, say one second, we perceive it as distinct images. When the frame rate becomes high enough, we instead perceive it as a continuous flow, a video (see Figure 4\). But when exactly does this transition from distinct images to continuous flow occur?

The current cinema standard of 24 fps is often cited, but silent cinema was actually projected as low as 16 fps. In interactive media, 30 fps is often cited today as the minimum, but that did not stop Mario 64 and Zelda: Ocarina of Time from being massively popular despite targeting 20 fps (and Star Fox even ran at 15). On the other side, 60 fps is considered the minimum for fast-paced games with high camera movement like competitive FPSs (FPS as in First Person Shooter, not Frames Per Second), with 100 fps or more being preferable. In VR applications, where motion sickness is particularly sensitive, 90 fps is the standard. Studies have even shown that humans can still perceive flickering at up to 500 fps under the right conditions [DHL15].

The main thing to keep in mind is that there is no single magic threshold. The transition from slideshow to continuous flow is fuzzy, and it highly depends on the type of content being shown, the experience of the individual, and even the display technology. For the purpose of this document, we adopt the current standard of 30 fps as the threshold for real-timeness.

#### II.2.3 – Real-time is not just about average frame time



Figure 5 The frame-time graph (blue) shows the time taken to process each frame, giving a complete view of performance. Averaging it (orange) loses the fluidity information.

When rendering complex scenes, frame time — the time needed to render each frame — tends to be highly variable. First, render time is usually highly dependent on what is currently visible. When looking at the sky, no objects are visible and render time can crash down; when looking far toward the horizon without any objects blocking the view, render times shoot up. As a result, camera movements can cause low-frequency oscillations in frame time. Similarly, looking at a plain corridor versus a vast plain filled with various entities has a very different performance profile, which can also cause low-frequency variations in frame time. Second, complex scenes usually require some form of dynamic streaming of scene content from disk, because the scene is just too big to fit entirely in fast random-access memory. Disk access is quite slow compared to processing time, even for solid-state drives (SSDs), so streaming events tend to cause big spikes in render time.

Averaging frame times loses all information on this variability. In offline rendering, variability is indeed irrelevant, as all that matters is the total render time, but in real-time rendering the information on frame-time variability is vital. Low-frequency oscillations caused by camera movements and environment changes can cause a form of motion sickness, and the resulting variations in input latency make the camera harder to control. Spikes caused by streaming events are even worse: they tend to really break

the sensation of immersion and make the whole experience unpleasant. Interestingly, tiny and infrequent spikes — say one 33 ms frame every couple of seconds of an otherwise smooth 16 ms render — are actually quite hard to notice for most people, so not all spikes should be considered equal.

In consequence, to give an accurate description of real-time performance, average frame time is not enough. Taking the maximum frame time would give the absolute worst-case performance, but it is even worse at representing the whole complexity of the frame-time distribution, as the absolute maximum is determined by a single event. Taking the 90th, 95th, or 99th percentile frame time is a good compromise between those two extremes. The 90th percentile frame time means that 90% of frames are rendered under this time. This guarantees an "almost minimum" performance while not being completely biased by singular events the way the maximum is. However, even the 9Xth percentile hides information about the frequency and type of frame-time spikes, not to mention low-frequency oscillations. Only a full frame-time graph, like the one shown in Figure 5, can give the complete picture of frame-time smoothness required for proper real-time rendering.

#### II.3.1 – Streaming

#### II.3.1.1 – From disk to RAM



Figure 10 (Left) Level-based games such as Portal (Valve, 2007) constrain players to relatively small environments, with loading screens between levels. (Right) Open-world games such as Zelda: Breath of the Wild (Nintendo, 2017) allow traversal of a large continuous world without explicit loading screens.

Video games are often classified into two categories: level-based games and openworld games. Level-based games, like Portal (Figure 10, left), cut their virtual worlds into discrete levels. Player movement inside a level is unrestricted (apart from gameplay restrictions, of course) and usually very smooth, but a loading screen that momentarily stops the flow of the game is necessary to change level. Open-world games, like Zelda: Breath of the Wild \(Figure 10, right), allow players to traverse the whole game world without any loading screen. This freedom can increase immersion, and more importantly it lets artists convey the vastness of the virtual world in a way that is not possible with level-based games.

The distinction between level-based and open-world games is not always that clear. Many level-based games hide their loading screens during door or elevator animations to make the transition more diegetic. The original Resident Evil only showed a static video of a door opening, lightly masking the loading screen, but in Metroid Prime the player remains in full control during the door animation, making it almost invisible. This trick lets developers make level-based games feel like a single connected world, but it still heavily restricts level design, as the player can only view one level at a time. Many games are also a hybrid of the two: in The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, you can freely explore the landscapes between cities, but entering or exiting a building triggers a loading screen.

Open-world games cannot avoid loading entirely; they are just better at hiding it. Instead of loading data at fixed, predetermined points like level-based games, open-world games continuously and dynamically stream game content based on player position and visibility (see Figure 11). In fact, many open-world games suffer from periodic frame-rate drops when loading data in the background (see Section II.2.3). Loading screens remain rightfully and universally disliked, and dynamic world streaming will surely play an even bigger role in the future than it does today.

#### II.3.1.2 – From server to disk



Figure 12 The world of Microsoft Flight Simulator 2020 (Asobo Studio) is so big and detailed that it cannot even fit on a hard drive \(HDD\); it has to be dynamically streamed from a remote server.

The fundamental challenge of ultra-large, ultra-detailed worlds is the sheer quantity of data they contain. The memory that a full ultra-large world at full resolution occupies is always too much to fit in video RAM or system RAM — this is true for almost every game — but sometimes it is even too much to fit on the system disk. Microsoft Flight Simulator 2020 \(Figure 12\) renders the entire earth, and even though it only needs to be detailed enough to look good from an airplane's window, the full game world is reportedly 2 petabytes [Win20]. That is about 2000 times the median hard-drive size [Val26b], so there was no hope of fitting it locally. Instead, Microsoft and Asobo Studio chose to keep the full world data on their remote servers and use the local disk as another level of the cache pyramid. It is still possible to play entirely offline, using previously cached data and reduced quality for areas that are not in cache. Livestreaming world data over the internet does require a connection that some players might not have: Microsoft recommends 20 Mb/s [Win20], which is reasonable, especially considering that it would already take about 17 hours to download the 150 GB required for the base install at that speed.

#### II.3.2 – Procedural generation

The solution to the size of ultra-large, ultra-detailed worlds might seem obvious: compression. Compression takes the full-resolution data, finds redundancies or acceptable simplifications, and keeps only the relevant parts — the compressed representation — such that the full-resolution data, or at least a good-enough approximation, can be rebuilt from it. Compression is lossless if the full-resolution data can be perfectly regenerated, and lossy if only an approximation can be recovered. Compressing can be relatively expensive, but it usually does not need to be performed live; decompressing is usually much cheaper, and since processing speed has for a long time increased faster than memory bandwidth, loading compressed data and decompressing it is often faster than loading full-resolution data. Indeed, compression is used everywhere nowadays: textures are compressed, vertices are quantized, and live compression and decompression is even used for transfers inside modern processors to reduce memory bandwidth pressure [AMD26a].



Figure 13 Fuel (Asobo Studio, 2009) fits a relatively detailed 14 400 km2 map on a single DVD by using procedural generation as a form of compression.

The gains achievable via standard compression vary widely depending on the type of data and the acceptable quality reduction, but a 2× to 10× compression ratio is a reasonable average. This is a significant gain, but it does not fundamentally change our problem. Compression is traditionally viewed as an after-the-fact post-processing step: data is originally authored or scanned at full resolution, then compressed. Procedural generation, on the other hand, fully embraces the compressed representation from the beginning. If the goal of compression is to transform fullresolution data into compressed data plus a decompression algorithm, why not directly design a custom algorithm that generates the desired full-resolution data from the most minimal representation possible? That is what procedural generation does, and it calls that representation a seed. Seeds are often extremely minimal — a few numbers suffice — and the generation algorithm is code, which weighs next to nothing compared to assets. As a result, the compression ratio achievable via procedural generation can be truly staggering (see Figure 13\).



Figure 14 No Man's Sky (Hello Games, 2016) uses procedural generation to generate alien planets as well as the plants and animals that populate them.

In fact, the most popular use of procedural generation is to create infinite worlds, which require an infinite compression ratio. To be more rigorous, true infinity is hard to achieve in practice because of issues like numerical precision (see Section II.3.3\), but in theory it seems reasonable to call it infinite. The most famous game that uses procedural generation is probably Minecraft, but an even more interesting example is No Man's Sky, which uses procedural generation not only for its terrain but also to dynamically generate alien flora and fauna (see Figure 14).

But these games view procedural generation as more than just a form of compression. By slightly tweaking the seed, infinite variations of the full-resolution data can be generated. One might say that the procedural generation algorithm defines a random process, and the seed selects a particular instance of that process. All instances are different, yet they respect the same rules and thus form a coherent whole. This allows games like Minecraft to offer a unique experience to each player, and even infinite

replay value, by generating a brand-new world for each game simply by changing the seed.





Figure 15 Tools such as Blender Geometry Nodes [Ble26] let artists define procedural generation algorithms as node graphs (bottom) to quickly create variations of repetitive patterns (top). Screenshot from Blender (Blender Foundation).

The fact that procedural generation defines a process and not a particular instance is its greatest strength but also its greatest weakness. With procedural generation you cannot have perfectly precise control over what is generated, making it unsuitable for most story-driven games. It is interesting to note, however, that procedural generation is also used a lot by artists not at run time, but at asset-creation time. Tools like Houdini [Sid26] or Blender Geometry Nodes [Ble26] (see Figure 15\) let artists quickly generate variations of repetitive geometry or textures — a task that would otherwise be very dull — while keeping full artistic control, since a procedurally generated result that is not deemed adequate can be scrapped with a quick undo. This use neglects the compression opportunity, since the generated data is then baked into the game files, but in exchange much more expensive procedural algorithms can be used, as they no longer need to run in real time.

#### II.3.3 – Numerical precision



Figure 16 Numerical precision errors can cause all sorts of issues. Here, procedural terrain generation in Minecraft (Mojang, 2011) breaks down after 12 550 821 blocks from the world origin; players refer to this glitched terrain as "The Far Lands".

Even with a perfect content-streaming and LOD system, virtual world size can be limited by a much more basic problem: numerical precision. Numerical precision can cause all sorts of issues, from physics-simulation instabilities to procedural-generation glitches (see Figure 16\), but the most common one is player and object positions becoming too coarse, resulting in visible jumps when moving. Positions are stored in variables, and are thus limited by the precision of their data type. 32-bit floating-point numbers are the standard today for general-purpose variables in computer graphics. A 32-bit float encodes a decimal number as a 23-bit mantissa representing a value between 1 and 2, multiplied by 2 to the power of an 8-bit exponent, plus a sign bit for negative numbers:

(−1) sign[1 bit] × 1.mantissa[23 bit] × 2 exponent[8 bit] .



Figure 17 Representable floating point values with 2 bit mantissa and exponent ∈ [−1, 1]. Our own diagram.

The exponent lets us represent numbers of vastly different scales, but precision is determined by the mantissa (see Figure 17). The 23-bit mantissa of a 32-bit float can represent 2 23 different numbers; the sign bit doubles that, and because the mantissa represents numbers between 1 and 2 we can double it again, so in total a 32-bit float resolves about 2 25 distinct steps. Is this enough? As explained in Figure 18, millimeter precision is sufficient for most games. This means a game world can be at most roughly 2 25 × 2 25 millimeters, or about 32 × 32 kilometers.



Figure 18 The precision required for positions is ultimately determined by screen resolution and minimum camera distance. Here, a Minecraft (Mojang, 2011) block projects to the full screen height. A block is 1 m, so for a 1080p screen this implies a target precision on the order of 1m ÷ 1000 = 1mm*.*

This is enough for most games [Soc26], but certainly not for all applications. Fortunately, computers also natively support 64-bit floating-point numbers. Consumer GPUs are actually quite bad at manipulating 64-bit floats, usually suffering a 32× or 64× slowdown compared to 32-bit floats [NVI26a] thankfully, 64-bit precision is only required for world-space position calculations, and we can go back to 32-bit once positions have been transformed to camera space. A 64-bit float has a 52-bit mantissa, so it can represent an 18-billion-by-18-billion-kilometer map with millimeter accuracy. That is enough to cover the solar system out to Pluto, but still nothing compared to the size of a galaxy — the Milky Way, for example, is approximately 8.6 × 10 17 kilometers wide [GGH98]. To go further, games can emulate higher-precision numbers even when they are not supported in hardware, or, in the extreme, switch to variable-length number types like Python's default integers [Pyt26] or the GMP library in C [The26c].

# II.4 – Thesis outline

These three goals are hard to reconcile. In this thesis, we tackle them with voxel-based rendering, building on the GigaVoxels method [CNL+09]: only the voxels that are visible on screen, at the resolution at which they appear, are produced and cached, directly on the GPU. In such a system, two recurring problems stand out. The first is scheduling: rendering and on-demand data production depend on each other, and orchestrating this dependency from the CPU leaves the GPU idle far too often. The second is storage: the voxel cache is a sparse, multi-resolution dataset whose virtual extent far exceeds GPU memory, so it must be virtualized. Both problems point toward the same underlying theme, making the GPU autonomous, and the rest of this thesis is organized around them.

Chapter III first introduces the GPU concepts and vocabulary that everything else builds on, since both of our problems are ultimately about using GPU hardware well. We then study the two problems in turn. Chapter IV addresses scheduling: it analyzes why CPU-driven control breaks down and classifies the mechanisms that let the GPU spawn its own work. Chapter V addresses storage: it studies virtual textures, the indirection layer that lets a small physical memory pool impersonate a huge sparse dataset, and measures its software and hardware implementations. Chapter VI combines the results of both studies into GigaVoxels-DP, our proposed renderer and the central contribution of this thesis, which doubles rendering performance by keeping scheduling entirely on the GPU. Chapter VII then reports a complementary, more exploratory attempt at rendering voxel scenes by rasterizing the octree instead of ray marching it. Finally, Chapter VIII summarizes our contributions and opens future directions. Two appendices complete the document: Chapter A deepens the comparison between CPU and GPU architectures for the curious reader, and Chapter B presents ShaderV, the graphics framework developed during this thesis to support our prototypes.



# What's a GPU?

Every contribution in this thesis lives on the GPU, so the following chapters constantly rely on GPU concepts and vocabulary. This chapter is here to establish that common foundation before diving in. If you are not sure what a GPU is, it will give you a very solid introduction. If you think you already know pretty well what a GPU is, it should still provide you some insights that are not commonly mentioned, and you can continue with Chapter A for an even deeper dive into GPU architecture. Without further introduction: What's a GPU?

GPUs are integrated circuits (ICs), small pieces of silicon onto which extremely complex electrical circuits are etched to give them the power of computing. GPU stands for Graphics Processing Unit, and as the name implies, their original purpose is to render graphics, i.e., to transform abstract data into images. Initially rigid, unprogrammable pieces of hardware, they gradually evolved into fully programmable processors, such that today they are routinely used for non-graphical tasks, often beating CPUs in raw power and efficiency. One of the fields they helped revolutionize is neural networks, and because AI has become so prominent, GPUs now also include machine learning acceleration hardware. This chapter briefly reviews the graphics role that GPUs were born for, and then focuses on what they fundamentally are today and what matters most for the rest of this thesis: massively parallel general-purpose processors.

# III.1 – Graphics accelerator

Rendering images in real time, even simple 2D interfaces, is an expensive task. Pixels are numerous: Standard Definition (SD) is already 640 × 480 ⪆ 300 000 pixels, 4K (UHD - Ultra High Definition) is a whopping 3840 × 2160 ⪆ 8 000 000; while frame rate must be at least 30 frames per second \(fps), preferably 60 or more, for a smooth experience. While it is possible to use a general purpose CPU to drive a display for low-demand applications like e-readers, smart appliances, or ATMs, most applications necessitate a dedicated graphics chip, a GPU. The specific graphics functions that GPUs are capable of have evolved over the years, but three main ones stand out: texture sampling and filtering, line and triangle rasterization, and ray-tracing [AHH+18]. Because this thesis is primarily concerned with GPUs as general-purpose processors, we do not detail these graphics-specific functions further here; instead, the rest of this chapter focuses on the programmable, massively parallel architecture that underlies them.

# III.2 – Massively parallel general purpose processor

GPUs originally implemented the graphics pipeline entirely in hardware. This was fast but very limiting, so small programs called shaders were progressively added to customize specific stages of the pipeline: the main ones being pixel shading and vertex transformation. This resulted in a pipeline that mixes fixed hardware stage with programmable software stages. People then realized that this programmability could be used to compute non-graphical things, and dedicated compute languages and compute "shaders" (also called kernels) were introduced to make this easier [AHH+18]. To better understand what this transformation implies, we first need to understand what a processor is.

#### III.2.1 – What's a processor?

Processors are so ubiquitous that it is easy to think that all Integrated Circuits (ICs) are processors, but we can actually separate ICs into three families: ASICs, FPGAs, and Processors. Those three types of ICs sit at different points of an efficiency ↔︎ versatility tradeoff.

| ASICs                 | FPGAs Processors    |
|-----------------------|---------------------|
| Program is burned on  |                     |
| chip at manufacturing |                     |
| Program is            | configured          |
| on chip at            | initialization      |
|                       | Program is executed |

| Efficiency ↔︎         | Versatility         |

ASICs (Application Specific ICs) hard-code programs into hardware. The high-level description of the program is synthesized into a set of transistors connected by wires, which is then used to fabricate chips that directly implement the program's logic. Since the chip's design precisely reflects the program, there is no added overhead and maximal efficiency is achieved. Programs implemented as ASICs run extremely fast and consume a minimal amount of energy. However, the price for this efficiency is inflexibility. An ASIC can only execute the specific program it was designed for. Another drawback of ASICs is that the entire program needs to fit on the chip. Longer and more complex programs will require bigger and more expensive ASICs, or might not even be manufacturable with current technology. Common applications of ASICs include video encoding and decoding, image and sound processing, networking, and cryptocurrency mining. GPUs used to be entirely ASICs too, but evolved into processors with some application specific blocks bolted on.

FPGAs (Field Programmable Gate Arrays) are generic sets of logic gates that can be reconfigured to implement programs. As with ASICs, the high-level description of the program is synthesized into logic gates and wires, but this design is used to configure the chip at initialization instead of burning it at fabrication. Once the chip is configured, it effectively implements the program in hardware, making it quite fast and efficient. However, because FPGAs need extra transistors and wires to accommodate reconfigurations, they are not as fast or efficient as ASICs. FPGAs also suffer from the fact that program length and complexity affect chip size. It is possible to break down a long program into smaller chunks that fit on chip and reconfigure the FPGA when changing chunk, but that requires flushing temporary variables in between reconfigurations, and reconfiguring an FPGA takes time. As a middle ground between ASICs and processors, FPGAs are mostly used to prototype ASIC and processor architectures (and even FPGA architectures), to commercialize low-volume products that require low latency but cannot afford the cost of developing an ASIC, and in highvalue markets like high-frequency trading, aerospace, or military applications.

Processors are instruction tape machines (see Figure 19). The high-level description of the program is compiled into a list of instructions that are executed at runtime. To be cheeky, one could say that processors are ASICs whose application is to execute an instruction tape. This indirection (processor hardware does not directly reflect program logic) makes processors much more versatile than ASICs or FPGAs. Processors can execute arbitrarily long and complex programs, regardless of the processor's size. Processors can also switch programs almost instantly; they do not suffer the heavy reconfiguration cost of FPGAs. The cost of this versatility is a massive efficiency overhead. Fetching, decoding, and executing instructions takes time, requires many transistors, and consumes power. Overall, the flexibility of processors and their ability to run extremely complex programs made them the dominant form of program-running ICs today.



Figure 19 An actual physical instruction tape from the early Harwell computer (1952). Shown here executing a "while true:" loop. Photo by Bad germ, taken at The National Museum of Computing and licensed CC BY-SA 3.0 [Bad09].

For all the theoretical reasons explained above, ASICs are more efficient than FPGAs, which are themselves more efficient than processors. But how wide exactly is this efficiency gap in practice? This can be hard to measure, as it requires implementing the same application on a processor, an FPGA, and an ASIC, and it will heavily depend on the quality of each implementation. Nurvitadhi et al. did it for neural networks in Accelerating binarized neural networks: Comparison of FPGA, CPU, GPU, and ASIC [NSS+16], and we used their results to create Figure 20. CPUs and GPUs are both forms of processors, yet GPUs massively outperformed CPUs in their tests, showing that differences between members of an IC family can be just as important as differences between families. If we then use GPUs as the best representative of the processors family, we end up with a 4.6× efficiency ratio between FPGAs and processors, and a 3.7× efficiency ratio between ASICs and FPGAs. Since those figures can heavily change with implementation and application, we can extract a rule of thumb of one order of magnitude in efficiency between each IC family.



Figure 20 Relative performance per Watt of CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs, and ASICs. Data from Nurvitadhi et al. [NSS+16].

### III.2.2 – The Von Neumann bottleneck and the memory wall

As seen in the previous section, an inherent inefficiency of processors is that they have to manipulate an instruction tape instead of implementing a program directly. But all computers (processors, FPGAs, ASICs) also suffer from another major source of inefficiency: accessing main memory is terribly slow. On current computers, completing a memory request takes as much time as executing hundreds of basic arithmetic instructions, depending on the specific processor and memory used. This issue has been present since the 70′s, but has continued to worsen over time. Advances in processor speed have vastly outpaced advances in memory speed, a phenomenon referred to as the memory wall [WM95].

One of the main reasons for the long access times is that main memory is physically separated from the processor. This means that signals have to travel (relatively) long distances, traverse many layers of gates, and get amplified along the way. This separation partly comes by design: it is a reflection of the Von Neumann architecture [Neu45] (see Figure 21). Having all memory in a single giant unified pool that can be accessed anywhere makes it easy to reason about. It is the most flexible organization; it lets programs partition memory however they want. As we did with instructionexecuting processors, we chose flexibility over performance. The chokepoint that this separation causes is even called the Von Neumann bottleneck. But the separation of compute and memory also comes as a constraint of our manufacturing capabilities. By using different lithography processes for memory than what we use for logic, we are able to manufacture memory ICs that are denser, cheaper, and more efficient. In turn, this allows computers to have more memory capacity than they would otherwise. Unfortunately, increasing memory capacity also means addresses take longer to

decode, adding to memory transaction delays. Finally, compounding the effects of separation and long address decoding, this denser memory type (DRAM) is also slower.



Figure 21 The Von Neumann architecture dissociates compute and memory. This clean distinction is conceptually elegant, but leads to impossibly high bandwidth and latency demands on the memory bus. This is known as the Von Neumann bottleneck. Diagram by Chris-martin and Aeroid, licensed CC BY-SA 3.0 [CA26].

Of course, we did not simply accept slow memory, otherwise computers would be orders of magnitude slower; we developed countermeasures. The first countermeasure is to break the Von Neumann architecture and have local memory blocks embedded directly inside processors. By sitting right next to computing blocks, being smaller, and using less dense, more expensive, less energy efficient but faster memory types (SRAM), local memory blocks are tremendously faster than main memory. Memory is no longer a single unified block, but a pyramid where the base has capacity and the peak has speed (see Figure 22). Unlike main memory, local memory blocks are usually not accessible by all parts of a processor but dedicated to specific cores or core clusters.



Figure 22 The memory pyramid illustrates how different memory types are layered to create a hierarchy with fast access at the top and high capacity at the bottom. The number of cache levels and their speed can vary significantly depending on the device. Our own diagram.

Local memory blocks can be used as main memory accelerators: caches. Caches use the same address space as main memory, and try to hold the memory addresses that benefit the most from faster accesses. Caches are not controlled by instructions, they are not programmable (although it is sometimes possible to control which address ranges should be cached and with what priority). Instead, they are managed by the hardware (although manual cache flushing is sometimes required). This makes caches transparent to use: programmers do not have to explicitly think about caches, their programs just magically get faster (although being aware of caches can certainly help write faster programs). In that sense, caches do break the Von Neumann architecture physically, but not conceptually. The drawback of this "hands-off" approach is that automatic caches do not always make the best decisions, and, ironically, testing if an address is cached takes time, so adding a cache makes uncached memory accesses slower.

Local memory blocks can also be used as explicitly separate memory ranges with their own address space. Explicit local memory blocks can either be directly controlled by programmers, as with scratchpad or shared memory on GPUs, or indirectly via the compiler as is the case with registers (it is possible to directly control registers by programming in assembly, but very few programmers do today). Explicitly controlled local memory blocks have the opposite characteristics to caches: they can be more effective and are slightly faster but require more thinking from programmers and are not portable (programs written with explicit local memory accesses can only run on hardware with explicitly accessible local memory).

The other countermeasure to the memory wall is a technique known as latency hiding. In processors with latency hiding, when a thread tries to access uncached memory, instead of simply waiting idly, the processor switches execution to another thread that is able to execute instructions. Instead of trying to make memory accesses faster, latency hiding takes advantage of the fact that main memory has more bandwidth than its latency might suggest (because multiple memory requests can be in-flight at the same time) to schedule many threads of computation concurrently. As explained in Section A.1.3.2, this technique is at the heart of the GPU architecture and is one of the main factors behind the massive computing capability of GPUs [AHH+18].

# III.3 – A.I. accelerators

GPUs started as graphics accelerators, then generalized to become general purpose processors, but have now started to respecialize to accelerate a new kind of task: A.I. A.I. is a complex notion that we will not attempt to define here, but most often what is hiding under it is a much more concrete concept: Artificial Neural Networks. The structure of artificial neural networks, illustrated in Figure 23, is such that it can be used as a universal function approximator [HSW89]. By changing the parameters of the network (the weights �� and biases ��), the network can approximate any function. And if any problem, even a problem as complex as: "What is in that image?", "What is next word of that sentence?", and perhaps one day "What is the meaning of life?" can be expressed as a function, then artificial neural networks can approximate them. Those parameters are not meant to be set by hand, but learned by optimization of the neural network function composed with a loss function that tests the model. The loss function can test against existing data (supervised learning), or more abstract objectives (unsupervised learning and reinforcement learning). This is a radical shift from conventional programming in which humans must write the functions by hand.

Artificial neural networks are not a recent idea: the core idea can be traced back all the way to 1943 [MP43], the perceptron (the artificial neuron model we still use today) was defined in 1958 [Ros58], and back-propagation (how networks are trained) in 1986 [RHW86]. However, artificial neural networks lacked another component to achieve their full potential: scale. In 2012, AlexNet [KSH17] used the power of GPUs to train a deep (many layers) neural network at identifying images better than any other algorithm of the time could, which kickstarted the modern GPU-based A.I. revolution.

The fact that GPUs are particularly good at accelerating artificial neural networks is not surprising. Neural networks are massively parallel by nature, use short programs that do not branch, and have a good arithmetic operation to memory transfer ratio. All those factors make training and inferring neural networks very GPU-friendly. In fact, neural networks are so simple, so standardized, and so useful that it made sense to "go ASIC" and start incorporating neural network accelerators inside GPUs, starting with Nvidia Tensor Cores1 in 2017 [NVI17]. This new execution unit automates matrix multiplications of medium (16×16) to large matrices, an operation that is at the heart of every neural network architecture. Since then, other neural network dedicated components have also been added to GPUs: instructions that directly encode common activation functions (ReLU, hyperbolic tangent, etc.), and DMA blocks (Direct Memory Access, hardware blocks that move memory efficiently) specialized for moving matrices to and from Tensor Cores. However, the core "A.I." part that is present in every modern GPU is the matrix multiplication unit.

And now the loop of GPU purpose is complete, as artificial neural networks are being used for graphical tasks, notably with Nvidia DLSS (a suite of tools to improve realtime graphics using Neural Networks, see Figure 24).

### III.3.1 – Tensor Core accelerated texture interpolation

The operation that the new "Neural" hardware blocks accelerate, matrix multiplication, is not unique to artificial neural networks. On the contrary, it is a ubiquitous operation in math that has applications in fields as different as physics, chemistry, statistics, or finance. This means that in theory, matrix-multiplication units can be used for applications far outside artificial neural networks. Indeed, attempts have been made in physics simulation [LLY+22], quantum molecular dynamics [FSM+21], or drug discovery [SP24]. We also supervised a first year master's internship that tried to use matrix-multiplication units to interpolate textures. The full report by Maxence Doktorcik, the internship student, can be found here [Dok25]. In the end, the implementation we developed was not very performant, and it seems to be the conclusion that most other attempts to use matrix-multiplication units outside of neural networks reached. A big difficulty is that even though matrix multiplication is a ubiquitous operation, matrix-multiplication units only work with predefined matrix sizes, only achieve peak efficiency with heavily reduced precision number formats, and are complicated to feed, and as a result, it is difficult to use them for applications that do not fit within those narrow constraints.



# Study of GPU-driven thread spawning

This chapter is the first of the two studies announced in the introduction, and it addresses the scheduling problem: how can the GPU decide by itself, without waiting on the CPU, when and how to launch new work? We analyze here why the traditional CPU-driven model falls short, and we survey and classify the mechanisms that exist to replace it. The conclusions of this study directly shape the two systems built later in this thesis: GigaVoxels-DP \(Chapter VI\), which relies on CUDA Dynamic Parallelism, and our octree rasterizer (Chapter VII\), which relies on a custom persistent-thread work queue implementation.

GigaVoxels rendering interleaves two mutually dependent tasks: ray-marching triggers voxel production upon voxel cache misses, and voxel production triggers raymarching on voxel cache updates.

In the original GigaVoxels implementation [CNL+09], these tasks were managed using the traditional CPU-driven model, where it is the CPU that decides when and how GPU tasks are launched. This approach, however, becomes increasingly inefficient as task granularity decreases. In practice, the overhead of CPU-driven launches proved to be a significant bottleneck in the original system.

To address this limitation, several GPU-driven thread spawning models have been developed. These approaches allow the GPU itself to decide when and how new tasks are launched, enabling more efficient utilization of GPU resources, particularly for fine-grain micro-tasks. In pursuit of improving GigaVoxels, we investigated how GPUdriven launches can be leveraged to manage complex GPU pipelines more effectively. Since tasks can be launched at a much finer granularity, down to a single execution thread, we prefer to use the broader term thread spawning.

In this section, we first analyze the limitations of traditional CPU-driven thread spawning \(Section IV.1\). We then introduce a classification of GPU-driven thread spawning capabilities (Section IV.2). Using this framework, we review and compare existing GPU-driven thread spawning implementations (Section IV.3). Finally, we discuss how GPUs have long supported forms of GPU-driven thread spawning in the context of fixed-function rasterization \(Section IV.4).

# IV.1 – CPU-driven thread spawning

GPUs have traditionally been viewed as coprocessors: they execute work requests originating from the CPU but cannot generate new work requests on their own. This coprocessor model theoretically kept things simple, but in practice became a bottleneck because of the high CPU-GPU latency. More specifically, it makes it

hard to use in pipelines where one stage computes the amount of work needed for the subsequent stage, or with fine-grain micro-dependencies between tasks. Asynchronous submission, indirect submission, queues, and barriers were invented to partially solve those issues, but they are only an imperfect solution.

#### IV.1.1 – CPU-GPU latency and asynchronous submission

Using the CPU as a GPU governor makes sense in principle. The CPU is the central processing unit of the computer, every other module connects to it (main memory, storage, network, peripherals, other coprocessors, etc.), and it is natural to take decisions where information is centralized.

The main CPU is also the fastest processor for single-threaded tasks, but that does not mean it has fast enough communication (low latency) with the other parts of the system to be able to control them in real time. A quick test we did measured a round trip CPU → GPU → CPU latency of about 20 to 30 µs for an empty submission1 . Compared to the sub-ns clocks of modern CPUs and GPUs, this is an eternity. Interestingly, the latency was about the same for both an integrated GPU right on the same silicon die (or at least silicon interposer) as the CPU (R9 7900X), and a dedicated GPU (RTX 4080) far away on another die and connected to the CPU through the motherboard. This indicates that most of the delay does not come from the signal having to physically travel between both processors, but rather from the many layers of drivers that commands must go through.

No matter the reason behind the high CPU-GPU communication latency, it is clear that it is too much for effective synchronous control. Indeed, all GPU APIs use asynchronous submission by default. With asynchronous submission, GPU commands are placed into a queue and executed by the GPU as soon as the required resources are available, but they return immediately from the point of view of the CPU. This has many benefits: it removes round-trip latency and lets CPU and GPU work at the same time; many small GPU tasks can run in parallel and fill the width of the GPU; big tasks can chain without delay, and even overlap (task B can start as soon as task A no longer occupies all the cores). All these factors yield a much improved GPU utilization.

If task B uses results generated by task A, or if task B writes into memory locations that are read by task A, then submissions cannot overlap. These are known as Read After Write (RAW) and Write After Read (WAR) dependencies, and they can be prevented using barriers. Barriers voluntarily stall the GPU to ensure program correctness.

1 The measurement program is the submissionLatency demo of our ShaderV repository [Ric26a].

Barrier commands are inserted in the submission queue by the CPU, just like task launch commands.

Commands can also force the CPU to test or wait on the completion of a submission to allow it to read back results. In the asynchronous model, this is required if task A computes the number of threads that task B must be launched with, because the number of threads used in a submission is decided by the CPU at command insertion time. However, as this causes the dreaded CPU-GPU communication delay to rear back its ugly head, the indirect submission model was invented.

#### IV.1.2 – Indirect submission, a semi-GPU-driven model

In the indirect submission model, it is still the CPU that emits all GPU commands, but the parameters of said commands are now filled by the GPU. Concretely, the command now contains an address in GPU memory, and the GPU reads this memory location right before executing the command to determine what parameters to use. This allows a first pass to write to that pointer and decide the command's parameters. A barrier must of course be placed between the two passes to ensure correct execution.

Indirect control of task size (number of workgroups in a compute dispatch, number of vertices in a rasterization draw call, number of rays in a ray-tracing dispatch) is widely available and in use today. More advanced iterations like DirectX ExecuteIndirect and Vulkan VK\_EXT\_device\_generated\_commands can even indirectly choose which shader gets executed, bind GPU resources (buffers and textures), or set fixed function rasterization parameters (depth test parameters, blend modes, etc).

Task size can be set to zero to effectively cancel it, but the submission overhead is still incurred. So while it is possible to cancel a submission from the GPU, it is not possible to dynamically create new submissions from the GPU. If the number of required passes in a pipeline is not known in advance, one must issue indirect submissions for a maximum number of passes and dynamically cancel those that are unnecessary, paying the overhead cost many times.

This makes indirect submission an awkward hybrid model where the GPU controls most aspects of submissions, but still cannot initiate them. This is quite unintuitive to program and very error prone. While it was a much needed patch on the asynchronous submission model, many GPU programmers would prefer to use a more straightforward fully GPU-driven approach.

#### IV.1.3 – Micro-tasks and multiple submission queues

Barrier commands are needed to enforce dependencies between tasks, but they have the inconvenience of only being able to do so at a very coarse granularity. The lack of precise control over barriers results in much more GPU stalling than necessary. Barriers do not impose dependencies between two specific tasks, they impose a dependency between all tasks submitted before the barrier and all tasks submitted after it. If a barrier is placed between task A and task B, it also forces task B to wait on all tasks submitted before A, and all tasks submitted after task B to wait for task A.

This can be partially solved by using multiple submission queues. GPU commands (be they draw/dispatch commands or barrier commands) are not submitted directly to the GPU but to a submission queue. GPUs can have multiple queues, and barriers only enforce dependencies between tasks of the same queue. By submitting task C to a different queue than task A and B, it is possible to let it run independently of task A or B. However, this approach has limits, because the number of queues on a GPU can be quite limited. Vulkan reports 24 queues for Nvidia GPUs, 16 for AMD, 5 for Intel, and usually only 1 for mobiles. CUDA lets you have an unlimited number of streams (the CUDA equivalent of queues), but behind the scenes maps them to 32 internal queues. This restricted number of queues might be sufficient for some coarse-grain algorithms, but it is not enough for micro-dependencies. As a point of comparison, modern GPUs can have 50 to 100 cores (core in the sense of Nvidia SM, AMD WGP, or Intel Xe Core, not individual SIMD execution lanes), so it is not possible to use queues to enforce dependencies at the GPU core granularity.

Another solution to the issue of barriers blocking all commands submitted after them instead of just the ones that need to be is to use split barriers. Split barriers split the barrier command into two: Set and Wait. Tasks submitted after Wait will wait for tasks submitted before Set, but tasks submitted in between Set and Wait can run independently. Set and Wait commands take an event parameter to allow for multiple split barriers to run in parallel. Split barriers add a bit of flexibility but mean you must carefully plan things in advance to schedule independent work before submitting the Wait command, again not an easy model to reason with.

Micro-dependencies can be more frequent than they seem. If you have N instantiations of task A that each need to be followed by an instantiation of task B, it only imposes dependencies between instances of task A and instances of task B, but barriers create a global dependency between all instances of task A and all instances of task B. This creates a pipeline bubble that would not exist if task B was able to start while task A is finishing. Dispatching task instances to different queues can help but is limited by the number of available queues. You can also merge kernel A and kernel B into a single kernel, but you will have idle threads if kernel thread count A ≠ kernel thread count B, and occupancy will be reduced as register and local memory use will be max(��, ��) (dynamic register allocation for the win). This concept of local dependencies is exactly what the VK\_DEPENDENCY\_BY\_REGION\_BIT flag is for, but it only works for tile-based mobile GPUs in specific contexts. Global dependency is also forced when using indirect submission (see Section IV.1.2). If each instance of task A can generate a variable number of instances of task B, indirect submission forces you to wait for all tasks A to finish before any task B can start. A much nicer approach would be to let instances of task B start as soon as some instances of task A decided that they will be needed.

We have only talked about the execution aspect of barriers: making task B wait on task A to finish before starting. In low-level APIs like Vulkan, this is not sufficient to ensure that task B can safely use results from task A. Unlike CPUs, GPU caches are not coherent [Khr26f]. Results written by task A may stay in a local cache and not be visible to task B. To ensure proper execution, local caches need to be flushed before starting task B. This can be done by specifying the adequate parameters in the barrier command. To avoid excessive flushing, it is possible to specify that only memory writes targeting a specific memory range need to be flushed. Another solution is to use cacheless memory accesses, making all threads see a coherent memory space and eliminating the need to flush. The term cacheless is a bit excessive, as the global cache can be kept and only local caches need to be skipped. This is detrimental if local caches were useful (multiple threads reading or writing to the same address), but may in fact be faster and more efficient if they were not.

#### IV.1.4 – There was a better CPU all along

We have shown that the system CPU is not apt at being the sole controller of the GPU, and hinted at the solution of letting the GPU control itself, but it should be noted that "letting the GPU control itself" actually means letting GPU threads talk to the embedded CPU controller inside the GPU. GPUs (including integrated GPUs\) have one (or sometimes multiple) tiny CPU embedded right inside their die, responsible for scheduling work and allocating resources [Lin26, Env26]. Those CPUs are of the microcontroller class, they are single-core, in-order CPUs running at a couple hundred MHz with hundreds of KBs of SRAM. This puts them far away from the capabilities of the main system CPU, but since they sit right inside the GPU itself and only have the single job of controlling it, they are much more efficient. When the system CPU sends commands to the GPU, it is actually sending commands to the embedded CPU inside the GPU. "Letting the GPU control itself" does not require new hardware, just an API to allow GPU threads to send commands to the embedded controller.

# IV.2 – Classification of GPU-driven thread spawning capabilities

GPU-driven thread spawning means that the GPU itself can decide when and how to spawn GPU threads. Many existing APIs already provide different ways of accomplishing this; they are reviewed in Section IV.3. But to better understand the difference between them, we have developed a classification of the capabilities that GPU-driven thread spawning permits. We identified three GPU-driven thread spawning capabilities:

Expansion A single thread can spawn multiple child threads.

Coalescing Spawned threads can be grouped to improve coherence.

Suspension Parent threads are paused on child spawning and resumed on child finish.

Supporting any of those capabilities is entirely optional, but any useful system should support at least one.

### IV.2.1 – Expansion





Figure 25 Expansion is when a single thread spawns multiple threads. Our own diagram.

If a single work item can spawn multiple children, we call this the expansion capability (see Figure 25). Because GPUs reach peak efficiency only when they have enough parallel work to fill all execution units (and enough oversubscription to hide memory latency), expansion can be a powerful way to "grow" parallelism where needed.



Figure 26 Hierarchical grid applied over the mandelbrot fractal, showing that the complexity of its boundary is concentrated in small areas, with big constant areas all around. Figure from NVIDIA's technical blog on CUDA Dynamic Parallelism [Adi14].

Expansion is useful for workloads with hierarchical exploration, such as traversing a tree or refining the boundary of a fractal \(Figure 26). With a fixed-size grid, one either undersamples (grid too small) or wastes work (grid too large). Dynamic refinement allocates work only where needed. Indirect dispatch can emulate this pattern, but it introduces global barriers between passes and is more cumbersome to program.

#### IV.2.2 – Coalescing





Figure 27 Coalescing sorts threads to form coherent thread groups. Our own diagram.

Newly spawned threads can be regrouped to form more coherent ensembles. Regrouping by program path is essential to benefit from SIMD execution. Threads can also be grouped by input locality to improve memory coalescing. We call this capability coalescing (see Figure 27\).

Thread coalescing is most useful in highly divergent programs, where it can recover coherence. For example, in path-traced rendering each thread computes the accumulated radiance along a path; threads quickly diverge both in the code they execute (materials, bounces) and in the data they access (scene regions), as illustrated in Figure 28. By regrouping work items according to those attributes, the GPU can execute more coherent warps and improve cache behavior.



Figure 28 NVIDIA illustration of their Shader Execution Reordering mechanism (their name for thread coalescing) and its usefulness in path tracing. Diagram from NVIDIA's Ada architecture whitepaper [NVI22b].

Regrouping threads requires a sorting/reordering mechanism, which is not free. As a result, coalescing adds overhead and is not a magic solution for highly branching code. It is advantageous only when amortized over sufficiently long segments of work, not at fine instruction granularity.

Thread coalescing can also be applied without spawning new threads: existing threads can be reordered and repacked to improve coherence. However, relocating existing work items requires transporting thread state (registers, call stacks), whereas coalescing newly spawned work only needs to compact launch parameters. Reordering also breaks the notion of a fixed thread group, which complicates the use of local shared memory.

#### IV.2.3 – Suspension





Figure 29 Suspension pauses the parent thread on child thread spawning and resumes it when its children are finished. Our own diagram.

By default, a parent thread that spawns children continues executing independently. In some scenarios, however, it is useful for the parent to wait until its children complete before resuming. We call this capability suspension (see Figure 29\). The main motivation is to enable GPU-side "call/return" like patterns, where a parent reads results produced by its children. On its own, suspension is not necessarily better than a regular function call where the parent thread executes the function, but when combined with expansion or coalescing it enables more flexible dynamic pipelines.

Internally, suspension requires saving the parent's state (program counter, registers, and possibly shared memory), pausing or evicting the parent, and later restoring state when children complete. In principle, some of this can be emulated manually, but dedicated support encourages correct usage patterns and can reduce overhead. Even with hardware help, saving and restoring thread state is expensive; existing APIs therefore warn against using suspension too frequently.

Thread suspension could also exist outside of spawning (pause/resume arbitrary work items), but existing GPU APIs typically tie suspension to child spawning. We do not pursue more general suspension models here and focus on the role of suspension in GPU-driven thread spawning.

# IV.3 – Comparison of GPU-driven thread spawning implementations

Over the years, a number of APIs have been proposed to give GPU application developers finer control over how work is generated and scheduled. In this section we review and compare them. More specifically, we examine how they relate to the three capabilities discussed in Section IV.2.

### IV.3.1 – Compute APIs \(CUDA, OpenCL)

The most direct environment for GPU-side thread spawning is provided by compute APIs. NVIDIA introduced CUDA Dynamic Parallelism [NVI12] in 2012, and Khronos followed with the vendor agnostic Device Side Enqueue in 2013 with OpenCL 2.0. They are very simple to use: you use the same kernel dispatch syntax from the GPU instead of the CPU. This provides the expansion capability.

Early versions of CUDA Dynamic Parallelism also exposed a powerful but costly feature: grid-level synchronization. Block-level synchronization is a core part of the compute programming model used to enable safe use of shared memory. Gridlevel synchronization, however, is fundamentally harder: blocks in a grid are not guaranteed to be resident on the GPU at the same time, so a global barrier can deadlock unless the runtime takes special measures. CUDA's approach was effectively to suspend blocks at the barrier: save their state (registers, shared memory, program counter) to global memory, evict them, and later resume them when the barrier condition is satisfied. This is conceptually similar to CPU context switching, and it is expensive. This provides the suspension capability at the grid level. The feature was later deprecated in CUDA 11.6 and disabled on newer GPUs, likely because the overhead was too high for practical use (see Figure 30\) [NVI22a].

### IV.3.2 – Work Graphs



Work graphs are a recent programming model for complex GPU-driven workloads (see Figure 31). They aim to provide a first-class API for dynamic work generation that spans both compute and graphics scenarios. Conceptually, each shader is a graph node, and graph edges are implicitly defined by record types: if node A produces records of type X and node B consumes X, then A can feed B. The number of records produced is data dependent, so the runtime can spawn work dynamically as records are emitted. This "records and nodes" view encourages thinking in terms of producer/ consumer pipelines. Work graphs also encourage modularity: the wiring between nodes is defined by the graph configuration (host-side) rather than being hard-coded as explicit kernel launches in shader code.

#### Do not confuse Work Graphs with CUDA Graphs

CUDA Graphs record a predefined sequence of operations (kernels, copies) so that it can be replayed efficiently. They can include conditional nodes (IF/WHILE/ SWITCH) that select paths based on GPU memory state, but the overall structure is still largely statically a recorded program. This is very similar to indirect submission (see Section IV.1.2\). Work Graphs are different: nodes are scheduled dynamically based on the availability of input records and the capacity of output record buffers.

Work graphs provide multiple node launch modes to cover different workload shapes:

Thread launch mode \(Figure 32) spawns one thread per input record, which matches embarrassingly parallel workloads. Because there is no notion of workgroup, such nodes cannot use shared memory. The runtime can pack threads into coherent SIMD groups to improve coherence, so the benefit of this launch mode is that it has the coalescing capability.



Figure 32 Thread mode spawns one thread per input record. Diagram from Microsoft's work graphs specification [Mic24].

Broadcast launch mode (Figure 33) spawns one entire grid of workgroups per input record, making it similar to a regular compute dispatch and giving it the expansion capability. The workgroup size is fixed, but the number of workgroups in the grid can vary per input record. Threads in the same workgroup can use shared workgroup memory.



Figure 33 Broadcast mode spawns a variable number of workgroups per input record. Diagram from Microsoft's work graphs specification [Mic24].

Coalescing launch mode \(Figure 34) spawns one workgroup for a batch of input records. The maximum batch size is specified in the node description; at runtime the system tries to fill batches, but the realized batch size can vary, so nodes must handle a variable number of input records. Here, "coalescing" refers to coalescing of input records into batches, not coalescing of threads to form full SIMD groups. It does not provide the coalescing capability, but it does provide expansion since many threads can be spawned for a single input record. Coalescing launch mode can be used to opportunistically merge input records to reduce workload. GPUOpen gives the example of merging rectangles before drawing them [AMD26c], merging draw call parameters into draw instance call parameters, or merging draw leaf records to pass a list of leaves to draw to a mesh node in order to have fuller meshlets [Obe24].



Figure 34 Coalesce mode spawns one workgroup for a variable number of input records. Diagram from Microsoft's work graphs specification [Mic24].

## IV.3.2.1 – Task mesh pipeline

Mesh shading replaces the traditional Vertex, Tessellation Control / Hull, Tessellation Evaluation / Domain, and Geometry shader stages with two stages: Task / Amplification and Mesh (see Figure 35\). Both follow a compute-like model: they execute at workgroup granularity and can use explicit local shared memory. A Mesh shader outputs a meshlet (a small list of vertices and indices). The optional Task shader can control how many Mesh workgroups are launched; each Task workgroup can emit a variable number of Mesh workgroups and pass data to them.



Figure 35 Traditional GPU rasterization pipeline VS the new Task-Mesh pipeline. Diagram from NVIDIA's Turing architecture whitepaper [NVI18b].

Although the task/mesh pipeline is designed for geometry processing, its underlying amplification mechanism is a canonical case of GPU-side work expansion: one Task

workgroup can emit a variable number of Mesh workgroups. From the viewpoint of this chapter, this is a single-level form of work expansion embedded in the graphics pipeline.

#### IV.3.3 – Callable shaders

Callable shaders are a ray-tracing mechanism that allows one shader invocation to invoke another via an indirection table (the shader binding table) [Mic26a]. Conceptually, a call suspends the parent invocation, runs the callable shader with an in/out payload, and resumes the parent when the callable returns. Data can therefore be exchanged between the parent and child through the payload. Recursion is allowed, and maximal recursion depth is unspecified. Callable shaders provide suspension and a form of modular function-call indirection on the GPU, but they do not provide expansion: each call produces at most one child invocation.

The decision of which callable shader is invoked is not hard-coded in shader code but controlled through the Shader Binding Table \(SBT\). The SBT associates shaders with indices, and call instructions reference those indices. The benefit of this indirection is that the SBT can be changed without recompiling the pipeline. Callable shaders generalize the "shader table" mechanism of the ray-tracing pipeline. They can be invoked from ray-generation, closest-hit, miss, or callable stages. While their origin is in ray tracing, they can also be used as a general-purpose indirection mechanism for modular GPU workloads.
