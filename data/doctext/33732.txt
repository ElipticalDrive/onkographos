#### Abstract

Novel view synthesis based on explicit Gaussian primitives has achieved an effective balance between rendering quality and real-time visualisation, but its training process can remain computationally expensive because adaptive density control may introduce many redundant primitives. This paper proposes an efficient training framework for compact Gaussian Splatting through lossguided densification, opacity-gradient pruning, and backward-pass optimisation. First, a loss accumulation guided density-control strategy is introduced. Instead of relying only on image-plane gradient magnitude, the method reuses the loss map generated during standard backpropagation to identify persistently under-reconstructed image regions. A binary high-loss mask is accumulated across multiple iterations, and each Gaussian primitive receives a densification score according to its coverage of these regions. Only primitives that consistently contribute to high-error areas are allowed to split or clone, thereby reducing unnecessary growth in well-reconstructed regions. Second, an opacity-gradient-based pruning criterion is designed to remove unstable or low-contribution primitives after densification. By combining opacity, opacity gradients, and training loss over multiple views, the proposed pruning strategy aims to preserve useful primitives while suppressing redundant ones. Third, the CUDA backward pass is optimised through warp-level parallelism and vectorised memory access to reduce redundant global memory transactions. Experiments on Mip-NeRF 360, Deep Blending, and Tanks & Temples indicate that the proposed framework substantially reduces training time and the final number of Gaussian primitives while maintaining competitive PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS performance compared with representative efficient Gaussian-splatting baselines. The results suggest that loss-aware density control can provide a practical route towards faster and more compact Gaussian Splatting. # 1 Introduction

Novel view synthesis (NVS) is a fundamental task in computer vision and computer graphics, which is critical for downstream applications such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and digital twins. Early neural scene representations, notably Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1], demonstrated impressive visual fidelity but were constrained by prohibitive computational demands. Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] (3DGS) has revolutionised this field by achieving state-of-the-art rendering quality and real-time performance through explicit Gaussian primitives. Owing to this efficiency, 3DGS has gained significant traction across various visual computing domains, including dynamic scene modelling [3], autonomous driving [4], and SLAM [5].

Despite these breakthroughs, the widespread adoption of 3DGS remains hindered by training overhead [6]. In particular, Adaptive Density Control continuously introduces new Gaussians as training progresses. If densification and pruning are not well designed, a large number of redundant Gaussians can be produced, which increases computational cost and reduces training efficiency [7]. Therefore, controlling the number of Gaussians while preserving reconstruction quality is important for reducing training time.

A limitation of existing density control strategies is that their criteria are not directly aligned with the optimisation objective. Conventional methods usually rely on image-plane gradients to guide densification. However, these signals mainly capture local variations and do not reflect the global loss distribution well. Some approaches [8, 9] try to estimate loss through additional sampling, which gives a more direct indication of high error regions. However, this requires repeated forward rendering and introduces extra computational cost.

To address these issues, we propose a Loss Accumulation Guided (LAG) densification strategy. Unlike existing methods that require external estimation, our method directly leverages the loss maps generated during the standard training pipeline. During the backward pass, we compute a single-channel binary mask to indicate which pixels already have small reconstruction errors. For each Gaussian, we count how many of its covered pixels have errors above a threshold and average this count over K iterations to obtain a Densification Score (DS). A Gaussian with a low DS mainly covers well-reconstructed pixels; splitting or cloning it is unnecessary because the optimisation objective for those pixels has already been met. Conversely, Gaussians with a high DS are allowed to densify. This principle suppresses unnecessary Gaussian growth in regions where the reconstruction is already adequate, without requiring additional rendering passes.

To further refine the scene representation, we introduce a pruning strategy after the densification stage. This strategy constructs a pruning metric by computing, on a per-pixel basis, the ratio of the opacity gradient to the opacity value. The metric is aggregated across the Gaussian's coverage and weighted by the current iteration's training loss. By accumulating these scores over K iterations to obtain a pruning score (P S), we remove Gaussians that exceed a threshold. This mechanism is integrated with opacity-based pruning to discard Gaussians with low rendering contribution.

In addition, we modify the CUDA implementation of the 3DGS backward pass kernel. By redesigning thread organisation for warp-level data reuse and adjusting memory layouts for vectorised access, we reduce redundant global memory transactions and improve cache utilisation. These low-level optimisations reduce the overhead of executing the backward pass.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

- A loss accumulation guided densification strategy that inhibits unnecessary Gaussian generation in well-reconstructed regions.
- A pruning strategy that identifies and removes Gaussians that are unstable or have low rendering contribution.
- CUDA optimisations for the backward pass, including warp-level parallelism and vectorised access schemes, to reduce memory access overhead.

# 2 Related Work

#### 2.1 Gaussian Densification Control

In 3DGS, adaptive density control dynamically adjusts the distribution of Gaussians according to the current reconstruction state, and it strongly affects both model size and reconstruction quality. The original 3DGS method performs densification based on image-plane gradients, where Gaussians with large gradients are split or cloned. However, since this strategy relies primarily on local gradient magnitude, it may lead to excessive densification in high-frequency textures, introducing redundant Gaussians and reducing training efficiency [10].

To address this issue, existing works improve densification from different perspectives. Some works reduce the need for densification by enhancing the representation ability of each Gaussian. For example, EAGLES [11] improves representation capacity by introducing lightweight feature encoding, while ADR-Gaussian [12] adaptively adjusts the radius to better match Gaussian coverage with scene structure. Other works [13–15] modify the Gaussian basis function to further enhance representation capacity.

Another line of work directly constrains or regulates the densification process. For example, Taming-3DGS [16] introduces a Gaussian budget to control model size, while DashGaussian [17] uses a resolution scheduling strategy to progressively refine the scene during training. AbsGS [18] improves sensitivity to high frequency regions by using absolute gradients. In addition, methods such as [19–23] restrict Gaussian generation by introducing colour or other constraints.

Furthermore, some works attempt to directly use reconstruction errors to guide densification. For example, FastGS [24] constructs loss maps through multi-view forward rendering and refines Gaussians based on pixel-wise L1 loss. Compared with gradient-based signals, these methods can more directly locate under-reconstructed regions. However, they usually require multiple additional forward rendering passes to estimate error scores, which introduces computational overhead.

In summary, obtaining stable error guidance without additional overhead remains an open problem.

#### 2.2 Gaussian Pruning

In addition to controlling the generation of Gaussians, another line of research focuses on reducing model complexity through pruning, thereby improving both training and rendering efficiency. Existing studies have shown that a large number of redundant Gaussians are often present in 3DGS models. For example, RadSplat [25] and LightGaussian [26] both highlight the significant redundancy in the original representation.

Early methods typically perform pruning based on heuristic saliency metrics. Works such as [27–30] design scoring functions based on opacity, scale, or contribution, and remove Gaussians with low importance.

To improve pruning accuracy, some works introduce evaluation mechanisms based on optimization sensitivity. For example, PUP 3DGS [31] performs an approximate analysis of parameter influence to identify Gaussians that contribute less to the overall loss across multiple views.

In addition, some methods approach compression from the perspective of structural constraints. For example, Mini-Splatting [32] achieves compact representations by limiting the number of Gaussians and incorporating resampling strategies, while DropGaussian [33] introduces structural regularisation through random dropping. MaskGaussian [34] further controls the participation of Gaussians in rendering in a probabilistic manner, balancing representation capability and compression ratio.

In summary, identifying low-contribution Gaussians with low computational overhead remains an area of interest for improving overall efficiency.

## 2.3 Rendering and System-Level Optimization

In addition to density control and pruning, computational redundancy in the rendering pipeline is another factor affecting the efficiency of 3DGS. The original rasterization method adopts a relatively conservative strategy when estimating Gaussian projection ranges in image-plane. As a result, many redundant Gaussian–tile correspondences are introduced, leading to unnecessary computations during the rasterization stage [12].

To address this issue, one line of work reduces redundant computation by more accurately estimating the influence region of Gaussians on the image-plane. For example, Speedy-Splat [35] proposes the SnugBox and AccuTile methods to tighten Gaussian projection regions. FastGS [24] introduces a Compact Box strategy to reduce the number of Gaussian–tile pairs. StopThePop [36] and FlashGS [37] further improve rasterization efficiency by optimising tile intersection computation. These methods mainly improve forward rendering efficiency by reducing the number of pixels involved in the computation.

Another line of work focuses on optimisation strategies. For instance, 3DGS-LM [38] adopts a customised optimiser to accelerate convergence, while 3DGS2 [39] introduces a near second-order update strategy to improve optimisation efficiency. At the GPU execution level, DISTWAR [40] reduces thread contention by minimising atomic operations during backpropagation. In addition, methods such as [41–43] improve rendering stability and reduce computational complexity by introducing structural constraints or geometric priors.

Although several works have explored GPU execution optimisation during training, memory access patterns and thread organisation during the backward pass are still relatively unexplored.

# 3 Proposed Method

#### 3.1 Preliminaries

3DGS represents a scene using a set of explicit anisotropic 3D Gaussians. The scene is composed of N Gaussian primitives, each defined as:

$$G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right) \quad (1)$$

where x is the 3D coordinate, µ is the centre and Σ is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian.

During forward rendering, these Gaussians are transformed into the camera coordinate system using the camera transformation. Their covariance matrices are also transformed into camera space through the Jacobian matrix. By marginalising along the viewing direction, a 2D covariance Σ2D is obtained, which forms an elliptical Gaussian distribution on the image-plane:

$$G_{2D}(P) = \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2}(P - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1}(P - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D}) \right) \quad (2)$$

where P is the pixel coordinate and µ2D is the coordinate of the centre on the imageplane.

During the backward pass, each Gaussian influences multiple pixels within its projected region on the image-plane. Its gradient is computed by accumulating gradients from these pixels. As a result, each Gaussian receives optimisation signals from multiple local pixels.

#### 3.2 Overview

As shown in Figure 1, our method is composed of three parts. At the algorithm level, we implement two strategies to maintain a compact Gaussian set: LAG densification inhibits unnecessary Gaussian generation in well-reconstructed regions, while opacitygradient-based pruning identifies and removes unstable or low-contribution Gaussians. At the implementation level, we introduce CUDA optimisations that reduce redundant memory accesses and improve warp-level parallelism during the backward pass. Together, these components reduce training time while maintaining rendering quality.

#### 3.3 Loss Accumulation Guided Densification

In the original 3DGS [2], the densification process is triggered by the average magnitude of 2D positional gradients. However, these gradients measure the sensitivity of the rendering loss to positional perturbations, which may not consistently correlate with the reconstruction quality [44]. Consequently, in regions containing high-frequency

textures or complex appearance variations, the gradients can become large [45]. This leads to the generation of many unnecessary Gaussians, increasing the computational load of the rasteriser.

The training efficiency of 3DGS is related to the number of Gaussians, as more Gaussians increase the per-iteration computation. Therefore, we propose the LAG densification strategy that uses an accumulated training loss constraint to suppress unnecessary Gaussian generation.

Our strategy establishes a connection between the optimisation objective and the density control mechanism. During the backward pass, we reuse the joint loss to identify pixels where the reconstruction error is already small. As shown in Figure 2, we average the three channels to obtain a single-channel loss map, then apply a thresholding operation. Pixels P with loss values below the threshold are considered well-reconstructed, while those above the threshold are marked as under-reconstructed. We define a binary mask M(P):

| $M(P) = \begin{cases} 1 & L_m(P) > \tau \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$ | (4) |

where Lm(P) is the mean loss at pixel P. When a Gaussian predominantly covers pixels with M(P) = 0 (i.e., low-loss, well-reconstructed pixels), generating additional Gaussians in that region is unnecessary because the optimisation objective for those pixels has already been met. Conversely, pixels with M(P) = 1 indicate under-reconstructed areas where densification may still be beneficial.

The loss distribution from a single rendering can be affected by viewpoint changes and scene noise. Therefore, using one iteration alone may not reliably reflect which regions are well-reconstructed. To address this, we accumulate M(P) over K iterations. For each Gaussian, we project it onto the image-plane to obtain the covered pixel area Ω and compute the DS averaged over K iterations:

|  | $DS = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{t=1}^K \sum_{P \in \Omega_t} M_t(P)$ | (5) |

An intuitive interpretation is: if a Gaussian mostly covers well-reconstructed pixels, splitting or cloning it will not help reduce the loss further, so the operation should be avoided.

As shown in Figure 3, by averaging the high loss pixel count over K iterations, DS provides a stable and reliable measure of whether a Gaussian is consistently located in a region where the current reconstruction is already sufficient.

During densification, we use the densification score as an additional constraint alongside the original 3DGS gradient criterion. A Gaussian is allowed to split or clone only when both constraints are satisfied. This dual-constraint mechanism inhibits Gaussian growth in well-reconstructed areas. By maintaining a smaller set of Gaussians, the per-iteration computation is reduced, which shortens training time.

#### 3.4 Pruning Based on Opacity and Its Gradients

While the LAG densification strategy suppresses unnecessary Gaussian generation, a pruning mechanism is needed to remove existing Gaussians that have become redundant during training. We consider two types of redundant Gaussians, each addressed by a different criterion. The first type is low-contribution Gaussians: primitives whose opacity has become negligible and therefore contribute little to the rendered image. These are directly removed by an opacity threshold. The second type is unstable Gaussians: primitives that still exhibit large opacity gradients while having relatively low opacity, indicating that their parameters have not yet converged despite their limited contribution. These are identified through a gradient-based scoring mechanism.

To capture unstable Gaussians, we design a pruning metric based on the ratio of the opacity gradient to the opacity value at pixel level. During the backward pass, the

loss function produces gradients with respect to pixel-wise opacity. Because Gaussians have spatially non-uniform responses on the image-plane, different pixels contribute differently to the same Gaussian. We define a pixel-wise score as:

$$r(P) = \frac{1}{\alpha(P)} \left| \frac{\partial L(P)}{\partial \alpha(P)} \right| \quad (6)$$

  where L(P) is the loss at pixel P and α(P) is the opacity of the Gaussian at that pixel. This formulation specifically targets unstable Gaussians: a primitive with low opacity but a large gradient produces a high score, indicating that it remains sensitive to optimisation. In contrast, a well-converged Gaussian, which has a stable α and small gradient, receives a low score.

For a single view, we accumulate the pixel-wise scores within the region covered by the Gaussian to obtain a view-level score:

$$R = \sum_{P \in \Omega} r(P) \quad (7)$$

As shown in Figure 6, over K consecutive views, we weight each view-level score by the corresponding training loss and accumulate:

$$PS = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{t=1}^K L_t \cdot R_t \quad (8)$$

where Lt is the loss at the t-th iteration and Rt is the view-level score. Weighting by the training loss prevents Gaussians located in already well-reconstructed regions from being penalised for moderate instability.

In practice, pruning is performed periodically after the densification stage is completed. During pruning, we directly remove Gaussians with P S exceeding the threshold. Additionally, we directly discard Gaussians with opacity values below a predefined minimum. Through this strategy, the model can continuously remove redundant Gaussians during the training process, thereby reducing the workload of the rasteriser and the time taken for the backward pass.

#### 3.5 CUDA Parallel Optimisation for the Backward Pass

In addition to the density control strategies above, we modify the CUDA implementation of the 3DGS backward pass to reduce memory access overhead. These changes do not alter the rendering results or the optimisation behaviour; they focus on thread organisation and data access patterns.

Background. In CUDA, threads are grouped into warps (typically 32 threads on NVIDIA GPUs) that execute instructions in lockstep. Multiple warps form a thread block, whose threads can communicate through shared memory — a small on-chip memory with lower latency than global device memory. Vectorised memory instructions (e.g., loading four floats with a single float4 instruction) and memory coalescing (arranging memory accesses so that consecutive threads access consecutive addresses) improve bandwidth utilisation.

Motivation. In the original 3DGS, rendering is performed on fixed-size image tiles (typically 16 × 16 pixels). During the backward pass, multiple warps processing different Gaussians may repeatedly access pixel data within the same tile. Because the original implementation uses 32 threads per blocks, pixel data are fetched from global memory multiple times by different warps, creating redundant transactions.

Warp-level data reuse. We assign one thread block of 8 warps to each tile, creating a one-to-one mapping between threads and pixels inside the tile. As shown in Figure 9, each 16 × 16 tile contains 256 pixels. Tile-level data (pixel colours ∈ R 3 , transmittance ∈ R, and the list of contributing Gaussians ∈ R) are loaded once from global memory into shared memory and reused by all warps in the block. This reduces redundant global accesses because shared memory is an order of magnitude faster than global memory for intra-block reuse.

Vectorised memory access. As shown in Figure 10, we pack intermediate variables into float4 format during the forward pass. For each pixel, the colour and transmittance are stored as a single float4. During the backward pass, one 128-bit transaction loads all four values, whereas four separate 32-bit loads would be needed otherwise. This reduces instruction count and improves memory bandwidth utilisation.

Memory coalescing via layout reorganisation. Both forward and backward passes involve indexed reads after depth sorting, which creates irregular access patterns. To address this, we reorganise the data layout during the forward pass by storing



Figure 10: Modification of the data storage format. Intermediate variables are written in float4 format during the forward pass. float4 packs four 32-bit floats into a single 128-bit operand, enabling one-instruction loads. The "float\*3" represents the three floating-point attributes of each Gaussian.

accessed data in contiguous memory according to the access order. This changes backward pass memory access from irregular to sequential, enabling coalesced reads that reduce access latency.

Portability. These optimisations make certain assumptions. (i) Warp size: The design assumes a warp size of 32, standard on NVIDIA GPUs. (ii) Memory alignment: float4 access requires 16-byte alignment, which we ensure during buffer allocation. (iii) GPU architecture: The speedup from shared memory reuse depends on the available shared memory per block (48 KB or more on modern NVIDIA architectures). Older GPUs with less shared memory may require adjustments, yet our method can still run on the RTX 3060.

These optimisations are independent of the proposed density control strategy and can be integrated into existing 3DGS pipelines.

#### 4 Experiments

#### 4.1 Experimental setup

Datasets. We evaluate on the same datasets as the original 3DGS paper: Mip-NeRF 360 [46], Deep Blending [47], and Tanks & Temples [48].

Metrics. We evaluate the performance using PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and training time. To ensure a fair comparison of training time, only the iterative training time is measured, excluding data loading and saving to avoid the influence of different storage devices.

Implementation Details. We follow the original 3DGS strategy by using gradients for cloning, while adopting absolute gradients from AbsGS [18] for splitting. In addition, we incorporate the Compact Box strategy from FastGS [24]. In our default setting, densification is performed every 500 iterations until 15,000 iterations, after which pruning is conducted every 3,000 iterations. The DS threshold is 30 by default, K is 10, and the P S threshold is 0.09. All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, and all compared methods are based on their official implementations. For fairness, all methods use the fused-ssim to calculate SSIM loss during training.

Effect of LAG. Comparing Base with LAG shows that the LAG strategy reduces the NGS. For example, on Mip-NeRF 360 [46], NGS decreases from 0.77M to 0.47M. This reduction leads to shorter training time because fewer Gaussians are processed per iteration. The rendering metrics decrease slightly, which reflects the trade-off between compactness and reconstruction accuracy.

Effect of pruning. Comparing Base with Prune shows that the pruning strategy removes additional Gaussians after densification, further reducing NGS and training time while having a negligible effect on rendering metrics. The pruning mechanism targets redundant Gaussians: low-contribution primitives are removed by the opacity threshold, and unstable ones by the P S.

Relationship between LAG and pruning. When both are used together (LAG+Prune), the reduction in NGS is larger than with either component alone. On Mip-NeRF 360 [46], LAG+Prune achieves 0.41M Gaussians, compared to 0.47M (LAG) and 0.63M (Prune), indicating that the two strategies are complementary.

Effect of CUDA optimisations. Comparing configurations with and without CUDA optimisations (e.g., Base vs. Base+cu-opt and LAG vs. LAG+cu-opt) shows a consistent reduction in training time across all datasets whilst rendering metrics remain nearly unchanged. The speedup from CUDA optimisations is independent of the number of Gaussians: even when the Gaussian count has already been reduced by LAG or pruning (LAG+cu-opt vs. LAG), the CUDA optimisations still provide additional time savings. For instance, on Mip-NeRF 360 [46], LAG requires 215.4s and LAG+cu-opt requires 200.1s, a reduction of approximately 7%. This is because the CUDA optimisations target memory access patterns in the backward pass kernel, which are present regardless of how many Gaussians are being processed. Therefore, the CUDA optimisations remain beneficial even in already-compact configurations.

Summary. The three components target different aspects of training efficiency: LAG controls Gaussian generation, Prune removes unhelpful primitives post-generation, and CUDA optimisations reduce per-iteration execution overhead. Together, they achieve shorter training time while maintaining reconstruction quality.

## 5 Conclusion

This paper proposes an optimisation framework to reduce the training time of 3DGS. At the algorithm level, we introduce a Loss Accumulation Guided densification strategy and an opacity-gradient-based pruning strategy. The densification strategy uses accumulated training loss to suppress unnecessary Gaussian generation in wellreconstructed regions. The pruning strategy removes redundant Gaussians through two criteria: an opacity threshold for low-contribution primitives and a gradient-based score for unstable ones. These two strategies work together to maintain a compact Gaussian set. At the implementation level, we introduce CUDA optimisations, including warp-level parallelism and vectorised data access, to reduce memory access overhead during the backward pass.

Experimental results across multiple datasets show that the proposed method reduces total training time while maintaining rendering quality.

# 6 Limitations

Limitations of the LAG Strategy. First, the LAG strategy uses a uniform threshold τ for all pixels to obtain M(P). Pixels in a black background and pixels in texture-rich foreground areas are treated equally when their loss values are identical. Second, M(P) is recorded over K consecutive iterations with randomly sampled training images. This stochastic selection may occasionally include non-critical views, which can introduce noise into the densification score.

Limitations of the Pruning Strategy. First, P S accumulated over K iterations cannot cover all Gaussians in the scene. Pruning is restricted to the subset of Gaussians that project onto the image-plane during these views, leaving unseen Gaussians unpruned. Second, pixel-level pruning scores may penalise Gaussians that are still adapting in difficult regions. As shown in Table 10, pruning that occurs too early can reduce rendering quality.

Limitations of the CUDA Optimisation. Our CUDA optimisations modify the execution patterns of the original backpropagation kernel without altering the mathematical formulation of 3DGS. Consequently, the improvement in training speed is limited to the backward pass overhead.
