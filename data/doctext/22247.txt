### 1 Introduction

In recent years, neural distance functions have become a powerful tool for representing and manipulating 3D shapes, achieving strong performance across various 3D deep learning tasks such as point cloud reconstruction [Chibane et al. 2020; Fainstein et al. 2024; Ren et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2023, 2022], multi-view reconstruction [Deng et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Long et al. 2023; Mittal et al. 2022; Park et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021; Yariv et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024a] and 3D shape generation [He et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025a; Liu et al. 2024b; Xiang et al. 2025b; Zhang et al. 2024b; Zhou et al. 2024]. In these applications, surfaces are typically defined as the zero-level set of a signed distance function (SDF), which assigns to each point in space the shortest distance to the surface, with the sign indicating whether the point is inside or outside the object. Although effective, SDFs are inherently limited to watertight surfaces and cannot directly represent open or nonmanifold geometries. Several variants have been proposed to extend SDFs to open surfaces [Chen et al. 2022a; Liu et al. 2024a; Meng et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024], but non-manifold structures remain difficult to handle.

To overcome these limitations, unsigned distance functions (UDFs), which discard the sign information in SDFs, have received increasing attention. UDFs offer greater flexibility in representing a wider range of surface types, including both open and closed shapes, manifolds and non-manifolds, and geometries with internal structures [Chibane et al. 2020; Deng et al. 2024; Fainstein et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024b, 2023; Long et al. 2023; Ren et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2024b,a; Zhou et al. 2023, 2022, 2024]. Despite this increased representational capacity, extracting zero-level sets from UDFs presents significant technical challenges. The main difficulty lies in the lack of a sign change across the surface, which traditional mesh extraction methods such as Marching Cubes (MC) [Lorensen and Cline 1987] and Dual Contouring (DC) [Ju et al. 2002] rely on

to detect surface intersections. Without this cue, these methods cannot be directly applied to UDFs. Furthermore, UDFs learned from raw data in real world applications often suffer from approximation errors and non-differentiability near the zero-level set [Fainstein et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023a]. These issues lead to geometric inaccuracies, topological artifacts, and instability during surface extraction.





Fig. 2. Active cube detection in SDFs versus UDFs. In SDFs, Dual Contouring unambiguously identifies valid active cubes (marked in green) by detecting sign changes (a). By solving for dual vertices within these cubes, it reconstructs the correct manifold topology (b). In UDFs, due to the absence of sign information, spurious (marked in blue) and missing (marked in yellow) cubes are prone to be misidentified, particularly when the target surface is located very near the boundary of adjacent cubes (c). These erroneous active cubes result in redundant non-manifold artifacts or holes (marked in red) during the standard connectivity process of Dual Contouring (d).

Dual Contouring [Ju et al. 2002] is naturally suited to reconstructing non-manifold surfaces, as it places one vertex per active cube and forms polygonal faces by connecting adjacent cube vertices. As illustrated in Figure 2 (a), the classical DC algorithm identifies active cubes based on edge sign changes in SDFs and solves a Quadratic Error Function (QEF) to compute vertex positions. However, in the context of UDFs, the absence of sign information prevents the direct application of standard DC. Neural Dual Contouring (NDC) [Chen et al. 2022b] addresses this by training a neural network to predict surface-intersecting edges, while DMUDF [Zhang et al. 2023a] relies on a predefined threshold to select active cubes. However, neither approach achieves the unambiguous active cube detection inherent to SDFs, particularly when the zero level set grazes the cube boundaries, as shown in Figure 2 (c). Such misclassifications can introduce spurious non-manifold structures in manifold regions or cause missing connections, resulting in topological artifacts such as holes or invalid mesh structures during polygonization, as illustrated in Figure 2 (d). Consequently, effective mesh extraction from UDFs demands a more robust, learning-free active cube identification scheme, coupled with a flexible polygonization criterion to resiliently handle potential active cube ambiguities.

In this paper, we propose DCx, which stands for Dual Contouring over expanded cubes. As an extension of the standard Dual Contouring algorithm [Ju et al. 2002], DCx introduces two key modifications: it replaces sign-based active cube detection with an optimizationbased strategy, and it expands the traditional per-edge (or 2 × 2 × 1 voxel blocks) face generation to operate over 2 × 2 × 2 voxel blocks (expanded cubes). The neighboring expanded cubes overlap, having 4 voxels in common. A voxel-to-mesh lookup table is designed to generate faces per expanded cube. Furthermore, by aggregating the case IDs of adjacent expanded cubes, we achieve a polygonization criterion with an effective receptive field 4 × 4 × 4. These changes

allow DCx to operate directly on unsigned distance fields and support the reconstruction of non-manifold surfaces. Computational results show that DCx produces more accurate and stable meshes than existing methods, particularly on shapes with complex geometry or non-manifold structures. See Figure 1 for a representative example showcasing non-manifold structures reconstructed by DCx. In contrast, existing methods such as NDC [Chen et al. 2022b] and DMUDF [Zhang et al. 2023a] fail to preserve these structures, resulting in outputs with numerous topological defects.

# 2 Related Works

# 2.1 Neural Distance Fields

Neural signed distance functions have become a popular representation for 3D shape modeling, surface reconstruction, and shape generation. A pioneering work in this area is DeepSDF [Park et al. 2019], which introduced the concept of learning continuous SDFs to represent a collection of 3D shapes with similar geometry. It employs a neural network to represent the SDFs, using a latent code as conditioning to differentiate specific object instances within a category of shapes.

Building on this foundation, follow-up works such as Deep Level Set [Zhou et al. 2023], Curriculum DeepSDF [Duan et al. 2020], SAL [Atzmon and Lipman 2020], SALD [Atzmon and Lipman 2021], and Neural-Pull [Ma et al. 2021], extend DeepSDF from a supervised setting, where ground-truth SDFs are required, to unsupervised or weakly supervised frameworks that directly learn SDFs from point clouds without signed distance supervision.

Although effective for watertight objects, SDFs are inherently limited in representing open surfaces and unbounded scenes, which are common in real-world data. These geometries cannot be cleanly partitioned into interior and exterior regions, rendering sign information ambiguous or invalid. To overcome this, unsigned distance functions, which retain only distance magnitude and discard sign information, have been proposed as a more flexible alternative to represent surfaces with arbitrary topology.

Early neural UDF methods, including NDF [Chibane et al. 2020] and AnchorUDF [Zhao et al. 2021], attempted to learn UDFs from point clouds. However, they struggled to produce smooth and accurate fields near the surface. This limitation stems from a theoretical mismatch: UDFs are not differentiable at the zero-level set, whereas neural networks are inherently smooth and differentiable. As a result, distance values and gradients near the surface often become unreliable.

Later works such as CAPUDF [Zhou et al. 2022], GeoUDF [Ren et al. 2023], LevelSetUDF [Ma et al. 2023], and DEUDF [Xu et al. 2025] improve UDF accuracy by introducing new network architectures or loss functions. Although neural networks are still fundamentally smooth and differentiable, these improvements significantly enhance the accuracy and stability of UDF-based reconstructions, particularly near the zero-level set.

In parallel, recent developments have explored the combination of neural distance functions with radiance field representations. Methods such as NeRF [Mildenhall et al. 2020] and 3DGS [Kerbl et al. 2023] integrate radiance fields with volumetric or point-based rendering for novel view synthesis, bridging the gap between 3D computer

vision and computer graphics. However, surfaces extracted directly from radiance fields often lack geometric precision. To improve reconstruction quality, several methods such as NeuS [Wang et al. 2021], VolSDF [Yariv et al. 2021], NeuralRecon [Chen et al. 2024], NeuralUDF [Long et al. 2023], NeUDF [Liu et al. 2023], NeuSG [Chen et al. 2025b], SuGaR [Guédon and Lepetit 2024], 3DGSR [Lyu et al. 2024], and GSDF [Yu et al. 2024], incorporate distance fields into radiance field models, resulting in more accurate and robust surfaces.

# 2.2 Zero-Level Set Extraction for Distance Functions

Zero-level set extraction from distance functions involves two key steps: (1) Identifying points on the zero-level set; and (2) Connecting those points to construct a surface.

Marching Cubes (MC) [Lorensen and Cline 1987] and Dual Contouring (DC) [Ju et al. 2002] are two classical surface extraction algorithms. MC partitions space into uniform cubes and interpolates zero-crossing points along cube edges based on scalar values at the eight cube corners. A predefined lookup table determines the triangulation pattern according to the sign configuration at the cube's vertices. Various extensions of MC, such as Marching Cubes 33 [Tcherniaev 1995], have been proposed to address issues such as topological ambiguity.

As a primal approach, MC generates vertices along grid edges, a strategy that inherently struggles to preserve sharp geometric features. In contrast, DC adopts a dual approach by computing vertex positions within the interior of intersecting cubes, thereby enabling the effective capture of sharp features. For each cube intersecting the isosurface, DC computes a representative vertex by minimizing a quadratic error function that incorporates both position and normal constraints at the intersection points of the surface. Finally, DC forms quadrilateral faces by connecting the vertices of four adjacent cubes that share a common edge. To avoid artifacts associated with QEF and the stringent requirements for high-precision normals, ODC [Hwang and Sung 2024] adapts Dual Contouring to extract meshes from occupancy fields, using binary search to determine vertex locations. However, occupancy fields are inherently restricted to representing watertight manifolds.

Several recent methods have adapted MC or DC to support surface extraction from neural UDFs. MeshUDF [Guillard et al. 2022] and CAPUDF [Zhou et al. 2022] use gradient information to assign pseudo-signs to cube vertices near the surface and then apply standard Marching Cubes for mesh extraction. DMUDF [Zhang et al. 2023a] proposes a three-stage pipeline: First, it estimates tangent planes from an initial point cloud using distances and gradients; Second, it samples points on those planes and groups them into local clusters; Finally, it computes optimal vertex positions by solving local least-squares problems, followed by DC-based polygonization using the filtered vertices. NSDUDF [Stella et al. 2024] introduces a neural preprocessing step that converts local neural UDF neighborhoods into pseudo-SDF representations using a pre-trained mesh network. NDC [Chen et al. 2022b] also use a pre-trained network to predict parameters needed by Dual Contouring.

DCUDF [Hou et al. 2023] and DCUDF2 [Chen et al. 2025c] leverage the observation that non-zero isosurfaces (e.g., ��-level sets for �� &gt; 0) tend to exhibit better smoothness than the zero-level set. These methods extract double-covered ��-isosurfaces using MC, then apply gradient descent to shrink them toward the actual zero-level set. Although this strategy improves robustness, it introduces several drawbacks. Specifically, DCUDF employs a post-processing scheme to extract single-layer surfaces from double-layered meshes. However, this mechanism proves to be effective only for simple manifold geometries. When applied to non-manifold surfaces or complex scenes, it frequently fails to resolve the dual layers since a non-manifold surface cannot be partitioned into two congruent components. These redundant layers not only increase storage overhead, but also complicate downstream tasks such as editing, collision detection, and rendering. Moreover, the shrinking process tends to over-smooth the geometry, leading to a noticeable loss of fine surface details.

Another class of approaches capable of reconstructing singlelayer non-manifold surfaces relies on Material Interfaces (MIs) or Implicit Arrangements (IAs) [Du et al. 2022; Ju et al. 2024; Wu and Sullivan Jr 2003]. By partitioning 3D space into distinct regions, these methods inherently support non-manifold representations. However, MIs and IAs are generally difficult to infer directly from raw inputs such as point clouds. MIND [Chen et al. 2025a] is a computational method for generating MI from UDF. However, due to intrinsic constraints in the MI representation, it cannot produce non-orientable, non-manifold surfaces.

# 3 From DC to DCx

### 3.1 Technical Challenges

Figure 3 illustrates the technical challenges in extracting zero-level sets from UDFs using a 2D toy example, where we visualize the error distributions of a neural UDF learned with CAPUDF [Zhou et al. 2022], a representative UDF learning algorithm. We have the following observations,

- Distance errors increase in regions farther from the zerolevel set. This occurs because most UDF learning algorithms prioritize minimizing errors near the target surface, where supervision is strongest. As a result, the learned representation tends to generalize poorly to areas farther from the surface, where supervision is sparse, leading to increased distance errors.
- Distance errors are significantly larger near non-manifold structures than in manifold regions. This is because nonmanifold configurations often involve sharp junctions, boundaries, or ambiguous local topology, which pose additional learning challenges for neural networks. These regions tend to exhibit inconsistent gradients and discontinuous spatial patterns in the distance field, making it difficult for models to fit smooth and accurate UDFs.
- Gradient errors are elevated on both the zero-level set and the medial axis. In contrast, regions slightly offset from the surface tend to exhibit much smaller gradient errors. This pattern can be attributed to the fact that both the zero-level set and the medial axis correspond to locations where the unsigned distance functions are not differentiable. These nonsmooth regions introduce inherent learning difficulties, making it harder for neural networks to produce reliable distance

and gradient estimates. In contrast, areas where the distance function is smooth and differentiable are more readily approximated.



Fig. 3. Analogous to the Figure 1 of DMUDF [Zhang et al. 2023a], we analyze the approximation errors inherent in learned UDFs. For the ground truth UDF of a T-junction geometry (a), we learn the approximate UDF via CAP-UDF [Zhou et al. 2022] (b). Due to the inherent difficulty of neural networks in fitting non-differentiable region, significant errors concentrate around the zero-level set, the medial axis, and non-manifold junctions (c). These numerical inaccuracies propagate into the gradient field, resulting in severe distortions and even completely erroneous gradient directions (d). This renders QEF-based dual vertex positioning highly unstable. Although DMUDF leverage off-surface gradients, it remains susceptible to these fundamental gradient artifacts.

These error patterns in neural UDFs, namely the degradation of accuracy away from the surface, elevated errors around nonmanifold configurations, and instability of gradients at critical regions, pose significant challenges for zero-level set extraction. Surface reconstruction algorithms heavily rely on both the accuracy of distance values and the consistency of gradients near the surface. Even moderate deviations in these quantities can propagate into

severe geometric and topological artifacts in the extracted mesh. These shortcomings of neural UDFs directly impact the performance of existing surface extraction algorithms. In particular, Marching Cubes-based methods [Guillard et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2022] face two critical limitations when applied to neural UDFs. First, errors in distance and gradient estimates near the zero-level set degrade the quality of sign estimation and zero-crossing interpolation, often leading to local reconstruction failures, such as holes or distorted geometry. Second, increased field errors in the vicinity of non-manifold regions reduce the effectiveness of advanced MC variants that incorporate rules for non-manifold handling, ultimately leading to reconstructions that are discontinuous or incomplete.

Dual Contouring-based methods, such as DMUDF [Zhang et al. 2023a] and NDC [Chen et al. 2022b], also suffer from these limitations. DMUDF employs an octree-based strategy to determine vertex positions using samples that may lie far from the zero-level set. When the UDF exhibits large approximation errors in these regions, the placement of the vertex becomes unreliable, resulting in partial surface loss or geometric inconsistencies. Moreover, inaccuracies in distances and gradient directions negatively influence QEF optimization in DMUDF and vertex prediction in NDC, both of which are used to determine vertex positions, thereby further degrading the reconstruction quality.

These challenges highlight the need for more robust strategies that reduce dependence on sign estimation, mitigate error sensitivity, and improve mesh connectivity under topological ambiguity.

#### 3.2 Key Design Improvements

DCx is designed to address the challenges outlined above, particularly the difficulty of extracting surfaces with non-manifold structures from neural UDFs.

The first key improvement is a replacement for the traditional QEF-based vertex computation in Dual Contouring [Ju et al. 2002]. As described in Section 4, instead of solving the QEF equations within each active cube, DCx adopts an optimization-based approach: for each cube whose UDF values fall below a predefined threshold ��0, the cube centroid is projected toward the zero-level set using gradient descent. In contrast to NDF, which utilizes Stochastic Gradient Descent, we employ the vector Adam optimizer [Ling et al. 2022] to enhance the robustness of point projection against noise, ensuring accurate convergence to the model surface. This strategy effectively mitigates errors resulting from unreliable gradients at specific sample locations, thereby significantly reducing artifacts associated with QEF instabilities. By defining active cubes based on the occupancy of projected points, the computed dual vertex is naturally confined within the local region, thereby eliminating the risk of boundary violation and obviating the need for corrective clamping.

The second key improvement addresses face construction. Standard DC forms faces by connecting the vertices of four adjacent cubes that share a common edge, relying on the assumption that all four cubes are active—a condition guaranteed by sign changes in SDFs, but not in UDFs. Since UDFs lack sign information, active cube distributions are often sparse or irregular, making the traditional face construction logic inapplicable and prone to introducing holes or topological inconsistencies, as illustrated in Figure 4\(b).



Fig. 4. DC vs DCx. Standard DC identifies active cubes (green cubes) by detecting sign changes at cube corners in the input SDF. To resolve this, in Section 5, we introduce a voxel-to-mesh (V2M) lookup table defined over 2×2×2 voxel blocks, which we refer to as expanded cubes. This lookup table encodes local voxel configurations and the corresponding mesh connectivity patterns, enabling robust triangle mesh generation even when active cubes are unevenly distributed. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on local edgebased adjacency, our expanded cube representation supports both manifold and non-manifold cases and is more robust to activation

errors. It provides a more flexible and consistent framework for assembling polygonal faces, improving surface connectivity and stability in challenging regions.

Together, these two design changes, optimization-based projection for vertex placement and expanded cube-based mesh assembly via the V2M lookup table, enable DCx to more effectively handle noisy, ambiguous, or non-manifold regions, outperforming existing DC variants in both accuracy and robustness.

# 4 Optimization-Guided Active Cube Selection

As discussed in Section 3.1, directly determining active cubes and computing the corresponding vertices from an inaccurate UDF are unreliable. To address this, we first employ an optimization-based approach that is less sensitive to noise, enabling the reconstruction of a point cloud that approximates the zero-level set. These optimized points then form the basis for identifying active cubes and defining the vertices used in the subsequent meshing step. To ensure a clean and compact representation, we further apply a thinning process to remove redundant voxels and enforce unit-thickness in the resulting voxel set.

Theoretically, at any point in the domain, the gradient vector should point toward the nearest location on the zero-level set. We leverage this property to identify points that approximate the surface. #### 4.2 Thinning

Since neural UDFs are often inaccurate, some points may fail to fall on the zero-level set during optimization. As a result, unnecessary voxels may be incorrectly marked as active, introducing noise into volumetric discretization. These redundant voxels can disrupt the intended one-layer structure and lead to two main problems, depending on the context.

To ensure structural correctness and mesh continuity, we introduce a thinning operation to remove these spurious voxels. First, for every expanded cube of Case 8, we check whether the local voxel configurations satisfy the structural conditions of Case 8 and eliminate any voxels that fail to meet these requirements according to Condition 1. This process is repeated until no further voxels are removed. An illustrative example is shown in Figure 6 (a). Second, as shown in Figure 6 (b) and Figure 7, for every active cube and its 3 × 3 × 3 neighborhood, we remove the redundant voxel according to Condition 2. This process is repeated until no such voxels can be removed.

# 5 Meshing via V2M Lookup Table on Expanded Cubes

In SDFs, Dual Contouring constructs quadrilateral faces by connecting vertices from four active cubes that share an edge with sign changes. However, in the case of UDFs, the absence of sign information makes such edge-based face construction unreliable.

1We first employ the V2M lookup table to generate an initial mesh, then mark the active cubes corresponding to open boundaries of the mesh as boundary active cubes.

Fig. 7. A thinning example of Condition 2.

To address the challenges arising from irregular active cube distributions in UDFs, we propose a new surface meshing strategy that does not rely on edge sign transitions. Instead of operating on individual voxels, our method examines expanded cubes that span 2 × 2 × 2 voxels. Adjacent expanded cubes overlap, sharing 2 × 2 × 1 voxels. Our goal is to connect the active voxels within each expanded cube and maintain consistency between adjacent expanded cubes. For each possible voxel configuration of an expanded cube, we define the corresponding mesh connectivity among active voxels. This gives rise to a voxel-to-mesh lookup table, which guides mesh construction from vertices inside active voxels and supports both manifold and non-manifold topologies. Treating the 8 cubes as 8 vertices could establish a cube, which is called the dual cube of an expanded cube. Generating faces for each expanded cube is to connect all active vertices (corresponding to active cubes) of the dual cube. For most expanded cubes, there are multiple possible configurations to connect all the active dual cube vertices. To systematically construct the voxel-to-mesh lookup table, we follow three design principles:

- A. Connectivity: The patches generated within each expanded cube should form a single connected component. Unlike SDFs, which naturally keep watertight, UDF-based reconstruction must explicitly enforce internal connectivity to prevent gaps.
- B. Consistent Spatial Partitioning: For a given expanded cube, the active voxels partition the space into several subregions (assuming 6-connectivity in 3D). The mesh patches generated inside the dual cube should partition the space into the same number of sub-regions, with consistent spatial locations.
- C. Minimality: Non-manifold structures should only be introduced when necessary. Among configurations that satisfy A and B, we select the one with the fewest non-manifold edges. If multiple configurations produce the same number of non-manifold edges, we choose the one with the fewest faces.

Based on these principles, our LUT introduces non-manifold structures only when necessary and significantly reduces the likelihood of holes. A detailed discussion is provided in Appendix A.

# 5.1 Voxel Configuration Encoding

Each expanded cube contains 8 voxels, giving rise to 2 8 = 256 possible occupancy patterns. We encode these patterns using 8-bit binary masks, where each bit indicates whether a voxel is active (1) or

not (0). To reduce redundancy, we group rotationally and mirrorsymmetric patterns into equivalence classes. Configurations with less than three active voxels are excluded due to insufficient geometry for face construction, reducing the total to 17 representative categories. These are labeled by voxel count and serial number (for example, 3-1, 4-2, 5-3, etc.). The first number indicates how many cubes are active, and the second number denotes the unique index of the case among those with the same number of active cubes.



We further divide the 17 categories into two major groups:

- Group 1: Patterns with face generation rules that are selfcontained and do not require information from neighboring expanded cubes.
- Group 2: Patterns that require adaptive face construction based on the categories of neighboring expanded cubes without generating the mesh.

The ambiguity in Group 2 arises from the need to maintain mesh continuity across neighboring expanded cubes. Since this dependency only relies on the case IDs of adjacent cubes, the process allows for efficient parallel execution. We define generation priorities to prevent duplicate faces in overlapping expanded cubes during parallel generation. Detailed rules are given in Appendix B. Another option is to eliminate the duplicate faces in a post-processing step, but this is both inefficient and redundant. In the following, we describe the face generation logic for each group.

# 5.4 Post-processing

LUT is still able to generate a few redundant non-manifold structures. Although it is possible to handle these inconsistencies during

the mesh extraction stage, doing so would significantly complicate the design of the V2M lookup table.

To address this efficiently, we incorporate a post-processing step after mesh extraction to prune small non-manifold branches, typically thin protrusions attached to boundary edges. This step helps improve the structural integrity of the mesh by removing artifacts that arise from locally inconsistent voxel configurations. Specifically, we examine all faces that contain any vertex on non-manifold edges. A face is identified for removal if it satisfies two conditions: (1) all three of its constituent vertices lie on either non-manifold edges or open boundary edges, and (2) the face itself contains an open boundary edge. A concrete example of this pruning process is illustrated in Figure 10. We repeat this process until no more faces could be removed.

### 8 Limitations

We demonstrate that DCx extends the performance of standard dual contouring [Ju et al. 2002] to UDFs. However, certain limitations remain to be addressed. First, our algorithm relies on sufficient grid resolution to correctly resolve local geometric details. As illustrated in Figure 14, at lower resolutions, the voxel configurations of sharp features often become indistinguishable from those of non-manifold geometry, leading to erroneous non-manifold generation due to this ambiguity.

Theoretically, our method generates correct reconstructions given sufficiently high resolution and accurate distance fields. However, considering the prohibitive computational costs of high-resolution grids and the inherent noise in practical UDF applications, we employ thinning and post-processing strategies to enhance algorithmic robustness and suppress incorrect topological artifacts. Nevertheless, in specific cases, the current post-processing pipeline may be overly aggressive, misclassifying valid geometry, and resulting in unwanted holes, as shown in Figure 18. If the desired reconstruction is a watertight manifold, SDF still holds an absolute advantage. For DCX, given a UDF to compute the active voxel distribution,

it is highly challenging to implement a lookup table consisting exclusively of manifold cases to accommodate all potential voxel distribution scenarios. It is challenging to guarantee that DCx produces strictly manifold surfaces. One possible remedy is to apply a post-processing step that cuts the mesh along non-manifold edges and then tries to group each resulting connected component into separate manifold objects.

Meanwhile, as a grid-based mesh extraction method, similar to methods like M3C [Wu and Sullivan Jr 2003], DCx cannot support arbitrary non-manifold structures. Our method currently allows non-manifold vertices formed by the intersection of up to 6 surfaces, e.g., the anchor point of Case 8. For topologies having more branches, our method might degenerate into multiple non-manifold vertices. One possible direction is to employ sub-grids to increase the degrees of freedom [Du et al. 2022], but so far we have not identified an effective way to integrate sub-grids into the DCX framework.

In this work, we further explore the broader applicability of DC and demonstrate that it can be effectively adapted to unsigned distance functions as well.

We introduce DCx, an extension of DC specifically designed for extracting zero-level sets from neural unsigned distance functions, with explicit support for non-manifold structures. By replacing traditional QEF-based vertex placement with an optimization-based projection strategy and introducing a voxel-to-mesh lookup table over expanded cubes, DCx overcomes several limitations of existing

methods, including sensitivity to sign estimation, poor handling of

non-manifold configurations, and unstable face assembly. DCx is conceptually simple and easy to implement. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DCx produces more accurate and topologically faithful meshes than existing approaches, especially in challenging regions. In its current form, DCx assigns a representative vertex to each active cube by averaging the projected points it contains. Although this strategy is simple, it may compromise the ability to preserve fine details (e.g., as shown in Figure 12, the non-manifold edges reconstructed by DCx are not as sharp as others). As part of future work, we plan to develop a more adaptive vertex computation strategy that takes into account the local distribution of projected points, allowing for better reconstruction of fine details. Overall, we believe that DCx represents a practical step forward in extracting surfaces from neural implicit fields and establishes a solid basis for future work on robust mesh reconstruction in the presence of complex topologies. A Discussion on the Lookup Tables Design Based on the Principles In this section, we present several examples of the lookup table, constructed according to the principles outlined in Section 5, to clarify and illustrate the reasoning behind our lookup table design.
  - Case 4-6. Without introducing anchor points, no configuration satisfies Principle B. Simply connecting the four voxels into a tetrahedron would partition the dual cube into 5 sub-regions. By introducing an anchor point, the third configuration of Case 4-6 (with 6 primitives) satisfies Principle B. However, this introduces multiple non-manifold edges. Following Principle C, we further utilize adjacent case IDs to generate only the necessary primitives, reducing redundant non-manifold structures.
  - Case 6-1. Although additional triangles could theoretically be generated on the left and right sides of the dual cube, we select the configuration with the fewest patches, following Principle C.
  - Case 5-1. An alternative configuration would remove the bottom triangular patch and connect the top two voxels to the bottom-right voxel.
