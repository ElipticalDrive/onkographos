# Refractive Radiative Transfer Equation

MARCO AMENT, CHRISTOPH BERGMANN, and DANIEL WEISKOPF  
University of Stuttgart

We introduce a refractive radiative transfer equation to the graphics community for the physically based rendering of participating media that have a spatially varying index of refraction. We review principles of geometric nonlinear optics that are crucial to discuss a more generic light transport equation. In particular, we present an optical model that has an integral form suitable for rendering. We show rigorously that the continuous bending of light rays leads to a nonlinear scaling of radiance. To obtain physically correct results, we build on the concept of basic radiance—known from discontinuous refraction—to conserve energy in such complex media. Furthermore, the generic model accounts for the reduction in the speed of light due to the index of refraction to render transient effects like the propagation of light echoes. We solve the refractive volume rendering equation by extending photon mapping with transient light transport in a refractive, participating medium. We demonstrate the impact of our approach on the correctness of rendered images of media that are dominated by spatially continuous refraction and multiple scattering. Furthermore, our model enables us to render visual effects like the propagation of light echoes or time-of-flight imagery that cannot be produced with previous approaches.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Global illumination of participating and refractive media exhibits fascinating visual effects due to the intricate interaction of photons with matter. In heterogeneous media, material properties vary spatially due to different physical processes such as advection or diffusion. Hence, in addition to the scattering and absorption coefficient, the index of refraction is also a function of position due to a varying concentration of different materials, or due to changing temperatures. In this case, light rays are bent continuously and propagate along curves according to the ray equation of geometric optics. Previous work on continuous refraction in computer graphics [Stam and Languénou 1996; Gutierrez et al. 2005; Ihrke et al. 2007; Sun et al. 2008; Cao et al. 2010] convincingly visualized the geometric distortion in such media, but without providing a more generic rendering equation.

The main motivation for a refractive volume rendering equation is a mathematical description of light transport independent of a specific algorithm or discretization, such as a piecewise constant approximation with Snell's law. A major issue of continuous refraction is conservation of energy due to the continuously changing solid angle along a curved ray. Therefore, radiance is no longer a conserved quantity in transparent media and the well-known light transfer equations [Chandrasekhar 1960; Kajiya 1986] are no longer valid in this general case. In this article, we discuss a rigorous theory from optics literature to close this gap in rendering. In addition to curved light rays, this unified model also guarantees conservation of energy by employing so-called basic radiance, which previous approaches did not account for. Although basic radiance is a known quantity in the graphics community [Hanrahan and Krueger 1993; Veach 1996] for the rendering of discontinuous refraction based on Snell's law; its application to participating media with continuous refraction and an extended volume rendering equation has not been studied yet.

Well-known real-world phenomena of continuous refraction include mirages and heat shimmerings. The refractive index of many materials depends on the temperature. In a mirage, air of different temperatures causes light rays to bend continuously in the atmosphere [Berger et al. 1990; Musgrave 1990; Gutierrez et al. 2006; Zhao et al. 2007]. Depending on the direction of the temperature gradient, inferior or superior images of the surrounding objects can be observed. Figure 1 shows a superior mirage of an ocean liner due to a thermal inversion layer in the presence of a participating medium. Global illumination effects are combined from continuous refraction and multiple scattering in the clouds. Other important sources of continuous refraction are impure materials due to mass transport or mixture processes. One of the most fundamental transport phenomena in nature is diffusion, which can be observed in gases, liquids, and solids. According to the laws of diffusion, the concentration of a material in steady state is described by a harmonic function; hence, the refractive index also becomes a smooth function in space [Strouse 1972; Ambrosini et al. 1997].

In general, refraction is a result of the varying speed of light in different media. In our everyday life, we perceive that light propagates instantaneously, since the speed of light is far beyond the



Fig. 1. Rendering of a superior mirage on a cloudy day with our extended photon mapping algorithm solving the refractive radiative transfer equation. Due to a thermal inversion layer, light is refracted continuously in the lower atmosphere. The inset shows a magnification of the far-distant ocean liner, which seems to be mirrored in the sky. reaction time scale of our perception. Recently, several Time-Of-Flight (TOF) visualizations [Velten et al. 2011a, 2011b, 2013; Wu et al. 2012a] and applications [Kirmani et al. 2011; Naik et al. 2011; Velten et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012b] have attracted attention in the graphics community to exploit the finite speed of light for the analysis of light echoes. The latter is also an active field of research in astronomy [Bond et al. 2003; Krause et al. 2008]. Stars or supernovae can induce strongly varying bursts of radiation that are reflected by dust in the surroundings of an emitter.

Our primary contribution is the introduction of a single transfer equation from optics to the graphics community that is suitable for rendering of participating and continuously refracting media. For the first time in rendering, we discuss a unified theory from Hamiltonian physics that describes global light transport in such complex media. We provide a thorough mathematical background based on statistical physics and the laws of geometric optics. Thereby, it can be shown formally that the continuous bending of light causes radiance to depend on the spatially varying index of refraction, which is crucial for conservation of energy. For rendering, there are two important implications. First, the conserved quantity is basic radiance. Second, we present a mathematical model for continuous refraction that does not rely on Snell’s law and a piecewise constant approximation of the spatially varying index of refraction. We provide comprehensive details of all mathematical transformations in the appendix for full reproducibility. A direct result of this generic approach is also a description of transient light transport which allows us to render TOF imagery and light echoes.

Our secondary contribution is an extension of photon mapping [Jensen and Christensen 1998] that solves the unified transfer equation. We discuss practical considerations for implementing continuous refraction in a participating medium using basic radiance and higher-order integration without relying on Snell’s law. For efficient rendering, we extend the density estimation method by Jarosz et al. [2008] to nonlinear beams with basic radiance. Furthermore, we show how transient light transport can be solved efficiently with photon mapping by storing additional information about the TOF in the photon map.

## 2. RELATED WORK

Our contributions are related to previous work in physics of light transport, rendering of participating and refractive media, as well as rendering of time-resolved phenomena.

### 2.1 Physics of Light Transport

The Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) [Chandrasekhar 1960] is probably the best-known optical model for physically based rendering of participating media. However, the RTE does not account for a spatially varying index of refraction. Therefore, we review more general optical models from physical optics that we studied to discuss a Refractive Radiative Transfer Equation (RRTE) for rendering.

Based on measure theory and functional analysis, Preisendorfer [1957a, 1957b, 1965] derived a general transfer equation that accounts for the finite speed of light and a heterogeneous distribution of the refractive index. Pomraning [2005] presented a more physically oriented derivation that accounts additionally for an explicit time dependency of the index of refraction. Both derivations require considerable knowledge in theoretical physics and mathematics, which makes it hard to follow all steps for nondomain experts. In an effort to make this topic more accessible to the graphics community, we provide a derivation of the more practical approach of Pomraning in the appendix. However, in contrast to Pomraning [2005, pages 144–153], we focus on a stationary index of refraction and we provide all mathematical transformations including the source and sink terms due to emission, absorption, and scattering. Common to Preisendorfer and Pomraning is the use of basic radiance in the final transfer equation. In subsequent work, several authors [Nicodemus 1963; Harris 1965; Liebes 1969; Lax and Nelson 1975; Aronson 1997] showed independently that basic radiance is, in fact, a fundamental conserved quantity which obeys the laws of geometric optics and thermodynamics.

More recently, alternative formulations of a generic transfer equation have raised attention in the optics literature. An overview is provided by Martí-López et al. [2006], who discuss the different assumptions and issues concerning conservation of energy of four recently published formulations [Tualle and Tinet 2003; Khan and Jiang 2003; Martí-López et al. 2003; Premaratne et al. 2005]. Common to all these approaches is that they do not employ basic radiance, leading to complex expressions in their transfer equations, which makes it hard to find an integral solution suitable for rendering. Furthermore, none of these approaches relates its work to Preisendorfer, Pomraning, or the work on basic radiance.

### 2.2 Rendering of Participating Media

Physically based rendering of participating media [Blinn 1982; Kajiya and Von Herzen 1984] relies on the RTE. The search for solutions of the RTE has led to a large number of different rendering techniques. In the following, we focus on related work that is most relevant to our method. For a more detailed overview, especially for methods other than photon mapping, we refer to the surveys by Cerezo et al. [2005] and by Jönsson et al. [2012].

Jensen and Christensen [1998] introduced photon mapping for participating media by emitting flux particles (virtual photons) from the light sources and by storing them in a map at scattering locations. For final rendering, ray marching gathers radiance by estimating the density of these virtual photons. For a comprehensive overview of photon mapping, we refer the reader to the textbook by Jensen [2005]. Jarosz et al. [2008] developed a radiance estimator that replaces traditional point queries with beam queries to obtain

high-quality images with fewer samples. More recently, Jarosz et al. [2011] have provided a comprehensive overview of radiance estimation techniques in volumetric photon mapping. In general, the estimation of radiance introduces bias to the final image, but in many cases this is preferable to the slow convergence of unbiased methods like volumetric path tracing [Rushmeier 1988; Lafortune and Willems 1996] or Metropolis rendering [Pauly et al. 2000].

### 2.3 Rendering of Refractive Media

Early techniques were able to approximate heterogeneous distributions of refractive indices by repeated application of Snell's law to render atmospheric effects like mirages [Berger et al. 1990; Musgrave 1990]. Discontinuous refraction effects were also achieved in real time on the GPU [Wyman 2005; Wyman and Davis 2006; Oliveira and Brauwers 2007; Davis and Wyman 2007; de Rousiers et al. 2011]. Walter et al. [2009] have presented a technique to quickly compute paths that connect points inside and outside a medium. However, these approaches optimize for rendering speed, whereas we focus on an accurate rendering model.

In general, the bending of light can be simulated with nonlinear ray tracing. Early work by Gröller [1995] describes a generic approach to nonlinear ray tracing for visualizing mathematical and physical systems. Later, Weiskopf et al. [2004] developed a GPU-based nonlinear ray tracer primarily for relativistic visualization.

In media with spatially varying refraction, the geometric bending of light is described by the solution of the ray equation of geometric optics [Born and Wolf 1999]. Stam and Languénou [1996] employed the eikonal equation to render heat shimmering in air with nonconstant refractive indices. Global illumination of participating and continuously refracting media was addressed by Gutierrez et al. [2005], who extended photon mapping with inelastic scattering and curved light paths, for example, for the simulation of atmospheric effects [Gutierrez et al. 2006]. However, the approach was not aware of a refractive transfer equation and did not employ basic radiance to conserve energy. With our contribution, we show the importance of basic radiance to obtain physically accurate results. Ihrke et al. [2007] achieved real-time frame rates with wavefront tracking based on the eikonal equation. Energy conservation was achieved by obeying the intensity law of geometric optics on a mesh. However, light transport was simplified to single scattering. Sun et al. [2008] presented an octree decomposition of the refractive index field for adaptive ray marching to accelerate rendering, but also limited to single scattering. Cao et al. [2010] presented an analytical solution of the ray equation of geometric optics by assuming a constant gradient of the index of refraction, but energy conservation was not discussed and illumination did not include participating media. However, we employ their analytical solution to validate our implementation for this special case.

### 2.4 Time-Aware Rendering

Probably, the most typical application of time-aware rendering in computer graphics is motion blur [Cook et al. 1984], simulating real cameras with finite exposure times. Cammarano and Jensen [2002] extended photon mapping to implement motion blur in scenes with moving objects. Although these methods are aware of time, they do not solve transient light transport, that is, the temporal change of radiance due to the finiteness of the speed of light.

The latter is a central aspect of special relativistic visualization [Hsiung et al. 1990; Weiskopf et al. 1999; Müller et al. 2010], but these methods do not solve global illumination. Transient global light transport was briefly discussed by Arvo [1993], who added the explicit time dependency and a temporal derivative to the classic

rendering equation. Later, Smith et al. [2008] presented a computer-vision-oriented model in operator form for TOF applications. Recently, Jarabo [2012] demonstrated how transient light transport can be solved with photon mapping and time-dependent density estimation [Cammarano and Jensen 2002] for surface-based rendering. However, neither of these approaches accounted for participating or even continuously refracting media.

Transient light transport can be exploited to infer hidden geometry [Kirmani et al. 2011; Velten et al. 2012] as well as its motion [Pandharkar et al. 2011], or to rapidly acquire reflectance properties of objects from a single viewpoint with a TOF camera [Naik et al. 2011]. Wu et al. [2012b] analyzed transient light transport in frequency space for designing a bare sensor imaging system. Time-resolved imaging can also be employed to separate direct light, subsurface scattering, and interreflections in a scene [Wu et al. 2012a]. Recently, Velten et al. [2013] presented an imaging technique to capture and visualize the propagation of short light pulses in macroscopic scenes with an effective temporal resolution of less than 2 picoseconds per frame. Heide et al. [2013] employed photonic mixer devices as an alternative instrumentation for transient imaging instead of expensive custom hardware like femtosecond lasers and streak cameras. Furthermore, light echoes in interstellar dust clouds [Bond et al. 2003; Patat 2005; Rest et al. 2011] belong to some of the most visually compelling phenomena in nature that cannot be rendered without transient light transport. However, to the best of our knowledge there exists no previous technique that accurately renders TOF or light echo effects in complex media.

## 3. PHYSICS BACKGROUND

Before we discuss the equation of transfer for a refractive, participating medium as illustrated in Figure 2, we briefly review some background of physics. In this way, this section provides the most important steps and ideas to establish a mathematical approach to the problem. At the end of this section, we provide an Ansatz that already describes global light transport, but that requires several transformations to obtain a transfer equation suitable for rendering. For interested readers, we provide all intermediate mathematical steps in the appendix. However, the subsequent sections can be followed without studying all the details of the appendix.

In the following, we rely on Hamiltonian mechanics, which describes the evolution of a conservative system in phase space with a set of first-order differential equations, namely the Hamilton equations [Goldstein et al. 2001]. We first provide a brief overview of the most important principles of Hamiltonian mechanics, before we discuss how the state of photons can be described in phase space and how the motion of photons can be formulated in terms of phase space variables.

### 3.1 Liouville's Theorem

In general, the phase space describes all possible states of a dynamical system, where each point in phase space corresponds to a unique state. In Hamiltonian mechanics, the phase space consists of canonical coordinates  $p_i$  for position and  $q_i$  for momentum. For systems with a high number of points in phase space, it is convenient to employ a distribution function  $f(p_1, \dots, p_N, q_1, \dots, q_N, t)$ , which describes the number of points per unit volume in phase space of a system with  $N$  degrees of freedom. For conservative systems, Liouville's theorem [Liouville 1838; Tolman 2010] states that the phase space density  $f$  remains constant over time:

$$\frac{df}{dt} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \frac{\partial (f\dot{q}_i)}{\partial q_i} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{p}_i)}{\partial p_i} \right)}_{\text{continuity equation}} = 0, \quad (1)$$

where the dot denotes the time derivative. Eq. (1) is a continuity equation in phase space with  $f$  being the conserved quantity.

In the following, we assume that energy is carried by massless photons, which are discrete wave packets that propagate with group velocity  $v_g$  in a medium. In vacuum, the group velocity equals the speed of light  $c$  and the photon's energy is  $W = h\nu$ , where  $h$  is Planck's constant and  $\nu$  is the photon's frequency. The phase space of a photon consists of three dimensions for its spatial location and three dimensions for its momentum. The momentum is composed of its magnitude  $W/c$  and its direction of travel. Since  $c$  and  $h$  are constants, the magnitude of the momentum only depends on the frequency. Therefore, the canonical coordinates in Eq. (1) can be replaced with

$$(p_1, p_2, p_3) \rightarrow (x, y, z), \quad (2)$$

$$(q_1, q_2, q_3) \rightarrow (\phi, \mu, \nu). \quad (3)$$

The state of a photon at time  $t$  is determined by  $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z)$  for its spatial location,  $(\phi, \mu)$  for its direction of propagation, and  $\nu$  for its frequency, which is proportional to the photon's energy. In rendering, it is common practice to describe the direction of propagation in spherical coordinates with a radial unit vector  $\boldsymbol{\omega} = (\phi, \theta, r = 1)$  and it is convenient to define  $\mu := \cos \theta$  for the following sections. Hence, a system of photons can be described with a phase space density distribution function:

$$f(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, \nu, t) = [m^{-3} sr^{-1} Hz^{-1}]. \quad (4)$$

However, in rendering, the preferred quantity to describe radiation is (spectral) radiance  $L$ . Therefore, it is necessary to substitute the distribution function in the subsequent sections:

$$L(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, \nu, t) = v_g h\nu f(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, \nu, t) = [W m^{-2} sr^{-1} Hz^{-1}]. \quad (5)$$

The important relationship between radiance and the distribution function in Eq. (5) is discussed in more detail by Arvo [1993] or Pomraning [2005], for example. For our extended transfer equation and for its integral solution, we require a compact description of the motion of photons that we discuss next.

### 3.2 Hamiltonian Optics

Since our objective is a transfer equation suitable for rendering, we seek a formulation of light transport based on geometric optics, that is, we do not account for effects of wave optics like diffraction, interference, or polarization. Furthermore, we assume elastic scattering in a participating medium, hence fluorescence and phosphorescence are not part of our discussion. In geometric optics, it is assumed that



Fig. 3. Light transport along a curved line element  $ds$ . Due to a participating medium, radiance can be reduced by out-scattering and absorption (red) or increased by in-scattering and emission (blue). With our contribution, we show that radiance also varies due to a spatially varying index of refraction.

the wavelength of light is very small compared to the scale of interest. Under these circumstances, light transfer can be described by a linear superposition of discrete wave packets (photons) that propagate along (possibly curved) rays with group velocity  $v_g$ . We rely on the formulation of Hamiltonian optics to describe light propagation along a curved line element  $ds$  as illustrated in Figure 3. A derivation of the following equations is beyond the scope of this work and we refer the reader to literature on optics [Harris 1965; Born and Wolf 1999; Pomraning 2005]:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} = \boldsymbol{\omega}, \quad (6)$$

$$\frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} = \frac{1}{n} (\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n - (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n) \boldsymbol{\omega}), \quad (7)$$

$$\frac{d\nu}{ds} = -\frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = 0, \quad (8)$$

$$\frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{c}{n + \nu \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu}} =: v_g, \quad (9)$$

where  $n = n(\mathbf{x}, \nu, t)$  is the index of refraction, equality with an exclamation mark means that we make an assumption, and equality with a colon means that we define a variable. Some of these equations are commonly found in rendering literature, for example, Eq. (6) appears in the standard Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) [Chandrasekhar 1960] as part of the directional derivative. A different formulation of Eq. (7) was employed to simulate continuous bending of light in refractive media [Gutierrez et al. 2005; Ihrke et al. 2007; Sun et al. 2008; Cao et al. 2010]. In Eq. (8), we make the assumption that the index of refraction is quasi-stationary, that is, the refractive properties of the medium vary slowly compared to the speed of light or remain even constant, which is a reasonable assumption for rendering purposes. Eq. (9) accounts for varying group velocities, which we define as  $v_g$ , in a refractive and dispersive medium.

### 3.3 Ansatz

We begin with the continuity Eq. (1) and replace the canonical coordinates according to Eqs. (2) and (3). In participating media, photons can be emitted, absorbed, and scattered, which introduces an inhomogeneous term  $\boldsymbol{Q} = \boldsymbol{Q}(f)$  on the right-hand side of Eq. (1). Together, we obtain the following Ansatz:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{x})}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{y})}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{z})}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{\phi})}{\partial \phi} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{\mu})}{\partial \mu} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{\nu})}{\partial \nu} = \boldsymbol{Q}, \quad (10)$$

In principle, Eq. (10) is already a complete description of global light transport in participating and refracting media. The basic recipe for obtaining a more practical transfer equation based on radiance is to substitute Eq. (5) and Eqs. (6)–(9). However, this requires a significant amount of transformations and we refer the interested reader to the appendix for reproducibility.

## 4. REFRACTIVE RADIATIVE TRANSFER EQUATION

By employing common notations for the absorption coefficient  $\sigma_a = \sigma_a(\mathbf{x}, v, t)$ , scattering coefficient  $\sigma_s = \sigma_s(\mathbf{x}, v, t)$ , and the phase function  $P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega})$ , we now present a suitable formulation of global light transport, following the results of Preisendorfer [1957a] and Pomraning [2005]. From Eq. (10) and the steps of Appendices A–F, the differential form of the RRTE can be obtained and summarized in the following theorem.

THEOREM 1. In a refractive and participating medium with an index of refraction  $n = n(\mathbf{x}, v)$  that varies continuously with position  $\mathbf{x}$  and frequency  $v$ , radiative transport is described by

$$\frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} = \sigma_a \tilde{L}_e - \sigma_t \tilde{L} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \tilde{L} d\boldsymbol{\omega}', \quad (11)$$

where  $\tilde{L} = \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, v, t)$  is called basic radiance and  $\tilde{L}_e = \tilde{L}_e(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, v, t)$  emissive basic radiance.

$$\tilde{L} = \frac{L}{n^2} \quad (12)$$

The total derivative of basic radiance  $\tilde{L}$  along a curved line element  $ds$  is

$$\frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} = \frac{dt}{ds} \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t} + \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \cdot \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}}. \quad (13)$$

The major advantage of Eq. (11) compared to other possible formulations of light transport [Martf-López et al. 2006] is that it employs basic radiance as the conserved quantity. In this way, Eq. (11) also obeys previous work in geometric optics and thermodynamics [Nicodemus 1963; Harris 1965; Liebes 1969; Lax and Nelson 1975; Aronson 1997] and it closely resembles the standard RTE [Chandrasekhar 1960] for nonrefractive media. Therefore, an integral solution can be obtained easily and common Monte-Carlo rendering techniques can be adapted with ease.

## 5. DISCUSSION

In this section, we discuss the differences between RRTE and standard RTE. Furthermore, we provide interesting connections to previous rendering algorithms that convincingly simulated refraction of light, albeit in a less general manner. As a consequence, we explain why our approach is indispensable to obtain physically correct results in the general sense.

### 5.1 Basic Radiance

The most noticeable difference between RRTE and RTE is the use of a different radiation quantity  $\tilde{L}$ , instead of  $L$ , but since the index of refraction is dimensionless, the measurement unit of  $\tilde{L}$  is the same as of  $L$ . In fact,  $\tilde{L}$  is called basic radiance and is a well-known quantity in optics literature [Nicodemus 1963, 1976; Liebes 1969; Lax and Nelson 1975; Aronson 1997]. Basic radiance was previously employed in computer graphics by Hanrahan and Krueger [1993] in the context of subsurface scattering and by Veach [1996] to correctly render caustics when light rays are refracted at material boundaries, as shown in Figure 4. By exploiting Snell's law and the conservation of energy, it can be shown that radiance is scaled according to

$$L_1 = \frac{n_1^2}{n_0^2} L_0. \quad (14)$$



Fig. 4. Illustration of light transport in two transparent media with different, but constant, indices of refraction  $n_1 < n_0$ . At the boundary  $dA$  of the two media, a nonsymmetric BTDF is evaluated and the observer receives scaled radiance  $L_1 < L_0$ .

An intuitive explanation of this phenomenon is that flux density changes when light enters a medium with another index of refraction. For the example, in Figure 4, it is assumed that  $n_1 < n_0$ . The energy from the lower region that passes through the area  $dA$  is dispersed into a larger solid angle in the upper region; hence, radiance decreases due to conservation of energy. To account for the change of radiance at such discontinuous material boundaries, Veach [1996] employed a nonsymmetric BTDF. This technique works well for discontinuous interfaces between two different homogeneous media as in Figure 4, where refraction only occurs at discrete locations that coincide with scattering locations.

However, this approach is not applicable for continuously refracting media as in our case. In general, it would not be possible to model continuous bending of light with a nonsymmetric phase function. In contrast, the RRTE describes light transport with a unified model and thereby generalizes the previously discussed approach to continuous refraction and anisotropic scattering. In fact, it is shown easily that discrete refraction at a boundary layer is approximated with a special case of the RRTE:

$$\frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} \approx \frac{\Delta \tilde{L}}{\Delta s} = \frac{\tilde{L}_1 - \tilde{L}_0}{\Delta s} = \frac{\frac{L_1}{n_1^2} - \frac{L_0}{n_0^2}}{\Delta s} \stackrel{(14)}{=} 0. \quad (15)$$

Eq. (15) states that basic radiance  $\tilde{L}$  remains constant when a ray crosses a material boundary. In addition, Eq. (15) is a special case of the RRTE by setting  $\sigma_a = \sigma_s = 0$ , namely a transparent medium. In general, the total derivative describes the change of basic radiance along any differential path element  $ds$ , which is, in this case, a straight line element, except at the material boundary. We note that true discontinuous refraction can only be approximated with the RRTE, since the gradient of  $n$  in Eq. (7) is not defined at the boundary. However, in this case, an evaluation of Snell's law can be employed. The transition from a continuous index of refraction to a discontinuous interface, which results in Snell's law, can be proved formally with Stokes' theorem [Born and Wolf 1999].

### 5.2 Streaming of Basic Radiance

The second major difference between RRTE and RTE is the total derivative of basic radiance and radiance, respectively. According to Eq. (13), the total derivative in the RRTE is

$$\underbrace{\frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds}}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\frac{dt}{ds} \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t}}_{\text{temporal}} + \underbrace{\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \tilde{L}}_{\text{directional}} + \underbrace{\frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \tilde{L}}_{\text{angular}}. \quad (16)$$

The total change of basic radiance along a differential line element  $ds$  is composed of temporal, directional, and angular contributions.

The directional derivative is known from the RTE, where it describes the change of radiance in a certain direction. In nonrefractive media this direction does not change, since rays propagate along straight lines: formally, the gradient of the index of refraction is zero in Eq. (7), in which case the angular term is zero in Eq. (16), too. Furthermore, the RTE assumes that temporal changes of radiance propagate instantaneously, which is equivalent to assuming that the group velocity  $v_g$  is infinite in Eq. (9). In this case, the temporal derivative in Eq. (16) vanishes and we obtain the common RTE, except for the formulation with basic radiance. We can now see that the RTE with radiance is only valid in media with a constant index of refraction; however, many participating media that are commonly rendered in computer graphics, like frosted glass, jade, amber, or wax, have varying refractive indices  $1 < n < 2$  [Lide 2007], depending on the level of purity or on the temperature of a material.

The physical interpretation of Eq. (16) is closely related to the partially coupled Eqs. (6)–(9). They describe the motion of a massless point-like particle that does not have any extent or explicit orientation. This means that a change of direction, according to Eq. (7), is always coupled to a change of position. This can be seen by substituting Eq. (6) into Eq. (7):

$$\frac{d}{ds} \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right) = \frac{d^2\mathbf{x}}{ds^2} = \frac{1}{n} (\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n - (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n) \boldsymbol{\omega}) \neq 0. \quad (17)$$

Since the second derivative with respect to the path length is required to be nonzero for a change of direction in Eq. (17), it follows that the first derivative must be nonzero, too; hence, the position of the particle must change. In contrast, a change of position does not necessarily implicate a change of direction, that is, when particles travel along straight lines. The temporal derivative in Eq. (16) accounts for the fact that any change of position or direction of a particle is coupled to its traveling speed in a refractive medium, namely to Eq. (9).

This allows one to reinterpret the total derivative in Eq. (16) as a Lagrangian derivative with respect to time by multiplying both sides of the RRTE with  $v_g = ds/dt$  and by applying the chain rule:

$$\frac{D\tilde{L}}{Dt} := \frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} \frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t} + \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \tilde{L} + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{dt} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \tilde{L}. \quad (18)$$

This formulation of light transport is similar to other time-dependent transport phenomena such as advection in fluid dynamics. The Lagrangian frame of reference is often advantageous because many rendering algorithms, especially Monte-Carlo techniques, solve radiative transfer directly in this frame via particle or ray tracing, such as photon mapping or path tracing. A parametrization of the particle trajectories or the light rays with respect to time facilitates the handling of transient radiative transfer; hence, we later employ this description to derive a formal solution of the transient RRTE. In summary, the total derivative in Eq. (16) describes the variation of basic radiance along the potentially curved trajectory of a particle that travels with a spatially varying group velocity due to inhomogeneous refractive indices.

### 5.3 Continuous Refraction

A significant advantage of RRTE compared to RTE is the comprehensive description of continuous refraction in terms of geometry and basic radiance. The geometric shape of refracted light rays can



Fig. 5. Illustration of the intensity law of geometric optics. (a) The product of irradiance and differential area remains constant along a tube of rays; (b) discretization of the wavefront area with a quad mesh.

be calculated by substituting Eq. (6) into Eq. (7):

$$\frac{d^2\mathbf{x}}{ds^2} = \frac{1}{n} \left( \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n - \frac{dn}{ds} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right) \quad (19)$$

$$\Leftrightarrow \frac{d}{ds} \left( n \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n. \quad (20)$$

Eq. (20) is the ray equation of geometric optics [Born and Wolf 1999] and was employed in several previous publications to calculate the bending of light rays for rendering objects with continuous refraction [Stam and Languénou 1996; Gutierrez et al. 2005; Ihrke et al. 2007; Sun et al. 2008; Cao et al. 2010]. While the solution of Eq. (20) describes the geometric effects of continuous refraction properly, it does not guarantee conservation of energy. In principle, the conservation of energy can be assured by obeying the intensity law of geometric optics [Born and Wolf 1999]:

$$E_0 dA_0 = E_1 dA_1, \quad (21)$$

where  $E_0$  and  $E_1$  denote irradiance values at both ends of a narrow tube formed by all rays proceeding from an area element  $dA_0$  to  $dA_1$  as shown in Figure 5(a). The area patches denote the wavefronts of propagating light, which comprise the solution of the eikonal equation. However, this formulation is not well suited for an energy-conserving transfer equation based on radiance such as the RRTE. As a consequence, many established rendering algorithms like path tracing or photon mapping could not be employed without significant redesign. In fact, to incorporate Eq. (21) into light transport, beam tracing [Heckbert and Hanrahan 1984; Watt 1990] or wavefront tracing [Ihrke et al. 2007] with a geometric mesh are mandatory to keep track of the continuously varying area patches as illustrated in Figure 5(b). This approach only works well as long as the wavefronts propagate without much hindrance. However, in participating media with multiple scattering, very complex meshes would be necessary, since at each scattering event the energy is distributed radially in all directions according to the phase function.

## 6. FORMAL SOLUTION

Since the RRTE is similar to the RTE, many principles can be reused to find a formal solution. Therefore, we focus only on the most important differences and we refer the reader to Arvo [1993] for the common steps. The most relevant difference is that rays propagate along curved rays, which requires the formal solution of Eq. (20). Furthermore, the curved trajectories also affect the required exponential factor to derive a formal solution due to the substitution theorem of integration. In the following, we distinguish between the steady-state (nontransient) and the transient RRTE, since, in many cases, it is not necessary to account for the explicit time dependency.

## 6.1 Steady-State RRTE

In the steady-state case, the time derivative in Eq. (13) vanishes and the solution does not depend explicitly on time. In the first step, we define a function  $\mathbf{x}(s)$  that yields the position on a curved ray depending on the arc length  $s$  and we obtain  $\mathbf{x}(s)$  by formally solving Eqs. (6) and (7). In Eq. (20), we have seen that this is equivalent to finding the solution of the ray equation of geometric optics:

$$\underbrace{\frac{d}{ds} \left( n \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right)}_{=: \mathbf{v}(s)} = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n. \quad (22)$$

With the definition of  $\mathbf{v}(s)$ , we formally integrate Eq. (22), twice:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{ds} = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{v}(s) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \int_{s_0}^s \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n \, ds', \quad (23)$$

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{n} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{x}(s) = \mathbf{x}_0 + \int_{s_0}^s \frac{\mathbf{v}(s')}{n} \, ds', \quad (24)$$

with initial conditions  $\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{v}(s_0)$  and  $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}(s_0)$ . In the second step, we require an exponential factor [Arvo 1993] in form of the transmittance, similar to the standard RTE, to find an integral solution. According to the substitution theorem, we obtain

$$\tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1) = \exp \left( - \int_{s_0}^{s_1} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}(s')) \left\| \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds'} \right\| ds' \right). \quad (25)$$

For straight rays in the form  $\mathbf{x}(s') = \mathbf{x}_0 + s'\omega$  with unit vector  $\omega$ , it is guaranteed that  $\|d\mathbf{x}/ds'\| = 1$  in Eq. (25). However, for curved rays, described by Eq. (24), a closed-form solution of Eq. (23) would be required to compute  $\|d\mathbf{x}/ds'\|$ . Nonetheless, according to Born and Wolf [1999] and Ihrke et al. [2007], we can exploit the following important relationship between the curved trajectory and the eikonal function  $S$ :

$$\omega(s) = \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} = \frac{\nabla S}{|\nabla S|} \Rightarrow \left\| \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right\| = 1. \quad (26)$$

Following common practice, we define the basic source term, which describes how much basic radiance is emitted or in-scattered at point  $\mathbf{x}$  in direction  $\omega$ 

$$\tilde{J}(\mathbf{x}, \omega) := (1 - \Lambda) \tilde{L}_e(\mathbf{x}, \omega) + \underbrace{\frac{\Lambda}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i) P(\omega_i, \omega) \, d\omega_i}_{=: \tilde{L}_i(\mathbf{x}, \omega)}, \quad (27)$$

where  $\Lambda = \sigma_s/\sigma_t$  is the albedo and  $\tilde{L}_i$  denotes the in-scattered basic radiance. Similar to the RTE, we observe that the RRTE is a linear first-order ordinary differential equation with variable coefficients, but in terms of basic radiance:

$$\frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} + \sigma_t \tilde{L} = \sigma_t \tilde{J}. \quad (28)$$

Similar to Arvo [1993], we employ the exponential factor of Eq. (25) to integrate both sides of Eq. (28), but along a curved path from  $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}(s_0)$  to  $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}(s)$ . We get the following integral solution of the steady-state RRTE

$$\tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \omega) = \tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}) \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}_0, \omega'(\mathbf{x}_0)) + \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \tau(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \sigma_t(\mathbf{u}) \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}, \omega'(\mathbf{u})) \, d\mathbf{u}, \quad (29)$$

where  $\omega'(\mathbf{u})$  yields the direction of propagation at point  $\mathbf{u}$  on the curved path similar to Eq. (26). Similar to the formal solution of

the RTE, the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (29) is the reduced basic radiance from the boundary condition, whereas the second term represents the contribution along the curved path due to emission, absorption, and scattering in the continuously refracting medium, which is also illustrated in Figure 2.

## 6.2 Transient RRTE

For the formal solution of the transient RRTE, the arc-length parameter  $s$  becomes a function of light propagation time and we redefine the function  $\mathbf{x}(s(t))$ . We employ Eq. (9) to get an expression for the differential line element in terms of light propagation time  $ds = v_g \, dt$  and that we substitute into Eq. (22). Similar to the steady-state case, we solve for  $\mathbf{x}$  with initial conditions  $\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{v}(s(t_0))$  and  $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}(s(t_0))$ . Furthermore, we rewrite the transmittance of Eq. (25) in terms of light propagation time and we employ again the substitution theorem

$$\tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1) = \exp \left( - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}(s(t'))) \left\| \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right\| \frac{ds}{dt'} \, dt' \right) \quad (30)$$

$$= \exp \left( - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}(s(t'))) v_g \, dt' \right), \quad (31)$$

with  $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}(s(t_0))$  and  $\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}(s(t_1))$ . Compared to the steady-state case, the group velocity  $v_g$  is added, which is crucial to obtain the subsequent formal solution. The basic source term of Eq. (27) can be reused for the transient equation, except that explicit time dependency is required in all radiance terms. We reformulate the RRTE in terms of light propagation time and we employ the Lagrangian derivative from Eq. (18) by multiplying both sides of Eq. (28) with  $v_g = ds/dt$ :

$$\frac{D\tilde{L}}{Dt} + \sigma_t v_g \tilde{L} = \sigma_t v_g \tilde{J}. \quad (32)$$

Similar to the steady-state equation, we get the following integral solution of the transient RRTE along a curved path from  $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}(s(t_0))$  to  $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}(s(t))$  with group velocity  $v_g$ :

$$\tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \omega, t) = \tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}) \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}_0, \omega'(\mathbf{x}_0), t_0) + \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \tau(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \sigma_t(\mathbf{u}) v_g(\mathbf{u}) \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}, \omega'(\mathbf{u}), t'(\mathbf{u})) \, d\mathbf{u}, \quad (33)$$

where  $t'(\mathbf{u})$  is a function that yields the time at point  $\mathbf{u}$  on the curved path. It can be observed that many principles from the steady-state equation can be reused and adapted by accounting for the finite and spatially varying group velocity.

## 7. RENDERING

A formal solution of light transport in terms of basic radiance is advantageous for many existing rendering algorithms, even if transient effects are not considered. In participating media with spatially varying refractive indices, radiance varies continuously along a curved ray due to refraction, in addition to emission, scattering, and absorption. However, with basic radiance, the different interactions of light with the medium can be discriminated from each other, while still retaining a unified model with conservation of energy. Instead of continuously adapting radiance due to refraction, for example, by redesigning existing algorithms to obey the intensity law of geometric optics in highly scattering media, we can solve light transport in terms of basic radiance and reuse a large collection of established rendering techniques for participating media with only very small adjustments. In the following, we provide details of an

extended photon mapping algorithm that we devised to solve the RRTE. Although the RRTE could be solved with path tracing as well, the curved trajectories do not allow us to send shadow rays, since two points in space cannot be connected anymore [Gutierrez et al. 2005], leading to very slow convergence.

## 7.1 Photon Mapping

Photon mapping is a two-pass rendering algorithm. In the first pass, flux particles are emitted from the light sources and are traced through the scene with a random walk. At each scattering event, these virtual photons are stored in a spatial data structure. In the second pass, ray marching directly solves the integral form of the transfer equation by gathering radiance with a density estimation of the virtual photons. We can exploit this observation and adapt photon mapping to solve the integral form of the RRTE. Although photon mapping was previously employed to simulate the bending of light in participating media [Gutierrez et al. 2005], the latter approach did not account for transient effects and was not aware of basic radiance. Especially, the latter is crucial to conserve energy and to obtain physically correct results, which we show in Section 9 with multiple comparisons. In the following, we assume that the volumetric distributions  $\sigma_s$ ,  $\sigma_i$  and the refractive index  $n$  of the medium as well as the light source positions are quasi-static compared to the speed of light.

7.1.1 Steady-State Photon Tracing. In the first pass, virtual photons are emitted from all light sources, similar to standard photon mapping. However, the trajectory of each virtual photon is described by Eqs. (6) and (7), which we employed to derive the RRTE. In Section 6, we obtained a formal solution in Eq. (24) for the steady-state case. For the curved path  $\mathbf{x}(s)$ , we compute a solution with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method. While a virtual photon propagates without interaction through the refractive, participating medium, energy is conserved. Intuitively, virtual photons do not gather radiance but carry flux, on which the index of refraction has no influence and the stage of photon tracing can be considered as the evolution of a dynamical system with phase space density  $f$  that we employed earlier in Section 3. The interaction of a photon with the participating medium is solved with a Markov random walk. For heterogeneous media, the inversion method [Pharr and Humphreys 2004] or Woodcock tracking [Szirmay-Kalos et al. 2011] can be employed to compute the free path distance between two interaction events along a curved path by importance sampling the transmittance term with an exponential distribution. At an interaction location, the photon is stored in the photon map and Russian roulette [Arvo and Kirk 1990] is used to decide if a photon is absorbed or scattered. Since we extend the beam radiance estimation method by Jarosz et al. [2008], we also assign a radius to each photon and we create a bounding box hierarchy over the photon discs for efficient rendering.

7.1.2 Transient Photon Tracing. We account for the finite speed of light by solving Eq. (22) numerically in terms of light propagation time with step size  $\Delta t$  and a spatially varying group velocity  $v_g(\mathbf{x}) = c/n(\mathbf{x})$  in a nondispersive medium. We solve for  $\mathbf{v}(t)$  and  $\mathbf{x}(t)$  numerically, similar to the steady-state case, but with step size  $\Delta s(\mathbf{x}) = v_g(\mathbf{x})\Delta t$  that depends on the local group velocity. Since we assume a quasi-static medium, we can simplify transient light transport. Instead of emitting and tracing photons continuously over time, we compute the spatial distribution of all photons once and adapt only their flux in each time step of the rendering process. Therefore, we store the TOF of each photon in the photon map

instead of absolute time values. The TOF is obtained by accumulating the time steps  $\Delta t$  from the numerical integration.

Furthermore, the  $N$  photons cannot carry flux  $\Phi/N$  anymore as in steady-state photon mapping, since the flux  $\Phi(t)$  now depends on absolute time. Instead, each photon is emitted with a weight of  $1/N$  and we reconstruct the flux of each photon by multiplying its weight with the dynamically queried flux during rendering. In addition to the TOF, we store a unique identifier for the light source that emitted the photon.

7.1.3 Scattering. The main reason why we chose photon mapping as our rendering method is the straightforward handling of single-scattered light. In nonrefractive media, the photon map is not required to solve the single scattering contribution, since straight shadow rays can be traced to sample each light source; hence, photons are only stored in the photon map after at least two bounces. However, in continuously refractive media, this is not possible since there is no general solution to finding a curved path connecting two arbitrary points in space. By also storing photons after the first bounce, the single scattering contribution can be solved with a radiance estimation from the photon map as well. The drawback of this approach is that the necessary amount of photons to obtain high-quality results increases.

In general, scattering of any order is described by the basic source term in Eq. (27), which includes the estimation of basic radiance  $\tilde{L}_i$  that is in-scattered at point  $\mathbf{x}$  in direction  $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ . For consistency, we provide a full derivation of our basic radiance estimator by employing the distribution function  $f$  from Section 3 and by regarding all dependencies:

$$\tilde{L}_i(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, v, t) = \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, v, t) P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \quad (34)$$

$$= \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi n^2} \int_{\Omega} v_g h v f P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \quad (35)$$

$$= \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi n^2} \int_{\Omega} \frac{hv}{\sigma_s} v_g \sigma_s f P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \quad (36)$$

$$= \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi n^2} \int_{\Omega} \frac{1}{\sigma_s} \frac{d^4 W}{d\mathbf{x} d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i dv} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \quad (37)$$

$$= \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi n^2} \int_{\Omega} \frac{1}{\sigma_s} \frac{d^3 \Phi}{d\mathbf{x} d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i dv} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \quad (38)$$

$$= \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi n^2} \int_{\Omega} L(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, v, t) P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \quad (39)$$

$$=: \frac{1}{n^2} L_i(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, v, t). \quad (40)$$

In transformation (35), we substitute the definition of basic radiance and Eq. (5) that we used previously in our derivation. Subsequently, we expand with  $\sigma_s$  and exploit the fact that a photon carries energy  $W = hv$  and that the distribution function denotes a density  $d^3/(d\mathbf{x} d\boldsymbol{\omega} dv)$ . By substituting the flux  $\Phi = dW/dt$ , we can reproduce the well-known relationship between scattered flux and radiance in a participating medium [Jensen and Christensen 1998].

7.1.4 Steady-State Rendering. In the second pass, basic radiance is gathered with nonlinear ray tracing that iteratively estimates and attenuates basic radiance along the curved ray. Therefore, we extend the beam radiance estimation method by Jarosz et al. [2008] with basic radiance and with curved beams in heterogeneous media as shown in Figure 6. In the first step, the trajectory of each curved beam is computed with an RK4 solver and the node positions are stored in a list. At the same time, we compute the transmittance of Eq. (25) with the composite Simpson's rule [Atkinson 1989] and



Fig. 6. Nonlinear beam estimation of basic radiance. The green nodes are obtained by numerical integration. At each node, the transmittance values are computed and stored in a list. Basic radiance is estimated by looping over all nearby photons, shown in red, and by computing their contribution with a smoothing function according to their distance to the beam.

we store these values with the nodes in the list. In the second step, basic radiance is estimated along the curved beam. However, in contrast to the pointwise density estimation method by Jensen and Christensen [1998], we compute basic radiance due to in-scattering for the entire curved beam in Eq. (29):

$$\tilde{L}_b(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}) := \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \tau(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \sigma_t(\mathbf{u}) \tilde{L}_t(\mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{u})) d\mathbf{u} \quad (41)$$

$$= \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \tau(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \frac{\sigma_t(\mathbf{u})}{n(\mathbf{u})^2} L_t(\mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{u})) d\mathbf{u}, \quad (42)$$

where we substituted the in-scattering term of Eq. (40). In Eq. (42), we can see that the spatially varying nonlinear term  $n(\mathbf{u})^{-2}$  is integrated along the curved beam in contrast to the standard radiance computation. The beam basic radiance of Eq. (42) is then approximated by looping over the  $k$  photons that contribute to the curved beam according to their radius  $r_j$  and their distance  $d_j$  to the beam:

$$\tilde{L}_b(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}) \approx \sum_{j=1}^k \tau(\mathbf{u}_j, \mathbf{x}) \frac{\sigma_t(\mathbf{u}_j)}{n(\mathbf{u}_j)^2} K(r_j, d_j) \Delta \Phi_j P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_j, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{u}_j)), \quad (43)$$

where  $\mathbf{u}_j$  is the projected location of the  $j$ -th photon onto the closest curve segment and  $\Delta \Phi_j$  is the photon's weight. We follow Jarosz et al. [2008] and employ the same smoothing function  $K$ . The transmittance values  $\tau(\mathbf{u}_j, \mathbf{x})$  can be looked up and interpolated from the previously computed list. To obtain the final beam radiance for rendering, we simply transform the result back to normal radiance with the local refractive index at location  $\mathbf{x}$ :

$$L_b(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}) = n^2(\mathbf{x}) \tilde{L}_b(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}). \quad (44)$$

It is important to note that  $n^2(\mathbf{x})$  in Eq. (44) does not cancel out with  $1/n^2(\mathbf{u}_j)$  in Eq. (43), since these values depend on the positions along the ray.

7.1.5 Transient Rendering. The extension to transient rendering is straightforward. We follow the same steps as in the previous section to obtain the transient basic radiance for a curved beam:

$$\tilde{L}_b(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, t) \approx \sum_{j=1}^k \tau(\mathbf{u}_j, \mathbf{x}) \frac{\sigma_t(\mathbf{u}_j)}{n(\mathbf{u}_j)^2} K(r_j, d_j) \Delta \Phi_j P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_j, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{u}_j)). \quad (45)$$

The function  $t'(\mathbf{u}_j)$  returns the time at point  $\mathbf{u}_j$ , which is the absolute time  $t$  minus the TOF from  $\mathbf{x}$  to  $\mathbf{u}_j$ . The TOF values are obtained

during time integration and are stored together with the nodes in a list. For transient rendering, the time-dependent flux  $\Delta \Phi_j(t'(\mathbf{u}_j))$  that each photon contributes with respect to absolute time  $t$  needs to be determined with the photon map. Since the TOF of each photon since emission is stored in the photon map, the absolute time of emission can be reconstructed. Together with the light source identifier, we perform a lookup of the flux in the emission function of the corresponding light source.

## 7.2 Discussion

Our extensions to standard photon mapping allow us to solve the RRTE and to demonstrate the practical consequences of our contributions. With our first extension, we rely on basic radiance instead of normal radiance to correctly render refraction in participating media. In addition to the curved geometry of light rays, this approach guarantees conservation of energy even in very complex participating media with continuous refraction, in contrast to the approach by Gutierrez et al. [2005] that only accounts for the geometric effects of refraction. The key difference to regular photon mapping is the strong nonlinear dependency of the spatially varying squared refractive index in the integral over the curved path. Since the index of refraction depends on the location, the influence of this additional term cannot be compensated easily, for example, with a simple rescaling of the light source fluxes. In Section 9, we investigate the impact on rendered images and we provide a quantitative analysis for one example. It can be observed that the rather technical and complex theory from the previous sections pays off, since the algorithmic extension is quite simple and can be included in any existing implementation of photon mapping without redesigning the entire algorithm.

With our second extension, we can visualize the propagation of global illumination in a participating, refractive medium with spatially varying speed of light. Therefore, we account for the explicit time dependency of light transport. The rendering pass integrates over time and estimates basic radiance in space and time. Since each photon travels along a different path through the medium and carries different flux, radiance estimation requires a lookup of the flux at the time of emission of each photon. In principle, this could be achieved by continuously emitting photons over time and storing them in a 4D photon map. In this case, a finite time interval is required to estimate photon density in time as well, which was employed by Cammarano and Jensen [2002] to render motion blur with photon mapping or more recently by Jarabo [2012] for surface-based transient rendering. However, this additional dimension would raise the number of photons even further, especially if the emission functions of the light sources contain high frequencies. We circumvent this issue by assuming that the medium and the light sources are quasi-static, which allows us to reuse the spatial distribution of the photons for each point in time. In this way, we can employ the TOF to reconstruct the emission signal without loss of information.

We implemented the nonlinear photon mapping algorithm of the previous section with C++ in the Mitsuba renderer [Jakob 2010]. Our extension for continuous refraction is fully integrated into the framework as a custom integrator plugin that is coupled with the existing render methods for surfaces and nonrefracting, participating media. We support a series of novel container types to model complex media with a spatially varying index of refraction. ## 9. RESULTS

We present results from our parallel implementation on a cluster with 16 compute nodes, each equipped with 2 Intel Xeon X5620 Quad Core CPUs with 2.4 GHz and 24 GB RAM. The physical nodes are interconnected over an InfiniBand network with a bandwidth of 20 GBit/s. We discuss various results that show the impact of our approach on rendered images in the presence of scattering and refracting media. In addition, we show image sequences of propagating light echoes that illustrate the transient behavior of light transport in such media.

## 9.2 Light Echoes

The image series of Figure 13 shows a result of TOF rendering. The XYZ RGB dragon [Stanford 2013] consists of a heterogeneous mix of amber with varying purity. The index of refraction varies in the range  $n \in [1.53, 1.55]$ . In Figure 13(a), the scene is rendered with stationary lighting to provide an overview. The dragon is illuminated by an area light from the top and by a spotlight from the left. In Figure 13(b), the area light is turned off completely and the dragon is only illuminated by a short burst of light from the spotlight. The head of the dragon appears more saturated than his body because light has already scattered more often. In Figure 13(c), the light pulse has almost passed the dragon’s body, which now appears also more reddish due to higher orders of scattering. Meanwhile, the light front has reached the dragon’s tail, which appears again more faint. In Figure 13(d), the pulse has passed the dragon completely and the

remaining saturated light originates only from multiple scattering inside the dragon.

Figure 14 shows a real-world application of light echo rendering in astronomy. A variable star emits an intense burst of light, which propagates through the surrounding dust and finally reaches the observer. A well-studied example of a real light echo is V838 Monocerotis3 and serves as a prototype for our result. For the volumetric dataset, we employ a single time step of a numerical supernova simulation [Blondin 2007] and we assume a radius of about 1 light year. For the anisotropy parameter, we follow Magnor et al. [2005] with  $g = 0.6$  and we simulate only slight refraction in the range  $n \in [1.0, 1.01]$ . The observation time range in Figures 14(a)–(h) corresponds to about 18 months of real time. Meanwhile, the visible structures of the nebula vary significantly due to the transient light transport. Although the volumetric model remains constant throughout the entire image sequence, the scene appears to be dynamic due to the changing lighting conditions. The vivid motion of the propagating light echo can be experienced best in the supplemental video. In addition to the image sequence of Figure 14, the video provides an additional animation of the light echo from a different point of view. For this result, we employed a CUDA-accelerated implementation of our transient photon mapping algorithm, highly optimized for pure voxelized scenes. The render time of each frame was 110 s on a desktop machine with a 3.4 GHz Intel Core i7 processor, 16 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GTX-680 graphics card.

## 10. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

We have introduced a physically based optical model to graphics that describes global illumination in continuously refracting and participating media with a generic volume rendering equation. In previous work, the combination of spatially varying refraction and participating media was solved with simplified models that did not guarantee conservation of energy or did not account for global illumination. The RRTE is independent of a specific algorithm or discretization and has a similar form as the well-known RTE. The main difference is the quantity that is conserved: basic radiance instead of radiance. Thereby, we achieve conservation of energy in continuously refracting media and we effectively generalize previous approaches that are based on Snell’s law. The derivation of the RRTE involved several mathematical transformations that are crucial for comprehension; however, the essential benefit of this elaborate discussion is a rather simple implementation in practice. In addition, the computational expense, due to energy conservation, does not increase further compared to pure geometric refraction. With our implementation of photon mapping, we demonstrated the impact of our theory on the correctness of rendered images. We were able to show that conservation of energy is important in practice as well and that its influence on renderings cannot be neglected, if high accuracy and physical correctness are required. To obtain authentic results, it is crucial that the input data is sufficiently accurate. As a simple example, we have presented a real-world experiment in Figure 7 to measure the distribution of the refractive index in a heterogeneous sugar solution. However, for more complex media, advanced techniques must be employed to acquire their refractive properties, similar to the scattering properties of participating media [Narasimhan et al. 2006].

In optics literature, several alternative formulations of light transport without basic radiance were discussed by Martí-López et al. [2006] and many of them fail to obey the laws of geometric optics, such as the inverse square law [Born and Wolf 1999]. A

noticeable exception is the approach by Martí-López et al. [2003], who introduced an explicit term for the divergence of rays that acts as an artificial attenuation or amplification coefficient in their transfer equation to account for the continuously varying solid angle and to achieve conservation of energy. In contrast, the RRTE does not require such an explicit handling of the diverging ray geometry, because basic radiance already accounts for the varying flux density in continuously refracting media and thereby simplifies conservation of energy. However, an interesting direction for future work is to formally investigate the equivalence of both formulations and in case of discrepancies to study the impact on rendering.

In our current implementation, we are limited to nondispersive media, since we do not account for a frequency-dependent index of refraction. However, this is not a limitation of our model, but just of our implementation. If scattering remains elastic, a discretization of the spectrum could be employed to render independent images for each frequency, since there is no explicit dependency of the frequency present in the RRTE. For practical rendering, our implementation is mainly limited by the high number of photons that

are necessary to estimate direct light. However, further research is required to connect curved light paths with curved eye paths in a continuously refracting medium. A promising approach is the line space gathering method by Sun et al. [2010]. Although this technique is currently limited to straight lines, a generalization to curved paths might be possible, such as with piecewise linear segments. Further algorithmic improvements could be achieved, for example, with progressive photon mapping techniques [Hachisuka et al. 2008; Hachisuka and Jensen 2009] to improve quality with less memory consumption.

Future work could investigate even more general transport equations that include additional effects to obtain a more complete theory of radiative transfer. For example, inelastic scattering plays an important role for accurate rendering of ocean water. Gutierrez et al. [2005] presented a transfer equation and an extension of photon mapping that account for absorption and reemission of photons of different wavelengths. Later, this approach was further extended with a full bio-optical model specifically designed for the different sources of inelastic scattering in ocean water [Gutierrez et al. 2008]. A combination of this model and our approach should

be possible to account for fluids with heterogeneous refractive indices.

The presented RRTE does also not describe light transport in media where the refractive index depends on the polarization or the propagation direction of light. Crystals with asymmetric structures often lead to birefringence, which splits an incident ray into two refracted parts. Weidlich and Wilkie [2008] presented the theoretical background for correctly rendering this effect in uniaxial crystals like calcite. Later, Latorre et al. [2012] further developed a solution to birefringence in biaxial crystals. In colored dielectrics, the index of refraction does not always decrease with shorter wavelengths, which leads to a nonmonotonic behavior of dispersion. Weidlich and Wilkie [2009a] demonstrated how the index of refraction is derived correctly from the absorption characteristics of a material. Such highly accurate approaches play an important role if the rendered results have to meet predictive quality standards, similar to our approach for accurate continuous refraction.

Visual effects from wave optics are also not modelled by the RRTE. In thin films and layers, iridescence leads to colorful reflections of incident light due to interference depending on the angle of view. Weidlich and Wilkie [2009b] presented a model for rendering this effect based on the same material parameters that govern its appearance in reality. More recently, Sadeghi et al. [2012] introduced a physically based model to render complex rainbows by accounting for interference and diffraction. Future research could study how such wave-based effects can be described by a more general transfer equation.

Applications for highly accurate optical models that are capable of rendering predictive results in terms of correct energy conservation and color reproduction can be found in domains where physical prototyping is either impossible or too expensive, such as architecture or automotive design [Wilkie et al. 2009], for example. The optimization of lighting and choice of materials requires reliable image synthesis for making important decisions prior to the real production process, especially in the high-end sector. In this context, we contributed an accurate optical model for continuously refracting materials, rigorously derived from fundamental physics.

## A. STREAMING IN REFRACTIVE MEDIA

We rewrite the spatial derivatives in Eq. (10) with the divergence operator in Cartesian coordinates with  $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z)$  and we get

$$\frac{\partial (f\dot{x})}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{y})}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{z})}{\partial z} = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot (f\dot{\mathbf{x}}). \quad (46)$$

The angular derivatives in Eq. (10) can also be rewritten with a divergence operator in spherical coordinates. Therefore, we substitute the angular coordinates  $(\phi, \mu)$  and  $(\dot{\phi}, \dot{\mu})$  with

$$\boldsymbol{\omega} = \hat{\mathbf{r}}, \quad (47)$$

$$\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \sin \theta \dot{\phi} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \dot{\theta} \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}, \quad (48)$$

respectively, where  $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$  denotes the azimuthal unit vector,  $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$  the polar unit vector, and  $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$  the radial unit vector. With this substitution and by employing the previous definition  $\mu := \cos \theta$ , we can write

$$\frac{\partial (f\dot{\phi})}{\partial \phi} + \frac{\partial (f\dot{\mu})}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\sin \theta} \frac{\partial (f\dot{\phi} \sin \theta)}{\partial \phi} + \frac{1}{\sin \theta} \frac{\partial (f\dot{\theta} \sin \theta)}{\partial \theta} \quad (49)$$

$$=: \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot (f\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}). \quad (50)$$

In Eq. (50), we define a divergence operator  $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$  in spherical coordinates evaluated at radius  $r = 1$  (see Appendix F.1 for more details). With Eqs. (46) and (50), we can rewrite the continuity equation (10), using the common variables  $(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, v, t)$ :

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot (f\dot{\mathbf{x}}) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot (f\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}) + \frac{\partial (f\dot{v})}{\partial v} = Q. \quad (51)$$

In the next step, we rewrite Eq. (51) by applying the chain rule to the time derivatives:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( f \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \frac{ds}{dt} \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( f \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \frac{ds}{dt} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( f \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{ds}{dt} \right) = Q. \quad (52)$$

We observe that we could simplify Eq. (52) by substituting Eq. (8) for the derivative of the frequency. However, for further transformations, it is crucial to preserve this term at the moment. Nonetheless, we insert the group velocity  $v_g = ds/dt$  according to Eq. (9):

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( f v_g \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( f v_g \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( f v_g \frac{dv}{ds} \right) = Q. \quad (53)$$

Replacing the distribution function with the spectral radiance of Eq. (5) and multiplying both sides of Eq. (53) with  $hv$  leads to

$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) + \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} L \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right)}_{\text{streaming term}} + v \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{v} \right) = hv Q. \quad (54)$$

The left-hand side of Eq. (54) describes a streaming term. However, the streaming quantity is not yet apparent in this formulation as it still involves compositions of different derivatives.

## B. INTERACTION IN PARTICIPATING MEDIA

Before we can formulate the transfer equation, we have yet to discuss how the right-hand side of Eq. (54) can be described in terms of radiance rather than the distribution function. Recall from Eq. (10) that  $Q = Q(f)$  denotes temporal variations of  $f$ . In participating media this can be due to emission (e), absorption (a), and scattering (s):

$$\frac{df}{dt} = Q(f) = Q_e(f) + Q_a(f) + Q_s(f). \quad (55)$$

In rendering, it is common practice to employ coefficients  $\sigma_a(\mathbf{x}, v, t)$  and  $\sigma_s(\mathbf{x}, v, t)$  that describe the quantity of absorption and scattering per unit distance, respectively. For example, due to absorption, the

distribution function changes along a differential line element  $ds$  to:

$$\left. \frac{df}{ds} \right|_a = \left. \frac{df}{dt} \right|_a \frac{dt}{ds} = -\sigma_a f. \quad (56)$$

By rearranging Eq. (56), we obtain the temporal change of the distribution function due to absorption:

$$Q_a(f) = \left. \frac{df}{dt} \right|_a = -\sigma_a v_g f, \quad (57)$$

where we have substituted Eq. (9) for the group velocity. Similar expressions can be derived for the emission and scattering terms as well and we can write the following expression for the source and sink terms of Eq. (55):

$$Q(f) = \underbrace{\sigma_a v_g f}_{\text{emission}} - \underbrace{(\sigma_a + \sigma_s) v_g f}_{\text{extinction}} + \underbrace{\frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) v_g f \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}'}_{\text{in-scattering}}, \quad (58)$$

where we have assumed that the scattering coefficient  $\sigma_s$  is a function of  $(\mathbf{x}, v, t)$  but not of  $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ , which is common in rendering. Similar to the RTE, the phase function  $P$  denotes the probability that photons are scattered from direction  $\boldsymbol{\omega}'$  to  $\boldsymbol{\omega}$  and we denote the extinction coefficient with  $\sigma_t = \sigma_s + \sigma_a$ . Substituting the distribution function in Eq. (58) with radiance from Eq. (5) yields

$$Q(L) = \sigma_a \frac{L_e}{hv} - \sigma_t \frac{L}{hv} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \frac{L}{hv} \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \quad (59)$$

We can now reformulate our approach in terms of radiance, which is the next step toward a unified transfer equation.

## C. PROOF OF THEOREM

PROOF. Since we require multiple steps in the following transformation, we provide separate appendices that prove important identities concerning the various derivatives. We begin the proof by substituting Eq. (59) into Eq. (54):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) + \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} L \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right) + v \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{v} \right) \\ = \sigma_a L_e - \sigma_t L + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) L \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \end{aligned} \quad (60)$$

The following discussion mainly relies on substitutions of Eqs. (6)–(9) and on algebraic transformations. To improve readability, we split the proof into two independent parts in Appendices C.1 and C.2. Subsequently, we combine both results in Appendix C.3 to finish the proof.

### C.1 Time and Frequency

In the first step, we require the following identities for the time and frequency derivatives in Eq. (60):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) \stackrel{D_{1,1}}{=} \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{n^2}{v_g} \right) \quad (61)$$

$$v \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{v} \right) \stackrel{D_{2,2}}{=} n^2 \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) - \frac{v}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial v} \right). \quad (62)$$

Detailed proofs of both identities can be found in Appendices D.1 and D.2, respectively. Next, we derive an expression for their sum

as it occurs in Eq. (60):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) + v \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{v} \right) &= \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{n^2}{v_g} \right) \\ &\quad + n^2 \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) - \frac{v}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial v} \right) \\ &\stackrel{E_{1,1}}{=} \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) \\ &\quad + \frac{3L}{c} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \quad (63)$$

The equality of the last transformation in Eq. (63) is shown in detail in Appendix E.1. In Appendix A, we stated that the frequency derivative in Eq. (52) should not be substituted with zero as provided in Eq. (8), since this course of action is crucial to derive the latter identity. Nonetheless, we can now perform the substitution to simplify the expression of Eq. (63) by exploiting the assumption of a quasi-stationary medium with  $\partial n / \partial t = 0$  and the resulting derivative  $dv/ds = 0$  of Eq. (8):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) + v \frac{\partial}{\partial v} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{v} \right) = \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right). \quad (64)$$

### C.2 Space and Direction

In the second step, we perform a similar process for the spatial and the angular derivative in Eq. (60) with the following identities:

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right) \stackrel{D_{3,3}}{=} -\frac{2L}{n} (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n) + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L) \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \quad (65)$$

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} L \right) \stackrel{D_{4,4}}{=} \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot (\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} L). \quad (66)$$

We refer the reader to Appendices D.3 and D.4 for more details on both identities. Similar to the first step, we derive an expression for the sum of the latter two equations and we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} L \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right) &= \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot (\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} L) - \frac{2L}{n} (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n) \\ &\quad + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L) \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \\ &\stackrel{E_{2,2}}{=} n^2 \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) \\ &\quad + n^2 \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right), \end{aligned} \quad (67)$$

where the proof of equality of the last transformation is provided in Appendix E.2.

### C.3 Aggregation

In the previous two sections, we derived expressions for the derivatives of Eq. (60) in terms of  $L/n^2$ . We can now combine the results from Eqs. (64) and (67) to rewrite the streaming term of Eq. (60):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) \\ = \sigma_a L_e - \sigma_t L + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) L \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \end{aligned} \quad (68)$$

Dividing both sides of Eq. (68) by  $n^2$  yields

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) \\ &= \sigma_a \left( \frac{L_e}{n^2} \right) - \sigma_t \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \end{aligned} \quad (69)$$

We can now substitute  $\tilde{L} = L/n^2$  to obtain the expanded RRTE:

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x \tilde{L} + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \tilde{L} \\ &= \sigma_a \tilde{L}_e - \sigma_t \tilde{L} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \tilde{L} d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \end{aligned} \quad (70)$$

By employing Eqs. (6) and (9), the streaming term of Eq. (70) can be reformulated as

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{dt}{ds} \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t} + \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \cdot \frac{d\tilde{L}}{d\mathbf{x}} + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \frac{d\tilde{L}}{d\boldsymbol{\omega}} \\ &= \sigma_a \tilde{L}_e - \sigma_t \tilde{L} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \tilde{L} d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \end{aligned} \quad (71)$$

With this notation, the left-hand side of Eq. (71) is equivalent to the total derivative of  $\tilde{L}$  and we obtain the final transfer equation:

$$\frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} = \sigma_a \tilde{L}_e - \sigma_t \tilde{L} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \tilde{L} d\boldsymbol{\omega}'. \quad (72)$$

## D. DERIVATIVES

### D.1 The Time Derivative

The time derivative can be rewritten to

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) \quad (73)$$

$$= \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{\partial L}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{1}{v_g} \right) L \quad (74)$$

$$= \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{\partial L}{\partial t} - \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{2L}{n} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{2L}{n} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{1}{v_g} \right) L \quad (75)$$

$$= \frac{n^2}{v_g} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial t} \frac{1}{n^2} - \frac{2}{n^3} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} L \right) + \frac{L}{n^2} \left( 2n \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \frac{1}{v_g} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{1}{v_g} \right) n^2 \right) \quad (76)$$

$$= \frac{n^2}{v_g} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial t} \frac{1}{n^2} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{1}{n^2} \right) L \right) + \frac{L}{n^2} \left( \frac{\partial n^2}{\partial t} \frac{1}{v_g} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{1}{v_g} \right) n^2 \right) \quad (77)$$

$$= \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{n^2}{v_g} \right). \quad (78)$$

### D.2 The Frequency Derivative

The frequency derivative can be rewritten to

$$\nu \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{d\nu}{ds} \frac{L}{\nu} \right) \quad (79)$$

$$= \nu \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \left( -\frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \right) \frac{L}{\nu} \right) \quad (80)$$

$$= -\frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} L \right) \quad (81)$$

$$= -\frac{\nu}{c} \left( \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} L + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \right) \quad (82)$$

$$= -\frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} L - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \left( -\frac{c}{\nu} \frac{d\nu}{ds} \right) \quad (83)$$

$$= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \frac{d\nu}{ds} - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} L \quad (84)$$

$$= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \frac{d\nu}{ds} + \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{2L}{n} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{2L}{n} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} L \quad (85)$$

$$= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \frac{d\nu}{ds} - \frac{2L}{n} \frac{d\nu}{ds} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{2L}{n} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} L \quad (86)$$

$$= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \frac{d\nu}{ds} + Ln^2 \frac{d\nu}{ds} \left( -\frac{2}{n^3} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \left( 2n \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} + \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} n^2 \right) \right) \quad (87)$$

$$= n^2 \frac{d\nu}{ds} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \nu} \frac{1}{n^2} + \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{1}{n^2} \right) L \right) - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \left( \frac{\partial n^2}{\partial t} \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} + \frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial \nu \partial t} n^2 \right) \quad (88)$$

$$= n^2 \frac{d\nu}{ds} \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} \right), \quad (89)$$

where we have substituted Eq. (8) in transformations (80), (83), and (86).

### D.3 The Angular Derivative

The angular derivative can be expanded as follows:

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right) = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \right) L + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L) \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds}. \quad (90)$$

By substituting Eq. (7) for the first  $d\boldsymbol{\omega}/ds$  in Eq. (90), we get

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right) = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{1}{n} (\nabla_x n - (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n) \boldsymbol{\omega}) \right) L + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L) \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \quad (91)$$

$$= \frac{L}{n} \underbrace{(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot (\nabla_x n) - \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot ((\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n) \boldsymbol{\omega}))}_{=0} + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L) \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \quad (92)$$

$$= -\frac{L}{n} (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot ((\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n) \boldsymbol{\omega})) + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L) \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds}, \quad (93)$$

where we have used the fact that  $1/n$  and  $\nabla_x n$  do not depend on  $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ , hence  $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} (1/n) = 0$  in Eq. (91) and  $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot (\nabla_x n) = 0$  in Eq. (92). We expand the first angular derivative in Eq. (93) and obtain

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot ((\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n) \boldsymbol{\omega}) = (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n)) \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega} + (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}) (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n). \quad (94)$$

We further investigate the first summand of the right-hand side of Eq. (94). First, we observe that the dot-product  $\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_x n$  is only a

function of  $\phi$  and  $\theta$ , but not of  $r$ . Hence, the gradient  $\nabla_\omega (\omega \cdot \nabla_x n)$  has a zero radial component. Second, we note that  $\omega$  is a unit vector in radial direction that has zero polar and azimuthal components. Therefore, the subsequent dot-product is equal to zero:

$$(\nabla_\omega (\omega \cdot \nabla_x n)) \cdot \omega = 0. \quad (95)$$

The angular derivative in the second summand of the right-hand side of Eq. (94) can be simplified as well. The unit vector  $\omega$  has components ( $\omega_\phi = 0$ ,  $\omega_\theta = 0$ ,  $\omega_r = 1$ ). According to Appendix F.1 the angular divergence becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\omega \cdot \omega &= \nabla_{(\phi, \theta, r)} \cdot \omega \Big|_{r=1} \\ &= \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial \omega_\phi}{\partial \phi} + \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} (\omega_\theta \sin \theta) + \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} (r^2 \omega_r) \Big|_{r=1} \end{aligned} \quad (96)$$

$$= \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} (r^2) \Big|_{r=1} = \frac{2}{r} \Big|_{r=1} = 2. \quad (98)$$

By combining Eqs. (95) and (98), we can rewrite Eq. (94):

$$\nabla_\omega \cdot ((\omega \cdot \nabla_x n) \omega) = 2(\omega \cdot \nabla_x n), \quad (99)$$

which can be substituted in Eq. (93) to obtain

$$\nabla_\omega \cdot \left( \frac{d\omega}{ds} L \right) = -\frac{2L}{n} (\omega \cdot \nabla_x n) + (\nabla_\omega L) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds}. \quad (100)$$

#### D.4 The Spatial Derivative

The spatial derivative can be rewritten to

$$\nabla_x \cdot \left( \frac{dx}{ds} L \right) = \nabla_x \cdot (\omega L) = \omega \cdot (\nabla_x L), \quad (101)$$

where we have substituted Eq. (6). This is the well-known directional derivative of the radiance in the classic radiative transfer equation [Chandrasekhar 1960].

### E. DERIVATIVE COMBINATIONS

#### E.1 The Time and the Frequency Derivative

Adding Eqs. (78) and (89) leads to

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) + \nu \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{\nu} \right) &= \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) \\ &\quad + \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{n^2}{v_g} \right) - \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} \right). \end{aligned} \quad (102)$$

Eq. (102) can be further transformed by substituting Eq. (9) for the group velocity in the third summand of the right-hand side:

$$\frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{n^2}{v_g} \right) = \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{n^2 (n + \nu \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu})}{c} \right) \quad (103)$$

$$= \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^3 + n^2 \nu \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} \right) \quad (104)$$

$$= \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{1}{c} \left( \frac{\partial n^3}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \nu \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} \right) \right) \quad (105)$$

$$= \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{1}{c} \left( 3n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \nu \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} \right) \right) \quad (106)$$

$$= \frac{3L}{c} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \frac{\nu}{c} \frac{L}{n^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( n^2 \frac{\partial n}{\partial \nu} \right). \quad (107)$$

In Eq. (105), we assume  $\partial \nu / \partial t = 0$ . Then, Eq. (102) becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{v_g} \right) + \nu \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{L}{\nu} \right) &= \frac{n^2}{v_g} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{dv}{ds} \frac{\partial}{\partial \nu} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) \\ &\quad + \frac{3L}{c} \frac{\partial n}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \quad (108)$$

#### E.2 The Spatial and the Angular Derivative

Adding Eqs. (100) and (101) leads to

$$\nabla_x \cdot \left( \frac{dx}{ds} L \right) + \nabla_\omega \cdot \left( \frac{d\omega}{ds} L \right) \quad (109)$$

$$= \omega \cdot (\nabla_x L) - \frac{2L}{n} (\omega \cdot \nabla_x n) + (\nabla_\omega L) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds} \quad (110)$$

$$\begin{aligned} &= \omega \cdot (\nabla_x L) - n^2 \omega \cdot \left( \frac{2}{n^3} \nabla_x n \right) L \\ &\quad + (\nabla_\omega L) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds} - \frac{2L}{n} \frac{d\omega}{ds} \cdot \underbrace{\nabla_\omega n}_{=0} \end{aligned} \quad (111)$$

$$\begin{aligned} &= n^2 \omega \cdot \left( \frac{1}{n^2} \nabla_x L + \nabla_x \left( \frac{1}{n^2} \right) L \right) \\ &\quad + (\nabla_\omega L) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds} - \frac{2L}{n} \frac{d\omega}{ds} \cdot \nabla_\omega n \end{aligned} \quad (112)$$

$$= n^2 \omega \cdot \nabla_x \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + (\nabla_\omega L) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds} - \frac{2L}{n} \frac{d\omega}{ds} \cdot \nabla_\omega n \quad (113)$$

$$= n^2 \omega \cdot \nabla_x \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + (\nabla_\omega L) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds} - n^2 \left( \frac{2}{n^3} \nabla_\omega n \right) \cdot \frac{d\omega}{ds} L \quad (114)$$

$$= n^2 \omega \cdot \nabla_x \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{d\omega}{ds} \cdot \left( \frac{1}{n^2} \nabla_\omega L + \nabla_\omega \left( \frac{1}{n^2} \right) L \right) \quad (115)$$

$$= n^2 \omega \cdot \nabla_x \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{d\omega}{ds} \cdot \nabla_\omega \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right). \quad (116)$$

In Eq. (111), we exploited the fact that  $n$  is not a function of  $\omega$ , hence  $\nabla_\omega n = 0$ .

### F. NABLA IN SPHERICAL COORDINATES

#### F.1 Divergence

Let  $\mathbf{A} = (\mathbf{A}_\phi, \mathbf{A}_\theta, \mathbf{A}_r)$  be a vector in spherical coordinates

$$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}_\phi \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \mathbf{A}_\theta \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \mathbf{A}_r \hat{\boldsymbol{r}}, \quad (117)$$

with azimuthal unit vector  $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ , polar unit vector  $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ , and radial unit vector  $\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}$ . The divergence in spherical coordinates  $(\phi, \theta, r)$  is

$$\nabla_{(\phi, \theta, r)} \cdot \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}_\phi}{\partial \phi} + \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial (\mathbf{A}_\theta \sin \theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial (r^2 \mathbf{A}_r)}{\partial r}. \quad (118)$$

We define the divergence operator  $\nabla_\omega$  with respect to  $\omega = \hat{\boldsymbol{r}}$  in spherical coordinates evaluated at radius  $r = 1$  with

$$\nabla_\omega \cdot \mathbf{A} := \nabla_{(\phi, \theta, r)} \cdot \mathbf{A} \Big|_{r=1}. \quad (119)$$

#### F.2 Gradient

Let  $f$  be a scalar field. The gradient in spherical coordinates  $(\phi, \theta, r)$  is

$$\nabla_{(\phi, \theta, r)} f = \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial r} \hat{\boldsymbol{r}}. \quad (120)$$

Similar to the divergence operator, we define the gradient operator  $\nabla_{\omega}$  with respect to  $\omega = \hat{r}$  in spherical coordinates evaluated at radius  $r = 1$  with

$$\nabla_{\omega} f := \nabla_{(\phi, \theta, r)} f \Big|_{r=1}. \quad (121)$$
