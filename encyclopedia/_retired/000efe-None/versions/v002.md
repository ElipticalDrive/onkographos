# Adaptive Sampling and Resolution
## Overview
Adaptive Sampling and Resolution is a family of volume rendering acceleration techniques that dynamically adjust computational effort based on local scene complexity or global performance budgets [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. It solves the problem of fixed-cost rendering by allocating more samples or higher resolution only where visually necessary, such as near boundaries or in high-variance regions, while skipping empty space or using lower fidelity elsewhere. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by encompassing both per-ray sample count/step adjustments and global or pixel-level resolution changes, rather than relying solely on early termination or static culling.

## Shared principles
The family shares the core mechanism of varying the discretization of the rendering process—either in the number of samples per ray, the step size along the ray, the spatial resolution of the output, or the subset of pixels processed—to balance visual fidelity against computational cost [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. Mathematically, these methods modify the standard front-to-back compositing loop:
$$ C_{acc} = C_{acc} + C_{sample} \cdot \alpha_{sample} \cdot (1 - \alpha_{acc}) $$
$$ \alpha_{acc} = \alpha_{acc} + \alpha_{sample} \cdot (1 - \alpha_{acc}) $$
by introducing conditions that break the loop early, skip iterations, or reduce the number of $C_{sample}$ queries [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Two-stage NeRF Rendering](925-two-stage-nerf-rendering.md)
Two-stage NeRF Rendering decouples the estimation of scene density from the detailed evaluation of color and density. It sets itself apart from single-stage methods by explicitly separating the "where to look" (coarse) from the "what it looks like" (fine) decisions, and from simple uniform sampling by concentrating network queries on high-density regions identified in the first pass. It is chosen when high-frequency detail is required but a single pass with many samples is too expensive.

### [Random Pixel Subsampling](1668-random-pixel-subsampling.md)
Random Pixel Subsampling selects a random subset of pixel coordinates from a full image grid to reduce the computational cost of training volume rendering models. It differs from [Two-stage NeRF Rendering](925-two-stage-nerf-rendering.md) by operating within a single training pass using stochastic gradient descent on a pixel subset, rather than separating coarse and fine rendering stages, and from [Resolution Adaptation](3063-resolution-adaptation.md) by keeping the image resolution constant while varying the number of active pixels. It is chosen to accelerate training by reducing the number of forward passes per iteration.

### [Adaptive Refinement for Volume Rendering](1892-adaptive-refinement-for-volume-rendering.md)
Adaptive Refinement for Volume Rendering balances rendering quality and performance by iteratively refining the image resolution based on local visual error. It solves the problem of high computational cost in volume ray-casting by casting rays non-uniformly, focusing computational effort on regions with high color variation while interpolating homogeneous regions. It distinguishes itself from [Two-stage NeRF Rendering](925-two-stage-nerf-rendering.md) by operating on explicit voxel grids with FEM-based error control rather than neural field approximations, and from [Adaptive Sampling](1385-adaptive-sampling.md) by using a hierarchical quadtree structure for screen-space subdivision rather than stochastic jittering. It is chosen for interactive exploration where frame rates must be maintained during refinement.

### [Temporal Upsampling and Dithering](2872-temporal-upsampling-and-dithering.md)
Temporal Upsampling and Dithering combines spatial resolution reduction with temporal accumulation to balance visual fidelity and computational cost. It solves the problem of slicing artifacts caused by insufficient sampling steps in volume rendering by applying blue noise dithering, while using temporal upsampling to reduce the noise introduced by the dithering process. It distinguishes itself from static low-resolution rendering by leveraging frame-to-frame coherence to reconstruct high-resolution details, and from pure dithering by mitigating the resulting visual noise through temporal accumulation. It is chosen for real-time rendering where performance budgets are tight but temporal coherence can be exploited.

### [Resolution Adaptation](3063-resolution-adaptation.md)
Resolution Adaptation encompasses techniques that vary the spatial discretization of the rendering process—either by adjusting the output image resolution, the underlying voxel grid density, or the sampling rate—to balance visual fidelity against computational cost. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Adaptive Sampling Step with Early Ray Termination](4573-adaptive-sampling-step-with-early-ray-termination.md) by focusing on the *spatial* granularity of the data or framebuffer rather than the *temporal* or *ray-centric* step size, although the two often combine. It differs from [Random Pixel Subsampling](1668-random-pixel-subsampling.md) by systematically targeting specific regions (e.g., based on distance, error, or frame time) rather than stochastic selection. It is chosen when the scene complexity varies significantly across the view or when strict frame time budgets must be met.

### [Adaptive Sampling Step with Early Ray Termination](4573-adaptive-sampling-step-with-early-ray-termination.md)
This method optimizes volume rendering performance by reducing sampling frequency for distant data and terminating ray evaluation once total opacity is reached. It addresses the inconsistency in opacity accumulation caused by variable step sizes by employing a 3D pre-integrated transfer function. Unlike uniform sampling, it maintains constant brightness across varying step sizes. It is chosen for explicit volume rendering where density gradients are available and efficient skipping of empty space is critical.

### [Spatiotemporal Importance Sampling](4696-spatiotemporal-importance-sampling.md)
Spatiotemporal importance sampling is an adaptive sampling strategy that assigns higher sampling probabilities to pixels occluded by tools or exhibiting extensive motion, while assigning lower probabilities to stationary tissues. It solves the problem of inefficient training and poor reconstruction quality in surgical scenes where tool occlusion leads to infrequent representation of tissue pixels, and stationary tissues cause redundant training updates. Unlike uniform sampling, it addresses the varying learning difficulty across pixels, thereby speeding up convergence and improving the rendering quality of occluded areas. It is chosen for training neural radiance fields on dynamic or occluded scenes.

### [Adaptive Undersampling](5397-adaptive-undersampling.md)
Adaptive Undersampling accelerates volume rendering by replacing the full volume integral calculation for a subset of pixels with interpolation, while storing pre-computed integral values over fixed intervals to allow partial recomputation for pixels where interpolation is inaccurate. It differs from standard adaptive undersampling methods that use recursive block division (which are inefficient on GPUs) by employing a fixed two-pass approach with pre-computed intervals, ensuring only necessary intervals are integrated on the second pass. It is chosen when rendering large volumes where many pixels share similar ray paths.

### [Adaptive Screen Sampling](5838-adaptive-screen-sampling.md)
Introduced by Levoy [Levoy-1990-HRT], this method adaptively switches sampling rates based on color change in a pixel neighborhood, tracing additional rays in areas with high value gradients. It is chosen for traditional ray tracing or volume rendering where adaptive sampling is needed to handle high-frequency color changes efficiently.

### [Ray-Centric Adaptive Step and Termination](5938-ray-centric-adaptive-step-and-termination.md)
Ray-Centric Adaptive Step and Termination adjusts the integration step size $\Delta t$ along a ray based on local volume properties to skip empty space and refine detail near boundaries. It differs from [Adaptive Sampling](1385-adaptive-sampling.md) by dynamically changing the step size within a single ray cast rather than changing the number of rays or blocks, and from [Two-stage NeRF Rendering](925-two-stage-nerf-rendering.md) by operating on explicit volumetric data structures rather than neural uncertainty estimates. It is chosen for explicit volume rendering where density gradients are available and efficient skipping of empty space is critical.

### [Adaptive Ray Shooting & Quadtree Subdivision](8158-adaptive-ray-shooting-quadtree-subdivision.md)
A general strategy to accelerate radiance field training by reducing ray redundancy through adaptive ray shooting and quadtree subdivision. It is chosen for training neural fields where ray paths exhibit high redundancy across views.

### [Adaptive Image Sampling with Pixel Sharing](8159-adaptive-image-sampling-with-pixel-sharing.md)
An adaptive sampling method that divides the image plane into square sample regions, casts rays only from corner pixels to measure complexity, and subdivides regions with high complexity. It handles dependencies between adjacent regions by communicating pixel values rather than replicating ray tracing. It is chosen for rendering scenes with sharp edges or high-frequency details where corner sampling efficiently identifies complex regions.

### [Context-Based Adaptive Ray Sampling with Quadtree Subdivision](8164-context-based-adaptive-ray-sampling-with-quadtree-subdivisio.md)
A training-stage sampling strategy that uses a quadtree to partition the image space, sampling more rays in nontrivial areas (high color change/high rendering error) and fewer in trivial areas to reduce training burden while capturing details. It is chosen for training neural radiance fields where computational resources are limited and error-driven sampling improves convergence.

#### Checkerboard Progressive Rendering
Reduces per-frame compute by tracing only half the pixels each frame while blending results over time. Alternate which pixels are freshly traced each frame, and non-traced pixels carry forward the previous frame's blended result. It is chosen when real-time performance is critical and temporal coherence can be leveraged to hide the reduced spatial sampling.

#### Multi-Resolution Rendering for Clouds
A technique to limit samples by rendering clouds at low resolution first to identify areas needing fewer samples, then accumulating with higher resolution passes. The author considered this technique but did not implement it, suspecting it could be very effective. It is chosen when cloud rendering requires balancing high-frequency scattering details with strict performance budgets.

#### Adaptive Sparse Ray Sampling
An optimization that casts a sparse grid of rays (less than one per pixel) and adaptively increases ray density in regions of high image complexity, exploiting coherence in projections. It casts a sparse grid of rays, measures local image complexity using color differences, performs recursive subdivision of image space to increase sampling density in complex regions, and forms images from the resulting nonuniform array of sample colors by interpolation and resampling at the display resolution. Performance savings of more than two orders of magnitude over brute-force rendering methods were obtained for many datasets. It is chosen for interactive visualization of large volumetric datasets where progressive refinement is acceptable.

## Background
Adaptive sampling and resolution techniques have evolved from early ray tracing methods to modern neural rendering pipelines [G]. Adaptive screen sampling was introduced by Levoy in 1990 for traditional ray tracing, adapting sampling rates based on color gradients. In the context of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), two-stage rendering was proposed to improve quality by refining coarse samples with a fine network. Random pixel subsampling has been adopted to accelerate NeRF training by reducing the number of pixels processed per iteration. Temporal upsampling and dithering techniques, such as those using blue noise, have been adapted for volume rendering to mitigate slicing artifacts and improve performance. Adaptive undersampling and ray-centric step adjustments have been developed for explicit volume rendering to handle varying density gradients efficiently.
