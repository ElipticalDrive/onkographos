# Stipple Volume Rendering

Also called: Illustrative Interactive Stipple Rendering, Interactive Volume Stippling, Stipple Volume Renderer, Stippling, The Stipple Volume Renderer, Volumetric Stippling, volume stipple renderer

## Overview
Stipple Volume Rendering is an object-space, point-based non-photorealistic rendering technique that replaces standard rasterization with stippled points to create an illustrative, sketch-like appearance [S2, S4]. It solves the problem of conveying complex volumetric shape and features efficiently by abstracting unimportant details and utilizing attentive focus, making it suitable for initial volume investigation, data previewing, and medical education [S3, unofficial][S4]. Unlike [Example-Based Volume Illustration](6614-example-based-volume-illustration.md), which relies on example textures, Stipple Volume Rendering generates point lists based on volume characteristics (gradient magnitude/direction) and applies feature enhancements (silhouette curves, varying point sizes, resolution adjustments) to convey tone, texture, and shape [S4]. It is distinct from image-space stippling because it determines stipples per geometric primitive (voxel) rather than computing grey levels in the image space [S2][S3, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** 3D volume dataset (scalar field), camera parameters (view direction, near plane), light direction, and user-defined enhancement parameters [S4].
*   **Output:** A set of point primitives (stipples) and optionally line segments (silhouette curves) rendered to the screen [S4].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Voxel Data:** Normalized scalar values $v_i$, positions, and precomputed gradient vectors $\nabla V_i$ and magnitudes $||\nabla V_i||$ [S4].
*   **Edge Field:** A binary or scalar field generated by applying a Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter to the voxel data to identify features for enhancement [S2][S4].
*   **Stipple List:** A pre-generated list of stipple positions for each voxel. For a voxel $i$, this list contains up to $N_{max}$ points. The positions are distributed to approximate a Poisson disc distribution within a subvolume defined by planes parallel to the gradient plane [S4].

**Preprocessing Stage (Viewpoint-Independent):**
1.  **Normalization:** Normalize voxel values and gradients [S4].
2.  **Gradient Estimation:** Estimate the gradient $\nabla V_i$ for each voxel. The method uses a three-dimensional regression hyperplane approximation (Neumann et al. [16]) to minimize a four-dimensional error function, providing a smoothed dataset and accurate gradient simultaneously [S4].
3.  **Resolution Adjustment:** Apply a simple box-filter to adjust the volume resolution so that the average projection of a voxel on the screen is at least $5 \times 5$ pixels [S4].
4.  **Calculate $N_{max}$:** Calculate the maximum number of stipples per voxel based on screen projection:
    $$ N_{max} = k_{max} \frac{A_{vol}}{(X_{res} Y_{res} Z_{res})^{2/3}} $$
    Where $A_{vol}$ is the volume, $X_{res}, Y_{res}, Z_{res}$ are resolution dimensions, and $k_{max}$ is a constant [S4].
5.  **Initial Point Generation:** For voxels with gradient magnitude above a user-specified threshold, generate up to $N_{max}$ stipples. Place them randomly around the voxel center, between two planes $p_1$ and $p_2$ parallel to the tangent plane $p_0$ (defined by the gradient), separated by a user-chosen distance. Adjust locations to be relatively equally spaced [S4].

**Interactive Rendering Loop (Per Frame):**
1.  **Calculate Enhancement Factors:** For each voxel, compute the total enhancement factor $T$ as the product of selected factors:
    $$ T = T_b \cdot T_s \cdot T_r \cdot T_d \cdot T_t \cdot T_l $$
    Each factor is normalized to $[0, 1]$. If a feature is not selected, its factor is excluded (effectively 1) [S4].
2.  **Determine Point Count:** Calculate the number of stipples to draw for voxel $i$:
    $$ N_i = N_{max} \cdot T $$
    [S2, S4]
3.  **Select Stipples:** Select the first $N_i$ stipples from the pre-generated list for voxel $i$. This maintains frame-to-frame coherence [S4].
4.  **Render Points:** Render the selected stipples as point primitives. The point size $S_i$ is scaled by the gradient magnitude:
    $$ S_i = ||\nabla V_i|| \cdot S_{max} $$
    [S4]
5.  **Render Silhouettes (Optional):** Identify silhouette voxels using the LoG edge field and view direction. Render silhouette curves as line segments for these voxels [S4].

**Formulas for Enhancement Factors:**
*   **Boundary Factor ($T_b$):**
    $$ T_b = v_i \cdot (k_{gc} + k_{gs} \cdot (||\nabla V_i||^{k_{ge}})) $$
    Where $v_i$ is the original voxel scalar value, $k_{gc}$ controls direct influence, $k_{gs}$ indicates maximum boundary enhancement, and $k_{ge}$ controls sharpness [S4].
*   **Silhouette Factor ($T_s$):**
    Constructed similarly to $T_b$ using silhouette-specific parameters ($k_{sc}, k_{ss}, k_{se}$) [S4], though some implementations use $T_s = 1 - (L \cdot \nabla V_i)$ [S2].
*   **Resolution Factor ($T_r$):**
    $$ T_r = \left[ \frac{D_{near} + d_i}{D_{near} + d_0} \right]^{k_{re}} $$
    Where $D_{near}$ is the near plane location, $d_i$ is the distance from the current volume location to the near plane, $d_0$ is the distance from the initial volume location to the near plane (reference), and $k_{re}$ controls the rate of change. If $k_{re}=0$, no enhancement [S4].
*   **Distance Factor ($T_d$):**
    $$ T_d = 1 + \left( \frac{z}{a} \right)^{k_{de}} $$
    Where $z$ is the depth of the current voxel, $a$ defines the original distance range $(-a, a)$, and $k_{de}$ controls distance attenuation. $k_{de}$ can be negative or positive [S4].
*   **Interior Transparency Factor ($T_t$):**
    $$ T_t = ||\nabla V_i||^{k_{te}} $$
    Where $||\nabla V_i||$ is the gradient magnitude to the center of the volume, and $k_{te}$ controls the falloff. Lower gradient voxels become more transparent [S4].
*   **Lighting Factor ($T_l$):**
    $$ T_l = 1 - (L \cdot \nabla V_i) $$
    Where $L$ is the light direction and $\nabla V_i$ is the gradient vector [S2, S4].

**Parameters:**
*   Default rendering style: $k_{gc}=0.39, k_{gs}=0.51, k_{ge}=1.0$ [S4].
*   Boundary & Silhouette enhancement style: $k_{sc}=0.279, k_{ss}=0.45, k_{se}=1.11$ [S4].
*   Interactive thresholds: $T_{hLOG}, T_{heye}, T_{hgrad}$ [S4].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Temporal Aliasing:** Occurs during rotation near silhouette edges and illuminated boundaries as new points are added. Alleviated by implementing simple partial opacity point rendering to fade points [S4].
*   **Noisy Data:** Noisy volume data creates incorrect gradient directions, affecting feature enhancements. The Neumann gradient estimation method helps mitigate this [S4].
*   **Silhouette Approximation:** Silhouette detection in volumes approximates surface normal using volume gradient direction and detects boundaries using volume gradient magnitude, which may not always align with true geometric boundaries [S2, S4].

## Subtopics

### Hardware Acceleration via Vertex Programs
A proposed optimization to move feature enhancements onto the graphics card using programmable vertex programs, avoiding expensive vertex downloads [S4]. Preprocessed points are stored as display lists or vertex arrays in the graphics card’s memory [S4]. Vertex programs evaluate the thresholds of feature enhancements; if a vertex falls below the threshold, its coordinates are modified to a position off screen to effectively cull it [S4]. This culling is possible because the system uses vertices rather than polygons, allowing simple thresholding formulae to be implemented directly [S4]. However, this technique is not generally possible for polygons due to the lack of connectivity information in vertex programs [S4].

### Flow Illustration via Stippling
An application of volume stippling to flow data, where flow features (critical points, vortices, etc.) are conveyed with simple strokes, avoiding high-gradient boundary emphasis [S3, unofficial]. Flow data often lacks high gradients, so boundaries are not the main concern [S3, unofficial]. Instead, the method identifies flow features such as vortices, sources, and sinks from the vector field data [S3, unofficial]. Stipples are generated to represent these structures, potentially using silhouette curves and contouring transfer functions to show major structures, avoiding visual clutter [S3, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Preprocessing is viewpoint-independent and takes seconds to a minute depending on dataset size and resolution [S4]. Interactive rendering is frame-rate dependent on the number of points (5,000 to 2,000,000) and silhouette curves (1,000 to 300,000) [S4].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volume data, precomputed gradients, edge fields, and the pre-generated stipple lists. Not limited by texture memory size for the stipple points themselves, as they are rendered as point primitives [S2].
*   **Measured Performance:** On a dual processor Intel Xeon 2.00 GHz computer with a Geforce 3 Ti 500 display card, frame rates ranged from 3.5 fps to 60.4 fps for datasets with resolutions from $64 \times 64 \times 64$ to $341 \times 341 \times 93$, depending on whether stipples, silhouettes, or both were rendered [S4].
*   **Strengths:** Interactive previewing, concise representation of complex data, frame-to-frame coherence, and suitability for medical education and initial investigation [S4].
*   **Limitations:** Temporal aliasing near edges, reliance on accurate gradient estimation for noisy data, and potential loss of fine detail due to abstraction [S4].
*   **When to use:** Initial volume investigation, data previewing, and medical education/teaching anatomy [S4].
*   **When not to use:** When photorealistic detail is required or when the volume data is extremely noisy and gradient estimation fails [S4].

## Background
Stipple Volume Rendering was introduced by Lu et al. in the paper "Non-Photorealistic Volume Rendering Using Stippling Techniques" (2003) [S4]. It is part of the broader field of illustrative volume rendering, which applies principles from artistic and scientific illustration to volume data [S4]. The technique builds on the concept of object-space stippling, where stipples are determined per geometric primitive, as opposed to image-space stippling [S2][S3, unofficial]. It is used in medical illustration and flow visualization [S2][S3, unofficial].
