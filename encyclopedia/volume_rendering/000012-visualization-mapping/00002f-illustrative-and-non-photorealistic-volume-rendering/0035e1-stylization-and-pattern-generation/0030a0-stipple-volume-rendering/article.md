# Stipple Volume Rendering

Also called: Illustrative Interactive Stipple Rendering, Stipple Volume Renderer, The Stipple Volume Renderer, Volumetric Stippling, volume stipple renderer

## Overview
Stipple Volume Rendering is an interactive direct volume illustration technique that replaces standard rasterization with stippled point primitives to create a sketch-like, hand-drawn appearance [S2, S3]. It solves the problem of conveying complex volumetric shape and features concisely by simulating traditional pen-and-ink stipple drawing, where point density and size modulate tone and texture [S3]. Unlike surface-based NPR methods that rely on well-defined geometric surfaces, this method determines features through the analysis of local volumetric properties (gradient magnitude and direction) because volumetric features vary continuously in 3D space [S2, S4, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from Image Space stippling by operating in Object Space, determining stipples per geometric primitive (voxel) rather than computing grey levels for the final image [S2].

## How it works
The method consists of two main phases: a viewpoint-independent preprocessing stage that generates a static list of potential stipple points, and an interactive rendering stage that selects and scales these points based on dynamic feature enhancements [S3].

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Volume dataset $V$ with resolution $X_{res} \times Y_{res} \times Z_{res}$ and scalar values $v_i$ for each voxel $i$ [S3].
*   Camera parameters: View direction, near plane distance $D_{near}$ [S3].
*   Light direction vector $L$ [S2].
*   User-defined enhancement parameters (e.g., $k_{gc}, k_{gs}, k_{ge}, k_{sc}, k_{ss}, k_{se}, k_{re}, k_{de}, k_{te}$) [S3].

**Outputs:**
*   A rendered image composed of point primitives (stipples) and optional line segments (silhouette curves) [S3].

### Data Structures
1.  **Voxel Data:** 3D array storing scalar values $v_i$ [S3].
2.  **Gradient Data:** 3D array storing normalized gradient vectors $\nabla V_i$ and magnitudes $||\nabla V_i||$ [S3].
3.  **Edge Field:** 3D array generated by applying a Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter to the voxel data [S2].
4.  **Stipple List:** For each voxel $i$, a list of pre-generated 3D positions $\{P_{i,j}\}$ where $j$ ranges from $0$ to $N_{max,i}-1$. These positions are distributed within the voxel volume [S3].
5.  **Silhouette List:** A list of line segments representing silhouette curves [S3].

### Preprocessing Steps
1.  **Normalization:** Normalize voxel values $v_i$ and gradients $\nabla V_i$ to a standard range [S3].
2.  **Gradient Estimation:** Compute the gradient magnitude and direction for each voxel. To reduce noise and handle discontinuities, approximate the density function in a local neighborhood with a three-dimensional regression hyperplane, minimizing a four-dimensional error function to obtain a smoothed dataset and estimated gradient simultaneously (Neumann et al. method) [S3].
3.  **Resolution Adjustment:** Apply a simple box-filter to the volume data. Adjust the resolution so that the average projection of a voxel on the screen is at least $5 \times 5$ pixels. This improves the stippling pattern and prevents areas of high gradient from appearing too dark [S3].
4.  **Calculate $N_{max}$:** Calculate the maximum number of stipples each voxel can contain to reduce redundant generation.
    $$ N_{max} = k_{max} \frac{A_{vol}}{(X_{res} \cdot Y_{res} \cdot Z_{res})^{2/3}} $$
    Where $A_{vol}$ is the volume, $X_{res}, Y_{res}, Z_{res}$ are resolution dimensions, and $k_{max}$ is a constant [S3].
5.  **Initial Point Generation:**
    *   For each voxel $i$, determine if the gradient magnitude exceeds a user-specified threshold.
    *   If yes, generate up to $N_{max}$ stipple positions within the voxel.
    *   Place points randomly around the voxel center, between two planes $p_1$ and $p_2$ parallel to the tangent plane $p_0$, separated by a user-chosen distance.
    *   Adjust point locations within this subvolume to be relatively equally spaced, approximating a Poisson disc distribution [S3].
    *   Store these positions in the Stipple List for voxel $i$ [S3].
6.  **Silhouette Curve Generation:** Identify voxels using the LoG-based edge field. Render silhouette curves as line segments for these voxels. The count ranges from 1,000 to 300,000 depending on the dataset [S3].

### Interactive Rendering Steps
For each frame:
1.  **Calculate Enhancement Factors:** For each voxel $i$, calculate the total enhancement factor $T_i$ based on selected features.
    $$ T_i = T_b \cdot T_s \cdot T_r \cdot T_d \cdot T_t \cdot T_l $$
    Each factor is normalized in $[0, 1]$. If a feature is not selected, its factor is excluded from the product [S3].
2.  **Calculate Point Count:** Determine the number of stipples to draw for voxel $i$:
    $$ N_i = N_{max} \cdot T_i $$
    Where $N_{max}$ is the maximum density for the volume position (calculated in preprocessing) [S2, S3].
3.  **Select Stipples:** Select the first $N_i$ points from the pre-generated Stipple List for voxel $i$. This maintains frame-to-frame coherence [S3].
4.  **Calculate Point Size:** Determine the size $S_i$ of the selected points. The basic size is scaled by the gradient magnitude:
    $$ S_i = ||\nabla V_i|| \cdot S_{max} $$
    The size may also be modulated by the enhancement factors (e.g., resolution, interior) [S3].
5.  **Render Points:** Render the selected points as point primitives. Apply partial opacity to alleviate temporal aliasing [S3].
6.  **Render Silhouettes:** Render the pre-generated silhouette line segments [S3].

### Formulas and Parameters

**Enhancement Factors:**

1.  **Boundary Factor ($T_b$):** Enhances regions with high gradient magnitude.
    $$ T_b = v_i \cdot (k_{gc} + k_{gs} \cdot (||\nabla V_i||^{k_{ge}})) $$
    *   $v_i$: Original normalized voxel scalar value.
    *   $||\nabla V_i||$: Gradient magnitude.
    *   $k_{gc}$: Controls direct influence of voxel value. Default: 0.39 [S3].
    *   $k_{gs}$: Indicates maximum boundary enhancement. Default: 0.51 [S3].
    *   $k_{ge}$: Controls sharpness of boundary enhancement. Default: 1.0 [S3].
    *   *Alternative Style Parameters:* $k_{sc}=0.279, k_{ss}=0.45, k_{se}=1.11$ [S3].

2.  **Silhouette Factor ($T_s$):** Enhances regions oriented orthogonally to the view plane.
    $$ T_s = 1 - (L \cdot \nabla V_i) $$
    *   $L$: Light direction vector.
    *   $\nabla V_i$: Normalized gradient vector.
    *   *Note:* Sources define $T_s$ using the light vector $L$ [S2]. Other sources imply silhouette is view-dependent, but the provided formula uses $L$. In the context of the parent article's shared principles, $L$ is often used as a proxy for view-dependent shading in NPR. [S2, S3].

3.  **Resolution Factor ($T_r$):** Adjusts point count/size based on camera distance to improve clarity at close range.
    $$ T_r = \left[ \frac{D_{near} + d_i}{D_{near} + d_0} \right]^{k_{re}} $$
    *   $D_{near}$: Near plane location.
    *   $d_i$: Distance from current voxel location to the near plane.
    *   $d_0$: Distance from the initial volume location to the near plane (reference point).
    *   $k_{re}$: Controls the rate of change. If $k_{re}=0$, no enhancement [S3].

4.  **Distance Factor ($T_d$):** Modulates point count/size based on depth within the volume.
    $$ T_d = 1 + \left( \frac{z}{a} \right)^{k_{de}} $$
    *   $z$: Depth of the current voxel (relative to volume center, range $-a$ to $a$).
    *   $a$: Half valid volume length.
    *   $k_{de}$: Controls contribution of distance attenuation. Can be negative or positive [S3].

5.  **Interior Transparency Factor ($T_t$):** Exaggerates transparency for interior features based on gradient.
    $$ T_t = ||\nabla V_i||^{k_{te}} $$
    *   $||\nabla V_i||$: Gradient magnitude of the voxel to the center of the volume.
    *   $k_{te}$: Controls the falloff of the transparency enhancement. Voxels with lower gradient magnitude become more transparent [S3].

6.  **Lighting Factor ($T_l$):** Modulates point count/size based on lighting.
    $$ T_l = 1 - (L \cdot \nabla V_i) $$
    *   $L$: Light direction vector.
    *   $\nabla V_i$: Normalized gradient vector.
    *   *Note:* This formula is identical to $T_s$ in the provided notes [S2, S3].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Temporal Aliasing:** Occurs during rotation near silhouette edges and illuminated boundaries as new points are added. Alleviated by implementing simple partial opacity point rendering to fade points [S3].
*   **Noisy Data:** Noisy volume data creates incorrect gradient directions, affecting feature enhancements, especially lighting. The Neumann et al. smoothing method is used to mitigate this [S3].
*   **Silhouette Approximation:** Silhouette detection in volumes approximates surface normals using volume gradient direction. This is an approximation and may not be accurate for all internal structures [S2].

## Subtopics

### Hardware Acceleration via Vertex Programs
A proposed optimization to move feature enhancements onto the graphics card using programmable vertex shaders, avoiding expensive vertex downloads [S3]. Preprocessed points are stored as display lists or vertex arrays in the graphics card’s memory. Vertex programs evaluate the thresholds of feature enhancements. If a vertex falls below the enhancement threshold, its coordinates are modified to a position off screen to effectively cull it. This culling technique is suitable because the system uses vertices rather than polygons, allowing simple thresholding formulae to be implemented directly. However, this culling technique is not generally possible for polygons because there is currently no connectivity information available in vertex programs [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Preprocessing is viewpoint-independent and takes seconds to a minute depending on dataset size and resolution [S3]. Interactive rendering is real-time, with frame rates ranging from 3.5 to 60.4 fps for datasets with resolutions from $64^3$ to $256^3$ [S3].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volume data, gradient data, edge field, and the pre-generated stipple list. The total number of point primitives in a typical dataset ranges from 5,000 to 2,000,000, and silhouette curves from 1,000 to 300,000 [S3].
*   **Measured Performance:** Tested on a dual processor Intel Xeon 2.00 GHz computer with a Geforce 3 Ti 500 display card [S3].
    *   Iron ($64^3$): 30.0 fps (stipples), 60.4 fps (silhouettes), 29.9 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Head ($256 \times 256 \times 113$): 4.0 fps (stipples), 26.1 fps (silhouettes), 3.5 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Engine ($256 \times 256 \times 128$): 4.0 fps (stipples), 20.3 fps (silhouettes), 3.6 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Leg ($341 \times 341 \times 93$): 5.0 fps (stipples), 30.5 fps (silhouettes), 4.6 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Lobster ($301 \times 324 \times 56$): 8.7 fps (stipples), 30.5 fps (silhouettes), 7.5 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Foot ($256^3$): 5.9 fps (stipples), 30.5 fps (silhouettes), 5.0 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Aneurysm ($256^3$): 15.1 fps (stipples), 30.5 fps (silhouettes), 12.1 fps (both) [S3].
    *   Bonsai ($256^3$): 4.3 fps (stipples), 20.6 fps (silhouettes), 3.9 fps (both) [S3] [S3].
*   **Strengths:** Not limited by texture memory size; quick interaction with transfer functions and parameters; effective for initial volume investigation and data previewing; good temporal coherence [S2, S3].
*   **Limitations:** Performance can degrade with very large datasets or high resolution; temporal aliasing near edges; relies on gradient estimation which can be noisy [S2, S3].
*   **When to use:** Initial volume investigation, data previewing, medical education/teaching anatomy [S3].
*   **When not to use:** When photorealistic rendering is required; when high-precision surface geometry is needed [S2].

## Background
Stipple Volume Rendering was introduced by Lu, Morris, Ebert, Rheingans, and Hansen in their paper "Non-Photorealistic Volume Rendering Using Stippling Techniques" [S3]. The work builds on the principles of artistic and scientific illustration, aiming to simulate traditional hand-drawn stipple drawing for volume data. It was developed to provide an effective, interactive way to preview large, complex volume datasets in a concise, meaningful manner. The method was tested on various medical and scientific datasets (iron, head, engine, leg, lobster, foot, aneurysm, bonsai) [S3].
