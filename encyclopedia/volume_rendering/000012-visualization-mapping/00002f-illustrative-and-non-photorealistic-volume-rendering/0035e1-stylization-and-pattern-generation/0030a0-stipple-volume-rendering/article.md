# Stipple Volume Rendering

Also called: Illustrative Interactive Stipple Rendering, Interactive Volume Stippling, Stipple Volume Renderer, Stippling, The Stipple Volume Renderer, Volumetric Stippling, volume stipple renderer

## Overview
Stipple Volume Rendering is an illustrative, non-photorealistic volume rendering technique that replaces standard rasterization with stippled points to create a sketch-like appearance [S2]. It solves the problem of conveying object shape and internal structure in complex volume data by abstracting unimportant details and utilizing attentive focus to emphasize features of interest [S3, unofficial]. Unlike [Example-Based Volume Illustration](6614-example-based-volume-illustration.md) which relies on example textures, this method uses object-space stippling to determine points per geometric primitive (voxel), allowing for direct feature enhancements like silhouette and boundary detection without being limited by texture memory size [S2].

## How it works
The method operates in two main phases: a viewpoint-independent preprocessing stage and an interactive rendering loop. It is an object-space technique, determining stipples to render each geometric primitive (triangle, voxel) directly, as opposed to image-space methods that compute grey levels and generate points using a Poisson distribution [S2].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** 3D volume dataset (scalar field), camera parameters (view direction, near plane), user-defined enhancement parameters.
*   **Output:** A set of point primitives (stipples) and optional line segments (silhouette curves) rendered to the screen.

### Data Structures
1.  **Voxel Data:** Normalized scalar values, positions, gradient magnitudes, and gradient directions.
2.  **Edge Field:** A secondary volume generated by applying a Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter to the voxel data to identify features [S2].
3.  **Stipple List:** A pre-generated list of stipple locations and attributes for each voxel. The number of stipples per voxel is capped at $N_{max}$ [S4].

### Preprocessing Stage
This stage generates the initial stipple distribution and calculates static properties.

1.  **Gradient Estimation:** Approximate the density function in a local neighborhood with a three-dimensional regression hyperplane to get a smoothed dataset and estimated gradient simultaneously [S4]. This method minimizes a four-dimensional error function [S4].
2.  **Normalization:** Normalize voxel values and gradients [S4].
3.  **Resolution Adjustment:** Use a simple box-filter to adjust the volume resolution so that the average projection of a voxel on the screen is at least $5 \times 5$ pixels [S4].
4.  **Calculate $N_{max}$:** Calculate the maximum number of stipples each voxel can contain to reduce redundant point generation:
    $$ N_{max} = k_{max} \frac{A_{vol}}{(X_{res} \cdot Y_{res} \cdot Z_{res})^{2/3}} $$
    Where $A_{vol}$ is the volume, $X_{res}, Y_{res}, Z_{res}$ are resolution dimensions, and $k_{max}$ is a constant [S4].
5.  **Initial Point Generation:**
    *   Approximate a Poisson disc distribution to position the maximum number of stipples [S4].
    *   For voxels with gradient magnitude above a user-specified threshold, generate stipples near the gradient plane [S4].
    *   Place stipples randomly around the voxel center, between two planes $p_1$ and $p_2$ parallel to the tangent plane $p_0$, separated by a user-chosen distance [S4].
    *   Adjust point locations in this subvolume to be relatively equally spaced [S4].
    *   Store these positions in the Stipple List.

### Interactive Rendering Loop
For each frame, the system selects stipples from the pre-generated list and applies feature enhancements.

1.  **Calculate Enhancement Factors:** Compute the total enhancement factor $T$ for each voxel based on selected features. Each factor is normalized in $[0, 1]$. If a feature is not selected, its factor is excluded from the product [S4].
    $$ T = T_b \cdot T_s \cdot T_r \cdot T_d \cdot T_t \cdot T_l $$
2.  **Calculate Point Count:** Determine the number of stipples to draw for voxel $i$:
    $$ N_i = N_{max} \cdot T $$
    Where $N_{max}$ is the maximum density for the volume position [S2].
3.  **Select Stipples:** Select $N_i$ stipples from the pre-generated list for the voxel. This maintains frame-to-frame coherence [S4].
4.  **Render:** Render the selected stipples as point primitives. The point size $S_i$ is scaled by the gradient magnitude:
    $$ S_i = \|\nabla V_i\| \cdot S_{max} $$
    [S4]. Additionally, point primitives are scaled with the result of the basic diffuse lighting equation [S1].
5.  **Silhouette Curves:** Render silhouette curves as line segments for voxels identified by the LoG-based detection method [S4].

### Feature Enhancement Formulas
Each factor $T_n$ modifies the point count or size.

**1. Boundary Enhancement ($T_b$):**
Enhances voxels with high gradient magnitude.
$$ T_b = v_i \cdot (k_{gc} + k_{gs} \cdot (\|\nabla V_i\|^{k_{ge}})) $$
Where $v_i$ is the original voxel scalar value, $\|\nabla V_i\|$ is the gradient magnitude, $k_{gc}$ controls direct influence of voxel value, $k_{gs}$ indicates maximum boundary enhancement, and $k_{ge}$ controls sharpness [S4].

**2. Silhouette Enhancement ($T_s$):**
Uses volume gradient direction to approximate surface normal.
$$ T_s = 1 - (L \cdot \nabla V_i) $$
Where $L$ is the light direction and $\nabla V_i$ is the gradient vector [S2]. Note: Some sources define this for back/front facing determination [S2].

**3. Resolution Enhancement ($T_r$):**
Adjusts density based on distance to the near plane.
$$ T_r = \left[ \frac{D_{near} + d_i}{D_{near} + d_0} \right]^{k_{re}} $$
Where $D_{near}$ is the near plane location, $d_i$ is the distance from the current volume location to the near plane, $d_0$ is the distance from the initial volume location to the near plane (reference point), and $k_{re}$ controls the rate of change. If $k_{re}=0$, there is no enhancement [S4].

**4. Distance Enhancement ($T_d$):**
Attenuates points based on depth.
$$ T_d = 1 + \left( \frac{z}{a} \right)^{k_{de}} $$
Where $z$ is the depth of the current voxel, $a$ defines the original distance range $(-a, a)$, and $k_{de}$ controls the contribution of distance attenuation. $k_{de}$ can be negative or positive [S4].

**5. Interior Transparency Enhancement ($T_t$):**
Makes voxels with lower gradient magnitude more transparent.
$$ T_t = \|\nabla V_i\|^{k_{te}} $$
Where $\|\nabla V_i\|$ is the gradient magnitude of the voxel to the center of the volume, and $k_{te}$ controls the falloff of the transparency enhancement [S4].

**6. Lighting Enhancement ($T_l$):**
$$ T_l = 1 - (L \cdot \nabla V_i) $$
Where $L$ is the light direction and $\nabla V_i$ is the gradient vector [S2].

### Parameters
*   **$k_{max}$:** Constant for $N_{max}$ calculation [S4].
*   **$k_{gc}, k_{gs}, k_{ge}$:** Default boundary parameters ($k_{gc}=0.39, k_{gs}=0.51, k_{ge}=1.0$) [S4].
*   **$k_{sc}, k_{ss}, k_{se}$:** Boundary and silhouette enhancement parameters ($k_{sc}=0.279, k_{ss}=0.45, k_{se}=1.11$) [S4].
*   **$k_{re}, k_{de}, k_{te}$:** Control rates for resolution, distance, and interior enhancements [S4].
*   **Brightness/Contrast:** Configurable interactively [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Temporal Aliasing:** Occurs during rotation near silhouette edges and illuminated boundaries as new points are added [S4]. Alleviated by implementing simple partial opacity point rendering to fade the points [S4].
*   **Silhouette Detection:** In volumes, surface normal is approximated using volume gradient direction, and boundaries are detected using volume gradient magnitude [S2]. Noisy data can create problems in generating correct gradient directions [S4].
*   **Performance:** Total points range from 5,000 to 2,000,000; silhouette curves from 1,000 to 300,000 [S4].

## Subtopics

### Hardware Acceleration via Vertex Programs
A proposed optimization to move feature enhancements onto the graphics card using programmable vertex shaders, avoiding expensive vertex downloads [S4]. Preprocessed points are stored as display lists or vertex arrays in the graphics card’s memory. Vertex programs evaluate the thresholds of feature enhancements; if a vertex falls below the threshold, its coordinates are modified to a position off screen to effectively cull it [S4]. This culling technique is not generally possible for polygons because there is currently no connectivity information available in vertex programs, but is suitable here because the system uses vertices [S4].

### Flow Illustration via Stippling
An application of volume stippling to flow data, where flow features (critical points, vortices, etc.) are conveyed with simple strokes, avoiding high-gradient boundary emphasis [S3, unofficial]. Flow features are identified from vector field data, and stipples are generated to represent flow structures, potentially using silhouette curves and contouring transfer functions to show major structures [S3, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Preprocessing is viewpoint-independent and performed once (seconds to a minute). Interactive rendering is frame-rate dependent on the number of points and enhancements.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volume data, edge field, and the pre-generated stipple list. Not limited by texture memory size compared to image-space methods [S2].
*   **Measured Performance:** On a dual processor Intel Xeon 2.00 GHz computer with a Geforce 3 Ti 500 display card, frame rates ranged from 3.5 fps to 60.4 fps for datasets with resolutions from $64 \times 64 \times 64$ to $341 \times 341 \times 93$ [S4].
*   **Strengths:** Interactive preview of large, complex volume datasets; concise, meaningful, and illustrative manner; good temporal coherence; works for both volumes and surfaces [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Temporal aliasing near edges; noisy data affects gradient accuracy; not suitable for photorealistic rendering [S4].
*   **Usage:** Initial volume investigation, data previewing, and medical education/teaching anatomy [S4].

## Background
Stipple Volume Rendering was introduced by Lu et al. in "The Stipple Volume Renderer" (IEEE TVCG 2003) [S2]. It combines principles of artistic and scientific illustration with direct volume rendering to create effective, interactive visualizations [S4]. The technique simulates traditional stipple drawing by generating point lists based on volume characteristics and applying feature enhancements [S4]. It is used for initial volume investigation and medical education [S4].
