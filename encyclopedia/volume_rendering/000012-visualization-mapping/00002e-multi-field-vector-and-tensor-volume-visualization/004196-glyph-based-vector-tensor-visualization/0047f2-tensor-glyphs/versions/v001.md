# Tensor Glyphs
Also called: Eigenvalue-based Glyphs, Superquadric Glyphs

## Overview
Tensor Glyphs are objects placed at every data point in space to represent scalar, vectorial, or tensorial information, specifically overcoming the limitation of colormaps which can display only three distinct metrics or one direction per point [S1, unofficial]. They are set apart from sibling methods like [Line Glyphs](2569-line-glyphs-for-vector-field-visualization.md) and [Arrow Glyphs](2663-arrow-glyphs-for-vector-field-visualization.md) by encoding the full tensor shape (eigenvalues and eigenvectors) into a 3D geometry rather than just direction or magnitude, and from [Interactive Glyph Placement](10455-interactive-glyph-placement-for-tensor-fields.md) by focusing on the geometric definition and rendering of the glyph itself rather than placement algorithms.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A tensor field $T$ where each voxel contains a symmetric positive-definite tensor. The tensor is decomposed into eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ and corresponding orthogonal eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$. A base glyph geometry $G$ (e.g., a unit sphere or superquadric).
*   **Output:** A rendered image where each data point is visualized as a transformed glyph $G_T$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Tensor Representation:** Typically stored as a $3 \times 3$ symmetric matrix or explicitly as the triplet $(\lambda_i, \mathbf{v}_i)$.
*   **Glyph Geometry $G$:** Defined as a set of vertices or an implicit surface equation. For superquadrics, parameters include shape exponents and scale factors.

**Transformation Formula:**
The glyph geometry $G$ is transformed to the tensor glyph geometry $G_T$ using the formula [S1, unofficial]:

$$G_T = R^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda_3 \end{pmatrix} RG$$

Where:
*   $R$ is the rotation matrix constructed from the eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$ as columns.
*   $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ are the eigenvalues representing the scaling along the principal axes.
*   $R^{-1}$ is the inverse of the rotation matrix (equivalent to $R^T$ for orthogonal matrices).

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Decomposition:** For each voxel, compute the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ and eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$ of the tensor $T$.
2.  **Matrix Construction:** Construct the rotation matrix $R = [\mathbf{v}_1 | \mathbf{v}_2 | \mathbf{v}_3]$.
3.  **Scaling Matrix:** Construct the diagonal scaling matrix $S = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)$.
4.  **Transformation:** Apply the transformation $G_T = R^T S R G$ to the base geometry $G$. Note: The source formula uses $R^{-1}$, which for orthogonal rotation matrices is $R^T$.
5.  **Rendering:** Render the transformed glyphs. For superquadrics, use raytracing to evaluate the implicit surface equation [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Eigenvalues:** $\lambda_i \ge 0$.
*   **Glyph Shape Parameters:** For superquadrics, shape exponents control the transition between ellipsoidal and cuboidal shapes.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Continuity (g1):** Minimal changes in neighboring tensors must not cause discontinuities in glyph geometry [S1, unofficial].
*   **Uniqueness (g2):** The mapping from tensor shape to glyph geometry must be unique [S1, unofficial].
*   **Unambiguity (g3):** Glyphs must be recognizable after projection regardless of viewing direction [S1, unofficial].
*   **Degenerate Glyphs:** Simple shapes like boxes/cuboids fail (g3) as they are not distinguishable from all view directions. Ellipsoids also suffer from this ambiguity [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_tensor_glyph(tensor_matrix, base_glyph_geometry):
    """
    Computes the transformed glyph geometry for a given tensor.
    
    Args:
        tensor_matrix: 3x3 symmetric positive-definite matrix.
        base_glyph_geometry: List of vertices or implicit surface function.
        
    Returns:
        transformed_vertices: List of transformed vertices.
    """
    # Step 1: Eigen decomposition
    eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(tensor_matrix)
    # eigenvalues are sorted in ascending order by np.linalg.eigh
    # We assume lambda1, lambda2, lambda3 correspond to the principal axes
    # Note: The source formula implies specific ordering or mapping.
    # Typically, eigenvectors are columns of R.
    
    # Step 2: Construct Rotation Matrix R
    # R is formed by eigenvectors as columns
    R = eigenvectors.T # Shape (3, 3)
    
    # Step 3: Construct Scaling Matrix S
    S = np.diag(eigenvalues)
    
    # Step 4: Compute Transformation Matrix M = R^T * S * R
    # Note: Source formula is R^-1 * S * R. Since R is orthogonal, R^-1 = R^T.
    M = R.T @ S @ R
    
    # Step 5: Apply transformation to base glyph vertices
    transformed_vertices = []
    for vertex in base_glyph_geometry:
        # Homogeneous coordinates for affine transformation if needed
        # Assuming vertex is 3D point
        transformed_vertex = M @ vertex
        transformed_vertices.append(transformed_vertex)
        
    return transformed_vertices
```

A direct analytical expression for visualizing rank two tensors in a 2D plane, designed to be accurate and unambiguous, satisfying requirements such as invariance under isometric domain transformation, scaling invariance, direct encoding of real eigenvalues/eigenvectors, glyph uniqueness, and continual glyph changes.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering thousands of superquadric glyphs is computationally expensive due to the need for triangulation or raytracing. Hlawitschka et al. [HES08] introduced a fast, GPU-based raytracing method to allow real-time rendering of thousands of superquadric glyphs [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the resolution of the glyph geometry (number of vertices/triangles) and the number of data points.
*   **Strengths:** Can display more than three metrics simultaneously (eigenvalues and eigenvectors). Superquadrics satisfy continuity, uniqueness, and unambiguity criteria better than ellipsoids or boxes [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally intensive to render. Hedgehog glyphs (arrays of scaled eigendirections) are nearly unusable for humans to recognize tensor field structures [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When full tensor shape information is critical and interactive exploration of large DTI datasets is required.
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are limited and only directional information is needed.

## Background
Superquadrics were introduced by A. H. Barr in 1981 [Bar81] and suggested for visualizing Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) data by Kindlmann in 2004 [Kin04a, Kin04b]. The method addresses the limitations of colormaps in displaying tensor information.
