# Perturbation Velocity Visualization

Also called: Subtracting free stream velocity

## Overview
Perturbation Velocity Visualization is a technique used in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) to isolate flow disturbances by making the bulk of irrelevant free stream flow invisible [S1, unofficial]. It achieves this by mapping the magnitude of the perturbation velocity vector to opacity [S1, unofficial]. This method sets itself apart from standard velocity visualization by explicitly removing the uniform background flow, thereby highlighting regions of interaction, such as wakes or boundary layers, which would otherwise be obscured by the high opacity of the free stream [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D velocity field $\mathbf{V}(x,y,z)$ and a constant free stream velocity vector $\mathbf{V}_{fs}$.
*   **Output:** A volume rendering where opacity is determined by the magnitude of the perturbation velocity.

**Data Structures:**
*   The velocity field is typically stored as a grid of vectors. Each cell contains three components $(u, v, w)$.
*   The free stream velocity is a constant vector $(u_{fs}, v_{fs}, w_{fs})$.

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Perturbation Velocity:** For every cell in the velocity field, subtract the free stream velocity vector from the local velocity vector to compute the perturbation velocity vector $\mathbf{V}_{pert}$ [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ \mathbf{V}_{pert} = \mathbf{V} - \mathbf{V}_{fs} $$
2.  **Calculate Magnitude:** Compute the Euclidean norm (magnitude) of the resulting perturbation velocity vector [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ |\mathbf{V}_{pert}| = \sqrt{u_{pert}^2 + v_{pert}^2 + w_{pert}^2} $$
3.  **Map to Opacity:** Map the calculated magnitude $|\mathbf{V}_{pert}|$ to the opacity value for volume rendering [S1, unofficial]. Typically, a magnitude of zero (matching the free stream) results in zero opacity, making the free stream invisible, while non-zero magnitudes result in higher opacity [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_perturbation_velocity_visualization(velocity_field, free_stream_velocity):
    """
    velocity_field: 3D array of shape (Nx, Ny, Nz, 3) containing (u, v, w)
    free_stream_velocity: tuple or array of length 3 (u_fs, v_fs, w_fs)
    returns: 3D array of shape (Nx, Ny, Nz) containing opacity values
    """
    # Extract components
    u = velocity_field[:, :, :, 0]
    v = velocity_field[:, :, :, 1]
    w = velocity_field[:, :, :, 2]
    
    u_fs, v_fs, w_fs = free_stream_velocity
    
    # Step 1: Subtract free stream velocity
    u_pert = u - u_fs
    v_pert = v - v_fs
    w_pert = w - w_fs
    
    # Step 2: Calculate magnitude
    magnitude = (u_pert**2 + v_pert**2 + w_pert**2)**0.5
    
    # Step 3: Map magnitude to opacity
    # Note: Specific transfer function (e.g., linear scaling) is not detailed in sources.
    # A common implementation maps magnitude directly to opacity, 
    # potentially normalized by a maximum expected perturbation.
    opacity = magnitude 
    
    return opacity
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **No-slip boundaries:** At solid surfaces, the fluid velocity is zero relative to the surface due to viscosity [S1, unofficial]. Consequently, the perturbation velocity at the boundary is equal to the negative of the free stream velocity ($\mathbf{V}_{pert} = -\mathbf{V}_{fs}$), resulting in a large magnitude [S1, unofficial]. This requires separate treatment to avoid rendering the entire surface as highly opaque or distorting the visualization of the wake [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Perturbation Velocity Component Visualization
This variant visualizes a specific component (typically the x-component) of the perturbation velocity field rather than its magnitude [S1, unofficial]. Negative values of the perturbation velocity x-component indicate locations where the flow is slower than the free stream velocity [S1, unofficial]. These negative values are mapped to cyan and high opacity, while positive values (faster than free stream) are mapped to yellow and near transparency [S1, unofficial]. The transfer function uses the extreme values of the data field to set the scale and offset into the linear color space [S1, unofficial]. This approach is useful for identifying flow reversal or regions of significant deceleration [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N)$, where $N$ is the number of cells in the volume, as it requires a single pass to compute the difference and magnitude.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store the velocity field and the resulting opacity field.
*   **Strengths:** Effectively removes the visual clutter of uniform free stream flow, allowing the viewer to focus on flow disturbances, wakes, and boundary layer interactions [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires accurate knowledge of the free stream velocity vector [S1, unofficial]. It may obscure the absolute velocity magnitude, making it difficult to assess flow speed without the context of the free stream [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When the primary interest is in flow structures caused by obstacles or interactions, rather than the absolute speed of the fluid [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When the absolute velocity magnitude is critical or when the free stream velocity is not uniform or well-defined [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The method was introduced in the context of volume rendering for Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) [S1, unofficial]. It was developed to address the challenge of visualizing vector fields where the dominant free stream flow obscures interesting features [S1, unofficial]. The technique is part of a broader effort to apply volume rendering to CFD data, leveraging the ability to map scalar fields (like perturbation magnitude) to visual properties like opacity [S1, unofficial].
