# Integral Surface

Also called: Path Surface, Rake, Stream Surface

## Overview
An integral surface is a geometric construct that generalizes streamlines and pathlines to two dimensions by sweeping a seeding curve through a vector field. It serves as a surface representation of flow, where the surface is generated by integrating the trajectories of all points along a one-dimensional seeding curve (also called a rake). It sets itself apart from single streamline tracing by providing a continuous surface topology that captures the collective behavior of a flow region, and from streaklines by tracking particles released simultaneously from the curve rather than continuously over time [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A vector field $v(\mathbf{x}, t)$ (steady or time-varying), a univariate seeding curve $c(s)$ mapping a parameter interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$, and a time interval $[t_0, t]$.
*   **Output:** A parametric surface $S(s, t)$ defined over the domain $I \times [t_0, t]$.

**Data Structures:**
The surface is represented parametrically. For implementation, this is typically discretized into a grid of points $(s_i, t_j)$ or a triangulated mesh where vertices correspond to integrated particle positions.

**Mathematical Definition:**
For a time-varying velocity field $v$, the integral surface $S(s, t)$ is defined by the integral equation [S1, unofficial]:

$$
S(s, t) = c(s) + \int_{t_0}^{t} v(\tau, S(s, \tau)) \, d\tau
$$

Where:
*   $s$ is the parameter along the seeding curve $c$.
*   $t$ is the time parameter.
*   $c(s)$ is the initial position of the particle at time $t_0$.
*   $v(\tau, \mathbf{x})$ is the velocity vector at time $\tau$ and position $\mathbf{x}$.

In a steady flow field, $v$ does not depend on $t$, and the surface becomes a stream surface. If the seeding curve $c$ varies over time, the definition characterizes a generalized path surface. Lines on the surface for a fixed $t$ are timelines [S1, unofficial].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Discretize the Seeding Curve:** Sample the curve $c(s)$ at discrete intervals $s_i$ to create a set of seed points.
2.  **Integrate Trajectories:** For each seed point $s_i$, integrate the ordinary differential equation $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = v(t, \mathbf{x})$ from $t_0$ to $t_{max}$ to generate a trajectory line. Standard numerical integrators (e.g., RK4) are used [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Construct Surface:** The collection of these trajectory lines forms the integral surface. The surface is parameterized by $s$ (along the rake) and $t$ (along the flow).

**On-the-Fly Generation (Hultquist's Method):**
An alternative implementation approach, first investigated by Hultquist, propagates a front of particles through the flow field [S2]:
1.  **Front Propagation:** Maintain a front of particles forming a surface segment.
2.  **Adaptive Integration:** Integrate particle trajectories as needed during the propagation of the front.
3.  **Triangulation:** Perform on-the-fly triangulation of the surface using a greedy approach to connect the integrated points into a mesh.
4.  **Resolution Control:** Treat divergence and convergence of particles in the advancing front using a simple distance criterion. Insert new particles where the distance between existing front particles exceeds a threshold, and remove particles where the distance is too small, ensuring a balanced resolution of the surface [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Self-Intersection:** In time-varying fields, the resulting lines may no longer be tangential to the flow field and may self-intersect [S1, unofficial].
*   **Parameterization Distortion:** Stretching and divergence of the surface can distort the parameterization, which is particularly problematic if texture mapping (e.g., stream arrows) is applied, resulting in very large or small visual elements [S2].
*   **Higher Dimensions:** The definition generalizes to higher-dimensional seeding geometry, creating path volumes and time surfaces, which increases computational complexity significantly [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Magnetosphere Field Line Tracing](12012-magnetosphere-field-line-tracing.md)
This method generates a synthetic dataset of Earth's magnetospheric magnetic field lines by tracing dipole field lines from ionospheric footpoints and magnetosheath draped lines, encoding field intensity to grayscale, and outputting the result as a compact JSON file. It serves as a data preparation step for volumetric visualization, distinct from general stream line tracing by its specific geometric constraints (ionospheric seeding, magnetopause boundaries) and reliance on an analytical dipole model rather than a grid-based vector field. It builds on [Integral Surface](11268-integral-surface.md) by providing the discrete point-cloud input required for surface generation.

### [Particle Traces on 2D Surfaces](12772-particle-traces-on-2d-surfaces.md)
Particle traces on 2D surfaces is a visualization technique that represents the magnitude and direction of a 3D vector field on arbitrary 2D surfaces (not necessarily contour surfaces) by drawing long, evenly spaced particle trace lines. Unlike random seed placement which does not guarantee coverage near every point, this method uses a hierarchical integer lattice to ensure some seed point is near every point on the surface, avoiding density issues where the visualization becomes too dense or too sparse. It sets itself apart from its parent [Integral Surface](11268-integral-surface.md) by explicitly breaking traces into segments of contrasting color to resolve directional ambiguity in still images and by projecting the vector field onto the surface geometry rather than integrating within the volume.

### Integral Surface Generation
A method to compute integral surfaces by propagating a front of particles through a flow field, integrating trajectories as needed, and performing on-the-fly triangulation [S2]. It was first investigated by Hultquist [S2]. The algorithm integrates particle trajectories dynamically during front propagation and uses a greedy approach for triangulation. It handles particle divergence and convergence using a distance criterion to insert or remove particles, ensuring balanced surface resolution [S2].

## Operating characteristics
**Time Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of seed points and the integration time step. The on-the-fly method adds overhead for dynamic mesh management and distance checks.
**Memory Complexity:** Proportional to the number of particles in the front and the stored trajectory history.
**Strengths:** Provides a continuous surface representation of flow, useful for visualizing flow topology and transport. The on-the-fly method allows for adaptive resolution.
**Limitations:** Susceptible to self-intersection in complex time-varying flows. Parameterization distortion can complicate texture mapping. Computationally expensive for high-resolution surfaces or long integration times.
**When to use:** When a continuous surface representation of a flow region is needed, such as for visualizing shear layers or coherent structures.
**When not to use:** When only individual streamline topology is required, or when the flow field is highly chaotic leading to excessive surface folding and self-intersection.

## Background
Integral surfaces were first investigated by Hultquist [S2]. Advanced visualization techniques, such as texture mapping arrows on stream surfaces (stream arrows) to convey local flow direction, were introduced by Löffelmann [S2]. This work addressed the challenge of conveying directionality on the surface but initially did not account for the distortion of the parameterization caused by surface stretching and divergence. Subsequent work by the same authors introduced regular, hierarchical tiling of texture space to adjust for these distortions [S2]. The concept is formally defined in the context of multi-field visualization, distinguishing between path surfaces (time-varying) and stream surfaces (steady) [S1, unofficial].
