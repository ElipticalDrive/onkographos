# Stream Surfaces

Also called: Streamsheet, Van Wijk Stream Surface Method, Van Wijk's method

## Overview
Stream surfaces visualize steady-state vector fields by stitching together adjacent streamlines to form a continuous geometric surface, solving the problem of ascertaining overall flow direction in areas where streamlines curl around each other [S1, unofficial]. The method builds on [Stream Lines](4796-stream-lines.md) and differs from siblings [Adaptive Stream Line Seeding](5604-adaptive-stream-line-seeding.md) and [Directional Textured Tracelines](6965-directional-textured-tracelines.md) by explicitly constructing a 3D manifold rather than relying on discrete trajectories or 2D texture mapping [S1, unofficial]. A single surface can be rendered efficiently using standard graphics hardware, though multiple surfaces would likely occlude each other [S1, unofficial]. The method suffers from the loss of tangential flow direction information [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A steady-state vector field $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})$ defined over a volume [S1, unofficial]. A starting curve $\mathcal{C}$ (line segment or closed loop) [S1, unofficial]. A discretized regular grid covering the volume [S1, unofficial].
**Outputs:** A triangular mesh representing the stream surface [S1, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
1. **Grid:** A regular 3D grid covering the volume [S1, unofficial]. Each interior grid point stores a scalar value representing the boundary intersection position or identifier [S1, unofficial].
2. **Boundary:** A set of points on the volume boundary $\partial \Omega$ [S1, unofficial].
3. **Surface Mesh:** A collection of triangles generated via iso-contouring [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1. **Precompute Grid:** Discretize the volume into a regular grid [S1, unofficial].
2. **Backward Integration:** For each interior grid point $\mathbf{p}_i$, integrate the vector field backward in time until the trajectory intersects the volume boundary $\partial \Omega$ at point $\mathbf{b}_i$ [S1, unofficial]. Store the position or identifier of $\mathbf{b}_i$ as the scalar value $f(\mathbf{p}_i)$ [S1, unofficial].
3. **Boundary Scalar Assignment:** Place scalar function values on the boundary points based on the starting curve $\mathcal{C}$ to result in a scalar function defined over the regular grid [S1, unofficial]. Not given in the sources. [S1, unofficial]
4. **Iso-Contour Extraction:** Extract the iso-surface of the scalar field $f(\mathbf{x})$ defined over the grid to construct the stream surface [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The stream surface is defined as the infinite set of streamlines emanating from the starting curve $\mathcal{C}$, assuming continuous flow where adjacent streamlines cannot suddenly diverge in an infinitesimally small time step [S1, unofficial]. For steady-state flows, the surface locally separates the three-dimensional flow such that streamlines to one side are restricted to that portion of the volume [S1, unofficial]. If $\mathcal{C}$ is a closed curve, the separation divides the flow into two disjoint parts: inside and outside the surface [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
* Grid resolution: Not given in the sources. [S1, unofficial]
* Integration step size: Not given in the sources. [S1, unofficial]

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
* **Unsteady Flows:** The method assumes steady-state flows and its utility is lost for unsteady flows [S1, unofficial].
* **Boundary Conditions:** The flow must enter or exit the volume boundaries for the technique to work; artificial boundaries can be placed to rectify this if they do not [S1, unofficial].
* **Distortion:** The algorithm suffers from severe distortion of the surface mesh as the flow diverges around obstacles, requiring an adaptive refinement algorithm that can handle tears in the surface [S1, unofficial].
* **Direction Loss:** The flow direction tangential to the surface is lost because merging many streamlines onto a continuous surface prevents determining whether the flow went straight down the surface or weaved back and forth [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Stream-Balls](7700-stream-balls.md)
Stream-Balls is a technique for visualizing steady-state vector fields that constructs a scalar potential field from advected particles or streamlines using blobby functions, then extracts iso-contours to mimic stream surfaces. It sets itself apart from its parent [Stream Surfaces](7696-stream-surfaces.md) by replacing explicit geometric stitching with an implicit scalar field, which allows the resulting surface to gracefully split apart in areas of high divergence and break apart in areas of high velocity to indicate magnitude. Choose it when you need a topology-preserving representation that naturally handles flow divergence without manual mesh tearing.

### [LIC on Stream Surfaces](7721-lic-on-stream-surfaces.md)
LIC on Stream Surfaces is a visualization method that superimposes a texture generated by the Line Integral Convolution (LIC) algorithm onto stream surfaces to resolve the lack of directional information inherent in the base geometry. While stream surfaces effectively show the topology of steady-state flows by stitching adjacent streamlines, they appear as blank, directionless sheets; this method solves that ambiguity by encoding local flow direction into a continuous noise texture mapped to the surface. It distinguishes itself from standard stream surfaces by providing dense, global flow pattern visibility, though it is computationally more expensive than optimized variants like Fast LIC. Choose it when precise flow directionality is required across the entire surface.

### Streak Surface
Models flow regions passing through a domain by tracking particles emerging from a seeding curve at different ages in a time-dependent flow [S2, unofficial]. The integral surface is defined as $S(r, s, t) = c(s) + \int_{t-r}^{t} f(S(r - (t - \tau), s, \tau), \tau) d\tau$, where $r \in [0, t]$ is the particle age parameter, $s$ indexes the seeding curve, and $f$ is the velocity field [S2, unofficial]. The age parameter $r$ identifies individual particle instances and ranges from $0$ to $t$ [S2, unofficial]. Choose it when visualizing time-dependent flows where particle history and age are critical to understanding transport pathways.

## Operating characteristics
* **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources. [S1, unofficial]
* **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources. [S1, unofficial]
* **Strengths:** Efficient rendering of a single surface using standard graphics hardware; provides a clear separation of flow regions (especially with closed seed curves) [S1, unofficial].
* **Limitations:** High computational cost for precomputation; severe mesh distortion requires adaptive refinement; loss of tangential flow direction; inapplicable to unsteady flows [S1, unofficial].
* **When to Use:** Steady-state flows where a unified geometric representation of flow separation is needed. [S1, unofficial]
* **When Not to Use:** Unsteady flows, or when precise tangential flow direction on the surface is critical without additional texturing. [S1, unofficial]

## Background
Stream surfaces were introduced by Hultquist as an extension of streamlines to solve the difficulty of ascertaining overall flow in 3D where streamlines curl around each other [S1, unofficial]. Van Wijk proposed a simpler scheme for closed environments using a grid-based backward integration and iso-contouring approach [S1, unofficial]. Interrante et al. later proposed using Line Integral Convolution to add directional information to these surfaces [S1, unofficial].
