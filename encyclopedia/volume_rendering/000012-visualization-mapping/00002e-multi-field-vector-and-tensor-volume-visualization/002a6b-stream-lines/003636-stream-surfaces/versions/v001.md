# Stream Surfaces

Also called: Streamsheet, Van Wijk Stream Surface Method, Van Wijk's method

## Overview
Stream surfaces visualize steady-state vector fields by stitching adjacent streamlines into a continuous surface, solving the ambiguity of overall flow direction in regions where streamlines curl around each other [S1, unofficial]. Unlike [Stream Lines](4796-stream-lines.md), which show individual trajectories, a stream surface represents the infinite set of streamlines emanating from a starting curve, locally separating the 3D flow into distinct portions [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A steady-state vector field $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})$ defined on a domain; a starting curve $\mathcal{C}$ (line segment or closed loop); a discretized volume grid.
**Outputs:** A triangular mesh representing the stream surface.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Grid:** A regular 3D grid storing scalar values. For Van Wijk's method, this grid stores the boundary intersection index or coordinate for each interior point.
2.  **Boundary Map:** A mapping from interior grid points to their corresponding intersection point on the domain boundary.
3.  **Surface Mesh:** A triangle mesh generated via iso-contouring.

**Algorithm (Van Wijk's Scheme for Closed Environments):**
This method constructs the surface by tracing streamlines backward from the interior to the boundary, effectively mapping the interior to a scalar field defined on the boundary [S1, unofficial].

1.  **Precompute Grid:** Discretize the volume of interest into a regular 3D grid.
2.  **Backward Integration:** For each interior grid point $\mathbf{p}_i$:
    *   Integrate the vector field $\mathbf{v}$ backward in time (i.e., solve $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = -\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})$) starting from $\mathbf{p}_i$.
    *   Continue integration until the trajectory intersects the domain boundary $\partial \Omega$.
    *   Store the boundary intersection point $\mathbf{b}_i$ associated with $\mathbf{p}_i$.
3.  **Assign Scalar Values:** Assign a scalar value $s_i$ to each interior grid point $\mathbf{p}_i$ based on the position of $\mathbf{b}_i$ on the boundary.
    *   *Note:* The specific scalar function is not detailed in the sources, but it must uniquely identify the boundary location to allow iso-contouring [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Iso-Contouring:** Extract the iso-contour surface of the resulting scalar field defined over the regular grid. This surface constitutes the stream surface [S1, unofficial].

**Algorithm (Hultquist's Scheme for General Cases):**
For open environments or when adaptive refinement is required:
1.  **Seeding:** Place seed points along the starting curve $\mathcal{C}$.
2.  **Integration:** Integrate forward in time to generate streamlines.
3.  **Stitching:** Connect adjacent streamlines to form a mesh.
4.  **Adaptive Refinement:** Detect areas of high distortion (divergence) and refine the mesh locally. Allow for tears in the surface if the flow diverges significantly around obstacles [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Time Step:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Grid Resolution:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Starting Curve:** Must be a line segment or closed loop. If closed, the surface separates the flow into two disjoint parts: bounded and unbounded [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Unsteady Flows:** The method is invalid for unsteady flows; the utility is lost because streamlines are time-dependent [S1, unofficial].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The flow must enter or exit the volume boundaries for Van Wijk's method to work. If the flow is entirely internal, artificial boundaries must be placed [S1, unofficial].
*   **Distortion:** The surface mesh suffers severe distortion as flow diverges around obstacles, requiring adaptive refinement [S1, unofficial].
*   **Direction Loss:** Merging streamlines into a surface loses the tangential flow direction information; it is unclear if the flow moved straight or weaved [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Stream-Balls](7700-stream-balls.md)
Constructs a scalar potential field from advected particles using blobby functions and extracts iso-contours. It sets itself apart by replacing explicit geometric stitching with an implicit scalar field, allowing the surface to gracefully split in high divergence areas.

### [LIC on Stream Surfaces](7721-lic-on-stream-surfaces.md)
Superimposes a texture using Line Integral Convolution (LIC) to solve the lack of direction information on the surface.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of grid points and the length of backward integration. Van Wijk's method requires one backward streamline per grid point [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the grid and the boundary map [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Single surfaces can be rendered efficiently using standard graphics hardware. It resolves the ambiguity of curling streamlines [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Expensive due to the need for adaptive refinement in general cases. Loses tangential flow direction. Inapplicable to unsteady flows. Multiple surfaces occlude each other [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** Steady-state flows in closed environments (Van Wijk) or when global flow separation is needed (Hultquist).
*   **When Not to Use:** Unsteady flows, or when precise tangential flow direction visualization is required without additional texturing.

## Background
Stream surfaces were introduced by Hultquist [1] as an extension of streamlines to solve the problem of ascertaining overall flow in areas of substantial directional change where streamlines curl around each other [S1, unofficial]. Van Wijk [2] later proposed a simpler scheme for closed environments using backward integration and iso-contouring. Interrante et al. [3] proposed using Line Integral Convolution (LIC) [4] to address the loss of direction on stream surfaces.
