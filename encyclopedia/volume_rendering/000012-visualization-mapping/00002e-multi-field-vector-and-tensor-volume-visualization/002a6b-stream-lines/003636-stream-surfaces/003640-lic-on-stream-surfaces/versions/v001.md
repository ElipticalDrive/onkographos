# LIC on Stream Surfaces
Also called: Line Integral Convolution on Stream Surfaces

## Overview
LIC on Stream Surfaces is a visualization method that superimposes a texture or pattern onto stream surfaces to resolve the lack of directional information inherent in standard stream surface rendering [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem where stream surfaces, while efficient to render as geometry, fail to convey flow direction in areas of substantial directional change or occlusion [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from its siblings by combining the geometric efficiency of stream surfaces with the directional clarity of Line Integral Convolution (LIC), whereas [Stream-Balls](7700-stream-balls.md) uses volumetric primitives instead of surfaces.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
1. A steady-state vector field $\mathbf{V}(x,y,z)$.
2. A scalar noise field $N(x,y,z)$, typically white noise.
3. A stream surface geometry, constructed by integrating streamlines from a seed curve and triangulating the resulting surface [S1, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
A rendered image where the stream surface texture correlates with the local flow direction, indicating orientation and magnitude.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Stream Surface:** A mesh of triangles. Vertices are points on streamlines. Edges connect vertices along streamlines and across the seed curve.
*   **Noise Field:** A 3D texture or grid of scalar values.
*   **LIC Buffer:** A 2D or 3D buffer storing the convolution result for each pixel or voxel on the surface.

**Steps:**
1.  **Generate Stream Surface:** Integrate streamlines from a seed curve to form the surface geometry [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Rasterize:** Project the 3D stream surface onto the 2D image plane. For each pixel covered by the surface, determine the local surface normal and the tangent vector of the streamline passing through that point.
3.  **Define Convolution Kernel:** For each pixel $p$ on the surface, define a line segment (or streamline arc) centered at $p$ and oriented in the direction of the flow vector $\mathbf{V}(p)$ [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Integrate Noise:** Sample the noise field $N$ along the defined line segment. Sum the sampled values to compute the LIC value $L(p)$ for that pixel [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ L(p) = \int_{-k}^{k} N(p + t \cdot \mathbf{V}(p)) \, dt $$
    where $k$ is the integration length. In discrete implementation, this is a sum over rasterized pixels along the streamline segment [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Render:** Use the LIC value $L(p)$ as the color or opacity for the pixel, effectively "painting" the flow direction onto the surface.

**Parameters:**
*   **Integration Length ($k$):** Determines the extent of the convolution. Not given in the sources.
*   **Noise Resolution:** Resolution of the white noise field. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **High Curvature/Vorticity:** In regions of high curvature or vorticity, the LIC spots may blur out the flow structure [S1, unofficial].
*   **Occlusion:** Single surfaces can have substantial occlusion, requiring careful examination from many viewpoints [S1, unofficial].
*   **Boundaries:** The flow must enter or exit the volume boundaries for the stream surface technique to work; artificial boundaries may be needed [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Optimized LIC
An extension to LIC that speeds up computation by avoiding redundant integrations and reusing LIC values for adjacent pixels on the same streamline [S1, unofficial]. It is chosen when performance is critical and the flow field is steady. It differs from standard LIC by exploiting the spatial coherence of streamlines.
1.  **Observation 1:** A streamline passes through many pixels. Only one rasterized streamline is sufficient to produce convolution results for all those pixels, avoiding redundant numerical integrations [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Observation 2:** Adjacent pixels along the same streamline use very similar sets of pixel values for convolution [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Step:** Reuse the LIC value computed for one pixel by its neighbors with small modifications (e.g., adding the noise value of the new pixel entering the kernel and subtracting the one leaving) [S1, unofficial].

### Dye-Advection LIC
A 3D extension of LIC that introduces dye advection into the computation, using the 'smearing' nature of LIC to simulate dye advection [S1, unofficial]. It is chosen when dynamic injection of flow markers is required. It differs from standard LIC by allowing user interaction to inject dye.
1.  **Step:** Use the smearing nature of LIC to simulate dye advection [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Step:** Include both regular LIC textures and 3D particle traces in the visualization [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Step:** Allow the user to dynamically inject dye at different locations using an efficient algorithm to locate "dyed" regions for fast updates [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Standard LIC is computationally expensive due to the integration of noise along streamlines for every pixel [S1, unofficial]. Optimized LIC reduces this by reusing calculations, gaining significant savings in computing time [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the noise field, the stream surface mesh, and the LIC buffer. 3D extensions may require additional memory for particle traces [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Provides clear directional information on surfaces; efficient rendering of single surfaces using standard graphics hardware [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive without optimization; single surfaces can occlude themselves; multiple surfaces may occlude each other; semi-transparent rendering requires slow ray-tracing for correct sorting [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When directional clarity is needed on stream surfaces and performance allows for standard LIC or optimized variants.
*   **When Not to Use:** When multiple surfaces are needed and occlusion is a major issue, or when real-time interaction with dynamic dye injection is required without 3D extensions.

## Background
The method was proposed by Interrante et al., building on the Line Integral Convolution algorithm by Van Wijk [S1, unofficial]. It addresses the limitations of stream surfaces, which were introduced as an extension of streamlines to visualize steady-state flows [S1, unofficial]. The technique is part of the broader field of flow visualization for CFD using volume rendering [S1, unofficial].
