# LIC on Stream Surfaces
Also called: Line Integral Convolution on Stream Surfaces

## Overview
LIC on Stream Surfaces is a visualization technique that superimposes a texture generated by the Line Integral Convolution (LIC) algorithm onto stream surfaces to resolve the inherent lack of directional information in the parent method [S1, unofficial]. While [Stream Surfaces](7696-stream-surfaces.md) effectively show the geometry of flow paths, they appear as blank, directionless sheets; this method solves that by aligning noise patterns with the local flow vector, providing a coherent image of flow direction and structure across the surface [S1, unofficial]. It is distinct from its sibling [Stream-Balls](7700-stream-balls.md) and its child [Line Integral Convolution (LIC)](7940-line-integral-convolution-lic.md) by requiring an intermediate geometric surface representation rather than operating directly on the volume or 2D domain [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
1. A steady-state 3D vector field $\mathbf{V}(x,y,z)$.
2. A set of seed points defining the boundary of the stream surface.
3. A scalar noise texture $N(u,v)$ or a procedural noise function.

**Outputs:**
1. A rendered image of the stream surface where the surface texture encodes flow direction.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Stream Surface Mesh:** A polygonal mesh (typically triangles or quads) representing the surface. Vertices are defined by integrating streamlines from seed points.
*   **LIC Texture Buffer:** A 2D array storing the convolution result for the surface parameterization.

**Steps:**
1.  **Construct Stream Surface:** Integrate streamlines from seed points to generate the geometry of the stream surface. The flow must enter or exit volume boundaries, or artificial boundaries must be placed [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Parameterize Surface:** Map the 3D surface geometry to a 2D parameter space $(u,v)$ to define the domain for the LIC texture.
3.  **Compute LIC on Surface:**
    *   For each pixel in the 2D parameter space, trace a streamline backward and forward along the surface tangent vectors defined by the vector field $\mathbf{V}$.
    *   Sample the noise function $N$ along this path.
    *   Compute the convolution integral:
        $$ L(u,v) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} N(u(s), v(s)) \cdot h(s) \, ds $$
        where $h(s)$ is a low-pass filter kernel (often a box filter or Gaussian) and $s$ is the arc length along the streamline.
    *   Normalize the result to fit the display range.
4.  **Render:** Map the computed LIC texture $L(u,v)$ onto the 3D stream surface mesh using standard texture mapping.

**Parameters:**
*   **Integration Step Size:** Small enough to capture flow curvature but large enough for performance.
*   **Filter Kernel Width:** Determines the "smearing" length of the texture, balancing detail and noise reduction.
*   **Seed Point Density:** Controls the resolution and coverage of the stream surface.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The method requires flow to enter or exit the volume boundaries. If the flow is closed or recirculating without crossing boundaries, artificial boundaries must be introduced [S1, unofficial].
*   **Occlusion:** Single surfaces can have substantial occlusion, requiring careful viewpoint selection or semi-transparent rendering with correct sorting, which may be slow [S1, unofficial].
*   **Direction Ambiguity:** Without the LIC texture, the surface has no inherent direction; the LIC computation must correctly align with the vector field direction to avoid ambiguous or reversed flow visualization.

## Subtopics

### Optimized LIC
An extension to LIC that speeds up computation by avoiding redundant integrations and reusing LIC values for adjacent pixels on the same streamline [S1, unofficial]. It is chosen when performance is critical for high-resolution stream surfaces. The method relies on two observations: first, a single rasterized streamline is sufficient for the convolution results of all pixels it passes through, avoiding redundant numerical integrations; second, adjacent pixels along the same streamline use very similar sets of pixel values for convolution, allowing the LIC value computed for one pixel to be reused by its neighbors with small modifications [S1, unofficial].

### Dye-Advection LIC
A 3D extension of LIC that introduces dye advection into the computation, using the 'smearing' nature of LIC to simulate dye advection [S1, unofficial]. It is chosen when dynamic, interactive visualization of flow features is required. The method uses the smearing nature of LIC to simulate dye advection, includes both regular LIC textures and 3D particle traces, and allows the user to dynamically inject dye at different locations using an efficient algorithm to locate "dyed" regions for fast updates [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** High. LIC is computationally expensive due to the integration along streamlines for every pixel. Optimized LIC reduces this by reusing computations [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Moderate. Requires storage for the stream surface mesh and the LIC texture buffer.
*   **Performance:** Stalling and Hege's optimized method gains significant savings in computing the LIC by reducing the number of streamlines computed and speeding up the convolution [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Provides clear directional information on complex flow surfaces; leverages standard graphics hardware for efficient surface rendering.
*   **Limitations:** Single surfaces can occlude themselves, requiring slow ray-tracing for correct semi-transparent sorting; computationally intensive without optimization; requires flow to cross boundaries or artificial boundaries to be defined [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When visualizing steady-state flows on specific surfaces where directionality is crucial and occlusion can be managed.
*   **When Not to Use:** For large-scale 3D flow visualization where multiple surfaces would occlude each other excessively, or when interactive performance is critical without optimization techniques [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The method was proposed by Interrante et al. as a solution to the lack of direction on stream surfaces, building on Van Wijk's work on constructing stream surfaces from iso-contour surfaces of a scalar function [S1, unofficial]. It utilizes the Line Integral Convolution (LIC) algorithm, originally developed for 2D vector fields, to texture the 3D surface. The approach addresses the limitations of streamlines in areas of substantial directional change and the occlusion issues of multiple surfaces.
