# LIC on Stream Surfaces
Also called: Line Integral Convolution on Stream Surfaces

## Overview
LIC on Stream Surfaces is a visualization technique that superimposes a texture generated by the Line Integral Convolution (LIC) algorithm onto a stream surface to resolve the inherent lack of directional information in the geometry [S1, unofficial]. While stream surfaces effectively show the topology of steady-state flows by stitching adjacent streamlines, they appear as smooth, featureless surfaces that obscure local flow direction; this method solves that problem by adding high-frequency directional cues directly to the surface [S1, unofficial]. It differs from standard stream surfaces by providing dense, local flow orientation without requiring the user to interpret the curvature of the surface itself, and it differs from global LIC by restricting the texture to a specific 2D manifold within the 3D volume rather than the entire domain [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
1. A steady-state 3D vector field $\mathbf{V}(x,y,z)$.
2. A set of seed points or a seed curve defining the stream surface.
3. A random noise texture (typically white noise).
4. An integration step size $h$ and integration length $L$.

**Outputs:**
A rendered image of the stream surface with an LIC texture overlay indicating flow direction.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Stream Surface:** A mesh (triangle strip or polygon soup) representing the iso-contour surface of the integrated flow field. Vertices are stored as 3D coordinates $(x,y,z)$.
*   **LIC Texture:** A 2D array of scalar values representing the convolution result, mapped to the surface geometry.

**Steps:**
1.  **Construct Stream Surface:** Integrate streamlines from seed points to generate the stream surface geometry. The flow must enter or exit volume boundaries, or artificial boundaries must be placed [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Rasterize Surface:** Project the 3D stream surface onto the 2D image plane. Identify the set of pixels covered by the surface.
3.  **Compute LIC for Surface Pixels:** For each pixel $(u,v)$ covered by the surface:
    a. Map the pixel coordinates to the 3D surface location to obtain the starting point $\mathbf{p}_0$ on the surface.
    b. Trace a streamline through the vector field starting at $\mathbf{p}_0$ for length $L$ (forward and backward).
    c. Sample the random noise texture along this 3D streamline path.
    d. Compute the convolution integral:
       $$ I(\mathbf{p}_0) = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} N(\mathbf{p}(t)) \, dt $$
       where $N$ is the noise function and $\mathbf{p}(t)$ is the position on the streamline at parameter $t$.
    e. Normalize the result to the range $[0, 1]$ or $[-1, 1]$.
4.  **Render:** Map the computed LIC values to colors and render the surface.

**Parameters:**
*   Integration length $L$: Determines the scale of flow features visualized. Not given in the sources.
*   Step size $h$: Determines integration accuracy. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The flow must enter or exit the volume boundaries for the technique to work correctly; otherwise, artificial boundaries must be introduced [S1, unofficial].
*   **Occlusion:** Stream surfaces can occlude themselves, requiring careful depth sorting or semi-transparent rendering, which may be slow if ray-tracing is used [S1, unofficial].
*   **Direction Ambiguity:** Without LIC, the surface geometry alone does not indicate flow direction; the LIC texture is essential for this [S1, unofficial].

def render_lic_on_stream_surface(vector_field, seed_points, noise_texture, integration_length, step_size):
    # 1. Construct stream surface
    stream_surface = construct_stream_surface(vector_field, seed_points)
    
    # 2. Rasterize surface to get covered pixels
    surface_pixels = rasterize_surface(stream_surface)
    
    # 3. Compute LIC for each pixel
    lic_values = {}
    for pixel in surface_pixels:
        # Map pixel to 3D point on surface
        p0 = get_3d_point_on_surface(pixel, stream_surface)
        
        # Trace streamline
        streamline_points = trace_streamline(vector_field, p0, integration_length, step_size)
        
        # Sample noise along streamline
        noise_samples = []
        for point in streamline_points:
            # Sample noise texture at 3D point (requires texture lookup in 3D or projection)
            # Note: Standard LIC is 2D; 3D LIC requires mapping 3D path to 2D noise or using 3D noise
            # The source implies standard LIC application, likely assuming 2D projection or specific mapping
            noise_val = sample_noise(noise_texture, point)
            noise_samples.append(noise_val)
        
        # Compute convolution
        lic_value = sum(noise_samples) * step_size
        lic_values[pixel] = normalize(lic_value)
        
    # 4. Render

## Subtopics

### [Dye-Advection LIC](7865-dye-advection-lic.md)
Dye-Advection LIC is a 3D extension of LIC that simulates the advection of colored dye within a vector field by exploiting the "smearing" nature of the LIC algorithm. It solves the problem of visualizing local flow features, such as wavefronts or specific material paths, which are difficult to observe in standard LIC due to the lack of directional correspondence between neighboring streamlines. Unlike standard LIC, which produces symmetric textures, this method allows users to dynamically inject dye at arbitrary locations, ensuring the color propagates only in the forward flow direction.

### [Line Integral Convolution (LIC)](7940-line-integral-convolution-lic.md)
Line Integral Convolution (LIC) is a texture-based flow visualization method that generates a scalar field by convolving a white noise texture with a one-dimensional low-pass filter along the streamlines of a vector field. It solves the problem of visualizing global flow direction densely across the entire field without requiring the selection of seed points, which is a limitation of streamline-based methods. Unlike streamlines that show individual particle paths, LIC produces streak-like textures that intuitively display the flow direction everywhere, making it suitable for global overviews rather than local trajectory tracing.

### [Fabric-like Visualization of Tensor Field Data](10568-fabric-like-visualization-of-tensor-field-data.md)
Fabric-like visualization is a multi-pass image-space rendering technique that visualizes symmetric second-order tensor fields on arbitrary, non-self-intersecting surfaces by mapping the two surface-projected eigendirections as thread-like structures. It solves the problem of visualizing complex directional data on complex geometries without requiring explicit surface parameterization or 3D noise textures, distinguishing itself from standard Line Integral Convolution (LIC) by using smooth reaction-diffusion noise instead of high-frequency white noise to allow the compositing of two distinct eigenvector textures.

### Optimized LIC
Optimized LIC is an extension to LIC that speeds up computation by avoiding redundant integrations and reusing LIC values for adjacent pixels on the same streamline [S1, unofficial]. It is based on two key observations: first, a streamline starting from any point in the domain actually passes through many pixels, so only one rasterized streamline is sufficient to produce the convolution results for those pixels, avoiding redundant numerical integrations; second, adjacent pixels along the same streamline use very similar sets of pixel values for the convolution, so the LIC value computed for one pixel can be reused by its neighbors with small modifications, accelerating the convolutions [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Standard LIC is computationally expensive due to the need to integrate streamlines for every pixel [S1, unofficial]. Optimized LIC reduces this by reusing integrations for adjacent pixels on the same streamline [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the stream surface geometry and the LIC texture values. Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** Provides clear directional information on stream surfaces; efficient rendering of single surfaces using standard graphics hardware [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Multiple surfaces can occlude each other, requiring careful examination from many viewpoints or slow ray-tracing for correct sorting [S1, unofficial]. The flow must enter or exit volume boundaries [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When visualizing steady-state flows where local direction on a specific surface is critical.
*   **When not to use:** When visualizing complex, occluding multi-surface scenes where performance is a concern, or when the flow does not cross volume boundaries.

## Background
The technique was proposed by Interrante and others, who suggested using the Line Integral Convolution (LIC) algorithm to solve the lack of direction on stream surfaces [S1, unofficial]. LIC itself was introduced by Van Wijk [S1, unofficial]. The optimization by Stalling and Hege was proposed to speed up the process [S1, unofficial]. Shen et al. proposed introducing dye advection into LIC computation for 3D visualization [S1, unofficial].
