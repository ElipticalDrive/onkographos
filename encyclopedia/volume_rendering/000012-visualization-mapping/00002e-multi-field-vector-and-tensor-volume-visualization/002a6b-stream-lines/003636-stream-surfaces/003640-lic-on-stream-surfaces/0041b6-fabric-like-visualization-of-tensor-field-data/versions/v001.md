# Fabric-like Visualization of Tensor Field Data

Also called: Fabric-like visualization, Image-space tensor fabric visualization

## Overview
Fabric-like visualization is a multi-pass image-space rendering technique that visualizes symmetric second-order tensor fields on arbitrary, non-self-intersecting surfaces by mapping the two surface-projected eigendirections as thread-like structures [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of visualizing complex directional data on complex geometries without requiring explicit surface parameterization or 3D noise textures, distinguishing itself from standard Line Integral Convolution (LIC) by using smooth reaction-diffusion noise instead of high-frequency white noise to allow the compositing of two distinct eigenvector textures [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
The method consists of four major rendering passes: Projection, Silhouette Detection, Advection, and Compositing [S1, unofficial]. Data is transferred between passes using textures [S1, unofficial].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**: A surface representation (mesh or implicit) providing depth and normals; a 2D or 3D tensor field defined in world space; camera parameters (modelview $M_M$ and projection $M_P$ matrices).
*   **Outputs**: A final image where the surface is textured with two orthogonal "fabric" threads representing the principal eigendirections.

### Data Structures
*   **Surface Textures**: Depth buffer and surface normal buffer (32-bit float or 16-bit fixed point) generated in Pass 1.
*   **Eigenvector Textures**: Two 8-bit integer textures storing the scaled projected eigenvectors. The scaling ensures one component is $\pm 1$, allowing 8-bit storage [S1, unofficial].
*   **Noise Textures**: Two 2D textures (channels $a$ and $b$) generated via reaction-diffusion, mapped to the surface geometry.

### Parameters
*   $D_a = 0.125$: Diffusion constant for chemical $a$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $D_b = 0.031$: Diffusion constant for chemical $b$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $s$: Scalar controlling spot size; smaller $s$ yields larger spots [S1, unofficial].
*   $l$: Edge length of the implicit voxel grid defining the parameterization resolution [S1, unofficial].
*   $b_{min}$: Minimum coordinate of the geometry's bounding box [S1, unofficial].

### Step 1: Projection into Image Space
1.  Render the surface using the standard OpenGL pipeline to obtain depth and surface normal $n$ for each pixel [S1, unofficial].
2.  Interpolate the tensor field $T$ from the world-space tensor field at the surface point [S1, unofficial].
3.  Project $T$ onto the surface tangent plane using the projection matrix $P$ derived from the surface normal $n$:
    $$ T' = P \cdot T \cdot P^T $$
    where $P$ is defined as:
    $$ P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - n_x^2 & -n_y n_x & -n_z n_x \\ -n_x n_y & 1 - n_y^2 & -n_z n_y \\ -n_x n_z & -n_y n_z & 1 - n_z^2 \end{pmatrix} $$
    [S1, unofficial]
4.  Decompose the projected tensor $T'$ into eigenvectors $v_{\lambda_i}$ and eigenvalues using the method from Hasan et al. [10] [S1, unofficial].
5.  Project the 3D eigenvectors $v_{\lambda_i}$ (where $i \in \{1, 2\}$) into 2D image space:
    $$ \gamma'_{\lambda_i} = M_P \times M_M \times v_{\lambda_i} $$
    [S1, unofficial]
6.  Scale the projected 2D eigenvectors to fit 8-bit storage and avoid numerical instability. Calculate the infinity norm:
    $$ \|v'_{\lambda_i}\|_\infty = \max\{|v_x|, |v_y|\} $$
    Scale the vector:
    $$ v'_{\lambda_i}' = \frac{v'_{\lambda_i}}{\|v'_{\lambda_i}\|_\infty} $$
    If $\|v'_{\lambda_i}\|_\infty = 0$ (surface normal perpendicular to view direction), ignore the division [S1, unofficial].

### Step 2: Silhouette Detection
Perform silhouette detection on the surface to identify boundaries. This step guarantees the integrity of the advection step in Pass 3 by defining the valid regions for texture mapping [S1, unofficial].

### Step 3: Advection
This pass produces the two eigenvector textures by advecting a noise field along the projected eigenvector fields.

#### 3.1 Generate Initial Noise Texture
Generate a seamless 2D noise texture using a reaction-diffusion scheme (Turing [24]) to create smooth "spots" rather than high-frequency white noise [S1, unofficial]. The discrete equations for channels $a$ and $b$ are:
$$ \Delta a_{i,j} = F(i,j) + D_a \cdot (a_{i+1,j} + a_{i-1,j} + a_{i,j+1} + a_{i,j-1} - 4 \cdot a_{i,j}) $$
$$ \Delta b_{i,j} = G(i,j) + D_b \cdot (b_{i+1,j} + b_{i-1,j} + b_{i,j+1} + b_{i,j-1} - 4 \cdot b_{i,j}) $$
Source terms:
$$ F(i,j) = s(16 - a_{i,j} \cdot b_{i,j}) $$
$$ G(i,j) = s(a_{i,j} \cdot b_{i,j} - b_{i,j} - \beta_{i,j}) $$
[Note: $\beta_{i,j}$ is not defined in the provided sources; assumed to be a constant or external input] [S1, unofficial].
Use continuous boundary conditions to ensure the texture is seamless in both directions [S1, unofficial].

#### 3.2 Map Noise to Geometry (Implicit Parameterization)
Map the 2D reaction-diffusion texture to the 3D surface geometry using an implicit voxel-based parameterization [S1, unofficial].

1.  Define a regular grid of voxels filling the geometry's bounding box. Each voxel has base coordinate $b_i$ and edge length $l$ [S1, unofficial].
2.  Transform an object-space vertex coordinate $v_g$ to voxel space $v_{voxel}$:
    $$ v_{voxel} = v_g \cdot M $$
    where $M$ is a $4 \times 4$ matrix:
    $$ M = \begin{pmatrix} l & 0 & 0 & -b_{\min_x} \\ 0 & l & 0 & -b_{\min_y} \\ 0 & 0 & l & -b_{\min_z} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$
    [S1, unofficial]
3.  Transform to local coordinate system $v_{hit}$ (components in $[0,1]$):
    $$ v_{hit} = v_{voxel} - [v_{voxel}] $$
    where $[v_{voxel}]$ denotes the integer part (floor) [S1, unofficial].
4.  Determine texture coordinates $t = (v_{hit_i}, v_{hit_j})$ by selecting the two components of $v_{hit}$ corresponding to the voxel side-plane whose normal is most similar to the surface normal $n$. Specifically, if $n_k = \max\{n_i, n_j, n_k\}$, then $i \neq j \neq k$:
    $$ t = (v_{hit_i}, v_{hit_j}) $$
    [S1, unofficial]
5.  Sample the reaction-diffusion texture at $t$ to initialize the noise field on the surface.
6.  Advect the noise field along the projected eigenvector fields $\gamma'_{\lambda_1}$ and $\gamma'_{\lambda_2}$ to produce the two eigenvector textures [S1, unofficial].

### Step 4: Compositing and Postprocessing
1.  Compose the intermediate textures (silhouette mask, eigenvector textures) in a final rendering pass [S1, unofficial].
2.  Apply postprocessing steps to add shading and improve perceptual quality [S1, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Zero Infinity Norm**: If $\|v'_{\lambda_i}\|_\infty = 0$, the scaling division is undefined. This occurs when the surface normal is perpendicular to the view direction. The method states this case can be ignored [S1, unofficial].
*   **Non-Orthogonality**: Projected 3D eigenvectors are not necessarily orthogonal in 2D image space. The method handles this by treating them independently as texture directions [S1, unofficial].
*   **Parameterization**: The implicit voxel parameterization avoids the need for explicit texture atlases or 3D noise textures, but the choice of $l$ affects the level of detail and coherence [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: Interactive frame rates are claimed for large datasets on desktop PCs [S1, unofficial]. The complexity is dominated by the advection step, which involves tracing along eigenvector fields for multiple iterations per pixel.
*   **Memory Complexity**: Requires storage for depth/normal buffers, two eigenvector textures (8-bit), and two noise textures. The implicit parameterization avoids storing 3D noise textures [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths**: Works on arbitrary non-self-intersecting surfaces; no explicit parameterization required; maintains local coherence during transformation; uses smooth noise suitable for compositing two directions [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations**: Limited to non-self-intersecting surfaces; relies on image-space rendering which may suffer from aliasing if not supersampled; the reaction-diffusion noise generation is a pre-processing step [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use**: When visualizing tensor fields on complex surfaces (e.g., neural pathways in the brain) where standard LIC's white noise is visually cluttered or unsuitable for multi-direction compositing [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use**: For self-intersecting surfaces or when explicit surface parameterization is already available and preferred [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The method was introduced by Bernd Hamann and Gerik Scheuermann [S1, unofficial]. It is motivated by image-space Line Integral Convolution (LIC) but adapts it for tensor visualization using a fabric metaphor [S1, unofficial]. It overcomes drawbacks of previous image-space LIC implementations by defining a fixed parameterization on the surface via implicit voxel space, avoiding the need for 3D noise textures at sub-voxel resolution [S1, unofficial].
