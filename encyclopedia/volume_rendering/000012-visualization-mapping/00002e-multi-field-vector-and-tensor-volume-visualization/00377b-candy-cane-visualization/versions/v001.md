# Candy Cane Visualization

Also called: Candy cane, Polkagris, Polkagris visualization, Two Fields Visualization

## Overview
Candy Cane Visualization is a multi-field direct volume rendering technique that simultaneously visualizes two distinct scalar fields by assigning one field to control opacity (structure) and the other to control color (texture) [S1, S2]. It specifically addresses the challenge of revealing the internal structure of vortical flow regions while preserving the directional information of the vorticity field [S1, S2]. Unlike standard two-field visualization which may use independent transfer functions for both color and opacity for each field, Candy Cane Visualization couples the fields such that enstrophy defines the volumetric presence (opacity) and a Line Integral Convolution (LIC) texture of the vorticity field defines the surface appearance (color) [S1, S2]. This creates vortex tube structures that resemble "candy canes" or "polkagris" [S1, S2].

## How it works
The method requires a velocity field $\mathbf{u}$ as input. It computes two derived fields: the vorticity field $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and the enstrophy field $\eta$. It also requires a pre-computed or dynamically generated Seed LIC texture for the vorticity field.

**Inputs:**
1.  Velocity field $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})$ defined on a 3D grid.
2.  Transfer function for opacity $T_{\eta}(\eta)$, mapping enstrophy values to opacity $\alpha \in [0, 1]$.
3.  Transfer function for color $T_{LIC}(\text{LIC}(\mathbf{x}))$, mapping LIC texture values to RGB color.

**Outputs:**
1.  A rendered image where vortex tubes are visible (via opacity) and colored by the directional flow structure (via LIC).

**Data Structures:**
*   **Velocity Field:** 3D array of vectors $\mathbf{u}$.
*   **Vorticity Field:** 3D array of vectors $\boldsymbol{\omega}$.
*   **Enstrophy Field:** 3D array of scalars $\eta$.
*   **LIC Texture:** 3D array of scalar values representing the convolved line integral intensity.

**Formulas:**
1.  **Vorticity:** The curl of the velocity field [S2]:
    $$ \boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u} $$
2.  **Enstrophy:** The squared magnitude of the vorticity field [S2]:
    $$ \eta = \|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^2 = \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega} $$
3.  **LIC Texture:** The Seed LIC texture is generated by convolving the velocity field with a kernel along streamlines. Let $K$ be a smoothing kernel (e.g., a 3x3 filter to smooth strokes) [S2]. The LIC value at point $\mathbf{x}$ is the integral of the kernel along the streamline passing through $\mathbf{x}$ [S1, S2].

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Vorticity:** Calculate $\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})$ for all grid points.
2.  **Compute Enstrophy:** Calculate $\eta(\mathbf{x}) = \|\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{x})\|^2$ for all grid points.
3.  **Generate LIC Texture:** Compute the Seed LIC texture for the vorticity field $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. This involves integrating a kernel along the streamlines of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ to convey directional structure [S1, S2].
4.  **Map Enstrophy to Opacity:** For each sample point $\mathbf{x}$ during rendering, evaluate the opacity transfer function: $\alpha(\mathbf{x}) = T_{\eta}(\eta(\mathbf{x}))$.
5.  **Map LIC to Color:** For each sample point $\mathbf{x}$, evaluate the color transfer function using the LIC texture value: $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}) = T_{LIC}(\text{LIC}(\mathbf{x}))$.
6.  **Volume Rendering:** Perform standard direct volume rendering (e.g., ray casting) using the computed opacity $\alpha(\mathbf{x})$ and color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x})$ at each sample point along the ray [S1, S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **LIC Kernel:** A smoothing kernel (e.g., 3x3 filter) is used to smear field lines outwards, making strokes thicker and smoother to improve 3D perception [S2].
*   **Transfer Functions:** Specific transfer functions for enstrophy (opacity) and LIC (color) are required. The sources reference specific TFs in Figures 13 and 14 of [S1], but do not provide the exact numerical mappings.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** Computing the curl and squared magnitude requires careful handling of numerical precision, especially near boundaries or in regions of low velocity [G].
*   **LIC Computation Cost:** Generating the LIC texture is computationally expensive as it involves integrating along streamlines for every point in the volume [S1, S2].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If the velocity field is zero or has no vorticity, the enstrophy will be zero and the LIC texture will be undefined or uniform, resulting in no visible structure [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method is dominated by the computation of the LIC texture, which is $O(N \cdot L)$ where $N$ is the number of sample points and $L$ is the average streamline length [S1, S2]. Volume rendering itself is $O(R \cdot S)$ where $R$ is the number of rays and $S$ is the number of samples per ray.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the velocity field, vorticity field, enstrophy field, and LIC texture. This is $O(N)$ for each field, where $N$ is the volume resolution [S1, S2].
*   **Strengths:** Effectively visualizes vortex tubes by combining structural information (enstrophy) with directional information (LIC) [S1, S2]. The "candy cane" appearance helps in perceiving the twisting nature of vortices [S1, S2].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to LIC generation [S1, S2]. The choice of transfer functions is critical and may require user tuning [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When visualizing vortical structures in flow fields where both the intensity of rotation (enstrophy) and the direction of rotation (vorticity/LIC) are important [S1, S2].
*   **When Not to Use:** When real-time performance is required and LIC computation is too slow [S1, S2]. When the flow field has no significant vorticity [G].

## Background
The Candy Cane Visualization technique was introduced in the context of visualizing vector fields using Seed LIC and Volume Rendering [S1, S2]. It is also known as "polkagris" visualization, a term derived from Norwegian [S1, S2]. The method was developed to address the limitations of visualizing multiple fields simultaneously, specifically by leveraging the strengths of LIC for directional information and volume rendering for structural information [S1, S2]. The technique is particularly useful in fluid dynamics for analyzing vortex tubes [S1, S2].
