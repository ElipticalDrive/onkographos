# Interval Arithmetic Inclusion Strategies

Also called: InclusionFunction strategies

## Overview
Interval Arithmetic Inclusion Strategies are a family of methods used within bisection ray casting to compute valid upper and lower bounds for an implicit scalar field function $f$ over a given domain (typically an axis-aligned box or ray segment). They solve the problem of determining whether a surface intersection (where $f(\mathbf{x})=0$) exists within a region without exhaustive sampling, enabling safe pruning of empty space. They differ from their siblings, such as [Lipschitz Constant Estimation](1572-lipschitz-constant-estimation.md) (which provides a global scalar bound) and [Forward Linear Inclusion Function](1714-forward-linear-inclusion-function.md) (which uses that bound for step sizing), by providing the specific interval arithmetic mechanism to bound the function's variation locally or analytically.

## Shared principles
All inclusion strategies in this family compute an interval $[f](\mathcal{B})$ such that for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{B}$, $f(\mathbf{x}) \in [f](\mathcal{B})$. This is the **inclusion property**. If $0 \notin [f](\mathcal{B})$, the box $\mathcal{B}$ is guaranteed to be empty of the zero-level set and can be pruned. The strategies trade off between generality (black-box compatibility) and tightness of bounds (performance/accuracy) [S1, unofficial].

The core mechanism involves evaluating the function or its derivatives using interval arithmetic or gradient information to construct a bounding box in the function's codomain. The tightness of this bound determines the efficiency of the bisection algorithm: tighter bounds allow larger steps or earlier pruning, while looser bounds require more subdivisions.

## Subtopics

### [Lipschitz Constant Estimation](1572-lipschitz-constant-estimation.md)
Estimates the global Lipschitz constant $\lambda$ for an implicit function $f: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ by sampling the gradient magnitude over the bounding box. This constant is required by black-box interval inclusion strategies (e.g., [SamplingInclusion](1410-interval-arithmetic-inclusion-strategies.md#samplinginclusion)) to bound function variation and enable safe pruning in bisection algorithms. It differs from [Forward Linear Inclusion Function](1714-forward-linear-inclusion-function.md) by providing a scalar bound for the entire domain rather than a local linear approximation, and from [Local Lipschitz Bound Computation for Hierarchical Implicit Models](3894-local-lipschitz-bound-computation-for-hierarchical-implicit-.md) by operating on the global bounding box rather than the specific geometric structure of a hierarchical tree.

### [Forward Linear Inclusion Function](1714-forward-linear-inclusion-function.md)
The Forward Linear Inclusion Function is a method for computing valid, linear interval bounds for a scalar field function $f$ along a ray, used to determine safe step sizes in sphere tracing. It solves the problem of estimating the distance to the nearest surface intersection by bounding the function's variation using a Lipschitz constant $\lambda$. It sets itself apart from its siblings, such as the [Improved Quadratic Taylor Inclusion Function](1902-improved-quadratic-taylor-inclusion-function.md), by using first-order linear bounds rather than second-order quadratic approximations, making it computationally cheaper but potentially less tight for functions with significant curvature.

### SamplingInclusion
**Definition:** A black-box baseline strategy that works with any function $f(\mathbf{x}) \rightarrow \text{scalar}$ without requiring symbolic access or gradient information [S1, unofficial]. It relies on a pre-computed or estimated Lipschitz constant $\lambda$ to bound the function's variation over the box [S1, unofficial].

**Mechanism:** The interval bound is constructed as $[f](\mathcal{B}) = [f_{min}, f_{max}]$, where the width is determined by $\lambda \cdot \text{diam}(\mathcal{B})$. Specifically, if $f(\mathbf{x}_c)$ is the value at the center of the box $\mathcal{B}$, the bound is $[f(\mathbf{x}_c) - \lambda \cdot r, f(\mathbf{x}_c) + \lambda \cdot r]$, where $r$ is the radius of the box [S1, unofficial].

**When to choose:** Use when only point evaluations of $f$ are available (black-box scenario) and gradient computation is impossible or too expensive [S1, unofficial]. It is the most general but often produces the loosest bounds among the four strategies, leading to more bisection steps [S1, unofficial].

### GradientInclusion
**Definition:** The default strategy that uses the gradient $\nabla f$ to compute tighter bounds than SamplingInclusion [S1, unofficial]. It requires access to the gradient of $f$ [S1, unofficial].

**Mechanism:** It utilizes the first-order Taylor expansion with interval remainder. The bound is computed as $[f](\mathcal{B}) = f(\mathbf{x}_c) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_c) \cdot (\mathcal{B} - \mathbf{x}_c)$, often combined with a second-order term or Lipschitz bound on the Hessian if available, or simply using the gradient norm to bound the linear variation [S1, unofficial]. By incorporating local gradient information, it adapts to the function's orientation and steepness, providing significantly tighter bounds than the isotropic SamplingInclusion [S1, unofficial].

**When to choose:** Use when the gradient $\nabla f$ is available and differentiable. It offers a good balance between computational cost and bound tightness, making it the recommended default for most implicit surfaces [S1, unofficial].

### NaturalIntervalInclusion
**Definition:** An analytic strategy that uses Natural Interval Arithmetic (NIA) on the symbolic formula of $f$ [S1, unofficial]. It provides tighter bounds than SamplingInclusion and GradientInclusion when the analytic formula is known [S1, unofficial].

**Mechanism:** It evaluates the symbolic expression of $f$ directly using interval arithmetic, replacing each variable with its interval representation in the box $\mathcal{B}$ and each operation with its interval counterpart (e.g., interval addition, multiplication) [S1, unofficial]. This propagates the uncertainty through the formula.

**When to choose:** Use when the implicit function is defined by a known symbolic formula and NIA provides sufficiently tight bounds. It is more accurate than gradient-based methods for functions with complex algebraic structures but may suffer from overestimation due to the dependency problem in interval arithmetic [S1, unofficial].

### AffineInclusion
**Definition:** An analytic strategy that uses Affine Arithmetic (AA) on the symbolic formula of $f$ [S1, unofficial]. It provides the tightest bounds among the four strategies, reducing the number of bisection steps needed [S1, unofficial].

**Mechanism:** It extends NIA by tracking linear noise symbols and correlations between variables. This mitigates the dependency problem inherent in NIA, resulting in much tighter interval bounds, especially for functions with repeated variables or complex dependencies [S1, unofficial].

**When to choose:** Use when the highest possible bound tightness is required to minimize bisection steps, and the function has a symbolic form compatible with Affine Arithmetic. It is computationally more expensive than NIA and GradientInclusion but yields the best pruning performance [S1, unofficial].

### Parallel Tape Reduction
**Definition:** A method that simplifies the SDF expression in specific regions of space by coupling pruning and rendering, using interval arithmetic to evaluate nodes [S2]. It relies on well-defined interval arithmetic queries for each node in the construction tree to determine pruning eligibility [S2].

**Comparison:** Differs from Lipschitz pruning by using interval arithmetic instead of 1-Lipschitz bounds, requiring explicit interval queries per node rather than treating primitives as black boxes [S2]. It is particularly effective for large scenes with thousands of nodes, scaling better than object-centric bounding volume techniques which struggle with smooth CSG operators [S2].

## Background
The family of interval arithmetic inclusion strategies was formalized in the context of robust iso-surface rendering for arbitrary implicit geometries. The primary implementation and documentation come from the `isoray` project, which targets black-box functions but provides analytic optimizations [S1, unofficial]. The four core strategies (Sampling, Gradient, Natural, Affine) were introduced to balance robustness and performance in bisection ray casting [S1, unofficial]. Parallel Tape Reduction was introduced in the context of hierarchical SDF simplification, offering an alternative pruning mechanism for construction trees [S2].
