# Ray Casting and Intersection


## Overview
Computes 3D ray origins and directions from 2D pixel coordinates and camera parameters, and finds the least-squares intersection point of two rays in 3D space [S1, unofficial]. It unprojects pixel coordinates to unit direction vectors in camera space, transforms them to world space, and solves for the point that minimizes the squared distance to both rays, handling non-intersecting rays due to noise or parallax [S1, unofficial]. It sets apart from simple ray casting by providing a robust geometric intersection for ray pairs that do not intersect exactly, building on [Ray-Ray Intersection](7624-ray-ray-intersection.md).

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- `pixel_coords`: Tensor of shape $(N, 2)$ containing 2D pixel coordinates $(u, v)$ for $N$ rays.
- `intrinsics`: Camera intrinsic matrix $K$, shape $(3, 3)$.
- `extrinsics`: Camera extrinsic matrix $T$, shape $(4, 4)$, representing the transformation from world to camera space. The upper-left $3 \times 3$ is rotation $R$, and the upper-right $3 \times 1$ is translation $t$.
- `eps`: Epsilon for detecting parallel rays. Default $10^{-5}$ [S1, unofficial].
- `inf`: Value used to represent infinity for parallel ray intersections. Default $10^{10}$ [S1, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
- `origins`: Tensor of shape $(N, 3)$, ray origins in world space.
- `directions`: Tensor of shape $(N, 3)$, unit ray directions in world space.
- `intersection_points`: Tensor of shape $(N, 3)$, the least-squares intersection points. For parallel rays, this is set to `inf` [S1, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
- Homogeneous coordinates: 4D vectors $(u, v, 1, 0)^T$ or similar, depending on implementation. The source implies standard homogeneous unprojection.
- Matrices: $K^{-1}$ (inverse intrinsics), $R$ (rotation from extrinsics), $t$ (translation from extrinsics).
- Tensor operations: Matrix multiplication, normalization, dot product, summation, least-squares solver.

**Steps:**
1. **Unproject to Camera Space:** Convert 2D pixel coordinates to homogeneous coordinates in camera space. Multiply by the inverse intrinsic matrix $K^{-1}$ to get unprojected direction vectors.
   $$
   \mathbf{d}_{cam} = K^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
   This yields vectors in camera space, not yet normalized.

2. **Normalize in Camera Space:** Normalize the direction vectors by dividing by their z-component (depth) to ensure they are unit vectors in camera space.
   $$
   \mathbf{d}_{cam} = \frac{\mathbf{d}_{cam}}{d_{cam, z}}
   $$
   This step ensures the directions are unit vectors pointing away from the camera center along the ray.

3. **Transform to World Space:** Transform the ray directions from camera space to world space using the extrinsic rotation matrix $R$.
   $$
   \mathbf{d}_{world} = R \mathbf{d}_{cam}
   $$
   The ray origins are set to the camera center (translation part $t$ of extrinsics), tiled to match the ray direction shape.
   $$
   \mathbf{o}_{world} = t
   $$
   Note: The source specifies setting origins to the camera center. In a standard pinhole camera model, the origin is the camera center. If multiple rays are generated from different pixels, they all share the same origin (the camera center).

4. **Detect Parallel Rays:** Compute the dot product of the direction vectors for the pair of rays being intersected. If the dot product is greater than $1 - \text{eps}$, the rays are considered parallel.
   $$
   \text{parallel} = \mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2 > 1 - \text{eps}
   $$
   This check is performed for each pair of rays.

5. **Filter Parallel Rays:** Filter out parallel rays from the batch before solving the linear system. Parallel rays do not have a unique least-squares intersection point in the same way, or the solution is degenerate.

6. **Compute Least-Squares System:** For non-parallel rays, compute the least-squares intersection point.
   - Compute the matrix $N_i = \mathbf{d}_i \mathbf{d}_i^T - I$ for each ray direction $\mathbf{d}_i$. Here, $I$ is the identity matrix.
   - Compute the left-hand side (LHS) of the least-squares equation by summing $N_i$ over the ray dimension (for two rays, sum $N_1 + N_2$).
     $$
     \text{LHS} = \sum_i (\mathbf{d}_i \mathbf{d}_i^T - I)
     $$
   - Compute the right-hand side (RHS) by summing $N_i \mathbf{o}_i$ over the ray dimension.
     $$
     \text{RHS} = \sum_i (\mathbf{d}_i \mathbf{d}_i^T - I) \mathbf{o}_i
     $$
   - Solve for the intersection point $\mathbf{p}$ using least squares:
     $$
     \mathbf{p} = \text{LHS}^{-1} \text{RHS}
     $$
     In practice, this is solved using a least-squares solver like `torch.linalg.lstsq`.

7. **Handle Parallel Rays:** For rays identified as parallel, set the intersection point to `inf` (e.g., $10^{10}$) to indicate no valid intersection [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.linalg

def ray_casting_and_intersection(pixel_coords, intrinsics, extrinsics, eps=1e-5, inf=1e10):
    """
    Computes 3D ray origins, directions, and least-squares intersection points.
    
    Args:
        pixel_coords: Tensor of shape (N, 2) containing 2D pixel coordinates.
        intrinsics: Tensor of shape (3, 3) containing camera intrinsic matrix K.
        extrinsics: Tensor of shape (4, 4) containing camera extrinsic matrix T.
        eps: Epsilon for detecting parallel rays.
        inf: Value for parallel ray intersections.
        
    Returns:
        origins: Tensor of shape (N, 3), ray origins in world space.
        directions: Tensor of shape (N, 3), unit ray directions in world space.
        intersection_points: Tensor of shape (N, 3), least-squares intersection points.
    """
    # Step 1: Unproject to Camera Space
    # Convert 2D pixel coordinates to homogeneous coordinates
    # Assuming pixel_coords is (N, 2), create (N, 3) homogeneous coordinates
    homogeneous_coords = torch.cat([pixel_coords, torch.ones_like(pixel_coords[:, :1])], dim=1) # (N, 3)
    
    # Multiply by inverse intrinsics
    K_inv = torch.inverse(intrinsics) # (3, 3)
    d_cam = torch.matmul(homogeneous_coords, K_inv.T) # (N, 3)
    
    # Step 2: Normalize in Camera Space
    # Divide by z-component to get unit vectors
    d_cam_z = d_cam[:, 2:3] # (N, 1)
    d_cam = d_cam / d_cam_z # (N, 3)
    
    # Step 3: Transform to World Space
    # Extract rotation and translation from extrinsics
    R = extrinsics[:3, :3] # (3, 3)
    t = extrinsics[:3, 3] # (3,)
    
    # Transform directions
    d_world = torch.matmul(d_cam, R.T) # (N, 3)
    
    # Set origins to camera center
    origins = t.unsqueeze(0).expand(d_world.shape[0], -1) # (N, 3)
    
    # Step 4: Detect Parallel Rays (for pairs)
    # Assuming we are intersecting ray i with ray j. 
    # The source implies a batch of rays, and we find intersection for pairs.
    # Let's assume we are intersecting ray 0 with ray 1 for each pair in a batch of pairs.
    # Or, more generally, for each ray i, we might intersect it with a reference ray.
    # The source mentions "summing n over the ray dimension", implying a system for multiple rays.
    # However, "intersect_rays" usually implies two rays. Let's assume we are intersecting two rays at a time.
    # If N is even, we pair (0,1), (2,3), etc.
    
    # Let's assume the input is a batch of ray pairs, so pixel_coords is (M, 2, 2) where M is number of pairs.
    # But the source says "pixel_coords" is (N, 2). Let's assume N is even and we pair them.
    
    if pixel_coords.shape[0] % 2 != 0:
        raise ValueError("Number of rays must be even for pairing.")
        
    M = pixel_coords.shape[0] // 2
    d_world_reshaped = d_world.view(M, 2, 3) # (M, 2, 3)
    origins_reshaped = origins.view(M, 2, 3) # (M, 2, 3)
    
    d1 = d_world_reshaped[:, 0, :] # (M, 3)
    d2 = d_world_reshaped[:, 1, :] # (M, 3)
    o1 = origins_reshaped[:, 0, :] # (M, 3)
    o2 = origins_reshaped[:, 1, :] # (M, 3)
    
    # Dot product
    dot_products = torch.sum(d1 * d2, dim=1) # (M,)
    parallel_mask = dot_products > (1 - eps) # (M,)
    
    # Step 5: Filter Parallel Rays
    # We will solve for non-parallel rays and set parallel rays to inf.
    
    # Step 6: Compute Least-Squares System
    # Compute N_i = d_i d_i^T - I for each ray
    # d1 is (M, 3), d2 is (M, 3)
    # N1 = d1 @ d1.T - I, but d1 is (M, 3), so d1 @ d1.T is (M, 3, 3)
    # We need to do this for each ray in the pair.
    
    # N1 = d1.unsqueeze(2) @ d1.unsqueeze(1) - torch.eye(3) # (M, 3, 3)
    # N2 = d2.unsqueeze(2) @ d2.unsqueeze(1) - torch.eye(3) # (M, 3, 3)
    
    # More efficient:
    N1 = torch.bmm(d1.unsqueeze(2), d1.unsqueeze(1)) - torch.eye(3).unsqueeze(0).expand(M, -1, -1) # (M, 3, 3)
    N2 = torch.bmm(d2.unsqueeze(2), d2.unsqueeze(1)) - torch.eye(3).unsqueeze(0).expand(M, -1, -1) # (M, 3, 3)
    
    # LHS = N1 + N2
    LHS = N1 + N2 # (M, 3, 3)
    
    # RHS = N1 @ o1 + N2 @ o2
    RHS = torch.bmm(N1, o1.unsqueeze(2)).squeeze(2) + torch.bmm(N2, o2.unsqueeze(2)).squeeze(2) # (M, 3)
    
    # Solve for p
    # p = LHS^{-1} RHS
    # Use lstsq
    # result = torch.linalg.lstsq(LHS, RHS).solution
    # However, lstsq expects (M, 3, 3) and (M, 3, 1) or (M, 3)
    # Let's use torch.linalg.solve if LHS is invertible, but lstsq is more robust.
    
    # For non-parallel rays, solve
    # We need to handle the batched solve.
    
    # Initialize intersection points with inf
    intersection_points = torch.full((M, 3), inf, device=d1.device)
    
    # Solve for non-parallel rays
    non_parallel_mask = ~parallel_mask
    if torch.any(non_parallel_mask):
        LHS_np = LHS[non_parallel_mask] # (K, 3, 3) where K is number of non-parallel pairs
        RHS_np = RHS[non_parallel_mask] # (K, 3)
        
        # Solve least squares
        # torch.linalg.lstsq returns a solution, residuals, rank, singular values
        # We only need the solution
        try:
            solution = torch.linalg.lstsq(LHS_np, RHS_np).solution # (K, 3)
            intersection_points[non_parallel_mask] = solution
        except Exception as e:
            # If solve fails, keep inf
            pass
            
    # Reshape back to (N, 3) if needed, but here we return per pair
    # The source says "intersection_points" is (N, 3). If N is number of rays, and we pair them,
    # then we have M pairs. The output should probably be per ray or per pair.
    # The source says "intersection point of two rays", so it's per pair.
    # Let's assume the output is per pair, so (M, 3).
    # But the source says "intersection_points" is (N, 3). This is ambiguous.
    # Let's assume N is the number of pairs for the intersection output.
    
    return origins, d_world, intersection_points
```

**Parameters:**
- `eps`: Epsilon for detecting parallel rays. Default $10^{-5}$ [S1, unofficial].
- `inf`: Value used to represent infinity for parallel ray intersections. Default $10^{10}$ [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Parallel Rays:** If rays are parallel, the least-squares system is singular or ill-conditioned. The method detects this using the dot product threshold and sets the intersection to `inf` [S1, unofficial].
- **Precision:** Numerical stability of the least-squares solver should be considered, especially for nearly parallel rays.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The method assumes valid camera parameters. Invalid intrinsics or extrinsics will lead to incorrect ray directions or origins.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The unprojection and transformation steps are $O(N)$ for $N$ rays. The least-squares solve is $O(1)$ per pair of rays (since the system is $3 \times 3$). Overall, $O(N)$.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store ray origins, directions, and intersection points.
- **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
- **Scaling:** Scales linearly with the number of rays.
- **Strengths:** Robust to non-intersecting rays due to noise or parallax. Provides a unique least-squares solution.
- **Limitations:** Degenerate for parallel rays. Requires valid camera parameters.
- **When to Use:** When rays do not intersect exactly, e.g., in multi-view geometry with noise or parallax.
- **When Not to Use:** When rays are guaranteed to intersect exactly, or when parallel rays are common and a different handling is required.

## Background
Origin: The method is part of the `depthsplat` library [S1, unofficial]. It is used in computer vision tasks involving multi-view geometry, such as depth estimation and 3D reconstruction.
