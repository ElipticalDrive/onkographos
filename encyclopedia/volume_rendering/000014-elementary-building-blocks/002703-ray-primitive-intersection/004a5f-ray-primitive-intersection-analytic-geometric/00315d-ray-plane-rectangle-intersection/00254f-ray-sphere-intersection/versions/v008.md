# Ray-Sphere Intersection

Also called: Analytical ray-sphere intersection, Ball Ray Intersection, Quadratic Ray-Sphere Intersection, Ray-Ball Intersection, Sphere Ray Intersection, Sphere-Ray Intersection Check, SphereCollider.forward, Spherical Volume Intersection

## Overview

Ray-sphere intersection computes the entry and exit distances (parameters $t$) where a ray intersects a sphere, defining the valid sampling interval for volume rendering within that spherical volume [S1, S9, S10, unofficial]. It solves the problem of efficiently determining the geometric bounds of a spherical primitive to restrict ray marching or integration, avoiding unnecessary computation for rays that miss the scene or to define the integration limits for density accumulation [S9, S10, unofficial]. Unlike [Ray-Plane/Rectangle Intersection](6498-ray-plane-rectangle-intersection.md), which handles flat boundaries, this method solves a quadratic equation to account for the curvature of the sphere, providing exact hit points and surface normals [S1, S11, unofficial].

## How it works

**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs**: Ray origin $\mathbf{o}$ (vec3), ray direction $\mathbf{d}$ (vec3, typically normalized), sphere center $\mathbf{c}$ (vec3), sphere radius $r$ (float). Optional: transformation matrices to convert between world and local space [S1, S10, unofficial].
*   **Outputs**: Boolean hit status, intersection distances $t_{near}$ and $t_{far}$ (floats), and optionally the hit point and surface normal [S1, S10, S11, unofficial].

**Data Structures**
*   **Sphere**: Defined by center $\mathbf{c}$ and radius $r$. In some implementations, the sphere is defined in local space, requiring a transformation matrix to map the ray to local coordinates [S1, unofficial].
*   **Ray**: Defined by origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$.

**Algorithm Steps**

1.  **Transform Ray (Optional)**: If the sphere is defined in local space, transform the ray origin and direction into the sphere's local coordinate system using the inverse world-to-local transformation matrix [S1, unofficial]. Let the transformed ray be $\mathbf{o}'$ and $\mathbf{d}'$. If the sphere is axis-aligned or centered at the origin in world space, this step is skipped.

2.  **Compute Vector to Center**: Calculate the vector from the ray origin to the sphere center:
    $$ \mathbf{oc} = \mathbf{o} - \mathbf{c} $$
    [S1, S2, S10, unofficial]

3.  **Compute Quadratic Coefficients**: Solve for the intersection parameters $t$ by substituting the ray equation $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + \mathbf{d}t$ into the sphere equation $|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c}|^2 = r^2$. This yields the quadratic equation $at^2 + bt + c = 0$ with coefficients:
    $$ a = \mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{d} $$
    $$ b = 2 (\mathbf{oc} \cdot \mathbf{d}) $$
    $$ c = (\mathbf{oc} \cdot \mathbf{oc}) - r^2 $$
    [S1, S2, S10, unofficial]
    
    *Optimization*: To avoid the factor of 2 in $b$ and the division by 2 in the quadratic formula, some implementations use half-b:
    $$ \text{half\_b} = \mathbf{oc} \cdot \mathbf{d} $$
    $$ \text{discriminant} = \text{half\_b}^2 - a \cdot c $$
    [S1, S2, unofficial]

4.  **Compute Discriminant**:
    $$ \Delta = b^2 - 4ac $$
    Or using the optimized coefficients:
    $$ \Delta = 4 (\text{half\_b}^2 - a \cdot c) $$
    [S10, unofficial]
    
    If $\Delta < 0$, the ray does not intersect the sphere. Return miss [S1, S2, S10, unofficial].

5.  **Compute Roots**:
    $$ t_{1,2} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} $$
    Or using half-b:
    $$ t_{1,2} = \frac{-\text{half\_b} \pm \sqrt{\text{half\_b}^2 - a \cdot c}}{a} $$
    [S2, S10, unofficial]
    
    Ensure $t_1 \le t_2$ by swapping if necessary [S10, unofficial].

6.  **Filter Roots**:
    *   If both $t_1$ and $t_2$ are negative, the sphere is behind the ray origin. Return miss [S10, S11, unofficial].
    *   If $t_1 < 0$ and $t_2 \ge 0$, the ray origin is inside the sphere. Set $t_{near} = 0$ (or a small epsilon to prevent self-intersection) and $t_{far} = t_2$ [S10, S11, unofficial].
    *   If $t_1 \ge 0$, set $t_{near} = t_1$ and $t_{far} = t_2$ [S10, unofficial].
    *   Clamp distances to the valid ray segment $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$ if applicable [S1, S2, unofficial].

7.  **Compute Hit Point and Normal (Optional)**:
    *   Hit point: $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{o} + t_{near} \mathbf{d}$ [S10, S13, unofficial].
    *   Surface normal: $\mathbf{n} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})$ [S1, S11, S13, unofficial].
    *   Transform hit point and normal back to world space if local space was used [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode**

```python
def ray_sphere_intersect(ray_origin, ray_direction, sphere_center, sphere_radius, t_min=0.0, t_max=float('inf')):
    """
    Computes the intersection of a ray with a sphere.
    
    Args:
        ray_origin: vec3, origin of the ray.
        ray_direction: vec3, direction of the ray (should be normalized).
        sphere_center: vec3, center of the sphere.
        sphere_radius: float, radius of the sphere.
        t_min: float, minimum distance for intersection (default 0.0).
        t_max: float, maximum distance for intersection (default inf).
        
    Returns:
        tuple: (hit: bool, t_near: float, t_far: float, hit_point: vec3, normal: vec3)
               If hit is False, t_near, t_far, hit_point, and normal are undefined/zero.
    """
    # Step 2: Vector from ray origin to sphere center
    oc = ray_origin - sphere_center
    
    # Step 3: Quadratic coefficients
    # a = d . d
    a = dot(ray_direction, ray_direction)
    
    # b = 2 * (oc . d)
    # Using half_b optimization: half_b = oc . d
    half_b = dot(oc, ray_direction)
    
    # c = oc . oc - r^2
    c = dot(oc, oc) - sphere_radius * sphere_radius
    
    # Step 4: Discriminant
    # delta = b^2 - 4ac => 4 * (half_b^2 - a*c)
    # We can just check half_b^2 - a*c < 0
    discriminant = half_b * half_b - a * c
    
    if discriminant < 0.0:
        return (False, 0.0, 0.0, vec3(0), vec3(0))
    
    # Step 5: Compute roots
    sqrt_disc = sqrt(discriminant)
    
    # t1 = (-half_b - sqrt_disc) / a
    # t2 = (-half_b + sqrt_disc) / a
    t1 = (-half_b - sqrt_disc) / a
    t2 = (-half_b + sqrt_disc) / a
    
    # Ensure t1 <= t2
    if t1 > t2:
        t1, t2 = t2, t1
        
    # Step 6: Filter roots
    # Check if the intersection is within the ray segment [t_min, t_max]
    # If t2 < t_min, the sphere is behind the ray
    if t2 < t_min:
        return (False, 0.0, 0.0, vec3(0), vec3(0))
    
    # If t1 < t_min, the ray origin is inside the sphere or the near hit is behind the start
    if t1 < t_min:
        t_near = t2
    else:
        t_near = t1
        
    t_far = t2
    
    # Check if t_far is within bounds
    if t_far > t_max:
        return (False, 0.0, 0.0, vec3(0), vec3(0))
        
    # Step 7: Compute hit point and normal
    hit_point = ray_origin + ray_direction * t_near
    normal = normalize(hit_point - sphere_center)
    
    return (True, t_near, t_far, hit_point, normal)
```

**Parameters**
*   `t_min`: Minimum intersection distance. Default 0.0. Used to prevent self-intersection or z-fighting [S11, unofficial].
*   `t_max`: Maximum intersection distance. Default infinity. Used to clip intersections to a specific range [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   `radius`: Sphere radius. Default 1.0 in some implementations [S5, S9, unofficial].
*   `center`: Sphere center. Default (0,0,0) if not specified [S7, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Ray Origin Inside Sphere**: If the ray origin is inside the sphere, $t_1$ will be negative. The near intersection should be clamped to 0.0 or a small epsilon to avoid self-intersection artifacts [S10, S11, unofficial].
*   **Tangent Ray**: If the discriminant is exactly 0, the ray is tangent to the sphere. This is a valid intersection with $t_1 = t_2$ [S10, unofficial].
*   **Numerical Stability**: When the ray origin is very close to the sphere surface, precision issues may arise. Clamping the discriminant to a small positive value (e.g., 0.01) can improve stability in some implementations [S5, unofficial].
*   **Uninitialized Variables**: In some codebases, the normal calculation references an uninitialized variable `t` if not careful. Ensure `t` is correctly assigned before computing the normal [S11, unofficial].
*   **Negative t Values**: Intersections with $t < 0$ are behind the camera and should be ignored or clamped to 0.0 [S10, unofficial].
*   **NaN Values**: Invalid intersections (e.g., negative discriminant not handled) can result in NaN values. These should be replaced with 0.0 or handled explicitly [S9, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity**: $O(1)$ per sphere. The method involves a constant number of arithmetic operations (dot products, square root, quadratic formula).
*   **Memory Complexity**: $O(1)$. No additional data structures are required beyond the ray and sphere parameters.
*   **Measured Performance**: Highly efficient and suitable for parallel execution on GPUs. Each thread can process one or more rays independently [G].
*   **Scaling**: Scales linearly with the number of rays. For scenes with many spheres, acceleration structures (e.g., BVH, grid) are typically used to cull non-intersecting spheres before performing the intersection test [G].
*   **Strengths**: Exact solution, fast computation, numerically stable with proper handling of edge cases.
*   **Limitations**: Only applies to spheres. For complex shapes, approximation or other primitives are needed. Precision issues can occur with very large or very small spheres relative to the ray origin.
*   **When to Use**: When rendering scenes with spherical objects or volumes. Ideal for basic ray tracing, volume rendering, and collision detection.
*   **When Not to Use**: When the primitive is not a sphere, or when higher-order approximations are required (e.g., ellipsoids, where [Ray-Splat Intersection](7123-ray-splat-intersection-sphere-approximation.md) might be used).

## Background

Ray-sphere intersection is a fundamental primitive in computer graphics, particularly in ray tracing and volume rendering. It has been used since the early days of ray tracing to efficiently test for intersections with spherical objects. The method is widely implemented in rendering engines, game engines, and scientific visualization tools. It is often the first intersection primitive taught in computer graphics courses due to its simplicity and analytical solution [S1, S3, S14, unofficial].
