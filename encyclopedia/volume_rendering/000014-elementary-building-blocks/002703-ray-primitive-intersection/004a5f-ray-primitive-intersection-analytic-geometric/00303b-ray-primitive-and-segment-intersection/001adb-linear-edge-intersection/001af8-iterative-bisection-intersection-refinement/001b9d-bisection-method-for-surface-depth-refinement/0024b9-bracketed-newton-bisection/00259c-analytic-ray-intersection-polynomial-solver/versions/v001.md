# Analytic Ray-Intersection Polynomial Solver

Also called: Polynomial SDF Intersection Solver

## Overview
The Analytic Ray-Intersection Polynomial Solver finds the intersection parameter $t$ where a ray intersects the polynomial approximation of a Signed Distance Function (SDF) leaf node [S1]. It solves the problem of slow convergence in sphere marching when rays pass near parallel surfaces or graze the isosurface [S1]. Unlike sphere marching, which evaluates the SDF iteratively, this method computes a single polynomial in $t$ per node and solves for its roots, offering a significant performance improvement near the surface at the cost of higher initial coefficient computation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Ray origin $\mathbf{p}$ and direction $\mathbf{v}$ (normalized).
- SDF leaf node coefficients defining the polynomial $P_N: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$.
- Node type: Trilinear (8 coefficients) or Tricubic (64 coefficients).

**Outputs:**
- The smallest positive real root $t$ of the intersection polynomial, representing the first intersection point.
- A boolean indicating if an intersection exists within the node.

**Data Structures:**
- **Trilinear Node:** Array of 8 scalar coefficients $c_{ijk}$ for $i,j,k \in \{0,1\}$.
- **Tricubic Node:** Array of 64 scalar coefficients $c_{ijkl}$ for $i,j,k,l \in \{0,1,2,3\}$.
- **Intersection Polynomial Coefficients:** Array of size 4 for trilinear ($c_{l3} \dots c_{l0}$) or size 10 for tricubic ($c_{c9} \dots c_{c0}$).

**Steps:**

1.  **Compute Intersection Polynomial Coefficients:**
    Substitute the ray equation $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{v}t$ into the node's polynomial $P_N(\mathbf{x})$. Expand and collect terms by powers of $t$.
    
    For **Trilinear** interpolation, the result is a cubic polynomial:
    $$ c_{l3}t^3 + c_{l2}t^2 + c_{l1}t + c_{l0} = 0 $$
    Computing these 4 coefficients requires 26 multiplications [S1].
    
    For **Tricubic** interpolation, the result is a degree 9 polynomial:
    $$ c_{c9}t^9 + c_{c8}t^8 + \dots + c_{c1}t + c_{c0} = 0 $$
    Computing these 10 coefficients requires 492 multiplications [S1].

2.  **Early Skip Test (Tricubic Only):**
    For tricubic nodes, apply an Affine Arithmetic-based early skip test to detect non-collisions in constant time or provide a better starting point [S1]. Factorize the function as a degree 2 polynomial:
    $$ P_N(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}t) \approx at^2 + bt + c = 0 $$
    where:
    $$ a = c_{c9}t^7 + c_{c8}t^6 + \dots + c_{c3}t + c_{c2} $$
    $$ b = c_{c1} $$
    $$ c = c_{c0} $$
    Note that $a$ depends on $t$. To make this a real degree 2 equation, $a$ must be approximated with a fixed value. If starting outside the object, check if the equation goes to the negative part to determine collision likelihood [S1]. If the test detects no collision, return "No Intersection".

3.  **Identify Monotonic Intervals:**
    Use the **Bracketed Newton Bisection** method to find all real roots of the derivative polynomials, which define the boundaries of monotonic intervals for the original polynomial [S1].
    -   **Trilinear:** The derivative is quadratic (degree 2). It has at most 2 roots, creating at most 3 monotonic intervals for the cubic polynomial [S1].
    -   **Tricubic:** The derivative is degree 8. The solver searches monotonic intervals of the seven previous derivatives iteratively, starting from the $(n-2)$-th derivative (degree 2) up to the original polynomial [S1]. This identifies up to 9 monotonic intervals for the degree 9 polynomial [S1].

4.  **Root Finding:**
    For each monotonic interval $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$:
    -   Evaluate the intersection polynomial $P(t)$ at the endpoints.
    -   If $P(t_{min})$ and $P(t_{max})$ have opposite signs (sign change), a root exists in this interval [S1].
    -   Apply **Bracketed Newton Bisection** to find the root within this interval [S1].
    -   Discard the Cardano formula or other analytical cubic solvers due to instability in certain situations [S1].

5.  **Select Intersection:**
    Return the smallest positive $t$ found among all intervals. If no sign changes are detected in any monotonic interval, the node does not intersect the surface [S1].

**Parameters:**
- **Trilinear Node:** 8 coefficients. Evaluating the polynomial requires 7 multiplications. Computing intersection coefficients requires 26 multiplications [S1].
- **Tricubic Node:** 64 coefficients. Evaluating the polynomial requires 84 multiplications. Computing intersection coefficients requires 492 multiplications [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Instability of Analytical Solvers:** Analytical formulas like Cardano's method for cubic roots are found to be unstable in some situations; therefore, numerical methods (Bracketed Newton Bisection) are preferred [S1].
- **Parallel Surfaces:** Sphere marching fails when rays pass close to parallel surfaces without colliding. This method handles such cases by solving the polynomial directly, avoiding the slow convergence of iterative distance estimation [S1].
- **Coefficient Computation Cost:** Computing the intersection coefficients is significantly more expensive than evaluating the SDF directly (e.g., 492 vs 84 multiplications for tricubic). However, this cost is amortized if the polynomial is evaluated multiple times before exiting the node [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The initial coefficient computation is $O(1)$ but with a high constant factor (26 or 492 multiplications). Root finding is $O(1)$ for trilinear (cubic) and $O(1)$ for tricubic (degree 9) due to the fixed number of monotonic intervals (at most 3 and 9, respectively) [S1].
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$, storing only the polynomial coefficients and derivative roots.
- **Measured Performance:** In scenarios where sphere marching requires many iterations (e.g., near the surface or grazing rays), this method improves performance because the intersection polynomial is computed only once per ray and node, and then evaluated several times [S1].
- **Strengths:** Robust near the surface; avoids slow convergence of sphere marching; constant-time early skip for tricubic nodes [S1].
- **Limitations:** High initial computational cost for coefficient generation; analytical cubic solvers are unstable [S1].
- **When to Use:** When rays are likely to intersect or graze the isosurface within a leaf node, particularly in high-resolution SDF representations (tricubic) [S1].
- **When Not to Use:** When the ray is far from the surface and sphere marching converges quickly, as the overhead of polynomial computation may not be justified [S1].

## Background
The method was introduced in the paper "Rendering piecewise approximations of SDFs through analytic intersections" [S1]. It addresses the limitations of sphere marching, which can be slow when rays pass near parallel surfaces or graze the isosurface [S1]. The approach leverages the known polynomial representation of the SDF within leaf nodes to replace iterative distance estimation with direct root finding [S1].
