# Tangent Plane-Based Bounding Box Calculation

Also called: SPaGS Bounding Box, Two-Stage Bounding Box

## Overview
A two-stage method to calculate an axis-aligned screen-space bounding box for 3D Gaussians in spherical panoramas, addressing deformation caused by omnidirectional pixel distribution and non-linear spherical projection [S1]. It sets itself apart from standard affine approximations by projecting a tangent-space bounding box onto the equirectangular plane, ensuring validity despite spherical distortion [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** 3D Gaussian splat parameters (mean $\mathbf{m}$ in camera space, covariance $\Sigma$), camera intrinsic parameters (focal length $f$), and equirectangular image dimensions $(W, H)$.
*   **Output:** Axis-aligned bounding box $(p_{x,\min}, p_{x,\max}, p_{y,\min}, p_{y,\max})$ in equirectangular pixel coordinates.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Tangent Space Basis:** Vectors $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ (tangent), $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ (bitangent), $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ (normal) in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
*   **Tangent Box:** Rectangle defined by $t_{x,\min}, t_{x,\max}, t_{y,\min}, t_{y,\max}$ in tangent space.
*   **Camera Space Box:** Derived from tangent box via transformation.

**Steps:**

1.  **Define Tangent Space:**
    For a splat with mean $\mathbf{m}$ in camera space, determine the reference vector $\mathbf{r}_{\text{ref}}$ to avoid pole singularities. If $\mathbf{m}$ is close to the north or south pole (z-axis), use $\mathbf{r}_{\text{ref}} = (1, 0, 0)^T$; otherwise, use $\mathbf{r}_{\text{ref}} = (0, 0, 1)^T$ [S1].
    Compute the normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{m} / \|\mathbf{m}\|$.
    Compute the tangent $\hat{\mathbf{t}} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{r}_{\text{ref}} \times \hat{\mathbf{n}})$.
    Compute the bitangent $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \hat{\mathbf{n}} \times \hat{\mathbf{t}}$ [S1].
    The tangent plane is defined as $z=1$ with origin at the camera space origin [S1].

2.  **Transform to Tangent Space:**
    Construct the transformation matrix $M$ from camera space to tangent space. The columns of $M$ are $\hat{\mathbf{t}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}, \hat{\mathbf{n}}$.
    Transform the Gaussian covariance $\Sigma$ to tangent space: $\Sigma_t = M^T \Sigma M$ [S1, G].
    Calculate the 2D bounding box $(t_{x,\min}, t_{x,\max}, t_{y,\min}, t_{y,\max})$ on the tangent plane using the standard 2D Gaussian projection method (e.g., as in HTGS [HFW*25]), treating the tangent space as a local Euclidean plane [S1].

3.  **Project to Camera Space:**
    Map the tangent box corners back to camera space. A point in tangent space is $\mathbf{t} = (t_x, t_y, 1)^T$. The corresponding point in camera space $\mathbf{c}$ is:
    $$ \mathbf{c} = M \mathbf{t} = t_x \hat{\mathbf{t}} + t_y \hat{\mathbf{b}} + \hat{\mathbf{n}} $$
    Let $c_x, c_y, c_z$ be the components of $\mathbf{c}$ [S1].

4.  **Project to Equirectangular Plane (Stage 2):**
    Convert camera space coordinates to spherical angles (longitude $\phi$, latitude $\lambda$) or directly to equirectangular coordinates $(p_x, p_y)$.
    The equirectangular coordinates are proportional to $\phi$ and $\lambda$. Specifically:
    $$ p_x \propto \text{atan2}(c_x, c_z) $$
    $$ p_y \propto \text{asin}(c_y / \|\mathbf{c}\|) $$
    Due to the non-linear nature of $\text{atan2}$ and $\text{asin}$, the bounding box in equirectangular space is not simply the projection of the tangent box corners.

5.  **Calculate $p_y$ Bounds:**
    Since $\text{asin}$ is monotonically increasing, the minimum and maximum $p_y$ are found by evaluating the function at the extreme values of the argument derived from the tangent box combinations [S1].
    Calculate the range of $c_y / \|\mathbf{c}\|$ based on $t_{x,\min/max}$ and $t_{y,\min/max}$ [S1].
    $$ p_{y,\min} = \text{asin}\left( \min_{\mathbf{t} \in \text{Box}} \frac{c_y(\mathbf{t})}{\|\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{t})\|} \right) $$
    $$ p_{y,\max} = \text{asin}\left( \max_{\mathbf{t} \in \text{Box}} \frac{c_y(\mathbf{t})}{\|\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{t})\|} \right) $$
    (Specific combinations of $t_x, t_y$ are evaluated to find these extrema [S1]).

6.  **Calculate $p_x$ Bounds:**
    Directly using $\text{atan2}$ is problematic due to discontinuity at the pole cut. Instead, calculate a maximized differential angle [S1].
    Compute the range of longitude $\phi$ covered by the box.
    $$ p_{x,\min} = \text{map}(\phi_{\min}) $$
    $$ p_{x,\max} = \text{map}(\phi_{\max}) $$
    Where $\phi_{\min}$ and $\phi_{\max}$ are derived from the tangent box extremes using the differential angle method to handle the wrap-around and singularity [S1].

7.  **Handle Pole Coverage:**
    Check if the projected bounding box covers the north or south pole. This is determined if the latitude range includes $\pm 90^\circ$ (or if the condition in the source text is met) [S1].
    *   If it covers a pole: Set $p_{x,\min} = 0$ and $p_{x,\max} = W$ (full image width). Recalculate $p_{y,\min}$ and $p_{y,\max}$ relative to the projection center on the respective sphere [S1].
    *   If the projection center is exactly on a pole: Use the simplified calculation with negligible overhead [S1].

8.  **Load Balancing:**
    Apply a load balancing scheme similar to Radl et al. [RSP*24] to mitigate the impact of occasional large bounding boxes on Gaussian/tile instance creation [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math
import numpy as np

def tangent_plane_bounding_box(mean_cam, cov_cam, f, W, H):
    """
    Calculate the equirectangular bounding box for a 3D Gaussian splat.
    
    Args:
        mean_cam: numpy array (3,), camera space mean of the Gaussian.
        cov_cam: numpy array (3,3), camera space covariance matrix.
        f: float, focal length.
        W: int, image width.
        H: int, image height.
        
    Returns:
        tuple: (px_min, px_max, py_min, py_max) in pixel coordinates.
    """
    # 1. Define Tangent Space
    m = mean_cam
    norm_m = np.linalg.norm(m)
    if norm_m < 1e-8:
        # Degenerate case, return zero box or handle appropriately
        return (0, 0, 0, 0)
    
    n_hat = m / norm_m
    
    # Reference vector to avoid singularity
    if abs(n_hat[2]) > 0.9: # Near poles
        r_ref = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
    else:
        r_ref = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0])
        
    t_hat = np.cross(r_ref, n_hat)
    t_hat = t_hat / np.linalg.norm(t_hat)
    b_hat = np.cross(n_hat, t_hat)
    
    # Tangent space basis matrix M (columns are t_hat, b_hat, n_hat)
    M = np.column_stack((t_hat, b_hat, n_hat))
    
    # 2. Transform Covariance to Tangent Space
    cov_tan = M.T @ cov_cam @ M
    
    # 3. Calculate 2D Bounding Box in Tangent Space
    # Using HTGS approach for 2D Gaussian projection
    # Sigma_tan is 3x3, but we only care about the 2D part on z=1 plane
    # The effective 2D covariance for the projection onto z=1 is derived from the 3D covariance
    # Standard 2DGS bounding box calculation:
    # sigma_2d = cov_tan[:2, :2]
    # But HTGS uses a specific method. We approximate with standard 2D bounding box
    # for a 2D Gaussian with covariance Sigma_2d.
    
    sigma_2d = cov_tan[:2, :2]
    # Eigen decomposition to find extents
    eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(sigma_2d)
    # Extents are typically k * sqrt(eigenvalue) for some k (e.g., 3 for 99.7%)
    # Assuming k=3 for standard deviation coverage
    k = 3.0
    extents = k * np.sqrt(eigenvalues)
    
    # Rotate extents back to tangent space axes
    # The bounding box in tangent space (tx, ty) is axis-aligned in the eigenbasis
    # But we need the axis-aligned box in the (t_hat, b_hat) frame.
    # This requires finding the min/max of the rotated ellipse.
    # For simplicity, we use the standard AABB of the rotated ellipse.
    
    # Let's use a robust method: sample the ellipse boundary or use closed form
    # Closed form for AABB of rotated ellipse:
    # Center is (0,0) in tangent space relative to mean projection
    # The box is centered at the projection of the mean.
    # Mean in tangent space: t_m = M.T @ m = [0, 0, norm_m]. 
    # On the plane z=1, the center is (0,0) in tangent coordinates relative to the origin?
    # Actually, the tangent space origin is camera origin. The plane is z=1.
    # The projection of m onto z=1 is m / m_z.
    # In tangent coords, this is M.T @ (m / m_z).
    
    m_proj_tan = M.T @ (m / m[2]) # Project mean onto z=1 plane
    cx_tan = m_proj_tan[0]
    cy_tan = m_proj_tan[1]
    
    # Calculate AABB of the 2D Gaussian in tangent space
    # Sigma_2d is the covariance of the 2D distribution on the tangent plane
    # The bounding box is [cx_tan - dx, cx_tan + dx] x [cy_tan - dy, cy_tan + dy]
    # where dx, dy are the half-widths of the AABB of the rotated ellipse.
    
    # Compute half-widths of AABB for rotated ellipse
    # Ellipse: x^T Sigma^{-1} x = 1 (scaled by k^2)
    # This is complex. We use the eigenvalues/vectors.
    # The ellipse is defined by v1 * sqrt(l1) and v2 * sqrt(l2)
    # The AABB half-widths are:
    # dx = sqrt( (v1_x * sqrt(l1))^2 + (v2_x * sqrt(l2))^2 )
    # dy = sqrt( (v1_y * sqrt(l1))^2 + (v2_y * sqrt(l2))^2 )
    
    v1 = eigenvectors[:, 0]
    v2 = eigenvectors[:, 1]
    e1 = k * np.sqrt(eigenvalues[0])
    e2 = k * np.sqrt(eigenvalues[1])
    
    dx = math.sqrt((v1[0] * e1)**2 + (v2[0] * e2)**2)
    dy = math.sqrt((v1[1] * e1)**2 + (v2[1] * e2)**2)
    
    tx_min = cx_tan - dx
    tx_max = cx_tan + dx
    ty_min = cy_tan - dy
    ty_max = cy_tan + dy
    
    # 4. Project to Camera Space and then to Equirectangular
    # We need to find the min/max px, py for all points (tx, ty) in [tx_min, tx_max] x [ty_min, ty_max]
    
    # Function to convert tangent (tx, ty) to equirectangular (px, py)
    def tan_to_px_py(tx, ty):
        # Point in tangent space: t = (tx, ty, 1)
        # Point in camera space: c = M @ t
        c = M @ np.array([tx, ty, 1.0])
        cx, cy, cz = c
        
        # Equirectangular coordinates
        # px = (atan2(cx, cz) / (2*pi) + 0.5) * W
        # py = (asin(cy / norm(c)) / pi + 0.5) * H
        
        norm_c = math.sqrt(cx**2 + cy**2 + cz**2)
        if norm_c < 1e-8:
            return (0, 0)
            
        phi = math.atan2(cx, cz)
        lam = math.asin(cy / norm_c)
        
        px = (phi / (2 * math.pi) + 0.5) * W
        py = (lam / math.pi + 0.5) * H
        
        return (px, py)

    # Check for pole coverage
    # Determine if the box covers the pole by checking if the latitude range includes +/- pi/2
    # We can check the min/max latitude of the box.
    
    # To find min/max py, we need to minimize/maximize asin(cy/norm(c))
    # This is equivalent to minimizing/maximizing cy/norm(c) since asin is monotonic.
    
    # To find min/max px, we need to handle the atan2 discontinuity.
    
    # Sample the boundary of the tangent box to find extrema
    # Since the function is not convex, we must check corners and potentially edges.
    # However, the source suggests using specific combinations.
    
    # For py:
    # Evaluate at 4 corners
    corners = [
        (tx_min, ty_min),
        (tx_min, ty_max),
        (tx_max, ty_min),
        (tx_max, ty_max)
    ]
    
    py_vals = [tan_to_px_py(tx, ty)[1] for tx, ty in corners]
    py_min = min(py_vals)
    py_max = max(py_vals)
    
    # For px:
    # Check corners
    px_vals = [tan_to_px_py(tx, ty)[0] for tx, ty in corners]
    
    # Check for wrap-around
    # If the box crosses the -pi/pi boundary, the simple min/max will be wrong.
    # We check if the range of phi covers a discontinuity.
    
    phi_vals = []
    for tx, ty in corners:
        c = M @ np.array([tx, ty, 1.0])
        phi_vals.append(math.atan2(c[0], c[2]))
        
    # Check if the box covers the pole
    # If any point in the box has |lam| > pi/2 - epsilon, it covers the pole.
    # We can check the max |lam| from the corners and midpoints of edges.
    
    midpoints = [
        ((tx_min + tx_max)/2, ty_min),
        ((tx_min + tx_max)/2, ty_max),
        (tx_min, (ty_min + ty_max)/2),
        (tx_max, (ty_min + ty_max)/2)
    ]
    
    all_points = corners + midpoints
    max_abs_lam = 0
    for tx, ty in all_points:
        c = M @ np.array([tx, ty, 1.0])
        norm_c = math.sqrt(c[0]**2 + c[1]**2 + c[2]**2)
        lam = math.asin(c[1] / norm_c)
        if abs(lam) > max_abs_lam:
            max_abs_lam = abs(lam)
            
    if max_abs_lam > math.pi/2 - 1e-6:
        # Covers pole
        px_min = 0
        px_max = W
        # Recalculate py for the specific pole
        # If covering north pole, py_min = 0, py_max = H/2 (or similar depending on mapping)
        # The source says: "calculate the minimum and maximum py in a similar way for the projection center on the north and south sphere respectively"
        # This implies we treat the pole as the center.
        # For simplicity, if it covers the pole, we set the full width and clamp py to the visible hemisphere?
        # Or use the full range?
        # The source says "use the full range of image width for the px axis".
        # For py, we calculate based on the projection center.
        # If north pole, py range is [0, H/2] (assuming top is north)
        # If south pole, py range is [H/2, H]
        # Let's assume standard equirectangular: North at y=0, South at y=H.
        if max_abs_lam > 0: # North pole
            py_min = 0
            py_max = H / 2
        else: # South pole
            py_min = H / 2
            py_max = H
            
    else:
        # No pole coverage
        # Check for wrap-around in px
        # If the difference between max and min phi is large, it might wrap.
        # A simple heuristic: if px_max - px_min > W/2, it might wrap.
        # Better: check if the interval [phi_min, phi_max] contains pi or -pi.
        
        # We need the min and max phi in the box.
        # This is non-trivial. We sample more points or use the differential angle method.
        # For implementation, we can sample the edges.
        
        edge_points = []
        # Top and bottom edges
        for tx in [tx_min, tx_max]:
            for ty in np.linspace(ty_min, ty_max, 10):
                edge_points.append((tx, ty))
        # Left and right edges
        for ty in [ty_min, ty_max]:
            for tx in np.linspace(tx_min, tx_max, 10):
                edge_points.append((tx, ty))
                
        phi_edge_vals = []
        for tx, ty in edge_points:
            c = M @ np.array([tx, ty, 1.0])
            phi_edge_vals.append(math.atan2(c[0], c[2]))
            
        phi_min = min(phi_edge_vals)
        phi_max = max(phi_edge_vals)
        
        # Convert to px
        px_min = (phi_min / (2 * math.pi) + 0.5) * W
        px_max = (phi_max / (2 * math.pi) + 0.5) * W
        
        # Normalize to [0, W]
        px_min = px_min % W
        px_max = px_max % W
        
        # If the box wraps, px_min > px_max
        # In this case, the bounding box is [0, px_max] U [px_min, W]
        # But for a single AABB, we usually expand to [0, W] if it wraps significantly?
        # Or we keep it as two boxes?
        # The source implies a single AABB. If it wraps, the AABB is [0, W].
        if px_max - px_min < 0:
            px_min = 0
            px_max = W
            
    return (px_min, px_max, py_min, py_max)
```

## Subtopics

### Bounding Box and T-matrix Computation
Computes the screen-space bounding rectangle of a projected surfel by finding clip-space planes tangent to the unit sphere under the mapping $\bar{h} = T^T h$, using the inverse-transpose of the T-matrix for numerical stability with degenerate surfels [S2, unofficial]. It stores the three non-degenerate rows of $T$ as vectors $T_1, T_2, T_4 \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and computes the screen-space center and half-extents using a closed-form expression involving dot products among these vectors [S2, unofficial]. This method is particularly useful for handling degenerate (infinitesimally thin) surfels where tangent scales become very small during optimization [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per splat for the bounding box calculation, involving constant number of matrix-vector multiplications and trigonometric functions.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ per splat, storing only the basis vectors and box coordinates.
*   **Performance:** The method is designed for real-time rendering, avoiding the need for iterative intersection tests. The load balancing scheme helps mitigate the performance impact of large bounding boxes [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles spherical projection distortion accurately; numerically stable at poles; valid bounding boxes even with significant deformation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** The calculation of $p_x$ bounds requires careful handling of the $\text{atan2}$ discontinuity and pole coverage, which can be complex to implement correctly [S1].

## Background
The method was introduced in the SPaGS paper [S1] to enable real-time rendering of 3D Gaussian splats on spherical panoramas. It addresses the limitations of standard 3DGS rasterization, which assumes a local affine approximation that fails under non-linear spherical projections. The approach builds on the tangent space formulation used in HTGS [HFW*25] and extends it to the equirectangular domain.
