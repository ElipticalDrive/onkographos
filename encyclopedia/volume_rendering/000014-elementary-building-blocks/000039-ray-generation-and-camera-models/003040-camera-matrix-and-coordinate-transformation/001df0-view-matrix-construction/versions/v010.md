# View Matrix Construction

Also called: LookAt Matrix, LookAt Matrix Construction, Lookat View Matrix Construction, Camera View Matrix Computation

## Overview
View Matrix Construction computes a $4 \times 4$ transformation matrix from a camera's eye position, a target (center) point, and an up vector to define the camera's orientation and position in world space [S1, S3, S7, S11, unofficial]. It solves the problem of establishing a consistent coordinate frame for volume rendering by mapping world-space coordinates to the camera's local view space (where the camera typically looks down the negative Z-axis) or vice versa [S3, S11, unofficial]. It differs from [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md) by handling the rigid-body pose definition rather than the perspective projection and depth mapping, and from [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md) by explicitly returning the full $4 \times 4$ affine matrix including translation, whereas the sibling may only provide the $3 \times 3$ rotation basis [S11, S3, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   `eye`: A 3D vector representing the camera position in world space.
*   `center` (or `target`): A 3D vector representing the point the camera is looking at in world space.
*   `up`: A 3D vector representing the desired "up" direction in world space (typically $(0, 1, 0)$ or $(0, 0, 1)$ depending on the coordinate system convention, e.g., RAS) [S1, S3, S7, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   A $4 \times 4$ matrix. The interpretation depends on the desired direction of transformation:
    *   **Camera-to-World (C2W):** The matrix columns are the right, up, and forward vectors, with the eye position in the translation column. This transforms view-space rays to world space [S1, S3, S11, unofficial].
    *   **World-to-View (W2V):** The inverse of the C2W matrix. The rows are the right, up, and negative forward vectors, with the dot products of these axes with the eye position in the translation column [S6, S7, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   Vectors are 3-component floating-point arrays.
*   The matrix is a $4 \times 4$ array of floats, stored in column-major or row-major order depending on the API, but logically defined by its basis vectors and translation.

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Forward Vector ($f$):** Calculate the direction from the eye to the center.
    $$ f = \text{normalize}(center - eye) $$
    Note: In many conventions (e.g., OpenGL, ray marching), the camera looks down the negative Z-axis in view space. Therefore, the world-space forward direction corresponds to $-Z_{view}$ [S3, S11, unofficial].

2.  **Compute Right Vector ($s$):** Calculate the side vector by taking the cross product of the forward vector and the up vector, then normalizing.
    $$ s = \text{normalize}(cross(f, up)) $$
    This ensures the right vector is orthogonal to the viewing direction and the provided up vector [S3, S7, S11, unofficial].

3.  **Compute True Up Vector ($u$):** Calculate the corrected up vector by taking the cross product of the right vector and the forward vector.
    $$ u = cross(s, f) $$
    This step ensures the basis is strictly orthonormal, correcting for any non-orthogonality in the input `up` vector relative to `f` [S3, S7, S11, unofficial].

4.  **Construct Matrix:**
    *   **For Camera-to-World (C2W):** Stack the basis vectors as the first three columns and the eye position as the fourth column. The bottom row is $(0, 0, 0, 1)$.
        $$ M_{c2w} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & u_x & -f_x & 0 \\ s_y & u_y & -f_y & 0 \\ s_z & u_z & -f_z & 0 \\ eye_x & eye_y & eye_z & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
        *Note: Some sources define the third column as $f$ (forward) if the camera looks down $+Z$, but the majority of cited implementations for ray marching use $-f$ to align with the negative Z-axis convention [S3, S7, S11, unofficial].*
    *   **For World-to-View (W2V):** The rotation part is the transpose of the C2W rotation. The translation part is $-R^T \cdot eye$.
        $$ M_{w2v} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & s_y & s_z & -dot(s, eye) \\ u_x & u_y & u_z & -dot(u, eye) \\ -f_x & -f_y & -f_z & dot(f, eye) \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

**Pseudocode (C2W Construction):**

```python
def construct_view_matrix_c2w(eye, center, up):
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 Camera-to-World matrix.
    
    Args:
        eye: list/tuple of 3 floats (x, y, z)
        center: list/tuple of 3 floats (x, y, z)
        up: list/tuple of 3 floats (x, y, z)
        
    Returns:
        list of 4 lists, each containing 4 floats (4x4 matrix)
    """
    # Step 1: Forward vector (from eye to center)
    fx = center[0] - eye[0]
    fy = center[1] - eye[1]
    fz = center[2] - eye[2]
    length_f = (fx**2 + fy**2 + fz**2)**0.5
    if length_f == 0:
        raise ValueError("Eye and center are identical")
    f = (fx/length_f, fy/length_f, fz/length_f)

    # Step 2: Right vector (side)
    # Cross product of f and up
    sx = f[1]*up[2] - f[2]*up[1]
    sy = f[2]*up[0] - f[0]*up[2]
    sz = f[0]*up[1] - f[1]*up[0]
    length_s = (sx**2 + sy**2 + sz**2)**0.5
    if length_s == 0:
        # Fallback if f and up are parallel
        s = (1.0, 0.0, 0.0)
    else:
        s = (sx/length_s, sy/length_s, sz/length_s)

    # Step 3: True Up vector
    # Cross product of s and f
    ux = s[1]*f[2] - s[2]*f[1]
    uy = s[2]*f[0] - s[0]*f[2]
    uz = s[0]*f[1] - s[1]*f[0]
    # s and f are normalized and orthogonal, so u is already normalized
    u = (ux, uy, uz)

    # Step 4: Construct Matrix (Column-Major logical layout)
    # Columns: s, u, -f, eye
    # Row 0: s.x, u.x, -f.x, 0
    # Row 1: s.y, u.y, -f.y, 0
    # Row 2: s.z, u.z, -f.z, 0
    # Row 3: eye.x, eye.y, eye.z, 1
    
    mat = [
        [s[0], u[0], -f[0], 0.0],
        [s[1], u[1], -f[1], 0.0],
        [s[2], u[2], -f[2], 0.0],
        [eye[0], eye[1], eye[2], 1.0]
    ]
    return mat
```

**Pseudocode (W2V Construction):**

```python
def construct_view_matrix_w2v(eye, center, up):
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 World-to-View matrix.
    """
    # Reuse basis calculation from C2W
    c2w = construct_view_matrix_c2w(eye, center, up)
    
    # Extract basis vectors from C2W columns
    # c2w[row][col]
    s = (c2w[0][0], c2w[1][0], c2w[2][0])
    u = (c2w[0][1], c2w[1][1], c2w[2][1])
    neg_f = (c2w[0][2], c2w[1][2], c2w[2][2])
    f = (-neg_f[0], -neg_f[1], -neg_f[2])

    # W2V is the inverse of C2W. For orthonormal rotation, inverse is transpose.
    # Translation part is -R^T * eye
    
    # Row 0: s.x, s.y, s.z, -dot(s, eye)
    dot_s_eye = s[0]*eye[0] + s[1]*eye[1] + s[2]*eye[2]
    
    # Row 1: u.x, u.y, u.z, -dot(u, eye)
    dot_u_eye = u[0]*eye[0] + u[1]*eye[1] + u[2]*eye[2]
    
    # Row 2: -f.x, -f.y, -f.z, dot(f, eye)
    dot_f_eye = f[0]*eye[0] + f[1]*eye[1] + f[2]*eye[2]
    
    # Row 3: 0, 0, 0, 1
    
    mat = [
        [s[0], s[1], s[2], -dot_s_eye],
        [u[0], u[1], u[2], -dot_u_eye],
        [-f[0], -f[1], -f[2], dot_f_eye],
        [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0]
    ]
    return mat
```

**Parameters:**
*   `eye`: Camera position. Typical values depend on scene scale.
*   `center`: Look-at target.
*   `up`: Usually $(0, 1, 0)$ in Y-up systems or $(0, 0, 1)$ in Z-up systems [S1, S3, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Parallel Vectors:** If the `forward` vector is parallel to the `up` vector, the cross product for the `right` vector will be zero, causing normalization to fail. A guard clause or fallback basis is required [G].
*   **Coordinate System Convention:** The sign of the forward vector in the matrix depends on whether the view space Z-axis points towards or away from the viewer. Most ray marching implementations assume the camera looks down the negative Z-axis, so the third column of C2W is $-f$ [S3, S11, unofficial].
*   **W-Component:** When transforming direction vectors (like ray directions), the W-component must be 0.0 to prevent translation from affecting the direction [S11, unofficial]. When transforming points (like the eye position), W must be 1.0.

## Subtopics

### [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)
Average Pose Calculation computes a single representative camera pose from a set of input poses, providing a stable reference frame for subsequent volume rendering operations. It differs from [Focus Point Calculation](7638-focus-point-calculation.md) by averaging origins and directions rather than minimizing perpendicular distance to viewing rays, and from [Bounding Sphere Estimation](7056-bounding-sphere-estimation.md) by yielding a transformation matrix rather than a scalar radius. It serves as the geometric anchor for [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) when a global coordinate system is required.

### [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md)
Camera Pose Transformation applies geometric operations—specifically translation and rotation—to camera-to-world (c2w) poses or 3D points to align the scene with a desired coordinate system or to model continuous motion. It solves the problem of converting raw reconstruction data (e.g., from COLMAP) or discrete pose samples into a format suitable for volume rendering pipelines, which often assume specific scene bounds or require smooth temporal interpolation. It differs from [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) by operating directly on the pose parameters (rotation matrix $R$ and translation vector $t$) rather than constructing a full view matrix from basis vectors, and from [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md) by focusing on individual pose adjustments rather than statistical aggregation.

### [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)
Orbit Camera Basis Computation computes an orthonormal camera basis (eye position, forward, right, and up vectors) for an orbit camera that looks at a fixed target center from a specified distance, oriented by spherical yaw and pitch angles. It solves the problem of determining the viewing direction and position for navigation in 3D space based on user input angles.

### [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md)
General Perspective Transformation is a camera model where both the eye position and the projection plane are displaced from the world origin, unlike standard canonical perspective projections which typically fix the eye at the origin or the projection plane at $z=0$. This method solves the problem of numerical instability in screen-space depth values (screen-z) when rendering scenes with large bounding boxes or when the visibility bounding box (Vbb) is far from the origin. By shifting the eye and projection plane together, it ensures the Jacobian of the transformation is the identity at the center of the visibility bounding box, minimizing floating-point errors in depth ordering during compositing. It differs from [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md) by focusing on the geometric projection matrix construction rather than pose averaging, and from [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md) by explicitly managing the off-origin projection plane geometry.

### [Custom Matrix Math Library](3216-custom-matrix-math-library.md)
A custom implementation of 3D transformation mathematics (translation, scaling, perspective projection, and look-at) written natively in JavaScript to eliminate dependencies on external linear algebra libraries. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods by providing a self-contained, lightweight API for constructing camera matrices and handling vector arithmetic directly within the browser environment, specifically optimized for WebGL 2 hardware instancing pipelines.

### [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md)
Camera Look-at Basis Construction constructs a 3x3 rotation matrix that transforms world-space vectors into camera-local space, given a camera origin and look-at target. It builds an orthogonal camera coordinate system (basis) from a camera center and a look-at point by deriving an up vector orthogonal to the viewing direction, then computing the right vector via cross product.

### [View Transformations](5333-view-transformations.md)
View Transformations define the sequence of affine and projective mappings that convert volume data from its native object coordinate system into the final image coordinate system. This method manages four distinct transformation matrices—modeling, viewing, projection, and viewport—to handle spatial positioning, camera orientation, and rasterization bounds. It is distinguished from sibling methods by its explicit support for a multi-stage pipeline (Object $\to$ World $\to$ Eye $\to$ Clip $\to$ Image) and its integration with light direction vector transformation, ensuring shading calculations occur in the correct coordinate space.

### Inverse View Matrix Loading
Copies the inverse view matrix (3 float4s, 12 floats) to device symbol for ray direction transformation [S17, unofficial]. It copies 12 floats (3x4 matrix) to `invViewMatrix` symbol [S17, unofficial].

### UVW-based Camera Translation
Defines a fixed up vector and camera position, normalizes them, and computes right and forward vectors via cross products to build rotation and translation matrices for world-to-camera and camera-to-world transformations [S18, unofficial]. It transforms the camera position using these combined matrices to adjust the view [S18, unofficial].

### Model-View Transformation
The modelview matrix is initialized to the identity matrix, then multiplied by transformation matrices as the scene is moved [S19, unofficial]. Translation by $(x,y,z)$ generates a specific matrix, rotation by $\theta$ about a unit vector generates another, and scaling by $(a,b,c)$ generates a third [S19, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ for a single camera pose, involving a constant number of vector operations (subtraction, normalization, cross products).
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$, storing a single $4 \times 4$ matrix.
*   **Strengths:** Simple, robust, and widely supported. Provides an intuitive way to specify camera orientation.
*   **Limitations:** Suffers from gimbal lock if used with Euler angles for input (though the LookAt method itself avoids this by using vectors). Numerical instability can occur if the eye and center are very close or if the up vector is parallel to the forward vector.
*   **When to use:** When the camera pose is defined by a position and a target point.
*   **When not to use:** When the camera orientation is defined by quaternions or Euler angles directly (use [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md) or conversion functions).

## Background
The LookAt matrix construction is a fundamental technique in computer graphics, originally popularized by the OpenGL utility library function `gluLookAt` [S3, unofficial]. It is used in various volume rendering frameworks, such as `flashdrr` for differentiable DRR synthesis [S1, unofficial], and in ray marching tutorials for constructing camera spaces [S3, S7, S11, unofficial]. It is also referenced in CUDA volume rendering implementations for loading inverse view matrices [S17, unofficial].
