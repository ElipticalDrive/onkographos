# View Matrix Construction

Also called: LookAt Matrix, LookAt Matrix Construction, Lookat View Matrix Construction, Camera View Matrix Computation

## Overview
View Matrix Construction computes the $4 \times 4$ transformation matrix that maps coordinates between world space and camera (view) space, given an eye position, a target (center) point, and an up vector [S1, S3, S7, S11, unofficial]. It solves the problem of defining a camera's orientation and position to generate rays for volume rendering or to transform scene geometry relative to the viewer [S3, S11, unofficial]. Unlike [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md) which handles projection and depth normalization, or [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md) which adjusts existing poses, this method constructs the fundamental basis vectors and translation from raw geometric parameters [S11, S16, unofficial]. It differs from [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md) by typically returning the full $4 \times 4$ affine matrix including translation, whereas the sibling may return only the $3 \times 3$ rotation component [S11, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   `eye`: A 3D vector representing the camera position in world space [S1, S3, S7, S11, unofficial].
*   `center` (or `target`): A 3D vector representing the point the camera is looking at in world space [S1, S3, S7, S11, unofficial].
*   `up`: A 3D vector representing the desired "up" direction in world space, typically $(0, 1, 0)$ [S1, S3, S7, S11, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   `view_matrix`: A $4 \times 4$ matrix. The interpretation depends on the convention:
    *   **Camera-to-World (C2W):** Used to transform ray directions from view space to world space for intersection testing. The first three columns are the basis vectors (right, up, forward), and the fourth column is the eye position [S1, S3, S5, S11, S12, unofficial].
    *   **World-to-View (W2V):** Used to transform world coordinates into camera space. The first three rows are the basis vectors, and the translation column is derived from dot products with the eye position [S7, S19, unofficial].
    *   *Note:* Sources vary in whether they construct the C2W or W2V matrix directly. The algorithm below constructs the **Camera-to-World** matrix, as this is the standard requirement for ray generation in volume rendering (transforming view-space rays to world space) [S1, S3, S5, S11, unofficial]. If a World-to-View matrix is required, it is the inverse of the constructed C2W matrix [S19, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   Vectors are 3-component floats ($x, y, z$).
*   Matrix is $4 \times 4$ floats, stored in column-major order (standard for OpenGL/GLSL) or row-major (standard for C++/Python linear algebra libraries). The pseudocode uses column-major layout for the C2W matrix.

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Forward Vector ($f$):** Calculate the direction from the eye to the center. Normalize it.
    $$ f = \frac{\text{center} - \text{eye}}{\| \text{center} - \text{eye} \|} $$
    In view space, the camera typically looks down the negative Z-axis. Therefore, the world-space forward vector $f$ corresponds to the negative Z-axis of the camera [S3, S11, unofficial].

2.  **Compute Right Vector ($s$):** Calculate the cross product of the forward vector $f$ and the up vector. Normalize the result. This vector is orthogonal to the plane defined by $f$ and `up`.
    $$ s = \frac{f \times \text{up}}{\| f \times \text{up} \|} $$
    *Note:* Some sources compute `cross(up, f)` and negate, or `cross(f, up)`. The standard right-handed system where $s$ is Right, $u$ is Up, and $-f$ is Forward requires $s = f \times \text{up}$ if $u = s \times f$ [S3, S7, S11, unofficial]. If $f$ and `up` are parallel, this step fails (degenerate case).

3.  **Compute True Up Vector ($u$):** Calculate the cross product of the right vector $s$ and the forward vector $f$. Normalize it. This ensures the up vector is orthogonal to both the viewing direction and the right vector.
    $$ u = \frac{s \times f}{\| s \times f \|} $$
    *Note:* Some sources skip normalization here if $s$ and $f$ are already normalized and orthogonal, but normalization is safer for precision [S3, S7, S11, unofficial].

4.  **Construct Matrix:** Assemble the $4 \times 4$ Camera-to-World matrix.
    *   The first three columns are the basis vectors $s$ (Right), $u$ (Up), and $f$ (Forward).
    *   The fourth column is the eye position.
    *   The bottom row is $(0, 0, 0, 1)$.
    
    $$ M_{c2w} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & u_x & f_x & \text{eye}_x \\ s_y & u_y & f_y & \text{eye}_y \\ s_z & u_z & f_z & \text{eye}_z \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
    
    *Clarification on Forward Vector Sign:* In many graphics APIs (like OpenGL), the camera looks down the negative Z-axis. If the ray direction in view space is $(0, 0, -1)$, then the world-space forward vector $f$ (which points from eye to center) is actually the *negative* of the camera's local Z-axis. However, for transforming a *direction* vector from view space to world space, if the view-space direction is $d_{view}$, the world-space direction is $M_{c2w} \cdot [d_{view}, 0]^T$. If $d_{view} = (0,0,-1)$, the result is $-f$. Thus, the third column of the rotation part should be $f$ if we define the basis as (Right, Up, Forward) where Forward points to the target. If the basis is defined as (Right, Up, -Forward), the third column is $-f$. The sources [S3, S7, S11, unofficial] explicitly use $f$ (center-eye) as the third column for the C2W matrix, implying the basis vector $z_{cam}$ in world space is $f$. This means a view-space ray pointing in $-z$ will be transformed to $-f$ in world space, which points towards the center. This is consistent [S3, S11, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math

def normalize(v):
    """Normalize a 3D vector."""
    length = math.sqrt(v[0]**2 + v[1]**2 + v[2]**2)
    if length == 0:
        return [0, 0, 0] # Guard against division by zero
    return [v[0]/length, v[1]/length, v[2]/length]

def cross(a, b):
    """Cross product of two 3D vectors."""
    return [
        a[1]*b[2] - a[2]*b[1],
        a[2]*b[0] - a[0]*b[2],
        a[0]*b[1] - a[1]*b[0]
    ]

def view_matrix_construction(eye, center, up):
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 Camera-to-World matrix.
    
    Args:
        eye: [x, y, z] Camera position.
        center: [x, y, z] Target point.
        up: [x, y, z] Up vector.
        
    Returns:
        mat: 4x4 matrix as list of lists (column-major representation flattened or structured).
             Here returned as a list of 4 columns, each column is a list of 4 floats.
    """
    # Step 1: Forward vector (from eye to center)
    forward = normalize([center[i] - eye[i] for i in range(3)])
    
    # Step 2: Right vector (side)
    # Cross product of forward and up
    side = cross(forward, up)
    side = normalize(side)
    
    # Step 3: True Up vector
    # Cross product of side and forward
    true_up = cross(side, forward)
    true_up = normalize(true_up)
    
    # Step 4: Construct Matrix
    # Columns: Right, Up, Forward, Position
    # Row 0: s.x, u.x, f.x, eye.x
    # Row 1: s.y, u.y, f.y, eye.y
    # Row 2: s.z, u.z, f.z, eye.z
    # Row 3: 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0
    
    mat = [
        [side[0], true_up[0], forward[0], eye[0]],
        [side[1], true_up[1], forward[1], eye[1]],
        [side[2], true_up[2], forward[2], eye[2]],
        [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0]
    ]
    
    return mat
```

**Parameters:**
*   `eye`: Camera position. Typical values depend on scene scale [S1, unofficial].
*   `center`: Look-at target. Often the center of the volume [S1, unofficial].
*   `up`: Usually $(0, 1, 0)$ [S1, S3, S7, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Degenerate Input:** If `eye` equals `center`, the forward vector is zero, and normalization fails [S3, S11, unofficial]. A guard must check for this distance.
*   **Parallel Vectors:** If `forward` and `up` are parallel (cross product is zero), the right vector cannot be computed [S3, S11, unofficial]. This occurs when the camera is looking straight up or down relative to the provided up vector. A common fix is to perturb the `up` vector or use a default up vector orthogonal to `forward` [G].
*   **Coordinate System Convention:** The matrix constructed above is Camera-to-World. To transform a ray origin (point), use $w=1$. To transform a ray direction (vector), use $w=0$ [S11, S3, unofficial].
*   **Forward Vector Sign:** Ensure consistency with the ray generation step. If rays are generated in view space pointing down $-Z$, the world-space direction is $-f$ (where $f$ is the third column) [S3, S11, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)
Computes a single representative camera pose from a set of input poses, providing a stable reference frame for subsequent volume rendering operations. It differs from [Focus Point Calculation](7638-focus-point-calculation.md) by averaging origins and directions rather than minimizing perpendicular distance to viewing rays, and from [Bounding Sphere Estimation](7056-bounding-sphere-estimation.md) by yielding a transformation matrix rather than a scalar radius. It serves as the geometric anchor for [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) when a global coordinate system is required.

### [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md)
Camera Pose Transformation applies geometric operations—specifically translation and rotation—to camera-to-world (c2w) poses or 3D points to align the scene with a desired coordinate system or to model continuous motion. It solves the problem of converting raw reconstruction data (e.g., from COLMAP) or discrete pose samples into a format suitable for volume rendering pipelines, which often assume specific scene bounds or require smooth temporal interpolation. It differs from [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) by operating directly on the pose parameters (rotation matrix $R$ and translation vector $t$) rather than constructing a full view matrix from basis vectors, and from [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md) by focusing on individual pose adjustments rather than statistical aggregation.

### [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)
Computes an orthonormal camera basis (eye position, forward, right, and up vectors) for an orbit camera that looks at a fixed target center from a specified distance, oriented by spherical yaw and pitch angles. It solves the problem of determining the viewing direction and position for navigation in 3D space based on user input angles.

### [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md)
General Perspective Transformation is a camera model where both the eye position and the projection plane are displaced from the world origin, unlike standard canonical perspective projections which typically fix the eye at the origin or the projection plane at $z=0$. This method solves the problem of numerical instability in screen-space depth values (screen-z) when rendering scenes with large bounding boxes or when the visibility bounding box (Vbb) is far from the origin. By shifting the eye and projection plane together, it ensures the Jacobian of the transformation is the identity at the center of the visibility bounding box, minimizing floating-point errors in depth ordering during compositing. It differs from [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md) by focusing on the geometric projection matrix construction rather than pose averaging, and from [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md) by explicitly managing the off-origin projection plane geometry.

### [Custom Matrix Math Library](3216-custom-matrix-math-library.md)
A custom implementation of 3D transformation mathematics (translation, scaling, perspective projection, and look-at) written natively in JavaScript to eliminate dependencies on external linear algebra libraries. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods by providing a self-contained, lightweight API for constructing camera matrices and handling vector arithmetic directly within the browser environment, specifically optimized for WebGL 2 hardware instancing pipelines.

### [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md)
Constructs a 3x3 rotation matrix that transforms world-space vectors into camera-local space, given a camera origin and look-at target. It builds an orthogonal camera coordinate system (basis) from a camera center and a look-at point by deriving an up vector orthogonal to the viewing direction, then computing the right vector via cross product.

### [View Transformations](5333-view-transformations.md)
View Transformations define the sequence of affine and projective mappings that convert volume data from its native object coordinate system into the final image coordinate system. This method manages four distinct transformation matrices—modeling, viewing, projection, and viewport—to handle spatial positioning, camera orientation, and rasterization bounds. It is distinguished from sibling methods by its explicit support for a multi-stage pipeline (Object $\to$ World $\to$ Eye $\to$ Clip $\to$ Image) and its integration with light direction vector transformation, ensuring shading calculations occur in the correct coordinate space.

#### Inverse View Matrix Loading
Copies the inverse view matrix (3 float4s, 12 floats) to device symbol for ray direction transformation [S17, unofficial]. It copies 12 floats (3x4 matrix) to `invViewMatrix` symbol [S17, unofficial].

#### UVW-based Camera Translation
Defines fixed up vector `t = {0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f}` and camera position `w = camPosition` [S18, unofficial]. Normalizes `w` [S18, unofficial]. Computes right vector `u = normalize(cross(t, w))` [S18, unofficial]. Computes forward vector `v = normalize(cross(w, u))` [S18, unofficial]. Builds translation matrix `matT` with `Change` offsets [S18, unofficial]. Builds rotation matrix `temp` from `u, v, w` columns [S18, unofficial]. Inverts `temp` to get `rev` [S18, unofficial]. Extracts axes `X, Y, Z` from `rev` and normalizes [S18, unofficial]. Builds world-to-camera rotation `w2cr` and translation `w2ct` [S18, unofficial]. Combines to `w2c = w2cr * w2ct` [S18, unofficial]. Transforms camera position: `adjustedvec = matT * w2c * camPosition` [S18, unofficial]. Builds camera-to-world rotation `c2wr` and translation `c2wt` [S18, unofficial]. Combines to `c2w = c2wr * c2wt` [S18, unofficial]. Updates `camPosition` with `c2w * adjustedvec` [S18, unofficial].

#### Model-View Transformation
The modelview matrix is initialized to the identity matrix, then multiplied by transformation matrices as the scene is moved [S19, unofficial]. Translation by $(x,y,z)$ generates the matrix $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & -x \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -z \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ [S19, unofficial]. Rotation of $\theta$ degrees about unit vector $(x,y,z)$ generates the matrix $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos(\theta) & -\sin(\theta) & 0 \\ 0 & \sin(\theta) & \cos(\theta) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ [S19, unofficial]. Scaling by $(a,b,c)$ generates the matrix $R = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & b & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & c & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ [S19, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ arithmetic operations (constant time) relative to scene size. Dominated by a few vector normalizations and cross products.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ for the matrix storage (16 floats).
*   **Performance:** Extremely fast; suitable for per-frame camera updates in interactive applications [S1, S11, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Simple to implement; provides intuitive control over camera orientation via eye/center/up [S3, S11, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Sensitive to degenerate cases (parallel vectors) [S3, S11, unofficial]. Does not handle projection or field-of-view; those are separate steps [S15, S16, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When a camera pose is defined by a position and a look-at target [S3, S11, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When the camera is an orbit camera with spherical coordinates (use [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)) or when a global average pose is needed (use [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)).

## Background
The LookAt matrix is a fundamental concept in computer graphics, originating from OpenGL's `gluLookAt` function [S3, unofficial]. It is widely used in volume rendering to define the camera pose for ray generation [S1, S11, unofficial]. The method assumes the camera center is aligned with the negative z-axis in view space [S3, S11, unofficial]. It is used in various volume rendering pipelines, including differentiable DRR synthesis [S1, unofficial] and ray marching [S3, S7, S11, unofficial].
