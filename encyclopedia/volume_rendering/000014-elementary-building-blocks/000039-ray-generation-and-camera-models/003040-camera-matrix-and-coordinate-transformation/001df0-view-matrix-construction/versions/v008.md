# View Matrix Construction

Also called: Camera Matrix Construction, Camera View Matrix Computation, LookAt Matrix, LookAt Matrix Construction, Lookat View Matrix Construction, gluLookAt, viewmatrix

## Overview
View Matrix Construction computes the $4 \times 4$ transformation matrix that maps coordinates between world space and camera (view) space, given an eye position, a target point (center), and an up vector [S1, S3, S6, S10, unofficial]. It solves the problem of defining a camera's orientation and position to generate rays for volume rendering or scene projection [S3, S10, unofficial]. It differs from [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md) by establishing the initial world-to-view or view-to-world basis rather than mapping to normalized device coordinates, and from [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md) by constructing the final matrix from geometric primitives rather than manipulating existing pose components algebraically [S1, S10, S11, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   `eye`: The camera position in world space (3D vector) [S1, S3, S6, S10, unofficial].
*   `center` (or `target`): The point the camera looks at in world space (3D vector) [S1, S3, S6, S10, unofficial].
*   `up`: The world-space up direction vector (3D vector), typically $(0, 1, 0)$ or $(0, 0, 1)$ [S1, S3, S6, S10, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   `view_matrix`: A $4 \times 4$ matrix. The specific layout depends on the desired direction of transformation (World-to-View or View-to-World). Most implementations in volume rendering contexts (e.g., ray marching) construct a **Camera-to-World** matrix to transform view-space ray directions into world space [S1, S3, S10, unofficial]. Some libraries (e.g., VTK-compatible) may construct a World-to-View matrix [S1, S6, unofficial]. The standard "LookAt" construction described below produces a Camera-to-World matrix where the camera looks down the negative Z-axis in view space [S3, S10, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   Vectors are 3-component floating-point arrays.
*   The matrix is a $4 \times 4$ array of floats, typically stored in column-major order for OpenGL/GLSL or row-major for C++/Python depending on the backend, but logically defined by its basis vectors.

**Steps:**
1.  **Calculate Forward Vector ($f$):** Compute the direction from the eye to the center.
    $$ f = \text{normalize}(center - eye) $$
    This vector represents the negative Z-axis of the camera in world space [S3, S6, S10, unofficial]. Note: Some implementations define forward as $eye - center$, resulting in a positive Z-axis look direction [S4, unofficial]. The standard convention for ray marching (looking down -Z) uses $center - eye$ [S3, S10, unofficial].

2.  **Calculate Right Vector ($s$):** Compute the side vector by taking the cross product of the forward vector and the up vector, then normalizing.
    $$ s = \text{normalize}(cross(f, up)) $$
    This vector represents the X-axis of the camera [S3, S6, S10, unofficial]. If $f$ and $up$ are parallel, this step fails (degenerate case) [G].

3.  **Calculate True Up Vector ($u$):** Compute the corrected up vector by taking the cross product of the right vector and the forward vector.
    $$ u = cross(s, f) $$
    This vector represents the Y-axis of the camera. It is orthogonal to both $s$ and $f$ [S3, S6, S10, unofficial]. Note: Some sources normalize this result, but since $s$ and $f$ are orthonormal, $u$ is already unit length [S3, S10, unofficial].

4.  **Construct Matrix:** Assemble the $4 \times 4$ matrix.
    *   **Camera-to-World Matrix ($M_{c2w}$):** Used to transform view-space rays to world space. The basis vectors $s, u, -f$ form the first three columns (or rows, depending on storage convention), and the eye position forms the translation column.
        $$ M_{c2w} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & u_x & -f_x & 0 \\ s_y & u_y & -f_y & 0 \\ s_z & u_z & -f_z & 0 \\ eye_x & eye_y & eye_z & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
        In column-major storage (common in GLSL/OpenGL), the columns are $[s, 0]^T, [u, 0]^T, [-f, 0]^T, [eye, 1]^T$ [S3, S10, unofficial].
    *   **World-to-View Matrix ($M_{w2v}$):** The inverse of $M_{c2w}$. Since it is a rigid body transformation, the inverse is the transpose of the rotation part combined with the negative translation.
        $$ M_{w2v} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & s_y & s_z & -dot(s, eye) \\ u_x & u_y & u_z & -dot(u, eye) \\ -f_x & -f_y & -f_z & dot(f, eye) \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
        [S6, S10, unofficial].

**Pseudocode (Camera-to-World Construction):**

```python
def construct_view_matrix(eye, center, up):
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 Camera-to-World view matrix.
    Assumes the camera looks down the negative Z-axis in view space.
    
    Args:
        eye: list/tuple of 3 floats (x, y, z)
        center: list/tuple of 3 floats (x, y, z)
        up: list/tuple of 3 floats (x, y, z)
        
    Returns:
        list of 4 lists, each containing 4 floats (4x4 matrix)
    """
    # Step 1: Forward vector (negative Z axis in view space)
    # f = normalize(center - eye)
    fx = center[0] - eye[0]
    fy = center[1] - eye[1]
    fz = center[2] - eye[2]
    length_f = (fx**2 + fy**2 + fz**2)**0.5
    if length_f == 0:
        raise ValueError("Eye and center are identical")
    f = (fx/length_f, fy/length_f, fz/length_f)

    # Step 2: Right vector (X axis)
    # s = normalize(cross(f, up))
    sx = f[1]*up[2] - f[2]*up[1]
    sy = f[2]*up[0] - f[0]*up[2]
    sz = f[0]*up[1] - f[1]*up[0]
    length_s = (sx**2 + sy**2 + sz**2)**0.5
    if length_s == 0:
        raise ValueError("Forward vector is parallel to Up vector")
    s = (sx/length_s, sy/length_s, sz/length_s)

    # Step 3: True Up vector (Y axis)
    # u = cross(s, f)
    ux = s[1]*f[2] - s[2]*f[1]
    uy = s[2]*f[0] - s[0]*f[2]
    uz = s[0]*f[1] - s[1]*f[0]
    # u is already normalized if s and f are orthonormal

    # Step 4: Construct Matrix (Column-Major Layout for OpenGL/GLSL)
    # Columns: s, u, -f, eye
    # Row 0: s.x, u.x, -f.x, 0
    # Row 1: s.y, u.y, -f.y, 0
    # Row 2: s.z, u.z, -f.z, 0
    # Row 3: eye.x, eye.y, eye.z, 1
    
    neg_f = (-f[0], -f[1], -f[2])
    
    matrix = [
        [s[0], u[0], neg_f[0], 0.0],
        [s[1], u[1], neg_f[1], 0.0],
        [s[2], u[2], neg_f[2], 0.0],
        [eye[0], eye[1], eye[2], 1.0]
    ]
    
    return matrix
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Parallel Vectors:** If the forward vector is parallel to the up vector, the cross product for the right vector becomes zero, causing a division by zero during normalization [G]. This results in an undefined camera orientation. A common fix is to check for this condition and substitute a default right vector or adjust the up vector [G].
*   **Axis Convention:** The matrix assumes the camera looks down the negative Z-axis in view space [S3, S10, unofficial]. If the rendering engine expects a positive Z-axis look direction, the sign of the forward vector in the matrix construction must be flipped [S4, unofficial].
*   **W-Component:** When transforming direction vectors (rays), the w-component must be 0.0 to prevent translation from affecting the direction [S10, unofficial]. When transforming points (eye position), the w-component must be 1.0 [S10, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)
Average Pose Calculation computes a single representative camera pose from a set of input poses, providing a stable reference frame for subsequent volume rendering operations. It differs from [Focus Point Calculation](7638-focus-point-calculation.md) by averaging origins and directions rather than minimizing perpendicular distance to viewing rays, and from [Bounding Sphere Estimation](7056-bounding-sphere-estimation.md) by yielding a transformation matrix rather than a scalar radius. It serves as the geometric anchor for [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) when a global coordinate system is required.

### [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md)
Camera Pose Transformation applies rigid body transformations—specifically rotation and translation—to camera coordinates or scene points to define the camera's position and orientation in 3D space. It solves the problem of mapping between different coordinate systems (e.g., world-to-camera vs. camera-to-world) and adjusting camera parameters for specific rendering pipelines. It differs from [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) by focusing on the algebraic manipulation of pose components (rotation matrices and translation vectors) rather than constructing the final view matrix from basis vectors. It differs from [Rigid Body Transformation (SE3)](11475-rigid-body-transformation-se3.md) by often operating on specific subsets of the transformation (e.g., rotation only or translation only) or handling format conversions between pose representations.

### [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)
Orbit Camera Basis Computation computes an orthonormal camera basis (eye position, forward, right, and up vectors) for an orbit camera that looks at a fixed target center from a specified distance, oriented by spherical yaw and pitch angles. It solves the problem of determining the viewing direction and position for navigation in 3D space based on user input angles.

### [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md)
General Perspective Transformation is a perspective transformation where both the eye and the projection plane may be positioned away from the world origin. It solves the problem of maintaining numerical stability in screen-z values by ensuring the Jacobian is the identity at the center of the visibility bounding box (Vbb). Unlike standard perspective projections that fix the eye at the origin, this method allows arbitrary translation along the viewing axis, which is critical for preventing floating-point errors when compositing thousands of closely spaced triangles far from the origin.

### [Custom Matrix Math Library](3216-custom-matrix-math-library.md)
Custom Matrix Math Library is a custom implementation of 3D transformation mathematics (translation, scaling, perspective projection, and look-at) written natively in JavaScript to eliminate dependencies on external linear algebra libraries. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods by providing a self-contained, lightweight API for constructing camera matrices and handling vector arithmetic directly within the browser environment, specifically optimized for WebGL 2 hardware instancing pipelines.

### [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md)
Camera Look-at Basis Construction constructs a 3x3 rotation matrix that transforms world-space vectors into camera-local space, given a camera origin and look-at target. It builds an orthogonal camera coordinate system (basis) from a camera center and a look-at point by deriving an up vector orthogonal to the viewing direction, then computing the right vector via cross product.

### [View Transformations](5333-view-transformations.md)
View Transformations define the sequence of affine and projective mappings that convert volume data from its native object coordinate system into the final image coordinate system. This method manages four distinct transformation matrices—modeling, viewing, projection, and viewport—to handle spatial positioning, camera orientation, and rasterization bounds. It is distinguished from sibling methods by its explicit support for a multi-stage pipeline (Object $\to$ World $\to$ Eye $\to$ Clip $\to$ Image) and its integration with light direction vector transformation, ensuring shading calculations occur in the correct coordinate space.

### Inverse View Matrix Loading
Inverse View Matrix Loading copies the inverse view matrix (3 float4s, 12 floats) to a device symbol for ray direction transformation [S16, unofficial]. It involves copying 12 floats (representing the 3x4 rotation/translation part of the inverse matrix) to a global variable `invViewMatrix` [S16, unofficial].

### UVW-based Camera Translation
UVW-based Camera Translation defines a fixed up vector and camera position, normalizes the position to get the forward vector, computes the right vector via cross product, and derives the true up vector [S17, unofficial]. It builds translation and rotation matrices, inverts the rotation, extracts axes, and combines them to transform the camera position into a new coordinate system [S17, unofficial].

### Model-View Transformation
Model-View Transformation initializes a modelview matrix to the identity and multiplies it by transformation matrices as the scene is moved [S18, unofficial]. It uses specific matrix forms for translation $T$, rotation $R$, and scaling $S$ to combine object and camera transformations [S18, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$. The construction involves a constant number of vector operations (subtraction, normalization, cross products) and matrix assembly.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$. Requires storage for a few temporary vectors and the final $4 \times 4$ matrix.
*   **Performance:** Extremely fast, suitable for per-frame camera updates in real-time rendering and per-ray initialization in volume rendering [S1, S10, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Simple to implement, numerically stable (provided the parallel vector edge case is handled), and widely supported across graphics APIs (OpenGL, Vulkan, Metal, CUDA) [S3, S6, S10, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Assumes a pinhole camera model. Does not handle lens distortion or complex camera intrinsics directly; those are handled by the projection matrix [S10, unofficial]. Degenerate inputs (parallel vectors) require explicit handling [G].

## Background
The LookAt matrix construction is a fundamental technique in computer graphics, originally popularized by the OpenGL utility library function `gluLookAt` [S3, unofficial]. It is used to define the camera's position and orientation in 3D space. In volume rendering, it is essential for transforming rays from the camera's local view space (where they are typically generated uniformly) into world space for intersection testing with the volume data [S1, S3, S10, unofficial]. The method is implemented in various libraries, including VTK-compatible renderers [S1, unofficial], neural rendering pipelines like NeRF [S2, S4, unofficial], and ray marching tutorials [S3, S6, S10, unofficial].
