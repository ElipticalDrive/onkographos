# View Matrix Construction

Also called: Camera Matrix Construction, LookAt Matrix, LookAt Matrix Construction, gluLookAt, viewmatrix

## Overview
View Matrix Construction generates a $4 \times 4$ transformation matrix that maps coordinates from world space to camera (view) space, or conversely transforms rays from view space to world space, given an eye position, a target point, and an up vector [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial]. It solves the problem of defining a camera's orientation and position in 3D space to enable ray generation for volume rendering [S2, unofficial]. It differs from [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md) by operating in world/view space rather than normalized device coordinates, and from [Point to Camera Transformation](1660-point-to-camera-transformation.md) by explicitly constructing the full extrinsic matrix from basis vectors rather than applying generic transforms [S3, S5, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   $\mathbf{e}$: Eye position (camera origin) in world space [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{t}$: Target point (center of interest) in world space [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{up}$: Arbitrary up vector in world space, typically $(0, 1, 0)$ or $(0, 0, 1)$ [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   A $4 \times 4$ matrix $M$.
    *   If constructing a **Camera-to-World (C2W)** matrix (used to transform view-space rays to world space): The first three columns are the right, up, and negative forward vectors, and the fourth column is the eye position [S3, S4, S9, unofficial].
    *   If constructing a **World-to-View (W2V)** matrix (used to transform world points to camera space): The first three rows are the right, up, and negative forward vectors, and the translation column is derived from $\mathbf{e}$ [S5, S9, unofficial].
    *   *Note:* Most volume rendering ray generation pipelines (e.g., [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md)) require the C2W matrix to transform ray directions from the camera's local view space (where the camera looks down the negative Z-axis) to world space [S3, S9, unofficial]. The following steps describe the construction of the basis vectors and the resulting C2W matrix, which is the most common form for ray marching [S3, S9, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   Vectors $\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{u}$ are 3D floating-point vectors.
*   Matrix $M$ is a $4 \times 4$ array of floats, stored in column-major order (standard for OpenGL/GLSL) or row-major (standard for C++/Python linear algebra libraries, requiring transpose). The sources consistently show the matrix constructed with basis vectors as columns for C2W [S3, S9, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1.  **Calculate Forward Vector ($\mathbf{f}$):** Compute the direction from the eye to the target and normalize it.
    $$ \mathbf{f} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{e}) $$
    In the camera's local view space, the camera looks down the negative Z-axis. Therefore, the world-space forward vector corresponds to the negative Z-axis of the camera [S3, S9, unofficial].

2.  **Calculate Right Vector ($\mathbf{s}$):** Compute the cross product of the forward vector and the provided up vector, then normalize. This yields the camera's local X-axis (right).
    $$ \mathbf{s} = \text{normalize}(\text{cross}(\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{up})) $$
    *Pitfall:* If $\mathbf{f}$ and $\mathbf{up}$ are parallel (or anti-parallel), the cross product is zero, resulting in a degenerate matrix [S3, S9, unofficial]. A guard clause should check for this and substitute a default up vector if necessary [G].

3.  **Calculate True Up Vector ($\mathbf{u}$):** Compute the cross product of the right vector and the forward vector. This ensures the up vector is orthogonal to both the right and forward vectors, forming an orthonormal basis.
    $$ \mathbf{u} = \text{cross}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{f}) $$
    Note that $\mathbf{u}$ is not normalized here if $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathbf{f}$ are unit vectors, as the cross product of two orthogonal unit vectors is a unit vector [S3, S9, unofficial].

4.  **Construct Matrix:** Assemble the $4 \times 4$ matrix.
    *   **Columns 0, 1, 2:** The right ($\mathbf{s}$), up ($\mathbf{u}$), and negative forward ($-\mathbf{f}$) vectors.
    *   **Column 3:** The eye position $\mathbf{e}$ (for C2W) or zero (for the rotational part of W2V, with translation handled separately).
    *   **Row 3:** $(0, 0, 0, 1)$.

    For a **Camera-to-World (C2W)** matrix $M_{c2w}$:
    $$
    M_{c2w} = \begin{bmatrix}
    s_x & u_x & -f_x & e_x \\
    s_y & u_y & -f_y & e_y \\
    s_z & u_z & -f_z & e_z \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    This matrix transforms a ray direction $\mathbf{d}_{view} = (d_x, d_y, d_z, 0)$ in view space to world space $\mathbf{d}_{world} = M_{c2w} \mathbf{d}_{view}$ [S3, S9, unofficial].

    For a **World-to-View (W2V)** matrix $M_{w2v}$ (the inverse of $M_{c2w}$):
    $$
    M_{w2v} = \begin{bmatrix}
    s_x & s_y & s_z & -\text{dot}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{e}) \\
    u_x & u_y & u_z & -\text{dot}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{e}) \\
    -f_x & -f_y & -f_z & \text{dot}(\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{e}) \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    This transforms world coordinates to camera coordinates [S5, S9, unofficial].

**Pseudocode (C2W Construction):**
```python
def construct_view_matrix_c2w(eye, target, up):
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 Camera-to-World matrix.
    eye: (3,) array-like, camera position
    target: (3,) array-like, look-at point
    up: (3,) array-like, arbitrary up vector
    Returns: (4, 4) array-like, column-major matrix
    """
    # Step 1: Forward vector (camera looks down -Z in view space)
    f = target - eye
    f = f / np.linalg.norm(f)
    
    # Step 2: Right vector
    s = np.cross(f, up)
    s = s / np.linalg.norm(s)
    
    # Step 3: True Up vector
    u = np.cross(s, f)
    
    # Step 4: Construct Matrix
    # Columns are s, u, -f, eye
    # Row 3 is [0, 0, 0, 1]
    mat = np.eye(4)
    mat[0, :3] = s
    mat[1, :3] = u
    mat[2, :3] = -f
    mat[0:3, 3] = eye
    
    return mat
```

**Pseudocode (W2V Construction):**
```python
def construct_view_matrix_w2v(eye, target, up):
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 World-to-View matrix.
    eye: (3,) array-like, camera position
    target: (3,) array-like, look-at point
    up: (3,) array-like, arbitrary up vector
    Returns: (4, 4) array-like, column-major matrix
    """
    # Step 1: Forward vector
    f = target - eye
    f = f / np.linalg.norm(f)
    
    # Step 2: Right vector
    s = np.cross(f, up)
    s = s / np.linalg.norm(s)
    
    # Step 3: True Up vector
    u = np.cross(s, f)
    
    # Step 4: Construct Matrix
    # Rows are s, u, -f
    # Translation is -dot(basis, eye)
    mat = np.eye(4)
    mat[0, :3] = s
    mat[1, :3] = u
    mat[2, :3] = -f
    mat[0, 3] = -np.dot(s, eye)
    mat[1, 3] = -np.dot(u, eye)
    mat[2, 3] = np.dot(f, eye) # Note: -(-f) dot e = f dot e
    
    return mat
```

## Subtopics

### [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)
Computes a representative camera pose (origin and orientation) from a set of input camera poses, typically used to initialize a scene or define a central viewing direction for volume rendering and NeRF training. It differs from [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md) by aggregating multiple poses into one, and from [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md) by not assuming a fixed orbital path. It addresses the problem of defining a canonical coordinate frame or initial camera position when no single pose is dominant.

### [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md)
Camera Pose Transformation applies translation and rotation to points or coordinate systems to position cameras or deform scenes. It maps local scene coordinates to world space or adjusts camera extrinsics relative to a reference frame. It operates directly on points or pose structures rather than constructing a full view matrix from basis vectors.

### [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)
Computes an orthonormal camera basis (eye position, forward, right, and up vectors) for an orbit camera that looks at a fixed target center from a specified distance, oriented by spherical yaw and pitch angles. It solves the problem of determining the viewing direction and position for navigation in 3D space based on user input angles. It sets itself apart from full 3-axis Euler rotation by excluding roll, and from quaternion-based approaches by using explicit trigonometric vector construction.

### [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md)
The General Perspective Transformation is a camera model where both the eye point and the projection plane are positioned away from the world origin. It solves the problem of numerical instability in screen-z values that occurs when standard perspective projections are applied to scenes where the visibility bounding box (Vbb) is far from the origin. By shifting the eye and projection plane, it ensures the Jacobian of the transformation is the identity at the center of the Vbb, minimizing floating-point errors during the compositing of closely spaced geometry. It differs from standard textbook perspective matrices by explicitly decoupling the eye position from the origin to optimize precision for specific scene bounds.

### [Custom Matrix Math Library](3216-custom-matrix-math-library.md)
A custom implementation of 3D transformation matrices in JavaScript, providing native handling of translation, scaling, perspective projection, and look-at operations without external dependencies. It serves as the computational core for view matrix construction and ray generation logic within the project.

### [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md)
Constructs a 3x3 rotation matrix that transforms world-space vectors into camera-local space, given a camera origin and look-at target. It builds an orthogonal camera coordinate system (basis) from a camera center and a look-at point by deriving an up vector orthogonal to the viewing direction, then computing the right vector via cross product. It differs from [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md) by accepting an arbitrary look-at target rather than spherical angles, and from [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md) by producing only the rotational component of the extrinsic transform, not the full projection matrix.

### [View Transformations](5333-view-transformations.md)
View Transformations define the pipeline for mapping volume data from its native object coordinate system to the final image plane. This method manages four distinct transformation matrices (modeling, viewing, projection, and viewport) that collectively handle the geometric positioning of the volume, the camera orientation, and the final clipping and mapping to screen coordinates. It is distinct from sibling methods like [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md) by managing the entire state stack of transformations rather than computing a single basis, and differs from [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) by handling the full pipeline including projection and viewport stages.

### Inverse View Matrix Loading
Copies the inverse view matrix (3 float4s, 12 floats) to device symbol for ray direction transformation [S13, unofficial]. It copies 12 floats (3x4 matrix) to `invViewMatrix` symbol [S13, unofficial].

### UVW-based Camera Translation
Defines a fixed up vector and camera position, normalizes the position to get the forward vector, computes the right vector via cross product, and computes the true up vector via cross product [S14, unofficial]. It builds translation and rotation matrices, inverts the rotation, extracts axes, and combines them to transform the camera position [S14, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ arithmetic operations (a few subtractions, cross products, normalizations, and dot products).
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ storage for the matrix and intermediate vectors.
*   **Strengths:** Simple, robust, and widely supported. It provides an intuitive way to specify camera orientation.
*   **Limitations:** Degenerate when the view direction is parallel to the up vector. Precision issues can arise if the eye and target are very far from the origin, though [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md) addresses this for projection [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When the camera position and look-at target are known.
*   **When not to use:** When the camera is orbiting a fixed center (use [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)) or when a canonical pose is needed from multiple views (use [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)).

## Background
The LookAt matrix is a standard concept in computer graphics, originally popularized by the OpenGL utility library function `gluLookAt` [S3, unofficial]. It is used to define the camera's extrinsic parameters. In volume rendering, it is essential for transforming rays from the camera's local coordinate system (where the camera looks down the negative Z-axis) to the world coordinate system, enabling ray marching through the volume [S3, S9, unofficial].
