# View Matrix Construction

Also called: LookAt Matrix, LookAt Matrix Construction, Camera Matrix Construction, gluLookAt

## Overview
View Matrix Construction generates a $4 \times 4$ transformation matrix that maps coordinates from world space to camera (view) space, or conversely transforms rays from view space to world space, given an eye position, a target point, and an up vector [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial]. It solves the problem of defining a camera's orientation and position in 3D space to enable ray generation for volume rendering [S3, S6, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md) by establishing the extrinsic camera pose before projection, and from [Point to Camera Transformation](1660-point-to-camera-transformation.md) by constructing the full basis matrix rather than transforming individual points [S1, S5, S9, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   $\mathbf{e}$: Eye position (camera origin) [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{t}$: Target point (center of interest) [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{up}_{raw}$: Arbitrary up vector (e.g., $(0, 1, 0)$) [S1, S3, S5, S9, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   A $4 \times 4$ matrix $\mathbf{M}$.
    *   If transforming **view-to-world** (ray direction transformation): The first three columns are the right, up, and forward basis vectors, and the fourth column is the eye position [S1, S3, S4, S9, unofficial].
    *   If transforming **world-to-view** (coordinate transformation): The first three rows are the right, up, and negative forward basis vectors, and the fourth row is the translation [S5, S9, unofficial].
    *   *Note:* Most volume rendering ray marchers require the view-to-world matrix to transform ray directions computed in view space (looking down $-z$) to world space [S3, S9, unofficial]. The implementation below constructs this view-to-world matrix, as it is the primary requirement for ray generation [S3, S9, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   Vectors $\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{u}$ are 3D floating-point vectors.
*   Matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is a $4 \times 4$ array of floats, stored in column-major order (standard for OpenGL/GLSL) or row-major depending on the API, but logically defined by its basis columns/rows.

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Forward Vector ($\mathbf{f}$):** Calculate the direction from the eye to the target and normalize it.
    $$ \mathbf{f} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{e}) $$
    In view space, the camera looks down the negative z-axis, so this vector represents the negative z-axis direction in world space [S3, S9, unofficial].

2.  **Compute Right Vector ($\mathbf{s}$):** Calculate the cross product of the forward vector and the raw up vector, then normalize. This yields the camera's local x-axis (right).
    $$ \mathbf{s} = \text{normalize}(\text{cross}(\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{up}_{raw})) $$
    *Pitfall:* If $\mathbf{f}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{up}_{raw}$, the cross product is zero, resulting in a degenerate matrix [S3, S9, unofficial].

3.  **Compute True Up Vector ($\mathbf{u}$):** Calculate the cross product of the right vector and the forward vector. This ensures the up vector is orthogonal to both the right and forward vectors, forming an orthonormal basis.
    $$ \mathbf{u} = \text{cross}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{f}) $$
    Note: Some implementations normalize this again, but if $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathbf{f}$ are orthonormal, $\mathbf{u}$ is already unit length [S3, S9, unofficial].

4.  **Construct Matrix:** Assemble the $4 \times 4$ matrix. For a view-to-world transformation (used to transform ray directions from view space to world space), the basis vectors form the first three columns, and the eye position forms the translation column. The w-components of the basis vectors are 0.0, and the bottom row is $(0, 0, 0, 1)$ [S3, S9, unofficial].

    $$
    \mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix}
    s_x & u_x & -f_x & 0 \\
    s_y & u_y & -f_y & 0 \\
    s_z & u_z & -f_z & 0 \\
    e_x & e_y & e_z & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    *Clarification:* The third column uses $-\mathbf{f}$ because the camera looks down the negative z-axis in view space. Transforming a vector $(0, 0, -1)$ (view space forward) by this matrix yields $\mathbf{f}$ (world space forward) [S3, S9, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def construct_view_matrix(eye: Vector3, target: Vector3, up_raw: Vector3) -> Matrix4x4:
    """
    Constructs a 4x4 view-to-world matrix (camera-to-world) from eye, target, and up vectors.
    This matrix transforms ray directions from view space (looking down -Z) to world space.
    """
    # Step 1: Forward vector (camera looks down negative Z in view space)
    # f points from eye to target
    f = normalize(target - eye)
    
    # Step 2: Right vector (X axis)
    # Cross product of f and up_raw gives the right direction
    s = normalize(cross(f, up_raw))
    
    # Step 3: True Up vector (Y axis)
    # Cross product of s and f ensures orthogonality
    u = cross(s, f)
    
    # Step 4: Construct the 4x4 matrix
    # Columns are: Right, Up, -Forward, Translation
    # Row 0: s.x, u.x, -f.x, 0
    # Row 1: s.y, u.y, -f.y, 0
    # Row 2: s.z, u.z, -f.z, 0
    # Row 3: eye.x, eye.y, eye.z, 1
    
    matrix = Matrix4x4(
        col0=Vector4(s.x, s.y, s.z, 0.0),
        col1=Vector4(u.x, u.y, u.z, 0.0),
        col2=Vector4(-f.x, -f.y, -f.z, 0.0),
        col3=Vector4(eye.x, eye.y, eye.z, 1.0)
    )
    
    return matrix
```

**Parameters:**
*   `eye`: Camera position. Typical values depend on scene scale (e.g., $(0, 1000, -130)$ in medical imaging RAS coordinates) [S1, unofficial].
*   `target`: Point the camera looks at. Often the center of the volume [S1, unofficial].
*   `up_raw`: Arbitrary up vector. Commonly $(0, 1, 0)$ or $(0, 0, 1)$ depending on coordinate system [S1, S5, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Degenerate Input:** If the eye position equals the target position, $\mathbf{f}$ is undefined (zero vector). The implementation must guard against this [S3, S9, unofficial].
*   **Parallel Vectors:** If $\mathbf{f}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{up}_{raw}$, $\mathbf{s}$ becomes zero. A common fix is to use a default up vector if the cross product magnitude is below a threshold [S3, S9, unofficial].
*   **Coordinate System Assumption:** The matrix assumes the camera looks down the negative z-axis in view space. When transforming a direction vector, the w-component must be 0.0; when transforming a point, it must be 1.0 [S9, unofficial].
*   **Row vs. Column Major:** The mathematical definition above uses column vectors (standard in OpenGL/GLSL). If using row-major storage (DirectX), the basis vectors become rows [S5, S9, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md)
Computes an average camera pose from a set of input poses by aggregating their translations and rotations. Implementations differ in how they handle rotation: some sum viewing and up vectors directly, while others use Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on a mean rotation matrix to enforce orthogonality.

### [Camera Pose Transformation](1672-camera-pose-transformation.md)
Applies geometric transformations—specifically translation and rotation—to a point or coordinate system to define the camera's position and orientation within a 3D scene. It solves the problem of mapping local scene coordinates to the camera's view space or adjusting the camera's extrinsic parameters relative to the world. It sets itself apart from [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) by operating on individual points or pose structures directly rather than constructing a full 4x4 matrix from basis vectors, and from [Average Pose Calculation](1645-average-pose-calculation.md) by performing a single rigid transformation rather than aggregating multiple poses.

### [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)
Computes an orthonormal camera basis (eye position, forward, right, and up vectors) for an orbit camera that looks at a fixed target center from a specified distance, oriented by spherical yaw and pitch angles. It solves the problem of determining the viewing direction and position for navigation in 3D space based on user input angles. It sets itself apart from full 3-axis Euler rotation by excluding roll, and from quaternion-based approaches by using explicit trigonometric vector construction.

### [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md)
The General Perspective Transformation is a camera model where both the eye point and the projection plane are positioned away from the world origin. It solves the problem of numerical instability in screen-z values that occurs when standard perspective projections are applied to scenes where the visibility bounding box (Vbb) is far from the origin. By shifting the eye and projection plane, it ensures the Jacobian of the transformation is the identity at the center of the Vbb, minimizing floating-point errors during the compositing of closely spaced geometry. It differs from standard textbook perspective matrices by explicitly decoupling the eye position from the origin to optimize precision for specific scene bounds.

### [Custom Matrix Math Library](3216-custom-matrix-math-library.md)
A custom implementation of 3D transformation matrices in JavaScript, providing native handling of translation, scaling, perspective projection, and look-at operations without external dependencies. It distinguishes itself by offering explicit, unoptimized implementations for small fixed-size matrices, prioritizing clarity and correctness over performance, serving as the computational core for view matrix construction and ray generation logic.

### [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md)
Constructs a 3x3 rotation matrix that transforms world-space vectors into camera-local space, given a camera origin and look-at target. It builds an orthogonal camera coordinate system (basis) from a camera center and a look-at point by deriving an up vector orthogonal to the viewing direction, then computing the right vector via cross product. It differs from [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md) by accepting an arbitrary look-at target rather than spherical angles, and from [General Perspective Transformation](1845-general-perspective-transformation.md) by producing only the rotational component of the extrinsic transform, not the full projection matrix.

### [View Transformations](5333-view-transformations.md)
View Transformations define the pipeline for mapping volume data from its native object coordinate system to the final image plane. This method manages four distinct transformation matrices (modeling, viewing, projection, and viewport) that collectively handle the geometric positioning of the volume, the camera orientation, and the final clipping and mapping to screen coordinates. It is distinct from sibling methods like [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md) by managing the entire state stack of transformations rather than computing a single basis, and differs from [View Matrix Construction](1644-view-matrix-construction.md) by handling the full pipeline including projection and viewport stages.

### Inverse View Matrix Loading
Copies the inverse view matrix (3 float4s, 12 floats) to device symbol for ray direction transformation [S12, unofficial]. It involves copying 12 floats (3x4 matrix) to `invViewMatrix` symbol [S12, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ constant time, involving a few vector normalizations and cross products.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$, storing a single $4 \times 4$ matrix.
*   **Strengths:** Simple, robust, and widely supported. Provides a clear geometric interpretation of camera pose.
*   **Limitations:** Sensitive to degenerate inputs (parallel vectors). Does not handle projection (FOV, aspect ratio) which is handled by separate projection matrices.
*   **When to use:** When a camera pose is defined by an eye position and a look-at target.
*   **When not to use:** When using spherical coordinates (use [Orbit Camera Basis Computation](1749-orbit-camera-basis-computation.md)) or when only rotation is needed (use [Camera Look-at Basis Construction](3990-camera-look-at-basis-construction.md)).

## Background
The LookAt matrix is a standard technique in computer graphics, originally popularized by OpenGL's `gluLookAt` function [S3, unofficial]. It is used to define the camera's extrinsic parameters in ray marching and volume rendering pipelines [S3, S9, unofficial]. It is commonly used in differentiable volume rendering frameworks like flashdrr to generate camera views for synthetic DRR (Digitally Reconstructed Radiograph) synthesis [S1, unofficial].
