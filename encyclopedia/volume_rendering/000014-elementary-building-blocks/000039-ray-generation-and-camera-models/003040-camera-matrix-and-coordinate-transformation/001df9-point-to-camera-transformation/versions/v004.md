# Point to Camera Transformation

Also called: ptstocam

## Overview
The Point to Camera Transformation maps 3D points from world coordinates into the camera's local coordinate system. It serves as the foundational step for ray generation and visibility checks by establishing the relative position of scene geometry with respect to the viewer. Unlike [NDC Ray Transformation](905-ndc-ray-transformation.md), which focuses on projecting ray directions into normalized device space for sampling, this method operates on absolute point positions to determine their location within the camera's view frustum. It builds on [Camera Matrix and Coordinate Transformation](6231-camera-matrix-and-coordinate-transformation.md) by utilizing the camera pose to define the target frame.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   $\mathbf{P}_{world}$: A 3D point in world coordinates.
*   $M_{c2w}$: The $4 \times 4$ Camera-to-World transformation matrix. This matrix transforms vectors from camera space to world space. Its upper-left $3 \times 3$ submatrix $R$ represents rotation, and its upper-right $3 \times 1$ submatrix $T$ represents translation (the camera origin in world space).

**Outputs:**
*   $\mathbf{P}_{cam}$: The 3D point in camera coordinates.

**Data Structures:**
*   $M_{c2w}$: A $4 \times 4$ matrix stored in row-major or column-major order.
    *   $R = \begin{bmatrix} r_{00} & r_{01} & r_{02} \\ r_{10} & r_{11} & r_{12} \\ r_{20} & r_{21} & r_{22} \end{bmatrix}$
    *   $T = \begin{bmatrix} t_x \\ t_y \\ t_z \end{bmatrix}$ (Camera origin $\mathbf{o}_{cam}$ in world space).

**Steps:**
1.  **Translate**: Compute the vector from the camera origin to the world point.
    $$ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{P}_{world} - \mathbf{o}_{cam} $$
    Where $\mathbf{o}_{cam} = T$.
2.  **Rotate**: Project the translated vector onto the camera's local basis vectors. Since $R$ transforms camera basis vectors to world basis vectors, its transpose $R^T$ transforms world vectors to camera basis vectors.
    $$ \mathbf{P}_{cam} = R^T \mathbf{v} $$
    Explicitly:
    $$
    \begin{bmatrix} x_{cam} \\ y_{cam} \\ z_{cam} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r_{00} & r_{10} & r_{20} \\ r_{01} & r_{11} & r_{21} \\ r_{02} & r_{12} & r_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \\ v_z \end{bmatrix}
    $$

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def point_to_camera(P_world: vec3, M_c2w: mat4) -> vec3:
    """
    Transforms a 3D point from world space to camera space.
    
    Args:
        P_world: World coordinates of the point.
        M_c2w: 4x4 Camera-to-World matrix.
        
    Returns:
        Camera coordinates of the point.
    """
    # Extract camera origin (translation part of c2w matrix)
    # Assuming standard column-major storage where M_c2w[3][0:3] is the origin
    cam_origin = vec3(M_c2w[3][0], M_c2w[3][1], M_c2w[3][2])
    
    # Extract rotation matrix (upper-left 3x3)
    # R_c2w transforms camera basis to world basis
    R_c2w = mat3(
        M_c2w[0][0], M_c2w[1][0], M_c2w[2][0],
        M_c2w[0][1], M_c2w[1][1], M_c2w[2][1],
        M_c2w[0][2], M_c2w[1][2], M_c2w[2][2]
    )
    
    # Step 1: Translate point relative to camera origin
    # v = P_world - cam_origin
    v = P_world - cam_origin
    
    # Step 2: Rotate into camera space
    # P_cam = R_c2w^T * v
    # Since R is orthogonal, R^T is the inverse rotation
    # R_c2w^T rows are the columns of R_c2w
    cam_x = dot(vec3(R_c2w[0][0], R_c2w[0][1], R_c2w[0][2]), v)
    cam_y = dot(vec3(R_c2w[1][0], R_c2w[1][1], R_c2w[1][2]), v)
    cam_z = dot(vec3(R_c2w[2][0], R_c2w[2][1], R_c2w[2][2]), v)
    
    return vec3(cam_x, cam_y, cam_z)
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Matrix Convention**: The implementation must match the storage order (row-major vs. column-major) of $M_{c2w}$. If $M_{c2w}$ is row-major, the rotation matrix $R$ is directly accessible as the first three rows, and no transpose is needed for the dot products if iterating correctly, but the logic $R^T$ remains conceptually the inverse rotation.
*   **Homogeneous Coordinates**: If $\mathbf{P}_{world}$ is provided in homogeneous coordinates $(x, y, z, w)$, it must be converted to Cartesian $(x/w, y/w, z/w)$ before transformation if $w \neq 1$. Directions (vectors) should use $w=0$ and not be translated, but this function specifically handles points.
*   **Precision**: For large scenes, subtracting large world coordinates from the camera origin may cause floating-point precision loss. Using double precision or centering the world space around the camera origin can mitigate this [G].

### World to Clip Space Conversion
Converts a world position to camera clip space coordinates (2D), used for sampling textures or determining pixel locations in camera space [S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1.  **Basis Construction**: Calculate camera right and up vectors from the forward vector and world up $(0,1,0)$.
    $$ \mathbf{camRight} = \text{normalize}(\text{cross}(\mathbf{camForward}, \text{vec3}(0.0, 1.0, 0.0))) $$
    $$ \mathbf{camUp} = \text{normalize}(\text{cross}(\mathbf{camRight}, \mathbf{camForward})) $$
2.  **View Space Position**: Compute the position relative to the camera.
    $$ \mathbf{camToWorldPos} = \mathbf{worldPosition} - \mathbf{camPos} $$
    $$ \text{viewX} = \text{dot}(\mathbf{camRight}, \mathbf{camToWorldPos}) $$
    $$ \text{viewY} = \text{dot}(\mathbf{camUp}, \mathbf{camToWorldPos}) $$
    $$ \text{viewZ} = \text{dot}(\mathbf{camForward}, \mathbf{camToWorldPos}) $$
3.  **Perspective Projection**:
    $$ \text{scale} = \tan\left(\frac{\text{radians}(\text{cameraData.fov})}{2.0}\right) $$
    $$ \text{clipX} = \frac{\text{viewX}}{\text{viewZ} \times \text{scale} \times \text{cameraData.aspectRatio}} $$
    $$ \text{clipY} = \frac{\text{viewY}}{\text{viewZ} \times \text{scale}} $$
4.  **Offset and Normalize**:
    $$ \text{clipX} \times= \left(1 - \frac{1.0}{\text{sceneData.screenWidth}}\right) $$
    $$ \text{clipY} \times= \left(1 - \frac{1.0}{\text{sceneData.screenHeight}}\right) $$
    $$ \text{clipX} = \frac{\text{clipX}}{2.0} + 0.5 $$
    $$ \text{clipY} = \frac{\text{clipY}}{2.0} + 0.5 $$
5.  **Pixel Coordinates**:
    $$ \text{pixelX} = \text{clipX} \times (\text{sceneData.screenWidth} - 1) $$
    $$ \text{pixelY} = \text{clipY} \times (\text{sceneData.screenHeight} - 1) $$

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: $O(1)$ per point. It involves a vector subtraction and a matrix-vector multiplication (3 dot products).
*   **Memory Complexity**: $O(1)$ auxiliary space.
*   **Strengths**: Computationally inexpensive; essential for early-z culling, visibility checks, and texture lookups in screen-space techniques.
*   **Limitations**: Does not account for perspective division or clipping planes directly (unless extended to Clip Space Conversion). Requires the camera matrix to be correctly constructed.
*   **When to use**: When determining if a 3D point is within the camera's view frustum or when mapping world positions to screen-space coordinates for post-processing.

## Background
The transformation of points between world and camera spaces is a fundamental concept in computer graphics, originating from the standard graphics pipeline defined in early OpenGL and DirectX specifications. In the context of neural radiance fields (NeRF) and modern ray marching, this transformation is critical for aligning sampled points with the camera's viewing direction. The specific implementation using the transpose of the rotation part of the C2W matrix is standard in differentiable rendering frameworks [S1, unofficial]. The variant involving explicit basis construction and clip space conversion is often found in custom GPU raymarching shaders [S2, unofficial].
