# Stereo Projection
Also called: Stereo Perspective, Dual Perspective Projection
## Overview
Stereo projection is a technique that applies perspective projection twice with specific adjustments to simulate the distinct viewpoints of an observer's left and right eyes, thereby creating a stereo effect [S1, unofficial]. It differs from standard perspective projection by generating two correlated view frustums rather than one, and differs from symmetric stereo methods by allowing for arbitrary frustum configurations (such as off-axis) to align viewing directions [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial].
## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D scene geometry, a base camera position, and the interpupillary distance (IPD) of the observer.
*   **Output:** Two 2D image buffers (left eye and right eye) or a single interleaved stereo buffer.

**Data Structures:**
The method relies on two view frustums. Each frustum is defined by:
1.  **Eye Position:** The 3D origin of the eye ($E_L$ for left, $E_R$ for right).
2.  **View Direction:** The vector pointing from the eye to the focal point.
3.  **Projection Parameters:** Field of view (FOV), aspect ratio, and near/far clipping planes.

**Steps:**
1.  **Calculate Eye Positions:** Determine the left and right eye positions based on the central camera position and the IPD. The eyes are typically offset horizontally from the center.
2.  **Define Frustums:** Construct two perspective projection matrices (or view frustums).
    *   For symmetric stereo, the frustums are symmetric around their respective optical axes.
    *   For off-axis stereo, the frustums are non-symmetric to ensure the projection planes coincide [S2, unofficial].
3.  **Transform Geometry:** Transform all 3D scene vertices into the clip space of the left eye frustum.
4.  **Render Left Image:** Rasterize the transformed geometry to produce the left eye image.
5.  **Transform Geometry:** Transform all 3D scene vertices into the clip space of the right eye frustum.
6.  **Render Right Image:** Rasterize the transformed geometry to produce the right eye image.

**Formulas:**
Let $C$ be the center of the screen or focal point. Let $IPD$ be the interpupillary distance.
The left eye position $E_L$ and right eye position $E_R$ are derived from the camera center.
The projection matrix $P$ for each eye is calculated using the standard perspective projection formula, adjusted for the specific eye's position and orientation.

**Parameters:**
*   **IPD:** Typical human value is ~63-65 mm [G].
*   **Convergence Distance:** The distance at which the left and right view directions intersect.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Vertical Parallax:** If the projection planes of the left and right eyes do not coincide, vertical parallax occurs, causing visual discomfort. This is mitigated by using off-axis projection or careful alignment [S2, unofficial].
*   **Clipping:** Geometry may be clipped differently by the two frustums, potentially causing popping artifacts if not handled carefully.
*   **Precision:** Floating-point precision errors in eye position calculation can lead to misalignment.

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def render_stereo(scene, camera_center, ipd, fov, aspect, near, far):
    # Calculate eye positions
    # Assuming camera looks along -Z, IPD along X
    eye_offset = ipd / 2.0
    left_eye_pos = camera_center + vec3(-eye_offset, 0, 0)
    right_eye_pos = camera_center + vec3(eye_offset, 0, 0)
    
    # Define view directions (assuming looking at camera_center from behind, or similar)
    # In many systems, the camera center is the origin of the view frustum
    # Here we assume the camera_center is the focal point for convergence
    left_view_dir = normalize(camera_center - left_eye_pos)
    right_view_dir = normalize(camera_center - right_eye_pos)
    
    # Create projection matrices
    left_proj = perspective_matrix(left_eye_pos, left_view_dir, fov, aspect, near, far)
    right_proj = perspective_matrix(right_eye_pos, right_view_dir, fov, aspect, near, far)
    
    # Render Left Eye
    left_image = render_scene(scene, left_proj)
    
    # Render Right Eye
    right_image = render_scene(scene, right_proj)
    
    return left_image, right_image
```
*Note: The exact implementation of `perspective_matrix` and `render_scene` depends on the underlying graphics API. The key is that two distinct projection matrices are used.* [S1, unofficial]

## Subtopics

### [Off-axis Stereoscopic Rendering](6787-off-axis-stereoscopic-rendering.md)
Uses non-symmetric camera frustums to generate coincident projection planes for the left and right eyes, preventing vertical parallax. It aligns the central viewing directions of both cameras in parallel while focusing on different points, ensuring a natural and comfortable stereo effect. Choose this when vertical parallax must be avoided, such as in CAVE systems or when the screen is not centered between the eyes.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Double the rendering time of a single perspective view, as two full passes are required.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for two image buffers (or one interleaved buffer).
*   **Performance:** Dependent on the complexity of the scene and the efficiency of the rasterization pipeline.
*   **Strengths:** Provides true depth perception for stereoscopic displays.
*   **Limitations:** Requires specialized hardware (shutter glasses, stereo viewers) or post-processing. Vertical parallax can cause discomfort if not managed.
*   **When to Use:** When stereoscopic depth is required for visualization or interaction.
*   **When Not to Use:** When hardware support is unavailable or when performance is critical and depth is not necessary.
## Background
Stereo projection is a fundamental technique in computer graphics for creating stereoscopic 3D images. It is based on the principle of binocular vision, where each eye sees a slightly different perspective of the world. The technique is widely used in virtual reality, 3D movies, and scientific visualization. The specific implementation described here is referenced in the context of the Smokeview tool for visualizing fire dynamics simulation data [S1, unofficial].
