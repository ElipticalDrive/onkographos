# Camera Intrinsics Simplification — FOV from Intrinsics, Field of View Calculation, Simplified Camera Intrinsics, Square Pixel Camera Model, get_fov): Computes the horizontal and vertical field of view (FOV) angles from intrinsic camera matrices.

Also called: Aperture-Zoom Formula, FOV Conversion, Vertical FOV to Horizontal FOV Conversion, Zoom and Aperture Relation

## Overview
This method computes the horizontal and vertical field of view (FOV) angles from intrinsic camera parameters, specifically converting between vertical and horizontal FOV based on image dimensions. It relies on the simplified square pixel camera model, assuming principal points are centered and pixel aspect ratios are unity. It sets itself apart from general intrinsic extraction by providing a direct geometric conversion without requiring full matrix decomposition, serving as a foundational step for ray generation in volume rendering.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- `vfov_deg`: Vertical field of view in degrees.
- `width`: Image width in pixels.
- `height`: Image height in pixels.

**Outputs:**
- `hfov_deg`: Horizontal field of view in degrees.

**Data Structures:**
No complex data structures are required; the operation is a scalar calculation.

**Steps:**
1. Convert the vertical field of view from degrees to radians.
2. Calculate the tangent of half the vertical field of view.
3. Multiply by the aspect ratio ($\text{width} / \text{height}$).
4. Take the arctangent of the result.
5. Multiply by 2 to get the horizontal field of view in radians.
6. Convert the result back to degrees.

**Formula:**
The horizontal field of view is calculated as:
$$ \text{hfov} = 2 \cdot \arctan\left(\frac{\text{width}}{\text{height}} \cdot \tan\left(\frac{\text{vfov}}{2}\right)\right) $$
where $\text{vfov}$ and $\text{hfov}$ are in radians during the calculation [S2, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import numpy as np

def get_fov(vfov_deg: float, width: int, height: int) -> float:
    """
    Computes the horizontal field of view (FOV) from the vertical FOV and image dimensions.
    
    Args:
        vfov_deg: Vertical field of view in degrees.
        width: Image width in pixels.
        height: Image height in pixels.
        
    Returns:
        Horizontal field of view in degrees.
    """
    # Convert vertical FOV from degrees to radians
    vfov_rad = np.deg2rad(vfov_deg)
    
    # Calculate horizontal FOV using the aspect ratio
    # Formula: hfov = 2 * arctan( (width/height) * tan(vfov/2) )
    hfov_rad = 2 * np.arctan(width * np.tan(vfov_rad / 2) / height)
    
    # Convert horizontal FOV back to degrees
    hfov_deg = np.rad2deg(hfov_rad)
    
    return hfov_deg
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Aspect Ratio:** The formula assumes a square pixel model. If pixels are not square, the effective aspect ratio must be adjusted [S2, unofficial].
- **Zero Dimensions:** Division by zero occurs if `height` is 0.
- **Precision:** Standard floating-point precision is sufficient for typical image resolutions.

## Subtopics

### Aperture-Zoom Formula
Relates the zoom factor to the aperture angle of view. The zoom is defined as the magnification of the scene, equivalent to changing the angle of view. The relationship is given by:
$$ \text{zoom} = \frac{\tan(45^\circ / 2)}{\tan(\text{aperture} / 2)} $$
A default aperture of $45^\circ$ is chosen for normal perspective [S1].

### Vertical FOV to Horizontal FOV Conversion
This is the core functionality of `get_fov`. It allows switching between vertical and horizontal FOV representations, which is necessary because different rendering APIs and camera models prefer different FOV definitions (e.g., OpenGL often uses vertical, while some computer vision libraries use horizontal) [S2, unofficial].

### Zoom and Aperture Relation
Zooming does not move the camera closer to the focus; it only reduces the width of the field of view. This is a critical distinction from translation. Methods like `set_width`, `zoom`, and `iter_zoom` change the FOV width without altering the camera position [S3].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ constant time, involving basic trigonometric operations.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ constant space.
- **Strengths:** Simple, fast, and requires minimal input data (just one FOV angle and image dimensions).
- **Limitations:** Assumes square pixels and centered principal point. Does not account for lens distortion or non-square pixels.
- **When to use:** When initializing a camera for ray generation where only the FOV and image size are known, and a simplified pinhole model is acceptable.
- **When not to use:** When high-precision camera calibration with non-square pixels or lens distortion is required.

## Background
The concept of relating zoom and aperture dates back to early visualization tools like Smokeview, where the Motion/View dialog box allowed users to change the magnification of the scene or equivalently the angle of view [S1]. The specific formula for converting vertical to horizontal FOV is widely used in computer vision and graphics libraries, such as MonoGS, to initialize camera parameters from GUI inputs [S2, unofficial]. The distinction between zooming (changing FOV) and moving (changing position) is emphasized in volume rendering visualization tools like yt to prevent user confusion about camera behavior [S3].
