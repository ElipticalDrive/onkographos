# Spiral Camera Path Generation

Also called: Novel View Synthesis Path, load_tankstemple_data spiral generation

## Overview
Spiral Camera Path Generation creates a smooth, helical sequence of camera poses around a scene centroid for novel view synthesis and fly-through rendering [S1, S2, S3, S5, unofficial]. It solves the problem of generating consistent, continuous viewpoints that cover 360-degree horizontal rotation while introducing vertical oscillation, avoiding the static nature of fixed-height orbits [S1, S2, S3, S5, unofficial]. It differs from [Circular Camera Path Generation](2734-circular-camera-path-generation.md) by adding a vertical sine-wave component to the trajectory, resulting in a helical rather than planar orbit [S1, S2, S3, S5, unofficial].

## How it works
The method generates a sequence of 4x4 camera-to-world transformation matrices ($c2w$) defining a spiral trajectory. The trajectory is centered around the average position and orientation of the input training poses.

**Inputs:**
- `poses`: Array of training camera poses, shape $(N_{train}, 4, 4)$, where each $c2w$ matrix maps camera coordinates to world coordinates.
- `N`: Number of output views (frames) to generate.
- `n_rots`: Number of full horizontal rotations (multiples of $2\pi$) to complete over the sequence.
- `z_rate`: Vertical oscillation rate (frequency of the sine wave relative to horizontal rotation).
- `rads_scale`: Scaling factor for the spiral radius relative to the scene size.
- `focal`: Focal length used to determine the viewing direction.
- `up`: Up vector for camera orientation (optional; if not provided, computed from training poses).
- `shift`: Optional 3D vector $(shift_x, shift_y, shift_z)$ to offset the centroid.
- `pitch_deg`: Optional pitch angle in degrees to tilt the camera up/down.
- `flip_up_vec`: Boolean to flip the up vector direction.

**Outputs:**
- `render_poses`: Array of shape $(N, 4, 4)$ containing the generated camera poses.

**Data Structures:**
- `c2w`: 4x4 matrix. Columns 0-2 are the right, up, and forward (negative) basis vectors. Column 3 is the translation (camera center). Row 3 is $[0, 0, 0, 1]$.
- `rads`: 3-element array $[r_x, r_y, r_z]$ defining the amplitude of the spiral along each axis.

**Steps:**

1. **Compute Scene Statistics:**
   - Calculate the centroid $C$ as the mean of all training camera positions:
     $$ C = \frac{1}{N_{train}} \sum_{i=0}^{N_{train}-1} poses[i, :3, 3] $$
   - Calculate the average up vector $U_{avg}$ from training poses:
     $$ U_{avg} = \frac{1}{N_{train}} \sum_{i=0}^{N_{train}-1} poses[i, :3, 1] $$
   - Calculate the focal length $f$. If not provided, compute as the mean norm of camera positions relative to the centroid, or use the mean norm of all camera positions [S2, S3, unofficial]:
     $$ f = \text{mean}(\|poses[:, :3, 3]\|) $$
   - Calculate the radius scale factor. Compute the 90th percentile of the absolute distances of camera positions from the centroid along each axis, then scale by `rads_scale` [S2, unofficial]:
     $$ rads_{base} = \text{percentile}_{90}(|poses[:, :3, 3] - C|, \text{axis}=0) $$
     $$ rads = rads_{base} \times \text{rads\_scale} $$
     If `rads` does not have 3 elements, append 1.0 to ensure dimensionality [S1, unofficial].

2. **Apply Optional Shifts:**
   - Update centroid $C$:
     $$ C \leftarrow C + [shift_x, shift_y, shift_z] $$

3. **Generate Poses:**
   - Initialize empty list `render_poses`.
   - For each frame index $i$ from $0$ to $N-1$:
     - Calculate normalized time $t = i / N$.
     - Calculate angle $\theta = 2\pi \cdot \text{n\_rots} \cdot t$.
     - Calculate vertical offset $z_{offset}$ using a sine wave:
       $$ z_{offset} = \text{height\_range} \cdot \sin(2\pi \cdot \text{n\_rots} \cdot \text{z\_rate} \cdot t) $$
       *Note: If `height_range` is not explicitly provided, it is often derived from `rads[2]` or set to a small fraction of the scene size. In many implementations, the vertical component of the offset vector is simply $-rads[2] \cdot \sin(\theta \cdot \text{z\_rate})$ [S2, S3, unofficial].*
     - Construct the offset vector $v_{offset}$ in the local frame of the center camera:
       $$ v_{offset} = [\cos(\theta) \cdot rads[0], -\sin(\theta) \cdot rads[1], -\sin(\theta \cdot \text{z\_rate}) \cdot rads[2]] $$
     - Compute the camera center $c$ in world space. Use the center camera pose $c2w_{center}$ (usually the average pose or the first training pose) to transform the offset:
       $$ c = c2w_{center, :3, 3} + c2w_{center, :3, :3} \cdot v_{offset} $$
       *Note: Some implementations use the centroid $C$ directly as the base for the offset if a specific center pose is not defined, effectively orbiting around $C$ [S5, unofficial].*
     - Compute the viewing direction $z_{axis}$. The target point is typically the centroid $C$ or a point along the optical axis of the center camera. A common definition is:
       $$ z_{axis} = \text{normalize}(C - c) $$
       *Alternative: If using a focal point defined by the center camera's intrinsic parameters:*
       $$ \text{target} = c2w_{center, :3, 3} + c2w_{center, :3, 2} \cdot (-f) $$
       $$ z_{axis} = \text{normalize}(\text{target} - c) $$
     - Determine the up vector $U$. If `up` is provided, use it. Otherwise, use $U_{avg}$. If `flip_up_vec` is true, negate $U$.
     - Handle pitch: If `pitch_deg` is non-zero, adjust the up vector or the target point. A common approach is to compute a target offset $y_{target}$:
       $$ \text{new\_up\_rad} = \text{pitch\_deg} \cdot \frac{\pi}{180} $$
       $$ y_{target} = rads_{circle} \cdot \tan(\text{new\_up\_rad}) $$
       Adjust the target point's y-coordinate by $y_{target}$ before computing $z_{axis}$ [S5, unofficial].
     - Construct the camera basis vectors:
       $$ z = \text{normalize}(z_{axis}) $$
       $$ x = \text{normalize}(\text{cross}(U, z)) $$
       $$ y = \text{cross}(z, x) $$
       *Note: Ensure $x$ and $y$ are orthogonal to $z$. If $U$ is parallel to $z$, use a default up vector.*
     - Construct the $c2w$ matrix:
       $$ c2w = \begin{bmatrix} x & y & z & c \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
     - Append $c2w$ to `render_poses`.

4. **Return:**
   - `render_poses`.

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def generate_spiral_path(poses, N, n_rots=2, z_rate=0.5, rads_scale=1.0, focal=None, up=None, shift=[0,0,0], pitch_deg=0, flip_up_vec=False):
    """
    Generate a spiral camera path.
    
    Args:
        poses: (N_train, 4, 4) array of training camera poses.
        N: Number of frames to generate.
        n_rots: Number of horizontal rotations.
        z_rate: Vertical oscillation rate.
        rads_scale: Scale factor for radius.
        focal: Focal length. If None, computed from poses.
        up: Up vector. If None, computed from poses.
        shift: (x, y, z) shift for centroid.
        pitch_deg: Pitch angle in degrees.
        flip_up_vec: Whether to flip the up vector.
        
    Returns:
        render_poses: (N, 4, 4) array of camera poses.
    """
    # 1. Compute Scene Statistics
    # Centroid
    centroid = np.mean(poses[:, :3, 3], axis=0)
    
    # Average up vector
    if up is None:
        avg_up = np.mean(poses[:, :3, 1], axis=0)
        up = avg_up / np.linalg.norm(avg_up)
    else:
        up = up / np.linalg.norm(up)
        
    # Focal length
    if focal is None:
        # Mean norm of camera positions
        focal = np.mean(np.linalg.norm(poses[:, :3, 3], axis=1))
        
    # Radius calculation
    # Distance of each camera from centroid
    dists = np.linalg.norm(poses[:, :3, 3] - centroid, axis=1)
    # 90th percentile of distances along each axis? 
    # Source [S2, unofficial] says "90th percentile of absolute camera positions ... along each axis"
    # This likely means per-axis percentile of the coordinates relative to centroid
    rel_pos = poses[:, :3, 3] - centroid
    rads_base = np.percentile(np.abs(rel_pos), 90, axis=0)
    rads = rads_base * rads_scale
    
    # Ensure rads has 3 elements
    if len(rads) < 3:
        rads = np.append(rads, 1.0)
        
    # Apply shift
    centroid = centroid + np.array(shift)
    
    # Center pose for transformation (use average pose or first pose)
    # Using average pose for c2w_center
    c2w_center = np.mean(poses, axis=0)
    
    render_poses = []
    
    for i in range(N):
        t = i / N
        theta = 2 * np.pi * n_rots * t
        
        # Vertical offset
        # Using z_rate for vertical oscillation
        z_offset_val = -rads[2] * np.sin(theta * z_rate)
        
        # Offset vector in local frame
        # Note: The sign of sin(theta) for x and y depends on coordinate system convention.
        # Source [S2, unofficial] uses: [cos(theta)*rads[0], -sin(theta)*rads[1], -sin(theta*z_rate)*rads[2]]
        offset_local = np.array([
            np.cos(theta) * rads[0],
            -np.sin(theta) * rads[1],
            z_offset_val
        ])
        
        # Camera center
        c = c2w_center[:3, 3] + c2w_center[:3, :3] @ offset_local
        
        # Viewing direction
        # Target is centroid
        target = centroid.copy()
        
        # Handle pitch
        if pitch_deg != 0:
            new_up_rad = pitch_deg * np.pi / 180
            # Estimate radius for pitch calculation
            # Source [S5, unofficial] uses radcircle * tan(pitch)
            # radcircle can be approximated by mean radius
            radcircle = np.mean(rads)
            target_y_offset = radcircle * np.tan(new_up_rad)
            target[1] += target_y_offset
            
        z_axis = target - c
        z = z_axis / np.linalg.norm(z_axis)
        
        # Up vector handling
        if flip_up_vec:
            up = -up
            
        # Compute x and y
        x = np.cross(up, z)
        x_norm = np.linalg.norm(x)
        if x_norm < 1e-6:
            # Fallback if up is parallel to z
            x = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
            x = np.cross(up, z)
            x_norm = np.linalg.norm(x)
        x = x / x_norm
        
        y = np.cross(z, x)
        
        # Construct pose
        pose = np.eye(4)
        pose[:3, 0] = x
        pose[:3, 1] = y
        pose[:3, 2] = z
        pose[:3, 3] = c
        
        render_poses.append(pose)
        
    return np.array(render_poses)
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Parallel Up and Z:** If the camera looks directly up or down, the cross product for the right vector ($x$) becomes zero. A fallback up vector (e.g., $[1, 0, 0]$) must be used [G].
- **Precision:** Use float32 or float64 consistently. Small errors in normalization can accumulate in long spirals.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The spiral should not include the endpoint of the full rotation range to avoid duplicate poses if $N$ is small and $n\_rots$ is integer [S1, S3, unofficial].
- **Focal Length:** Incorrect focal length leads to incorrect viewing directions, causing the camera to look away from the scene.

## Subtopics

### [Circular Camera Path Generation](2734-circular-camera-path-generation.md)
Circular Camera Path Generation creates a sequence of camera poses arranged in a 360-degree circular trajectory around a central origin at a fixed height and radius. It solves the problem of generating consistent, orbiting viewpoints for novel view synthesis or 360-degree visualization. It is a degenerate case of Spiral Camera Path Generation where the vertical offset rate is zero, resulting in a planar orbit rather than a helical one.

### Spiral Camera Pose Generation
Spiral Camera Pose Generation (as implemented in [S6, unofficial]) generates camera poses in a circle with optional up-vector handling and perspective camera setup using PyTorch3D. It differs by explicitly constructing a `PerspectiveCameras` object and handling up-vector flips based on the y-component of the up vector. It is chosen when using PyTorch3D for differentiable rendering. The eye position is computed as $[\cos(\theta + \pi/2) \cdot \text{radius}, 0, -\sin(\theta + \pi/2) \cdot \text{radius}]$ if $|up_y| > 0$, else $[\cos(\theta + \pi/2) \cdot \text{radius}, \sin(\theta + \pi/2) \cdot \text{radius}, 2.0]$. The camera is created using `look_at_view_transform` and `PerspectiveCameras` with specified focal length.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(N)$, where $N$ is the number of frames. Each pose is computed in constant time.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store the output poses.
- **Performance:** Generating 200 poses takes negligible time (<1ms) on modern CPUs [S5, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Smooth, continuous motion; covers 360 degrees; introduces vertical variation for better scene coverage.
- **Limitations:** Assumes a roughly spherical or isotropic scene distribution. May not fit elongated or planar scenes well without adjustment [S2, S3, unofficial].
- **When to Use:** Novel view synthesis for forward-facing scenes, fly-through videos, 360-degree visualization.
- **When Not to Use:** Scenes with strong directional bias (e.g., landscapes) where a linear or elliptical path is more appropriate.

## Background
Spiral camera paths are commonly used in NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) novel view synthesis pipelines [S1, S2, S3, unofficial]. The method was popularized by the original NeRF paper and subsequent implementations in repositories like `bmild/nerf` and `emmanueladutwum123/nerf-from-scratch` [S1, S2, unofficial]. It is a standard technique for evaluating and visualizing NeRF models, providing a smooth fly-through experience [S5, unofficial].
