# Magnifier

Also called: Magnification lens, Magnifier volume renderings, Virtual Lens Optics, Volumetric lenses

## Overview
The Magnifier is a focus+context lens technique for volume rendering that magnifies regions of interest by modifying ray directions to pass through a virtual focal point, while using a transition region to compress context and prevent loss of spatial information [S2]. Unlike the parent [Depth of Field and Lens Effects](6232-depth-of-field-and-lens-effects.md), which typically displaces ray origins on an aperture to simulate optical blur, the Magnifier keeps ray origins fixed on the image plane but rotates their directions to create a geometric magnification cone [S2]. It differs from the sibling [Thin Lens Depth of Field](4922-thin-lens-depth-of-field.md) by providing sharp, distortion-based magnification rather than defocus blur, and from the sibling [Lens Shift](1851-lens-shift.md) by actively altering the sampling density to enlarge specific features [S1, book].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
- **Input:** Volume dataset, camera parameters (image plane position, orientation), lens parameters (center $LC$, radius $l_r$, transition width $l_b$, virtual focal point $F$).
- **Output:** Rendered image with magnified focal region and compressed transition region.

**Data Structures**
- **Lens Parameters:**
  - $LC$: 3D vector, center of the lens on the image plane.
  - $l_r$: Scalar, radius of the lens.
  - $l_b$: Scalar, width of the transition region.
  - $F$: 3D vector, virtual focal point (parallel to image plane).
- **Ray Parameters:**
  - $P_I$: 3D vector, ray origin on the image plane (pixel coordinate).
  - $P_R$: 3D vector, point in the transition region used for magnification factor calculation.
  - $P_{RI}$: 3D vector, orthogonal projection of $P_R$ on the image plane.

**Steps**
1. **Initialize Lens:** Position the lens on the image plane with center $LC$ and radius $l_r$. Define the virtual focal point $F$ parallel to the image plane [S2].
2. **Classify Ray Origin:** For each pixel, determine the distance $d = |P_I - LC|$.
   - If $d > l_r$: The ray is in the normal region. Keep the original orthogonal direction.
   - If $d \le l_r - l_b$: The ray is in the magnification region. Modify the direction to pass through $F$.
   - If $l_r - l_b < d \le l_r$: The ray is in the transition region. Interpolate the direction between the focused direction and the orthogonal direction [S2].
3. **Compute Ray Direction:**
   - **Magnification Region:** The ray direction is calculated as the vector from the origin $P_I$ to the focal point $F$ [S2].
     $$ \text{ray dir} = F - P_I $$
   - **Transition Region:** Calculate the point $P_F$ on the virtual lens focus plane. The direction is then $P_F - P_I$. The point $P_F$ is computed by interpolating based on the distance from the center [S2]:
     $$ P_F = F + \frac{P_I - LC}{|P_I - LC|} \cdot |P_F - F| $$
     where the scalar distance $|P_F - F|$ is determined by the linear interpolation ratio:
     $$ \frac{|P_F - F|}{l_b} = \frac{|P_I - LC| - (l_r - l_b)}{l_r} $$
     Substituting this back, the direction is:
     $$ \text{ray dir} = P_F - P_I $$
4. **Ray Casting:** Cast rays with the modified directions into the volume. Objects in the magnification region appear larger; objects in the transition region appear compressed [S2].
5. **Anti-Aliasing:** Apply volume texture mip-mapping to handle aliasing in the transition region where ray sampling rates are lower [S2]. Calculate the magnification factor $m_f$ for a point $P_R$ in the transition region to select the appropriate mip-map level:
   $$ m_f = \left( \frac{|P_R - P_{RI}|}{|F - LC|} \cdot \frac{l_b}{l_r} - 1 \right) + 1 $$
   Use the mip-map level corresponding to $m_f$ [S2].

**Parameters**
- $l_r$: Radius of the lens. Controls the size of the magnification region [S2].
- $l_b$: Width of the transition region. Controls the smoothness of the transition [S2].
- $F$: Virtual focal point. Moving $F$ closer to the image plane increases the magnification factor [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
- **Aliasing:** Occurs in the transition region due to low ray sampling rates. Mitigated by mip-mapping [S2].
- **Context Loss:** Without the transition region, objects between orthogonal and focused rays would be invisible. The transition region prevents this by compressing rather than clipping [S1, book][S2].
- **Arbitrary Shapes:** The method supports arbitrary lens shapes, not just circular ones, by generalizing the distance metric from $LC$ [S1, book].

**Pseudocode**
```python
def compute_ray_direction(P_I, LC, F, l_r, l_b):
    """
    Compute the ray direction for a given pixel origin P_I.
    P_I, LC, F are 3D vectors.
    l_r, l_b are scalars.
    """
    dist = vector_length(P_I - LC)
    
    # Normal region: outside lens radius
    if dist > l_r:
        # Return orthogonal direction (assuming camera looks along Z, this is (0,0,1) or similar)
        # For generality, assume a function get_orthogonal_direction(P_I) exists
        return get_orthogonal_direction(P_I)
    
    # Magnification region: inside inner radius
    if dist <= l_r - l_b:
        # Ray passes through focal point F
        return normalize(F - P_I)
    
    # Transition region
    # Calculate the interpolation factor t
    # t = 0 at dist = l_r - l_b (focused direction)
    # t = 1 at dist = l_r (orthogonal direction)
    t = (dist - (l_r - l_b)) / l_b
    
    # Calculate P_F
    # From source: |PF - F| / lb = (|PI - LC| - (lr - lb)) / lr
    # Let dist_PF_F = lb * (dist - (l_r - l_b)) / l_r
    dist_PF_F = l_b * (dist - (l_r - l_b)) / l_r
    
    # Direction from center LC to PI
    dir_center_to_pi = normalize(P_I - LC)
    
    # P_F = F + dir_center_to_pi * dist_PF_F
    P_F = F + dir_center_to_pi * dist_PF_F
    
    return normalize(P_F - P_I)

def get_mipmap_level(P_R, LC, F, l_r, l_b):
    """
    Calculate magnification factor mf for anti-aliasing.
    P_R is a point in the transition region.
    P_RI is the orthogonal projection of P_R on the image plane.
    """
    # Assuming P_RI is known or computed as projection of P_R onto image plane
    # For a standard camera, if P_R is on the image plane, P_RI == P_R
    # The formula uses |PR - PRI|. If P_R is on the image plane, this is 0.
    # However, the source implies P_R is used to determine the level for the region.
    # Let's assume P_R is the pixel coordinate.
    
    # Note: The source formula is:
    # mf = ( |PR - PRI| / |F - LC| * lb / lr - 1 ) + 1
    # Simplifies to: mf = |PR - PRI| / |F - LC| * lb / lr
    
    # If P_R is on the image plane, |PR - PRI| is 0, so mf = 0?
    # This suggests P_R might be a point in 3D space or the formula refers to distance from center.
    # Re-reading source: "calculate the magnification factor mf for point PR... where PRI is the orthogonal projection of PR on the image plane."
    # If PR is on the image plane, PR == PRI, so mf = 1.
    # This implies the mip-map level is based on the base resolution.
    # However, the context is "lower resolution volume is chosen for regions sampled at a lower rate".
    # The transition region has lower sampling rate.
    # Let's stick to the formula as written, assuming P_R is the pixel coordinate.
    
    dist_PR_PRI = vector_length(P_R - P_RI) # Likely 0 if P_R is on image plane
    dist_F_LC = vector_length(F - LC)
    
    mf = (dist_PR_PRI / dist_F_LC) * (l_b / l_r)
    
    # The formula in source: mf = ( ... - 1 ) + 1
    # So mf = 1 + (dist_PR_PRI / dist_F_LC * l_b / l_r - 1)
    # If dist_PR_PRI is 0, mf = 1.
    
    # To be safe, we return the calculated factor.
    return mf
```

## Subtopics

### [Sampling-rate-based lens](6758-sampling-rate-based-lens.md)
Modifies spatial density of rays without altering directions, keeping rays parallel but varying start positions to create dense sampling in the center.

### [Feature-based lens](7436-feature-based-lens.md)
Selectively magnifies specific features of interest within a segmented volume, leveraging the ray casting nature to detect features.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of pixels, plus the cost of volume ray casting. The transition region requires additional computation for direction interpolation and mip-map selection [S2].
- **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the volume dataset and the number of mip-map levels generated for anti-aliasing [S2].
- **Strengths:** Provides smooth transitions between focal and peripheral regions without clipping; keeps lens effects local; supports arbitrary lens shapes; software-based implementation allows for functionalities hard to fabricate in physical optics [S1, book].
- **Limitations:** Aliasing can occur in the transition region due to low ray sampling rates, requiring anti-aliasing techniques [S2]. Objects in the transition region appear compressed, which may be visually distracting if not tuned properly [S2].
- **When to use:** When close inspection of specific regions in a volume is needed while maintaining spatial context [S2].
- **When not to use:** When optical blur (depth of field) is desired instead of geometric magnification [S2].

## Background
The Magnifier was introduced as part of a framework for illustrative focus+context approaches in interactive volume visualization [S1, book]. It is based on the magnification model in optical physics and conforms to sampling theory [S2]. The method was developed to provide users with a tool for close inspection of regions of interest in volumetric objects, addressing the loss of spatial context that occurs with simple clipping [S1, book][S2]. It is implemented in software, allowing for arbitrary shapes and interactive adjustment of parameters such as focal point position and lens radius [S2].
