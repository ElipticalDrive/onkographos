# Near-Far Linear Ray Generation

Also called: Coarse Sampling, NeRF Near-Far Linear Ray Generation, Near-Far Disparity Linear Ray Generation, Ray Marching Sampling, Ray Marching with Linear/Disparity Sampling

## Overview
Near-Far Linear Ray Generation is a method for sampling 3D points along camera rays within a specified near and far depth range. It solves the problem of defining the spatial domain for volume rendering or neural radiance field (NeRF) evaluation by distributing sample points either linearly in depth or linearly in disparity (inverse depth) [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. It differs from its siblings by providing the foundational uniform or jittered sampling strategy that subsequent methods like [Near-Middle-Far Ray Generation](6817-near-middle-far-ray-generation.md) or [Refined Ray Generation via PDF Sampling](6837-refined-ray-generation-via-pdf-sampling.md) build upon, typically serving as the "coarse" sampling stage in a multi-stage rendering pipeline [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   `rays_o`: Ray origins, shape `(num_rays, 3)`.
*   `rays_d`: Ray directions, shape `(num_rays, 3)`.
*   `near`: Near plane distance scalar or tensor, shape `()`.
*   `far`: Far plane distance scalar or tensor, shape `()`.
*   `num_points` (or `N_samples`): Number of sample points per ray [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   `lindisp` (boolean): If true, sample in disparity space ($1/z$); if false, sample in linear depth space ($z$) [S3, unofficial].
*   `perturb` (float): Amount of random jitter to add to samples (0.0 for no jitter, 1.0 for full jitter) [S3, unofficial].
*   `bbox_3D` (optional): Axis-Aligned Bounding Box (AABB) defined by min/max coordinates. If provided, ray segments are clipped to this volume [S3, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   `z_vals`: Depth values (or disparity values if `lindisp` is true) for each sample point, shape `(num_rays, num_points)`.
*   `xyz_coarse_sampled`: 3D coordinates of the samples, shape `(num_rays, num_points, 3)`.

**Data Structures:**
*   The method operates on batched tensors. `rays_o` and `rays_d` are typically batched over all pixels in an image or a subset thereof.
*   `z_vals` stores the parameter $t$ (or depth $z$) along the ray.

**Steps:**

1.  **Decompose Rays:** Extract origins `rays_o`, directions `rays_d`, near `near`, and far `far` [S3, unofficial].
2.  **Generate Base Steps:** Create a 1D tensor `z_steps` of length `num_points` linearly spaced between 0 and 1:
    $$ z\_steps = \text{linspace}(0, 1, \text{num\_points}) $$
    [S3, unofficial]
3.  **Compute Depth/Disparity Values (`z_vals`):**
    *   If `lindisp` is false (Linear Depth):
        $$ z\_vals = \text{near} \cdot (1 - z\_steps) + \text{far} \cdot z\_steps $$
        [S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial]
    *   If `lindisp` is true (Linear Disparity):
        $$ z\_vals = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{\text{near}} \cdot (1 - z\_steps) + \frac{1}{\text{far}} \cdot z\_steps} $$
        [S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial]
4.  **Perturbation (Jitter):** If `perturb > 0`:
    *   Compute midpoints between adjacent samples:
        $$ z\_vals\_mid = \frac{z\_vals[:, :-1] + z\_vals[:, 1:]}{2} $$
        [S3, unofficial]
        *(Note: For the first and last intervals, bounds are typically handled by extending or clamping, though specific implementations may vary. The source implies using midpoints to define intervals for jittering)* [S3, unofficial].
    *   Define lower and upper bounds for each interval. For interval $i$, let $lower_i$ and $upper_i$ be the bounds.
    *   Generate random noise `rand_vals` uniform in $[0, 1]$ with shape `(num_rays, num_points)`.
    *   Update `z_vals`:
        $$ z\_vals = lower + (upper - lower) \cdot \text{perturb} \cdot \text{rand\_vals} $$
        [S3, unofficial]
    *   *Alternative Jitter Implementation (from S2, unofficial):* Some implementations generate `tvals` in $[0,1]$, convert to disparity/linear, then jitter `tvals` directly within segments before final conversion.
5.  **Bounding Box Clipping (Optional):** If `bbox_3D` is provided:
    *   Compute inverse directions: `inv_ray_d = 1.0 / (rays_d + 1e-6)` [S3, unofficial].
    *   Calculate intersection intervals $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$ for each ray with the AABB using the slab method.
    *   Clamp the generated `z_vals` (or corresponding $t$ values) to the valid intersection range within the box. If a ray does not intersect the box, it may be discarded or handled as an edge case.
6.  **Compute 3D Coordinates:**
    $$ xyz\_coarse\_sampled = \text{rays\_o} + \text{rays\_d} \cdot z\_vals $$
    [S3, unofficial]
    *(Note: This assumes `z_vals` represents the distance along the ray direction. If `z_vals` represents depth $z$ in camera space and rays are not aligned with the Z-axis, a more complex projection is required. However, in NeRF-style pipelines, `z_vals` often represents the parameter $t$ along the ray vector, or the rays are assumed to be in a coordinate system where this multiplication holds, or `z_vals` is explicitly the distance along the ray. The source [S3, unofficial] uses `rays_o + rays_d * z_vals`, implying `z_vals` is treated as the ray parameter $t$.)*

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch

def near_far_linear_ray_generation(rays_o, rays_d, near, far, num_points, lindisp=False, perturb=1.0, bbox_3d=None):
    """
    Generate sample points along rays.
    
    Args:
        rays_o: Tensor of shape (num_rays, 3), ray origins.
        rays_d: Tensor of shape (num_rays, 3), ray directions.
        near: Tensor of shape (num_rays,) or scalar, near plane distance.
        far: Tensor of shape (num_rays,) or scalar, far plane distance.
        num_points: int, number of samples per ray.
        lindisp: bool, if True, sample in disparity space.
        perturb: float, amount of jitter (0.0 to 1.0).
        bbox_3d: Optional tuple (min_xyz, max_xyz) for AABB clipping.
        
    Returns:
        z_vals: Tensor of shape (num_rays, num_points), depth/disparity values.
        xyz_coarse_sampled: Tensor of shape (num_rays, num_points, 3), 3D coordinates.
    """
    # Step 1: Generate linear steps in [0, 1]
    # z_steps shape: (num_points,)
    z_steps = torch.linspace(0, 1, num_points)
    
    # Expand dimensions for broadcasting with rays_o/d (num_rays,)
    # near and far might be scalars or (num_rays,)
    if near.dim() == 0:
        near = near.expand(num_rays)
    if far.dim() == 0:
        far = far.expand(num_rays)
        
    # Step 2: Compute z_vals based on lindisp
    if not lindisp:
        # Linear depth: z = near * (1 - t) + far * t
        # z_steps is (num_points,), near/far are (num_rays,)
        # Result z_vals: (num_rays, num_points)
        z_vals = near.unsqueeze(1) * (1 - z_steps) + far.unsqueeze(1) * z_steps
    else:
        # Linear disparity: z = 1 / (1/near * (1 - t) + 1/far * t)
        inv_near = 1.0 / near
        inv_far = 1.0 / far
        inv_z_vals = inv_near.unsqueeze(1) * (1 - z_steps) + inv_far.unsqueeze(1) * z_steps
        z_vals = 1.0 / inv_z_vals
        
    # Step 3: Perturbation (Jitter)
    if perturb > 0:
        # Compute midpoints for interval bounds
        # z_vals shape: (num_rays, num_points)
        # z_vals_mid shape: (num_rays, num_points - 1)
        z_vals_mid = (z_vals[:, :-1] + z_vals[:, 1:]) / 2.0
        
        # Define lower and upper bounds for each sample
        # For the first sample, lower is near (or z_vals[:, 0])? 
        # Standard NeRF jitter uses midpoints to define intervals.
        # Interval i is [z_vals_mid[i-1], z_vals_mid[i]] for i in 1..N-2
        # Edge cases: 
        # Sample 0: interval [near, z_vals_mid[0]]? Or [z_vals[0], z_vals_mid[0]]?
        # Source [S3, unofficial] implies: lower + (upper - lower) * perturb * rand
        # Let's assume standard implementation:
        # lower = z_vals[:, :-1] (left bound of interval)
        # upper = z_vals[:, 1:] (right bound of interval)
        # But we need bounds for ALL points.
        
        # Common approach:
        # lower = z_vals[:, :-1] -> (num_rays, num_points-1)
        # upper = z_vals[:, 1:] -> (num_rays, num_points-1)
        # This covers intervals between points.
        # For the first point, lower = near? For the last, upper = far?
        # Let's use the midpoint-based interval definition from NeRF:
        # t_vals = z_vals
        # t_mids = (t_vals[:, :-1] + t_vals[:, 1:]) / 2
        # t_lower = torch.cat([near.unsqueeze(1), t_mids], dim=1)
        # t_upper = torch.cat([t_mids, far.unsqueeze(1)], dim=1)
        # t_rand = torch.rand_like(t_vals)
        # t_vals = t_lower + (t_upper - t_lower) * perturb * t_rand
        
        # Re-implementing based on S3 description:
        # "define upper/lower bounds; add random noise: z_vals = lower + (upper - lower) * perturb * torch.rand(...)"
        
        # Construct bounds
        # lower bounds: [near, z_vals_mid[0], z_vals_mid[1], ...]
        # upper bounds: [z_vals_mid[0], z_vals_mid[1], ..., far]
        
        lower = torch.cat([near.unsqueeze(1), z_vals_mid], dim=1)
        upper = torch.cat([z_vals_mid, far.unsqueeze(1)], dim=1)
        
        rand_vals = torch.rand_like(z_vals)
        z_vals = lower + (upper - lower) * perturb * rand_vals
        
    # Step 4: Bounding Box Clipping (if provided)
    if bbox_3d is not None:
        min_xyz, max_xyz = bbox_3d
        # Compute ray-box intersection intervals [t_min, t_max]
        # inv_ray_d shape: (num_rays, 3)
        inv_ray_d = 1.0 / (rays_d + 1e-6)
        
        # t_min for each axis
        t_min_axis = (min_xyz.unsqueeze(0) - rays_o.unsqueeze(1)) * inv_ray_d.unsqueeze(0) # (num_rays, 3)
        t_max_axis = (max_xyz.unsqueeze(0) - rays_o.unsqueeze(1)) * inv_ray_d.unsqueeze(0) # (num_rays, 3)
        
        # t_enter = max(t_min_axis) over axes
        t_enter = torch.max(t_min_axis, dim=1).values # (num_rays,)
        # t_exit = min(t_max_axis) over axes
        t_exit = torch.min(t_max_axis, dim=1).values # (num_rays,)
        
        # Ensure t_enter < t_exit
        t_enter, t_exit = torch.min(t_enter, t_exit), torch.max(t_enter, t_exit)
        
        # Clamp z_vals (which are treated as t) to [t_enter, t_exit]
        # Note: This assumes z_vals are distances along the ray. 
        # If z_vals are depth Z, this step is more complex and depends on ray orientation.
        # Given S3 uses `rays_o + rays_d * z_vals`, z_vals is t.
        z_vals = torch.clamp(z_vals, t_enter.unsqueeze(1), t_exit.unsqueeze(1))
        
    # Step 5: Compute 3D coordinates
    # xyz_coarse_sampled shape: (num_rays, num_points, 3)
    # rays_o: (num_rays, 3), rays_d: (num_rays, 3), z_vals: (num_rays, num_points)
    xyz_coarse_sampled = rays_o.unsqueeze(1) + rays_d.unsqueeze(1) * z_vals.unsqueeze(2)
    
    return z_vals, xyz_coarse_sampled
```

**Parameters:**
*   `num_points`: Typical values range from 48 to 192 for coarse sampling, and up to 1000+ for fine sampling [S3, unofficial].
*   `perturb`: Typically 1.0 for training to break symmetry, 0.0 for inference [S3, unofficial].
*   `lindisp`: Typically False for scenes with small depth variations, True for scenes with large depth variations (e.g., outdoor scenes) [S3, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Division by Zero:** When computing inverse disparity ($1/near$, $1/far$), ensure `near` and `far` are not zero. A small epsilon (e.g., $10^{-6}$) should be added if necessary [S3, unofficial].
*   **Ray-BBox Intersection:** If a ray does not intersect the bounding box, `t_enter > t_exit`. The clamping step should handle this, potentially resulting in invalid samples or requiring ray culling.
*   **Precision:** Floating-point precision issues may arise when `near` and `far` differ significantly in magnitude, especially in disparity space.

## Subtopics

### [Near-Far Clipping](7709-near-far-clipping.md)
A post-processing step that restricts sampling to a viewing volume defined by near and far planes, operating on ray parameter space.

### [Inverse CDF Ray Sampling](11412-inverse-cdf-ray-sampling.md)
Generates samples based on a cumulative distribution function derived from per-hit probabilities, allowing non-uniform sampling.

### [Near-Far Linear/Disparity Ray Generation with Stratification](11841-near-far-linear-disparity-ray-generation-with-stratification.md)
Extends linear/disparity sampling with stratification to reduce variance within depth intervals.

### Near-Far Disparity Linear Ray Generation with Jitter
Generates ray samples using disparity spacing with random jitter within segments. It differs from basic disparity sampling by explicitly calculating segment lengths and jittering `tvals` within those segments before converting to final positions. It is chosen when variance reduction via jitter is needed in disparity-based sampling. [S2, unofficial]

### Linear Seed Line Generation
A simpler method to generate seed points along a line segment between a start and end point by linear interpolation. It does not involve camera rays or depth planes, but rather a fixed line segment. It is used for basic line sampling tasks. [S4, unofficial]

### Near-Far Linear Ray Generation with NDC Depth Simulation
Generates samples in linear depth, then converts to NDC (Normalized Device Coordinates) depth and rescales to simulated Euclidean depth. This is used in pipelines that require NDC space for rendering or training stability. It differs from standard linear generation by the additional NDC conversion and rescaling steps. [S5, unofficial]

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot R)$, where $N$ is the number of samples per ray and $R$ is the number of rays. The operations are vectorizable and highly parallelizable on GPUs.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N \cdot R \cdot 3)$ for storing the 3D coordinates, plus $O(N \cdot R)$ for depth values.
*   **Performance:** Very fast, as it involves only basic arithmetic operations. It is the bottleneck only if $N$ is extremely large.
*   **Strengths:** Simple to implement, deterministic (without jitter), provides uniform coverage of the depth range.
*   **Limitations:** Uniform sampling may waste samples in empty space or undersample regions of high detail. Disparity sampling is better for scenes with large depth ranges but can be less intuitive.
*   **When to use:** As a coarse sampling stage in multi-stage rendering, or when scene depth distribution is unknown or uniform.
*   **When not to use:** When adaptive sampling (e.g., based on opacity or gradient) is required, or when high-frequency details are concentrated in specific depth intervals.

## Background
The method originates from the NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) paper [Kerbl et al., 2020] and has been widely adopted in subsequent neural rendering works such as TensoRF [Chen et al., 2022] and PointNeRF [Xharlie, 2021]. It serves as the foundational sampling strategy for defining the domain of integration in volume rendering. The use of disparity sampling (`lindisp`) was introduced to handle scenes with large depth variations more effectively.
