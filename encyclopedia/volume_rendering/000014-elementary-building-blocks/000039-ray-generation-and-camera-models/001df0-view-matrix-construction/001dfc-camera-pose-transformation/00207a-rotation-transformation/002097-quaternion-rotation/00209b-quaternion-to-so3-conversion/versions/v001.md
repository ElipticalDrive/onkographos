# Quaternion to SO3 Conversion

Also called: Matrix to Quaternion, Quaternion Rotation Matrix Conversion, Quaternion to Rotation Matrix, Quaternion to Rotation Matrix Conversion, Rotation Matrix to Quaternion, Rotation Matrix to Quaternion Conversion, q2r, quaternion rotation conversion, qvec2rotmat, rotmat2qvec

## Overview
Quaternion to SO3 Conversion transforms a quaternion representation into a 3x3 rotation matrix $R \in SO(3)$, or vice versa, enabling the use of quaternions for compact rotation storage and matrices for linear algebra operations such as matrix multiplication and transformation composition. This method is distinct from [Quaternion Rotation](2302-quaternion-rotation.md), which applies a rotation to a vector, by providing the explicit basis transformation matrix. It differs from [Quaternion Operations](3474-quaternion-operations.md) by focusing specifically on the structural conversion between the two mathematical representations rather than algebraic manipulation of quaternions themselves.

## How it works
The conversion can proceed in two directions: Quaternion to Rotation Matrix ($q \to R$) and Rotation Matrix to Quaternion ($R \to q$). Both directions require handling numerical stability and sign ambiguity.

### Inputs and Outputs
- **Quaternion to Matrix**:
  - Input: A quaternion $q = [x, y, z, w]$ (or $[w, x, y, z]$ depending on convention) represented as a vector of 4 floats. Often provided as a batch of shape $(N, 4)$.
  - Output: A rotation matrix $R$ of shape $(3, 3)$ or batch $(N, 3, 3)$.
- **Matrix to Quaternion**:
  - Input: A 3x3 rotation matrix $R$.
  - Output: A quaternion $q = [x, y, z, w]$ (or $[w, x, y, z]$).

### Data Structures
- **Quaternion**: 4-component vector. Sources use different conventions:
  - $[x, y, z, w]$ (scalar last) is common in [S2, S3, S5].
  - $[w, x, y, z]$ (scalar first) is common in [S4].
  - The implementation must adhere to the specific convention of the surrounding system. The formulas below assume $q = [x, y, z, w]$ (scalar last) unless noted.
- **Rotation Matrix**: 3x3 orthogonal matrix with determinant +1.

### Quaternion to Rotation Matrix ($q \to R$)
This direction is direct and numerically stable if the input quaternion is normalized.

1. **Normalization**: Ensure the input quaternion $q$ is a unit quaternion. If not, normalize it:
   $$ q_{norm} = \frac{q}{\|q\|_2} $$
   Let $q = [x, y, z, w]$ where $w$ is the scalar part [S2, S3, S5].

2. **Compute Matrix Elements**: Calculate the 9 elements of $R$ using the following formulas [S2, S3, S5]:
   $$
   R = \begin{bmatrix}
   1 - 2(y^2 + z^2) & 2(xy - zw) & 2(xz + yw) \\
   2(xy + zw) & 1 - 2(x^2 + z^2) & 2(yz - xw) \\
   2(xz - yw) & 2(yz + xw) & 1 - 2(x^2 + y^2)
   \end{bmatrix}
   $$
   Note: Some sources use $[q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3]$ where $q_0=w, q_1=x, q_2=y, q_3=z$ [S4, S5]. The formula above maps $x,y,z$ to the vector components and $w$ to the scalar.

3. **Batch Processing**: For a batch of quaternions, compute the outer product $q \otimes q$ (element-wise multiplication of components) and apply the formulas vectorized [S3].

### Rotation Matrix to Quaternion ($R \to q$)
This direction requires handling numerical instability near singularities (e.g., when the trace is near zero or a diagonal element is dominant). The standard approach uses the trace of $R$ to determine the largest component [S3].

1. **Compute Trace**:
   $$ T = R_{00} + R_{11} + R_{22} $$

2. **Determine Dominant Component**:
   Identify which component ($w, x, y, z$) has the largest magnitude to avoid division by small numbers. The conditions are [S3]:
   - If $T > 0$: $w$ is largest.
   - Else if $R_{00} > R_{11}$ and $R_{00} > R_{22}$: $x$ is largest.
   - Else if $R_{11} > R_{22}$: $y$ is largest.
   - Else: $z$ is largest.

3. **Compute Quaternion Components**:
   Depending on the dominant component, use the corresponding case [S3]:

   - **Case 1: $T > 0$ (w is largest)**
     $$ S = \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{1 + T}} $$
     $$ w = 0.25 / S $$
     $$ x = (R_{21} - R_{12}) \cdot S $$
     $$ y = (R_{02} - R_{20}) \cdot S $$
     $$ z = (R_{10} - R_{01}) \cdot S $$

   - **Case 2: $R_{00}$ is max (x is largest)**
     $$ S = 2 \sqrt{1 + R_{00} - R_{11} - R_{22}} $$
     $$ x = 0.25 \cdot S $$
     $$ y = (R_{01} + R_{10}) / S $$
     $$ z = (R_{02} + R_{20}) / S $$
     $$ w = (R_{21} - R_{12}) / S $$

   - **Case 3: $R_{11}$ is max (y is largest)**
     $$ S = 2 \sqrt{1 + R_{11} - R_{00} - R_{22}} $$
     $$ x = (R_{01} + R_{10}) / S $$
     $$ y = 0.25 \cdot S $$
     $$ z = (R_{12} + R_{21}) / S $$
     $$ w = (R_{02} - R_{20}) / S $$

   - **Case 4: $R_{22}$ is max (z is largest)**
     $$ S = 2 \sqrt{1 + R_{22} - R_{00} - R_{11}} $$
     $$ x = (R_{02} + R_{20}) / S $$
     $$ y = (R_{12} + R_{21}) / S $$
     $$ z = 0.25 \cdot S $$
     $$ w = (R_{10} - R_{01}) / S $$

4. **Sign Ambiguity Handling**: Quaternions $q$ and $-q$ represent the same rotation. Some implementations (e.g., [S4]) enforce a canonical sign convention, such as ensuring the first non-zero component is positive, or specifically $w \ge 0$. If the resulting $w < 0$, negate the entire quaternion $q = -q$ [S4].

### Pseudocode (Quaternion to Matrix)
```python
def quaternion_to_rotation_matrix(q):
    """
    Converts a quaternion [x, y, z, w] to a 3x3 rotation matrix.
    Assumes q is normalized. If not, normalize first.
    q shape: (4,) or (N, 4)
    Returns R shape: (3, 3) or (N, 3, 3)
    """
    # Unpack components
    x, y, z, w = q[..., 0], q[..., 1], q[..., 2], q[..., 3]
    
    # Compute squares
    x2 = x * x
    y2 = y * y
    z2 = z * z
    w2 = w * w
    
    # Compute cross terms
    xy = x * y
    xz = x * z
    xw = x * w
    yz = y * z
    yw = y * w
    zw = z * w
    
    # Construct matrix elements
    # R[0,0] = 1 - 2(y^2 + z^2)
    r00 = 1.0 - 2.0 * (y2 + z2)
    # R[0,1] = 2(xy - zw)
    r01 = 2.0 * (xy - zw)
    # R[0,2] = 2(xz + yw)
    r02 = 2.0 * (xz + yw)
    
    # R[1,0] = 2(xy + zw)
    r10 = 2.0 * (xy + zw)
    # R[1,1] = 1 - 2(x^2 + z^2)
    r11 = 1.0 - 2.0 * (x2 + z2)
    # R[1,2] = 2(yz - xw)
    r12 = 2.0 * (yz - xw)
    
    # R[2,0] = 2(xz - yw)
    r20 = 2.0 * (xz - yw)
    # R[2,1] = 2(yz + xw)
    r21 = 2.0 * (yz + xw)
    # R[2,2] = 1 - 2(x^2 + y^2)
    r22 = 1.0 - 2.0 * (x2 + y2)
    
    # Stack and reshape
    # For batched input, ensure proper broadcasting
    R = torch.stack([
        torch.stack([r00, r01, r02], dim=-1),
        torch.stack([r10, r11, r12], dim=-1),
        torch.stack([r20, r21, r22], dim=-1)
    ], dim=-2)
    
    return R
```

### Pseudocode (Matrix to Quaternion)
```python
def rotation_matrix_to_quaternion(R):
    """
    Converts a 3x3 rotation matrix R to a quaternion [x, y, z, w].
    R shape: (3, 3) or (N, 3, 3)
    Returns q shape: (4,) or (N, 4)
    """
    # Extract elements
    r00 = R[..., 0, 0]
    r01 = R[..., 0, 1]
    r02 = R[..., 0, 2]
    r10 = R[..., 1, 0]
    r11 = R[..., 1, 1]
    r12 = R[..., 1, 2]
    r20 = R[..., 2, 0]
    r21 = R[..., 2, 1]
    r22 = R[..., 2, 2]
    
    # Compute trace
    trace = r00 + r11 + r22
    
    # Initialize quaternion components
    x = torch.zeros_like(trace)
    y = torch.zeros_like(trace)
    z = torch.zeros_like(trace)
    w = torch.zeros_like(trace)
    
    # Case 1: Trace > 0
    mask_t = trace > 0
    if mask_t.any():
        S = 0.5 / torch.sqrt(1.0 + trace[mask_t])
        w[mask_t] = 0.25 / S
        x[mask_t] = (r21[mask_t] - r12[mask_t]) * S
        y[mask_t] = (r02[mask_t] - r20[mask_t]) * S
        z[mask_t] = (r10[mask_t] - r01[mask_t]) * S
        
    # Case 2: R00 is max
    mask_x = (~mask_t) & (r00 > r11) & (r00 > r22)
    if mask_x.any():
        S = 2.0 * torch.sqrt(1.0 + r00[mask_x] - r11[mask_x] - r22[mask_x])
        x[mask_x] = 0.25 * S
        y[mask_x] = (r01[mask_x] + r10[mask_x]) / S
        z[mask_x] = (r02[mask_x] + r20[mask_x]) / S
        w[mask_x] = (r21[mask_x] - r12[mask_x]) / S
        
    # Case 3: R11 is max
    mask_y = (~mask_t) & (~mask_x) & (r11 > r22)
    if mask_y.any():
        S = 2.0 * torch.sqrt(1.0 + r11[mask_y] - r00[mask_y] - r22[mask_y])
        x[mask_y] = (r01[mask_y] + r10[mask_y]) / S
        y[mask_y] = 0.25 * S
        z[mask_y] = (r12[mask_y] + r21[mask_y]) / S
        w[mask_y] = (r02[mask_y] - r20[mask_y]) / S
        
    # Case 4: R22 is max
    mask_z = (~mask_t) & (~mask_x) & (~mask_y)
    if mask_z.any():
        S = 2.0 * torch.sqrt(1.0 + r22[mask_z] - r00[mask_z] - r11[mask_z])
        x[mask_z] = (r02[mask_z] + r20[mask_z]) / S
        y[mask_z] = (r12[mask_z] + r21[mask_z]) / S
        z[mask_z] = 0.25 * S
        w[mask_z] = (r10[mask_z] - r01[mask_z]) / S
        
    # Stack components
    q = torch.stack([x, y, z, w], dim=-1)
    
    # Handle sign ambiguity (optional, based on convention)
    # Ensure w >= 0 for canonical representation
    neg_mask = q[..., 3] < 0
    q[neg_mask] = -q[neg_mask]
    
    return q
```

### Parameters
- **Normalization**: Input quaternions should be unit quaternions. If not, normalization is required [S2, S3, S5].
- **Convention**: The order of components $[x, y, z, w]$ vs $[w, x, y, z]$ must be consistent. The formulas above assume $[x, y, z, w]$ (scalar last) [S2, S3, S5]. If using $[w, x, y, z]$, adjust indices accordingly [S4].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
- **Numerical Instability**: When the trace is near zero or a diagonal element is near zero, direct division can cause instability. The case-based approach using the largest component avoids this [S3].
- **Sign Ambiguity**: $q$ and $-q$ represent the same rotation. Implementations may enforce a canonical sign (e.g., $w \ge 0$) to ensure uniqueness [S4].
- **Non-Rotation Matrices**: If the input matrix is not a valid rotation matrix (e.g., due to numerical error or non-orthogonality), the output quaternion may not be a unit quaternion. Normalization of the output quaternion is recommended [S3].
- **Batch Processing**: Ensure broadcasting rules are handled correctly for batched inputs [S2, S3, S5].

## Subtopics

### SE3 to Quaternion and Translation
Extracts the quaternion and translation vector from a 4x4 SE3 transformation matrix.
- **Definition**: Decomposes an SE3 matrix into its rotational (quaternion) and translational (vector) components [S3].
- **Steps**:
  1. Extract the top-left 3x3 submatrix $R$ and the top-right 3x1 column vector $t$ from the 4x4 SE3 matrix [S3].
  2. Convert $R$ to a quaternion using the `rotation_matrix_to_quaternion` method described above [S3].
  3. Return the quaternion $q$ and translation vector $t$ [S3].
- **When to use**: When working with full rigid body transformations (position and orientation) stored as 4x4 matrices, and a compact quaternion representation is needed for optimization or interpolation [S3].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**:
  - $q \to R$: $O(1)$ for a single quaternion, $O(N)$ for a batch of size $N$. Involves a fixed number of multiplications and additions.
  - $R \to q$: $O(1)$ for a single matrix, $O(N)$ for a batch. Involves square roots and conditional branching.
- **Memory Complexity**: $O(1)$ for single conversions, $O(N)$ for batched conversions to store the output matrix or quaternion.
- **Measured Performance**: Not given in the sources.
- **Strengths**:
  - $q \to R$ is computationally cheap and numerically stable.
  - $R \to q$ using the trace-based method is robust against numerical instability near singularities [S3].
- **Limitations**:
  - $R \to q$ requires branching logic, which may be less efficient on GPUs if not vectorized properly [S3].
  - Sign ambiguity requires additional handling if a canonical representation is needed [S4].
- **When to use**: Use $q \to R$ when matrix operations (e.g., composition with other transformations) are required. Use $R \to q$ when compact storage or interpolation (e.g., SLERP) is needed.

## Background
- **Origin**: The conversion between quaternions and rotation matrices is a fundamental operation in 3D graphics and robotics, dating back to the early use of quaternions in computer graphics [S1, S2, S3, S4, S5].
- **History**: The specific trace-based algorithm for $R \to q$ to avoid numerical instability is widely cited in computer graphics literature [S3]. The implementation details in the sources reflect modern deep learning frameworks (PyTorch) used in recent 3D Gaussian Splatting and NeRF research [S1, S2, S3, S5].
- **Usage**: Commonly used in camera pose estimation, 3D reconstruction, and robotics for converting between rotation representations [S3, S4].
