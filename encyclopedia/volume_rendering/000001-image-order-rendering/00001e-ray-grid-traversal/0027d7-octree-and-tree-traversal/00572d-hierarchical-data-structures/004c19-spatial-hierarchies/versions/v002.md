# Spatial Hierarchies
Also called: Spatial Tree Structures
## Overview
Spatial hierarchies accelerate volume rendering by performing point location through recursive search through the tree structure [S1, unofficial]. They organize spatial data into a hierarchy of bounding volumes, allowing the renderer to quickly cull empty or irrelevant regions. What sets them apart from sibling methods like Hierarchical Spatial Enumeration Optimization is their explicit geometric subdivision of space rather than linear enumeration or silhouette-based approximations.
## Shared principles
The core mechanism involves building a tree where leaf nodes correspond to unique regions in space [S1, unofficial]. Insertion into these structures follows a recursive subdivision strategy governed by two primary parameters: `MAXDEPTH`, the maximum depth of the tree (typically 16), and `MAXPRIMS`, the maximum number of primitives allowed in a leaf node before subdivision (typically between 2 and 8) [S1, unofficial].

The insertion algorithm proceeds as follows:
```python
def insert(node, prim):
    # Check if the primitive overlaps with the node's bounding volume
    if overlap(node.bound, prim):
        if is_leaf(node):
            # Leaf node reached
            if node.nprims > MAXPRIMS and node.depth < MAXDEPTH:
                # Subdivide the leaf node
                subdivide(node)
                # Recursively insert the primitive into all children
                for child in node.children:
                    insert(child, prim)
            else:
                # Add primitive to the leaf's list
                list_insert(node.prims, prim)
        else:
            # Internal node: recurse into all children
            for child in node.children:
                insert(child, prim)
```
This process ensures that primitives are stored in the smallest appropriate nodes, balancing tree depth and leaf density [S1, unofficial].
## Subtopics

### Octtrees
Octrees subdivide space into eight equal octants at each level. They are widely used for their regular structure and ease of implementation. They are chosen when the data distribution is relatively uniform or when a fixed subdivision scheme is preferred. See [Octree and Tree Traversal](52-octree-and-tree-traversal.md).

### BSP Trees
Binary Space Partitioning (BSP) trees split space using arbitrary planes, allowing for non-axis-aligned subdivisions. They are chosen when complex geometry requires precise partitioning or when the scene has strong directional features. They differ from octrees by not requiring equal subdivisions.

### KD trees
KD trees split space using axis-aligned planes, alternating the splitting dimension at each level. They are chosen for their efficiency in high-dimensional spaces and their ability to handle anisotropic data distributions better than octrees. They offer a compromise between the rigidity of octrees and the complexity of BSP trees.

### Insertion into Spatial Hierarchies
This variant focuses on the specific algorithms used to build the hierarchy, such as Median-Cut or Surface-Area Heuristic (SAH) based splitting. Median-Cut builds the hierarchy bottom-up by choosing the direction and position of splits carefully [S1, unofficial]. SAH minimizes the expected traversal cost by considering the surface area of bounding volumes and the probability of rays hitting objects, which is proportional to the projected area [S1, unofficial]. Crofton’s theorem relates the number of rays hitting a convex body to its surface area [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Spatial hierarchies are a fundamental acceleration technique in ray tracing and volume rendering. The basic insertion algorithm and parameter values (MAXDEPTH=16, MAXPRIMS=2-8) are documented in standard ray tracing educational materials [S1, unofficial]. Variations such as octrees, BSP trees, and KD trees are well-established in computer graphics literature. The use of surface area heuristics for splitting is based on the principle that the number of rays hitting an object is proportional to its projected area, a concept supported by Crofton’s theorem [S1, unofficial].
