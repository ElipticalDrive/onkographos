# Nishita et al. Ray Casting Method
Also called: Nishita Ray Casting
## Overview
A ray casting technique for rendering participating media that integrates radiance only over segments of a ray that are illuminated by light sources, utilizing precomputed shadow and illumination volumes [S1]. It distinguishes itself from standard ray marching by avoiding integration in shadowed regions and from Max’s original scan-line approach by operating in ray space, while extending support to spotlights with angular distributions beyond parallel light sources [S1].
## How it works
The method computes the radiance along a ray by intersecting it with shadow volumes generated by occluding geometry and illumination volumes generated by light sources. Integration is performed only where these volumes overlap (lit segments) [S1].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Scene geometry (polygons), light sources (parallel or spotlights), participating medium properties (density, phase function).
- Output: Pixel color/intensity.

**Data Structures:**
- Shadow Volumes: Constructed from scene polygons relative to light sources.
- Illumination Volumes: Constructed from light source geometry.
- Ray Data: Origin $\mathbf{o}$, direction $\mathbf{d}$.

**Steps:**
1.  **Volume Construction:** Generate shadow volumes for all occluders relative to each light source. Generate illumination volumes for each light source [S1].
2.  **Ray Intersection:** For each pixel, cast a ray from the camera. Intersect the ray with the shadow and illumination volumes to determine intervals of shadow and light [S1].
3.  **Segment Identification:** Identify "lit segments" where the ray is inside an illumination volume but outside any shadow volume [S1].
4.  **Integration:** For each lit segment, integrate the radiance transfer equation along the ray interval. For a point light source and constant density medium with isotropic scattering, this can be an analytical function of distance [S1]. For parallel light and layered density, numerical integration is used [S1].
5.  **Accumulation:** Sum the contributions from all lit segments.

**Formulas:**
The radiance $L$ along a ray is integrated over the path length $s$ within lit segments $S_{lit}$:
$$ L = \int_{S_{lit}} \sigma_t(s) L_{in}(s) e^{-\int_0^s \sigma_t(s') ds'} ds $$
Where $\sigma_t$ is the extinction coefficient and $L_{in}$ is the incident radiance from the light source [S1].

**Parameters:**
- Light type: Parallel or Spotlight [S1].
- Medium density: Constant or layered [S1].

**Edge Cases:**
- Spotlights with angular distributions require specific volume construction compared to parallel lights [S1].
- Degenerate cases where shadow and illumination boundaries coincide must be handled to avoid double counting or gaps [G].

## Subtopics

### Watt's Backward Beam Tracing
Applies Nishita’s method to consider light/water interaction in a two-pass backward beam tracing method to display underwater shafts of light [S1]. It is chosen when rendering complex light/water interactions in underwater scenes [S1].

### Stam and Fiume Blob Renderer
A method dealing with turbulent fields (i.e. wind) in gaseous phenomena using a front-to-back strategy (a blob renderer) [S1]. It is chosen for rendering gaseous phenomena with turbulent dynamics [S1].

## Operating characteristics
The method avoids integration in shadowed regions, potentially reducing computation compared to full ray marching [S1]. However, it requires the construction of shadow and illumination volumes, which adds preprocessing complexity [S1]. Performance depends on the number of light sources and occluders [S1].
## Background
Originated from Nishita et al. [62], building on Max’s ideas of shadow and illumination volumes but shifting from scan-line to ray casting [S1]. Extended to handle spotlights with angular distributions [S1]. Used in computer graphics for rendering participating media, particularly for effects like shafts of light in fog or water [S1].
