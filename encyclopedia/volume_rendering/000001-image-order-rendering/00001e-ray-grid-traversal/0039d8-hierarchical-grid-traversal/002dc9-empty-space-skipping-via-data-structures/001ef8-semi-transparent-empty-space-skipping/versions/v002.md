# Semi-transparent Empty-Space Skipping

Also called: Analytic Integration with Empty-Space Skipping, Empty-Space Skipping for Semi-transparent Volumes, Semi-transparent Volumetric Rendering with Empty-Space Skipping

## Overview
Semi-transparent Empty-Space Skipping is a rendering approach for transparent or semi-transparent objects with constant density material where the ray continues inside toward the exit point, and the optical contribution of the inner ray segment is evaluated analytically from its length [S1]. It solves the problem of expensive ray marching inside semi-transparent objects by allowing the ray to skip empty regions outside the volume and then analytically compute the contribution of the segment inside the volume based on its length, rather than sampling it discretely [S1]. It sets itself apart from opaque empty-space skipping by using lower bounds for sphere tracing inside objects and from full volumetric rendering by avoiding numerical integration for constant-density materials [S1].

## How it works
The method adapts the BVH-based sphere tracing approach used for opaque volumes to handle transparency [S1]. The core distinction lies in how the ray interacts with the volume boundaries and how the optical depth is computed [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
- **Input**: Ray origin $\mathbf{o}$, ray direction $\mathbf{d}$ (normalized), volume density function $\rho(\mathbf{p})$, transfer function (opacity $\alpha$ per unit length), and a BVH representing the volume's spatial extent [S1].
- **Output**: Final color $C$ and accumulated opacity $\alpha_{acc}$ for the pixel [S1].

### Data Structures
The method relies on a Bounding Volume Hierarchy (BVH) [S1]. For sphere tracing, each node in the BVH must store bounds that allow for lower-bound distance queries [S1].
- **BVH Node**: Contains bounding volume (e.g., AABB or Sphere) and children indices [G].
- **Bounds**: For semi-transparent skipping, the bounds must support querying the *minimum* distance from the ray origin to the volume interior, rather than the maximum distance to the surface [S1].

### Steps
1. **Ray Initialization**: Initialize accumulated opacity $\alpha_{acc} = 0$ and color $C = (0,0,0,0)$ [G]. Normalize $\mathbf{d}$ if not already normalized [G].
2. **Empty-Space Skipping (Outside)**: While the ray is outside the volume:
   - Perform sphere tracing using the BVH [S1].
   - Use **lower bounds** to determine the distance to the nearest point inside the volume [S1].
   - This allows the ray to quickly advance to the entry point [S1].
   - If the ray misses the volume entirely, terminate and return background color [G].
3. **Entry Point Detection**: Identify the entry point $\mathbf{p}_{in}$ where the ray intersects the volume boundary [S1].
4. **Inside Volume Processing**:
   - **Case A: Constant Density ($\rho(\mathbf{p}) = \rho_0$)**:
     - Determine the exit point $\mathbf{p}_{out}$ by continuing sphere tracing inside the volume using lower bounds to find the farthest exit or by solving the intersection analytically if the shape is simple [S1].
     - Calculate the segment length $L = \|\mathbf{p}_{out} - \mathbf{p}_{in}\|$ [S1].
     - Compute the optical depth $\tau = \rho_0 \cdot L$ [S1].
     - Compute the transmittance $T = e^{-\tau}$ [S1].
     - Update accumulated opacity: $\alpha_{acc} = 1 - T$ [S1].
     - Compute color contribution: $C = C_{in} \cdot (1 - T)$, where $C_{in}$ is the color of the volume material [S1].
     - Terminate ray [S1].
   - **Case B: Varying Density ($\rho(\mathbf{p})$ varies)**:
     - Switch to constant-size ray-marching to numerically integrate density when inside of volumes, as full volumetric rendering is required [S1].
     - Numerically integrate density along the segment: $\tau = \int_{\mathbf{p}_{in}}^{\mathbf{p}_{out}} \rho(\mathbf{p}(t)) dt$ [S1].
     - This part is not optimized by the skipping technique and requires full volumetric rendering [S1].
5. **Termination**: If $\alpha_{acc} \ge 1$ or ray length exceeds max distance, terminate [G].

### Formulas
- **Optical Depth (Constant Density)**:
  $$ \tau = \rho_0 \cdot L $$
  Where $\rho_0$ is the constant density and $L$ is the length of the ray segment inside the volume [S1].
- **Transmittance**:
  $$ T = e^{-\tau} $$
- **Accumulated Opacity**:
  $$ \alpha_{acc} = 1 - T $$

### Parameters
- **Density Function**: $\rho(\mathbf{p})$. Can be constant $\rho_0$ or varying (e.g., FBM noise) [S1].
- **Sphere Tracing Step Size**: For varying density, a constant step size $h$ is used for numerical integration. Not given in the sources.
- **BVH Bounds**: Quadratic bounds are recommended for faster convergence away from the entry surface [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
- **Varying Density Performance**: Performance gains from empty-space skipping for varying density depend heavily on the amount of empty space in the scene [S1].
- **Precision**: Quadratic bounds allow the ray to quickly move away from the entry surface, performing significantly better than naive approaches [S1].
- **Exit Point Calculation**: For complex shapes, determining the exact exit point $\mathbf{p}_{out}$ may require additional sphere tracing steps or analytical solutions [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: Depends on the BVH traversal cost and the number of samples inside the volume. For constant density, it is $O(\log N)$ for skipping plus $O(1)$ for analytic integration. For varying density, it is $O(\log N)$ for skipping plus $O(M)$ for ray marching, where $M$ is the number of steps inside the volume [G].
- **Memory Complexity**: $O(N)$ for the BVH, where $N$ is the number of nodes [G].
- **Measured Performance**: On a Clouds scene with constant density, the 'Ours, Impr Quad.' method achieves a 5.7x speedup over naive Sphere tracing (5.6ms vs 31.9ms), reducing average octaves from 1892.0 to 196.3 [S1]. On the same scene with varying density, it achieves a 1.8x speedup over naive Sphere tracing (37.9ms vs 66.5ms), reducing average octaves from 4087.4 to 1675.9 [S1].
- **Strengths**: Significant speedup for constant density volumes; efficient empty-space skipping for varying density volumes [S1].
- **Limitations**: Performance gains for varying density depend on the amount of empty space; requires accurate exit point calculation [S1].
- **When to Use**: Ideal for semi-transparent volumes with constant density or sparse varying density volumes [S1].
- **When Not to Use**: Not suitable for dense varying density volumes with little empty space, as the overhead of BVH traversal may not be justified [S1].

## Background
The method was introduced in "Fast sphere tracing of procedural volumetric noise for very large and detailed scenes" [S1]. It builds on the concept of using BVHs for sphere tracing in opaque volumes and extends it to semi-transparent cases by incorporating analytic integration for constant density materials [S1]. The technique leverages quadratic bounds to improve convergence speed near the entry surface [S1].
