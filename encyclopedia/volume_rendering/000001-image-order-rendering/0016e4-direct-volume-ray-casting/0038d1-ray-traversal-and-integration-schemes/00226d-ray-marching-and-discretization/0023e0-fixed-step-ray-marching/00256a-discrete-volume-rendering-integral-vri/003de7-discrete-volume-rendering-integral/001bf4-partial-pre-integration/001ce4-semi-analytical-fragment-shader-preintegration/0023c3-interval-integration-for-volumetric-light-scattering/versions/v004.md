# Interval Integration for Volumetric Light Scattering

Also called: Analytic Interval Integration, Sum & Difference of Intervals

## Overview
Interval Integration computes in-scattered radiance by decomposing the ray path into intervals defined by the front and back faces of a light volume, allowing analytical evaluation for directional lights and numerical or lookup-based evaluation for point and spot lights [S1, unofficial]. It differs from its parent, Semi-Analytical Fragment Shader Preintegration, by explicitly using the geometric boundaries of the light volume (front/back faces) to determine integration limits, rather than relying solely on local extrema of scalar polynomials within a fragment [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Ray origin and direction.
- Light volume geometry (front and back faces).
- Light type (Directional, Omnidirectional, Spot).
- Medium properties (extinction coefficient $\tau_{ex}$, density $\rho_m$, phase function).
- Shadow map data.

**Outputs:**
- In-scattered radiance $L_S$ for the pixel.

**Data Structures:**
- **Light Volume Mesh:** Defined by front-face geometry $G_f$ and back-face geometry $G_b$.
- **Lookup Texture (Omnidirectional):** Maps $\cos^{-1}(v \cdot l)$ to 2D space.
- **Shadow Map:** Depth buffer for occlusion testing.

**Formulas:**
The in-scattered radiance $L_S$ is computed as the sum of contributions from front-face intervals minus the sum of contributions from back-face intervals [S1, unofficial]:
$$ L_S = \sum_{n \in G_f} L'_S(t_n) - \sum_{m \in G_b} L'_S(t_m) $$
where $L'_S(t)$ is the indefinite integral of the scattering term evaluated at distance $t$ along the ray [S1, unofficial].

For a directional light, assuming constant direction and power (light distance $\gg$ medium depth), the analytic solution is [S1, unofficial]:
$$ L'_S(d) = L(d, l) \rho_m(x_l, \omega_x) \frac{1 - e^{-\tau_{ex} d}}{\tau_{ex}} $$
where $d$ is the distance along the ray, $L$ is the light intensity, $\rho_m$ is the medium density/scattering, and $\tau_{ex}$ is the extinction coefficient [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1. **Determine Light Volume Intersections:**
   Calculate the intersection distances of the view ray with the front faces ($G_f$) and back faces ($G_b$) of the light volume. Let these distances be $t_n$ for $n \in G_f$ and $t_m$ for $m \in G_b$ [S1, unofficial].

2. **Evaluate Integral Based on Light Type:**

   - **Directional Lights:**
     Use the analytic solution $L'_S(d)$ defined above. Evaluate this function in the pixel shader for each interval endpoint $t_n$ and $t_m$ [S1, unofficial].

   - **Omnidirectional Lights:**
     The integral is too complex for an analytic solution.
     1. Create a lookup texture mapping $\cos^{-1}(v \cdot l)$ to 2D space [S1, unofficial].
     2. Evaluate the differential term using the texture.
     3. Numerically integrate using a Compute Shader (CS) [S1, unofficial].

   - **Spot Lights:**
     1. Perform cone intersection to clamp the integration interval to the light cone [S1, unofficial].
     2. If the spot light has variable angular falloff, perform numerical integration using Newton-Cotes methods in the pixel shader [S1, unofficial].
     3. If the spot light has no falloff, treat it as a point light. If it has fixed falloff, use a modified point light lookup texture [S1, unofficial].

3. **Compute Final Radiance:**
   Apply the sum and difference formula:
   $$ L_S = \sum_{n \in G_f} L'_S(t_n) - \sum_{m \in G_b} L'_S(t_m) $$
   [S1, unofficial].

4. **Composite:**
   Additively blend the resolved/filtered output with the scene. Use dual-source blending to attenuate based on medium and depth [S1, unofficial].

**Pitfalls:**
- **Shadow Map Inconsistencies:** If the shadow map does not include both front and back faces of occluders, or if distant occluders are missing, artifacts appear. Ensure shadow map contents are consistent with scene geometry [S1, unofficial].
- **Temporal AA Jitter:** Temporal AA jitter causes flickering in down-sampled buffers because a 1px flicker at full resolution becomes a 4x4px artifact in a 1/4 resolution buffer. Add a separate TAA resolve to down-sampled buffers before compositing [S1, unofficial].
- **High-Frequency Occluders:** Performance drops at low angles due to high pixel coverage. Adjust tessellation factor based on view angle and pre-filter shadow maps [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Depends on light type. Directional lights are $O(1)$ per interval in the pixel shader. Omnidirectional and Spot lights require numerical integration or texture lookups, which are more expensive [S1, unofficial].
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for light volume geometry, shadow maps, and potentially lookup textures for omnidirectional lights [S1, unofficial].
- **Performance:** Fast enough for entire spec range on DirectX 11 hardware. Performance is proportional to total pixel coverage. High-frequency occluders at low angles can cause significant overhead [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Physically-based, flexible, compatible with real-time engines. Analytic solution for directional lights is very efficient [S1, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** Complex for omnidirectional and spot lights. Requires careful handling of shadow maps and temporal filtering to avoid artifacts [S1, unofficial].

## Background
This method was developed by NVIDIA for use in *Fallout 4* [S1, unofficial]. Development began in February 2014, with artists authoring environments in March [S1, unofficial]. It is part of the Gameworks Volumetric Lighting system, which is compatible with physically-based engines and available on DirectX 11 [S1, unofficial]. The approach builds on earlier work such as Hoffman and Preetham (2002) and Sun et al. (2005) [S1, unofficial].
