# Interval Integration for Volumetric Light Scattering

Also called: Analytic Interval Integration, Sum & Difference of Intervals

## Overview
Interval Integration is a fragment shader technique for computing in-scattered radiance by decomposing the integration path into intervals defined by the front and back faces of a light volume [S2, unofficial]. It solves the problem of efficiently evaluating the scattering integral for directional, omnidirectional, and spot lights by leveraging geometric intersections and analytical or numerical solvers tailored to the light type [S2, unofficial]. Unlike its parent method, Semi-Analytical Fragment Shader Preintegration, which relies on adaptive preintegration over extrema of scalar polynomials, Interval Integration explicitly uses the light volume's entry and exit points to form integration bounds, allowing for closed-form solutions for directional lights and lookup-based or numerical integration for complex light sources [S2, unofficial].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Camera ray origin and direction.
*   Light volume geometry (front and back faces) or beam parameters.
*   Light type (Directional, Omnidirectional, Spot).
*   Medium properties: Extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s(x)$, phase function $p(\cdot)$.
*   Incident radiance $L_{in}(\omega')$ from the light source.

**Outputs:**
*   Scattered radiance $L(\omega)$ towards the viewer.

### Data Structures
*   **Light Volume Bounds:** Defined by front face intersection distance $t_n$ and back face intersection distance $t_m$ along the ray [S2, unofficial].
*   **Lookup Texture:** For omnidirectional lights, a 2D texture mapping $\cos^{-1}(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{l})$ to precomputed differential values [S2, unofficial].
*   **Integration Intervals:** A set of intervals $[t_n, t_m]$ derived from the intersection of the viewing ray with the light volume's front and back faces [S2, unofficial].

### Steps
1.  **Ray-Volume Intersection:** Determine the intersection of the viewing ray with the light volume (or beam). For directional lights, this defines the integration interval $[t_n, t_m]$ where $t_n$ is the distance to the front face and $t_m$ is the distance to the back face [S2, unofficial]. For beams, compute the intersection of the beam (not just the bounding volume) with the viewing ray [S1].
2.  **Interval Definition:** Identify the integration bounds. The contribution is computed as the sum over front faces ($G_f$) minus the sum over back faces ($G_b$) [S2, unofficial]:
    $$ L_S = \sum_{n \in G_f} L'_S(t_n) - \sum_{m \in G_b} L'_S(t_m) $$
    where $L'_S(t)$ is the indefinite integral of the scattering term [S2, unofficial].
3.  **Light-Specific Evaluation:**
    *   **Directional Lights:** Evaluate the analytic solution $L'_S(d)$ in the pixel shader. This assumes constant direction and constant power (light distance >> medium depth) [S2, unofficial].
    *   **Omnidirectional Lights:** Create a lookup texture mapping $\cos^{-1}(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{l})$ to 2D space. Evaluate the differential and numerically integrate using a Compute Shader [S2, unofficial].
    *   **Spot Lights:** Perform cone intersection to clamp the integration interval. For variable falloff, numerically integrate using Newton-Cotes methods in the pixel shader [S2, unofficial].
4.  **Radiance Computation:** Compute the incident radiance at the sample on the ray and the scattered fraction thereof towards the camera [S1].

### Formulas
The general integral for scattered radiance is:
$$ L(\omega) = \int e^{-\sigma_t(s_1+s_2)} \sigma_s(x) p(\omega \cdot \omega') L_{in}(\omega') ds $$
where:
*   $L(\omega)$ is the radiance scattered towards the viewer [S1].
*   $\Delta x$ is the interval of the ray-beam intersection [S1].
*   $\sigma_t$ is the extinction coefficient [S1].
*   $\sigma_s(x)$ is the scattering coefficient at point $x$ [S1].
*   $p(\cdot)$ is the phase function [S1].
*   $s_1 + s_2$ is the length of the light's path from the caustic triangle to the camera [S1].

For directional lights, assuming constant direction and power, the integral simplifies to an analytic function [S2, unofficial]:
$$ L'_S(d) = \frac{1 - e^{-\tau_{ex} d}}{\tau_{ex}} L(d, \mathbf{l}) \rho_m(x_l, \omega_x) $$
where $\tau_{ex}$ is the optical depth [S2, unofficial].

For narrow beams, a single sample approximation can be used [S1]:
$$ L(\omega) \approx \Delta x e^{-\sigma_t(s_1+s_2)} \sigma_s p(\omega \cdot \omega') L_{in}(\omega') $$
where $\Delta x$ is obtained by a cheap 2D ray-triangle edge intersection [S1].

### Parameters
*   $\sigma_t$: Extinction coefficient [S1].
*   $\sigma_s(x)$: Scattering coefficient at point $x$ [S1].
*   $p(\cdot)$: Phase function [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Shadow Map Inconsistencies:** Shadow map contents must be consistent with scene geometry. Render both front and back faces if needed, and include distant occluders [S2, unofficial].
*   **Temporal AA Jitter:** Temporal AA jitter causes flickering in down-sampled buffers. Add a separate TAA resolve to down-sampled buffers before compositing [S2, unofficial].
*   **High-Frequency Occluders:** Performance drops at low angles due to high pixel coverage. Adjust tessellation factor based on view angle and pre-filter shadow maps [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Directional Light Interval Integration
*   **What it is:** A specialized case of interval integration where the light source is at infinity, allowing for an analytic solution.
*   **What sets it apart:** It uses a closed-form formula evaluated in the pixel shader, avoiding numerical integration or lookups.
*   **When to choose it:** Use for sun or moon lighting where the light direction is constant and the light distance is much larger than the medium depth.
*   **Link:** [Directional Light Interval Integration](#)

### Omnidirectional Light Interval Integration
*   **What it is:** A case where the light source is a point, requiring a lookup texture and numerical integration.
*   **What sets it apart:** It uses a precomputed lookup texture mapping $\cos^{-1}(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{l})$ to handle the complexity of the angular dependence.
*   **When to choose it:** Use for point lights or lamps where the light direction varies significantly across the medium.
*   **Link:** [Omnidirectional Light Interval Integration](#)

### Spot Light Interval Integration
*   **What it is:** A case where the light source is a cone, requiring cone intersection and numerical integration.
*   **What sets it apart:** It involves clamping the integration interval to the spot cone and using Newton-Cotes methods for variable falloff.
*   **When to choose it:** Use for spotlights with complex falloff profiles.
*   **Link:** [Spot Light Interval Integration](#)

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** Depends on the light type. Directional lights are $O(1)$ per fragment due to the analytic solution. Omnidirectional and spot lights require numerical integration or texture lookups, which are more expensive but still constant per fragment.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the light volume geometry and lookup textures for omnidirectional and spot lights.
*   **Measured Performance:** The method is fast enough for real-time rendering on modern hardware. For example, rendering 90 beams took 4.2 ms in one implementation [S1].
*   **Scaling:** Performance scales with the number of fragments rasterized and the complexity of the light volume. High-frequency occluders can cause performance drops at low angles [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Efficient for directional lights; flexible for other light types; compatible with physically-based engines.
*   **Limitations:** Requires consistent shadow maps; sensitive to temporal AA jitter; performance can drop with high-frequency occluders.
*   **When to use it:** Use for real-time volumetric light scattering in games and interactive applications.
*   **When not:** Avoid for offline rendering where higher accuracy is required, or for scenes with extremely complex light volumes that exceed memory limits.

*   **Usage:** It is used in real-time rendering of volumetric light scattering in games, particularly for simulating light beams in foggy or dusty environments.
