# Hybrid Ray Tracing

Also called: Hybrid Ray Tracer, Hybrid Ray Tracing for Geometric/Volumetric Mixing, Mixing Polygons and Volumes

## Overview
Hybrid Ray Tracing is a method for mixing geometric and volumetric data by casting rays through the ensemble of both, drawing samples of each, and compositing them in depth-sorted order [S1, S2, unofficial]. It solves the problem of rendering scenes containing both scalar fields (volumes) and polygonal surfaces simultaneously, ensuring correct visibility where polygons obscure volume and vice versa [S1, S2, unofficial]. Unlike [Texture-Based Volume Rendering](4707-texture-based-volume-rendering.md) which rasterizes proxy geometry, or [Mixed Geometry Rendering](3442-mixed-geometry-rendering.md) which may use hardware acceleration for static views, Hybrid Ray Tracing performs a unified ray-casting pass that explicitly handles the interaction between discrete polygonal intersections and continuous volumetric attenuation [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** An $N \times N \times N$ voxel array of scalar values, a set of embedded polygons, a camera position, and a transfer function mapping scalar values to color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:** A final image where each pixel contains the composite color of the ray.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Volumetric Data:** A 3D texture or array storing pre-shaded color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$ for each voxel [S2, unofficial].
*   **Geometric Data:** A list of polygons (triangles/quads) with associated surface color $c_p$ and opacity $o_p$ [S2, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Volumetric Pre-shading:** Shade and classify the volumetric data to yield a color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$ for each voxel [S2, unofficial].
2.  **Ray Casting:** Trace parallel viewing rays from the observer position into the data [S2, unofficial].
3.  **Volumetric Sampling:** For each ray, compute a vector of colors $c_v$ and opacities $o_v$ by resampling the volumetric data at equally spaced positions along the ray. The color and opacity at each sample point are computed by tri-linearly interpolating from the eight voxels surrounding the sample location [S2, unofficial].
    *   The spacing between samples along the ray equals the slab thickness.
    *   The spacing between adjacent rays equals the slab width [S2, unofficial].
4.  **Geometric Intersection:** Independently compute all intersections between the ray and polygons in the environment. Shade each intersection to yield a color $c_p$ and opacity $o_p$ [S2, unofficial].
5.  **Slab Decomposition:** Treat the volume as a series of slabs, where the thickness of each slab is equal to the spacing between samples along the viewing ray [S2, unofficial].
6.  **Polygon-Volume Interaction (Sliver Calculation):** When a ray intersects a polygon within a slab, treat the polygon locally as a plane perpendicular to the ray at the intersection point [S2, unofficial]. Compute the opacities for the slivers of volumetric gel immediately in front of and behind this plane:
    *   Let $o_v$ be the opacity of the entire slab.
    *   Let $t_v$ be the total thickness of the slab.
    *   Let $t_f$ be the thickness of the front portion and $t_b$ be the thickness of the back portion.
    *   The opacity of the front sliver $o_f$ is:
        $$ o_f = 1 - (1 - o_v)^{t_f/t_v} $$
    *   The opacity of the back sliver $o_b$ is:
        $$ o_b = 1 - (1 - o_v)^{t_b/t_v} $$
    *   Note: The opacity of a slab is related to density $d_v$ and thickness $t_v$ by $o_v = 1 - e^{-d_v t_v}$ [S2, unofficial].
7.  **Depth-Sorted Compositing:** Composite the resampled volumetric colors and opacities with the polygonal colors and opacities in depth-sorted order [S2, unofficial].
    *   If a ray intersects more than one polygon within a slab, compute and composite the contribution of each polygon and each sliver of volumetric gel separately [S2, unofficial].
    *   For a single polygon intersection in a slab, working from front to back:
        1.  Composite the front volumetric sliver ($c_v, o_f$).
        2.  Composite the polygon ($c_p, o_p$).
        3.  Composite the back volumetric sliver ($c_v, o_b$).
    *   Standard front-to-back compositing (Over operator) is used:
        $$ C_{out} = C_{in} + C_{src}(1 - \alpha_{in}) $$
        $$ \alpha_{out} = \alpha_{in} + \alpha_{src}(1 - \alpha_{in}) $$
8.  **Adaptive Supersampling:** To avoid aliasing at polygonal edges, intersections, and shading highlights, increase the number of rays cast per pixel in these regions [S2, unofficial].
    *   Compute two additional colors during the initial ray trace:
        *   $C_x$: Contributions only from polygons (set $o_v=0$ for all volumetric samples).
        *   $C_q$: Contributions only from polygons, attenuated by passage through volumetric data (set $o_v=0$ for all volumetric samples, but apply volumetric attenuation to the polygon contribution) [S2, unofficial].
    *   Let $\theta$ be a threshold.
    *   If range($C_x$) > $\theta$ and range($C_q$) $\le \theta$: The difference is due to volumetric data; do not supersample [S2, unofficial].
    *   If range($C_q$) > $\theta$ and range($C_x$) $\le \theta$: The geometric event is hidden by opaque volumetric data; do not supersample [S2, unofficial].
    *   If range($C_x$) > $\theta$ and range($C_q$) > $\theta$: The region contains a visible geometric event; cast more rays (supersample) [S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Visibility Errors:** Volumetric samples lying immediately in front of and behind polygons require special treatment (sliver calculation) to avoid errors in visibility [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Aliasing:** Polygonal edges, polygon-polygon intersections, and shading highlights cause aliasing. Adaptive supersampling is required [S2, unofficial].
*   **Supersampling Decision:** Distinguishing between color differences caused by volumetric data versus geometric data is critical to avoid unnecessary supersampling [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Hybrid Ray Tracing with Volume Data
A rendering method that combines shaded polygons with volumetric data by treating surfaces as infinitesimally thin solids, using a binary voxel representation for the geometry, and rendering the composite dataset using volume rendering techniques to avoid aliasing artifacts [S1, unofficial]. Unlike the primary hybrid ray tracer which casts rays through both, this variant converts polygons into a sampled volume via 3D scan-conversion [S1, unofficial].
*   **Steps:** 1. Treat surfaces as infinitesimally thin solids. 2. Use efficient 3D scan-conversion algorithms (e.g., Kaufman 1987b) to create a binary voxel representation. 3. Bandlimit the object to the Nyquist frequency in all three dimensions. 4. Sample the object to limit quantization losses. 5. Shade the polygons. 6. Filter the sampled geometry. 7. Combine the filtered geometry with volumetric data. 8. Render the composite dataset using volume rendering techniques [S1, unofficial].
*   **Parameters:** The object must be bandlimited to the Nyquist frequency in all three dimensions prior to sampling to avoid aliasing artifacts [S1, unofficial].
*   **Pitfalls:** Binary voxel representations result in aliasing. This is handled by bandlimiting the object to the Nyquist frequency before sampling [S1, unofficial].
*   **Comparison:** Unlike supersampling, this approach does not require supersampling if polygons are sufficiently bandlimited prior to sampling [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method requires intersecting rays with all polygons and sampling the volume along the ray. The complexity is $O(R \cdot (N + P))$ where $R$ is the number of rays, $N$ is the number of volume samples per ray, and $P$ is the number of polygons. Adaptive supersampling increases $R$ in complex regions [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volumetric data ($O(N^3)$) and the polygonal data ($O(P)$) [S2, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** The method is computationally expensive due to the need for ray-polygon intersection tests and adaptive supersampling [S2, unofficial]. It is slower than pure rasterization methods but provides higher accuracy for mixed scenes [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Handles complex interactions between geometry and volume correctly; avoids aliasing through adaptive supersampling or bandlimiting [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally intensive; difficult to accelerate with standard rasterization hardware without significant modification [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality visualization of mixed geometric and volumetric data is required, and performance is not the primary constraint [S2, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** For real-time applications with large polygon counts or very high-resolution volumes, where rasterization-based proxy methods are preferred [S2, unofficial].

## Background
Hybrid Ray Tracing was proposed by Levoy in 1990 as a method to extend volume rendering algorithms to handle polygonally defined objects [S2, unofficial]. The concept was also explored by Kajiya (1984), Levoy (1988a), and Upson (1988) [S1, unofficial]. The method addresses the limitations of earlier approaches that either converted polygons to volumes (losing geometric precision) or rendered them separately (losing correct occlusion) [S1, S2, unofficial]. It is particularly relevant in medical applications such as virtual endoscopy, where synthetic instruments must be visualized within volumetric anatomical data [S3, unofficial].
