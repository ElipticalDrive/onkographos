# Mixed Geometry Rendering

Also called: Combining geometric objects with volumetric data, Direct Volume Rendering with Z-Buffer Geometry, Intermixing Volumes and Geometry, Wireframe and Surface Combination

## Overview
Mixed geometry rendering integrates explicit geometric primitives (isosurfaces, bounding boxes, wireframes) with procedural volumetric data in a single image [S2, book]. It solves the problem of visualizing the spatial relationship between discrete objects and continuous scalar fields, such as showing a bounding box around a CT scan or a wireframe grid over a fluid density field [S2, book]. Unlike pure volume ray casting, which processes every pixel independently without explicit surface primitives, this method leverages hardware depth buffers or explicit sorting to manage occlusion between surfaces and volumes [S2, book]. Unlike pure surface rendering, it handles semi-transparent volumetric media that transmit light from underlying geometry [S1].

## How it works
The method combines two primary rendering pipelines: geometric rendering (using standard graphics hardware) and volume rendering (using ray casting or splatting). The core challenge is managing the depth ordering between opaque/transparent surfaces and the volumetric samples.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   A set of geometric primitives (triangles, lines, polygons) [S2, book].
    *   A volumetric dataset (scalar field) [S1].
    *   Transfer functions mapping scalar values to color and opacity [S1].
    *   Camera parameters (view matrix, projection matrix) [S2, book].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   A 2D image where geometry and volume are correctly composited [S2, book].

### Data Structures
1.  **Geometry Buffer:** Standard triangle/line list for geometric primitives [S2, book].
2.  **Depth Buffer (Z-Buffer):** Hardware buffer storing the depth ($z$) of the closest opaque geometric surface for each pixel [S2, book].
3.  **Volume Data:** Array of scalar values, potentially organized in cells (e.g., tetrahedra) or regular grids [S1].
4.  **Gradient Field:** Pre-computed or on-the-fly gradient vectors $\nabla f$ at voxel centers or nodes, used for lighting [S1].

### Numbered Steps

#### Phase 1: Geometric Rendering
1.  **Render Opaque Geometry:** Render all opaque geometric primitives (isosurfaces, bounding boxes) into the frame buffer and depth buffer using standard rasterization [S2, book].
2.  **Store Depth:** The hardware depth buffer now contains the distance $z_{geo}$ to the nearest opaque surface for each pixel [S2, book].

#### Phase 2: Volume Rendering (Software Ray Casting)
*Note: This section describes the software ray casting approach detailed in [S1] and [S2, book]. Hardware texture mapping approaches follow a simpler blending order [S2, book].*

3.  **Initialize Ray:** For each pixel $(x, y)$, construct a view ray starting at the camera origin and passing through the pixel center [S1].
4.  **Bound Ray Segment:** Retrieve the depth value $z_{geo}$ from the depth buffer for pixel $(x, y)$ [S2, book].
    *   Convert $z_{geo}$ into a distance $t_{geo}$ along the view ray [S2, book].
    *   The volume rendering ray segment is bounded: it starts at the near plane and ends at $t_{geo}$ [S2, book]. If $z_{geo}$ indicates no geometry, the ray extends through the entire volume [S2, book].
5.  **Sample Volume:** Step along the bounded ray segment through the volumetric data [S1].
6.  **Compute Gradient (Lighting):** At each sample point, compute the gradient $\nabla f$ of the scalar field [S1].
    *   The gradient is computed using differences of values and coordinates between three pairs of points in the neighborhood [S1].
    *   The X component of the gradient is calculated as the ratio of two determinants [S1]:
        $$
        \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{
        \begin{vmatrix}
        A_{f0} & A_{y0} & A_{z0} \\
        A_{f1} & A_{y1} & A_{z1} \\
        A_{f2} & A_{y2} & A_{z2}
        \end{vmatrix}
        }{
        \begin{vmatrix}
        A_{x0} & A_{y0} & A_{z0} \\
        A_{x1} & A_{y1} & A_{z1} \\
        A_{x2} & A_{y2} & A_{z2}
        \end{vmatrix}
        }
        $$
        Where $A$ represents the differences in coordinates and values between the reference point and three neighboring points [S1].
    *   The Y and Z components are computed similarly by substituting the value differences into the second and third columns of the numerator determinant, respectively [S1].
    *   Use $\nabla f$ as the normal vector in a simple lighting equation (e.g., diffuse shading) to determine the color contribution of the volume [S1].
7.  **Map Scalar to Color/Opacity:** Convert the scalar value $f$ at the sample point to color $C$ and opacity $\alpha$ using transfer functions [S1].
8.  **Integrate (Compositing):** Accumulate the color and opacity along the ray using the standard volume rendering integral [S1]:
    $$
    C_{vol} = \sum_{i} c_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)
    $$
    Where $c_i$ and $\alpha_i$ are the color and opacity of the $i$-th sample [S1].
9.  **Composite with Geometry:** Blend the accumulated volume color $C_{vol}$ with the color $C_{geo}$ stored in the frame buffer from Phase 1 [S2, book].
    *   The final pixel color $C_{final}$ is determined by multiplying the stored geometric color by the transparency of the volume and adding the volume's contribution [S1]:
        $$
        C_{final} = C_{geo} \cdot (1 - \alpha_{total}) + C_{vol}
        $$
        Where $\alpha_{total}$ is the integral of opacity along the ray [S1]. This effectively places the volume *in front* of the geometry if the geometry was rendered first and the volume is treated as a transparent layer overlaying it [S1].

#### Phase 3: Alternative Hardware Texture Mapping Approach
*If using hardware texture mapping for volume rendering [S2, book]:*
1.  Render all opaque geometry first [S2, book].
2.  Sort semi-transparent geometry and texture-mapped polygons back-to-front [S2, book].
3.  Blend semi-transparent texture-mapped polygons (representing the volume) into the image in back-to-front order [S2, book].
4.  If semi-transparent geometry is present, sort it with the volume polygons before rendering [S2, book].

#### Phase 4: Alternative Object-Order Splatting Approach
*If using object-order splatting [S2, book]:*
1.  Render opaque geometry and capture results in the hardware depth buffer [S2, book].
2.  For each volume sample (splat):
    *   Project the sample's depth $z_{sample}$ to screen space [S2, book].
    *   Compare $z_{sample}$ to the value in the depth buffer at each pixel within the sample's image extent [S2, book].
    *   Accumulate the sample's contribution only if $z_{sample}$ is in front of the geometry for that pixel [S2, book].
3.  Blend the accumulated volume image over the geometric image [S2, book].

### Parameters
*   **Resolution:** Typical performance tests use a 512x512 pixel window [S1].
*   **Lighting Model:** Simple diffuse lighting equation using the gradient as normal [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Wireframe Occlusion:** Wireframe geometry completely occludes volume data behind it, although volume data in front of the wireframe correctly transmits colors from the geometry [S1]. This can lead to visual artifacts if the wireframe is dense [S1].
*   **Sorting Complexity:** If semi-transparent geometry is included, polygons may need to be split to obtain a correct sorted order for blending [S2, book].
*   **Memory Usage:** For cell-organized data (e.g., tetrahedral meshes), memory usage can be high. A model with 381,548 tetrahedral cells required over 50 Megabytes [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Hybrid Scanline A-Buffer Volume Rendering](3616-hybrid-scanline-a-buffer-volume-rendering.md)
Hybrid Scanline A-Buffer Volume Rendering is a method for rendering scenes containing both surface-defined objects and procedural volumetric gases by combining scanline-based surface rendering with volume rendering. It solves the integration problem of mixed geometry by using a fast scanline a-buffer algorithm for surfaces and volume rendering for gases, merging them via Z-sorted a-buffer fragments. Unlike pure volume ray casting which processes every pixel independently, this method leverages spatial coherence within scanlines to manage fragment lists, and unlike pure surface rendering, it handles semi-transparent volumetric media.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Volume rendering is computationally intensive. If $n$ is the size of the volume on all three dimensions and every voxel is visited, the complexity is $O(n^3)$ [S2, book].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   On a Stardent ST1000 (4 processors, 5 Mips each, no vector unit used):
        *   Bearing Housing (16,896 tetrahedral cells, 512x512 window): 149.2 seconds [S1].
        *   CFD Analysis (381,548 tetrahedral cells, 512x512 window): 591.6 seconds [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** High for cell-organized data. The CFD model required over 50 Megabytes [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Allows clear visualization of spatial relationships between discrete objects and continuous fields [S2, book].
    *   Hardware acceleration is available for the geometric part [S2, book].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Software volume rendering is slow for large datasets [S2, book].
    *   Wireframe geometry can cause severe occlusion artifacts [S1].
    *   Sorting semi-transparent geometry and volume polygons can be complex [S2, book].
*   **When to Use:** When it is necessary to show bounding boxes, cut planes, or isosurfaces in conjunction with volumetric data to aid viewer understanding [S2, book].
*   **When Not to Use:** When interactive rates are required for very large volumes without hardware acceleration, or when the geometric primitives are not essential for interpretation.

## Background
Mixed geometry rendering techniques were developed to address the limitations of pure volume rendering, which often lacks explicit structural context. Early work by Kajiya and Von Herzen (1984) established raytracing volume densities [S1]. Upson and Keeler (1988) introduced the V-Buffer for visible volume rendering [S1]. The specific technique of combining wireframe geometry with volume rendering using gradient-based lighting was demonstrated in the late 1980s and early 1990s, with performance results published in 1989-1990 [S1]. The VTK book (Chapter 7) provides a comprehensive overview of these hybrid approaches, distinguishing between hardware texture mapping and software ray casting/splatting methods [S2, book].
