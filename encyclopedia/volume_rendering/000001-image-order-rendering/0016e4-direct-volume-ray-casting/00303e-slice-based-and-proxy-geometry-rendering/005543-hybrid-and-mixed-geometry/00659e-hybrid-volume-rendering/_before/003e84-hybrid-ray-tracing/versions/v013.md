# Hybrid Ray Tracing

Also called: Hybrid Ray Tracer, Hybrid Ray Tracing for Geometric/Volumetric Mixing, Mixing Polygons and Volumes

## Overview
Hybrid Ray Tracing is a method for mixing geometric and volumetric data by casting rays through the ensemble of both, drawing samples of each, and compositing them in depth-sorted order [S1, S2, unofficial]. It solves the problem of rendering scenes containing both polygonal surfaces and volumetric fields (such as CT/MR data) simultaneously. It sets itself apart from [Mixed Geometry Rendering](3442-mixed-geometry-rendering.md) by explicitly handling the interaction between polygonal occlusion and volumetric attenuation within a single ray-casting pass, rather than relying solely on scan-conversion or separate rendering passes. It differs from [Hybrid Volume Rendering](15456-hybrid-volume-rendering.md) by maintaining the geometric primitives as polygons during the intersection phase, rather than converting them to voxel data.

## How it works
The method consists of two primary approaches: the Hybrid Ray Tracer (simultaneous ray casting) and 3D Scan-Conversion. The Hybrid Ray Tracer is the core method described below.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   A volumetric dataset: An $N \times N \times N$ voxel array of scalar values, pre-classified into color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$ for each voxel [S2, unofficial].
    *   A geometric dataset: A set of embedded polygons with shading information yielding color $c_p$ and opacity $o_p$ at intersection points [S2, unofficial].
    *   Camera parameters: Observer position and viewing direction.
*   **Outputs:**
    *   A final image where each pixel contains a color resulting from the depth-sorted compositing of volumetric slabs and polygonal intersections [S2, unofficial].

### Data Structures
1.  **Volumetric Grid:** A 3D array storing color ($c_v$) and opacity ($o_v$) per voxel. Tri-linear interpolation is used to sample values between voxels [S2, unofficial].
2.  **Polygon Database:** A list of polygons with vertices and normals, supporting ray-intersection queries [S2, unofficial].
3.  **Ray Buffer:** For each pixel, stores the accumulated color and opacity during front-to-back compositing.
4.  **Supersampling Buffers:** Two auxiliary color buffers ($C_x$ and $C_q$) used to determine if adaptive supersampling is required [S2, unofficial].

### Parameters
*   **Slab Thickness:** Equal to the spacing between samples along a viewing ray. Typically set equal to the voxel spacing [S2, unofficial].
*   **Slab Width:** Equal to the spacing between adjacent rays. Typically set equal to the voxel spacing [S2, unofficial].
*   **Supersampling Threshold ($\theta$):** A threshold value for the range of color differences to trigger supersampling [S2, unofficial].

### Algorithm: Hybrid Ray Tracer
The following steps describe the simultaneous ray casting method [S2, unofficial].

1.  **Preprocessing:** Shade and classify the volumetric data to yield $c_v$ and $o_v$ for each voxel [S2, unofficial].
2.  **Ray Casting:** For each pixel, cast a parallel viewing ray from the observer position into the data [S2, unofficial].
3.  **Volumetric Sampling:** Compute a vector of colors $c_v$ and opacities $o_v$ by resampling the volumetric data at equally spaced positions along the ray. Use tri-linear interpolation from the eight surrounding voxels [S2, unofficial].
4.  **Geometric Intersection:** Independently compute all intersections between the ray and polygons in the environment. Shade them to yield $c_p$ and $o_p$ for each intersection point [S2, unofficial].
5.  **Slab Decomposition:** Treat the volumetric data as a series of slabs. The thickness of each slab ($t_v$) is the sample spacing. If a ray intersects a polygon within a slab, treat the polygon locally as a plane perpendicular to the ray at the intersection point [S2, unofficial].
6.  **Sliver Opacity Calculation:** Calculate the opacity of the volumetric slivers immediately in front of and behind the polygon plane using exponential attenuation [S2, unofficial]:
    *   Total slab opacity: $o_v = 1 - e^{-d_v t_v}$, where $d_v$ is density and $t_v$ is total slab thickness [S2, unofficial].
    *   Front sliver opacity ($o_f$): $o_f = 1 - (1 - o_v)^{t_f/t_v}$, where $t_f$ is the thickness of the front portion [S2, unofficial].
    *   Back sliver opacity ($o_b$): $o_b = 1 - (1 - o_v)^{t_b/t_v}$, where $t_b$ is the thickness of the back portion [S2, unofficial].
7.  **Compositing:** Composite the contributions in depth-sorted order from front to back [S2, unofficial]:
    *   Composite the front volumetric sliver ($c_v, o_f$).
    *   Composite the polygon ($c_p, o_p$).
    *   Composite the back volumetric sliver ($c_v, o_b$).
    *   If multiple polygons intersect a slab, compute and composite each polygon and its associated slivers separately [S2, unofficial].
8.  **Adaptive Supersampling:** To avoid aliasing at polygon edges, intersections, and highlights, employ adaptive supersampling [S2, unofficial]:
    *   Compute two additional colors during the initial ray trace:
        *   $C_x$: Contributions from polygons only (set $o_v=0$ for all volumetric samples).
        *   $C_q$: Contributions from polygons only, attenuated by volumetric data (set $o_v=0$ for all volumetric samples, but apply volumetric attenuation to the polygon color) [S2, unofficial].
    *   Determine the range of $C_x$ and $C_q$ within the sample region.
    *   If range($C_x$) > $\theta$ and range($C_q$) $\le \theta$: Color difference is due to volumetric data. Do not supersample [S2, unofficial].
    *   If range($C_q$) > $\theta$ and range($C_x$) $\le \theta$: Geometric event is hidden by opaque volumetric data. Do not supersample [S2, unofficial].
    *   If range($C_x$) > $\theta$ and range($C_q$) > $\theta$: Visible geometric event. Cast more rays (supersample) [S2, unofficial].

### Algorithm: 3D Scan-Conversion Variant
An alternative method converts geometry to volume [S1, S2, unofficial]:
1.  Treat surfaces as infinitesimally thin solids [S1, unofficial].
2.  Use 3D scan-conversion algorithms (e.g., Kaufman 1987b) to create a binary voxel representation of the polygons [S1, unofficial].
3.  Bandlimit the object to the Nyquist frequency in all three dimensions prior to sampling to avoid aliasing [S1, unofficial].
4.  Sample the object to limit quantization losses [S1, unofficial].
5.  Shade the polygons [S1, unofficial].
6.  Filter the sampled geometry [S1, unofficial].
7.  Combine the filtered geometry with the volumetric data [S1, unofficial].
8.  Render the composite dataset using standard volume rendering techniques [S1, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Visibility Errors:** Volumetric samples lying immediately in front of and behind polygons require special treatment (sliver decomposition) to avoid errors in visibility [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Aliasing:** Binary voxel representations result in aliasing. This is handled by bandlimiting the object to the Nyquist frequency before sampling in the scan-conversion variant [S1, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Rendering all polygons even if obscured by volumetric data can be expensive [S2, unofficial]. Supersampling adds significant cost but is necessary for high-quality edges [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Hybrid Ray Tracing with Volume Data
A rendering method that combines shaded polygons with volumetric data by treating surfaces as infinitesimally thin solids, using a binary voxel representation for the geometry, and rendering the composite dataset using volume rendering techniques to avoid aliasing artifacts [S1, unofficial]. It differs from the primary Hybrid Ray Tracer by converting polygons to voxels first, thus avoiding the need for adaptive supersampling if polygons are sufficiently bandlimited prior to sampling [S1, unofficial]. Choose this method when hardware acceleration for volume rendering is available and polygonal aliasing is a primary concern, but computational cost of supersampling is prohibitive [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of voxels sampled per ray and the number of polygon intersections. Adaptive supersampling increases complexity in regions with geometric edges [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Proportional to the size of the volumetric grid and the polygon database [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Handles complex mixtures of geometry and volume accurately; avoids the need to convert geometry to voxels in the primary method; handles transparency and opacity correctly [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to ray-polygon intersections and potential supersampling; requires careful handling of sliver opacities to avoid artifacts [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality visualization of mixed geometric/volumetric data is required, and performance allows for ray-tracing-based compositing [S2, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When real-time performance is critical and the scene is dominated by opaque volumetric data obscuring geometry, or when hardware does not support efficient ray-triangle intersection [S2, unofficial].

## Background
The concept of a hybrid ray tracer handling both scalar fields and polygons was proposed by multiple researchers including Kajiya (1984), Levoy (1988a), Dellaert (1988), and Upson (1988) [S1, unofficial]. However, no implementation was reported until Levoy (1990b) presented two methods: the hybrid ray tracer and 3D scan-conversion [S1, S2, unofficial]. The method is widely used in medical applications such as virtual endoscopy, where CT/MR images are combined with synthetic objects like surgical instruments or anatomical surface models [S3, unofficial].
