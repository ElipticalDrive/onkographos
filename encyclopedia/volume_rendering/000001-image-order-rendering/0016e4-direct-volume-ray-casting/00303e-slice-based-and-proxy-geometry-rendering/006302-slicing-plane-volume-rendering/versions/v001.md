# Slicing Plane Volume Rendering
Also called: slicing plane framework, three-pass volume rendering

## Overview
Slicing Plane Volume Rendering is an image-order direct volume ray casting method that decomposes a 3D density volume into a series of view-aligned 2D planes [S1]. It solves the problem of efficient hardware-accelerated volume visualization by rasterizing these planes front-to-back and blending the results, allowing complex lighting models (such as single and multiple scattering) to be applied per fragment [S1]. It differs from standard texture-based volume rendering by explicitly generating geometric slice primitives rather than relying solely on ray-marching through a 3D texture, and it differs from proxy geometry methods by using planes that are strictly aligned to the camera view direction for every frame [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D density volume (stored as a 3D texture map) [S1].
*   **Output:** A 2D image with accumulated lighting contributions [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **3D Texture:** Stores the scalar density values of the volume [S1].
*   **Slice Geometry:** A series of planar polygons (quads or triangles) intersecting the volume bounding box [G].
*   **Precomputed Data:** The distance from each sample point to the light source (`l_light`) is precomputed and stored for all sample points in the volume [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Slice Spacing:** Planes are equally spaced along the view direction [S1].
*   **Scattering Phase Function:** The Henyey-Greenstein phase function is used, defined by the mean cosine of the scattering angle $g$ [S1].
*   **Blending:** Front-to-back alpha blending is used to accumulate results [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **View-Aligned Slice Generation:** For each frame, generate a series of planes equally spaced along the view direction that intersect the volume [S1].
2.  **Texture Coordinate Assignment:** Assign 3D texture coordinates to the vertices of each plane corresponding to the density volume [S1].
3.  **Front-to-Back Rendering:** Render the planes in front-to-back order relative to the camera [S1].
4.  **Fragment Processing (Per Slice):** For each fragment inside the medium volume on a slice, execute the following lighting passes [S1]:
    *   **Pass 1 (Preliminary):** Compute `l_eye` (distance from fragment to eye) and `s_eye` (distance from light to eye path) [S1]. Determine the blur width $w$ based on `l_eye` and `s_eye` [S1].
    *   **Pass 2 (Single Scattering):** Compute the direct lighting contribution $L_{direct}$ [S1].
    *   **Pass 3 (Multiple Scattering):** Compute the indirect lighting contribution $L_{indirect}$ [S1].
5.  **Accumulation:** Add the contributions to the total accumulated lighting $L_{total}$ [S1]:
    $$ L_{total} = L_{total} + L_{direct} + L_{indirect} $$

**Formulas:**
*   **Henyey-Greenstein Phase Function:**
    $$ P(\theta) = \frac{1 - g^2}{4\pi(1 + g^2 - 2g \cos \theta)^{3/2}} $$
    where $g$ is the mean cosine of the scattering angle [S1].
*   **Direct Lighting ($L_{direct}$):**
    $$ L_{direct} = L \cdot s_m \cdot P \cdot atten \cdot \Delta l $$
    where $L$ is light intensity, $s_m$ is material scattering coefficient, $P$ is the phase function, $atten$ is attenuation, and $\Delta l$ is the slice thickness [S1].
*   **Attenuation:**
    $$ atten = \exp(-(c/b) \cdot l) $$
    where $l$ is the path length [S1].
*   **Multiple Scattering ($L_{indirect}$):**
    $$ L_{indirect} = L_{blurred} \cdot 2\langle h_b \theta \rangle_w \cdot P_{MS} \cdot atten \cdot \Delta l $$
    where $L_{blurred}$ is the blurred light image, $P_{MS}$ is the multiple scattering phase function, and $atten = \exp(-\gamma (c/b) \cdot l)^2$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
# Precomputed data available in volume texture or auxiliary buffers
# l_light[x, y, z] is precomputed for all sample points

def render_slices(volume_3d_texture, light_source, camera):
    # 1. Generate view-aligned planes
    slices = generate_view_aligned_planes(volume_3d_texture.bounds, camera.view_direction, spacing)
    
    # 2. Sort slices front-to-back relative to camera
    slices.sort_by_depth(camera.position)
    
    # Initialize total accumulated lighting
    L_total = 0
    
    for slice in slices:
        # 3. Assign texture coordinates to slice vertices
        assign_texture_coordinates(slice, volume_3d_texture)
        
        # 4. Rasterize slice and execute fragment program for each fragment
        for fragment in slice.fragments:
            # Get 3D position from texture coordinates
            pos = fragment.world_position
            
            # Pass 1: Preliminary computation
            l_eye = distance(fragment.position, camera.position)
            s_eye = distance(light_source.position, fragment.position) # Simplified; actual s_eye depends on light path geometry
            
            # Compute blur width w based on l_eye and s_eye
            w = compute_blur_width(l_eye, s_eye)
            
            # Sample blurred light image
            L_blurred = sample_light_image(fragment.uv, w)
            
            # Pass 2: Single Scattering
            # Compute angle theta between view direction and light direction
            view_dir = normalize(camera.position - fragment.position)
            light_dir = normalize(light_source.position - fragment.position)
            cos_theta = dot(view_dir, light_dir)
            
            # Henyey-Greenstein phase function
            P = heney_greenstein_phase(cos_theta, g)
            
            # Attenuation along eye path
            # Note: l_light is precomputed, but attenuation formula uses l (path length)
            # In the context of the slice, l is approximated by l_eye or slice thickness
            atten = exp(-(c/b) * l_eye)
            
            L_direct = compute_direct_lighting(L, s_m, P, atten, slice_thickness)
            
            # Pass 3: Multiple Scattering
            # Compute multiple scattering phase function P_MS
            P_MS = multiple_scattering_phase(cos_theta, g)
            
            # Attenuation for multiple scattering (squared term in source)
            atten_ms = exp(-gamma * (c/b) * l_eye) ** 2
            
            # Compute indirect contribution
            # 2<h_b theta>_w is a precomputed or sampled factor related to blur width
            factor = 2 * sample_blur_factor(w)
            
            L_indirect = L_blurred * factor * P_MS * atten_ms * slice_thickness
            
            # Composite
            L_total = L_total + L_direct + L_indirect
            
    return L_total
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Orthogonal View:** When the viewing direction becomes orthogonal to the slice planes, the slices may disappear due to back-face culling. This requires enabling double-sided rendering [S2, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Implementing single and multiple scattering in separate passes is less efficient. Combining Passes 2 and 3 into a single rendering pass provides a 40% performance increase [S1].
*   **Precision:** The attenuation formula involves exponential decay; precision issues may arise for large path lengths or high density values [S1].

## Subtopics

### DoubleSide Slice Rendering
A technique to prevent volume rendering slices from disappearing when they become orthogonal to the viewing direction by enabling rendering of both front and back faces of the plane geometry [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when the camera view angle causes standard back-face culling to hide the slice planes. Implementation involves setting the material side property to `DoubleSide` (e.g., `THREE.DoubleSide` in Three.js) [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of slices and the number of fragments per slice. The complexity of the fragment shader depends on the number of passes (1-3) and the cost of sampling the blurred light image [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the 3D volume texture and precomputed `l_light` data for all sample points [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** Combining single and multiple scattering passes yields a 40% performance increase over the three-pass method [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Leverages standard GPU rasterization hardware; allows for complex lighting models (scattering) to be applied efficiently per fragment [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires regeneration of slice geometry every frame to maintain view-alignment; performance degrades if the number of slices is very high or if the fragment shader is complex [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Suitable for real-time visualization of participating media where lighting effects are important and hardware rasterization is preferred over ray marching [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When the view direction changes frequently and geometry generation overhead is prohibitive, or when the volume is extremely large and memory bandwidth for 3D texture sampling is a bottleneck [S1].

## Background
The method was introduced by Cabral et al. in 1994 as a texture-based volume rendering approach using graphics hardware [S1]. It was later extended by [S1] to include a lighting model for general participating media, incorporating single and multiple scattering effects. The method is widely used in scientific visualization and medical imaging for real-time volume rendering with lighting effects [S1].
