# Mixed Geometry Rendering

Also called: Direct Volume Rendering with Z-Buffer Geometry, Wireframe and Surface Combination

## Overview
Mixed Geometry Rendering combines direct volume rendering of scalar fields with standard Z-buffer rendering of explicit geometric primitives (such as wireframes or surfaces) to provide structural context for volumetric data [S1]. It solves the problem of ambiguous spatial relationships in pure volume rendering by overlaying precise geometric boundaries, allowing viewers to distinguish between the interior density distribution and the external shell or grid structure [S1]. Unlike pure volume methods, it introduces a hard occlusion boundary where the geometry completely blocks the view of volume data behind it, while volume data in front of the geometry correctly transmits the geometry's color [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: A 3D scalar field (volume data) defined on a grid or unstructured mesh (e.g., tetrahedral cells) [S1].
- Input: Explicit geometric primitives (e.g., wireframe edges, surface meshes) representing boundaries or internal structures [S1].
- Input: Camera parameters (position, orientation, projection) [S1].
- Input: Transfer functions mapping scalar values to color and opacity [S1].
- Input: A simple lighting model (diffuse) [S1].
- Output: A 2D image where volume data is composited with geometric primitives [S1].

**Data Structures:**
- Volume Data: Stored as a 3D array or cell-organized structure (e.g., tetrahedral mesh) [S1]. Memory usage is high for cell-organized data; for example, a model with 381,548 tetrahedral cells required over 50 Megabytes [S1].
- Geometry: Standard Z-buffer representation [S1].
- Z-Buffer: Stores depth values for geometry fragments [S1].

**Method Steps:**
1.  **Gradient Computation:** Compute the gradient of the scalar field at each voxel or node. The gradient is used as a normal vector for lighting calculations [S1].
    -   To compute the gradient at a point, use differences of values and coordinates between any three pairs of points in the neighborhood [S1].
    -   This yields three simultaneous equations with three independent variables (the gradient components) [S1].
    -   The X component of the gradient is calculated as the ratio of two determinants [S1]:
        $$ \text{Gradient}_x = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} A_f0 & A_y0 & A_z0 \\ A_f1 & A_y1 & A_z1 \\ A_f2 & A_y2 & A_z2 \end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix} A_x0 & A_y0 & A_z0 \\ A_x1 & A_y1 & A_z1 \\ A_x2 & A_y2 & A_z2 \end{vmatrix}} $$
        where $A$ represents differences in value ($A_f$) and coordinates ($A_x, A_y, A_z$) [S1].
    -   The Y and Z components are computed similarly by substituting the value differences into the second and third columns of the numerator determinant, respectively [S1].
2.  **Volume Raycasting:** Perform raycasting from each pixel into the volume [S1].
    -   Convert the Z-buffer depth value to a $t$ value along the ray [S1].
    -   Integrate opacity and color values along the ray using the transfer functions [S1].
    -   The raytracing is performed until the ray reaches the depth stored in the Z-buffer [S1].
3.  **Geometric Rendering:** Render the explicit geometry (e.g., wireframe) using a standard Z-buffer technique [S1].
4.  **Compositing:** Combine the volume and geometry results [S1].
    -   Let $C_{vol}$ be the accumulated color from the volume raycasting.
    -   Let $C_{geo}$ be the color of the geometry fragment found in the Z-buffer.
    -   Let $\alpha_{vol}$ be the integral of opacity along the ray from the volume data [S1].
    -   The final pixel color $C_{final}$ is calculated as:
        $$ C_{final} = C_{geo} + (1 - \alpha_{vol}) C_{vol} $$
        This is equivalent to multiplying the stored color in the pixel (geometry) by $(1 - \text{integral of opacity})$ and adding the product to the color of the ray (volume) [S1].
    -   Note: The geometry is rendered first into the Z-buffer and color buffer. The volume rendering then adds its contribution based on its opacity [S1].

**Parameters:**
-   Resolution: 512x512 pixel square window used for performance results [S1].
-   Lighting: Simple diffuse lighting equation [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
-   **Occlusion Artifact:** Wireframe geometry completely occludes volume data behind it. Volume data in front of the wireframe correctly transmits colors from the geometry, but nothing behind the geometry is visible [S1].
-   **Memory Usage:** High memory consumption for cell-organized data [S1].

## Subtopics

### Hybrid Scanline A-Buffer Volume Rendering
A hybrid rendering system that uses a fast scanline a-buffer algorithm for surface-defined objects and volume rendering for procedural gases, combining them via Z-sorted a-buffer fragments [S2, unofficial]. It creates an a-buffer for a scanline containing a list for each pixel of all fragments that partially or fully cover the pixel [S2, unofficial]. For each volume covering the scanline, volume rendering is performed, creating a-buffer fragments for separate sections of the volumes [S2, unofficial]. Volume rendering ceases once full coverage of the pixel by volume or surface-defined elements is achieved [S2, unofficial]. These volume a-buffer fragments are sorted into the a-buffer fragment list based on their average Z-depth values, and the list is rendered to produce the final color [S2, unofficial]. It uses a physically-based low-albedo illumination and atmospheric attenuation model for gases, and combines volumetric shadows using three-dimensional shadow tables [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time Complexity:** Performance is heavily dependent on the complexity of the volume data and the geometry. For a model with 16,896 tetrahedral cells, rendering a 512x512 image took 149.2 seconds on a Stardent ST1000 [S1]. For a model with 381,548 tetrahedral cells, it took 591.6 seconds on the same hardware [S1].
-   **Memory Complexity:** High memory usage for cell-organized data. A model with 381,548 tetrahedral cells required over 50 Megabytes [S1].
-   **Strengths:** Provides clear structural context for volumetric data; allows visualization of internal grids or external shells alongside density fields [S1].
-   **Limitations:** The geometry completely occludes volume data behind it, which may hide relevant volumetric information [S1]. High memory requirements for large cell-organized datasets [S1].
-   **When to use:** When structural boundaries (e.g., FEM grids, CFD boundaries) are critical for interpretation and need to be overlaid on density data [S1].
-   **When not to use:** When the volume data behind the geometry is important and must be visible, or when memory is severely constrained [S1].

## Background
Mixed Geometry Rendering was introduced by Upson and Keeler in 1989 [S1]. It was developed to address the limitations of pure volume rendering in visualizing coarse volumes and finite element models, where structural information is as important as scalar density [S1]. The technique was demonstrated on a Stardent ST1000, a machine with four processors executing instructions at five Mips each [S1]. It is particularly useful for Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and Finite Element Method (FEM) visualizations [S1].
