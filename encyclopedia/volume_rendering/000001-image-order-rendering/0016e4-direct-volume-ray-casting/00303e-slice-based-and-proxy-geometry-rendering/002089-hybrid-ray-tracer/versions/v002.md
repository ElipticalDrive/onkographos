# Hybrid Ray Tracer
Also called: simultaneous ray tracing of volume and geometry

## Overview
The Hybrid Ray Tracer is a rendering method that simultaneously traces rays through volumetric sample arrays and polygonal databases, resampling both at equally spaced intervals, and compositing the resulting colors and opacities in depth-sorted order to handle mixtures of geometric and volumetric data [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from pure volume ray casting by explicitly handling polygonal intersections within the volumetric slabs, treating polygons locally as planes perpendicular to the ray to compute attenuated volumetric "slivers" before and after the intersection [S1, unofficial]. Unlike 3D scan-conversion methods, it performs compositing in image space (ray space) rather than object space, avoiding the need to filter and sample polygons into the voxel grid [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Input:** An $N \times N \times N$ voxel array of scalar values, a set of embedded polygons, a transfer function mapping scalar values to color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$, and a camera position.
- **Output:** A 2D image where each pixel contains the final composited color.

**Data Structures:**
- **Volumetric Data:** A 3D grid of scalar values. Pre-shaded and classified to yield per-voxel color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$ [S1, unofficial].
- **Polygonal Data:** A list of polygons with geometric definitions and shading attributes.
- **Ray State:** For each ray, accumulated color $C'$ and opacity $\alpha'$, plus two auxiliary colors $C_x$ and $C_q$ for adaptive supersampling [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
- **Slab thickness:** Equal to the spacing between samples along a viewing ray [S1, unofficial].
- **Slab width:** Equal to the spacing between adjacent rays [S1, unofficial].
- **Supersampling threshold ($\theta$):** A threshold value for color range comparison to trigger adaptive supersampling [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1.  **Shade and Classify Volume:** Compute color $c_v$ and opacity $o_v$ for each voxel using the transfer function [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Cast Rays:** Trace parallel viewing rays from the observer position into the data [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Resample Volume:** For each ray, compute a vector of colors $c_v$ and opacities $o_v$ by resampling the volumetric data at equally spaced positions (slabs) along the ray. Use tri-linear interpolation from the eight surrounding voxels [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Compute Polygon Intersections:** Independently compute all intersections between the ray and polygons in the environment. Shade them to yield color $c_p$ and opacity $o_p$ for each intersection point [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Composite in Depth-Sorted Order:**
    -   Sort all volumetric slabs and polygon intersections by depth along the ray.
    -   For each polygon intersection within a slab, treat the polygon locally as a plane perpendicular to the ray at the intersection point [S1, unofficial].
    -   Calculate the opacity of the volumetric sliver immediately in front of the polygon ($o_f$) and the sliver immediately behind it ($o_b$) using exponential attenuation formulas [S1, unofficial].
    -   Composite the front sliver ($c_v, o_f$), then the polygon ($c_p, o_p$), then the back sliver ($c_v, o_b$) working from front to back [S1, unofficial].
    -   If a ray intersects more than one polygon within a slab, compute and composite the contribution of each polygon and each sliver of volumetric gel separately [S1, unofficial].
6.  **Adaptive Supersampling:**
    -   Compute two additional colors during ray tracing:
        -   $C_x$: Contributions only from polygons (set $o_v=0$ for all volumetric samples) [S1, unofficial].
        -   $C_q$: Contributions only from polygons, attenuated by passage through volumetric data (set $o_v=0$ for all volumetric samples) [S1, unofficial].
    -   If the range of $C_x$ within a sample region exceeds $\theta$ but the range of $C_q$ does not, the difference is due to volumetric data; do not supersample [S1, unofficial].
    -   If the range of $C_q$ exceeds $\theta$ but the range of $C_x$ does not, the geometric event is hidden by opaque volumetric data; do not supersample [S1, unofficial].
    -   If the ranges of both $C_x$ and $C_q$ exceed $\theta$, the region contains a visible geometric event; cast more rays (supersample) [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
-   **Slab Opacity:** The opacity $o_v$ of a slab is related to density $d_v$ and thickness $t_v$ by [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ o_v = 1 - e^{-d_v t_v} $$
    where $0 < o_v < 1$ and $o_v = 1$ signifies complete attenuation.
-   **Front Sliver Opacity:** The opacity $o_f$ of the portion of a slab lying immediately in front of an embedded plane perpendicular to the viewing ray is [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ o_f = 1 - (1 - o_v)^{t_f/t_v} $$
    where $t_f$ is the thickness of the front portion and $t_v$ is the total slab thickness.
-   **Back Sliver Opacity:** The opacity $o_b$ of the portion of a slab lying immediately behind an embedded plane perpendicular to the viewing ray is [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ o_b = 1 - (1 - o_v)^{t_b/t_v} $$
    where $t_b$ is the thickness of the back portion.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
-   **Polygon within Slab:** If a polygon intersects a slab, the slab must be split into front and back slivers. The opacities of these slivers are calculated using the exponential attenuation formulas [S1, unofficial].
-   **Multiple Polygons in Slab:** If a ray intersects more than one polygon within a slab, the contribution of each polygon and each sliver of volumetric gel must be computed and composited separately [S1, unofficial].
-   **Aliasing:** At polygonal edges, polygon-polygon intersections, and polygon shading highlights, the approximation of treating polygons as planes perpendicular to the ray may not suffice to prevent aliasing. Adaptive supersampling is used to increase the number of rays cast per pixel in these regions [S1, unofficial].
-   **Distinguishing Volumetric vs. Geometric Events:** It is crucial to distinguish whether color differences are due to volumetric data or geometric data to avoid unnecessary supersampling. This is done by comparing the ranges of $C_x$ and $C_q$ [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time Complexity:** The method is more expensive than pure volume ray casting due to the additional computation of polygon intersections and the splitting of slabs. Adaptive supersampling can further increase the computational cost in regions with complex geometric features [S1, unofficial].
-   **Memory Complexity:** The method requires storing the volumetric data and the polygonal data. The auxiliary colors $C_x$ and $C_q$ require additional memory per ray [S1, unofficial].
-   **Strengths:** It correctly handles the visibility of polygons embedded within volumetric data, avoiding the errors that would occur if polygons were simply rendered on top of or behind the volume [S1, unofficial]. It also handles the occlusion of polygons by opaque volumetric data [S1, unofficial].
-   **Limitations:** It is computationally expensive, especially when adaptive supersampling is required. The approximation of treating polygons as planes perpendicular to the ray may introduce errors in some cases [S1, unofficial].
-   **When to Use:** Use this method when rendering mixtures of geometric and volumetric data where the visibility of polygons within the volume is important. It is suitable for applications such as medical imaging, where bones (geometric) may be embedded in soft tissue (volumetric) [S1, unofficial].
-   **When Not to Use:** Avoid this method if the scene contains only volumetric data or only geometric data, as the overhead is unnecessary. It is also not suitable for scenes with very complex geometric structures that would require excessive supersampling [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The Hybrid Ray Tracer was introduced by Michael Levoy in 1988 in the paper "Rendering Mixtures of Geometric and Volumetric Data" [S1, unofficial]. The paper presents two methods for extending the volume rendering algorithm described by Levoy [1988a] to handle polygonally defined objects. The first method is the hybrid ray tracer, and the second is 3D scan-conversion with analytic anti-aliasing. The hybrid ray tracer was one of the first methods to address the problem of rendering mixtures of geometric and volumetric data [S1, unofficial].
