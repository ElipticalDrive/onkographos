# Doppler Volume Rendering

Also called: Piecewise Linear Spectral Representation, Spectral Volume Rendering with Doppler Effect

## Overview
Doppler Volume Rendering is a spectral volume rendering technique that accounts for the Doppler effect of light in an emission-absorption model, where volumetric matter moves according to a 3D velocity field and emits/attenuates light at wavelengths subject to Doppler shifts [S1]. It employs a novel piecewise linear spectral representation to achieve interactive frame rates while maintaining energy conservation, overcoming the inaccuracies of traditional point-sampled spectral representations [S1]. Unlike standard direct volume ray casting which maps scalar values to RGB colors, this method operates in the spectral domain, allowing for the visualization of non-thermal radiation (spectral lines) and thermal radiation (blackbody spectra) with accurate wavelength shifts due to motion [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A 3D scalar field representing density or temperature, a 3D velocity field $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})$ defining the motion of the volumetric matter, a light source spectrum (e.g., blackbody or specific spectral lines), and camera parameters.
*   **Outputs:** A 2D image with RGB color values per pixel.

**Data Structures:**
The method replaces the standard point-sampled spectrum (uniform frequency bins) with a dynamic piecewise linear representation.
*   **Spectral Representation:** Instead of discrete bins, the spectrum is represented as a set of linear segments defined by their endpoints (wavelength $\lambda$ and intensity $I$). This allows for precise tracking of spectral lines as they shift due to the Doppler effect [S1].
*   **Voxel Data:** Each voxel stores scalar values (e.g., density $\rho$, temperature $T$) and vector values (velocity $\mathbf{v}$) [S1].

**Step-by-Step Method:**

1.  **Ray Casting Setup:** For each pixel, cast a ray through the volume. Sample the scalar field $V(\mathbf{p}_i)$ and velocity field $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}_i)$ at sample points $\mathbf{p}_i$ along the ray [S1].

2.  **Spectral Emission/Absorption Calculation:**
    *   **Thermal Radiation:** If the matter emits thermal radiation, calculate the spectral distribution using Planck's radiation law [S1]:
        $$ B_\lambda(T, \lambda) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5} \left[ \exp\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right) - 1 \right]^{-1} $$
        Where $h$ is Planck's constant, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $c$ is the speed of light, and $T$ is the temperature [S1].
    *   **Non-Thermal Radiation:** If the matter emits via energy transitions (e.g., hydrogen Balmer lines), define the emission as specific spectral lines at rest wavelengths $\lambda_0$ with intensity $I_0$ [S1].

3.  **Doppler Shift Computation:**
    *   Calculate the Doppler shift for each wavelength component based on the relative velocity between the emitter (voxel) and the observer (camera).
    *   The observed wavelength $\lambda_{obs}$ is shifted from the rest wavelength $\lambda_{rest}$ due to the velocity component along the line of sight [S1].
    *   The piecewise linear representation allows the method to track these shifts accurately without binning errors [S1].

4.  **Attenuation:**
    *   Apply the Lambert-Bouguer law for attenuation. The transmitted intensity $I(\lambda)$ is dependent on the density $\rho$, thickness $l$, and extinction coefficient $\tau(\lambda)$ [S1]:
        $$ I(\lambda) = I_0(\lambda) e^{-l\rho\tau(\lambda)} $$
    *   The extinction coefficient $\tau(\lambda)$ is determined by the material's absorption properties at the shifted wavelength [S1].

5.  **Accumulation:**
    *   Accumulate the spectral contributions along the ray using front-to-back alpha compositing in the spectral domain. The final spectrum $S_{final}(\lambda)$ is computed by integrating the emitted and attenuated light [S1].

6.  **Color Conversion:**
    *   Convert the final spectral distribution $S_{final}(\lambda)$ to RGB values using standard color matching functions (e.g., CIE 1931) [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Temperature ($T$):** Typical values depend on the simulation (e.g., Sun's effective temperature $T \approx 5770$K) [S1].
*   **Spectral Resolution:** The number of linear segments used to represent the spectrum. Not given in the sources.
*   **Velocity Field:** The magnitude and direction of motion. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Energy Conservation:** Point-sampled methods suffer from energy conservation inaccuracies when spectral lines shift between bins. The piecewise linear representation avoids this [S1].
*   **Spectral Loss:** If the bin range is not large enough, information can be lost when emission/absorption computations shift from the visible into the invisible spectrum. The dynamic representation handles shifts outside the visible range by tracking the full spectrum before conversion [S1].
*   **Spectral Broadening:** Velocities vary continuously along ray segments, causing spectral broadening. The method accounts for this by integrating the Doppler shift over the segment [S1].

    Renders a single ray using Doppler Volume Rendering.
    
    Args:
        ray_origin: 3D vector, origin of the ray.
        ray_direction: 3D vector, direction of the ray.
        volume_data: 3D array, scalar field (density, temperature).
        velocity_field: 3D array, vector field (velocity).
        light_source: Object, defines the initial spectrum (e.g., blackbody or spectral lines).
        
    Returns:
        RGB color tuple.
    """

    accumulated_spectrum = PiecewiseLinearSpectrum()
    
    # Sample along the ray
    t = 0.0
    dt = 0.01  # Step size, not given in sources [G]
    max_t = 10.0  # Max ray length, not given in sources [G]
    
    while t < max_t:
        p = ray_origin + ray_direction * t
        
        # Sample volume and velocity
        density = sample_scalar_field(volume_data, p)
        velocity = sample_vector_field(velocity_field, p)
        temperature = sample_temperature_field(volume_data, p) # Assuming T is stored or derived
        
        # Calculate emitted spectrum at this point
        emitted_spectrum = compute_emission(temperature, velocity, light_source)
        
        # Calculate Doppler shift for the emitted spectrum
        # The shift depends on the velocity component along the ray direction
        velocity_along_ray = dot(velocity, ray_direction)
        shifted_spectrum = apply_doppler_shift(emitted_spectrum, velocity_along_ray)
        
        # Calculate attenuation
        # Extinction coefficient depends on density and wavelength
        extinction_coeff = compute_extinction_coefficient(density, shifted_spectrum.wavelengths)
        
        # Apply Lambert-Bouguer law
        # I(lambda) = I_0(lambda) * exp(-density * dt * tau(lambda))
        attenuated_spectrum = shifted_spectrum * exp(-density * dt * extinction_coeff)
        
        # Accumulate the spectrum
        accumulated_spectrum = accumulate_spectra(accumulated_spectrum, attenuated_spectrum)
        
        t += dt
        
    # Convert final spectrum to RGB
    rgb = spectrum_to_rgb(accumulated_spectrum)
    return rgb

def compute_emission(temperature, velocity, light_source):
    """
    Computes the emitted spectrum based on temperature and light source type.
    """
    if light_source.type == "blackbody":
        # Planck's radiation law
        # B_lambda(T, lambda) = (2*h*c^2 / lambda^5) * (exp(h*c / (lambda * k_B * T)) - 1)^-1
        wavelengths = light_source.wavelengths
        h = 6.626e-34
        c = 3.0e8
        k_B = 1.38e-23
        
        intensities = []
        for lambda_val in wavelengths:
            exponent = (h * c) / (lambda_val * k_B * temperature)
            intensity = (2 * h * c**2) / (lambda_val**5) * (1 / (exp(exponent) - 1))
            intensities.append(intensity)
            
        return PiecewiseLinearSpectrum(wavelengths, intensities)
    elif light_source.type == "spectral_lines":
        # Non-thermal radiation: specific lines
        # Example: Hydrogen Balmer lines
        lines = light_source.lines # List of (rest_wavelength, intensity)
        wavelengths = [line[0] for line in lines]
        intensities = [line[1] for line in lines]
        return PiecewiseLinearSpectrum(wavelengths, intensities)

def apply_doppler_shift(spectrum, velocity_along_ray):
    """
    Applies the Doppler shift to the spectrum.
    lambda_obs = lambda_rest * (1 + v/c) for non-relativistic speeds
    """
    c = 3.0e8
    shifted_wavelengths = []
    for lambda_rest in spectrum.wavelengths:
        lambda_obs = lambda_rest * (1 + velocity_along_ray / c)
        shifted_wavelengths.append(lambda_obs)
    
    # Note: Intensities may also change due to relativistic beaming, 
    # but the sources do not specify this. We assume intensity remains constant for simplicity [G].
    return PiecewiseLinearSpectrum(shifted_wavelengths, spectrum.intensities)

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method is more computationally expensive than standard volume rendering due to the spectral calculations and Doppler shift computations. However, the piecewise linear representation allows for interactive frame rates [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the velocity field and potentially larger spectral data structures compared to RGB [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Accurate representation of the Doppler effect, energy conservation, ability to visualize non-thermal radiation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** More complex implementation, higher computational cost than standard methods [S1].
*   **When to use:** When visualizing astrophysics simulations or other volumetric data where the motion of matter significantly affects the observed light spectrum [S1].

## Background
Doppler Volume Rendering was introduced by Alberto Jaspe et al. in 2023 [S1]. It builds on the work of Noordmans et al. [NvdVS00] who first introduced spectral volume rendering using point-sampled spectra. The method was developed to address the limitations of point-sampled representations in the context of the Doppler effect, particularly for astrophysics simulations like galaxy formation [S1].
