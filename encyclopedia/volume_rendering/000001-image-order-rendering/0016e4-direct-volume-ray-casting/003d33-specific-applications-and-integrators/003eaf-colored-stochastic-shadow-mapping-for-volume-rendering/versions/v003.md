# Colored Stochastic Shadow Mapping for Volume Rendering

Also called: CSSM for DVR, Colored Stochastic Shadow Mapping (CSSM), Colored Stochastic Shadow Mapping (CSSM) for Direct Volume Rendering, Stochastic Shadow Mapping for Volume Rendering

## Overview
Colored Stochastic Shadow Mapping (CSSM) is a two-step rendering technique for Direct Volume Rendering (DVR) that generates physically plausible, colored shadows for translucent volumetric data using stochastic shadow maps [S1]. Unlike traditional shadow mapping which produces binary, often incorrect shadows for translucent objects, CSSM computes partial, color-dependent shadow factors by storing stochastic depth samples in an RGB texture, allowing light to be attenuated based on the volume's spectral absorption properties [S1]. It sets itself apart from Deep Shadow Maps (DSM) by using significantly less memory (one depth value per color channel vs. multiple control points) while maintaining high-quality color fidelity and soft shadows through spatial averaging [S1].

## How it works
CSSM operates in two distinct phases: CSSM Generation (building the shadow map) and DVR Rendering (using the shadow map to modulate illumination). It builds on the foundational ray casting loop defined in [Direct volume ray casting](19-direct-volume-ray-casting.md).

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**:
    *   Volumetric dataset $V$ with opacity $\alpha$ and color-dependent transmission properties.
    *   Light source position and direction (assumed to be a single point light source [S1]).
    *   Camera parameters for DVR.
    *   CSSM resolution (e.g., $1024^2$, $2048^2$, $4096^2$) [S1].
    *   Number of offset queries $m$ for denoising (e.g., 7, 13, 21) [S1].
*   **Outputs**:
    *   CSSM Texture: An RGB texture where each fragment stores three depth values $(c_R, c_G, c_B)$.
    *   Final Image: Volume rendered image with colored shadows.

### Data Structures
*   **CSSM Texture**: An RGB texture of resolution $W \times H$. Each texel stores three floating-point or fixed-point depth values:
    *   $c_R$: Depth of the closest segment where a red photon was absorbed/blocked.
    *   $c_G$: Depth of the closest segment where a green photon was absorbed/blocked.
    *   $c_B$: Depth of the closest segment where a blue photon was absorbed/blocked.
    *   Initial value for all accumulators is 1.0 (representing "no shadow" or maximum distance) [S1].

### CSSM Generation
This step constructs the shadow map by simulating photon traversal through the volume from the light source's perspective.

1.  **Initialization**: For every fragment $(u, v)$ in the CSSM texture, initialize the depth accumulators for the RGB channels:
    $$ (c_R, c_G, c_B) \leftarrow (1.0, 1.0, 1.0) $$
2.  **Ray Casting**: Cast a ray from the light source through the fragment $(u, v)$ into the volume. Discretize this ray into $n$ segments $i = 1 \dots n$.
3.  **Segment Iteration**: For each segment $i$:
    a. Compute the normalized depth $z_i \in [0, 1]$ of segment $i$ along the ray.
    b. Generate a random number $r_i$ uniformly distributed in $[0, 1]$.
    c. For each wavelength $\lambda \in \{R, G, B\}$:
        i. Compute the probability $\rho_{i,\lambda}$ that a photon of wavelength $\lambda$ is **not** transmitted through segment $i$.
        ii. The probability is defined as:
        $$ \rho_{i,\lambda} = \alpha_i \cdot (1 - t_{i,\lambda}) $$
        where:
        *   $\alpha_i$ is the opacity of the volume in segment $i$ [S1].
        *   $t_{i,\lambda}$ is the conditional probability that a photon of wavelength $\lambda$ is transmitted through the volume of segment $i$, given it interacts with the volume [S1].
        iii. If $\rho_{i,\lambda} > r_i$, update the depth accumulator for channel $\lambda$:
        $$ c_\lambda \leftarrow \min(c_\lambda, z_i) $$
        This records the closest depth where absorption occurred for that color.
    d. **Early Termination**: If $c_R < 1$, $c_G < 1$, and $c_B < 1$, terminate the segment loop early, as the photon has been fully absorbed or blocked for all colors in previous segments [S1].
4.  **Storage**: Store the final accumulated depths $(c_R, c_G, c_B)$ in the CSSM fragment at $(u, v)$ [S1].

### DVR Rendering
This step integrates the CSSM into the standard DVR ray casting loop to modulate illumination.

1.  **Initialization**: For each volumetric sample $s$ along the camera ray, initialize shadow factors:
    $$ (l_R, l_G, l_B) \leftarrow (0, 0, 0) $$
2.  **Light Coordinates**: Compute the sample position $(s_x, s_y, s_z)$ in the light coordinate system (shadow map space).
3.  **Stochastic Query**: Iterate through $m$ offset queries $j = 1 \dots m$:
    a. Determine the texture coordinates for the $j$-th offset: $(u_j, v_j) = (s_x, s_y) + \Delta_j$, where $\Delta_j$ is the spatial offset for the $j$-th sample [S1].
    b. Read the CSSM entries $(c_{R,j}, c_{G,j}, c_{B,j})$ from the fragment at $(u_j, v_j)$ [S1].
    c. For each color channel $\lambda \in \{R, G, B\}$:
        i. Perform a binary test: if $c_{\lambda,j} > s_z$, the sample is in shadow for that color.
        ii. Update the shadow factor:
        $$ l_\lambda \leftarrow l_\lambda + \frac{1}{m} $$
        This effectively computes the average shadow factor:
        $$ l_\lambda = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \mathbb{I}(c_{\lambda,j} > s_z) $$
        where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function [S1].
4.  **Illumination**: Use the computed shadow factors $(l_R, l_G, l_B)$ to modulate the light contribution for the sample during compositing [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
# CSSM Generation
def generate_cssm(cssm_texture, light_ray_sampler, volume_data):
    # cssm_texture is an RGB texture of size W x H
    # light_ray_sampler generates rays from light through each fragment
    # volume_data provides opacity alpha and transmission t for segments
    
    for u, v in cssm_texture.fragments():
        # Step 1: Initialize accumulators
        c_R, c_G, c_B = 1.0, 1.0, 1.0
        
        # Step 2: Cast and discretize ray
        ray = light_ray_sampler.get_ray(u, v)
        segments = ray.discretize(volume_data)
        
        # Step 3: Iterate segments
        for i, segment in enumerate(segments):
            z_i = segment.normalized_depth
            r_i = random.uniform(0, 1)
            
            # Check if already fully absorbed
            if c_R < 1.0 and c_G < 1.0 and c_B < 1.0:
                break
                
            # Step 6-7: Compute probabilities and update accumulators
            for lambda_channel in ['R', 'G', 'B']:
                alpha_i = volume_data.get_opacity(segment, lambda_channel)
                t_i_lambda = volume_data.get_transmission_probability(segment, lambda_channel)
                
                # Probability photon is NOT transmitted
                rho_i_lambda = alpha_i * (1.0 - t_i_lambda)
                
                if rho_i_lambda > r_i:
                    if lambda_channel == 'R':
                        c_R = min(c_R, z_i)
                    elif lambda_channel == 'G':
                        c_G = min(c_G, z_i)
                    elif lambda_channel == 'B':
                        c_B = min(c_B, z_i)
        
        # Step 9: Store result
        cssm_texture.set_fragment(u, v, (c_R, c_G, c_B))

# DVR Rendering with CSSM
def render_dvr_with_cssm(camera_ray_sampler, volume_data, cssm_texture, offsets):
    # offsets is a list of (dx, dy) tuples of size m
    m = len(offsets)
    inv_m = 1.0 / m
    
    for u, v in camera_ray_sampler.framebuffer_coords():
        ray = camera_ray_sampler.get_ray(u, v)
        samples = ray.discretize(volume_data)
        
        final_color = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
```

### Parameters and Typical Values
*   **$\alpha_i$**: Opacity of the volume in segment $i$. Derived from the volume's transfer function [S1].
*   **$t_{i,\lambda}$**: Conditional transmission probability for wavelength $\lambda$. Depends on color properties [S1].
*   **$m$**: Number of offset queries. Typical values: 7 (pleasing results, no noticeable noise), 13 (used in experiments), 21 (softer shadows) [S1].
*   **$\Delta_j$**: Spatial offsets. Example set for $m=13$: $\{(0,0), (\pm3,\pm3), (\pm4,0), (0,\pm4), (\pm7,0), (0,\pm7)\}$ [S1].
*   **CSSM Resolution**: Tested resolutions include $1024^2$, $2048^2$, and $4096^2$. Higher resolution improves shadow detail but increases memory [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Noise**: Stochastic generation introduces noise. Mitigated by averaging over multiple CSSM lookups with offsets. Complex scenes may require more offsets ($m$) to achieve pleasing results [S1].
*   **Blurring**: Increasing $m$ softens shadows but increases computation time and causes blurring [S1].
*   **Single Light Source**: CSSM is limited to a single point light source. Additional light sources require additional shadow maps, a general limitation of shadow mapping [S1].
*   **Precision**: Depth values are normalized to $[0, 1]$. Precision issues may arise if the volume depth range is not well-conditioned relative to the shadow map resolution [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**:
    *   CSSM Generation: $O(W \cdot H \cdot n)$, where $W, H$ are shadow map dimensions and $n$ is the number of segments per ray [S1].
    *   DVR Rendering: $O(N \cdot m)$, where $N$ is the number of volumetric samples and $m$ is the number of offsets [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity**:
    *   CSSM Texture: $3 \cdot W \cdot H$ depth values (one per color channel). Constant memory regardless of $m$ [S1].
    *   Compared to Deep Shadow Maps (DSM): CSSM uses significantly less memory. DSM with 3 nodes per channel stores 12 values per fragment (4x storage of CSSM). DSM memory scales linearly with nodes (+4 values per node), whereas CSSM is constant [S1].
*   **Measured Performance**:
    *   Rendering time for CSSM at $1024^2$ resolution: 4.9 ms [S1].
    *   Comparison: Higher than Traditional Shadow Mapping (TSM, 3.2 ms) and Deep Shadow Mapping (DSM, 3.3 ms) due to increased texture fetches (13 per query for CSSM vs. 1 per node for DSM (3 nodes) vs. 1 for TSM) [S1].
    *   All approaches scale equally with resolution and produce interactive frame rates [S1].
*   **Strengths**:
    *   Produces high-quality, colored shadows for translucent objects [S1].
    *   Lower memory usage compared to Deep Shadow Maps [S1].
    *   Compatible with mixed scenes (volume and surface) [S1].
*   **Limitations**:
    *   Limited to single point light source [S1].
    *   Stochastic noise requires averaging (increases compute cost) [S1].
    *   Blurring with high $m$ [S1].
*   **When to Use**: When colored shadows for translucent volumes are required, and memory is constrained compared to DSM. Suitable for interactive applications where slightly higher rendering time is acceptable for quality.
*   **When Not to Use**: When multiple light sources are needed (requires multiple CSSMs), or when binary shadows are sufficient (use TSM for speed).

## Background
*   **Origin**: Proposed in 2018 by [S1] as an alternative to Deep Shadow Maps for Direct Volume Rendering.
*   **History**: Adapted from the Colored Stochastic Shadow Mapping (CSSM) concept originally developed for surface rendering [S1]. The method modifies probabilistic events to represent photon traversal through a volume with changing opacities, adopting computations from the discrete volume rendering integral [S1].
*   **Usage**: Implemented in the Voreen framework using a GPU-based ray casting approach. Tested on synthetic datasets (Spheres) and medical data (Walnut CT scan) [S1]. Compatible with mixed scenes containing both volume and surface renderings [S1].
