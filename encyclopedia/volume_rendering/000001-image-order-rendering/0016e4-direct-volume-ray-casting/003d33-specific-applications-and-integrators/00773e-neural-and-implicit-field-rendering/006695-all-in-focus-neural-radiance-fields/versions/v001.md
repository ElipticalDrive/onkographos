# All-in-Focus Neural Radiance Fields

Also called: DC-NeRF, Dual-Camera All-in-Focus Neural Radiance Fields

## Overview
All-in-Focus Neural Radiance Fields (DC-NeRF) is a framework for synthesizing all-in-focus (AiF) novel views from dual-camera image pairs, specifically addressing the failure of standard NeRF methods when input views suffer from consistent defocus blur due to fixed autofocus [S1]. It leverages the complementary characteristics of a smartphone's dual-camera system: the main camera provides high-resolution details, while the ultra-wide camera provides a deeper depth-of-field (DoF) as a sharp reference [S1]. Unlike standard DVR which assumes sharp inputs or inconsistent blur, DC-NeRF modifies the integration step by fusing aligned main and ultra-wide radiance fields using a defocus-aware blending mask, allowing it to restore sharp details in defocused main-camera views while preserving their high-quality regions [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ viewpoints. For each viewpoint, the main camera provides a focal stack (foreground-focused and background-focused images) and the ultra-wide camera provides a single sharp image [S1]. Camera poses and intrinsics for both cameras are required [S1].
*   **Outputs:** An all-in-focus novel view for any arbitrary camera pose within the scene [S1].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **NeRF Models:** Two separate Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), denoted as $\mathcal{M}_{main}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{uw}$, representing the radiance fields for the main and ultra-wide cameras respectively [S1]. Each MLP takes a 3D position $\mathbf{x}$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ as input and outputs color $\mathbf{c}$ and volume density $\sigma$ [S1, implied by standard NeRF].
2.  **Fusion MLPs:** Two additional MLPs used for the defocus-aware fusion module. One predicts the blending weight (mask) and the other predicts the final radiance [S1].
3.  **Alignment Maps:**
    *   **Homography Matrix:** For image-level alignment [S1].
    *   **Optical Flow Field:** For pixel-level alignment [S1].
    *   **Confidence Map ($M_c$):** A binary or soft mask indicating reliable alignment regions for the ultra-wide camera [S1].
    *   **Disparity Map ($D$):** Used as a fallback blending mask if the defocus-aware module is disabled [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Spatial Alignment:**
    Align the ultra-wide camera image $I_{uw}$ to the main camera coordinate system $I_{main}$ to handle the large displacement between the two lenses [S1].
    *   Apply image-level homography warping [S1].
    *   Apply pixel-level optical flow warping to refine alignment [S1].
    *   Generate the aligned ultra-wide image $I_{w,a}$ [S1].
    *   Compute a confidence map $M_c$ to identify regions where alignment is reliable, discarding rays from low-confidence regions during training [S1].

2.  **Color Matching:**
    Perform histogram matching between the aligned ultra-wide image $I_{w,a}$ and the main camera image $I_{main}$ to ensure color consistency [S1].

3.  **Stage 1: Separate NeRF Training:**
    Train the two NeRF models independently using the aligned inputs [S1].
    *   **Ultra-Wide Model ($\mathcal{M}_{uw}$):** Supervised by rays from $I_{w,a}$. Rays corresponding to low-confidence regions in $M_c$ are discarded from the start of training [S1].
    *   **Main Model ($\mathcal{M}_{main}$):** Supervised by rays from the main camera focal stack [S1].
    *   **Loss:** Both models use Mean Squared Error (MSE) reconstruction loss:
        $$ L_{recon} = \sum_{r=1}^{N} \| C(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) \|_2^2 $$
        where $C(\mathbf{r})$ is the rendered color and $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ is the target color for ray $\mathbf{r}$ [S1].

4.  **Stage 2: Defocus-Aware Fusion:**
    Learn to fuse the outputs of $\mathcal{M}_{main}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{uw}$ to produce the AiF view [S1].
    *   **Defocus Parameter Prediction:** A defocus-aware fusion module predicts learnable defocus parameters from bokeh rendering to estimate the amount of defocus blur on each main-camera ray [S1].
    *   **Blending Mask Synthesis:** The defocus parameters and the disparity map $D$ are used to calculate a defocus map, which generates a blending mask (weight) [S1]. If the fusion module is disabled, the disparity map $D$ is used directly as the fallback mask [S1].
    *   **Weighted Volume Rendering:** For a novel view ray $\mathbf{r}$, the final color is computed by blending the radiance from both NeRFs using the predicted weight $w(\mathbf{r})$:
        $$ C_{final}(\mathbf{r}) = w(\mathbf{r}) C_{main}(\mathbf{r}) + (1 - w(\mathbf{r})) C_{uw}(\mathbf{r}) $$
        where $C_{main}$ and $C_{uw}$ are the colors rendered by $\mathcal{M}_{main}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{uw}$ respectively [S1, implied]. The fusion MLPs also predict the radiance directly to refine this output [S1].
    *   **Loss:** Training applies two loss terms: $L_{fusion}$ (reconstruction loss on the fused output) and $L_{focus}$ (focus-specific loss, likely ensuring sharpness) [S1]. Removing either degrades performance [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Learnable Defocus Parameters:** Used in the defocus-aware fusion module to predict the defocus map [S1].
*   **Loss Weights:** For $L_{fusion}$ and $L_{focus}$ [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Severe Defocus Blur:** Optical flow networks suffer from artifacts when processing severely defocused main-camera images, causing misalignment at object edges [S1].
*   **Large Displacement:** The large physical displacement between smartphone cameras makes alignment difficult [S1].
*   **Unaligned Inputs:** Directly using unaligned ultra-wide inputs with a single NeRF fails to restore blurred regions because consistent blur across main-camera views provides no supervision for ray correspondence [S1].
*   **Low-Quality Ultra-Wide Details:** The ultra-wide camera may lack fine details, preventing the restoration of specific main-camera regions [S1].

## Subtopics

### Refocusing
Adjusting the focal plane to a different depth and modifying blur intensity using the disparity map and defocus parameters [S1].

### Split Diopter Effect
Keeping foreground and background in focus while blurring the middle region, distinct from continuous DoF [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training and inference times are higher than standard NeRF methods due to the dual-MLP fusion architecture and alignment steps [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing two NeRF models and fusion MLPs [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** On an Nvidia 3090 GPU, DC-NeRF requires 25 hours for training and 30 seconds for inference per view, compared to 18-22 hours and 14-16 seconds for baselines like Deblur-NeRF and DoF-NeRF [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles consistent defocus blur (fixed autofocus) where other methods fail; preserves high-fidelity details from the main camera; does not require pre-training on external datasets [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Sensitive to alignment errors at edges due to defocus; computational cost is higher; relies on the availability of a dual-camera system with a sharp ultra-wide lens [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When synthesizing novel views from smartphone dual-camera data where the main camera has consistent defocus blur and no manual refocusing is possible [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When inputs are sharp (standard NeRF is sufficient) or when only single-camera data is available [S1].

## Background
DC-NeRF was introduced by Xianrui Luo, Zijin Wu, Juewen Peng, Huiqiang Sun, Zhiguo Cao, and Guosheng Lin in 2025 [S1]. It addresses the limitation of existing All-in-Focus NeRF methods (such as Deblur-NeRF, DoF-NeRF, and DP-NeRF) which require inconsistent blur (images focused on different depth planes) and fail when the camera is focused on the same target [S1]. Previous single-view deblurring methods (EasyAIF, DC2) required pre-training on external datasets and extra deblurring steps [S1]. DC-NeRF is the first to integrate a dual-camera setup directly into multi-view AiF NeRF synthesis, using the ultra-wide camera's deep DoF as a reference for the main camera's consistent blur [S1].
