# Directional Filtering for Occlusion Reduction

Also called: Directional transparency filtering, Eigenvector-based alpha modulation

## Overview
A technique to reduce occlusion in volume rendering by modulating the alpha (opacity) value based on the alignment between the local fiber direction (major eigenvector) and a user-defined direction vector, allowing selective fading of irrelevant fiber bundles while preserving context [S1, book]. Unlike previous methods that represent isosurfaces of anisotropy values, this method uses directional tensor information to select distinct fiber bundles and hide others that occlude important parts, as anisotropy measures do not provide information concerning fiber bundle boundaries [S1, book]. It sets itself apart from [Directional Occlusion Shading](10970-directional-occlusion-shading.md) and [ISMOS](15205-ismos.md) by explicitly leveraging the full tensor directional information for interactive region selection rather than relying solely on scalar invariants or surface-based approximations.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A diffusion tensor field (DTI data), a user-defined direction vector $\mathbf{v}$, and a base opacity transfer function.
*   **Output:** A rendered image with modulated alpha values where fibers aligned with $\mathbf{v}$ are preserved and others are faded.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Tensor Field:** Each voxel contains a $3 \times 3$ symmetric positive-definite diffusion tensor.
*   **Eigenvector:** The major eigenvector $\mathbf{e}$ is computed for each voxel. It is a 3D vector $(x, y, z)$.
*   **Direction Vector:** A user-defined 3D vector $\mathbf{v}$.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Compute Eigenvectors:** For each voxel, compute the eigenvalues and the required major eigenvector $\mathbf{e}$ on the GPU. Use the non-iterative approach by Hasan et al. [HBPA01] to ensure efficiency and precision [S1, book].
2.  **Normalize Vectors:** Normalize the major eigenvector $\mathbf{e}$ and the user-defined direction vector $\mathbf{v}$ to unit length.
3.  **Handle Hemisphere Symmetry:**
    *   Define the user-specified direction vector $\mathbf{v}$ in one hemisphere.
    *   Mirror $\mathbf{v}$ at a plane separating both hemispheres of the brain to restrict comparison to one direction on the hemisphere [S1, book].
    *   **Pitfall:** Filtering out single directions breaks the typical symmetry of DTI data sets provided by standard color coding (red/green/blue for cardinal directions), producing unfamiliar images and interfering with hemisphere comparison [S1, book]. To maintain symmetry, compare all directions that have the same color. Due to eigenvector orientation independence ($\mathbf{e}$ and $-\mathbf{e}$ are equivalent), there are up to eight vectors in general, or four unique directions [S1, book].
4.  **Compute Alignment:** Calculate the dot product of the normalized eigenvector $\mathbf{e}$ and the normalized direction vector $\mathbf{v}$: $k = \langle \mathbf{e}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$.
5.  **Apply Filtering Function:** Pass the dot product $k$ through a filtering function $f(x)$ to determine the modulation factor. The alpha value is modified by:
    $$ \alpha \propto f(\langle \mathbf{e}, \mathbf{v} \rangle) $$
    [S1, book]
6.  **Modulate Opacity:** Multiply the base opacity (derived from FA or other transfer functions) by the result of $f(k)$.

**Formulas:**

The filtering function $f(x)$ maps the dot product $x \in [-1, 1]$ to $[0, 1]$. Two common implementations are provided:

1.  **GLSL Smoothstep:**
    $$
    f(x) = \begin{cases} 
    0 & \text{when } x < 0 \\
    3x^2 - 2x^3 & \text{when } 0 \le x \le 1 \\
    1 & \text{when } x > 1 
    \end{cases}
    $$
    [S1, book]

2.  **Higher-Order Polynomial:**
    $$
    f(x) = \begin{cases} 
    0 & \text{when } x < 0 \\
    (126 + (-420 + (540 + (-315 + 70x)x)x)x)x^5 & \text{when } 0 \le x \le 1 \\
    1 & \text{when } x > 1 
    \end{cases}
    $$
    [S1, book]

**Parameters:**
*   **Transparency Coefficients:** The selection of good occlusion coefficients is critical. Painting too many semitransparent surfaces leads to visual clutter produced by both mixture of color and additional geometry [S1, book].
*   **Hidden Area Opacity:** To preserve context, "hidden" areas (where $f(x) \approx 0$) can be assigned a small non-zero opacity rather than being completely transparent, allowing the user to see more important parts that may lie behind the uninteresting parts [S1, book].

    """
    Computes directional filtering for occlusion reduction.
    
    Args:
        tensor_field: 3D array of 3x3 symmetric tensors.
        user_direction_v: 3D vector representing the direction of interest.
        
    Returns:
        alpha_modulation: 3D array of alpha modulation factors.
    """

    # Ensure eigenvectors are unit length
    eigenvectors = normalize(eigenvectors)
    
    # Normalize user direction
    v = normalize(user_direction_v)
    
    # Step 3: Handle Hemisphere Symmetry
    # Define a plane separating hemispheres (e.g., x=0 for left/right brain)
    # Mirror v to cover symmetric directions
    # Note: The source mentions comparing all directions with the same color.
    # For a single vector v, we consider v and -v (orientation independence).
    # If hemisphere symmetry is required, we mirror v across the separation plane.
    
    # Example: If separating plane is normal n, mirror v:
    # v_mirrored = v - 2 * dot(v, n) * n
    # However, the source implies restricting comparison to one direction on the hemisphere.
    # We assume v is already defined in the target hemisphere.
    
    # Step 4 & 5: Compute alignment and apply filtering
    alpha_modulation = zeros_like(tensor_field.shape[:3])
    
    for each voxel in tensor_field:
        e = eigenvectors[voxel]
        
        # Compute dot product
        k = dot(e, v)
        
        # Apply filtering function
        # Using GLSL smoothstep as default
        alpha_modulation[voxel] = smoothstep_filter(k)
        
    return alpha_modulation

def smoothstep_filter(x):
    """
    GLSL smoothstep function.
    """
    if x < 0:
        return 0.0
    elif x > 1:
        return 1.0
    else:
        return 3 * x**2 - 2 * x**3

    Computes the major eigenvector for each tensor using the non-iterative approach by Hasan et al. [HBPA01].
    This function is a placeholder for the specific algorithm.
    """

    # It involves solving the characteristic equation or using closed-form solutions for 3x3 symmetric matrices.
    # [G] Standard closed-form solution for 3x3 symmetric eigenvalue problem.
    eigenvectors = []
    for tensor in tensors:
        # Compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors
        # Select the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue
        eigenvalues, eigenvectors_matrix = numpy.linalg.eigh(tensor)
        idx = numpy.argmax(eigenvalues)

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Symmetry Breaking:** Filtering out single directions breaks the typical symmetry of DTI data sets provided by standard color coding (red/green/blue for cardinal directions), producing unfamiliar images and interfering with hemisphere comparison [S1, book]. To maintain symmetry, compare all directions that have the same color (up to eight vectors, or four due to eigenvector orientation independence) [S1, book].
*   **Context Loss:** Completely removing large parts of the image (making it completely transparent) may confuse the user. It is recommended to select an opacity for "hidden" areas making them translucent to preserve context [S1, book].
*   **Interpolation:** Interpolation of eigenvalues and eigenvectors directions is not the same as interpolating tensor values and recomputing eigenvalues and eigenvectors afterwards [ZB02, Kin04]. Therefore, eigenvectors should be computed on the GPU for each voxel during rendering rather than pre-interpolating them [S1, book].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method requires computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors for each voxel. Using the non-iterative approach by Hasan et al. [HBPA01] allows this to be done efficiently on the GPU [S1, book]. The filtering function evaluation is $O(1)$ per voxel.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the tensor field and the computed eigenvectors. The eigenvectors can be computed on-the-fly or stored if memory permits.
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Allows interactive selection of regions of interest by considering local fiber direction [S1, book].
    *   Provides better selection of distinct fiber bundles compared to anisotropy-based methods, which do not provide information concerning fiber bundle boundaries [S1, book].
    *   Preserves context by allowing translucent "hidden" areas [S1, book].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   The selection of good occlusion coefficients is critical; poor selection leads to visual clutter [S1, book].
    *   Breaking symmetry can produce unfamiliar images and interfere with hemisphere comparison [S1, book].
    *   Requires GPU computation for eigenvectors to be efficient [S1, book].
*   **When to Use:** When the user needs to selectively view specific fiber bundles while hiding occluding structures, and when maintaining the symmetry of DTI color coding is important.
*   **When Not to Use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and GPU eigenvector computation is not feasible, or when the user is not interested in directional fiber information.

## Background
*   **Origin:** The method was proposed by M. Hlawitschka et al. in the paper "Interactive Volume Rendering of Diffusion Tensor Data" [S1, book].
*   **History:** The work builds upon previous volume rendering techniques for DTI data, such as mapping normalized major eigenvectors to color and using FA or anisotropy for opacity [S1, book]. It addresses the occlusion problem in volume rendering, which is a major challenge when handling volumetric data sets [S1, book].
*   **Usage:** The method is used in the visualization of Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) data, particularly in medical contexts where neuroscientists and doctors need to inspect specific fiber bundles in the brain [S1, book].
