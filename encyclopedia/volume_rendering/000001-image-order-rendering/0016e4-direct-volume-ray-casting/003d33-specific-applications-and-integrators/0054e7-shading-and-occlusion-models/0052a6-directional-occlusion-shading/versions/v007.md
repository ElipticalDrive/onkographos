# Directional Occlusion Shading

Also called: DOS, Directional Occlusion, Directional Occlusion Shading Model, Schott et al, Slice-based directional occlusion shading

## Overview
Directional Occlusion Shading (DOS) is a slice-based shading technique for interactive direct volume rendering that simulates ambient occlusion effects by integrating soft shadowing and first-order scattering using a backward-peaked cone phase function [S1, S4]. It differs from its siblings by deriving occlusion factors from the radiative transport equation using a viewer-oriented cone, allowing semi-transparent materials to influence occlusion and clipping planes to interactively modify shadows without precomputation, unlike Vicinity Shading or Filterable Occlusion Maps [S4].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Input:** 3D volume texture (scalar or multivariate), transfer function (mapping attributes to color, opacity $\sigma_a$, and scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$), camera parameters (view matrix, projection matrix), aperture angle $\theta$, inter-slice distance $d$, sampling rate/grid resolution.
- **Output:** Final color buffer (Eye buffer) with occlusion shading applied.

**Data Structures:**
- **Volume Texture:** 3D texture storing volume attributes.
- **Eye Buffer:** Floating-point render target (typically 16-bit) storing final composited color and opacity.
- **Occlusion Buffers:** Two floating-point render targets (Current and Next) storing the fractional visibility (occlusion factor) for each slice. These are swapped after each slice update [S4].
- **Proxy Geometry:** View-aligned slices (quads) representing intersections of the volume bounding box with planes parallel to the view plane [S4].

**Parameters:**
- **Aperture Angle ($\theta$):** Controls the cone's spread. Range $[0, \pi/2)$. Typical value: $80^\circ$ [S4].
- **Inter-slice Distance ($d$):** Distance between adjacent view-aligned slices. Typical value: $0.001$ [S4].
- **Sampling Rate/Grid Resolution:** Controls the number of samples taken from the previous occlusion buffer. Typical values tested: 0.3, 0.5, 1.0 (relative to slice resolution) [S3].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Compute Proxy Geometry:** On the CPU, generate view-aligned slice geometry intersecting the volume bounding box [S4].
2.  **Initialize Buffers:** Allocate two floating-point buffers for occlusion factors (Current and Next) and one for the eye buffer [S4].
3.  **Render Loop (Front-to-Back):** For each slice $i$ from front to back:
    a.  **Update Eye Buffer:** Render the slice geometry into the Eye buffer.
        -   Sample the volume at the slice position to get color $C$ and opacity $\alpha$.
        -   Read the occlusion factor $V$ from the *Current* Occlusion Buffer at the fragment's projected position.
        -   Compute the shaded color contribution: $C_{shaded} = C \cdot V \cdot L_a$ (where $L_a$ is ambient radiance) [S4].
        -   Composite $C_{shaded}$ and $\alpha$ into the Eye buffer using front-to-back blending [S4].
    b.  **Update Occlusion Buffer:** Render the slice geometry into the *Next* Occlusion Buffer to compute the new occlusion factor for the subsequent slice.
        -   For each fragment, calculate the occlusion extent radius: $r = d \tan(\theta)$ [S4].
        -   Compute the perspective correction factor $\vec{c}$ in the vertex shader using the projection matrix $P$ and the view-space $z$-coordinate of the slice vertices [S4]:
            $$ \vec{c} = P \cdot \begin{bmatrix} x_{scale} \\ y_{scale} \\ z_{scale} \end{bmatrix} $$
            (Note: The source defines $\vec{c}$ components derived from $P$ and view $z$, effectively scaling the cone base in screen space) [S4].
        -   Generate sample positions $\vec{p}$ on a uniform grid disk with radius $r$ centered at the fragment [S4].
        -   Calculate Normalized Device Coordinate (NDC) sample positions $\vec{p}_d$:
            $$ \vec{p}_d = \vec{x}_{y,d} + \vec{c} \otimes \vec{p} $$
            where $\vec{x}_{y,d}$ is the fragment's NDC position, $\otimes$ is component-wise multiplication, and $\vec{p}$ is the disk sample [S4].
        -   Convert to texture coordinates $\vec{p}_t$:
            $$ \vec{p}_t = 0.5 \cdot \vec{p}_d + \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} $$
        -   Read the occlusion factor $V_{prev}$ from the *Current* Occlusion Buffer at $\vec{p}_t$.
        -   Attenuate $V_{prev}$ by the transmittance $T$ between the current slice and the sample point. The source notes that approximating the distance with $d$ is acceptable and improves performance [S4]:
            $$ T \approx e^{-\sigma_t \cdot d} $$
        -   Average the weighted samples to compute the new occlusion factor $V_{new}$ for the *Next* buffer [S4].
    c.  **Swap Buffers:** Swap the Current and Next Occlusion Buffers [S4].
4.  **Final Output:** Copy the Eye buffer to the frame buffer, converting from floating-point to fixed-point [S4].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
# Constants
theta = 80.0 * PI / 180.0  # Aperture angle in radians
d = 0.001                  # Inter-slice distance
La = 1.0                   # Ambient radiance (constant)

# Buffers
eye_buffer = allocate_floating_point_buffer(width, height)
occlusion_curr = allocate_floating_point_buffer(width, height)
occlusion_next = allocate_floating_point_buffer(width, height)
occlusion_curr.fill(1.0)   # Initialize with full visibility

# Precompute proxy geometry (view-aligned slices)
slices = compute_view_aligned_slices(volume_bounding_box, view_direction)

for slice in slices:
    # Pass 1: Update Eye Buffer
    render_slice_to_buffer(
        geometry=slice,
        target=eye_buffer,
        mode='composite',
        vertex_shader=compute_vertex_data,
        fragment_shader=compute_eye_color
    )

    # Pass 2: Update Occlusion Buffer
    render_slice_to_buffer(
        geometry=slice,
        target=occlusion_next,
        mode='occlusion_update',
        vertex_shader=compute_vertex_data,
        fragment_shader=compute_occlusion_factor,
        source_buffer=occlusion_curr
    )

    # Swap buffers
    swap(occlusion_curr, occlusion_next)

# Final copy
frame_buffer = convert_to_fixed_point(eye_buffer)

def compute_vertex_data(vertex):
    # Compute correction factor c for perspective correction
    # P is projection matrix, z is view-space z of the slice
    P = get_projection_matrix()
    z = vertex.view_z
    # Simplified derivation from source: c depends on P and z
    # The source defines c components based on P and z scale
    # In practice, this scales the cone radius in screen space
    c_x = P[0][0] / (P[3][2] * z + P[3][3]) # Approximation of projection scaling
    c_y = P[1][1] / (P[3][2] * z + P[3][3])
    return c_x, c_y

def compute_eye_color(fragment):
    # Sample volume
    color, opacity = sample_volume(fragment.world_pos)
    
    # Read occlusion factor from current buffer
    v = read_buffer(occlusion_curr, fragment.screen_pos)
    
    # Apply shading
    shaded_color = color * v * La
    
    # Front-to-back compositing into eye_buffer
    # Note: Actual compositing is handled by GPU blending or manual accumulation
    return shaded_color, opacity

def compute_occlusion_factor(fragment):
    # Cone extent radius
    r = d * tan(theta)
    
    # Get correction factor from vertex shader output
    c_x, c_y = fragment.vertex_data.c
    
    # Generate sample positions on a disk
    samples = generate_uniform_disk_samples(r)
    
    total_weighted_v = 0.0
    total_weight = 0.0
    
    for p in samples:
        # p is 2D vector (x, y) in local disk coordinates
        # Calculate NDC sample position
        # x_y_d is fragment's NDC position
        p_d_x = fragment.ndc_x + c_x * p.x
        p_d_y = fragment.ndc_y + c_y * p.y
        
        # Convert to texture coordinates
        p_t_x = 0.5 * p_d_x + 0.5
        p_t_y = 0.5 * p_d_y + 0.5
        
        # Read previous occlusion factor
        v_prev = read_buffer(occlusion_curr, (p_t_x, p_t_y))
        
        # Attenuate by transmittance (approximated with d)
        # sigma_t is extinction coefficient from transfer function
        sigma_t = get_extinction_coefficient(fragment.world_pos)
        t = exp(-sigma_t * d)
        
        total_weighted_v += v_prev * t
        total_weight += t
        
    # Average
    if total_weight > 0:
        return total_weighted_v / total_weight
    else:
        return 1.0
```

## Subtopics

### [Extinction Optimized Shading](14805-extinction-optimized-shading.md)
Extinction Optimized Shading is a specific integrator mode within Directional Occlusion Shading that highlights interior structures occluded by others by calculating optimized extinction coefficients using a closed-form solution based on the transfer function's importance function. It sets itself apart from the standard directional occlusion mode by simplifying transfer function design to setting low or high importance, thereby revealing internal structures better than standard occlusion shading.

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
- **Time:** Interactive frame rates are achieved. Performance depends on the sampling rate/grid resolution and the number of slices. For a Brain MRI dataset (4 × 240 × 240 × 155) at 600 × 600 viewport: 14 fps at sampling rate 0.3, 11 fps at 0.5, and 4 fps at 1.0 [S3].
- **Memory:** Low memory footprint, requiring only two additional floating-point buffers for occlusion factors and one for the eye buffer, in addition to the volume texture [S1, S4].

**Measured Performance:**
- **Stag Beetle (1046 slices, 80° aperture):** 10.6 FPS [S4].
- **Brain MRI (256x256x160, 1000 slices):** 3.7 FPS (DOS) vs 1.6 FPS (Lambertian) [S4].
- **Carp CT (256x288x512, 220 slices):** 48.3 FPS (DOS) vs 8.8 FPS (Lambertian) [S4].
- **Visible Male (512x512x302, 962 slices):** 5.0 FPS (DOS) vs 25.7 FPS (Lambertian) [S4].
- **Engine Block CT (256x256x128, 679 slices):** 5.6 FPS (DOS) vs 5.6 FPS (Lambertian) [S4].
- **Hand CT (244x124x257, 619 slices):** 13.3 FPS (DOS) vs 16.0 FPS (Lambertian) [S4].
- **Tree CT (512x512x512, 1563 slices):** 59.0 FPS (DOS) vs 29.1 FPS (Lambertian) [S4].
- **Extinction Optimized Mode (Brain MRI):** 8 fps at sampling rate 0.3, 4 fps at 0.5, and 1 fps at 1.0 [S3].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
- **Strengths:** No precomputation required; supports interactive editing of transfer functions and clipping planes; accentuates concavities, depth discontinuities, and soft shadows better than Lambertian shading; handles semi-transparent materials correctly [S4].
- **Limitations:** Bound to the slice-based rendering paradigm; supports only a single light source located at the camera position; perspective distortion requires correction; inter-frame artifacts can occur with camera movement (mitigated by symmetric uniform grid) [S1, S4].
- **MRT vs SRT:** Multiple Render Targets (MRT) increases performance by 44-99% compared to Single Render Target (SRT), especially for low sample counts. Bottleneck shifts to memory bandwidth for high sample counts [S4].

## Background
Directional Occlusion Shading was introduced by Mathias Schott, Vincent Pegoraro, Charles Hansen, Kévin Boulanger, and Kadi Bouatouch in 2009 at the Eurographics/ IEEE-VGTC Symposium on Visualization [S4]. It was published in the Journal of Computer Graphics Forum (Eurographics), 2009 [S4]. The method is derived from the radiative transport equation using a specialized backward-peaked cone phase function to model directional occlusion, allowing visualization of local and global structures without precomputation [S4]. It is used in interactive direct volume rendering to provide perceptual cues similar to ambient occlusion for solid and transparent surface-like features [S4].
