# Directional Occlusion Shading

Also called: DOS, Directional Occlusion, Directional Occlusion Shading Model, Schott et al, Slice-based directional occlusion shading

## Overview
Directional Occlusion Shading (DOS) is a slice-based technique for interactive direct volume rendering that integrates soft shadowing and first-order scattering effects using a backward-peaked cone phase function [S1, S3]. Unlike standard direct volume ray casting which typically uses Lambertian shading or precomputed ambient occlusion, DOS computes an image-space occlusion factor during front-to-back slice traversal, allowing for interactive manipulation of clipping planes and transfer functions without precomputation [S3]. It differs from half-angle slicing by keeping the slicing axis fixed relative to the view rather than adapting to the light position, and it provides perceptual cues for both solid and transparent surface-like features [S1, S3].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** 3D scalar volume data, a 2D transfer function mapping scalar values to color and opacity, an aperture angle $\theta$ for the cone phase function, and an inter-slice distance $d$.
*   **Output:** A 2D image with occlusion-aware shading.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Volume Texture:** 3D texture storing the scalar field.
2.  **Eye Buffer:** A floating-point buffer (typically 16-bit precision) storing the accumulated radiance (color and opacity) for the current slice traversal [S3].
3.  **Occlusion Buffers:** Two floating-point buffers (current and next) storing the fractional visibility (occlusion factor) for each pixel. These are swapped after each slice update [S3].
4.  **Proxy Geometry:** View-aligned slices (quads) representing the intersection of the volume bounding box with planes parallel to the view plane [S3].

**Parameters:**
*   $\theta$: Aperture angle of the cone phase function. Range $[0, \pi/2)$. Typical value: $80^\circ$ [S3].
*   $d$: Inter-slice distance. Typical value: $0.001$ [S3].
*   Sample resolution: User-specified uniform grid resolution for sampling the occlusion buffer [S3].

**Formulas:**
The radiance $L(\vec{x}, \vec{\omega})$ at point $\vec{x}$ in viewing direction $\vec{\omega}$ is:
$$ L(\vec{x}, \vec{\omega}) = T(\vec{x}, \vec{x}_0) L_b(\vec{x}_0, \vec{\omega}) + L_m(\vec{x}, \vec{\omega}) $$
where $L_b$ is background radiance, $T$ is transmittance, and $L_m$ is medium radiance [S3].

Medium radiance $L_m$ considers only first-order scattering:
$$ L_m(\vec{x}, \vec{\omega}) = \int_{\vec{x}}^{\vec{x}_0} T(\vec{x}, \vec{x}') \sigma_s(\vec{x}') L_i(\vec{x}', \vec{\omega}) d\vec{x}' $$
where $\sigma_s$ is the scattering coefficient and $L_i$ is in-scattered radiance [S3].

In-scattered radiance is approximated as:
$$ L_i(\vec{x}', \vec{\omega}) = L_a V(\vec{x}', \vec{\omega}) $$
where $L_a$ is constant ambient radiance and $V$ is fractional visibility [S3].

Fractional visibility $V$ is computed using a backward-peaked cone phase function $\Phi_\theta$:
$$ V(\vec{x}', \vec{\omega}) = \int_{4\pi} T(\vec{x}', \vec{x}_{0,i}) \Phi_\theta(\vec{\omega}, \vec{\omega}_i) d\vec{\omega}_i $$
where $\vec{x}_{0,i}$ is the point where the ray leaves the volume [S3].

The phase function is:
$$ \Phi_\theta(\vec{\omega}, \vec{\omega}_i) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2\pi(1-\cos \theta)} & \text{if } \langle \vec{\omega}, \vec{\omega}_i \rangle > \cos(\theta) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
where $\vec{\omega}$ is the viewing direction and $\vec{\omega}_i$ are directions within the cone [S3].

Transmittance is:
$$ T(\vec{x}_a, \vec{x}_b) = e^{-\tau(\vec{x}_a, \vec{x}_b)} $$
where optical thickness $\tau(\vec{x}_a, \vec{x}_b) = \int_{\vec{x}_a}^{\vec{x}_b} \sigma_t(\vec{x}) d\vec{x}$ and $\sigma_t = \sigma_a + \sigma_s$ [S3].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Compute Proxy Geometry:** On the CPU, generate view-aligned slice planes intersecting the volume bounding box [S3].
2.  **Initialize Buffers:** Clear Eye Buffer and both Occlusion Buffers to zero [S3].
3.  **For each slice** (from front to back):
    a.  **Render to Eye Buffer:**
        i.   Render the slice quad.
        ii.  In the fragment shader, sample the volume texture at the fragment's world position to get color $C$ and opacity $\alpha$.
        iii. Read the current occlusion factor $V$ from the *current* Occlusion Buffer at the fragment's screen position.
        iv.  Compute the contribution: $C_{out} = C \cdot V \cdot L_a \cdot \alpha$.
        v.   Blend $C_{out}$ into the Eye Buffer using front-to-back compositing: $C_{eye} = C_{eye} + C_{out} (1 - C_{eye, \alpha})$. Update Eye Buffer alpha.
    b.  **Update Occlusion Buffer:**
        i.   Render the slice quad again, targeting the *next* Occlusion Buffer.
        ii.  Compute the correction factor $\vec{c}$ in the vertex shader to handle perspective distortion:
             $$ \vec{c} = P \begin{bmatrix} x_{scale} \\ y_{scale} \\ z_{scale} \end{bmatrix} $$
             where $P$ is the projection matrix and the vector components are derived from the view-space z-coordinate of the slice vertices [S3].
        iii. In the fragment shader, generate sample positions $\vec{p}$ on a disk with radius $r = d \tan(\theta)$ using a uniform grid [S3].
        iv.  Compute the NDC sample position:
             $$ \vec{p}_d = \vec{x}_{y,d} + \vec{c} \otimes \vec{p} $$
             where $\vec{x}_{y,d}$ is the fragment's NDC position, $\otimes$ is component-wise multiplication, and $\vec{p}$ is the disk sample [S3].
        v.   Convert to texture coordinates:
             $$ \vec{p}_t = 0.5 \cdot \vec{p}_d + \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} $$
        vi.  Read the previous occlusion factor $V_{prev}$ from the *previous* Occlusion Buffer at $\vec{p}_t$.
        vii. Compute transmittance $T$ using the approximate distance $d$ (or exact distance $d \sqrt{1 + \|\vec{p}\|^2/d^2}$ for higher precision, though $d$ is sufficient for performance) [S3].
        viii. Accumulate weighted samples: $V_{new} = \text{average}(V_{prev} \cdot T)$ over all grid samples [S3].
        ix.  Write $V_{new}$ to the *next* Occlusion Buffer.
    c.  **Swap Buffers:** Swap the current and next Occlusion Buffers [S3].
4.  **Finalize:** Copy the Eye Buffer to the frame buffer, converting from floating-point to fixed-point [S3].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def directional_occlusion_shading(volume, transfer_function, theta, d, slice_count, sample_grid_size):
    # Initialize buffers
    eye_buffer = create_floating_point_buffer(width, height)
    occlusion_buf_1 = create_floating_point_buffer(width, height)
    occlusion_buf_2 = create_floating_point_buffer(width, height)
    
    current_occlusion = occlusion_buf_1
    next_occlusion = occlusion_buf_2
    
    # Precompute correction factor components based on projection matrix P and view Z
    # c_x, c_y, c_z derived from P and slice view-space Z
    correction_factor = compute_correction_factor(P, view_z)
    
    # Generate uniform grid samples on a disk of radius r = d * tan(theta)
    samples = generate_uniform_disk_samples(sample_grid_size, radius=d * tan(theta))
    
    for i in range(slice_count):
        # 1. Render to Eye Buffer
        render_slice_to_eye(
            slice_geometry[i],
            volume,
            transfer_function,
            eye_buffer,
            current_occlusion,
            ambient_radiance=L_a
        )
        
        # 2. Update Occlusion Buffer
        render_slice_to_occlusion(
            slice_geometry[i],
            volume,
            current_occlusion,
            next_occlusion,
            correction_factor,
            samples,
            d
        )
        
        # 3. Swap Buffers
        swap(current_occlusion, next_occlusion)
        
    # 4. Copy to Framebuffer
    copy_to_framebuffer(eye_buffer)

def render_slice_to_eye(slice, volume, tf, eye_buf, occlusion_buf, L_a):
    for fragment in slice:
        # Sample volume
        color, alpha = sample_volume(volume, fragment.world_pos, tf)
        
        # Read occlusion factor
        v = read_buffer(occlusion_buf, fragment.screen_pos)
        
        # Compute contribution
        contribution = color * v * L_a * alpha
        
        # Front-to-back compositing
        eye_color, eye_alpha = read_buffer(eye_buf, fragment.screen_pos)
        new_alpha = eye_alpha + contribution.alpha * (1 - eye_alpha)
        new_color = eye_color + contribution.color * (1 - eye_alpha)
        
        write_buffer(eye_buf, fragment.screen_pos, new_color, new_alpha)

def render_slice_to_occlusion(slice, volume, prev_occlusion, next_occlusion, c, samples, d):
    for fragment in slice:
        accumulated_v = 0.0
        count = 0
        
        for p in samples:
            # Compute NDC sample position
            p_ndc = fragment.ndc_pos + c * p # Component-wise multiplication
            
            # Convert to texture coordinates
            p_tex = 0.5 * p_ndc + [0.5, 0.5]
            
            # Read previous occlusion
            v_prev = read_buffer(prev_occlusion, p_tex)
            
            # Approximate transmittance using inter-slice distance d
            # T = exp(-sigma_t * d). Assuming sigma_t is implicitly handled or normalized in V
            # The source notes say "attenuate by transmittance (using approx distance d)"
            # In the specific DOS derivation, V is fractional visibility. 
            # The attenuation is T(x', x_0,i). 
            # Source [S3] says: "attenuating each sample by the transmittance computed using the distance... 
            # or approximating it with the inter-slice distance d"
            t = exp(-sigma_t * d) # sigma_t is extinction coefficient
            
            accumulated_v += v_prev * t
            count += 1
            
        v_new = accumulated_v / count
        write_buffer(next_occlusion, fragment.screen_pos, v_new)
```

## Subtopics

### Elliptical Cone Blurring for Ambient Occlusion
An extension of slice-based occlusion shading that replaces the radial blurring cone with an elliptical cone [S2, unofficial]. This allows user control over the direction of shadows and ambient occlusion by adjusting the cone's angle and height (distance to the light), rather than being bound to the view direction [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Interactive. Performance depends on the number of slices, resolution, and sample count per occlusion update.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Low. Requires two additional floating-point buffers (Eye and two Occlusion buffers) in addition to the volume texture [S1, S3].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   Stag Beetle (1046 slices, 80° aperture): 10.6 FPS [S3].
    *   Brain MRI (256x256x160, 1000 slices): 3.7 FPS (DOS) vs 1.6 FPS (Lambertian) [S3].
    *   Carp CT (256x288x512, 220 slices): 48.3 FPS (DOS) vs 8.8 FPS (Lambertian) [S3].
    *   Visible Male (512x512x302, 962 slices): 5.0 FPS (DOS) vs 25.7 FPS (Lambertian) [S3].
    *   Engine Block CT (256x256x128, 679 slices): 5.6 FPS (DOS) vs 5.6 FPS (Lambertian) [S3].
    *   Hand CT (244x124x257, 619 slices): 13.3 FPS (DOS) vs 16.0 FPS (Lambertian) [S3].
    *   Tree CT (512x512x512, 1563 slices): 59.0 FPS (DOS) vs 29.1 FPS (Lambertian) [S3] [S3].
*   **Strengths:** Interactive frame rates without precomputation; supports interactive clipping planes and transfer function changes; accentuates concavities and depth discontinuities better than Lambertian shading; handles semi-transparent materials [S3].
*   **Limitations:** Bound to the slice-based rendering paradigm; supports only a single light source at the camera position; perspective distortion requires correction; inter-frame artifacts can occur with camera movement if sample grid is not symmetric [S1, S3].
*   **Scaling:** Multiple Render Targets (MRT) increase performance by 44-99% compared to Single Render Target (SRT), especially for low sample counts. For high sample counts, memory bandwidth becomes the bottleneck [S3].

## Background
Directional Occlusion Shading was introduced by Mathias Schott, Vincent Pegoraro, Charles Hansen, Kévin Boulanger, and Kadi Bouatouch in 2009 at the Eurographics/ IEEE-VGTC Symposium on Visualization [S3]. It was published in the Journal of Computer Graphics Forum (Eurographics) in 2009 [S3]. The method is derived from the radiative transport equation, simplifying it using a backward-peaked cone phase function to approximate in-scattered radiance [S3]. It is used in interactive direct volume rendering to provide perceptual cues similar to ambient occlusion for solid and transparent surface-like features [S3].
