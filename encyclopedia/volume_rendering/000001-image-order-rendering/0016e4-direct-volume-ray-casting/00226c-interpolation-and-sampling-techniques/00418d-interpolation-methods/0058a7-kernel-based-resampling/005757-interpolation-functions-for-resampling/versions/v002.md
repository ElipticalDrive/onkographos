# Interpolation Functions for Resampling

Also called: Cubic-spline

## Overview
Interpolation functions for resampling are mathematical functions used to estimate continuous values between discrete samples for edge reconstruction and shading [S1, unofficial]. They differ from general interpolation schemes by focusing on the specific error characteristics of the resampling chain (acquisition plus reconstruction) relative to the point-spread function (PSF) [S1, unofficial]. Unlike [Lanczos Resampling](11246-lanczos-resampling.md) which uses windowed sinc functions, these methods (linear, quadratic, cubic-spline) are evaluated based on their ability to accurately locate step edges and their sensitivity to noise [S1, unofficial].

## Shared principles
The accuracy of an interpolation function is evaluated by its ability to reconstruct a step edge. The error in edge location estimation grows approximately with the third power of the sample distance relative to the PSF width [S1, unofficial]. To minimize artifacts such as ripples on oblique surfaces, the sample distance should be at most 0.43 times the width of the PSF to meet the Nyquist criterion [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Linear Interpolation
Linear interpolation connects sample points with straight lines. In the context of edge location estimation, it performs better than cubic-spline interpolation but worse than quadratic re-sampling [S1, unofficial]. It is less sensitive to noise than cubic-spline but more sensitive than quadratic [S1, unofficial].

### Quadratic Re-sampling
Quadratic re-sampling uses second-degree polynomials to interpolate between samples. It yields the smallest edge location error among the common interpolation functions [S1, unofficial]. Additionally, it is less sensitive to noise in the data compared to linear and cubic-spline functions [S1, unofficial]. However, in a general grey-value reconstruction context (without edge location constraints), some evaluations suggest it may be the worst technique for grey-value accuracy compared to cubic-spline [S1, unofficial].

### Cubic-Spline Interpolation
Cubic-spline interpolation uses third-degree polynomials. While it may provide better high-frequency grey-value reconstruction in some contexts [S1, unofficial], it causes overshoot in step responses, producing negative values [S1, unofficial]. This is invalid for MR data due to the complex modulus operator in reconstruction, which cannot represent negative values [S1, unofficial]. Consequently, cubic-spline interpolation yields the largest edge location error [S1, unofficial].

### Edge location estimation via mean thresholding
This is a method for estimating the position of a 1D step edge rather than a distinct interpolation function itself. It estimates the edge position by finding the coordinate where the re-sampled grey-value equals the arithmetic mean of the grey-values on both sides of the step [S1, unofficial]. The steps are: 1. Re-sample discrete samples across the step using an interpolation function. 2. Compute the mean of the grey-values on the low and high sides of the step. 3. Locate the coordinate where the re-sampled curve intersects this mean value [S1, unofficial]. When using this method, quadratic re-sampling yields the smallest edge location error, linear interpolation performs better than cubic-spline but worse than quadratic, and cubic-spline interpolation yields the largest error [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The evaluation of these interpolation functions in the context of edge reconstruction and volume rendering originates from research into medical imaging applications, specifically CT and MR data [S1, unofficial]. The analysis highlights the trade-off between grey-value reconstruction accuracy and edge location accuracy, noting that the "best" interpolation for grey-values (cubic-spline) is often the worst for edge location [S1, unofficial]. The phenomenon of ripple artifacts due to large sample distances was also documented in this context [S1, unofficial].
