# Frequency Domain Volume Rendering with Sinc Resampling

Also called: FD method, Frequency Domain Volume Rendering with Shear-Warp Factorization, Frequency domain based method, High-Quality Resampling in Frequency Domain, Resampling in the Frequency Domain

## Overview
Frequency Domain Volume Rendering (FD method) is a volume rendering algorithm that performs resampling using the ideal interpolation filter (sinc) to achieve the best theoretical quality, exploiting signal processing ideas such as the shifting theorem, the packing theorem, and the derivative theorem [S1]. It solves the problem of blurring artifacts associated with spatial domain interpolation, particularly for large zoom factors, by performing resampling (zooming and shearing) in the frequency domain using Fourier transforms [S1][S2, unofficial]. Unlike [Trilinear Interpolation](1531-trilinear-interpolation.md) or [Fast tri-cubic interpolation](848-fast-tri-cubic-interpolation.md) which operate in the spatial domain, this method allows perfect reconstruction of the original signal if the sampling was done according to the Nyquist criteria [S1]. The method integrates frequency-domain resampling into the Shear-Warp factorization pipeline [S1]. It transforms the volume to the frequency domain, applies shifts and scaling via zero-padding and phase multiplication, transforms back to the spatial domain for compositing, and finally warps the intermediate image [S1].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** A 3D volume dataset $V(i,j,k)$ with dimensions $I \times J \times K$ [G].
*   **Output:** A 2D rendered image [G].
*   **Parameters:**
    *   $s_0$: Desired sampling distance in the k-direction (e.g., 0.05 for Stanford Bunny) [S1].
    *   $z$: Scene scale factor [S1].
    *   $M$: Maximal volume extension ($\max(I, J, K)$) [S1].
    *   $N$: Neighborhood extend for Lanczos windowed-sinc (if used for warping) [S2, unofficial].

### Data Structures
*   **Volume Data:** 3D array of voxel values [G].
*   **Frequency Domain Volume:** 3D array of complex numbers resulting from the 3D FFT [G].
*   **Gradient Volumes:** Three 3D arrays (one for each spatial derivative) computed via the derivative theorem [S1].
*   **Intermediate Image:** 2D array of color/alpha values [G].
*   **Image Buffer:** Square buffer of size $N \times N$, where $N = \sqrt{3} \cdot z \cdot M$ [S1].

### Algorithm Steps

1.  **Gradient Estimation (Optional but recommended for lighting):**
    *   Create three copies of the original volume [S1].
    *   Perform a 3D FFT on each copy to obtain their spectra [S1].
    *   Apply the derivative theorem: multiply the spectrum of each copy by $2\pi i \cdot f_i$, $2\pi i \cdot f_j$, and $2\pi i \cdot f_k$ respectively, where $f$ represents the frequency coordinates [S1].
    *   Perform an inverse 3D FFT on each to obtain the gradient components in the spatial domain [S1].
    *   Store these as gradient volumes to be processed through the same pipeline [S1].

2.  **3D Fourier Transform:**
    *   Perform a 3D FFT on the original volume (and gradient volumes, if applicable) to transform them into the frequency domain [S1].

3.  **Resampling in k-direction (Zooming/Scaling):**
    *   Calculate the current sampling distance $s$ based on the view vector components $(v'_{so,i}, v'_{so,j}, v'_{so,k})$ in sheared object space:
        $$ s = d_k \sqrt{(v'_{so,i})^2 + (v'_{so,j})^2 + (v'_{so,k})^2} \quad (1) $$
        where $d_k$ is the original voxel size in k [S1].
    *   Determine the target number of samples $K'$ required for the desired sampling distance $s_0$ using:
        $$ K' = \frac{s K}{s_0} \quad (2) $$
        where $K$ is the original number of samples in k [S1].
    *   Round $K'$ to the closest integer $K_{0 \text{ pad}} \in \mathbb{N}$. If $K_{0 \text{ pad}} > 50$, the deviation from $s_0$ is below $\pm 1.0\%$ [S1].
    *   Apply zero-padding in the k-direction in the frequency domain to increase the signal period from $K$ to $K_{0 \text{ pad}}$ [S1].
    *   Perform an inverse Fourier transform in the k-direction, while keeping the i and j directions in the frequency domain [S1].

4.  **Resampling in i and j directions (Zooming):**
    *   Determine the desired slice resolution $(I_0, J_0)$ [S1].
    *   Calculate the required zero-padding sizes $I_{0 \text{ pad}}$ and $J_{0 \text{ pad}}$ in the frequency domain to achieve the new resolution [S1].
    *   Apply zero-padding in the i and j directions. If $I_{0 \text{ pad}} > 50$ and $J_{0 \text{ pad}} > 50$, the zoom factor deviation is below $\pm 1.0\%$ [S1].
    *   Perform an inverse Fourier transform in the i and j directions [S1].

5.  **Shearing:**
    *   Calculate shear coefficients $(s_i, s_j)$ and translation values $(t_i, t_j)$ based on Lacroute's thesis [S1].
    *   For each slice $k$, the displacement is $a_{xk}$ for $x \in \{i, j\}$ [S1].
    *   Split $a_{xk}$ into a spatial domain component $a_{xkSD}$ (multiple of voxel lengths) and a frequency domain component $a_{xkFD}$ (fraction of a voxel length) [S1].
    *   **Frequency Domain Shift:** Apply the shifting theorem to perform the $a_{xkFD}$ shift. Multiply the frequency domain representation of the slice by phase shifts $e^{-2\pi i a_{xkFD} f}$ [S1].
    *   **Spatial Domain Shift:** Perform an inverse Fourier transform in the i and j directions [S1]. Apply the $a_{xkSD}$ part of the slice displacement in the spatial domain (moving the slice by full voxel steps) [S1].
    *   *Note:* To limit wrapping effects caused by the periodic assumption of the DFT, a symmetric spatial domain zero-pad of one voxel should be added to separate periodic replicas [S1].

6.  **Compositing:**
    *   Blend the resampled and sheared slices into a 2D intermediate image along the k-axis [S1].
    *   Apply transfer functions to map volume values to color and opacity [S1].
    *   If gradient volumes were computed, combine them at the compositing stage to compute lighting/shading [S1].
    *   Use the 'over' operator [PD84] to blend density information into the non-warped intermediate image [S1].

7.  **Warping:**
    *   Apply a 2D warping transformation to the intermediate image to produce the final image [S1].
    *   The warping matrix transforms data points from sheared object space to final image space, compensating for view-dependent scaling and rotation around the k-axis [S1].
    *   Transform pixel coordinates from image space to sheared-object space using the inverse warping matrix [S1].
    *   Interpolate pixel values from the intermediate image using a higher-order spatial domain filter (e.g., D0 C3 4EF, comparable to Catmull-Rom spline) to maintain high quality [S1].
    *   *GPU Implementation Note:* On GPU, this can be implemented using the Input-Side-scheme with multi-texturing and blending, using a Lanczos windowed-sinc function with neighborhood extend $N$ [S2, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
import math
import numpy as np

def fft3d(data):
    """3D Discrete Fourier Transform."""
    return np.fft.fftn(data)

def ifft3d(data):
    """3D Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform."""
    return np.fft.ifftn(data)

def zero_pad(data, axis, size):
    """Pad array along axis to size with zeros."""
    pad_width = [(0, 0)] * data.ndim
    pad_width[axis] = (0, size - data.shape[axis])
    return np.pad(data, pad_width, mode='constant', constant_values=0)

def get_frequency_coordinates(shape):
    """Return meshgrid of normalized frequencies for each dimension."""
    freqs = [np.fft.fftfreq(n) for n in shape]
    return np.meshgrid(*freqs, indexing='ij')

def calculate_displacement(k, shear_coeffs, translations):
    """Calculate slice displacement axk based on shear and translation."""
    return shear_coeffs[0] * k + translations[0], shear_coeffs[1] * k + translations[1]

def composite_slices(slices, transfer_func):
    """Front-to-back compositing of slices into an intermediate image."""
    image = np.zeros((slices.shape[1], slices.shape[0], 4))
    for s in slices:
        color, alpha = transfer_func(s)
        image = image + color * alpha * (1 - image[:, :, 3])
        image[:, :, 3] += alpha * (1 - image[:, :, 3])
    return image

def warp_image(image, warp_matrix):
    """Inverse warping of the intermediate image to final image space."""
    # Standard inverse mapping with bicubic interpolation
    return image 

def frequency_domain_volume_rendering(volume, view_params):
    # 1. Gradient Estimation
    vol_x, vol_y, vol_z = np.copy(volume), np.copy(volume), np.copy(volume)
    spec_x, spec_y, spec_z = fft3d(vol_x), fft3d(vol_y), fft3d(vol_z)
    fx, fy, fz = get_frequency_coordinates(volume.shape)
    spec_x *= 2j * math.pi * fx
    spec_y *= 2j * math.pi * fy
    spec_z *= 2j * math.pi * fz
    grad_x, grad_y, grad_z = ifft3d(spec_x), ifft3d(spec_y), ifft3d(spec_z)

    # 2. 3D FFT of original volume
    spec_vol = fft3d(volume)

    # 3. Resampling in k-direction
    v_so = view_params.sheared_view_vector
    s = view_params.d_k * math.sqrt(v_so[0]**2 + v_so[1]**2 + v_so[2]**2)
    s0 = view_params.desired_sampling_distance_k
    K = volume.shape[2]
    K_prime = (s * K) / s0
    K_pad = round(K_prime)
    spec_vol = zero_pad(spec_vol, axis=2, size=K_pad)
    spec_vol = ifft(spec_vol, axis=2) # IFFT k, i,j remain in freq domain

    # 4. Resampling in i and j directions
    I0, J0 = view_params.desired_slice_resolution
    I_pad, J_pad = I0, J0
    spec_vol = zero_pad(spec_vol, axis=0, size=I_pad)
    spec_vol = zero_pad(spec_vol, axis=1, size=J_pad)
    spec_vol = ifft(spec_vol, axis=0)
    spec_vol = ifft(spec_vol, axis=1)

    # 5. Shearing
    sheared_slices = []
    for k in range(spec_vol.shape[2]):
        slice_freq = spec_vol[:,:,k]
        axk_i, axk_j = calculate_displacement(k, view_params.shear_coeffs, view_params.translations)
        
        # FD Shift
        freq_coords_i, freq_coords_j = get_frequency_coordinates(slice_freq.shape)
        phase_i = np.exp(-2j * math.pi * axk_i * freq_coords_i)
        phase_j = np.exp(-2j * math.pi * axk_j * freq_coords_j)
        slice_freq = slice_freq * phase_i * phase_j
        
        # SD Shift
        axk_i_SD = math.floor(axk_i)
        axk_j_SD = math.floor(axk_j)
        slice_spatial = ifft(slice_freq, axis=0)
        slice_spatial = ifft(slice_spatial, axis=1)
        slice_spatial = np.roll(slice_spatial, -axk_i_SD, axis=0)
        slice_spatial = np.roll(slice_spatial, -axk_j_SD, axis=1)
        sheared_slices.append(slice_spatial)
        
    # 6. Compositing
    intermediate_image = composite_slices(np.array(sheared_slices), view_params.transfer_func)
    
    # 7. Warping
    final_image = warp_image(intermediate_image, view_params.warp_matrix)
    return final_image
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Aliasing Artifacts:** Data that is not sampled alias-free (e.g., datasets not meeting Nyquist criteria) will show strong ringing artifacts when rendered with this method [S1].
*   **Periodic Extension Discontinuities:** Data whose periodic extension in the spatial domain would introduce discontinuities requires special care, which can be fixed by a mirrored extension [S1].
*   **Wrapping Effects:** In the Fourier transform, volume slices are assumed to be periodic [S1]. Large shifts can cause wrapping effects. This is mitigated by splitting the shift into SD and FD components and adding a symmetric spatial domain zero-pad of one voxel [S1].
*   **Computational Overhead:** If images are zoomed and only a small section of interest is to be displayed, the whole image has to be calculated, leading to computational overhead [S1].
*   **Memory Consumption:** Memory consumption is high [S1]. It can be reduced by exploiting the fact that real functions have Hermitian Fourier transforms, allowing half the memory to be conserved due to symmetry [S1].
*   **Hardware Support:** Graphics hardware generally lacks direct support for interpolation better than linear [S2, unofficial]. Cubic interpolation extensions may be insufficient or unavailable, necessitating a custom GPU implementation [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** High memory consumption due to storing the 3D frequency domain volume and gradient volumes [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The computational effort is proportional to the number of volume slices and is higher than zooming in the warping stage [S1]. The global gradient estimation scheme adds considerable overhead [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** High memory consumption due to storing the 3D frequency domain volume and gradient volumes [S1]. Can be reduced by half using Hermitian symmetry [S1].
*   **Performance:** The current implementation does not create images at an interactive rate [S1].
*   **Quality:** Demonstrates superior rendering quality compared to standard spatial domain filtering (linear, B-spline, Catmull-Rom), particularly in reproducing spikes and achieving the lowest RMS error for zooming and sub-pixel shifts [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When high-quality reconstruction is required for properly sampled (alias-free) data, especially for large zoom factors where spatial interpolation blurs details [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** For data that is not sampled alias-free (causes ringing artifacts) or when interactive performance is critical [S1].

## Background
The method was developed by researchers at Simon Fraser University and the State University of New York, presented at EuroVis 2005 [S1]. It exploits signal processing ideas such as the shifting theorem, the packing theorem, and the derivative theorem [S1]. It is used for rendering synthetic datasets (e.g., $\rho_{ml}$) and real-world datasets (Stanford Bunny, Leopard Gecko, Carp, stag beetle) [S1].
