# Local Phong Shading with Semi-Analytical Integration

Also called: Quadratic Shading Integration, Semi-Analytical Volume Rendering with Shading

## Overview
This method extends semi-analytical volume rendering by incorporating local Phong diffuse shading into the integration process [S1]. It solves the problem of accurate lighting in volume rendering by reconstructing the shading function as a quadratic polynomial along the ray, allowing for analytical integration of the light contribution [S1]. It sets itself apart from scalar-only semi-analytical methods by requiring at most one additional sampling point per cell to capture the shading extremum, while maintaining the topological accuracy of the underlying trilinear interpolation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Trilinear interpolation coefficients for the scalar field $s(x,y,z)$ within a hexahedral cell; light direction vector $\vec{L} = (l_x, l_y, l_z)$; preintegration tables $C_{front}$, $C_{back}$, and $\alpha$; ray segment parameters $t_{start}, t_{end}$ within the cell.
*   **Outputs:** Integrated color contribution $I$ for the ray segment, accounting for shading.

**Data Structures:**
The method relies on the trilinear coefficients $s_{ijk}$ (where $i,j,k \in \{0,1\}$) defining the scalar field $s(x,y,z)$ in the unit cube. The shading function is represented by quadratic coefficients $e, f, g$ for $d(t) = e t^2 + f t + g$.

**Steps:**

1.  **Compute Gradient and Shading Polynomial:**
    Compute the analytical gradient $\nabla s(x,y,z)$ from the trilinear coefficients. The partial derivatives are:
    $$
    \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} = \sum_{i,j,k} c_{ijk} \frac{\partial B_{ijk}}{\partial x}
    $$
    (Specific expansion provided in [S1] Eq. 11).
    Compute the diffuse shading function $d(x,y,z) = \nabla s(x,y,z) \cdot \vec{L}$.
    Parametrize the ray as $x(t), y(t), z(t)$ for $t \in [0, L]$ (local cell coordinates). Substitute into $d(x,y,z)$ to obtain the quadratic form:
    $$
    d(t) = e \cdot t^2 + f \cdot t + g
    $$
    where $e, f, g$ are computed from the gradient coefficients and ray direction [S1].

2.  **Find Local Extremum:**
    Solve for the local extremum $r$ of $d(t)$ by setting the derivative to zero:
    $$
    \frac{d d(t)}{dt} = 2e t + f = 0 \implies r = -\frac{f}{2e}
    $$
    If $e=0$, the function is linear and has no internal extremum (set $r$ outside the interval or handle as linear case) [S1].

3.  **Sample Shading Function:**
    Sample $d(t)$ at the cell entry ($t=0$), cell exit ($t=L$), and the local extremum $r$ (if $0 < r < L$). This requires at most one additional sample per cell compared to the scalar-only method [S1].

4.  **Split Integration Interval:**
    Split the ray segment $[0, L]$ at the extremum $r$ into $[0, r]$ and $[r, L]$ (if $r$ is within the interval). The integral becomes:
    $$
    I = \int_0^r c(s(t)) d(t) e^{-\int_0^t \tau(s(u)) du} dt + \int_r^L c(s(t)) d(t) e^{-\int_0^t \tau(s(u)) du} dt
    $$
    [S1]

5.  **Approximate with Preintegration:**
    Approximate the scalar field $s(t)$ as linear between sample points. For an interval $[t_1, t_2]$ with scalar values $s_1, s_2$ and shading values $d_1, d_2$, the contribution is approximated using preintegration functions:
    $$
    I \approx d(s(t_1)) C_{front}(s(t_1), s(t_2), t_2-t_1) + d(s(t_2)) C_{back}(s(t_1), s(t_2), t_2-t_1)
    $$
    For the full cell with extremum $r$, the total integral is:
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    I \approx & d(s(0)) C_{front}(s(0), s(r), r) + d(s(r)) C_{back}(s(0), s(r), r) \\
    & + (1 - \alpha(s(0), s(r), r)) \times \\
    & [ d(s(r)) C_{front}(s(r), s(L), L-r) + d(s(L)) C_{back}(s(r), s(L), L-r) ]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    [S1]

6.  **Preintegration Functions:**
    The functions $C_{front}, C_{back}, \alpha$ are defined as:
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    C_{back}(a, b, l) &= \int_0^1 c((1-t') a + t' b) t' e^{-\int_0^{t'} \tau((1-t') a + t' b) l du} dt' \\
    C_{front}(a, b, l) &= \int_0^1 c((1-t') a + t' b) (1-t') e^{-\int_0^{t'} \tau((1-t') a + t' b) l du} dt' \\
    \alpha(a, b, l) &= 1 - e^{-\int_0^1 \tau((1-t') b + t' a) l dt'}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    where $a, b$ are scalar values at interval endpoints, $l$ is the interval length, and $c(\cdot), \tau(\cdot)$ are the color and absorption transfer functions [S1].

**Pitfalls:**
*   **Discontinuity:** The reconstructed scalar field is only $C^0$ continuous across cell faces. Consequently, the gradient $\nabla s$ is discontinuous, causing shading discontinuities across cell boundaries [S1].
*   **Extremum Handling:** If the extremum $r$ lies outside the cell interval $[0, L]$, the shading function is monotonic within the cell, and no split is needed.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** Adds at most one additional sample per cell compared to scalar-only semi-analytical rendering. The computational cost of evaluating the quadratic shading polynomial is negligible compared to the preintegration table lookups.
*   **Performance:** Maintains the high performance of semi-analytical methods while providing significantly improved visual quality for lit volumes [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Captures local lighting variations within cells, reducing shading artifacts common in standard ray casting.
*   **Limitations:** Shading discontinuities at cell faces may be visible in some cases. Not given in the sources for specific hardware performance metrics.

## Background
This method is an extension of the semi-analytical volume rendering approach for AMR (Adaptive Mesh Refinement) data, presented by Marchesin et al. in 2009 [S1]. It builds on the trilinear interpolation-based semi-analytical integration described in the parent article.
