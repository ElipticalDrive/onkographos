# Adaptive Filter Size for Rendering Time Reduction

Also called: Adaptive interpolation, Adaptive interpolation filter, Energy-based adaptive filtering, Energy-based filter selection

## Overview
Adaptive Filter Size for Rendering Time Reduction is a technique to reduce rendering time by adaptively selecting smaller interpolation filters for low-energy frequency components, maintaining image quality within a user-defined tolerance [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from standard interpolation schemes by dynamically switching between a high-quality large filter and a low-cost small filter based on the spectral energy of the input data, rather than using a fixed kernel for all samples [S2, unofficial]. This method is specifically designed for frequency-domain volume rendering, building on [Fast tri-cubic interpolation](848-fast-tri-cubic-interpolation.md) by optimizing the convolution cost during slice extraction.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D volume dataset transformed into its 3D frequency spectrum (complex floating-point values).
*   **Input:** A user-defined tolerance $\epsilon$ for the maximum acceptable mean square error.
*   **Input:** Two interpolation filters: a large filter $H_1$ (e.g., $3 \times 3 \times 3$) and a small filter $H_2$ (e.g., $1 \times 1 \times 1$).
*   **Output:** A rendered 2D slice image with reduced computational cost compared to using $H_1$ uniformly.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Spectrum:** A 3D array of complex numbers representing the Fourier transform of the volume.
*   **Mark Array:** A 3D boolean array of the same dimensions as the spectrum. `Mark[i,j,k]` is true if the voxel at `(i,j,k)` is eligible for the small filter $H_2$.

**Parameters:**
*   **Filter Sizes:** $H_1$ is typically $3 \times 3 \times 3$ (27 input samples per output). $H_2$ is typically $1 \times 1 \times 1$ (1 input sample per output) [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Tolerance ($\epsilon$):** User-defined bound for mean square error.
*   **Energy Distribution:** Empirically, 99% of the spectral energy is contained in about 10% of the frequency components [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The adaptive scheme approximates the result of filtering the entire spectrum $F$ by $H_1$ by splitting $F$ into two sets: $F_1$ (samples filtered by $H_1$) and $F_2$ (samples filtered by $H_2$), where $F_1 + F_2 = F$. The spatial domain result $f$ is approximated as:
$$ f \approx f_1 * h_1 + f_2 * h_2 $$
where $f_1 = F_1 * H_1$ and $f_2 = F_2 * H_2$ in the spatial domain. This can be rewritten to highlight the error term:
$$ f \approx f_1 * h_1 + f_2 * (h_2 - h_1) + f_2 * h_1 $$
$$ f \approx (F_1 + F_2) * H_1 + F_2 * (H_2 - H_1) $$
$$ f \approx F * H_1 + F_2 * (H_2 - H_1) $$
The term $F_2 * (H_2 - H_1)$ represents the error introduced by using $H_2$ for those components. The mean square error (MSE) upper bound is derived using Rayleigh’s theorem in the frequency domain [S2, unofficial]:
$$ \text{MSE} \le \frac{1}{L} h_{\max}^2 \int |f|^2 dx $$
Where:
*   $L$ is the length of the non-zero region of the signal $f$.
*   $h_{\max}$ is the maximum value of $|H_2 - H_1|$ in the spatial domain.
*   The integral $\int |f|^2 dx$ is the total energy of the signal.

To ensure the MSE is below the tolerance $\epsilon$, samples are marked for $H_2$ if their magnitude satisfies the error bound equation derived from the above inequality [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Preprocessing (Marking):**
    *   Iterate through every voxel in the 3D spectrum.
    *   Calculate the magnitude of the frequency component.
    *   Determine if the component satisfies the error bound equation for the user-defined tolerance.
    *   **Optimization:** To avoid testing all neighbors during rendering, modify the marking criteria: mark a voxel only if it satisfies the error bound AND all neighboring voxels within a distance equal to half the support of $H_1$ also satisfy the bound [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   Store the result in the Mark Array. This step is performed once after the volume is transformed to the frequency domain [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

2.  **Slice Extraction (Rendering):**
    *   For each sample point on the slice plane:
    *   Identify the spectrum voxels falling within the support of the larger filter $H_1$.
    *   **Standard Check:** If all spectrum voxels within the support of $H_1$ are marked in the Mark Array, use the small filter $H_2$ for this sample [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   **Optimized Check:** Due to the conservative marking in Step 1, it is sufficient to test only the spectrum voxel closest to the slice sample position. If that specific voxel is marked, it is guaranteed that all other voxels in the support of $H_1$ are also marked. Use $H_2$ if marked; otherwise, use $H_1$ [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def preprocess_spectrum(spectrum, tolerance, h_max, L, total_energy):
    """
    Marks spectrum voxels eligible for the small filter H2.
    spectrum: 3D array of complex numbers.
    tolerance: User-defined MSE bound.
    h_max: Maximum value of |H2 - H1| in spatial domain.
    L: Length of non-zero region of signal.
    total_energy: Integral of |f|^2.
    """
    # Calculate the threshold magnitude for a voxel to be marked.
    # From MSE <= (1/L) * h_max^2 * Integral(|f|^2 dx),
    # and assuming the error contribution of a single voxel is proportional to its magnitude squared.
    # The sources state: "those whose magnitude is small enough to satisfy equation (16)".
    # Equation (16) is not explicitly written, but derived from the MSE bound.
    # We assume a per-voxel energy contribution check.
    # Let E_voxel = |spectrum[i,j,k]|^2.
    # The condition is roughly: E_voxel * h_max^2 / L <= tolerance * (some scaling factor).
    # Since the exact constant is not given, we define a threshold based on the bound.
    # Note: The sources imply a direct magnitude check against a derived threshold.
    
    threshold = tolerance * L / (h_max * h_max) # Placeholder for exact constant from Eq 16
    
    # Determine support radius for H1. For 3x3x3, radius is 1.
    # Half support radius is 0.5. Neighbors within distance 0.5 means immediate neighbors?
    # The text says "neighboring voxels lying within a distance ... equal to one-half of the support".
    # For a 3x3x3 filter, support is 3 units wide. Half is 1.5.
    # Voxels within distance 1.5 include the voxel itself and its 6 face-neighbors, 12 edge-neighbors, 8 corner-neighbors?
    # Actually, "within a distance" usually implies Euclidean distance.
    # To implement "all neighbors within half support", we check a local neighborhood.
    
    marked = np.zeros_like(spectrum, dtype=bool)
    rows, cols, depth = spectrum.shape
    
    for i in range(rows):
        for j in range(cols):
            for k in range(depth):
                mag_sq = np.abs(spectrum[i, j, k]) ** 2
                
                # Check if this voxel satisfies the error bound
                if mag_sq <= threshold:
                    # Check neighbors within half support of H1
                    # Assuming H1 is 3x3x3, half support is 1.5.
                    # Neighbors within distance 1.5 from (i,j,k) are those with dx,dy,dz in {-1,0,1}
                    # such that sqrt(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2) <= 1.5.
                    # This includes (0,0,0), (1,0,0) type neighbors, but NOT (1,1,1) which is dist 1.732.
                    # So we check the 6 face neighbors and the center.
                    
                    is_safe = True
                    for di in [-1, 0, 1]:
                        for dj in [-1, 0, 1]:
                            for dk in [-1, 0, 1]:
                                if di == 0 and dj == 0 and dk == 0:
                                    continue
                                dist_sq = di**2 + dj**2 + dk**2
                                if dist_sq <= 2.25: # 1.5^2
                                    ni, nj, nk = i + di, j + dj, k + dk
                                    if 0 <= ni < rows and 0 <= nj < cols and 0 <= nk < depth:
                                        if np.abs(spectrum[ni, nj, nk]) ** 2 > threshold:
                                            is_safe = False
                                            break
                            if not is_safe: break
                        if not is_safe: break
                    
                    if is_safe:
                        marked[i, j, k] = True
    return marked

def render_slice(spectrum, marked, slice_points, h1_kernel, h2_kernel):
    """
    Renders a slice using adaptive filtering.
    slice_points: List of (x, y, z) coordinates in spectrum space.
    """
    image = []
    
    for point in slice_points:
        # Find the closest spectrum voxel to the slice sample
        closest_voxel = get_closest_voxel(point)
        
        # Optimized check: if the closest voxel is marked, use H2
        if marked[closest_voxel]:
            # Use small filter H2 (e.g., 1x1x1, just fetch the value)
            value = spectrum[closest_voxel] * h2_kernel_weight
        else:
            # Use large filter H1 (e.g., 3x3x3, fetch 27 neighbors)
            value = 0
            for neighbor in get_neighbors(closest_voxel, radius=1):
                value += spectrum[neighbor] * h1_kernel_weight(neighbor, closest_voxel)
        
        image.append(value)
        
    return image
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** The 3D spectrum is complex and requires floating-point representation. Using double precision consumes 16 times more memory than single precision, which is a significant cost [S3, unofficial].
*   **Ghosts:** Using the small filter $H_2$ uniformly causes strong ghosts in the image because the smaller filter has a less sharp cutoff in the spatial domain [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Testing Cost:** Testing all voxels in the larger filter support during slice extraction is expensive. The optimization of conservative marking (checking neighbors during preprocessing) avoids this runtime cost [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The cost of interpolation is $O(n^3)$ and is determined by the filter size [S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. A $3 \times 3 \times 3$ filter requires 27 input samples per output sample, while a $1 \times 1 \times 1$ filter requires 1 [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Adaptive filtering reduces average cost by using smaller filters for low-energy components. Since 99% of energy is in 10% of components, the majority of samples can potentially use the cheaper filter [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Significant rendering time reduction while maintaining high image quality within a user-defined tolerance [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires a preprocessing step (FFT and marking) which adds overhead. The memory cost for the complex spectrum is high [S3, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When rendering time is critical and the volume data has a sparse frequency spectrum (most energy in low frequencies).
*   **When not to use:** When memory is extremely constrained (due to complex spectrum storage) or when the volume data has high-frequency content distributed uniformly, reducing the benefit of adaptive filtering.

## Background
*   **Origin:** The method was introduced by Takashi Totsuka and Marc Levoy in their work on Frequency Domain Volume Rendering [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **History:** It is part of the broader frequency domain volume rendering pipeline, which transforms volume data to the frequency domain to perform shading and filtering operations more efficiently [S2, unofficial].
*   **Usage:** Used in frequency domain volume rendering systems to optimize the slice extraction step, which is often the bottleneck [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
