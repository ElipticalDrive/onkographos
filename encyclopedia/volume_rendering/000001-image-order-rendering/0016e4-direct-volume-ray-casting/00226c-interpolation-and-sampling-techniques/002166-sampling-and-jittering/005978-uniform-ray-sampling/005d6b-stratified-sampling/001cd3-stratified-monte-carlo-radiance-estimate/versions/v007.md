# Stratified Monte Carlo Radiance Estimate

Also called: RVS Stratified Estimate

## Overview
The Stratified Monte Carlo Radiance Estimate is an unbiased radiance estimation technique that uses stratified sampling to reduce variance compared to uniform sampling, particularly effective for degenerate distributions [S1, unofficial]. It partitions the ray depth interval $[t_n, t_f]$ into $k$ equal-sized bins and generates one sample per bin, avoiding the clustering and gaps inherent in pure uniform sampling [S1, unofficial]. This method sets itself apart from its siblings by focusing specifically on the variance reduction of the radiance integral along a single ray segment, whereas [Batch Stratified Sampling](2730-batch-stratified-sampling.md) optimizes for batched execution and [Stratified Ray Sampling](3014-stratified-ray-sampling.md) extends stratification to the spatial domain of the image plane.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Ray origin $\mathbf{o}$, ray direction $\mathbf{d}$, near clipping plane $t_n$, far clipping plane $t_f$, and number of samples $k$.
*   **Outputs:** A set of sample distances $\{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^k$ along the ray, and the corresponding 3D positions $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}_{i=1}^k$. The final radiance estimate $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ is computed from these samples.

**Data Structures:**
The method requires storing the bin boundaries and the generated samples.
*   `bins`: An array of size $k+1$ containing the boundaries $t_0, \dots, t_k$.
*   `samples`: An array of size $k$ containing the sampled distances $\tau_i$.
*   `positions`: An array of size $k$ containing the 3D positions $\mathbf{p}_i$.

**Steps:**
1.  **Bin Definition:** Divide the interval $[t_n, t_f]$ into $k$ equal-sized bins. The boundaries are defined as:
    $$ t_i = t_n + i \cdot \frac{t_f - t_n}{k}, \quad \text{for } i = 0, \dots, k $$
    This creates $k$ bins where the $i$-th bin spans $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$ [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

2.  **Sampling:** For each bin $i$ (where $i=1, \dots, k$), a sample $\tau_i$ is drawn.
    *   **Randomized (Unbiased):** A uniform random number $u_i \sim U[0, 1]$ is generated, and the sample is computed as:
        $$ \tau_i = t_{i-1} + u_i \cdot (t_i - t_{i-1}) $$
        This ensures the sample is uniformly distributed within the bin [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   **Deterministic (Midpoint):** The center of the bin is used:
        $$ \tau_i = 0.5 \cdot (t_{i-1} + t_i) $$
        This is a deterministic variant that removes randomness but may introduce aliasing if the scene has periodic structures [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

3.  **Position Calculation:** The 3D position $\mathbf{p}_i$ for each sample is calculated from the ray origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$:
    $$ \mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{o} + \tau_i \mathbf{d} $$

4.  **Radiance Estimation:** The stratified estimate of the radiance $\hat{C}_{\text{IW}}(\mathbf{r})$ is computed as [S1, unofficial]:
    $$ \hat{C}_{\text{IW}}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^k (t_i - t_{i-1}) c_{\mathbf{r}}(\tau_i) \frac{dF_{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} \bigg|_{t=\tau_i} $$
    where $c_{\mathbf{r}}(\tau_i)$ is the color contribution at sample $\tau_i$, and $\frac{dF_{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} \big|_{t=\tau_i}$ is the derivative of the transmittance or relevant radiance term with respect to distance at $\tau_i$. The term $(t_i - t_{i-1})$ represents the width of the bin, which is constant for uniform stratification: $\Delta t = \frac{t_f - t_n}{k}$.

**Parameters:**
*   $k$: Number of samples per ray. Typical values vary by scene complexity. In a 'Foggy' field experiment, $k=32$ samples matched the baseline performance of $k=256$ uniform samples. In a 'Wall' field experiment, only $k=4$ samples were needed to match the baseline [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** When $t_f - t_n$ is very small, floating-point precision issues may arise in bin boundary calculation.
*   **Degenerate Distributions:** The method is particularly effective for degenerate distributions where uniform sampling performs poorly [S1, unofficial].
*   **Bias:** The randomized version is unbiased. The deterministic midpoint version is biased but may have lower variance in specific smooth scenarios [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Antithetic Sampling](7196-antithetic-sampling.md)
Antithetic sampling is a variance reduction technique for estimating the derivative of a BSDF with respect to scene parameters when the integrand approximates an odd function. It exploits the symmetry of Normal Distribution Functions (NDFs) to make the derivative term an odd function, allowing paired samples to cancel out noise. It sets itself apart from general stratified sampling by targeting the specific symmetry of microfacet derivatives, whereas its sibling [Decomposed sampling](1374-decomposed-sampling.md) addresses cases where derivatives are even functions.

### Ratio Estimator with Equidistant Sampling (REES)
REES is a stochastic integrator that distributes samples at equal distance, jittered by noise, combined with a ratio estimator using analytic unoccluded radiance [S5]. It uses the basic framework of stratified sampling paired with the analytic unoccluded radiance computation [S5]. REES yields better convergence in unoccluded parts of the scene but leads to overall poor distribution of samples in occluded parts compared to MCWVDS [S5].

### Sample Trimmed Mean Estimator
This technique reduces variance from rare outliers in importance sampling by using a sample trimmed mean over each pixel, discarding extreme samples [S6]. It stores multiple independent, intermediate (low sample count) results. For each pixel, it discards the top 1-5% and bottom 1-5% of samples, then averages the remaining samples [S6]. The parameter is to discard 1% to 5% of the highest and lowest samples per pixel [S6]. A pitfall is that rare outliers can be generated through occasional bad sampling decisions. The trimmed mean trades a slight bias for a large reduction in variance, reducing overall error. The estimator remains consistent because the distribution approaches Gaussian for large sample counts, making the bias zero for symmetric distributions [S6].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(k)$ per ray, where $k$ is the number of samples. The complexity is linear in the number of samples.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(k)$ to store the samples and positions.
*   **Measured Performance:** In a 'Foggy' field experiment, approximately $k=32$ samples of the stratified estimate match the baseline performance of $k=256$ uniform samples [S1, unofficial]. In a 'Wall' field experiment, only $k=4$ samples of the stratified estimate are needed to match the baseline performance [S1, unofficial].
*   **Scaling:** The method scales linearly with the number of samples. It is effective for reducing variance without requiring a proportional increase in samples compared to uniform sampling.
*   **Strengths:** Reduces variance significantly compared to uniform sampling, especially for degenerate distributions. Unbiased when randomized.
*   **Limitations:** Requires computing bin boundaries and samples. The deterministic variant may introduce aliasing.
*   **When to use:** When variance reduction is critical and the integrand is smooth or degenerate. Particularly useful in differentiable rendering where stable gradients are needed [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When the scene has high-frequency details that require many samples, or when computational resources are extremely limited and $k$ must be very small.

## Background
The stratified Monte Carlo radiance estimate is based on the well-known technique of stratified sampling, which has been used in Monte Carlo rendering to reduce variance [S4]. The specific formulation for radiance estimation along a ray with stratified bins is detailed in [S1, unofficial]. The method has been applied in differentiable rendering contexts to improve the stability of gradients [S1, unofficial][S4]. Variants like REES have been explored for specific volume rendering scenarios [S5].
