# Hierarchical Stratified Sampling

Also called: Adaptive Stratified Sampling, Importance Stratified Sampling

## Overview
Hierarchical Stratified Sampling is a ray sampling technique used in volume rendering and neural radiance fields (NeRFs) to optimize the distribution of sample points along camera rays. It addresses the problem of inefficient uniform sampling by first establishing a baseline stratified coverage and then adaptively refining regions that contribute significantly to the final pixel color. Unlike standard stratified sampling, which divides the ray into fixed bins, this method leverages the contribution of initial samples to guide subsequent sampling density, thereby improving reconstruction quality and preventing overfitting [S1]. It builds on [Stratified Ray Sampling](3014-stratified-ray-sampling.md) by adding an adaptive, importance-driven refinement layer.

## How it works
**Inputs:** Camera ray geometry, initial volume representation (e.g., NeRF or voxel grid), and a target number of initial samples $N$.
**Outputs:** A set of sample positions and weights along the ray used to compute the final pixel color.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Ray Segments:** A list of intervals $[t_i, t_{i+1}]$ along the ray, each associated with an importance value or accumulated opacity/transmittance.
2.  **Sample List:** A sorted list of sample positions $t$ and their corresponding contributions.

**Steps:**
1.  **Initial Stratified Sampling:** Divide the camera ray into $N$ equally spaced bins (strata). Draw one sample uniformly at random from each bin [S1]. This provides a baseline coverage of the ray [S1].
2.  **Contribution Analysis:** Render the scene using these initial samples to determine which regions contributed most to the final pixel color. This involves aggregating the radiance and opacity contributions from the samples [S1].
3.  **Adaptive Refinement:** Identify regions with high contribution or high variance. Use a probabilistic function to determine the expected color value along the ray and sample more points around these high-contribution regions [S1]. This step is iterative or hierarchical, allowing for progressive refinement [S2].
4.  **Aggregation:** Combine the initial stratified samples and the refined adaptive samples to compute the final pixel color [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $N$: Number of initial strata. Typical values are not specified in the sources, but increasing $N$ improves initial coverage [S1].
*   Number of adaptive passes: In experiments, one uniform sampling pass followed by three adaptive sampling steps yielded fast accuracy increases [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Overfitting:** Using stratified samples helps prevent overfitting, which is a risk when sampling too sparsily or adaptively without baseline coverage [S1].
*   **View Dependency:** NeRFs may produce incorrect depth maps if they rely on all spatial points behind a reflecting surface rather than explaining view-dependent appearance directly [S1].
*   **Precision:** The accuracy of the estimated integral $\tilde{k}(t)$ depends on the number of adaptive steps [S2].

## Subtopics

### Stratified Adaptive Ray Sampling
This variant maintains one data structure per camera ray and uses stratified sampling by dividing the ray into $N$ equally-sized regions to obtain a sorted list of new samples [S2]. It alternates between uniform strata sampling and adaptive sampling in subsequent progressive rendering passes [S2]. Adaptive sampling targets non-sampled regions between segments, within segments to detect splits, and non-sampled regions close to the begin and end of the ray [S2]. In experiments, accuracy of $\tilde{k}(t)$ increased extremely fast when performing one uniform sampling, followed by three adaptive sampling steps in subsequent progressive rendering passes [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The initial stratified sampling is $O(N)$. The adaptive refinement adds complexity depending on the number of passes and the cost of evaluating the probabilistic function. With $P$ adaptive passes, the complexity is roughly $O(N + P \cdot M)$, where $M$ is the number of adaptive samples per pass [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the sample list and contribution data for each ray. For $R$ rays, this is $O(R \cdot (N + P \cdot M))$ [S2].
*   **Performance:** The method improves reconstruction quality by preventing overfitting and focusing samples on important regions [S1]. The adaptive approach allows for faster convergence of accuracy compared to uniform sampling alone [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Reduces overfitting, improves accuracy in complex scenes, and allows for progressive refinement [S1, S2].
*   **Limitations:** More complex to implement than uniform or simple stratified sampling. Requires multiple passes for optimal results [S2].
*   **When to Use:** When high-quality reconstruction is needed and computational resources allow for multiple sampling passes [S1, S2].
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications with strict latency constraints where adaptive sampling overhead is prohibitive [S2].

## Background
Hierarchical Stratified Sampling was introduced in the context of 3D geometric reconstruction and evaluation of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [S1]. It addresses the limitations of NeRFs in handling view-dependent appearance and incorrect depth maps by leveraging hierarchical sampling strategies [S1]. The method has been further developed in the context of view-dependent visibility optimization for Monte Carlo volume visualization [S2].
