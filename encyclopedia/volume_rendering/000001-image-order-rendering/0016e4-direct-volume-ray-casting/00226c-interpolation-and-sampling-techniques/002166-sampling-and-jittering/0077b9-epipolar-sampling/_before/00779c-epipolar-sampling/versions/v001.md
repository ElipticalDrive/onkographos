# Epipolar Sampling

Also called: Epipolar Sampling for Inscattering

## Overview
Epipolar sampling is a rendering strategy that places sample points in screen space to reduce the computational cost of evaluating the rendering equation for volumetric inscattering [S1, unofficial]. It determines sample locations based on abrupt changes in intensity at inscattering positions and uses filtering between epipolar lines to reconstruct the final image [S1, unofficial]. This approach yields a crisper appearance compared to uniform sampling but introduces specific artifacts related to the discrete placement of samples [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A volume dataset, camera parameters, light source position, and a screen-space grid.
*   **Output:** A rendered image with volumetric inscattering effects.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Screen-space sample grid:** A set of 2D coordinates $(u, v)$ corresponding to pixel locations.
*   **Epipolar lines:** Lines in screen space connecting the camera center projection to the light source projection (or defined by the geometry of the view and light).
*   **Filtering kernel:** A function used to interpolate or filter values between epipolar lines.

**Steps:**
1.  **Determine Sample Points:** Identify sample points in screen space. The selection is based on detecting abrupt changes in intensity at inscattering positions [S1, unofficial]. This implies an adaptive or edge-aware placement rather than a uniform grid.
2.  **Define Epipolar Lines:** For each sample point, define the corresponding epipolar line in screen space. This line represents the path along which inscattering is computed.
3.  **Ray Marching for Inscattering:** For each sample point, perform ray marching along the epipolar line towards the light source to calculate the inscattering contribution [S1, unofficial]. This involves integrating the scattering equation along the ray segment defined by the epipolar line.
4.  **Filtering:** Apply filtering between epipolar lines to reconstruct the final image intensity. This step interpolates the computed inscattering values from the discrete sample points to fill the gaps between epipolar lines [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The inscattering radiance $L_{insc}$ at a point $x$ along a ray is typically computed by integrating the scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, the phase function $p$, and the incident radiance $L_{inc}$:
$$ L_{insc}(x) = \int_{0}^{t} \sigma_s(x(t')) p(\omega_i, \omega_o) L_{inc}(x(t')) e^{-\int_{0}^{t'} \sigma_t(x(t'')) dt''} dt' $$
In epipolar sampling, the integration path is constrained to the epipolar line in screen space, and the integral is approximated at discrete sample points [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Sample density:** Determined by the threshold for "abrupt changes in intensity." Not given in the sources.
*   **Filtering kernel width:** Determines the extent of interpolation between epipolar lines. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Artifacts:** The technique can produce artifacts resulting from the discretization of low-resolution samples, similar to issues seen in dimensional min-max mipmap shadow mapping [S1, unofficial].
*   **Crispness vs. Smoothness:** While it provides a crisper appearance, the discrete nature of the samples may lead to visible seams or aliasing if the filtering is not adequate [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Partial Sum Tree Inscattering
A method presented by [25] to compute inscattering derived from a ray that increases smoothly along the same epipolar line toward the light source [S1, unofficial]. It is based on a partial sum tree, making the cost for computing inscattering only logarithmic along a view ray [S1, unofficial]. This variant optimizes the ray marching step of the base epipolar sampling method by accelerating the integration of inscattering contributions.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The base epipolar sampling method reduces computational cost compared to full volumetric ray marching by limiting samples to screen-space locations and using filtering [S1, unofficial]. The Partial Sum Tree variant reduces the cost of computing inscattering to logarithmic time along a view ray [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the screen-space sample grid and the partial sum tree (for the variant) [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Reduced computational cost; crisper appearance [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Potential artifacts from discretization; dependence on effective filtering [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When computational resources are limited but a crisp volumetric appearance is desired, and when the scene geometry allows for well-defined epipolar lines.

## Background
Epipolar sampling was proposed by Engelhardt [24] to address the high computational cost of rendering volumetric inscattering [S1, unofficial]. The technique was later improved by [25] to optimize the inscattering calculation using a partial sum tree [S1, unofficial]. It is used in the context of light shafts rendering for indoor scenes [S1, unofficial].
