# View-Dependent Transparency

Also called: Angle-Based Transparency, Image fading with incidence angle, Incidence angle fading

## Overview
View-Dependent Transparency (VDT) is a compositing technique that modulates sample opacity based on the angle between the surface normal and the view vector, making regions orthogonal to the viewer more transparent and regions curving towards or away from the viewer more opaque [S2, S3]. It differs from standard Direct Volume Rendering (DVR) by introducing view-dependent opacity to improve the readability of nested structures and surface details [S2, S3]. Unlike [Compositing and Blending](2515-compositing-and-blending.md) which typically uses fixed transfer functions, VDT dynamically adjusts transparency per sample or pixel based on geometric orientation [S3].

## How it works
The method modifies the standard front-to-back compositing loop by replacing or scaling the sample opacity $\alpha_i$ with a view-dependent value $\alpha_{view}$. Two primary variants are defined: Angle-Based Transparency and Normal-Variation Transparency [S3].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**:
    *   Volume data or integral surface representation.
    *   Camera position and view direction.
    *   Surface normal $\mathbf{n}$ at the sample point (or pixel).
    *   View vector $\mathbf{v}$ (normalized).
    *   Parameters: $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ (for Normal-Variation), $\alpha_{min}, \alpha_{max}$ (clamping range).
*   **Outputs**:
    *   Final pixel color $C_{final}$ and accumulated opacity $\alpha_{acc}$.

### Data Structures
*   **Sample**: $(c_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{n}_i)$ where $c_i$ is color, $\alpha_i$ is base opacity, and $\mathbf{n}_i$ is the normal.
*   **Pixel Buffer**: Stores accumulated color $C_{acc}$ and transmittance $T$.

### Algorithm Steps

#### 1. Angle-Based Transparency
This method computes transparency based on the incidence angle between the normal and the view vector [S3].

1.  **Compute View Vector**: For each sample $i$ along the ray, determine the normalized view vector $\mathbf{v}$ from the sample point to the camera.
2.  **Compute Dot Product**: Calculate the dot product between the surface normal $\mathbf{n}$ and the view vector $\mathbf{v}$:
    $$ d = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v} $$
3.  **Compute Angle-Based Opacity**: Apply the arccosine function and scale by $2/\pi$ to map the angle $[0, \pi]$ to the opacity range $[0, 1]$ [S3]:
    $$ \alpha_{view} = \frac{2}{\pi} \arccos(d) $$
    *   Note: $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 1$ (facing viewer) yields $\alpha_{view} = 0$ (transparent). $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -1$ (back-facing) yields $\alpha_{view} = 2$ (clamped to 1). $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ (grazing) yields $\alpha_{view} = 0.5$.
4.  **Composite**: Use $\alpha_{view}$ (or scale the original $\alpha_i$ by $\alpha_{view}$) in the standard compositing equation [S3]:
    $$ C_{final} \leftarrow C_{final} + c_i \cdot \alpha_{view} \cdot T $$
    $$ T \leftarrow T \cdot (1 - \alpha_{view}) $$

#### 2. Normal-Variation Transparency
This method approximates local surface curvature perpendicular to the viewer using image-space partial derivatives of the normal's z-component [S3].

1.  **Retrieve Normal**: At pixel $(i, j)$, retrieve the surface normal $\mathbf{n}(i, j)$ and extract its z-component $n_z$.
2.  **Compute Partial Derivatives**: Calculate the partial derivatives of $n_z$ with respect to pixel coordinates $i$ and $j$:
    $$ \frac{\partial n_z}{\partial i}, \quad \frac{\partial n_z}{\partial j} $$
3.  **Compute Curvature Measure**: Calculate the magnitude of the gradient of $n_z$:
    $$ g = \left( \left(\frac{\partial n_z}{\partial i}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial n_z}{\partial j}\right)^2 \right)^{1/2} $$
4.  **Clamp and Power**: Clamp $g$ to the unit interval $[0, 1]$ to prevent values outside this range before applying the exponent $\gamma$ [S3]:
    $$ g_{clamped} = \text{clamp}(g, 0, 1) $$
    $$ \alpha_{view} = g_{clamped}^\gamma $$
    *   Here, $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ controls the strength. Larger values emphasize silhouettes [S3].
5.  **Map to Transparency Range**: Constrain the resulting transparency to a user-adjustable interval $[\alpha_{min}, \alpha_{max}]$ [S3]:
    $$ \alpha_{final} = (1 - \alpha_{view}) \cdot \alpha_{min} + \alpha_{view} \cdot \alpha_{max} $$
6.  **Composite**: Use $\alpha_{final}$ in the compositing loop.

### Pseudocode
```python
def composite_view_dependent(samples, camera_pos, gamma=1.0, alpha_min=0.0, alpha_max=1.0):
    """
    Composite samples using View-Dependent Transparency.
    samples: list of (color, base_alpha, normal) sorted front-to-back.
    camera_pos: (x, y, z)
    gamma: parameter for Normal-Variation transparency.
    alpha_min, alpha_max: clamping range for Normal-Variation.
    """
    C_acc = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
    T = 1.0
    
    for sample in samples:
        c_i, alpha_base, n = sample
        
        # Determine view vector
        v = normalize(camera_pos - sample.position)
        
        # Choose transparency method (example: Angle-Based)
        # For Normal-Variation, n would be from image-space derivatives, not sample normal
        dot_product = dot(n, v)
        
        # Angle-Based Formula
        # arccos domain is [-1, 1], result in [0, pi]
        # Scale by 2/pi to get [0, 2], then clamp to [0, 1] if necessary for alpha
        alpha_view = (2.0 / PI) * acos(dot_product)
        
        # Optional: Scale base alpha by view-dependent factor
        # Or replace base alpha entirely. The source implies alpha_view IS the transparency.
        alpha = alpha_view 
        
        # Standard Front-to-Back Compositing
        contribution = c_i * alpha * T
        C_acc = C_acc + contribution
        T = T * (1.0 - alpha)
        
        # Early termination
        if T < 0.001:
            break
            
    return C_acc, T
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Ghosting**: Fading billboards or surfaces at grazing angles can introduce ghosting effects (duplicated features) when the camera moves, as blending two half-faded textures is not equivalent to a single opaque texture [S1].
*   **Flat Surface Obscuration**: Large, almost flat parts of an integral surface result in high opacity (in Angle-Based, regions facing the viewer are transparent, but if the surface is flat and facing, it might be too transparent? This implies that for flat parts, the angle might be such that opacity is high, or the gradient is low. Specifically, Angle-Based makes regions orthogonal to viewer transparent. If a large flat part is orthogonal, it is transparent. If it is not orthogonal, it is opaque. The pitfall is that large flat parts can have high opacity if they are not orthogonal, obscuring layers [S3].
*   **Clamping**: In Normal-Variation, $\alpha_{view}$ is not necessarily limited to the unit interval before applying the exponent $\gamma$, so clamping is required [S3].
*   **Derivative Noise**: Image-space derivatives of normals can be noisy, affecting the stability of Normal-Variation transparency [S3].

## Subtopics

### Angle-Based Transparency
A transparency variation technique that modulates opacity based on the angle between the surface normal and the view vector, making regions orthogonal to the viewer more transparent and regions curving towards/away from the viewer more opaque [S3]. Choose this when you want to emphasize surface curvature and silhouettes through simple geometric orientation. It is computationally cheap but can obscure large flat surfaces if they are not oriented orthogonally to the viewer [S3].

### Normal-Variation Transparency
A transparency variation technique that approximates local surface curvature perpendicular to the viewer using image-space partial derivatives of the normal's z-component, making the surface more opaque where it curves away and more transparent when oriented perpendicular to the viewer [S3]. Choose this when you need to highlight small details, creases, or nested structures (like flow tubes) that are visible even when viewed frontally. It provides stronger opacity gradients than Angle-Based Transparency but requires image-space normal derivatives [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: $O(N)$ per ray, where $N$ is the number of samples. Angle-Based requires one `acos` and one dot product per sample. Normal-Variation requires image-space derivatives (typically computed via finite differences in screen space) and a power operation.
*   **Memory Complexity**: $O(1)$ additional memory per sample for the view vector and normal.
*   **Performance**: Angle-Based is very fast. Normal-Variation requires access to image-space derivatives, which may necessitate storing normals in a texture or computing them on the fly, adding overhead [S3].
*   **Strengths**: Improves readability of complex, nested structures; emphasizes silhouettes and curvature; interactive performance is achievable [S2, S3].
*   **Limitations**: Can introduce ghosting artifacts with billboards [S1]; large flat surfaces may become too opaque and obscure underlying layers [S3]; Normal-Variation depends on the quality of normal derivatives [S3].
*   **When to use**: When visualizing integral surfaces or volumes where understanding the 3D shape and layering is critical, and standard DVR results in ambiguous or cluttered images [S2, S3].
*   **When not to use**: When real-time performance is critical and the hardware cannot support the additional arithmetic or derivative calculations; when the volume is homogeneous and shape cues are not needed.

## Background
View-Dependent Transparency was introduced by Bruckner and Gröller in 2007 [S2, S3]. It was developed to enhance the readability of volumetric and integral surface visualizations, particularly in medical imaging (e.g., musculoskeletal systems) and flow visualization [S2, S3]. The technique addresses the limitations of standard DVR where opacity is fixed, often leading to occlusion of internal structures [S2]. The Normal-Variation variant was proposed to provide better control over surface curvature perception in image space [S3].
