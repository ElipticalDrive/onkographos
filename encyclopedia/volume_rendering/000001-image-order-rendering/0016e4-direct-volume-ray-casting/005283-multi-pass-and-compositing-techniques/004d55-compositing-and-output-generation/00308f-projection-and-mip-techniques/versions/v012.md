# Projection and MIP Techniques

Also called: MIP (Maximum Intensity Projection)

## Overview
Projection and MIP techniques generate 2D images from 3D volumetric data by aggregating scalar values along viewing rays, prioritizing specific intensity characteristics (maximum, minimum, or sum) over the physical light transport model used in standard alpha blending. Unlike [Compositing and Output Generation](13463-compositing-and-output-generation.md), which accumulates emission and absorption to preserve depth cues, these methods are often order-independent and discard intermediate sample information, making them ideal for highlighting high-contrast structures like bones or vessels in medical imaging [S1, unofficial][S2][S3, unofficial][S4].

## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on a sequence of samples $(c_i, \alpha_i)$ encountered along a ray, where $c_i$ is the sample color (RGB) and $\alpha_i$ is the sample opacity (A) [S1, unofficial][S2]. The core mechanism involves an accumulator state $(C_{acc}, \alpha_{acc})$ initialized to $(0, 0)$ (or background color/opacity) and updated iteratively for each sample [S1, unofficial][S2].

The standard optical model assumes that light is emitted or scattered by the volume and attenuated by absorption [S1, unofficial]. The standard front-to-back compositing equation updates these accumulators for each sample $i$ from front to back:
$$
C_{acc}^{(i)} = C_{acc}^{(i-1)} + (1 - \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)}) \alpha_i C_i
$$
$$
\alpha_{acc}^{(i)} = \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)} + (1 - \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)}) \alpha_i
$$
where $C_{acc}^{(0)} = 0$ and $\alpha_{acc}^{(0)} = 0$ . The term $(1 - \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)})$ represents the transmittance, i.e., the fraction of light reaching sample $i$ from the camera side [S1, unofficial].

In iterative form using transmittance $T = 1 - \alpha_{acc}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{final} &\leftarrow C_{final} + c_i \cdot \alpha_i \cdot T \\
T &\leftarrow T \cdot (1 - \alpha_i)
\end{aligned}
$$
Where $T$ is initialized to $1.0$ and represents the accumulated transparency $\prod (1-\alpha_j)$ [G].

The final pixel color is often composited over a background color $C_{bg}$ using $C_{final} = C_{acc} + (1 - \alpha_{acc}) C_{bg}$ [S1, unofficial].

Early ray termination is a common optimization where the loop breaks if $\alpha_{acc}$ exceeds a threshold (e.g., 1.0), as further samples contribute negligibly to the color .

## Subtopics

### [Maximum Intensity Projection](902-maximum-intensity-projection.md)
Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP) projects the maximum scalar value encountered along a viewing ray onto the 2D image plane. Unlike standard alpha blending, MIP ignores intermediate values and opacity accumulation, making it order-independent. It is primarily used in medical imaging to highlight high-intensity structures such as bones in CT scans or contrast-enhanced blood vessels in MRI/CT angiography.

### [Projected Slabs Approximation](1449-projected-slabs-approximation.md)
Projected Slabs Approximation approximates perspective volume rendering using parallel projected slabs to enable acceleration on hardware designed for parallel projection. It solves the problem of rendering perspective views on hardware that typically supports only orthographic (parallel) projection, such as specialized volume rendering accelerators. It sets itself apart from siblings like [Maximum Intensity Projection](902-maximum-intensity-projection.md) by providing a full volumetric compositing result rather than a single scalar projection, and from [Forward Projection (aTx)](1492-forward-projection-atx.md) by using a geometric slab-based approximation rather than a texture-based transformation.

### [Forward Projection (aTx)](1492-forward-projection-atx.md)
Forward Projection (aTx) is a volume rendering composition scheme where interpolated samples along a ray are simply summed to produce an image. It solves the problem of generating 2D projections from 3D volumetric data by mimicking the physical process of X-ray attenuation, where the total signal is the integral of density along the ray path. Unlike alpha blending, which preserves depth cues through opacity accumulation, Forward Projection loses depth information, making it ideal for visualizing high-density structures like bones or vessels where relative depth is less critical than total integrated intensity.

### [Minimum Intensity Projection](5299-minimum-intensity-projection.md)
Minimum Intensity Projection (MinIP) identifies and renders the voxel with the lowest intensity value along each viewing ray. Unlike standard front-to-back compositing which blends all samples, MinIP discards all but the single minimum sample, effectively highlighting dark structures such as air-filled spaces, vessels in angiography, or low-density tissues against a brighter background. It is the inverse counterpart to Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP).

### [Projection](7205-projection.md)
Projection is a direct volume rendering technique that visualizes the total accumulated intensity of a 3D volume along a specific axis by summing voxel values. It projects volume elements onto a 2D screen plane, effectively creating a 2D representation of the 3D data. Unlike [Maximum Intensity Projection](902-maximum-intensity-projection.md), which highlights the brightest structures, projection preserves the total mass or intensity, making it suitable for visualizing diffuse or low-contrast structures where cumulative signal is informative. It is distinct from [Projected Slabs Approximation](1449-projected-slabs-approximation.md) by summing individual samples rather than pre-integrating slabs, and from [Forward Projection (aTx)](1492-forward-projection-atx.md) by lacking attenuation modeling.

### [Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP)](10702-maximum-intensity-projection-mip.md)
Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP) is a volume rendering technique that projects the maximum scalar value encountered along each viewing ray onto the 2D image plane. It is primarily used to highlight high-intensity structures, such as blood vessels in angiography or bone in CT scans, by suppressing lower-intensity background noise. Unlike standard front-to-back compositing which blends all samples, MIP discards all intermediate values in favor of the single maximum, which causes it to lose depth differentiation and the ability to distinguish whether the maximum lies in the front or back of the volume.

### [Attenuated Maximum-Intensity Projection](16662-attenuated-maximum-intensity-projection.md)
Attenuated Maximum-Intensity Projection (Attenuated MIP) projects the maximum intensity along rays while suppressing contributions from deep or weak samples using an exponential falloff based on accumulated intensity. Unlike standard [Maximum Intensity Projection](10702-maximum-intensity-projection-mip.md) which simply selects the highest value regardless of depth, Attenuated MIP dims background haze and weaker structures, ensuring that only strong, distinct peaks survive the projection. It is chosen when the goal is to highlight high-intensity features without the visual clutter of overlapping lower-intensity volumes that often obscures detail in standard MIP.

### Perspective Approximation via Orthogonal Slices
An approximation for perspective volume rendering that uses view-orthogonal slices instead of concentric spherical shells, which are expensive to generate [S2]. The only difference from exact ray casting is that the distances of sample positions along a ray depend on the ray's orientation [S2]. Exact perspective ray casting produces associated sample positions forming concentric spherical shells, not planes. Generating these shells is expensive [S2].

### ProjectionVolumeStyle
A non-composable render style. An example image shows Bronchial tubes rendered with maximum intensity projection (MIP) [S3, unofficial].

### Reverse-Video Rendering
A display mode where bright image pixels are mapped to dark display pixels, and vice versa, often combined with MIP to enhance visualization of dark objects [S4]. It is used to visualize a passive guiding catheter (signal void) embedded in bright surrounding tissue by suppressing the myocardium and blood via MIP through reverse-video [S4].

## Background
The concept of Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP) emerged in the early 1990s as a method to visualize high-contrast structures in volumetric medical data, particularly in CT angiography and bone imaging. It was popularized for its ability to project the most salient features (highest density/intensity) onto a 2D plane, effectively removing lower-density background clutter [S1, unofficial][S2][S3, unofficial][S4]. The technique is order-independent, allowing for efficient parallelization and hardware acceleration, often implemented via fragment shaders or specialized volume rendering libraries [S1, unofficial][S2][S3, unofficial][S4].
