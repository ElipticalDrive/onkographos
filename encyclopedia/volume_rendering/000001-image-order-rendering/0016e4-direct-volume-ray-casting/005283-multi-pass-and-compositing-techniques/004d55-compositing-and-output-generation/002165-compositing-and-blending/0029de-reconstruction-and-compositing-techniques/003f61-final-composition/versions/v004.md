# Final Composition

Also called: Density-dependent interpolation, Segment compositing, Volume imaging, Volumetric Compositing

## Overview
Final Composition is a volume rendering technique that integrates voxel data into a 2D image by defining organs via intensity ranges and applying lighting to depict surfaces [S1, unofficial]. It distinguishes itself from pure segmentation methods by incorporating the entire volume's information while avoiding exact boundary determination, and from pure projection methods by using illumination to enhance surface perception [S1, unofficial]. It is chosen when organ surface visualization is required without exact boundary segmentation [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: A 3D volume dataset (voxel intensities), a set of intensity ranges defining specific organs/segments, and lighting parameters [S1, unofficial].
- Output: A 2D rendered image [S1, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
- Voxel Grid: 3D array of intensity values [G].
- Segment Definitions: Mappings from intensity ranges to organ labels or material properties [S1, unofficial].
- Convolutions: Pre-computed or runtime-computed values for low-density segments, stored for the right-hand column of the previous block and the bottom row of the previous sweep [S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1. **Segmentation by Intensity:** Define ranges of voxel values to identify organs. Intermediate values are allowed, meaning voxels can contribute to multiple or blended segments [S1, unofficial].
2. **Convolution Calculation:** Compute convolutions for low-density segments. These are necessary for the optical depth weighting [S2, unofficial].
3. **Ray Casting and Compositing:** For each pixel, cast a ray through the volume. Composite voxel segments along the viewing ray from front to back [S2, unofficial].
4. **Solution Selection:** For each segment on the ray, determine the appropriate rendering solution:
   - **Low-density convolution solution:** Used for sparse regions, weighted by the optical depth of the voxel segment [S2, unofficial].
   - **High-density diffusion solution:** Used for dense regions [S2, unofficial].
   - **Density-dependent interpolation:** Interpolate between the low-density and high-density solutions to eliminate aliasing artifacts that arise when switching between the two [S2, unofficial].
5. **Lighting Application:** Apply lighting information to the composited volume data to depict organ surfaces [S1, unofficial].
6. **Final Image Generation:** Evaluate the final color per pixel using the composited data [S2, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The compositing process integrates the volume using a front-to-back accumulation. The contribution $c_i$ for each sample is modified by the segment solution:
$$
C_{final} = \sum_{i} T_i \cdot \alpha_i \cdot c_i^{segment}
$$
Where $c_i^{segment}$ is determined by the low-density convolution, high-density diffusion, or their density-dependent interpolation [S2, unofficial]. $T_i$ is the transmittance up to sample $i$ [G].

**Parameters:**
- Intensity Ranges: User-defined or algorithmically determined thresholds for organ segmentation. These are critical; the result is heavily dependent on them [S1, unofficial].
- Optical Depth: Used to weight the low-density convolution solution [S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Intensity Range Dependency:** The method is heavily dependent on the chosen intensity ranges. Incorrect ranges can lead to poor visualization or misrepresentation of organs [S1, unofficial].
- **Aliasing:** Switching between low-density and high-density solutions can cause aliasing artifacts. Density-dependent interpolation is required to mitigate this [S2, unofficial].
- **Surface Only:** The method portrays organ surfaces but not the interior details, as it relies on lighting and surface depiction rather than full volumetric transparency [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of rays and the complexity of the convolution and diffusion calculations. The need to compute convolutions and interpolate between solutions adds overhead compared to simple compositing [S2, unofficial].
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volume data and intermediate convolution results (specifically for the right-hand column of the previous block and the bottom row of the previous sweep) [S2, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Incorporates information from the entire volume; uses lighting to enhance surface perception [S1, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** Heavily dependent on user-selected intensity ranges; only portrays organ surfaces, not interior structures; potential for aliasing if interpolation is not handled correctly [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial].
- **When to use:** When visualizing organ surfaces in nuclear medicine or similar modalities where relative uptake is important, and exact boundary segmentation is not desired [S1, unofficial].
- **When not to use:** When interior volume details are required, or when user-defined intensity ranges are not feasible or desirable [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Final Composition, also referred to as Volume Imaging or Volumetric Compositing, was developed as an alternative to methods that rely on exact boundary determination or pure maximum activity projection [S1, unofficial]. It emerged in the context of 3D display of SPECT images, aiming to incorporate information from the entire volume while using lighting to depict surfaces [S1, unofficial]. The technique involves a blend of convolution-based low-density handling and diffusion-based high-density handling, interpolated based on density [S2, unofficial].
