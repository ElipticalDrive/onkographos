# Compositing
Also called: Composite ARGB
## Overview
Compositing is the final step in direct volume ray casting where multiple semi-transparent sample contributions along a viewing ray are combined into a single pixel color [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of projecting a 3D density field into a 2D image by correctly blending overlying and underlying colors using ARGB blending [S2, unofficial]. It is distinguished from its siblings by its reliance on the standard optical model of light attenuation and emission, typically implemented via front-to-back accumulation which enables early ray termination, unlike back-to-front or order-independent variants [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: A sequence of samples $(c_i, \alpha_i)$ encountered along a ray, where $c_i$ is the sample color (RGB) and $\alpha_i$ is the sample opacity (A) [G]. The samples are sorted front-to-back [G].
- Input: A background color $C_{bg}$ (default black) [G].
- Output: The final pixel color $C_{final}$.

**Data Structures:**
The method maintains an accumulator state $(C_{acc}, \alpha_{acc})$ initialized to $(0, 0)$ [G].
- $C_{acc}$: Accumulated color, a 3-component vector (RGB).
- $\alpha_{acc}$: Accumulated opacity, a scalar.

**Formulas:**
The standard front-to-back compositing equation updates the accumulators for each sample $i$:
$$
C_{acc}^{(i)} = C_{acc}^{(i-1)} + (1 - \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)}) \alpha_i c_i
$$
$$
\alpha_{acc}^{(i)} = \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)} + (1 - \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)}) \alpha_i
$$
where $C_{acc}^{(0)} = 0$ and $\alpha_{acc}^{(0)} = 0$ [G]. The term $(1 - \alpha_{acc}^{(i-1)})$ represents the transmittance $T$, i.e., the fraction of light reaching sample $i$ from the camera side [G].

In iterative form using transmittance $T = 1 - \alpha_{acc}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{final} &\leftarrow C_{final} + c_i \cdot \alpha_i \cdot T \\
T &\leftarrow T \cdot (1 - \alpha_i)
\end{aligned}
$$
Where $T$ is initialized to $1.0$ and represents the accumulated transparency $\prod (1-\alpha_j)$ [G].

The final pixel color is composited over the background color $C_{bg}$ using:
$$
C_{final} = C_{acc} + (1 - \alpha_{acc}) C_{bg}
$$
[G].

**Parameters:**
- Early ray termination threshold: Typically $1.0$ (or a value slightly less, e.g., $0.99$, to avoid precision issues) [G].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- Precision: If $\alpha_{acc}$ approaches $1.0$, the transmittance approaches $0$. Floating-point precision errors may cause $T$ to become slightly negative or non-zero when it should be zero, leading to minor artifacts. Clamping $T$ to $[0, 1]$ is recommended [G].
- Boundary: If the ray exits the volume before $\alpha_{acc}$ reaches $1.0$, the remaining transmittance is applied to the background color [G].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compositing_loop(samples, background_color):
    """
    Standard front-to-back compositing loop.
    samples: list of (color: vec3, opacity: float) sorted front-to-back.
    background_color: vec3 for the scene background.
    """
    C_acc = vec3(0.0)
    alpha_acc = 0.0
    
    for c_i, alpha_i in samples:
        transmittance = max(0.0, 1.0 - alpha_acc)
        C_acc = C_acc + transmittance * alpha_i * c_i
        alpha_acc = min(1.0, alpha_acc + transmittance * alpha_i)
        
        # Early ray termination
        if alpha_acc >= 1.0:
            break
            
    # Composite over background
    return C_acc + (1.0 - alpha_acc) * background_color
```

## Subtopics

### [Alpha Compositing Ray Casting](922-alpha-compositing-ray-casting.md)
Alpha compositing ray casting evaluates the volume rendering integral by marching along viewing rays and iteratively blending semi-transparent samples from the front of the ray to the back. It solves the problem of projecting a 3D density field into a 2D image without requiring binary classification, thereby eliminating false positives and false negatives. It is distinguished from its siblings by its recursive accumulation of color and opacity using the OVER operator, which enables early ray termination when the accumulated opacity reaches unity, a feature not available in back-to-front compositing. [Alpha Compositing Ray Casting](922-alpha-compositing-ray-casting.md)

### [Ray Compositing and Accumulation](3449-ray-compositing-and-accumulation.md)
Ray Compositing and Accumulation is a hardware-accelerated method for aggregating color and opacity contributions into final pixel values during direct volume ray casting, specifically designed to handle sample streams that are not presented in strict ray order. Unlike software-based approaches that require sorting or random access memory for per-ray buffers, this method uses dedicated hardware structures (FIFOs and shift registers) to buffer partially accumulated values and resolve predecessor dependencies based on view direction and spatial coordinates. It is distinguished from [Alpha Compositing Ray Casting](922-alpha-compositing-ray-casting.md) by its explicit hardware implementation for non-ordered data streams, and from [Raymarch Blending](5322-raymarch-blending.md) by its focus on fixed-function accumulation logic within the VolumePro architecture. [Ray Compositing and Accumulation](3449-ray-compositing-and-accumulation.md)

### [Average Compositing](5286-average-compositing.md)
Average Compositing is a volume rendering technique that produces an X-ray-like image by sending a ray through the volume and compositing all found values with the same weight, taking no opacity into account. It differs from Alpha Compositing and Accumulation methods by treating every sample equally regardless of depth or transparency. [Average Compositing](5286-average-compositing.md)

### [Raymarch Blending](5322-raymarch-blending.md)
Raymarch Blending combines a ray-marched volumetric result with an existing background scene texture using standard alpha compositing. It solves the problem of integrating semi-transparent volumetric data into a pre-rendered scene containing opaque geometry, ensuring correct occlusion where the volume interacts with the solid meshes. Unlike [Alpha Compositing Ray Casting](922-alpha-compositing-ray-casting.md) which typically handles volume-to-volume or volume-to-black blending, this method explicitly blends against a pre-existing framebuffer color. [Raymarch Blending](5322-raymarch-blending.md)

### [Alpha Blending](5750-alpha-blending.md)
Alpha blending is a compositing technique used in direct volume ray casting to combine multiple semi-transparent contributions along a viewing ray into a final pixel color. It solves the problem of rendering translucent volumes by discretizing the continuous light transport equation, allowing for physically plausible accumulation of emitted and attenuated light. It is distinguished from simple averaging or maximum intensity projection by its use of an associative, non-commutative operator that preserves depth ordering, enabling correct compositing of independent sub-volumes or distributed rendering nodes. [Alpha Blending](5750-alpha-blending.md)

### [Front-to-Back Compositing](7859-front-to-back-compositing.md)
Front-to-Back Compositing is a direct volume rendering technique that projects semi-transparent volume data to the screen by traversing viewing rays from the camera viewpoint toward the far plane, accumulating color and opacity contributions. It is distinguished from its sibling [Alpha Compositing Ray Casting](922-alpha-compositing-ray-casting.md) and [Back-to-Front Compositing](922-alpha-compositing-ray-casting.md) by its ability to exploit early ray termination, allowing the renderer to stop processing a ray once the accumulated opacity approaches 1.0, thereby significantly reducing computational cost for opaque or dense regions. [Front-to-Back Compositing](7859-front-to-back-compositing.md)

### [Order-Independent Volumetric Compositing via Visibility Function](16292-order-independent-volumetric-compositing-via-visibility-func.md)
This method composites semi-transparent volumetric samples in an order-independent manner by employing a visibility function that represents the total transmittance between a sample and the camera. It solves the order-dependent transparency problem in direct volume rendering, enabling correct visibility composition beyond simple Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP) or X-ray representations. Unlike its siblings which often rely on sorting or front-to-back accumulation, this approach refactors the alpha-blending equation to avoid recursion and sorting, allowing light sources to be processed independently of the camera location. [Order-Independent Volumetric Compositing via Visibility Function](16292-order-independent-volumetric-compositing-via-visibility-func.md)

### Front-to-Back Alpha Blending
**Definition:** A projection technique that composites samples along the ray using alpha blending until the ray exits the volume or becomes fully opaque [S3, unofficial].
**Steps:**
1. Initialize contribution = 1.0f [S3, unofficial].
2. Composite each sample: outputColor.rgb += contribution * transfer.a * transfer.rgb. contribution *= (1 - transfer.a) [S3, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
- Time: $O(N)$ per ray, where $N$ is the number of samples. Early ray termination reduces the average $N$ for opaque regions [G].
- Memory: $O(1)$ per ray for the accumulator state [G].

**Measured Performance:**
- Performance depends heavily on the density of the volume and the effectiveness of early ray termination. For sparse volumes, the cost is proportional to the number of occupied samples. For dense volumes, the loop may terminate early, reducing cost [G].

**Scaling:**
- Scales linearly with the number of rays (pixels). Parallelization is straightforward across pixels [G].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
- Strengths: Physically plausible results for semi-transparent volumes; efficient early ray termination; simple implementation [G].
- Limitations: Order-dependent (requires front-to-back sorting or traversal); not suitable for order-independent transparency without modification; early ray termination can cause artifacts if not handled carefully (e.g., in distributed rendering) [G].

**When to Use:**
- Use for standard direct volume rendering where semi-transparency is required and early ray termination is beneficial [G].
- Do not use when order-independent transparency is required or when samples are not available in front-to-back order without additional buffering [G].

## Background
Compositing in volume rendering originated from the need to combine multiple semi-transparent samples along a ray to produce a realistic 2D projection [G]. The front-to-back approach is used in interactive volume ray tracing [S1, unofficial]. The technique is fundamental to direct volume rendering and is implemented in various graphics pipelines and hardware accelerators [S2, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
