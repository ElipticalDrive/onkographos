# Hardware-accelerated Multimodality Volume Fusion

Also called: Depth compositing of the volume, Multimodality Volume Fusion

## Overview
Hardware-accelerated Multimodality Volume Fusion is a technique for combining multiple volume datasets (e.g., MR and PET) into a single image representation using graphics hardware to solve depth cueing problems and reduce processing time compared to software-based methods [S1]. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [Illumination model level intermixing](13581-illumination-model-level-intermixing.md) by performing compositing operations on texture coordinates before alpha blending, thereby avoiding the need to manually set slice ordering for volumes of different sizes [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Two volume datasets (a "reference" volume and a "float" volume) assumed to be registered via rigid transformation [S1]. The volumes may have different dimensions (e.g., $256 \times 256 \times 96$ for MR and $128 \times 128 \times 40$ for PET) [S1].
*   **Outputs:** A single fused image [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **3D Textures:** Each volume is loaded into 3D texture memory [S1].
*   **Sample Planes:** Planes orthogonal to the viewing direction are generated for both volumes [S1].
*   **Pixel Program:** A shader program (e.g., HLSL) handles the compositing logic [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Preprocessing:** Ensure reference and float volume datasets have the same orientation by applying a rigid transformation sufficient for registration [S1].
2.  **Sample Plane Generation:** Generate sample planes orthogonal to the viewing direction for both volumes, following 3D texture mapping volume rendering principles [S1].
3.  **Texture Combination:** Combine textures from different volumes using the same texture coordinates before blending [S1]. Two voxels (ultimately texels) referred to the same texture coordinates are combined by compositing operations and then mapped to the same slice [S1]. This avoids the troublesome work of setting orders of each slice [S1].
4.  **Alpha Blending:** Perform alpha blending for all slices using a pixel program written in HLSL [S1].

**Compositing Operations:**
The method supports several compositing operations to combine color $C$ and opacity $\alpha$ from volume A ($C_A, \alpha_A$) and volume B ($C_B, \alpha_B$) [S1]:

1.  **Over Compositing:**
    $$ C = \frac{\alpha_A \cdot C_A + (1 - \alpha_A)\alpha_B \cdot C_B}{\alpha_A + (1 - \alpha_A) \cdot \alpha_B} $$
    $$ \alpha = \alpha_A + (1 - \alpha_A) \cdot \alpha_B $$
    [S1]

2.  **XOR Compositing:**
    $$ C = \frac{(1 - \alpha_B) \cdot \alpha_A \cdot C_A + (1 - \alpha_A)\alpha_B \cdot C_B}{(1 - \alpha_B) \cdot \alpha_A + (1 - \alpha_A) \cdot \alpha_B} $$
    $$ \alpha = (1 - \alpha_B) \cdot \alpha_A + (1 - \alpha_A) \cdot \alpha_B $$
    [S1]

3.  **Plus Compositing:**
    $$ C = C_A + C_B $$
    $$ \alpha = \alpha_A + \alpha_B $$
    [S1]

4.  **Weighted Addition Compositing:**
    $$ C = \frac{weight \cdot \alpha_A \cdot C_A + (1 - weight) \cdot \alpha_B \cdot C_B}{weight \cdot \alpha_A + (1 - weight) \cdot \alpha_B} $$
    $$ \alpha = weight \cdot \alpha_A + (1 - weight) \cdot \alpha_B $$
    where $0 \le weight \le 1$ [S1].

**Weighted Opacity for Surface Extraction:**
To allow seeing a float volume through a reference volume, the weight is determined by gradient magnitude $|\nabla|$ [S1]:
$$ weight = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } |\nabla| < c \\ 1 & \text{else} \end{cases} $$
where $c$ is a constant [S1]. Gradient magnitude acts as a numerical divergence; surfaces have higher values, while homogeneous regions have lower values [S1]. Setting weight to 0 in homogeneous areas makes them transparent, eliminating opacity in overlapping regions [S1].

**Pitfalls:**
*   **Color Overflow:** The Plus operation causes color overflow, resulting in a mainly white image because colors are simply added [S1].
*   **Slice Ordering:** Unlike methods that require setting orders of each slice, this method combines textures with the same texture coordinates, avoiding this issue [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Performance:** Hardware-accelerated fusion takes 625 msec compared to 3703 msec for software-based fusion, yielding a 5 to 6 times speed enhancement [S1].
*   **Hardware:** Tested on an Intel Pentium IV PC with an ATI Radeon X800 256 MB RAM [S1].
*   **Resolution:** Tested with brain MR images of size $256 \times 256 \times 96$ and PET images of size $128 \times 128 \times 40$ with 2 bytes-intensity [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Solves depth cueing problems, reduces processing time, and handles volumes of different sizes without manual slice ordering [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Plus compositing can cause color overflow [S1].

## Background
The method was proposed by H. Hong, J. H. Bay, K. Eui, and Y. Shin from the School of Computer Science and Engineering and the Computer Graphics Laboratory (CGLab) at Seoul National University, Korea [S1]. It was published in 2005 by Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg [S1]. The work addresses the need for radiologists and surgeons to simultaneously view complementary information from different modalities (PET, CT, MRI) for diagnosis and treatment planning [S1].
