# Intermixing

Also called: accumulation-level intermixing, per-sample channel combination

## Overview
Intermixing is a compositing strategy for multi-channel volume rendering that separately evaluates the illumination model for each data channel at every ray sample before combining the results into the final color and opacity accumulator [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of correctly handling occlusions and depth ordering between different data modalities (e.g., CT and MRI) or deformed states of the same object, which image-level compositing fails to do due to missing depth information in intermediate results [S1, unofficial]. It is distinguished from image-level intermixing by its preservation of depth ordering through per-sample combination, and from single-volume rendering by its ability to intermix multiple data sources [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
Intermixing modifies the standard direct volume ray casting loop to handle $N$ co-registered channels. Instead of rendering each channel to a separate image and blending them later, it maintains a single accumulation buffer where color and opacity are updated per sample by combining contributions from all active channels.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Input:** A set of $N$ co-registered volume datasets $V_1, \dots, V_N$. For each ray, the sampling positions $s_j$ are identical across all volumes.
- **Output:** A final pixel color $C_{final}$ and opacity $\alpha_{final}$.

**Data Structures:**
- **Accumulator:** A single structure holding the current accumulated color $C_{acc}$ and opacity $\alpha_{acc}$.
  - $C_{acc} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (or $\mathbb{R}^4$ if including pre-multiplied alpha).
  - $\alpha_{acc} \in [0, 1]$.
- **Per-Sample State:** For each sample $j$ along the ray:
  - $v_{i,j}$: The scalar value sampled from volume $V_i$ at position $s_j$.
  - $c_i(v_{i,j})$: The color contribution from volume $i$, derived from its transfer function and illumination model.
  - $\alpha_i(v_{i,j})$: The opacity contribution from volume $i$, derived from its transfer function.

**Numbered Steps:**
1. **Initialize Accumulator:** Set $C_{acc} = (0, 0, 0)$ and $\alpha_{acc} = 0$.
2. **Ray Casting Loop:** For each pixel, cast a ray through the volume. For each sample position $s_j$ along the ray:
   a. **Sample All Channels:** For each channel $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$, retrieve the scalar value $v_{i,j}$ from volume $V_i$ at $s_j$.
   b. **Evaluate Illumination:** Compute the color $c_i$ and opacity $\alpha_i$ for each channel $i$ using its specific transfer function and lighting model (e.g., Phong shading, in-scattering). This step is performed independently for each channel [S1, unofficial].
   c. **Composite Per-Sample:** Combine the contributions from all $N$ channels into a single intermediate color $C_{sample}$ and opacity $\alpha_{sample}$. The standard front-to-back compositing formula is applied to merge these $N$ contributions. If the channels are treated as independent semi-transparent layers at the same depth, they are blended together:
      $$ \alpha_{sample} = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{N} (1 - \alpha_i) $$
      $$ C_{sample} = \frac{1}{\alpha_{sample}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i \alpha_i \prod_{k=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_k) $$
      (Note: The order of channels $i$ may affect the result if they are not physically independent; typically, a fixed order or physical layering is assumed [G]).
   d. **Update Accumulator:** Blend the per-sample result into the global accumulator using standard front-to-back compositing:
      $$ \alpha_{acc}^{new} = \alpha_{acc} + \alpha_{sample} (1 - \alpha_{acc}) $$
      $$ C_{acc}^{new} = C_{acc} + C_{sample} (1 - \alpha_{acc}) $$
      Update $\alpha_{acc} \leftarrow \alpha_{acc}^{new}$ and $C_{acc} \leftarrow C_{acc}^{new}$.
   e. **Early Ray Termination:** If $\alpha_{acc}$ exceeds a threshold (e.g., 0.99), terminate the ray [G].
3. **Final Output:** The final pixel color is $C_{final} = C_{acc}$ and opacity is $\alpha_{final} = \alpha_{acc}$.

**Parameters:**
- **Number of Channels ($N$):** Implementation-defined. In GPU-based implementations using standard texture formats, $N$ is often limited to 4 due to the four-component limit (RGBA) [S1, unofficial].
- **Homogeneity Threshold:** A user-defined threshold for determining if a brick or node is homogeneous across all channels. A node is marked homogeneous only if its corresponding bricks in *all* channels are homogeneous [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Image-Level Intermixing Failure:** If intermixing is performed at the image level (rendering complete images per channel and blending), occlusions between different channels are handled incorrectly because depth information is lost in the accumulated intermediate results [S1, unofficial]. Accumulation-level intermixing avoids this by maintaining depth coherence per sample.
- **Channel Order Dependency:** If the illumination models or transfer functions imply a physical layering, the order in which channels are combined in step 2c matters. If channels represent independent modalities without physical occlusion, the order may be less critical, but consistency is required [G].
- **Performance:** Intermixing is computationally more expensive than single-channel rendering or image-level compositing because the illumination model must be evaluated for each channel at every sample, and the per-sample combination adds arithmetic overhead [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Multi-volume rendering](6407-multi-volume-rendering.md)
Multi-volume rendering refers to the simultaneous rendering of multiple co-registered volumes by evaluating samples along a viewing ray for all visible channels. It solves the problem of visualizing complex interactions between different data modalities (e.g., CT and MRI) or deformed states of the same object. It is distinguished from single-volume rendering by its ability to intermix data sources, and from image-level compositing by its preservation of depth ordering through per-sample combination.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(R \cdot S \cdot N)$, where $R$ is the number of rays, $S$ is the number of samples per ray, and $N$ is the number of channels. This is $N$ times more expensive than single-channel rendering per sample due to the separate illumination evaluation and combination [S1, unofficial].
- **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the data structure. In octree-based approaches, nodes store information for up to $N$ channels. The number of channels is often limited by hardware texture formats (e.g., $N \le 4$ for RGBA textures) [S1, unofficial].
- **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
- **Strengths:** Correctly handles occlusions between different channels; flexible; preserves depth ordering.
- **Limitations:** Computationally expensive; channel count may be limited by hardware texture formats; implementation complexity increases with $N$.
- **When to use:** When visualizing multi-modal data (e.g., CT/MRI) or multiple states of the same object where depth interactions between channels are important.
- **When not to use:** When performance is critical and channels are independent/non-occluding; when $N$ exceeds hardware limits for single-texture storage.

## Background
Intermixing, specifically accumulation-level intermixing, was presented by Brix et al. in 2014 as part of an out-of-core rendering approach for multi-channel volume data sets [S1, unofficial]. It builds on Crassin et al.’s octree-based memory virtualization scheme [CNLE09]. The method is used in systems such as Voreen [DLJL22], Viv [MGP+22], and Neuroglancer [Goo22]. The concept is also referenced in the author's method [S1, unofficial].
