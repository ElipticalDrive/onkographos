# Doppler Volume Rendering

Also called: Piecewise Linear Spectral Representation, Spectral Volume Rendering with Doppler Effect

## Overview
Doppler Volume Rendering is a spectral volume rendering technique that accounts for the Doppler effect of light in an emission-absorption model, where volumetric matter moves according to a 3D velocity field and emits/attenuates light at wavelengths subject to Doppler shifts [S1]. It employs a novel piecewise linear spectral representation to achieve interactive frame rates, overcoming the energy conservation inaccuracies and information loss associated with traditional point-sampled spectral representations [S1]. Unlike standard direct volume ray casting which maps scalar values to RGB colors, this method tracks spectral radiance across wavelengths, shifting them dynamically based on local velocity [S1].

## How it works
The method adapts spectral volume rendering for a per-voxel emission/absorption scheme, supporting both thermal radiation (blackbody emission) and photon generation by free charged particles [S1]. It computes the Doppler effect accurately corresponding to the motion given by a 3D velocity field [S1].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D volume dataset containing scalar values (e.g., density, temperature) and a 3D velocity vector field. Camera parameters (origin, direction).
*   **Output:** A 2D image with spectral color values (RGB).

**Data Structures:**
*   **Volume Data:** 3D grid of scalar values (density, temperature).
*   **Velocity Field:** 3D grid of 3D vectors $(v_x, v_y, v_z)$.
*   **Spectral Representation:** Piecewise linear segments defining spectral radiance $B(\lambda)$, rather than uniform frequency bins [S1].

**Formulas:**
1.  **Planck's Radiation Law:** For thermal radiation, the spectral distribution function per unit wavelength $\lambda$ is:
    $$ B_\lambda(T, \lambda) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5} \left[ \exp\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right) - 1 \right]^{-1} $$
    where $h$ is Planck's constant, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $c$ is the speed of light, and $T$ is temperature [S1].
2.  **Wien's Displacement Law:** The wavelength of maximum emission is:
    $$ \lambda_{\max} = \frac{2.897 \times 10^{-3} \text{ mK}}{T} $$
    where $T$ is temperature [S1].
3.  **Doppler Shift:** The observed wavelength $\lambda_{obs}$ is shifted from the emitted wavelength $\lambda_{emit}$ based on the relative velocity $v$ along the line of sight [S1]. The specific formula for the shift is implied by the "accurate computation" of the Doppler effect corresponding to the 3D velocity field, but the exact relativistic or non-relativistic shift equation is not explicitly provided in the source text beyond the concept of compression/expansion [S1].
4.  **Attenuation:** Described by the Lambert-Bouguer law:
    $$ I(\lambda) = I_0(\lambda) e^{-l\rho\tau(\lambda)} $$
    where $I_0$ is incident intensity, $l$ is thickness, $\rho$ is density, and $\tau$ is the extinction coefficient for wavelength $\lambda$ [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Ray Casting:** Cast rays from the camera through the volume [S1].
2.  **Sampling:** For each sample point along the ray, sample the scalar value (density, temperature) and the 3D velocity vector from the volume data [S1].
3.  **Spectral Emission Calculation:**
    *   If the material emits thermal radiation, calculate the spectral radiance $B_\lambda(T, \lambda)$ using Planck's law for the sampled temperature $T$ [S1].
    *   If the material emits via non-thermal processes (e.g., spectral lines from atoms/molecules like Hydrogen Balmer lines or Carbon), define the emission spectrum as specific spectral lines [S1].
4.  **Doppler Shift Application:**
    *   Compute the Doppler shift for each wavelength component of the emission spectrum based on the local velocity vector and the ray direction [S1].
    *   Use the **piecewise linear spectral representation** to handle these shifts. This representation dynamically computes and combines piecewise linear segments of the spectrum, avoiding the errors of point-sampled bins where energy conservation fails if lines shift between bins or out of the visible spectrum [S1].
5.  **Attenuation:** Apply the Lambert-Bouguer law to attenuate the light based on the density and extinction coefficient along the path [S1].
6.  **Compositing:** Perform front-to-back compositing of the spectral radiance values [S1]. The compositing follows the standard volume rendering integral but operates on spectral data [S1].
7.  **Color Conversion:** Convert the final spectral radiance to RGB values for display [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Sun's Effective Temperature:** $T \approx 5770$ K, resulting in $\lambda_{\max} \approx 502$ nm and spectral radians $B(T, \lambda_{\max}) \approx 26.2$ kWm⁻² nm⁻¹ sr⁻¹ [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Energy Conservation:** Point-sampled spectral representations suffer from energy conservation inaccuracies when spectral lines shift between bins [S1]. The piecewise linear representation mitigates this [S1].
*   **Information Loss:** If the bin range is not large enough, information can be lost when emission/absorption computations shift from the visible into the invisible spectrum, and vice versa [S1].
*   **Spectral Broadening:** Varying velocities along ray segments can cause spectral broadening errors in point-sampled methods [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import math

def planck_radiation(T, wavelength):
    """
    Calculate spectral radiance using Planck's law.
    T: Temperature in Kelvin
    wavelength: Wavelength in meters
    Returns: Spectral radiance in W/m^2/sr/m
    """
    h = 6.62607015e-34  # Planck's constant
    c = 299792458         # Speed of light
    kB = 1.380649e-23     # Boltzmann's constant
    
    exponent = (h * c) / (wavelength * kB * T)
    if exponent > 700: # Avoid overflow
        return 0.0
    return (2 * h * c**2) / (wavelength**5) * (1.0 / (math.exp(exponent) - 1))

def doppler_shift(wavelength_emit, velocity_vector, ray_direction):
    """
    Calculate the observed wavelength due to Doppler effect.
    wavelength_emit: Emitted wavelength in meters
    velocity_vector: 3D velocity vector of the medium [vx, vy, vz]
    ray_direction: 3D ray direction vector [dx, dy, dz]
    Returns: Observed wavelength in meters
    """
    # Normalize ray direction
    ray_len = math.sqrt(ray_direction[0]**2 + ray_direction[1]**2 + ray_direction[2]**2)
    if ray_len == 0:
        return wavelength_emit
    rd = [d / ray_len for d in ray_direction]
    
    # Calculate relative velocity along the line of sight
    # v_rel = v_medium . ray_direction (assuming observer is stationary)
    v_rel = velocity_vector[0]*rd[0] + velocity_vector[1]*rd[1] + velocity_vector[2]*rd[2]
    
    c = 299792458 # Speed of light
    # Non-relativistic Doppler shift: lambda_obs = lambda_emit * (1 + v_rel/c)
    # Note: The source does not specify relativistic vs non-relativistic, 
    # but for astrophysics simulations, relativistic might be needed. 
    # Using non-relativistic as a basic implementation based on "compression or expansion" description.
    wavelength_obs = wavelength_emit * (1 + v_rel / c)
    return wavelength_obs

def piecewise_linear_spectral_rendering(volume_data, velocity_field, camera, transfer_function):

    """
    h, w = camera.screen_height, camera.screen_width
    image = [[vec3(0.0, 0.0, 0.0) for _ in range(w)] for _ in range(h)]
    
    # Define spectral range (e.g., visible spectrum)
    lambda_min = 380e-9 # 380 nm
    lambda_max = 750e-9 # 750 nm
    num_segments = 100 # Number of piecewise linear segments
    
    for y in range(h):
        for x in range(w):
            ray_origin, ray_direction = camera.cast_ray(x, y)
            accumulated_color = vec3(0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
            accumulated_opacity = 0.0
            
            # Ray initialization
            t = random.uniform(0.0, step_size)
            t_end = compute_ray_exit(ray_origin, ray_direction, volume_bounds)

                # In reality, this would involve dynamic computation of segments
                emission_spectrum = []
                if scalar_value > 0: # Assume scalar_value is temperature
                    T = scalar_value
                    # Create piecewise linear segments for blackbody radiation
                    # For simplicity, we sample a few points and interpolate
                    num_points = 10
                    wavelengths = [lambda_min + i * (lambda_max - lambda_min) / (num_points - 1) for i in range(num_points)]
                    radiances = [planck_radiation(T, wl) for wl in wavelengths]
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Interactive frame rates are achieved on the GPU [S1]. The complexity depends on the number of sample points along the ray and the number of spectral segments [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volume data, velocity field, and spectral segments [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Accurate computation of the Doppler effect, energy conservation, no information loss when shifting out of the visible spectrum, no spectral broadening errors [S1].
*   **Limitations:** More complex than standard volume rendering due to spectral calculations and piecewise linear representation [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When visualizing astrophysics simulations or other volumetric data where the motion of matter significantly affects the observed light spectrum [S1].

## Background
Doppler Volume Rendering was introduced by Alberto Jaspe et al. in 2023 [S1]. It builds on spectral volume rendering introduced by Noordmans et al. [NvdVS00] [S1]. The method was applied to a simulation of galaxy formation [S1].
