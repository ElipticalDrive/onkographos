# Spherical Harmonics Approximation

Also called: SH Approximation, Spherical Harmonics Basis Approximation

## Overview
Spherical Harmonics (SH) Approximation is a technique to represent directional signals, such as lighting or view-dependent appearance, as a linear combination of orthogonal basis functions defined on the unit sphere $S^2$ [S1]. It solves the problem of expensive Monte Carlo integration for spherical integrals by converting them into efficient, differentiable algebraic operations (dot products and matrix-vector multiplications) on coefficient vectors [S1]. It is chosen when low-frequency directional signals need to be evaluated repeatedly in inverse problems or real-time rendering, trading high-frequency detail for computational speed and differentiability.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   A spherical function $f(\omega)$ defined over the domain $\Omega$ (the unit sphere).
*   A maximum bandwidth $n$ (number of bands), determining the truncation level.

**Outputs:**
*   A coefficient vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ representing the SH approximation.
*   The ability to reconstruct the function $\tilde{f}(\omega)$ or compute integrals of products of functions via algebraic operations on their coefficients.

**Data Structures:**
*   **SH Coefficient Vector:** A 1D array of size $n$. The index $i$ linearizes the degree $\ell$ and order $m$ using the mapping:
    $$i = \ell^2 + \ell + m$$
    where $\ell \in \{0, \dots, L\}$ is the band index, $m \in \{-\ell, \dots, \ell\}$ is the order within the band, and $n = (L+1)^2$ is the total number of coefficients [S1].
*   **Transfer Matrix ($C$):** A precomputed 3D tensor of size $n \times n \times n$, where each entry $C_{ijk}$ is defined as:
    $$C_{ijk} = \int_{\Omega} y_i(\omega) y_j(\omega) y_k(\omega) d\omega$$
    This matrix allows the SH projection of a product to be computed as a bilinear form [S1].

**Steps:**

1.  **Projection (Coefficient Extraction):**
    Compute the SH coefficients $\mathbf{f}$ for the input function $f(\omega)$. The $i$-th coefficient is given by the integral:
    $$f_i = \int_{\Omega} f(\omega) y_i(\omega) d\omega$$
    where $y_i(\omega)$ is the $i$-th real-valued spherical harmonic basis function [S1].
    *Note:* In practice, this integral is often approximated via numerical integration or Monte Carlo sampling at initialization. For inverse problems, this step may be replaced by optimization to make the coefficients differentiable with respect to scene parameters [S1].

2.  **Reconstruction (Evaluation):**
    To evaluate the approximate function $\tilde{f}(\omega)$ at a specific direction $\omega$, compute the dot product of the coefficient vector and the basis vector:
    $$\tilde{f}(\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i y_i(\omega)$$
    The accuracy of this reconstruction increases with the number of bands $n$ [S1].

3.  **Integral of Product (Dot Product):**
    To compute the integral of the product of two approximated functions $\tilde{a}(\omega)$ and $\tilde{b}(\omega)$, use the dot product of their coefficient vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$:
    $$\int_{\Omega} \tilde{a}(\omega) \tilde{b}(\omega) d\omega = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$$
    This property holds because the SH basis functions are orthonormal [S1].

4.  **SH Projection of Product (Convolution):**
    To compute the SH coefficients of the product of two functions $\tilde{a}(\omega)$ and $\tilde{b}(\omega)$, denoted as $\tilde{a} * \tilde{b}$, use the precomputed transfer matrix $C$:
    $$(\mathbf{a} * \mathbf{b})_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} C_{ijk} a_j b_k$$
    This operation effectively convolves the coefficient vectors using the structure constants of the SH algebra [S1].

5.  **Radiance Computation (Example Application):**
    In rendering, outgoing radiance $B(x)$ can be computed analytically from SH coefficients of light $\mathbf{L}$, visibility $\mathbf{V}(x)$, and a hemisphere function $\mathbf{H}(x)$:
    $$B(x) = a(x) \left( \mathbf{L} \cdot (\mathbf{V}(x) * \mathbf{H}(x)) \right)$$
    where $a(x)$ is the albedo at point $x$. The term $\mathbf{V}(x) * \mathbf{H}(x)$ represents the SH projection of the product of visibility and the hemisphere function [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Bandwidth ($n$):** The number of bands. A bandwidth of $n=8$ is cited as providing good low-frequency approximations for typical inverse problem scenarios [S1]. Higher bandwidths increase accuracy but also computational cost ($O(n^2)$ for projection, $O(n^3)$ for product convolution).

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Gibbs Phenomenon:** Truncating the series at a finite $n$ can cause ringing artifacts for high-frequency signals.
*   **Non-Differentiability of Input:** If the input function $f(\omega)$ contains discontinuities (e.g., hard shadows), the SH coefficients may converge slowly. The method assumes the function is sufficiently smooth for the low-frequency approximation to be valid [S1].
*   **Rotation:** Rotating a SH function requires applying a rotation matrix to the coefficient vector. This is done via a linear transformation defined by the Wigner D-matrices, which can be precomputed [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Projection is $O(n^2)$ if using precomputed basis evaluations on samples, or $O(N \cdot n)$ where $N$ is the number of samples. Reconstruction is $O(n)$. Computing the product of two SH functions is $O(n^3)$ due to the triple summation in the transfer matrix application.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Storing the coefficient vector is $O(n)$. Storing the transfer matrix $C$ is $O(n^3)$.
*   **Strengths:** Enables differentiable rendering; converts expensive integrals into fast linear algebra; efficient for low-frequency signals.
*   **Limitations:** Poor representation of high-frequency details (sharp shadows, specular highlights) unless very high bandwidths are used; $O(n^3)$ cost for product convolution limits practical bandwidth.
*   **When to use:** When the directional signal is smooth and low-frequency, or when differentiability with respect to scene parameters is required for optimization.
*   **When not to use:** For high-frequency directional effects where Monte Carlo or ray tracing is more appropriate.

## Background
Spherical Harmonics Approximation in the context of differentiable shadow computation and inverse problems was introduced in "Efficient and Differentiable Shadow Computation for Inverse Problems" [S1]. The method leverages the properties of SH to avoid expensive Monte Carlo integration for spherical integrals, enabling gradient-based optimization of scene parameters. The use of SH for lighting and visibility approximation has roots in earlier computer graphics literature, such as Green's work on SH convolution [S1].
