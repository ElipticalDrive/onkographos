# Spherical Harmonics Approximation

Also called: SH Approximation, Spherical Harmonics Basis Approximation

## Overview
Spherical Harmonics (SH) Approximation is a technique to approximate a spherical function $f(\omega)$ using a finite subset of spherical harmonics basis functions. It enables efficient and differentiable computation for inverse problems, avoiding the high computational cost of Monte Carlo integration [S1]. It sets itself apart from direct sampling methods by converting complex directional integrals into linear algebra operations (dot products and matrix-vector multiplications) on coefficient vectors, at the cost of assuming input functions are sufficiently smooth to avoid Gibbs phenomenon artifacts [Parent].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A spherical function $f(\omega)$ (e.g., radiance, visibility, or a product of such functions) defined on the unit sphere $\Omega$.
*   **Output:** A vector of SH coefficients $\mathbf{f}$ representing the approximation $\tilde{f}(\omega)$, or the result of operations (like product convolution) on such vectors.

**Data Structures:**
*   **SH Coefficient Vector:** A 1D array of size $n = (L+1)^2$, where $L$ is the maximum degree (bandwidth).
*   **Index Mapping:** Coefficients are linearized into the 1D vector using the index $i = l^2 + l + m$, where $l \in \{0, \dots, L\}$ is the degree and $m \in \{-l, \dots, l\}$ is the order [S1, Parent].
*   **Transfer Matrix:** A precomputed 3D tensor $C_{ijk}$ of size $n \times n \times n$, where $C_{ijk} = \int_{\Omega} y_i(\omega)y_j(\omega)y_k(\omega)d\omega$ [S1].

**Steps:**

1.  **Projection (Coefficient Extraction):**
    Compute the SH coefficients $\mathbf{f}$ for a given spherical function $f(\omega)$ by projecting it onto the basis functions $y_i(\omega)$:
    $$
    f_i = \int_{\Omega} f(\omega)y_i(\omega)d\omega
    $$
    In practice, this integral is often solved numerically (e.g., Monte Carlo) at initialization or via precomputation if $f(\omega)$ is static [S1]. For inverse problems, precomputing terms allows for differentiable updates of the coefficients [S1].

2.  **Reconstruction:**
    Approximate the function value at a direction $\omega$ by summing the basis functions weighted by the coefficients:
    $$
    \tilde{f}(\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i y_i(\omega)
    $$
    where $n$ is the number of bands (coefficients) [S1].

3.  **Product Convolution:**
    To compute the SH coefficients of the product of two functions $\tilde{a}(\omega)$ and $\tilde{b}(\omega)$, use the precomputed transfer matrix $C_{ijk}$:
    $$
    (\mathbf{a} * \mathbf{b})_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} C_{ijk} a_j b_k
    $$
    This avoids evaluating the integral of the product directly [S1].

4.  **Integral of Product:**
    The integral of the product of two approximated functions over the sphere is simply the dot product of their coefficient vectors:
    $$
    \int_{\Omega} \tilde{a}(\omega)\tilde{b}(\omega)d\omega = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}
    $$
    This property is useful for energy conservation or loss functions [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def project_to_sh(spherical_function, basis_functions, num_bands):
    """
    Projects a spherical function into SH coefficients.
    spherical_function: callable f(omega) returning value at direction omega.
    basis_functions: callable y(i, omega) returning basis value i at omega.
    num_bands: int n, total number of coefficients (L+1)^2.
    Returns: list of coefficients f_i.
    """
    coefficients = []
    # In practice, this integral is solved numerically (e.g., Monte Carlo)
    # or precomputed. For differentiable inverse problems, this step
    # may be replaced by an update rule for the coefficients themselves.
    for i in range(num_bands):
        # f_i = integral of f(omega) * y_i(omega) d(omega)
        # Placeholder for numerical integration routine
        coeff = numerical_integral(spherical_function, basis_functions[i])
        coefficients.append(coeff)
    return coefficients

def reconstruct_from_sh(coefficients, omega, basis_functions):
    """
    Reconstructs the function value at direction omega from SH coefficients.
    coefficients: list of f_i.
    omega: direction vector (theta, phi).
    basis_functions: callable y(i, omega).
    Returns: approximated value f_tilde(omega).
    """
    value = 0.0
    for i, coeff in enumerate(coefficients):
        value += coeff * basis_functions[i](omega)
    return value

def product_convolution(coeff_a, coeff_b, transfer_matrix):
    """
    Computes SH coefficients of the product of two functions.
    coeff_a: list of coefficients a_j.
    coeff_b: list of coefficients b_k.
    transfer_matrix: 3D array C_ijk.
    Returns: list of coefficients (a * b)_i.
    """
    n = len(coeff_a)
    result = []
    for i in range(n):
        sum_val = 0.0
        for j in range(n):
            for k in range(n):
                sum_val += transfer_matrix[i][j][k] * coeff_a[j] * coeff_b[k]
        result.append(sum_val)
    return result

def integral_of_product(coeff_a, coeff_b):
    """
    Computes the integral of the product of two approximated functions.
    Returns: dot product of coefficient vectors.
    """
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(coeff_a, coeff_b))
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Bandwidth ($n$):** The number of coefficients. A bandwidth of $n=8$ (corresponding to $L=1$ or $L=2$ depending on indexing convention, though the source implies $n$ is the count) is chosen for good low-frequency approximations [S1]. The total number of coefficients is $n = (L+1)^2$ [Parent].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Gibbs Phenomenon:** Discontinuities in the input function (e.g., hard shadows) cause slow convergence and ringing artifacts due to truncation of the basis [Parent].
*   **Smoothness Assumption:** The method assumes input functions are sufficiently smooth. [Parent]
*   **Precomputation Cost:** The transfer matrix $C_{ijk}$ is large ($O(n^3)$) and must be precomputed [S1].

## Subtopics

### Hemisphere Projection in SH Space
This variant computes the SH coefficients for the hemisphere visibility term $H(x) = \int_{\Omega} \max(\omega \cdot n(x), 0)y(\omega)d\omega$. Instead of numerical integration for every normal $n(x)$, it uses the rotation property of SHs. The integral is reformulated as $H(x) = \phi_{SH}(n(x)) \int_{\Omega} \max(i_z \cdot \omega, 0)y(\omega)d\omega$, where $\phi_{SH}(x)$ is a linear rotation function and $i_z = (0, 0, 1)^T$ [S1]. This makes the computation differentiable with respect to the normal $n(x)$ and allows the integral (which is symmetric along the z-axis) to be computed analytically or via fixed numerical integration at initialization [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Reconstruction is $O(n)$. Product convolution is $O(n^3)$ due to the transfer matrix, but can be optimized. Projection is $O(n \cdot S)$ where $S$ is the number of samples if done via Monte Carlo, or $O(1)$ if precomputed [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(n^2)$ for coefficient vectors and $O(n^3)$ for the transfer matrix [S1].
*   **Performance:** Significantly faster than Monte Carlo integration for inverse problems where differentiability is required [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Differentiable, efficient for low-frequency functions, enables analytic solutions for complex integrals (like the rendering equation in [S1]).
*   **Limitations:** Poor representation of high-frequency details or discontinuities (ringing artifacts) [Parent]. Fixed basis cannot adapt to specific features [Parent].
*   **When to use:** When the directional function is smooth and low-frequency, and differentiability is required (e.g., inverse rendering, optimization) [S1].
*   **When not to use:** For functions with sharp discontinuities or high-frequency variations where Gibbs artifacts are unacceptable [Parent].

## Background
The method is based on the mathematical properties of Spherical Harmonics, which form a complete basis of functions mapping the unit sphere $S^2$ to the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ [S1, Parent]. In graphics, real-valued bases are used to represent directional functions like radiance, visibility, or BRDFs [S2, unofficial]. The specific application to efficient and differentiable shadow computation for inverse problems was introduced in [S1]. The use of SH for product convolution via transfer matrices is a standard technique in global illumination, with detailed derivations available in Green [6] (cited in [S1]).
