# NeRV Rendering

Also called: NeRV ray marching, NeRV volume rendering

## Overview
NeRV (Neural Reflectance and Visibility Fields) is a continuous volumetric representation that renders relightable scenes by combining neural implicit fields for geometry, appearance, and visibility [S1]. Unlike standard neural volume rendering which typically assumes fixed lighting or simple diffuse shading, NeRV explicitly models surface normals and microfacet BRDF parameters via separate MLPs, and predicts visibility for accurate direct and indirect illumination [S1]. It distinguishes itself by enabling view-dependent relighting and realistic indirect lighting effects within a ray-marched volume framework, setting it apart from [Neural volume rendering](1576-neural-volume-rendering.md) which often lacks explicit reflectance models, and from [Direct volume ray casting](19-direct-volume-ray-casting.md) which relies on discrete voxel data and static transfer functions.

## How it works
NeRV rendering extends the standard ray-marching loop by querying three distinct neural networks at each sample point: a shape MLP for density and normals, a reflectance MLP for BRDF parameters, and a visibility MLP for occlusion estimates [S1]. The method integrates these properties using a quadrature rule similar to NeRF but with physically-based shading components [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Camera ray origin $\mathbf{c}$ and direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_o$.
*   Trained neural networks:
    *   Shape MLP $\text{MLP}_\theta$: Inputs 3D position $\mathbf{x}$, outputs volume density $\sigma$ and surface normal $\mathbf{n}$.
    *   Reflectance MLP $\text{MLP}_\psi$: Inputs 3D position $\mathbf{x}$, outputs BRDF parameters $(a, \gamma)$.
    *   Visibility MLP $\text{MLP}_\phi$: Inputs position $\mathbf{x}$ and direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, outputs expected termination depth $\tilde{D}_\phi$.
*   Environment lighting map $E(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i)$.
*   Rendering parameters: Number of stratified samples $N=256$, environment lighting grid resolution $12 \times 24$, indirect sampling directions $M=128$ [S1].

**Outputs:**
*   Pixel color $V(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o)$.

### Data Structures
The method relies on the internal weights of the MLPs. No explicit voxel grid or texture is stored; the "volume" is defined implicitly by the neural functions [S1].

### Steps
1.  **Ray Sampling**: Define the camera ray $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{c} - t\boldsymbol{\omega}_o$. Draw $N=256$ stratified samples along the ray [S1]. Let $\delta$ be the distance between adjacent samples [S1].
2.  **Neural Query**: For each sample point $\mathbf{x}(t)$:
    *   Query $\text{MLP}_\theta(\mathbf{x}(t))$ to obtain volume density $\sigma(t)$ and surface normal $\mathbf{n}(t) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \text{MLP}_\theta(\mathbf{x}(t))$ [S1].
    *   Query $\text{MLP}_\psi(\mathbf{x}(t))$ to obtain BRDF parameters $(a(t), \gamma(t))$ [S1].
3.  **Direct Illumination Estimation**: For each sample point $\mathbf{x}(t)$:
    *   Sample $K$ directions $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ from a $12 \times 24$ grid on the sphere [S1].
    *   For each $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$, query the visibility MLP $\tilde{D}_\phi(\mathbf{x}(t), \boldsymbol{\omega}_i)$ to estimate visibility [S1].
    *   Compute the microfacet BRDF value $R(\mathbf{x}(t), \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o)$ using the parameters $(a, \gamma)$ and the normal $\mathbf{n}(t)$ [S1].
    *   Evaluate the environment lighting $E(\mathbf{x}(t), -\boldsymbol{\omega}_i)$ [S1].
    *   Estimate the direct illumination contribution $L_{dir}(\mathbf{x}(t), \boldsymbol{\omega}_o)$ by integrating the product of lighting, visibility, and BRDF over the sphere [S1].
4.  **Indirect Illumination Estimation**: For each sample point $\mathbf{x}(t)$:
    *   Compute the expected camera ray termination depth $t_0 = D(\mathbf{c}, -\boldsymbol{\omega}_o)$ using the density samples $\sigma$ from Step 2 [S1].
    *   Determine the termination point $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}(t_0)$ [S1].
    *   Sample $M=128$ random directions $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ on the upper hemisphere at $\mathbf{x}_0$ [S1].
    *   For each $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$, query the visibility MLP $\tilde{D}_\phi(\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i)$ to get expected termination depth $t''_0$ [S1].
    *   Compute secondary intersection points $\mathbf{x}_0(t''_0) = \mathbf{x}_0 + t''_0 \boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ [S1].
    *   Shade these secondary points with direct illumination (following Step 3) [S1].
    *   Estimate the indirect illumination incident at $\mathbf{x}_0$ by integrating the direct illumination from secondary points weighted by the BRDF $R(\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o)$ over the hemisphere [S1].
5.  **Radiance Composition**: Compute the total reflected radiance $L_r(\mathbf{x}(t), \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) = L_{dir} + L_{indirect}$ for each sample [S1].
6.  **Volume Compositing**: Composite the radiance along the ray using the quadrature rule [S1]:
    $$
    V(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) = \sum_{t} V(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{c}) \alpha(\sigma(\mathbf{x}(t)) \delta) L_r(\mathbf{x}(t), \boldsymbol{\omega}_o)
    $$
    where the transmittance $V(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{c})$ and opacity $\alpha$ are defined as:
    $$
    V(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{c}) = \exp\left(-\sum_{s < t} \sigma(\mathbf{x}(s)) \delta\right)
    $$
    $$
    \alpha(z) = 1 - \exp(-z)
    $$
    Here, $\delta$ is the step size between samples [S1].

### Parameters
*   **Stratified samples**: 256 [S1].
*   **Environment lighting grid**: $12 \times 24$ directions [S1].
*   **Indirect sampling directions**: 128 random directions on the upper hemisphere [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Visibility MLP Output**: The visibility MLP outputs expected termination depths, which must be correctly interpreted as visibility weights or used to find secondary intersections. Misinterpreting depth as a binary occlusion flag will break indirect lighting [S1].
*   **Surface Normal Gradient**: The normal is computed as the gradient of the shape MLP. This requires differentiable MLPs and can be noisy if the MLP is not smooth [S1].
*   **Termination Depth**: The expected camera ray termination depth $t_0$ is critical for indirect lighting. If the density is low everywhere, $t_0$ may be undefined or at the max bound, leading to incorrect indirect shading [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: High. Each sample requires multiple MLP queries (shape, reflectance, visibility). Direct lighting requires $12 \times 24 = 288$ MLP queries per sample. Indirect lighting requires 128 secondary rays, each with 288 lighting queries. Total queries per pixel can exceed $256 \times (3 + 288 + 128 \times 288)$, making it extremely slow for real-time [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity**: Low. The model size is determined by the MLP weights, not the resolution. No voxel grids or textures are stored [S1].
*   **Strengths**: Relightable scenes, view-dependent appearance, realistic indirect lighting, continuous representation [S1].
*   **Limitations**: Very slow rendering speed, requires pre-training of MLPs, sensitive to MLP smoothness for normals [S1].
*   **When to use**: Offline rendering, relighting applications, high-quality view synthesis [S1].
*   **When not to use**: Real-time applications, static lighting scenarios [S1].

## Background
NeRV (Neural Reflectance and Visibility Fields) was introduced by Liu et al. in 2021 [S1]. It extends neural implicit representations by explicitly modeling reflectance and visibility to enable relighting and indirect illumination, building on the foundations of NeRF [S1].
