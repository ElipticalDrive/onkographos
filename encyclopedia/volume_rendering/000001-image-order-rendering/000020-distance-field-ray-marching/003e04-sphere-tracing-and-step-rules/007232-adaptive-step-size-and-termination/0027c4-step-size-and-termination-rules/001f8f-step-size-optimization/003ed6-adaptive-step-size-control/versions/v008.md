# Adaptive Step Size Control

Also called: Adaptive Step Size Controller, Integrator Step Controller, Step Safety Factor

## Overview
Adaptive Step Size Control is a family of techniques that dynamically adjust the ray marching step size $\Delta t$ during rendering to balance computational cost and visual fidelity. It solves the problem of fixed-step inefficiency by increasing step sizes in empty or homogeneous regions and decreasing them in high-frequency or high-density areas. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [Safe Raymarching Step Size](1462-safe-raymarching-step-size.md) by focusing on continuous adaptation based on local scene metrics (density, variance, or interaction state) rather than solely on geometric safety bounds or minimal sample distance calculations.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on a ray defined by origin $\mathbf{o}$ and normalized direction $\mathbf{d}$, parameterized by distance $t$. The core loop evaluates a scene function (SDF, density field, or volume texture) at the current position $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ to determine the next step size $\Delta t$ and check for termination.

**Termination Conditions:**
1.  **Surface Hit:** The ray has intersected the target geometry. For SDFs, this is typically $|f(\mathbf{p})| < \epsilon$ or $f(\mathbf{p}) \le 0$. For volumes, this may be a density threshold or opacity accumulation [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Escape/Miss:** The ray has traveled beyond a maximum distance $t_{max}$ or exceeded a maximum iteration count $N_{max}$.
3.  **Early Termination (Volumes):** Accumulated opacity $\alpha_{acc}$ exceeds a threshold (e.g., $1 - \epsilon$), or accumulated transmission falls below a minimum threshold, allowing the ray to stop early as further contributions are negligible [S1, unofficial].

**Step Size Determination:**
The step size $\Delta t$ is computed based on the local scene properties:
*   **SDF-based:** $\Delta t$ is often derived from the SDF value $f(\mathbf{p})$, potentially scaled by a relaxation factor or Lipschitz constant $\lambda$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Volume-based:** $\Delta t$ may be fixed, adaptive based on density gradients, or constrained by minimum/maximum bounds to prevent aliasing or excessive computation [S2, unofficial].
*   **Safety:** Steps must be chosen to ensure the ray does not penetrate the surface or skip over significant volumetric features, often requiring conservative clamping.

**Pseudocode Structure:**
```python
def ray_march_loop(ray_origin, ray_direction, scene_func, max_dist, max_steps, epsilon):
    t = 0.0
    for i in range(max_steps):
        if t > max_dist:
            return False, t
        
        p = ray_origin + ray_direction * t
        val = scene_func(p)
        
        # Termination: Hit
        if is_hit(val, epsilon):
            return True, t
        
        # Termination: Early Exit (Volume specific)
        if is_early_exit(val, t):
            return False, t # Or handle partial contribution
            
        # Step Size Calculation
        step = compute_step_size(val, t, i)
        
        # Safety Clamp
        step = max(step, min_step)
        step = min(step, max_step)
        
        t += step
        
    return False, t
```

## Subtopics

### [Adaptive Step Size Ray Marching](2312-adaptive-step-size-ray-marching.md)
Adaptive Step Size Ray Marching is a volume rendering technique that optimizes computational cost by varying the sampling interval along the ray based on local density changes and transmittance. It distinguishes itself from fixed-step ray marching by dynamically adjusting the step size to maintain visual fidelity in high-density regions while skipping empty space, and it often incorporates temporal accumulation and denoising to manage noise inherent in sparse sampling. It builds on [Adaptive Step Size Control](9843-adaptive-step-size-control.md).

### [Dynamic Sampling Step Adjustment](2442-dynamic-sampling-step-adjustment.md)
Dynamic Sampling Step Adjustment accelerates ray marching through homogeneous or empty regions by exponentially increasing the step size when no density is detected, then restoring the original step size upon encountering geometry. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [Adaptive Step Size Ray Marching](2312-adaptive-step-size-ray-marching.md) by relying on binary emptiness checks rather than continuous density metrics, and from [Variance-based Step Size Adaptation](13161-variance-based-step-size-adaptation.md) by using discrete doubling rather than statistical variance to drive step selection.

### [Adaptive Step Size for Interaction](6615-adaptive-step-size-for-interaction.md)
Adaptive Step Size for Interaction is a performance optimization technique that temporarily increases the ray marching step size during user interaction (e.g., viewpoint changes) to maintain frame rate, followed by a high-quality render with standard parameters. It differs from static step size reduction by being context-aware, triggering only during active manipulation, and from variance-based adaptation by relying on interaction state rather than local data density.

### [Variance-based Step Size Adaptation](13161-variance-based-step-size-adaptation.md)
Variance-based Step Size Adaptation is a preprocessing technique that determines the adaptive ray traversal step size ($\Delta t$) based on the statistical variance of voxel data within spatial bricks. It solves the problem of selecting an appropriate step size for sparse or local ray traversal without the computational expense of calculating iso-surface normals at runtime. By mapping brick variance to a scaling coefficient $\kappa$, it balances rendering speed and image quality, allowing larger steps in homogeneous regions and smaller steps in high-frequency areas.

## Background
The concept of adaptive step sizing in ray marching originated from the need to optimize the computational cost of volume rendering and SDF evaluation. Early implementations focused on safety factors to prevent surface penetration [S2, unofficial]. Modern implementations, such as those found in web-based volume renderers [S1, unofficial] and deep learning-based reconstruction pipelines [S2, unofficial], have expanded these techniques to include variance-based precomputation, dynamic doubling for empty space, and interaction-aware rendering. The method is widely used in real-time volume visualization, medical imaging, and scientific visualization where performance is critical.
