# Step Size Optimization
Also called: Adaptive Step Sizing, Dynamic Step Control

## Overview
Step Size Optimization encompasses techniques that dynamically determine the ray marching interval $\Delta t$ during traversal to balance computational cost and visual fidelity. It solves the problem of fixed-step methods either wasting samples in empty space or missing thin geometry due to insufficient sampling density. It differs from its siblings by focusing on the *mechanism* of step size selection: [Safe Raymarching Step Size](1462-safe-raymarching-step-size.md) relies on Lipschitz bounds for safety, [Compute Minimal Sample Distance Per Pixel](2684-compute-minimal-sample-distance-per-pixel.md) uses precomputed per-pixel metrics, [Error Estimate](2768-error-estimate.md) targets integration error, [Gap-Limited Ray Marching](4412-gap-limited-ray-marching.md) handles sparse data gaps, and [Adaptive Volume Rendering Integration](9817-adaptive-volume-rendering-integration.md) focuses on integration accuracy.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on a ray defined by origin $\mathbf{o}$ and normalized direction $\mathbf{d}$, parameterized by distance $t$. The core loop evaluates a scene function (SDF, density field, or volume texture) at the current position $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ to determine the next step size $\Delta t$ and check for termination.

**Termination Conditions:**
1.  **Surface Hit:** The ray has intersected the target geometry. For SDFs, this is typically $|f(\mathbf{p})| < \epsilon$ or $f(\mathbf{p}) \le 0$ . For volumes, this may be a density threshold or opacity accumulation [S1].
2.  **Escape/Miss:** The ray has traveled beyond a maximum distance $t_{max}$ or exceeded a maximum iteration count $N_{max}$ .
3.  **Early Termination (Volumes):** Accumulated opacity $\alpha_{acc}$ exceeds a threshold (e.g., $1 - \epsilon$), or accumulated transmission falls below a minimum threshold, allowing the ray to stop early as further contributions are negligible [S1].

**Step Size Determination:**
The step size $\Delta t$ is computed based on the local scene properties:
*   **SDF-based:** $\Delta t$ is often derived from the SDF value $f(\mathbf{p})$, potentially scaled by a relaxation factor or Lipschitz constant $\lambda$ [S2].
*   **Volume-based:** $\Delta t$ may be fixed, adaptive based on density gradients, or constrained by minimum/maximum bounds to prevent aliasing or excessive computation [S2].
*   **Safety:** Steps must be chosen to ensure the ray does not penetrate the surface or skip over significant volumetric features, often requiring conservative clamping .

**Pseudocode Structure:**
```python
def ray_march_loop(ray_origin, ray_direction, scene_func, max_dist, max_steps, epsilon):
    t = 0.0
    for i in range(max_steps):
        if t > max_dist:
            return False, t
        
        p = ray_origin + ray_direction * t
        val = scene_func(p)
        
        # Termination: Hit
        if is_hit(val, epsilon):
            return True, t
        
        # Termination: Early Exit (Volume specific)
        if is_early_exit(val, t):
            return False, t # Or handle partial contribution
            
        # Step Size Calculation
        step = compute_step_size(val, t, i)
        
        # Safety Clamp
        step = max(step, min_step)
        step = min(step, max_step)
        
        t += step
        
    return False, t
```

## Subtopics

### [Safe Raymarching Step Size](1462-safe-raymarching-step-size.md)
Safe Raymarching Step Size is a technique for traversing a Signed Distance Field (SDF) where the step length at each iteration is determined by the local distance to the nearest surface, ensuring the ray never overshoots geometry. It solves the problem of missing thin structures or artifacts caused by fixed-step ray marching by adapting the step size to the local geometry. It differs from its siblings by explicitly using the SDF value as a lower bound on the distance to any surface, whereas [Compute Minimal Sample Distance Per Pixel](2684-compute-minimal-sample-distance-per-pixel.md) focuses on pixel-level sampling density, and [Gap-Limited Ray Marching](4412-gap-limited-ray-marching.md) handles specific occlusion gaps.

### [Compute Minimal Sample Distance Per Pixel](2684-compute-minimal-sample-distance-per-pixel.md)
This method computes the minimal sample distance per pixel by projecting each dataset cell axis onto the viewport, accounting for camera and volume transforms. It determines the smallest valid step size for ray marching based on data resolution limits rather than surface Lipschitz constraints. It differs from [Safe Raymarching Step Size](1462-safe-raymarching-step-size.md) by focusing on grid resolution, and from [Projected Voxel Size / LOD Calculation](4189-projected-voxel-size-lod-calculation.md) by providing a direct scalar metric for step sizing rather than a general LOD metric.

### [Error Estimate](2768-error-estimate.md)
Error Estimate calculates a relative error metric to determine whether a ray marching step is acceptable, enabling adaptive step sizing in distance-field ray marching. It differs from [Safe Raymarching Step Size](1462-safe-raymarching-step-size.md), which computes a geometric upper bound on the step length, by evaluating the numerical consistency of the current integration step against the field values, allowing for smaller steps in regions of high variation or numerical instability.

### [Gap-Limited Ray Marching](4412-gap-limited-ray-marching.md)
Gap-Limited Ray Marching is a heightfield intersection technique that prevents ray stalling and surface overshooting on ridgelines by combining a distance-proportional step size with a vertical gap bound. Unlike standard sphere tracing which relies on a global distance field, this method uses the local vertical clearance to the terrain surface to cap the step, ensuring the ray never jumps over narrow peaks while maintaining efficiency on flat or gently sloping ground. It builds on [Step Size Optimization](2054-step-size-optimization.md) and [Step Size and Termination Rules](4132-step-size-and-termination-rules.md).

### [Adaptive Volume Rendering Integration](9817-adaptive-volume-rendering-integration.md)
Adaptive Volume Rendering Integration (VRI) is an iterative numerical method that evaluates the Volume Rendering Integral using adaptive step sizes for both the inner (transparency/extinction) and outer (color/emission) integrals. It solves the problem of balancing accuracy and performance in volume rendering by dynamically adjusting step sizes based on local function variation, avoiding the recursion depth and stack-context issues of standard recursive adaptive methods on GPU architectures. Unlike naive recursive approaches that re-evaluate the inner integral for every outer sample, this method advances both integrals in consonance, reusing intermediate Simpson's rule evaluations to minimize redundant computation.

### [Adaptive Step Size Control](9843-adaptive-step-size-control.md)
Adaptive Step Size Control is a family of techniques that dynamically adjust the ray marching interval $\Delta t$ during volume rendering to balance computational cost and visual fidelity. Unlike fixed-step methods that enforce a uniform trade-off, these methods vary the sampling density based on local scene properties (such as density gradients, emptiness, or statistical variance) or user interaction state. This family is distinguished from its siblings by its focus on the *mechanism* of step size selection: [Safe Raymarching Step Size](1462-safe-raymarching-step-size.md) relies on Lipschitz bounds for safety, [Compute Minimal Sample Distance Per Pixel](2684-compute-minimal-sample-distance-per-pixel.md) uses precomputed per-pixel metrics, [Error Estimate](2768-error-estimate.md) targets integration error, [Gap-Limited Ray Marching](4412-gap-limited-ray-marching.md) handles sparse data gaps, and [Adaptive Volume Rendering Integration](9817-adaptive-volume-rendering-integration.md) focuses on integration accuracy.

#### definition
- [S1] Using knowledge of convexity to increase step size along the ray. For a convex set, the line segment from the current point to the tangent plane intersection does not intersect the surface except possibly at the endpoint.
- [S1] An optimization that hastens convergence by leveraging convexity properties, providing a faster order of convergence than standard sphere tracing.

## Background
Originating from the geometric method of sphere tracing, step size optimization evolved to address the inefficiencies of fixed-step ray marching. Early work by Williams et al. introduced convexity enhancement to sphere tracing, demonstrating quadratic convergence similar to Newton's method [S1]. Later developments in volume rendering introduced adaptive integration strategies, such as adaptive Simpson's rule, to handle the complexity of the volume rendering integral [S1]. Procedural noise optimization techniques, particularly for fractal Brownian motion (FBM), further refined step sizing by linking sampling density to distance and frequency content [S2]. These methods are widely used in real-time ray marching for implicit surfaces, terrain rendering, and high-quality volume visualization.
