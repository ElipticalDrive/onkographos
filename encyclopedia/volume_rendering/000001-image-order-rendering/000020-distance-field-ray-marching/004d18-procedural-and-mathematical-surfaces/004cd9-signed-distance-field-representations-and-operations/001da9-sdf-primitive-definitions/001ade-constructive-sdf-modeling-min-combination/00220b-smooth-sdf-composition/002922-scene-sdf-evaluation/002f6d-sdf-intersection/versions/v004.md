# SDF Intersection
Also called: Intersection of SDFs, SDF Max, SDF Intersection via Max

## Overview
SDF Intersection is a boolean set operation that computes the region common to two or more Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) by returning the maximum distance value at any given point [S1, S3, unofficial]. It solves the problem of combining procedural shapes into complex geometries where the final surface must satisfy the boundary conditions of all constituent shapes simultaneously [S4]. Unlike the union operation (min), which blends shapes into a single continuous volume, intersection requires the ray to be inside all input shapes, effectively carving out the space outside any single component [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
- Input: Two SDF evaluation functions, $S_1(p)$ and $S_2(p)$, and a query point $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
- Output: A scalar distance value $d_{intersect} \in \mathbb{R}$ representing the signed distance to the intersection surface.

**Data Structures**
No specific data structures are required beyond the scalar values returned by the primitive SDF evaluations. The operation is point-wise and stateless.

**Algorithm**
1. Evaluate the signed distance to the first shape at point $p$:
   $$ a = S_1(p) $$
2. Evaluate the signed distance to the second shape at point $p$:
   $$ b = S_2(p) $$
3. Compute the intersection distance by taking the maximum of the two values:
   $$ d_{intersect} = \max(a, b) $$

**Formulas**
The core formula for the intersection of two SDFs $A$ and $B$ at point $p$ is:
$$ S_{intersect}(p) = \max(S_A(p), S_B(p)) $$

Where:
- $S_A(p)$ is the signed distance from $p$ to the surface of shape A.
- $S_B(p)$ is the signed distance from $p$ to the surface of shape B.
- $\max(x, y)$ returns the larger of $x$ and $y$.

**Parameters**
- No additional parameters are required for the standard boolean intersection.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
- **Discontinuous Derivatives:** The standard `max` operator produces non-smooth intersections with discontinuous derivatives at the boundary where $S_A(p) = S_B(p)$ [S1, unofficial]. This results in visible creases or sharp edges in the rendered surface, which may be undesirable for organic shapes [S1, unofficial].
- **Precision:** In regions where the surfaces are very close, floating-point precision errors can cause artifacts in the intersection boundary.
- **Empty Intersection:** If the shapes do not overlap, the resulting SDF will represent the union of the two shapes' exteriors, effectively rendering nothing if the ray never enters the interior of both.

**Pseudocode**
```python
def sdf_intersection(p, sdf_a, sdf_b):
    """
    Computes the intersection of two SDFs at point p.
    
    Args:
        p: vec3, the query point in 3D space.
        sdf_a: function, returns signed distance to shape A.
        sdf_b: function, returns signed distance to shape B.
        
    Returns:
        float, the signed distance to the intersection surface.
    """
    # Evaluate distance to first shape
    dist_a = sdf_a(p)
    
    # Evaluate distance to second shape
    dist_b = sdf_b(p)
    
    # Intersection is the maximum of the two distances
    # This ensures the point is inside both shapes (distance <= 0)
    # or outside the closest boundary of either.
    return max(dist_a, dist_b)
```

### Smooth SDF Composition
Uses smooth minimum/maximum operators (e.g., `smin`) to approximate the intersection with continuous derivatives, avoiding the sharp creases of the boolean `max`. Choose when organic blending is required. [Smooth SDF Composition](4453-smooth-sdf-composition.md)

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: $O(1)$ per evaluation relative to the number of shapes being intersected (assuming a fixed number of inputs). The cost is dominated by the cost of evaluating the underlying primitive SDFs.
- **Memory Complexity**: $O(1)$. No additional memory is required beyond the stack variables for the distance values.
- **Measured Performance**: The operation is extremely fast, consisting of two function calls and one comparison. It is often used in real-time ray marching where performance is critical [S2, unofficial].
- **Strengths**: Exact watertight surfaces without robustness problems associated with mesh-based CSG [S2, unofficial]. Simple to implement and integrate into procedural scene definitions [S4].
- **Limitations**: The resulting surface has discontinuous normals at the intersection boundary, which can cause artifacts in lighting calculations (e.g., incorrect shading at the crease) unless smoothed [S1, unofficial].
- **When to use**: When constructing complex procedural geometry via Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) where sharp, precise intersections are desired [S1, unofficial][S4].
- **When not to use**: When smooth, organic transitions between shapes are required; use Smooth SDF Composition instead [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The use of min and max operators for boolean set operations on SDFs was proven by John Hart in his work on sphere tracing [S4]. Hart demonstrated that the union of two SDFs corresponds to the minimum of their distances, and the intersection corresponds to the maximum [S4]. This insight allows for the manipulation of procedural-based SDF representations using CSG approaches, as later utilized by Reiner [S4]. The technique has become a standard building block in ray marching shaders, popularized by community resources such as Shadertoy and tutorials by Inigo Quilez [S1, unofficial].
