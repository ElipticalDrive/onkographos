# Distance to Superquadrics

Also called: Superellipsoid distance, Superellipsoids, Superquadric Distance via p-norm, Supertori, pq-norm distance

## Overview
Distance to Superquadrics generalizes standard natural quadrics (spheres, cylinders, cones) and tori into superquadrics and supertori by replacing the Euclidean $L_2$ norm with the $L_p$ (or $L_{pq}$) norm [S1, S2]. This method allows for the creation of shapes with rounded, square, or pinched cross-sections by varying the exponent parameter $p$ [S1]. It is distinct from [Distance to Natural Quadrics](1559-distance-to-natural-quadrics.md) because it operates in a non-Euclidean metric space, requiring a specific conversion to Euclidean distances to ensure consistent ray marching behavior and blending with other primitives [S1, S2].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Point $p = (x, y, z)$ in 3D space.
*   Shape exponent $p$ (scalar, $p > 0$).
*   Radius $r$ (scalar, defining the scale of the superellipsoid).
*   Optional: Exponents $p, q$ for asymmetric superquadrics (supertori or generalized ellipsoids).

**Outputs:**
*   Signed distance $d(p)$ in Euclidean space.

**Data Structures:**
*   No persistent data structures required. The function is stateless and computes the distance analytically per query.

**Formulas:**
The core distance metric is the $L_p$ norm. In 2D, the $L_p$ norm of a vector $(x, y)$ is defined as [S1]:
$$ ||(x, y)||_p = (|x|^p + |y|^p)^{1/p} $$
In 3D, for a superellipsoid centered at the origin, the implicit surface is defined by [S1]:
$$ (|x|^p + |y|^p + |z|^p)^{1/p} = r $$
The raw distance in the $L_p$ metric, denoted $d_p(p)$, is calculated as [S1]:
$$ d_p(p) = (|x|^p + |y|^p + |z|^p)^{1/p} - r $$

**Euclidean Conversion:**
Because ray marching requires a unifying Euclidean metric space to compare distances between different primitives and to ensure the step size is safe (i.e., the ray does not pass through the surface), the $L_p$ distance must be converted to an equivalent Euclidean distance [S1, S2].

Let $r_e$ be the radius of the largest Euclidean sphere inscribed within the $L_p$-norm superellipsoid of radius $r$ (in the $L_p$ metric). The conversion factor depends on $p$ [S1, S2]:

$$ r_e = \begin{cases} r \cdot \frac{3^{1/p}}{3^{1/2}} & \text{if } p < 2 \\ r & \text{if } p \ge 2 \end{cases} $$

The final Euclidean signed distance $d_{eucl}(p)$ is scaled by the ratio of the inscribed radius to the nominal radius [S1, S2]:
$$ d_{eucl}(p) = d_p(p) \cdot \frac{r_e}{r} $$

Substituting $r_e/r$:
$$ d_{eucl}(p) = \begin{cases} \left( (|x|^p + |y|^p + |z|^p)^{1/p} - r \right) \cdot \frac{3^{1/p}}{\sqrt{3}} & \text{if } p < 2 \\ (|x|^p + |y|^p + |z|^p)^{1/p} - r & \text{if } p \ge 2 \end{cases} $$

**Parameters:**
*   $p$: Controls the shape.
    *   $p = 2$: Euclidean sphere (round).
    *   $p = 1$: Octahedron (diamond in 2D).
    *   $p \to \infty$: Cube (square in 2D, chessboard metric).
    *   $0 < p < 1$: Pinched or star-shaped variations [S1].
*   $r$: Base radius.

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import math

def distance_to_superquadric(x, y, z, p, r):
    """
    Computes the Euclidean signed distance to a superellipsoid.
    
    Args:
        x, y, z: Coordinates of the query point.
        p: Shape exponent (p > 0).
        r: Nominal radius.
        
    Returns:
        Euclidean signed distance.
    """
    # Calculate Lp norm distance from origin
    # Use abs() for negative coordinates to handle the metric correctly
    lp_norm = (abs(x)**p + abs(y)**p + abs(z)**p)**(1.0/p)
    
    # Raw distance in Lp metric
    d_lp = lp_norm - r
    
    # Calculate Euclidean conversion factor
    # The radius of the largest Euclidean sphere inscribed in the Lp superellipsoid
    # is r_e = r * (3^(1/p) / 3^(1/2)) for p < 2, else r_e = r
    if p < 2.0:
        # Conversion factor: r_e / r = 3^(1/p) / sqrt(3)
        # This scales the Lp distance to be consistent with Euclidean space
        # for the purpose of sphere tracing step sizes.
        conversion_factor = (3.0**(1.0/p)) / math.sqrt(3.0)
    else:
        conversion_factor = 1.0
        
    return d_lp * conversion_factor
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Metric Unification:** Using $L_p$ distances directly for blending or comparison with other primitives (like spheres or cylinders) is invalid because they exist in different metric spaces [S1, S2]. The Euclidean conversion is mandatory for correct visual results in composite scenes.
*   **Precision:** For very large $p$, $|x|^p$ can overflow or underflow. Standard floating-point handling applies.
*   **Degenerate Input:** $p=0$ is undefined. $p < 0$ produces non-convex, pinched shapes that may require specialized handling not covered by the standard convex superellipsoid definition [S1].

## Subtopics

### Supertori
A generalization of the torus using $L_p$ norms for both the major and minor circles. It follows the same conversion principles as superellipsoids but applies the norm to the 2D cross-section of the torus [S1].

### pq-norm Superquadrics
Uses different exponents $p$ and $q$ for different axes (e.g., $|x|^p + |y|^q + |z|^p$). This allows for asymmetric shapes. The Euclidean conversion factor must be derived based on the specific $p, q$ values, typically involving the maximum inscribed sphere in the asymmetric metric [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per query. Requires $O(1)$ arithmetic operations (powers, roots, absolute values).
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$. No auxiliary data structures.
*   **Strengths:** Provides a continuous family of shapes from spheres to cubes to pinched forms with a single parameter. Efficient for ray marching when the Euclidean conversion is applied.
*   **Limitations:** The gradient is not always smooth at $p=1$ or $p \to \infty$ boundaries in the raw metric, though the Euclidean conversion helps maintain continuity in the signed distance field. The conversion factor is an approximation based on the inscribed sphere; it ensures safety (no tunneling) but may result in slightly larger step sizes than strictly necessary for non-spherical shapes, potentially slowing convergence slightly compared to exact analytical normals for simple primitives.
*   **When to use:** When a shape with adjustable "squareness" or "pinchedness" is needed, or when blending between different geometric primitives in a unified SDF framework.

## Background
Superquadrics were introduced by Alan Barr in 1981 [S1, S2]. The concept arises from generalizing the distance metrics used in basic primitive definitions. While natural quadrics rely on the Euclidean $L_2$ norm, superquadrics utilize the $L_p$ norm to create a broader class of implicit surfaces. This method is integral to the Sphere Tracing algorithm, as it allows for the rendering of complex, organic, or mechanical shapes that are not representable by simple quadratic equations. The requirement for Euclidean conversion was highlighted to ensure that the geometric method of sphere tracing remains valid across different metric spaces [S1, S2].
