# Offset Surface Evaluation

Also called: Distance to Offset Surfaces, Global offset, Local offset, Point-to-set distance

## Overview
Offset Surface Evaluation computes the signed distance from a query point to a surface generated by offsetting a base "skeleton" geometry by a fixed distance. It solves the problem of defining smooth, thickened representations of curves, surfaces, or meshes for use in implicit modeling and ray marching. It is distinguished from its siblings by its reliance on a global point-to-set distance search (global offset) rather than a local parametric displacement, which avoids the interior self-intersections and high-degree algebraic artifacts common in local offset methods [S1].

## How it works
The method evaluates the distance to an offset surface by finding the closest point on the underlying skeleton geometry.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Query point $\mathbf{p}$, skeleton geometry $S$ (e.g., a space curve or surface), constant offset distance $c$.
- Output: Signed distance $d(\mathbf{p})$ to the offset surface.

**Data Structures:**
- Skeleton representation: Parametric form $\mathbf{s}(t)$ for curves or $\mathbf{s}(u,v)$ for surfaces, or a mesh vertex list with normals.
- Distance metric: Euclidean distance.

**Steps:**
1. **Identify Skeleton Type:** Determine if the skeleton is a parametric curve, parametric surface, or mesh.
2. **Find Closest Point:** Search the skeleton $S$ for the point $\mathbf{s}^*$ that minimizes the Euclidean distance to $\mathbf{p}$.
   - For a parametric curve $\mathbf{p}(t)$, the closest point satisfies the orthogonality condition:
     $$ (\mathbf{p}(t) - \mathbf{p}) \cdot \mathbf{p}'(t) = 0 $$
     where $\mathbf{p}'(t)$ is the tangent vector [S1]. This equation is solved for $t$ (e.g., converting a cubic Bezier curve to a degree-5 Bernstein polynomial and solving via convex hull bounding) [S1].
   - For a mesh, the closest point is found by iterating over primitives (triangles/vertices) and finding the minimum distance [S2, unofficial].
3. **Compute Distance:** Calculate the Euclidean distance $d_{raw} = \| \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}^* \|$.
4. **Apply Offset:** The signed distance to the offset surface is:
   $$ d(\mathbf{p}) = d_{raw} - c $$
   where $c$ is the constant offset distance [S1]. If $\mathbf{p}$ is inside the offset volume, $d(\mathbf{p})$ is negative.

**Parameters:**
- $c$: Constant offset distance. Typical values depend on the scale of the scene [S1].

**Pitfalls:**
- **Interior Surfaces:** Global offsets can create interior surfaces (self-intersections) if the skeleton has high curvature or sharp features, causing artifacts in ray-tracing and CSG operations [S1].
- **Gradient Discontinuities:** Local offsets (parametric) can cause gradient discontinuities or result in high-degree algebraic representations, making them less desirable for smooth implicit rendering compared to global offsets [S1].
- **Mesh Self-Intersection:** When offsetting a mesh by moving vertices along normals, self-intersections are not handled automatically [S2, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def offset_surface_distance(p, skeleton, c):
    """
    Computes distance from point p to the offset surface of skeleton by distance c.
    skeleton: object with method closest_point(p) returning (closest_point, is_inside)
    c: float, offset distance
    """
    # Step 1: Find closest point on the skeleton
    closest_pt = skeleton.closest_point(p)
    
    # Step 2: Compute raw Euclidean distance
    raw_dist = length(p - closest_pt)
    
    # Step 3: Apply offset
    # Note: Sign convention depends on implementation. 
    # Typically, distance to surface is raw_dist - c.
    # If the skeleton is a solid volume, sign might need inversion.
    # Here we assume standard SDF convention: outside positive.
    dist = raw_dist - c
    
    return dist
```
*Note: The specific implementation of `skeleton.closest_point(p)` varies by skeleton type (curve, surface, mesh) and is not detailed here as it is a sub-problem of the primitive definition [S1][S2, unofficial].*

## Subtopics

### Offset Surface Creation
Creates an offset surface from a mesh by moving vertices along their computed normals by a given distance. It is a local offset method, distinct from the global point-to-set distance approach. It is chosen for mesh-based modeling where normals are available. It does not handle self-intersections, as vertices are moved independently along their original normals [S2, unofficial].
- **Definition:** Parametric displacement of mesh vertices.
- **Steps:**
  1. Ensure normals are computed: `mesh->need_normals()`.
  2. Iterate over all vertices $i$ from $0$ to $nv-1$.
  3. Update vertex position: `mesh->vertices[i] += amount * mesh->normals[i]`.
  4. Invalidate bounding box and bounding sphere flags.
- **Parameters:** `amount`: distance to offset along normals [S2, unofficial].
- **Pitfalls:** Does not handle self-intersections; vertices are moved independently along their original normals [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Depends on the skeleton search. For a mesh, it is $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of primitives, unless accelerated by a BVH or grid. For parametric curves, it involves solving polynomial equations, which is constant time but potentially expensive per evaluation.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store the skeleton geometry.
- **Strengths:** Global offsets avoid interior surfaces and are more desirable for ray-tracing and CSG operations [S1]. They provide smooth, continuous distance fields.
- **Limitations:** Can create interior surfaces if the skeleton is complex. Local offsets can cause gradient discontinuities [S1]. Mesh-based local offsets do not handle self-intersections [S2, unofficial].
- **When to use:** When a smooth, thickened representation of a curve or surface is needed for ray marching or implicit modeling.
- **When not to use:** When the skeleton has high curvature leading to self-intersections, or when performance is critical and the skeleton is a large mesh without acceleration structures.

## Background
The concept of offset surfaces is rooted in computer-aided geometric design (CAGD) and implicit surface rendering. Global offsets are defined geometrically by the implicit equation $d(\mathbf{p}) = 0$, where $d(\mathbf{p})$ is the point-to-set distance to a closed skeleton geometry $S$ [S1]. When $d$ is constant, the surface fleshes out the skeleton [S1]. The formulation of point-to-set distance reduces the search space for closest point determination to primitives such as parametric surfaces [S1]. Global offsets are usually defined as geometric implicit surfaces, whereas local offsets are usually defined parametrically [S1]. Global offsets are considered more desirable [Hoffman, 1989] as they avoid interior surfaces which can cause problems in ray-tracing and CSG [van Wijk, 1984]. The offset of an algebraic implicit surface is algebraic, though of higher degree in general [S1].
