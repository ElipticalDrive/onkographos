# Offset Surface Evaluation

Also called: Distance to Offset Surfaces, Global offset, Local offset, Point-to-set distance

## Overview
Offset Surface Evaluation computes the signed distance from a query point $\mathbf{p}$ to a surface generated by offsetting a base "skeleton" geometry $S$ by a fixed distance $c$ [S1]. It solves the problem of defining smooth, thickened versions of curves, surfaces, or meshes for use in implicit modeling and ray tracing [S1]. It is distinguished from its siblings by its reliance on a global point-to-set distance search rather than simple parametric displacement or local Boolean combinations [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Query point $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
*   Skeleton geometry $S$ (e.g., a space curve, parametric surface, or mesh).
*   Offset distance $c \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].

**Outputs:**
*   Signed distance $d(\mathbf{p})$ to the offset surface. The offset surface is defined implicitly by $d(\mathbf{p}) = 0$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   The skeleton $S$ is represented by its parametric equations or vertex/normal arrays.
*   For parametric surfaces, partial derivatives $\frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial u}, \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial v}$ are required to compute normals [S1].

**Method:**
The core operation is finding the closest point on the skeleton $S$ to the query point $\mathbf{p}$. The distance to the offset surface is then the distance to this closest point minus the offset $c$ [S1].

1.  **Find Closest Point on Skeleton:** Search the entire set $S$ for the point $\mathbf{s} \in S$ that minimizes $\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}\|$ [S1].
    *   If $S$ is a smooth surface with defined normals, the closest point $\mathbf{s}$ lies on $S$ such that the vector $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}$ is parallel to the surface normal at $\mathbf{s}$ [S1].
    *   For a space curve $\mathbf{p}(t)$, the closest point satisfies the orthogonality condition:
        $$ (\mathbf{p}(t) - \mathbf{p}) \cdot \mathbf{p}'(t) = 0 $$
        where $\mathbf{p}'(t)$ is the tangent vector [S1].
    *   For a cubic Bezier curve, this condition converts into a degree-five 1-D Bernstein polynomial, solvable via root-finding techniques [S1].
2.  **Compute Distance:** Calculate the Euclidean distance to the closest point found in Step 1:
    $$ d_{raw} = \|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}\| $$
3.  **Apply Offset:** The signed distance to the offset surface is:
    $$ d(\mathbf{p}) = d_{raw} - c $$
    [S1]

**Local Offset Variant (Parametric):**
If a local offset is required (parametric definition), the surface is defined by:
$$ \mathbf{p}_{local}(u,v) = \mathbf{p}(u,v) + c \cdot \mathbf{n}(u,v) $$
where $\mathbf{n}(u,v)$ is the unit-length surface normal:
$$ \mathbf{n}(u,v) = \frac{\frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial v}}{\left\| \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial v} \right\|} $$
[S1]

**Mesh Offset Variant:**
For mesh-based skeletons, vertices are displaced along their normals [S2, unofficial]:
1.  Ensure normals are computed: `mesh->need_normals()` [S2, unofficial].
2.  Iterate over all vertices $i$ from $0$ to $nv-1$:
    $$ \mathbf{v}_i^{new} = \mathbf{v}_i + \text{amount} \cdot \mathbf{n}_i $$
    [S2, unofficial]
3.  Invalidate bounding box and bounding sphere flags [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   $c$: Constant offset distance. Typical values depend on the scale of the scene [S1].
*   `amount`: Distance to offset along normals for mesh variants [S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Interior Surfaces:** Global offsets can create interior self-intersections, causing artifacts in ray-tracing and CSG operations [S1].
*   **Gradient Discontinuities:** Local offsets (parametric) can cause gradient discontinuities or result in high-degree algebraic representations [S1].
*   **Self-Intersections (Mesh):** Moving vertices independently along original normals does not handle self-intersections [S2, unofficial].
*   **Precision:** The use of distance (requiring square roots and cross products) in local offset definitions can lead to loss of precision [S1].

## Subtopics

### Offset Surface Creation
Creates an offset surface from a mesh by moving vertices along their normals by a given distance [S2, unofficial]. It is a local, parametric approach that is computationally simple but does not handle self-intersections, as vertices are moved independently along their original normals [S2, unofficial]. Use this for simple mesh thickening where self-intersection is not a concern.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** The global offset requires a search through the entire skeleton set $S$ for the closest point, which can be $O(N)$ for a mesh with $N$ vertices or $O(1)$ with spatial acceleration structures [S1]. Local offsets are $O(1)$ per point but may involve higher-degree polynomial evaluations [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Global offsets provide a smooth, implicit representation that fleshes out skeletons effectively [S1]. They avoid the high-degree algebraic representations associated with local parametric offsets [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Risk of interior surfaces in global offsets [S1]. Local offsets suffer from gradient discontinuities [S1]. Mesh offsets fail at self-intersections [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Use global offsets for smooth, thickened representations of curves or surfaces in ray tracing. Use local offsets for parametric modeling where interior surfaces are not an issue.

## Background
The concept of offset surfaces is rooted in computer-aided geometric design (CAGD) and implicit surface modeling. Global offsets are considered the more desirable representation for avoiding interior surfaces in ray-tracing and CSG [S1, citing Hoffman, 1989; van Wijk, 1984]. The mathematical formulation for point-to-set distance and its application to space curves (e.g., Bezier curves) relies on solving polynomial equations derived from orthogonality conditions [S1, citing Schneider, 1990; Rockwood & Owen, 1987].
