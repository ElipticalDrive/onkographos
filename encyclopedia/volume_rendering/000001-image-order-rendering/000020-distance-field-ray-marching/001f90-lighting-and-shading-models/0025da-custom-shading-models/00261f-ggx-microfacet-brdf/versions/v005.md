# GGX Microfacet BRDF

Also called: GGX BRDF, GGX Microfacet Model, GGX Microfacet Specular Model, GGX Shading, Walter et al. Specular

## Overview
The GGX Microfacet BRDF is a specular reflection model that approximates surface roughness using the Gaussian distribution of microfacet normals (GGX) combined with the Schlick Fresnel approximation and a Smith geometry function [S1, S2, unofficial]. It is distinguished from its siblings, such as [Blinn-Phong with Metallic Reflections](4252-blinn-phong-with-metallic-reflections.md), by providing physically plausible energy conservation and accurate specular highlights for rough surfaces, whereas Blinn-Phong is an empirical approximation [S1, unofficial]. It serves as the standard for high-quality specular shading in real-time engines and voxel renderers [S1, S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Surface normal $\mathbf{n}$ (unit vector).
*   View direction $\mathbf{v}$ (unit vector, from surface to camera).
*   Light direction $\mathbf{l}$ (unit vector, from surface to light).
*   Roughness parameter $\alpha_{rough}$ (scalar, typically $[0, 1]$).
*   Specular reflectance at normal incidence $F_0$ (scalar or vector, typically $\approx 0.04$ for dielectrics, material-specific for metals).

**Outputs:**
*   Specular reflectance value (scalar or vector).

**Data Structures:**
*   No persistent data structures required. Computation is stateless per shading point.

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Half Vector:** Calculate the half vector $\mathbf{h}$ between the view and light directions:
    $$ \mathbf{h} = \frac{\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{l}}{\|\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{l}\|} $$
2.  **Compute Alpha:** Square the roughness parameter to get the variance $\alpha$:
    $$ \alpha = \alpha_{rough}^2 $$
3.  **Compute Normal Distribution Function (D):** Calculate the GGX distribution $D(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{h}, \alpha)$:
    $$ \text{dotNH} = \max(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{h}, 0.0) $$
    $$ D = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi \left( (\text{dotNH}^2 (\alpha^2 - 1) + 1)^2 \right) + \epsilon_D} $$
    Where $\epsilon_D$ is a small epsilon (e.g., $10^{-6}$) to prevent division by zero [S1, S2, unofficial].
4.  **Compute Geometry Function (G):** Calculate the Smith geometry function using the Schlick-GGX approximation. First, compute the parameter $k$:
    $$ k = \frac{(\alpha_{rough} + 1)^2}{8} $$
    Then compute the visibility for view and light:
    $$ \text{dotNV} = \max(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}, 0.0) $$
    $$ \text{dotNL} = \max(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}, 0.0) $$
    $$ G_1(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\text{dotNV}}{\max(\text{dotNV}(1 - k) + k, \epsilon_G)} $$
    $$ G_1(\mathbf{l}) = \frac{\text{dotNL}}{\max(\text{dotNL}(1 - k) + k, \epsilon_G)} $$
    $$ G = G_1(\mathbf{v}) \cdot G_1(\mathbf{l}) $$
    Where $\epsilon_G$ is a small epsilon (e.g., $10^{-6}$) [S2, unofficial].
5.  **Compute Fresnel Function (F):** Calculate the Schlick Fresnel approximation:
    $$ \text{dotLH} = \max(\mathbf{l} \cdot \mathbf{h}, 0.0) $$
    $$ F = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \text{dotLH})^5 $$
6.  **Combine:** Compute the final specular term:
    $$ \text{Specular} = \frac{D \cdot G \cdot F}{4 \cdot \text{dotNV} \cdot \text{dotNL} + \epsilon_{final}} $$
    Where $\epsilon_{final}$ is a small epsilon (e.g., $10^{-4}$) [S2, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def ggx_microfacet_brdf(n, v, l, roughness, F0):
    # 1. Half vector
    h = normalize(v + l)
    
    # 2. Alpha
    alpha = roughness * roughness
    
    # 3. Normal Distribution D
    dotNH = max(dot(n, h), 0.0)
    alpha_sqr = alpha * alpha
    denom = dotNH * dotNH * (alpha_sqr - 1.0) + 1.0
    D = alpha_sqr / (PI * denom * denom + 1e-6)
    
    # 4. Geometry G
    k = (roughness + 1.0) * (roughness + 1.0) / 8.0
    dotNV = max(dot(n, v), 0.0)
    dotNL = max(dot(n, l), 0.0)
    
    G1_v = dotNV / max(dotNV * (1.0 - k) + k, 1e-6)
    G1_l = dotNL / max(dotNL * (1.0 - k) + k, 1e-6)
    G = G1_v * G1_l
    
    # 5. Fresnel F
    dotLH = max(dot(l, h), 0.0)
    F = F0 + (1.0 - F0) * pow(1.0 - dotLH, 5.0)
    
    # 6. Combine
    denominator = 4.0 * dotNV * dotNL + 1e-4
    specular = (D * G * F) / denominator
    
    return specular
```

**Parameters:**
*   `roughness`: Typically $0.54$ in specific voxel shader implementations [S1, unofficial]. General range $[0, 1]$.
*   `F0`: Specular reflectance at normal incidence. $0.14$ is used in specific voxel implementations [S1, unofficial]. Standard dielectrics often use $0.04$ [G].

**Edge Cases:**
*   Division by zero is prevented by adding epsilon values ($10^{-6}$ or $10^{-4}$) to denominators in $D$, $G$, and the final combination step [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   If $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{l}$ is near zero (light and view are opposite), $\mathbf{h}$ is undefined. In practice, this corresponds to grazing incidence or back-facing lights, where the BRDF value is negligible or clamped [G].

## Subtopics

### [Microfacet BRDF with Simplified GGX and Schlick Fresnel](10259-microfacet-brdf-with-simplified-ggx-and-schlick-fresnel.md)
This variant defines a microfacet-based BRDF that combines the GGX normal distribution with Schlick-GGX geometry and Fresnel approximations, as implemented in the Filament and Unreal Engine renderers. It is distinguished from the parent [GGX Microfacet BRDF](3715-ggx-microfacet-brdf.md) by its specific algebraic simplifications for the distribution and geometry terms, and by the inclusion of a Lambertian diffuse term multiplied by the cosine of the incident angle within the BRDF definition itself. This formulation is primarily used in neural radiance field variants like NeRV to enable efficient factorization of light transport.

### Multi-Scattering Energy Compensation for Rough Metals
A method to ensure energy conservation for rough metals by compensating for energy lost in single-scattering GGX specular lobes, using a precomputed directional albedo table [S3, unofficial]. It uses a precomputed $32 \times 32$ directional albedo table $E(\mu, \alpha)$, where $\mu$ is cosine and $\alpha$ is roughness [S3, unofficial]. This ensures white rough metal reflects $\sim 100\%$ at every roughness, correcting the energy loss inherent in single-scattering approximations [S3, unofficial].

### Specular Reflection
Models perfectly smooth surfaces where outgoing radiance is not scattered but mirrors the incident trajectory across the surface normal [S4, unofficial]. The BRDF is defined using Dirac delta functions:
$$ f_{r,m} = \frac{\delta(\cos \theta_i - \cos \theta_r)}{\cos \theta_i} \delta(\phi_i - (\phi_r \pm \pi)) $$
where $\delta$ is the Dirac delta function, $\theta_i, \theta_r$ are incident and reflected polar angles, and $\phi_i, \phi_r$ are azimuthal angles [S4, unofficial]. The pitfall is that the Dirac delta function yields zero for any non-zero parameter mismatch, meaning no light is reflected if the geometric reflection conditions are not perfectly satisfied [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per shading point. Requires several trigonometric operations (dot products, normalization, power functions).
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$. No auxiliary buffers required for the base model. Multi-scattering variants require a $32 \times 32$ lookup table [S3, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Highly efficient for real-time rendering. The Schlick approximation for Fresnel and Smith geometry is computationally cheaper than exact integrals [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Physically plausible appearance for rough surfaces; widely supported in rendering engines; handles a wide range of roughness values smoothly [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Single-scattering approximation loses energy for very rough metals (addressed by multi-scattering variants) [S3, unofficial]. Does not account for subsurface scattering or thin-film effects without extensions [S3, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** High-quality specular shading for rough surfaces in real-time or offline rendering.
*   **When not to use:** Perfectly smooth mirrors (use Specular Reflection variant) [S4, unofficial]; surfaces requiring subsurface scattering or complex thin-film interference [S3, unofficial].

## Background
The GGX distribution was introduced by James T. Kajiya and Timothy L. Kay in 1989, but the specific microfacet model using GGX for rough surfaces was popularized by Bruce Walter, Stephen R. Marschner, Hongsong Li, and Kenneth E. Torrance in their 2007 paper "Microfacet Models for Refraction through Rough Surfaces" [S1, unofficial]. It has become the industry standard for specular shading in real-time graphics, adopted by engines like Unreal Engine and Filament [S1, unofficial]. In the context of voxel and ray-marched rendering, it is used to provide high-quality surface detail without explicit geometry [S1, S2, unofficial].
