# Multidirectional Occlusion Shading

Also called: M-DOS, Multidirectional OS, Multidirectional Occlusion Model

## Overview
Multidirectional Occlusion Shading (M-DOS) is a technique for interactive direct volume rendering that simulates directional light scattering to provide realistic soft shadowing and depth cues [S1]. It extends the original Multidirectional Shading (MS-DOS) model by allowing arbitrary light source positioning within the hemisphere defined by the view vector, rather than constraining the light to the viewing direction [S1]. Unlike MS-DOS, which uses symmetric disc-shaped Gaussian kernels, M-DOS employs tilted cone-shaped phase functions that leave elliptic footprints in the opacity buffer, enabling interactive modification of light position with no performance penalty [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Volume data (scalar field), camera parameters (view direction $\mathbf{v}$), light source direction ($\mathbf{l}$), aperture angle $\theta$, and tilt angle $\alpha$.
*   **Output:** Rendered image with directional occlusion shading.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Opacity Buffer:** A 2D buffer aligned with the view plane, storing accumulated opacity values. It is updated incrementally during slice-based rendering.
*   **Slice Plane:** A plane perpendicular to the view direction $\mathbf{v}$ at a specific depth.

**Parameters:**
*   $\theta$ (theta): The aperture angle of the tilted cone-shaped phase function.
*   $\alpha$ (alpha): The tilt angle of the cone, determining the direction of the light source relative to the view. The tilt angle is limited to $[0, \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta)$ to prevent the cone-section from degenerating into hyperbolas or parabolas [S1].

**Method Steps:**
1.  **Light Transport Approximation:** Approximate the in-scattered radiance $L_i$ by replacing the integral over the sphere with a tilted cone-shaped phase function $\Phi_{\theta,\alpha}(\omega, \omega_i)$ [S1]:
    $$ L_i(\mathbf{x}, \omega) \approx \int \Phi_{\theta,\alpha}(\omega, \omega_i) d\omega_i $$
    where $\Phi_{\theta,\alpha}$ is defined by the aperture angle $\theta$ and tilt angle $\alpha$ [S1].

2.  **Elliptical Footprint Calculation:** Determine the elliptical footprint $\epsilon$ left by the light energy on the current slice plane. This footprint is the intersection of the tilted cone (representing the phase function) and the slice plane [S1].

3.  **Incremental Blurring:** Perform an incremental blurring operation on the opacity buffer for each slice in front-to-back order. The blur kernel is an elliptical Gaussian kernel corresponding to the footprint $\epsilon$ [S1].

4.  **Occlusion Determination:** Use the blurred opacity buffer to define the degree of occlusion for the subsequent slice. The accumulated opacity from previous slices modulates the lighting contribution of the current slice [S1].

5.  **Compositing:** Composite the current slice using the modulated lighting, combining base material color with the shading effects [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Tilt Angle Limit:** The tilt angle $\alpha$ must be restricted to $[0, \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta)$ to ensure the footprint remains an ellipse and does not degenerate into a hyperbola or parabola [S1].
*   **Light Direction:** Unlike the original MS-DOS, M-DOS allows arbitrary light directions, but the light must be within the hemisphere defined by the view vector for the model to be valid [S1].
*   **Precision:** Incremental blurring can accumulate numerical errors over many slices. Use appropriate data types for the opacity buffer [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method requires no pre-computation and allows interactive frame rates [S1]. The complexity is dominated by the incremental blurring operation, which is linear with respect to the number of slices and the kernel size [G].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires an additional opacity buffer proportional to the screen resolution [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Generates high-quality soft shadowing effects, allows interactive modification of all illumination and rendering parameters, and provides important shape and depth-perception cues [S1].
*   **Limitations:** The approximation of light transfer delivers slightly different results compared to reference images from a raytracer [S1]. The method is limited to interactive rendering and may not capture complex global illumination effects [S1].
*   **When to use:** Suitable for interactive visualization of volume data where conveying 3D structural arrangements is important [S1].
*   **When not to use:** Not suitable for offline rendering requiring high-fidelity global illumination [S1].

## Background
*   **Origin:** The method was introduced by Veronika Šoltészová, Daniel Patel, Stefan Bruckner, and Ivan Viola in 2010 [S1].
*   **History:** It extends the directional occlusion shading model (MS-DOS) by Mathias Schott et al. (2009) [S1]. The original MS-DOS model constrained the light direction to the viewing direction [S1].
*   **Usage:** Used in direct volume rendering for interactive visualization of scientific data [S1].
