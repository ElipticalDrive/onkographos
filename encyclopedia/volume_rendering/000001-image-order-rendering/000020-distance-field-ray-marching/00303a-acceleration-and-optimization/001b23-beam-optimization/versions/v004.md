# Beam Optimization

Also called: Ray Batching, Ray Grouping Optimization

## Overview
Beam optimization is a technique to improve the performance of primary rays in distance-field ray marching by grouping adjacent rays and marching them together in a single step [S1][S2, unofficial]. It reduces computations for rays far from geometry by treating a block of pixels as a single unit, splitting only when necessary [S2, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Performance Optimizations for SDF Ray Marching](1629-performance-optimizations-for-sdf-ray-marching.md) by focusing on spatial coherence of ray bundles rather than per-ray acceleration structures or early stopping logic.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: A set of primary rays covering a pixel region (typically a beam).
- Output: Hit distances or intersection points for the rays in the beam.

**Data Structures:**
- **Beam Group:** A rectangular block of pixels.
  - Fields: `top_left_pixel`, `width`, `height`, `ray_origin`, `ray_direction`.
  - The group is defined by its center pixel's ray for marching purposes [S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1. **Initialize Group:** Start with a beam of size $W \times H$ pixels (typically $8 \times 8$) [S1]. Calculate the center ray $(o_c, d_c)$ for the group.
2. **March Center Ray:** Perform standard Sphere Tracing (or ray marching) along the center ray $d_c$ from origin $o_c$ [S2, unofficial].
3. **Check Collision Potential:** Let $d_{min}$ be the smallest distance to the scene geometry found during the march step. Let $R_{group}$ be the maximum distance from the center ray to any corner of the beam group at the current step position. If $d_{min} > R_{group}$, the entire group is safe from collision at this step [S2, unofficial].
4. **Split Decision:** If $d_{min} \le R_{group}$, there is a potential collision for at least one ray in the group. Split the group into four sub-groups (quadrants) [S2, unofficial].
5. **Recursive Marching:** Each new sub-group repeats steps 2-4 using its own center ray.
6. **Termination:**
   - If a group steps far enough into the scene such that $d_{min}$ exceeds the remaining budget or the group is discarded entirely, stop processing that group [S2, unofficial].
   - If a group contains only one ray and it has hit an object, record the hit and stop [S2, unofficial].
   - If a group is far from an object, it likely will not split, reducing computations for those pixels [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
- Beam size: $8 \times 8$ pixels is a common setting [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** Comparing $d_{min}$ with $R_{group}$ requires careful handling of floating-point precision, especially when rays are nearly parallel or the group is large [G].
- **Degenerate Input:** If the scene has no geometry, the entire beam may be discarded early, which is efficient. If geometry is dense, the beam may split frequently, approaching per-ray performance [S2, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def beam_optimization(ray_origins, ray_directions, scene, beam_size=8):
    # Initialize beams from ray origins and directions
    beams = create_beams(ray_origins, ray_directions, beam_size)
    results = {}

    while beams:
        current_beam = beams.pop()
        center_ray = current_beam.get_center_ray()
        
        # March center ray
        hit_dist = sphere_trace(center_ray, scene)
        
        # Calculate max distance from center to any ray in beam at hit_dist
        max_offset = current_beam.get_max_offset_at_distance(hit_dist)
        
        if hit_dist > max_offset:
            # No collision possible for any ray in this beam
            for ray_id in current_beam.get_ray_ids():
                results[ray_id] = hit_dist # Or infinity if no hit
        else:
            # Potential collision, split beam
            sub_beams = current_beam.split_into_quadrants()
            beams.extend(sub_beams)
            
            # If beam is size 1x1 and hit, record hit
            if current_beam.is_single_ray() and hit_dist < INFINITY:
                results[current_beam.get_ray_id()] = hit_dist

    return results
```

## Subtopics

### Beam Optimization for Soft Shadows
An extension of beam optimization to soft shadows by shooting a beam of rays from the view-sample position to the area light to find conservative furthest distances [S1].
- **Definition:** Uses a beam of four rays per pixel from the view-sample position to the area light [S1].
- **Steps:**
  1. Shoot a beam of four rays per pixel from the view-sample position to the area light [S1].
  2. Find the conservative furthest distance along these rays where no geometry is intersected [S1].
  3. Shorten all shadow rays accordingly based on the conservative furthest distance [S1].
  4. Trace the same beam rays in the reverse direction (from the light source) to find a conservative interval where geometry may be intersected [S1].
- **Pitfall:** Pixels whose entire beams have no intersection with geometry require no additional shadow rays, which is an optimization opportunity [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Depends on the scene density. In empty space, beams are discarded early, leading to near-constant time per beam. In dense scenes, beams split frequently, approaching $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of pixels.
- **Memory Complexity:** Low, as it processes beams iteratively and does not require large auxiliary structures beyond the beam stack [S1].
- **Measured Performance:** Improves performance of primary rays by reducing redundant intersection tests for rays far from geometry [S1][S2, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Reduces computation for rays in empty space; simple to implement with existing sphere tracing logic [S2, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** May not provide significant benefits in highly complex scenes where beams split often; requires careful calculation of beam offsets [S2, unofficial].
- **When to Use:** When primary rays cover large areas of empty space; when hardware supports efficient parallel processing of ray bundles.
- **When Not to Use:** When the scene is extremely dense and beams rarely stay intact; when per-ray precision is critical and beam approximation introduces errors.

## Background
Beam optimization was described by Laine and Karras [S1]. It is used in high-resolution sparse voxel DAG rendering to improve performance [S1]. The technique leverages the spatial coherence of primary rays to reduce the number of intersection tests [S1][S2, unofficial].
