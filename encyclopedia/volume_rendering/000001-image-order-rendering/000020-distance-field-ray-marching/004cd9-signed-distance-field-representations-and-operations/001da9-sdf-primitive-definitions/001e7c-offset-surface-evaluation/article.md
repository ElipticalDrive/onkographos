# Offset Surface Evaluation

Also called: Distance to Offset Surfaces, Global offset, Local offset, Point-to-set distance

## Overview
Offset Surface Evaluation computes the shortest Euclidean distance from a query point $\mathbf{p}$ to a surface generated by offsetting a base "skeleton" geometry $S$ by a fixed distance $c$ [S1]. It solves the problem of defining implicit surfaces for complex shapes (such as generalized cylinders or space curves) that are difficult to represent with standard primitives [S1]. It is distinguished from its siblings by its reliance on a global point-to-set distance search rather than local parametric expansions, which avoids the interior surface artifacts common in local offset methods [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Query point $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
- Skeleton geometry $S$ (e.g., a space curve, surface, or mesh).
- Constant offset distance $c \ge 0$ [S1].

**Outputs:**
- Signed distance $d(\mathbf{p})$ to the offset surface. By definition, the offset surface is the locus of points where $d(\mathbf{p}) = 0$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
- The skeleton $S$ must support a point-to-set distance query. For parametric surfaces or curves, this involves storing control points or coefficients.
- For mesh-based offsets, the structure includes vertices $\mathbf{v}_i$ and normals $\mathbf{n}_i$ [S2, unofficial].

**Method:**
The evaluation depends on whether the offset is defined globally (implicit) or locally (parametric) [S1].

### Global Offset (Implicit)
The global offset surface is defined by the implicit equation $d(\mathbf{p}) = 0$, where $d(\mathbf{p})$ is the point-to-set distance to the skeleton $S$ [S1]. When $c$ is constant, the surface "fleshes out" the skeleton [S1].

1.  **Search for Closest Point:** Find the point $\mathbf{s} \in S$ that minimizes the Euclidean distance $\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}\|$ [S1].
2.  **Compute Distance:** The distance to the offset surface is the distance to the skeleton minus the offset constant:
    $$ d(\mathbf{p}) = \|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}\| - c $$
    If $S$ has a well-defined surface normal everywhere, the closest point $\mathbf{s}$ lies on the surface such that the vector $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{s}$ is parallel to the normal at $\mathbf{s}$ [S1].

**Example: Space Curve**
For a space curve $\mathbf{p}(t)$, the closest point satisfies the condition that the tangent is perpendicular to the vector pointing to the query point (assuming the query point is relative to the curve's local origin or the vector is $\mathbf{p}(t) - \mathbf{p}_{query}$) [S1]. Specifically, if finding the closest point on the curve to the origin (or relative vector), the condition is:
$$ (\mathbf{p}(t) - \mathbf{0}) \cdot \mathbf{p}'(t) = 0 \quad \text{(Eq. 35)} $$
For a cubic Bezier curve, this equation converts into a degree-5 Bernstein polynomial (1-D Bezier curve) [S1]. The roots are found using efficient root-finding techniques for Bernstein polynomials [S1].

### Local Offset (Parametric)
The local offset surface is defined parametrically. If the skeleton is a generator surface $\mathbf{g}(u, v)$, the local offset surface $\mathbf{o}(u, v)$ is:
$$ \mathbf{o}(u, v) = \mathbf{g}(u, v) + c \cdot \mathbf{n}(u, v) \quad \text{(Eq. 33)} $$
where $\mathbf{n}(u, v)$ is the unit-length surface normal:
$$ \mathbf{n}(u, v) = \frac{\mathbf{g}_u(u, v) \times \mathbf{g}_v(u, v)}{\|\mathbf{g}_u(u, v) \times \mathbf{g}_v(u, v)\|} \quad \text{(Eq. 34)} $$
and $\mathbf{g}_u, \mathbf{g}_v$ are partial derivatives [S1].

**Pitfalls:**
- **Interior Surfaces:** Global offsets can create interior surfaces that cause problems in ray-tracing and CSG operations [S1].
- **Self-Intersections:** Local offsets do not handle self-intersections; vertices are moved independently along their original normals [S2, unofficial].
- **Degree Increase:** Parametric offsets typically result in higher-degree algebraic representations due to the cross product and square root in the normal calculation [S1].

## Subtopics

### Offset Surface Creation
Creates an offset surface from a mesh by moving vertices along their normals by a given distance [S2, unofficial].
- **Definition:** A geometric modification of a mesh where each vertex is displaced by `amount` along its normal vector [S2, unofficial].
- **Steps:**
    1.  Ensure normals are computed: `mesh->need_normals()` [S2, unofficial].
    2.  Iterate over all vertices $i$ from $0$ to $nv-1$ [S2, unofficial].
    3.  Update vertex position: `mesh->vertices[i] += amount * mesh->normals[i]` [S2, unofficial].
    4.  Invalidate bounding box and bounding sphere flags [S2, unofficial].
- **Parameters:** `amount`: distance to offset along normals [S2, unofficial].
- **Pitfalls:** Does not handle self-intersections; vertices are moved independently along their original normals [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Complexity:** Global offset evaluation requires a search through the entire skeleton set $S$ for the closest point, which can be computationally expensive for complex skeletons [S1]. Local offset evaluation is $O(1)$ per point if the parametric form is known, but the resulting algebraic degree increases [S1].
- **Strengths:** Global offsets are implicit and avoid the high-degree algebraic representations and gradient discontinuities associated with local parametric offsets [S1].
- **Limitations:** Global offsets may produce interior surfaces that complicate ray-tracing and CSG operations [S1]. Local offsets suffer from self-intersections and loss of precision due to high-degree representations [S1].
- **When to use:** Use global offsets for "fleshing out" skeletons where implicit representation is preferred [S1]. Use local offsets for specific parametric surfaces where the degree increase is manageable or approximated [S1].

## Background
The concept of offset surfaces is rooted in computer-aided geometric design (CAGD) and implicit surface rendering. Hoffman (1989) and van Wijk (1984) are cited as foundational works discussing the desirability of global offsets to avoid interior surface problems in ray-tracing and CSG [S1]. The method is closely related to sphere tracing, where the distance to the offset surface serves as the step size for ray marching [S1].
