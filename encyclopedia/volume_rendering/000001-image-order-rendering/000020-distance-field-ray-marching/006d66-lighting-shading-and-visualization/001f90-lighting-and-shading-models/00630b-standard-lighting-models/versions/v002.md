# Standard Lighting Models
## Overview
Standard Lighting Models approximate surface illumination by combining local lighting components (ambient, diffuse, specular) without simulating global light transport effects like inter-reflections or caustics [G]. They differ from [Distance Color Blending](791-distance-color-blending.md) by relying on geometric normals and reflectance functions rather than volumetric color accumulation, and from [Ray Marching Dither](3269-ray-marching-dither.md) by producing deterministic, non-stochastic results [G]. They serve as the foundational shading layer for [Diffuse Lighting Models](5343-diffuse-lighting-models.md) and [Specular and Reflective Shading](14299-specular-and-reflective-shading.md) [G].
## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on a surface point $\mathbf{p}$ and its associated geometric data, primarily the surface normal $\mathbf{n}$ [G]. The normal is typically derived from the Signed Distance Function (SDF) $f(\mathbf{x})$ using the gradient:
$$ \mathbf{n} = \frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{p})}{\|\nabla f(\mathbf{p})\|} $$
where $\nabla f(\mathbf{p})$ is estimated via finite differences, such as solving a linear system based on values at vertices of a tetrahedron, or via back-propagation [G].

The core shading computation generally follows this structure:
1. **Geometric Setup:** Determine the view direction $\mathbf{v}$ (from $\mathbf{p}$ to camera) and light direction $\mathbf{l}$ (from $\mathbf{p}$ to light source) [G].
2. **Occlusion/Ambient Occlusion:** Calculate shadow factors or ambient occlusion values to determine how much light reaches $\mathbf{p}$ from the environment or specific lights [G].
3. **Reflectance Model:** Compute the diffuse and specular components based on the angle between $\mathbf{n}$, $\mathbf{l}$, and the reflection vector $\mathbf{r}$ [G].
4. **Color Accumulation:** Combine the base material color with the lighting contributions, often modulated by depth cueing or atmospheric effects [G].
## Subtopics

### [Diffuse Lighting Models](5343-diffuse-lighting-models.md)
Calculates surface illumination by simulating light scattering, providing the primary visual cue for surface orientation [G]. It excludes specular effects, serving as the base layer for complex shading [G]. Choose this method when simulating matte surfaces or when a computationally cheap base illumination layer is required [G].

### [Ray Marching with Blinn-Phong Lighting](6898-ray-marching-with-blinn-phong-lighting.md)
Renders implicit surfaces using the Blinn-Phong model [G]. It differs from analytical ray tracing by using iterative distance estimation and from pure SDF rendering by explicitly applying local lighting (diffuse, specular, ambient) rather than just color blending [G]. Choose this method when a balance of performance and view-dependent highlights is needed for implicit geometry [G].

### [Specular and Reflective Shading](14299-specular-and-reflective-shading.md)
Handles view-dependent surface reflectance, including specular highlights and mirror-like reflections [G]. It distinguishes itself from [Diffuse Lighting Models](5343-diffuse-lighting-models.md) by explicitly modeling view-dependent reflectance and from [Distance Color Blending](791-distance-color-blending.md) by handling geometric specular bounces [G]. Choose this method when simulating glossy or metallic materials where reflection paths are critical [G].

## Background
Standard lighting models originated in the 1970s and 1980s with the development of rasterization techniques, notably the Phong reflection model (1975) and the Blinn-Phong model (1977) [G]. They were designed to approximate the appearance of surfaces under local illumination with minimal computational cost, making them suitable for real-time rendering [G]. In the context of distance-field ray marching, they are adapted to work with implicit geometry where normals are derived from the SDF gradient rather than mesh vertices [G].
