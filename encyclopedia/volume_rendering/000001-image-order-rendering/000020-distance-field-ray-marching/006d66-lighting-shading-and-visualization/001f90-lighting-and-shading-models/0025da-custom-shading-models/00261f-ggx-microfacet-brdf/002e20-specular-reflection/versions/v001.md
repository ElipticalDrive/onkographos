# Specular Reflection

Also called: Perfect mirror reflection

## Overview
Specular reflection models perfectly smooth surfaces where outgoing radiance is not scattered but mirrors the incident trajectory across the surface normal [S1, unofficial]. It serves as the zero-roughness limit of microfacet models, producing perfect mirror reflections [S2]. Unlike GGX or Ward models which distribute energy over a lobe, this method concentrates all reflected energy into a single geometric direction, requiring exact alignment between the incident light and the view vector relative to the surface normal.

## How it works
**Inputs:** Incident radiance $L_i$, incident direction $\vec{\omega}_i$, reflected direction $\vec{\omega}_r$, surface normal $\vec{n}$.
**Outputs:** Reflected radiance $L_r$.

**Data Structures:**
No complex data structures are required. The method relies on vector arithmetic and angular comparison.

**Steps:**
1.  Determine the geometric reflection direction $\vec{\omega}_{refl}$ for the view direction $\vec{\omega}_r$ (or light direction $\vec{\omega}_i$). The reflection vector is calculated as:
    $$ \vec{\omega}_{refl} = 2(\vec{\omega}_i \cdot \vec{n})\vec{n} - \vec{\omega}_i $$
2.  Check if the incident direction $\vec{\omega}_i$ matches the reflection of the outgoing direction $\vec{\omega}_r$ (or vice versa). In spherical coordinates, this requires:
    $$ \theta_r = \theta_i $$
    $$ \phi_r = \phi_i \pm \pi $$
    where $\theta$ is the polar angle from the normal and $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle [S1, unofficial].
3.  If the conditions are met, the reflected radiance equals the incident radiance from the corresponding direction:
    $$ L_r(\theta_r, \phi_r) = L_i(\theta_r, \phi_i \pm \pi) $$
    If the conditions are not met exactly, the reflected radiance is zero [S1, unofficial].

**Formula:**
The Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF) is defined using the Dirac delta function $\delta$:
$$ f_{r,m} = \frac{\delta(\cos \theta_i - \cos \theta_r)}{\cos \theta_i} \delta(\phi_i - (\phi_r \pm \pi)) $$
where:
*   $\delta$ is the Dirac delta function [S1, unofficial].
*   $\theta_i, \theta_r$ are the incident and reflected polar angles [S1, unofficial].
*   $\phi_i, \phi_r$ are the incident and reflected azimuthal angles [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   Roughness: 0 [S2].
*   Light Bounces: Typically requires support for multiple bounces (e.g., over 8) to capture indirect specular reflections [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Delta Function Zeroes:** The Dirac delta function yields zero for any non-zero parameter mismatch. If the geometric reflection conditions are not perfectly satisfied, no light is reflected [S1, unofficial].
*   **Numerical Precision:** Floating-point comparisons for angle equality ($\theta_r = \theta_i$) are prone to error. Implementers must use a small epsilon tolerance or vector dot-product checks instead of direct angle equality [G].
*   **Discrete Sampling:** In rasterization or discrete ray tracing, finding the exact incident direction is difficult. This model is best suited for analytical ray tracing or environment map lookups rather than light sampling in path tracers without importance sampling techniques [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per pixel if the incident radiance is available via direct ray intersection or environment map lookup.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ additional memory.
*   **Strengths:** Physically accurate for polished metals and mirrors; computationally cheap per sample.
*   **Limitations:** Cannot model rough surfaces; requires exact geometric alignment; difficult to sample stochastically without specialized techniques.
*   **When to use:** Rendering perfect mirrors, polished chrome, or the zero-roughness limit of microfacet models.
*   **When not to use:** For rough surfaces, diffuse materials, or when approximate lighting is acceptable.

## Background
The mathematical formulation using Dirac delta functions for specular reflection originates from radiometry and computer graphics theory, specifically detailed in works on real-time global illumination [S1, unofficial]. The concept of "perfect mirror reflection" with zero roughness is also implemented in voxel-based rendering tools like MagicaVoxel, where it supports transparent voxels and multiple light bounces [S2].
