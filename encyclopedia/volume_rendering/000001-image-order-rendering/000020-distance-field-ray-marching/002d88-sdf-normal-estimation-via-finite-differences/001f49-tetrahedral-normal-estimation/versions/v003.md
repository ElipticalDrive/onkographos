# Tetrahedral Normal Estimation

Also called: Tetrahedral Gradient Estimation, Tetrahedral SDF Normal Approximation, 4-point normal, Quilez Normal Estimation

## Overview
Tetrahedral Normal Estimation approximates the surface normal of a Signed Distance Field (SDF) at a query point $p$ by evaluating the distance function at four vertices of a tetrahedron inscribed in the voxel cube surrounding $p$ [S3, unofficial]. It solves the problem of normal computation in ray marching by reducing the number of SDF queries from six (standard central difference) to four, thereby lowering computational cost by approximately 33% while maintaining accuracy comparable to central differences [S2, unofficial][S4]. It is distinguished from its siblings by its reliance on the geometric symmetry of a regular tetrahedron rather than axis-aligned offsets [S3, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Query point $p$ (vec3), SDF evaluation function $map(p)$ returning a scalar distance, step size $h$ (scalar).
- Output: Estimated surface normal $n$ (vec3).

**Data Structures:**
- No persistent data structures. The method is stateless and computes the normal on-the-fly during ray marching.

**Steps:**
1. Define the four directional vectors $k_i$ corresponding to the vertices of a regular tetrahedron centered at the origin. A common symmetric configuration uses the vectors:
   $$ k_0 = (1, 1, 1) $$
   $$ k_1 = (1, -1, -1) $$
   $$ k_2 = (-1, 1, -1) $$
   $$ k_3 = (-1, -1, 1) $$
   These vectors are equidistant from the origin and from each other [S3, unofficial][S4].
2. Evaluate the SDF at the four offset points $p + h k_i$ for $i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$.
3. Compute the weighted sum of the directional derivatives. Since the tetrahedron vertices are symmetric, the gradient approximation is the sum of the distance values scaled by their respective direction vectors [S4]:
   $$ g = \sum_{i=0}^{3} k_i \cdot map(p + h k_i) $$
   Note: Some implementations scale the direction vectors by the step size $h$ in the denominator, but since the final result is normalized, the scalar factor $1/h$ cancels out [S2, unofficial][S4].
4. Normalize the resulting vector $g$ to obtain the normal:
   $$ n = normalize(g) $$

**Formula:**
$$ n = normalize\left( \sum_{i=0}^{3} k_i \cdot map(p + h k_i) \right) $$
where $k_i$ are the tetrahedral direction vectors defined above [S4].

**Parameters:**
- $h$ (epsilon): The step size for the finite difference. Empirical studies suggest setting $h$ to 20% of the voxel size yields the best visual appearance [S1, unofficial]. In specific implementations, a fixed small value like $0.0006$ has been used [S2, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def tetrahedral_normal_estimation(p, map_func, h):
    """
    Estimate the surface normal at point p using tetrahedral finite differences.
    
    Args:
        p: vec3, query point.
        map_func: function taking vec3 and returning float (SDF value).
        h: float, step size (epsilon).
        
    Returns:
        vec3, estimated normal.
    """
    # Define the 4 symmetric tetrahedral directions
    # These correspond to vertices of a regular tetrahedron inscribed in a cube
    k0 = vec3(1.0, 1.0, 1.0)
    k1 = vec3(1.0, -1.0, -1.0)
    k2 = vec3(-1.0, 1.0, -1.0)
    k3 = vec3(-1.0, -1.0, 1.0)
    
    # Evaluate SDF at the 4 offset points
    d0 = map_func(p + h * k0)
    d1 = map_func(p + h * k1)
    d2 = map_func(p + h * k2)
    d3 = map_func(p + h * k3)
    
    # Sum the weighted distances
    # The gradient approximation is the sum of (direction * distance)
    # Note: Mathematically, the gradient is approximated by summing k_i * d_i
    # because the tetrahedron is symmetric around the origin.
    grad = k0 * d0 + k1 * d1 + k2 * d2 + k3 * d3
    
    # Normalize to get the normal
    normal = normalize(grad)
    
    return normal
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Sharp Intersections:** The normal estimate is inaccurate at sharp intersections of multiple surfaces because the tetrahedron samples may straddle different material boundaries [S4]. This can be mitigated by masking the SDF evaluation to only include the closest object's distance [S4].
- **Voxel Boundaries:** When using traversal methods other than Grid Sampling Traversal (GST), neighboring voxels may not exist. The "baseline" version of this method extends the current voxel's surface outside its box to allow sampling, which breaks C0 continuity across voxel edges [S1, unofficial]. To achieve C0 continuity, one must sample neighboring voxels (Falcao normals), but this increases cost [S1, unofficial].
- **Precision:** The method is less numerically accurate than central differences if the step size $h$ is not chosen carefully relative to the voxel size [S4].

## Subtopics

### [Local Least Squares Gradient Estimation](9937-local-least-squares-gradient-estimation.md)
Local Least Squares Gradient Estimation computes the gradient of an SDF by fitting a local linear plane to the SDF values at the vertices of a surrounding tetrahedron. It improves upon standard finite differences by utilizing all four vertices simultaneously to minimize the residual error, providing a more robust and accurate normal estimate for ray marching. It is chosen when higher accuracy is required at the cost of additional computation (solving a least squares problem or eigenvalue decomposition) compared to the simple sum method.

#### Tetrahedral Normal Estimation via Covariance Matrix
This variant derives the normal by minimizing the average distance of the tetrahedron vertices to a plane defined by the normal [S5].
- **Step:** Derive the loss with respect to the normal $\mathbf{n}_j$ and set to 0 to obtain the eigenvalue equation $\mathbf{C}_j \mathbf{n}_j = \lambda \mathbf{n}_j$ [S5]. Identify $\mathbf{n}_j$ as the eigenvector of $\mathbf{C}_j$ associated with the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda$ [S5].
- **Formula:** The loss function is $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{n}_j, \lambda) = \mathbf{n}_j^\top \mathbf{C}_j \mathbf{n}_j - \lambda (\mathbf{n}_j^\top \mathbf{n}_j - 1)$, where $\mathbf{C}_j = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}_j} \Delta \mathbf{s}'_i \Delta \mathbf{s}'_i^\top$ is the covariance matrix of centered surface deltas $\Delta \mathbf{s}'_i$ for tetrahedron $\mathcal{T}_j$ [S5]. The average distance to the plane is $\lambda$ [S5].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per query, requiring exactly 4 SDF evaluations. This is 33% faster than the standard 6-evaluation central difference method [S2, unofficial].
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$, no additional memory required.
- **Measured Performance:** In comparisons, the baseline tetrahedral method (without neighbor sampling) performs similarly to analytic normals and slightly better than Falcao normals in terms of frame time reduction, as Falcao normals require sampling additional neighboring voxels [S1, unofficial]. Table 3 in [S1, unofficial] shows that Falcao normals perform consistently slightly worse than the baseline due to the cost of sampling additional distance values.
- **Strengths:** Low computational cost, good accuracy for smooth surfaces, simple implementation.
- **Limitations:** Inaccurate at sharp intersections; not C0 continuous across voxel edges unless extended to sample neighbors (which increases cost) [S1, unofficial][S4].
- **When to use:** Real-time ray marching where performance is critical and surfaces are relatively smooth.
- **When not to use:** Scenes with sharp creases or intersections where normal accuracy is paramount, or when C0 continuity across voxel boundaries is required without the overhead of neighbor sampling.

## Background
The method was popularized by Inigo Quilez in the context of ray marching SDFs [S3, unofficial]. It is also referred to as "Falcao normals" in some literature, referencing the work by Falcao et al. [S1, unofficial]. The technique leverages the symmetry of a regular tetrahedron to approximate the gradient with fewer samples than axis-aligned finite differences [S4]. It is widely used in real-time graphics and ray marching renderers due to its efficiency [S2, unofficial][S4].
