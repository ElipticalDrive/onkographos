# Adaptive Ray Marching for GPIS Intersection
Also called: Adaptive Ray-GPIS Intersection

## Overview
Adaptive Ray Marching for GPIS Intersection is a geometric tracing algorithm that marches along a ray with adaptive step sizes to locate the first zero crossing of a Gaussian Process Implicit Surface (GPIS) [S1]. It solves the problem of efficiently finding isosurface intersections in probabilistic implicit fields by drastically reducing the number of expensive GP samples compared to fixed discretization methods [S1]. Unlike fixed-step approaches, it dynamically adjusts the step size based on statistical bounds of the GP mean and covariance to ensure the probability of missing a root remains below a user-specified tolerance [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Ray origin $\mathbf{x}_0$ and direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ (normalized).
- GPIS model defined by mean function $\mu(\mathbf{x})$, covariance function $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$, and their derivatives.
- Tolerance parameter $\eta$ (default $0.001$) [S1].
- Lipschitz constant of the mean $\Lambda_\mu$ [S1].
- Uniform bound on covariance $\sigma$ (where $\sigma^2 = \sup_{\mathbf{x}} |k(\mathbf{x})|$) [S1].
- Uniform bound on the second derivative of the covariance $M$ (where $M = \sup_{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}} \left| \frac{\partial^2 k}{\partial \mathbf{x} \partial \mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \right|$) [S1].
- Constants $C_\eta$ and $Q_\eta$ derived from $\eta$ and the ratio $\sqrt{M}/\Lambda_\mu$ [S1].
- Minimum step size $\Delta_{\min}$ [S1].

**Outputs:**
- Intersection distance $\tau$ along the ray.
- Intersection point $\mathbf{x}_\tau = \mathbf{x}_0 + \tau\boldsymbol{\omega}$.
- Surface normal $\mathbf{n}_s = \frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_\tau)}{\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_\tau)\|}$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
- A history of sampled points along the ray to condition the Online Gaussian Process Sampler. Each entry stores the parameter $t$ and the sampled value $v = f(\mathbf{x}_0 + t\boldsymbol{\omega})$ [S1].

**Assumptions:**
- The mean $\mu$ and covariance $k$ are continuously differentiable [S1].
- $\mu$ is Lipschitz-continuous with constant $\Lambda_\mu$ [S1].
- $k$ and its second derivative are uniformly bounded by $\sigma^2$ and $M$ respectively [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1. Initialize the ray parameter $t_i = 0$ and sample the initial value $v_i = f(\mathbf{x}_0 + t_i\boldsymbol{\omega})$ using the Online Gaussian Process Sampler. Store $(t_i, v_i)$ in the history [S1].
2. Determine the adaptive step size $\Delta_i$ using the bounds derived from the GP statistics (see Formulas below) [S1].
3. Advance the ray parameter: $t_{i+1} = t_i + \Delta_i$ [S1].
4. Sample the function value at the new position: $v_{i+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_0 + t_{i+1}\boldsymbol{\omega})$ using the Online Gaussian Process Sampler, conditioning on all previous samples including $(t_i, v_i)$ [S1]. Store $(t_{i+1}, v_{i+1})$ in the history [S1].
5. Check for root crossing:
   - If $v_i \cdot v_{i+1} > 0$, the interval $[t_i, t_{i+1}]$ is considered to contain no root. Set $t_i = t_{i+1}$, $v_i = v_{i+1}$, and repeat from Step 2 [S1].
   - If $v_i \cdot v_{i+1} \leq 0$, the interval is considered to contain exactly one root. Proceed to Step 6 [S1].
6. Locate the root $\tau$ within $[t_i, t_{i+1}]$ using a bracketed root solver (e.g., bisection or Dekker-Brent) [S1]. During the root solving process, evaluate $f$ at query points using the Online Gaussian Process Sampler, appending each new evaluation to the history to condition future evaluations [S1].
7. Return $\tau$ as the intersection distance [S1].
8. Compute the surface normal $\mathbf{n}_s$ at $\mathbf{x}_\tau$ using the gradient $\nabla f$ sampled via the GP model [S1].

**Formulas:**
The step size $\Delta_i$ is chosen such that the probability of missing a root in $[t_i, t_i + \Delta_i]$ is no more than $\eta$ [S1]. Assuming $f(t_i) > 0$ (if $f(t_i) < 0$, the signs are inverted in the logic), the lower bound of $f$ on the interval is given by [S1]:

$$
\inf_{[t_i, t_{i+1}]} f \geq \mu(t_i) - \Lambda_\mu \Delta_i - Q_\eta \sigma
$$

$$
\inf_{[t_i, t_{i+1}]} f \geq f(t_i) - (\Lambda_\mu + C_\eta \sqrt{M}) \Delta_i
$$

with probability no less than $1 - \eta$ [S1].

The step size $\Delta_i$ is calculated by ensuring the lower bound remains positive (assuming $f(t_i) > 0$) or by solving for the maximum $\Delta_i$ that satisfies the tolerance. The source provides the clamping logic:

$$
\Delta_i = \max \left( \Delta_{\min}, \dots \right)
$$

The exact numerator for the first bound is $|\mu(t_i)| - Q_\eta \sigma$. If this value is negative or zero, the step size is limited by the second bound or $\Delta_{\min}$ [S1]. The source text cuts off the full explicit formula for $\Delta_i$, but implies it is derived from setting the lower bound inequalities to zero or a safe margin. Specifically, from the first inequality:
$$
\Delta_i \leq \frac{\mu(t_i) - Q_\eta \sigma}{\Lambda_\mu}
$$
From the second inequality:
$$
\Delta_i \leq \frac{f(t_i)}{\Lambda_\mu + C_\eta \sqrt{M}}
$$
The algorithm takes the minimum of these valid upper bounds, clamped by $\Delta_{\min}$ [S1, G].

**Parameters:**
- $\eta$: User-specified tolerance for the probability of missing a root. Typical value: $0.001$ [S1].
- $C_\eta$: Constant controlling tail probability $\eta$. For $\eta = 0.001$, $C_\eta \approx 4.5$ [S1].
- $Q_\eta$: Constant controlling tail probability $\eta$, depending on $\sqrt{M}/\Lambda_\mu$. For $\eta = 0.001$, $Q_\eta \approx 4.1$ when $\sqrt{M}/\Lambda_\mu = 1$ and $Q_\eta \approx 5.4$ when $\sqrt{M}/\Lambda_\mu = 1000$ [S1].
- $\Lambda_\mu$: Lipschitz constant of the mean function $\mu$, defined as $\sup_{\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{y}} |\mu(\mathbf{x}) - \mu(\mathbf{y})| / \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|$ [S1].
- $\sigma$: Uniform bound on the covariance function $k$, defined as $\sup_{\mathbf{x}} |k(\mathbf{x})|$ [S1].
- $M$: Uniform bound on the second derivative of the covariance function, defined as $\sup_{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}} \left| \frac{\partial^2 k}{\partial \mathbf{x} \partial \mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \right|$ [S1].

**Pitfalls:**
- If the interval $[t_i, t_{i+1}]$ is chosen too large, multiple zero crossings may exist within it (cases c and d in Figure 3 of the source), causing errors in root finding [S1]. The adaptive step size limits this risk [S1].
- The algorithm assumes the mean $\mu$ and covariance $k$ are continuously differentiable, $\mu$ is Lipschitz-continuous, and $k$ and its second derivative are uniformly bounded [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def adaptive_ray_marching_gpis(x0, omega, gp_model, eta, Lambda_mu, sigma, M, Delta_min):
    """
    Finds the first zero crossing of a GPIS along a ray.
    
    Args:
        x0: Ray origin (3D vector).
        omega: Ray direction (normalized 3D vector).
        gp_model: Object providing mean, covariance, gradient, and online sampling.
        eta: Tolerance for missing root (default 0.001).
        Lambda_mu: Lipschitz constant of mean.
        sigma: Uniform bound on covariance.
        M: Uniform bound on second derivative of covariance.
        Delta_min: Minimum step size.
        
    Returns:
        tau: Intersection distance.
        n_s: Surface normal.
    """
    # Precompute constants based on eta and ratio sqrt(M)/Lambda_mu
    # Note: Exact mapping for Q_eta and C_eta depends on supplemental document.
    # For eta=0.001: C_eta approx 4.5. Q_eta approx 4.1 (if ratio=1) to 5.4 (if ratio=1000).
    C_eta = 4.5 
    ratio = sqrt(M) / Lambda_mu
    if ratio <= 1:
        Q_eta = 4.1
    elif ratio >= 1000:
        Q_eta = 5.4
    else:
        # Interpolate or use exact formula from supplemental doc [G]
        Q_eta = 4.1 
        
    # Initialize
    t_i = 0.0
    # Sample initial point
    v_i = gp_model.sample_online(x0 + t_i * omega)
    history = [(t_i, v_i)]
    
    while True:
        # Determine step size Delta_i
        # Calculate upper bounds for Delta_i to ensure probability of missing root < eta
        # Assuming f(t_i) > 0 logic applies; if f(t_i) < 0, use absolute values or invert logic
        
        val_mu = gp_model.mean(x0 + t_i * omega)
        val_f = v_i # f(t_i)
        
        # Bound 1: based on mean
        # inf f >= mu(t_i) - Lambda_mu * Delta - Q_eta * sigma
        # To ensure inf f > 0 (assuming mu > 0), Delta <= (mu - Q_eta * sigma) / Lambda_mu
        bound1_num = abs(val_mu) - Q_eta * sigma
        if bound1_num > 0:
            delta1 = bound1_num / Lambda_mu
        else:
            delta1 = float('inf') # This bound doesn't restrict if mean is already small/negative
            
        # Bound 2: based on sampled value
        # inf f >= f(t_i) - (Lambda_mu + C_eta * sqrt(M)) * Delta
        # To ensure inf f > 0 (assuming f > 0), Delta <= f(t_i) / (Lambda_mu + C_eta * sqrt(M))
        denom2 = Lambda_mu + C_eta * sqrt(M)
        if denom2 > 0:
            delta2 = abs(val_f) / denom2
        else:
            delta2 = float('inf')
            
        # Choose step size
        Delta_i = min(delta1, delta2)
        Delta_i = max(Delta_i, Delta_min)
        
        # Advance
        t_next = t_i + Delta_i
        v_next = gp_model.sample_online(x0 + t_next * omega, history)
        history.append((t_next, v_next))
        
        # Check for root crossing
        if v_i * v_next > 0:
            # No root in interval
            t_i = t_next
            v_i = v_next
        else:
            # Root bracketed [t_i, t_next]
            # Use bracketed root solver (e.g., Brent's method)
            tau = solve_root_brent(lambda t: gp_model.sample_online(x0 + t * omega, history), t_i, t_next)
            
            # Compute normal at intersection
            grad_f = gp_model.gradient(x0 + tau * omega)
            n_s = grad_f / norm(grad_f)
            
            return tau, n_s
```

## Subtopics

### [Coarse-to-Fine Volume Maximum Search](4657-coarse-to-fine-volume-maximum-search.md)
A sibling method that uses a hierarchical grid to find volume maxima. It is chosen when rendering volumetric data rather than implicit surfaces, and does not rely on GP statistical bounds for step sizing.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The method reduces the number of GP samples by a cubic factor compared to fixed discretization [S1]. The number of steps depends on the smoothness of the GP; smoother surfaces allow larger steps.
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the history of samples for the Online Gaussian Process Sampler. The size of the history grows with the number of steps, but is generally much smaller than the full GP kernel matrix required for batch sampling [S1].
- **Strengths:** Drastically reduces computation by taking large, conservative steps [S1]. Provides probabilistic guarantees on not missing roots [S1].
- **Limitations:** Requires precomputed or analytically computable bounds ($\Lambda_\mu, \sigma, M$) [S1]. Relies on the Online GP Sampler, which has its own computational cost per sample [S1].
- **When to use:** Rendering GPIS where the GP model is available and bounds are known.
- **When not to use:** When the GP bounds are unknown or difficult to compute, or when the GP is not continuously differentiable.

## Background
The method was introduced in "Adaptive Ray Marching for Rendering Gaussian Process Implicit Surfaces" [S1]. It addresses the high computational cost of rendering GPIS by replacing fixed-step ray marching with an adaptive approach that leverages the statistical properties of the Gaussian Process to determine safe step sizes. The approach builds on the Online Gaussian Process Sampler to allow efficient incremental sampling along the ray [S1].
