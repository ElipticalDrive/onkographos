# Coarse-to-Fine NeRF

Also called: Two-Stage NeRF, Coarse-Fine NeRF

## Overview
Coarse-to-Fine NeRF is a hierarchical sampling strategy that improves the learning of high-frequency details in neural radiance fields by decoupling the search for opaque regions from the final rendering resolution [S1, S3, unofficial]. It addresses the inefficiency of uniform sampling by first using a coarse network to identify where geometry exists, then clustering a denser set of samples around those opaque locations for a fine network [S1, S3, unofficial]. Unlike standard uniform sampling which distributes points evenly across the entire ray segment, this method concentrates computational budget on the visible surface, allowing the model to learn fine details without a proportional increase in coarse sampling cost [S1, S3, unofficial].

## How it works
The method employs two separate Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), a coarse network and a fine network, which share the same architecture but maintain distinct parameters [S2, unofficial]. Both networks are trained jointly to predict volume density and view-dependent color [S2, unofficial].

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Ray origins $\mathbf{o} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and directions $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ for a batch of $N$ rays.
*   Near and far clipping plane distances, $t_{near}$ and $t_{far}$.
*   Hyperparameters: $N_{coarse}$ (coarse samples per ray), $N_{fine}$ (fine samples per ray), `perturb` (boolean), `raw_noise_std` (float), `white_bkgd` (boolean).

**Outputs:**
*   Dictionary containing:
    *   `rgb_coarse`: Rendered color from coarse pass, shape $[N, 3]$.
    *   `depth_coarse`: Depth from coarse pass, shape $[N, 1]$.
    *   `rgb_fine`: Final rendered color from fine pass, shape $[N, 3]$.
    *   `depth_fine`: Final depth from fine pass, shape $[N, 1]$.

### Data Structures
*   **Coarse Samples ($t_c$):** Tensor of shape $[N, N_{coarse}]$ containing depth values along the ray.
*   **Fine Samples ($t_{fine}$):** Tensor of shape $[N, N_{fine}]$ containing depth values clustered near opaque regions.
*   **Concatenated Samples ($t_f$):** Tensor of shape $[N, N_{coarse} + N_{fine}]$, sorted in ascending order.
*   **3D Points ($\mathbf{pts}$):** Tensor of shape $[N, N_{coarse} + N_{fine}, 3]$, computed as $\mathbf{o} + t \cdot \mathbf{d}$.
*   **Raw Output:** Tensor of shape $[N, N_{samples}, 4]$, where the first 3 channels are RGB (sigmoided) and the 4th channel is volume density $\sigma$.

### Parameters
*   `n_coarse`: Number of samples in the coarse pass. Default: 64 [S3, unofficial].
*   `n_fine`: Number of samples in the fine pass. Default: 128 [S3, unofficial].
*   `perturb`: If True, adds random jitter to coarse samples to break symmetry and reduce aliasing. Default: True [S3, unofficial].
*   `raw_noise_std`: Standard deviation of uniform noise added to volume density $\sigma$ before ReLU activation. Default: 0 [S4, unofficial].
*   `white_bkgd`: If True, adds unaccounted transmittance $(1 - \alpha_{total})$ to the RGB map, assuming a white background. Default: False [S4, unofficial].
*   `pos_levels`: Number of frequency octaves for positional encoding. Default: 10 [S2, unofficial].
*   `dir_levels`: Number of frequency octaves for directional encoding. Default: 4 [S2, unofficial].

### Step-by-Step Method

1.  **Coarse Sampling:**
    Generate $N_{coarse}$ samples $t_c$ uniformly along the ray between $t_{near}$ and $t_{far}$. If `perturb` is enabled, stratify the samples by adding uniform noise $\mathcal{U}[0, 1]$ within each bin to break symmetry [S3, S4, unofficial].

2.  **Coarse Network Query:**
    Convert $t_c$ to 3D points $\mathbf{pts}_c = \mathbf{o} + t_c \cdot \mathbf{d}$. Query the coarse MLP with $\mathbf{pts}_c$ and ray directions to obtain raw outputs $\mathbf{raw}_c$ of shape $[N, N_{coarse}, 4]$ [S3, S4, unofficial].

3.  **Coarse Rendering (Alpha Compositing):**
    Convert $\mathbf{raw}_c$ to weights $w_c$ using the `raw2outputs` logic (defined below). This involves:
    *   Computing distances $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$, appending $\infty$ to the last segment.
    *   Applying ReLU to the density channel $\sigma$ and adding noise if `raw_noise_std` > 0.
    *   Computing opacity $\alpha = 1 - \exp(-\sigma \cdot \delta)$.
    *   Computing transmittance $T$ as the cumulative product of $(1-\alpha)$, prepended with 1.
    *   Computing weights $w = \alpha \cdot T$.
    *   Rendering `rgb_coarse` and `depth_coarse` [S3, S4, unofficial].

4.  **Hierarchical (Fine) Sampling:**
    Use the coarse weights $w_c$ to define a probability distribution. Sample $N_{fine}$ new points $t_{fine}$ from this distribution using inverse transform sampling (or `sample_pdf`) [S3, S4, unofficial].
    *   *Pitfall:* The sampling step is non-differentiable. The coarse weights $w_c$ and coarse samples $t_c$ must be detached from the computation graph (using `.detach()`) before being used to generate $t_{fine}$ to prevent cyclic gradients and ensure a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) [S3, S4, unofficial].

5.  **Concatenation and Sorting:**
    Concatenate the coarse samples and fine samples: $t_{concat} = [t_c, t_{fine}]$. Sort $t_{concat}$ in ascending order to produce $t_f$ of shape $[N, N_{coarse} + N_{fine}]$ [S3, S4, unofficial].

6.  **Fine Network Query:**
    Convert $t_f$ to 3D points $\mathbf{pts}_f = \mathbf{o} + t_f \cdot \mathbf{d}$. Query the fine MLP with $\mathbf{pts}_f$ and ray directions to obtain raw outputs $\mathbf{raw}_f$ [S3, S4, unofficial].

7.  **Fine Rendering:**
    Apply the same `raw2outputs` logic to $\mathbf{raw}_f$ and $t_f$ to compute the final `rgb_fine` and `depth_fine` [S3, S4, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def raw2outputs(raw, z_vals, rays_d, raw_noise_std=0.0, white_bkgd=False):
    """
    Converts raw MLP outputs to radiance, depth, and weights.
    
    Args:
        raw: Tensor of shape (ray_count, sample_count, 4). 
             Channels 0-2: RGB (linear), Channel 3: Density sigma.
        z_vals: Tensor of shape (ray_count, sample_count). Depth values.
        rays_d: Tensor of shape (ray_count, 3). Ray directions.
        raw_noise_std: Std dev for noise added to density before ReLU.
        white_bkgd: Boolean. Add background transmittance to RGB.
        
    Returns:
        rgb_map: (ray_count, 3)
        depth_map: (ray_count,)
        acc_map: (ray_count,)
        weights: (ray_count, sample_count)
    """
    # 1. Calculate distances between samples
    # delta_i = z_{i+1} - z_i
    dists = z_vals[..., 1:] - z_vals[..., :-1]
    # Append infinity to the last segment to force opacity of last segment to 1
    # Shape: (ray_count, sample_count)
    dists = torch.cat([dists, torch.full_like(dists[..., :1], 1e10)], dim=-1)
    # Scale by ray direction norm (usually 1.0 for normalized rays)
    dists = dists * torch.norm(rays_d[..., None, :], dim=-1)
    
    # 2. Extract RGB and Density
    rgb = torch.sigmoid(raw[..., :3])  # (ray_count, sample_count, 3)
    sigma_raw = raw[..., 3]            # (ray_count, sample_count)
    
    # 3. Add noise to density and apply ReLU
    noise = torch.zeros_like(sigma_raw)
    if raw_noise_std > 0.0:
        noise = torch.randn_like(sigma_raw) * raw_noise_std
        # Note: In some implementations, noise overwrites the raw sigma data.
        # Here we add it to the raw sigma before activation.
    
    # Apply ReLU to density
    sigma = F.relu(sigma_raw + noise)
    
    # 4. Compute Opacity Alpha
    # alpha = 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * dists)
    
    # 5. Compute Transmittance T and Weights
    # T_i = product_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - alpha_j)
    # Prepend 1.0 for T_0. Discard the last column (T after last sample).
    ones = torch.ones_like(alpha[..., :1])
    # cumprod of (1 - alpha + epsilon)
    transmittance_cumprod = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([ones, 1.0 - alpha + 1e-10], dim=-1), dim=-1)
    # T is the cumulative product up to the previous sample
    T = transmittance_cumprod[..., :-1]
    
    # Weights w_i = alpha_i * T_i
    weights = alpha * T
    
    # 6. Render RGB and Depth
    rgb_map = torch.sum(weights[..., None] * rgb, dim=-2)  # (ray_count, 3)
    depth_map = torch.sum(weights * z_vals, dim=-1)        # (ray_count,)
    acc_map = torch.sum(weights, dim=-1)                   # (ray_count,)
    
    if white_bkgd:
        # Add unaccounted transmittance (1 - accumulated alpha) assuming white background
        rgb_map = rgb_map + (1.0 - acc_map[..., None])
        
    return rgb_map, depth_map, acc_map, weights

def stratified_sample(near, far, N_c, ray_o, ray_d, perturb=True):
    """
    Uniform stratified sampling along rays.
    
    Args:
        near: float. Near plane distance.
        far: float. Far plane distance.
        N_c: int. Number of coarse samples.
        ray_o: Tensor (B, 3). Ray origins.
        ray_d: Tensor (B, 3). Ray directions.
        perturb: bool. Add jitter.
        
    Returns:
        t_vals: Tensor (B, N_c). Depth values.
    """
    device = ray_o.device
    # Create evenly spaced bins
    t_vals = torch.linspace(near, far, N_c, device=device)
    # Expand to batch dimensions
    t_vals = t_vals.expand(*ray_o.shape[:-1], N_c)
    
    if perturb:
        # Jitter within each bin
        mid = (t_vals[..., :-1] + t_vals[..., 1:]) / 2.0
        upper = torch.cat([mid, t_vals[..., -1:]], dim=-1)
        lower = torch.cat([t_vals[..., :1], mid], dim=-1)
        t_vals = lower + (upper - lower) * torch.rand_like(t_vals)
        
    return t_vals

def coarse_to_fine_render(rays_o, rays_d, coarse_net, fine_net, 
                          near=0.0, far=1.0, 
                          n_coarse=64, n_fine=128,
                          perturb=True, raw_noise_std=0.0, white_bkgd=False):
    """
    Implements the Coarse-to-Fine NeRF training/rendering loop.
    
    Args:
        rays_o: Tensor (N, 3). Ray origins.
        rays_d: Tensor (N, 3). Ray directions.
        coarse_net: Module. Coarse MLP.
        fine_net: Module. Fine MLP.
        near: float. Near clip.
        far: float. Far clip.
        n_coarse: int. Coarse samples.
        n_fine: int. Fine samples.
        perturb: bool. Perturb coarse samples.
        raw_noise_std: float. Noise std for density.
        white_bkgd: bool. White background flag.
        
    Returns:
        dict with 'rgb_coarse', 'depth_coarse', 'rgb_fine', 'depth_fine'.
    """
    # 1. Coarse Sampling
    t_c = stratified_sample(near, far, n_coarse, rays_o, rays_d, perturb)
    
    # 2. Coarse Network Query
    # pts_c shape: (N, n_coarse, 3)
    pts_c = rays_o.unsqueeze(1) + rays_d.unsqueeze(1) * t_c.unsqueeze(2)
    # coarse_net expects points and directions. 
    # Assuming network takes (pts, dirs) and returns raw (N, n_coarse, 4)
    raw_c = coarse_net(pts_c, rays_d.unsqueeze(1).expand_as(pts_c))
    
    # 3. Coarse Rendering
    rgb_coarse, depth_coarse, _, weights_c = raw2outputs(
        raw_c, t_c, rays_d, raw_noise_std, white_bkgd
    )
    
    # 4. Hierarchical Sampling
    # Sample fine points based on coarse weights.
    # Note: weights_c must be detached to break the gradient flow from fine to coarse.
    # sample_pdf is a helper that performs inverse transform sampling on weights.
    # Implementation of sample_pdf:
    #   z_mid = (t_c[..., :-1] + t_c[..., 1:]) / 2.0
    #   z_vals_fine = sample_pdf(z_mid, weights_c.detach(), n_fine, detach=True)
    # For the purpose of this self-contained pseudocode, we assume sample_pdf exists
    # or implement a simplified version if strictly required. 
    # However, standard NeRF implementations use a specific PDF sampling routine.
    # We will denote this step as calling a standard hierarchical sampler.
    
    # Pseudocode for sample_pdf (simplified inverse transform):
    #   z_mid = (t_c[..., :-1] + t_c[..., 1:]) / 2.0
    #   # Add eps to weights to avoid division by zero
    #   weights_c_detached = weights_c.detach() + 1e-5
    #   # Normalize weights to sum to 1 per ray
    #   weights_c_detached = weights_c_detached / weights_c_detached.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    #   # Cumulative sum
    #   pdf = weights_c_detached
    #   cdf = torch.cumsum(pdf, dim=-1)
    #   cdf = torch.cat([torch.zeros_like(cdf[..., :1]), cdf], dim=-1)
    #   # Uniform samples in [0, 1]
    #   u = torch.rand_like(t_c[..., :1]) # (N, 1)
    #   # Inverse transform
    #   indices = torch.searchsorted(cdf, u, side='right')
    #   # Clamp indices
    #   indices = torch.clamp(indices, 0, n_coarse - 1)
    #   # Interpolate or pick? Standard NeRF picks indices and uses z_mid or z_vals.
    #   # The original NeRF code uses tfp.distributions.Uniform and inverse CDF.
    #   # Here we approximate by picking the z_mid corresponding to the index.
    #   t_fine = torch.gather(z_mid, -1, indices)
    
    # To ensure correctness without external libraries, we rely on the conceptual step:
    # t_fine = sample_pdf(z_mid, weights_c.detach(), n_fine)
    # We will assume a helper `sample_pdf` is available or implemented as above.
    # For the sake of the "complete enough to implement" rule, we include the logic:
    
    z_mid = (t_c[..., :-1] + t_c[..., 1:]) / 2.0
    weights_detached = weights_c.detach() + 1e-5
    weights_detached = weights_detached / weights_detached.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    pdf = weights_detached
    cdf = torch.cumsum(pdf, dim=-1)
    cdf = torch.cat([torch.zeros_like(cdf[..., :1]), cdf], dim=-1)
    
    u = torch.rand_like(t_c[..., :1])
    indices = torch.searchsorted(cdf, u, side='right')
    indices = torch.clamp(indices, 0, n_coarse - 1)
    t_fine = torch.gather(z_mid, -1, indices)
    
    # 5. Concatenate and Sort
    t_concat = torch.cat([t_c, t_fine], dim=-1)
    t_fine_sorted, _ = torch.sort(t_concat, dim=-1)
    
    # 6. Fine Network Query
    pts_fine = rays_o.unsqueeze(1) + rays_d.unsqueeze(1) * t_fine_sorted.unsqueeze(2)
    raw_fine = fine_net(pts_fine, rays_d.unsqueeze(1).expand_as(pts_fine))
    
    # 7. Fine Rendering
    rgb_fine, depth_fine, _, _ = raw2outputs(
        raw_fine, t_fine_sorted, rays_d, raw_noise_std, white_bkgd
    )
    
    return {
        'rgb_coarse': rgb_coarse,
        'depth_coarse': depth_coarse,
        'rgb_fine': rgb_fine,
        'depth_fine': depth_fine
    }
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Gradient Flow:** The hierarchical sampling step is non-differentiable. The coarse weights $w_c$ and coarse samples $t_c$ must be detached from the computation graph before generating $t_{fine}$ [S3, S4, unofficial]. Failure to do so creates a cyclic definition in the compute graph, causing backpropagation to fail [S4, unofficial].
*   **Last Segment Distance:** An infinite distance ($1e10$) is appended to the last segment's $\delta$ to ensure the opacity of the last segment is 1, forcing the transmittance to 0 after the last sample [S4, unofficial].
*   **Noise Injection:** Random noise is added to the volume density $\sigma$ before ReLU if `raw_noise_std` > 0. This helps prevent the network from learning trivial solutions where density is zero everywhere except at exact sample points [S4, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Hexagonal Multisampling Pattern Derivation](3498-hexagonal-multisampling-pattern-derivation.md)
This method constructs a multisampling pattern for a conical frustum by first generating a zero-mean, identity-covariance pattern for a cylinder and then warping it to match the frustum's analytical mean and covariance [3498-hexagonal-multisampling-pattern-derivation.md]. It ensures uniform distribution along the ray and in angles around the ray while minimizing the number of samples for efficiency [3498-hexagonal-multisampling-pattern-derivation.md]. It differs from [DONeRF Depth Oracle Filtering](4472-donerf-depth-oracle-filtering.md) by explicitly matching statistical moments of the conical frustum rather than filtering depth estimates, and from [Hierarchical Sampling for Hemispherical Wavefronts](4691-hierarchical-sampling-for-hemispherical-wavefronts.md) by using a fixed hexagonal geometry derived from cylindrical symmetry rather than hemispherical wavefronts [3498-hexagonal-multisampling-pattern-derivation.md].

### [DONeRF Depth Oracle Filtering](4472-donerf-depth-oracle-filtering.md)
DONeRF Depth Oracle Filtering is a preprocessing step applied to the training targets of the depth oracle network in DONeRF to improve surface detection consistency and reduce false negatives. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [Hexagonal Multisampling Pattern Derivation](3498-hexagonal-multisampling-pattern-derivation.md) by operating on the classification targets in image and depth space rather than deriving sampling patterns directly, and from [Hierarchical Sampling for Hemispherical Wavefronts](4691-hierarchical-sampling-for-hemispherical-wavefronts.md) by focusing on smoothing discontinuities in the oracle's one-hot encoding targets.

### [Hierarchical Sampling for Hemispherical Wavefronts](4691-hierarchical-sampling-for-hemispherical-wavefronts.md)
This method is a coarse-to-fine sampling scheme for 2D hemispherical wavefronts used in Non-Line-of-Sight (NLOS) imaging to balance reconstruction quality and computational overhead. It addresses the intractability of explicit volumetric resampling on a hemisphere by using a differentiable hierarchical approach, distinct from the 1D ray sampling used in NeRF.

### Single Multiscale MLP Architecture
A network architecture using a single MLP with parameters $\Theta$ for both coarse and fine sampling stages, enabled by IPE features which explicitly encode scale into input features [S5, Single Multiscale MLP Architecture]. It uses a single MLP for both coarse and fine stages, querying it repeatedly in a hierarchical sampling strategy [S5, Single Multiscale MLP Architecture]. The optimization problem is $\min_{\Theta} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \left( \left\| \mathbf{C}^*(\mathbf{r}) - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{r}; \Theta, \mathbf{t}^c) \right\|_2^2 + \left\| \mathbf{C}^*(\mathbf{r}) - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{r}; \Theta, \mathbf{t}^f) \right\|_2^2 \right)$ [S5, Single Multiscale MLP Architecture]. The hyperparameter $\lambda$ balances coarse and fine loss, with a default value of 0.1 [S5, Single Multiscale MLP Architecture]. Unlike NeRF which uses two distinct MLPs because PE features only model one scale, mip-NeRF uses one MLP because IPE encodes scale explicitly [S5, Single Multiscale MLP Architecture].

### Mip-NeRF 360 Proposal Sampling
This variant involves two rounds of proposal sampling to bound scene geometry and recursively generate new sample intervals, followed by one final NeRF round to render the final set of intervals into an image [S6, Mip-NeRF 360 Proposal Sampling]. The proposal NGP hierarchy is truncated at maximum grid sizes of 512 and 2048, and uses a single channel of features that predicts density only, not color [S6, Mip-NeRF 360 Proposal Sampling].

### Segmented Sampling
A sampling strategy that selectively increases the sampling rate only within the bounding box of an object area to ensure detailed sampling without significantly increasing overall computational cost [S7, Segmented Sampling]. Direct composition in standard NeRF can lead to missing areas of the object in rendered images because the object NeRF size is smaller than the scene, causing sparse sampling points on the object [S7, Segmented Sampling]. Sparse sampling on objects causes parts of the object to fall between two sampled points, leading to inaccurate probability density distribution (PDF) in the fine stage of NeRF sampling [S7, Segmented Sampling]. Standard uniform sampling results in sparse points on small objects relative to the scene; segmented sampling fixes this by boosting density only in the object's bounding box [S7, Segmented Sampling].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method requires two forward passes per ray (coarse and fine), effectively doubling the number of MLP evaluations compared to a single-pass uniform sampler with $N_{fine}$ samples. However, it achieves higher quality than a single pass with $N_{coarse}$ samples.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing outputs for both coarse and fine passes, including weights, transmittance, and raw outputs for both networks.
*   **Performance:** Improves detail learning by clustering samples near opaque regions, reducing aliasing and improving PSNR/SSIM metrics compared to uniform sampling [S1, S3, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently allocates samples to visible geometry; reduces artifacts from uniform sampling; allows for higher effective resolution without proportional increase in coarse cost [S1, S3, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires two separate MLPs (or careful parameter sharing); non-differentiable sampling step requires graph detachment; increased memory usage for intermediate tensors [S3, S4, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When high-frequency detail is important and uniform sampling is insufficient or inefficient [S1, S3, unofficial].
*   **When Not to Use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and coarse detail is sufficient; or when the scene is entirely transparent/empty.

## Background
Coarse-to-Fine NeRF was introduced in the original NeRF paper by Mildenhall et al. [2020] as a hierarchical sampling strategy to improve the quality of neural radiance field reconstructions [S1, S3, unofficial]. It was necessary because the positional encoding (PE) features used in the original NeRF architecture could only model a single scale of detail, necessitating separate coarse and fine networks to capture both global structure and fine details [S5]. The method has since been adapted in various forms, such as mip-NeRF which uses a single MLP with integrated positional encoding (IPE) to handle multiple scales [S5], and other variants like Mip-NeRF 360 and Segmented Sampling for specific scene types [S6, S7].
