# Hierarchical Viewing Cube Sampling

Also called: Adaptive cube placement, Quadtree cube sampling

## Overview
Hierarchical Viewing Cube Sampling is a method for computing scattering convolutions in volume rendering by adaptively placing viewing cubes along rays to balance accuracy and performance. It solves the problem of efficiently evaluating high-dimensional scattering integrals by exploiting the spatial coherence of scattering convolutions: higher-order terms vary slowly and can be interpolated, while lower-order terms require precise sampling near the forward scattering peak. It sets itself apart from uniform sampling by using a quadtree hierarchy to determine where new cubes are needed, and from pure interpolation by computing exact values at sparse vertices to guide the interpolation.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs**: A set of low-density ray segments $S_{jkl}$ indexed by segment $j$ and pixel coordinates $(k, l)$. A viewing cube geometry defined by faces (rear five faces $ABCD, AEFB, BFGC, CGHD, DHEA$ and front face $EFGH$).
*   **Outputs**: Scattering convolutions $K_{ijkl}$ for each segment and pixel, and probabilities $q_{ijkl}$. The final radiance contribution $E_j$ for each segment.

**Data Structures**
*   **Convolutions $K_{ijkl}$**: Stored for each segment $j$ and pixel $(k,l)$.
*   **Probabilities $q_{ijkl}$**: Computed for every pixel, even when $K$ is interpolated.
*   **Quadtree Vertices**: Used to hierarchically determine cube placement.
*   **Memory Buffer**: Stores only the right-hand column of the previous 9x9 block and one full image row (bottom row of the previous sweep) to enable horizontal sweep computation.

**Formulas**
The radiance contribution $E_j$ of segment $S_j$ to a viewing ray through a pixel is:
$$E_j = \sum_i q_{ij} K_{ij} \quad (9)$$
where $i$ indexes the scattering order/direction, $q_{ij}$ is the probability, and $K_{ij}$ is the scattering convolution.

**Parameters**
*   **Sampling Pattern Codes**:
    *   `'4'`: Place a new viewing cube at pixels where $x$ and $y$ are divisible by 4.
    *   `'2'`: Place a new viewing cube at pixels where $x$ and $y$ are divisible by 2.
    *   `'1'`: Place a new viewing cube at pixels where $x$ or $y$ is odd.
    *   `'-'` or blank: No new cube; interpolate from existing cubes if distances are close enough.

**Steps**
1.  **Rear Face Sampling**: Sample the rear five faces ($ABCD, AEFB, BFGC, CGHD, DHEA$) initially along rays at every 8 output image pixels in $x$ and $y$.
2.  **Front Face Sampling (Coarse)**: Sample the front face $EFGH$ initially every 4 pixels in $x$ and $y$. This resolution is doubled relative to the rear faces to provide more accurate interpolated convolutions near the forward scattering peak.
3.  **Front Face Sampling (Fine)**: Sample only the center quarter $IJKL$ of the front face $EFGH$ every 2 pixels in $x$ and $y$. This central section is disregarded when computing the convolution for the whole front face to ensure every direction is counted exactly once across the three patterns.
4.  **Hierarchical Cube Placement**:
    *   Compute convolutions hierarchically at the vertices of a quadtree.
    *   Use the pattern codes ('4', '2', '1') to determine where new cubes are placed.
    *   If the distances along the ray for positions indicated by '-' are close enough, bilinearly interpolate $K_{ijkl}$ from existing cubes.
    *   If distances are not close enough, place a new viewing cube at the appropriate distance and calculate new $K_{ijkl}$.
    *   This hierarchy finds values satisfactory for interpolation in sub-squares.
5.  **Accuracy Adaptation**:
    *   For higher-order (large $i$) convolutions, which are blurred and change slowly with pixel index $(k,l)$, rely on interpolation from sparser sampling. Compute $q_{ijkl}$ at every pixel.
    *   For smaller $i$, ensure the front face of the cube containing the viewing direction is accurate, as scattering is mainly forward.
    *   For even smaller $i$, only a central section of the front face is required. This allows octree clipping to eliminate large cells, saving rendering time.
6.  **Sweep Computation**:
    *   Compute convolutions on 9x9 pixel blocks in horizontal sweeps.
    *   Save only the right-hand column of the previous block and one full image row (bottom row of the previous sweep) to save memory.
    *   The left-hand column of the current block is known from the previous block, and the top row is known from the previous sweep.
7.  **Final Contribution**: Multiply $K_{ijkl}$ by $q_{ijkl}$ and sum over $i$ to get $E_j$.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Precision**: Interpolation is only valid if distances along the ray are "close enough." If not, a new cube must be placed to avoid artifacts.
*   **Boundaries**: The sweep computation requires careful handling of image boundaries to ensure the left column and top row are correctly initialized for the first block and sweep.
*   **Degenerate Input**: If a viewing ray has no low-density segments, the pattern code is blank, and no cube is placed.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: Reduced compared to uniform sampling due to adaptive placement and interpolation. The quadtree hierarchy allows skipping large areas where interpolation is sufficient.
*   **Memory Complexity**: Optimized by storing only the right-hand column of the previous block and one full image row, significantly reducing memory footprint for large images.
*   **Strengths**: Efficiently handles forward scattering by focusing sampling on the front face and center. Hierarchical placement reduces redundant calculations.
*   **Limitations**: Requires careful tuning of interpolation distance thresholds. Complexity increases with the number of scattering orders and segments.
*   **When to Use**: Suitable for volume rendering with significant forward scattering, such as clouds or fog, where high-order scattering terms vary slowly.
*   **When Not to Use**: Not ideal for scenes with highly isotropic scattering or where rapid spatial changes in scattering properties occur, as interpolation may introduce errors.

## Background
Hierarchical Viewing Cube Sampling was introduced in the context of global illumination rendering for clouds and participating media. It builds on the principles of adaptive sampling and quadtree subdivision to optimize the computation of scattering convolutions. The method is detailed in the work on diffusion and multiple anisotropic scattering for global illumination in clouds.
