# Resolution Adaptation

Also called: Adaptive Resolution, Dynamic Resolution, Resolution Scaling

## Overview
Resolution Adaptation is a family of techniques that vary the spatial discretization of the rendering process—specifically the resolution of the output image or the underlying data representation—to balance visual fidelity against computational cost. It solves the problem of maintaining target frame rates or managing memory bandwidth when rendering complex volume datasets by reducing the number of samples or the pixel count in regions or frames where high detail is not perceptually critical. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Adaptive Sampling and Resolution](1412-adaptive-sampling-and-resolution.md) by focusing specifically on the spatial granularity of the representation (voxel grid or screen pixels) rather than just the temporal sampling density along rays, though it often works in concert with them.

## Shared principles
The family shares the core mechanism of varying the discretization of the rendering process—either in the number of samples per ray, the step size along the ray, the spatial resolution of the output, or the subset of pixels processed—to balance visual fidelity against computational cost.

1.  **Metric Evaluation**: A metric is computed to determine the required level of detail. This metric may be local (e.g., density $\sigma$, gradient magnitude $\nabla \sigma$, or color variance $\text{Var}(C)$ in a pixel neighborhood) or global (e.g., frame time, training iteration count) [S7, S5, S4].
2.  **Discretization Adjustment**: Based on the metric, the rendering parameters are adjusted:
    *   **Step Size**: The integration step $\Delta t$ is varied. For density-based methods, $\Delta t$ is often inversely proportional to density or gradient magnitude to maintain constant error bounds [S7, S5].
    *   **Sample Count**: The number of samples $K$ per ray is increased in high-detail regions and decreased in empty regions [S1].
    *   **Resolution**: The spatial resolution of the rendered image or the underlying voxel grid is adjusted [S1][S2, unofficial].
3.  **Integration/Rendering**: The volume rendering integral is computed using the adjusted discretization. For ray-centric methods, this involves a ray marching loop where $\Delta t$ changes at each step [S7, S5]. For resolution-based methods, this may involve rendering at a lower resolution and upsampling or subsampling pixels [S1][S2, unofficial].
4.  **Coherence and Stability**: To maintain GPU thread coherence, step sizes are often quantized or limited to a small set of discrete values, preventing divergence in warp execution [S5]. For temporal methods, dithering (e.g., blue noise) is used to break up coherent aliasing patterns, and temporal accumulation is used to smooth noise [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Level-of-detail (LOD) methods](1007-level-of-detail-lod-methods.md)
Level-of-detail (LOD) methods change the resolution of the rendering during viewpoint interaction to maintain responsive frame rates. They solve the problem of visualizing large scalar fields or unstructured grids by generating reduced-resolution approximations of the original data, thereby minimizing memory bandwidth and sample counts. Unlike [Dynamic Volume Resolution & Alpha Mask Update](2539-dynamic-volume-resolution-alpha-mask-update.md), which often adjusts the grid structure itself, LOD methods typically maintain the original geometry or data structure and vary the sampling density or number of rendered primitives.

### [Dynamic Volume Resolution & Alpha Mask Update](2539-dynamic-volume-resolution-alpha-mask-update.md)
This method dynamically adjusts the spatial resolution of the underlying volume representation and the density of the sampling process during training to balance computational cost and reconstruction quality. It solves the problem of inefficient resource usage in early training stages (where the volume is sparse or low-resolution) and late stages (where high-frequency details are required). It sets itself apart from static resolution methods by coupling the geometric pruning of empty space (via an alpha mask) with the explicit upscaling of the voxel grid and the adaptive adjustment of samples per ray, all triggered by predefined iteration schedules.

### [Mixed-Resolution Multi-Volume Rendering](3193-mixed-resolution-multi-volume-rendering.md)
Mixed-resolution multi-volume rendering is a technique where different channels of a multi-volume dataset are rendered at different spatial resolution levels to save memory and reduce sample evaluation costs. It addresses the inefficiency of rendering all channels at the highest resolution when some channels have lower frequency content or lower visual importance. It is distinct from sibling methods like Level-of-detail (LOD) methods, which typically adjust resolution based on distance for the entire volume, and Dynamic Volume Resolution & Alpha Mask Update, which adjusts resolution based on occupancy or alpha thresholds [1007-level-of-detail-lod-methods.md][2539-dynamic-volume-resolution-alpha-mask-update.md].

### Mixed Resolution Scene Rendering
#### definition
- [S1] Combines full-resolution scene elements with low-resolution high-density volume data by rendering the volume to off-screen targets and projecting them onto a quad.
#### step
- [S1] Setup two off-screen cameras, one for each eye, updating each frame.
- [S1] Setup two low-resolution render targets (e.g., 200x200) for the cameras.
- [S1] Set up a quad that moves in front of the user.
- [S1] Each frame: Draw the low-resolution render targets for each eye (containing volume data and expensive shaders).
- [S1] Each frame: Draw the scene normally at full resolution (meshes, UI).
- [S1] Each frame: Draw a quad in front of the user and project the low-res renders onto it.

### Dynamic Resolution Adaptation
#### definition
- [S2, unofficial] Dynamically adjusts the render viewport reduction factor based on previous frame render time and allocated time budget to maintain target frame rate.
#### step
- [S2, unofficial] Compute new ReductionFactor ensuring it stays in range (0.0, 1.0].

### parameter
- [S2, unofficial] allocatedTime: double in seconds representing the time budget for the next frame.

### pitfall
- [S2, unofficial] Requires existing ReductionFactor to be >0.0 and <=1.0, and TimeToDraw >= 0.0.

### Volume Level-of-Detail (LOD) Rendering
#### definition
- [S4] A rendering process that maximizes image quality within user-specified frame rate and system constraints (RAM, texture memory, bandwidth) by adaptively modifying the set of rendered cells.
#### step
- [S4] Adapt/Load Phase: Determine which octree cells to render. Load non-resident textures from RAM or disk to texture memory. Maintain an 'active cut' (nodes covering space exactly once).
- [S4] Active Cut Maintenance: Split queue contains non-leaf nodes on active cut, sorted by decreasing error. To decrease error: Move first split node to merge queue (mark inactive), add its children to split queue (mark active). To increase error (frame rate): Remove first node from merge queue to split queue (mark active), add its parent to merge queue, remove children from split queue (mark inactive).
- [S4] Cull/Render Phase: Traverse octree from root. Perform hierarchical cull. Render flagged nodes using 3D texture mapping and blending in back-to-front order.

### parameter
- [S4] User parameters: Desired frame rate, transfer function (data to RGBA), view position.
- [S4] System parameters: RAM, texture memory size, bandwidths, polygon and fill performance.

## Background
Resolution adaptation in volume rendering has evolved from static hierarchical structures to dynamic, performance-driven techniques. Early work on interactive visualization of unstructured grids introduced hierarchical 3D LOD systems that maintained an "active cut" of octree nodes to balance quality and frame rate [S4]. These systems relied on adaptive voxelization to preserve data fidelity where needed while merging cells in less critical areas. With the advent of real-time graphics and VR, techniques like Mixed Resolution Scene Rendering emerged to handle high-density volume data (such as clouds or smoke) by offloading it to lower-resolution render targets, allowing full-resolution rendering of other scene elements [S1]. In the context of neural rendering and training, dynamic resolution adaptation became crucial for managing the computational cost of optimizing large voxel grids, where resolution is scaled up periodically alongside alpha mask updates to refine geometry [S1]. More recently, in real-time cloud rendering, hybrid approaches combining resolution reduction (e.g., 1/2x1/2) with frame splitting and temporal upsampling have been employed to maintain high frame rates on varying hardware configurations [S3, unofficial].
