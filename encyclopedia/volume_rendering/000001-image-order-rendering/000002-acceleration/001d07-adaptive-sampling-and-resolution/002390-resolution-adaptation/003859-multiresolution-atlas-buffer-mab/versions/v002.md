# Multiresolution Atlas Buffer (MAB)

Also called: VR DVR acceleration wrapper, bracket approach for VR raycasting, gaze-dependent resolution DVR

## Overview
The Multiresolution Atlas Buffer (MAB) is an acceleration wrapper for Direct Volume Rendering (DVR) in Virtual Reality (VR) that reduces ray density adaptively based on peripheral vision and Head-Mounted Display (HMD) lens distortion, then reconstructs the full-resolution image via compositing [S1]. It differs from sibling methods like [Level-of-detail (LOD) methods](1007-level-of-detail-lod-methods.md) by operating as a post-processing/compositing bracket around existing raycasters rather than modifying the traversal itself, and unlike [Dynamic Volume Resolution & Alpha Mask Update](2539-dynamic-volume-resolution-alpha-mask-update.md), it adapts the output framebuffer resolution rather than the input volume data resolution [S1].

## How it works
The method consists of two steps injected into existing DVR pipelines: (1) adaptive ray density reduction during ray construction, and (2) compositing computation after ray traversal to re-sample results across the screen and reconstruct the final image [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Current frame time $T_{draw}$ [S1].
    *   Screen-space coordinates $(u, v)$ of the framebuffer fragment [S1].
    *   Gaze center position (assumed fixed or slowly varying due to HMD lens constraints) [S1].
    *   Pre-computed MAB structure [S1].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Final composite framebuffer image submitted to the HMD [S1].
    *   Adjusted ray density parameters for the next frame [S1].

### Data Structures
**Multiresolution Atlas Buffer (MAB):**
*   **Layout:** The MAB is a single buffer partitioned into multiple regions. Each region corresponds to the entire framebuffer at a specific resolution level [S1].
*   **Resolution Levels:** Levels are ordered to minimize the overall framebuffer size [S1]. Resolution scaling values between subsequent levels are arbitrary and not necessarily a power of two [S1].
*   **Black Areas:** Regions in each level that are not used (e.g., peripheral areas at high resolutions) are discarded before ray traversal to save computation [S1].
*   **Storage:** Stores the rendered color/transparency values for each resolution level [S1].

### Mapping Functions
A bidirectional mapping between the framebuffer space and MAB space is mandatory [S1].

1.  **Forward Mapping (Ray Construction):**
    *   Determines which MAB region (resolution level) a ray should be cast into.
    *   Reduces ray density in peripheral regions [S1].
2.  **Inverse Mapping (Compositing):**
    *   Maps a framebuffer fragment to multiple MAB fragments.
    *   Computes blending factors for these fragments [S1].

Two functions compute the mapping for compositing:
*   $f_a(d)$: Takes the distance $d$ to the center of gaze (based on screen-space coordinates) and outputs a set of blending factors $\{w_i\}$ for the MAB levels [S1].
*   $f_b(u, v)$: Takes the screen-space coordinates $(u, v)$ and outputs a set of corresponding coordinates $\{(u_i, v_i)\}$ in the MAB [S1].

### Parameters
*   **HMD Update Rate:** At least 90 Hz, tending toward 120 Hz or more [S1].
*   **HMD Resolution:** Around 2880 x 1600 pixels [S1].
*   **Resolution Scaling:** Arbitrary ratios between levels, not restricted to powers of two [S1].

### Algorithm Steps

1.  **MAB Construction (Pre-pass/Setup):**
    *   Define resolution levels $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_n$ where $L_0$ is the highest resolution (full framebuffer) and $L_n$ is the lowest.
    *   Partition the MAB buffer into regions corresponding to each level.
    *   Order levels to minimize total buffer size [S1].
    *   Define the mapping functions $f_a$ and $f_b$ based on gaze distance and screen coordinates [S1].

2.  **Ray Casting (Opening Brace):**
    *   For each framebuffer pixel, determine the appropriate resolution level using the forward mapping.
    *   Generate primary rays only for the selected MAB region.
    *   Traverse rays through the volume and store results in the corresponding MAB region [S1].
    *   *Note:* Black areas (unused regions) are skipped [S1].

3.  **Compositing (Closing Brace):**
    *   Render a full-screen primitive covering the output framebuffer.
    *   For each generated fragment with screen coordinates $(u, v)$:
        a. Calculate distance to gaze center $d$ [S1].
        b. Evaluate $f_a(d)$ to get blending factors $\{w_i\}$ [S1].
        c. Evaluate $f_b(u, v)$ to get MAB coordinates $\{(u_i, v_i)\}$ [S1].
        d. For each $i$ where $w_i > 0$:
            i. Sample color $C_i$ from the MAB at $(u_i, v_i)$ in level $L_i$ [S1].
        e. Compute final color $C_{final} = \frac{\sum w_i C_i}{\sum w_i}$ [G] (Standard weighted blending implied by "blended together").
        f. Write $C_{final}$ to the framebuffer [S1].

4.  **Adaptive Adjustment:**
    *   Evaluate frame timings $T_{draw}$ [S1].
    *   Adjust rendering parameters (e.g., resolution levels, ray density) for the next subsampling stage to maintain a consistent refresh rate [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Gaze Tracking:** The method does not consider rapid eye movements because HMD lenses are fixed and provide only a slight angle with the highest projected resolution [S1]. This simplifies the gaze model but may lead to artifacts if the user moves their head quickly relative to the scene [S1].
*   **Blending Artifacts:** The use of arbitrary resolution ratios (not powers of two) requires careful implementation of $f_a$ and $f_b$ to avoid visible seams or popping during transitions [S1].
*   **Precision:** High HMD resolutions (2880 x 1600) demand precise mapping to avoid misalignment between MAB regions and framebuffer pixels [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of rays cast, which is reduced by the adaptive resolution. The compositing step is $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of framebuffer pixels, with a small constant factor for blending [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N \cdot \sum \frac{1}{r_i^2})$ where $r_i$ are the resolution scaling factors. The MAB size is minimized by ordering levels appropriately [S1].
*   **Performance:** Designed to make DVR feasible in VR by guaranteeing stable high refresh rates (90-120+ Hz) [S1]. Reduces the number of primary rays significantly in peripheral regions [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Works as a wrapper around existing raycasters with minimal code changes; adapts to hardware characteristics (HMD resolution, refresh rate); leverages HVS and HMD lens distortion [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Does not account for rapid eye movements; requires pre-computed mapping functions; blending artifacts possible if resolution ratios are not chosen carefully [S1].
*   **When to use:** VR setups with HMDs where DVR is performance-bound and stable high refresh rates are critical to avoid cyber-sickness [S1].
*   **When not to use:** Desktop rendering without HMDs; scenarios requiring precise foveated rendering based on rapid eye movements [S1].

## Background
The Multiresolution Atlas Buffer (MAB) method was introduced by Jens Krüger and Andre Waschk in WSCG 2022 Proceedings (CSRN 3201) [S1]. It builds on the FAVR (Foveated Adaptive Volume Rendering) approach by Waschk and Krüger (2020) and gaze-dependent rendering works [S1]. The method addresses the challenge of rendering high-resolution DVR in VR, where HMDs feature resolutions around 2880 x 1600 pixels and update rates of at least 90 Hz, to avoid cyber-sickness symptoms caused by latency and low frame rates [S1]. It utilizes the nonuniform resolution distribution of the human visual system and the fixed lens distortion of HMDs to reduce computational load without significant visual quality loss [S1].
