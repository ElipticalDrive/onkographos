# Bounding Volume Culling

Also called: Distance-based Culling, Ray Termination by Distance

## Overview
Bounding Volume Culling is an optimization that limits raymarching to a specific bounding volume, dropping rays that exceed a certain distance from the eye to speed up rendering [S1, unofficial]. It differs from its siblings, [Empty-space skipping](1002-empty-space-skipping.md) and [Early ray termination](1019-early-ray-termination.md), by terminating rays based on absolute geometric distance from the camera rather than accumulated opacity or empty space traversal [S1, unofficial]. Unlike [Conservative Early Ray Termination](1949-conservative-early-ray-termination.md), which uses probabilistic bounds to ensure unbiasedness, Bounding Volume Culling is a deterministic hard cutoff [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Ray origin $\mathbf{o}$, ray direction $\mathbf{d}$, maximum travel distance $T_{max}$.
*   **Output:** Boolean flag indicating whether the ray should be processed or discarded.

**Data Structures:**
*   $T_{max}$: A scalar float representing the maximum allowed distance from the ray origin.

**Steps:**
1.  Initialize the total distance traveled by the ray, $t$, to $0$.
2.  During the raymarching loop, update $t$ by adding the step size $\Delta t$ at each iteration: $t \leftarrow t + \Delta t$.
3.  Check if $t > T_{max}$.
4.  If true, terminate the raymarch process immediately [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   $T_{max}$: The predefined limit. Typical values depend on the scene scale; for example, a value of $64$ steps was used in demonstrations where step size was normalized [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** If $T_{max}$ is too small, valid geometry near the camera may be clipped. If too large, the optimization provides no benefit.
*   **Degenerate Input:** Rays originating inside the bounding volume must still respect the limit if they exit and re-enter, or simply terminate upon exit if the volume is convex and the ray is outside.

## Subtopics

### Heap-based Bounding Volume Culling for Sphere Tracing
An algorithm to find the closest object in a collection by maintaining a heap of bounding volume distances [S2]. It computes distances to bounding volumes first, then contents, culling based on the smallest bounding volume distance [S2]. It differs from [Bounding Spheres Optimization](1567-bounding-spheres-optimization.md) by actively managing a heap to defer expensive content distance calculations until necessary, whereas Bounding Spheres Optimization groups objects into enclosing spheres to eliminate distance calculations entirely for distant objects [S2].

**Steps:**
1.  Compute distances to each bounding volume in the union/collection.
2.  Create a min-heap of these distances.
3.  Initialize $d_{min}$ to infinity.
4.  Loop:
    a. Let $d_{content}$ be $\min(d_{min}, \text{distance to contents of top BV})$.
    b. Remove top of heap.
    c. Let $d_{bv}$ be the distance to the new top BV.
    d. Stop if $d_{content} < d_{bv}$ or heap is empty.
5.  Return $d_{content}$ [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   $d_{bv}$: The distance to the bounding volume currently at the top of the heap [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per ray for the basic distance check. For heap-based variants, $O(\log N)$ for heap operations where $N$ is the number of bounding volumes.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store bounding volumes and the heap.
*   **Strengths:** Simple to implement; effectively culls rays that miss the scene entirely or travel too far into empty space.
*   **Limitations:** Does not account for opacity or density; may terminate rays that are close to interesting geometry but just outside the bound. Performance degradation can occur in perspective cases for typical implicit surfaces if the bounding volume checks are expensive [S2].
*   **When to use:** When the scene has a well-defined spatial extent and rays frequently miss the geometry or travel long distances through empty space.

## Background
Bounding Volume Culling originated as a method to speed up raymarching by limiting rays to a specific bounding volume [S1, unofficial]. It is often used in conjunction with fog to avoid artifacts at large distances [S1, unofficial]. The heap-based variant for sphere tracing was developed to efficiently find the closest object in a collection of implicit surfaces by leveraging bounding volume distances [S2].
