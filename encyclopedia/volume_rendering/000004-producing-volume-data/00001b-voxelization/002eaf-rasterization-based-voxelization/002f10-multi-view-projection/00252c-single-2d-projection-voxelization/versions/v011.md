# Single 2D Projection Voxelization

Also called: Dominant axis voxelization, Thin voxelization via single projection

## Overview
Single 2D Projection Voxelization is a method that produces 6-separating (tunnel-free) voxelization using only one 2D projection along the dominant axis of a triangle's normal vector [S1, unofficial]. It leverages the "thinness" property of voxelization to reduce the computational cost of multi-view approaches by eliminating redundant projections for pixels where the triangle center is visible, while handling edge cases via crosshair-like tests [S1, unofficial]. Unlike [Orthographic Multi-View Voxelization](3892-orthographic-multi-view-voxelization.md) which requires multiple views to ensure coverage, this method relies on a single optimized view direction determined by the geometry's orientation [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A planar triangle defined by its three vertices in 3D space.
**Outputs:** A set of voxels (3D grid cells) that form a 6-separating representation of the triangle.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Triangle:** Defined by vertices $V_0, V_1, V_2$ with coordinates $(x, y, z)$.
*   **Normal Vector:** $\mathbf{n} = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$, computed as the cross product of two edge vectors, normalized.
*   **Voxel Grid:** A 3D array or hash map mapping integer coordinates $(i, j, k)$ to occupancy or depth values.

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Identify Dominant Axis:**
    Compute the geometric normal $\mathbf{n}$ of the triangle. Determine the dominant axis $A \in \{x, y, z\}$ corresponding to the component of $\mathbf{n}$ with the largest absolute value. Let the other two axes be $B$ and $C$. The projection direction is along axis $A$.

2.  **Project and Rasterize:**
    Project the triangle onto the 2D plane defined by axes $B$ and $C$. Rasterize this 2D projection into a buffer covering the bounding box of the projected triangle. For each pixel $(u, v)$ in the buffer (corresponding to voxel column indices $j, k$):

3.  **Center Test (Primary Case):**
    If the pixel center $(u, v)$ lies inside the projected triangle:
    *   Evaluate the depth $d_{tri}$ of the triangle at the pixel center. This is done by intersecting the ray parallel to axis $A$ passing through $(u, v)$ with the triangle plane.
    *   Determine the voxel index $i$ along axis $A$ such that the voxel center depth $d_{vox}$ is closest to $d_{tri}$.
    *   Output the voxel $(i, j, k)$.
    *   *Note:* This step corresponds to intersecting the triangle against the crosshair segment parallel to the projection axis [S1, unofficial].

4.  **Edge Test (Horizontal):**
    If the pixel $(u, v)$ contains an edge of the triangle but the triangle does not overlap the pixel center:
    *   Define a horizontal line bisecting the pixel (constant $v$, varying $u$).
    *   Find the intersection extents of the triangle with this line. Let these be $u_{min}$ and $u_{max}$.
    *   Clamp these extents to the horizontal pixel boundaries $[u-0.5, u+0.5]$. Let the clamped points be $P_1$ and $P_2$.
    *   Evaluate the depth of the triangle at $P_1$ and $P_2$, yielding $d_1$ and $d_2$.
    *   Let $d_{vox}$ be the depth of the voxel center for the column $(j, k)$ at the current candidate slice $i$.
    *   If $d_1$ and $d_2$ are on different sides of $d_{vox}$ (i.e., $(d_1 - d_{vox})(d_2 - d_{vox}) < 0$), output the voxel $(i, j, k)$.

5.  **Edge Test (Vertical):**
    If the horizontal test fails (values are not on different sides):
    *   Define a vertical line bisecting the pixel (constant $u$, varying $v$).
    *   Find the intersection extents of the triangle with this line. Let these be $v_{min}$ and $v_{max}$.
    *   Clamp these extents to the vertical pixel boundaries $[v-0.5, v+0.5]$. Let the clamped points be $Q_1$ and $Q_2$.
    *   Evaluate the depth of the triangle at $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, yielding $d_3$ and $d_4$.
    *   If $d_3$ and $d_4$ are on different sides of $d_{vox}$, output the voxel $(i, j, k)$.
    *   *Note:* These tests correspond to testing the triangle against the two crosshair segments perpendicular to the projection axis [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Voxel Size:** Determined by the desired resolution and scene bounds. Not given in the sources.
*   **Precision:** Floating-point arithmetic is used for depth comparisons. Edge cases involving coplanar triangles or vertices exactly on pixel boundaries require careful handling to avoid double-counting or gaps [G].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Degenerate Triangles:** Triangles with zero area or collinear vertices should be skipped or handled as lines [G].
*   **Parallel to Projection Axis:** If the triangle normal is perpendicular to the dominant axis (impossible by definition of dominant axis, but if the triangle is edge-on to the view), the projection may be a line. The rasterization step must handle this gracefully [G].
*   **Precision Errors:** Comparing depths to voxel centers requires epsilon tolerance to avoid missing voxels due to floating-point inaccuracies [G].

## Subtopics

### Crosshair Target Equivalence
This method is equivalent to testing the triangle against crosshair segments (one parallel and two perpendicular to the projection axis) within each pixel [S1, unofficial].

### Thin Voxelization
The method produces a "thin" voxelization, meaning no two voxels are adjacent along the projection axis if they represent the same surface point, ensuring 6-connectivity separation [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot R^2)$, where $N$ is the number of triangles and $R$ is the resolution of the 2D projection buffer. This is significantly faster than multi-view methods which require $O(N \cdot R^2 \cdot V)$ where $V$ is the number of views.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(R^3)$ for the output voxel grid. The intermediate 2D buffer is $O(R^2)$.
*   **Strengths:** Reduced computational cost due to single projection; maintains topological correctness (6-separating) [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** May produce artifacts if the dominant axis is not well-defined (e.g., very flat triangles) or if the triangle is nearly perpendicular to the dominant axis, leading to poor depth resolution [G]. Not suitable for scenes with complex internal structures that require multiple views to resolve occlusions [G].
*   **When to Use:** When computational resources are limited and the geometry is relatively flat or oriented along principal axes.
*   **When Not to Use:** For highly complex, curved, or self-occluding geometry where multiple views are necessary to capture all surfaces [G].

## Background
This method is derived from the work of Laine et al. (2013) on a topological approach to voxelization [S1, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of "thinness" in voxelization and the equivalence of rasterization with crosshair targets [S1, unofficial]. The approach simplifies the multi-view voxelization pipeline by exploiting the geometric properties of planar triangles.
