# Analytic Tetrahedron Voxelization

Also called: 3D rasterization using tetrahedral mesh, Exact Conservative Voxelization of Tetrahedra, Graph-traversal voxelization, Remeshing-based voxelization, Tetrahedral Voxelization with Philipp B's Algorithm, analytic volume sampling of tetrahedra, voxelization of tetrahedra

## Overview
Analytic Tetrahedron Voxelization converts a parametrically defined continuous tetrahedral mesh into a digital voxel representation by computing physically conservative moments (volume, linear, quadratic) for each grid cell [S2, S3, unofficial]. Unlike standard binary voxelization which only stores occupancy, this method ensures that the total mass in each grid cell exactly equals the integral of the input density field over the cell, avoiding aliasing noise [S1, S3, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from [Monte Carlo 2D Voxelization](1734-monte-carlo-2d-voxelization.md) and [Differentiable Voxelization (MC/CF)](1742-differentiable-voxelization-mc-cf.md) by providing exact, deterministic geometric intersection results rather than statistical approximations, and from [Analytic Moment Voxelization](3307-analytic-moment-voxelization.md) by focusing specifically on the tetrahedral primitive and its robust clipping implementation.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A tetrahedral mesh defined by vertices. The mesh must have high quality: large minimum angle, no overlapping elements, and small variance in element quality [S1, unofficial].
*   **Output:** A Cartesian grid containing moments. The grid stores `moments` (array of arrays for constant, linear, and quadratic orders) and `orient` (orientation flags) [S3, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
The implementation relies on specific structures for grid storage and geometric representation [S3, unofficial]:

1.  `r3d_dest_grid`:
    *   `r3d_real* moments[10]`: Gridded moments in row-major C order. Size is at least $n_x \times n_y \times n_z$.
    *   `r3d_int polyorder`: Polynomial order (0=constant, 1=linear, 2=quadratic).
    *   `r3d_orientation* orient`: Buffer for gridpoint orientation checks. Size is at least $(n_x+1) \times (n_y+1) \times (n_z+1)$.
    *   `r3d_long bufsz`: Buffer size.
    *   `r3d_dvec3 n`: Grid dimensions $(n_x, n_y, n_z)$.

2.  `r3d_orientation`:
    *   `r3d_real fdist[4]`: Signed distances to the four clip planes.
    *   `unsigned char fflags`: Bit flags indicating inside/outside status for each of the 4 clip planes.

3.  `r3d_plane` (Face representation):
    *   `r3d_dvec3 n`: Unit normal vector.
    *   `r3d_real d`: Signed distance to the origin.

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Preconditioning (Orientation):**
    Ensure the tetrahedron vertices are oriented such that the volume is positive. Calculate the signed volume using the determinant of vectors spanning the tetrahedron. If the orientation function returns negative, swap vertices 2 and 3 [S3, unofficial].
    $$ \text{Signed Volume} = \frac{\det(\vec{AB}, \vec{AC}, \vec{AD})}{6} $$
    Exchanging two rows in the determinant leads to opposite signed volume [S1, unofficial].

2.  **Face Conversion:**
    Convert the vertex representation to a face representation to obtain the four clip planes defining the tetrahedron [S3, unofficial].

3.  **Voxel Search:**
    Identify voxels that need processing. Two methods are described:
    *   **Brute-force:** Check every grid point against each tetrahedron face. Scales as $g^3$ [S2, unofficial].
    *   **Binary Space Partitioning (BSP):** Use a spatial tree. Scales as $g^2 \log g$ [S2, unofficial].
    *   *Note:* Brute-force performs better for coarser grids due to BSP overhead; BSP overtakes brute-force at grid resolution $128^3$ [S2, unofficial].

4.  **Voxel Classification (Inside/Outside/Boundary):**
    For each voxel, determine its relationship to the tetrahedron.
    *   **Fully Inside:** All 4 clip planes contain the voxel.
    *   **Fully Outside:** The voxel is outside at least one clip plane.
    *   **Boundary:** The voxel intersects the tetrahedron boundary.

    *Classification using Philipp B's Algorithm:*
    To test if a point $P$ lies inside tetrahedron $ABCD$, define vectors $\mathbf{a}=\vec{PA}, \mathbf{b}=\vec{PB}, \mathbf{c}=\vec{PC}, \mathbf{d}=\vec{PD}$. Calculate determinants:
    $$ D_a = \det(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}) $$
    $$ D_b = \det(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}) $$
    $$ D_c = \det(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{d}) $$
    $$ D_d = \det(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}) $$
    Point $P$ is inside if:
    $$ (D_a > 0 \land D_b < 0 \land D_c > 0 \land D_d < 0) \lor (D_a < 0 \land D_b > 0 \land D_c < 0 \land D_d > 0) $$
    [S1, unofficial]

5.  **Moment Calculation (Reduction):**
    *   **Fully Inside Voxels:** Compute analytic moments directly. For a voxel at grid index $(i,j,k)$ with cell size $d$, the constant moment is volume $V=d^3$. Linear moments are $V \cdot (i+0.5)d$. Quadratic moments involve terms like $V \cdot \frac{1}{3} d^2 (1+3i+3i^2)$ [S3, unofficial].
    *   **Boundary Voxels:** Perform clipping and integration.

6.  **Clipping and Integration (Boundary Voxels):**
    For boundary voxels, intersect the voxel (a convex polyhedron, typically a cube) with the tetrahedron.
    *   **Clipping:** Clip the voxel against the four faces of the tetrahedron. This is done by successively clipping one polyhedron against the faces of the other.
    *   **Graph Traversal:** Store polyhedra using only vertex locations and neighbors. Use a depth-first graph traversal on the planar graph representation to ensure topological validity and geometric robustness. This method inserts new vertices in the correct order on the fly, requiring no post-clipping reordering or face normals [S2, unofficial].
    *   **Integration:** Calculate moment integrals (constant, linear, quadratic) over the resulting clipped intersection domain. The results are accumulated into `grid.moments` [S3, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def analytic_tetrahedron_voxelization(tet_vertices, grid_dims, cell_size, polyorder):
    """
    Voxelize a single tetrahedron onto a Cartesian grid.
    tet_vertices: list of 4 points [A, B, C, D]
    grid_dims: (nx, ny, nz)
    cell_size: d
    polyorder: 0, 1, or 2
    """
    # 1. Preconditioning: Ensure positive volume
    # Calculate signed volume
    A, B, C, D = tet_vertices
    vec_AB = B - A
    vec_AC = C - A
    vec_AD = D - A
    signed_vol = determinant(vec_AB, vec_AC, vec_AD) / 6.0
    
    if signed_vol < 0:
        # Swap vertices 2 and 3 (C and D) to fix orientation
        tet_vertices = [A, B, D, C]
        A, B, C, D = tet_vertices
        vec_AB = B - A
        vec_AC = C - A
        vec_AD = D - A
        signed_vol = determinant(vec_AB, vec_AC, vec_AD) / 6.0

    # 2. Convert to Face Representation (Clip Planes)
    # Each face is defined by a unit normal n and distance d to origin
    # Face 0: BCD, Face 1: ACD, Face 2: ABD, Face 3: ABC
    faces = []
    # Helper to get plane from 3 points
    def get_plane(p1, p2, p3):
        n = normalize(cross(p2 - p1, p3 - p1))
        d = -dot(n, p1)
        return {'n': n, 'd': d}
    
    faces.append(get_plane(B, C, D))
    faces.append(get_plane(A, C, D))
    faces.append(get_plane(A, B, D))
    faces.append(get_plane(A, B, C))

    # Initialize grid moments
    nx, ny, nz = grid_dims
    d = cell_size
    # moments array structure: moments[order][index]
    # For simplicity, assume a flat array or list of arrays
    moments = [0.0] * (nx * ny * nz * 10) # Max 10 moments (1 const + 3 lin + 6 quad)
    
    # 3. Search and Process Voxels
    # Using brute-force for clarity, though BSP is faster for large grids
    for i in range(nx):
        for j in range(ny):
            for k in range(nz):
                # Define voxel corners (8 corners)
                # Corner indices: 000, 100, 010, 110, 001, 101, 011, 111
                # x varies fastest in some layouts, but let's define explicitly
                corners = []
                for dx in [0, 1]:
                    for dy in [0, 1]:
                        for dz in [0, 1]:
                            x = i * d + dx * d
                            y = j * d + dy * d
                            z = k * d + dz * d
                            corners.append([x, y, z])
                
                # 4. Classify Voxel
                # Check if fully inside, fully outside, or boundary
                # Using Philipp B's algorithm for center point or corner checks?
                # The r3d implementation uses corner orientations (fflags)
                
                fflags_all = 0x00
                andcmp = 0x0F
                orcmp = 0x00
                
                for corner in corners:
                    fflags = 0x00
                    for f_idx, face in enumerate(faces):
                        # Signed distance from corner to plane
                        dist = dot(corner, face['n']) + face['d']
                        if dist > 0.0:
                            fflags |= (1 << f_idx)
                    
                    fflags_all |= fflags
                    andcmp &= fflags
                    orcmp |= fflags
                
                # andcmp == 0x0F means all corners are inside (all bits 1)
                # orcmp != 0x0F means at least one corner is outside
                
                if andcmp == 0x0F:
                    # Fully Inside
                    add_analytic_moments(moments, i, j, k, d, polyorder)
                elif orcmp != 0x0F:
                    # Fully Outside (at least one corner outside, and since convex, if any corner is outside, 
                    # we need to check if it's completely outside. 
                    # Actually, if orcmp != 0x0F, it doesn't mean fully outside. 
                    # Fully outside means the voxel does not intersect the tet.
                    # The r3d code checks: if orcmp != 0x0F, it might still intersect.
                    # Wait, r3d logic:
                    # if andcmp == 0x0F: inside
                    # elif orcmp != 0x0F: This condition in r3d is actually checking if ANY corner is OUTSIDE?
                    # No, fflags bit is 1 if dist > 0 (inside).
                    # So orcmp == 0x0F means ALL corners are inside.
                    # If orcmp != 0x0F, then at least one corner is outside.
                    # If andcmp == 0x00, then ALL corners are outside -> Fully Outside.
                    pass 
                
                # Correct Logic based on r3d snippet:
                # andcmp = 0x0F -> All corners inside (bits 1)
                # orcmp != 0x0F -> Not all corners inside.
                # If andcmp == 0x00 -> All corners outside -> Skip.
                
                if andcmp == 0x00:
                    continue # Fully Outside
                
                if andcmp == 0x0F:
                    add_analytic_moments(moments, i, j, k, d, polyorder)
                    continue
                
                # Boundary: Clip and Integrate
                # This requires the full clipping routine which is complex.
                # We call the clipping function.
                clipped_moments = clip_and_integrate_tet_voxel(tet_vertices, corners, faces, polyorder)
                add_to_grid(moments, i, j, k, clipped_moments)

    return moments

def add_analytic_moments(moments, i, j, k, d, polyorder):
    """Add analytic moments for a fully inside voxel."""
    idx = (i * ny * nz + j * nz + k)
    vol = d * d * d
    
    # Constant moment
    moments[idx * 10 + 0] += vol
    
    if polyorder >= 1:
        # Linear moments
        cx = (i + 0.5) * d
        cy = (j + 0.5) * d
        cz = (k + 0.5) * d
        moments[idx * 10 + 1] += vol * cx
        moments[idx * 10 + 2] += vol * cy
        moments[idx * 10 + 3] += vol * cz
        
    if polyorder >= 2:
        # Quadratic moments
        # x^2
        moments[idx * 10 + 4] += vol * (1.0/3.0) * d * d * (1 + 3*i + 3*i*i)
        # y^2
        moments[idx * 10 + 5] += vol * (1.0/3.0) * d * d * (1 + 3*j + 3*j*j)
        # z^2
        moments[idx * 10 + 6] += vol * (1.0/3.0) * d * d * (1 + 3*k + 3*k*k)
        # xy
        moments[idx * 10 + 7] += vol * 0.25 * d * d * (1 + 2*i) * (1 + 2*j)
        # xz
        moments[idx * 10 + 8] += vol * 0.25 * d * d * (1 + 2*i) * (1 + 2*k)
        # yz
        moments[idx * 10 + 9] += vol * 0.25 * d * d * (1 + 2*j) * (1 + 2*k)

def clip_and_integrate_tet_voxel(tet_vertices, corners, faces, polyorder):
    """
    Perform clipping of the voxel against the tetrahedron and integrate moments.
    This is a simplified placeholder for the complex graph-traversal clipping.
    """
    # In the actual r3d implementation, this involves:
    # 1. Initializing the voxel as a convex polyhedron (cube).
    # 2. Successively clipping against each of the 4 tetrahedron faces.
    # 3. Using depth-first graph traversal to maintain topological validity.
    # 4. Integrating the resulting polyhedron's moments.
    
    # Due to complexity, we return zeros for this summary.
    # A full implementation requires the r3d library's clipping logic.
    return [0.0] * 10

def determinant(v1, v2, v3):
    return v1[0]*(v2[1]*v3[2] - v2[2]*v3[1]) - v1[1]*(v2[0]*v3[2] - v2[2]*v3[0]) + v1[2]*(v2[0]*v3[1] - v2[1]*v3[0])

def cross(v1, v2):
    return [
        v1[1]*v2[2] - v1[2]*v2[1],
        v1[2]*v2[0] - v1[0]*v2[2],
        v1[0]*v2[1] - v1[1]*v2[0]
    ]

def dot(v1, v2):
    return v1[0]*v2[0] + v1[1]*v2[1] + v1[2]*v2[2]

def normalize(v):
    mag = (v[0]**2 + v[1]**2 + v[2]**2)**0.5
    return [v[0]/mag, v[1]/mag, v[2]/mag]

def add_to_grid(moments, i, j, k, clipped_moments):
    idx = (i * ny * nz + j * nz + k)
    for m in range(10):
        moments[idx * 10 + m] += clipped_moments[m]
```

## Subtopics

### [Exact General Remeshing Scheme for Conservative Voxelization](3774-exact-general-remeshing-scheme-for-conservative-voxelization.md)
This method voxelizes a single tetrahedron onto a Cartesian grid by computing physically conservative moments (volume, linear, quadratic) for a specified polynomial order. It ensures that the total mass in each grid cell exactly equals the integral of the input density field over the cell, avoiding aliasing noise. Unlike standard rasterization or Sutherland-Hodgman clipping, which can fail due to geometric degeneracy and roundoff errors, this scheme uses planar graph representations to maintain topological consistency during clipping, guaranteeing robustness even when vertices lie on clip planes.

### [Platonic Solid Voxelization](16069-platonic-solid-voxelization.md)
Platonic Solid Voxelization generates a 3D binary voxel array representing the interior of regular platonic solids (tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron). It solves the problem of creating exact, resolution-dependent geometric primitives for volume rendering or collision detection without relying on mesh remeshing. Unlike [Analytic Tetrahedron Voxelization](3724-analytic-tetrahedron-voxelization.md), which computes conservative moments for arbitrary tetrahedra, this method outputs a simple binary mask (1 if inside, 0 if outside) for specific regular polyhedra. It differs from [Exact General Remeshing Scheme for Conservative Voxelization](3774-exact-general-remeshing-scheme-for-conservative-voxelization.md) by using a direct half-space intersection test rather than remeshing, making it faster for simple shapes but limited to regular solids and binary output.

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
*   Clipping and reduction operations scale roughly quadratically with the grid dimension $g$ (linearly with surface area) [S2, unofficial].
*   Brute-force search scales as $g^3$ [S2, unofficial].
*   Binary space partitioning (BSP) search scales as $g^2 \log g$ [S2, unofficial].
*   Memory usage is dominated by the grid storage for moments and orientation flags [S3, unofficial].

**Measured Performance:**
*   On a $128^3$ grid with $10^5$ tetrahedra, the method produces $1.3 \times 10^4$ interior and $1.5 \times 10^4$ boundary voxels per tetrahedron on average [S2, unofficial].
*   The proposed clipping operation is 3-4 times faster than López and Hernández (2008), with an overall speed-up of 2x [S2, unofficial].
*   Philipp B's algorithm is faster than other point-in-tetrahedron algorithms [S1, unofficial].
*   BSP overtakes brute-force at grid resolution $128^3$ [S2, unofficial].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Physically conservative (sum of voxel moments equals analytic moments up to floating-point precision) [S3, unofficial]. Immune to geometric degeneracies (e.g., vertices on clip planes) [S2, unofficial]. Does not require watertight or topologically closed meshes [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Cancellation errors arise when shifting voxels to a local coordinate frame, causing accuracy to be a few orders of magnitude away from machine precision despite the reduction process being accurate to machine precision [S2, unofficial]. Requires high-quality input tetrahedral meshes (large minimum angle, no overlapping) [S1, unofficial].

**When to Use:**
*   Use when physical conservation of mass/moments is critical (e.g., cosmological N-body systems) [S2, unofficial].
*   Use when exact geometric robustness is required for degenerate cases [S2, unofficial].
*   Do not use if binary occupancy is sufficient and performance is paramount for very coarse grids (brute-force is simple) [S2, unofficial].

## Background
Analytic Tetrahedron Voxelization originates from the need for exact, mass-conservative voxelization in cosmological N-body systems [S2, unofficial]. The specific clipping and integration framework was developed to address geometric degeneracies in polyhedron intersection, building on ideas from Sugihara (1994) regarding planar graph representations [S2, unofficial]. The point-in-tetrahedron test known as "Philipp B's algorithm" was introduced by Philipp Beisel in June 2020 in response to a question by Denis Lolik, and was proven correct in the paper by [S1, unofficial]. The C implementation `r3d` is available from Los Alamos National Laboratory [S3, unofficial].
