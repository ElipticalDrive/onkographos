# 3D Discrete Topology
## Overview
3D Discrete Topology is the theoretical framework for defining topological properties, such as surface separation and connectivity, within a 3D discrete voxel grid [S1, unofficial]. It addresses "discrete disagreement," where topological properties in continuous space do not directly translate to discrete grids [S1, unofficial].
## Shared principles
**Space Definition**
The 3D discrete space is a set of integral grid points in 3D Euclidean space defined by Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ [S1, unofficial]. A voxel is the unit cubic volume centered at each integral grid point [S1, unofficial].

**Voxel Values**
In the binary case, voxel values are mapped to the set $\{0, 1\}$ [S1, unofficial]. Voxels assigned '1' are called black voxels representing opaque objects, and those assigned '0' are white voxels representing the transparent background [S1, unofficial].

**Adjacency and Connectivity**
Two voxels are 26-adjacent if they share a vertex, an edge, or a face [S1, unofficial]. Every voxel has exactly 26 adjacent neighbors: 8 share a vertex, 12 share an edge, and 6 share a face [S1, unofficial]. Face-sharing voxels are 6-adjacent, and edge-sharing and face-sharing voxels are 18-adjacent [S1, unofficial]. The prefix $N$ denotes the adjacency type, where $N \in \{6, 18, 26\}$ [S1, unofficial].

**Connectivity and Paths**
An $N$-path is a sequence of voxels having the same value where all consecutive pairs are $N$-adjacent [S1, unofficial]. A set of voxels $W$ is $N$-connected if there is an $N$-path between every pair of voxels in $W$ [S1, unofficial]. An $N$-connected component is a maximal $N$-connected set [S1, unofficial].

**Opposite Connectivity**
To avoid penetration scenarios, opposite connectivity types are used for foreground and background sets [S1, unofficial]. In 2D space, 4 and 8 are opposite, while in 3D space 6 is opposite to 26 or to 18 [S1, unofficial].

**Separation and Minimality**
A subset $S$ of black voxels is $N$-separating if it is not $N$-connected, meaning it consists of at least two white $N$-connected components [S1, unofficial]. Let $W$ be an $N$-separating surface. A voxel $p \in W$ is $N$-simple if $W \setminus \{p\}$ is still $N$-separating [S1, unofficial]. An $N$-separating surface is $N$-minimal if it contains no $N$-simple voxels [S1, unofficial].

**Covering**
A cover of a continuous surface is a set of voxels such that every point of the continuous surface lies in a voxel of the cover [S1, unofficial]. A cover is minimal if none of its subsets is also a cover [S1, unofficial].

**Discrete Disagreement**
In 2D, 8-connected black curves can be penetrated by 8-connected white pixels without intersection, unlike continuous curves [S1, unofficial]. There are no analogous results in 3D space [S1, unofficial].

**Limitations**
A cover is not necessarily separating [S1, unofficial]. Even a minimal cover is not necessarily $N$-minimal for any $N$ [S1, unofficial].
## Subtopics

### 3D Discrete Topology Fundamentals
*   **What it is:** The foundational definitions of 3D discrete space, voxel geometry, and basic adjacency relations.
*   **Distinction:** Provides the static geometric and set-theoretic definitions (coordinates, unit cubes, basic adjacency counts) without addressing dynamic topological properties like separation or minimality.
*   **When to choose:** Use this as the starting point for understanding the coordinate system and voxel structure.

## Background
The theory of 3D discrete topology was formalized to address topological issues in 3D discrete space, such as defining the separation of both sides of a surface, which cannot be well-defined by employing 2D terminology [S1, unofficial]. It originated in the context of volume visualization and voxelization, specifically to support applications like fast ray tracing where space subdivision is employed [S1, unofficial]. The concepts were detailed by Arie Kaufman in the chapter "Fundamentals of 3D Discrete Topology" within the book *Volume Visualization: Principles and Advances* [S1, unofficial]. The theory is essential for binary voxelization techniques that aim to generate $N$-separating, $N$-minimal, and covering surfaces [S1, unofficial].
