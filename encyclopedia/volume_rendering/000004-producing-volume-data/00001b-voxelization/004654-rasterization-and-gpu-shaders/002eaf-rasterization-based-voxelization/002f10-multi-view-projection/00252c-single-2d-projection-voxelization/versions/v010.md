# Single 2D Projection Voxelization

Also called: Dominant axis voxelization, Thin voxelization via single projection

## Overview
This method produces 6-separating (tunnel-free) voxelization using only a single 2D orthographic projection along the dominant axis of a triangle's normal vector, leveraging the thinness property of the voxel grid. It differs from its sibling [Orthographic Multi-View Voxelization](3892-orthographic-multi-view-voxelization.md) and [3-Pass Voxelization](5300-3-pass-voxelization.md) by eliminating the need for multiple projection passes, reducing computational overhead at the cost of more complex per-pixel edge handling. It builds on the shared principles of [Multi-view projection](5937-multi-view-projection.md).

## How it works
**Inputs:** A planar triangle defined by vertices $\mathbf{v}_0, \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ in world space; the voxel grid resolution $(V_x, V_y, V_z)$; the bounding box minimum $B_{min}$.
**Outputs:** A set of occupied voxel indices $(i, j, k)$ in the 3D grid.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Voxel Grid:** A 3D array or hash map storing occupancy. Indices are integers $[0, V_x-1] \times [0, V_y-1] \times [0, V_z-1]$.
*   **Triangle Plane:** Defined by normal $\mathbf{n} = (\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_0) \times (\mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_0)$ and a point $\mathbf{p}_0 = \mathbf{v}_0$.

**Steps:**
1.  **Identify Dominant Axis:** Compute the geometric normal $\mathbf{n}$. Determine the dominant axis $A \in \{x, y, z\}$ corresponding to the component of $\mathbf{n}$ with the largest absolute magnitude. This is the projection axis.
2.  **2D Rasterization:** Project the triangle onto the 2D plane perpendicular to $A$. Rasterize this 2D projection to identify all pixels $(u, v)$ covered by the triangle.
3.  **Center Test:** For each covered pixel $(u, v)$:
    *   Calculate the pixel center in world space, $\mathbf{c}_{uv}$.
    *   Evaluate the depth of the triangle along axis $A$ at $\mathbf{c}_{uv}$, denoted $d_{tri}$.
    *   Determine the voxel center depth $d_{vox}$ for the voxel column at $(u, v)$.
    *   Output the voxel whose center depth is closest to $d_{tri}$ [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Edge Test (Horizontal):** For pixels $(u, v)$ that contain an edge of the triangle but do not contain the triangle's center (i.e., the center test did not trigger or was insufficient):
    *   Define a horizontal line bisecting the pixel (constant $v$ coordinate, varying $u$).
    *   Find the intersection extents of the triangle with this line, $[u_{min}, u_{max}]$.
    *   Clamp these extents to the horizontal pixel boundaries $[u_{pixel\_min}, u_{pixel\_max}]$.
    *   Evaluate the triangle depth $d_{tri}$ at the two clamped points $(u_{min}, v)$ and $(u_{max}, v)$.
    *   Let $d_{vox}$ be the center depth of the voxel at $(u, v)$.
    *   If $d_{tri}(u_{min}, v)$ and $d_{tri}(u_{max}, v)$ lie on different sides of $d_{vox}$ (i.e., $(d_{tri}(u_{min}, v) - d_{vox}) \times (d_{tri}(u_{max}, v) - d_{vox}) < 0$), output the voxel [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Edge Test (Vertical):** If the horizontal test fails (values are not on different sides):
    *   Define a vertical line bisecting the pixel (constant $u$ coordinate, varying $v$).
    *   Find the intersection extents of the triangle with this line, $[v_{min}, v_{max}]$.
    *   Clamp these extents to the vertical pixel boundaries.
    *   Evaluate the triangle depth $d_{tri}$ at the two clamped points.
    *   If the evaluated depths lie on different sides of the voxel's center depth $d_{vox}$, output the voxel [S1, unofficial].

*Note: The pseudocode above is a structural representation. The exact implementation of "voxel whose center depth is closest" and the relative depth comparison requires mapping the 2D pixel to the 1D depth range of the voxel column. The source [S1, unofficial] describes the geometric logic: the first test corresponds to intersecting the triangle against the crosshair segment parallel to the projection axis, and the latter tests correspond to testing the triangle against the two crosshair segments perpendicular to the projection axis.*

**Parameters:**
*   Dominant axis selection: Based on max absolute component of the geometric normal.
*   Clamping: To pixel boundaries.

**Edge Cases:**
*   **Degenerate Triangles:** Triangles with zero area or normal magnitude near zero. The dominant axis selection may be unstable. [S2, unofficial] suggests handling degeneracy by rasterizing along a different axis or using specific geometric tests.
*   **Parallel Edges:** If an edge is parallel to the bisecting line, the intersection extents may be undefined or infinite. Clamping handles this.

## Subtopics

### Crosshairs Target Equivalence
This method is equivalent to the crosshairs target approach but optimized for single-axis projection. It ensures 6-separating voxelization. [S1, unofficial]

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P)$ where $N$ is the number of triangles and $P$ is the average number of pixels covered by the projection. Significantly faster than multi-view methods as it avoids $K$ projection passes.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(V_x \cdot V_y \cdot V_z)$ for the voxel grid.
*   **Strengths:** Reduced computational cost due to single projection; maintains topological correctness (6-separating).
*   **Limitations:** More complex per-pixel logic for edge cases compared to simple center-based voxelization.
*   **When to use:** When computational resources are limited and a single dominant orientation exists or per-triangle axis selection is feasible.

## Background
Originated from the work on topological voxelization, specifically leveraging the thinness property of voxel grids. The method is described in [S1, unofficial]. It addresses the need for tunnel-free voxelization with reduced overhead compared to multi-axis approaches.
