# Smoke Column with Helical Displacement

Also called: Helical Smoke Column, Rising Smoke with Turbulence

## Overview
Creates a single column of rising smoke by deforming a vertical cylinder, simulating dispersion, turbulence, and swirling motion [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from static procedural volumes by using a vertical helix to displace points before calculating distance from the center, allowing for dynamic bending and swirling as the smoke rises [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Point position $P = (x, y, z)$ in world or object space.
- Cylinder axis (typically the $z$-axis).
- Cylinder radius $R$.
- Height of the column $H_{max}$.
- Time parameter $t$ (for animation).
- Turbulence intensity parameter $T_{base}$.
- Density falloff parameter $D_{decay}$.

**Outputs:**
- Smoke density value $\rho \in [0, 1]$.
- Optional: Turbulence displacement vector.

**Data Structures:**
- No complex data structures required; this is a point-wise procedural function.
- Intermediate values: radial distance $r$, height $z$, helical angle $\theta$.

**Steps:**
1. **Calculate Radial Distance:** Compute the distance of point $P$ from the cylinder axis (z-axis).
   $$ r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} $$
2. **Apply Helical Displacement:** To simulate bending and swirling, displace the point $(x, y)$ using a vertical helix based on height $z$ and time $t$. The helix angle $\theta$ increases with height and time.
   $$ \theta = k_h \cdot z + k_t \cdot t $$
   where $k_h$ is the helix twist rate and $k_t$ is the rotation speed.
   The displaced coordinates $(x', y')$ are:
   $$ x' = x \cos(\theta) - y \sin(\theta) $$
   $$ y' = x \sin(\theta) + y \cos(\theta) $$
   [G] Note: The source describes displacing the point *before* calculating distance. This implies the radial distance should be calculated from the displaced position relative to the axis, or the axis itself is rotated. A common implementation is to rotate the point around the z-axis by $\theta$ and then compute the distance from the axis.
   $$ r_{disp} = \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2} $$
   However, rotation preserves distance from the axis. The source states "using a vertical helix to displace each point before calculating its distance from the center". This suggests the helix might involve a radial component or the "distance" is measured in a transformed space. A more literal interpretation of "helical displacement" in smoke often involves adding a tangential velocity component to the position. Let $v_{helix}$ be the helical displacement vector.
   $$ x_{final} = x + \Delta x_{helix} $$
   $$ y_{final} = y + \Delta y_{helix} $$
   $$ r_{final} = \sqrt{x_{final}^2 + y_{final}^2} $$
   The source cites [4] for a detailed description. Without [4], we assume the standard helical rotation for swirling. If the helix implies a spiral path, the displacement might be:
   $$ \Delta x = A \cos(\theta) $$
   $$ \Delta y = A \sin(\theta) $$
   where $A$ is an amplitude. The source says "displace each point... to simulate bending and swirling". Bending suggests the axis itself curves. A simple helix rotation simulates swirling. Bending is often achieved by adding a lateral offset proportional to height.
   Let's stick to the core mechanism described: "vertical helix to displace each point".
   $$ x_{disp} = x + R_{swirl} \cos(\theta) $$
   $$ y_{disp} = y + R_{swirl} \sin(\theta) $$
   $$ r_{disp} = \sqrt{x_{disp}^2 + y_{disp}^2} $$
3. **Calculate Base Density:** Determine if the point is inside the cylinder.
   $$ \rho_{base} = \max(0, 1 - \frac{r_{disp}}{R}) $$
4. **Apply Height-Dependent Turbulence:** Increase turbulence as a function of height $z$.
   $$ T(z) = T_{base} \cdot (1 + \alpha \cdot \frac{z}{H_{max}}) $$
   where $\alpha$ controls the rate of turbulence increase.
   [G] The source mentions "Turbulence can be added to the cylinder’s center". This might imply adding a noise function scaled by $T(z)$ to the radial distance or position.
   $$ r_{turbulent} = r_{disp} + \text{Noise}(P, \text{scale}=T(z)) \cdot \text{amplitude} $$
   $$ \rho_{turb} = \max(0, 1 - \frac{r_{turbulent}}{R}) $$
5. **Apply Height-Dependent Density Falloff:** Decrease density as a function of height to simulate dispersion.
   $$ \rho_{final} = \rho_{turb} \cdot \max(0, 1 - \frac{z}{H_{max}})^{D_{decay}} $$
   [G] The source says "decrease the density as a function of height". A simple linear or exponential falloff is typical.

**Parameters:**
- $R$: Cylinder radius. Typical: 1.0 unit.
- $H_{max}$: Column height. Typical: 10.0 units.
- $k_h$: Helix twist rate. Typical: 0.5 to 2.0.
- $k_t$: Rotation speed. Typical: 0.1 to 1.0.
- $T_{base}$: Base turbulence. Typical: 0.1.
- $\alpha$: Turbulence growth rate. Typical: 1.0 to 5.0.
- $D_{decay}$: Density falloff exponent. Typical: 1.0 to 3.0.

**Edge Cases:**
- $z < 0$ or $z > H_{max}$: Density should be 0.
- $r_{disp} > R$: Density is 0.
- Precision: Near $r=0$, noise functions should be stable.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per sample, as it involves basic arithmetic and trigonometric functions.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$, no data structures stored.
- **Strengths:** Simple to implement, computationally cheap, produces visually plausible rising smoke with swirling motion.
- **Limitations:** Lacks complex fluid dynamics (e.g., buoyancy-driven instabilities, interaction with other objects). The turbulence is procedural and not physically simulated.
- **When to use:** Background smoke, simple atmospheric effects, real-time rendering where performance is critical.
- **When not to use:** High-fidelity scientific visualization requiring accurate fluid dynamics, or scenes with complex smoke interactions.

## Background
Originated in procedural modeling of gases and fluids, specifically in the work of David Ebert. The method is described in "Procedural Modeling, Animation, and Rendering of Gases, Fluids, and Textures" [S1, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of animating solid spaces by changing the space over time or moving the point through the space. The specific technique of using a helix for displacement is cited from reference [4] in the source, which is not provided here. The approach is similar to steam rising procedures but adapted for smoke with added turbulence and swirling.
