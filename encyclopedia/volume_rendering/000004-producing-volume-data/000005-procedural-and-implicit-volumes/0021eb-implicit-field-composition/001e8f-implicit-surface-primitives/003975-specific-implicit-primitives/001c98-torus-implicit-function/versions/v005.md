# Torus Implicit Function

Also called: Torus SDF, Signed Distance Function (SDF) for Torus, Signed Distance Field (SDF) Torus Primitive, Torus distance, Torus Scalar Field, sdTorus

## Overview
The Torus Implicit Function defines a torus as the zero-level set of a scalar field, typically providing the exact signed Euclidean distance from a query point to the surface of a torus centered at the origin. It is distinguished from approximate quadric representations by its analytical closed-form solution, which avoids the gradient singularities and approximation errors inherent in first-order Taylor expansions of implicit quadrics [S2, S6, unofficial][S7].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
- **Input**: A query point $\vec{p} = (p_x, p_y, p_z) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and torus parameters.
- **Output**: A scalar value $d \in \mathbb{R}$ representing the signed distance. $d < 0$ indicates the point is inside the torus, $d > 0$ indicates outside, and $d = 0$ lies on the surface.

### Data Structures
No persistent data structures are required for evaluation. The function is stateless and computes the value on-demand [Implicit Surface Primitives](1800-implicit-surface-primitives.md).

### Parameters
The torus is defined by two radii:
1.  **Major Radius ($R$)**: The distance from the center of the torus to the center of the tube.
2.  **Minor Radius ($r$)**: The radius of the tube itself.

*Note on Parameterization*: Some implementations use diameter and thickness instead of radii. If $t_x$ is the diameter and $t_y$ is the thickness, then $R = t_x / 2$ and $r = t_y / 2$ [S6, unofficial]. The sources generally assume the torus lies in the XZ plane (aligned with the Y-axis) or XY plane depending on the coordinate system used in the specific code snippet, but the geometric logic remains invariant under rotation. The standard analytical form assumes the torus is centered at the origin [S1, unofficial][S7].

### Formula
The exact signed distance $d$ is computed by projecting the point onto the plane of the torus's major ring and measuring the distance to the minor ring's center [S1, S6, unofficial][S7].

Let $\rho$ be the distance of the point's projection onto the plane of the major ring from the axis of revolution. For a torus aligned with the Y-axis (lying in the XZ plane):
$$ \rho = \sqrt{p_x^2 + p_z^2} $$

Define an intermediate 2D vector $\vec{q}$ in the plane containing the axis of revolution and the point's projection:
$$ q_x = \rho - R $$
$$ q_y = p_y $$

The signed distance is the Euclidean distance from $\vec{q}$ to the origin, minus the minor radius:
$$ d = \sqrt{q_x^2 + q_y^2} - r $$

Substituting the definitions:
$$ d = \sqrt{(\sqrt{p_x^2 + p_z^2} - R)^2 + p_y^2} - r $$

If the torus is aligned with the Z-axis (lying in the XY plane), swap $p_y$ and $p_z$ in the $\rho$ calculation or the final distance calculation accordingly [S5, S6, unofficial].

### Pseudocode
The following implementation computes the exact signed distance for a torus centered at the origin, aligned with the Y-axis (major ring in XZ plane).

```python
import math

def sd_torus(p_x, p_y, p_z, R, r):
    """
    Compute the signed distance from point p to a torus.
    
    Args:
        p_x, p_y, p_z: Coordinates of the query point.
        R: Major radius (distance from torus center to tube center).
        r: Minor radius (tube radius).
        
    Returns:
        Signed distance. Negative if inside, positive if outside.
    """
    # 1. Calculate distance from the Y-axis (projection onto XZ plane)
    rho = math.sqrt(p_x * p_x + p_z * p_z)
    
    # 2. Construct the 2D vector q in the radial plane
    # q_x is the distance from the major ring center line
    # q_y is the height along the axis of revolution
    q_x = rho - R
    q_y = p_y
    
    # 3. Calculate Euclidean distance to the tube center line
    dist_to_center_line = math.sqrt(q_x * q_x + q_y * q_y)
    
    # 4. Subtract minor radius to get signed distance to surface
    return dist_to_center_line - r
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
1.  **Singularity at Axis**: When $\rho = 0$ (the point lies on the axis of revolution), the term $\sqrt{p_x^2 + p_z^2}$ is zero. The formula remains numerically stable because it does not involve division by $\rho$. However, naive gradient implementations that divide by $\rho$ (e.g., $\frac{x}{\rho}$) will fail. The distance formula itself is safe [S4, S5, unofficial].
2.  **Approximation Failure**: First-order Taylor approximations of the implicit function (e.g., $f(\vec{x}) = (\sqrt{x^2+y^2}-R)^2 + z^2 - r^2$) require normalization by the gradient magnitude. If the gradient magnitude approaches zero (near the center of the torus hole or at specific symmetry points), the approximation becomes inaccurate or undefined [S2, unofficial]. The exact SDF formula avoids this division.
3.  **Coordinate System Alignment**: The formula depends on the axis of revolution. If the torus is rotated, the query point must be transformed into the torus's local coordinate system before evaluation [S5, S6, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: $O(1)$. The evaluation involves a constant number of arithmetic operations, square roots, and absolute values.
- **Memory Complexity**: $O(1)$. No storage is required beyond the parameters $R$ and $r$.
- **Strengths**: Provides the exact Euclidean distance, which is crucial for stable sphere tracing and accurate normal estimation via the gradient of the SDF. It is robust and does not suffer from the approximation errors of quadric implicit forms [S6, unofficial][S7].
- **Limitations**: Requires a square root operation, which is more expensive than simple polynomial evaluations, though modern hardware handles this efficiently. It is only applicable to perfect tori; deformed tori require different methods.

## Background
The signed distance function for a torus is a standard primitive in Signed Distance Field (SDF) rendering and ray marching. It was popularized in the computer graphics community by Inigo Quilez, who provided efficient implementations and geometric derivations for common primitives [S6, unofficial]. The geometric derivation relies on the torus being the product of two circles, allowing the distance to be decomposed into radial and axial components [S7]. It is widely used in real-time rendering, procedural modeling, and implicit surface reconstruction [S1, S3, S4, unofficial].
