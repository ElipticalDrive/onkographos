# Sabella's Density Emitter

Also called: Sabella's Method

## Overview
Sabella's Density Emitter is a volume rendering technique that models the density of particles as a continuous function rather than discrete entities, allowing for varying light intensity along a ray [S1, unofficial]. It differs from Blinn’s method [Bli82] by permitting variable density contributions, whereas Blinn’s approach uses a fixed intensity model [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A parameterized density field $\rho(x, y, z)$, ray start $t_1$, ray end $t_2$, and attenuation parameters.
*   **Output:** The calculated brightness (intensity) reaching the eye.

**Data Structures:**
Not given in the sources.

**Parameters:**
*   $\rho(t)$: Short-hand for the parameterized density field $\rho(x(t), y(t), z(t))$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $t_1, t_2$: Start and end voxel positions on the path of the ray [S1, unofficial].
*   $M$: Maximum (peak) value encountered along the ray [S1, unofficial].
*   $D$: Distance at which the peak value $M$ is encountered [S1, unofficial].
*   $C$: Center of gravity [S1, unofficial].
*   $\beta$: Parameter value to adjust the rate of attenuation [S1, unofficial].
*   $R$: A constant related to the energy emission, described as emitting energy of $C\rho$ [S1, unofficial].

**Formula:**
The brightness is calculated by integrating along the ray path:

$$
\text{Brightness} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \frac{R}{\rho(t)} e^{-\int_{t_1}^{t} \rho(\tau) d\tau} dt
$$

Where:
*   The term $e^{-\int_{t_1}^{t} \rho(\tau) d\tau}$ represents the attenuation along the ray due to the density field [S1, unofficial].
*   The term $\frac{R}{\rho(t)}$ relates to the emission contribution, where the density $\rho$ emits energy [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1.  Define the ray path from $t_1$ to $t_2$ through the volume.
2.  Evaluate the density field $\rho(t)$ at points along the ray.
3.  Compute the optical depth (attenuation) from the ray start $t_1$ to the current point $t$: $\tau(t) = \int_{t_1}^{t} \rho(\tau) d\tau$.
4.  Calculate the integrand $\frac{R}{\rho(t)} e^{-\tau(t)}$.
5.  Integrate the result from $t_1$ to $t_2$ to obtain the final brightness.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Division by Zero:** The term $\frac{R}{\rho(t)}$ implies that if $\rho(t) = 0$, the contribution is undefined or infinite. The sources do not specify a guard for zero density. [G: In practice, a small epsilon or clamping is required to prevent division by zero when density is zero.]
*   **Precision:** The nested integral requires accurate numerical integration to avoid artifacts.

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
Not given in the sources.

**Measured Performance:**
Not given in the sources.

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Allows varying light intensity based on density, unlike Blinn’s fixed method [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** The method involves nested integrals, which can be computationally expensive. The division by density $\rho(t)$ poses numerical stability risks at zero density.

**When to Use:**
Use when a continuous density model is preferred over discrete particle models and variable light intensity is desired.

## Background
**Origin:**
The method was presented by Sabella in 1988 [S1, unofficial]. It is described as a variation on the general ray-casting theme.

**History:**
It is similar to Blinn’s method [Bli82] but extends it by allowing varying densities. The work is cited in an overview of rendering from volume data [S1, unofficial].
