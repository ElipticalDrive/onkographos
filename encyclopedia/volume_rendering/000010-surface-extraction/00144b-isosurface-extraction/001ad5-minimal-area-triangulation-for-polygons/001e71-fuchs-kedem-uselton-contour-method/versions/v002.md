# Fuchs-Kedem-Uselton Contour Method
Also called: Minimum-cost cycle contour method
## Overview
The Fuchs-Kedem-Uselton Contour Method is an isosurface extraction technique that generates a minimum-area triangulated surface by connecting contours from adjacent volume slices. It solves the inter-slice triangulation problem by modeling the connection between two contours as a minimum-cost cycle in a directed-toroidal graph, ensuring the resulting surface area is locally minimized [S1, unofficial]. Unlike heuristic methods that prioritize speed over optimality, this method guarantees the minimal area solution for the triangulation between two given contours [S1, unofficial].
## How it works
The method operates on a volume dataset to produce a triangle mesh representing an isosurface.

**Inputs:**
*   A 3D volume dataset.
*   An isovalue threshold.
*   Two adjacent parallel slices (e.g., $z=k$ and $z=k+1$) containing intra-slice contours.

**Outputs:**
*   A set of triangles connecting the vertices of the two contours.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Contour:** A list of vertices $(x, y, z)$ ordered along the contour path. Let $C_1$ be the contour in slice $k$ with $m$ points, and $C_2$ be the contour in slice $k+1$ with $n$ points.
*   **Directed-Toroidal Graph:** A graph $G=(V, E)$ where vertices $V$ correspond to the points in $C_1$ and $C_2$. Edges represent potential triangle connections. The "toroidal" aspect implies that the contours are treated as cyclic sequences, allowing connections between the first and last points.

**Steps:**
1.  **Preprocess:** Generate intra-slice contours within single slices of the data using a standard marching squares or similar 2D contouring algorithm [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Construct Graph:** For two adjacent contours $C_1$ (size $m$) and $C_2$ (size $n$), construct a directed graph where edges represent potential triangles formed by vertices from $C_1$ and $C_2$. The cost of an edge or cycle segment is typically proportional to the area of the resulting triangle or the geometric distance between vertices.
3.  **Solve Minimum-Cost Cycle:** Find the minimum-cost cycle in the directed-toroidal graph. This cycle defines the optimal pairing of vertices between $C_1$ and $C_2$ to form triangles such that the total surface area is minimized [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Generate Triangles:** Form the inter-slice triangles based on the edges selected in the minimum-cost cycle.
5.  **Render:** Pass the generated triangles to a conventional polygon rendering engine to create the image [S1, unofficial].

**Formula:**
The computational complexity (cost) $\tau(m, n)$ to find the optimal solution for contours with $m$ and $n$ points is bounded by:
$$ \tau(m, n) < (\lceil \log_2 m \rceil + 2) \times (2mn + m) \quad \text{for} \quad (m < n) $$
where $m$ and $n$ are the number of points in the two contours [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   $m, n$: Number of vertices in the two adjacent contours.
*   Typical values: Not specified in sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Complexity:** The cost bound grows with $mn$, which can be significant for high-resolution contours.
*   **Torus Topology:** The method assumes a toroidal connectivity, which must be correctly implemented to handle the cyclic nature of contours.
*   **Non-convexity:** The method handles non-convex contours by finding the global minimum area cycle, avoiding self-intersections that might arise from simpler greedy approaches.

## Subtopics
## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The cost to find the optimal solution for two contours with $m$ and $n$ points is bounded by $\tau(m, n) < (\lceil \log_2 m \rceil + 2) \times (2mn + m)$ for $m < n$ [S1, unofficial]. This is significantly higher than heuristic methods which may require only $m + n$ steps [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** Generates the minimum-area surface, reducing visual artifacts associated with non-optimal triangulations [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Higher computational cost compared to heuristic methods like Ganapathy and Dennehy's approach [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When visual quality and minimal surface area are prioritized over computational speed.
*   **When not to use:** When real-time performance is critical and approximate solutions are acceptable.
## Background
The method was introduced by Fuchs, Kedem, and Uselton in 1977 [S1, unofficial]. It was the first widely used coercion method for isosurface extraction that addressed the inter-slice triangulation problem by minimizing surface area [S1, unofficial]. It laid the groundwork for subsequent heuristic methods that sought to reduce computational cost [S1, unofficial].
