# Interpolant Induced by Marching Cases

Also called: Continuous Marching Cases Interpolant, Contour Spectrum-based Interpolant

## Overview
This method constructs piecewise-polynomial (or non-polynomial) splines that correspond exactly to the contours generated by standard Marching Squares/Cubes cases, enabling the computation of geometric properties (area, volume) as continuous functions of the isovalue [S1, book]. Unlike standard Marching Cubes, which approximates the trilinear interpolant and produces discrete triangle meshes, this method defines an interpolant whose contours are identical to the discrete Marching Cases, allowing for the analysis of the "Contour Spectrum" where geometric measures are tracked across a range of isovalues [S1, book]. It sets itself apart by treating the sequence of Marching Cases as a finite state machine, ensuring that the resulting surface is continuous with respect to the isovalue sweep, rather than being a static approximation of a fixed field interpolant [S1, book].

## How it works
The method constructs an interpolant by analyzing the sequence of Marching Cases encountered as the isovalue sweeps through the data [S1, book]. It relies on the assumption that vertex isovalues are distinct ($h_1 < h_2 < h_3$ for simplicial cases) and uses linear interpolation along cell edges to define contour fragments for each case [S1, book].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** A structured grid of scalar values (isovalues) at vertices. For simplicial meshes, distinct vertex isovalues are assumed [S1, book]. For multi-linear meshes (cubes), the method acknowledges inherent non-linearity [S1, book].
*   **Output:** A continuous surface representation defined by the union of contour fragments swept out by the Marching Cases, along with associated geometric properties (area, length) as functions of the isovalue [S1, book].

### Data Structures
The core data structure is a **Finite State Machine (FSM)** representing all possible sequences of Marching Cases [S1, book].
*   **States:** Individual Marching Cases (e.g., Case 0–15 for Marching Cubes) [S1, book].
*   **Transitions:** Occur as vertices change from "white" (above isovalue) to "black" (below isovalue) during an isovalue sweep [S1, book].
*   **Paths:** Distinct paths through the FSM, identified up to rotational symmetry. For a 2D cell (Marching Squares), there are three distinct paths (red, blue, green) representing the sequence of cases as vertices are swept past [S1, book].

### Steps
1.  **Identify Vertex Order:** Determine the order in which vertices are swept past as the isovalue decreases from high to low [S1, book].
2.  **Construct FSM:** Map all possible sequences of Marching Cases based on the vertex sweep order [S1, book].
3.  **Define Contour Fragments:** For each case in a path, define the contour fragment using linear interpolation along the cell edges [S1, book].
4.  **Sweep Surface:** As the isovalue changes, the contour fragment moves, sweeping out a continuous region (surface) within the cell over the range of isovalues for that case [S1, book].
5.  **Compute Geometric Properties:** Calculate area and length for the swept surfaces [S1, book]. For simplicial meshes, this involves summing polynomial terms [S1, book]. For multi-linear meshes (bilinear/trilinear), this involves logarithmic terms and numerical integration [S1, book].

### Formulas
For a bilinear cell, the contour is a hyperbola [S1, book]. The area $B$ under the hyperbolic contour is calculated using an integral involving logarithms [S1, book]:

$$
B = \int_a^b \left( \frac{k}{x - x_v} + y_h \right) dx = k \ln |b - x_v| - k \ln |a - x_v| + (b - a)y_h
$$

Where:
*   $x_v$ is the x-coordinate of the vertical asymptote [S1, book].
*   $y_h$ is the y-coordinate of the horizontal asymptote [S1, book].
*   $k$ is a scaling constant [S1, book].
*   $a, b$ are the bounds of integration [S1, book].

The arclength $L$ of a bilinear contour is defined by an integral with no simple closed-form solution [S1, book]:

$$
L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + (f'(x))^2} dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{x^2}{a}\right)^2} dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{4x^4}} dx
$$

### Parameters
*   **Vertex Isovalues:** Must be distinct ($h_1 < h_2 < h_3$) for the simplicial case analysis [S1, book].
*   **Interpolation:** Linear interpolation is used for edge points in the construction of the Marching Cases [S1, book].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Non-Simplicial Meshes:** Geometric properties for non-simplicial meshes (like cubes) are inherently non-linear and cannot be summed as in the Contour Spectrum for simplicial meshes [S1, book].
*   **Logarithmic Precision:** The appearance of logarithmic terms in area calculations for bilinear/trilinear meshes requires managing numerical precision for large numbers of multiplications if using the identity $\log(a) + \log(b) = \log(ab)$ [S1, book].
*   **Integration Bounds:** Computing the bounds of integration $a, b$ for bilinear and trilinear interpolants requires accurate analysis of the MC cases, which can be complex [S1, book].
*   **Surface Intersection:** The method assumes that surfaces corresponding to different cases in a path do not intersect, though this is guaranteed only for certain cases (e.g., the "Red Path" in ambiguous faces) [S1, book].

### Pseudocode
```python
import math

def compute_contour_spectrum(grid, isovalues):
    """
    Computes geometric properties (area, length) as functions of isovalue
    using the Interpolant Induced by Marching Cases method.
    """
    results = {}
    for cell in grid.cells:
        # 1. Identify Vertex Order: sort vertices by isovalue descending
        vertex_vals = [cell.vertices[i].value for i in range(4)]
        sorted_indices = sorted(range(4), key=lambda i: vertex_vals[i], reverse=True)
        
        # 2. Construct FSM: determine case sequence from sweep order
        # The path is fixed by the combinatorial order of vertex crossings.
        path = [0, 4, 6, 14, 15]  # Example path for a specific vertex order
        
        for iso in isovalues:
            # Find active case for current isovalue
            thresholds = sorted(vertex_vals)
            active_case = None
            for i, case_idx in enumerate(path):
                if i == 0:
                    if iso > thresholds[-1]:
                        active_case = case_idx
                        break
                elif i == len(path) - 1:
                    if iso < thresholds[0]:
                        active_case = case_idx
                        break
                else:
                    if thresholds[i] >= iso > thresholds[i-1]:
                        active_case = case_idx
                        break
            
            if active_case is None:
                continue
            
            # 3. Define Contour Fragments: linear interpolation on edges
            contour_points = []
            for edge in cell.edges:
                v0, v1 = edge.vertices
                if (v0.value - iso) * (v1.value - iso) < 0:
                    t = (iso - v0.value) / (v1.value - v0.value)
                    contour_points.append(v0.lerp(v1, t))
            
            # 4 & 5. Sweep Surface & Compute Properties
            area = 0.0
            length = 0.0
            if cell.is_simplicial():
                # Polynomial summation for simplicial meshes
                area = sum(p.area for p in contour_points)
                length = sum(p.length for p in contour_points)
            else:
                # Logarithmic calculation for bilinear cells
                # Sources do not specify exact asymptote extraction or bounds calculation.
                xv, yh, k = 0.0, 0.0, 1.0
                a, b = 0.0, 1.0
                area = k * math.log(abs(b - xv)) - k * math.log(abs(a - xv)) + (b - a) * yh
                
                # Arclength requires numerical integration as no closed form exists
                # Sources do not provide a specific quadrature routine.
                length = 1.0
            results[iso] = results.get(iso, {'area': 0.0, 'length': 0.0})
            results[iso]['area'] += area
            results[iso]['length'] += length
    return results
```

## Subtopics

### Contour Spectrum
A method for computing geometric properties over a range of isovalues for simplicial meshes, relying on the summation of polynomial terms [S1, book].

### Marching Squares/Cases FSM
The finite state machine representation of Marching Cases sequences, used to identify distinct paths up to symmetry [S1, book].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Tracking geometric properties via summation of polynomial terms is O(1) size for simplicial meshes [S1, book]. For bilinear/trilinear meshes, the complexity increases due to the need for numerical integration and managing logarithmic terms [S1, book].
*   **Memory Complexity:** O(1) for simplicial meshes when summing polynomial terms [S1, book]. For multi-linear meshes, memory usage may increase due to the need to store logarithmic terms and manage numerical precision [S1, book].
*   **Strengths:** Provides a continuous representation of geometric properties as a function of isovalue. Ensures that the interpolant's contours are identical to the discrete Marching Cases, avoiding the approximation errors of standard Marching Cubes with trilinear interpolation [S1, book].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive for multi-linear meshes due to logarithmic terms and numerical integration. Requires accurate analysis of MC cases to determine integration bounds [S1, book]. Not suitable for real-time applications where performance is critical [S1, book].
*   **When to Use:** When accurate geometric measurements (area, volume) over a range of isovalues are required, and the data is represented on simplicial meshes or when the overhead of multi-linear integration is acceptable [S1, book].
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time visualization or when working with large multi-linear meshes where the computational cost of logarithmic integration is prohibitive [S1, book].

## Background
The method was introduced by H. Carr and E. Murphy in 2011 in the paper "Interpolants Induced by Marching Cases" [S1, book]. It builds upon the concept of the Contour Spectrum, which was previously used for simplicial meshes [S1, book]. The work addresses the known issue that standard Marching Cubes cases do not correspond exactly to the trilinear interpolant, and proposes a method to construct an interpolant whose contours are identical to the discrete Marching Cases [S1, book]. The method has been applied in scientific and medical visualization for the analysis of significant features in data [S1, book].
