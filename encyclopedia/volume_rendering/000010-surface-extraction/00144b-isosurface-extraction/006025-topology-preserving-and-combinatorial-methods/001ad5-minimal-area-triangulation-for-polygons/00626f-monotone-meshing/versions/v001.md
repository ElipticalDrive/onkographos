# Monotone Meshing

Also called: Monotone Triangulation, Polygon Triangulation via Monotone Decomposition

## Overview
Monotone meshing is a surface extraction method that treats voxel boundary generation as a standard computational geometry problem: polygon triangulation. It decomposes the complex, potentially non-simple polygons formed by voxel contours into monotone sub-polygons and then triangulates them. It differs from its sibling [Merging Triangulations](3936-merging-triangulations.md) and [Fuchs-Kedem-Uselton Contour Method](1780-fuchs-kedem-uselton-contour-method.md) by explicitly leveraging sweep-line monotone decomposition to handle non-simple polygons (polygons with holes or self-intersections) that arise in voxel marching, whereas simpler methods often assume simple convexity or use different decomposition strategies. It builds on [Minimal Area Triangulation for Polygons](859-minimal-area-triangulation-for-polygons.md) by providing the triangulation step for the decomposed polygons.

## How it works
**Inputs:** A 3D scalar volume $V$ and an isovalue $I$.
**Outputs:** A set of triangles representing the isosurface.

**Data Structures:**
- `frontier`: A list of active polygon objects. Each polygon $p$ has fields: `bottom_right` (x-coordinate), `type` (boolean, indicating orientation/phase), and `vertices` (list of points).
- `runs`: A list of run-length encoded segments on the current scan line. Each run $r$ has fields: `start_x`, `end_x`, `type` (boolean).

**Algorithm Steps:**
The method processes the volume slice-by-slice (or layer-by-layer). For each 2D slice containing the isosurface intersection:

1.  **Scan Line Processing:** Iterate through each horizontal scan line $y$ in the slice.
2.  **Run-Length Encoding:** Identify contiguous segments of voxels where the scalar value crosses the isovalue $I$. Encode these as `runs`. A run $r$ is defined by its x-interval $[start_x, end_x]$ and a `type` (e.g., entering or exiting the surface region).
3.  **Monotone Decomposition via Sweep Line:** Maintain a `frontier` of open polygons. Initialize `frontier` as empty.
    - Let $pf$ be a pointer to the current polygon in `frontier`.
    - Let $pr$ be a pointer to the current run in `runs`.
    - While $pf$ and $pr$ are not at the end of their respective lists:
        - Let $r = runs[pr]$ and $p = frontier[pf]$.
        - **Case 1: Merge.** If $r$ overlaps with the bottom of $p$ and $r.type == p.type$:
            - Merge $r$ into $p$ (extend $p$'s geometry).
            - Increment $pr$ and $pf$.
            - Continue to next iteration.
        - **Case 2: Close Polygon.** If the end of $r$ is past the bottom-right corner of $p$ (i.e., $r.end_x > p.bottom\_right$):
            - Close off polygon $p$ (finalize its geometry).
            - Remove $p$ from `frontier`.
            - Increment $pf$.
        - **Case 3: New Polygon.** If the bottom end of $p$ is past the end of $r$ (i.e., $p.bottom\_right > r.end_x$):
            - Turn $r$ into a new monotone polygon.
            - Add this new polygon to `frontier`.
            - Increment $pr$.
    - **Finalization:** Close off all remaining polygons on the `frontier` list.
4.  **Triangulation:** For each resulting monotone polygon, apply a linear-time triangulation algorithm (e.g., using a stack-based approach for monotone polygons) to generate triangles.
5.  **Assembly:** Collect all triangles from all slices to form the final mesh.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Non-simple Polygons:** The method is designed to handle non-simple polygons (with holes), which are common in voxel marching. Standard triangulation algorithms may fail on these without decomposition.
- **Precision:** Floating-point comparisons for `bottom_right` and `end_x` must be robust to avoid incorrect merging or closing of polygons.
- **Performance:** The implementation in the source was in JavaScript, which may introduce overhead and subtle bugs (e.g., silent out-of-bounds accesses) that affect performance benchmarks [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
**Time Complexity:** The monotone decomposition step is generally $O(n \log n)$ for general polygons, but for voxel marching, the sweep-line adaptation can be more efficient, potentially approaching $O(n)$ per slice where $n$ is the number of voxels or vertices. Triangulation of monotone polygons is $O(n)$.
**Memory Complexity:** Linear in the number of vertices in the slice.
**Measured Performance:** In a benchmark on a $65 \times 65 \times 65$ volume with frequency 10, Monotone meshing took 297.60ms, producing 209,838 vertices and 113,559 faces. Greedy meshing took 198.30ms, producing 213,452 vertices and 53,363 faces [S1, unofficial].
**Strengths:** Produces a triangulated mesh directly, which is often required for rendering. Handles complex, non-simple polygons robustly.
**Limitations:** Slower than greedy meshing in benchmarks due to higher algorithmic complexity and more work per triangle. Generates triangles instead of quads, which may be less efficient for some rendering pipelines.
**When to use:** When a triangulated mesh is required and the geometry is complex enough that simple convex decomposition fails.
**When not to use:** When performance is critical and quads are acceptable (use Greedy Meshing).

## Background
Monotone meshing was proposed as an efficient alternative to greedy meshing for Minecraft-type voxel meshing. It draws inspiration from computational geometry techniques, specifically the monotone decomposition algorithm by Fournier et al. The method was discussed in a blog post by 0fps in July 2012, which explored optimizing voxel meshing using standard polygon triangulation methods [S1, unofficial].
