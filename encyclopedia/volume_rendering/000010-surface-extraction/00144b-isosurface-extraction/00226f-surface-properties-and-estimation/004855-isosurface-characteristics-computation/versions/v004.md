# Isosurface Characteristics Computation

Also called: Integral of Gradient, Mean Gradient, Total Area, Confined Volume, Curvature, Pekar et al. Isosurface Characteristics

## Overview
Isosurface Characteristics Computation is a method for quantifying the geometric and topological properties of an extracted isosurface mesh, specifically calculating the integral of the gradient along the surface, mean gradient, total area, confined volume, and curvature [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Marching Cubes Surface Normal Estimation](1005-marching-cubes-surface-normal-estimation.md) and [Marching Cubes Surface Area Estimation](1315-marching-cubes-surface-area-estimation.md) by providing a unified framework for multiple global and local metrics rather than focusing on a single attribute, enabling downstream tasks such as transfer function specification and hierarchical segmentation [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
The method operates on a triangular mesh generated by an isosurface extraction algorithm (e.g., Marching Cubes) from a scalar field $V$. It computes five primary characteristics: total area, confined volume, integral of the gradient, mean gradient, and curvature [S1, unofficial].

**Inputs:**
- A triangular mesh $\mathcal{M}$ with vertices $\mathbf{v}_i$ and faces $\mathcal{F}$.
- The underlying scalar field $V(\mathbf{x})$ and its gradient $\nabla V(\mathbf{x})$ at arbitrary positions.
- The isovalue $\alpha$ used for extraction.

**Outputs:**
- Total Area $A$.
- Confined Volume $V_{conf}$.
- Integral of Gradient $I_{\nabla}$.
- Mean Gradient $\bar{g}$.
- Curvature $K$ (typically Gaussian or Mean curvature per vertex/face).

**Data Structures:**
- Mesh representation: List of vertices $\mathbf{v}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, list of faces $f_j = (i_1, i_2, i_3)$, and optionally precomputed face normals $\mathbf{n}_j$ and areas $a_j$.
- Scalar field: Grid-based storage for $V$ and $\nabla V$.

**Steps:**

1. **Compute Total Area ($A$):**
   Sum the areas of all triangles in the mesh. For a triangle with vertices $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$:
   $$ \mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_1, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{v}_3 - \mathbf{v}_1 $$
   $$ a_j = \frac{1}{2} \| \mathbf{e}_1 \times \mathbf{e}_2 \| $$
   $$ A = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} a_j $$

2. **Compute Confined Volume ($V_{conf}$):**
   Calculate the volume enclosed by the closed manifold mesh. Using the divergence theorem or tetrahedron decomposition from the origin (or centroid):
   $$ V_{conf} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{1}{6} (\mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_2 \times \mathbf{v}_3 + \mathbf{v}_3 \times \mathbf{v}_1) \cdot \mathbf{n}_{face} $$
   Where $\mathbf{n}_{face}$ is the unit normal of the face. [G] Note: Sign depends on winding order; absolute value is taken for total volume.

3. **Compute Integral of Gradient ($I_{\nabla}$):**
   Integrate the magnitude of the scalar field gradient over the isosurface. This approximates the surface integral $\int_S \|\nabla V\| dS$.
   For each face $j$, evaluate $\|\nabla V\|$ at the face centroid $\mathbf{c}_j = \frac{1}{3}(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_3)$.
   $$ I_{\nabla} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} a_j \|\nabla V(\mathbf{c}_j)\| $$
   [G] If $\nabla V$ is not precomputed, it can be estimated from the scalar field grid using central differences.

4. **Compute Mean Gradient ($\bar{g}$):**
   The average gradient magnitude over the surface.
   $$ \bar{g} = \frac{I_{\nabla}}{A} $$

5. **Compute Curvature ($K$):**
   Estimate curvature for each vertex or face. For a vertex $\mathbf{v}_i$, collect neighboring faces.
   [G] A common discrete approach is to compute the mean curvature vector $\mathbf{H}$ and Gaussian curvature $K_G$ using the cotangent weight scheme or area-weighted normals.
   $$ \mathbf{H}_i = \frac{1}{2A_i} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} (\cot \alpha_j + \cot \beta_j) (\mathbf{v}_{k} - \mathbf{v}_{l}) $$
   $$ K_{G,i} = \frac{2 \pi - \sum \theta_{j}}{A_i} $$
   Where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ are adjacent faces, $\alpha, \beta$ are angles opposite to the edge, and $\theta_j$ are angles at vertex $i$ in face $j$. [G] The specific curvature metric (Mean vs Gaussian) should be specified by the application.

**Parameters:**
- Isovalue $\alpha$: Determines the surface geometry.
- Gradient estimation method: Central differences, finite elements, etc.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- Non-manifold meshes: Volume calculation fails. Ensure mesh is watertight.
- Boundary artifacts: If the isosurface is clipped by the domain boundary, volume and area are partial.
- Precision: Gradient evaluation at face centroids may be inaccurate if the scalar field is noisy. Smoothing the field before extraction is recommended [G].

## Subtopics

### Hierarchical Surface Segmentation
This variant segments the isosurface into four types based on the combination of mean curvature (convex/concave) and Gaussian curvature (elliptic/hyperbolic) [S2]. It computes the mean curvature operator to determine convexity/concavity and the Gaussian curvature sign operator to determine elliptic/hyperbolic behavior [S2]. Vertices are classified according to these Boolean combinations, allowing explicit localization of minima of sulci and maxima of gyri as seed points for topology graph generation [S2]. It is chosen when topological analysis of complex surfaces (e.g., cortical brain data) is required [S2].

### [Volumetric Divergence Metric](12575-volumetric-divergence-metric.md)
The Volumetric Divergence Metric (VDC) estimates the accuracy of isosurfaces by calculating the total volume between the produced mesh and the ideal trilinear interpolation isosurface. It sets itself apart from sibling metrics like the Tetrahedron Volumetric Divergence (TVD) Predictor by providing a highly accurate ground truth through recursive subdivision, whereas TVD uses a faster, closed-form geometric approximation.

### [Curvature-Based Anisotropy Estimation](16471-curvature-based-anisotropy-estimation.md)
This method estimates anisotropy for surface shading by computing the principal curvatures of an isosurface. It serves as a baseline comparison against covariance-based methods, relying on differential geometry properties where principal curvature directions form an orthonormal basis in the tangent plane. Unlike covariance-based approaches that evaluate feature extent in both spatial and data domains, this method focuses strictly on the local geometry of the isosurface.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of triangles in the mesh, assuming gradient evaluation is $O(1)$ per face. Curvature computation may be $O(N)$ or $O(N \log N)$ depending on neighbor search.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store mesh attributes and intermediate sums.
- **Measured Performance:** Pekar et al. describe the method as "quite efficient" [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Provides a comprehensive set of geometric metrics in a single pass; enables advanced segmentation and transfer function design.
- **Limitations:** Requires access to the underlying scalar field for gradient and volume calculations; sensitive to mesh quality and noise.
- **When to use:** When global surface statistics or topological features are needed for visualization or analysis.
- **When not to use:** When only local shading normals are needed (use [Marching Cubes Surface Normal Estimation](1005-marching-cubes-surface-normal-estimation.md)) or when computational resources are extremely limited and only area is needed.

## Background
The method was proposed by Pekar et al. in 2001 as an efficient approach for computing isosurface characteristics including the integral of the gradient, mean gradient, total area, confined volume, and curvature [S1, unofficial]. It builds on earlier strategies by Bajaj et al. [S1, unofficial]. The hierarchical segmentation variant based on discrete curvature was developed for cortical surface analysis, using mean and Gaussian curvature operators to segment surfaces and generate topology graphs [S2].
