# Discrete Multi-Material Interface Reconstruction

Also called: Potts-model Interface Reconstruction, Subcell Labeling for Material Interfaces, Discrete Volume Fraction Labeling, MIR (Material Interface Reconstruction) labeling, Volume Fraction Labeling via Potts Model Optimization

## Overview
Discrete Multi-Material Interface Reconstruction (MIR) is a method to reconstruct piecewise linear material interfaces from volume fraction data by discretizing cells into subcells, labeling them with material IDs, and optimizing the labeling to minimize interface energy while preserving volume fractions [S1, unofficial]. It improves upon the [Volume-Adaptive Active Interface Model](13766-volume-adaptive-active-interface-model.md) by producing interfaces that are continuous across cell boundaries and suitable for visualization, whereas the parent model and alternatives like Piecewise Linear Interface Calculation (PLIC) are discontinuous across cell boundaries [S1, unofficial]. Unlike PLIC, which is ambiguous for three or more materials due to the ordering of its binary segmentations, MIR explicitly handles $n$-material problems through a discrete labeling optimization [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- A grid of cells $C$.
- For each cell $c \in C$, a tuple of volume fractions $V_c = (v_1, \dots, v_n)$, where $v_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n v_i = 1$. Pure cells have one $v_i=1$ and others $0$; mixed cells have multiple non-zero $v_i$.
- Number of materials $n$.
- Subdivision rate $R$ (defining subcells per cell $S = R^d$ for dimension $d$).
- Annealing temperature $T$.

**Outputs:**
- A labeling $f$ of the discretization $D$, where each subcell is assigned a material ID $f_x \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.
- Optionally, a smoothed field or surface mesh extracted from $f$.

**Data Structures:**
- **Discretization $D$**: A set of subcells. Only mixed cells are subdivided to save memory [S1, unofficial].
- **Subcell Layout**: Each subcell $x$ stores its label $f_x$. In 3D, with subdivision rate $R$, there are $S = R^3$ subcells per cell.
- **Volume Fraction Storage**: For each mixed cell, store $V_c$. Pure cells do not require subdivision or storage of subcell labels.

**Parameters:**
- $S$: Number of subcells per cell. $S = R^d$. Typical $R \in [5, 10]$ [S1, unofficial].
- $T$: Annealing temperature. Set to a low constant $0.25$ [S1, unofficial].
- $N$: Neighborhood. Directly incident subcells (8 neighbors in 2D, 26 in 3D) [S1, unofficial].
- $W_{x,y}$: Weighting function. Inverse magnitude of the offset between subcells $x$ and $y$ [S1, unofficial].

**Algorithm:**

1.  **Discretize**: For each mixed cell $c \in C$, divide it into $S$ subcells of uniform fractional volume $dA = 1/S$. Form the discretization $D$. [S1, unofficial]
2.  **Initialize Labeling**: For each mixed cell $c$, assign initial labels to its subcells such that for each material $i$, there are approximately $k_i = \lfloor v_i / dA \rfloor$ or $\lceil v_i / dA \rceil$ subcells with label $i$. The total number of subcells with label $i$ must sum to the integer count closest to $v_i/dA$ while preserving the total volume constraint within the cell. Specifically, ensure $\sum_{x \in c} \delta(f_x = i) \approx v_i / dA$. Randomly assign labels among subcells to satisfy these counts [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Optimize via Simulated Annealing**:
    -   Repeat until convergence or time limit:
        -   Select a mixed cell $c$ at random.
        -   Select two distinct subcells $x, y$ within $c$ at random.
        -   Calculate the energy change $\Delta E$ resulting from swapping labels $f_x$ and $f_y$.
        -   Accept the swap with probability $P$:
            $$
            P = \begin{cases} 
            1 & \text{if } \Delta E < 0 \\
            e^{-\Delta E / T} & \text{otherwise}
            \end{cases}
            $$
        -   If accepted, update $f_x \leftrightarrow f_y$.
4.  **Post-Processing (Optional)**: Apply a narrow Gaussian kernel to the labeling field $f$ to smooth interfaces for visualization. Extract surfaces using multi-material segmentation algorithms (e.g., multi-label Marching Cubes, Dual Contouring) on the smoothed field [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
-   **Subcell Volume**: $dA = 1/S$ [S1, unofficial].
-   **Local Potts-Model Energy**: The energy at subcell $x$ is:
    $$
    E_x(f) = \sum_{y \in N} W_{x,y} \cdot \delta(f_x \neq f_y)
    $$
    where $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta (1 if true, 0 if false), $N$ is the neighborhood of $x$, and $W_{x,y}$ is the weight [S1, unofficial].
-   **Total Energy**:
    $$
    E(f) = \sum_{x \in D} E_x(f)
    $$
    [S1, unofficial]
-   **Energy Change for Swap**: Let $f'$ be the labeling after swapping labels of $x$ and $y$. The energy change is:
    $$
    \Delta E = E_{x,y}(f') - E_{x,y}(f)
    $$
    where $E_{x,y}(f) = E_x(f) + E_y(f)$ is the initial energy of the pair [S1, unofficial]. Note: Only neighbors of $x$ and $y$ contribute to the change in $E_x$ and $E_y$.
-   **Volume Error Bound**: The upper bound on per-cell error $\epsilon(c)$ is:
    $$
    \epsilon(c) \le \begin{cases} 
    (n-1)dA & \text{if } c \text{ is mixed} \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
    $$
    [S1, unofficial]

def calculate_potential_energy(subcell, labeling, neighborhood, weights):
    """
    Calculates the local Potts-model energy for a single subcell.
    """
    energy = 0.0
    label_x = labeling[subcell]
    for neighbor in neighborhood[subcell]:
        label_y = labeling[neighbor]
        if label_x != label_y:
            energy += weights[(subcell, neighbor)]
    return energy

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
-   **Sharp Boundaries**: Extracting interfaces by identifying co-incident faces between adjacent subcells with different labels can result in unpleasant, sharp boundaries at the sub-cell scale [S1, unofficial]. Smoothing the labeling field with a narrow Gaussian kernel before extraction is recommended [S1, unofficial].
-   **Volume Conservation**: Graph cuts cannot be used because their optimization moves do not conserve volume [S1, unofficial]. The swap-based simulated annealing ensures volume conservation.
-   **Pure Cells**: Pure cells do not require subdivision or optimization, reducing memory usage significantly (by ~90% in sparse datasets) [S1, unofficial].
-   **Convergence**: Optimization for longer than 10 seconds per timestep often does not significantly improve results for complex cases, though brief optimization smooths interfaces [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time Complexity**: Optimization is not cheap. Simulated annealing convergence time depends on the subdivision rate $R$ and the complexity of the interface. Higher $R$ lowers error but increases convergence time [S1, unofficial]. In practice, 10 seconds per timestep is often sufficient [S1, unofficial].
-   **Memory Complexity**: Memory usage is proportional to the number of mixed cells and the subdivision rate $S$. By only subdividing mixed cells, memory can be reduced by approximately 90% in datasets where mixed cells constitute less than 6% of the total [S1, unofficial].
-   **Accuracy**: The upper bound on per-cell volume error is $\epsilon(c) \le (n-1)dA$ for mixed cells. For a 3D 2-material problem, the error is 0.8% with $R=5$ ($S=125$) and 0.1% with $R=10$ ($S=1000$) [S1, unofficial].
-   **Strengths**: Produces continuous interfaces across cell boundaries; suitable for visualization; handles $n$-materials; volume conservation with bounded error; easily parallelized [S1, unofficial].
-   **Limitations**: Computationally expensive compared to PLIC; sharp artifacts if not smoothed; requires careful tuning of $R$ and $T$ (though $T=0.25$ is robust) [S1, unofficial].
-   **When to Use**: When visualizing material regions or extracting smooth, continuous surface meshes from volume fraction data is required, especially for $n \ge 3$ materials where PLIC is ambiguous [S1, unofficial].
-   **When Not to Use**: When real-time performance is critical and PLIC's discontinuity is acceptable; when volume conservation must be exact (not bounded error) [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Discrete Multi-Material Interface Reconstruction was introduced as a reinterpretation of MIR as a segmentation problem over a discretization of the problem’s original spatial domain [S1, unofficial]. It builds on the Potts-model energy metric, widely used in interface problems from studying cellular structures to interpolating region boundaries between segmented images [S1, unofficial]. The method was developed to address the limitations of PLIC, which is discontinuous across cell boundaries and ambiguous for three or more materials [S1, unofficial]. It was tested on 2D and 3D fluid flow datasets, including bubble simulations and multi-fluid cylinder interactions [S1, unofficial].
