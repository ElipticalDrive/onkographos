# Discrete Multi-Material Interface Reconstruction

Also called: Potts-model Interface Reconstruction, Subcell Labeling for Material Interfaces, Discrete Volume Fraction Labeling, MIR (Material Interface Reconstruction) labeling, Volume Fraction Labeling via Potts Model Optimization

## Overview
Discrete Multi-Material Interface Reconstruction (MIR) reconstructs material interfaces from volume fraction data by discretizing cells into subcells, labeling them with material IDs, and optimizing the labeling to minimize interface energy while preserving volume fractions [S1, unofficial]. It produces interfaces continuous across cell boundaries suitable for visualization, unlike the discontinuous [Volume-Adaptive Active Interface Model](13766-volume-adaptive-active-interface-model.md) and Piecewise Linear Interface Calculation (PLIC) [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- A grid of cells $C$ [S1, unofficial].
- For each cell $c \in C$, a tuple of volume fractions $V_c = (v_1, \dots, v_n)$, where $v_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n v_i = 1$ [S1, unofficial]. Pure cells have one $v_i=1$ and others $0$; mixed cells have multiple non-zero $v_i$ [S1, unofficial].
- Number of materials $n$ [S1, unofficial].
- Subdivision rate $R$ (defining subcells per cell $S = R^d$ for dimension $d$) [S1, unofficial].
- Annealing temperature $T$ [S1, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
- A labeling $f$ of the discretization $D$, where each subcell is assigned a material ID $f_x \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ [S1, unofficial].
- Optionally, a smoothed field or surface mesh extracted from $f$ [S1, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
- **Discretization $D$**: A set of subcells formed by subdividing mixed cells [S1, unofficial].
- **Subcell Layout**: Each subcell $x$ stores its label $f_x$ [S1, unofficial]. In 3D, with subdivision rate $R$, there are $S = R^3$ subcells per cell [S1, unofficial].
- **Volume Fraction Storage**: For each mixed cell, store $V_c$ [S1, unofficial]. Pure cells do not require subdivision or storage of subcell labels [S1, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**
1. **Discretize**: For each mixed cell $c \in C$, divide it into $S$ subcells of uniform fractional volume $dA = 1/S$ to form the discretization $D$ [S1, unofficial].
2. **Initialize Labeling**: For each mixed cell $c$, assign initial labels to its subcells such that for each material $i$, there are approximately $v_i / dA$ subcells with label $i$ [S1, unofficial]. Randomly assign labels among subcells to satisfy these counts [S1, unofficial].
3. **Optimize via Simulated Annealing**: Repeat until convergence or time limit: Select a mixed cell $c$ at random [S1, unofficial]. Select two distinct subcells $x, y$ within $c$ at random [S1, unofficial]. Calculate the energy change $\Delta E$ resulting from swapping labels $f_x$ and $f_y$ [S1, unofficial]. Accept the swap with probability $P$: $P = 1$ if $\Delta E < 0$, and $P = e^{-\Delta E / T}$ otherwise [S1, unofficial]. If accepted, update $f_x \leftrightarrow f_y$ [S1, unofficial].
4. **Post-Processing (Optional)**: Apply a narrow Gaussian kernel to the labeling field $f$ to smooth interfaces for visualization [S1, unofficial]. Extract surfaces using multi-material segmentation algorithms (e.g., multi-label Marching Cubes, Dual Contouring) on the smoothed field [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
- **Subcell Volume**: $dA = 1/S$ [S1, unofficial].
- **Local Potts-Model Energy**: The energy at subcell $x$ is $E_x(f) = \sum_{y \in N} W_{x,y} \cdot \delta(f_x \neq f_y)$, where $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta (1 if true, 0 if false), $N$ is the neighborhood of $x$, and $W_{x,y}$ is the weight [S1, unofficial].
- **Total Energy**: $E(f) = \sum_{x \in D} E_x(f)$ [S1, unofficial].
- **Energy Change for Swap**: Let $f'$ be the labeling after swapping labels of $x$ and $y$ [S1, unofficial]. The energy change is $\Delta E = E_{x,y}(f') - E_{x,y}(f)$, where $E_{x,y}(f) = E_x(f) + E_y(f)$ is the initial energy of the pair [S1, unofficial].
- **Volume Error Bound**: The upper bound on per-cell error $\epsilon(c)$ is $\epsilon(c) \le (n-1)dA$ if $c$ is mixed, and $0$ otherwise [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
- $S$: Number of subcells per cell. $S = R^d$. Typical $R \in [5, 10]$ [S1, unofficial].
- $T$: Annealing temperature. Set to a low constant $0.25$ [S1, unofficial].
- $N$: Neighborhood. Directly incident subcells (8 neighbors in 2D, 26 in 3D) [S1, unofficial].
- $W_{x,y}$: Weighting function. Inverse magnitude of the offset between subcells $x$ and $y$ [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Sharp Boundaries**: Extracting interfaces by identifying co-incident faces between adjacent subcells with different labels can result in unpleasant, sharp boundaries at the sub-cell scale [S1, unofficial]. Smoothing the labeling field with a narrow Gaussian kernel before extraction is recommended [S1, unofficial].
- **Volume Conservation**: Graph cuts cannot be used because their optimization moves do not conserve volume [S1, unofficial]. The swap-based simulated annealing ensures volume conservation [S1, unofficial].
- **Pure Cells**: Pure cells do not require subdivision or optimization, reducing memory usage significantly (by ~90% in sparse datasets) [S1, unofficial].
- **Convergence**: Optimization for longer than 10 seconds per timestep often does not significantly improve results for complex cases, though brief optimization smooths interfaces [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import random
import math
import time

def calculate_potential_energy(subcell_id, labeling, cell_subcells, subcell_coords):
    """Calculates the local Potts-model energy for a single subcell."""
    energy = 0.0
    label_x = labeling[subcell_id]
    coord_x = subcell_coords[subcell_id]
    for neighbor_id in cell_subcells:
        if neighbor_id == subcell_id:
            continue
        coord_y = subcell_coords[neighbor_id]
        offset = [coord_x[i] - coord_y[i] for i in range(len(coord_x))]
        weight = 1.0 / math.sqrt(sum(o**2 for o in offset))
        if labeling[neighbor_id] != label_x:
            energy += weight
    return energy

def optimize_mir(cells, volume_fractions, R, T, max_time_seconds):
    """
    Optimizes the subcell labeling using simulated annealing.
    Args:
        cells: List of cell objects. Each has 'is_mixed' flag and 'volume_fractions' (list of n floats).
        R: Subdivision rate. S = R^d.
        T: Annealing temperature.
        max_time_seconds: Time limit for optimization.
    Returns:
        labeling: Dictionary mapping subcell_id -> material_id.
        subcell_coords: Dictionary mapping subcell_id -> coordinate tuple.
    """
    d = cells[0].dimension  # e.g., 2 or 3
    S = R ** d
    dA = 1.0 / S
    
    labeling = {}
    subcell_coords = {}
    cell_subcells_map = {}
    
    start_time = time.time()
    
    for cell in cells:
        if not cell.is_mixed:
            continue
            
        n_materials = len(cell.volume_fractions)
        base_id = len(subcell_coords)
        cell_subcells = [base_id + i for i in range(S)]
        cell_subcells_map[cell] = cell_subcells
        
        # Generate coordinates for subcells in a grid
        for i in cell_subcells:
            coords = []
            temp = i
            for dim in range(d):
                coords.append((temp % R) / R)
                temp //= R
            subcell_coords[i] = tuple(coords)
            
        # Calculate target counts for each material
        target_counts = [int(round(v / dA)) for v in cell.volume_fractions]
        current_sum = sum(target_counts)
        diff = S - current_sum
        if diff != 0:
            fractional_parts = [(v / dA - int(v / dA), idx) for idx, v in enumerate(cell.volume_fractions)]
            fractional_parts.sort(key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)
            for k in range(abs(diff)):
                idx = fractional_parts[k][1]
                if diff > 0:
                    target_counts[idx] += 1
                else:
                    target_counts[idx] -= 1
                
        # Assign labels randomly to subcells respecting target_counts
        labels_list = []
        for mat_id, count in enumerate(target_counts, start=1):
            labels_list.extend([mat_id] * count)
        random.shuffle(labels_list)
        
        for subcell_id, mat_id in zip(cell_subcells, labels_list):
            labeling[subcell_id] = mat_id

    # Optimization Loop
    while time.time() - start_time < max_time_seconds:
        mixed_cells = [c for c in cells if c.is_mixed]
        if not mixed_cells:
            break
        cell = random.choice(mixed_cells)
        cell_subcells = cell_subcells_map[cell]
        
        if len(cell_subcells) < 2:
            continue
            
        x, y = random.sample(cell_subcells, 2)
        
        ex_old = calculate_potential_energy(x, labeling, cell_subcells, subcell_coords)
        ey_old = calculate_potential_energy(y, labeling, cell_subcells, subcell_coords)
        E_old = ex_old + ey_old
        
        labeling[x], labeling[y] = labeling[y], labeling[x]
        
        ex_new = calculate_potential_energy(x, labeling, cell_subcells, subcell_coords)
        ey_new = calculate_potential_energy(y, labeling, cell_subcells, subcell_coords)
        E_new = ex_new + ey_new
        
        delta_E = E_new - E_old
        
        if delta_E < 0:
            pass
        else:
            if random.random() < math.exp(-delta_E / T):
                pass
            else:
                labeling[x], labeling[y] = labeling[y], labeling[x]

    return labeling, subcell_coords
```

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: Optimization is not cheap [S1, unofficial]. Simulated annealing convergence time depends on the subdivision rate $R$ and the complexity of the interface [S1, unofficial]. Higher $R$ lowers error but increases convergence time [S1, unofficial]. In practice, 10 seconds per timestep is often sufficient [S1, unofficial].
- **Memory Complexity**: Memory usage is proportional to the number of mixed cells and the subdivision rate $S$ [S1, unofficial]. By only subdividing mixed cells, memory can be reduced by approximately 90% in datasets where mixed cells constitute less than 6% of the total [S1, unofficial].
- **Accuracy**: The upper bound on per-cell volume error is $\epsilon(c) \le (n-1)dA$ for mixed cells [S1, unofficial]. For a 3D 2-material problem, the error is 0.8% with $R=5$ ($S=125$) and 0.1% with $R=10$ ($S=1000$) [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths**: Produces continuous interfaces across cell boundaries; suitable for visualization; handles $n$-materials; volume conservation with bounded error; easily parallelized [S1, unofficial].
- **Limitations**: Computationally expensive compared to PLIC; sharp artifacts if not smoothed; requires careful tuning of $R$ and $T$ (though $T=0.25$ is robust) [S1, unofficial].
- **When to Use**: When visualizing material regions or extracting smooth, continuous surface meshes from volume fraction data is required, especially for $n \ge 3$ materials where PLIC is ambiguous [S1, unofficial].
- **When Not to Use**: When real-time performance is critical and PLIC's discontinuity is acceptable [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Discrete Multi-Material Interface Reconstruction was introduced as a reinterpretation of MIR as a segmentation problem over a discretization of the problem’s original spatial domain [S1, unofficial]. It builds on the Potts-model energy metric, widely used in interface problems from studying cellular structures to interpolating region boundaries between segmented images [S1, unofficial]. The method was developed to address the limitations of PLIC, which is discontinuous across cell boundaries and ambiguous for three or more materials [S1, unofficial]. It was tested on 2D and 3D fluid flow datasets, including bubble simulations and multi-fluid cylinder interactions [S1, unofficial].
