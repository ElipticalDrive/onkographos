# SHG-based Volumetric Display

Also called: Frequency upconversion volumetric display, Glass-Ceramic (GC) Volumetric Display, SHG Volumetric Display, Volumetric Display via Frequency Upconversion, Volumetric display via frequency upconversion (SHG) with laser scanning

## Overview
A volumetric display technique that generates 3D images by rapidly scanning the focal point of a near-infrared laser inside a transparent composite material containing dispersed nonlinear optical (NLO) crystals [S1]. The NLO crystals generate visible light via Second-Harmonic Generation (SHG), creating localized, isotropic emission spots (voxels) without ghost images [S1]. Unlike its parent method, which relies on two-step fluorescence from rare-earth ions, this method uses the instantaneous nonlinear optical response of SHG to achieve ultrafast emission and full-color purity [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Input:** A femtosecond (fs) laser source emitting at a fundamental wavelength $\lambda_{fund}$; a transparent glass-ceramic (GC) composite containing dispersed NLO crystals; a scanning system (galvanometer mirrors and focusing lens).
*   **Output:** A 3D volumetric image composed of visible light voxels emitted from the focal volume within the material.

**Data Structures**
The display medium is a Glass-Ceramic (GC) composite.
*   **Matrix:** Glass (e.g., Ba$_2$TiSi$_2$O$_8$ or BTS glass).
*   **Inclusions:** NLO crystals (e.g., BTS crystals).
*   **Crystal Geometry:** Spherulitic aggregates with radius $R$ typically 50–300 nm [S1].
*   **Volume Fraction:** $\varphi$ typically 0.01 to 0.2 [S1].
*   **Optical Properties:** Refractive index of glass $n_g$ and crystal $n_c$ are closely matched to minimize scattering (e.g., $n_g=1.77$, $n_c=1.76$ at 632.8 nm) [S1].

**Numbered Steps**
1.  **Laser Selection:** Select a fundamental laser wavelength $\lambda_{fund}$ such that its second harmonic $\lambda_{fund}/2$ corresponds to the desired visible color. Common mappings:
    *   800 nm $\rightarrow$ 400 nm (purple)
    *   900 nm $\rightarrow$ 450 nm (blue)
    *   1080 nm $\rightarrow$ 540 nm (green)
    *   1230 nm $\rightarrow$ 615 nm (red) [S1].
2.  **Beam Steering (XY):** Direct the fundamental laser beam using two galvanometer mirrors to illuminate a specific 2D point $(x, y)$ on the display volume [S1].
3.  **Depth Positioning (Z):** Specify the depth position $z$ of the voxels by changing the distance between the GC sample and the galvanometer mirrors (or by moving the focus along the optical axis) [S1].
4.  **Emission Generation:** Focus the laser into the GC material. At the focal point, the high intensity $I_0$ induces Second-Harmonic Generation in the randomly oriented NLO crystals, emitting visible light at $\lambda_{fund}/2$ [S1].
5.  **Scanning:** Rapidly scan the focal point through the volume to construct moving 3D objects. The scanning frequency must be over 30 Hz to ensure image fusion [S1].

**Formulas**
*   **Scattering Turbidity:** The light attenuation due to scattering in the GC material is estimated by [S1]:
    $$ \sigma = \frac{14}{15\pi} \varphi(1 - \varphi) k^8 R^7 \left( \frac{\Delta n}{n} \right)^2 $$
    where $\varphi$ is the volume fraction of the crystalline phase, $R$ is the crystal radius, $n$ is the refractive index of the crystal, $\Delta n$ is the refractive index difference between the glassy and crystal phases, and $k=2\pi/\lambda$ ($\lambda$ is the wavelength of light).
*   **Voxel Dimension:** The resolution is determined by the focal spot size, expressed as [S1]:
    $$ d = \frac{4\lambda f}{\pi D} $$
    where $\lambda$ is the wavelength, $f$ is the focal length of the lens, and $D$ is the diameter of the fundamental beam.
*   **Total SHG Intensity:** The total intensity generated by dispersed crystals is [S1]:
    $$ I_{2,\text{tot}} = A f T_2 T_1^2 I_0^2 L $$
    where $A$ is a constant determined by crystal size and optical constants, $f$ is the volume fraction of the crystalline phase, $T_1$ and $T_2$ are the fundamental beam and SHG signal transmissions, $I_0$ is the fundamental beam intensity, and $L$ is the sample thickness.

**Parameters**
*   **Laser Source:** Femtosecond laser. Example: 800 nm, 1 kHz repetition rate [S1].
*   **Pumping Power ($I_0$):** Typical value 100 mW for a 1 mm$^3$ voxel [S1].
*   **Crystal Size:** ~100–300 nm [S1]. This size is smaller than the coherent length of BTS crystal (~10 $\mu$m), preventing exact phase-matching for all particles and ensuring isotropic emission [S1].
*   **Volume Fraction ($f$):** 0.05 to 0.2 for solid displays, 0.01 for liquid colloidal displays [S1].
*   **Sample Thickness ($L$):** 0.02 m to 1 m depending on display type [S1].
*   **Minimal Emission Energy:** Calculated minimal energy to observe green light (540 nm) in a dimly lit room (assuming 1 $\mu$W per 1 mm$^3$ spot):
    *   Solid-1 (0.02 m$^3$): 8 mW
    *   Solid-2 (0.1 m$^3$): 103 mW
    *   Liquid (1 m$^3$): 106 mW [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Ghost Images:** Avoided by using SHG (response time ~100 fs) instead of Rare-Earth ions (lifetime $\mu$s to ms) [S1].
*   **Image Drift:** Slight image drift occurs due to frequency upconversion along the light path before and after the focus [S1].
    *   *Mitigation:* Use an optical lens with a large size and short focal length to create a large divergence angle [S1].
    *   *Mitigation:* Use a laser system with smaller peak power to eliminate self-focusing and filamentation [S1].
*   **Scattering Loss:** High transparency is maintained if crystal size and volume fraction are controlled. For BTS GC with 5% crystalline phase and 50 nm crystals, scattering loss is <1% for a 10-mm-thick plate. Even with 200 nm crystals and 20% volume fraction, attenuation is <30% [S1].
*   **Anisotropy:** Single crystals are unsuitable because SHG emission is strongly anisotropic, causing image distortion. Dispersed randomly oriented NLO crystals ensure isotropic emission [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The emission is instantaneous (~100 fs response time) [S1]. The frame rate is limited by the scanning hardware (galvanometer mirrors), typically requiring >30 Hz for image fusion [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not applicable (physical display).
*   **Scaling:** Full-volume display requires laser power scaling with $L^3$, making large sizes unrealistic. Surface-only display scales with $L^2$, dramatically reducing required power [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Full-color capability with high color purity (near-monochromatic emissions); unlimited viewing angles (isotropic emission); no ghost images; viewable without glasses [S1].
*   **Limitations:** High laser power requirements for large volumes; image drift due to nonlinear effects along the beam path; limited by scanner hardware speed [S1].
*   **When to use:** Proof-of-concept 3D displays, head-up displays (2D transparent displays), applications requiring wide viewing angles and no glasses [S1].

## Background
*   **Origin:** Developed by researchers publishing in *Advanced Materials* (2017) [S1].
*   **History:** The method utilizes Ba$_2$TiSi$_2$O$_8$ (BTS) glass-ceramics, which offer high second-order optical nonlinearity and small index mismatch between glass and crystal phases [S1].
*   **Usage:** Demonstrated as a proof-of-concept for volumetric 3D displays viewable from any angle, and potential for 2D transparent displays [S1].
