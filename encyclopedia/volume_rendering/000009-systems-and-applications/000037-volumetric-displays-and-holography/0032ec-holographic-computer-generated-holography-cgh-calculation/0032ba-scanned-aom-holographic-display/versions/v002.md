# Scanned AOM Holographic Display

Also called: MIT holovideo display, Scanned Acousto-Optic Modulator (AOM) Holographic Display, Scanned-AOM, Synthetic aperture holography

## Overview
The Scanned AOM Holographic Display is a time-multiplexed volumetric display technique that generates large apparent holographic fringes by rapidly scanning a phase-modulated laser beam. It solves the problem of limited spatial light modulator (SLM) aperture size by using a fast Acousto-Optic Modulator (AOM) to process a small number of samples (~1000) at any instant, while mechanical mirrors scan the modulated light to cover a larger area. Unlike static SLM-based holography, which is constrained by the physical size of the modulator, this method scales to produce images in volumes greater than one liter by integrating the rapidly scanned fringes over the human visual system's latency period [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Digitally computed holographic fringes (phase data).
*   **Output:** A time-integrated 3D holographic image visible to the observer.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Fringe Data:** A sequence of phase values representing the holographic fringe. The data is streamed to the AOM.
*   **AOM State:** The AOM maintains an acoustic wave profile within a crystal. At any instant, it phase-modulates approximately 1000 samples [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Human Visual Latency:** Typically 20 ms. The eye time-integrates the displayed fringe during this interval [S1].
*   **AOM Instantaneous Capacity:** ~1000 samples modulated simultaneously [S1].
*   **System Scale:** Early systems produced 50 ml volumes from 2 MB fringes; scaled systems with 18 parallel channels produced >1 liter volumes from 36 MB fringes [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Fringe Computation:** Compute the holographic fringes for the desired 3D scene.
2.  **Digital-to-Analog Conversion:** Convert the digitally computed fringes into high-frequency analog signals. This requires a specialized synchronized high-speed framebuffer system to drive the RF processing [S1].
3.  **Acoustic Modulation:** Drive the AOM with the analog signals. An acoustic wave propagates through the crystal, phase-modulating a beam of laser light passing through it. The AOM modulates a small segment of the fringe (approx. 1000 samples) at any given time [S1].
4.  **Optical Processing:** Lenses image and demagnify the diffracted light from the AOM to a plane in front of the viewer [S1].
5.  **Horizontal Scanning:** A rapidly moving mirror scans the image of the modulated light horizontally. This time-multiplexes the image of the modulated light, effectively sweeping the small modulated segment across the larger aperture [S1].
6.  **Vertical Scanning:** A vertical scanning system (implied by the need for 2D coverage) completes the 2D fringe pattern [S1].
7.  **Visual Integration:** The human visual system integrates the rapidly scanned fringes over its latency period (typically 20 ms) to perceive the complete holographic image [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Digital-to-Analog Conversion:** Converting digitally computed fringes into high-frequency analog signals is a major practical obstacle, requiring specialized hardware [S1].
*   **Synchronization:** State-of-the-art scanning mirrors must be precisely synchronized to the fringe data stream, adding significant complexity compared to LCD-based displays [S1].
*   **Intensity Drop-off:** In single-channel systems, increasing the voxel count can lead to intensity drop-off due to finite acoustic fill time and refresh rate constraints [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Acousto-Optic Random Access Scanning with Parallel Channels](8508-acousto-optic-random-access-scanning-with-parallel-channels.md)
This method addresses the voxel count and intensity limitations of single-channel acousto-optic (AO) random-access scanning in volumetric displays. By splitting a single laser source into four parallel optical paths, each processed by independent AO modulators and deflectors, the system increases the total addressable voxel count while maintaining image brightness. It sets itself apart from single-scanner approaches by decoupling the total voxel capacity from the fixed refresh rate and finite acoustic fill time constraints of a single beam, preventing the intensity drop-off that occurs when increasing voxels in a single channel.

### Surface Acoustic Wave (SAW) AOM Holographic Display
An AOM device variant using multiple ultrasonic transducers (electrodes) fed a complex pattern to launch surface acoustic waves (SAWs) across the device aperture, diffracting light to form a holographic image without time multiplexing [S1].
*   **Steps:**
    1.  Feed a complex pattern to multiple electrodes on the AOM device [S1].
    2.  Launch surface acoustic waves (SAWs) across the device aperture [S1].
    3.  Diffracted light forms a holographic image directly, eliminating the need for time multiplexing and scanning mirrors [S1].
*   **Pitfalls:** The large number of electrodes may be prohibitively expensive. Additionally, the array of SAW electrodes necessitates an additional numerical inversion transformation, making rapid computation difficult [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method relies on real-time processing of holographic fringes. The computational load is dominated by the fringe calculation and the high-speed digital-to-analog conversion. The scanning mirrors must operate at high frequencies to match the human visual integration time (20 ms) [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** The system requires significant memory to store the fringe data. Early systems used 2 MB fringes for 50 ml volumes, while scaled systems used 36 MB fringes for >1 liter volumes [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** The first images produced were 50 ml in volume, generated from 2 MB fringes. A scaled system with 18 parallel modulation channels produced images occupying a volume greater than one liter from 36 MB fringes [S1].
*   **Strengths:** It can be scaled up to produce larger images than static SLMs. It produced the largest holovideo images at the time of its development [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires specialized high-speed framebuffer systems for digital-to-analog conversion. Needs state-of-the-art scanning mirrors synchronized to the fringe data stream. More complex to interface with digital electronics compared to LCDs or DMDs [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When large-volume holographic imagery is required and the complexity of mechanical scanning and specialized hardware can be accommodated.
*   **When Not to Use:** When simplicity of interfacing with standard digital computers is a priority, or when cost constraints prohibit specialized high-speed framebuffer and scanning mirror systems [S1].

## Background
The Scanned AOM Holographic Display was invented and exploited by researchers at the MIT Media Laboratory to produce the world's first real-time 3D holographic display in 1989 [S1]. It was developed to overcome the limitations of static holographic displays, which were constrained by the physical size of the modulator. The technique leverages the time-multiplexing of a very fast AOH SLM, using a rapidly propagating acoustic wave within a crystal to phase-modulate light, and a rapidly moving mirror to scan the image [S1].
