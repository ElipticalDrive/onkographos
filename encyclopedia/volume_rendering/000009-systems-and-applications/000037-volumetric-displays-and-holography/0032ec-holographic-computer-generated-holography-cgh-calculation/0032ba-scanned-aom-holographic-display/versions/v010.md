# Scanned AOM Holographic Display
Also called: MIT holovideo display, Scanned Acousto-Optic Modulator (AOM) Holographic Display, Scanned-AOM, Synthetic aperture holography

## Overview
The Scanned AOM Holographic Display is a time-multiplexed volumetric display technique that generates large apparent holographic fringes by rapidly scanning a phase-modulated laser beam across the viewer's eye [S1]. It solves the hardware limitation of Acousto-Optic Modulators (AOMs), which can only phase-modulate a small number of samples (approximately 1000) at any single instant due to the finite speed of acoustic waves in the crystal [S1]. By leveraging the human visual system's integration time (typically 20 ms), the system creates a persistent, larger 3D image that is significantly larger than the physical aperture of the modulator [S1]. It differs from static SLM-based methods by requiring high-speed mechanical scanning and analog signal conversion, but allows for the creation of holovideo images with volumes exceeding one liter, far surpassing the physical constraints of single-panel displays [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Digitally computed holographic fringes (complex amplitude data).
*   **Output:** A time-integrated 3D holographic image perceived by the viewer.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Fringe Data:** A stream of digital values representing the phase modulation required for the AOM. In early implementations, this was stored in a specialized high-speed framebuffer [S1].
*   **AOM State:** The acoustic wave profile within the crystal, which acts as a dynamic phase grating.

**Parameters:**
*   **Human Visual Latency:** $\tau_{eye} \approx 20$ ms [S1].
*   **AOM Instantaneous Capacity:** $N_{inst} \approx 1000$ samples [S1].
*   **Scan Rate:** Must be fast enough to complete the full fringe scan within $\tau_{eye}$.

**Steps:**
1.  **Fringe Computation:** Compute the holographic fringes for the desired 3D scene. These fringes represent the phase modulation required to reconstruct the wavefront [S1].
2.  **Digital-to-Analog Conversion:** Convert the digitally computed fringes into high-frequency analog electrical signals. This requires a specialized synchronized high-speed framebuffer system to handle the data rate [S1].
3.  **Acoustic Wave Generation:** Drive the AOM with the analog signals. An acoustic wave propagates through the AOM crystal, creating a phase grating that modulates the phase of a passing laser beam. At any instant, this modulates approximately 1000 samples of the beam [S1].
4.  **Beam Scanning:** Use a rapidly moving mirror (horizontal scanning system) to scan the image of the modulated light across the aperture. This time-multiplexes the small modulated image into a much larger apparent fringe [S1].
5.  **Optical Projection:** Lenses image and demagnify the diffracted light to a plane in front of the viewer [S1].
6.  **Visual Integration:** The human eye integrates the rapidly scanned fringes over its latency period (20 ms) to perceive a continuous, large-volume 3D holographic image [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Signal Conversion:** Converting digitally computed fringes into high-frequency analog signals is a major practical obstacle, requiring specialized hardware not found in standard LCD/DMD interfaces [S1].
*   **Synchronization:** State-of-the-art scanning mirrors must be precisely synchronized with the fringe data stream [S1].
*   **Complexity:** The need for optical processing and mechanical scanning adds significant complexity compared to LCD-based displays [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Acousto-Optic Random Access Scanning with Parallel Channels](8508-acousto-optic-random-access-scanning-with-parallel-channels.md)
Acousto-Optic Random Access Scanning with Parallel Channels is a volumetric display technique that uses multiple acousto-optic (AO) deflectors operating in parallel to address voxels in a display volume, thereby increasing the total voxel count and maintaining image intensity. It solves the bottleneck of finite acoustic wave travel time ("access time") in single-channel scanners, which limits the number of addressable voxels per frame at a fixed refresh rate. Unlike single-channel scanners, which suffer from intensity drop-off as voxel density increases due to reduced on-time, this method couples several scanners to distribute the workload. It differs from [Four-Channel Scanner Convergence Correction](11734-four-channel-scanner-convergence-correction.md) by defining the parallel architecture itself, whereas the sibling method addresses the geometric alignment errors inherent in that architecture.

### Surface Acoustic Wave (SAW) AOM Holographic Display
An AOM device variant using multiple ultrasonic transducers (electrodes) fed a complex pattern to launch surface acoustic waves (SAWs) across the device aperture, diffracting light to form a holographic image without time multiplexing [S1].
1.  Feed a complex pattern to multiple electrodes on the AOM device.
2.  Launch surface acoustic waves (SAWs) across the device aperture.
3.  Diffracted light forms a holographic image directly, eliminating the need for time multiplexing and scanning mirrors [S1].
Pitfall: Large number of electrodes may be prohibitively expensive. Requires an additional numerical inversion transformation for the array of SAW electrodes, making rapid computation difficult [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Volume:** Capable of producing large 3D images. Early versions produced 50 ml volumes from 2 MB fringes [S1]. Scaled systems with 18 parallel modulation channels produced images >1 liter volume from 36 MB fringes [S1].
*   **Complexity:** High. Requires specialized high-speed analog framebuffer systems and state-of-the-art scanning mirrors synchronized to the fringe data stream [S1].
*   **Comparison to SLMs:** More complex to interface with digital electronics than LCDs or DMDs, which can plug directly into computers or be built on integrated circuits [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Produces the largest holovideo images among the techniques discussed [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Cost and complexity of analog signal conversion and mechanical scanning [S1].

## Background
The Scanned AOM Holographic Display was invented and exploited by researchers at the MIT Media Laboratory to produce the world's first real-time 3D holographic display in 1989 [S1]. It was a significant advancement in the field of interactive computer graphics, providing extremely realistic imagery, although its cost and complexity limited its widespread adoption [S1].
