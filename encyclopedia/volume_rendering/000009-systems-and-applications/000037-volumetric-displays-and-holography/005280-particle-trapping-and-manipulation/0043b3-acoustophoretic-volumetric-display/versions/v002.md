# Acoustophoretic Volumetric Display

Also called: AVD, Acoustophoretic-trap free-space volumetric display

## Overview
An Acoustophoretic Volumetric Display (AVD) renders 3D graphics in free space by trapping a millimetric dielectric particle using acoustic radiation forces from a phased ultrasonic array and moving it rapidly along a 3D trajectory while synchronously illuminating it with colored light [S1]. Unlike optical traps that rely on photophoretic forces and high-power lasers, AVDs use standing acoustic waves to create potential wells, offering a larger operational region (up to half a meter) and avoiding laser safety hazards, though they currently support smaller, simpler shapes at high speeds compared to photophoretic counterparts [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Input:** A 3D graphic defined as a sequence of particle positions $(x, y, z)$ at time $t$ and corresponding RGB color values.
*   **Output:** A visible 3D volumetric image formed by the persistence of vision of the illuminated, moving particle.

**Data Structures and Hardware Layout**
The system relies on a specific hardware control chain and signal parameters:
1.  **Particle:** Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) sphere, diameter $d \approx 1.5$ mm [S1].
2.  **Transducer Array:** 60 ultrasonic transducers (Murata MA40S4S) arranged in two opposed arrays of 30 transducers each [S1].
3.  **Control Chain:** PC $\rightarrow$ UART (1.5 Mbauds) $\rightarrow$ FPGA (ALTERA CoreEp4CE6) $\rightarrow$ MOSFET drivers (TC4427a) $\rightarrow$ Transducers [S1].
4.  **Signal Parameters:**
    *   Square waves generated by the FPGA.
    *   Phase resolution: $\pi/64$ rad [S1].
    *   Color depth: 8-bit signal per RGB channel [S1].
5.  **Working Volume:** Approximately $4 \times 5 \times 8$ cm$^3$ [S1].

**Algorithm Steps**
1.  **Trajectory and Color Definition:** Represent the desired 3D graphic as a sequence of target positions $(x_i, y_i, z_i)$ at discrete times $t_i$ and associated RGB color values $C_i = (R_i, G_i, B_i)$ [S1].
2.  **Color Transmission:** Send the 8-bit RGB signal for each channel to the RGB LED source positioned adjacent to the acoustic levitator [S1].
3.  **Phase Calculation:** For each target position $(x_i, y_i, z_i)$, calculate the required emission phases $\phi_{j,i}$ for each of the 60 transducers $j$ to focus the acoustic field and create an antinode at the target location [S1].
4.  **FPGA Control:** Transmit the calculated phases to the FPGA. The FPGA generates square waves with the specified phase resolution ($\pi/64$ rad) for each of the 60 channels [S1].
5.  **Amplification:** The FPGA signals are sent to MOSFET drivers (TC4427a), which amplify the signals using an external DC power supply to drive the transducers [S1].
6.  **Acoustic Trapping:** The transducers emit ultrasonic waves, creating a standing wave field with an antinode at the calculated position. The particle is trapped in this potential well [S1].
7.  **Illumination and Movement:** Move the trap (by updating phases) to the next position $(x_{i+1}, y_{i+1}, z_{i+1})$ while illuminating the particle with color $C_i$. The particle follows the trap, scattering the light to form a voxel [S1].
8.  **Persistence of Vision:** Repeat steps 3-7 rapidly enough that the human eye perceives a continuous 3D path [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Particle Size Constraints:** Particles $>1.5$ mm invalidate the small particle assumptions in the Gor’kov model, causing force per unit volume to decrease. Particles $<0.5$ mm are swept out by air currents because drag force (scaling with area) becomes significant relative to acoustic radiation force (scaling with volume) [S1].
*   **Static Calibration Deviation:** Equilibrium points deviate from ideal focal points by approximately 0.5 mm due to scattering, temperature variations, manufacturing inaccuracies, or transducer position errors. This affects image accuracy [S1].
*   **Dynamic Distortion:** Increasing particle velocity distorts the shape of the volumetric image and can eject the particle from the trap. Static calibration becomes less effective at higher speeds due to the nonlinear dynamics of the particle in the acoustic field [S1].
*   **Speed Limitations:** At 10 Hz rendering frequency, only small and simple shapes (size 5-6 mm) can be achieved [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Performance:** Renders simple figures in real time at 10 frames per second (fps) [S1].
*   **Raster Images:** Rendered at lower frame rates; draw times were 20 seconds for logos/stop signs and 5 seconds for a 3D wireframe airplane in static tests [S1].
*   **Limitations:** High-speed rendering is currently limited to small (5-6 mm) and simple shapes [S1]. Dynamic distortion increases with drawing speed [S1].
*   **Comparison to Photophoretic Displays:** AVDs offer a larger operational region (up to half a meter) and avoid high-power laser hazards, but are currently limited to smaller shapes at high speeds compared to Photophoretic-Trap Volumetric Displays (OTD), which can reach speeds of 1.8 m/s [S1].

## Background
The Acoustophoretic Volumetric Display was proposed as a free-space volumetric display technology that utilizes acoustic radiation forces to levitate and move particles, addressing the clipping and limited field of view issues associated with holograms and light-field displays [S1]. The work builds on previous attempts to create free-space displays using acoustophoresis, such as Ochiai et al.'s crosses and grids, Sahoo et al.'s flip-screen particles, and Uno et al.'s LED levitation [S1]. The specific implementation described uses a phased array of 60 ultrasonic transducers controlled by an FPGA to trap and move a 1.5 mm EPS particle [S1].
