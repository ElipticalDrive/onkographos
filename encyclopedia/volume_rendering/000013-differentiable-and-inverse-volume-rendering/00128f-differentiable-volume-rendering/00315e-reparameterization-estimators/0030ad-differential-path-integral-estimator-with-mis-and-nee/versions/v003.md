# Differential path integral estimator with MIS and NEE

Also called: Differential NEE estimator, MIS-weighted differential path estimator

## Overview
The Differential Path Integral Estimator with Multiple Importance Sampling (MIS) and Next Event Estimation (NEE) computes the gradient of the rendered image with respect to scene parameters $\theta$ by integrating differential measurement contributions over path space [S1]. It solves the problem of high variance in gradient estimation for specular and complex materials by combining standard BRDF sampling with emitter sampling via NEE, allowing the "differential vertex" (the point where the derivative is taken) to be located at any intermediate vertex or at the emitter connection [S1]. Unlike standard NEE which only connects paths to emitters at the final vertex, this method uses MIS to combine BRDF and emitter sampling probabilities into a single mixture weight, significantly reducing variance for microfacet and mixture BRDFs [S1]. It builds on [Reparameterization Estimators](6499-reparameterization-estimators.md).

## How it works
The method estimates the gradient $\nabla_\theta I$ of the rendering integral $I(\theta)$ by decomposing the derivative of the path contribution into a sum of terms, each associated with a specific vertex $i$ in the path [S1]. It uses MIS to weight contributions from standard path sampling and NEE paths [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Scene parameters $\theta$ (e.g., material roughness, light intensity, geometry).
    *   Camera state and pixel coordinates.
    *   Maximum path depth $N_{max}$ (integer).
    *   Number of samples $M$ (integer).
    *   Switching probability $q$ (float, $0 < q \le 1$), used to determine the geometric series weights for the differential vertex location [S1].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Gradient estimate $\nabla_\theta I$ (vector or tensor, same shape as $\theta$).

### Data Structures
*   **Path Vertex:** A structure containing position $x$, incoming direction $\omega_{in}$, outgoing direction $\omega_{out}$, and accumulated throughput $W$.
*   **Path:** A list of vertices $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_N$. $x_0$ is the camera position.
*   **NEE Path:** A path $x_0, \dots, x_j, x_{e,j}$ where $x_{e,j}$ is a point on an emitter sampled via NEE.

### Formulas and Definitions
The rendering integral for a pixel is defined as [S1]:
$$
I = \int_{S^2} W_e(x_0, \omega_{x_1,x_0}) G(x_0, x_1) L_i(x_0, \omega_{x_1,x_0}) d\omega_{x_1,x_0}
$$
where $W_e$ is the pixel weight, $G$ is the geometry term, and $L_i$ is the incoming radiance [S1].

The derivative of the integral with respect to $\theta$ is expressed as a sum over all vertices $i=0 \dots N$ in the path [S1]:
$$
\partial_\theta I = \int_{S^2} \left[ \sum_{i=0}^N g(x, i; \theta) \right] dx
$$
where $g(x, i; \theta)$ is the differential measurement contribution of the path $x$ with the differential vertex at index $i$ [S1].

For a standard path $x = x_0 \dots x_N$, the contribution $g(x, i; \theta)$ is derived from the product of BRDFs $f_k$ and the derivative of the $i$-th BRDF [S1]:
$$
g(x, i; \theta) = \left( \prod_{k=0, k \neq i}^N f_k(x) \right) \frac{\partial_\theta f_i(x)}{f_i(x)} \cdot W_e(x_0, \omega_{x_1,x_0}) G(x_0, x_1) L_i(x_0, \omega_{x_1,x_0})
$$
Note: For $i=N$, $f_N$ represents the emitter emission $L_e$ if the path terminates at an emitter, or the BSDF if it terminates at a surface. The term $\frac{\partial_\theta f_i(x)}{f_i(x)}$ is the logarithmic derivative [S1].

For NEE paths, let $x_j x_{e,j}$ be a connection from vertex $x_j$ to an emitter point $x_{e,j}$. The differential contribution $g(x, x_j x_{e,j}, i; \theta)$ for $i < j$ is [S1]:
$$
g(x, x_j x_{e,j}, i; \theta) = \left[ \prod_{k=1}^{j-1} f_k(x) \right] G(x_j, x_{e,j}) \rho_s(x_{j-1}, x_j, x_{e,j}) \cdot \frac{\partial_\theta f_i(x)}{f_i(x)} \cdot L_e(x_{e,j})
$$
where $\rho_s$ is the scattering term connecting $x_{j-1}$ to $x_j$ and then to $x_{e,j}$ [S1].

### Probability Distributions and MIS Weights
The method uses a mixture distribution to sample paths and NEE connections [S1].

1.  **Standard Path Probability $p(x)$:** The probability of generating path $x$ via standard BRDF sampling [S1].
2.  **Differential Vertex Probability $p(x, i)$:** The probability of generating path $x$ with the differential vertex at $i$. This is modeled using a geometric distribution with parameter $q$ [S1]:
    $$
    p(x, i) = p(x) \cdot (1-q)^{i-1} q \quad \text{for } i \ge 1
    $$
    (For $i=0$, the weight is handled separately or included in the base path probability depending on implementation details, but the sum covers all $i$) [S1].
3.  **NEE Path Probability $p_{\text{nee}}(x, x_j x_{e,j})$:** The probability of generating the NEE path $x_0 \dots x_j x_{e,j}$ [S1]:
    $$
    p_{\text{nee}}(x, x_j x_{e,j}) = \left[ \prod_{k=0}^{j-1} p_f(x_k) \right] p_e(x_j x_{e,j}) (1-q)^{j-1}
    $$
    where $p_f(x_k)$ is the BRDF sampling probability at vertex $k$, and $p_e(x_j x_{e,j})$ is the emitter sampling probability [S1].
4.  **NEE Differential Vertex Probability $p_{\text{nee}}(x, x_j x_{e,j}, i)$:** The probability of generating the NEE path with differential vertex at $i$ ($1 \le i < j$) [S1]:
    $$
    p_{\text{nee}}(x, x_j x_{e,j}, i) = \frac{\prod_{k=0}^{j-1} p_f(x_k)}{p_f(x_i)} p_{\partial f}(x_i) (1-q)^{i-1} q
    $$
    where $p_{\partial f}(x_i)$ is the probability density associated with the derivative term (often related to the sensitivity of the BRDF to $\theta$) [S1].

The total mixture probability for a standard path $x$ is [S1]:
$$

$$

### Algorithm Steps
For each pixel, perform $M$ independent samples [S1]:

1.  **Initialize Accumulator:** Set gradient accumulator $G_{acc} = 0$.
2.  **Loop over Samples:** For $m = 1$ to $M$:
    a.  **Generate Standard Path:** Generate a standard path $x^m = x_0 \dots x_{N^m}$ using standard path tracing (BRDF sampling) [S1].
    b.  **Compute Standard Path Weight:** Calculate $p_{\text{mix}}(x^m)$ using Eq (41) [S1].
        *   Note: $p(x^m)$ is the standard path probability.
        *   $p(x^m, i)$ are the differential vertex probabilities for $i=1 \dots N^m-1$.
        *   $p_{\text{nee}}(x^m, x_{N^m-1}x_{N^m})$ is the probability of the NEE connection at the last vertex (if applicable).
        *   *Correction based on Eq 41:* The sum includes NEE terms? The note says Eq (41) is for $p_{\text{mix}}(x)$. It includes $p_{\text{nee}}(x, x_{N-1}x_N)$. This implies that even for a standard path, we account for the NEE alternative at the end? Or is it that the "standard path" estimator includes the NEE contribution at the last vertex as part of the mix?
        *   Let's follow the estimator Eq (44) strictly. It sums two main parts:
            1.  $\sum_{i=0}^{N^m} \frac{g(x^m, i; \theta)}{p_{\text{mix}}(x^m)}$
            2.  $\sum_{j=1}^{N^m-1} \sum_{i=0}^j \frac{g(x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j}, i; \theta)}{p_{\text{mix}}(x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j})}$
    c.  **Accumulate Standard Path Gradient:**
        *   For each $i = 0$ to $N^m$:
            *   Compute $g(x^m, i; \theta)$ [S1].
            *   Add $\frac{g(x^m, i; \theta)}{p_{\text{mix}}(x^m)}$ to $G_{acc}$ [S1].
    d.  **Generate NEE Paths:** For each $j = 1$ to $N^m-1$:
        *   Generate an NEE path $x^m_j x_{e,j}$ connecting vertex $x^m_j$ to an emitter [S1].
        *   Form the full path $x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j}$ [S1].
    e.  **Compute NEE Path Weight:** Calculate $p_{\text{mix}}(x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j})$ using Eq (42) [S1].
    f.  **Accumulate NEE Path Gradient:**
        *   For each $i = 0$ to $j$:
            *   Compute $g(x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j}, i; \theta)$ [S1].
            *   Add $\frac{g(x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j}, i; \theta)}{p_{\text{mix}}(x^m, x^m_j x^m_{e,j})}$ to $G_{acc}$ [S1].
3.  **Finalize:** Divide $G_{acc}$ by $M$ to get $\nabla_\theta I_{\text{NEE}}$ [S1].

### Parameters
*   $M$: Number of independent samples per pixel. Typical values: 16–256 for real-time, 1024+ for offline [S1].
*   $q$: Probability of switching to differential sampling at each vertex. Typical values: 0.5–1.0 [S1].
*   $N_{max}$: Maximum path depth. Typical values: 3–10 [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Zero Probability:** If $p_{\text{mix}}(x)$ is zero, the term is undefined. Ensure all sampling methods have non-zero probability for the generated samples [S1].
*   **Precision:** The geometric series terms $(1-q)^{i-1}$ can underflow for large $i$. Use log-probabilities if necessary [S1].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If the scene has no emitters, NEE terms are zero. If the path depth is 0, only the camera vertex contributes [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Differential Vertex Sampling with MIS](6338-differential-vertex-sampling-with-mis.md)
Differential Vertex Sampling with MIS is a method to estimate the gradient of a loss function with respect to scene parameters by importance sampling an intermediate path vertex as the "differential vertex". It solves the problem of high variance in gradient estimation, particularly for specular materials, by combining differential vertex sampling with standard BRDF sampling using Multiple Importance Sampling (MIS). Unlike standard BRDF sampling, which treats all vertices identically, this method explicitly biases the sampling distribution to capture the boundary component of the gradient integral, significantly reducing variance for microfacet and mixture BRDFs. [Differential Vertex Sampling with MIS](6338-differential-vertex-sampling-with-mis.md)

### [Differential Edge Sampling](6708-differential-edge-sampling.md)
Differential Edge Sampling is a method for computing the gradient of physically-based rendered images with respect to arbitrary scene parameters (camera, lights, materials, geometry) by explicitly sampling visibility discontinuities. It solves the problem that standard Monte Carlo estimators fail to capture derivatives at boundaries where visibility changes abruptly, such as object silhouettes and occlusion edges. Unlike its sibling [Differential Vertex Sampling with MIS](6338-differential-vertex-sampling-with-mis.md), which focuses on vertex-based contributions, this method decomposes the rendering derivative into a smooth interior integral (estimated via automatic differentiation) and a boundary integral (estimated by sampling triangle edges). [Differential Edge Sampling](6708-differential-edge-sampling.md)

### Vectorized Total Derivative Estimation
This approach effectively shares all paths traced by Algorithm 1 to calculate derivatives with respect to all scene parameters simultaneously [S2, unofficial]. Execute Algorithm 1 once in a vectorized fashion to estimate the total scene derivative $d_\pi L$. Replace all partial derivatives with their vector-valued total derivative counterparts and perform component-wise calculations between all total-derivative-related quantities [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(M \cdot N^2)$ per pixel, where $M$ is the number of samples and $N$ is the path depth. The $N^2$ factor comes from generating NEE paths for each vertex and summing over all differential vertices [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ per path, storing the path vertices and intermediate values [S1].
*   **Variance:** Significantly lower variance than standard BRDF sampling for specular materials and complex lighting, due to the combination of BRDF and NEE sampling via MIS [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Unbiased gradient estimation; handles complex materials and lighting; reduces variance for specular surfaces [S1].
*   **Limitations:** High computational cost due to NEE path generation and MIS weight calculation; sensitive to the choice of $q$ and $M$ [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** Differentiable rendering, inverse rendering, and joint material/lighting estimation where high-quality gradients are required [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** Real-time applications with strict latency constraints due to high computational cost [S2, unofficial].

## Background
The method originates from the work of T. Su and I. Gkioulekas, "Path Sampling Methods for Differentiable Rendering," published in the Computer Graphics Forum (Eurographics) in 2024 [S1]. It builds on earlier work in differentiable rendering, such as "Inverse path tracing for joint material and lighting estimation" by Zinovic et al. (2019) [S1] and "Differentiable Monte Carlo ray tracing through edge sampling" by Li et al. (2018) [S1]. The use of NEE in differentiable rendering was explored in "Unbiased inverse volume rendering with differential trackers" by Nimier-David et al. (2022) [S1]. The vectorized total derivative estimation is discussed in "A Differential Theory of Radiative Transfer" by Zhang et al. (2019) [S2, unofficial].
