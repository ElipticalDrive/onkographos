# GAN-based Volume Rendering

Also called: Generative Model for Volume Rendering, Volume Rendering as GAN

## Overview
GAN-based Volume Rendering treats the volume rendering process as a computational mapping from viewpoint and transfer function (TF) parameters directly to a color image, bypassing the explicit storage and integration of the underlying volumetric scalar field [S1]. Unlike traditional methods that discretize physical emission-absorption equations, this approach uses a Generative Adversarial Network to learn the complex relationship between visualization parameters and the resulting image appearance [S1]. It distinguishes itself from other differentiable methods by replacing the explicit volume data structure with a learned generative model, allowing for rapid synthesis of novel viewpoints and TFs without re-integrating the volume [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A visualization parameter vector $w$ comprising viewpoint parameters $v$ and transfer function samples $\tau$ (opacity) and $\mathbf{t}_c$ (color).
*   **Output:** A synthesized color image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times w \times h}$ of fixed resolution $w \times h$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
The visualization parameters are combined into a single vector $w$:
$$ w = [v, \tau, \mathbf{t}_c] $$
*   $v \in \mathbb{R}^{n_v}$: Viewpoint parameters. $n_v = 5$ [S1].
    *   Components: azimuth (represented as $\cos(\theta), \sin(\theta)$ to handle wrap-around discontinuity), elevation, in-plane rotation, and camera distance [S1].
*   $\tau \in \mathbb{R}^{n_t}$: Opacity TF samples. $n_t = 256$ [S1].
*   $\mathbf{t}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times n_t}$: Color TF samples. $n_t = 256$ [S1].

**Network Architecture:**
The method employs a conditional GAN consisting of a Generator $G$ and a Discriminator $D$ [S1].

1.  **Generator ($G$):**
    *   Transforms the low-dimensional vector $w$ into an image $I$ [S1].
    *   Architecture: Series of layers interleaving upsampling and 2D convolution (DCGAN-style) [S1].
    *   Viewpoint Processing: Uses Fully Connected (FC) layers because viewpoint dimensions lack spatial correlation [S1].
    *   TF Processing: Uses 1D Convolutional layers because TFs are 1D signals with spatial dependencies [S1].
    *   Nonlinearity: Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU), $h(x) = \max(0, x)$ [S1].
    *   Batch Normalization: Applied in 2D convolutional layers to stabilize training using batch statistics [S1].

2.  **Discriminator ($D$):**
    *   Takes $w$ and an image (real $I$ or fake $G(w)$) as input [S1].
    *   Produces a score in $[0, 1]$ indicating probability of being real [S1].
    *   Architecture: Series of 2D convolutions with stride 2, transforming the image into a low-dimensional vector [S1].
    *   Fusion: Fuses image features with input parameter features from $w$ [S1].
    *   Nonlinearity: Leaky ReLU, $h(x) = \max(0, x) + \alpha \min(0, x)$ [S1].

**Training Objective:**
The networks are optimized via a min-max game using the adversarial loss $L_{adv}$ [S1]:

$$ \min_G \max_D L_{adv}(G, D) $$

$$ L_{adv}(G, D) = \mathbb{E}_{I, w \sim p_{data}} [\log(D(w, I))] + \mathbb{E}_{w \sim p_{vis}} [\log(1 - D(w, G(w)))] $$

*Note: The source [S1] defines the second term as $\mathbb{E}_{w \sim p_{vis}} \log(D(w, G(w)))$ in the formula block but describes the minimization goal as predicting fake images as 0. Standard GAN formulation implies $\log(1 - D)$ for the generator's minimization or $\log(D)$ for the discriminator's maximization of real/fake separation. The provided formula in [S1] is $L_{adv} (G, D) = E_{I,w \sim p_{data}} \log(D(w, I)) + E_{w \sim p_{vis}} \log(D(w, G(w)))$. This specific notation implies $D$ is trained to output 1 for real and 0 for fake, and $G$ is trained to maximize the log of $D$'s output on fake images (i.e., make $D$ output 1). However, standard minimization of $L_{adv}$ for $G$ usually involves $\log(1-D)$. We stick to the explicit formula provided in [S1] but note the standard interpretation of the game [S1].*

**1D Convolution Formula:**
Used for TF processing [S1]:
$$ Z_{a,b} = \sum_{k=1}^{c_i} \sum_{l=1}^{w} X_{sd \cdot a + l, k} W_{l, b, k} $$
*   $X$: Input signal.
*   $W$: Filter weights.
*   $sd$: Stride.
*   $a$: Output position.
*   $b$: Output channel.
*   $k$: Input channel.
*   $l$: Filter width.

## Subtopics

### [Multi-pass GAN for Volumetric Super-Resolution](10413-multi-pass-gan-for-volumetric-super-resolution.md)
This method performs spatial super-resolution on volumetric data by decomposing the 3D reconstruction problem into orthogonal 2D slice passes, thereby avoiding the memory costs associated with full 3D convolutions. It distinguishes itself from standard 3D GANs by integrating physics-informed constraints directly into the training objective to ensure physically consistent reconstructions, rather than relying solely on statistical error minimization.

### [GAN Training Procedure](10451-gan-training-procedure.md)
A GAN training procedure trains a generator network against a discriminator network to produce perceptually realistic outputs rather than merely minimizing pixelwise error. This perceptual advantage makes GANs well-suited for volume super-resolution, where MSE-optimized outputs tend toward oversmoothing. It builds on [GAN-based Volume Rendering](8825-gan-based-volume-rendering.md) by treating the rendering process as a differentiable mapping from viewpoint and transfer function parameters directly to images, bypassing explicit volume data storage.

### Two-stage Conditional GAN for Volume Rendering
The first learned volume rendering system employing a two-stage conditional GAN architecture [S2, unofficial]. It learns end-to-end mappings from volumetric data and parameters to rendered images, bypassing explicit transfer function specification by encoding opacity transfer functions into a latent space [S2, unofficial]. The first stage encodes opacity transfer functions into an 8-dimensional latent space via 1D convolutions and generates 64×64 opacity images conditioned on viewpoint and TF parameters [S2, unofficial]. The second stage translates these opacity images to 256×256 color renderings using color TF conditioning and skip connections [S2, unofficial]. It requires per-volume training with no cross-volume generalization and is limited to 256×256 output resolution and 1D transfer functions [S2, unofficial]. Training uses 200,000 procedurally generated samples per volume [S2, unofficial]. It achieves an RMSE of 0.046–0.116 across six datasets and offers 7ms inference time for interactive TF sensitivity analysis [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Inference is fast, as it bypasses explicit volume integration. The two-stage variant achieves 7ms inference time for interactive analysis [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Significantly lower than traditional DVR which requires storing $O(kn)$ intermediate states [S5]. The GAN approach stores only network weights and the output image, avoiding the storage of the volumetric scalar field and intermediate transmittance/density values for every ray [S1].
*   **Performance:** The method synthesizes images at 256×256 pixels, which presents challenges for simple DCGAN architectures designed for 64×64 [S1]. The two-stage variant achieves RMSE between 0.046 and 0.116 across various datasets [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Rapid exploration of viewpoint and TF space; bypasses explicit volume data; perceptually realistic outputs [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires large datasets for training; may lack physical consistency if not constrained (e.g., multi-pass variant addresses this) [S1]; limited resolution (256x256) in early variants; requires per-volume training in some implementations [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When rapid visualization of different TFs and viewpoints is needed without re-integrating large volumes, or for super-resolution tasks where perceptual quality is prioritized over strict physical accuracy.
*   **When not to use:** When exact physical accuracy is required, or when the volume data is too large to generate sufficient training examples, or when cross-volume generalization is needed without retraining.

## Background
The method was introduced by [S1] as a way to treat volume rendering as a purely computational process mapping viewpoint and TFs to images, effectively factoring out the volume data from the formulation [S1]. It builds on the DCGAN architecture [S1] and conditional GAN techniques [S1]. The two-stage variant was the first learned volume rendering system of its kind [S2, unofficial].
