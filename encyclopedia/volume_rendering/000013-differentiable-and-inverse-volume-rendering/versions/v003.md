# Differentiable and inverse volume rendering
## Overview
This family encompasses methods that treat volume rendering as a differentiable operation to solve inverse problems, optimizing 3D scene representations from 2D observations without requiring 3D ground truth supervision [Differentiable volume rendering](43-differentiable-volume-rendering.md). It sets itself apart from [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md) by focusing on the general differentiability of the volume rendering integral rather than specific neural field architectures, and from [Tomographic reconstruction as inverse rendering](45-tomographic-reconstruction-as-inverse-rendering.md) by encompassing both tomographic and non-tomographic (e.g., RGB-only) optimization loops [Differentiable volume rendering](43-differentiable-volume-rendering.md).
## Shared principles
All methods in this family rely on the differentiability of the forward rendering operator and the propagation of gradients through the integration steps [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md). The core mechanism is the evaluation of the volume rendering equation and the computation of gradients with respect to scene parameters (density, geometry, transfer functions, camera poses) [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md).

The standard emission-absorption model computes the color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ for a ray $\mathbf{r}$ as [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md):

$$
\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) \mathbf{c}_i
$$

where $\sigma_i$ is the density, $\delta_i$ is the step size, $\mathbf{c}_i$ is the color, and $T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$ is the transmittance [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md).

Differentiation strategies fall into three main categories [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md):
1. **Reverse-Mode Automatic Differentiation (AD):** Standard backpropagation through the computational graph. This requires storing $O(kn)$ intermediate states (where $n$ is pixels and $k$ is samples), leading to high memory usage [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md).
2. **Adjoint Method:** Computes gradients by propagating adjoint variables backward through the rendering algorithm, evaluating the chain rule from output to input. This has computational complexity largely constant in the number of parameters, making it preferable for optimizing many parameters like the transfer function [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md).
3. **Analytic Inversion / Explicit Gradients:** Uses a-priori knowledge of the DVR operations to invert the order of operations or derive closed-form gradients, reducing memory consumption to $O(n)$ by avoiding the storage of intermediate results [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md).
## Subtopics

### [Differentiable volume rendering](43-differentiable-volume-rendering.md)
Computes the gradient of a rendered image (or derived metric like depth) with respect to scene parameters by making the rendering integral differentiable. It solves the inverse problem of optimizing 3D scene representations from 2D observations without requiring 3D ground truth supervision. It sets itself apart from [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md) by focusing on the general differentiability of the volume rendering integral itself, rather than specific neural field architectures, and from [Tomographic reconstruction as inverse rendering](45-tomographic-reconstruction-as-inverse-rendering.md) by encompassing both tomographic and non-tomographic (e.g., RGB-only) optimization loops. Choose it when optimizing general volumetric scenes from RGB observations without strict tomographic constraints.

### [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md)
Reconstructs 3D scene representations (radiance fields) from 2D observations by minimizing the photometric error between rendered images and ground-truth inputs. It solves the inverse problem of determining scene parameters (density, color, geometry, or camera poses) such that the differentiable volume rendering equation produces images matching the input data. It sets itself apart from explicit forward rendering methods by treating the scene representation as a set of trainable parameters optimized via gradient descent, and from tomographic reconstruction by relying on the differentiability of the rendering operator rather than algebraic inversion of projection data. Choose it when reconstructing high-fidelity, view-dependent neural or implicit scene representations from multi-view images.

### [Tomographic reconstruction as inverse rendering](45-tomographic-reconstruction-as-inverse-rendering.md)
Treats computed tomography (CT) not as a linear algebra problem to be solved via closed-form filters or static iterative solvers, but as an optimization problem where a differentiable forward renderer (the projector) is minimized against measured projection data. This approach unifies classical algebraic reconstruction techniques (ART, SIRT) with modern neural representations and compressive sensing priors under a single gradient-based framework. It distinguishes itself from [Differentiable volume rendering](43-differentiable-volume-rendering.md) by focusing specifically on the tomographic geometry (parallel/fan/cone beam) and the reconstruction of density/absorption fields from sparse or noisy sinograms, and from [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md) by explicitly modeling the X-ray physics (Beer-Lambert law) rather than just RGB appearance. Choose it when reconstructing density or absorption fields from X-ray projection data.

## Background
The integration of differentiability into volume rendering emerged from the need to optimize 3D structures from 2D data without explicit 3D supervision [Differentiable volume rendering](43-differentiable-volume-rendering.md). Early work focused on analytic gradients for differentiable volume rendering [Differentiable volume rendering](43-differentiable-volume-rendering.md), while later developments unified tomographic reconstruction with inverse rendering techniques [Tomographic reconstruction as inverse rendering](45-tomographic-reconstruction-as-inverse-rendering.md). The field has evolved to support both neural implicit representations and explicit grid-based optimizations, driven by applications in novel view synthesis and medical imaging [G].
