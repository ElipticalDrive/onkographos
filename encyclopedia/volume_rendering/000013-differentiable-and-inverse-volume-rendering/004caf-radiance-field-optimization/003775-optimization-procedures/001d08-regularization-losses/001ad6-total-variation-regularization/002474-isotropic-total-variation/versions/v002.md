# Isotropic Total Variation

Also called: GroupL1Norm TV, Isotropic TV Reconstruction

## Overview
Isotropic Total Variation (ITV) is a regularization prior for volumetric data that sums the magnitude of the gradient at each voxel, treating the three spatial gradient components as a single group [S2, unofficial]. It enforces isotropic smoothing by using the Euclidean norm ($L_2$) on the gradient vector rather than the sum of absolute differences ($L_1$) [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- $\mathbf{f}$: The volume data vector of size $n = N_x \times N_y \times N_z$.
- $\lambda$: Regularization weight applied to the prior during optimization.

**Outputs:**
- Regularization energy $h(\mathbf{f})$.
- Proximal gradient update for optimization solvers.

**Data Structures:**
- $\mathbf{f}$: Array of shape $(N_x, N_y, N_z)$ or flattened vector of size $n$.
- $\mathbf{q}$: Gradient vector of size $3n$, storing forward differences in the $x$, $y$, and $z$ directions for each voxel.
- $\mathbf{E}$: Implicit operator mapping $\mathbf{q}$ to per-voxel gradient magnitudes. Explicit storage is not required; operations are computed on-the-fly [S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1. Compute forward differences $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{Df}$ for all voxels. For voxel $(i,j,k)$, $\mathbf{q}^v = [\mathbf{f}_{i+1,j,k}-\mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}, \mathbf{f}_{i,j+1,k}-\mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}, \mathbf{f}_{i,j,k+1}-\mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}]^T$ [S2, unofficial].
2. Compute the squared gradient magnitude per voxel: $m_v = \|\mathbf{q}^v\|_2 = \sqrt{q_{x}^2 + q_{y}^2 + q_{z}^2}$ [S2, unofficial].
3. The energy is $h(\mathbf{f}) = \sum_v m_v$ [S2, unofficial].
4. For optimization, compute the proximal step on $\mathbf{q}$ using the formula below, which scales each voxel's gradient vector by $\min(1, 1/m_v)$ when $m_v > 0$, and leaves it unchanged when $m_v = 0$ due to the denominator's lower bound [S2, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The ITV energy function is defined as:
$$ h(\mathbf{f}) = \sum_{i,j,k} \sqrt{|\mathbf{f}_{i+1,j,k} - \mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}|^2 + |\mathbf{f}_{i,j+1,k} - \mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}|^2 + |\mathbf{f}_{i,j,k+1} - \mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}|^2} $$
where $\mathbf{f}_{i,j,k}$ is the value at voxel $(i,j,k)$ [S2, unofficial].

Let $\mathbf{D}$ be the forward difference matrix and $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{Df}$. The norm $\|\mathbf{q}\|_E$ is defined as:
$$ \|\mathbf{w}\|_E = \|\sqrt{\mathbf{E}^T \mathbf{w}^2}\|_1 = \sum_v \|\mathbf{w}^v\|_2 $$
where $\mathbf{w}^v$ is the 3D gradient vector at voxel $v$, and the square root and square are component-wise [S2, unofficial].

The proximal operator for the convex conjugate $\tilde{g}^*$ is the projection onto the unit ball of the dual norm:
$$ \text{prox}_{\mu \tilde{g}^*}(\mathbf{q}) = \Pi_{B^*}(\mathbf{q}) = \frac{\mathbf{q}}{\mathbf{E}_{\max}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{E}^T \mathbf{q}^2}, 1\right)} $$
where division and max are performed component-wise [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
- `l1_norm` weight: 0.26 (regularization strength for isotropic TV using GroupL1Norm) [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Zero Gradient:** If $\mathbf{q}^v = \mathbf{0}$, the proximal operator denominator becomes $\max(0, 1) = 1$, leaving the zero gradient unchanged [S2, unofficial].
- **Boundary Conditions:** Forward differences at the last voxel in each dimension require boundary handling. Common approaches include zero-padding or clamping [G].
- **Precision:** Squaring small gradient values may lead to underflow; squaring large values may lead to overflow. Use stable computation if necessary [G].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def isotropic_tv_energy(f):
    # f is a 3D list or array-like of shape (Nx, Ny, Nz)
    fx = [[[0.0 for _ in range(len(f[0][0]))] for _ in range(len(f[0]))] for _ in range(len(f))]
    fy = [[[0.0 for _ in range(len(f[0][0]))] for _ in range(len(f[0]))] for _ in range(len(f))]
    fz = [[[0.0 for _ in range(len(f[0][0]))] for _ in range(len(f[0]))] for _ in range(len(f))]
    
    for i in range(len(f) - 1):
        for j in range(len(f[0])):
            for k in range(len(f[0][0])):
                fx[i][j][k] = f[i+1][j][k] - f[i][j][k]
    for i in range(len(f)):
        for j in range(len(f[0]) - 1):
            for k in range(len(f[0][0])):
                fy[i][j][k] = f[i][j+1][k] - f[i][j][k]
    for i in range(len(f)):
        for j in range(len(f[0])):
            for k in range(len(f[0][0]) - 1):
                fz[i][j][k] = f[i][j][k+1] - f[i][j][k]
                
    energy = 0.0
    for i in range(len(f)):
        for j in range(len(f[0])):
            for k in range(len(f[0][0])):
                mag = (fx[i][j][k]**2 + fy[i][j][k]**2 + fz[i][j][k]**2)**0.5
                energy += mag
    return energy

def prox_isotropic_tv(q, mu):
    # q is a 3D list or array-like of shape (3, Nx, Ny, Nz)
    # q[0] is x-diff, q[1] is y-diff, q[2] is z-diff
    prox_q = [[[0.0 for _ in range(len(q[0][0]))] for _ in range(len(q[0]))] for _ in range(3)]
    for i in range(len(q[0])):
        for j in range(len(q[0][0])):
            for k in range(len(q[0][0][0])):
                mag = (q[0][i][j][k]**2 + q[1][i][j][k]**2 + q[2][i][j][k]**2)**0.5
                scale = min(1.0, 1.0 / mag) if mag > 0 else 1.0
                prox_q[0][i][j][k] = q[0][i][j][k] * scale
                prox_q[1][i][j][k] = q[1][i][j][k] * scale
                prox_q[2][i][j][k] = q[2][i][j][k] * scale
    return prox_q
```

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(n)$ for energy computation and proximal operator, where $n$ is the number of voxels.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(n)$ to store the volume and gradients. No explicit matrix storage required [S2, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Directionally invariant smoothing, effective at removing noise while preserving edges.
- **Limitations:** May oversmooth fine details compared to anisotropic methods. Computationally more intensive than ATV due to square root and vector norm computations.
- **When to Use:** When isotropic smoothing is desired, such as in CT reconstruction or volume rendering regularization where directional bias is undesirable [S1, S2, unofficial].
- **When Not to Use:** When computational resources are extremely limited, or when directional features are important and should be preserved differently.

## Background
Isotropic Total Variation was introduced in the context of CT reconstruction and sparse view super-resolution [S2, unofficial]. It is based on the work of Zang et al. (2018–2019) on space-time tomography and warp-and-project tomography [S2, unofficial]. The method uses proximal algorithms for optimization, leveraging the convex conjugate and proximal operator of the ITV prior [S2, unofficial]. It is widely used in tomographic reconstruction and volume rendering regularization to prevent artifacts [S1, S2, unofficial].
