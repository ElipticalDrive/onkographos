# Perceptual Loss Training

Also called: Feature Matching Objective, PIPS Loss, Perceptual Distance Loss, Perceptual Loss for 3DGS, Perceptual loss for dynamic NeRF

## Overview
Perceptual Loss Training is an optimization strategy that incorporates a perceptual loss term to improve the visual fidelity of rendered images, addressing the smoothness and texture distortion issues inherent in pixel-wise losses like L1 or L2 [S4, S5, S6]. It sets itself apart from its siblings by leveraging features from pre-trained convolutional neural networks (typically VGG) to enforce high-level structural consistency, whereas methods like [Total Variation Regularization](860-total-variation-regularization.md) operate directly on spatial gradients of the output or scene parameters [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I}$ (prediction), ground truth image $I_g$, pre-trained feature extractor network $\Phi$ (e.g., VGG-19), and loss weights $\lambda_l$ or $w_{\cdot}$ [S2, S5, S6].
*   **Output:** Scalar loss value $\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}$ used for backpropagation [S2, S5, S6].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Feature Maps:** Intermediate activations from the pre-trained network [S6].
*   **Weights:** Scalars $\lambda_l$ balancing the contribution of each layer's feature distance [S2, S5].

**Steps:**
1.  **Feature Extraction:** Pass both the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and the ground truth image $I_g$ through the pre-trained network $\Phi$ [S5]. Extract feature maps from a set of layers $L$ [S2, S5, S6].
    $$ \mathbf{F}_{\hat{I}}^l = \Phi_l(\hat{I}), \quad \mathbf{F}_{I_g}^l = \Phi_l(I_g) \quad \forall l \in L $$
2.  **Distance Calculation:** Compute the distance between the feature maps of the prediction and the ground truth [S5]. The L1 norm is commonly used [S2, S5, S6].
    $$ D^l = \| \mathbf{F}_{\hat{I}}^l - \mathbf{F}_{I_g}^l \|_1 $$
3.  **Aggregation:** Sum the distances weighted by layer-specific coefficients $\lambda_l$ [S2, S5, S6].
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}} = \sum_{l \in L} \lambda_l D^l $$

**Formulas:**
The general perceptual loss is defined as [S2, S5]:
$$ L(P_f, \theta) = \sum_l \lambda_l \| \Phi_l(I_g) - \Phi_l(f(P_f; \theta)) \|_1 $$
where $P_f$ represents the scene parameters (e.g., point features), $\theta$ are the network parameters, $f$ is the rendering function, and $\Phi_l$ are the VGG-19 layers [S2, S5]. Some formulations normalize the distance by the total number of features $N_l$ in layer $l$ [S3, unofficial]:
$$ L_{\text{perc}}(G) = \mathbb{E}_{x,y} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \lambda_t \frac{1}{N_t} \| F^{(t)}(G(x)) - F^{(t)}(y) \|_1 $$
where $F^{(t)}$ is the feature extractor in the $t$-th layer of the pretrained network $F$ with $T$ layers total [S3, unofficial].

In the context of Gaussian Splatting (PIPS), the total loss combines L1, SSIM, and Perceptual (PIPS) terms [S4]:
$$ \mathcal{L} = (1 - \lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda_1\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}} + \lambda_2\mathcal{L}_{\text{PIPS}} $$
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PIPS}}$ is the perceptual loss term [S4].

For large reconstruction models (GS-LRM), the loss combines MSE and Perceptual terms over $M$ supervision views [S8]:
$$ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i'=1}^M \left( \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{I}^*_{i'}, I^*_{i'}) + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{Perceptual}(\hat{I}^*_{i'}, I^*_{i'}) \right) $$
where $\lambda$ is the perceptual loss weight [S8].

**Parameters:**
*   **VGG-19 Layers:** Commonly used layers include 'input', 'conv1-2', 'conv2-2', 'conv3-2', 'conv4-2', and 'conv5-2' [S2, S5].
*   **Weights ($\lambda_l$):** Balancing weights for each layer [S2, S5]. Specific values follow the scheme in Chen et al. [S5].
*   **Weights ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2$):** In the PIPS formulation, $\lambda_1$ weights the SSIM term and $\lambda_2$ weights the perceptual term [S4]. Exact values are not given [S4].
*   **Optimization:** Adam optimizer is typically used [S2, S5, S7].
    *   Learning rate initialized at 0.01, decreased every 7 epochs: $0.01 \to 0.005 \to 0.001$ [S2, S5].
    *   DINER uses a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ [S7].

**Pitfalls:**
*   **Computational Overhead:** CNN-based perceptual loss computation is expensive [S4]. It is recommended to compute it every 20 training iterations to alleviate overhead without significantly impacting quality [S4].
*   **Color Shifts:** Perceptual loss can introduce color shifts in the rendered output [S7]. This may require auxiliary terms like Anti-Bias Loss to correct [S7].
*   **Visual Quality Degradation:** Omitting perceptual loss increases LPIPS distance, indicating degraded visual quality even if pixel-wise metrics (PSNR/SSIM) remain acceptable [S6]. The learned generator using $L_p$ norm tends to synthesize blurry images or average results over multiple plausible outputs [S6].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_perceptual_loss(rendered, ground_truth, vgg_layers, weights):
    # [G] Standard VGG preprocessing: normalize inputs by ImageNet mean and std.
    loss = 0.0
    for layer in vgg_layers:
        # [G] Standard VGG forward pass to extract layer activations
        feat_pred = get_vgg_features(rendered, layer)
        feat_gt = get_vgg_features(ground_truth, layer)
        # Compute mean L1 distance over all spatial and channel dimensions
        diff_sum = 0.0
        count = 0
        for c in range(feat_pred.shape[0]):
            for h in range(feat_pred.shape[1]):
                for w in range(feat_pred.shape[2]):
                    diff_sum += abs(feat_pred[c, h, w] - feat_gt[c, h, w])
                    count += 1
        l1_dist = diff_sum / count
        loss += weights[layer] * l1_dist
    return loss

def compute_pips_loss(rendered, ground_truth, vgg_layers, weights, lambda1, lambda2):
    # L1 loss
    l1_loss = sum(abs(rendered[i] - ground_truth[i]) for i in range(len(rendered))) / len(rendered)
    # [G] SSIM is a standard structural similarity metric; compute as 1 - SSIM(rendered, ground_truth)
    ssim_loss = 1.0 - compute_ssim(rendered, ground_truth)
    # Perceptual loss
    pips_loss = compute_perceptual_loss(rendered, ground_truth, vgg_layers, weights)
    # Total loss
    return (1 - lambda1 - lambda2) * l1_loss + lambda1 * ssim_loss + lambda2 * pips_loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Stable View Synthesis (SVS) Baseline](2483-stable-view-synthesis-svs-baseline.md)
Stable View Synthesis (SVS) is a view synthesis method that blends training images together to render novel views, relying on proxy geometry from an external system. It is distinguished from its siblings by its explicit dependence on external proxy geometry (typically piecewise planar meshes) and access to all training images, whereas [High-Frequency Aware Loss](7589-high-frequency-aware-loss.md) and [WD-Regularized Loss](15613-wd-regularized-loss.md) operate within different optimization frameworks without this specific blending-and-proxy requirement.

### [High-Frequency Aware Loss](7589-high-frequency-aware-loss.md)
High-Frequency Aware Loss (LHF) is a perceptual regularization term designed to recover high-frequency details in rendered images while avoiding the texture distortion common in deeper perceptual losses. It sets itself apart from standard VGG-based perceptual losses by restricting feature extraction to the first block of the network, thereby preserving fine structural details without enforcing semantic texture consistency that may conflict with the ground truth. It builds on [Perceptual Loss Training](2000-perceptual-loss-training.md) by providing a differentiable proxy for high-frequency similarity.

### [WD-Regularized Loss](15613-wd-regularized-loss.md)
WD-Regularized Loss (WD-R) is a training objective for 3D Gaussian Splatting that combines the Wasserstein Distance (WD) perceptual loss with a lightly weighted version of the original pixel-level loss. It solves the problem of visual artifacts that occur in regions with constrained Gaussian splat counts or limited training views, where the pure WD loss may fail to enforce sufficient pixel fidelity. Unlike its sibling [Stable View Synthesis (SVS) Baseline](2483-stable-view-synthesis-svs-baseline.md) which focuses on gradient stability, or [High-Frequency Aware Loss](7589-high-frequency-aware-loss.md) which targets high-frequency details, WD-R specifically balances perceptual fidelity against raw pixel error to maintain compact representations without sacrificing sharpness.

### Anti-Bias Loss
An l1 loss applied to downsampled versions of prediction and ground truth to eliminate color shifts introduced by perceptual loss while remaining robust against minor misalignments [S7].
**Formula:**
Calculate $L_{ab}$ as the l1 distance between k-fold downsampled ground truth patch $P$ and predicted patch $\hat{P}$:
$$ L_{ab} = \| DSk(P) - DSk(\hat{P}) \|_1 $$
where $DSk(\cdot)$ denotes k-fold downsampling [S7].
The full objective function is:
$$ L = w_{l1} \cdot L_{l1} + w_{vgg} \cdot L_{vgg} + w_{ab} \cdot L_{ab} $$
where $L_{l1}$ is pixel-wise l1 distance, $L_{vgg}$ is perceptual loss, and $w_{\cdot}$ are weights [S7].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** High [G]. Perceptual loss requires forward passes through a deep CNN (VGG-19) for both the rendered and ground truth images [S4]. Computing it every 20 iterations is necessary to alleviate this overhead [S4]. Training takes on average 41.5 hours per scene on one GeForce 1080 Ti [S5].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Performance:** Improves perceptual quality (LPIPS) significantly compared to pixel-wise losses [S6]. However, it may introduce color shifts if not regularized (e.g., with Anti-Bias Loss) [S7].
*   **Strengths:** Better visual fidelity, recovers high-frequency details, reduces structural artifacts [S1, S6].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive, prone to color shifts, requires careful tuning of weights [S4, S7].
*   **When to use:** When visual quality is prioritized over training speed, and when pixel-wise losses result in overly smooth or blurry outputs [S5, S6].
*   **When not to use:** When real-time training is required, or when color accuracy is critical and auxiliary bias correction is not feasible [S4, S7].

## Background
Perceptual loss was originally introduced for image generation tasks [S6]. In the context of volume rendering and radiance fields, it has been adopted to address the "smoothness" problem of L1/L2 losses, which often produce blurry results [S4, S5]. It is used in various NeRF and Gaussian Splatting optimizations to enhance high-frequency details and structural consistency [S4, S5, S7, S8].
