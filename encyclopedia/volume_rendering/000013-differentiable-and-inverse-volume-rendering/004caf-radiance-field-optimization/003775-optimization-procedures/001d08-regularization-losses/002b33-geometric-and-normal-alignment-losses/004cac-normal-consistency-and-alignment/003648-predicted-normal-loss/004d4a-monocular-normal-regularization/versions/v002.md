# Monocular Normal Regularization

Also called: Monocular Normal Loss, Omnidata Normal Supervision, Predicted Normal Loss (Monocular), Stable Normal supervision

## Overview
Monocular Normal Regularization is a geometric supervision technique that constrains the predicted surface normals of a volume rendering or radiance field optimization by comparing them against smooth normal estimates derived from a pretrained monocular vision model [S1, S2]. Unlike methods that derive ground-truth normals from the gradients of rendered depth maps—which are often noisy and artifact-prone in complex scenes—this method leverages the semantic and geometric priors of foundation models to provide stable, smooth normal cues [S1]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Predicted Normal Loss](7713-predicted-normal-loss.md) by replacing the rendered normal (which may be inconsistent with the underlying geometry) with an external monocular prediction, thereby decoupling the supervision signal from the noisy differentiable rendering pipeline [S1, S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:**
    *   $\hat{\boldsymbol{N}}$: The predicted normals from the scene representation (e.g., Gaussian splats or neural field).
    *   $\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}$: The monocular normal estimate from a pretrained model (e.g., Omnidata [S1] or Stable Normal [S2]).
    *   $m$: A margin parameter to handle view-dependent inconsistencies in monocular predictions [S2].
    *   $\lambda_n$: The weight for the monocular normal loss in the total objective [S2].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   $\mathcal{L}_{mono}$: The scalar loss value.

**Data Structures:**
*   $\hat{\boldsymbol{N}}$: A tensor of shape $(H, W, 3)$ containing unit normal vectors for each pixel.
*   $\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}$: A tensor of shape $(H, W, 3)$ containing unit normal vectors from the monocular network.

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Predicted Normals:** Extract normals from the scene representation. For Gaussian Splatting, this is often derived from the scaling axes. If a Gaussian $i$ has rotation matrix $R_i$ and scaling coefficients $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, the normal is approximated by the axis with the smallest scale [S1]:
    $$ \hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_i = R_i \cdot \text{OneHot}(\arg\min(s_{i,1}, s_{i,2}, s_{i,3})) $$
    [S1]
2.  **Retrieve Monocular Cues:** Pass the input image through the pretrained monocular model to obtain $\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}$. [S1, S2]
3.  **Compute Loss:** Calculate the discrepancy between $\hat{\boldsymbol{N}}$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}$. Two common formulations exist:
    *   **L1 Loss:** Used in DN-Splatter [S1]:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{\hat{N}} = \frac{1}{|\hat{N}|} \sum \| \hat{\boldsymbol{N}} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{N}} \|_1 $$
        [S1]
    *   **Margin-based Loss:** Used in OMeGa [S2]:
        $$ L_n = \sum \max(\| \boldsymbol{N} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{N}} \| - m, 0) $$
        where $\boldsymbol{N}$ is the predicted normal, $\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}$ is the monocular cue, and $m$ is a small margin [S2].
4.  **Update Objective:** Add the weighted loss to the total training objective:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \dots + \lambda_n \mathcal{L}_{mono} $$
    [S2]

**Parameters:**
*   $m$: A small margin value to handle inherent normal inconsistency across different views produced by monocular foundational models. [S2]
*   $\lambda_n$: The weight controlling the influence of the monocular normal loss. [S2]

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Monocular Inconsistency:** Monocular models may produce normals that are inconsistent across different views or slightly misaligned with the true 3D geometry. The margin $m$ in the OMeGa formulation helps mitigate this by ignoring small discrepancies [S2].
*   **Noise in Gradient-Based Normals:** The primary motivation for this method is the noise in rendered depth gradients. If the monocular model is not used, artifacts may appear in the reconstruction [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def monocular_normal_loss(predicted_normals, monocular_normals, margin=0.0, use_margin=False):
    """
    Computes the monocular normal regularization loss.
    
    Args:
        predicted_normals: Tensor of shape (H, W, 3), unit normals from scene representation.
        monocular_normals: Tensor of shape (H, W, 3), normals from pretrained monocular model.
        margin: Float, margin for hinge-like loss (default 0.0).
        use_margin: Bool, whether to use the margin-based loss from OMeGa [S2].
        
    Returns:
        Scalar loss value.
    """
    # Compute element-wise L1 distance
    diff = torch.abs(predicted_normals - monocular_normals)
    
    if use_margin:
        # OMeGa formulation: max(||N - N_hat|| - m, 0)
        # Note: The source [S2] uses L2 norm for the difference magnitude inside max
        l2_diff = torch.norm(predicted_normals - monocular_normals, dim=-1)
        loss_per_pixel = torch.clamp(l2_diff - margin, min=0.0)
        return torch.mean(loss_per_pixel)
    else:
        # DN-Splatter formulation: L1 loss
        # Source [S1] uses L1 norm on the vector difference, which is sum of abs diffs
        # Normalized by number of pixels (|N|)
        return torch.mean(diff)
```
[G] The pseudocode assumes `torch.norm` computes the L2 norm of the vector difference as implied by the OMeGa formula $\|N - \hat{N}\|$. The DN-Splatter formula $\|\hat{N} - N\|_1$ is interpreted as the sum of absolute differences per component, averaged over pixels.

## Subtopics

### Depth and Normal Losses
This variant, described in Ray-Distance Volume Rendering [S3], extends monocular supervision to include depth. It constrains 3D geometry by comparing rendered depth $D$ and normals $\mathbf{N}$ against ground truth estimates $\bar{D}, \bar{\mathbf{N}}$ from a pretrained Omnidata model [S3]. The rendered values are computed via transmittance-weighted accumulation: $D = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i \alpha_i z_i$ and $\mathbf{N} = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{n}_i$, where $T_i, \alpha_i$ are transmittance and opacity weights from SRDF density [S3]. The loss functions are:
$$ \mathcal{L}_d = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{S}^2} \| (w D + q) - \bar{D} \|_2 $$
$$ \mathcal{L}_n = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{S}^2} \left( \| \mathbf{N} - \bar{\mathbf{N}} \|_1 + \| 1 - \mathbf{N}^T \bar{\mathbf{N}} \|_1 \right) $$
[S3]
Parameters $w$ (scale) and $q$ (shift) are optimized per minibatch to resolve depth scale and shift ambiguities, following MonoSDF [S3]. This approach is chosen when both depth and normal consistency are critical for surface reconstruction, particularly in neural SDF-based rendering [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The additional cost is the forward pass of the pretrained monocular model, which is independent of the scene resolution in terms of gradient computation but adds inference time proportional to the image size. [S1, S2]
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the monocular normal map $\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}$ in memory during training. [S1, S2]
*   **Performance:** Improves surface quality and reduces artifacts compared to gradient-based normal supervision, especially in textureless or complex regions [S1, S2].
*   **Strengths:** Provides smooth, semantically consistent normals; robust to noise in rendered depth. [S1, S2]
*   **Limitations:** Relies on the quality of the pretrained monocular model; may introduce biases if the model's priors conflict with the scene geometry; monocular normals may be inconsistent across views [S2].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality surface normals are required for mesh extraction or rendering, and gradient-based normals are too noisy. [S1, S2]
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and the monocular model inference is prohibitive; or when the scene contains strong specularities that confuse monocular normal estimation.

## Background
Monocular normal regularization was introduced to address the limitations of using gradients of rendered depth maps for normal supervision, which are prone to noise and artifacts [S1]. DN-Splatter [S1] first proposed using Omnidata [7] for this purpose. OMeGa [S2] later adopted similar principles using Stable Normal [42] and introduced a margin-based loss to handle view inconsistencies. Ray-Distance Volume Rendering [S3] extended this to include depth supervision with affine alignment. These methods build on the broader trend of using pretrained foundation models for geometric priors in 3D reconstruction [S1, S2, S3].
