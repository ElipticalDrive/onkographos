# Predicted Normal Loss

Also called: Predicted Normal Consistency Loss

## Overview
Predicted Normal Loss is a geometric regularization term used in differentiable volume rendering that supervises the consistency between normals explicitly predicted by the neural network and normals derived from the rendered scene geometry [S1, unofficial]. It ensures that the surface orientation inferred from the radiance field aligns with the network's own geometric predictions, stabilizing training in inverse rendering pipelines [S1, unofficial]. Unlike sibling methods that align normals with ray directions, this loss focuses on internal geometric coherence between predicted and derived surface normals [S1, 4202-normal-ray-alignment-loss.md, unofficial].

## How it works
The method computes a weighted L2 loss between predicted normals and derived normals for each sample along a ray.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Predicted normals $\mathbf{n}^{\text{pred}}$, derived normals $\mathbf{n}^{\text{derived}}$, render weights $w_k$, and loss multiplier $C_{\text{normals}}$.
- **Outputs:** Scalar loss value $\mathcal{L}_{\text{normals}}$.

**Data Structures:**
- $\mathbf{n}^{\text{pred}}$: Vector of shape $(3)$, normalized, representing the normal predicted by the network at position $\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}$.
- $\mathbf{n}^{\text{derived}}$: Vector of shape $(3)$, normalized, computed from the density gradient.
- $w_k$: Scalar weight for sample $k$, typically the alpha weight from volume rendering integration.

**Steps:**
1. For each sample $k$ along a ray:
   a. Retrieve the predicted normal $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}}$ from the network output.
   b. Compute the density gradient $\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}_k})$ at the sample position $\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}_k}$.
   c. Normalize the gradient to obtain the derived normal:
      $$ \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}} = -\frac{\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}_k})}{\| \nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}_k}) \|} $$
      [S2]
   d. Compute the squared Euclidean distance between $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}}$ and $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}}$.
   e. Weight the distance by the render weight $w_k$.
2. Sum the weighted distances over all samples $k$.
3. Multiply the sum by the loss weight $C_{\text{normals}}$.

**Formula:**
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{normals}} = C_{\text{normals}} \sum_{k} w_k \| \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}} - \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}} \|^2 $$
[S2]

**Parameters:**
- $C_{\text{normals}}$: Loss multiplier. Values vary by dataset:
  - Open Illumination: $1.0$ [S2].
  - TensoIR-synthetic: Linearly interpolates from $0.0001$ to $1.0$ between iteration $20,000$ and $40,000$ [S2].
- $w_k$: Render weights for sample $k$ [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Zero Gradient:** If $\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}_k})$ is near zero (e.g., in empty space or uniform density), the derived normal is undefined. A guard must prevent division by zero, typically by skipping the sample or using a small epsilon [G].
- **Precision:** Gradient computation requires differentiable density evaluation. Numerical instability may arise if the density field is not smooth [S2].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_predicted_normal_loss(pred_normals, density_field, positions, weights, loss_weight):
    """
    Compute the Predicted Normal Loss.
    
    Args:
        pred_normals: Tensor of shape (N, 3), predicted normals for N samples.
        density_field: Function or module that returns density sigma(x) and gradient nabla sigma(x).
        positions: Tensor of shape (N, 3), sample positions.
        weights: Tensor of shape (N,), render weights w_k.
        loss_weight: Scalar C_normals.
        
    Returns:
        Scalar loss value.
    """
    # Compute density and its gradient
    sigma, grad_sigma = density_field(positions)
    
    # Compute derived normals
    # Avoid division by zero by adding epsilon
    grad_norm = torch.norm(grad_sigma, dim=1, keepdim=True) + 1e-8
    derived_normals = -grad_sigma / grad_norm
    
    # Compute L2 distance between predicted and derived normals
    # pred_normals and derived_normals are already normalized
    diff = pred_normals - derived_normals
    squared_dist = torch.sum(diff ** 2, dim=1)
    
    # Weight by render weights
    weighted_loss = weights * squared_dist
    
    # Sum over all samples
    total_loss = torch.sum(weighted_loss)
    
    # Apply loss weight
    final_loss = loss_weight * total_loss
    
    return final_loss
```

## Subtopics

### Density Gradient Normal Loss
An L2 loss constraining predicted normals to match the negative gradient of the density field [S2]. It is the specific formulation used in Flash Cache [S2]. It sets apart from generic predicted normal loss by explicitly defining the derived normal via the density gradient $\nabla \sigma$ rather than a separate normal prediction head [S2]. Choose this when the density field is differentiable and you want to enforce geometric consistency with the underlying scalar field [S2]. See [Flash Cache: Reducing Bias in Radiance Cache Based Inverse Rendering](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-73390-1_2).

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of samples, due to gradient computation and element-wise operations [S2].
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store gradients and normals [S2].
- **Performance:** Stabilizes training in inverse rendering by providing geometric supervision [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Enforces geometric consistency without requiring ground-truth normals [S1, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** Relies on the differentiability and smoothness of the density field [S2]. May be sensitive to noise in gradient estimation [S2].
- **When to use:** When training radiance fields with explicit normal predictions and density gradients are available [S2].
- **When not to use:** When the density field is not differentiable or when normals are derived from other sources (e.g., ray alignment) [S1, 4202-normal-ray-alignment-loss.md, unofficial].

## Background
Predicted Normal Loss originates from differentiable volume rendering techniques that incorporate geometric regularization [S1, unofficial]. It is used in frameworks like GaNeRF to improve the quality of rendered normals and surface reconstruction [S1, unofficial]. The specific formulation using density gradients is detailed in Flash Cache for inverse rendering [S2].
