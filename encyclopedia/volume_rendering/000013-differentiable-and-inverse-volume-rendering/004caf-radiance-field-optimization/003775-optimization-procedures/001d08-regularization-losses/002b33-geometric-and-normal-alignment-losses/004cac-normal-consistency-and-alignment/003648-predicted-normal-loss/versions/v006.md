# Predicted Normal Loss

Also called: Predicted Normal Consistency Loss

## Overview
Predicted Normal Loss is a geometric regularization term used in radiance-field optimization that supervises the consistency between normals predicted by the neural network and normals derived from the rendered geometry [S1, unofficial][S2]. It addresses the problem of geometric artifacts and noise in rendered depth and normal maps by enforcing that the explicit normal predictions align with the implicit surface gradients of the density field [S1, unofficial][S2]. Unlike sibling methods that rely on gradient-based normal estimates from rendered depth or ground truth alignment, this method directly constrains the network's predicted output against the negative density gradient [S1, unofficial][S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Rendered ray weights $w_k$, predicted normals $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}}$, and the density field $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ evaluated at sample positions $\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k$.
- **Outputs:** A scalar loss value $\mathcal{L}_{\text{normals}}$.

**Data Structures:**
- $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}}$: The normal vector predicted by the network for the $k$-th sample along a ray.
- $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k)$: The scalar density value at the sample position.
- $\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k)$: The spatial gradient of the density field at the sample position.

**Steps:**
1. Compute the derived normal $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}}$ by taking the negative gradient of the density field and normalizing it:
   $$
   \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}} = -\frac{\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k)}{\| \nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k) \|}
   $$
   [S2]
2. Compute the L2 distance between the predicted normal and the derived normal, weighted by the render weight $w_k$.
3. Sum the weighted distances over all samples $k$ and rays.
4. Multiply the sum by the loss weight coefficient $C_{\text{normals}}$.

**Formula:**
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{normals}} = C_{\text{normals}} \sum_{k} w_k \| \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}} - \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}} \|^2
$$
[S2]

**Parameters:**
- $w_k$: Render weight for the $k$-th sample [S2].
- $C_{\text{normals}}$: Loss multiplier. For the Open Illumination dataset, $C_{\text{normals}} = 1.0$. For the TensoIR-synthetic dataset, it linearly interpolates from $0.0001$ to $1.0$ from iteration $20,000$ to $40,000$ [S2].
- `pred_normal_loss_mult`: A multiplier for the predicted normal loss in the total loss computation [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_predicted_normal_loss(
    predicted_normals: Tensor,  # Shape: [num_samples, 3]
    density_field: Callable,    # Function returning density and gradient
    sample_positions: Tensor,   # Shape: [num_samples, 3]
    render_weights: Tensor,     # Shape: [num_samples]
    loss_weight: float          # C_normals
) -> Tensor:
    """
    Computes the Predicted Normal Loss.
    
    Args:
        predicted_normals: The normals predicted by the network.
        density_field: A function that takes positions and returns (density, gradient).
        sample_positions: The 3D positions of the samples.
        render_weights: The alpha/weight contribution of each sample.
        loss_weight: The scalar multiplier C_normals.
        
    Returns:
        The computed loss scalar.
    """
    # Compute density and its gradient at sample positions
    density, density_gradient = density_field(sample_positions)
    
    # Compute derived normals: negative gradient normalized
    # Handle degenerate cases where gradient norm is near zero
    grad_norm = torch.norm(density_gradient, dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    # [G] Add epsilon to prevent division by zero
    grad_norm = torch.clamp(grad_norm, min=1e-8)
    derived_normals = -density_gradient / grad_norm
    
    # Compute L2 distance between predicted and derived normals
    # predicted_normals and derived_normals are both [num_samples, 3]
    normal_diff = predicted_normals - derived_normals
    l2_distance = torch.sum(normal_diff ** 2, dim=-1)
    
    # Weight by render weights and sum
    weighted_loss = torch.sum(render_weights * l2_distance)
    
    # Apply loss weight
    total_loss = loss_weight * weighted_loss
    
    return total_loss
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Degenerate Gradients:** If $\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x})$ is zero (e.g., in empty space or uniform density regions), the derived normal is undefined. A small epsilon must be added to the norm denominator to prevent NaN gradients [G].
- **Precision:** The gradient computation $\nabla \sigma$ requires differentiable density evaluation. Ensure the density field supports automatic differentiation [S2].

## Subtopics

### [Monocular Normal Regularization](13425-monocular-normal-regularization.md)
Monocular Normal Regularization replaces noisy gradient-based normal estimates with smooth normal cues predicted by pretrained monocular foundation models (e.g., Omnidata, Stable Normal). It solves the problem of artifacts in rendered depth and normal maps, particularly in complex scenes, by providing stable geometric priors during radiance-field optimization. Unlike sibling methods that rely on rendered depth gradients, this approach leverages external monocular networks to guide the optimization of scene geometry. See [Monocular Normal Regularization](13425-monocular-normal-regularization.md).

### Density Gradient Normal Loss
An L2 loss constraining predicted normals to match the negative gradient of the density field [S2]. It uses the formula $\mathcal{L}_{\text{normals}} = C_{\text{normals}} \sum_{k} w_k \| \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{pred}} - \mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}} \|^2$ where $\mathbf{n}_k^{\text{derived}} = -\frac{\nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k)}{\| \nabla \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{\text{pos}}_k) \|}$ [S2]. The loss weight $C_{\text{normals}}$ varies per dataset: $1.0$ for Open Illumination, and linearly interpolated from $0.0001$ to $1.0$ for TensoIR-synthetic [S2].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
The complexity is dominated by the evaluation of the density field and its gradient at the sample positions. For $N$ samples, it is $O(N)$ in both time and memory, assuming the density field evaluation is $O(1)$ per sample.

**Measured Performance:**
Specific performance metrics are not provided in the sources. The method is used in conjunction with other losses in radiance field optimization pipelines [S1, unofficial][S2].

**Scaling:**
The loss scales linearly with the number of samples and rays. The weight $C_{\text{normals}}$ can be adjusted to balance the influence of this term against other losses [S2].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
- **Strengths:** Enforces geometric consistency between explicit predictions and implicit density gradients, reducing artifacts [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Limitations:** Relies on the quality of the density gradient, which can be noisy in sparse or unoptimized regions [S2].

**When to Use:**
Use when geometric consistency is critical and the density field is sufficiently optimized to provide meaningful gradients [S2].

## Background
The method originates from work on radiance field optimization, specifically in the context of Flash Cache and GANerf [S1, unofficial][S2]. It builds on the idea of emitting predicted normals from the density field, similar to Ref-NeRF and TensoIR [S2]. The approach was developed to reduce bias in radiance cache based inverse rendering [S2].
