# Regularization losses

Also called: Auxiliary losses, Regularization terms

## Overview
Regularization losses are auxiliary objective terms added to the primary photometric reconstruction loss (e.g., MSE or L1) during the optimization of radiance fields to constrain the learned representation beyond pixel-level fidelity [S1][S2, unofficial]. They solve the problem of overfitting to noisy observations, degenerate solutions (such as exploding uncertainty or non-compact geometry), and visual artifacts like "floaters" or "holes" [S1][S2, unofficial]. Unlike sibling methods that focus on spatial smoothness (Total Variation) or geometric alignment, this family encompasses a broad spectrum of constraints including uncertainty penalization, structural priors, and perceptual metrics [S1][S2, unofficial].

## Shared principles
All methods in this family augment the total loss function $\mathcal{L}_{total}$ with one or more regularization terms $\mathcal{L}_{reg}^{(k)}$ weighted by hyperparameters $\lambda_k$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photo} + \sum_k \lambda_k \mathcal{L}_{reg}^{(k)}
$$

The core mechanism is the differentiability of these terms with respect to the scene parameters (density, geometry, transfer functions, camera poses), allowing gradients to propagate through the rendering operator [Parent](44-radiance-field-optimization.md). The specific form of $\mathcal{L}_{reg}^{(k)}$ varies by implementation goal:
1.  **Spatial/Structural Priors:** Penalize gradients or deviations in the spatial domain (e.g., Total Variation, Beta priors) to enforce smoothness or sparsity [S3].
2.  **Geometric Priors:** Enforce physical constraints on depth, normals, or surface alignment (e.g., Eikonal loss, Chamfer distance) [S2, unofficial][S1].
3.  **Uncertainty/Confidence Priors:** Penalize predicted uncertainty values to prevent them from blowing up or to model aleatoric noise (e.g., Zero Regularization Loss) [S1][S2, unofficial].
4.  **Perceptual/Feature Priors:** Compare high-level features rather than raw pixels to improve visual quality [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Total Variation Regularization](860-total-variation-regularization.md)
Total Variation (TV) regularization is a loss term applied to explicit radiance field representations (voxel grids, Gaussian splats) to suppress visual artifacts such as "floaters" (spurious geometry) and "holes" (missing geometry). It encourages spatial smoothness by penalizing large gradients between neighboring voxels. Unlike implicit MLP-based methods, explicit representations are more prone to these artifacts due to the non-convex optimization of high-dimensional discrete grids. TV regularization operates directly on the 3D spatial structure of the volume, distinguishing it from sibling methods like Distortion Loss (which penalizes depth ambiguity along rays) or MSE/SSIM (which enforce pixel-level photometric consistency).

### [Efficient O(N) Distortion Loss](1051-efficient-o-n-distortion-loss.md)
The Efficient O(N) Distortion Loss is a regularization term for differentiable volume rendering that prevents "floaters" (small, disconnected regions of volumetrically dense space) and "background collapse" (distant surfaces modeled as semi-transparent clouds close to the camera). It minimizes the integral of distances between all pairs of points along the 1D step function parameterized by ray distances and weights. It sets itself apart from naive implementations by reducing computational complexity from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N)$, enabling the handling of point-based queries with large numbers of samples (e.g., >256) without excessive GPU memory consumption. Unlike NeRF's noise injection regularizer, it more effectively eliminates artifacts without reducing reconstruction quality.

### [MSE Loss and PSNR Conversion](1651-mse-loss-and-psnr-conversion.md)
This method computes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and target RGB values to serve as the primary photometric loss for training neural volume rendering models, and converts this error into Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) for logging and evaluation. It distinguishes itself from sibling regularization methods by providing the fundamental differentiable objective function that drives gradient-based optimization, rather than enforcing structural or geometric constraints. Unlike [SSIM Metric Calculation](3650-ssim-metric-calculation.md) which models structural similarity, MSE treats all pixel errors equally, making it sensitive to large smooth regions but less responsive to high-frequency details.

### [Perceptual Loss Training](2000-perceptual-loss-training.md)
Perceptual Loss Training is an optimization strategy that incorporates a perceptual loss term into the training objective to improve the perceptual quality of rendered images, addressing the inherent smoothness and blurriness issues associated with standard pixel-wise reconstruction losses. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Total Variation Regularization](860-total-variation-regularization.md) and [L1+SSIM Loss](2890-l1-ssim-loss.md) by leveraging high-level feature representations from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) rather than relying solely on low-level pixel statistics or structural similarity metrics.

### [Second-Order Chamfer Distance](2170-second-order-chamfer-distance.md)
Second-Order Chamfer Distance is a supervised geometric loss function that measures the discrepancy between a predicted mesh vertex and a ground truth surface by incorporating local surface curvature. Unlike standard Chamfer Distance, which uses a simple quadratic point-to-point distance, this method approximates the distance function quadratically to account for the local geometry of the reference surface. It serves as a baseline for meshing implicit functions where ground truth meshes are available.

### [Color-Corrected Error Metrics](2737-color-corrected-error-metrics.md)
Color-Corrected Error Metrics are an evaluation procedure designed to mitigate photometric variations—such as autoexposure, white balance shifts, and RGB clipping—in "in the wild" datasets like Tanks and Temples. By solving a per-image least squares problem to fit a quadratic polynomial expansion of the rendering's RGB values to the true image, the method neutralizes global brightness and color shifts, making view synthesis models quantitatively comparable.

### [L1+SSIM Loss](2890-l1-ssim-loss.md)
L1+SSIM Loss is a composite photometric objective used to optimize radiance fields (specifically 3D Gaussian Splatting) by minimizing the difference between rendered images and ground-truth photographs. It combines a pixel-wise L1 error with a Structural Similarity (SSIM) term to balance color accuracy with perceptual sharpness. It differs from pure MSE-based approaches by being less sensitive to outliers and from pure SSIM approaches by retaining strict pixel-level fidelity, addressing the blurriness often caused by SSIM-only optimization.

### [Model Regularization](2912-model-regularization.md)
Model regularization refers to the application of auxiliary loss terms during the optimization of radiance fields to constrain the learned representation beyond photometric fidelity. It solves the problem of overfitting to noisy observations or degenerate solutions (such as exploding uncertainty or non-compact geometry) by penalizing specific undesirable properties of the model parameters. Unlike sibling methods that focus on spatial smoothness (Total Variation) or geometric alignment, model regularization often targets internal model statistics, such as uncertainty estimates or network output magnitudes.

### [Visibility-Based Photometric Loss](2917-visibility-based-photometric-loss.md)
Visibility-Based Photometric Loss computes photometric error only on pixels identified as visible across source and target views. It differs from standard photometric losses by filtering out pixels where the squared difference between reprojected source depth and directly rendered target depth exceeds a threshold, preventing the model from fitting to occluded or transient geometry.

### [Uncertainty Prediction Loss](2961-uncertainty-prediction-loss.md)
Uncertainty Prediction Loss is a regularization technique used in differentiable volume rendering and neural radiance field optimization to predict and penalize the aleatoric uncertainty of rendered outputs or geometric fields. It differs from standard photometric losses by explicitly modeling the confidence of the prediction, allowing the optimizer to down-weight noisy or ambiguous data points. Unlike [Uniform and Gaussian Noise Models](5127-uniform-and-gaussian-noise-models.md), which visualize uncertainty distributions, this method directly incorporates uncertainty parameters into the training objective to improve geometry and color reconstruction.

### [Data Consistency Loss](2976-data-consistency-loss.md)
Data Consistency Loss enforces fidelity between the rendered output of a volume model and the measured input data by minimizing the discrepancy between forward-projected predictions and ground-truth projections. It differs from standard photometric losses (which operate on pixel-space RGB errors) by operating in the measurement space (e.g., sinogram or projection space), ensuring that the reconstructed volume physically explains the observed raw data. This method is distinct from sibling regularization terms like Total Variation, which enforce spatial smoothness, by directly constraining the geometric consistency of the reconstruction against measurements.

### [Regularised Gaussian Fitting Loss](3104-regularised-gaussian-fitting-loss.md)
The Regularised Gaussian Fitting Loss is a composite objective function used to optimize 3D Gaussian splats for volumetric data reconstruction. It augments the base L1 reconstruction error with regularization penalties on splat amplitude and spatial extent to prevent overfitting and enforce physical plausibility. It is distinguished from sibling methods like [Total Variation Regularization](860-total-variation-regularization.md) by operating directly on the parametric attributes of the Gaussian primitives (amplitude and covariance diagonal) rather than on the rendered volume grid or field values.

### [Total Loss Function](4177-total-loss-function.md)
The Total Loss Function is the composite objective used to optimize radiance field models (such as Neural Volumes, NeRFs, and Gaussian Splatting) by combining a primary photometric reconstruction error with one or more regularization terms. While the parent article [Regularization losses](1413-regularization-losses.md) defines the general structure $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photo} + \sum_k \lambda_k \mathcal{L}_{reg}^{(k)}$, the Total Loss Function article specifies the concrete instantiation of these terms, their weights, and the optimization context for specific radiance field variants. It differs from sibling methods like [Total Variation Regularization](860-total-variation-regularization.md) or [Efficient O(N) Distortion Loss](1051-efficient-o-n-distortion-loss.md) by serving as the aggregate container for all active loss components during a specific training run, rather than a single regularization term itself.

### [Joint Optimization with Loss Functions](4506-joint-optimization-with-loss-functions.md)
This method defines a composite objective function for the joint optimization of 3D surface splat (surfel) parameters and an HDR environment map in inverse rendering. It solves the problem of unstable geometry and lighting estimation by combining photometric fidelity with geometric regularization terms that enforce depth consolidation and normal consistency. It is distinguished from sibling methods by its specific focus on multi-layer surfel compositing constraints (inter- and intra-layer depth penalties) and its joint update of material properties alongside the lighting environment.

### [Forplane Optimization Losses](4672-forplane-optimization-losses.md)
Forplane Optimization Losses are a set of regularization terms designed for differentiable volume rendering in dynamic scenes, specifically within the Forplane framework. They address the problem of disentangling static and dynamic components in spatiotemporal neural fields by enforcing structural priors on orthogonal 2D planes. Unlike standard spatial smoothness regularizers, these losses operate on space-time planes to enforce temporal consistency and feature disentanglement, ensuring that dynamic elements do not corrupt the static background representation.

### Output Noise Regularization
During optimization for real scene data, random Gaussian noise with zero mean and unit variance is added to the output $\sigma$ values before passing them through the ReLU activation [S1]. This regularization slightly improves visual performance for rendering novel views in real scenes [S1].

### Temporal Instability Metric
A metric measuring temporal variation in reconstruction error (flickering) based on frame-to-frame changes in error [S2, unofficial]. The 95th percentile of the TI signal is sometimes presented to omit big one-frame outliers and align better with visually perceived flicker [S2, unofficial]. TI does not distinguish between rapid changes in ground truth and slow responses in the reconstructed signal; high TI does not necessarily mean visual flicker, but high visual flicker implies high TI [S2, unofficial].

### Temporal Reconstruction Error Metric
A metric measuring constant reconstruction error (ghosting) by comparing froxel data directly, rather than final image pixels [S2, unofficial].

### Beta Distribution Prior on Exit Opacity
Regularization term using the negative log-likelihood of a Beta(0.5, 0.5) distribution on final image opacities to reduce entropy, based on the intuition that rays should strike the object or background rather than grazing surfaces [S3].

### Leak Metric
Measures the fraction of a method's near-plane foreground energy that incorrectly falls on the culled side where the reference is background. Combines proxy reconstruction error, renderer mismatch, soft tails, and overshooting culls [S4, unofficial].

### Band Fidelity Metric (Sb)
A fidelity metric measuring the structural similarity between a method's render and a reference render over the exposed cut face (reference foreground) [S4, unofficial].

### Distortion Loss
If `learned_background` is enabled and `lambda_distortion_bg` > 0, compute `loss_distortion_bg` by calling `flatten_eff_distloss` with background-specific output keys: `weights_bg`, `points_bg`, `intervals_bg`, and `ray_indices_bg` [S5, unofficial].

### Proposal Supervision Loss
A loss function that supervises the proposal MLP by minimizing excess histogram mass that violates a bound established between histograms of 1D data, encouraging consistency between proposal weights $(\hat{t}, \hat{w})$ and NeRF weights $(t, w)$ despite different bin locations [S6].

### Uncertainty-aware Squared Error Loss
Punishes predicted uncertainty $\delta_i$ less than the squared error $E(r)$, ensuring that if a pixel has large error, the associated uncertainty is also large [S7].

### Multi-Task Differentiable Loss for 3D Gaussian Splatting
A set of loss functions used to optimize 3D Gaussians from video input, combining segmentation mask loss, color loss, and optical flow loss [S8, unofficial].

### TensoRF Reconstruction Optimization
Optimizing the tensorial radiance field per scene via gradient descent to minimize L2 rendering loss, using ground truth pixel colors as supervision. Applies regularization terms to vector and matrix factors to address overfitting and local minima [S9, unofficial].

### Variance Loss
Adds a variance-based loss term to the scene optimization, either total variance loss or voxel disparity loss [S10, unofficial].

## Background
The use of regularization in volume rendering and neural radiance fields emerged alongside the need to stabilize training and improve generalization. Early NeRF implementations used noise injection to regularize density outputs [S1]. Subsequent works introduced Total Variation (TV) loss for explicit representations [S1][S9, unofficial], distortion loss to prevent floaters [S1], and uncertainty-aware losses to handle noisy data [S1][S7]. The family has expanded to include perceptual losses [S1], geometric priors like Chamfer distance [S1], and specialized terms for dynamic scenes [S1] and Gaussian splatting [S1].
