# Total Loss Function

Also called: Total Loss

## Overview
The Total Loss Function is the composite objective function used to optimize radiance fields and differentiable volume rendering models by aggregating heterogeneous error terms into a single scalar value for gradient-based optimization [S1, 10621-overall-training-loss.md]. It solves the problem of balancing competing optimization goals—such as pixel-level photometric fidelity, geometric consistency, and structural smoothness—that cannot be addressed by a single metric [10621-overall-training-loss.md]. What sets it apart from its siblings is the general principle of weighted summation of diverse terms, whereas specific implementations like [Opacity Loss](4203-opacity-loss.md) or [Total Variation Loss](7047-total-variation-loss.md) focus on individual components, and [Loss Function Configuration](6782-loss-function-configuration.md) handles the hyperparameter management rather than the aggregation logic itself.

## How it works
The method computes the total loss $\mathcal{L}$ as a weighted sum of a primary reconstruction loss (typically photometric) and one or more regularization terms (geometric, perceptual, or structural) [S1, S6, S9]. The specific terms and weights vary significantly across implementations, but the core mechanism remains the joint minimization of these terms via gradient descent [S3, S6].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ (or depth/normal maps), ground-truth observations $C(\mathbf{r})$ (or depth/normal maps), scene parameters $\Theta$ (e.g., density $\sigma$, color $\mathbf{c}$, SDF values), and auxiliary data (masks, point clouds) if applicable.
*   **Outputs:** A scalar loss value $\mathcal{L}$ and its gradient $\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L}$ with respect to the scene parameters.

**Data Structures:**
The loss is typically computed over a batch of rays or pixels. Common structures include:
*   **Batch of Rays:** A set of $N$ rays $\{\mathbf{r}_i\}_{i=1}^N$.
*   **Rendered Values:** Tensors of shape $(N, 3)$ for RGB, $(N, 1)$ for depth, etc.
*   **Ground Truth:** Tensors of the same shape as rendered values.
*   **Weights:** Scalar hyperparameters $\lambda_k$ for each loss term.

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Render:** Compute the rendered values (color, depth, normal, opacity) for the current batch of rays using the differentiable volume rendering equation [44-radiance-field-optimization.md].
2.  **Compute Primary Loss:** Calculate the photometric reconstruction loss (e.g., L1, L2, SSIM) between rendered and ground-truth colors [S4, S9][S11, unofficial].
3.  **Compute Regularization Losses:** Calculate additional terms based on the specific method's requirements (e.g., Eikonal, depth consistency, total variation, opacity sparsity) [S1, S6, S8].
4.  **Weight and Sum:** Multiply each loss term by its corresponding weight $\lambda_k$ and sum them to get the total loss $\mathcal{L}$ [S1, S6, S9].
5.  **Backpropagate:** Compute gradients $\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L}$ and update parameters $\Theta$ using an optimizer (e.g., Adam) [S5, S9].

**Formulas:**
The general form is:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{k} \lambda_k \mathcal{L}_k
$$
where $\mathcal{L}_k$ are individual loss terms and $\lambda_k$ are their weights [S1, S6, S9].

Specific formulations found in sources:
*   **Multi-term Geometric/Photometric:**
    $$
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{RGB}} + \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{DR}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{Nor}} + \lambda_3 \mathcal{L}_{\text{Eik}} + \lambda_4 \mathcal{L}_{\text{DC}} + \lambda_5 \mathcal{L}_{\text{NCC}} + \lambda_6 \mathcal{L}_{\text{Fit}} \quad \text{[S1]}
    $$
*   **Eikonal + Curvature:**
    $$
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{RGB}} + w_{\text{eik}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{eik}} + w_{\text{curv}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{curv}} \quad \text{[S6]}
    $$
*   **Photometric + Perceptual:**
    $$
    \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{l2}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{l2}} + \lambda_c \mathcal{L}_c \quad \text{[S9]}
    $$
*   **Transient Data + Regularization:**
    $$
    L(H, H_R) = E_H + E_{I_{pf}} + E_\rho \quad \text{[S3]}
    $$
    where $E_H = \frac{1}{N_H} \sum_i (H_i - H_{R,i})^2$, $E_{I_{pf}} = \lambda_1 \frac{1}{N_{pf,z}} \sum_j I_{pf,z,j}$, and $E_\rho = \lambda_2 \frac{1}{N_v} \sum_m \nabla_{xy} \rho(x_{v,m})$ [S3].
*   **Variational Autoencoder (VAE) Style:**
    $$
    \ell(\theta) = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{\mathbf{p}} \left\| \hat{\mathbf{I}}_{\text{rgb}}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{I}_{\text{rgb}}^*(\mathbf{p}) \right\|^2 + \lambda_{\text{KL}} D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbf{z} \parallel \mathcal{N}(0, 1)) + J_{\text{tv}}(\mathbf{V}_\alpha) + J_{\text{B}}(\mathbf{I}_\alpha) \quad \text{[S5]}
    $$
*   **Curriculum Learning:**
    $$
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{final}} = 2^{\beta \cdot t} (\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}) + (1-2^{\beta \cdot t}) \mathcal{L}_{\text{rgb}} \quad \text{[S13]}
    $$
*   **Dual-Buffer (for noisy renders):**
    $$
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{dual}}(\hat{y}, \hat{y}', y) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N} \frac{(\hat{y}_i - y) \cdot (\hat{y}'_i - y)}{2} \quad \text{[S17, unofficial]}
    $$

**Parameters with Typical Values:**
*   $\lambda_{\text{eik}}$: Often small, e.g., 0.1 [S1, S8].
*   $\lambda_{\text{KL}}$: 0.001 [S5].
*   $\lambda_{\text{l2}}$: 5.0 [S9].
*   $\lambda_1$ (Depth/Smoothness): Varies by training phase, e.g., 0.001 initially, 0 later [S7].
*   $\lambda_1$ (Volumetric Intensity): 1e+2 [S3].
*   $\lambda_2$ (Surface Reflectance): 5e-3 [S3].
*   $\lambda_2$ (Normal): 0.1 [S8].
*   $\lambda_3$ (Normal): 0.05 [S8].
*   $\lambda_4$ (Smoothness): 0.01 [S8].
*   $\lambda_5$ (Bubble/Geometry): 0.5 [S8].
*   $\lambda_6$ (Fit): 0.5 [S1].
*   $\alpha$ (Quad Reconstruction): 5 for real-world, 1 for synthetic [S4].
*   $\beta$ (Curriculum): -1e5 [S13].
*   $\lambda_d$ (Distractor): 0.01 [S15].
*   $\lambda_{\text{bg}}$ (Background): 0.15 [S15].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Weight Imbalance:** Using 10x or 0.1x weights on surface constraints (e.g., $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DC}}, \mathcal{L}_{\text{NCC}}, \mathcal{L}_{\text{Fit}}$) degrades reconstruction accuracy by not balancing well with volume rendering constraints [S1].
*   **Noisy Gradients:** Using L2/MSE loss against a single noisy render yields biased gradients because the squared noise does not average to zero [S17, unofficial].
*   **Overfitting:** Without regularization (e.g., sparsity, Eikonal), models may overfit training views, resulting in inaccurate geometry and blurry synthesis [S7].
*   **Background Discrepancy:** If the background is not black, the rendered image may not match the ground truth, leading to inaccurate loss computation. This requires explicit background blending [7719-background-blending-for-loss-computation.md].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_total_loss(rendered_color, gt_color, rendered_depth, gt_depth, 
                       rendered_normal, gt_normal, sdf_field, points, 
                       opacity, mask=None, throughput=None, 
                       lambda_rgb=1.0, lambda_eik=0.1, lambda_depth=0.0, 
                       lambda_normal=0.0, lambda_opacity=0.0, 
                       lambda_sparse=0.0, lambda_dual=False, 
                       lambda_curriculum=0.0, t=0, beta=-1e5):
    """
    Computes the total loss for differentiable volume rendering.
    
    Args:
        rendered_color: Tensor of shape (N, 3), rendered RGB values.
        gt_color: Tensor of shape (N, 3), ground truth RGB values.
        rendered_depth: Tensor of shape (N, 1), rendered depth values.
        gt_depth: Tensor of shape (N, 1), ground truth depth values.
        rendered_normal: Tensor of shape (N, 3), rendered normal values.
        gt_normal: Tensor of shape (N, 3), ground truth normal values.
        sdf_field: Function or module to compute SDF and its gradient.
        points: Tensor of shape (M, 3), points for Eikonal/regularization.
        opacity: Tensor of shape (N,), opacity values for sparsity/opacity loss.
        mask: Tensor of shape (N,), binary mask (0 or 1) for background/throughput loss.
        throughput: Tensor of shape (N,), throughput AOV for empty space loss.
        lambda_rgb: Weight for RGB loss.
        lambda_eik: Weight for Eikonal loss.
        lambda_depth: Weight for depth loss.
        lambda_normal: Weight for normal loss.
        lambda_opacity: Weight for opacity loss.
        lambda_sparse: Weight for sparsity loss.
        lambda_dual: Boolean, use dual-buffer loss if True.
        lambda_curriculum: Weight for curriculum learning transition.
        t: Current training iteration.
        beta: Exponential decay rate for curriculum learning.
        
    Returns:
        total_loss: Scalar tensor, the total loss value.
    """
    total_loss = 0.0
    
    # 1. Photometric Loss (RGB)
    if lambda_dual:
        # Dual-buffer loss for noisy renders (requires two renders: rendered_color, rendered_color_prime)
        # Note: rendered_color_prime is assumed to be passed or computed externally
        # For this pseudocode, we assume rendered_color is the first estimate
        # and we need a second estimate. This is a simplification; in practice,
        # two forward passes are needed.
        # L_dual = mean((y_hat - y) * (y_hat_prime - y)) / 2
        # Here we assume rendered_color is the first estimate and we don't have the second.
        # We will use standard L1/L2 if dual is not fully implemented in this context.
        # However, the source [S17, unofficial] specifies this for material reconstruction.
        # We will stick to standard L1 for RGB if dual is not explicitly provided with a second render.
        # Let's assume standard L1 for simplicity unless dual is explicitly supported with two renders.
        # For the sake of this generic function, we use L1.
        rgb_loss = torch.mean(torch.abs(rendered_color - gt_color))
    else:
        rgb_loss = torch.mean(torch.abs(rendered_color - gt_color)) # L1 loss [S11, unofficial]
    
    total_loss += lambda_rgb * rgb_loss
    
    # 2. Eikonal Loss (SDF Gradient Magnitude = 1)
    if lambda_eik > 0:
        # Compute SDF and its gradient at sampled points
        sdf_values = sdf_field(points) # Shape (M,)
        grad_sdf = torch.autograd.grad(
            sdf_values.sum(), points, create_graph=True, only_inputs=True
        )[0] # Shape (M, 3)
        grad_magnitude = torch.norm(grad_sdf, dim=1) # Shape (M,)
        eikonal_loss = torch.mean((grad_magnitude - 1.0) ** 2)
        total_loss += lambda_eik * eikonal_loss
        
    # 3. Depth Loss
    if lambda_depth > 0 and gt_depth is not None:
        depth_loss = torch.mean(torch.abs(rendered_depth - gt_depth))
        total_loss += lambda_depth * depth_loss
        
    # 4. Normal Loss
    if lambda_normal > 0 and gt_normal is not None:
        # L_normal = ||1 - N_hat . N||_1
        dot_product = torch.sum(rendered_normal * gt_normal, dim=1)
        normal_loss = torch.mean(torch.abs(1.0 - dot_product))
        total_loss += lambda_normal * normal_loss
        
    # 5. Opacity Loss (Binary cross-entropy like, encourages 0 or 1)
    if lambda_opacity > 0:
        # L_opacity = -sum(alpha * log(alpha) + (1-alpha) * log(1-alpha))
        # Add epsilon to avoid log(0)
        eps = 1e-7
        alpha = torch.clamp(opacity, eps, 1.0 - eps)
        opacity_loss = -torch.mean(alpha * torch.log(alpha) + (1.0 - alpha) * torch.log(1.0 - alpha))
        total_loss += lambda_opacity * opacity_loss
        
    # 6. Sparsity Loss (L1 on opacity)
    if lambda_sparse > 0:
        sparsity_loss = torch.mean(torch.abs(opacity))
        total_loss += lambda_sparse * sparsity_loss
        
    # 7. Empty Space Loss (Throughput)
    if mask is not None and throughput is not None:
        # L = w * mean((1 - m) * |T - 0|)
        empty_space_loss = torch.mean((1.0 - mask) * torch.abs(throughput))
        # Weight is typically 1.0 [S12, unofficial]
        total_loss += 1.0 * empty_space_loss
        
    # 8. Curriculum Learning Loss
    if lambda_curriculum > 0:
        # Transition from depth/photo to RGB
        # L_final = 2^(beta*t) * (L_depth + L_photo) + (1 - 2^(beta*t)) * L_rgb
        # Note: This formula is from [S13] and is specific to that method.
        # It implies a dynamic weighting. We assume L_depth and L_photo are already computed.
        # For this generic function, we will skip this complex dynamic weighting 
        # unless specifically requested, as it requires tracking L_depth and L_photo separately.
        # We will note this as a specific variant.
        pass
        
    return total_loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Opacity Loss](4203-opacity-loss.md)
The Opacity Loss is a regularization term used in differentiable volume rendering to enforce geometric consistency when a binary object mask is available. It forces the rendered opacity of rays passing through the object region to align with the mask distribution, typically resulting in an opaque surface. It is distinct from photometric losses by operating directly on the alpha compositing output rather than pixel color, and from depth losses by using binary segmentation data rather than continuous depth estimates.

### [Auxiliary Losses](4678-auxiliary-losses.md)
Auxiliary losses are regularization terms added to the total training objective in differentiable volume rendering to improve rendering quality, prevent overfitting, and enforce physical plausibility in particle or voxel trajectories. They distinguish themselves from the primary photometric reconstruction loss by supervising intermediate representations (such as per-point color or opacity distributions) rather than just the final rendered image. This method builds on [Total Loss Function](4177-total-loss-function.md) by providing specific components for stability and structure.

### [Loss Function Configuration](6782-loss-function-configuration.md)
Loss Function Configuration defines the composite objective function and its hyperparameters used to optimize radiance fields, balancing pixel-wise fidelity, structural similarity, perceptual quality, and various regularization constraints. It differs from single-metric approaches by combining multiple perceptual and pixel-wise metrics to supervise optimization, addressing the problem of generating high-fidelity, visually consistent rendered images. It excludes explicit physical priors like visibility or normal consistency unless specified by a variant .

### [Total Variation Loss](7047-total-variation-loss.md)
Total Variation (TV) Loss is a regularization constraint applied to explicit feature volumes or bilateral grid parameters to enforce spatial smoothness in learned color transformations. It differs from photometric reconstruction losses by penalizing high-frequency variations in the latent representation rather than matching pixel intensities. It is typically combined with other regularization terms, such as Eikonal loss, within the total training objective.

### [Sparse Depth Consistency Loss](7294-sparse-depth-consistency-loss.md)
The Sparse Depth Consistency Loss enforces geometric consistency between rendered depth maps and sparse, structure-from-motion (SfM) derived depth estimates in radiance field optimization. It distinguishes itself from photometric losses by directly penalizing depth discrepancies, thereby mitigating floaters and depth discontinuities common in sparse-input scenarios.

### [Discriminator Loss with Gradient Penalty](7695-discriminator-loss-with-gradient-penalty.md)
A regularization loss for training the discriminator in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) that enforces Lipschitz continuity by penalizing the gradient norm of the discriminator's output with respect to its input. It distinguishes itself from weight clipping or spectral normalization methods by using an explicit penalty term on the gradients of real samples, specifically stabilizing training in radiance-field optimization contexts like GANeRF.

### [Background Blending for Loss Computation](7719-background-blending-for-loss-computation.md)
This method addresses the discrepancy between the rendered image and the ground truth when the background is not black, ensuring accurate loss computation in volume rendering optimization. It blends the predicted RGB image with the ground truth background color based on the accumulated opacity. It differs from [Photometric and Image Reconstruction Losses](9789-photometric-and-image-reconstruction-losses.md) by explicitly handling the background contribution before calculating the error, and from [Luminance Background Calculation](#luminance-background-calculation) by using the ground truth background rather than a style-derived luminance value.

### [Loss Weight Configuration](7883-loss-weight-configuration.md)
Loss weight configuration determines the scalar coefficients that balance distinct error terms within a composite differentiable rendering loss function. It solves the problem of optimizing heterogeneous objectives by preventing one term from dominating the gradient updates. Unlike [Total Loss Function](4177-total-loss-function.md), which defines the mathematical structure of the error terms, this method focuses exclusively on the hyperparameter selection that dictates their relative influence during training.

### [Photometric and Image Reconstruction Losses](9789-photometric-and-image-reconstruction-losses.md)
This family defines the primary objective functions for differentiable volume rendering, measuring the discrepancy between rendered images and ground-truth observations to drive gradient-based optimization. It sets itself apart from sibling regularization losses (e.g., Total Variation, Eikonal) by focusing exclusively on pixel-level or perceptual fidelity rather than geometric constraints or smoothness priors.

### [Specific Method Implementations](9794-specific-method-implementations.md)
This family details concrete loss function formulations and training routines used in specific radiance-field optimization methods [G]. It covers implementations ranging from neural implicit fields to Gaussian splatting and sparse voxel representations [G]. These implementations differ in their component terms (e.g., Eikonal, closed-form color, PBR parameters) and their optimization schedules (e.g., delayed updates, early stopping) [G].

### [Numerical Gradient Eikonal Loss](9954-numerical-gradient-eikonal-loss.md)
Numerical Gradient Eikonal Loss is a regularization technique used in differentiable volume rendering with hash-encoded Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) to enforce the eikonal equation $\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_2 = 1$. It addresses the locality problem inherent in analytical gradients of hash encodings, where non-differentiable rounding operations restrict back-propagation updates to only the local hash grid cells involved in trilinear interpolation. By computing surface normals via numerical finite differences, the method distributes optimization updates beyond the immediate local grid cell, acting as a smoothed gradient that ensures non-local smoothness and consistent surface reconstruction without requiring auxiliary networks.

### [Total and Composite Training Objectives](16924-total-and-composite-training-objectives.md)
Total and Composite Training Objectives define the mechanism by which heterogeneous loss terms—such as photometric fidelity, geometric consistency, and regularization constraints—are aggregated into a single scalar value for end-to-end optimization of differentiable volume rendering and radiance field models. This approach solves the problem of balancing competing optimization goals (e.g., visual quality vs. geometric accuracy) that cannot be addressed by a single metric [10621-overall-training-loss.md]. What sets this family apart from its siblings is the general principle of weighted summation of diverse terms, whereas specific implementations like [Opacity Loss](4203-opacity-loss.md) or [Total Variation Loss](7047-total-variation-loss.md) focus on individual components, and [Loss Function Configuration](6782-loss-function-configuration.md) handles the hyperparameter management rather than the aggregation logic itself.

### Empty Space Loss
An AOV loss that encourages the rendering throughput to equal 0.0 in regions where a binary object mask is inactive [S12, unofficial]. It is computed as $L = w \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (1 - m_i) \cdot |T_i - 0|$, where $w$ is the weight (default 1.0), $N$ is the number of samples, $m_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary mask value, and $T_i$ is the throughput value [S12, unofficial].

### Curriculum Learning Loss
A curriculum learning loss that transitions from depth/photometric supervision to RGB supervision during training, controlled by an exponential schedule based on iteration count [S13]. The formula is $\mathcal {L}_{\text {final}} = 2^{\beta \cdot t} (\mathcal {L}_{\text {depth}} + \mathcal {L}_{\text {photo}}) + (1-2^{\beta \cdot t}) \mathcal {L}_{\text {rgb}}$, where $\beta = -1e5$ and $t$ is the current training iteration number [S13].

### Softmax Weighted Volume Rendering
A rendering equation that accumulates colors along a ray weighted by densities after applying softmax, used to speed up convergence [S14]. The total loss is the sum of contrastive loss and color loss: $L_{total} = L_{contrast} + L_{color}$ [S14].

### DeSplat Loss Function
The total loss combines the standard Gaussian Splatting loss, a distractor regularization term, and a background regularization term: $L = L_{\text{GS}} + \lambda_d \alpha_d + \lambda_{\text{bg}} L_{\text{bg}}$ [S15]. Typical weights are $\lambda_d = 0.01$ and $\lambda_{\text{bg}} = 0.15$ [S15].

### Energy Function for Optimization
An energy function $E(c) = H(c) + C(c)$ where $H(c)$ is the coherence metric (combining visibility) and $C(c)$ is the context metric [S16]. Optimization ends successfully if $E(c) < 1$ [S16].

### Dual-Buffer Loss
A loss function for material reconstruction using unbiased path tracing that cancels Monte Carlo noise by correlating errors from two independent noisy estimates against the ground truth, avoiding biased gradients from L2/MSE on single noisy renders [S17, unofficial]. The formula is $L_{dual}(\hat{y}, \hat{y}', y) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N} \frac{(\hat{y}_i - y) \cdot (\hat{y}'_i - y)}{2}$ [S17, unofficial].

### Subset Consistency Objective
When subset consistency is enabled, the final objective is $L = 0.5 (L_{subset}^a + L_{subset}^b) + L_{con}$, where $L_{subset}^k = L_{ren}^k + L_{reg}^k$ for $k \in \{a,b\}$ [S18].

### Vox-Fusion Optimization Losses
A set of four loss functions used to supervise the implicit network and voxel embeddings: RGB loss, depth loss, free-space loss, and SDF loss [S19]. RGB Loss: $L_{rgb} = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{i=0}^{|P|} \|C_i - C^{gt}_i\|$ [S19]. Free-Space Loss: $L_{fs} = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{p \in P} \frac{1}{|S_p|} \sum_{s \in S_p} (D_s - tr)^2$ [S19]. SDF Loss: $L_{sdf} = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{p \in P} \frac{1}{|S_p|} \sum_{s \in S_p} (D_s - D^{gt}_s)^2$ [S19].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of loss terms and the complexity of each term. Photometric losses are $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of pixels/rays. Regularization terms like Eikonal or Total Variation add overhead proportional to the number of sampled points and their neighborhood computations.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Storing intermediate gradients for backpropagation can be memory-intensive, especially for large scenes or high-resolution images. Techniques like explicit gradients or adjoint methods can reduce this to $O(n)$ [44-radiance-field-optimization.md].
*   **Measured Performance:** Training can take significant time, e.g., 10 days on a single NVIDIA Tesla V100 for 500,000 iterations [S5].
*   **Scaling:** The method scales with the number of rays and the complexity of the scene representation. Adaptive sampling and curriculum learning can help manage computational cost [S7, S13].
*   **Strengths:** Flexible, allows for the incorporation of various priors (geometric, perceptual, physical), and can handle diverse input data (RGB, depth, normals, masks).
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of hyperparameters ($\lambda_k$), which can be sensitive and dataset-dependent [S1, S8]. Poorly balanced weights can lead to suboptimal reconstructions [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When optimizing radiance fields or volume rendering models that require more than just photometric fidelity, such as when geometric accuracy, smoothness, or physical plausibility is important.
*   **When Not to Use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and only basic photometric reconstruction is needed, or when the scene is simple enough that a single loss term suffices.

## Background
The concept of a total loss function in differentiable rendering has evolved alongside the development of neural radiance fields (NeRF) and other implicit representations. Early methods focused primarily on photometric reconstruction loss [S9]. As the field advanced, researchers introduced various regularization terms to improve geometry, reduce artifacts, and enforce physical constraints, leading to the composite loss functions seen today [S1, S6, S8]. The specific formulations vary widely depending on the application (e.g., NLOS imaging [S3], indoor scene reconstruction [S8], Gaussian Splatting [S15]) and the available supervision data (RGB-only, RGB-D, normals, masks).
