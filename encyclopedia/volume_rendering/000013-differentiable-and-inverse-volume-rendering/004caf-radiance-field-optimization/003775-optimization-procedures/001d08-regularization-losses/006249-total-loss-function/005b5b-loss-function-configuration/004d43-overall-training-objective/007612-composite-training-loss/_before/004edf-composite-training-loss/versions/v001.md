# Composite Training Loss

Also called: L1+SSIM+LPIPS Loss

## Overview
Composite Training Loss combines multiple perceptual and pixel-wise metrics to supervise the optimization of radiance fields. It addresses the problem of generating high-fidelity, visually consistent rendered images by penalizing errors in color accuracy, structural similarity, and high-level perceptual features. It differs from single-metric approaches (like pure L1 or MSE) by balancing local pixel fidelity with global structural integrity and perceptual quality, and from complex inverse rendering objectives by excluding explicit physical priors like visibility or normal consistency unless specified by a variant.

## How it works
The method computes a weighted sum of three distinct loss components between the rendered image and the ground truth image.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Rendered image $I_{render}$ and Ground Truth image $I_{gt}$. Both are typically normalized to $[0, 1]$ or $[-1, 1]$.
- Output: Scalar loss value $\mathcal{L}_{composite}$.

**Data Structures:**
- Images are represented as tensors of shape $(H, W, C)$, where $H$ and $W$ are height and width, and $C$ is the number of channels (typically 3 for RGB).
- Patches for SSIM and LPIPS are extracted from these tensors.

**Steps:**
1. **L1 Loss Calculation:** Compute the mean absolute error between the rendered and ground truth pixels.
   $$ \mathcal{L}_{L1} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |I_{render}^{(i)} - I_{gt}^{(i)}| $$
   where $N = H \times W \times C$.

2. **SSIM Loss Calculation:** Compute the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) between the images. SSIM is typically calculated over local windows. The loss is often defined as $1 - \text{SSIM}$ to maximize similarity.
   $$ \mathcal{L}_{SSIM} = 1 - \text{SSIM}(I_{render}, I_{gt}) $$
   Standard SSIM parameters include a window size (e.g., $3 \times 3$ or $11 \times 11$), and constants $C_1, C_2$ for stability.

3. **LPIPS Loss Calculation:** Compute the Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) distance. This involves passing image patches through a pretrained network (e.g., VGG or AlexNet) and comparing the activations.
   $$ \mathcal{L}_{LPIPS} = \text{LPIPS}(I_{render}, I_{gt}) $$

4. **Composite Loss Aggregation:** Sum the losses. In the standard configuration described in [S1], equal weighting is implied or the terms are summed directly without explicit $\lambda$ coefficients in the base definition, though implementations may use weights.
   $$ \mathcal{L}_{composite} = \mathcal{L}_{L1} + \mathcal{L}_{SSIM} + \mathcal{L}_{LPIPS} $$

**Parameters:**
- **Training Strategy:** Random crops are used from the full image for training [S1]. This reduces memory usage and focuses optimization on local details.
- **Weights:** The base method [S1] sums the three losses. Specific weights are not detailed in [S1] beyond the addition. Variants may adjust these.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Normalization:** Ensure $I_{render}$ and $I_{gt}$ are in the same range expected by the LPIPS network (often $[-1, 1]$ or $[0, 1]$). Mismatched ranges cause incorrect gradient magnitudes.
- **Crop Size:** The crop size must be sufficient for the SSIM window and LPIPS patch size. If crops are too small, SSIM calculation may be unstable or invalid.
- **LPIPS Pretrained Model:** The choice of backbone (VGG vs AlexNet) affects the perceptual penalty. VGG is more common for high-quality results.

    # LPIPS requires normalized inputs (typically [-1, 1]) and a pretrained network.

    # Base method [S1] sums them.
    total_loss = l1_loss + ssim_loss + lpips_loss

    return total_loss

## Subtopics

### UrbanIR Training Objective
An extension of the composite loss framework used in UrbanIR [S2]. It addresses the ill-posed inverse rendering problem by incorporating 2D priors. It adds visibility, normal, and semantic losses to the rendering loss. The total loss is $\min _{\theta , \mathbf{L}} \mathcal {L}_{\text {render}} + \lambda _1\mathcal {L}_{\text {visibility}} + \lambda _2\mathcal {L}_{\text {normal}} + \lambda _3\mathcal {L}_{\text {semantics}} + \lambda _4\mathcal {L}_{\text {reg}}$ [S2]. It uses pretrained networks for shadow mask estimation (MTMT), normal estimation (Omnidata), and semantic segmentation (MMSegmentation) [S2]. It is chosen for large-scale urban scene inverse rendering where geometry and material decomposition are critical [S2]. Parameters: $\lambda_1=0.001, \lambda_2=0.01, \lambda_3=0.04, \lambda_4=0.1$ [S2]. Optimizer: Adam, lr=0.002, 100 epochs [S2].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Dominated by the forward pass of the radiance field decoder and the LPIPS network. LPIPS is computationally expensive due to the deep network evaluation. SSIM is moderate. L1 is negligible.
- **Memory Complexity:** Depends on image resolution and batch size. Using random crops [S1] significantly reduces memory footprint compared to full-image processing, allowing higher resolutions or larger batches.
- **Performance:** Provides superior perceptual quality compared to L1-only or MSE-only losses, particularly in texture detail and structural coherence [S1].
- **Strengths:** Robust to high-frequency details; balances pixel accuracy with perceptual fidelity.
- **Limitations:** LPIPS requires a pretrained model and is slower. SSIM can be sensitive to small shifts. The combination may require careful tuning of weights if not summed equally.
- **When to use:** When visual quality is paramount and computational resources allow for the overhead of SSIM and LPIPS.
- **When not to use:** In real-time applications where latency is critical, or when memory is severely constrained and full-image processing is unavoidable without cropping.

## Background
The composite loss combining L1, SSIM, and LPIPS is a standard practice in differentiable rendering and neural radiance field optimization. It was notably employed in MuRF (Multi-Baseline Radiance Fields) [S1] to handle multi-baseline scenarios, demonstrating effectiveness on both small and large baseline datasets. The UrbanIR variant [S2] extends this to inverse rendering tasks in urban environments, integrating additional physical and semantic priors to solve the ill-posed nature of the problem.
