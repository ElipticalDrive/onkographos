# Depth Regularization

Also called: Depth constraints, Depth supervision, Depth priors

## Overview
Depth regularization methods solve the problem of geometric ambiguity and instability in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by enforcing consistency between rendered depth maps and external or internal depth targets. They are distinguished from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) by their explicit focus on metric or relative depth accuracy rather than spectral smoothness or 2D image gradients alone.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family compute a differentiable auxiliary loss term $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ that is added to the total training loss:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \lambda_{reg} \mathcal{L}_{reg} $$
where $\lambda_{reg}$ is a scalar weight. The core mechanism involves extracting a geometric or spectral property from the rendered output (or the input data) and comparing it against a target or constraint. The gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ flow back through the differentiable rasterizer to update the Gaussian parameters $\theta$ (centers $\mu$, scales $s$, rotations $q$, opacities $\alpha$, and colors $c$) [Parent].

## Subtopics

### [SparseGS](6135-sparsegs.md)
SparseGS is a training optimization method for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) designed to solve the problem of sparse-view 3D reconstruction, specifically addressing issues such as misplaced Gaussians ("floaters"), geometry collapse, and texture noise that arise when input view coverage is insufficient. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods by combining a novel depth rendering bias (softmax-scaling) with external priors: monocular depth estimation and Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) from a diffusion model, whereas siblings like DNGaussian rely on depth-normal regularizers and FSGS uses Gaussian unpooling.

### [PixelSplat Training and Depth Regularization](6420-pixelsplat-training-and-depth-regularization.md)
PixelSplat Training and Depth Regularization is a specific training protocol for the PixelSplat architecture, which reconstructs 3D scenes from image pairs using 3D Gaussian Splatting. It solves the problem of producing high-quality, dense point clouds from generalizable 3D reconstruction by combining a multi-stage visual loss with a geometric depth regularization term. It is distinguished from its siblings by its specific use of multiple Gaussians per ray to enable multi-depth estimation and its explicit fine-tuning phase for depth map consistency, whereas [DNGaussian](7399-dngaussian.md) focuses on differentiable normal rendering and [Edge-Aware Disparity Regularization](9140-edge-aware-disparity-regularization.md) focuses on disparity edge preservation [Parent].

### [DNGaussian](7399-dngaussian.md)
DNGaussian is a depth-regularized framework for few-shot novel view synthesis that combines Hard and Soft Depth Regularization with Global-Local Depth Normalization to restore accurate scene geometry under coarse monocular depth supervision while preserving fine-grained color appearance. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by replacing spherical harmonics with a Neural Color Renderer to prevent overfitting on sparse views and by introducing a two-stage depth regularization process that explicitly manages the trade-off between surface positioning (Hard) and surface completeness (Soft). Unlike NeRF-based methods that interpolate colors into invisible areas, DNGaussian exposes black backgrounds on empty spaces, which lowers PSNR/SSIM metrics for invisible regions but allows the optimization to focus strictly on visible geometry.

### [Edge-Aware Disparity Regularization](9140-edge-aware-disparity-regularization.md)
Edge-Aware Disparity Regularization is a technique that encourages local depth smoothness in rendered scenes while preserving depth discontinuities that align with image edges. It solves the problem of noisy or artifact-prone depth maps by weighting the disparity gradient penalty with the inverse of the RGB image gradient, ensuring that smoothness is enforced in flat regions but not across object boundaries. Unlike standard smoothness regularization which penalizes all depth changes equally, this method is edge-preserving, distinguishing it from siblings like [DNGaussian](7399-dngaussian.md) which uses geometric patch-based smoothing.

### Opacity-Depth Regularization
**Definition:** A regularization term applied during novel-view synthesis optimization to encourage spheres to move to the correct scene depth [S1].
**Step:** Thresholding: Apply a threshold to spheres with low opacity to remove them [S1].
**Formula:** Energy = $-z_i \cdot O_i$, where $z_i$ is the depth of sphere $i$ and $O_i$ is its opacity [S1].

### Sparse Input Reconstruction via Regularization
**Definition:** Methods that introduce additional constraints such as depth information to enhance detail and global consistency when observational data is sparse [S2].
**Pitfall:** Efficacy of regularization techniques tends to diminish when dealing with a limited number of views, or even just one [S2].
**Comparison:** Relies on strong constraints and per-scene optimization, favoring geometric quality but incurring higher computation compared to generalizability-based methods [S2].
**Usage:** DNGaussian uses a depth-regularized scheme to address geometry degradation [S2]. FSGS uses a Gaussian Unpooling process for initialization and introduces depth regularization [S2]. MVSplat proposes a cost volume representation to provide geometry cues [S2].

### 2DGS + $\tilde{L}_d$ variant
**Definition:** A variant of 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS) that incorporates a depth regularization strategy by enabling supervision and gradient flow to depths within the CUDA backend rasterizer, supervised with sensor or monocular depth estimates [S3].
**Formula:** The overall optimization loss is defined as $L = L_{rgb} + \tilde{L}_d$, where $L_{rgb}$ is the original loss from 2DGS [16] and $\tilde{L}_d$ is the depth regularization term [S3].

### Frustum Constraint Loss
**Definition:** A regularization term preventing Gaussians from drifting to unsupported regions of space by penalizing continuous violations of image bounds and valid depth ranges across input views [S4].
**Step:** For each Gaussian $n$ with center $\mu_n$, transform to camera coordinates, project to image coordinates, and measure continuous violations of image bounds and valid depth range for each input view [S4]. If a Gaussian lies inside at least one input-view frustum, its penalty is zero. Otherwise, it receives a smooth positive penalty based on the minimum violation $v_n$ [S4].
**Formula:** Compute frustum loss: $L_{fru} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log(1 + v_n/\tau)$, where $v_n$ is the minimum frustum violation of Gaussian $n$ across input views, and $\tau$ controls softness [S4].
**Parameter:** $\tau$: Parameter controlling the softness of the penalty [S4].

## Background
Depth regularization in the context of 3D Gaussian Splatting emerged as a critical component for addressing the "floaters" and geometry collapse issues inherent in sparse-view reconstruction. Early sphere-based neural rendering methods like Pulsar demonstrated that pure sphere geometry could represent complex scenes but required careful initialization and regularization to avoid artifacts [S1]. As 3DGS gained popularity, the lack of explicit depth supervision led to ambiguous geometries, particularly when input views were sparse. Methods like DNGaussian introduced hard and soft depth regularization to explicitly constrain Gaussian centers and opacities against monocular depth estimates [S1]. Concurrently, generalizable reconstruction methods like PixelSplat utilized multi-depth Gaussians and second-order difference losses to ensure depth consistency [S1]. Surveys on 3DGS highlight that regularization-based methods, which rely on strong constraints like depth, favor geometric quality but incur higher computation compared to generalizability-based methods [S2]. Variants such as the 2DGS + $\tilde{L}_d$ approach integrate depth supervision directly into the rasterizer backend [S3], while global methods like GlobalSplat employ frustum constraints to keep Gaussians within valid viewing volumes [S4].
