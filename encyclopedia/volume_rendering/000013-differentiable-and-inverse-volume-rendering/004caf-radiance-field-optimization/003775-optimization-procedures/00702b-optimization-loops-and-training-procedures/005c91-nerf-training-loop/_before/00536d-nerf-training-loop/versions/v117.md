# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Fields Training, Training Ray Sampling Strategy for Correspondence Disparity, Ray Sampling Strategy for Correspondence Disparity

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric reconstruction error between rendered images and ground-truth training views by updating the parameters of a continuous radiance field representation [S5, S10, S15, unofficial]. It solves the inverse problem of learning scene geometry and appearance from unstructured multi-view images by coupling differentiable volume rendering with stochastic gradient descent [S5, S15, unofficial]. Unlike [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md), which uses deterministic rendering for reproducible metrics, this method employs stochastic sampling with perturbation to facilitate gradient-based optimization [S2, S5, S10, unofficial]. It differs from [Volume Rendering Integration and Forward Pass](15462-volume-rendering-integration-and-forward-pass.md) by encompassing the entire training-specific pipeline—including ray generation, adaptive pruning via occupancy grids, and network querying—rather than focusing solely on the mathematical compositing mechanics [S1][S2, S3, S5, S10, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ training images $\{I_n\}_{n=1}^N$ with known camera poses $P_n$ (extrinsics) and intrinsics $K_n$. The radiance field is parameterized by a neural network $\mathcal{N}_\Theta$ (typically an MLP) mapping 5D coordinates $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ to density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$.
*   **Outputs:** Updated network weights $\Theta$ that minimize the reconstruction loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Ray Bundle:** A tensor of shape $[B, 3, 3]$ or $[B, 6]$ containing batched ray origins $\mathbf{o}$, directions $\mathbf{d}$, and target RGB colors $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$. In pre-computed batching, rays are stored in a flat array `rays_rgb` of shape $[(N-1) \cdot H \cdot W, 3, 3]$ where axis 1 is $[\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{c}_{gt}]$ and axis 2 is the XYZ/RGB components [S5, S15, unofficial].
*   **Optimizer State:** Maintained by the optimizer (e.g., Adam), including moment estimates for each parameter group [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint:** A dictionary containing `model` (state dict), `optimizer` (state dict), and `epoch`/`step` counters [S10, S11, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Load training and validation datasets [S10, S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the radiance field model $\mathcal{N}_\Theta$ with random weights [S13, S16, unofficial]. For hierarchical models, initialize coarse and fine networks [S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with learning rate $\eta$ [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize a learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Precompute ray bundles if using batching: generate all rays from training images, stack origins, directions, and target colors, and shuffle the array [S5, S15, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop:** For iteration $i$ from 1 to $N_{iters}$:
    *   **Ray Sampling:**
        *   Select a batch of $B$ rays. If pre-computed batching is used, slice the `rays_rgb` array at index $i_{batch}$ to $i_{batch} + B$ [S5, S15, unofficial]. If not, randomly select pixel coordinates from a random training image and generate rays using camera intrinsics [S15, unofficial].
        *   **Perturbation:** If enabled, perturb sample depths along rays to break symmetry and aid optimization. Compute midpoints between samples, generate random uniform values $t_{rand}$, and interpolate between lower and upper bounds of sample intervals [S5, unofficial].
    *   **Forward Pass (Differentiable Volume Rendering):**
        *   Evaluate $\mathcal{N}_\Theta$ at sample points $\mathbf{x}_j = \mathbf{o} + t_j \mathbf{d}$ along each ray to obtain raw outputs $(\sigma_j, \mathbf{c}_j)$ [S5, S6, unofficial].
        *   Compute transmittance $T_j = \exp(-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \sigma_k \delta_k)$ and weights $w_j = T_j (1 - \exp(-\sigma_j \delta_j))$ [S5, S19, unofficial].
        *   Composite color: $\hat{\mathbf{C}} = \sum_j w_j \mathbf{c}_j$ [S5, S19, unofficial].
        *   If using coarse-fine hierarchy, compute coarse loss from the coarse network output $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{coarse}$ [S11, S19, unofficial].
    *   **Loss Computation:**
        *   Compute Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted color $\hat{\mathbf{C}}$ and ground truth $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$:
            $$ \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \| \hat{\mathbf{C}}_b - \mathbf{c}_{gt,b} \|^2 $$
            [S13, S15, S18, unofficial].
        *   If coarse output is available, add coarse loss: $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \mathcal{L}_{coarse}$ [S11, S19, unofficial].
        *   Add regularization terms if applicable (e.g., sparsity, TV, Eikonal) [S7, unofficial][S8].
    *   **Backpropagation and Update:**
        *   Zero gradients: `optimizer.zero_grad()` [S19, unofficial].
        *   Compute gradients: `loss.backward()` [S19, unofficial].
        *   Step optimizer: `optimizer.step()` [S19, unofficial].
        *   Update learning rate according to scheduler [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   **Logging and Checkpointing:**
        *   Log loss and PSNR ($PSNR = -10 \log_{10}(\mathcal{L}_{rgb})$) every $K_{log}$ steps [S11, S15, unofficial].
        *   Render validation images every $K_{render}$ steps [S11, unofficial].
        *   Save checkpoint every $K_{save}$ steps [S11, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

def train_nerf_loop(model, optimizer, scheduler, dataloader, num_epochs, device):
    """
    Standard NeRF training loop.
    
    Args:
        model: Neural Radiance Field model (MLP).
        optimizer: Optimizer (e.g., Adam).
        scheduler: Learning rate scheduler.
        dataloader: Iterator yielding batches of (rays_o, rays_d, target_rgb).
        num_epochs: Number of training epochs.
        device: 'cpu' or 'cuda'.
    """
    mse_loss = nn.MSELoss()
    
    for epoch in range(num_epochs):
        model.train()
        for batch_idx, (rays_o, rays_d, target_rgb) in enumerate(dataloader):
            # Move data to device
            rays_o = rays_o.to(device)
            rays_d = rays_d.to(device)
            target_rgb = target_rgb.to(device)
            
            # 1. Forward Pass
            # model.render_rays returns predicted RGB, depth, etc.
            # This function internally handles sampling, network querying, and volume integration.
            predicted_rgb, extras = model.render_rays(rays_o, rays_d)
            
            # 2. Loss Computation
            # Primary photometric loss
            loss = mse_loss(predicted_rgb, target_rgb)
            
            # Optional: Add coarse loss if hierarchical training is used
            if 'rgb_coarse' in extras:
                loss += mse_loss(extras['rgb_coarse'], target_rgb)
                
            # Optional: Add regularization losses (e.g., sparsity, TV)
            # loss += regularization_weight * compute_regularization(extras, model)

            # 3. Backpropagation
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            
            # 4. Optimization Step
            optimizer.step()
            
            # 5. Learning Rate Scheduling
            # Note: Scheduler step() is typically called after optimizer.step()
            # or per iteration depending on the scheduler type (e.g., LambdaLR vs CosineAnnealing)
            scheduler.step()
            
            # 6. Logging (every N steps)
            if batch_idx % 100 == 0:
                psnr = -10.0 * torch.log10(loss).item()
                print(f"Epoch {epoch}, Batch {batch_idx}, Loss: {loss.item():.6f}, PSNR: {psnr:.2f}")

        # 7. Checkpointing (every N epochs)
        if epoch % 10 == 0:
            torch.save({
                'epoch': epoch,
                'model_state_dict': model.state_dict(),
                'optimizer_state_dict': optimizer.state_dict(),
                'loss': loss.item(),
            }, f'checkpoint_epoch_{epoch}.pth')
```

**Parameters with Typical Values:**
*   **Learning Rate ($\eta$):** $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for Adam [S11, unofficial].
*   **Batch Size ($N_{rand}$):** 1024 or 2048 rays per iteration [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   **Iterations ($N_{iters}$):** 100,000 to 1,000,000 steps [S11, S15, unofficial].
*   **Positional Encoding Frequency ($L$):** 10 for position, 4 for direction [S11, unofficial].
*   **Decay:** Exponential decay with rate 0.1 every 1000 steps, or Cosine Annealing over total iterations [S5, S11, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Rendering all rays at once may exceed GPU memory. Mitigate by chunking rays into smaller batches during the forward pass [S5, S16, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Freezing:** Higher-order derivatives (e.g., for logging gradients) must be computed *before* `loss.backward()` as the autograd graph is freed [S3, unofficial].
*   **Resuming Training:** When loading a checkpoint, ensure the scheduler's last epoch is set to `start_epoch - 1` to maintain correct scheduling [S9, unofficial].
*   **Empty Checkpoints:** If a checkpoint is loaded but the step counter is 0, treat it as invalid and log an error [S16, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Growth:** Directly growing sub-networks without initializing weights from the parent can cause density to collapse to zero [S1].

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S23]. For every pixel, multiple jittered rays are sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel, rendered with NeRF, and averaged to produce the predicted color in the loss function [S23]. This approach relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets and performs worse than mip-NeRF while being significantly more expensive [S23].

### Two-stage training for proposers
A training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S24, unofficial]. Phase 1 trains the learnt proposer to mimic a heuristic proposer by minimizing the L2 matching loss between their proposal sets. Phase 2 trains all network components end-to-end, resetting the optimizer state and performing a linear warmup for 1,000 SGD steps to reduce shock from switching regimes [S24, unofficial]. This two-stage approach is consistently effective where training from scratch often fails [S24, unofficial].

### JaxNeRF
A re-implementation of the original NeRF in JAX, used as a benchmark comparison for early works seeking to improve on training and rendering speed [S25]. It is slightly faster and more suited for distributed computing than the original TensorFlow implementation of NeRF [S25].

### Gradient Detachment in Training
A training strategy where the object field component is detached from the backbone to prevent gradient backpropagation to the rendering part, ensuring independent optimization [S26, unofficial]. Without detachment, gradients from the object field backpropagate to the NeRF backbone, causing mutual influence and a significant drop in rendering quality [S26, unofficial]. With detachment, better scene rendering and decomposition quality are achieved. The hyper-parameter $\Delta d$ for empty 3D point losses is typically optimized to 0.05 meters [S26, unofficial].

### Ray Generator Network (RGN)
Used in MEIL-NeRF for incremental learning, the RGN learns to map past principal rays and current ground-truth principal rays to generate new rays [S27]. During training, it maps rays from previous tasks to current ones. During inference, it generates a principal ray, and non-principal rays are generated around it using camera intrinsics to form a cone [S27]. The network has 3 hidden layers with 16, 64, and 32 units [S27]. Estimated camera poses accumulate errors, but the generated approximate rays still effectively direct to the scene of interest, reducing the learning burden [S27].

### DFF
A method adopting pre-trained feature extraction networks (LSeg, DINO) as teacher networks for knowledge distillation, resulting in a scene-specific 3D feature field [S28]. This enables extraction and deletion of target objects based on image patches or text [S28].

### ISRF
A method that learns additional feature fields to realize self-supervised 3D segmentation [S28].

### NeRF Volumetric Rendering
The core rendering equation used in the loop computes final pixel color and depth via volumetric rendering: $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i$ and $D(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} T_i \alpha_i t_i$, where $T_i = \exp(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_t)$, $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_t)$, $t_i$ is the distance of the $i$-th point from the camera, and $\delta_t$ is the distance between sampled points [S29].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot B \cdot N_{samples} \cdot D)$, where $N_{iters}$ is the number of iterations, $B$ is the batch size (number of rays), $N_{samples}$ is the number of samples per ray, and $D$ is the dimensionality of the network input/output [S5, S15, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(B \cdot N_{samples} \cdot D_{hidden})$ for storing intermediate activations during backpropagation, plus $O(N_{rays} \cdot 3)$ for pre-computed ray buffers if batching is used [S5, S15, unofficial]. VRAM usage is a primary constraint, often requiring chunking of ray batches [S5, S16, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Training on 128x128 images typically takes 250 epochs or ~100,000 iterations [S4, S11, unofficial]. On LLFF with 3 views, specific sampling ratios (e.g., 50 target rays) yield PSNR ~20.33 [S21].
*   **Scaling:** Performance scales with batch size, but larger batches hinder data loading performance and reduce throughput, while smaller batches cap iterations per second [S16, unofficial]. A batch size of $2^{10}$ (1024) is often chosen as a trade-off [S16, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Simple to implement, differentiable end-to-end, produces high-quality novel views.
*   **Limitations:** Slow convergence (requires many iterations), high memory usage for large scenes, prone to artifacts (floaters) without regularization [S1][S18, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When high-quality novel view synthesis is required from multi-view images and computational resources allow for long training times.
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications or when training data is extremely sparse without additional priors [S1][S18, unofficial].

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop was introduced in the seminal paper "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" by Mildenhall et al. in 2020 [S5, unofficial][S25]. It established the standard approach of optimizing a coordinate-based MLP to represent a 5D radiance field using differentiable volume rendering. The method builds on earlier work in neural implicit representations and differentiable rendering [S25]. It is widely used in computer vision and graphics for novel view synthesis, 3D reconstruction, and as a foundation for many subsequent NeRF variants [S1, S25].
