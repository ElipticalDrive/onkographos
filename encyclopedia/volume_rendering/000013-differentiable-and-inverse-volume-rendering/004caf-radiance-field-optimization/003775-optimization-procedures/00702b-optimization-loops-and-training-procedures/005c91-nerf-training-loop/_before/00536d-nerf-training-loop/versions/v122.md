# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Fields Training, Training Ray Sampling Strategy for Correspondence Disparity, Ray Sampling Strategy for Correspondence Disparity

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric reconstruction error between rendered images and ground-truth training views by updating the parameters of a continuous radiance field representation [S5, S10, S15, unofficial]. It solves the inverse problem of recovering 3D geometry and appearance from 2D images by treating the neural network as a differentiable volume renderer [S5, S15, unofficial]. Unlike [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md), which uses deterministic rendering for reproducible metrics, this method employs stochastic sampling with perturbation to facilitate gradient-based optimization [S2, S5, S10, unofficial]. It differs from [Volume Rendering Integration and Forward Pass](15462-volume-rendering-integration-and-forward-pass.md) by encompassing the entire training-specific pipeline—including ray generation, adaptive pruning via occupancy grids, and network querying—rather than focusing solely on the mathematical compositing mechanics [S1][S2, S3, S5, S10, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ training images with known camera poses (extrinsics $C$ and intrinsics $K$), typically stored as a dataset of rays. The radiance field model $F_\Theta$ (usually an MLP or hash grid) with parameters $\Theta$.
*   **Outputs:** Updated parameters $\Theta$ that minimize the loss function $\mathcal{L}$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Ray Bundle:** A tensor of shape $[B, 3, 3]$ or $[B, 9]$ where $B$ is the batch size (number of rays). The first 3 elements are the ray origin $(o_x, o_y, o_z)$, the next 3 are the ray direction $(d_x, d_y, d_z)$, and the last 3 are the target RGB color $(r, g, b)$ [S5, S15, unofficial].
*   **Pre-computed Rays Array:** In many implementations, all possible rays from training images are pre-computed and stored in a flat array `rays_rgb` of shape $[(N-1) \cdot H \cdot W, 3, 3]$ to allow efficient random sampling without regenerating rays every iteration [S5, S15, unofficial].
*   **Optimizer State:** Maintained by the optimizer (e.g., Adam), storing exponential moving averages of gradients and squared gradients [S5, S18, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Load training and validation datasets [S10, S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the radiance field model $F_\Theta$ with random weights [S13, S16, unofficial]. For hierarchical models, initialize coarse and fine networks [S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with a learning rate $lr$ [S5, S10, S13, unofficial].
    *   Initialize a learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Pre-compute all ray origins, directions, and target RGB values from training images into `rays_rgb` [S5, S15, unofficial]. If using NDC (Normalized Device Coordinates) for forward-facing scenes, transform rays accordingly [S6, S15, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop:** For iteration $i$ from 1 to $N_{iters}$:
    *   **Ray Sampling:**
        *   Select a batch of $N_{rand}$ rays from `rays_rgb` [S5, S15, unofficial].
        *   If `use_batching` is true, slice the pre-computed array; otherwise, randomly select an image and sample pixels from it [S15, unofficial].
        *   If `precrop_iters` > $i$, sample pixels only from the central crop of the image (fraction `precrop_frac`) to focus on the main subject early in training [S2, S15, unofficial].
        *   Extract ray origins $o$, directions $d$, and target colors $C_{gt}$ from the selected batch [S7, S15, unofficial].
    *   **Forward Pass (Rendering):**
        *   Sample $N$ points along each ray within the near/far bounds [S5, S14, unofficial].
        *   Apply perturbation to sample positions if `perturb` > 0 to break symmetry and improve gradient flow [S2, S5, unofficial].
        *   Query the radiance field $F_\Theta$ at each sample point to obtain density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ [S5, S6, unofficial].
        *   Compute transmittance $T$ and weights $w$ using the emission-absorption model [S5, unofficial].
        *   Composite the final predicted color $\hat{C}$ for each ray [S5, unofficial].
        *   If using a coarse-to-fine strategy, render coarse predictions $\hat{C}_{coarse}$ and fine predictions $\hat{C}_{fine}$ [S11, unofficial].
    *   **Loss Computation:**
        *   Compute the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and target colors:
            $$ \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^{B} (\hat{C}_j - C_{gt,j})^2 $$
            [S13, S15, S18, unofficial].
        *   If coarse-to-fine is used, add the coarse loss: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb, fine} + \mathcal{L}_{rgb, coarse}$ [S11, S19, unofficial].
        *   Add any regularization terms (e.g., sparsity, Eikonal, Total Variation) if defined in the specific variant [S7, unofficial][S8].
    *   **Backpropagation and Update:**
        *   Zero the gradients of the optimizer [S19, unofficial].
        *   Compute gradients $\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L}$ via automatic differentiation [S19, unofficial].
        *   Step the optimizer: $\Theta \leftarrow \Theta - \eta \cdot \text{Adam}(\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L})$ [S19, unofficial].
        *   Update the learning rate according to the scheduler [S19, unofficial].
    *   **Logging and Checkpointing:**
        *   Log loss and PSNR ($\text{PSNR} = -10 \log_{10}(\mathcal{L})$) every `i_print` steps [S15, S2, unofficial].
        *   Render validation images every `i_img` steps [S2, unofficial].
        *   Save model checkpoints every `i_weights` steps [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters with Typical Values:**
*   $N_{rand}$ (Batch Size): 1024 or 2048 rays per iteration [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   $N$ (Samples per Ray): 128 (coarse) and 128 (fine) [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   $lr$ (Learning Rate): $5 \times 10^{-4}$ [S11, unofficial] or $1 \times 10^{-3}$ [S13, unofficial].
*   $N_{iters}$: 100,000 to 1,000,000 steps [S11, S15, unofficial].
*   `perturb`: 1.0 for training (to add jitter), 0.0 for evaluation [S2, S5, unofficial].
*   `chunk`: Maximum number of rays processed per forward pass to limit VRAM (e.g., 4096) [S5, S6, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Rendering all rays at once can exceed GPU memory. Mitigated by chunking the ray batch during the forward pass [S5, S6, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Graph Freeing:** Higher-order derivatives (for logging gradients/laplacians) must be computed *before* calling `loss.backward()` [S3, unofficial].
*   **Empty Checkpoints:** If loading a checkpoint with step 0, it is treated as invalid/empty [S16, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Growth:** When growing sub-networks, initializing their weights to match the parent prevents density collapse to zero [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

def train_nerf_loop(model, rays_rgb, target_colors, num_iters, batch_size, optimizer, scheduler, near, far, num_samples, perturb=1.0):
    """
    Standard NeRF Training Loop.
    
    Args:
        model: Radiance field MLP (returns raw sigma and rgb).
        rays_rgb: Pre-computed array of shape [Total_Rays, 3, 3] (origin, dir, target_rgb).
        target_colors: Not used directly if rays_rgb contains targets, but kept for signature clarity.
        num_iters: Total number of optimization steps.
        batch_size: Number of rays per iteration (N_rand).
        optimizer: PyTorch optimizer (e.g., Adam).
        scheduler: Learning rate scheduler.
        near: Near plane distance.
        far: Far plane distance.
        num_samples: Number of samples per ray.
        perturb: Jitter factor for sampling.
    """
    total_rays = rays_rgb.shape[0]
    ray_indices = torch.randperm(total_rays)
    current_idx = 0
    
    for i in range(num_iters):
        # 1. Ray Sampling
        # Slice batch from pre-computed rays
        end_idx = min(current_idx + batch_size, total_rays)
        batch_indices = ray_indices[current_idx:end_idx]
        
        # Handle wrap-around and shuffle if exhausted
        if end_idx >= total_rays:
            ray_indices = torch.randperm(total_rays)
            current_idx = 0
            batch_indices = ray_indices[0:batch_size]
        else:
            current_idx += batch_size
            
        batch_data = rays_rgb[batch_indices] # Shape: [batch_size, 3, 3]
        
        # Extract origins, directions, and targets
        # Axis 0: origin, Axis 1: direction, Axis 2: target_rgb
        origins = batch_data[:, 0, :] # [batch_size, 3]
        directions = batch_data[:, 1, :] # [batch_size, 3]
        targets = batch_data[:, 2, :] # [batch_size, 3]
        
        # 2. Forward Pass
        # Sample points along rays
        # t_vals = linearly spaced between near and far
        t_vals = torch.linspace(0., 1., num_samples).to(origins.device)
        z_vals = near * (1. - t_vals) + far * t_vals
        
        # Perturb sampling
        if perturb > 0.:
            mids = 0.5 * (z_vals[..., 1:] + z_vals[..., :-1])
            upper = torch.cat([mids, z_vals[..., -1:]], -1)
            lower = torch.cat([z_vals[..., :1], mids], -1)
            t_rand = torch.rand(z_vals.shape).to(origins.device)
            z_vals = lower + (upper - lower) * t_rand
            
        # Compute 3D points
        pts = origins.unsqueeze(1) + directions.unsqueeze(1) * z_vals.unsqueeze(2) # [batch_size, num_samples, 3]
        
        # Query Model
        # Model returns raw density and color
        raw = model(pts, directions) # [batch_size, num_samples, 4] (sigma, r, g, b)
        
        # Convert raw to alpha and weights
        # alpha = 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
        deltas = z_vals[..., 1:] - z_vals[..., :-1]
        delta_inf = 1e10 * torch.ones_like(deltas[..., :1])
        deltas = torch.cat([deltas, delta_inf], -1)
        
        sigma = raw[..., 0]
        rgb = raw[..., 1:]
        
        alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * deltas)
        
        # Transmittance
        T = torch.cumprod(1.0 - alpha + 1e-10, dim=1, exclusive=True)
        
        # Weights
        weights = alpha * T # [batch_size, num_samples]
        
        # Composite Color
        rgb_map = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * rgb, dim=1) # [batch_size, 3]
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(rgb_map, targets)
        
        # 4. Backpropagation and Update
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        scheduler.step()
        
        # Logging (optional)
        if i % 100 == 0:
            psnr = -10.0 * torch.log(loss) / torch.log(torch.tensor(10.0))
            print(f"Iter {i}: Loss {loss.item():.6f}, PSNR {psnr.item():.2f}")
```

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S23]. For every pixel, it casts multiple jittered rays sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel, rendering each with NeRF and using the mean as the predicted color in the loss function [S23]. This approach relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets and performs worse than mip-NeRF while being significantly more expensive during both training and evaluation [S23].

### Two-stage training for proposers
A training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S24, unofficial]. Phase 1 trains the learnt proposer to mimic the heuristic proposer by minimizing the L2 matching loss between their proposal sets [S24, unofficial]. Phase 2 trains all network components end-to-end, resetting the optimizer state and performing a linear warmup for 1,000 SGD steps to reduce shock from switching regimes [S24, unofficial]. This two-stage approach is consistently effective where training from scratch often fails [S24, unofficial].

### JaxNeRF
A re-implementation of the original NeRF in JAX, used as a benchmark comparison for early works seeking to improve on training and rendering speed [S25]. It is slightly faster and more suited for distributed computing than the original TensorFlow implementation of NeRF [S25].

### Gradient Detachment in Training
A training strategy where the object field component is detached from the backbone to prevent gradient backpropagation to the rendering part, ensuring independent optimization [S26, unofficial]. With detachment, gradients do not backpropagate to the NeRF backbone; without it, gradients cause mutual influence and a significant drop in rendering quality [S26, unofficial]. The hyper-parameter $\Delta d$ for empty 3D point losses is optimally set to 0.05 meters [S26, unofficial].

### Ray Generator Network (RGN)
Used in MEIL-NeRF for incremental learning, the RGN $f_T^{RGN}$ learns to map past principal rays from $f_{T-1}^{RGN}$ and current GT principal rays during training [S27]. During inference, it generates a principal ray, and non-principal rays are generated around it using camera intrinsics to form a cone covering the image plane [S27]. The network has 3 hidden layers with 16, 64, and 32 units [S27]. It creates approximate rays directed to the scene of interest, allowing small errors to reduce learning burden without requiring exact reconstruction of past rays [S27].

### DFF
A method adopting pre-trained feature extraction networks (LSeg, DINO) as teacher networks for knowledge distillation, resulting in a scene-specific 3D feature field that enables extraction and deletion of target objects based on image patches or text [S28].

### ISRF
A method that learns additional feature fields to realize self-supervised 3D segmentation [S28].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot C_{model})$, where $C_{model}$ is the cost of the neural network forward pass. Training typically requires 100,000 to 1,000,000 iterations [S11, S15, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot C_{model})$ per iteration, plus $O(N_{total\_rays})$ if pre-computing rays [S5, S15, unofficial]. VRAM is a primary constraint, often mitigated by chunking rays [S5, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** On standard datasets (e.g., Blender), training takes several hours to days depending on hardware and resolution [S11, S15, unofficial]. PSNR typically converges to 30+ dB for synthetic scenes [S12].
*   **Scaling:** Performance scales with the number of training views and resolution, but computational cost grows linearly with the number of rays and samples [S5, S18, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** End-to-end differentiable, handles complex lighting and geometry implicitly, produces high-quality novel views.
*   **Limitations:** Slow training and rendering due to MLP queries, prone to overfitting in sparse-view settings, requires careful hyperparameter tuning [S1][S18, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When high-fidelity novel view synthesis is required and sufficient computational resources are available.
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications or extremely sparse views without additional regularization/priors [S1][S18, unofficial].

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop was introduced by Mildenhall et al. in "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" (2020) [S5, S10, unofficial]. It established the paradigm of optimizing a continuous volumetric representation via stochastic gradient descent on photometric loss. Early implementations used TensorFlow [S5, unofficial], followed by PyTorch [S6, unofficial] and JAX [S13, unofficial][S25] re-implementations. The method has since been extended to various architectures (HashNeRF [S7, unofficial], Instant NGP [S16, unofficial]) and optimization strategies (coarse-to-fine [S5, unofficial], adaptive sampling [S2, S24, unofficial]).
