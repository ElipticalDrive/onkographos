# NeRF in the Wild

Also called: Martin-Brualla et al, NeRF-W (NeRF in the Wild), Neural Radiance Fields for Unconstrained Photo Collections

## Overview
NeRF in the Wild (NeRF-W) is an extension of Neural Radiance Fields designed to handle unconstrained, internet-scale photo collections where strict consistency assumptions fail due to variable illumination and transient occluders [S1, S4]. Unlike standard NeRF, which assumes a static scene and constant lighting, NeRF-W disentangles the scene into a shared static geometry and image-dependent components: a learned appearance embedding for photometric variations (lighting, exposure) and a transient head for moving objects [S1, S2]. It achieves this by optimizing per-image latent codes and using a data-dependent uncertainty field to discount transient regions during training, allowing for robust novel view synthesis of static structures [S1, 5].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ images $\{I_i\}_{i=1}^N$ with known camera poses (estimated via COLMAP [S1]), and a 3D position $\mathbf{x}$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ for ray sampling.
*   **Outputs:** Rendered color $\hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r})$ for each image $i$ along ray $\mathbf{r}$, decomposed into static and transient components.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Static Radiance Field ($F_\theta$):** A shared MLP (MLP$_{\theta_1}$ for density, MLP$_{\theta_2}$ for color) parameterized by $\theta$. It takes $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{d}$ as input.
    *   *Note:* The density prediction $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ is generated *before* conditioning on appearance embeddings to ensure geometry is shared [S1].
2.  **Appearance Embeddings ($\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}$):** A real-valued vector of length $n^{(a)}$ for each training image $i$. Optimized alongside $\theta$ [S1].
3.  **Transient Radiance Field ($F_\tau$):** A separate MLP (TransientNet) parameterized by $\theta_\tau$. It takes $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{d}$, and a per-image transient embedding $\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}$ (length $n^{(\tau)}$) as input [S1, S2].
4.  **Uncertainty Field ($\beta_i(\mathbf{r})$):** A scalar variance output by the transient head (or a shared uncertainty mechanism) for each ray, modeled via a softplus activation shifted by $\beta_{min}$ [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Initialize MLP weights $\theta$ randomly [S1].
    *   Initialize per-image appearance embeddings $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}\}_{i=1}^N$ and transient embeddings $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}\}_{i=1}^N$ [S1].
    *   Set hyperparameters: batch size 2048, training steps 300,000, $\beta_{min} > 0$, $\lambda_u$ [S1].

2.  **Ray Sampling:**
    *   For each image $i$, sample rays $\mathbf{r}_{ij}$ passing through pixels.
    *   Sample $K$ points $\{t_k\}_{k=1}^K$ along each ray using stratified sampling or importance sampling (coarse/fine models as in standard NeRF) [S1].

3.  **Static Component Forward Pass:**
    *   For each point $t_k$ on ray $\mathbf{r}$, compute static density $\sigma(t_k)$ using MLP$_{\theta_1}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{d}_k)$ [S1].
    *   Compute static color $\mathbf{c}(t_k)$ using MLP$_{\theta_2}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{d}_k, \mathbf{l}_i^{(a)})$. Note that $\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}$ is concatenated or injected into the color MLP, but *not* the density MLP [S1].

4.  **Transient Component Forward Pass:**
    *   Compute transient density $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k)$ using TransientNet$(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{d}_k, \mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)})$. Output activation: Sigmoid [S1].
    *   Compute transient color $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}(t_k)$ using TransientNet$(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{d}_k, \mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)})$. Output activation: Sigmoid [S1].
    *   Compute uncertainty variance $\beta_i(t_k)^2$ using TransientNet (or a dedicated branch). Output activation: Softplus shifted by $\beta_{min}$ [S1].

5.  **Volumetric Rendering (Compositing):**
    *   Compute transmittance $T_i(t_k)$ accounting for *both* static and transient densities:
        $$ T_i(t_k) = \exp \left( -\sum_{k'=1}^{k-1} (\sigma(t_{k'}) + \sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_{k'})) \delta_{k'} \right) $$
    *   Compute alpha weights for static and transient components:
        $$ \alpha(t_k) = 1 - \exp(-\sigma(t_k)\delta_k) $$
        $$ \alpha_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k)\delta_k) $$
    *   Render composite color $\hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r})$:
        $$ \hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{k=1}^K T_i(t_k) \left[ \alpha(t_k)\mathbf{c}(t_k) + \alpha_i^{(\tau)}(t_k)\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) \right] $$
        [S1, Eq. 8]

6.  **Loss Calculation:**
    *   The total loss for ray $\mathbf{r}$ in image $i$ combines the coarse and fine model predictions [S1]:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_i(\mathbf{r}) = \mathcal{L}_i^{fine}(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{1}{2} \| \hat{C}_i^{coarse}(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}_i^{fine}(\mathbf{r}) \|^2 $$
    *   The fine model loss $\mathcal{L}_i^{fine}(\mathbf{r})$ consists of three terms [S1]:
        1.  **Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL):** Models the color difference weighted by uncertainty $\beta_i(\mathbf{r})$.
            $$ \text{NLL} = \frac{\| C_i(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r}) \|^2}{2\beta_i(\mathbf{r})^2} + \frac{1}{2} \log \beta_i(\mathbf{r})^2 $$
        2.  **Uncertainty Regularization:** The $\log \beta$ term prevents $\beta \to \infty$ (ignoring all pixels) by balancing the NLL [S1].
        3.  **Transient Density L1 Regularization:** Discourages the model from using transient density to explain static features.
            $$ \text{Reg}_{\tau} = \frac{\lambda_u}{2K} \sum_{k=1}^K \sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) $$
    *   Total Ray Loss:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_i(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\| C_i(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r}) \|^2}{2\beta_i(\mathbf{r})^2} + \frac{1}{2} \log \beta_i(\mathbf{r})^2 + \frac{\lambda_u}{2K} \sum_{k=1}^K \sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) + \frac{1}{2} \| \hat{C}_i^{coarse}(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}_i^{fine}(\mathbf{r}) \|^2 $$
        [S1, Eq. 13, 14]

7.  **Optimization:**
    *   Minimize the sum of losses over all rays in the batch using Adam optimizer ($\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999$) [S1].
    *   Update $\theta$, $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}\}$, and $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}\}$ [S1].

8.  **Test-Time Evaluation:**
    *   Omit the transient head and uncertainty fields. Render only static $\sigma(t)$ and $\mathbf{c}(t)$ [S1].
    *   For test images, optimize a single appearance embedding $\mathbf{l}^{(a)}$ using only the left half of the image to avoid information leakage, then evaluate metrics on the right half [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $\beta_{min}$: Hyperparameter $>0$ for softplus shift [S1].
*   $\lambda_u$: Weight for transient density regularization [S1].
*   $n^{(a)}, n^{(\tau)}$: Dimensionalities of appearance and transient embeddings [S1].
*   Batch Size: 2048 [S1].
*   Training Steps: 300,000 [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Information Leakage:** When evaluating on test sets, using the whole image to optimize the appearance embedding leads to overfitting. The left-half/right-half split is required [S1].
*   **Transient vs. Static Confusion:** Without the L1 regularization on transient density ($\lambda_u$), the model may use transient density to explain away static high-frequency details or lighting variations, leading to "foggy" or inaccurate geometry [S1].
*   **Pose Estimation:** If camera poses are inaccurate (common in in-the-wild data), the static assumption breaks down more severely. NeRF-W assumes poses are provided (e.g., from COLMAP) [S1].

## Subtopics

### Transient Filtering via Candidate Head
An architectural variant (used in UP-NeRF) that isolates the transient network. It uses a "candidate head" to predict transient properties (color, density, beta) alongside static properties, enabling robust pose optimization by filtering out transient occluders [S6, unofficial]. It predicts transient color $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}$, density $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}$, and beta $\beta_i$ using predicted candidate features $\mathbf{f}_i$ and candidate embeddings $\mathbf{l}_i^{(c)}$ [S6, unofficial]. Merging transient and candidate heads degrades performance; separation is necessary to prevent failure in estimating correct poses and reducing rendering quality [S6, unofficial]. This differs from NeRF-W by isolating the transient network and using deep features (DINO) for candidate filtering, whereas NeRF-W does not isolate the transient network [S6, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training requires 300,000 steps with a batch size of 2048 on 8 GPUs [S1]. This is significantly longer than standard NeRF due to the additional MLPs and embeddings.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Increased memory usage due to storing per-image appearance and transient embeddings ($N \times (n^{(a)} + n^{(\tau)})$) and the additional transient MLP parameters [S1].
*   **Performance:** NeRF-W improves over the previous state-of-the-art (NRW) by an average margin of 4.4dB in PSNR across 6 scenes and up to 40% improvements in MS-SSIM [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles unconstrained internet photo collections; disentangles static geometry from transient objects and lighting variations; enables controllable appearance synthesis [S1, 4].
*   **Limitations:** Requires accurate camera poses (COLMAP); struggles with scenes undergoing major structural changes (e.g., construction) or significant seasonal changes not captured by appearance embeddings alone [S5, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Novel view synthesis of cultural landmarks or tourist sites from internet photo collections where lighting and transient occluders vary [S1, 4].
*   **When not to use:** Controlled studio settings (standard NeRF is sufficient); scenes with major structural changes over time [S5, unofficial].

## Background
NeRF in the Wild (NeRF-W) was introduced by Martin-Brualla et al. in 2021 [S4]. It was developed to address the limitations of the original NeRF, which assumed static scenes and constant lighting, making it unsuitable for unstructured internet photo collections [S1]. The method builds on the framework of Generative Latent Optimization (GLO) to learn appearance embeddings [S1]. It has been used to reconstruct famous landmarks such as the Trevi Fountain, Sacre Coeur, Brandenburg Gate, Taj Mahal, Prague Old Town Square, and Hagia Sophia from the Phototourism dataset [S1, 4]. Subsequent developments, such as those by Sun et al. (2022), applied appearance embedding concepts to mesh output rather than radiance fields [S4].
