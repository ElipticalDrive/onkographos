# Generative Radiance Fields (GRAF)


## Overview
Generative Radiance Fields (GRAF) is a method for 3D-aware image synthesis and shape estimation from unposed images, enabling volume reconstruction without explicit camera pose information [S1]. Unlike standard NeRF which requires known camera poses, GRAF uses an adversarial training scheme where a generator conditioned on pose, shape, and appearance codes learns to synthesize images that fool a patch-based discriminator [S1]. It builds on [NeRF Network Query with Hashed Embeddings](3107-nerf-network-query-with-hashed-embeddings.md) by replacing the supervised reconstruction loss with an adversarial objective, allowing it to handle datasets where relative camera positions are unknown [S1].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A dataset of unposed 2D images (RGB). During training, the generator receives a requested viewing pose (elevation and azimuth angles), and optionally shape and appearance codes. The discriminator receives patches from real images and generated images.
*   **Outputs:** Synthesized RGB images of the scene from specified viewpoints; depth maps; learned 3D implicit representation (NeRF weights).

### Data Structures
The core representation is a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) acting as the radiance field, identical to the original NeRF architecture [S1].
*   **MLP Network:** 8 fully-connected layers.
*   **Channels:** 256 channels per layer.
*   **Activation:** ReLU for hidden layers.
*   **Input to MLP:** 5D coordinate (3D position $x, y, z$ + 2D viewing direction $\theta, \phi$) [S1].
*   **Output of MLP:** Volume density ($\sigma$) and emitted RGB color ($c$) [S1].
*   **Generator Conditioners:**
    *   Pose: Elevation and azimuth angles.
    *   Shape Code: Latent vector controlling volume density distribution.
    *   Appearance Code: Latent vector controlling color/albedo.
*   **Discriminator:** Patch-based CNN (architecture details not specified in sources, but operates on square arrays of pixels) [S1].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Patch Selection:** Instead of predicting full images, the generator selects a patch position and a distance between pixels within that patch. A patch is a square array of pixels [S1].
2.  **Conditioning:** The generator takes the requested pose, and optionally shape and appearance codes, as input [S1].
3.  **Ray Marching:** For each pixel in the patch, a ray is cast based on the requested pose. The ray is sampled at multiple points along its path [S1].
4.  **NeRF Query:** At each sample point, the MLP is queried with the 3D position and viewing direction to obtain density and color [S1].
5.  **Volume Rendering:** The color and density values are integrated along the ray using the standard NeRF rendering equation (physics-inspired integration) to produce the color for each pixel in the patch [S1].
6.  **Adversarial Training (Generator):** The generated patch is compared against a real patch from the dataset by the discriminator [S1].
7.  **Discriminator Update:** The discriminator learns to differentiate real patches from generated ones [S1].
8.  **Generator Update:** The generator's weights are updated to minimize the discriminator's ability to distinguish generated patches from real ones, effectively matching the probabilistic distribution of real image pixels [S1].
9.  **Receptive Field Decay:** The size of the receptive field of the patch (or the distance between pixels) is decreased during training. This allows the model to first capture global contours and progressively improve local details [S1].
10. **Inference:** To synthesize an image, the generator is fed a specific viewing angle (pose). Optionally, shape and appearance codes can be varied to change the volume density or color, respectively [S1].

### Formulas
The internal NeRF representation and rendering equation are identical to the original NeRF [S1]. Let $C(r)$ be the rendered color for a ray $r$ passing through sample points $t_i$:

$$
C(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \left( 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i) \right) c_i
$$

Where:
*   $T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$ is the accumulated transmittance.
*   $\sigma_i$ is the volume density predicted by the MLP at $t_i$.
*   $c_i$ is the color predicted by the MLP at $t_i$.
*   $\delta_i$ is the distance between sample points $t_i$ and $t_{i+1}$.

The adversarial loss is not explicitly defined in the sources, but the generator optimizes its loss function to fool the discriminator [S1].

### Parameters
*   **MLP Layers:** 8 fully-connected layers [S1].
*   **MLP Channels:** 256 [S1].
*   **Activation:** ReLU [S1].
*   **Image Resolution:** 256 × 256 pixels (used in experiments for computational reasons) [S1].
*   **Typical Values:** Not given in the sources.

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Overfitting:** Training for a high number of iterations leads to overfitting, resulting in a heavily under-regularized depth map with many floating artifacts in the background, which decreases the Intersection over Union (IoU) score [S1].
*   **Underfitting/Early Stopping:** Stopping training after a smaller number of iterations results in a more accurate depth map but a blurry, non-detailed RGB image [S1].
*   **Pose Ambiguity:** While GRAF does not require pose input, the predicted pose may not be precise, shifting the depth map from ground truth, although the shape may be more plausible [S1].
*   **Memory Constraints:** Predicting color for every pixel in the target image in the same batch is too memory-intensive, necessitating the patch-based approach [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
# GRAF Training Loop
# Inputs: Dataset of unposed images D, Generator G, Discriminator D_net
# G takes: pose (theta, phi), shape_code, appearance_code
# G outputs: rendered patch colors
# D_net takes: real_patch, generated_patch
# D_net outputs: discriminator scores

def train_graf(dataset, generator, discriminator, num_iterations):
    # Initialize weights
    init_weights(generator)
    init_weights(discriminator)
    
    # Receptive field size starts large and decreases
    receptive_field_size = initial_large_size
    
    for iteration in range(num_iterations):
        # 1. Sample a batch of real images from dataset
        real_images = sample_batch(dataset)
        
        # 2. Generate random or fixed pose, shape, and appearance codes
        # For unposed datasets, pose is often sampled uniformly or from a prior
        poses = sample_poses(batch_size)
        shape_codes = sample_shape_codes(batch_size)
        appearance_codes = sample_appearance_codes(batch_size)
        
        # 3. Generate patches using the generator
        # G queries NeRF for each pixel in the patch based on pose
        generated_patches = []
        for i in range(batch_size):
            patch = generator.forward(
                pose=poses[i],
                shape_code=shape_codes[i],
                appearance_code=appearance_codes[i],
                receptive_field_size=receptive_field_size
            )
            generated_patches.append(patch)
            
        # 4. Update Discriminator
        # D_net differentiates real patches from generated patches
        real_scores = discriminator.forward(real_images, receptive_field_size)
        fake_scores = discriminator.forward(generated_patches, receptive_field_size)
        
        # Standard GAN discriminator loss (e.g., WGAN-GP or vanilla GAN)
        # Note: Specific loss function not detailed in sources [S1]
        d_loss = compute_discriminator_loss(real_scores, fake_scores)
        update_weights(discriminator, d_loss)
        
        # 5. Update Generator
        # Generator tries to fool the discriminator
        # The generator's loss is based on the discriminator's output on generated patches
        fake_scores_gen = discriminator.forward(generated_patches, receptive_field_size)
        
        # GAN generator loss (e.g., minimize log(1 - D(G(z))) or max log D(G(z)))
        # Note: Specific loss function not detailed in sources [S1]
        g_loss = compute_generator_loss(fake_scores_gen)
        update_weights(generator, g_loss)
        
        # 6. Decay receptive field size to capture finer details
        # "The size of the receptive field of the patch is decreased during the training"
        receptive_field_size = decay(receptive_field_size)
        
        # Monitor metrics: FID, KID, PSNR, SSIM, IoU
        if iteration % eval_interval == 0:
            evaluate(generator, dataset)

def generator_forward(pose, shape_code, appearance_code, receptive_field_size):
    # 1. Define patch grid based on receptive field size
    patch_pixels = define_patch_grid(receptive_field_size)
    
    # 2. For each pixel in the patch, cast a ray based on pose
    rendered_patch = []
    for pixel in patch_pixels:
        ray = cast_ray(pose, pixel)
        
        # 3. Sample points along the ray
        samples = sample_ray(ray, num_samples)
        
        # 4. Query NeRF MLP for density and color at each sample
        densities = []
        colors = []
        for sample_point in samples:
            # MLP input: 3D position + viewing direction
            mlp_input = concatenate(sample_point.position, ray.direction)
            
            # MLP output: density and color
            # Note: Shape and appearance codes condition the MLP
            # Implementation detail: Codes are likely concatenated to input or used as bias
            density, color = mlp_forward(mlp_input, shape_code, appearance_code)
            densities.append(density)
            colors.append(color)
            
        # 5. Volume Render the patch pixel
        rendered_color = volume_render(densities, colors, ray.sample_distances)
        rendered_patch.append(rendered_color)
        
    return rendered_patch

def mlp_forward(input_5d, shape_code, appearance_code):
    # 8 fully-connected layers, 256 channels, ReLU activation
    x = input_5d
    for i in range(8):
        x = fully_connected(x, 256)
        if i < 7: # ReLU on hidden layers
            x = relu(x)
        # Conditioning mechanism not explicitly detailed in sources [S1]
        # [G] Typically, latent codes are concatenated to the input or added as bias terms
        # Here we assume concatenation to input for simplicity, though exact mechanism is unspecified
        if i == 0:
            x = concatenate(x, shape_code)
            x = concatenate(x, appearance_code)
            
    # Output: density and color
    density = sigmoid(x[:, 0]) # Density is non-negative
    color = tanh(x[:, 1:4])    # Color is typically in [-1, 1] or [0, 1]
    return density, color

def volume_render(densities, colors, distances):
    # Standard NeRF rendering equation
    # T_i = exp(-sum_{j=1}^{i-1} sigma_j * delta_j)
    # C(r) = sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i * (1 - exp(-sigma_i * delta_i)) * c_i
    
    transmittance = 1.0
    rendered_color = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
    
    for i in range(len(densities)):
        sigma = densities[i]
        delta = distances[i]
        c = colors[i]
        
        alpha = 1.0 - exp(-sigma * delta)
        weight = transmittance * alpha
        
        rendered_color += weight * c
        
        transmittance *= (1.0 - alpha)
        
    return rendered_color
```

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources, but patch-based rendering reduces memory usage compared to full-image prediction [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** On CubeSat and SMOS satellite datasets, GRAF renders precise novel views with accurate details even when parts of the satellites are shadowed, whereas NeRF may struggle with dark scenes [S1]. NeRF generally renders more accurate images regarding material specularity and pose [S1]. GRAF's depth prediction captures shape accurately but pose may be imprecise [S1].
*   **Scaling:** Patch-based approach allows application to high-resolution images by avoiding memory-intensive full-image prediction [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Does not require camera pose information; disentangles shape and appearance; handles dark scenes better than NeRF; allows explicit camera control [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Prone to overfitting with high iteration counts (floating artifacts in depth maps); early stopping leads to blurry RGB images; pose estimation is less precise than NeRF [S1].
*   **When to use:** When camera poses are unknown (unposed images); when dealing with dark or shadowed scenes; when disentangling shape and appearance is desired [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When precise pose estimation is required; when high-fidelity material specularity is critical (NeRF may be better); when computational resources are extremely limited and full-image rendering is necessary [S1].

## Background

Generative Radiance Fields (GRAF) was introduced by Schwarz et al. [S1]. It is based on the original NeRF paper [31] and extends it with an adversarial training scheme. The method was applied to space situational awareness, specifically for identifying non-cooperative spacecraft from unposed images [S1]. The authors note that while other 3D-aware GANs (like PlatonicGAN and HoloGAN) use voxel grids, GRAF uses a NeRF as a 3D implicit representation, allowing it to learn without pose information [S1]. The method was evaluated on synthetic datasets of satellites (CubeSat and SMOS) generated using Unity [S1].
