# NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)

Also called: NeRF Integration, NeRF Rendering Equation, Alpha Compositing in NeRF, Neural Radiance Field Volume Rendering

## Overview
NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing) is the differentiable volume rendering technique that synthesizes novel views by integrating predicted volume density and view-dependent color along camera rays [S1, S12]. It solves the problem of mapping a continuous 5D implicit radiance field to a 2D image by numerically approximating the volume rendering integral [S1, S11]. It sets itself apart from standard ray tracing by using a probabilistic density function to determine the expected color contribution of samples along a ray, rather than deterministic surface intersections [S12]. Unlike [NeRF-style Volume Rendering Integrator](5964-nerf-style-volume-rendering-integrator.md) which may generalize the integrator structure, this method specifically refers to the standard alpha-compositing formulation introduced by Mildenhall et al. [S12].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Camera ray $r(t) = o + td$, where $o$ is the camera origin and $d$ is the normalized viewing direction [S1, S12].
*   Integration bounds $t_n$ (near, typically camera center) and $t_f$ (far, scene boundary) [S1, S11].
*   Number of samples $N$ [S3, S10].
*   Neural network model $F_\Theta$ that maps $(x, d) \rightarrow (\sigma, c)$ [S11, S16].

**Outputs:**
*   Rendered RGB color $\hat{C}(r)$ [S1][S6, unofficial].
*   Rendered Depth $\hat{D}(r)$ [S1][S6, unofficial].
*   (Optional) Accumulated opacity $\hat{\alpha}(r)$ [S6, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   `t_vals`: Array of shape `(B, H, W, N)` or `(B, H, W, N+1)` containing sample distances $t_i$ along the ray [S8].
*   `points`: Array of shape `(B, H, W, N, 3)` containing 3D coordinates $x_i = o + t_i d$ [S7, unofficial].
*   `predictions`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N, 4)` containing raw MLP outputs for each sample [S8].
*   `rgb`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N, 3)` containing predicted colors $c_i$ [S8].
*   `sigma`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N)` containing predicted densities $\sigma_i$ [S8].
*   `deltas`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N)` containing distances between adjacent samples $\delta_i$ [S6, unofficial].
*   `alpha`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N)` containing opacity $\alpha_i$ [S8].
*   `transmittance`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N)` containing accumulated transmittance $T_i$ [S8].
*   `weights`: Tensor of shape `(B, H, W, N)` containing final weights $w_i$ [S8].

**Steps:**

1.  **Ray Generation:** For each pixel $(u, v)$ in the target image, compute the ray origin $o$ and direction $d$ using the camera intrinsics and extrinsics [S3, S12].
2.  **Sampling:** Divide the interval $[t_n, t_f]$ into $N$ equally spaced bins. Draw one sample $t_i$ uniformly at random from each bin $[t_n + \frac{i-1}{N}(t_f - t_n), t_n + \frac{i}{N}(t_f - t_n)]$ [S1, S11, S12]. This is known as non-deterministic stratified sampling [S1].
    *   *Note:* Some variants use uniform sampling for coarse networks and density-weighted sampling for fine networks [S24].
3.  **Point Computation:** Compute the 3D coordinates for each sample: $x_i = o + t_i d$ [S7, unofficial][S12].
4.  **Network Query:** Query the neural network $F_\Theta$ for each point $x_i$ and direction $d$ to obtain raw outputs. Reshape outputs to separate color and density [S8, S16].
    *   Apply Sigmoid to color channels to bound them to $[0, 1]$: $c_i = \text{Sigmoid}(\text{raw\_rgb}_i)$ [S8].
    *   Apply ReLU to density to ensure non-negativity: $\sigma_i = \text{ReLU}(\text{raw\_sigma}_i)$ [S8].
5.  **Delta Calculation:** Compute the distance between adjacent samples: $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ [S6, unofficial][S8].
    *   Append a large value (e.g., $10^{10}$) to the end of `deltas` to handle the segment from the last sample to infinity: $\delta_N = \infty$ [S6, unofficial][S8].
    *   Scale deltas by the norm of the ray direction if $d$ is not normalized: $\delta_i = \delta_i \cdot \|d\|$ [S6, unofficial].
6.  **Alpha Calculation:** Compute the opacity (alpha) for each sample using the volume density and interval distance [S1, S8]:
    $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i) $$
7.  **Transmittance Calculation:** Compute the accumulated transmittance $T_i$, representing the probability that the ray travels from $t_n$ to $t_i$ without being intercepted [S1, S3].
    $$ T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right) $$
    *   Implementation note: Use `cumprod` on $(1 - \alpha_i + \epsilon)$ with `exclusive=True` to avoid numerical instability, where $\epsilon$ is a small constant (e.g., $10^{-10}$) [S8].
8.  **Weight Calculation:** Compute the weight $w_i$ for each sample, which determines its contribution to the final pixel color [S8]:
    $$ w_i = \alpha_i T_i $$
9.  **Color Compositing:** Compute the final RGB color by summing the weighted colors [S1][S6, unofficial][S8]:
    $$ \hat{C}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i c_i $$
10. **Depth Compositing:** Compute the expected depth by summing the weighted sample distances [S1][S6, unofficial][S8]:
    $$ \hat{D}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i t_i $$
11. **Background Handling:** If the background is assumed to be white (common for synthetic datasets), add the complement of the accumulated opacity to the RGB result [S6, S7, unofficial]:
    $$ \hat{C}_{final}(r) = \hat{C}(r) + (1 - \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i) \cdot 1.0 $$
    *   If the background is not white, this step is omitted or uses a different background color [S6, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def nerf_render(rays_o, rays_d, t_vals, model, white_bkgd=True, noise_std=0.0):
    """
    rays_o: (B, H, W, 3) - Ray origins
    rays_d: (B, H, W, 3) - Ray directions (normalized)
    t_vals: (B, H, W, N) - Sample distances along the ray
    model: Neural network function F_theta(x, d) -> (sigma, rgb_raw)
    white_bkgd: Boolean, whether to assume white background
    noise_std: Float, standard deviation of Gaussian noise added to sigma during training
    """
    # 1. Compute 3D points
    # points shape: (B, H, W, N, 3)
    points = rays_o.unsqueeze(-2) + rays_d.unsqueeze(-2) * t_vals.unsqueeze(-1)
    
    # 2. Query Model
    # Flatten for batched model evaluation if necessary, but assuming vectorized model:
    # model returns raw_sigma (B, H, W, N) and raw_rgb (B, H, W, N, 3)
    raw_sigma, raw_rgb = model(points, rays_d)
    
    # 3. Process Outputs
    # Apply ReLU to sigma
    sigma = F.relu(raw_sigma)
    # Apply Sigmoid to RGB
    rgb = torch.sigmoid(raw_rgb)
    
    # Add noise to sigma during training (G)
    if noise_std > 0:
        sigma = sigma + torch.randn_like(sigma) * noise_std
        
    # 4. Compute Deltas
    # deltas shape: (B, H, W, N)
    deltas = t_vals[..., 1:] - t_vals[..., :-1]
    
    # Append infinity for the last segment
    # delta_inf shape: (B, H, W, 1)
    delta_inf = 1e10 * torch.ones_like(deltas[..., :1])
    deltas = torch.cat([deltas, delta_inf], dim=-1)
    
    # Scale deltas by ray direction norm (if rays_d are not unit length)
    # Assuming rays_d are normalized, this step might be skipped or identity
    dir_norm = torch.norm(rays_d, dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    deltas = deltas * dir_norm
    
    # 5. Compute Alpha
    # alpha shape: (B, H, W, N)
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * deltas)
    
    # 6. Compute Transmittance
    # exp_term shape: (B, H, W, N)
    exp_term = 1.0 - alpha + 1e-10 # Epsilon for stability
    # cumprod with exclusive=True means T_i = prod_{j<i} (1-alpha_j)
    # transmittance shape: (B, H, W, N)
    transmittance = torch.cumprod(exp_term, dim=-1, exclusive=True)
    
    # 7. Compute Weights
    # weights shape: (B, H, W, N)
    weights = alpha * transmittance
    
    # 8. Compute Final RGB
    # rgb_final shape: (B, H, W, 3)
    rgb_final = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * rgb, dim=-2)
    
    # 9. Compute Final Depth
    # depth_final shape: (B, H, W)
    # Use t_vals for depth calculation. Note: t_vals usually has shape (B, H, W, N)
    # If t_vals includes the start point, ensure alignment with weights.
    # Standard NeRF uses t_vals corresponding to the samples.
    depth_final = torch.sum(weights * t_vals, dim=-1)
    
    # 10. Background Handling
    if white_bkgd:
        # weights_sum shape: (B, H, W, 1)
        weights_sum = torch.sum(weights, dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        rgb_final = rgb_final + (1.0 - weights_sum)
        
    return rgb_final, depth_final
```

**Parameters:**
*   $N$: Number of samples per ray. Typical value: 64 for both coarse and fine networks [S10].
*   $t_n, t_f$: Integration bounds. $t_n$ is the camera center, $t_f$ is determined by the scene's bounding volume [S1].
*   $\epsilon$: Small constant for numerical stability in transmittance calculation. Typical value: $10^{-10}$ [S8].
*   `white_bkgd`: Boolean flag for background assumption. Default: True [S6, unofficial].
*   `noise_std`: Standard deviation for Gaussian noise added to density during training. Typical value: $>0$ during training, $0$ during evaluation [S7, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Numerical Instability:** If $\sigma_i \delta_i$ is very large, $\exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ underflows to 0, causing $\alpha_i$ to be 1. If $\sigma_i \delta_i$ is very small, $\alpha_i$ is close to 0. Using `cumprod` with `exclusive=True` and adding epsilon prevents NaNs in gradients [S8].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The last segment from the last sample to infinity must be handled. Appending a large delta ($10^{10}$) ensures that if the ray exits the scene, the remaining transmittance is accounted for in the background color [S6, unofficial][S8].
*   **View Direction:** The view direction $d$ must be consistent with the coordinate system used by the MLP. If positional encoding is used, $d$ must be encoded similarly to $x$ [S11, S16].
*   **Memory Usage:** Storing intermediate tensors for all samples can be memory-intensive. Chunking rays or samples is often required for high-resolution images [S7, S17, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Depth-Guided Sampling](7490-depth-guided-sampling.md)
Uses predicted depth maps to bias sample placement towards surfaces, reducing redundant sampling in free space.

### [Rendered Depth-Guided Warping](8893-rendered-depth-guided-warping.md)
Uses rendered depth to warp images for consistency regularization, not for direct color compositing.

### [Depth-Prior Guided Stratified Sampling](11292-depth-prior-guided-stratified-sampling.md)
Replaces uniform sampling with depth-prior guided stratified sampling to focus on likely surfaces.

### [Neus SDF Volume Rendering](12443-neus-sdf-volume-rendering.md)
Uses SDFs to derive density via logistic distribution derivative, concentrating weight at the zero-level set for precise surface extraction.

### [Progressive Blur Estimation](14151-progressive-blur-estimation.md)
Models blur as a distribution of rays, predicting multiple origins and directions per pixel.

### [MipNerfModel](14207-mipnerfmodel.md)
Treats rays as cones/cylinders and uses integrated positional encoding to solve aliasing.

### [Event Simulation from NeRF](15450-event-simulation-from-nerf.md)
Simulates temporal integration of light for event cameras, modeling motion blur via asynchronous event streams.

### NeRF Rendering Pipeline
A standard inference pipeline that iterates over camera poses, renders images with optional downsampling, and saves results [S17, unofficial].
*   **Steps:** Iterate over `render_poses`. For each pose, call `render(H, W, focal, chunk, c2w)` to get `rgb`, `disp`, `acc`. If `render_factor != 0`, downsample `H`, `W`, `focal` by `render_factor`. Append results to lists. If ground truth images are available and `render_factor == 0`, compute PSNR. Save `rgb` as 8-bit PNGs. Stack and return results [S17, unofficial].
*   **Parameters:** `render_factor`: Downsampling factor for speed. Default 0. Typical values 4 or 8 for preview [S17, unofficial]. `chunk`: Number of rays processed in parallel. Default 32768. Decrease if out of memory [S17, unofficial].

### NeRF-XL Multi-GPU Volume Rendering
A method for scaling NeRF rendering across multiple GPUs by partitioning the ray into segments [S18].
*   **Steps:** Initialize global transmittance $T_{1 \to i} = 1$ and global color $C_{1 \to N+1} = 0$. For each segment $s$ from 1 to $N$, get the GPU index $i$. If $i \neq k$ (current GPU), gather segment data $T_{i \to i+1}, C_{i \to i+1}$. Update global color: $C_{1 \to N+1} += T_{1 \to i} \times C_{i \to i+1}$. Update global transmittance: $T_{1 \to i} *= T_{i \to i+1}$ [S18].
*   **Parameters:** $I$: List of GPU indices $\{i_1, i_2, ..., i_N\}$ defining the order of segment processing [S18]. $k$: Index of the current GPU [S18].
*   **Pitfall:** During inference, data gathering is only required on a single GPU (e.g., GPU 0) to render the final image, reducing data transfer compared to training [S18].

### NeRF Volume Rendering via Numerical Quadrature
Approximates the volume rendering integral by summing contributions from sampled spheres inside the cone [S19].
*   **Formula:** $\text{Color} = \sum_i T_i (1 - \exp(-\text{density}_i (t_{i+1} - t_i))) \text{color}_i$, with $T_i = \exp(-\sum_{k<i} \text{density}_k (t_{k+1} - t_k))$ [S19].
*   **Steps:** Compute photometric loss between rendered colors and captured colors to back-propagate gradients [S19].

### Raymarcher
A rendering procedure using RM-LSTM to predict a single intersection point and output RGB directly, skipping volume rendering [S20, unofficial].
*   **Steps:** Extract latent features from PixelNeRF CNN encoder. Feed into RM-LSTM. RM-LSTM predicts step length and updates depth for 10 steps with 512 feature channels. Pass final depth point through PixelNeRF network to obtain RGB [S20, unofficial].
*   **Parameters:** RM-LSTM feature channels: 512. RM-LSTM steps: 10. Regularization depth bounds: 0.5 (near) and 2.0 (far) [S20, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** PSNR 23.02, SSIM 0.9013, LPIPS 0.137, Rendering speed 34.9 fps on ShapeNet cars [S20, unofficial].
*   **Comparison:** ~80 times faster than baseline but slightly lower quality (PSNR 23.02 vs 23.34). AVR is slower (4.2 fps) but higher quality (PSNR 23.22) than Raymarcher [S20, unofficial].

### 2D Neural Rendering Module
Maps low-resolution feature maps to final images using upsampling and 2D convolution to alleviate multi-view inconsistencies [S21].
*   **Formula:** $\text{Upsample}(X) = \text{Conv2D}(Y, K)$, where $Y = \text{Pixelshuffle}(\text{repeat}(X, 4) + \beta_\zeta(X), 2)$. $\beta_\zeta$ is a learnable 2-layer MLP. $K$ is a fixed blur kernel [S21].
*   **Steps:** 1. Repeat input feature tensor $X$ by factor 4. 2. Apply learnable MLP $\beta_\zeta$ to $X$. 3. Add results. 4. Apply PixelShuffle with scale 2 to $Y$. 5. Apply 2D Convolution with fixed blur kernel $K$ to produce output [S21].
*   **Parameters:** Convolution kernel size: 1x1 (to avoid multi-view inconsistencies). Activation: Leaky ReLU [S21].
*   **Comparison:** Unlike GIRAFFE which sums RGB images, HeadNeRF uses 1x1 convolutions to preserve geometric structure [S21].

### HeadNeRF Inference
Real-time rendering using the 2D Neural Rendering Module [S21].
*   **Steps:** 1. Sample 64 points per ray. 2. Compute volume rendering to get feature map IF. 3. Pass IF through 2D neural rendering module to get final image I [S21].
*   **Performance:** >40 fps on NVIDIA 3090 GPU [S21].
*   **Comparison:** Renders in real-time, whereas baseline HeadNeRF-vanilla takes ~5s per frame [S21].

### Depth Estimation from NeRF
Extracts 3D point-cloud data by estimating depth where transmittance changes significantly [S22].
*   **Formula:** $t_{surface} \approx \arg \min_t \left| \frac{dT(t)}{dt} \right|$. $p = o + t_{surface} d$. $color = c(p, d)$ [S22].
*   **Steps:** 1. Analyze transmittance function $T(t)$ along ray $r(t)$. 2. Pinpoint surface depth $t_{surface}$ by minimizing the first order derivative of $T(t)$. 3. Calculate 3D point $p$. 4. Query color at $p$. 5. Repeat for all pixels [S22].

### PixelNeRF Rendering Module
Produces approximate rendering (RGB and features) at a target pose for conditioning a diffusion U-Net [S23].
*   **Steps:** 1. Pass N input images through 2D U-Net to create feature images (128 channels). 2. Cast rays through target image pixels. 3. Sample 128 points along each ray (uniform up to distance 1, then linear in disparity). 4. Reproject 3D points into N input cameras. 5. Gather features to form tensor of size $64 \times 64 \times N \times 128$. 6. Append positionally-encoded 3D locations and mean/variance statistics [S23].
*   **Parameters:** Input resolution: Shorter dimension resized to 512 pixels [S23].
*   **Layout:** Output tensor: $64 \times 64 \times 131$ (3 RGB + 128 feature channels) [S23].

### NeRF Rendering with Stratified Sampling
Uses coarse and fine sampling strategies [S24].
*   **Formula:** Continuous: $c(r) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\sigma(r(t))c(r(t), d)dt$. Discrete: $\hat{c}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) c_i$. Loss: $L = \sum_{r \in R} [ (\hat{c}_c(r) - c(r))^2 + (\hat{c}_f(r) - c(r))^2 ]$ [S24].
*   **Steps:** 1. Discretize integral using stratified sampling. 2. Perform coarse sampling with uniform distribution. 3. Perform fine sampling based on volume density weights [S24].

### Motion Blur Simulation via Pose Averaging
Models motion blur by averaging sharp renders from multiple poses [S10].
*   **Parameters:** $p$: Number of poses sampled evenly over exposure time. Typical value: 5 [S10].
*   **Pitfall:** Assumes uniform motion or linear interpolation for intermediate poses, which may be inaccurate for non-uniform motion [S10].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P)$, where $N$ is the number of samples per ray and $P$ is the number of pixels (rays) [S1, S12]. With chunking, memory complexity is reduced, but time complexity remains linear in the number of samples [S7, S17, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P \cdot C)$, where $C$ is the number of channels in intermediate tensors (sigma, rgb, transmittance, etc.) [S7, S17, unofficial]. Chunking reduces peak memory usage [S7, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Original NeRF required extremely high computing power and long training times [S12]. Instant NGP reduced processing time significantly using multi-resolution hash encoding [S12]. HeadNeRF achieves >40 fps on NVIDIA 3090 using 2D neural rendering [S21]. Raymarcher achieves 34.9 fps on ShapeNet cars [S20, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Handles view-dependent effects (non-Lambertian materials) [S12]. Produces high-quality, photorealistic novel views with fine details [S12]. Differentiable, allowing end-to-end training from multi-view images [S1, S11].
*   **Limitations:** High computational cost for training and inference without acceleration [S12]. Not a point cloud or mesh; consists of continuous voxels, affecting 3D reconstruction interpretation [S12]. Sensitive to camera pose errors [S12].
*   **When to Use:** For high-quality novel view synthesis, especially for challenging scenes (metallic, translucent, transparent surfaces, homogeneous textures, occlusions, vegetation, fine details) [S12]. For cultural heritage reconstruction [S12].
*   **When Not to Use:** When real-time performance is critical and acceleration techniques (like Instant NGP or HeadNeRF) are not employed [S12, S21]. When explicit mesh or point cloud output is required without additional post-processing [S12].

## Background
NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) was first presented by Mildenhall et al. in 2020 [S12]. It represents a static scene as a continuous 5D function outputting volume density and view-dependent emitted radiance [S11, S12]. The method was trained using a squared error photometric loss on multi-view images [S1, S11]. Significant implementations include Instant NGP by Müller et al. (2022), which introduced multi-resolution hash encoding [S12], and Nerfstudio by Tancik et al. (2023) [S12]. It is used in platforms like Luma AI [S12] and for cultural heritage reconstruction [S12].
