# Pano-NeRF

Also called: irradiance fields

## Overview
Pano-NeRF is a method for geometry recovery and High Dynamic Range (HDR) novel view synthesis from sparse Low Dynamic Range (LDR) panoramic images [S1]. It models the scene using irradiance fields, which treat radiance as both a signal conveying lighting information and a light source illuminating other pixels, thereby increasing observation counts for geometry and leveraging irradiance-radiance attenuation for HDR reconstruction [S1]. It differs from standard radiance field methods by integrating surface rendering principles into the volume rendering framework to explicitly model inter-reflection and lighting interactions [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Sparse set of LDR panoramic images; camera poses for the input images.
*   **Outputs:** Optimized scene representation (geometry $\sigma$, color $c_r$) and HDR novel view synthesis; byproduct of spatially varying lighting estimation [S1].

**Data Structures:**
The method extends the standard NeRF representation. It maintains the standard volumetric density $\sigma(x)$ and view-dependent color $c_r(x, \mathbf{d})$ for a point $x$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$ [S1]. Additionally, it computes outgoing radiance $C_i(x)$ via surface rendering integration over incoming directions [S1].

**Formulas:**
The outgoing radiance $C_i(x)$ at position $x$ is computed by integrating irradiance from incoming light directions $\omega_i$ distributed on a sphere $\Omega$ centered at $x$ [S1]:

$$
C_i(x) = \int_{\omega_i \in \Omega} f_r(x, \omega_o, \omega_i) \cdot \max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0) \cdot c_i(x, \omega_i) \, d\omega_i
$$

Where:
*   $f_r(x, \omega_o, \omega_i)$ is the Bidirectional Reflection Distribution Function (BRDF) [S1].
*   $\mathbf{n}$ is the surface normal at point $x$ [S1].
*   $\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}$ is the dot product between the incoming light direction and the surface normal [S1].
*   $c_i(x, \omega_i)$ is the radiance from the incoming direction $\omega_i$ [S1].
*   $\max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0)$ handles back-face culling [S1].

The term $c_i(x, \omega_i)$ is calculated as the outgoing radiance $C_r$ of the incident ray, computed via standard volume rendering along that ray [S1]. This effectively links the irradiance field to the existing radiance field formulation [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Scene Representation:** Model the scene as surface pieces with reflection effects, where radiance is the interaction result of incoming light, reflectance, and surface normal [S1].
2.  **Incoming Radiance Calculation:** For each position $x$ and incoming direction $\omega_i$, calculate $c_i(x, \omega_i)$ by performing volume rendering along the ray defined by $\omega_i$ using the standard NeRF formulation [S1].
3.  **Outgoing Radiance Integration:** Compute the outgoing radiance $C_i(x)$ by integrating the product of the BRDF, the geometric term $\max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0)$, and the incoming radiance $c_i(x, \omega_i)$ over all incoming directions $\omega_i$ on the sphere $\Omega$ [S1].
4.  **Joint Optimization:** Integrate the irradiance fields into the existing radiance fields framework. Perform joint optimization to recover geometry ($\sigma$) and HDR radiance ($c_r$) by minimizing the training error between the observed radiance $C_{gt}$ and the computed outgoing radiance $C_i$ [S1]. Although $c_i$ is not directly supervised, its integral is constrained through this error, facilitating the optimization of $\sigma$ and $c_r$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_outgoing_radiance(x, normal_n, brdf_func, query_volume_rendering):
    """
    Computes outgoing radiance Ci(x) by integrating irradiance from incoming directions.
    
    Args:
        x: 3D position vector.
        normal_n: Surface normal vector at x.
        brdf_func: Function f_r(x, wo, wi) returning BRDF value.
        query_volume_rendering: Function that performs standard NeRF volume rendering 
                                along a ray to return radiance Cr.
    
    Returns:
        Ci: Scalar outgoing radiance at x.
    """
    # Define integration domain: sphere Omega centered at x
    # In practice, this is approximated by sampling N directions on the sphere
    num_samples = 1024 # [G] Typical number of samples for Monte Carlo integration
    total_irradiance = 0.0
    
    for k in range(num_samples):
        # Sample incoming direction wi uniformly on the sphere
        wi = sample_direction_on_sphere(k, num_samples)
        
        # Compute incoming radiance ci(x, wi)
        # This is the radiance Cr of the incident ray computed via volume rendering
        ci = query_volume_rendering(x, wi)
        
        # Compute geometric term: max(wi . n, 0)
        # This handles back-face culling for negative dot products [S1]
        geometric_term = max(dot(wi, normal_n), 0.0)
        
        # Compute BRDF value
        # Note: wo (outgoing direction) is typically the view direction for the pixel
        # being rendered, but for the irradiance field definition itself, 
        # we are computing the light arriving at x. The integration computes the 
        # total light leaving x due to reflections.
        # The formula in [S1] implies ci is the source, and we integrate its contribution.
        # The BRDF term f_r(x, wo, wi) modulates how much of ci contributes to the 
        # outgoing radiance in direction wo.
        # For the purpose of the irradiance field definition as a "light source",
        # we assume wo is fixed for the current view ray or integrated over.
        # However, the text says "integrate irradiance ... around different incoming 
        # light directions". The result Ci(x) is the outgoing radiance.
        # To match the formula Ci(x) = integral(...), we need a specific wo.
        # In the context of Pano-NeRF, this Ci(x) likely serves as the color 
        # contribution for the view ray passing through x.
        # Let's assume wo is the view direction for the current pixel.
        wo = get_view_direction_for_pixel() # [G] Context-dependent
        
        fr = brdf_func(x, wo, wi)
        
        # Accumulate contribution
        # domega is the solid angle of the sample
        domega = 4.0 * pi / num_samples # [G] Uniform sampling approximation
        total_irradiance += fr * geometric_term * ci * domega
        
    return total_irradiance
```

**Parameters:**
*   Number of incoming directions sampled on the sphere: Not given in the sources.
*   BRDF type: Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Back-facing surfaces:** The dot product $\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}$ can be negative for back-facing surfaces. This is handled by replacing $\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}$ with $\max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0)$ in the implementation [S1].
*   **Sparse Inputs:** Sparse inputs bring a small number of observation counts, which might cause unconstrained geometry [S1]. The irradiance fields address this by increasing observation counts for volumetric particles through inter-reflection between irradiance and outgoing radiance [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:** Extensive experiments demonstrate that irradiance fields outperform state-of-the-art methods on both geometry recovery and HDR reconstruction [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Increases observation counts for faithful geometry recovery even with sparse inputs; leverages irradiance-radiance attenuation for HDR reconstruction from LDR inputs; provides a byproduct of spatially varying lighting estimation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on sparse LDR panoramic images; requires joint optimization of irradiance and radiance fields [S1].
*   **When to use:** When geometry recovery and HDR novel view synthesis are required from sparse LDR panoramic inputs [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When dense multi-view stereo inputs are available and HDR reconstruction is not required [G].

## Background
Pano-NeRF was proposed to reconstruct HDR radiance from LDR inputs [S1]. The authors observe that radiance from each pixel in panoramic images can be modeled as both a signal to convey scene lighting information and a light source to illuminate other pixels [S1]. This insight led to the proposal of irradiance fields from sparse LDR panoramic images [S1]. The method integrates these fields into the existing radiance fields framework for joint optimization [S1]. The code is available at https://github.com/Lu-Zhan/Pano-NeRF [S1].
