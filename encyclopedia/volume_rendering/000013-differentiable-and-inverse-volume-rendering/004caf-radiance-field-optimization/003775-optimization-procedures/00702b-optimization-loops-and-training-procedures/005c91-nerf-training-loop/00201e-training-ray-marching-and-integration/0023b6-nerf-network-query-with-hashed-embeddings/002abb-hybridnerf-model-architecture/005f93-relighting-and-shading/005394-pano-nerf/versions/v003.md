# Pano-NeRF

Also called: irradiance fields

## Overview
Pano-NeRF is a method for geometry recovery and High Dynamic Range (HDR) novel view synthesis from sparse Low Dynamic Range (LDR) panoramic images [S1]. It models the scene using irradiance fields, which treat radiance as both a signal conveying lighting information and a light source illuminating other pixels, thereby increasing observation counts for geometry and leveraging irradiance-radiance attenuation for HDR reconstruction [S1]. It differs from standard NeRF by integrating surface rendering principles into the volumetric framework to explicitly model inter-reflection, and it differs from sibling methods like [Shading Replacement for Relighting](6346-shading-replacement-for-relighting.md) by deriving shading through volumetric irradiance integration rather than explicit light source estimation or normal correlation [S1, 6346-shading-replacement-for-relighting.md].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Sparse LDR panoramic images [S1].
**Outputs:** HDR radiance field, geometry (density $\sigma$), and spatially varying lighting estimates [S1].

**Data Structures:**
The method integrates irradiance fields into the existing radiance fields framework [S1]. The scene is modeled as surface pieces with reflection effects, where radiance is the interaction result of incoming light, reflectance, and surface normal [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Scene Representation:** Model the scene as surface pieces with reflection effects and integrate the irradiance fields into the existing radiance fields framework for joint optimization [S1].
2.  **Incoming Radiance Calculation ($c_i$):** For a point $x$ on a surface, consider incoming light directions $\omega_i$ distributed on a sphere $\Omega$ centered at $x$ [S1]. For each direction $\omega_i$, calculate the incoming radiance $c_i(x, \omega_i)$ [S1]. This value equals the outgoing radiance $C_r$ of the incident ray traveling in direction $-\omega_i$, computed via standard volume rendering along that ray [S1].
3.  **Outgoing Radiance Calculation ($C_i$):** Compute the outgoing radiance $C_i(x)$ at position $x$ through surface rendering by integrating irradiance from incoming light directions $\omega_i$ [S1]. The formula is:
    $$ C_i(x) = \int_{\omega_i \in \Omega} f_r(x, \omega_o, \omega_i) \cdot \max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0) \cdot c_i(x, \omega_i) \, d\omega_i $$
    where:
    *   $f_r(x, \omega_o, \omega_i)$ is the bidirectional reflection distribution function (BRDF) [S1].
    *   $\mathbf{n}$ is the surface normal at point $x$ [S1].
    *   $\omega_o$ is the outgoing viewing direction [S1].
    *   $\max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0)$ handles back-face culling, replacing the dot product $\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}$ when it is negative [S1].
4.  **Joint Optimization:** Integrate the irradiance fields into the radiance fields framework and perform joint optimization to recover geometry and HDR radiance [S1]. Although $c_i$ is not directly supervised, its integral is constrained through the training error between the observed ground-truth radiance $C_{gt}$ and the computed outgoing radiance $C_i$ [S1]. This facilitates the optimization of density $\sigma$ and radiance $c_r$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math
import random

def sample_sphere_uniform(num_samples):
    """Generate num_samples uniformly distributed directions on the unit sphere [G]."""
    samples = []
    for _ in range(num_samples):
        phi = 2.0 * math.pi * random.random()
        cos_theta = 2.0 * random.random() - 1.0
        sin_theta = math.sqrt(1.0 - cos_theta * cos_theta)
        samples.append((sin_theta * math.cos(phi), sin_theta * math.sin(phi), cos_theta))
    return samples

def volume_render(ray_origin, ray_direction, nerf_model):
    """Standard NeRF volume rendering along a ray [G]."""
    t_near, t_far = 0.1, 100.0
    num_steps = 128
    delta_t = (t_far - t_near) / num_steps
    accumulated_color = 0.0
    accumulated_transmittance = 1.0
    for i in range(num_steps):
        t = t_near + (i + 0.5) * delta_t
        point = ray_origin + t * ray_direction
        sigma, color = nerf_model.query(point, ray_direction)
        alpha = 1.0 - math.exp(-sigma * delta_t)
        weight = accumulated_transmittance * alpha
        accumulated_color += weight * color
        accumulated_transmittance *= (1.0 - alpha)
    return accumulated_color

def compute_outgoing_radiance(x, normal, view_direction, brdf, nerf_model, num_samples=1024):
    """Computes outgoing radiance C_i(x) using irradiance field integration [S1]."""
    omega_i_samples = sample_sphere_uniform(num_samples)
    total_irradiance = 0.0
    for omega_i in omega_i_samples:
        # 1. Calculate incoming radiance c_i(x, omega_i) via standard volume rendering
        incident_dir = (-omega_i[0], -omega_i[1], -omega_i[2])
        c_i = volume_render(x, incident_dir, nerf_model)
        # 2. Calculate BRDF term
        fr = brdf(x, view_direction, omega_i)
        # 3. Calculate cosine term with back-face culling
        dot_product = omega_i[0] * normal[0] + omega_i[1] * normal[1] + omega_i[2] * normal[2]
        cosine_term = max(dot_product, 0.0)
        # 4. Accumulate contribution
        total_irradiance += fr * cosine_term * c_i
    # Monte Carlo integration over sphere: multiply by sphere area (4*pi) and divide by samples
    C_i = total_irradiance * (4.0 * math.pi) / num_samples
    return C_i
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Sphere Sampling:** Uniform distribution on $\Omega$ [S1].
*   **BRDF:** Not specified in the sources [S1].
*   **Number of Monte Carlo samples:** Not given in the sources [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Back-facing surfaces:** The dot product $\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}$ can be negative for back-facing surfaces. This is handled by replacing $\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}$ with $\max(\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}, 0)$ in the implementation [S1].
*   **Sparse inputs:** Sparse inputs bring a small number of observation counts, which might cause unconstrained geometry. The irradiance fields mitigate this by increasing observation counts through inter-reflection between irradiance and outgoing radiance [S1].
*   **LDR to HDR:** The method leverages irradiance-radiance attenuation to reconstruct HDR radiance from LDR inputs [S1].

## Operating characteristics
**Measured Performance:**
The method outperforms state-of-the-art methods on both geometry recovery and HDR reconstruction [S1]. It achieves faithful geometry recovery even with sparse inputs [S1].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Increases observation counts for geometry recovery; leverages irradiance-radiance attenuation for HDR reconstruction; provides spatially varying lighting estimation as a byproduct [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to the integration over the sphere and multiple volume rendering passes; relies on sparse LDR inputs which may limit the quality of the initial geometry [S1].

**When to Use:**
*   Use when reconstructing HDR novel views and geometry from sparse LDR panoramic images [S1].

**When Not to Use:**
Not given in the sources.

## Background
Pano-NeRF was proposed by Lu Zhan et al. in 2023 [S1]. It addresses the problem of geometry recovery and HDR novel view synthesis from sparse LDR panoramic images [S1]. The method builds on the concept of irradiance fields, observing that radiance from each pixel in panoramic images can be modeled as both a signal to convey scene lighting information and a light source to illuminate other pixels [S1]. This approach increases observation counts for faithful geometry recovery and leverages irradiance-radiance attenuation for HDR reconstruction [S1]. The code is available at https://github.com/Lu-Zhan/Pano-NeRF [S1].
