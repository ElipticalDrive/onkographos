# Aleth-NeRF

Also called: Aleth-NeRF with Concealing Field Assumption

## Overview
Aleth-NeRF is a NeRF variant that trains on under- and over-exposed images to render normal-light novel views by introducing a 'Concealing Field' assumption, where air particles attenuate emission rather than objects lacking emission [S1]. It differs from RAW-NeRF by taking 8-bit LDR images as inputs, whereas RAW-NeRF requires 16-bit HDR RAW images [S1]. Unlike methods that apply enhancement networks before or after NeRF training, Aleth-NeRF trains end-to-end without pre-processing or post-processing [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Multi-view images captured under adverse lighting conditions (low-light or over-exposed). The implementation uses 8-bit LDR images [S1]. The dataset used for evaluation is the LOM dataset, containing 5 real-world scenes ('buu', 'chair', 'sofa', 'bike', 'shrub') with paired low-light, normal-light, and over-exposure images [S1].
*   **Outputs:** Novel views rendered in normal-light conditions [S1].

**Data Structures:**
The model uses the standard NeRF architecture with two Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) [S1]:
1.  **Density MLP ($F_\sigma$):** Maps 3D location $\mathbf{r}(i)$ to density $\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))$ [S1].
2.  **Color MLP ($F_c$):** Maps a hidden feature vector $\mathbf{h}$ (derived from $F_\sigma$) and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ to color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(i), \mathbf{d})$ [S1].
3.  **Local Concealing Field ($\Omega$):** Generated by applying a large kernel convolution layer of size 7 to the output of the density MLP $F_\sigma$ [S1].
4.  **Global Concealing Field ($\Theta_G$):** A set of learnable parameters corresponding to the camera distance $i$ for all camera rays [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Positional Encoding and Density Prediction:**
    For a camera ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t \cdot \mathbf{d}$, sample $N$ points $\mathbf{r}(i)$ along the ray. Pass each $\mathbf{r}(i)$ through the density MLP $F_\sigma$ to obtain density $\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))$ and hidden feature $\mathbf{h}$ [S1].
    $$ \sigma(\mathbf{r}(i)) = F_\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i)) $$
    $$ \mathbf{h} = F_\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i)) $$
    Activate $\sigma$ with ReLU and $\mathbf{h}$ with Sigmoid [S1].

2.  **Local Concealing Field Generation:**
    Compute the local concealing field $\Omega(\mathbf{r}(i))$ by applying a convolution with kernel size 7 to the density output [S1]:
    $$ \Omega(\mathbf{r}(i)) = \text{conv}_{\text{size}:7}(F_\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))) $$
    This establishes spatial relationships and enriches the field with light-related information [S1].

3.  **Global Concealing Field Initialization:**
    Initialize the global concealing field parameters $\Theta_G(i)$ for each camera distance $i$ with a value of 0.5 [S1]. These parameters remain constant within a scene and are independent of voxels [S1].

4.  **Accumulated Transmittance Calculation:**
    Calculate the standard transmittance $T(\mathbf{r}(i))$ and the concealed transmittance $T_{\text{conceal}}(\mathbf{r}(i))$ [S1].
    Standard transmittance:
    $$ T(\mathbf{r}(i)) = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(j)) \cdot \delta\right) $$
    Concealed transmittance:
    $$ T_{\text{conceal}}(\mathbf{r}(i)) = T(\mathbf{r}(i)) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} \Omega(\mathbf{r}(j)) \Theta_G(j) $$
    Here, $\delta$ is the constant distance between adjacent sample points under uniform sampling [S1].

5.  **Color Prediction:**
    Pass the hidden feature $\mathbf{h}$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ through the color MLP $F_c$ to obtain color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(i), \mathbf{d})$ [S1].

6.  **Volume Rendering:**
    Compute the rendered color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ using the discrete approximation [S1]:
    $$ \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T(\mathbf{r}(i)) (1 - \exp(-\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i)) \cdot \delta)) \cdot \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(i), \mathbf{d}) $$
    The choice of transmittance ($T$ or $T_{\text{conceal}}$) depends on the training scenario (see Step 7) [S1].

7.  **Training Scenarios:**
    *   **Low-Light Training:**
        *   Generate adverse-light images $\hat{C}_{\text{adv}}$ using $T_{\text{conceal}}$ in the rendering equation [S1].
        *   Generate normal-light images $\hat{C}_{\text{nor}}$ by removing concealing fields (using original $T$) in the rendering equation [S1].
    *   **Over-Exposed Training:**
        *   Generate very-bright images $\hat{C}_{\text{adv}}$ using original $T$ [S1].
        *   Generate normal-light images $\hat{C}_{\text{nor}}$ using $T_{\text{conceal}}$ to suppress overly bright emissions [S1].

8.  **Loss Computation:**
    Compute the total loss $L$ as a sum of four components [S1]:
    $$ L = L_{\text{it-mse}} + \lambda_1 L_{\text{de}} + \lambda_2 L_{\text{co}} + \lambda_3 L_{\text{cc}} $$

    *   **Inverse Tone MSE Loss ($L_{\text{it-mse}}$):**
        Standard MSE causes imbalance where dark pixels contribute minimally and bright pixels dominate [S1]. To rectify this, an inverse tone curve $\Phi(x)$ is applied to re-balance pixel weights [S1]:
        $$ \Phi(x) = \frac{1}{3} \left( \sin^{-1}(1 - 2x) - \sin\left(\frac{1 - 2x}{2}\right) \right) $$
        $$ L_{\text{it-mse}} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \| \Phi(\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) + \epsilon) - \Phi(C(\mathbf{r}) + \epsilon) \|^2 $$
        where $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ is a small value for accurate estimation [S1].

    *   **Degree Loss ($L_{\text{de}}$):**
        Controls the enhancement degree $e$ via global average pooling of $\hat{C}_{\text{nor}}$ [S1]:
        $$ L_{\text{de}} = \| \text{avgpool}(\hat{C}_{\text{nor}}) - e \|^2 $$
        The enhancement degree $e$ is set to 0.45 in all experiments [S1].

    *   **Contrast Degree Loss ($L_{\text{co}}$):**
        Maintains structural similarity and controls contrast between adjacent sampling pixels of predicted normal-light and ground truth adverse-light images [S1]:
        $$ L_{\text{co}} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \sum_{k \in \{+1, -1\}} \| \hat{C}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}_{\text{adv}}(\mathbf{r} + k) \|^2 $$
        The hyperparameter $\eta$ controls contrast. In over-exposure scenes, $e \cdot \eta$ is fixed to 0.5 [S1].

    *   **Color Constancy Loss ($L_{\text{cc}}$):**
        Regularizes color based on the gray-world assumption to prevent color imbalance when removing/adding concealing fields [S1]:
        $$ L_{\text{cc}} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \sum_{p,q} (\hat{C}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r})_p - \hat{C}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r})_q)^2 $$
        where $p, q$ denote different color channels [S1].

9.  **Optimization:**
    Optimize the network parameters using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-4}$ and a cosine learning rate decay strategy every 2500 iterations [S1]. The batch size is 4096, and training runs for 62,500 iterations [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_1$ (degree loss weight): $10^{-3}$ [S1]
*   $\lambda_2$ (contrast loss weight): $10^{-3}$ [S1]
*   $\lambda_3$ (color constancy loss weight): $10^{-8}$ [S1]
*   $e$ (enhancement degree): 0.45 [S1]
*   $\epsilon$ (MSE stability): $10^{-3}$ [S1]
*   Learning rate: $5 \times 10^{-4}$ [S1]
*   Batch size: 4096 [S1]
*   Total iterations: 62,500 [S1]

**Pitfalls and Edge Cases:**
*   **MSE Imbalance:** Direct application of standard MSE loss in low-light/over-bright scenes results in poor training because dark pixels contribute minimally while bright pixels dominate [S1]. The inverse tone curve $\Phi$ is required to solve this [S1].
*   **Post-Processing Failure:** Applying enhancement methods to NeRF results ('NeRF + *') underperforms pre-processing ('* + NeRF') because NeRF produces poor-quality dark images that struggle enhancement methods [S1].
*   **Color Imbalance:** Removing or adding concealing fields can cause color imbalance; the color constancy loss mitigates this [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class AlethNeRF:
    def __init__(self, num_samples, hidden_dim, num_layers, kernel_size=7):
        """
        Initialize Aleth-NeRF model.
        
        Args:
            num_samples (int): Number of sample points per ray.
            hidden_dim (int): Dimension of hidden features.
            num_layers (int): Number of layers in MLPs.
            kernel_size (int): Kernel size for local concealing field convolution.
        """
        self.num_samples = num_samples
        self.hidden_dim = hidden_dim
        
        # Density MLP: maps 3D location to density and hidden feature
        self.density_mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim), # Assuming positional encoding input size is hidden_dim
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim + 1) # Output: hidden_dim features + 1 density
        )
        
        # Color MLP: maps hidden feature and viewing direction to color
        self.color_mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim + 3, hidden_dim), # 3 for viewing direction
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, 3) # Output: RGB color
        )
        
        # Local Concealing Field Convolution Layer
        # Applied to the density output (scalar) to generate local concealing field
        # Note: In practice, this might be applied to a grid of densities or features.
        # Based on source: conv_size:7 (F_sigma(r(i))) -> Omega(r(i))
        self.local_conv = nn.Conv1d(1, 1, kernel_size=kernel_size, padding=kernel_size//2)
        
        # Global Concealing Field Parameters
        # Learnable parameters for camera distance i
        # Initialized to 0.5
        self.global_concealing_field = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(num_samples) * 0.5)
        
        # Hyperparameters
        self.e = 0.45
        self.epsilon = 1e-3
        self.lambda1 = 1e-3
        self.lambda2 = 1e-3
        self.lambda3 = 1e-8
        self.delta = 0.01 # Constant distance between adjacent sample points (example value, depends on scene scale)

    def inverse_tone_curve(self, x):
        """
        Inverse tone curve Phi(x) to re-balance pixel weights.
        Phi(x) = (1/3) * (sin^-1(1 - 2x) - sin((1 - 2x)/2))
        """
        # Clamp x to [0, 1] to avoid domain errors in asin
        x_clamped = torch.clamp(x, 0.0, 1.0)
        term1 = torch.asin(1 - 2 * x_clamped)
        term2 = torch.sin((1 - 2 * x_clamped) / 2)
        return (1/3) * (term1 - term2)

    def forward(self, ray_origins, ray_directions, positions, viewing_directions, is_low_light=True):
        """
        Forward pass for volume rendering.
        
        Args:
            ray_origins (Tensor): Origin of rays.
            ray_directions (Tensor): Direction of rays.
            positions (Tensor): 3D positions of sample points along rays. Shape: [num_rays, num_samples, 3]
            viewing_directions (Tensor): Viewing directions for each sample point. Shape: [num_rays, num_samples, 3]
            is_low_light (bool): If True, train for low-light scenario. If False, train for over-exposure.
            
        Returns:
            rendered_color (Tensor): Rendered color for each ray.
            adv_color (Tensor): Adverse light color (used for loss).
            nor_color (Tensor): Normal light color (used for loss).
        """
        # 1. Density and Hidden Feature Prediction
        # positions shape: [num_rays, num_samples, 3]
        # density_mlp expects input of size hidden_dim (positional encoding)
        # Assuming positional encoding is handled externally or passed as 'positions'
        # For this pseudocode, we assume 'positions' are already positional encoded to hidden_dim
        # If not, positional encoding step is needed here.
        
        # Reshape for MLP: [num_rays * num_samples, hidden_dim]
        num_rays = positions.shape[0]
        num_samples = positions.shape[1]
        flat_positions = positions.view(-1, positions.shape[-1])
        
        density_output = self.density_mlp(flat_positions)
        hidden_features = density_output[:, :-1] # [num_rays * num_samples, hidden_dim]
        densities = density_output[:, -1] # [num_rays * num_samples]
        
        # Reshape back
        hidden_features = hidden_features.view(num_rays, num_samples, -1)
        densities = densities.view(num_rays, num_samples)
        
        # Apply ReLU to densities
        densities = F.relu(densities)
        
        # 2. Local Concealing Field Generation
        # conv_size:7 (F_sigma(r(i))) -> Omega(r(i))
        # densities shape: [num_rays, num_samples]
        # Reshape for Conv1d: [num_rays, 1, num_samples]
        densities_conv = densities.unsqueeze(1)
        local_concealing = self.local_conv(densities_conv).squeeze(1) # [num_rays, num_samples]
        
        # 3. Global Concealing Field
        # global_concealing_field shape: [num_samples]
        global_concealing = self.global_concealing_field.unsqueeze(0).expand(num_rays, -1) # [num_rays, num_samples]
        
        # 4. Transmittance Calculation
        # Standard Transmittance T(r(i))
        # T(r(i)) = exp(-sum_{j=1}^{i-1} sigma(r(j)) * delta)
        # Cumulative sum of densities
        cum_densities = torch.cumsum(densities * self.delta, dim=1)
        # Shift for T(r(i)): T(r(i)) corresponds to transmittance BEFORE sample i
        # T(r(1)) = 1 (exp(0))
        # T(r(i)) = exp(-cum_densities[:, i-2]) for i > 1
        # Handle first sample
        T_standard = torch.exp(-F.pad(cum_densities[:, :-1], (1, 0), value=0)) # [num_rays, num_samples]
        
        # Concealed Transmittance T_conceal(r(i))
        # T_conceal(r(i)) = T(r(i)) * product_{j=1}^{i-1} Omega(r(j)) * Theta_G(j)
        # Product of concealing fields up to i-1
        # local_concealing * global_concealing
        concealing_product = local_concealing * global_concealing
        cum_concealing = torch.cumsum(torch.log(concealing_product + 1e-8), dim=1) # Use log to avoid underflow
        # Shift for T_conceal(r(i))
        T_conceal = T_standard * torch.exp(F.pad(cum_concealing[:, :-1], (1, 0), value=0))
        
        # 5. Color Prediction
        # viewing_directions shape: [num_rays, num_samples, 3]
        # Concatenate hidden_features and viewing_directions
        color_input = torch.cat([hidden_features, viewing_directions], dim=-1)
        flat_color_input = color_input.view(-1, color_input.shape[-1])
        colors = self.color_mlp(flat_color_input) # [num_rays * num_samples, 3]
        colors = colors.view(num_rays, num_samples, 3)
        
        # 6. Volume Rendering
        # Alpha: 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
        alpha = 1 - torch.exp(-densities * self.delta)
        
        # Render Normal Light (C_nor)
        # Low-light: use T_standard. Over-exposure: use T_conceal.
        if is_low_light:
            T_render_nor = T_standard
        else:
            T_render_nor = T_conceal
            
        # Render Adverse Light (C_adv)
        # Low-light: use T_conceal. Over-exposure: use T_standard.
        if is_low_light:
            T_render_adv = T_conceal
        else:
            T_render_adv = T_standard
            
        # C(r) = sum_{i=1}^N T(r(i)) * (1 - exp(-sigma(r(i)) * delta)) * c(r(i), d)
        # T_render_nor and T_render_adv are T(r(i))
        
        weights_nor = T_render_nor * alpha
        weights_adv = T_render_adv * alpha
        
        rendered_color_nor = torch.sum(weights_nor.unsqueeze(-1) * colors, dim=1) # [num_rays, 3]
        rendered_color_adv = torch.sum(weights_adv.unsqueeze(-1) * colors, dim=1) # [num_rays, 3]
        
        return rendered_color_nor, rendered_color_adv

    def compute_loss(self, pred_nor, pred_adv, gt_adv, gt_nor=None):
        """
        Compute the total loss.
        
        Args:
            pred_nor (Tensor): Predicted normal-light color. Shape: [num_rays, 3]
            pred_adv (Tensor): Predicted adverse-light color. Shape: [num_rays, 3]
            gt_adv (Tensor): Ground truth adverse-light color. Shape: [num_rays, 3]
            gt_nor (Tensor): Ground truth normal-light color. Shape: [num_rays, 3] (Optional, used for L_it-mse if available)
            
        Returns:
            total_loss (Tensor): Scalar loss value.
        """
        # 1. Inverse Tone MSE Loss
        # L_it-mse = sum || Phi(C_hat + eps) - Phi(C + eps) ||^2
        # If gt_nor is available, compare pred_nor with gt_nor.
        # If not, the source implies comparing with ground truth adverse-light images for the adverse component,
        # but the loss description says "compare predicted adverse-light images with predicted adverse-light images" 
        # which seems like a typo in the source or implies using gt_adv for the adverse part.
        # The source says: "The NeRF regression loss is applied to compare predicted adverse-light images with ground truth adverse-light images"
        # And "inverse tone MSE loss... enhance scene clarity".
        # Let's assume L_it-mse is applied to the adverse prediction against ground truth adverse.
        # And potentially normal prediction against ground truth normal if available.
        
        # For this implementation, we assume gt_adv is the ground truth for the adverse view.
        # And if gt_nor is provided, we use it for normal view.
        
        pred_nor_clamped = torch.clamp(pred_nor, 0.0, 1.0)
        pred_adv_clamped = torch.clamp(pred_adv, 0.0, 1.0)
        gt_adv_clamped = torch.clamp(gt_adv, 0.0, 1.0)
        
        phi_pred_nor = self.inverse_tone_curve(pred_nor_clamped + self.epsilon)
        phi_pred_adv = self.inverse_tone_curve(pred_adv_clamped + self.epsilon)
        phi_gt_adv = self.inverse_tone_curve(gt_adv_clamped + self.epsilon)
        
        # L_it-mse for adverse view
        loss_it_mse_adv = torch.mean((phi_pred_adv - phi_gt_adv) ** 2)
        
        # If ground truth normal light is available, compute for normal view
        if gt_nor is not None:
            gt_nor_clamped = torch.clamp(gt_nor, 0.0, 1.0)
            phi_gt_nor = self.inverse_tone_curve(gt_nor_clamped + self.epsilon)
            phi_pred_nor_gt = self.inverse_tone_curve(pred_nor_clamped + self.epsilon)
            loss_it_mse_nor = torch.mean((phi_pred_nor_gt - phi_gt_nor) ** 2)
            loss_it_mse = loss_it_mse_adv + loss_it_mse_nor
        else:
            loss_it_mse = loss_it_mse_adv
            
        # 2. Degree Loss
        # L_de = || avgpool(C_hat_nor) - e ||^2
        avg_pred_nor = torch.mean(pred_nor)
        loss_de = (avg_pred_nor - self.e) ** 2
        
        # 3. Contrast Degree Loss
        # L_co = sum_r sum_{k in [+1, -1]} || C_hat_nor(r) - C_hat_adv(r+k) ||^2
        # This requires spatial structure. In ray sampling, 'r' is a ray index.
        # The source mentions "adjacent sampling pixels", implying spatial adjacency in the image plane.
        # This requires reshaping the batch of rays into an image grid.
        # For this pseudocode, we assume the batch is structured as an image or we approximate with ray indices if spatial info is lost.
        # However, standard NeRF training uses random rays, so spatial adjacency is not guaranteed in the batch.
        # The source likely implies that during training, rays are sampled such that spatial neighbors are included,
        # or the loss is computed on rendered images.
        # Given the constraint of "plain text alone", we note this dependency.
        # We will implement a placeholder for spatial contrast if spatial coordinates are available.
        # For now, we assume the batch contains spatially adjacent rays or this loss is computed on rendered images.
        # Let's assume we have a way to identify adjacent rays.
        # This is a significant implementation detail not fully specified in the source text regarding ray sampling order.
        # We will skip the exact implementation of L_co in the simple ray-based forward pass and note it requires spatial adjacency.
        loss_co = torch.tensor(0.0) # Placeholder: Requires spatial adjacency of rays in batch
        
        # 4. Color Constancy Loss
        # L_cc = sum_r sum_{p,q} (C_hat_nor(r)_p - C_hat_nor(r)_q)^2
        # p, q are color channels (0, 1, 2)
        loss_cc = 0.0
        for p in range(3):
            for q in range(p + 1, 3):
                diff = pred_nor[:, p] - pred_nor[:, q]
                loss_cc += torch.mean(diff ** 2)
                
        # Total Loss
        total_loss = loss_it_mse + self.lambda1 * loss_de + self.lambda2 * loss_co + self.lambda3 * loss_cc
        
        return total_loss
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time and Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:** Evaluated on the LOM dataset (5 scenes: 'buu', 'chair', 'sofa', 'bike', 'shrub') with images down-sampled to 375x500 resolution [S1]. Aleth-NeRF achieved the best or second-best performance on most scenes compared to baselines like vanilla NeRF, NeRF + Image Enhancement Methods, and RAW-NeRF [S1].
*   **Scaling:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Handles both low-light and over-exposed scenes [S1].
    *   Works with 8-bit LDR images, unlike RAW-NeRF which requires 16-bit HDR RAW [S1].
    *   End-to-end training without pre- or post-processing [S1].
    *   The inverse tone curve loss effectively balances pixel weights in adverse lighting [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Post-processing NeRF results ('NeRF + *') underperforms pre-processing ('* + NeRF') due to poor quality dark images generated by NeRF [S1].
    *   The contrast degree loss requires spatial adjacency of rays, which may complicate random ray sampling strategies [S1].
*   **When to use:** When synthesizing novel views from under- or over-exposed LDR images without paired HDR data or pre-processing capabilities [S1].
*   **When not:** When RAW HDR data is available and RAW-NeRF is preferred, or when pre-processing enhancement is feasible and preferred [S1].

## Background
Aleth-NeRF was introduced by Ziteng Cui, Lin Gu, Xiao Sun, Xianzheng Ma, Yu Qiao, and Tatsuya Harada at The University of Tokyo, Shanghai AI Laboratory, RIKEN AIP, and University of Oxford [S1]. It was presented at The Thirty-Eighth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI-24) [S1]. The method is motivated by the ancient Greek emission theory, which posits that visual perception is formed by visual rays emitted from the eye, and darkness is caused by particles between the object and the eye [S1]. The implementation uses the open-source PyTorch toolbox NeRF-Factory [S1].
