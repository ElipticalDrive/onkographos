# Multi-Space Neural Radiance Field (MS-NeRF)

Also called: MS-Mip-NeRF 360, MS-NeRFB, Multi-space NeRF

## Overview
MS-NeRF addresses the failure of standard single-space Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) to render reflective and refractive objects, which often results in blurry or distorted images due to violated multi-view consistency [S1, S2]. It represents the scene using a group of feature fields in parallel sub-spaces instead of a single radiance field in Euclidean space, allowing virtual images to be placed in sub-spaces visible only from certain views [S1]. Unlike single-space NeRF, which often requires manual labeling of reflective surfaces to avoid sub-optimal convergence, MS-NeRF automatically handles mirror-like objects without such labeling [S1].

## How it works
MS-NeRF modifies the output layer of a backbone NeRF method (e.g., NeRF, Mip-NeRF 360, TensoRF) by replacing the standard single-output head with a multi-space module [S1]. This module leverages the Feature Fields principle, where the network predicts arbitrary per-point feature signals in addition to color and density [Parent].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** Ray $r$ defined by origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$; sampled points $\mathbf{x}_i$ along the ray.
*   **Output:** Final rendered color $\hat{C}(r)$ for the pixel.

### Data Structures
The method introduces $K$ parallel sub-spaces. For each sampled point $i$ along the ray, the backbone network outputs:
*   $K$ densities: $\{\sigma_{ik}\}_{k=1}^K$, where $\sigma_{ik}$ is the density at point $i$ in sub-space $k$.
*   $K$ feature vectors: $\{\mathbf{f}_{ik}\}_{k=1}^K$, where $\mathbf{f}_{ik} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the feature vector at point $i$ in sub-space $k$.
*   $K$ is the total sub-space number (hyperparameter).
*   $d$ is the feature dimension of the neural feature field (hyperparameter) [S1].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Ray Sampling and Feature Extraction:**
    Sample points $\mathbf{x}_i$ along the ray $r$. Query the backbone network to obtain $K$ densities $\sigma_{ik}$ and $K$ feature vectors $\mathbf{f}_{ik}$ for each point $i$ and sub-space $k$.

2.  **Per-Subspace Volume Rendering:**
    Compute the rendered feature map $\hat{\mathbf{F}}^k(r)$ for each sub-space $k$ independently using volume integration [S1, Parent]. The formula for the $k$-th sub-space is:
    $$
    \hat{\mathbf{F}}^k(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_{ik} (1 - \exp(-\sigma_{ik} \delta_i)) \mathbf{f}_{ik}
    $$
    where:
    *   $N$ is the number of samples along the ray.
    *   $\delta_i$ is the distance between adjacent samples.
    *   $T_{ik} = \exp(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_{jk} \delta_j)$ is the accumulated transmittance for sub-space $k$ up to sample $i$ [S1, Parent].
    *   $\alpha_{ik} = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_{ik} \delta_i)$ is the opacity weight [Parent].

3.  **Decoder MLP (Color Prediction):**
    Pass the rendered feature maps $\{\hat{\mathbf{F}}^k\}_{k=1}^K$ through a Decoder MLP ($\Theta_D$) with one hidden layer to predict the RGB color $\mathbf{C}_k$ for each sub-space [S1]:
    $$
    \Theta_D(\{\hat{\mathbf{F}}^k\}) \rightarrow \{\mathbf{C}_k\}_{k=1}^K
    $$
    Here, $\mathbf{C}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the RGB vector for sub-space $k$ [S1].

4.  **Gate MLP (Visibility Weighting):**
    Pass the rendered feature maps $\{\hat{\mathbf{F}}^k\}_{k=1}^K$ through a Gate MLP ($\Theta_G$) with one hidden layer to predict visibility weights $w_k$ for each sub-space [S1]:
    $$
    \Theta_G(\{\hat{\mathbf{F}}^k\}) \rightarrow \{w_k\}_{k=1}^K
    $$
    The weights $w_k$ control the visibility of the corresponding sub-space [S1].

5.  **Softmax Weighting and Final Composition:**
    Apply the softmax function to the weights $\{w_k\}$ to determine the contribution of each sub-space to the final color. The final rendered color $\hat{C}(r)$ is computed as [S1]:
    $$
    \hat{C}(r) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\exp(w_k)}{\sum_{i=1}^{K} \exp(w_i)} \mathbf{C}_k
    $$
    This ensures that the composition weights change according to spatial location and view direction, dynamically combining sub-spaces [S1].

### Parameters
*   $K$: Total sub-space number. Typical values are not explicitly fixed in the text, but experiments use specific values for Mip-NeRF 360 and TensoRF [S1].
*   $d$: Feature dimension of the neural feature field [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Complex Scenes:** More sub-spaces may be required to accurately model complex reflective and refractive scenes [S2].
*   **Computational Overhead:** The method adds small computational overheads for training and inferring extra-space outputs, but remains lightweight compared to the backbone [S1].
*   **No Additional Loss:** The formulation requires no additional loss terms compared to vanilla NeRF; supervision is performed via standard photometric loss on the final color $\hat{C}(r)$ [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
def ms_nerf_render(ray_origin, ray_direction, backbone_network, decoder_mlp, gate_mlp, K, d):
    """
    Renders a single ray using the Multi-Space NeRF method.
    
    Args:
        ray_origin: 3D vector, origin of the ray.
        ray_direction: 3D vector, direction of the ray.
        backbone_network: Function that takes (ray_origin, ray_direction, num_samples) 
                          and returns K densities and K feature vectors.
        decoder_mlp: Function that takes K feature maps and returns K RGB colors.
        gate_mlp: Function that takes K feature maps and returns K visibility weights.
        K: Integer, number of sub-spaces.
        d: Integer, feature dimension.
        
    Returns:
        final_color: 3D vector, the final rendered RGB color.
    """
    # 1. Sample points along the ray
    # Assume backbone_network handles sampling and returns densities and features
    # densities: shape (K, N), features: shape (K, N, d)
    densities, features = backbone_network(ray_origin, ray_direction, K, d)
    
    # 2. Compute transmittance and opacity for each sub-space
    # densities shape: (K, N)
    # deltas: distances between samples, shape (N,)
    # We assume deltas are passed or computed externally. 
    # For simplicity, we assume deltas are available as 'deltas' variable.
    
    # Compute opacity alpha: 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
    # alpha shape: (K, N)
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-densities * deltas)
    
    # Compute transmittance T: exp(-sum(sigma * delta))
    # accumulated_sigma_delta shape: (K, N)
    accumulated_sigma_delta = torch.cumsum(densities * deltas, dim=1)
    # T shape: (K, N), T[i] = exp(-sum_{j=0}^{i-1} sigma_j * delta_j)
    # Note: T[0] should be 1.0 (exp(0))
    T = torch.exp(-torch.cat([torch.zeros(K, 1, device=densities.device), accumulated_sigma_delta[:, :-1]], dim=1))
    
    # 3. Render feature maps for each sub-space
    # features shape: (K, N, d)
    # weights: alpha * T, shape: (K, N, 1) to broadcast with features
    weights = (T * alpha).unsqueeze(-1)  # Shape: (K, N, 1)
    
    # Rendered feature maps F_hat: sum over N samples
    # F_hat shape: (K, d)
    F_hat = torch.sum(weights * features, dim=1)
    
    # 4. Decoder MLP: Predict RGB colors for each sub-space
    # F_hat shape: (K, d)
    # C_k shape: (K, 3)
    C_k = decoder_mlp(F_hat)
    
    # 5. Gate MLP: Predict visibility weights for each sub-space
    # F_hat shape: (K, d)
    # w_k shape: (K,)
    w_k = gate_mlp(F_hat)
    
    # 6. Softmax weighting
    # w_k shape: (K,)
    # softmax weights shape: (K,)
    softmax_weights = torch.softmax(w_k, dim=0)
    
    # 7. Final color composition
    # C_k shape: (K, 3)
    # softmax_weights shape: (K,)
    final_color = torch.sum(softmax_weights.unsqueeze(-1) * C_k, dim=0)
    
    return final_color
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time and Memory Complexity:** The method adds small computational overheads for training and inferring the extra-space outputs compared to single-space NeRF methods [S1]. The complexity scales with the number of sub-spaces $K$.
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   On Mip-NeRF 360: Improves PSNR by 4.15 dB on reflective regions with 0.5% extra parameters, without degrading rendering quality on other regions [S1].
    *   On TensoRF: Improves PSNR by 2.71 dB on reflective regions with 0.046% extra parameters, without degrading rendering quality on other regions [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Automatically handles mirror-like objects in 360-degree rendering without manual labeling; overcomes multi-view consistency violations by placing virtual images in specific sub-spaces; works as a general enhancement module for MLP-based and grid-based NeRF methods [S1].
*   **Limitations:** May require more sub-spaces for highly complex reflective and refractive scenes [S2].
*   **When to Use:** When rendering scenes with complex reflections and refractions (e.g., mirror-like objects) where single-space NeRF fails or requires manual intervention [S1, S2].
*   **When Not to Use:** For scenes without significant reflective or refractive elements, the added complexity and parameters may not be justified [S1].

## Background
MS-NeRF was proposed to address the limitations of existing NeRF methods in handling reflective objects, which often result in blurry or distorted rendering [S1]. The method was introduced by Z.X. Yin, P.Y. Jiao, M.M. Cheng, B. Ren, and J. Qiu from Nankai University and Horizon Robotics [S1]. A preliminary version appeared at CVPR, and the full version was published in IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence in 2025 [S1]. The authors also constructed a novel dataset consisting of 33 synthetic scenes and 7 real captured scenes with complex reflection and refraction to benchmark the robustness of NeRF-based methods [S1].
