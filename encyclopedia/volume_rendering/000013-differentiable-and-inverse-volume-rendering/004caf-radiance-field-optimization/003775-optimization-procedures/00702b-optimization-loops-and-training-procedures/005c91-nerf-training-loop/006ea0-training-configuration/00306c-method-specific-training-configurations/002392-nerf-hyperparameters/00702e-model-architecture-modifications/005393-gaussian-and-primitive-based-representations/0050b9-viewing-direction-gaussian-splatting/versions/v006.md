# Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting


## Overview
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) is a hybrid neural rendering method that combines the explicit, differentiable rasterization of 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) with the view-dependent appearance modeling of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [S1]. While standard GS represents geometry and static color/opacity via learnable Gaussian primitives, VDGS uses a neural network to modulate the opacity (and optionally color) of these Gaussians based on the viewing direction, thereby resolving view-dependent artifacts such as incorrect glass reflections and transparency issues [S1]. It differs from [Differentiable Primitive Assembly](13657-differentiable-primitive-assembly.md) by using continuous Gaussian distributions rather than discrete primitives, and from [Point-NeRF Representation](15538-point-nerf-representation.md) by leveraging fast differentiable rasterization instead of ray marching [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \{ \mathcal{G}_i \}_{i=1}^N$, where each $\mathcal{G}_i$ is defined by mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$, covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i$, base color $\mathbf{c}_i$, and base opacity $\sigma_i$. Camera parameters (position, orientation, intrinsics) and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ for each pixel.
*   **Outputs:** Rendered image color $C(\mathbf{p})$ for each pixel $\mathbf{p}$.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Gaussian Primitives:**
    *   $\mathbf{m}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Mean position.
    *   $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix (symmetric positive definite).
    *   $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Base color (RGB).
    *   $\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}$: Base opacity (scalar).
2.  **Neural Network (MLP):**
    *   Input: Hash-encoded Gaussian mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$.
    *   Output: Opacity update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$.
    *   Architecture: 2-layer MLP with LeakyReLU activation [S1].
    *   Encoding: 3D structured hash grid with 12 levels, hash table size $2^{14}$, feature dimension 2 [S1].

**Formulas:**
The vanilla NeRF model is defined as $F_{\text{NeRF}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}; \Theta) = (c, \sigma)$ [S1]. In VDGS, the neural network $F_{\text{VDGS}}$ outputs only the density/opacity update:
$$ F_{\text{VDGS}}(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{d}; \Theta) = \Delta \sigma(\mathbf{d}) $$
where $\mathbf{m}$ is the Gaussian mean and $\mathbf{d}$ is the viewing direction [S1].

The effective opacity $\alpha_i$ for a sample $i$ along a ray is calculated as:
$$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\left( - (\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i) \right) $$
where $\delta_i$ is the distance between adjacent samples along the ray, $\sigma_i$ is the base opacity, and $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ is the NeRF-based density update [S1].

The pixel color $C(\mathbf{p})$ is computed via alpha compositing of $N$ ordered overlapping Gaussian samples:
$$ C(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of samples contributing to pixel $\mathbf{p}$, sorted by depth [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   Hash Grid Levels: 12 [S1].
*   Hash Table Size: $2^{14}$ (16384) [S1].
*   Feature Dimension: 2 [S1].
*   Activation: LeakyReLU [S1].
*   Update Strategy: The sources indicate that updating **only opacity** yields the most consistently high-quality results. Updating only color or both color and opacity simultaneously results in slightly worse performance, attributed to the dependency of opacity on color making joint training ineffective [S1]. The final recommended model is "VDGS Opacity Multiply" [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize the scene with Structure from Motion (SfM) points, either from COLMAP or randomly generated, to establish initial 3D Gaussian positions and covariances [S1].
2.  **Hash Encoding:** Encode the mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$ of each Gaussian using the 3D structured hash grid [S1].
3.  **Neural Update:** Feed the hashed mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and the viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ into the 2-layer MLP to compute the opacity update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ [S1].
4.  **Rasterization:** Use a differential Gaussian rasterization pipeline to render the image. This involves projecting the 3D Gaussians to 2D, sorting them by depth, and computing $\alpha_i$ and $C(\mathbf{p})$ using the formulas above [S1].
5.  **Optimization:** Jointly optimize all parameters: Gaussian means $\mathbf{m}_i$, covariance matrices $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i$, base colors $\mathbf{c}_i$, base opacities $\sigma_i$, and the MLP weights $\Theta$, using gradient descent [S1].
6.  **Pruning and Densification:** Every 100 iterations, eliminate Gaussians with opacity below a specific threshold. Generate new Gaussians in unoccupied areas of 3D space to fill vacant regions [S1].

**Subtopics:**
*   **VDGS Opacity Multiply:** The final recommended variant where only opacity is modulated by the MLP. It yields the best scores on real-world scenes (Mip-NeRF360, Tanks and Temples, Deep Blending) [S1].
*   **VDGS Color Multiply:** A variant where only color is modulated. It yields the best scores on the NeRF Synthetic dataset but performs worse on real scenes compared to Opacity Multiply [S1].
*   **VDGS Joint Update:** A variant where both color and opacity are modulated. It performs slightly worse than single-update variants, likely due to conflicting gradients between opacity and color dependencies [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is comparable to standard Gaussian Splatting (GS), with a slight increase due to the MLP query. On an NVIDIA RTX 3090, VDGS-30K trains in ~46 minutes on Mip-NeRF360, ~30 minutes on Tanks and Temples, and ~41 minutes on Deep Blending [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing Gaussian parameters plus the MLP weights and hash grid. The hash grid size is fixed at $2^{14}$ entries with dimension 2 [S1].
*   **Inference Speed:** Inference FPS on RTX 3090 is 41.35 (Mip-NeRF360), 38.53 (Tanks and Temples), and 44.72 (Deep Blending) [S1]. This is significantly faster than NeRF-based methods but slightly slower than vanilla GS due to the MLP evaluation per sample [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Accurately models view-dependent effects like glass reflections, shadows, and transparency without adding explicit light sources or textures. Inherits the fast training and inference of GS [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires tuning between opacity-only and color-only updates depending on the dataset (real vs. synthetic). Joint updates are ineffective [S1].
*   **When to use:** When rendering scenes with significant view-dependent artifacts (shiny surfaces, transparent objects) using Gaussian Splatting, and when faster training/inference than NeRF is required.
*   **When not to use:** For scenes with purely diffuse, view-independent materials where vanilla GS suffices, as the MLP adds computational overhead.

## Background
VDGS was proposed by researchers from the Jagiellonian University and the University of Cambridge [S1]. It was introduced to mitigate the caveats of both NeRF (slow, hard to condition) and GS (hard to condition for view-dependent effects, requires many components) [S1]. The method leverages the hash encoding technique from Instant NGP and the differentiable rasterization from 3D Gaussian Splatting [S1]. It was implemented using PyTorch and TinyCUDA frameworks [S1].
