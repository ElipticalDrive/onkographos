# NeurMiPs
Also called: NeurMiPs, Neural Mixture of Planar Experts, Neural Mixture of Planar Experts for View Synthesis

## Overview
NeurMiPs represents a 3D scene as a mixture of $K$ learnable rectangular planar surfaces, where each surface acts as an independent "expert" containing a small neural radiance field that predicts view-dependent color and transparency [S1]. It solves the frontal-parallel limitation of traditional Multi-Plane Imagery (MPI) and the high memory/compute costs of dense volume rendering by leveraging closed-form ray-plane intersections and local MLPs, achieving real-time rendering speeds while maintaining superior extrapolation capabilities compared to voxel-based methods [S1].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Sparse 3D point cloud $\{x_i\}$ from Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [S1].
    *   Training images with known camera poses [S1].
    *   Number of planar experts $K$ (500 for Replica, 1000 for Tanks & Temples) [S1].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Rendered image color $c(\mathbf{r})$ for a ray $\mathbf{r}$ [S1].
    *   Estimated depth $d(\mathbf{r})$ [S1].
    *   Baked alpha textures $A_k$ for each plane $k$ (optional, for acceleration) [S1].

### Data Structures
**Planar Expert Geometry Parameter $s_k$**
Each planar expert $k$ is defined by a tuple $s_k = (p_k, n_k, u_k, w_k, h_k)$ [S1]:
*   $p_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$: The 3D center coordinate of the rectangle.
*   $n_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$: The normalized 3D normal vector of the plane.
*   $u_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$: The normalized up vector defining the y-axis direction of the plane's local coordinate system.
*   $w_k \in \mathbb{R}$: The width of the rectangle.
*   $h_k \in \mathbb{R}$: The height of the rectangle.

**Neural Radiance Field $f_k$**
Each expert $k$ has an associated MLP $f_k$ [S1]:
*   **Architecture:** 3 fully-connected hidden layers with ReLU activation; final output layer with sigmoid activation.
*   **Input:** 3D coordinate $\mathbf{x}_k$ on the plane and normalized ray direction $\mathbf{d} \in S^2$. The direction $\mathbf{d}$ is transformed into a higher-dimensional space using high-frequency positional encoding before input [S1].
*   **Output:** Color $\mathbf{c}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and transparency $\alpha_k \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].

**Alpha Texture $A_k$**
*   A 2D texture map of size $w \times h$ storing pre-computed, view-independent alpha values for plane $k$ [S1].

### Numbered Steps

**Phase 1: Plane Initialization**
1.  Initialize plane centers $p_k$ using farthest point sampling on the SfM point cloud $\{x_i\}$ [S1].
2.  Initialize normals $n_k$ and up vectors $u_k$ based on local point sets around $p_k$ [S1].
3.  Optimize geometric parameters $\{s_k\}$ by minimizing the geometry loss $\mathcal{L}_g$:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_g = \sum_i \min_k d(x_i, s_k) + \lambda \sum_k (w_k h_k)^2 $$
    where $d(x_i, s_k)$ is the distance from point $x_i$ to the closest rectangular surface $s_k$, and $(w_k h_k)^2$ is an area regularization term to prevent arbitrarily large rectangles. $\lambda$ is a weighting factor [S1].

**Phase 2: Teacher Model Training**
1.  Train a large-capacity standard NeRF (Teacher) as a reference model [S1].
2.  Sample points along rays using the current NeurMiPs ray-plane intersection logic [S1].
3.  Jointly optimize the planar experts' geometric parameters and the teacher model using the total loss:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_g + \mathcal{L}_c $$
    where $\mathcal{L}_c$ is the photometric loss:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_c = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \|c(\mathbf{r}) - c_{\text{gt}}(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2 $$
    [S1].

**Phase 3: Distillation**
1.  For each planar expert $k$, draw random points uniformly from its rectangle and random view directions from the half unit sphere [S1].
2.  Evaluate the Teacher model at these points to get target color $\mathbf{c}_{teacher}$ and alpha $\alpha_{teacher}$ [S1].
3.  Update the student network $f_k$ parameters by minimizing the L2 loss between the student's output $(\mathbf{c}_k, \alpha_k)$ and the teacher's output [S1].

**Phase 4: Fine-Tuning**
1.  Fix the plane geometry parameters $\{s_k\}$ [S1].
2.  Fine-tune the student radiance field models $f_k$ to improve rendering quality by minimizing the photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_c$ between rendered pixel color and ground-truth color [S1].

**Rendering Pipeline (Inference)**
1.  **Ray-Plane Intersection:** For a ray $r = \{o, d\}$ and plane $k$ with normal $n_k$ and center $p_k$, compute the intersection point $x_k$:
    $$ x_k = o + \frac{(p_k - o) \cdot n_k}{d \cdot n_k} d $$
    Keep only rectangles where the intersection lies within the bounds defined by $w_k, h_k$ and the local coordinate system defined by $n_k, u_k$ [S1].
2.  **Radiance Evaluation:** For each intersecting planar expert $k$, evaluate $f_k(x_{p,k}, d)$ to obtain transparency $\alpha_k$ and color $c_k$. If alpha baking is used, retrieve $\alpha_k$ via bilinear interpolation from texture $A_k$ [S1].
3.  **Alpha Composition:**
    *   Collect all intersecting points $\{x_j, c_j, \alpha_j\}$.
    *   Sort points from closest to furthest relative to the eye $o$ [S1].
    *   Compute final ray color:
        $$ c(r) = \sum_j \left( \prod_i^{j-1} (1 - \alpha_i) \right) \alpha_j c_j $$
    *   Compute expected depth:
        $$ d(r) = \sum_j \left( \prod_i^{j-1} (1 - \alpha_i) \right) \alpha_j t_j $$
        where $t_j$ is the depth of sampled point $j$ [S1].
4.  **Early Ray Termination:** If the accumulated transmittance $\prod (1 - \alpha_i)$ is close to zero, stop evaluating further planes. Re-normalize if the sum of alpha values is smaller than 1 [S1].

def ray_plane_intersection(ray_o, ray_d, plane_center, plane_normal):
    """
    Computes intersection of ray and infinite plane.
    Returns distance t along ray, or None if parallel or behind ray.
    """
    denom = np.dot(ray_d, plane_normal)
    if abs(denom) < 1e-8:
        return None
    t = np.dot(plane_center - ray_o, plane_normal) / denom
    if t < 0:
        return None
    return t

def point_in_rectangle(point, plane_center, plane_normal, plane_up, width, height):
    """
    Checks if point lies within the rectangle defined by center, normal, up, width, height.
    """
    # Construct local basis
    # u is plane_up
    # v is cross(normal, u)
    v = np.cross(plane_normal, plane_up)
    v = v / np.linalg.norm(v)
    u = plane_up # Already normalized
    
    # Vector from center to point
    vec = point - plane_center
    
    # Project onto u and v
    proj_u = np.dot(vec, u)
    proj_v = np.dot(vec, v)
    
    # Check bounds
    if abs(proj_u) <= height / 2.0 and abs(proj_v) <= width / 2.0:
        return True
    return False

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Finite Plane Size:** Planes may not cover all regions in extreme viewpoints, leading to higher depth errors for background scenes [S1]. Solution: Adopt multiple-layer boxes [S1].
*   **SfM Dependency:** Performance degrades if sparse point clouds are noisy or unavailable [S1].
*   **Unbounded Scenes:** The method currently cannot handle unbounded scenes. Solution: Incorporate techniques from NeRF++ to model background texture with non-Euclidean coordinates [S1].
*   **Alpha Baking Artifacts:** Applying alpha baking directly during inference may bring a minor rendering performance drop or quality drop due to discretization of the continuous radiance field [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering is accelerated by closed-form ray-plane intersection and early ray termination. Only a small fraction of planes intersect a given ray [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Significantly lower than voxel-based approaches due to the compact representation of planes and tiny MLPs [S1]. Alpha baking increases memory usage for textures [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   **Replica:** 19.16 FPS on a single TITAN RTX using a custom CUDA kernel [S1].
    *   **Tanks & Temples (Truck scene, 1000 planes, 1920x1080):** 976 FPS on a single RTX 3090 using texture baking [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   More efficient in memory and computation than NeRF [S1].
    *   More sample-efficient with better extrapolation than NeRF [S1].
    *   Captures detailed textures on planar surfaces with sharp boundaries, avoiding the blurriness of NeRF in large-scale scenes [S1].
    *   Better geometry reflection than Multi-Plane Imagery (MPI) [S1].
    *   Generalizes better in view extrapolation than voxel-based methods (PlenOctrees, KiloNeRF) which prune redundant voxels and cannot estimate appearances of unseen regions [S1].
    *   Avoids "fog-like" artifacts of NeRF and "stack-of-cards" effects of NeX [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Cannot handle unbounded scenes natively [S1].
    *   Relies heavily on SfM point clouds [S1].
    *   Depth error increases in extreme viewpoints due to finite plane size [S1].

## Background
NeurMiPs was introduced in the paper "NeurMiPs: Neural Mixture of Planar Experts for View Synthesis" [S1]. It builds upon the success of neural rendering [44, 73] and MPI [S1]. The method addresses the limitations of dense volume rendering and standard MPI by using a mixture of learnable planar experts. It is used for view synthesis on datasets like Tanks & Temples and Replica [S1].
