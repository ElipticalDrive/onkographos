# NeRF-Casting

Also called: NeRF-Casting: Improved View-Dependent Appearance with Consistent Reflections

## Overview
NeRF-Casting is a method for synthesizing photorealistic specular appearance and reflections in real-world scenes by integrating ray tracing into the NeRF rendering pipeline [S1]. It solves the problem of view-dependent appearance modeling by casting reflection rays from points along camera rays into the NeRF geometry to render feature vectors, which are then decoded into color using a small MLP, rather than querying a large MLP for outgoing radiance [S1]. Unlike Ref-NeRF, which reparameterizes outgoing radiance as a function of reflected view direction and requires a large MLP to encode the entire environment, NeRF-Casting uses ray tracing to naturally synthesize consistent reflections of nearby and distant content without requiring the MLP to represent the full environment at every point [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Camera ray origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$; NeRF geometry model (density $\tau$); spatial contraction function $C(\mathbf{x})$; color decoder MLP $f_{\text{dec}}$.
*   **Outputs:** Volume-rendered color $\bar{\mathbf{c}}$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **NeRF Geometry:** Implicit function mapping contracted 3D coordinates to volume density $\tau$.
*   **Color Decoder:** A small Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) $f_{\text{dec}}$ that maps feature vectors to RGB color.
*   **Reflection Ray:** A ray $\mathbf{r}_{\text{refl}}(t) = \mathbf{x} + t \mathbf{d}_{\text{refl}}$ cast from a sample point $\mathbf{x}$ on the primary camera ray.

**Spatial Contraction:**
To give higher representational capacity to points near the cameras, a spatial contraction function $C(\mathbf{x})$ maps points in $\mathbb{R}^3$ to a sphere of radius 2 [S1]:
$$
C(\mathbf{x}) = 
\begin{cases} 
\mathbf{x} & \text{if } \|\mathbf{x}\| \le 1 \\
\frac{1}{2 - \|\mathbf{x}\|} \frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|} & \text{if } \|\mathbf{x}\| > 1 
\end{cases}
$$

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Primary Ray Sampling:** For a given camera ray, sample $N$ points $\mathbf{x}_i$ along the ray at depths $t_i$.
2.  **Reflection Ray Casting:** For each sample point $\mathbf{x}_i$, cast a reflection ray into the NeRF geometry. The direction of this reflection ray is determined by the geometry at $\mathbf{x}_i$ (typically the surface normal if a surface is detected, or a heuristic reflection direction) [S1].
3.  **Feature Rendering via Ray Tracing:** Trace the reflection ray through the NeRF representation. Sample points along the reflection ray. Compute the volume-rendered feature vector $\mathbf{f}_i$ for the reflection ray using the standard volume rendering equation (Equation 1 below), where the "color" at each sample is a learned feature vector rather than RGB [S1]. This step naturally synthesizes consistent reflections of nearby and distant content [S1].
4.  **Color Decoding:** Pass the sampled feature vector $\mathbf{f}_i$ through the small color decoder MLP $f_{\text{dec}}$ to obtain the reflected color $\mathbf{c}^{(i)}_{\text{refl}}$ [S1].
5.  **Final Compositing:** Composite the reflected colors $\mathbf{c}^{(i)}_{\text{refl}}$ along the primary camera ray using the standard NeRF volume rendering equation to produce the final pixel color $\bar{\mathbf{c}}$ [S1].

**Formulas:**
The volume rendering equation used as the base for NeRF is [S1]:
$$
\bar{\mathbf{c}} = \sum_i w^{(i)} \mathbf{c}^{(i)}
$$
where $\bar{\mathbf{c}}$ is the volume-rendered color, $w^{(i)}$ is the weight of the $i$-th sample, and $\mathbf{c}^{(i)}$ is the color at the $i$-th sample location.

The weight $w^{(i)}$ for the $i$-th sample is calculated as [S1]:
$$
w^{(i)} = \left( 1 - \exp\left(-\tau^{(i)} \left( t^{(i+1)} - t^{(i)} \right) \right) \right) \exp\left( -\sum_{j < i} \tau^{(j)} \left( t^{(j+1)} - t^{(j)} \right) \right)
$$
where $\tau^{(i)}$ is the volume density and $t^{(i)}$ is the depth of the $i$-th sample.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method involves casting reflection rays for each sample on the primary ray, which increases the computational cost compared to standard NeRF. However, it is described as having "low computational overhead" compared to inverse rendering methods that use hundreds to thousands of sampled reflection rays [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the NeRF geometry and the small color decoder MLP. No additional large environment maps are needed, unlike Ref-NeRF [S1].
*   **Performance:** Outperforms prior methods for view synthesis of scenes containing shiny objects and is the only existing NeRF method that can synthesize photorealistic specular appearance and reflections in real-world scenes while requiring comparable optimization time to current state-of-the-art view synthesis models [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently renders accurate reflections with low computational overhead; naturally synthesizes consistent reflections of nearby and distant content; avoids the need for large MLPs to encode the entire environment [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on the NeRF geometry being accurate enough to support meaningful reflection ray tracing; performance may degrade in scenes with complex, non-Lambertian surfaces where the reflection direction is hard to estimate [S1].

## Background
NeRF-Casting was introduced in the paper "NeRF-Casting: Improved View-Dependent Appearance with Consistent Reflections" [S1]. It addresses the limitations of prior NeRF methods that rely on large, computationally expensive neural networks to model outgoing radiance, which limits optimization and rendering speed [S1]. The method builds on the standard NeRF volume rendering framework [S1].
