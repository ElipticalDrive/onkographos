# Dynamic Appearance Particle Neural Radiance Field

Also called: Dynamic Appearance Particle NeRF, Lagrangian dynamic model in NeRF

## Overview
Dynamic Appearance Particle Neural Radiance Field (DAP-NeRF) is a method for novel view synthesis from monocular video that models scene dynamics using a particle-based representation within a neural radiance field framework [S1]. It solves the problem of reconstructing dynamic scenes without prior geometric knowledge or multi-view stereo data, which are requirements for related methods like ParticleNeRF [S1]. Unlike ParticleNeRF, which encodes the entire scene with particles and requires multiple camera views per frame, DAP-NeRF applies appearance particles only to dynamic elements and uses a grid-based field for efficient querying, enabling it to handle monocular input [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Monocular video data (one camera view per time frame) [S1].
*   **Output:** Rendered pixel color $C(r, t)$ for any ray $r$ and time $t$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
The method uses a superposition of two feature fields:
1.  **Static Field ($V_s$):** A time-independent grid-based model (e.g., hash grid or dense grid) storing features for static scene components [S1].
2.  **Dynamic Field ($V_{td}$):** A particle-based representation where the field is formulated as a convolution with a kernel [S1].

**Notation:**
*   $t$: Time, denoted in subscripts on the right-hand side of the superposition equation (e.g., $V_{td}$) to clarify that the dynamic component consists of an ensemble of particles [S1].
*   $w_t(x)$: A weight function taking value 1 if the dynamic field is effective at location $x$ and time $t$, and 0 otherwise [S1].
*   $\mathbf{r}(s) = \mathbf{o} + s\mathbf{d}$: Ray equation, where $\mathbf{o}$ is the camera center, $\mathbf{d}$ is the direction, and $s$ is the distance [S1].

**Formulas:**
The rendered color $C(r, t)$ is computed by integrating along the ray from near bound $s_n$ to far bound $s_f$ [S1]:

$$
C(r, t) = \int_{s_n}^{s_f} T(s, t)\sigma(\mathbf{r}(s), t)c(\mathbf{r}(s), \mathbf{d}, t)ds
$$

where the accumulated transmittance $T(s, t)$ is:

$$
T(s, t) = \exp\left(-\int_{s_n}^{s} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s'), t)ds'\right)
$$

The total feature field $V(x, t)$ is a superposition of static and dynamic components [S1]:

$$
V(x, t) = (1 - w_t(x))V_s(x) + w_t(x)V_{td}(x)
$$

The dynamic field $V_{td}$ is formulated as a convolution with a Dirac function to integrate over finite volumes [S1]:

$$
V_{td}(x) = (V_{td} * \delta)(x) = \int V_{td}(x')\delta(x - x')d\nu(x')
$$

where $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac function [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Ray Casting:** For a target time $t$ and camera pose, cast a ray $\mathbf{r}(s)$ from the camera center $\mathbf{o}$ through each pixel [S1].
2.  **Sampling:** Sample points $\mathbf{r}(s)$ along the ray between near bound $s_n$ and far bound $s_f$ [S1].
3.  **Field Query:** For each sample point $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{r}(s)$:
    a. Query the static field $V_s(\mathbf{x})$ using the grid-based model [S1].
    b. Query the dynamic field $V_{td}(\mathbf{x})$. This involves evaluating the particle contributions. To implement this efficiently, replace the Dirac function $\delta$ with a kernel $W$ with finite support [S1]:
       $$ W(x) = 0 \text{ when } \|x\| \text{ exceeds a small radius, and } \int W(x)d\nu(x) = 1 $$
       The dynamic field is then approximated by summing contributions from particles within the kernel's support [S1].
    c. Compute the combined field $V(\mathbf{x}, t)$ using the superposition formula:
       $$ V(\mathbf{x}, t) = (1 - w_t(\mathbf{x}))V_s(\mathbf{x}) + w_t(\mathbf{x})V_{td}(\mathbf{x}) $$
       where $w_t(\mathbf{x})$ is 1 if the dynamic field is effective at $(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and 0 otherwise [S1].
4.  **Density and Color Prediction:** Use the combined field $V(\mathbf{x}, t)$ to predict volume density $\sigma(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and emitted radiance $c(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}, t)$ via neural networks (MLPs) [S1].
5.  **Volume Rendering:** Compute the transmittance $T(s, t)$ and integrate the color contribution along the ray to produce the final pixel color $C(r, t)$ [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Kernel Support Radius:** A small radius defining the support of the kernel $W$ [S1]. Not given in the sources.
*   **Weight $w_t(x)$:** Binary indicator of dynamic field effectiveness [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** The approximation of the Dirac function with a finite-width kernel $W$ introduces quantization errors [S1].
*   **Boundaries:** The near and far bounds $s_n$ and $s_f$ must be chosen to encompass the scene dynamics at time $t$ [S1].
*   **Dynamic Effectiveness:** Determining $w_t(x)$ requires knowing which parts of the scene are dynamic at time $t$ [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Handles monocular video data (one camera view per time frame) [S1].
    *   Does not require prior geometric knowledge [S1].
    *   More efficient querying than ParticleNeRF due to grid-based static field and linear interpolation [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Requires training a particle motion network [S1].
    *   The support of the dynamic field is implied by the particle representation, which may require careful tuning [S1].
*   **When to use:** When only monocular video data is available and the scene contains dynamic elements that need to be modeled without prior shape information [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When multi-view stereo data is available and the scene is static or can be modeled with existing multi-view methods [S1].

## Background
Dynamic Appearance Particle Neural Radiance Field was proposed in a paper published in IEEE Transactions on Circuits and Systems for Video Technology in 2025 [S1]. It builds on the Lagrangian dynamic model and extends the NeRF framework to handle dynamic scenes. The method differs from ParticleNeRF, which encodes the entire scene with particles and requires 20 camera views per time frame [S1]. DAP-NeRF is designed to handle monocular video data and applies appearance particles only to dynamic elements [S1]. The technique is not tightly coupled to a specific architecture and can be integrated into alternative frameworks such as InstantNGP [S1].
