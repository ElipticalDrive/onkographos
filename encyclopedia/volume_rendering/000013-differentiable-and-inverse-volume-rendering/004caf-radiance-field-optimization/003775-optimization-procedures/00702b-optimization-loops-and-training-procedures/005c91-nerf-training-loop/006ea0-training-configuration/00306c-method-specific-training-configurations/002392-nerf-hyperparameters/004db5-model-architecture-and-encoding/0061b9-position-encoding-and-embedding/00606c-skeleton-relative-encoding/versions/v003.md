# Skeleton-Relative Encoding

Also called: A-NeRF Skeleton-Relative Encoding, Relative Encoding, Skeleton-Relative Input Encoding

## Overview
Skeleton-Relative Encoding transforms 3D query positions and view directions into local bone-relative coordinate systems before processing them through a neural radiance field, explicitly incorporating domain knowledge of human body part linkage [S1, unofficial]. Unlike standard NeRF which treats space as continuous and unstructured, this method reparameterizes the input space to respect the piece-wise rigidity of articulated motion, making it suitable for dynamic human avatars where global coordinate systems are insufficient [S1, unofficial].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   World-space query position $q \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1, unofficial].
    *   World-space view direction $d \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1, unofficial].
    *   Skeleton pose parameters $\theta_k$ for frame $k$, defining bone transformations $T(\theta_k, m)$ for each bone $m$ [S1, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Combined encoding vector $e_k \in \mathbb{R}^D$, which serves as the input to the density/color MLP $F_\phi$ [S1, unofficial].

### Data Structures
*   **Skeleton Representation:** A set of $M$ bones (typically $M=24$ for a standard human skeleton, indices $1$ to $24$) [S1, unofficial].
*   **Bone Transformation:** $T(\theta_k, m)$ is a $4 \times 4$ homogeneous transformation matrix mapping local bone coordinates to world coordinates for bone $m$ at pose $\theta_k$ [S1, unofficial].
*   **Encoding Components:**
    *   Relative Distance $\tilde{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^M$: Scalar distance from $q$ to each bone [S1, unofficial].
    *   Relative Direction $\tilde{r}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3M}$: Unit vector from $q$ to each bone [S1, unofficial].
    *   Relative Ray Direction $\tilde{d}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3M}$: View direction rotated to local bone frame [S1, unofficial].

### Steps and Formulas

1.  **Compute Bone-relative Position:**
    For each bone $m \in \{1, \dots, M\}$, transform the world query $q$ into the local coordinate system of bone $m$ using the inverse transformation:
    $$ \tilde{q}_{k,m} = T^{-1}(\theta_k, m) q $$
    where $\tilde{q}_{k,m} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1, unofficial].

2.  **Compute Relative Distance:**
    Calculate the L2 norm of the bone-relative position to obtain the radial distance:
    $$ \tilde{v}_{k,m} = \| \tilde{q}_{k,m} \|_2 \in \mathbb{R} $$
    The vector of distances for all bones is $\tilde{v}_k = [\tilde{v}_{k,1}, \dots, \tilde{v}_{k,M}]$ [S1, unofficial].

3.  **Compute Relative Direction:**
    Normalize the bone-relative position to capture orientation information:
    $$ \tilde{r}_{k,m} = \frac{\tilde{q}_{k,m}}{\| \tilde{q}_{k,m} \|_2} \in \mathbb{R}^3 $$
    The vector of directions is $\tilde{r}_k = [\tilde{r}_{k,1}, \dots, \tilde{r}_{k,M}]$ [S1, unofficial].

4.  **Compute Relative Ray Direction:**
    Rotate the incoming world-space view direction $d$ by the rotational part of the inverse bone-to-world transformation:
    $$ \tilde{d}_{k,m} = [T^{-1}(\theta_k, m)]_{3\times3} d \in \mathbb{R}^3 $$
    where $[T^{-1}(\theta_k, m)]_{3\times3}$ is the upper-left $3 \times 3$ rotation submatrix [S1, unofficial].
    The vector of ray directions is $\tilde{d}_k = [\tilde{d}_{k,1}, \dots, \tilde{d}_{k,M}]$ [S1, unofficial].

5.  **Apply Cutoff Function:**
    To prevent distant bones from influencing the prediction, apply a sigmoid-based cutoff function $h(\cdot)$ to the distance and ray direction encodings:
    $$ h(\tilde{v}_{k,m}) = 1 - S(\tau (\tilde{v}_{k,m} - t)) $$
    where $S(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function, $t$ is the cutoff threshold, and $\tau$ controls the sharpness of the cutoff [S1, unofficial].

6.  **Construct Final Encoding:**
    Combine the components into the final input vector $e_k$. Positional Encoding $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is applied to the relative distance and relative ray direction, but **not** to the relative direction $\tilde{r}_k$ [S1, unofficial].
    $$ e_k = [h(\tilde{v}_k) \odot \Gamma(\tilde{v}_k), \quad \tilde{r}_k, \quad h(\tilde{v}_k) \odot \Gamma(\tilde{d}_k)] $$
    Here, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is the hierarchical sinusoidal positional encoding defined in the parent article [Model Architecture and Encoding](13550-model-architecture-and-encoding.md) [S1, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
import numpy as np

def skeleton_relative_encoding(q, d, bone_transforms_inv, t=1.0, tau=10.0, L=10):
    """
    Computes the skeleton-relative encoding for a query point and view direction.
    
    Args:
        q: np.array, shape (3,), world-space query position.
        d: np.array, shape (3,), world-space view direction.
        bone_transforms_inv: list of np.array, shape (4, 4), inverse bone-to-world transforms for M bones.
        t: float, cutoff threshold for the sigmoid function.
        tau: float, sharpness of the cutoff.
        L: int, number of frequency bands for positional encoding.
        
    Returns:
        e_k: np.array, the combined encoding vector.
    """
    M = len(bone_transforms_inv)
    
    # Lists to store components for each bone
    rel_distances = []
    rel_directions = []
    rel_ray_directions = []
    
    for m in range(M):
        # Step 1: Bone-relative position
        q_homogeneous = np.append(q, 1.0)
        q_local_homogeneous = bone_transforms_inv[m] @ q_homogeneous
        q_local = q_local_homogeneous[:3]
        
        # Step 2: Relative Distance
        v_m = np.linalg.norm(q_local)
        rel_distances.append(v_m)
        
        # Step 3: Relative Direction
        if v_m < 1e-8:
            r_m = np.zeros(3)
        else:
            r_m = q_local / v_m
        rel_directions.append(r_m)
        
        # Step 4: Relative Ray Direction
        R_inv = bone_transforms_inv[m][:3, :3]
        d_local = R_inv @ d
        rel_ray_directions.append(d_local)
        
    # Convert to arrays
    v_k = np.array(rel_distances)          # Shape (M,)
    r_k = np.array(rel_directions)         # Shape (M, 3)
    d_k = np.array(rel_ray_directions)     # Shape (M, 3)
    
    # Step 5: Cutoff Function
    sigmoid_input = tau * (v_k - t)
    h = 1.0 - (1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-sigmoid_input)))
    
    # Step 6: Positional Encoding and Combination
    v_pe = positional_encoding(v_k, L)   # Shape (M, 2*L)
    
    # Apply PE to each component of d_k
    d_pe_flat = positional_encoding(d_k.flatten(), L) # Shape (3*M, 2*L)
    d_pe = d_pe_flat.reshape(M, 3 * 2 * L) # Shape (M, 6*L)
    
    # Apply cutoff h to the PE outputs
    v_pe_cropped = v_pe * h[:, np.newaxis]
    d_pe_cropped = d_pe * h[:, np.newaxis]
    
    # Concatenate: [h(v)Gamma(v), r, h(d)Gamma(d)]
    e_k = np.concatenate([v_pe_cropped, r_k, d_pe_cropped], axis=1)
    
    return e_k

def positional_encoding(values, L):
    """
    Applies hierarchical sinusoidal positional encoding to a 1D array of values.
    
    Args:
        values: np.array, shape (N,), scalar values to encode.
        L: int, number of frequency bands.
        
    Returns:
        encoded: np.array, shape (N, 2*L).
    """
    N = len(values)
    encoded = np.zeros((N, 2 * L))
    for i in range(L):
        freq = 2**i * np.pi
        encoded[:, 2*i] = np.sin(values * freq)
        encoded[:, 2*i+1] = np.cos(values * freq)
    return encoded
```

### Parameters
*   **Number of Bones ($M$):** 24, corresponding to indices 1 to 24 of the human skeleton [S1, unofficial].
*   **Cutoff Threshold ($t$):** Not given in the sources.
*   **Sharpness ($\tau$):** Not given in the sources.
*   **Frequency Bands ($L$):** Not given in the sources (inherited from parent [Model Architecture and Encoding](13550-model-architecture-and-encoding.md)).

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Dimensionality Explosion:** Using bone-relative *position* ($\tilde{q}_{k,m}$) instead of relative *distance* ($\tilde{v}_{k,m}$) increases the input dimensionality by an order of magnitude, which can hinder learning [S1, unofficial]. The method prefers relative distance for lower dimensionality and better reconstruction accuracy [S1, unofficial].
*   **Relative Direction Encoding:** The relative direction $\tilde{r}_k$ is passed directly into the final encoding $e_k$ without subsequent positional encoding (PE) [S1, unofficial]. Applying PE to direction vectors is not beneficial in this context [S1, unofficial].
*   **Ambiguity:** While bone-relative position removes ambiguities of assigning a query to a single part, the radial distance encoding naturally captures spherically shaped limbs and is robust to the ill-posed association to a single part [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Bone-relative Position Encoding
Maps the query $q$ to local coordinates of each bone [S1, unofficial]. Sets itself apart by preserving full 3D local structure but suffers from high dimensionality [S1, unofficial].

### Relative Distance Encoding
Computes the L2 norm from $q$ to each bone [S1, unofficial]. Sets itself apart by being lower-dimensional and capturing radial symmetry of limbs [S1, unofficial]. This is the preferred encoding in the final A-NeRF model [S1, unofficial].

### Relative Direction Encoding
Normalizes the bone-relative position vector [S1, unofficial]. Sets itself apart by providing orientation information without PE [S1, unofficial]. Always used in conjunction with distance encoding [S1, unofficial].

### Relative Ray Direction Encoding
Rotates the view direction into the local bone frame [S1, unofficial]. Sets itself apart by enabling view-dependent appearance modeling relative to the body part [S1, unofficial].

### Cutoff Function
A sigmoid-based weighting mechanism [S1, unofficial]. Sets itself apart by localizing the influence of each bone, preventing distant bones from affecting the prediction [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** $O(M \cdot L)$ per query point, where $M$ is the number of bones and $L$ is the number of frequency bands [S1, unofficial]. The transformation $T^{-1}$ is $O(1)$ per bone [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(M \cdot L)$ for the encoding vector [S1, unofficial]. The skeleton transforms must be stored or computed on the fly [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Incorporates explicit domain knowledge of human articulation [S1, unofficial].
    *   Invariant to global shift and rotation of the person [S1, unofficial].
    *   Preserves piece-wise rigidity of articulated motion [S1, unofficial].
    *   Lower dimensionality compared to full bone-relative position encoding [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Requires a skeleton pose estimator for initialization and runtime [S1, unofficial].
    *   Performance depends on the accuracy of the skeleton tracking [S1, unofficial].
    *   Does not model complex soft-tissue deformation (muscle bulging) as explicitly as parametric surface models, though it approximates it via the neural field [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Dynamic human avatar rendering where pose information is available or can be estimated [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** Static scenes, scenes without articulated structures, or when skeleton tracking is unreliable [S1, unofficial].

## Background

Skeleton-Relative Encoding was introduced in **A-NeRF: Articulated Neural Radiance Fields for Learning Human Shape, Appearance, and Pose** by Gkioxari et al. (2021) [S1, unofficial]. The method was developed to address the limitations of standard NeRF in modeling dynamic humans, where global coordinates fail to capture the rigid motion of body parts [S1, unofficial]. It inverts the approach of parametric models like SMPL: instead of deforming a surface, it deforms the query space [S1, unofficial]. The work builds on the foundational NeRF architecture [Model Architecture and Encoding](13550-model-architecture-and-encoding.md) and integrates it with articulated skeleton data [S1, unofficial].
