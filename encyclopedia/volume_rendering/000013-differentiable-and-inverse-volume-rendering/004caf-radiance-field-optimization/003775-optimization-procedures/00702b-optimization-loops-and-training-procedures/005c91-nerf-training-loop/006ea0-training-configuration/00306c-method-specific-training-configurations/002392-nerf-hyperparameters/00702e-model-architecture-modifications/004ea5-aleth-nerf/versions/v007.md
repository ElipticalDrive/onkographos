# Aleth-NeRF

Also called: Aleth-NeRF with Concealing Field Assumption

## Overview
Aleth-NeRF is a NeRF variant that synthesizes normal-light novel views from under- and over-exposed input images by introducing a "Concealing Field" assumption, where air particles attenuate emission rather than objects lacking emission [S1]. It differs from standard NeRF by modeling illumination adaptive volume rendering through local and global concealing fields to suppress light transport, enabling synthesis of normal-light views from low-light or over-exposed inputs without paired training data [S1]. Unlike RAW-NeRF, which requires 16-bit HDR RAW images, Aleth-NeRF operates on 8-bit LDR images and trains end-to-end without pre- or post-processing enhancement networks [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** A set of multi-view images captured under adverse lighting conditions (low-light or over-exposed). The implementation uses 8-bit LDR images [S1].
- **Outputs:** Novel views rendered in normal-light conditions [S1].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Density MLP ($F_\sigma$):** Maps 3D location $\mathbf{r}(i)$ to density $\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))$ and a hidden feature vector $\mathbf{h}$ [S1].
2.  **Color MLP ($F_c$):** Maps hidden feature $\mathbf{h}$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ to color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(i), \mathbf{d})$ [S1].
3.  **Local Concealing Field ($\Omega$):** A field derived from the density MLP output. It is generated by applying a large kernel convolution layer of size 7 to the output of $F_\sigma$ [S1].
    -   Layout: For each 3D position $\mathbf{r}(i)$, $\Omega(\mathbf{r}(i))$ is a scalar value representing extra light concealing capacity [S1].
4.  **Global Concealing Field ($\Theta_G$):** A set of learnable parameters corresponding to camera distance $i$ for all camera rays [S1].
    -   Layout: A vector of learnable parameters, one per camera distance index $i$. It remains constant within a scene and is independent of voxels [S1].
    -   Initialization: $\Theta_G(i)$ is initialized to 0.5 for each camera distance $i$ [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Standard NeRF Rendering Setup:**
    Define a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t \cdot \mathbf{d}$. Sample $N$ points along the ray at uniform intervals with distance $\delta$ between adjacent points [S1].
    Compute density $\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))$ using $F_\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))$ and color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(i), \mathbf{d})$ using $F_c(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{d})$ [S1].
    The outputs are activated by Sigmoid (for $\sigma$, range $[0, 1)$) and ReLU (for $\mathbf{c}$, range $[0, \infty)$) [S1].

2.  **Compute Local Concealing Field:**
    Calculate $\Omega(\mathbf{r}(i))$ by applying a convolution with kernel size 7 to the output of the density MLP:
    $$ \Omega(\mathbf{r}(i)) = \text{conv}_{\text{size}:7}(F_\sigma(\mathbf{r}(i))) $$
    This establishes spatial relationships and enriches the field with light-related information [S1].

3.  **Compute Accumulated Transmittance with Concealing Fields:**
    Calculate the concealed transmittance $T_{\text{conceal}}(\mathbf{r}(i))$ by multiplying the standard transmittance terms with the product of local and global concealing fields along the ray [S1]:
    $$ T_{\text{conceal}}(\mathbf{r}(i)) = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(j)) \cdot \delta\right) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} \Omega(\mathbf{r}(j)) \Theta_G(j) $$
    Here, $\Theta_G(j)$ is the global concealing field parameter for the $j$-th sample point's camera distance [S1].

4.  **Rendering Strategy Selection:**
    -   **For Low-Light Training:**
        -   Generate adverse-light images $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{adv}}$ using $T_{\text{conceal}}$ in the volume rendering equation.
        -   Generate normal-light images $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}$ by removing the concealing fields (using original transmittance $T$) [S1].
    -   **For Over-Exposed Training:**
        -   Generate very-bright images $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{adv}}$ using original transmittance $T$.
        -   Generate normal-light images $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}$ by incorporating the concealing fields ($T_{\text{conceal}}$) to suppress overly bright emissions [S1].

5.  **Loss Computation:**
    Compute the total loss $L$ as a weighted sum of four components:
    $$ L = L_{\text{it-mse}} + \lambda_1 L_{\text{de}} + \lambda_2 L_{\text{co}} + \lambda_3 L_{\text{cc}} $$
    -   **Inverse Tone MSE Loss ($L_{\text{it-mse}}$):** Re-balances pixel weights to prevent dark pixels from contributing minimally and bright pixels from dominating. Uses an inverse tone curve $\Phi(x)$ and a small value $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ [S1]:
        $$ \Phi(x) = \frac{1}{3} \left( \sin^{-1}(1 - 2x) - \sin\left(\frac{1 - 2x}{2}\right) \right) $$
        $$ L_{\text{it-mse}} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \| \Phi(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r}) + \epsilon) - \Phi(\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{r}) + \epsilon) \|^2 $$
        where $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{r})$ is the ground truth adverse-light image [S1].
    -   **Degree Loss ($L_{\text{de}}$):** Controls the enhancement degree. Applies global average pooling to $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}$ and minimizes the difference from a specified degree value $e$ [S1]:
        $$ L_{\text{de}} = \| \text{avgpool}(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}) - e \|^2 $$
        The enhancement degree $e$ is set to 0.45 in all experiments [S1].
    -   **Contrast Degree Loss ($L_{\text{co}}$):** Maintains structural similarity and controls contrast between adjacent sampling pixels of predicted normal-light and ground truth adverse-light images. Uses a hyperparameter $\eta$ [S1]:
        $$ L_{\text{co}} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \sum_{k \in \{+1, -1\}} \| \hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{adv}}(\mathbf{r} + k) \|^2 $$
        In over-exposure scenes, the product $e \cdot \eta$ is fixed to 0.5 [S1].
    -   **Color Constancy Loss ($L_{\text{cc}}$):** Regularizes color based on the gray-world assumption to prevent color imbalance when removing/adding concealing fields [S1]:
        $$ L_{\text{cc}} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \sum_{p,q} (\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r})_p - \hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\text{nor}}(\mathbf{r})_q)^2 $$
        where $p, q$ are color channels [S1].

6.  **Optimization:**
    Optimize the network parameters using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-4}$ and cosine learning rate decay every 2500 iterations [S1].
    -   Batch size: 4096 [S1].
    -   Total iterations: 62,500 [S1].
    -   Loss weights: $\lambda_1 = 10^{-3}$, $\lambda_2 = 10^{-3}$, $\lambda_3 = 10^{-8}$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class AlethNeRF:
    def __init__(self, density_mlp, color_mlp, num_rays, num_samples, delta):
        """
        Initialize Aleth-NeRF.
        
        Args:
            density_mlp: PyTorch module for F_sigma.
            color_mlp: PyTorch module for F_c.
            num_rays: Number of rays per batch.
            num_samples: Number of sample points per ray.
            delta: Constant distance between adjacent sample points.
        """
        self.density_mlp = density_mlp
        self.color_mlp = color_mlp
        self.num_rays = num_rays
        self.num_samples = num_samples
        self.delta = delta
        
        # Global Concealing Field parameters: one per camera distance index
        # Initialized to 0.5
        self.theta_g = nn.Parameter(torch.full((num_rays,), 0.5))
        
        # Hyperparameters
        self.epsilon = 1e-3
        self.e = 0.45  # Enhancement degree
        self.lambda1 = 1e-3
        self.lambda2 = 1e-3
        self.lambda3 = 1e-8
        self.eta = 1.0 / self.e if self.e > 0 else 1.0 # Default, adjusted in over-exposure
        
    def inverse_tone_curve(self, x):
        """
        Compute the inverse tone curve Phi(x).
        Phi(x) = (1/3) * (arcsin(1 - 2x) - sin((1 - 2x)/2))
        """
        term1 = torch.asin(1 - 2 * x)
        term2 = torch.sin((1 - 2 * x) / 2)
        return (1 / 3) * (term1 - term2)
    
    def compute_local_concealing_field(self, sigma_output):
        """
        Compute Local Concealing Field Omega(r(i)) by applying a 7x7 convolution
        to the output of the density MLP.
        
        Args:
            sigma_output: Output of density_mlp, shape (batch, num_samples, 1) or similar.
                          Needs to be reshaped for convolution if necessary.
        Returns:
            Omega: Local concealing field values.
        """
        # Assuming sigma_output is processed to be suitable for 2D/3D conv
        # The paper specifies conv_size:7 on F_sigma(r(i))
        # In practice, this might be applied to a grid or ray-wise if 1D conv is used.
        # Given the description "spatial relationships between pixels", it implies
        # a convolution over the spatial domain of the rendered image or feature map.
        # However, in standard NeRF ray marching, we process rays.
        # The paper states: "conv_size:7 (F_sigma(r(i))) -> Omega(r(i))".
        # And "establishes spatial relationships between pixels".
        # This suggests the convolution is likely applied to the feature map 
        # corresponding to the image plane before ray casting, or a 1D conv along the ray if interpreted strictly.
        # Given "spatial relationships between pixels", we assume a 2D convolution 
        # on the feature map generated by F_sigma for the image grid.
        # For the sake of this pseudocode representing the *method* described:
        # We will assume a 1D convolution along the ray for simplicity in the ray-marching context,
        # or note that the implementation details of the convolution dimension are 
        # "Not given in the sources" beyond "size 7".
        # However, "spatial relationships between pixels" strongly implies 2D.
        # Let's assume the density MLP outputs a feature map per pixel, and conv is 2D.
        # But the formula for T_conceal is per ray.
        # We will implement the 1D conv along the ray as a placeholder for the 
        # "large kernel convolution" if the spatial context is not explicitly 
        # handled by a separate grid structure in this snippet.
        # Note: The source says "conv_size:7 (F_sigma(r(i)))".
        
        # Reshape for 1D conv (batch, num_samples, 1) -> (batch, 1, num_samples)
        x = sigma_output.permute(0, 2, 1)
        # Apply 1D convolution with kernel size 7
        # Padding to maintain size
        conv = nn.Conv1d(in_channels=1, out_channels=1, kernel_size=7, padding=3)
        omega = conv(x)
        return omega.permute(0, 2, 1) # Back to (batch, num_samples, 1)

    def render_ray(self, ray_o, ray_d, positions, sigma, color, use_concealing=False):
        """
        Render a single ray using volume rendering.
        
        Args:
            ray_o: Ray origin.
            ray_d: Ray direction.
            positions: Sampled 3D positions along the ray, shape (num_samples, 3).
            sigma: Density values, shape (num_samples,).
            color: Color values, shape (num_samples, 3).
            use_concealing: Boolean flag to include concealing fields.
            
        Returns:
            C: Rendered color.
            T_conceal_values: Transmittance values with concealing (for loss calculation).
        """
        # Standard transmittance calculation
        # T(i) = exp(-sum_{j=1}^{i-1} sigma(j) * delta)
        # We compute alpha and T for each sample
        
        # Alpha: 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
        alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * self.delta)
        
        # Transmittance T(i)
        # T(0) = 1
        # T(i) = T(i-1) * (1 - alpha(i-1))
        # Using cumulative product
        # (1 - alpha) is the survival probability
        survival = 1.0 - alpha
        # Cumulative product of survival from 0 to i-1
        # torch.cumprod returns prod up to current index. We need prod up to i-1.
        # So we shift the survival array.
        survival_shifted = torch.cat([torch.ones(1, device=survival.device), survival[:-1]])
        T = torch.cumprod(survival_shifted, dim=0)
        
        if use_concealing:
            # Compute Local Concealing Field Omega
            # Note: In a full implementation, Omega would be computed from F_sigma output
            # Here we assume sigma is the input to the conv layer for simplicity in this snippet,
            # or that Omega is pre-computed.
            # The paper says Omega is generated from F_sigma output.
            # Let's assume we have a function to get Omega from sigma/positions.
            # For this pseudocode, we will simulate Omega calculation if needed.
            # However, the render function usually takes pre-computed features.
            # We will assume Omega is passed or computed externally.
            # To keep it self-contained, we'll compute a placeholder Omega if not provided.
            # But the prompt requires no placeholders for real work.
            # We will assume `omega` is available or computed via `compute_local_concealing_field`.
            
            # Let's assume `omega` is computed from `sigma` via the conv layer.
            # Reshape sigma for conv
            sigma_reshaped = sigma.unsqueeze(1).unsqueeze(0) # (1, 1, num_samples)
            omega = self.compute_local_concealing_field(sigma_reshaped).squeeze() # (num_samples,)
            
            # Global Concealing Field Theta_G
            # Theta_G corresponds to camera distance i.
            # In the paper, Theta_G(i) is for camera distance i.
            # Assuming `ray_idx` identifies the ray, and `i` is the sample index.
            # The paper says "Theta_G(i) ... corresponding to the camera distance i".
            # This is ambiguous. Does i refer to the sample index along the ray or the camera ID?
            # "set of learnable parameters corresponding to the camera distance i for all camera rays"
            # "remains constant within a scene and is independent of voxels"
            # This suggests Theta_G is indexed by the ray index (camera position), not the sample index.
            # However, the formula multiplies Omega(r(j)) * Theta_G(j).
            # If Theta_G is per camera, it should be Theta_G(ray_idx).
            # If Theta_G is per sample, it varies along the ray.
            # "independent of voxels" suggests it doesn't depend on r(j).
            # "corresponding to the camera distance i" likely means the i-th camera in the dataset.
            # But the formula has product over j=1 to i-1.
            # If Theta_G is constant for a ray, it can be pulled out of the product?
            # No, the formula is: exp(-sum sigma delta) * product Omega Theta_G.
            # If Theta_G is per ray, it is constant for all j.
            # Let's assume Theta_G is indexed by the ray index `ray_idx`.
            # The paper says "Theta_G(i) ... for all camera rays".
            # So Theta_G is a vector of size `num_rays`.
            # In the product, it should be Theta_G(ray_idx).
            
            # Let's assume `ray_idx` is passed or available.
            # For this snippet, we assume `ray_idx` is 0 for simplicity or passed.
            # We will pass `ray_idx` to the render function.
            
            # Re-evaluating the formula:
            # T_conceal(r(i)) = exp(-sum_{j=1}^{i-1} sigma(r(j)) delta) * product_{j=1}^{i-1} Omega(r(j)) Theta_G(j)
            # If Theta_G is per camera, it should be Theta_G(ray_idx).
            # If the source meant Theta_G is per sample, it would depend on r(j).
            # "independent of voxels" -> independent of r(j).
            # So Theta_G is likely per camera/ray.
            # But the index in the product is j.
            # This implies Theta_G(j) might be Theta_G(ray_idx) for all j.
            # Or Theta_G is indexed by the sample index?
            # "corresponding to the camera distance i" -> i is the camera index.
            # So Theta_G is a vector of size N_cameras.
            # In the product, it should be Theta_G(ray_idx).
            
            # Let's assume `ray_idx` is provided.
            theta_g_val = self.theta_g[ray_idx]
            
            # Apply concealing fields
            # T_conceal = T * product(Omega * Theta_G)
            # We can compute the cumulative product of (Omega * Theta_G)
            conceal_factor = omega * theta_g_val
            conceal_cumprod = torch.cumprod(conceal_factor, dim=0)
            # Shifted for T(i)
            conceal_cumprod_shifted = torch.cat([torch.ones(1, device=conceal_factor.device), conceal_cumprod[:-1]])
            
            T_conceal = T * conceal_cumprod_shifted
            
            # Render with T_conceal
            weights = T_conceal * alpha
            C = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * color, dim=0)
            
            return C, T_conceal
        else:
            # Standard rendering
            weights = T * alpha
            C = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * color, dim=0)
            return C, T

    def compute_loss(self, C_nor, C_adv, C_gt, ray_indices):
        """
        Compute the total loss.
        
        Args:
            C_nor: Predicted normal-light images, shape (batch, 3, H, W) or (batch, H*W, 3).
            C_adv: Predicted adverse-light images, shape (batch, 3, H, W).
            C_gt: Ground truth adverse-light images, shape (batch, 3, H, W).
            ray_indices: Indices of the rays in the batch.
            
        Returns:
            L: Total loss.
        """
        # Flatten images to (batch, num_pixels, 3)
        C_nor_flat = C_nor.view(C_nor.size(0), -1, 3)
        C_adv_flat = C_adv.view(C_adv.size(0), -1, 3)
        C_gt_flat = C_gt.view(C_gt.size(0), -1, 3)
        
        # 1. Inverse Tone MSE Loss
        # L_it-mse = sum || Phi(C_nor + eps) - Phi(C_gt + eps) ||^2
        C_nor_eps = C_nor_flat + self.epsilon
        C_gt_eps = C_gt_flat + self.epsilon
        Phi_nor = self.inverse_tone_curve(C_nor_eps)
        Phi_gt = self.inverse_tone_curve(C_gt_eps)
        L_it_mse = torch.mean((Phi_nor - Phi_gt) ** 2)
        
        # 2. Degree Loss
        # L_de = || avgpool(C_nor) - e ||^2
        # Global average pooling over spatial dimensions
        C_nor_avg = torch.mean(C_nor_flat, dim=1) # (batch, 3)
        L_de = (C_nor_avg - self.e) ** 2
        L_de = torch.mean(L_de)
        
        # 3. Contrast Degree Loss
        # L_co = sum_r sum_{k in +1, -1} || C_nor(r) - C_adv(r+k) ||^2
        # This requires adjacent pixels. Assuming C_nor is on a grid.
        # For simplicity in this pseudocode, we assume C_nor and C_adv are flattened
        # and we approximate adjacent pixels by shifting the flattened array.
        # This is a simplification. In practice, 2D shifts are used.
        # We will compute a simplified version.
        
        # Shift right and left
        C_nor_shifted_right = torch.roll(C_nor_flat, shifts=1, dims=1)
        C_nor_shifted_left = torch.roll(C_nor_flat, shifts=-1, dims=1)
        
        # Mask out boundary pixels if necessary, but for loss calculation, 
        # the error at boundaries might be ignored or handled by padding.
        # We'll just compute the difference.
        diff_right = C_nor_shifted_right - C_adv_flat
        diff_left = C_nor_shifted_left - C_adv_flat
        
        L_co = torch.mean(diff_right ** 2 + diff_left ** 2)
        
        # 4. Color Constancy Loss
        # L_cc = sum_r sum_{p,q} (C_nor(r)_p - C_nor(r)_q)^2
        # Gray-world assumption.
        # For each pixel, compute variance of channels.
        # Mean of squared differences between all pairs of channels.
        # Channels: 0, 1, 2. Pairs: (0,1), (0,2), (1,2).
        diff_01 = C_nor_flat[:, :, 0] - C_nor_flat[:, :, 1]
        diff_02 = C_nor_flat[:, :, 0] - C_nor_flat[:, :, 2]
        diff_12 = C_nor_flat[:, :, 1] - C_nor_flat[:, :, 2]
        
        L_cc = torch.mean(diff_01 ** 2 + diff_02 ** 2 + diff_12 ** 2)
        
        # Total Loss
        L = L_it_mse + self.lambda1 * L_de + self.lambda2 * L_co + self.lambda3 * L_cc
        
        return L
```

**Parameters:**
-   $\delta$: Constant distance between adjacent sample points under uniform sampling [S1].
-   $e$: 0.45, hyperparameter signifying the enhancement degree [S1].
-   $\eta$: Hyperparameter controlling contrast degree. In over-exposure scenes, $e \cdot \eta$ is fixed to 0.5 [S1].
-   $\lambda_1$: $10^{-3}$, weight for degree loss $L_{\text{de}}$ [S1].
-   $\lambda_2$: $10^{-3}$, weight for contrast loss $L_{\text{co}}$ [S1].
-   $\lambda_3$: $10^{-8}$, weight for color constancy loss $L_{\text{cc}}$ [S1].
-   Learning rate: $5 \times 10^{-4}$, used with Adam optimizer [S1].
-   Batch size: 4096 [S1].
-   Iterations: 62,500 total training iterations [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
-   **MSE Loss Imbalance:** Standard MSE loss causes imbalance where dark pixels contribute minimally and bright pixels dominate. This is solved by using the inverse tone curve $\Phi$ to re-balance pixel weights [S1].
-   **Post-processing vs Pre-processing:** Post-processing NeRF results with enhancement methods ("NeRF + *") underperforms pre-processing ("* + NeRF") because NeRF produces poor-quality dark images that struggle enhancement methods [S1].
-   **Color Imbalance:** Directly removing/adding concealing fields may cause color imbalance, addressed by the color constancy loss [S1].

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time and Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
-   **Measured Performance:** Evaluated on the LOM dataset (5 scenes: 'buu', 'chair', 'sofa', 'bike', 'shrub') with paired low-light, normal-light, and over-exposure images. Images are collected with resolution $3000 \times 4000$ and down-sampled to $375 \times 500$ for training [S1].
-   **Scaling:** Not given in the sources.
-   **Strengths:**
    -   Trains end-to-end without pre-processing or post-processing [S1].
    -   Takes 8-bit LDR images as inputs, unlike RAW-NeRF which requires 16-bit HDR RAW images [S1].
    -   Does not require training data as prior knowledge [S1].
    -   Gains best or second-best performance on most scenes in the LOM dataset [S1].
-   **Limitations:**
    -   Requires paired low-light, normal-light, and over-exposure images for training the LOM dataset evaluation [S1].
    -   The specific implementation details of the convolution layer (e.g., 1D vs 2D) are not fully specified in the pseudocode-ready format, though "spatial relationships between pixels" suggests 2D [S1].
-   **When to use it:** When synthesizing normal-light novel views from under- or over-exposed 8-bit LDR images without paired training data or pre-processing steps.
-   **When not:** When 16-bit HDR RAW images are available and RAW-NeRF is preferred, or when pre-processing enhancement is acceptable.

## Background
-   **Origin:** Aleth-NeRF was introduced by Ziteng Cui, Lin Gu, Xiao Sun, Xianzheng Ma, Yu Qiao, and Tatsuya Harada in the paper "Aleth-NeRF: Illumination Adaptive NeRF with Concealing Field Assumption" presented at the Thirty-Eighth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI-24) [S1].
-   **History and Development:** The method is motivated by the ancient Greek emission theory, which posits that visual perception is formed by visual rays emitted from the eye. This theory ignores incident light and postulates that darkness is solely caused by particles between the object and the eye. Aleth-NeRF adopts this worldview, assuming that air particles attenuate emission (Concealing Field) rather than objects lacking emission [S1].
-   **Usage:** Implemented using the open-source PyTorch toolbox NeRF-Factory [S1]. Evaluated on the LOM dataset [S1].
