# Neural Field Regularization by Denoising (NEF-RED)

Also called: NeF-RED, RED-based neural field optimization

## Overview
NEF-RED is a method for optimizing neural fields to reconstruct high-resolution 3D volumes from sparse-view measurements, specifically targeting X-ray Computed Tomography (CT) [S1]. It solves the problem of ill-posed inverse reconstruction by integrating a learned regularizer directly into the neural field optimization loop, avoiding the computational overhead of two-stage approaches that generate synthetic projections before pixel-basis reconstruction [S1]. Unlike sibling methods that rely on explicit smoothness priors or perceptual distillation, NEF-RED leverages the Regularization by Denoising (RED) framework to decouple the conditioning network from the neural field optimization, allowing the use of pre-trained denoisers as implicit priors [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A sparse set of measurements $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ (where $m \ll n$), a forward operator $A$ (e.g., Radon Transform for CT), and a pre-trained denoiser network $D_\theta$ [S1].
*   **Outputs:** Optimized neural field parameters $\hat{\Phi}$ representing the reconstructed volume [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Neural Field $F_\Phi$:** A function mapping 3D coordinates to scalar values. In this implementation, it uses the **Instant-NGP** architecture [S1].
    *   Domain: $X \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ (positions within the projection area).
    *   Range: $\mathbb{R}$ (attenuation value).
    *   Formula: $F_\Phi: X \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ [S1].
*   **Reconstruction Grid $X_{grid}$:** A finite set of voxel coordinates in the reconstruction grid, size $n \times 3$ [S1].
*   **Denoiser $D_\theta$:** A convolutional neural network (CNN) trained for artifact removal or denoising [S1].
*   **Blocks:** A set $S = \{f_k\}$ of all possibly overlapping regular blocks of size $p$ (where $p \ll n$) within the volume [S1].

**Formulas:**
1.  **Data Fidelity Term:**
    $$ L(f) = \frac{1}{2} \|Af - b\|_2^2 $$
    where $A$ is the forward operator, $f$ is the object representation, and $b$ is the measurement [S1].
2.  **Regularization Term (RED):**
    The regularization potential $\rho(f)$ is defined such that its gradient is approximated by the denoiser residual:
    $$ \nabla \rho(f) = f - D(f) $$
    This holds if $D$ is differentiable with a symmetric Jacobian and locally homogeneous, or converges if $D$ is nonexpansive [S1].
3.  **Optimization Objective:**
    $$ \hat{\Phi} = \arg \min_\Phi L(F_\Phi(X_{grid})) + \lambda R(F_\Phi(X_{grid})) $$
    where $\lambda > 0$ is the relaxation weight [S1].
4.  **Update Step:**
    $$ f^{(k+1)} = f^{(k)} - \tau \nabla L(f^{(k)}) - \lambda H(f^{(k)}) $$
    where $H(f) = f - D_\theta(f)$ and $\tau > 0$ is the step size parameter [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $\eta$: Learning rate for the Adam optimizer ($\eta > 0$) [S1].
*   $\lambda$: Relaxation weight for the regularization term ($\lambda > 0$) [S1].
*   $\tau$: Step size parameter for the gradient descent update ($\tau > 0$) [S1].
*   $l$: Number of rays sampled per step ($l \ll m$) [S1].
*   $p$: Size of the regular block sampled for regularization ($p \ll n$) [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**
The optimization uses a randomized coordinate gradient procedure to handle high-resolution grids (e.g., $1024^3$) where full gradient computation is infeasible [S1].

1.  **Initialization:** Set learning rate $\eta > 0$, relaxation weight $\lambda > 0$, and initialize neural field parameters $\Phi_0$ randomly [S1].
2.  **Iteration Loop:** For each step $j = 1, 2, \dots$:
    a.  **Sample Rays:** Sample $l$ rays uniformly at random from the total $m$ rays [S1].
    b.  **Sample Block:** Sample a block of coordinates $X_k$ uniformly at random from the set of all possible overlapping blocks $S$ [S1].
    c.  **Data Fidelity Gradient:** Compute the gradient of the data fidelity term with respect to $\Phi_j$ using the sampled rays. Since the forward operator $A$ can be separated into $m$ single ray-integration operators, the gradient is estimated stochastically:
        $$ \nabla_{\Phi_j} L(F_{\Phi_j}(X_{grid})) \approx \frac{1}{l} \sum_{s=1}^l \nabla_{\Phi_j} L_{is}(F_{\Phi_j}(X_{grid})) $$
        where $L_{is}(f) = \|A_{is} f - b_i\|_2^2$ [S1].
    d.  **Regularization Step:** Compute the RED update step on the sampled block $f_k$ (where $f_k$ corresponds to $F_{\Phi_j}(X_k)$):
        $$ H_j(F_{\Phi_j}(X_k)) = f_k - D_\theta(f_k) $$
        Note: This computes the residual on the block rather than the full volume [S1].
    e.  **Update Parameters:** Update $\Phi$ using the Adam optimizer with the computed gradients and the regularization residual:
        $$ \Phi_{j+1} \leftarrow \text{Adam}(\nabla L(F_{\Phi_j}(X_{grid})), H_j(F_{\Phi_j}(X_k)), \Phi_j, \eta, \lambda) $$
        [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def nef_red_optimization(measurements_b, forward_operator_A, denoiser_D_theta, 
                         neural_field_F, grid_coords_X_grid, num_rays_m, 
                         num_sampled_rays_l, block_size_p, learning_rate_eta, 
                         relaxation_lambda, num_iterations):
    """
    Optimizes a neural field using NEF-RED.
    
    Args:
        measurements_b: Array of sparse measurements (size m).
        forward_operator_A: Function or object representing the forward operator (e.g., Radon Transform).
        denoiser_D_theta: Pre-trained denoiser network D_theta.
        neural_field_F: Instant-NGP neural field instance with parameters Phi.
        grid_coords_X_grid: Array of voxel coordinates (n x 3).
        num_rays_m: Total number of available rays.
        num_sampled_rays_l: Number of rays to sample per step (l << m).
        block_size_p: Size of the block for regularization (p << n).
        learning_rate_eta: Learning rate for Adam.
        relaxation_lambda: Weight for the regularization term.
        num_iterations: Total number of optimization steps.
        
    Returns:
        Optimized neural field parameters Phi.
    """
    # Initialize parameters randomly
    Phi = random_initialize_parameters()
    
    for j in range(1, num_iterations + 1):
        # 1. Sample l rays at random
        # Assuming rays are indexed 0 to m-1
        sampled_ray_indices = random_sample(range(num_rays_m), num_sampled_rays_l)
        
        # 2. Sample a block of coordinates X_k at random
        # Generate all possible blocks of size p from the grid
        all_blocks = generate_all_overlapping_blocks(grid_coords_X_grid, block_size_p)
        sampled_block_indices = random_sample(range(len(all_blocks)), 1)[0]
        X_k = all_blocks[sampled_block_indices]
        
        # 3. Compute Data Fidelity Gradient
        # Extract measurements corresponding to sampled rays
        b_sampled = measurements_b[sampled_ray_indices]
        
        # Compute gradient of L(F_Phi(X_grid)) w.r.t Phi
        # L(f) = 0.5 * ||A*f - b||^2
        # Gradient estimation: (1/l) * sum( grad_L_is )
        grad_L = compute_stochastic_data_fidelity_gradient(
            Phi, neural_field_F, forward_operator_A, 
            sampled_ray_indices, b_sampled, grid_coords_X_grid
        )
        
        # 4. Compute Regularization Step H_j
        # Extract values for the sampled block
        f_k = neural_field_F.evaluate(X_k, Phi)
        
        # Apply denoiser to the block
        denoised_f_k = denoiser_D_theta(f_k)
        
        # H(f) = f - D(f)
        H_j = f_k - denoised_f_k
        
        # 5. Update Phi using Adam
        # Note: H_j is computed on a block, but needs to be integrated into the 
        # parameter update. In the source, it is passed to Adam alongside grad_L.
        # The source implies H_j acts as a gradient-like term for the regularization.
        Phi = adam_update(Phi, grad_L, H_j, learning_rate_eta, relaxation_lambda)
        
    return Phi

def compute_stochastic_data_fidelity_gradient(Phi, F, A, ray_indices, b_sampled, X_grid):
    """
    Computes the stochastic gradient of the data fidelity term.
    L(f) = 0.5 * ||Af - b||^2
    """
    # Evaluate neural field on the full grid to get f
    f_full = F.evaluate(X_grid, Phi)
    
    # Initialize gradient accumulator
    grad_accum = zeros_like(Phi)
    
    # Sum gradients for each sampled ray
    for i, ray_idx in enumerate(ray_indices):
        # Get the specific ray operator A_is and measurement b_i
        # A_is is the projection of f along ray 'ray_idx'
        # In practice, this involves ray marching through the neural field
        f_ray = ray_march(F, ray_idx, Phi) # Returns scalar projection value
        b_i = b_sampled[i]
        
        # Loss for this ray: L_is = 0.5 * (f_ray - b_i)^2
        # Gradient dL_is/dPhi = (f_ray - b_i) * d(f_ray)/dPhi
        error = f_ray - b_i
        grad_ray = ray_gradient(F, ray_idx, Phi) # Gradient of ray projection w.r.t Phi
        
        grad_accum += error * grad_ray
        
    # Average over l rays
    grad_L = grad_accum / len(ray_indices)
    return grad_L

def adam_update(Phi, grad_L, H_j, eta, lambda_reg):
    """
    Performs Adam update.
    Note: H_j is a block-level residual. The source algorithm passes it directly 
    to the optimizer step. In a strict implementation, H_j must be mapped back 
    to the full parameter space or the optimizer must support block-wise updates.
    The source pseudocode implies a direct update:
    Phi_{i+1} <- Adam(grad_L, H_j, Phi_i, eta, lambda)
    
    Since H_j is defined on a block, this implies the regularization gradient 
    is applied only to the parameters influencing that block, or H_j is zero-padded.
    Given the disjoint coordinate estimation, we assume H_j contributes to the 
    gradient for the parameters associated with X_k.
    """
    # Construct full regularization gradient from block residual
    # This is a simplification; the source states "disjoint coordinate estimation"
    # meaning we update based on the sampled block.
    
    # For the purpose of this self-contained pseudocode, we assume 
    # the optimizer handles the combination of the full gradient and the 
    # block-specific regularization term.
    
    # Combined gradient for Adam
    # The source uses lambda in the update step.
    # Standard RED update: f - tau * grad_L - lambda * H
    # Here, we use Adam which adapts the step size.
    
    # We approximate the full regularization gradient by zero-padding H_j
    # to the size of Phi, assuming H_j only affects parameters in X_k.
    grad_reg = zero_pad_to_phi_size(H_j, X_k, Phi)
    
    combined_grad = grad_L + lambda_reg * grad_reg
    
    # Standard Adam step
    Phi = adam_step(Phi, combined_grad, eta)
    return Phi
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Cone-Beam Geometry:** Unlike parallel-beam geometries, cone-beam rays intersect the object non-axially, preventing independent evaluation of the forward model on different slices [S1]. This prevents coordinate-friendly partitioning of the problem [S1].
*   **Theoretical Convergence:** Because of the cone-beam geometry and the non-linearity of the neural field w.r.t $\Phi$, the method deviates from Block Coordinate RED conditions. Theoretical convergence guarantees are lost, but empirical convergence to a fixed point is observed [S1].
*   **Denoiser Assumptions:** The gradient formula $\nabla \rho(f) = f - D(f)$ assumes $D$ is differentiable with a symmetric Jacobian and locally homogeneous. While CNNs often do not strictly respect the symmetric Jacobian assumption, convergence is still observed if the denoiser is nonexpansive [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method uses stochastic sampling for both data fidelity ($l$ rays) and regularization (1 block), making each iteration significantly faster than full-grid updates. However, the neural field evaluation (Instant-NGP) and ray marching are computationally intensive per sample [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** NEF-RED is memory-efficient compared to two-stage methods (like IntraTomo or CoIL) because it avoids storing synthetic projections and performing pixel-basis reconstruction [S1]. It allows the entire pipeline to run on a single GPU, even for high-resolution volumes ($1024^3$) [S1].
*   **Performance:** Quantitatively, NEF-RED is either better or on par with state-of-the-art methods regarding reconstruction quality on sparse-view CT datasets (e.g., Cork-CBCT) [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Decouples conditioning from optimization; leverages pre-trained denoisers; single-stage optimization; memory efficient.
*   **Limitations:** Loss of theoretical convergence guarantees due to non-linear neural field and cone-beam geometry; requires a pre-trained denoiser $D_\theta$ [S1].
*   **When to use:** Sparse-view X-ray CT reconstruction, particularly in industrial inspection with Cone-Beam geometries [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When theoretical convergence guarantees are required; when parallel-beam geometry allows for simpler block-coordinate updates.

## Background
NEF-RED was introduced by Romain Vo, Etienne Decencière, and colleagues from Mines Paris, PSL University, and CEA [S1]. The method was presented in 2024 at the International Conference on 3D Vision (3DV) [S1]. It builds upon the Regularization by Denoising (RED) framework proposed by Romano et al. [S1] and extends it to neural fields. The work addresses limitations in previous neural field applications for CT, such as IntraTomo and CoIL, which used two-stage approaches [S1]. The authors note that leveraging the neural field solution as a prior in a second stage is inefficient, and directly regularizing its optimization yields better results with a lower computation budget [S1]. The specific neural field architecture used is Instant-NGP by Müller et al. [S1].
