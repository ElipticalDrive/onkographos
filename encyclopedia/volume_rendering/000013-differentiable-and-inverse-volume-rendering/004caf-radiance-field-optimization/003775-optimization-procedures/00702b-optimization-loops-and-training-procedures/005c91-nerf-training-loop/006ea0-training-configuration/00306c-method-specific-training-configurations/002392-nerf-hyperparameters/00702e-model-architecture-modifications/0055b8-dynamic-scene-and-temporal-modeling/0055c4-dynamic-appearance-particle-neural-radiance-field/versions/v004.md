# Dynamic Appearance Particle Neural Radiance Field

Also called: Dynamic Appearance Particle NeRF, Lagrangian dynamic model in NeRF

## Overview
Dynamic Appearance Particle Neural Radiance Field is a method for modeling dynamic scenes from monocular video data by combining a static grid-based radiance field with a particle-based dynamic component [S1]. It solves the problem of reconstructing time-varying scenes without requiring multi-view stereo data or prior geometric knowledge of dynamic objects [S1]. Unlike ParticleNeRF, which encodes the entire scene with particles and requires multiple camera views per frame, this method applies appearance particles only to dynamic elements and uses a grid-based field for efficient querying via linear interpolation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Monocular video data (a sequence of images at different times $t$), camera poses for each frame.
- **Outputs:** A dynamic radiance field model capable of rendering novel views at arbitrary times $t$.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Static Field ($V_s$):** A time-independent grid-based model (e.g., hash grid or dense voxel grid) storing spatial features. It does not depend on time $t$ [S1].
2.  **Dynamic Field ($V_{td}$):** A particle-based representation. The field is defined as a convolution of a continuous function with a Dirac delta function, quantized using a kernel with finite support [S1].
    -   **Particles:** Each particle represents a dynamic element in the scene.
    -   **Motion Network:** A network (denoted as $NN_{motion}$ in source notation) that models the motion of particles over time [S1].
    -   **Appearance Network:** A network (denoted as $MLP_c$ in source notation) that maps particle features to color/radiance [S1].
3.  **Weight Map ($w_t(x)$):** A binary or soft weight indicating whether the dynamic field is effective at location $x$ and time $t$. The support of the dynamic field is implied by the particle representation [S1].

**Notation Convention:**
Time $t$ is denoted in subscripts on the right-hand side of the superposition equation (e.g., $V_{td}$) to clarify that the dynamic component consists of an ensemble of particles, where the time argument is fixed for the instantaneous status construction [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Ray Casting:** For a target pixel at time $t$, cast a ray $\mathbf{r}(s) = \mathbf{o} + s\mathbf{d}$ from the camera center $\mathbf{o}$ in direction $\mathbf{d}$, where $s \in [s_n, s_f]$ [S1].

2.  **Field Superposition:** Compute the combined feature field $V(\mathbf{x}, t)$ at any point $\mathbf{x}$ along the ray using the superposition of the static and dynamic fields [S1]:
    $$
    V(\mathbf{x}, t) = (1 - w_t(\mathbf{x}))V_s(\mathbf{x}) + w_t(\mathbf{x})V_{td}(\mathbf{x})
    $$
    where:
    -   $V_s(\mathbf{x})$ is the static field value at $\mathbf{x}$ [S1].
    -   $V_{td}(\mathbf{x})$ is the dynamic field value at $\mathbf{x}$ [S1].
    -   $w_t(\mathbf{x})$ is the weight, taking value 1 if the dynamic field is effective at $(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and 0 otherwise [S1].

3.  **Dynamic Field Evaluation ($V_{td}$):**
    The dynamic field is formulated as a convolution with a Dirac function to integrate over finite volumes [S1]:
    $$
    V_{td}(\mathbf{x}) = (V_{td} * \delta)(\mathbf{x}) = \int V_{td}(\mathbf{x}')\delta(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}')d\nu(\mathbf{x}')
    $$
    where $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac function [S1].
    
    To enable efficient computation and quantization, the Dirac function $\delta$ is replaced with a kernel $W$ with finite support [S1]:
    -   $W(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ when $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ exceeds a small radius.
    -   $\int W(\mathbf{x})d\nu(\mathbf{x}) = 1$ [S1].
    
    The value $V_{td}(\mathbf{x})$ is determined by querying the particles. The particle motion network $NN_{motion}$ predicts the position of particles at time $t$ based on their initial positions and time input [S1]. The appearance network $MLP_c$ maps the features of the nearest particles to the radiance/color [S1]. Linear interpolation is used for efficient querying of the grid-based static field, while particle-based querying is used for the dynamic field [S1].

4.  **Volume Rendering:** Compute the pixel color $C(\mathbf{r}, t)$ by integrating along the ray [S1]:
    $$
    C(\mathbf{r}, t) = \int_{s_n}^{s_f} T(s, t) \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s), t) \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(s), \mathbf{d}, t) ds
    $$
    where:
    -   $\sigma(\mathbf{r}(s), t)$ is the volume density at $\mathbf{r}(s)$ and time $t$.
    -   $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(s), \mathbf{d}, t)$ is the emitted radiance (color) at $\mathbf{r}(s)$, direction $\mathbf{d}$, and time $t$.
    -   $T(s, t) = \exp\left(-\int_{s_n}^{s} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s'), t) ds'\right)$ is the accumulated transmittance [S1].

**Parameters:**
-   **Kernel Radius:** The small radius for the kernel $W$ is not explicitly given in the sources [S1].
-   **Weight $w_t(x)$:** Binary (0 or 1) based on particle presence/support [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
-   **Static vs. Dynamic Boundary:** The weight $w_t(x)$ must correctly identify regions where dynamic particles are present. If $w_t(x)$ is incorrect, static and dynamic features may conflict [S1].
-   **Monocular Ambiguity:** Since the method uses monocular video, depth and motion disambiguation rely heavily on the particle motion network's ability to model temporal consistency [S1].
-   **Particle Density:** The support of the dynamic field is implied by the particle representation. Sparse particles may lead to holes in the dynamic field [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def render_dynamic_apearance_particle_nerf(ray_o, ray_d, time_t, static_grid, particles, motion_net, appearance_net, weight_map, near_bound, far_bound):
    """
    Renders a pixel for a given ray and time using Dynamic Appearance Particle NeRF.
    
    Args:
        ray_o: Origin of the ray (3,)
        ray_d: Direction of the ray (3,)
        time_t: Current time step (float)
        static_grid: Grid-based static field V_s(x)
        particles: List of particles, each with initial position, feature, and motion parameters
        motion_net: Neural network predicting particle positions at time t
        appearance_net: Neural network predicting color/radiance from particle features
        weight_map: Function or grid to determine w_t(x)
        near_bound: s_n
        far_bound: s_f
        
    Returns:
        color: Rendered color (3,)
    """
    # Sample points along the ray
    num_samples = 128
    s = np.linspace(near_bound, far_bound, num_samples)
    points = ray_o + s[:, np.newaxis] * ray_d  # (num_samples, 3)
    
    # Initialize transmittance and accumulated color
    T = np.ones(num_samples)
    color_accum = np.zeros(3)
    
    # Precompute particle positions at time t
    particle_positions_t = motion_net(particles, time_t)  # (num_particles, 3)
    particle_features = particles.features  # (num_particles, feature_dim)
    
    for i, point in enumerate(points):
        # 1. Evaluate Static Field V_s(point)
        # Assuming static_grid is a hash grid or dense grid with linear interpolation
        v_s = static_grid.query(point)  # (feature_dim,)
        
        # 2. Evaluate Dynamic Field V_td(point)
        # Find nearest particles to point
        # Using a simple nearest neighbor search for demonstration
        # In practice, use a spatial index (e.g., KDTree)
        dists = np.linalg.norm(particle_positions_t - point, axis=1)
        k = 3  # Number of neighbors
        nearest_indices = np.argsort(dists)[:k]
        nearest_positions = particle_positions_t[nearest_indices]
        nearest_features = particle_features[nearest_indices]
        
        # Compute weights for kernel W (finite support)
        # W(x) = 0 if ||x|| > radius, else normalized kernel value
        radius = 0.1  # Example radius, not given in sources
        kernel_weights = np.zeros(k)
        for j, idx in enumerate(nearest_indices):
            d = np.linalg.norm(nearest_positions[j] - point)
            if d < radius:
                # Simple linear kernel for demonstration
                kernel_weights[j] = 1.0 - d / radius
            else:
                kernel_weights[j] = 0.0
        kernel_weights = kernel_weights / (np.sum(kernel_weights) + 1e-8)
        
        # Interpolate features from nearest particles
        v_td = np.zeros_like(nearest_features[0])
        for j in range(k):
            v_td += kernel_weights[j] * nearest_features[j]
        
        # 3. Determine Weight w_t(point)
        # w_t is 1 if dynamic field is effective, 0 otherwise
        # Based on particle presence within radius
        w_t = 1.0 if np.any(dists < radius) else 0.0
        
        # 4. Superposition
        v_combined = (1 - w_t) * v_s + w_t * v_td
        
        # 5. Decode Density and Color
        # Assuming a shared MLP or separate MLPs for sigma and c
        # This is a placeholder for the actual decoding network
        sigma, color = decode_network(v_combined, ray_d)
        
        # 6. Volume Rendering Step
        # T(s) = exp(-sum(sigma * delta_s))
        # alpha = 1 - exp(-sigma * delta_s)
        delta_s = s[1] - s[0] if i > 0 else 0.01  # Approximate delta_s
        alpha = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigma * delta_s)
        
        # Accumulate color
        # C = sum(T * alpha * color)
        T_current = T[i]
        color_accum += T_current * alpha * color
        
        # Update transmittance for next sample
        T[i+1] = T_current * (1 - alpha)
        
    return color_accum

def decode_network(v_combined, ray_d):
    """
    Placeholder for the MLP that decodes features into density and color.
    In the actual implementation, this would be a learned MLP.
    """
    # Concatenate view direction for view-dependent color
    v_with_view = np.concatenate([v_combined, ray_d])
    # Simple linear projection for demonstration
    sigma = np.exp(np.sum(v_with_view))  # Ensure positive density
    color = np.tanh(v_with_view[:3])  # Assume first 3 dims are color
    return sigma, color
```

## Subtopics

### ParticleNeRF
Encodes the entire scene with particles and uses radius nearest neighbor searching. Requires 20 camera views per time frame. [S1]

### InstantNGP
An alternative framework that can integrate the Lagrangian representation of Dynamic Appearance Particle NeRF. [S1]

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources. However, the use of a grid-based field for static components and linear interpolation suggests efficient querying compared to pure particle-based methods [S1].
-   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources. The method uses a grid for static parts and particles for dynamic parts, which may be more memory-efficient than storing full dynamic grids for all time steps [S1].
-   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
-   **Strengths:**
    -   Handles monocular video data (single camera view per time frame) [S1].
    -   Does not require prior geometric knowledge of dynamic objects [S1].
    -   Efficient querying via linear interpolation for static parts and particle-based querying for dynamic parts [S1].
-   **Limitations:**
    -   Relies on the accuracy of the particle motion network for temporal consistency [S1].
    -   The weight $w_t(x)$ must correctly separate static and dynamic regions [S1].
-   **When to Use:** When reconstructing dynamic scenes from monocular video without multi-view data or prior geometry [S1].
-   **When Not to Use:** When multi-view data is available and ParticleNeRF's multi-view consistency is preferred, or when the scene is entirely static (use standard NeRF) [S1].

## Background
-   **Origin:** The method was proposed in the paper "Dynamic Appearance Particle Neural Radiance Field" [S1].
-   **History:** It builds upon the concept of Lagrangian dynamic models and particle-based representations in NeRF [S1]. It addresses limitations of ParticleNeRF, which requires multi-view data and encodes the entire scene with particles [S1].
-   **Usage:** Designed for monocular video reconstruction of dynamic scenes [S1].
