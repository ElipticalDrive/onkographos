# Bilateral Edge-Aware Constraint

Also called: Lbilateral

## Overview
The Bilateral Edge-Aware Constraint ($L_{bilateral}$) is a geometric regularization term used in NeRF optimization to preserve sharp structural discontinuities (such as building roofs and walls) while suppressing high-frequency noise on continuous surfaces [S1]. Unlike standard smoothness constraints that blur boundaries, this method dynamically weights geometric penalties based on both spatial proximity and photometric similarity, ensuring that neighbors with inconsistent color cues are not forced into coplanarity [S1]. It addresses the lack of fixed connectivity in implicit radiance fields by sampling neighbors via image-plane offsets [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** For a central ray $i$, the method requires the 3D position $P_i$, the unit surface normal $n_i$, and the albedo feature $c_i$. It also requires $K$ neighboring rays $j \in N_i$.
*   **Outputs:** A scalar loss value $L_{bilateral}$ added to the total training loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Neighbor Set $N_i$:** A set of $K$ indices corresponding to rays sampled at integer offsets in the image plane.
*   **Albedo Feature $c$:** A vector representing the diffuse color component of the radiance field at the sampled point.

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Neighbor Sampling:** For each central ray $i$, sample $K=4$ neighbors by drawing integer image-plane offsets uniformly from the range $[-2, 2] \times [-2, 2]$, excluding the zero offset $(0,0)$ [S1].
2.  **Neighbor Rendering:** Render the corresponding neighbor rays to recover their 3D positions $P_j$, normals $n_j$ (if needed for other terms), and albedo features $c_j$ [S1].
3.  **Compute Adaptive Scales:** Calculate the adaptive spatial scale $\sigma_d$ and photometric scale $\sigma_w$ using the sampled neighbors [S1]:
    $$ \sigma_d = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{j \in N_i} \| P_i - P_j \|^2} $$
    $$ \sigma_w = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{j \in N_i} \| c_i - c_j \|^2} $$
    [S1]
4.  **Calculate Bilateral Weights:** Compute the weight $w_{i,j}$ for each neighbor $j$ using the exponential terms for spatial and photometric distances [S1]:
    $$ w_{i,j} = \exp\left(-\frac{\| P_i - P_j \|^2}{2\sigma_d^2}\right) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{\| c_i - c_j \|^2}{2\sigma_w^2}\right) $$
    [S1]
5.  **Compute Normalization Factor:** Calculate $l_i$, the average edge length for the central point, defined as [S1]:
    $$ l_i = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{j \in N_i} \| P_i - P_j \| $$
    [S1]
6.  **Calculate Loss:** Minimize the projected distance of neighboring points onto the tangent plane of the central point. The loss is the sum of squared distances weighted by $w_{i,j}$ and normalized by $l_i$ [S1]:
    $$ L_{bilateral} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} l_i \sum_{j \in N_i, j \neq i} w_{i,j} \left( (P_i - P_j) \cdot n_i \right)^2 $$
    [S1]
    where $N$ is the total number of rays processed in the batch.

**Parameters:**
*   $K$: Number of neighbors sampled per ray. Value: 4 [S1].
*   $\gamma_{bi}$: Weight for the bilateral edge-aware term in the total loss. Selected from $\{0.1, 0.01, 0.001\}$ based on validation performance [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Implicit Geometry Connectivity:** NeRF does not provide fixed surface connectivity. Relying on image-plane offsets ensures neighbors are spatially relevant in the view, but rendering them is required to find their 3D positions [S1].
*   **Zero Division:** If $\sigma_w$ or $\sigma_d$ becomes zero (e.g., if all neighbors are identical in position or color), the exponential terms become 1. The normalization $l_i$ prevents division by zero in the loss scaling, but $\sigma_w=0$ with non-zero color differences is mathematically impossible by definition. However, if $K$ is small and neighbors are identical, $\sigma_d$ may be small, leading to large weights for very close points. [G] Guard against numerical instability in the denominator of the exponential if $\sigma$ is extremely small (e.g., clamp $\sigma$ to a minimum epsilon).
*   **Boundary Rays:** Rays near the image border may have fewer valid neighbors within the $[-2, 2]$ offset range. The method assumes uniform sampling; boundary handling is not explicitly detailed, so rays with fewer than $K$ neighbors should be skipped or padded. [G]

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def bilateral_edge_aware_constraint(
    P_center: torch.Tensor,  # [N, 3] 3D positions of central rays
    n_center: torch.Tensor,  # [N, 3] Unit normals of central rays
    c_center: torch.Tensor,  # [N, C] Albedo features of central rays
    P_neighbors: torch.Tensor, # [N, K, 3] 3D positions of K neighbors
    c_neighbors: torch.Tensor, # [N, K, C] Albedo features of K neighbors
    K: int = 4
) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the Bilateral Edge-Aware Constraint loss.
    
    Args:
        P_center: [N, 3]
        n_center: [N, 3]
        c_center: [N, C]
        P_neighbors: [N, K, 3]
        c_neighbors: [N, K, C]
        
    Returns:
        Loss scalar.
    """
    # Step 3: Compute adaptive scales
    # Spatial distances squared
    diff_P = P_center.unsqueeze(1) - P_neighbors # [N, K, 3]
    dist_P_sq = torch.sum(diff_P ** 2, dim=2)    # [N, K]
    
    # Photometric distances squared
    diff_c = c_center.unsqueeze(1) - c_neighbors # [N, K, C]
    dist_c_sq = torch.sum(diff_c ** 2, dim=2)    # [N, K]
    
    # Adaptive sigma_d and sigma_w
    # Note: Source uses sqrt(mean). We add a small epsilon to avoid division by zero or log(0) issues later if needed,
    # though exp handles 0 fine.
    sigma_d = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(dist_P_sq, dim=1) + 1e-8) # [N]
    sigma_w = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(dist_c_sq, dim=1) + 1e-8) # [N]
    
    # Step 4: Calculate Bilateral Weights
    # w_ij = exp(-||Pi-Pj||^2 / (2*sigma_d^2)) * exp(-||ci-cj||^2 / (2*sigma_w^2))
    # Reshape sigma for broadcasting: [N, 1]
    sigma_d_sq = (sigma_d ** 2).unsqueeze(1) # [N, 1]
    sigma_w_sq = (sigma_w ** 2).unsqueeze(1) # [N, 1]
    
    w_spatial = torch.exp(-dist_P_sq / (2 * sigma_d_sq)) # [N, K]
    w_photometric = torch.exp(-dist_c_sq / (2 * sigma_w_sq)) # [N, K]
    
    w_ij = w_spatial * w_photometric # [N, K]
    
    # Step 5: Compute Normalization Factor l_i (average edge length)
    # l_i = mean(||Pi - Pj||)
    dist_P = torch.sqrt(dist_P_sq + 1e-8)
    l_i = torch.mean(dist_P, dim=1) # [N]
    
    # Step 6: Calculate Loss
    # Projected distance: (Pi - Pj) . ni
    # n_center is [N, 3], diff_P is [N, K, 3]
    # Dot product: sum over dim 2
    projected_dist = torch.sum(diff_P * n_center.unsqueeze(1), dim=2) # [N, K]
    
    # Squared projected distance
    squared_proj = projected_dist ** 2 # [N, K]
    
    # Weighted sum
    # l_i is [N], w_ij is [N, K], squared_proj is [N, K]
    # Element-wise multiply l_i with the sum over j
    loss_per_ray = l_i.unsqueeze(1) * torch.sum(w_ij * squared_proj, dim=1) # [N]
    
    # Average over N rays
    loss = torch.mean(loss_per_ray)
    
    return loss
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot K)$, where $N$ is the number of rays and $K$ is the number of neighbors (typically 4). Rendering neighbor rays adds a constant factor to the forward pass cost.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N \cdot K)$ to store neighbor positions and features during the backward pass.
*   **Strengths:** Preserves sharp geometric boundaries (roofs, walls) that standard smoothness constraints blur. Adapts to local scene structure via photometric cues.
*   **Limitations:** Requires rendering additional rays for neighbors, increasing computational overhead. Performance depends on the quality of albedo features $c$; if albedo is noisy or inconsistent with geometry, weights may be misleading.
*   **When to use:** When reconstructing scenes with distinct man-made structures or sharp terrain features from sparse or multi-view satellite imagery [S1].
*   **When not to use:** For scenes with homogeneous geometry and no sharp edges, where standard smoothness is sufficient and cheaper.

## Background
The Bilateral Edge-Aware Constraint was proposed in the context of DSM (Digital Surface Model) reconstruction from multi-view satellite images using a Shading and Geometric Constraint Neural Radiance Field [S1]. It was introduced to address the limitation of standard smoothness constraints that over-smooth distinct boundaries of buildings and terrain features [S1]. The method is part of a geometric structure constraint framework that also includes a Normal Smoothness Constraint, designed to guide the network in learning density field distributions for accurate elevation estimates [S1].
