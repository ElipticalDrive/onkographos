# Geometry Focused Model Optimization

Also called: Geometry-Material Decoupling Optimization

## Overview
Geometry Focused Model Optimization stabilizes the training of radiance fields by decoupling the strong mutual dependency between geometry and material parameters [S2]. It addresses the issue where simultaneous optimization of geometry and material leads to noisy gradients and poor convergence [S2]. It sets itself apart from standard end-to-end training by introducing specific approximations and staged rendering strategies to isolate geometry updates [S1, unofficial][S2].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Scene parameters (Gaussian primitives with position, covariance, opacity, color, and material properties); Camera poses; Training images.
**Outputs:** Optimized scene representation (geometry and material); Rendered images.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Primitive:** Contains position $\mathbf{p}$, covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$, opacity $\alpha$, base color $\mathbf{c}$, and material parameters (e.g., roughness, metallic, normal).
*   **Compensation Factors:** Learnable scalar or vector parameters used to approximate the decoupled material response [S2].

**Steps:**

1.  **Initial Stage (Per-Gaussian Shading):**
    To stabilize gradient transfer to geometry in the initial phase, apply the rendering equation directly to each Gaussian primitive [S1, unofficial].
    *   Compute outgoing radiance $L_{out}$ for each Gaussian using its specific material and geometry properties [S1, unofficial].
    *   Alpha-blend these per-Gaussian radiance values during rasterization to produce the final image [S1, unofficial].
    *   This avoids pixel-level shading disturbances that can mislead geometry gradients [S1, unofficial].

2.  **Decoupled Optimization (Approximation):**
    Apply an approximation to decouple the optimization of geometry and material [S2].
    *   Use learnable compensation factors during training to model the interaction between geometry and material without computing the full coupled gradient [S2].
    *   This relaxation prevents the "tricky task" of optimizing both simultaneously, which causes noise in forward rendering and gradient calculation [S2].

3.  **Final Stage (Full Rendering):**
    *   Remove the approximation and compensation factors only for baseline comparison or final inference if required [S2].
    *   Perform standard differentiable rendering with full material-geometry coupling [S2].

**Pitfalls:**
*   Without the approximation, the strong dependency between geometry and material makes optimization significantly harder, yielding worse view synthesis and noisy geometry [S2].
*   Pixel-level shading during the initial phase can disturb gradient directions of geometry [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Geometry-Material Decoupling Optimization
A relaxation technique that decouples the strong mutual dependency between geometry and material optimization to stabilize training [S2]. It uses learnable compensation factors during training and removes them only for baseline comparison [S2]. Without this approximation, optimization is harder due to noise in forward rendering and gradients, leading to worse view synthesis and noisy geometry [S2]. On a 2000-image test set, the baseline without approximation achieves PSNR 26.44, SSIM 0.9255, LPIPS 0.0681, while the decoupled method achieves PSNR 30.01, SSIM 0.9542, LPIPS 0.0430 [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Performance:** The decoupled approach significantly boosts performance compared to the baseline without approximation [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Stabilizes training, reduces noise in geometry reconstruction, improves view synthesis quality [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Requires additional learnable compensation factors; approximation must be removed for final comparison or inference [S2].
*   **When to use:** When optimizing both geometry and material simultaneously leads to convergence issues or noisy results [S2].

## Background
Originated in the context of differentiable light transport with Gaussian Surfels for efficient relighting and geometry reconstruction [S2]. The method addresses the challenges of optimizing geometry and material in radiance fields, particularly in the presence of global illumination effects [S2].
