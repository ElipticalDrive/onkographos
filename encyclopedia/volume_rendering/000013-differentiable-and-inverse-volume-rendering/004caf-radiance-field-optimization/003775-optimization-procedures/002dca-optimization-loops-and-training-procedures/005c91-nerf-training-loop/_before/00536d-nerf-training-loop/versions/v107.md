# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Fields Training, Training Ray Sampling Strategy for Correspondence Disparity, Ray Sampling Strategy for Correspondence Disparity

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that learns a continuous volumetric scene representation from a set of multi-view images. It solves the inverse problem of reconstructing a 3D radiance field by minimizing the photometric difference between rendered images and ground truth observations [S5, S6, S15, unofficial]. It differs from its siblings by defining the core stochastic gradient descent cycle—specifically the random ray sampling, differentiable volume rendering forward pass, and MSE-based weight update—that serves as the foundation for all subsequent NeRF variants [S11, S15, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A dataset of $N$ training images with known camera poses (extrinsics) and intrinsics. Each image has height $H$, width $W$, and focal length $f$.
*   **Outputs:** Optimized neural network weights $\theta$ (and potentially camera poses or other parameters) representing the scene.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Rays Array (`rays_rgb`):** A pre-computed or on-the-fly generated array containing ray origins, directions, and target RGB colors.
    *   Layout: Shape $[(N-1) \cdot H \cdot W, 3, 3]$ or flattened batch $[B, 3, 3]$ [S5, S15, unofficial].
    *   Axis 0: Pixel/Ray index.
    *   Axis 1: Channel type (0: Ray Origin $\mathbf{o}$, 1: Ray Direction $\mathbf{d}$, 2: Target RGB $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$).
    *   Axis 2: Components (x, y, z for rays; R, G, B for color).
    *   Type: `float32` [S5, S15, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint:** A dictionary storing model state, optimizer state, and iteration count.
    *   Keys: `'step'` (int), `'coarse'` (state_dict), `'fine'` (state_dict), `'optim'` (optimizer state_dict) [S11, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   $N_{rand}$: Number of rays sampled per iteration (batch size). Typical values: 1024 or 2048 [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   $N_{iters}$: Total number of optimization steps. Typical value: 100,000 [S11, unofficial].
*   $lr$: Learning rate for the Adam optimizer. Typical value: $5 \times 10^{-4}$ [S11, unofficial].
*   $N_{samples}$: Number of points sampled per ray. Typical value: 128 [S18, unofficial].
*   $near, far$: Near and far clipping planes for ray sampling. For synthetic Blender data: $near=2.0, far=6.0$ [S14, unofficial]. For real-world data, computed from bounding depths [S2, unofficial].
*   $chunk$: Maximum number of rays processed per forward pass to limit VRAM usage [S5, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Load training images and camera poses.
    *   Compute ray origins and directions for all pixels in all training images. Store in `rays_rgb` [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the NeRF neural network weights (e.g., MLP parameters) [S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with learning rate $lr$ [S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize a learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, S11, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop (for $i = 1$ to $N_{iters}$):**
    a.  **Ray Sampling:**
        *   Randomly select $N_{rand}$ unique indices from the `rays_rgb` array [S5, S15, unofficial].
        *   Extract the corresponding ray origins $\mathbf{o}$, directions $\mathbf{d}$, and target colors $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$ [S5, S15, unofficial].
        *   *Optional:* If $i < \text{precrop\_iters}$, restrict sampling to a central crop of the image to stabilize early training [S15, unofficial].
        *   *Optional:* Apply jitter to sampling positions along the ray to reduce aliasing [S5, unofficial].

    b.  **Forward Pass (Volume Rendering):**
        *   For each sampled ray, sample $N_{samples}$ points along the segment $[near, far]$ [S5, unofficial].
        *   Query the NeRF network at each point to obtain density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ [S5, unofficial].
        *   Compute transmittance $T_i$ and weights $\alpha_i$ using the emission-absorption model [44-radiance-field-optimization.md].
        *   Render the predicted color $\hat{\mathbf{c}}$ by compositing the weighted colors [S5, unofficial].
        *   *Optional (Coarse-Fine):* If using a coarse-fine model, render a coarse prediction $\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{coarse}$, sample additional points based on coarse weights, and render a fine prediction $\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{fine}$ [S11, unofficial].

    c.  **Loss Computation:**
        *   Compute the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted color $\hat{\mathbf{c}}$ and the target color $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$ [S15, unofficial]:
            $$ \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{N_{rand}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{rand}} \|\hat{\mathbf{c}}_j - \mathbf{c}_{gt,j}\|^2 $$
        *   *Optional:* If coarse output is available, compute coarse loss $\mathcal{L}_{coarse}$ and sum it with the fine loss [S11, unofficial].
        *   *Optional:* Add regularization terms (e.g., sparsity, TV loss) if applicable [S7, unofficial].

    d.  **Backpropagation and Update:**
        *   Zero the gradients of the optimizer [S19, unofficial].
        *   Compute gradients of the total loss with respect to the network weights via automatic differentiation [S19, unofficial].
        *   Step the optimizer to update the weights [S19, unofficial].
        *   Update the learning rate according to the scheduler [S19, unofficial].

    e.  **Logging and Checkpointing:**
        *   Log the loss and PSNR every $k$ steps [S11, unofficial].
        *   Render a validation image every $m$ steps [S11, unofficial].
        *   Save a checkpoint every $n$ steps [S11, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

def train_nerf(rays_rgb, target_colors, model, optimizer, scheduler, 
               n_iters=100000, n_rand=1024, near=2.0, far=6.0, chunk=8192):
    """
    rays_rgb: Tensor of shape [N_total_rays, 3, 3] 
              [origin_x, origin_y, origin_z, dir_x, dir_y, dir_z, rgb_r, rgb_g, rgb_b]
              Or split into rays_o, rays_d, target_s.
    """
    # Pre-compute total number of rays
    n_total_rays = rays_rgb.shape[0]
    
    for i in range(n_iters):
        # 1. Ray Sampling
        # Randomly select n_rand indices
        indices = torch.randint(0, n_total_rays, (n_rand,))
        
        # Extract ray origins, directions, and target colors
        # Assuming rays_rgb is [N, 9] where last 3 are RGB
        batch_rays = rays_rgb[indices] 
        rays_o = batch_rays[:, :3]
        rays_d = batch_rays[:, 3:6]
        target_s = batch_rays[:, 6:9]
        
        # 2. Forward Pass
        # Render rays in chunks to manage memory
        rgb_map = []
        for j in range(0, n_rand, chunk):
            chunk_o = rays_o[j:j+chunk]
            chunk_d = rays_d[j:j+chunk]
            # Call the differentiable volume rendering function
            # This function handles sampling, network query, and compositing
            chunk_rgb = render_rays(model, chunk_o, chunk_d, near, far)
            rgb_map.append(chunk_rgb)
        
        rgb_map = torch.cat(rgb_map, dim=0)
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(rgb_map, target_s)
        
        # 4. Backpropagation and Update
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        
        # 5. Learning Rate Scheduling
        scheduler.step()
        
        # 6. Logging (every 100 steps)
        if i % 100 == 0:
            psnr = -10.0 * torch.log10(loss)
            print(f"Step {i}: Loss {loss.item():.6f}, PSNR {psnr.item():.2f}")
            
        # 7. Checkpointing (every 5000 steps)
        if i % 5000 == 0:
            save_checkpoint(model, optimizer, i)

def render_rays(model, rays_o, rays_d, near, far):
    """
    Differentiable volume rendering for a batch of rays.
    """
    # Sample points along the ray
    t_vals = torch.linspace(0., 1., steps=128).to(rays_o.device)
    z_vals = near * (1. - t_vals) + far * (t_vals)
    
    # Add jitter for anti-aliasing
    mids = .5 * (z_vals[..., 1:] + z_vals[..., :-1])
    upper = torch.cat([mids, z_vals[..., -1:]], -1)
    lower = torch.cat([z_vals[..., :1], mids], -1)
    t_rand = torch.rand(z_vals.shape).to(rays_o.device)
    z_vals = lower + (upper - lower) * t_rand
    
    # Compute 3D points
    pts = rays_o[..., None, :] + rays_d[..., None, :] * z_vals[..., :, None]
    
    # Query NeRF
    raw = model(pts, rays_d) # Returns density and color
    
    # Compute weights and composite
    # ... (standard volume rendering compositing logic) ...
    rgb = composite(raw, z_vals, rays_d)
    
    return rgb
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Rendering all rays at once can exceed GPU memory. Mitigate by processing rays in chunks (`chunk` parameter) [S5, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Computation Order:** If computing higher-order derivatives (e.g., gradients for logging) for debugging, they must be computed *before* `loss.backward()` because the backward pass frees the autograd graph [S3, unofficial].
*   **Resuming Training:** When loading from a checkpoint, ensure the learning rate scheduler's last epoch is set to `start_epoch - 1` to maintain correct scheduling [S9, unofficial].
*   **Empty Checkpoints:** If a checkpoint is loaded but the step counter is 0, it may indicate an empty or corrupted checkpoint, requiring error handling [S16, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Growth:** In dynamic growth methods, initializing new sub-network weights to match the parent prevents density collapse [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md)
The differentiable forward pass that synthesizes RGB images from a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) during optimization. It solves the problem of computing gradients for scene parameters by sampling points along camera rays, querying implicit density and color functions, and integrating them via alpha compositing. It differs from its siblings by focusing on the computational pipeline of ray sampling, neural query, and volume integration required for gradient-based training, whereas [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md) operates in inference mode with deterministic sampling, and [NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)](5124-nerf-rendering-alpha-compositing.md) describes the mathematical compositing principle rather than the training-specific optimization loops.

### [Training Configuration](2332-training-configuration.md)
Training Configuration defines the hyperparameters, optimization schedules, and architectural constraints required to train differentiable radiance field representations from multi-view images. It addresses the problem of stabilizing convergence and achieving high-fidelity reconstruction while managing computational cost and training stability. What sets this configuration apart from generic volume rendering is the tight coupling between the sampling strategy, the network activation functions, and the loss landscape tailored to the specific representation's high-frequency nature or geometric priors. It serves as the foundational setup for the [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md), providing the specific numerical values and procedural rules that govern the optimization process.

### [Neural Volume Scene Setup](4168-neural-volume-scene-setup.md)
Constructs a Mitsuba scene containing a volumetric cube and a custom neural radiance field integrator, initializing the density grid and spherical harmonics coefficients. It differs from generic training setups by directly binding the neural network parameters to the scene graph's heterogeneous volume and integrator components.

### [Regularization and Loss Functions](8586-regularization-and-loss-functions.md)
Regularization and Loss Functions define the objective landscape for optimizing Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), extending the standard photometric reconstruction loss to address specific failure modes such as overfitting in sparse views, geometric ambiguity ("floaters"), semantic incoherence, and style mismatches. This family sets itself apart from general optimization procedures by providing specialized mathematical formulations that constrain the density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ fields, or the rendered outputs, to adhere to prior knowledge (geometric, semantic, or statistical) rather than relying solely on pixel-wise alignment with ground truth images.

### [KNN-NeRF](8672-knn-nerf.md)
KNN-NeRF is a baseline method for training Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) using sparse 3D point clouds as supervision instead of multi-view images. It solves the problem of generating ground-truth color labels for rays that do not pass through existing points by assigning the color of the nearest point in the cloud to the ray. Unlike standard NeRF which relies on pixel colors from images, KNN-NeRF constructs a dense training signal from sparse geometry, though it struggles with sharp object borders due to the low-frequency bias of neural networks and the sparsity of the input.

### [Progressive and Dynamic Scene Growth](8869-progressive-and-dynamic-scene-growth.md)
Progressive and Dynamic Scene Growth refers to a family of training strategies that expand the capacity, data, or topology of a neural radiance field during optimization, rather than optimizing a static representation on a fixed dataset. This family solves the problems of representing complex, large-scale, or dynamic scenes with limited initial data, and mitigates issues such as "foggy" densities, floating artifacts, and catastrophic forgetting in online settings. It sets itself apart from static NeRF variants by coupling the network architecture or training data directly to the training error or temporal sequence, growing $k$ new branches per step based on uncertainty, iteratively expanding reference views for panoramic scenes [8666-progressive-view-synthesis-training.md], or continuously updating the field as new camera views arrive [12066-online-incremental-nerf-optimization.md].

### [Sparse View and Initialization Methods](9394-sparse-view-and-initialization-methods.md)
This family addresses the degradation of NeRF quality—such as blurred geometry, slow convergence, and floaters—when trained on sparse input views (typically 15–20 images or fewer than 50–100 views). It distinguishes itself from standard NeRF training by decoupling the initialization and regularization processes from the default random or empty-space assumptions, instead leveraging external geometric scaffolds, semantic masks, or visibility priors to anchor the optimization.

### [NeRV Training](10253-nerv-training.md)
NeRV training optimizes a continuous volumetric representation that decouples scene geometry (shape) from material appearance (reflectance) and lighting interactions (visibility) to enable efficient relighting and view synthesis. It differs from standard NeRF training by introducing a separate "visibility MLP" that predicts ray visibility and termination depth, supervised by consistency terms with the primary shape MLP rather than ground-truth labels, allowing the simulation of direct and one-bounce indirect illumination within the training loop.

### [Scene Representation and Architecture](11368-scene-representation-and-architecture.md)
Scene Representation and Architecture defines the neural network structures that map 6D input coordinates (3D position $\mathbf{x}$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$) to radiance field properties (density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$) [Parent]. While the canonical NeRF uses a simple two-MLP structure, this family encompasses modifications to network topology, input representations, and output heads to address challenges such as single-view synthesis, dynamic scenes, high-resolution rendering, and semantic editing. These architectures differ from their siblings by introducing specialized components like diffusion priors, multi-scale grids, or physical material decompositions to enhance reconstruction quality, efficiency, or controllability.

### [Acceleration and Sparse Structures](11405-acceleration-and-sparse-structures.md)
This family of methods accelerates NeRF training and rendering by reducing the volume of space queried by the neural network, thereby lowering memory usage and computational cost. It addresses the bottleneck where standard NeRFs sample uniformly across a large bounding box, evaluating the MLP in empty space or on transient objects. It sets itself apart from [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by modifying *where* samples are taken (spatial constraints) rather than *how* they are integrated, and from [Adaptive Ray Sampling](8870-adaptive-ray-sampling.md) by using static or pre-computed geometric priors (hulls, blocks) rather than dynamic error metrics during training.

### [Camera Pose and Extrinsic/Intrinsic Optimization](12051-camera-pose-and-extrinsic-intrinsic-optimization.md)
This family of methods addresses the problem of optimizing camera parameters (extrinsics and intrinsics) simultaneously with Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) weights, eliminating the reliance on pre-computed Structure-from-Motion (SfM) pipelines like COLMAP. By treating camera poses as learnable parameters, these methods enable 3D reconstruction from sparse or uncalibrated image sets. What sets this family apart from siblings like [Time-dependent NeRF with Continuous Camera Motion](12399-time-dependent-nerf-with-continuous-camera-motion.md) is the explicit optimization of discrete camera poses per image rather than continuous motion functions, and from [Camera Preconditioning](12545-camera-preconditioning.md) by focusing on the joint gradient-based update of poses and scene geometry rather than preconditioning the optimizer for faster convergence.

### [Monocular Depth Estimation Adaptation and Supervision](12353-monocular-depth-estimation-adaptation-and-supervision.md)
This family of methods integrates pre-trained Monocular Depth Estimation (MDE) networks into NeRF optimization to resolve the shape-radiance ambiguity inherent in implicit volume representations, particularly in sparse-view or few-shot settings. Unlike standard NeRF which relies solely on photometric consistency, these methods use MDE outputs as geometric priors to constrain ray sampling, guide volume rendering, or directly supervise the NeRF's depth output. They differ from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by introducing external geometric constraints, and from [Regularization and Loss Functions](8586-regularization-and-loss-functions.md) by specifically leveraging the semantic geometric understanding of MDE models rather than generic smoothness penalties.

### [NeRF Editing and Inpainting Procedures](12494-nerf-editing-and-inpainting-procedures.md)
NeRF Editing and Inpainting Procedures modify a pre-trained Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) to remove objects, change appearance, or fill occluded regions while maintaining multi-view consistency. Unlike standard training which optimizes for reconstruction of input views, these methods optimize for a target state defined by external priors (e.g., 2D inpainting, diffusion models, or semantic masks). They are distinguished from [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by their use of auxiliary guidance signals beyond photometric error, and from [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by their focus on localized or semantic modifications rather than global scene reconstruction.

### [FlipNeRF Training Pipeline](12501-flipnerf-training-pipeline.md)
FlipNeRF is a training procedure for few-shot novel view synthesis that enhances the mip-NeRF representation by introducing flipped reflection rays into the training loop. It distinguishes itself from standard NeRF pipelines by enforcing consistency between original and geometrically flipped rays through specific uncertainty-aware and bottleneck feature losses, rather than relying solely on color reconstruction.

### [MLI-NeRF](12709-mli-nerf.md)
MLI-NeRF is a two-stage optimization procedure that learns an intrinsic-aware Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) capable of novel view synthesis, relighting, and intrinsic decomposition (separating reflectance and shading) without relying on statistical priors or ground-truth intrinsic labels. It differs from standard NeRF variants by explicitly modeling multiple light sources to generate physics-based pseudo-labels, and it differs from prior intrinsic NeRF methods (like [IntrinsicNeRF](10253-nerv-training.md)) by leveraging 3D geometric constraints and multi-light visibility rather than chromaticity or semantic statistical constraints. The method solves the ill-posed nature of inverse rendering by decoupling the geometry learning (Stage 1) from the intrinsic component separation (Stage 2), using pseudo-labels derived from the Stage 1 model to guide the decomposition.

### [Diffusion-guided Optimization](13230-diffusion-guided-optimization.md)
Diffusion-guided optimization methods train Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) by leveraging pretrained 2D or 3D diffusion models as priors to guide the optimization of 3D geometry and texture. These methods solve the problem of 3D data scarcity and underconstrained reconstruction by distilling generative knowledge from 2D image distributions into 3D scene representations. They set themselves apart from standard NeRF training by backpropagating gradients from a frozen or jointly trained diffusion model through a differentiable renderer, rather than relying solely on photometric reconstruction losses from input images.

### [Application-Specific Methods](13294-application-specific-methods.md)
Application-Specific Methods adapt the general NeRF training loop to solve domain-specific inverse problems by modifying the neural field architecture, loss functions, and optimization strategy. Unlike standard NeRF, which optimizes a single MLP for photometric reconstruction, these pipelines decompose fields to isolate physical properties, enforce domain priors (geometric, semantic, or physical), and employ iterative multi-stage optimization to handle complex constraints.

### [Cross-view Self-enhancement](13434-cross-view-self-enhancement.md)
Cross-view Self-enhancement (CSE) is a training strategy for semantic NeRFs that replaces view-inconsistent 2D semantic labels (e.g., from CLIP) with 3D-consistent pseudo-maps rendered from the model itself. It addresses the ambiguity where 2D vision-language models fail to maintain consistent object identities across diverse perspectives, which degrades multi-view consistent rendering. Unlike methods that rely solely on external supervision, CSE leverages the inherent 3D consistency of NeRFs to iteratively refine and synthesize semantic supervision, ensuring that the semantic field learns coherent structures across all views.

### [PlanarNeRF](13791-planarnerf.md)
PlanarNeRF is an online learning framework for detecting dense 3D planar primitives from monocular RGB and depth sequences. It combines plane segmentation rendering, an efficient plane fitting module, and a global memory bank to learn plane primitives in an online fashion using neural field representation, appearance, and geometry knowledge. It distinguishes itself from standard NeRF training loops by explicitly modeling planar instances via a dedicated rendering branch and maintaining a global memory bank for consistent plane tracking across views.

### [Progressive Camera Placement for NeRF](13844-progressive-camera-placement-for-nerf.md)
This method is a greedy optimization algorithm that selects camera positions to maximize a reconstruction quality metric $E(C, B_{32})$, which combines observation frequency and angular uniformity, to improve NeRF reconstruction quality with fewer images. Unlike uncertainty-based methods (e.g., ActiveNeRF), this method does not require additional image acquisition to estimate reconstruction quality, making it cheaper to compute. It builds on [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by dynamically selecting the next best view based on the current state of the occupancy grid and camera set.

### [Neural Refractive Radiance Field (NeRRF)](13882-neural-refractive-radiance-field-nerrf.md)
Neural Refractive Radiance Field (NeRRF) is an inverse rendering method for transparent scenes that reconstructs surface geometry and a radiance field from multi-view images without prior knowledge of the object's Index of Refraction (IOR). It solves the problem of incorrect radiance field learning and visual artifacts (such as holes and color errors) that occur when standard NeRF or SDF-based methods like NeuS attempt to model refraction with a fixed or unknown IOR. NeRRF distinguishes itself by automatically searching for the optimal IOR during training and correcting the calculation of refractive effects, specifically enabling accurate rendering of foreground darkening caused by refraction at object edges.

### [NeRF-Neural ODE](13904-nerf-neural-ode.md)
NeRF-Neural ODE replaces the discrete quadrature summation of standard NeRF with a continuous Neural Ordinary Differential Equation (Neural ODE) solver to integrate radiance and transmittance along rays. It differs from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by treating the ray integration as a continuous dynamical system solved via adaptive ODE solvers rather than fixed-step numerical integration.

### [OV-NeRF](13995-ov-nerf.md)
OV-NeRF is a method for accurate NeRF-based open-vocabulary 3D semantic understanding that leverages pre-trained vision and language foundation models (CLIP and SAM) to enhance semantic field learning through single-view and cross-view strategies. It addresses the limitations of prior methods (e.g., LERF, 3DOVS) that directly use noisy and view-inconsistent CLIP relevancy maps for training. OV-NeRF introduces Region Semantic Ranking (RSR) regularization to rectify coarse boundaries in single views using SAM mask proposals, and Cross-view Self-enhancement (CSE) to enforce view-consistent supervision using refined 3D semantics from the trained field itself.

### [LOLNeRF](14071-lolnerf.md)
LOLNeRF is a method for single-shot view synthesis of human faces that learns a generative 3D model from single-view images without relying on explicit geometric information like depth or point clouds. It differs from adversarial approaches like $\pi$-GAN by using Generative Latent Optimization (GLO) to learn a per-image table of latent codes, allowing it to reconstruct specific training images with high fidelity while avoiding the resolution limits imposed by GAN discriminators.

### [Robustness and Generalization](14094-robustness-and-generalization.md)
This family encompasses techniques that improve the stability of NeRF optimization against noise and the ability of the resulting model to synthesize novel views from limited or unconstrained data. It addresses the generalization gap where standard optimization yields inconsistent geometries or fails on scenes with transient objects and lighting variations. Methods in this family differ from standard training by injecting adversarial perturbations [Aug-NeRF](10294-aug-nerf.md), decomposing scene appearance into static and per-image latent codes [NeRF in the Wild](13541-nerf-in-the-wild.md), enforcing cross-view geometric consistency via contrastive learning [ContraNeRF](13659-contranerf.md), or using post-processing reprojection to restore high-frequency details [RoGUENeRF](13731-roguenerf.md).

### [FusedRF](14268-fusedrf.md)
FusedRF is a method to fuse multiple teacher Radiance Fields (RFs) into a single, compact student RF representation using distillation, allowing for composited scene rendering with the memory and time complexity of a single RF. Unlike naive composition which requires parallel tracing into multiple RFs (increasing memory and render time linearly with scene count), FusedRF fuses them into one RF, maintaining constant memory and render time. Unlike retraining from scratch using rendered RGB views, FusedRF uses 3D distillation from existing RFs, reducing training time by 3x.

### [MEIL-NeRF](14629-meil-nerf.md)
MEIL-NeRF is a memory-efficient incremental learning algorithm for Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) that prevents catastrophic forgetting without increasing memory usage by treating the NeRF network itself as a memory storage for pixel RGB values. Unlike replay-based methods that suffer from monotonically increasing memory due to storing past data, MEIL-NeRF maintains constant memory consumption by using a Ray Generator Network (RGN) to retrieve past task information from the NeRF network itself, rather than storing raw data or exemplars. It builds on [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by introducing a self-distillation mechanism guided by generated rays.

### [NeRF-HuGS](14802-nerf-hugs.md)
NeRF-HuGS is a training procedure for Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) that mitigates artifacts caused by transient distractors (e.g., moving objects, shadows) in non-static scenes. It solves the problem of distinguishing static geometry from transient elements by combining hand-crafted heuristics with the Segment Anything Model (SAM). Unlike prior segmentation-based methods that require semantic class priors or heuristic-only methods that miss low-frequency static details, HuGS fuses Structure-from-Motion (SfM) features (high-frequency textures) and color residuals from a partially trained NeRF (low-frequency textures) to guide SAM in generating precise static masks.

### [SGCNeRF](14984-sgcnerf.md)
SGCNeRF is a few-shot neural rendering architecture for novel view synthesis that addresses the performance decline of standard frequency regularization in high-frequency regions (rich textures or geometry variations) where methods like FreeNeRF suffer from excessive smoothing. It distinguishes itself by integrating a feature-matching-based sparse geometry regularization module with spatially consistent geometry filtering and frequency-guided geometric regularization, thereby preserving high-frequency details while preventing overfitting.

### [NeRF-Det Training Objectives](14994-nerf-det-training-objectives.md)
NeRF-Det training objectives jointly optimize a 3D object detection branch and a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) branch end-to-end, using the NeRF branch to learn a geometry-aware volumetric representation that mitigates the ambiguity inherent in explicit depth or cost-volume methods. Unlike sibling methods that rely on per-scene optimization or separate pre-training stages, this approach trains a generalizable network where the NeRF branch provides multi-view consistency cues to the detector without requiring per-scene tuning at inference time.

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S23]. For every pixel, multiple jittered rays are sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel. Each ray is rendered with NeRF, and the mean of those rendered values is used as the predicted color in the loss function [S23]. This baseline relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets [S23]. Performance-wise, the 16x supersampling baseline performs worse than mip-NeRF and is significantly more expensive during both training and evaluation [S23].

### Two-stage training for proposers
A training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S24, unofficial]. Phase 1 trains the learnt proposer to mimic the heuristic proposer by minimizing the L2 matching loss between their proposal sets [S24, unofficial]. Phase 2 trains all network components end-to-end. At the start of this phase, the optimizer state is reset and a linear warmup for 1,000 steps is performed to reduce shock from switching regimes [S24, unofficial]. Training from scratch sometimes works but often fails; two-stage training is consistently effective [S24, unofficial].

### JaxNeRF
A re-implementation of the original NeRF in JAX, used as a benchmark comparison for early works seeking to improve on training and rendering speed [S25]. It is slightly faster and more suited for distributed computing than the original TensorFlow implementation of NeRF [S25].

### Gradient Detachment in Training
A training strategy where the object field component is detached from the backbone to prevent gradient backpropagation to the rendering part, ensuring independent optimization [S26, unofficial]. With detach, gradients do not backpropagate to the NeRF backbone; without detach, gradients from the object field component backpropagate to the NeRF backbone, causing mutual influence [S26, unofficial]. With detach achieves better scene rendering and decomposition quality, while without detach causes a significant drop in rendering quality [S26, unofficial]. The hyper-parameter $\Delta d$ for empty 3D point losses ($\ell_{3d \text{ empty}} + \ell_{3d \text{ obj}}$) is tested at 0.025, 0.05, 0.10 meters, with 0.05 meters found optimal [S26, unofficial].

### Ray Generator Network (RGN)
Used in MEIL-NeRF for incremental learning of NeRF [S27]. During training on task $T$, the RGN $f_T^{RGN}$ learns to map past principal rays from $f_{T-1}^{RGN}$ and current GT principal rays [S27]. During inference, a number $x \in [0, 1]$ is randomly selected to generate a principal ray using RGN. Using camera intrinsic $K$, non-principal rays are randomly generated around the principal rays, forming a cone emitted from the camera center covering the region of the image plane [S27]. The network architecture consists of 3 hidden layers with 16, 64, and 32 hidden units respectively [S27]. Estimated camera poses accumulate errors over tasks, but the generated approximate rays still effectively direct to the scene of interest near past task cameras, so exact reconstruction of past rays is not required [S27]. Unlike methods that reproduce exact past rays, RGN creates approximate rays directed to the scene of interest, allowing errors under a certain level to reduce the learning burden at a small accuracy cost [S27].

### DFF
A method adopting pre-trained feature extraction networks as teacher networks for knowledge distillation, resulting in a scene-specific 3D feature field, enabling extraction and deletion of target objects based on image patches or text [S28]. It adopts pre-trained feature extraction networks (LSeg [115] and DINO [116]) as teacher networks, performs knowledge distillation, and results in a scene-specific 3D feature field [S28].

### ISRF
A method that learns additional feature fields to realize self-supervised 3D segmentation [S28].

### NeRF Volumetric Rendering
Final pixel color and depth are produced via volumetric rendering: $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i$, $D(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} T_i \alpha_i t_i$. Where $T_i = \exp(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta t)$, $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta t)$, $t_i$ is distance of $i$-th point from camera, $\delta t$ is distance between sampled points [S29].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot C)$, where $C$ is the cost of the neural network forward pass. The total number of rays processed is $N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand}$ [S5, S15, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot D)$, where $D$ is the dimensionality of the network activations. VRAM usage is managed by chunking rays [S5, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** In the original NeRF implementation, training on Blender synthetic data (800x800) takes approximately 5-10 hours on a single GPU (e.g., RTX 2080 Ti) for 100,000 iterations [S5, S11, unofficial]. On LLFF with 3 views, increasing the number of target rays from 50 to 200 degrades performance (PSNR 20.33 to 20.15) [S21].
*   **Scaling:** Scaling to larger scenes requires sparse structures or hierarchical representations to avoid querying empty space [11405-acceleration-and-sparse-structures.md].
*   **Strengths:** Simple to implement, differentiable end-to-end, produces high-quality novel views.
*   **Limitations:** Slow training due to repeated MLP evaluations, prone to overfitting and "floaters" in sparse views, requires careful tuning of hyperparameters [S1][S18, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high-fidelity novel view synthesis is required and sufficient computational resources are available.
*   **When not to use:** When real-time training or inference is required, or when data is extremely sparse and specialized initialization is needed [9394-sparse-view-and-initialization-methods.md].

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop was introduced by Mildenhall et al. in "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" (2020) [S5, unofficial]. It established the standard approach for training continuous volumetric representations using stochastic gradient descent and volume rendering. The method has since become the foundation for numerous extensions, including mip-NeRF, Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (NGP), and various sparse-view and dynamic scene variants [S11, S16, unofficial].
