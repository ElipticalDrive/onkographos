# NeRF in the Wild

Also called: NeRF-W, Neural Radiance Fields for Unconstrained Photo Collections

## Overview
NeRF in the Wild (NeRF-W) extends the standard NeRF architecture to handle unconstrained internet photo collections by relaxing the strict consistency assumptions of static scenes and controlled lighting [S1]. It disentangles scene content into shared static geometry and image-dependent transient objects (e.g., pedestrians) and photometric variations (e.g., lighting changes, auto-exposure) using per-image appearance embeddings and a secondary volumetric radiance field combined with a data-dependent uncertainty field [S1, S2]. Unlike its siblings, [Neural Pre-Integrated Lighting (Neural-PIL)](13783-neural-pre-integrated-lighting-neural-pil.md) which focuses on pre-integrated lighting, and [Spherical Harmonics NeRF](14480-spherical-harmonics-nerf.md) which uses spherical harmonics for view-dependent appearance, NeRF-W explicitly models transient occluders and heteroscedastic observation noise to enable robust novel view synthesis from unstructured data [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** 3D position $\mathbf{x}$, viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$, per-image appearance embedding $\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}$, and per-image transient embedding $\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}$.
*   **Outputs:** Static color $\mathbf{c}^{(s)}$, static density $\sigma^{(s)}$, transient color $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}$, transient density $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}$, and uncertainty variance $\beta_i^2$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Appearance Embedding:** A real-valued vector $\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n^{(a)}}$ assigned to each training image $I_i$.
*   **Transient Embedding:** A real-valued vector $\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n^{(\tau)}}$ assigned to each training image $I_i$.
*   **Networks:**
    *   $MLP_{\theta_1}$: Static geometry network. Inputs: $\mathbf{x}$. Outputs: Latent feature $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})$ and static density $\sigma^{(s)}(\mathbf{x})$.
    *   $MLP_{\theta_2}$: Static color network. Inputs: $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})$, positional encoded direction $\gamma(\mathbf{d})$, appearance embedding $\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}$. Outputs: Static color $\mathbf{c}^{(s)}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$.
    *   $MLP_{\theta_3}$: Transient network. Inputs: $\mathbf{x}$, $\gamma(\mathbf{d})$, transient embedding $\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}$. Outputs: Transient density $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}(\mathbf{x})$ and transient color $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$.
    *   $MLP_{\theta_4}$: Uncertainty network. Inputs: $\mathbf{x}$, $\gamma(\mathbf{d})$. Outputs: Variance $\beta_i^2(\mathbf{x})$.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:** Initialize networks $MLP_{\theta_1}, MLP_{\theta_2}, MLP_{\theta_3}, MLP_{\theta_4}$ with random weights. Initialize appearance embeddings $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}\}_{i=1}^N$ and transient embeddings $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}\}_{i=1}^N$ for all $N$ training images.
2.  **Sampling:** For each training image $I_i$, sample a batch of rays $\mathbf{r}_{ij}$. For each ray, sample $K$ quadrature points $t_k$ along the ray segment.
3.  **Static Geometry Prediction:** For each point $t_k$ on ray $\mathbf{r}_{ij}$:
    *   Compute static latent feature $\mathbf{z}(t_k) = MLP_{\theta_1}(t_k)$.
    *   Compute static density $\sigma^{(s)}(t_k) = \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{z}(t_k))$ (Note: Source [S1] implies ReLU for density, [S3, unofficial] specifies Sigmoid for transient sigma, but standard NeRF/W uses ReLU for static sigma to ensure non-negativity; [S1] text says "ReLU and sigmoid activations are used for $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t)$ and $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}(t)$", implying static sigma uses ReLU or similar non-negative activation).
4.  **Static Color Prediction:**
    *   Compute static color $\mathbf{c}^{(s)}(t_k) = MLP_{\theta_2}(\mathbf{z}(t_k), \gamma(\mathbf{d}), \mathbf{l}_i^{(a)})$.
5.  **Transient Prediction:**
    *   Compute transient density $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) = \text{ReLU}(MLP_{\theta_3}^{\sigma}(t_k, \gamma(\mathbf{d}), \mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}))$.
    *   Compute transient color $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) = \text{Sigmoid}(MLP_{\theta_3}^{c}(t_k, \gamma(\mathbf{d}), \mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}))$.
6.  **Uncertainty Prediction:**
    *   Compute variance $\beta_i^2(t_k) = \text{Softplus}(MLP_{\theta_4}(t_k, \gamma(\mathbf{d}))) + \beta_{min}$.
7.  **Volumetric Rendering (Coarse):**
    *   Render coarse color $\hat{C}_c^i(\mathbf{r}_{ij})$ using only the static component and appearance embedding (as per [S1] Section 4.3, the coarse model uses only the latent appearance modeling component).
    *   $\hat{C}_c^i(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_c} T_k^{(s)} \alpha_k^{(s)} \mathbf{c}^{(s)}(t_k)$, where $\alpha_k^{(s)} = 1 - \exp(-\sigma^{(s)}(t_k)\delta_k)$ and $T_k^{(s)} = \prod_{j=1}^{k-1} (1 - \alpha_j^{(s)})$.
8.  **Volumetric Rendering (Fine/Composite):**
    *   Compute transmittance $T_i(t_k)$ accounting for both static and transient densities:
        $$ T_i(t_k) = \exp\left( -\sum_{k'=1}^{k-1} (\sigma^{(s)}(t_{k'}) + \sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_{k'})) \delta_{k'} \right) $$
    *   Compute opacity for static and transient components:
        $$ \alpha_k^{(s)} = 1 - \exp(-\sigma^{(s)}(t_k)\delta_k) $$
        $$ \alpha_k^{(\tau)} = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k)\delta_k) $$
    *   Render composite color $\hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r}_{ij})$:
        $$ \hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} T_i(t_k) \left( \alpha_k^{(s)} \mathbf{c}^{(s)}(t_k) + \alpha_k^{(\tau)} \mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) \right) $$
9.  **Loss Calculation:**
    *   Compute per-ray loss $L_i(\mathbf{r}_{ij})$:
        $$ L_i(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\|\mathbf{C}_i(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}_i(\mathbf{r})\|^2}{2\beta_i^2(\mathbf{r})} + \frac{\log \beta_i^2(\mathbf{r})}{2} + \frac{\lambda_u}{2K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sigma_i^{(\tau)}(t_k) $$
        where $\mathbf{C}_i(\mathbf{r})$ is the ground truth pixel color.
    *   Compute total loss for the batch, including the coarse rendering term:
        $$ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i,j} \left( L_i(\mathbf{r}_{ij}) + \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{C}_i(\mathbf{r}_{ij}) - \hat{C}_c^i(\mathbf{r}_{ij})\|^2 \right) $$
10. **Optimization:**
    *   Update network parameters $\theta$ and embeddings $\{\mathbf{l}_i^{(a)}\}, \{\mathbf{l}_i^{(\tau)}\}$ using Adam optimizer.
11. **Test-Time Evaluation:**
    *   For a test image, optimize a single appearance embedding $\mathbf{l}^{(a)}$ using only the left half of the image to avoid information leakage.
    *   Render novel views using only the static component ($\sigma^{(s)}$ and $\mathbf{c}^{(s)}$), omitting the transient head and uncertainty fields.

**Parameters:**
*   $\beta_{min}$: Hyperparameter $> 0$ added to the softplus output for variance to ensure minimum importance is assigned to each ray [S1].
*   $\lambda_u$: Multiplier for the L1 regularization on transient density [S1].
*   $n^{(a)}$: Dimensionality of appearance embedding [S1].
*   $n^{(\tau)}$: Dimensionality of transient embedding [S1].
*   Batch size: 2048 [S1].
*   Training steps: 300,000 [S1].
*   Optimizer: Adam ($\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999$) [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Information Leakage:** When evaluating on test sets, optimizing the appearance embedding on the entire test image can lead to overfitting and leakage. The method specifies optimizing on the left half and evaluating on the right half [S1].
*   **Transient vs. Static Disentanglement:** The L1 regularization on transient density ($\lambda_u$) is critical to prevent the model from using transient density to explain away static phenomena (e.g., shadows or texture) [S1].
*   **Test-Time Rendering:** At test time, the transient and uncertainty fields are omitted. Rendering them would introduce noise and artifacts as they are not optimized for novel views without corresponding training embeddings [S1].

## Subtopics

### Transient Filtering via Candidate Head
An architectural variant (often associated with UP-NeRF) that uses a candidate head to predict transient properties (color, density, beta) alongside static properties, enabling robust pose optimization by filtering out transient occluders [S3, unofficial]. It predicts transient color $\mathbf{c}_i^{(\tau)}$, transient sigma $\sigma_i^{(\tau)}$, and beta $\beta_i$ using predicted candidate feature $\mathbf{f}_i$ and candidate embedding $\mathbf{l}_i^{(c)}$ [S3, unofficial]. It differs from NeRF-W by isolating the transient network and using deep features (DINO) for candidate filtering, whereas NeRF-W does not isolate the transient network and relies on different architecture assumptions [S3, unofficial]. A key pitfall is that merging transient and candidate heads degrades performance; separating the transient head is necessary, as failing to do so causes the model to fail in estimating correct poses and reduces rendering quality [S3, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time and Memory Complexity:** The method requires optimizing additional embeddings for each image ($N$ images $\times$ ($n^{(a)} + n^{(\tau)}$) parameters), increasing memory usage compared to standard NeRF. The computational cost is higher due to the additional MLPs for transient and uncertainty prediction and the composite rendering of static and transient components.
*   **Measured Performance:** NeRF-W improves over the previous state of the art (NRW) by an average margin of 4.4dB in PSNR across 6 scenes and achieves up to 40% improvements in MS-SSIM [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles variable illumination, auto-exposure, and transient occluders (pedestrians) in unconstrained photo collections. Produces temporally consistent novel view renderings [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of hyperparameters ($\lambda_u, \beta_{min}$). Performance on perceptual metrics (LPIPS) is competitive but not always superior to baselines that use perceptual losses, as NeRF-W minimizes per-pixel squared error [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Ideal for novel view synthesis from unstructured internet photo collections of landmarks or scenes with significant lighting changes and moving objects [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** Not suitable for strictly controlled, static scenes where standard NeRF is sufficient and more efficient.

## Background
NeRF in the Wild (NeRF-W) was introduced by Mildenhall et al. in 2021 [S1]. It addresses the limitations of the original NeRF, which assumes a static scene and controlled lighting, making it unsuitable for real-world "in-the-wild" photo collections [S1]. The method builds on the framework of Generative Latent Optimization (GLO) for appearance modeling and Bayesian learning for uncertainty estimation [S1]. It has been applied to cultural landmarks such as the Trevi Fountain, Sacre Coeur, Brandenburg Gate, Taj Mahal, Prague Old Town Square, and Hagia Sophia [S1].
