# NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)

Also called: NeRF Integration, NeRF Rendering Equation, Neural Radiance Fields rendering, Alpha Compositing in NeRF, Standard NeRF

## Overview
NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing) is the differentiable forward pass that synthesizes a pixel's color and depth by integrating the volume density and emitted radiance predicted by a neural network along a camera ray [S1, S2, S3]. It solves the problem of mapping a continuous 5D implicit function (mapping spatial coordinates and view directions to density and color) to a 2D image by discretizing the ray integral [S12, S13]. It sets itself apart from standard ray tracing by using a probabilistic density field to determine the expected color contribution of samples, handling view-dependent effects (non-Lambertian materials) natively, whereas standard photogrammetry often assumes diffuse reflection [S13]. Unlike [NeRF-style Volume Rendering Integrator](5964-nerf-style-volume-rendering-integrator.md), which may refer to the general mathematical framework, this method specifically denotes the implementation using the standard MLP-based NeRF architecture with stratified sampling and alpha compositing [S1, S2, S13].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of training images with known camera poses (extracted via COLMAP) [S13]; a neural network $F_\Theta$ (typically two MLPs or one multi-output MLP) parameterized by weights $\Theta$ [S1, S12]; ray parameters (origin $o$, direction $d$) for each pixel [S13]; sampling strategy parameters (number of samples $N$, near/far bounds $t_n, t_f$) [S2, S11].
*   **Outputs:** Rendered RGB color $\hat{C}(r)$ and expected depth $\hat{D}(r)$ for each ray $r$ [S7, unofficial][S15].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Ray Samples:** A set of $N$ points $t_i$ along the ray $r(t) = o + td$. In standard stratified sampling, the interval $[t_n, t_f]$ is divided into $N$ bins, and one sample is drawn uniformly from each bin [S2, S12, S13].
*   **Network Output:** For each sample point $x_i = o + t_i d$ and direction $d$, the network outputs a vector containing volume density $\sigma_i$ and RGB color $c_i = [R, G, B]$ [S1, S12]. The density $\sigma_i$ is typically constrained to be non-negative (e.g., via ReLU) [S9][S8, unofficial]. The color is often constrained to $[0, 1]$ (e.g., via Sigmoid) [S9].
*   **Intermediate Values:**
    *   $\delta_i$: Distance between sample $i$ and $i+1$. For the last sample, $\delta_N$ is often set to a large value (e.g., $10^{10}$) to ensure the ray terminates [S7, unofficial][S9].
    *   $\alpha_i$: Opacity of sample $i$, computed as $1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ [S2, S9].
    *   $T_i$: Transmittance up to sample $i$, computed as $\exp(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_i)$ or $\prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)$ [S1, S2, S9].
    *   $w_i$: Weight of sample $i$, computed as $\alpha_i T_i$ [S9][S8, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Ray Generation:** For each pixel in the target image, generate a camera ray $r(t) = o + td$ using the camera's intrinsic and extrinsic parameters [S1, S4, S13].
2.  **Sampling:** Sample $N$ points along the ray within the bounds $[t_n, t_f]$. The original NeRF implementation uses non-deterministic stratified sampling: divide the ray into $N$ equally spaced bins and draw a sample uniformly from each bin [S2, S12, S13].
3.  **Feature Query:** For each sample point $x_i$ and ray direction $d$, apply positional encoding $\gamma(\cdot)$ to the coordinates and direction, then pass them through the neural network $F_\Theta$ to predict density $\sigma_i$ and color $c_i$ [S1, S12, S17].
4.  **Opacity Calculation:** Compute the opacity $\alpha_i$ for each sample:
    $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i) $$
    where $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ is the distance between adjacent samples [S2, S9].
5.  **Transmittance Calculation:** Compute the accumulated transmittance $T_i$ for each sample. To prevent numerical instability (NaNs) during gradient computation, a small epsilon $\epsilon$ (typically $10^{-10}$) is added to $(1 - \alpha_j)$ before the cumulative product [S1, S9]:
    $$ T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right) = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j + \epsilon) $$
    Note: $T_1 = 1.0$ [S9].
6.  **Weight Calculation:** Compute the weight $w_i$ for each sample:
    $$ w_i = \alpha_i T_i $$
    [S9][S8, unofficial].
7.  **Color Compositing:** Compute the final rendered color $\hat{C}(r)$ as the weighted sum of the sample colors:
    $$ \hat{C}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i c_i = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) c_i $$
    [S1, S2][S7, unofficial][S9].
8.  **Background Handling:** If a white background $c_{bg} = (1, 1, 1)$ is assumed, add the contribution of rays that pass through the scene without terminating:
    $$ \hat{C}_{final}(r) = \hat{C}(r) + \left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\right) c_{bg} $$
    [S1][S7, unofficial][S9].
9.  **Depth Estimation:** Compute the expected depth $\hat{D}(r)$ analogously to color, using the sample distances $t_i$ from the camera origin:
    $$ \hat{D}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i t_i $$
    [S1, S2][S7, unofficial][S9].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def render_nerf(rays, t_vals, model, white_bkgd=True, noise_std=0.0):
    """
    Render RGB and Depth for a batch of rays using NeRF alpha compositing.
    
    Args:
        rays: Tensor of shape (B, 3) containing ray origins and directions.
              Note: In practice, rays are often expanded to (B, H, W, 3) or similar.
              Here we assume a simplified batch of rays for clarity.
        t_vals: Tensor of shape (N,) containing sample distances along the ray.
        model: The NeRF MLP model.
        white_bkgd: Boolean, whether to composite against a white background.
        noise_std: Float, standard deviation of Gaussian noise added to sigma during training.
        
    Returns:
        rgb: Tensor of shape (B, 3) containing rendered RGB colors.
        depth: Tensor of shape (B,) containing rendered expected depths.
    """
    # 1. Expand t_vals to match ray batch dimensions if necessary
    # Assuming rays are (B, 3) and t_vals is (N,), we need to create points
    # For a full image, rays would be (B, H, W, 3) and t_vals (N,)
    # Here we assume a simplified case where we process rays in batches
    
    # 2. Sample points along the ray
    # rays_o: (B, 3), rays_d: (B, 3)
    rays_o, rays_d = rays[:, :3], rays[:, 3:6] # Assuming rays contains o and d
    
    # Create sample points: x = o + t * d
    # t_vals needs to be broadcasted to (B, N)
    t_vals_expanded = t_vals.unsqueeze(0).expand(rays_o.shape[0], -1) # (B, N)
    
    # Points: (B, N, 3)
    points = rays_o.unsqueeze(1) + t_vals_expanded.unsqueeze(2) * rays_d.unsqueeze(1)
    
    # 3. Query the model
    # Model expects (x, d) -> (sigma, rgb)
    # We need to repeat directions for each sample
    dirs = rays_d.unsqueeze(1).expand(-1, t_vals.shape[0], -1) # (B, N, 3)
    
    # Flatten for model input: (B*N, 3) and (B*N, 3)
    points_flat = points.reshape(-1, 3)
    dirs_flat = dirs.reshape(-1, 3)
    
    # Get predictions: (B*N, 4) -> (sigma, rgb)
    # Note: In original NeRF, positional encoding is applied inside the model or before
    # Here we assume the model handles it or it's pre-applied.
    # We split the output into sigma and rgb
    predictions = model(points_flat, dirs_flat) # (B*N, 4)
    
    # Reshape back to (B, N, 4)
    predictions = predictions.reshape(rays_o.shape[0], t_vals.shape[0], 4)
    
    # Slice into rgb and sigma
    rgb = predictions[..., :3] # (B, N, 3)
    sigma = predictions[..., 3] # (B, N)
    
    # Apply activation functions if not done in model
    # Original NeRF uses sigmoid for rgb and relu for sigma
    rgb = torch.sigmoid(rgb)
    sigma = torch.relu(sigma)
    
    # Add noise during training
    if noise_std > 0:
        sigma = sigma + torch.randn_like(sigma) * noise_std
        
    # 4. Compute deltas
    # delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i
    deltas = t_vals[1:] - t_vals[:-1] # (N-1,)
    
    # Append a large value for the last segment to ensure termination
    # delta_inf = 1e10
    delta_inf = torch.ones_like(deltas[:1]) * 1e10
    deltas = torch.cat([deltas, delta_inf], dim=0) # (N,)
    
    # Scale deltas by ray direction norm if directions are not unit length
    # In standard NeRF, directions are normalized, so norm is 1.
    # But to be safe:
    dir_norm = torch.norm(rays_d, dim=-1, keepdim=True) # (B, 1)
    deltas = deltas * dir_norm # (B, N)
    
    # 5. Compute alpha
    # alpha = 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * deltas) # (B, N)
    
    # 6. Compute transmittance
    # T_i = product_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - alpha_j)
    # Use cumprod with exclusive=True to start with T_1 = 1.0
    # Add epsilon for numerical stability
    epsilon = 1e-10
    transmittance = torch.cumprod((1.0 - alpha + epsilon), dim=-1, exclusive=True) # (B, N)
    
    # 7. Compute weights
    # w_i = alpha_i * T_i
    weights = alpha * transmittance # (B, N)
    
    # 8. Compute RGB
    # C(r) = sum(w_i * c_i)
    rgb_final = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * rgb, dim=-2) # (B, 3)
    
    # 9. Background handling
    if white_bkgd:
        # Sum of weights is the total opacity
        weights_sum = torch.sum(weights, dim=-1, keepdim=True) # (B, 1)
        # Add background contribution
        rgb_final = rgb_final + (1.0 - weights_sum) * torch.ones_like(rgb_final)
        
    # 10. Compute Depth
    # D(r) = sum(w_i * t_i)
    # t_vals needs to be broadcasted to (B, N)
    t_vals_expanded = t_vals.unsqueeze(0).expand(rays_o.shape[0], -1)
    depth_final = torch.sum(weights * t_vals_expanded, dim=-1) # (B,)
    
    return rgb_final, depth_final
```

**Parameters:**
*   $N$: Number of samples along the ray. Typical value: 64 for both coarse and fine networks [S11].
*   $t_n, t_f$: Near and far clipping planes. $t_n$ is typically the camera center or a small offset, $t_f$ is determined by the scene's bounding volume [S2].
*   $\epsilon$: Epsilon for numerical stability. Typical value: $10^{-10}$ [S1, S9].
*   $c_{bg}$: Background color. Typically white $(1, 1, 1)$ [S1][S7, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Numerical Stability:** If $\sigma_i \delta_i$ is very large, $\alpha_i$ approaches 1, and $T_i$ approaches 0. If not handled with epsilon, cumulative products can underflow to 0, causing NaNs in gradients [S1, S9].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The last sample's delta must be handled to ensure the ray terminates. Using a large value like $10^{10}$ ensures the final transmittance is effectively 0 [S7, unofficial][S9].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If the ray direction is not normalized, deltas must be scaled by the norm of the direction vector to represent true Euclidean distance [S7, unofficial].
*   **Memory:** Processing all rays simultaneously can exceed GPU memory. Batching rays (e.g., chunk size 32768) is recommended [S18, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Depth-Guided Sampling](7490-depth-guided-sampling.md)
Uses predicted depth maps to bias sample placement toward surfaces, reducing redundant sampling in free space.

### [Rendered Depth-Guided Warping](8893-rendered-depth-guided-warping.md)
Uses rendered depth to warp existing image data for consistency regularization, rather than guiding sampling.

### [Depth-Prior Guided Stratified Sampling](11292-depth-prior-guided-stratified-sampling.md)
Replaces uniform sampling with depth-prior guided stratified sampling to focus on likely surfaces.

### [Neus SDF Volume Rendering](12443-neus-sdf-volume-rendering.md)
Uses an SDF-based weighting scheme to concentrate weight at the zero-level set, ensuring precise surface extraction.

### [Progressive Blur Estimation](14151-progressive-blur-estimation.md)
Models blur as a distribution of rays, using a two-stage module to predict kernel locations and blending weights.

### [MipNerfModel](14207-mipnerfmodel.md)
Treats rays as cones to approximate integrated positional encoding, solving aliasing artifacts.

### [Event Simulation from NeRF](15450-event-simulation-from-nerf.md)
Simulates asynchronous event streams from NeRF to supervise training with event camera data.

### NeRF Rendering Pipeline
Iterates over a set of render poses. For each pose, it calls a render function with specified height, width, focal length, and chunk size. If a render factor is specified, the image dimensions and focal length are downsampled. The rendered RGB and depth are appended to lists. If ground truth images are available and no downsampling is used, PSNR is computed. The final RGB images are converted to 8-bit and saved. [S18, unofficial]

### NeRF-XL Multi-GPU Volume Rendering
Scales NeRF rendering across multiple GPUs. It initializes global transmittance and color. For each segment assigned to a GPU, it gathers data from other GPUs if necessary, updates the global color and transmittance using alpha compositing, and passes the result to the next GPU. Data gathering is only required on a single GPU during inference to render the final image. [S19]

### NeRF Volume Rendering via Numerical Quadrature
Approximates the volume rendering integral by summing contributions from sampled spheres inside the cone using numerical quadrature. It computes photometric loss between rendered and captured colors to back-propagate gradients. [S20]

### Raymarcher
A rendering procedure that uses an RM-LSTM to predict a single intersection point and directly outputs RGB values, skipping volume rendering. It extracts latent features from a CNN encoder, feeds them into the RM-LSTM, which predicts step length and updates depth for a fixed number of steps. The final depth point is passed through the network to obtain RGB. It is significantly faster than baseline volume rendering but has slightly lower quality. [S21, unofficial]

### 2D Neural Rendering Module
Maps a low-resolution feature map to the final predicted image using upsampling layers and a 2D convolution. It repeats the input feature tensor, applies a learnable MLP, adds the results, applies PixelShuffle, and then applies a 2D convolution with a fixed blur kernel. It uses 1x1 convolutions to preserve geometric structure and reduce multi-view inconsistency. [S22]

### HeadNeRF Inference
Samples 64 points per ray, computes volume rendering to get a feature map, and passes it through the 2D neural rendering module to get the final image. It renders in real-time (>40 fps on NVIDIA 3090). [S22]

### Depth Estimation from NeRF
Extracts 3D point-cloud data by estimating depth where transmittance changes significantly. It minimizes the first-order derivative of the transmittance function $T(t)$ to find the surface depth $t_{surface}$, then calculates the 3D point and queries the color. [S23]

### PixelNeRF Rendering Module
Takes input images and a target camera pose to produce an approximate rendering. It passes input images through a 2D U-Net to create feature images, casts rays through the target image, samples points, reprojects them into input cameras, gathers features, and appends positional encoding and statistics. The output is a tensor of RGB and feature channels. [S24]

### NeRF Rendering with Stratified Sampling
Discretizes the integral using stratified sampling. It performs coarse sampling with uniform distribution to determine object positions, then fine sampling based on volume density weights to improve detail. The loss is the sum of squared errors for coarse and fine samples. [S25]

### Motion Blur Simulation via Pose Averaging
Models image motion blur by discretizing exposure time into evenly sampled poses, rendering a sharp image for each, and averaging them. It assumes uniform motion or uses linear interpolation for intermediate poses. [S11]

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P)$, where $N$ is the number of samples per ray and $P$ is the number of rays (pixels) [S1, S13]. The original NeRF required extremely high computing power and long training times due to the iterative nature of the MLP queries and the large number of samples [S13].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P)$ to store intermediate values (alpha, transmittance, weights) for all samples and rays [S18, unofficial]. This can be mitigated by batching rays [S18, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** The original NeRF implementation took approximately 0.177s per step on an RTX 2080Ti [S7, unofficial]. Variants like Raymarcher can achieve 34.9 fps on ShapeNet cars, which is ~80 times faster than baseline volume rendering (4.2 fps) but with slightly lower PSNR (23.02 vs 23.34) [S21, unofficial]. HeadNeRF renders in real-time (>40 fps on NVIDIA 3090) [S22].
*   **Scaling:** Scaling to multiple GPUs (NeRF-XL) reduces data transfer during inference by gathering data only on a single GPU [S19]. Multi-resolution hash encoding (Instant NGP) significantly reduces processing time [S13].
*   **Strengths:** Handles view-dependent effects (non-Lambertian materials) [S13]; synthesizes high-quality, photorealistic novel views with fine details [S13]; differentiable, allowing end-to-end training from images [S1, S12].
*   **Limitations:** High computational cost for training and rendering [S13]; neither a point cloud nor a mesh, consisting of continuous voxels, which affects 3D reconstruction interpretation [S13]; original implementation required long training times [S13].
*   **When to Use:** When high-quality novel view synthesis is required, especially for scenes with complex lighting and view-dependent effects [S13]. Suitable for cultural heritage reconstruction of challenging objects (metallic, translucent, transparent surfaces) [S13].
*   **When Not to Use:** When real-time performance is critical and the original NeRF is used without acceleration (e.g., Instant NGP) [S13]. When a explicit mesh or point cloud is required directly from the representation [S13].

## Background
NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) was first presented by Mildenhall et al. in 2020 [S13]. It represents a static scene as a continuous 5D function that outputs radiance and density [S12, S13]. The method was trained using a squared error photometric loss between rendered and ground truth colors [S12, S13]. Instant NGP by Müller et al. (2022) introduced multi-resolution hash encoding, significantly reducing processing time [S13]. NeRF is used in cultural heritage for image-based reconstruction of 3D models of real, multi-scale objects, especially for metallic, translucent, transparent surfaces, homogeneous textures, occlusions, vegetation, and fine details [S13]. It is implemented in platforms like Instant NGP, Nerfstudio, and Luma AI [S13].
