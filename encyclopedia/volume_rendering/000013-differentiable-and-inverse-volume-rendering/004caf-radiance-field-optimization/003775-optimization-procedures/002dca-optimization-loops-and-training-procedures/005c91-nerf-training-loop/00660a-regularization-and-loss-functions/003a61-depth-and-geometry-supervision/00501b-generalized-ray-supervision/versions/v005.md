# Generalized Ray Supervision

Also called: Ray Sensor Supervision, Arbitrary Ray Supervision

## Overview
Generalized Ray Supervision is a framework for optimizing neural scene representations by treating arbitrary rays in a scene as "ray sensors" that record incoming radiance, extending the standard NeRF approach which is limited to camera rays [S1]. It enables the use of non-camera rays, such as shadow rays, to supervise the optimization of density and geometry fields, leveraging the fact that each ray's 3D location and orientation are well-defined if the sensor and light source are known [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- A neural scene representation (e.g., density field $\sigma$).
- A set of "ray sensors" with known 3D positions and orientations.
- For shadow ray supervision: A known light source (direction or position) and an assumption that the scene does not emit light (only absorbs/scatters) [S1].

**Outputs:**
- Optimized neural scene parameters.

**Data Structures:**
- Ray definition: A ray is defined by an origin $\mathbf{o}$ and a direction $\mathbf{d}$.
- Sensor definition: A ray sensor is defined by its position $\mathbf{o}_s$ and the direction $\mathbf{d}_s$ from which it "receives" radiance (typically opposite to the light direction for shadow rays) [S1].

**Steps:**
1. **Define Ray Sensors:** Place hypothetical ray sensors at known 3D locations within or around the scene [S1].
2. **Generate Rays:** For each sensor, generate a ray based on the sensor's orientation and the light source configuration. For shadow rays, the ray originates from the light source (or travels from the light direction) and passes through the sensor location [S1].
3. **Simulate Radiance:** Compute the incoming radiance $C$ along the ray using the neural field. For shadow rays, assuming no self-emission, the radiance is the light source intensity attenuated by the transmittance along the ray [S1].
4. **Compute Loss:** Compare the simulated radiance $C$ with the ground truth radiance recorded by the sensor (if available) or use the consistency of the radiance field to update the neural field parameters [S1].
5. **Backpropagate:** Update the neural scene representation using gradients from the radiance loss [S1].

**Formula:**
For shadow ray supervision, the incoming radiance $C$ at a sensor is expressed using quadrature similar to NeRF's volume rendering equation, but without the emission term:
$$ C = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t) \sigma(t) L_{\text{light}} dt $$
where $T(t) = \exp(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(s) ds)$ is the transmittance, $\sigma(t)$ is the density, and $L_{\text{light}}$ is the light source intensity. In discrete form:
$$ C = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \tau_i L_{\text{light}} $$
where $T_i$ is the light transmittance up to sample $i$, and $\tau_i$ is the discrete opacity (probability of absorption) at sample $i$ [S1].

**Parameters:**
- Number of samples per ray: Not specified in sources.
- Light source intensity: Assumed known [S1].

**Edge Cases:**
- Unknown light source: The method requires a known light source for shadow ray supervision [S1].
- Sensor placement: Sensors must be placed at known locations; arbitrary placement without calibration is not supported [S1].

## Subtopics

### Shadow Ray Supervision
A specific application of generalized ray supervision using shadow rays (connecting scene and light source) to reconstruct neural scenes [S1]. It assumes the scene does not emit light, only absorbs light from the source, which is more physically correct and simplifies the process compared to NeRF [S1]. The incoming radiance is simulated using quadrature where $T_i$ is light transmittance and $\tau_i$ is discrete opacity [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of rays and samples per ray, similar to standard volume rendering [S1].
- **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the neural field size and the number of rays processed [S1].
- **Strengths:** Enables the use of additional geometric constraints from non-camera rays, potentially improving reconstruction quality in areas with limited camera coverage [S1].
- **Limitations:** Requires known light source and sensor positions, which may not be available in all scenarios [S1].

## Background
Generalized Ray Supervision was introduced in the context of ShadowNeuS, which extends the concept of ray supervision beyond camera rays to include shadow rays [S1]. The idea builds on the observation that NeRF's success relies on supervising camera rays, and this principle can be generalized to other types of rays if their geometry is known [S1].
