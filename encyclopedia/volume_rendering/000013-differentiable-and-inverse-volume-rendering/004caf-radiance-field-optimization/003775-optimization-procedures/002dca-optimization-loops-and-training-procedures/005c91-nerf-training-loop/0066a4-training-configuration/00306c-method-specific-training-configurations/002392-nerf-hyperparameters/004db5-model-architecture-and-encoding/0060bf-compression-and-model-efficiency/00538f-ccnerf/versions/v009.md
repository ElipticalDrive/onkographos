# CCNeRF

Also called: Compressible-Composable NeRF, Rank-residual Decomposition NeRF

## Overview
CCNeRF is a neural field representation that enables efficient compression and composition of 3D scenes by learning a hybrid tensor rank decomposition, eliminating the need for neural networks in the rendering pipeline [S1]. Unlike standard NeRF variants that rely on shared MLP renderers (e.g., NSVF) or hybrid tensor-MLP approaches (e.g., TensoRF), CCNeRF models the radiance field *entirely* with tensor rank decomposition, preserving low-rank approximation properties to allow dynamic model size adjustment via rank truncation and arbitrary scene composition via rank concatenation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   3D spatial coordinate $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
*   Viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$, typically represented as spherical coordinates $(\theta, \phi)$ or a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
*   Target feature dimension $F$ (for density and color).

**Outputs:**
*   Volume density $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}$.
*   Emitted color $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (RGB).

**Data Structures:**
The model consists of two main components:
1.  **Rank Weights Matrix ($W$):** A matrix of shape $(F, R_{total})$, where $F$ is the number of feature channels (e.g., 1 for density, 3 for color) and $R_{total} = R_{vec} + R_{mat}$ is the total rank. $W$ stores the importance weights for each rank component.
2.  **Rank Components:** A set of decomposed vectors or matrices representing the spatial basis functions.
    *   **Vector-based (CP) components:** Stored as factor matrices $U^x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times R_{vec}}$, $U^y \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times R_{vec}}$, $U^z \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times R_{vec}}$. These correspond to CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition.
    *   **Matrix-based (Triple Plane) components:** Stored as factor matrices $U^{xy} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times R_{mat}}$, $U^{xz} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times D \times R_{mat}}$, $U^{yz} \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times D \times R_{mat}}$. These correspond to a triple plane variant.
    *   The ratio of vector-based to matrix-based components is controllable.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Scene Representation (Training):**
    The 3D scene is modeled as a 4D feature volume. The method employs a **rank-residual learning strategy** during training. This strategy encourages lower-rank components to preserve the primary information of the scene, while higher-rank components capture residual details. This ensures that truncating higher ranks results in a near-optimal low-rank approximation.

2.  **Querying a 3D Coordinate:**
    To evaluate the field at coordinate $\mathbf{x}$:
    a.  **Projection:** Project $\mathbf{x}$ onto the stored rank components.
        *   For vector-based components (rank index $r < R_{vec}$): Compute the scalar product of the coordinate with the corresponding vectors: $v_r(\mathbf{x}) = U^x_{x, r} \cdot U^y_{y, r} \cdot U^z_{z, r}$.
        *   For matrix-based components (rank index $r \ge R_{vec}$): Compute the scalar product of the coordinate with the corresponding planes: $m_r(\mathbf{x}) = U^{xy}_{x,y, r} \cdot U^{xz}_{x,z, r} \cdot U^{yz}_{y,z, r}$ (Note: The source describes projecting to decomposed vectors or matrices; the specific interpolation formula for the triple plane variant involves combining the three plane projections).
    b.  **Weighted Interpolation:** Combine the projected values using the Rank Weights Matrix $W$. For a specific feature channel $f$ (e.g., density or one color channel):
        $$ \text{Feature}_f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{r=1}^{R_{total}} W_{f, r} \cdot \text{Component}_r(\mathbf{x}) $$
        where $\text{Component}_r(\mathbf{x})$ is the value obtained from either the vector-based or matrix-based projection for rank $r$.

3.  **Volume Rendering:**
    Sample points $x_i$ along a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ at positions $t_i$. Compute density $\sigma_i$ and color $\mathbf{c}_i$ for each point using the query method above. Render the pixel color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ using the standard volume rendering equation:
    $$ \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i $$
    where:
    *   $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ is the opacity, with $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$.
    *   $T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)$ is the transmittance.
    *   $\sigma_i$ is the density and $\mathbf{c}_i$ is the color at sample $i$.

4.  **Compression (Rank Truncation):**
    To reduce model size, perform an empirical sort-and-truncate strategy.
    a.  Sort the rank components based on their contribution (derived from the weights in $W$ or energy).
    b.  Truncate the highest-ranked components to reach a target rank $R_{target} < R_{total}$.
    c.  This allows dynamic trade-off between model size and rendering quality without retraining. Lower ranks preserve primary information due to the rank-residual learning strategy.

5.  **Composition (Rank Concatenation):**
    To compose multiple models (objects) into a single scene:
    a.  Concatenate the rank components of different models along the rank dimension.
    b.  Record a transformation matrix for each object to control its position and orientation.
    c.  Since there are no MLP renderers, different models can be arbitrarily composed without compatibility constraints or retraining.
    d.  To mitigate storage growth in complex scenes, compress less important objects by truncating their ranks before concatenation.

**Parameters:**
*   **Rank ($R$):** The number of components kept in the decomposition. Determines model size and detail level. Not given in the sources.
*   **Ratio of Vector-to-Matrix Components:** Controllable parameter to trade off between model size and performance. Not given in the sources.
*   **Number of Frequencies ($L$):** For positional encoding if used in preprocessing (though CCNeRF focuses on tensor decomposition, the parent article notes standard NeRF uses positional encoding; CCNeRF's specific encoding of coordinates into the tensor basis is implicit in the projection step). Not given in the sources.

**Pitfalls:**
*   **Truncated HOSVD:** Standard truncated Higher-Order SVD (HOSVD) does not hold the optimal property of truncated SVD, yielding a quasi-optimal solution. While sufficient for practical use, it may not be the absolute best low-rank approximation [S1].
*   **Composition Size:** Concatenating ranks increases storage linearly. Compression of unimportant objects is necessary to mitigate growth in composed scenes [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering is fast as it involves no neural network inference, only tensor contractions (dot products) and volume integration. Complexity depends on the number of samples $N$ and the total rank $R_{total}$.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Proportional to $R_{total} \times (\text{grid dimensions})$. Specifically, $O(R_{vec} \cdot (H \cdot W \cdot D \text{ for vectors? No, vectors are } H+W+D) + R_{mat} \cdot (H \cdot W + H \cdot D + W \cdot D))$. The source describes factor matrices $U^x, U^y, U^z$ (vectors) and $U^{xy}, U^{xz}, U^{yz}$ (matrices).
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Enables dynamic compression (LOD) without retraining.
    *   Allows arbitrary composition of models without retraining or compatibility constraints.
    *   No neural networks in the rendering path, reducing inference overhead.
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Relies on low-rank approximation; may struggle with highly complex, high-frequency scenes if the rank is too low.
    *   Quasi-optimal approximation via HOSVD may lose some detail compared to optimal methods (though sources state it is sufficient).
*   **When to use:** When scene manipulation (compression, composition, LOD) is required, and computational efficiency in rendering is prioritized over the expressive power of MLPs.
*   **When not to use:** When the scene has high-frequency details that cannot be captured by low-rank approximations, or when maximum possible rendering quality is needed regardless of model size/manipulability.

## Background
CCNeRF was introduced by Jiaxiang Tang, Xiaokang Chen, Jingbo Wang, and Gang Zeng from Peking University and Chinese University of Hong Kong in 2022 [S1]. It addresses the difficulty of manipulating implicit NeRF representations, which are often restricted by shared MLP renderers or suffer from large model sizes. The method is motivated by the low-rank approximation property of SVD and builds on tensor decomposition techniques like CP and Tucker decomposition, as well as prior work like TensoRF, but distinguishes itself by removing MLPs to preserve low-rank properties [S1].
