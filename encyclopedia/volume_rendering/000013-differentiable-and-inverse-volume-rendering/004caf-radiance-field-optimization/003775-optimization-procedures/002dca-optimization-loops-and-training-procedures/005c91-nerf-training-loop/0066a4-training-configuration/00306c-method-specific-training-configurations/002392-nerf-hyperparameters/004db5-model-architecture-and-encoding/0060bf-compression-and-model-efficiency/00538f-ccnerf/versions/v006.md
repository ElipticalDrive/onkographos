# CCNeRF
Also called: Rank-residual Decomposition NeRF

## Overview
CCNeRF is a neural field representation that enables efficient compression and composition of 3D scenes by learning a hybrid tensor rank decomposition of the scene without using neural networks for rendering [S1]. Unlike NeRF variants that rely on a shared MLP renderer (e.g., NSVF) or hybrid tensor-MLP approaches (e.g., TensoRF) which prevent direct compression or composition, CCNeRF models the field *only* with tensor rank decomposition, preserving low-rank approximation properties [S1]. This allows arbitrary composition of different models by concatenating rank components and dynamic adjustment of model size via rank truncation without retraining [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A set of 2D images with known camera poses (origin $o$ and direction $d$) used for training; a 3D coordinate $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ for inference.
*   **Output:** Volume density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c} = (r, g, b)$ for a given ray sample point.

**Data Structures:**
The model consists of two main parts [S1]:
1.  **Rank Weights Matrix ($W$):** A matrix storing weights for different feature channels. It has dimensions $R \times C$, where $R = R_{vec} + R_{mat}$ is the total rank (number of components) and $C$ is the number of feature channels (typically 3 for RGB density/color or separate channels for $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$).
2.  **Rank Components:** A set of decomposed vectors or matrices representing the spatial features. The ratio of vector-based (CP) to matrix-based (triple plane) components is controllable [S1].
    *   **Vector-based (CP):** Factor matrices $U^x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times R_{vec}}$, $U^y \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times R_{vec}}$, $U^z \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times R_{vec}}$.
    *   **Matrix-based (Triple Plane):** Factor matrices $U^{xy} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times R_{mat}}$, $U^{xz} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times D \times R_{mat}}$, $U^{yz} \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times D \times R_{mat}}$.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Training (Rank-Residual Learning):**
    The scene is modeled as a 4D feature volume. The network is trained to predict density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ using the standard volume rendering equation [S1]:
    $$ \hat{C}(r) = \sum_i T_i \alpha_i c_i $$
    where $T_i = \prod_{j<i} (1 - \alpha_i)$, $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$, and $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$. Here $\sigma_i$ is density, $c_i$ is color, and $t_i$ is the sample position along ray $r$ from origin $o$ with direction $d$.
    During training, a **rank-residual learning strategy** is employed. This encourages lower ranks to preserve more important information of the whole scene, ensuring that truncating higher ranks later yields a quasi-optimal low-rank approximation [S1].

2.  **Querying a 3D Coordinate (Inference):**
    To query a coordinate $\mathbf{x}$:
    a.  **Project:** Project $\mathbf{x}$ to the decomposed vectors or matrices.
        *   For vector components: Compute dot products $\mathbf{u}^x = U^x \mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{u}^y = U^y \mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{u}^z = U^z \mathbf{x}$ (conceptually, though typically these are lookup tables or grid interpolations in practice, the source describes projection) [S1]. *Note: The source describes "projecting" and "weighted interpolation". In a tensor decomposition context, this usually implies evaluating the tensor at $\mathbf{x}$ via the factor matrices.*
        *   For matrix components: Extract slices from $U^{xy}, U^{xz}, U^{yz}$ at the coordinates corresponding to $\mathbf{x}$.
    b.  **Interpolate:** Perform weighted interpolation using the stored rank weights $W$ and the projected features to obtain the raw feature vector $\mathbf{f}$ [S1].
    c.  **Decode:** Apply activation functions (e.g., ReLU for density, Sigmoid for color) to $\mathbf{f}$ to get $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$ [S1].

3.  **Compression (Rank Truncation):**
    Model size is dynamically adjusted by rank truncation. An empirical **sort-and-truncate** strategy is used [S1]:
    a.  Compute the importance of each rank component (e.g., based on the magnitude of weights in $W$ or reconstruction error).
    b.  Sort components by importance.
    c.  Keep the top $R'$ components and discard the rest.
    This achieves near-optimal low-rank approximation at any targeted rank, allowing trade-off between model size and rendering quality without retraining [S1].

4.  **Composition:**
    Different models are composed by concatenating their rank components along the rank dimension [S1].
    a.  For each object, a transformation matrix $M$ is recorded to control its position and orientation.
    b.  To render a composed scene, coordinates are transformed by $M$ for each object, and the features from each object's rank components are concatenated or summed (the source specifies concatenation along the rank dimension) [S1].
    c.  Unimportant objects in the composed scene can be compressed (via rank truncation) to mitigate storage cost growth [S1].

**Formulas:**
*   **Tucker Decomposition (Reference):** The source cites Tucker decomposition as a related concept: $T_{i,j,k} = \sum_{p=1}^P \sum_{q=1}^Q \sum_{r=1}^R S_{p,q,r} U^x_{i,p} U^y_{j,q} U^z_{k,r}$, where $S$ is the core tensor and $U^x, U^y, U^z$ are factor matrices [S1]. CCNeRF uses a hybrid of CP (vector) and this structure (matrix/triple plane).
*   **Volume Rendering:** $\hat{C}(r) = \sum_i T_i \alpha_i c_i$ [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Rank ($R$):** The number of components kept in the decomposition determines the model size and detail level. Lower ranks preserve primary information due to rank-residual learning [S1].
*   **Ratio of Vector-to-Matrix Components:** Controllable to trade off between model size and performance [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Truncation Optimality:** Standard truncated Higher-Order SVD (HOSVD) does not hold the optimal property of truncated SVD, but yields a quasi-optimal solution sufficient for practical use [S1].
*   **No Neural Networks:** The method explicitly avoids MLPs for rendering to enable composition. If MLPs were used, compression/composition would be hindered [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Querying is faster than MLP-based NeRF as it involves tensor operations (projections and interpolations) rather than forward passes through deep networks. Training requires optimization of the tensor factors.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Scales with the rank $R$ and the dimensions of the factor matrices. Compression reduces memory linearly with the retained rank.
*   **Strengths:** Enables dynamic compression (LOD) without retraining; allows arbitrary composition of different 3D models; no MLP overhead during rendering.
*   **Limitations:** Quasi-optimal truncation (not strictly optimal like SVD); requires careful tuning of the vector/matrix component ratio.
*   **When to use:** When scene manipulation (compression, composition, LOD) is required.
*   **When not to use:** When maximum single-view rendering quality is prioritized over manipulability, or when the scene does not benefit from low-rank assumptions.

## Background
CCNeRF was introduced by Jiaxiang Tang, Xiaokang Chen, Jingbo Wang, and Gang Zeng from Peking University and Chinese University of Hong Kong in 2022 [S1]. It addresses the difficulty of manipulating implicit NeRF representations, which are hard to compress or compose compared to explicit meshes. Previous attempts like NSVF were restricted by shared renderers, and TensoRF used MLPs that disturbed low-rank properties [S1]. CCNeRF builds on the concept of tensor rank decomposition (CP and Tucker) to provide a purely tensor-based representation that supports these manipulations [S1].
