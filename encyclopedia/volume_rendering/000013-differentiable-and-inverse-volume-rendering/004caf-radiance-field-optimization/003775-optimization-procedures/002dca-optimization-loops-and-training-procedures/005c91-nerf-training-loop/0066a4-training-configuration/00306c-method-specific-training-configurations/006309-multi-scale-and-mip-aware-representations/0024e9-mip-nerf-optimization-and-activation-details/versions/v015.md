# Mip-NeRF Optimization and Activation Details

Also called: JaxNeRF Training Procedure, Mip-NeRF Misc. Changes

## Overview
This article details the specific implementation optimizations, activation functions, and learning rate schedules used to train Mip-NeRF and JaxNeRF models [S1]. These changes are designed to improve training stability and robustness, particularly against catastrophic failure modes, without significantly altering the model's accuracy or computational speed [S1]. Unlike standard NeRF implementations, Mip-NeRF removes identity encoding from the MLP input and employs shifted/widened activation functions to handle the Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE) features more effectively [S1].

## How it works
The training procedure optimizes the parameters of the neural radiance field using the Adam optimizer. The method differs from the standard NeRF baseline in three key areas: input representation, activation functions, and learning rate scheduling [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** Training images (RGB), camera poses (intrinsics and extrinsics), and ray origins/directions.
*   **Outputs:** Rendered pixel colors $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ and their gradients with respect to the network parameters.

### Data Structures and Network Architecture
The MLP architecture follows the standard NeRF structure but with specific input/output handling:
1.  **Input Layer:** Receives only the Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE) of the 3D position and viewing direction. The concatenation of the raw position and view direction (identity encoding) is removed [S1].
2.  **Hidden Layers:** Standard MLP layers with ReLU activations (unless specified otherwise for density/color heads).
3.  **Density Head:** Predicts the density parameter $\tau$ (log-density).
4.  **View Direction Injection:** The viewing direction is processed separately and injected into the last layer before the color prediction [S1].
5.  **Color Head:** Predicts the color $\mathbf{c}$.

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Ray Sampling:** Generate rays from training images. For Mip-NeRF, rays are represented as cones (or frustums) rather than lines to handle anti-aliasing [S1].
2.  **Point Sampling:** Sample points along the rays. Mip-NeRF uses a proposal network to guide sampling, concentrating points in high-density regions [S1].
3.  **Feature Encoding (IPE):** Compute the Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE) for the sampled points. This involves integrating the standard positional encoding over the Gaussian distribution defined by the ray's cone at the sample point [S1].
4.  **Density Prediction:**
    *   Pass the IPE features through the MLP to get a raw output $x_\tau$.
    *   Apply the **shifted softplus** activation to predict $\tau$:
        $$ \tau = \log(1 + \exp(x_\tau - 1)) $$
    *   This function ensures $\tau$ is positive and initializes biases to -1, resulting in small initial density values [S1].
5.  **View Direction Handling:**
    *   Normalize the ray direction $\mathbf{d}$.
    *   Apply positional encoding with $L=4$ levels.
    *   Inject this encoded direction into the last layer of the MLP, after $\tau$ is predicted but before $\mathbf{c}$ is predicted [S1].
6.  **Color Prediction:**
    *   Pass the combined features (from step 4 and 5) through the final layer to get a raw output $x_c$.
    *   Apply the **widened sigmoid** activation to predict color $\mathbf{c}$:
        $$ \mathbf{c} = \frac{1 + 2\epsilon}{1 + \exp(-x_c)} - \epsilon $$
        where $\epsilon = 0.001$. This allows the network to saturate slightly outside the $[0, 1]$ range, preventing optimization failure when explaining pure black or white pixels [S1].
7.  **Volume Rendering:** Compute the accumulated color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ using the differentiable volume rendering integral [S1]:
    $$ \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i $$
    where $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$, $T_i = \exp(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j)$, and $\delta_i$ is the distance between samples. Note that $\sigma_i = \exp(\tau_i)$ [S1].
8.  **Loss Computation:** Calculate the photometric loss (typically MSE) between $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ and the ground truth image color.
9.  **Optimization:** Update parameters using the Adam optimizer with the specific learning rate schedule described below [S1].

### Learning Rate Schedule
The learning rate $\eta_i$ at iteration $i$ is computed using a warmup phase followed by logarithmic annealing [S1]:

$$ \eta_i = \left( \lambda_w + (1 - \lambda_w) \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} \operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{i}{n_w}, 0, 1\right)\right) \right) \times \exp\left( \left(1 - \frac{i}{n}\right) \log(\eta_0) + \frac{i}{n} \log(\eta_n) \right) $$

Where:
*   $n_w = 2500$ is the number of warmup iterations.
*   $n$ is the total number of training iterations.
*   $\eta_0 = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ is the initial learning rate.
*   $\eta_n = 5 \cdot 10^{-6}$ is the final learning rate.
*   $\lambda_w$ is a scale factor annealed from $0.01$ to $1$ during the warmup period. Specifically, $\lambda_w$ is not a constant in the formula above but is part of the warmup mechanism described in the source. The source states: "For the first $n_w = 2500$ iterations of optimization, we scale the basic learning rate by an additional scale factor that is smoothly annealed between $\lambda_w = 0.01$ and $1$ during this warm up period." The formula provided in the source [S1] uses $\lambda_w$ as the *current* value of this annealed factor. To implement this, one must define $\lambda_w(i)$:
    $$ \lambda_w(i) = 0.01 + (1 - 0.01) \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} \operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{i}{n_w}, 0, 1\right)\right) \quad \text{for } i < n_w $$
    And for $i \ge n_w$, the warmup term is effectively 1 (or the formula simplifies if $\lambda_w$ is treated as the multiplier). The source formula [S1] combines these. Let's clarify the implementation logic based on the source text:
    The source gives the formula for $\eta_i$ directly. It implies that during warmup ($i < n_w$), the term $(\lambda_w + (1 - \lambda_w) \sin(...))$ modulates the rate. However, the text says $\lambda_w$ is *annealed* from 0.01 to 1. The formula uses $\lambda_w$ as a variable. It is most likely that $\lambda_w$ in the formula represents the *current* annealed value.
    Let's re-read carefully: "we scale the basic learning rate by an additional scale factor that is smoothly annealed between $\lambda_w = 0.01$ and $1$... The learning rate at iteration $i$ ... is: [Formula]".
    This implies the formula *is* the definition of $\eta_i$. The variable $\lambda_w$ inside the formula must be the *current* value of the annealed factor.
    So, step 9 implementation:
    1. Calculate `warmup_factor`:
       If $i < n_w$: `warmup_factor = 0.01 + 0.99 * sin(pi/2 * clip(i/n_w, 0, 1))`
       Else: `warmup_factor = 1.0`
    2. Calculate `anneal_factor`:
       `anneal_factor = exp((1 - i/n) * log(eta_0) + (i/n) * log(eta_n))`
    3. `eta_i = warmup_factor * anneal_factor`
    
    *Correction*: The source formula [S1] is:
    $$ \eta_i = (\lambda_w + (1 - \lambda_w) \sin((\pi/2) \operatorname{clip}(i/n_w, 0, 1))) \times (\exp((1 - i/n) \log(\eta_0) + (i/n) \log(\eta_n))) $$
    The text says $\lambda_w$ is annealed from 0.01 to 1. This is slightly ambiguous. Does $\lambda_w$ in the formula refer to the *initial* value (0.01) or the *current* value?
    If $\lambda_w$ is constant 0.01, the term becomes $0.01 + 0.99 \sin(...)$. This starts at 0.01 and goes to 1.0. This matches the description "scale factor ... annealed between 0.01 and 1".
    Therefore, $\lambda_w$ in the formula is the **initial** lower bound (0.01), not a variable. The variable part is the sine term.
    So, $\lambda_w = 0.01$ (constant).
    The term $(\lambda_w + (1 - \lambda_w) \sin(...))$ evaluates to a value that starts at 0.01 and ends at 1.0 during warmup.
    After warmup ($i \ge n_w$), $\operatorname{clip}(i/n_w, 0, 1) = 1$, so $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$. The term becomes $\lambda_w + (1-\lambda_w) = 1$.
    So the formula works for all $i$.

### Parameters
*   **Optimizer:** Adam [S1].
*   **Total Iterations:** 1,000,000 [S1].
*   **Batch Size:** 4096 [S1].
*   **Initial Learning Rate ($\eta_0$):** $5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ [S1].
*   **Final Learning Rate ($\eta_n$):** $5 \cdot 10^{-6}$ [S1].
*   **Warmup Iterations ($n_w$):** 2500 [S1].
*   **Positional Encoding Levels for View Direction ($L$):** 4 [S1].
*   **Widened Sigmoid Epsilon ($\epsilon$):** 0.001 [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Catastrophic Failure (Negative Density):** Using standard ReLU for density can cause the MLP to emit negative values everywhere, leading to zero gradients and optimization failure. The shifted softplus prevents this [S1].
*   **Black/White Pixel Saturation:** Standard sigmoid saturation can cause optimization failure when trying to explain pure black or white pixels. The widened sigmoid avoids this by saturating slightly outside $[0, 1]$ [S1].
*   **Identity Encoding:** Including the raw position and view direction concatenated with the positional encoding is unnecessary and makes the formalization of IPE features challenging. Removing it simplifies the method without performance loss [S1].

### [Scale-Dependent Downweighting for Grid Interpolation](8571-scale-dependent-downweighting-for-grid-interpolation.md)
Scale-Dependent Downweighting is a technique to anti-alias trilinear interpolation in neural radiance fields by downweighting features interpolated from grid cells that are small relative to the sampling Gaussian's spread. Unlike naive trilinear interpolation which causes aliasing at high frequencies, this method uses the Gaussian CDF to weight samples, similar to Mip-NeRF's IPE features but applied to grid-based representations. See [Scale-Dependent Downweighting for Grid Interpolation](8571-scale-dependent-downweighting-for-grid-interpolation.md).

### [S-NeRF Training Configuration](15165-s-nerf-training-configuration.md)
S-NeRF training configuration defines the specific hyperparameters, network architectures, and confidence-based loss weighting used to optimize Neural Radiance Fields for dynamic street scenes using LiDAR and RGB data. It distinguishes itself from standard NeRF variants by employing a dual-phase training loop (foreground vehicles vs. background street views) and integrating geometry/projection confidence maps to mitigate depth outliers from moving objects. This configuration builds on [Mip-NeRF Optimization and Activation Details](3409-mip-nerf-optimization-and-activation-details.md) but adds specific LiDAR-guided confidence mechanisms and distinct MLP topologies for foreground/background separation. See [S-NeRF Training Configuration](15165-s-nerf-training-configuration.md).

### Alternative Gaussian Positional Encoding
An alternative to the Integrated Positional Encoding (IPE) is to concatenate the positional encodings of the mean and the (signed) square root of the covariance matrix elements as input to the MLP [S1]. Specifically, positional encoding with $L=12$ is applied to the mean $\mu$, and positional encoding with $L=2$ is applied to the vectorized upper triangular part of the signed covariance matrix $\Sigma$ [S1]. This approach performs comparably to IPE in accuracy but is sensitive to hyperparameters $L$ and covariance parameterization, whereas IPE is hyperparameter-free and compact [S1]. Additionally, IPE features are insensitive to off-diagonal elements of $\Sigma$ (correlation), but experiments show that modeling this correlation does not improve accuracy, suggesting it is not a helpful cue [S1].

### Reflected Cone Derivation
A geometric derivation used in Mip-NeRF to handle reflections. To ensure consistent view-dependent appearance, the origin of a reflected ray is shifted so that its cone radius matches the incident ray's cone radius at the intersection point [S2]. The condition for equating radii is:
$$ \dot{r}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{o}\| = \dot{r}'\|\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{o}'\| $$
where $\dot{r}$ is the incident ray radius parameter, $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is the intersection point, $\mathbf{o}$ is the incident ray origin, $\dot{r}'$ is the reflected ray radius parameter, and $\mathbf{o}'$ is the reflected ray origin [S2]. This is solved along with the constraint that $\mathbf{o}'$ lies on the reflected ray [S2].

### RawNeRF
RawNeRF adapts Mip-NeRF for High Dynamic Range (HDR) image view synthesis and denoising by using raw linear images as training data [S3]. It renders in a linear color space and supervises with variable exposure images, scaling the NeRF models’ “exposure” by the training image’s shutter speed, as well as a per-channel learned scaling factor [S3]. Unlike HDR-NeRF which uses LDR images, RawNeRF allows varying exposure and tone-mapping curves to be applied after NeRF rendering [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Training Speed:** Mip-NeRF is 13x faster to train than a brute-force supersampling baseline (16 rays/pixel) [S1].
*   **Evaluation Speed:** Mip-NeRF is 16x faster to evaluate than the supersampling baseline [S1].
*   **Quality:** Mip-NeRF outperforms the 16x supersampling baseline in quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) [S1].
*   **Stability:** The activation functions and learning rate schedule improve training stability, particularly with large learning rates, preventing catastrophic failure modes [S1].
*   **Memory:** The removal of identity encoding and use of IPE makes the feature representation more compact compared to concatenating mean and covariance encodings [S1].

## Background
Mip-NeRF was introduced by Barron et al. in 2021 [S1]. The optimization details described here are from the supplementary material of the Mip-NeRF paper, specifically section E ("Miscellaneous Changes") and section G ("Alternative Gaussian Positional Encoding") [S1]. The JaxNeRF codebase provided the default training procedure and warmup functionality used in these experiments [S1]. RawNeRF, a variant using raw images, was created by Mildenhall et al. in November 2021 [S3].
