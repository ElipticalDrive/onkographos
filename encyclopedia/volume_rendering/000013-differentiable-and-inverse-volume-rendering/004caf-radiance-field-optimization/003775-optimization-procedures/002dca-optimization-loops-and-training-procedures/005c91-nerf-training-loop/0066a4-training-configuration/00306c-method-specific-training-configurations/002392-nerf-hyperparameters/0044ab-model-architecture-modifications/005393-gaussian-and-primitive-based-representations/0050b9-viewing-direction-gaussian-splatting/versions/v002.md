# Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting


## Overview
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) is a hybrid rendering method that combines 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) for efficient shape representation with a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF)-based Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to encode viewing-direction-dependent color and opacity updates [S1]. It solves the limitation of classical GS, where color and opacity are static per Gaussian, by allowing these properties to vary with the viewing direction, thereby accurately modeling light reflections, transparency, and glass artifacts without a significant increase in training time [S1]. Unlike classical GS, VDGS retains the fast training and inference speeds of Gaussian rasterization while inheriting the view-dependent appearance modeling capabilities of NeRF [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of initial 3D Gaussian parameters (means, covariances, base colors, base opacities) derived from Structure from Motion (SfM) points or random initialization; camera poses; viewing directions $d$ for each pixel ray.
*   **Outputs:** Rendered pixel colors $C(p)$ and gradients for optimizing Gaussian parameters and MLP weights.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Gaussian Set $G$:** A collection of $n$ Gaussian components. Each component $i$ is defined by:
    *   Mean position $\mathbf{m}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
    *   Covariance matrix $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ (symmetric positive definite).
    *   Base color $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (RGB).
    *   Base opacity $\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}$ (scalar).
2.  **NeRF-based MLP Network $F_{VDGS}$:**
    *   **Input:** Hash-encoded Gaussian mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and viewing direction $d$.
    *   **Output:** Density update $\Delta \sigma_i(d) \in \mathbb{R}$.
    *   **Hash Grid:** 3D structured hash grid with 12 levels of 3D embedding (resolutions ranging from 16 to 512). Hash table size is $2^{14}$ (16384). Feature dimension is 2.
    *   **MLP Architecture:** 2-layer MLP with LeakyReLU activation [S1].
    *   **Parameters:** $\Theta$ (weights and biases of the MLP).

**Formulas:**
The vanilla NeRF model is defined as $F_{NeRF}(x, d; \Theta) = (c, \sigma)$, where $x$ is spatial location, $d$ is viewing direction, $\Theta$ are MLP parameters, $c$ is emitted color, and $\sigma$ is volume density [S1].

In VDGS, the NeRF-based network $F_{VDGS}$ takes the mean of the Gaussian distribution $\mathbf{m}_i$ and the viewing direction $d$ to return a volume density update $\Delta \sigma_i(d)$:
$$ \Delta \sigma_i(d) = F_{VDGS}(\mathbf{m}_i, d; \Theta) $$

The effective opacity $\alpha_i$ for each Gaussian sample $i$ along a ray is calculated using the base opacity $\sigma_i$, the distance between adjacent samples $\delta_i$, and the NeRF-based density update:
$$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\left( -\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(d) \cdot \delta_i \right) $$
Note: The source text states $\alpha_i = (1 - \exp(\sigma_i \cdot F_{VDGS}(m, d; \Theta) \cdot \delta_i))$. Standard alpha compositing requires the exponent to be negative for opacity to be between 0 and 1. Given $\sigma, \Delta \sigma, \delta > 0$, the term inside exp must be negative. The source formula lacks the negative sign explicitly in the text snippet "$\alpha_i = (1 - \exp(\sigma_i \cdot F_{V DGS} (mi , d; \Theta) \cdot \deltai ))$", but physically $\alpha$ must be $\le 1$. We assume the standard volumetric rendering formulation where density $\sigma_{eff} = \sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(d)$ and $\alpha = 1 - e^{-\sigma_{eff} \delta}$. If the source implies $\Delta \sigma$ is a multiplier that can be negative, the sign is handled by the value. However, opacity updates are typically positive. We follow the standard interpretation: $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(d) \cdot \delta_i)$. If the source strictly means the formula as written without the negative sign, it implies $\Delta \sigma$ might be negative or the formula is a typo in the source. Given "density update", it acts as a modulator. We will use the standard physical interpretation for the implementation note, but cite the source's variable names.

The final color $C(p)$ of a pixel $p$ is computed by blending $N$ ordered overlapping Gaussian samples using alpha compositing:
$$ C(p) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of indices of Gaussians intersecting the pixel ray, ordered by depth.

**Parameters:**
*   Hash Grid Levels: 12.
*   Hash Table Size: $2^{14}$ (16384).
*   Feature Dimension: 2.
*   Activation Function: LeakyReLU.
*   MLP Layers: 2 [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize the model with Structure from Motion (SfM) points, sourced from COLMAP or generated randomly. These points establish the initial means $\mathbf{m}_i$, covariances $\Sigma_i$, colors $\mathbf{c}_i$, and opacities $\sigma_i$ for the 3D Gaussians [S1].
2.  **Hash Encoding:** For each Gaussian $i$, encode its mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$ using the 3D structured hash grid to produce a feature vector [S1].
3.  **NeRF Update:** Feed the hashed Gaussian mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and the viewing direction $d$ into the MLP network $F_{VDGS}$ to compute the density update $\Delta \sigma_i(d)$ [S1].
4.  **Opacity Calculation:** Calculate the effective opacity $\alpha_i$ for each Gaussian sample using the formula:
    $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(d) \cdot \delta_i) $$
    where $\delta_i$ is the distance between adjacent samples along the ray [S1].
5.  **Rasterization:** Use a differential Gaussian rasterization pipeline to render the image. This involves projecting the 3D Gaussians to 2D, sorting them by depth, and blending them according to the alpha compositing formula [S1].
6.  **Optimization:** Jointly optimize all parameters:
    *   Gaussian parameters: mean $\mathbf{m}_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$ (3x3 matrix), color $\mathbf{c}_i$, and base opacity $\sigma_i$.
    *   MLP parameters: $\Theta$.
    *   The optimization is performed using gradient descent, leveraging the differentiable rasterization pipeline [S1].
7.  **Pruning and Densification:**
    *   **Pruning:** Every 100 iterations, eliminate Gaussians with an opacity below a specific limit (threshold not explicitly given in sources, but standard GS practice applies) [S1].
    *   **Densification:** Generate new Gaussians in unoccupied areas of 3D space to fill vacant areas during the optimization process [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Joint Color and Opacity Update:** The sources indicate that jointly updating both color and opacity yields slightly worse results than updating only one. This is attributed to the dependency of opacity on color, making joint training ineffective [S1].
*   **Opacity vs. Color Update:** Changing only opacity (Opacity Multiply) gives the most consistently high-quality results on real scenes. Changing only color (Color Multiply) yields the best scores on the NeRF Synthetic dataset [S1].
*   **Precision:** The hash grid and MLP introduce approximation errors. The choice of hash table size and grid levels affects memory and accuracy [S1].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The hash grid has a finite resolution range (16 to 512). Points outside this range may not be encoded correctly [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is comparable to classical Gaussian Splatting (GS), with a slight increase due to the MLP forward/backward passes. Inference time is also comparable to GS, significantly faster than NeRF [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage is higher than classical GS due to the storage of the hash grid and MLP parameters, but lower than full NeRF [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   **Mip-NeRF360:** SSIM 0.813, PSNR 27.64, LPIPS 0.220. Training time: 46m11s. Inference FPS: 41.35.
    *   **Tanks and Temples:** SSIM 0.851, PSNR 24.02, LPIPS 0.176. Training time: 29m45s. Inference FPS: 38.53.
    *   **Deep Blending:** SSIM 0.906, PSNR 29.54, LPIPS 0.243. Training time: 41m29s. Inference FPS: 44.72.
    *   **Shiny Blender:** Avg PSNR 30.62 [S1].
*   **Hardware:** Implemented and tested on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU using PyTorch and TinyCUDA frameworks [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Fast training and inference (similar to GS); accurate modeling of view-dependent effects (reflections, transparency) without significant speed penalty; better handling of shiny surfaces and glass compared to classical GS [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Slightly worse performance than classical GS on some metrics for non-shiny objects; joint color/opacity update is less effective than single update; requires careful tuning of pruning/densification parameters [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When view-dependent effects (shiny surfaces, glass, transparency) are important and fast rendering is required.
*   **When Not to Use:** When the scene is static and view-independent, classical GS may be simpler and faster. If joint color/opacity update is attempted, performance may degrade [S1].

## Background
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) was proposed by researchers from the Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science, Jagiellonian University, and the Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge [S1]. The method was published in 2023 (arXiv:2312.13729) [S1]. It builds on the foundations of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [S1] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) [S1]. The authors aimed to combine the fast rendering of GS with the view-dependent appearance modeling of NeRF, addressing the limitations of both: GS's difficulty in conditioning and NeRF's slow training/inference and geometry placement issues [S1].
