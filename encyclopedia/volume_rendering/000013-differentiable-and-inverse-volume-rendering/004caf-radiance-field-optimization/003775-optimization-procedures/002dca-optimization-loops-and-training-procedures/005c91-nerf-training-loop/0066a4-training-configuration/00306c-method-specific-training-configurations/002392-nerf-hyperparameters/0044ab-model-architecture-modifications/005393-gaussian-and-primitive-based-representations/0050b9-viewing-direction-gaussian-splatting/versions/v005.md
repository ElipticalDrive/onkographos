# Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting


## Overview
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) is a hybrid neural rendering method that combines the geometric efficiency of 3D Gaussian Splatting with the view-dependent appearance modeling of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [S1]. While standard Gaussian Splatting uses static per-Gaussian color and opacity, VDGS employs a neural network to modulate opacity (and optionally color) based on the viewing direction, enabling accurate rendering of specular reflections, transparency, and glass without the computational overhead of full NeRF integration [S1]. It solves the limitation of classical Gaussians in handling view-dependent artifacts by conditioning the radiance field on the camera position relative to the Gaussian center [S1].

## How it works
VDGS represents the scene as a set of 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where each Gaussian $G_i$ is defined by a mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$, a covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i$, a base color $\mathbf{c}_i$, and a base opacity $\sigma_i$ [S1]. Unlike standard Gaussian Splatting, the effective opacity of each Gaussian is dynamically adjusted by a neural network $F_{VDGS}$ that takes the Gaussian's position and the viewing direction as input [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of training images with known camera poses; initial 3D points (from Structure from Motion or random initialization).
*   **Outputs:** Rendered pixel colors $\mathbf{C}(p)$ for a given camera pose.

### Data Structures
The scene is represented by two main components:
1.  **Gaussian Set $\mathcal{G}$:**
    *   $\mathbf{m}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Mean position (3D vector).
    *   $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix (symmetric, positive definite). In practice, this is often parameterized by a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_i$ and a scaling vector $\mathbf{s}_i$ such that $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i = \mathbf{R}_i \mathbf{S}_i \mathbf{S}_i^T \mathbf{R}_i^T$ [S1, G].
    *   $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Base color (RGB).
    *   $\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}$: Base opacity (scalar, typically in $[0, 1]$).
2.  **Neural Network $F_{VDGS}$:**
    *   **Input Encoding:** The mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$ is encoded using a 3D structured hash grid [S1].
        *   Hash Grid Levels: 12 levels.
        *   Hash Table Size: $2^{14}$ (16,384).
        *   Feature Dimension: 2.
        *   Resolution Range: 16 to 512.
    *   **Network Architecture:** A 2-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) [S1].
    *   **Activation:** LeakyReLU [S1].
    *   **Output:** A scalar density update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ [S1].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Initialization:** Initialize the Gaussian set $\mathcal{G}$ using Structure from Motion (SfM) points (e.g., from COLMAP) or random points [S1]. Set initial values for $\mathbf{m}_i, \mathbf{\Sigma}_i, \mathbf{c}_i, \sigma_i$. Initialize the MLP $F_{VDGS}$ with random weights [S1].
2.  **Ray Casting:** For each pixel $p$ in the target image, cast a ray $\mathbf{r}$ from the camera origin through the pixel center.
3.  **Gaussian Rasterization:** Identify all Gaussians $G_i$ that intersect the ray or fall within the pixel's bounding box. Sort these Gaussians by their depth along the ray [S1].
4.  **View-Dependent Opacity Calculation:** For each Gaussian $G_i$ in the sorted list:
    *   Compute the viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ from the camera origin to the Gaussian center $\mathbf{m}_i$.
    *   Encode $\mathbf{m}_i$ using the hash grid.
    *   Feed the encoded position and $\mathbf{d}$ into $F_{VDGS}$ to obtain the density update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ [S1].
    *   Calculate the effective opacity $\alpha_i$ for the Gaussian sample. The source defines the effective opacity $\alpha_i$ using the base opacity $\sigma_i$, the distance between adjacent samples $\delta_i$, and the NeRF-based density update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ [S1]. The formula provided is:
        $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\left( - (\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i) \right) $$
        *Note: The source text contains a typo in the exponent sign in one instance ($1 - \exp(...)$ vs $1 - \exp(-...)$). Standard alpha compositing and density integration imply the negative sign in the exponent for attenuation. The source formula explicitly writes `1 - exp(...)` but defines $\Delta \sigma$ as a density update. Given $\sigma$ is opacity and $\Delta \sigma$ is density, the product is optical depth. The standard volume rendering opacity formula is $1 - e^{-\sigma \delta}$. The source writes $\alpha_i = (1 - \exp(\sigma_i \cdot F_{VDGS} \cdot \delta_i))$. This is likely a typo for $-\sigma_i \cdot ...$. However, strictly following the source text's explicit formula:*
        $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i) $$
        *Wait, if $\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma \cdot \delta$ is positive, the exponent is positive, leading to negative alpha. This is physically impossible. The source likely meant:*
        $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i) $$
        *Or that $\Delta \sigma$ is negative? No, density is positive. We assume the standard physical interpretation where the exponent is negative, correcting the likely typo in the source text for implementability [G].*
5.  **Alpha Compositing:** Compute the final pixel color $\mathbf{C}(p)$ by blending the colors of the sorted Gaussians using alpha compositing [S1]:
    $$ \mathbf{C}(p) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
    where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of overlapping Gaussians for pixel $p$ [S1].
6.  **Loss Calculation:** Compute the photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{ph}$ between the rendered image $\mathbf{C}(p)$ and the ground truth image [S1].
7.  **Optimization:** Jointly optimize all parameters:
    *   Gaussian parameters: $\mathbf{m}_i, \mathbf{\Sigma}_i, \mathbf{c}_i, \sigma_i$.
    *   Neural network parameters: Weights of $F_{VDGS}$ [S1].
    *   Use differential Gaussian rasterization to backpropagate gradients through the rasterization step [S1].
8.  **Densification and Pruning:**
    *   Every 100 iterations, eliminate Gaussians with opacity below a specific limit [S1].
    *   Generate new Gaussians in unoccupied areas of 3D space to fill vacant areas [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Opacity vs. Color Update:** The source notes that changing only opacity yields the most consistently high-quality results. Jointly updating color and opacity is slightly worse than using only one, attributed to the dependency of opacity on color making joint training ineffective [S1].
*   **Dataset Specifics:** "VDGS Opacity Multiply" (updating only opacity) yields the best scores on real scenes (Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, Deep Blending). "VDGS Color Multiply" yields the best scores on the NeRF Synthetic dataset [S1].
*   **Initialization:** The model requires initial SfM points. If random points are used, the initial conditions for Gaussians must be established [S1].
*   **Precision:** The hash grid provides fast queries but requires careful handling of coordinate scaling to map 3D positions to hash table indices [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is comparable to standard Gaussian Splatting, with a slight increase due to the MLP evaluation. For VDGS-30K on RTX 3090: Mip-NeRF360 46m11s, Tanks & Temples 29m45s, Deep Blending 41m29s [S1].
*   **Inference Speed:** Inference FPS on RTX 3090 for VDGS-30K: Mip-NeRF360 41.35, Tanks & Temples 38.53, Deep Blending 44.72 [S1]. This is significantly faster than NeRF-based methods but slightly slower than standard GS due to the MLP lookup [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the number of Gaussians and the hash grid size. The hash grid is fixed size ($2^{14}$ levels, dim 2), while Gaussians scale with scene complexity [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Accurately models light reflections, transparency, and glass; removes view-dependent artifacts; inherits fast training/inference from GS [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires tuning of opacity vs. color updates based on dataset type (real vs. synthetic); joint optimization of color and opacity is less effective than single updates [S1].

## Background
VDGS was proposed by researchers from Jagiellonian University and the University of Cambridge [S1]. It was published in December 2023 (arXiv:2312.13729) [S1]. The method addresses the limitations of both NeRF (slow training/inference, difficulty in precise geometry localization) and Gaussian Splatting (static appearance, difficulty in modeling view-dependent effects like reflections without excessive Gaussian counts) [S1]. It builds on the differential Gaussian rasterization pipeline and the hash encoding techniques popularized by Instant NGP and 3D Gaussian Splatting [S1].
