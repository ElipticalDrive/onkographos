# Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting


## Overview
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) is a hybrid neural rendering method that combines the geometric efficiency of 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) with the view-dependent appearance modeling of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [S1]. While standard GS represents scene geometry and static color using discrete 3D Gaussians, VDGS introduces a neural network to modulate the opacity (and optionally color) of these Gaussians based on the viewing direction [S1]. This approach solves the limitation of standard GS in rendering view-dependent effects such as glass reflections, transparency, and specular highlights, which static Gaussian attributes cannot capture [S1]. Unlike classical GS where color and opacity are fixed per Gaussian, VDGS achieves view sensitivity without a notable increase in training time compared to standard GS, inheriting the fast inference speeds of rasterization while maintaining the quality of MLP-based renderers [S1].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   A set of initial 3D Gaussian primitives $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$, initialized from Structure from Motion (SfM) points (e.g., from COLMAP) or random generation [S1].
*   Camera parameters (position and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$) for each training image [S1].
*   Ground truth images for loss computation [S1].

**Outputs:**
*   Rendered pixel color $C(\mathbf{p})$ for a given camera pose [S1].
*   Optimized parameters: Gaussian means $\mathbf{m}_i$, covariance matrices $\Sigma_i$, base colors $\mathbf{c}_i$, base opacities $\sigma_i$, and MLP weights $\Theta$ [S1].

### Data Structures
The scene is represented by two main components:
1.  **Gaussian Set $G$**: A list of $N$ Gaussian distributions. Each Gaussian $i$ stores:
    *   Mean position $\mathbf{m}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1].
    *   Covariance matrix $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ (symmetric positive definite) [S1].
    *   Base color $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (RGB) [S1].
    *   Base opacity $\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}$ (scalar, independent of viewing direction) [S1].
2.  **Neural Network $F_{VDGS}$**: A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with parameters $\Theta$ [S1].
    *   **Input**: Hash-encoded Gaussian mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ [S1].
    *   **Output**: Opacity update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].
    *   **Encoding**: A 3D structured hash grid is used to encode $\mathbf{m}_i$ before feeding it to the MLP [S1].
        *   Hash Grid Levels: 12 levels of 3D embedding (resolutions ranging from 16 to 512) [S1].
        *   Hash Table Size: $2^{14}$ (16,384) [S1].
        *   Feature Dimension: 2 [S1].
    *   **Architecture**: 2-layer MLP with LeakyReLU activation [S1].

### Formulas
The effective opacity $\alpha_i$ of a Gaussian sample $i$ is computed by combining its base opacity $\sigma_i$ with the neural network's output $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ and the distance between adjacent samples $\delta_i$ [S1]:

$$
\alpha_i = 1 - \exp\left( \sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i \right)
$$

where $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) = F_{VDGS}(\mathbf{m}_i, \mathbf{d}; \Theta)$ [S1].

The final color $C(\mathbf{p})$ of a pixel $\mathbf{p}$ is computed by alpha compositing $N$ ordered overlapping Gaussian samples [S1]:

$$
C(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)
$$

where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of indices of Gaussians overlapping the pixel, sorted by depth [S1].

### Parameters
*   **Hash Grid**: 12 levels, max table size $2^{14}$, feature dim 2 [S1].
*   **MLP**: 2 layers, LeakyReLU activation [S1].
*   **Gaussian Attributes**: Mean, Covariance (3x3), Color (RGB), Base Opacity [S1].
*   **Training Hardware**: NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU [S1].

### Steps
1.  **Initialization**: Initialize the Gaussian set $G$ using SfM points (from COLMAP) or random points [S1].
2.  **Hash Encoding**: For each Gaussian $i$, encode its mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$ using the 3D structured hash grid [S1].
3.  **Opacity Update**: Feed the hashed mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and the viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ into the MLP $F_{VDGS}$ to compute the opacity update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ [S1].
4.  **Effective Opacity Calculation**: Compute the effective opacity $\alpha_i$ for each Gaussian using the formula $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i)$ [S1].
5.  **Rasterization**: Use a differential Gaussian rasterization pipeline to render the image by blending the Gaussians according to their effective opacities and colors [S1].
6.  **Loss Computation**: Calculate the loss between the rendered image and the ground truth image [S1].
7.  **Optimization**: Jointly optimize all parameters (Gaussian means, covariances, colors, base opacities, and MLP weights $\Theta$) using gradient descent [S1].
8.  **Pruning and Densification**:
    *   Every 100 iterations, eliminate Gaussians with opacity below a specific limit [S1].
    *   Generate new Gaussians in unoccupied areas of 3D space to fill vacant areas [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Joint Color and Opacity Update**: Updating both color and opacity via the MLP yields slightly worse results than updating only opacity. This is attributed to the dependency of opacity on color, making joint training ineffective [S1].
*   **Opacity vs Color Update**: Changing only opacity provides the most consistently high-quality results across real scenes. However, changes to opacity indirectly change color by exposing other Gaussians [S1].
*   **Model Variant Selection**: "VDGS Opacity Multiply" (updating only opacity) yields the best scores on real scenes, while "VDGS Color Multiply" yields the best scores on the NeRF Synthetic dataset [S1].
*   **Precision**: The hash grid encoding is critical for performance; using frequency encoding instead of hash encoding results in inferior performance and slower queries [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: Training time is comparable to standard Gaussian Splatting (GS), with a slight increase due to the MLP queries. For example, on RTX 3090, VDGS-30K takes ~46 minutes on Mip-NeRF360, compared to ~41 minutes for GS-30K [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity**: The memory footprint includes the Gaussian parameters and the hash grid/MLP weights. The hash grid size is $2^{14}$ with feature dimension 2, which is relatively small [S1].
*   **Measured Performance**:
    *   **Mip-NeRF360**: SSIM 0.813, PSNR 27.64, LPIPS 0.220 [S1].
    *   **Tanks and Temples**: SSIM 0.851, PSNR 24.02, LPIPS 0.176 [S1].
    *   **Deep Blending**: SSIM 0.906, PSNR 29.54, LPIPS 0.243 [S1].
    *   **Shiny Blender**: Avg PSNR 30.62 [S1].
*   **Inference Speed**: Fast, comparable to GS. On RTX 3090, VDGS-30K achieves ~41 FPS on Mip-NeRF360, ~38 FPS on Tanks and Temples, and ~44 FPS on Deep Blending [S1].
*   **Strengths**: Fast training and inference, ability to model view-dependent effects (reflections, transparency), high quality on real scenes.
*   **Limitations**: Slightly worse performance on synthetic NeRF datasets compared to color-only updates, joint color/opacity update is ineffective.

## Background
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) was proposed by researchers from the Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science, Jagiellonian University, and the Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge [S1]. The method was published in December 2023 [S1]. It addresses the limitations of both NeRF and GS by combining the geometric efficiency of GS with the view-dependent appearance modeling of NeRF [S1]. The implementation uses PyTorch and TinyCUDA frameworks on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs [S1].
