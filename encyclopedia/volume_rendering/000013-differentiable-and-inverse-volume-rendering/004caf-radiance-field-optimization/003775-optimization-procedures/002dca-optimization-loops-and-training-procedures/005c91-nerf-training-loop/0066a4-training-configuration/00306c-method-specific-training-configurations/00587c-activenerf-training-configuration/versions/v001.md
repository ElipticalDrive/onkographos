# ActiveNeRF Training Configuration

Also called: Active Learning with Uncertainty Estimation, ActiveNeRF, ActiveNeRF optimization settings, NeRF training hyperparameters

## Overview
ActiveNeRF is a learning framework that models a 3D scene with a constrained input budget by incorporating uncertainty estimation into a NeRF model and using an active learning scheme to select new training samples based on information gain [S1]. It supplements the existing training set with newly captured samples to improve model performance upper bounds under limited training budgets [S1]. Unlike NeRF-W which models uncertainty for transient objects across images, ActiveNeRF models uncertainty for inherent noise in training data to guide active sampling [S1]. Unlike S-NeRF which uses variational inference and requires multiple identical inferences, ActiveNeRF follows the volume rendering procedure strictly and does not require multiple inferences for uncertainty [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Initial set of posed images (training set), camera poses, and a budget for additional observations.
*   **Outputs:** A trained radiance field model (coarse and fine networks with uncertainty branches) and a sequence of selected new viewpoints to capture.

**Data Structures:**
The model consists of two parallel networks: a coarse model and a fine model.
*   **Coarse Model:** Identical to vanilla NeRF. Outputs density $\sigma$ and feature $f$.
*   **Fine Model:** Includes an additional uncertainty branch. Outputs density $\sigma$, feature $f$, and variance $\beta^2$.
*   **Positional Encoding:** $\gamma_x(\mathbf{x})$ for 3D coordinates and $\gamma_d(\mathbf{d})$ for viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ (unit vector).

**Network Architecture:**
The coarse model computes:
$$ [\sigma, f] = \text{MLP}_{\theta_1}(\gamma_x(\mathbf{x})) $$
$$ c = \text{MLP}_{\theta_2}(f, \gamma_d(\mathbf{d})) $$

The fine model computes:
$$ [\sigma, f, \beta^2(\mathbf{r}(t))] = \text{MLP}_{\theta_1, \theta_3}(\gamma_x(\mathbf{r}(t))) $$
$$ \bar{c}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \text{MLP}_{\theta_2}(f, \gamma_d(\mathbf{d})) $$
$$ \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \beta_0^2 + \log(1 + \exp(\beta^2(\mathbf{r}(t)))) $$
where $\beta_0^2$ ensures a minimum variance for all locations [S1].

**Rendering with Uncertainty:**
The rendered color $C(\mathbf{r})$ for a ray $\mathbf{r}$ follows a Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\bar{C}(\mathbf{r}), \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}))$ [S1]:
$$ \bar{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \alpha_i \bar{c}(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) $$
$$ \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \alpha_i^2 \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) $$
where $N_s$ is the number of sampled locations per ray, and $\alpha_i$ are the alpha weights from volume rendering [S1].

**Loss Function:**
The optimization objective minimizes the negative log-likelihood of rays in a batch $\mathcal{B}$:
$$ \min_\theta -\log p_\theta(\mathcal{B}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \frac{\|C(\mathbf{r}_i) - \bar{C}(\mathbf{r}_i)\|_2^2}{2 \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_i)} + \frac{\log \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_i)}{2} \right] $$
Simply minimizing this leads to suboptimal solutions where weights $\alpha_i$ are driven closer, causing large fractions of non-zero density and blurs [S1]. Therefore, an uncertainty loss $\mathcal{L}^{uct}$ adds a regularization term to force sparser volume density:
$$ \mathcal{L}^{uct} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \frac{\|C(\mathbf{r}_i) - \bar{C}(\mathbf{r}_i)\|_2^2}{2 \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_i)} + \frac{\log \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_i)}{2} + \frac{\lambda}{N_s} \sum_{j=1}^{N_s} \sigma_i(\mathbf{r}_i(t_j)) \right) $$
where $\lambda$ controls the regularization strength [S1].

The final training loss combines the uncertainty loss for the fine model and the squared reconstruction error for the coarse model:
$$ \mathcal{L}^{uct}(C(\mathbf{r}), \bar{C}^f(\mathbf{r})) + \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \|C(\mathbf{r}_i) - \hat{C}^c(\mathbf{r}_i)\|_2^2 $$
The coarse model remains the same as vanilla NeRF, while only the fine model includes the uncertainty branch [S1].

**Active Learning Pipeline:**
The pipeline consists of 4 steps [S1]:
1.  **Train:** Train an ActiveNeRF model using initial observation data.
2.  **Render:** Render novel views from the trained model to estimate uncertainty.
3.  **Select:** Estimate the new viewpoint that most reduces uncertainty using an acquisition function.
4.  **Capture:** Capture a new perception and add it to the training set.

**Acquisition Function:**
Given a hypothetical new input, the posterior distribution of the whole scene is analyzed via Bayesian estimation. The information gain is the subtraction of the variance from the prior to posterior distribution [S1].

For a single new ray $\mathbf{r}_2$, the likelihood of the observed color $C(\mathbf{r}_2)$ given the specific location color $c(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k))$ is modeled as a Gaussian:
$$ p(C(\mathbf{r}_2)|c(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k))) \sim \mathcal{N}(\alpha_k \bar{c}(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)) + b(t_k), \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2)) $$
where $b(t_k)$ represents the contribution of unrelated sampled locations in the ray [S1].

The posterior distribution $P^{(\text{post})}$ for a location $\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)$ given new ray $\mathbf{r}_2$ and existing data $D_1$ is Gaussian with mean:
$$ \mu^{(\text{post})} = \gamma \frac{C(\mathbf{r}_2) - b(t_k)}{\alpha_k} + (1 - \gamma) \bar{c}(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)) $$
and variance:
$$ \text{Var}^{(\text{post})}(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)) = \frac{\bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)) \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2)}{\alpha_k^2 \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)) + \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2)} $$
where $\gamma = \frac{\alpha_k^2 \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k))}{\alpha_k^2 \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2(t_k)) + \bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_2)}$ [S1].

For multiple input images, the posterior variance is:
$$ \text{Var}^{(\text{post})}(\mathbf{x}) = \left( \frac{1}{\bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{x})} + \sum_i \frac{\alpha_{k_i}^2}{\bar{\beta}^2(\mathbf{r}_i(t_{k_i}))} \right)^{-1} $$
where $\mathbf{r}_i$ denotes a ray from different images and $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{r}_i(t_{k_i})$ [S1].

The acquisition function $\mathcal{A}(D_2)$ for a candidate image $D_2$ is the sum of variance reductions over all sampled rays $\mathbf{r}_i$ and locations $j$:
$$ \mathcal{A}(D_2) = \sum_{\mathbf{r}_i \in D_2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_s} \left( \text{Var}^{(\text{pri})}(\mathbf{r}_i(t_j)) - \text{Var}^{(\text{post})}(\mathbf{r}_i(t_j)) \right) $$
where $N = H \times W$ is the number of independent rays for image resolution $H, W$, and $N_s$ is the number of sampled locations per ray [S1].

**View Selection:**
1.  Sample candidate views from a spherical space [S1].
2.  Evaluate candidates using the acquisition function. To achieve quality-efficiency trade-off, evaluate new inputs with lower resolution by sampling $H/r \times W/r$ rays instead of full $H \times W$ rays, approximating the influence of the whole image with $1/r^2$ time consumption [S1].
3.  Choose the top-$k$ candidates that score highest in the acquisition function as the supplementary of the current training set [S1].

**Incorporation Strategies:**
Two approaches to incorporate new inputs are provided [S1]:
*   **Bayesian Estimation (BE):** Compute the posterior distribution of scene locations using ground-truth values from new inputs, avoiding collateral training. The mean of the distribution becomes the Bayesian estimation of emitted radiance value [S1].
*   **Continuous Learning (CL):** Add captured inputs to the training set and tune the model based on the current one. The fraction of training rays from new images can be controlled to optimize newly observed regions [S1].

## Subtopics

### Uncertainty-Guided Novel View Selection Policy
A view selection policy that uses U-NeRF's color/depth uncertainties and E-SCRNet's epistemic uncertainties to select samples with the most information gain, improving data efficiency [S2]. It generates a novel view dataset $S_{new}$ by synthesizing more views within the scene boundary using U-NeRF [S2]. It prunes invalid views from $S_{new}$ based on U-NeRF’s uncertainties (color and depth reliability) [S2]. It selects views with high information gain ($S_{high}$) based on E-SCRNet’s epistemic uncertainties from the pruned set [S2]. Unlike random view synthesis which may contain large redundancy, this policy selects samples with the most information gain [S2].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
*   **Bayesian Estimation (BE):** Saves 75% of time consumption compared to Continuous Learning (CL) [S1]. It avoids collateral training by computing the posterior distribution directly [S1].
*   **Continuous Learning (CL):** Requires retraining or fine-tuning the model on the expanded dataset, which is computationally more expensive [S1].

**Measured Performance:**
*   **Setting I (20 total observations, synthetic scenes):** ActiveNeRF-BE achieved 25.67 PSNR in 30 minutes, while ActiveNeRF-CL achieved 26.24 PSNR in 2.2 hours [S1].
*   **Setting II (10 total observations, synthetic scenes):** ActiveNeRF-BE achieved 18.25 PSNR in 16 minutes, while ActiveNeRF-CL achieved 20.01 PSNR in 1.1 hours [S1].
*   ActiveNeRF performs superior to or on par with the original NeRF in all settings, especially with scarce training data [S1].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Improves the upper bound of model performance under limited budgets; robust under few observations; provides interpretation of how NeRF understands the scene via uncertainty [S1].
*   **Limitations:** BE shows inferior performance to CL, though it is competitive with heuristic approaches under CL [S1]. Requires careful tuning of hyperparameters like $\lambda$ and $\beta_0^2$ [S1].

**When to Use:**
*   Use when training data is scarce and computational resources for retraining are limited (prefer BE).
*   Use when maximum quality is required and sufficient time/compute is available (prefer CL) [S1].

## Background
ActiveNeRF is presented in a paper by X. Pan et al., building on the NeRF framework and Bayesian neural network concepts for uncertainty estimation [S1]. It addresses the limitation that NeRF fails to generalize well from one or few input views and may collapse to trivial solutions in unobserved regions [S1]. The work leverages active learning to supplement training data efficiently [S1].
