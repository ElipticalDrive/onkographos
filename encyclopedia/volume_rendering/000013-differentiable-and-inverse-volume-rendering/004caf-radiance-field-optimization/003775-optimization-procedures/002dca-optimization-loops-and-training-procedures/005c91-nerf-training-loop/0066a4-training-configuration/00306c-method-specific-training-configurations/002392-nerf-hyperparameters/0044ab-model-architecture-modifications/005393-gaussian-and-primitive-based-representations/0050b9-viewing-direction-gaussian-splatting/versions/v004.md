# Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting


## Overview
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) is a hybrid neural rendering method that combines the geometric efficiency of 3D Gaussian Splatting with the view-dependent appearance modeling of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [S1]. While standard 3D Gaussian Splatting represents scene geometry and static color/opacity using discrete Gaussian primitives, VDGS introduces a neural network to modulate opacity (and optionally color) based on the viewing direction, thereby resolving view-dependent artifacts such as incorrect reflections on shiny surfaces or transparency issues in glass [S1]. It differs from its siblings by retaining the fast differentiable rasterization of Gaussians for shape while offloading view-dependent appearance updates to a lightweight MLP, avoiding the high computational cost of querying a global NeRF for every sample [S1].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   A set of initial 3D Gaussian primitives $\mathcal{G} = \{p_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where each primitive $p_i$ is defined by a mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$, a covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i$ (or its rotation/scale decomposition), a base color $\mathbf{c}_i$, and a base opacity $\sigma_i$ [S1].
*   Camera parameters (position and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$) for each pixel [S1].
*   Initial point cloud, typically derived from Structure from Motion (SfM) using COLMAP or generated randomly [S1].

**Outputs:**
*   Rendered pixel color $C(\mathbf{p})$ for each pixel $\mathbf{p}$ in the image plane [S1].
*   Optimized parameters for the Gaussians ($\mathbf{m}_i, \mathbf{\Sigma}_i, \mathbf{c}_i, \sigma_i$) and the MLP weights $\Theta$ [S1].

### Data Structures
1.  **Gaussian Primitives:**
    *   $\mathbf{m}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Mean position.
    *   $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix, representing shape and orientation. In implementation, this is often stored as a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_i$ and a scale vector $\mathbf{s}_i$ such that $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i = \mathbf{R}_i \mathbf{S}_i \mathbf{S}_i^T \mathbf{R}_i^T$ [S1].
    *   $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Base color (RGB).
    *   $\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}$: Base opacity (transparency) independent of viewing direction.
2.  **Neural Network (MLP):**
    *   Input: Hash-encoded Gaussian mean $\mathbf{m}_i$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$.
    *   Output: Opacity update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ [S1].
    *   Architecture: 2-layer MLP with LeakyReLU activation [S1].
    *   Encoding: 3D structured hash grid with 12 levels of 3D embedding (resolutions 16 to 512), hash table size $2^{14}$ (16384), and feature dimension 2 [S1].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Initialization:** Initialize the Gaussian set $\mathcal{G}$ using SfM points (e.g., from COLMAP) or random points. Set initial parameters for the MLP $\Theta$ [S1].
2.  **Hash Encoding:** For each Gaussian $i$, encode its mean position $\mathbf{m}_i$ using the 3D hash grid to produce a feature vector $\mathbf{h}_i$ [S1].
3.  **View-Dependent Update Calculation:**
    *   Compute the view-dependent opacity update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$ by passing the hash-encoded mean $\mathbf{h}_i$ and the viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ through the MLP:
        $$ \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) = F_{\text{VDGS}}(\mathbf{m}_i, \mathbf{d}; \Theta) $$
    *   Note: The sources indicate that while the model *can* update color or both color and opacity, updating **only opacity** yields the most consistently high-quality results. Joint updates of color and opacity were found to be slightly worse due to dependency issues [S1].
4.  **Effective Opacity Computation:**
    *   For each sample $i$ along a ray intersecting Gaussian $i$, calculate the effective opacity $\alpha_i$ using the base opacity $\sigma_i$, the distance between adjacent samples $\delta_i$, and the NeRF-based update $\Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d})$:
        $$ \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\left( -\sigma_i \cdot \Delta \sigma_i(\mathbf{d}) \cdot \delta_i \right) $$
    *   Here, $\sigma_i$ acts as a scaling factor for the neural network's output [S1].
5.  **Rasterization and Compositing:**
    *   Project the 3D Gaussians to 2D screen space using the camera parameters.
    *   Sort the contributing Gaussians by depth.
    *   Compute the final pixel color $C(\mathbf{p})$ using alpha compositing:
        $$ C(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
    *   Where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of ordered overlapping Gaussian samples contributing to pixel $\mathbf{p}$ [S1].
6.  **Optimization Loop:**
    *   Compute the loss between the rendered image and the ground truth image (e.g., $\mathcal{L}_{ph}$) [S1].
    *   Perform gradient descent to jointly optimize:
        *   Gaussian parameters: $\mathbf{m}_i, \mathbf{\Sigma}_i, \mathbf{c}_i, \sigma_i$.
        *   MLP parameters: $\Theta$ [S1].
    *   Use differential Gaussian rasterization to backpropagate gradients through the rasterization process [S1].
7.  **Pruning and Densification:**
    *   Every 100 iterations, eliminate Gaussians with opacity below a specific threshold [S1].
    *   Generate new Gaussians in unoccupied areas of 3D space to fill vacant regions [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Joint Color and Opacity Update:** Updating both color and opacity simultaneously yields slightly worse results than updating only opacity. This is attributed to the dependency of opacity on color, making joint training ineffective [S1].
*   **Opacity vs. Color Update:** Changing only opacity provides the most consistently high-quality results across real scenes. However, changing opacity indirectly changes color by exposing other Gaussians [S1].
*   **Dataset Specifics:** The "Opacity Multiply" variant (updating only opacity) achieves the best scores on real scenes (Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, Deep Blending). The "Color Multiply" variant achieves the best scores on the NeRF Synthetic dataset [S1].

## Subtopics

### Differentiable Primitive Assembly
Uses quadrics instead of Gaussians for shape representation, allowing for more complex geometry but requiring differentiable assembly logic. [Link](13657-differentiable-primitive-assembly.md)

### Point-NeRF Representation
Uses a point cloud with local feature aggregation via MLPs, offering a middle ground between NeRF and GS in terms of memory and view-dependence. [Link](15538-point-nerf-representation.md)

### Frequency-Modulated Point Cloud Rendering
Modulates point cloud features with frequency information to capture high-frequency details, distinct from the view-dependent opacity modulation in VDGS. [Link](15903-frequency-modulated-point-cloud-rendering.md)

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is comparable to standard 3D Gaussian Splatting, with only a modest increase due to the MLP queries. Inference is fast, similar to GS, as the MLP is only queried during rasterization for opacity updates.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Higher than standard GS due to the storage of the hash grid and MLP weights, but significantly lower than NeRF as it does not store a dense voxel grid or large MLP for every sample.
*   **Measured Performance (RTX 3090, VDGS-30K*):**
    *   **Mip-NeRF360:** SSIM 0.813, PSNR 27.64, LPIPS 0.220. Training: 46m 11s. Inference: 41.35 FPS.
    *   **Tanks and Temples:** SSIM 0.851, PSNR 24.02, LPIPS 0.176. Training: 29m 45s. Inference: 38.53 FPS.
    *   **Deep Blending:** SSIM 0.906, PSNR 29.54, LPIPS 0.243. Training: 41m 29s. Inference: 44.72 FPS.
    *   **Shiny Blender:** Avg PSNR 30.62.
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Accurately models light reflections, shadows, and transparency (e.g., glass) without additional texture or light components [S1].
    *   Eliminates view-dependent artifacts common in standard GS [S1].
    *   Fast training and inference compared to NeRF-based methods [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Requires careful tuning of the opacity update mechanism (opacity-only vs. color-only) depending on the dataset (real vs. synthetic) [S1].
    *   Joint optimization of color and opacity is ineffective [S1].

## Background
Viewing Direction Gaussian Splatting (VDGS) was proposed by researchers from the Jagiellonian University and the University of Cambridge [S1]. It was published in December 2023 (arXiv:2312.13729) [S1]. The method addresses the limitations of both NeRF (slow training/inference, difficulty in precise geometry placement) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (static appearance, difficulty in modeling view-dependent effects like reflections) by creating a hybrid representation [S1]. It builds on the differentiable Gaussian rasterization pipeline introduced in 3D Gaussian Splatting and the hash encoding techniques from Instant NGP [S1].
