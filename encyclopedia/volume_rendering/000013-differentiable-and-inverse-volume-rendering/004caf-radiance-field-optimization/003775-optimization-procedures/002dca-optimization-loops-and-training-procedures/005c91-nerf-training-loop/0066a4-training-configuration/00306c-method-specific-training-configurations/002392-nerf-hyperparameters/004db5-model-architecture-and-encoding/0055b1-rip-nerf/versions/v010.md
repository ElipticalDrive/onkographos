# Rip-NeRF

Also called: Rip-NeRF25k

## Overview
Rip-NeRF is a hybrid radiance field method that renders high-fidelity, anti-aliased images by combining cone casting with an anisotropic 3D Gaussian characterization [S1]. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like Zip-NeRF by using Ripmap-Encoded Platonic Solids to enable efficient anisotropic area-sampling, avoiding the discrete point-based sampling of continuous signals and the inefficiency of multi-sampling mechanisms [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   A set of calibrated multi-view 2D images.
*   Camera poses for the input images.
*   Ray origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$ for each pixel.

**Outputs:**
*   Rendered RGB image.
*   Optimized scene representation (Ripmap Encodings, 3D Gaussian parameters).

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Ripmap Encoding:** A hierarchical feature grid.
    *   Base level $R_{0,0}$: Learnable tensor of shape $H \times W \times C$. Default: $512 \times 512 \times 16$ [S1].
    *   Higher levels: Derived from the base level using averaging operations, not learnable [S1].
2.  **Platonic Solid:** A geometric proxy for area sampling.
    *   Default: Icosahedron with 10 unparalleled faces [S1].
    *   Each face $P_i$ has an associated Ripmap Encoding and a projection matrix $M_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$ mapping 3D world coordinates to the 2D plane of face $P_i$ [S1].
3.  **3D Gaussian:** Characterizes a conical frustum.
    *   Parameters: Mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and Covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ [S1].
4.  **Tiny MLP ($F_\theta$):**
    *   Input: Concatenated feature vectors from Platonic faces and encoded view direction.
    *   Output: Density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Cone Casting and Frustum Division:**
    For each pixel, cast a cone defined by the pixel footprint and camera intrinsics. Divide the cone into multiple conical frustums along the ray depth [S1].

2.  **3D Gaussian Characterization:**
    Characterize each conical frustum as an anisotropic 3D Gaussian parameterized by mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ [S1].

3.  **Platonic Solid Projection:**
    For each face $P_i$ of the Platonic solid (where $i = 1, \dots, n$ and $n=10$ for an icosahedron):
    a. Project the 3D Gaussian onto the 2D plane of face $P_i$ using the projection matrix $M_i$.
    b. Compute the projected mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{proj}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{proj}$:
       $$ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{proj} = M_i^T \boldsymbol{\mu} $$
       $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{proj} = M_i^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} M_i $$
       [S1]

4.  **Ripmap Encoding Query:**
    For each face $P_i$, query a feature vector $\mathbf{f}_i$ from the Ripmap Encoding associated with $P_i$ using tetra-linear interpolation [S1].
    a. Determine the position $(p_x, p_y)$ in the Ripmap grid from $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{proj}$ [S1].
    b. Determine the level $(l_x, l_y)$ in the Ripmap hierarchy from $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{proj}$ [S1].
    c. Perform tetra-linear interpolation on the Ripmap Encoding at $(p_x, p_y, l_x, l_y)$ to obtain $\mathbf{f}_i$ [S1].
    *Note: The Ripmap levels are derived from the base level $R_{0,0}$ via averaging. For indices $i, j$ of levels in x and y directions:*
    $$ R_{i,j} = \begin{cases} \text{Avg}_{2\times1} R_{i, j-1} & \text{if } j \neq 0 \\ \text{Avg}_{1\times2} R_{i-1, j} & \text{if } i \neq 0 \text{ and } j = 0 \\ \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
    [S1]

5.  **Feature Aggregation:**
    Concatenate all feature vectors $\{\mathbf{f}_i \mid i = 1, \dots, n\}$ from the $n$ Platonic faces to form the final feature vector $\mathbf{f}$ [S1].

6.  **Color and Density Estimation:**
    Pass the aggregated feature vector $\mathbf{f}$ and the encoded view direction $\gamma(\mathbf{d})$ through the tiny MLP $F_\theta$:
    $$ (\sigma, \mathbf{c}) = F_\theta(\mathbf{f}, \gamma(\mathbf{d})) $$
    [S1]

7.  **Volume Rendering:**
    Render the pixel color by integrating the estimated density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ along the ray using standard volume rendering [S1]:
    $$ C(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i $$
    where $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$, $T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)$, and $\delta_i$ is the distance between sample points [S1].

8.  **Optimization:**
    Optimize the system end-to-end using a photometric loss between rendered and observed images. The loss is scaled by the area of the pixel footprint on the image plane (area loss $L_{area}$) [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Ripmap Base Level ($R_{0,0}$):** Shape $H=512, W=512, C=16$ [S1].
*   **Platonic Solid:** Default is icosahedron (10 faces) [S1].
*   **Hyper-parameter $w$:** Set to 2.0 to adjust Gaussian footprint coverage [S1].
*   **Optimizer:** AdamW [S1].
*   **Iterations:** 120k for Rip-NeRF, 25k for Rip-NeRF25k [S1].
*   **Weight Decay:** $1 \times 10^{-5}$ [S1].
*   **Initial Learning Rate:** $2 \times 10^{-3}$, modulated by MultiStepLR [S1].
*   **Ripmap Learning Rate:** Scaled up by 10.0 times [S1].
*   **Libraries:** `tiny-cuda-nn` for MLP, CUDA kernels for Platonic Solid Projection and Ripmap Encoding, `NerfAcc` for binary occupancy grid [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Empty Space:** Use of a binary occupancy grid (via NerfAcc) is required to efficiently skip samples in empty areas [S1].
*   **Precision:** The Ripmap levels are derived from the base level, ensuring consistency and compactness [S1].
*   **Degenerate Input:** The method assumes calibrated multi-view images [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Training Time:** 2.6 hours on NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU for Rip-NeRF; 32 minutes for Rip-NeRF25k [S1].
*   **Model Size:** 160 MB [S1].
*   **GPU Memory:** 20 GB during training [S1].
*   **Rendering Speed:** 3 FPS [S1].
*   **Comparison:** Outperforms Zip-NeRF in training time (2.6h vs 4.5h), model size (160MB vs 592MB), and rendering speed (3 FPS vs 0.25 FPS). Zip-NeRF has slightly better SSIM [S1].
*   **Strengths:** High-fidelity anti-aliased rendering, efficient anisotropic area-sampling, compact representation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Designed for bounded objects; Zip-NeRF is more general for unbounded scenes [S1].

## Background
Rip-NeRF was introduced in 2024 by SRN et al. in the paper "Rip-NeRF: Anti-aliasing Radiance Fields with Ripmap-Encoded Platonic Solids" [S1]. It builds on the hybrid representation approach of methods like Mip-NeRF and Tri-MipRF, aiming to improve efficiency and rendering quality through Ripmap-Encoded Platonic Solids [S1].
