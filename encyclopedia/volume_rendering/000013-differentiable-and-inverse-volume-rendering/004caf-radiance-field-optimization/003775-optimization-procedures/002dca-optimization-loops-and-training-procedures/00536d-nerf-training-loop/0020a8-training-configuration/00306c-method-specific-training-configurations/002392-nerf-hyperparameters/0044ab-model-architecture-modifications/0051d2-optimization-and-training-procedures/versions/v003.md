# Optimization and Training Procedures


## Overview
This family covers the algorithmic procedures for updating the parameters of neural radiance field models during training and editing. It addresses the problem of converging the implicit representation to match observed images while managing computational cost and stability. It differs from sibling articles, which focus on architectural changes (such as input expansion or representation substitution), by focusing exclusively on the optimization dynamics, loss functions, and hyperparameter schedules that drive the learning process.

## Shared principles
The optimization of neural radiance fields generally relies on minimizing a photometric loss between the rendered image $\hat{C}$ and the ground truth image $C$. The standard loss function is the Mean Squared Error (MSE):

$$
L = \sum_{(i,j) \in \text{pixels}} \| C_{ij} - \hat{C}_{ij} \|^2
$$

where $C_{ij}$ is the RGB value at pixel $(i,j)$ and $\hat{C}_{ij}$ is the volume-rendered RGB value [S1]. The rendering process is differentiable with respect to the network parameters, allowing gradient descent to update the weights.

Training typically involves sampling rays through the camera and evaluating the neural network at discrete points along these rays. The batch size is often managed dynamically to maintain a constant number of samples or primitives per batch, ensuring stable memory usage and gradient statistics [S1]. Optimizers such as Adam or AdamW are standard, often with specific learning rate schedules involving step-wise reductions to refine the solution in later stages [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Activation-Informed Coarse-to-Fine Rendering](12277-activation-informed-coarse-to-fine-rendering.md)
This method accelerates rendering by conditionally skipping the expensive fine-pass MLP evaluation. It differs from standard coarse-to-fine rendering by using intermediate activation statistics from the coarse pass to identify empty rays, rather than relying solely on density thresholds. It is chosen when inference speed is critical and the scene contains transparent backgrounds (e.g., synthetic datasets).

### [Last Layer Re-training](13532-last-layer-re-training.md)
This technique enables instant color editing of a pre-trained NeRF by retraining only the final layer of the color MLP. It differs from full retraining by preserving the learned feature representations and view-dependent effects in earlier layers, achieving multi-view consistent edits in seconds. It is chosen for interactive editing tasks where geometric changes are not required.

### Rip-NeRF Optimization
Rip-NeRF optimization manages the training of tiny MLP weights and ripmaps $R$. It is distinguished by its specific initialization and dynamic batch sizing strategy.

**Definition**
The optimization process updates the tiny MLP weights and the ripmaps $R$, which serve as the direct scene representation [S1].

**Steps**
1.  Initialize MLP weights using the Glorot and Bengio (2010) method [S1].
2.  Initialize ripmaps $R$ from a uniform distribution over $[-0.01, 0.01]$ [S1].
3.  Use the AdamW optimizer [S1].
4.  Set the base learning rate for $R$ to $2 \times 10^{-3}$, scaled by 10x relative to other parameters due to its direct scene representation role [S1].
5.  Reduce the learning rate by 0.6x at steps 60K, 90K, 100K, and 108K, over a total of 120K iterations [S1].
6.  Dynamically adjust the batch size to maintain approximately 256K spheres per batch [S1].

**Parameters**
*   **Optimizer:** AdamW [S1].
*   **Base Learning Rate:** $2 \times 10^{-3}$ [S1].
*   **Ripmap Learning Rate Scaling:** 10x [S1].
*   **Total Iterations:** 120K [S1].
*   **Batch Size Target:** ~256K spheres [S1].

## Background
The standard NeRF architecture [S6, unofficial] consists of two Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs): a coarse network and a fine network. The coarse network takes positional encoded position $\gamma(\mathbf{x})$ and outputs a latent feature vector $\mathbf{h}$ and volume density $\sigma$. The fine network takes $\mathbf{h}$, positional encoded position $\gamma(\mathbf{x})$, and positional encoded view direction $\gamma(\mathbf{d})$ to output color $\mathbf{c}$.

$$
\gamma(\mathbf{x}, d_1, d_2) = [\mathbf{x}, \sin(2^{d_1}\pi\mathbf{x}), \cos(2^{d_1}\pi\mathbf{x}), \dots, \sin(2^{d_2-1}\pi\mathbf{x}), \cos(2^{d_2-1}\pi\mathbf{x})]
$$

Modifications generally fall into three categories:
1.  **Input Expansion:** Adding new conditioning vectors (e.g., appearance embeddings $\mathbf{z}_i$, fluid volume $v$, or previous stage RGB) to the input concatenation.
2.  **Output Expansion:** Adding auxiliary heads to predict values other than $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$, such as semantic labels $y_{aux}$, object codes $o$, or area encoding probabilities $p$. These are rendered using the same transmittance weights $T_i$ and opacity $\alpha_i$ as color:
    $$
    \hat{Y}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \alpha_i y_{aux}(x_i)
    $$
3.  **Representation Substitution:** Replacing the continuous MLP function with discrete structures (voxel grids, hash tables) or different implicit surfaces (SDFs).
