# DeblurNeRF

Also called: DeRF, Deblur-NeRF, Deblur-NeRF: Neural Radiance Fields from Blurry Images

## Overview
DeblurNeRF is a framework that reconstructs a sharp Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) from blurry input images by explicitly modeling the blurring process via an analysis-by-synthesis approach [S1]. It differs from sibling methods like [Sharp-NeRF](14270-sharp-nerf.md) by learning a deformable sparse kernel to warp ray origins and directions, approximating spatially varying blur through an MLP that models local ray mixing [S2]. Unlike [DP-NeRF](14270-sharp-nerf.md) which uses scene-wise shared rigid transformations, DeblurNeRF employs pixel-wise independent transformations, which is computationally efficient but can introduce 3D geometric inconsistency [S3].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of blurry multi-view images $B$. Camera poses and intrinsics are assumed known or estimated (e.g., via COLMAP) [S1].
*   **Outputs:** A sharp NeRF representation $F_\Theta$ and a Deformable Sparse Kernel (DSK) module $G_\Phi$. At inference, the output is sharp novel views rendered directly from $F_\Theta$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **NeRF MLP ($F_\Theta$):** Maps 3D position $x$ and view direction $d$ to color $c$ and density $\sigma$.
    *   Structure: Standard NeRF architecture (coordinate-based MLP with positional encoding) [S1].
    *   Positional Encoding $\gamma_L$: Maps vector elements to higher dimensional frequency space:
        $$ \gamma_L(x) = [\sin(\pi x), \cos(\pi x), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi x), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi x)]^T $$
        where $L$ is the highest frequency used [S1].
2.  **DSK MLP ($G_\Phi$):** Parameterizes the spatially varying blur kernel.
    *   Structure: 4 fully-connected hidden layers, each with 64 channels and ReLU activations. Includes a shortcut connection from the first layer to the last layer [S1].
    *   Inputs: Pixel coordinate $p$, canonical kernel location $q^0$, and a learned view embedding $l$ (length 32) [S1].
    *   Outputs: For each of $N$ kernel points, a tuple $(\Delta o_q, \Delta q, w_q)$:
        *   $\Delta o_q \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Ray origin translation.
        *   $\Delta q \in \mathbb{R}^2$: Kernel location deformation (2D image plane offset).
        *   $w_q \in \mathbb{R}$: Composition weight.
3.  **Canonical Kernel:** A set of $N$ fixed locations $N^0(p) = \{q^0_i\}_{i=0}^{N-1}$ distributed around pixel $p$ [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $N$: Number of sparse kernel locations per pixel. Default: 5 [S1].
*   $L$: Highest frequency in positional encoding [S1].
*   $\epsilon$: Initialization gain. Default: 0.1 [S1].
*   $\lambda_o$: Alignment loss weight for origin translation. Default: 10 [S1].
*   $\lambda_a$: Alignment loss weight in total loss. Default: 0.1 [S1].
*   Batch size: 1024 rays [S1].
*   Sampling: 64 coordinates in coarse volume, 64 additional coordinates in fine volume [S1].
*   Optimizer: Adam with default parameters [S1].
*   Learning Rate: Starts at $5 \times 10^{-4}$, decays exponentially to $8 \times 10^{-5}$ [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Initialize $F_\Theta$ and $G_\Phi$.
    *   Initialize the output tuples $(\Delta o_q, \Delta q, w_q)$ of $G_\Phi$ by multiplying a small gain $\epsilon = 0.1$ to each element. This ensures optimized rays start close to the input ray and kernel points are close to the pixel center [S1].

2.  **Training Loop (per batch of pixels):**
    For each pixel $p$ in the batch:
    a.  **Generate Canonical Rays:**
        Emit a primary ray $r_p(t) = o + t d_p$ from camera center $o$ along direction $d_p$ [S1].
    b.  **Predict DSK Parameters:**
        For each canonical location $q^0 \in N^0(p)$, compute the deformation and weight using $G_\Phi$:
        $$ (\Delta o_q, \Delta q, w_q) = G_\Phi(p, q^0, l) $$
        where $l$ is the view embedding for the current view [S1].
    c.  **Generate Optimized Rays:**
        Compute the deformed kernel location $q = q^0 + \Delta q$.
        Derive the direction $d_q$ from the deformed location $q$ (conceptually, the ray points towards $q$ on the image plane).
        Compute the deformed ray origin $o_q = o + \Delta o_q$.
        Construct the optimized ray:
        $$ r_q(t) = (o + \Delta o_q) + t d_q $$
        [S1].
    d.  **Render Optimized Rays:**
        For each optimized ray $r_q$, perform hierarchical volume rendering to obtain color $c^0_q$:
        i.  **Coarse Sampling:** Stratified sampling divides ray bounds $[t_n, t_f]$ into $N_s$ intervals. Generate samples $r_i = o_q + t_i d_q$. Render coarse color $\hat{C}_c$ using standard volume rendering formula:
            $$ \hat{C}_c(r_q) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) c_i $$
            where $T_i = \exp(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j)$ and $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ [S3].
        ii. **Fine Sampling:** Compute normalized weights $\hat{w}_i = w_i / \sum_j w_j$ as a PDF. Use these weights to sample fine points. Render fine color $\hat{C}_f$ [S3].
        iii. **Final Color:** The color $c^0_q$ is the result of the fine rendering [S1].
    e.  **Composite Blurred Pixel:**
        Blend the rendered colors of the optimized rays using the predicted weights $w_q$. Apply gamma correction to compensate for the Camera Response Function (CRF):
        $$ \hat{b}_p = g\left( \sum_{q \in N(p)} w_q c^0_q \right) $$
        where $g(c^0) = (c^0)^{1/2.2}$ [S1].
    f.  **Compute Loss:**
        i.  **Reconstruction Loss:** L2 loss between the predicted blurry pixel $\hat{b}_p$ and the ground truth blurry pixel $b^{gt}_p$:
            $$ L_{reconstruct} = \sum_{p \in R} \|\hat{b}_p - b^{gt}_p\|_2^2 $$
            [S1].
        ii. **Alignment Loss:** Constrain the NeRF to align with observations by forcing one canonical ray (corresponding to a fixed $q^0$) to remain close to the original ray $r_p$:
            $$ L_{align} = \|q^0 - p\|_2^2 + \lambda_o \|\Delta o_{q^0}\|_2^2 $$
            [S1].
        iii. **Total Loss:**
            $$ L = L_{reconstruct} + \lambda_a L_{align} $$
            [S1].
    g.  **Update:**
        Backpropagate $L$ to update parameters $\Theta$ (NeRF) and $\Phi$ (DSK) using Adam optimizer [S1].

3.  **Inference:**
    *   Render novel views by emitting rays from the new camera pose and querying $F_\Theta$ directly, bypassing the DSK module $G_\Phi$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class DeblurNeRF:
    def __init__(self, nerf_mlp, dsk_mlp, N=5, lambda_o=10, lambda_a=0.1, epsilon=0.1):
        self.nerf = nerf_mlp
        self.dsk = dsk_mlp
        self.N = N
        self.lambda_o = lambda_o
        self.lambda_a = lambda_a
        self.epsilon = epsilon
        
        # Canonical kernel locations relative to pixel p (e.g., grid around p)
        # Shape: (N, 2)
        self.register_buffer('canonical_offsets', torch.zeros(N, 2)) 
        # Note: In implementation, these are relative to p, so q0 = p + offset
        # The paper defines q0 as canonical locations. We assume they are fixed relative to p.
        
        # View embedding (learned per view)
        self.view_embedding = None 

    def initialize_dsk(self):
        """Initialize DSK outputs with small gain epsilon."""
        # Assuming dsk_mlp outputs (delta_o, delta_q, w) concatenated
        # We need to ensure the initial forward pass returns small values
        # The paper says: "Multiply a small gain of epsilon = 0.1 to each element of the output tuples"
        # This implies the MLP weights are initialized such that output is small, 
        # or we clamp/modify the output. 
        # Implementation note: Usually achieved by initializing bias/weights to produce ~0 output.
        pass

    def positional_encoding(self, x, L=10):
        """Standard NeRF positional encoding."""
        # x: (..., 3) or (..., 2)
        scales = torch.tensor([2**i for i in range(L)], dtype=x.dtype, device=x.device)
        xb = torch.reshape((x * scales).unsqueeze(-1), (*x.shape[:-1], L * x.shape[-1]))
        freq_bands = torch.cat([torch.sin(xb), torch.cos(xb)], dim=-1)
        return freq_bands

    def render_ray(self, ray_o, ray_d, num_coarse=64, num_fine=64):
        """
        Render a single ray using hierarchical sampling.
        ray_o: (3,)
        ray_d: (3,)
        Returns: color (3,), density (1,)
        """
        # 1. Coarse sampling
        t_coarse = torch.linspace(0.0, 1.0, num_coarse + 1) # Normalized t, needs scaling to scene bounds
        # Assuming near/far planes are handled by t scaling. 
        # For simplicity, assume t is in [0, 1] mapped to [tn, tf]
        t_coarse_samples = t_coarse[:-1] + (t_coarse[1:] - t_coarse[:-1]) * torch.rand(num_coarse)
        
        points_coarse = ray_o.unsqueeze(0) + t_coarse_samples.unsqueeze(1) * ray_d.unsqueeze(0)
        # points_coarse: (num_coarse, 3)
        
        # Query NeRF
        # Note: NeRF takes (x, d). d is constant for the ray.
        # We need to encode x and d.
        # In standard NeRF, d is also encoded.
        
        # Simplified query: assume nerf_mlp handles encoding internally or we pass encoded inputs
        # Here we assume nerf_mlp expects raw x and d, or we encode them.
        # Let's assume standard NeRF input: (x, d) -> (c, sigma)
        
        # Encode positions and directions
        # x: (num_coarse, 3), d: (3,) -> (1, 3)
        d_enc = self.positional_encoding(ray_d.unsqueeze(0).expand(num_coarse, -1), L=10)
        x_enc = self.positional_encoding(points_coarse, L=10)
        
        # Concatenate for NeRF input if it expects separate, or pass as tuple
        # Standard NeRF MLP usually takes concatenated encoding or separate.
        # Let's assume nerf_mlp(x_enc, d_enc) -> (c, sigma)
        c_coarse, sigma_coarse = self.nerf(x_enc, d_enc)
        
        # Volume rendering
        # delta_t
        delta_t = t_coarse_samples[1:] - t_coarse_samples[:-1]
        delta_t = torch.cat([delta_t, torch.tensor([1.0 - t_coarse_samples[-1]])]) # Last segment
        
        # Transmittance
        alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma_coarse * delta_t)
        T = torch.cumprod(torch.exp(-sigma_coarse * delta_t), dim=0)
        T = torch.cat([torch.ones_like(T[:1]), T[:-1]], dim=0)
        
        weight = alpha * T
        color_coarse = torch.sum(weight.unsqueeze(-1) * c_coarse, dim=0)
        
        # 2. Fine sampling
        # Use normalized weights as PDF
        pdf = weight / (weight.sum() + 1e-8)
        # Sample indices
        indices = torch.multinomial(pdf, num_fine, replacement=True)
        # Interpolate t
        t_fine = t_coarse_samples[indices]
        points_fine = ray_o.unsqueeze(0) + t_fine.unsqueeze(1) * ray_d.unsqueeze(0)
        
        x_enc_fine = self.positional_encoding(points_fine, L=10)
        c_fine, sigma_fine = self.nerf(x_enc_fine, d_enc)
        
        # Re-render with fine samples
        # Note: delta_t for fine samples needs to be defined. 
        # Simplified: assume uniform or use coarse deltas if indices align.
        # For correctness, we should define t_fine bounds.
        # Let's assume t_fine are points, and we approximate delta as coarse delta or uniform.
        # Standard approach: use the sampled t's to define intervals.
        # For brevity, we assume a helper `render_samples` exists or implement simple uniform delta.
        # Here, we just return coarse color as approximation if fine logic is complex, 
        # but DeblurNeRF uses fine.
        
        # Simplified fine render:
        delta_t_fine = torch.ones(num_fine) * 0.01 # Placeholder for delta
        alpha_fine = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma_fine * delta_t_fine)
        T_fine = torch.cumprod(torch.exp(-sigma_fine * delta_t_fine), dim=0)
        T_fine = torch.cat([torch.ones_like(T_fine[:1]), T_fine[:-1]], dim=0)
        weight_fine = alpha_fine * T_fine
        color_fine = torch.sum(weight_fine.unsqueeze(-1) * c_fine, dim=0)
        
        return color_fine

    def forward(self, pixel_p, ray_o, ray_d, view_embedding):
        """
        Forward pass for a single pixel.
        pixel_p: (2,)
        ray_o: (3,)
        ray_d: (3,)
        view_embedding: (32,)
        Returns: blurry_color (3,)
        """
        N = self.N
        # Canonical locations q0 = p + offset
        # self.canonical_offsets is (N, 2)
        q0 = pixel_p.unsqueeze(0) + self.canonical_offsets # (N, 2)
        
        # Prepare inputs for DSK
        # p: (2,), q0: (N, 2), l: (32,)
        # DSK expects batched input? Or loop?
        # Paper: "forward the MLP G_phi N times".
        # We can batch them.
        
        p_batch = pixel_p.unsqueeze(0).expand(N, -1)
        l_batch = view_embedding.unsqueeze(0).expand(N, -1)
        
        # DSK Output: (delta_o, delta_q, w)
        # delta_o: (N, 3), delta_q: (N, 2), w: (N, 1)
        dsk_out = self.dsk(p_batch, q0, l_batch)
        
        delta_o = dsk_out[:, :3]
        delta_q = dsk_out[:, 3:5]
        w = dsk_out[:, 5:]
        
        # Deform locations
        q = q0 + delta_q # (N, 2)
        
        # Generate optimized rays
        # Direction d_q: derived from q. 
        # If q is image coordinate, d_q is direction from camera center to q.
        # Assuming pinhole camera: d_q = normalize(K^{-1} [q, 1]) - o?
        # Simplified: d_q is direction towards pixel q.
        # In ray space, if ray_o is origin, and q is target on image plane at z=1:
        # d_q = normalize(q_3d - ray_o)
        # Let's assume a helper `get_direction_from_pixel`
        d_q = self.get_direction_from_pixel(q, ray_o) # (N, 3)
        
        # Deform origin
        o_q = ray_o.unsqueeze(0) + delta_o # (N, 3)
        
        # Render each ray
        colors = []
        for i in range(N):
            c = self.render_ray(o_q[i], d_q[i])
            colors.append(c)
        colors = torch.stack(colors) # (N, 3)
        
        # Composite
        # w: (N, 1), colors: (N, 3)
        blurred_color = torch.sum(w * colors, dim=0) # (3,)
        
        # Gamma correction
        blurred_color = blurred_color ** (1.0 / 2.2)
        
        return blurred_color

    def get_direction_from_pixel(self, pixel_coords, origin):
        """
        pixel_coords: (N, 2) in image space (e.g. normalized or pixel indices)
        origin: (3,) camera center
        Returns: directions (N, 3)
        """
        # Assuming pixel_coords are in camera space (x, y) at z=1
        # Convert to 3D: (x, y, 1)
        points_3d = torch.cat([pixel_coords, torch.ones_like(pixel_coords[:, :1])], dim=1)
        # Direction is from origin to point
        # But ray_o is origin.
        # d = normalize(point - origin)
        # However, in NeRF, ray_d is usually normalized.
        # If pixel_coords are relative to principal point, this works.
        # Simplified: assume pixel_coords are already in direction space or handled by camera model.
        # For this pseudocode, we assume d_q is computed correctly by external camera logic.
        # Here we return a placeholder direction based on q.
        # In reality, d_q = normalize(K^{-1} [q_x, q_y, 1])
        return F.normalize(points_3d - origin.unsqueeze(0), dim=1)

    def compute_loss(self, blurry_pred, blurry_gt, canonical_offsets, pixel_p, delta_o_pred):
        """
        Compute total loss.
        """
        # Reconstruction Loss
        L_recon = torch.mean((blurry_pred - blurry_gt) ** 2)
        
        # Alignment Loss
        # q0 is pixel_p + canonical_offsets
        q0 = pixel_p.unsqueeze(0) + canonical_offsets
        # Pick one fixed element, e.g., index 0
        q0_fixed = q0[0]
        delta_o_fixed = delta_o_pred[0]
        
        L_align = torch.norm(q0_fixed - pixel_p) ** 2 + self.lambda_o * torch.norm(delta_o_fixed) ** 2
        
        L_total = L_recon + self.lambda_a * L_align
        
        return L_total
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training is computationally expensive due to the need to render $N$ rays per pixel. With $N=5$, this is 5x the cost of standard NeRF per pixel, plus the overhead of the DSK MLP.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the NeRF MLP and the DSK MLP. The view embeddings add minimal memory overhead ($32 \times$ number of views).
*   **Performance:** On synthetic scenes, DeblurNeRF achieves higher PSNR/SSIM and lower LPIPS compared to ablations without gamma correction, alignment, or origin optimization [S1]. It handles both camera motion blur and defocus blur effectively [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Efficient approximation for moderate blur; does not require sharp ground truth images; joint optimization recovers sharp NeRFs [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Pixel-wise independent blur modeling can lead to 3D inconsistency (geometric artifacts) compared to methods with rigid scene-wise constraints [S3]. Performance may degrade under severe motion blur compared to physically grounded methods like BAD-NeRF [S2].
*   **When to use:** When input images are blurry (motion or defocus) and sharp ground truth is unavailable, and computational resources are limited compared to physically accurate blur models.
*   **When not to use:** When severe motion blur is present and high geometric fidelity is critical, as rigid transformation models (e.g., DP-NeRF) may be more accurate [S2, S3].

## Background
DeblurNeRF was proposed by Xiaoyu Li et al. in 2021/2022 [S1]. It is the first method to recover a sharp NeRF from blurry inputs by simulating the blurring process. The method builds on the original NeRF framework [S1] and addresses the degradation caused by defocus and motion blur in real-world captures. It was tested on synthetic scenes generated with Blender and real-world scenes captured with a Canon EOS RP [S1].
