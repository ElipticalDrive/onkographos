# NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)

Also called: NeRF Integration, NeRF Rendering Equation, Standard NeRF rendering, Vanilla NeRF integration, Vanilla NeRF Volume Rendering, Neural Radiance Field Volume Rendering

## Overview
NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing) is the differentiable volume rendering technique used to synthesize novel views from a continuous 5D radiance field represented by a neural network [S1, S2]. It solves the problem of mapping discrete 3D samples of density and color along a camera ray into a final 2D pixel color and depth by numerically integrating the volume rendering equation [S1][S6, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from explicit volume rendering methods by using a continuous implicit function $F_\Theta(x, d) \rightarrow (\sigma, c)$ evaluated at arbitrary points, and from ray marching solvers by relying on stratified sampling and alpha compositing rather than surface intersection detection [S1][S19, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Camera ray $r(t) = o + td$, where $o$ is the camera origin and $d$ is the unit viewing direction [S1, S4].
*   Integration bounds $t_n$ (near) and $t_f$ (far), defining the segment of the ray to render [S1, S10].
*   Number of samples $N$ (or $K$) [S3, S12].
*   Neural network model $F_\Theta$ that takes 3D position $x$ and view direction $d$ and outputs volume density $\sigma$ and RGB color $c$ [S10].

**Outputs:**
*   Rendered RGB color $\hat{C}(r)$ [S1].
*   Rendered expected depth $\hat{D}(r)$ [S1].
*   (Optional) Accumulated opacity $\hat{\alpha}(r)$ [S6, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   Sample positions: Array $t$ of size $N$ containing distances from the camera origin [S4].
*   Predictions: Tensor of shape $(N, 4)$ containing $[\sigma, r, g, b]$ for each sample [S8].
*   Deltas: Array of size $N$ (or $N-1$ with padding) representing distances between adjacent samples [S6, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Sampling:** Generate $N$ sample points along the ray $r(t)$ within the bounds $[t_n, t_f]$. The standard approach uses non-deterministic stratified sampling: divide the interval $[t_n, t_f]$ into $N$ equally spaced bins and draw one sample uniformly at random from each bin [S1, S10].
    $$t_i \sim U\left[t_n + \frac{i-1}{N}(t_f - t_n), t_n + \frac{i}{N}(t_f - t_n)\right], \quad i=1,\dots,N$$
    [S10]

2.  **Network Query:** For each sample point $t_i$, compute the 3D position $x_i = o + t_i d$. Query the neural network to obtain density $\sigma_i$ and color $c_i$.
    $$[\sigma_i, c_i] = F_\Theta(x_i, d)$$
    [S10]
    *Note:* In many implementations, $c_i$ is passed through a sigmoid activation to bound it to $[0, 1]$, and $\sigma_i$ is passed through a ReLU to ensure non-negativity [S8].

3.  **Delta Calculation:** Compute the distance $\delta_i$ between adjacent samples. For the last sample, append a large value (e.g., $10^{10}$) to handle the termination of the ray, ensuring the accumulated transmittance goes to zero [S6, unofficial][S8].
    $$\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i \quad \text{for } i < N$$
    $$\delta_N = 10^{10}$$
    [S6, unofficial][S8]
    If the ray direction $d$ is not normalized, multiply $\delta_i$ by $\|d\|$ to convert to real-world distance [S6, unofficial].

4.  **Alpha and Transmittance:** Compute the opacity $\alpha_i$ and accumulated transmittance $T_i$ for each sample.
    $$\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$$
    [S1, S8]
    $$T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right) = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)$$
    [S1, S8]
    To prevent numerical instability, add a small epsilon ($\epsilon = 10^{-10}$) to the term $(1-\alpha_j)$ before the cumulative product [S8]. $T_1$ is initialized to 1.0.

5.  **Weight Calculation:** Compute the weight $w_i$ for each sample, representing the fraction of light contributed by that sample to the final pixel.
    $$w_i = \alpha_i T_i$$
    [S8]

6.  **Color Compositing:** Compute the final rendered color $\hat{C}(r)$ as the weighted sum of the sample colors.
    $$\hat{C}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i c_i = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) c_i$$
    [S1, S2, S8]

7.  **Depth Compositing:** Compute the expected depth $\hat{D}(r)$ as the weighted sum of the sample distances.
    $$\hat{D}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i t_i = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) t_i$$
    [S1, S12]

8.  **White Background Handling (Optional):** If the background is assumed to be white, add the remaining transmittance (opacity complement) to the RGB result.
    $$\hat{C}_{final}(r) = \hat{C}(r) + (1 - \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i) \cdot 1.0$$
    [S6, S7, unofficial]

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import math

def nerf_rendering(rays_o, rays_d, t_vals, model, white_background=False, noise_std=0.0):
    """
    Performs NeRF volume rendering (alpha compositing).
    
    Args:
        rays_o: Tensor of shape (B, 3), ray origins.
        rays_d: Tensor of shape (B, 3), ray directions (should be normalized).
        t_vals: Tensor of shape (B, N), sample distances along the ray.
        model: Neural network function F_theta(x, d) -> (sigma, rgb).
        white_background: Boolean, if True, adds background color (white) for transparent pixels.
        noise_std: Float, standard deviation of Gaussian noise added to sigma for training stability.
        
    Returns:
        rgb: Tensor of shape (B, 3), rendered colors.
        depth: Tensor of shape (B), rendered expected depths.
        acc: Tensor of shape (B), accumulated opacity.
    """
    B, N = t_vals.shape
    device = t_vals.device
    
    # 1. Compute 3D points
    # x_i = o + t_i * d
    # rays_o: (B, 3), rays_d: (B, 3), t_vals: (B, N)
    # Expand dims for broadcasting: (B, 1, 3) * (B, N, 1) -> (B, N, 3)
    pts = rays_o.unsqueeze(1) + rays_d.unsqueeze(1) * t_vals.unsqueeze(2)
    
    # 2. Query Model
    # Flatten to (B*N, 3) for model input
    pts_flat = pts.reshape(-1, 3)
    # Repeat directions for each sample
    dirs_flat = rays_d.unsqueeze(1).expand(-1, N, -1).reshape(-1, 3)
    
    # Query model: returns (sigma, rgb)
    # Note: In practice, positional encoding is applied to pts_flat and dirs_flat before the MLP.
    # Here we assume 'model' handles the full forward pass including encoding.
    raw = model(pts_flat, dirs_flat) # Shape: (B*N, 4)
    
    # Reshape back to (B, N, 4)
    raw = raw.reshape(B, N, 4)
    
    # 3. Extract sigma and rgb
    # Apply activations: sigmoid for rgb, relu for sigma
    rgb = torch.sigmoid(raw[..., :3])
    sigma = torch.relu(raw[..., 3])
    
    # Add noise to sigma for training stability (G)
    if noise_std > 0:
        sigma = sigma + torch.randn_like(sigma) * noise_std
        
    # 4. Compute deltas
    # delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i
    deltas = t_vals[:, 1:] - t_vals[:, :-1] # Shape: (B, N-1)
    
    # Append a large value for the last segment to ensure T_N -> 0
    # delta_inf shape: (B, 1)
    delta_inf = torch.ones(B, 1, device=device) * 1e10
    deltas = torch.cat([deltas, delta_inf], dim=-1) # Shape: (B, N)
    
    # 5. Compute alpha
    # alpha_i = 1 - exp(-sigma_i * delta_i)
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * deltas) # Shape: (B, N)
    
    # 6. Compute transmittance T
    # T_i = product_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - alpha_j)
    # Use exclusive cumulative product to start with T_1 = 1.0
    # Add epsilon to prevent numerical instability (0 * 0)
    eps = 1e-10
    transmittance = torch.cumprod((1.0 - alpha + eps)[:, :-1] + eps, dim=-1) # Shape: (B, N-1)
    # Prepend 1.0 for T_1
    transmittance = torch.cat([torch.ones(B, 1, device=device), transmittance], dim=-1) # Shape: (B, N)
    
    # 7. Compute weights
    # w_i = alpha_i * T_i
    weights = alpha * transmittance # Shape: (B, N)
    
    # 8. Compute RGB
    # C(r) = sum(w_i * c_i)
    rgb_final = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * rgb, dim=1) # Shape: (B, 3)
    
    # 9. Compute Depth
    # D(r) = sum(w_i * t_i)
    depth_final = torch.sum(weights * t_vals, dim=1) # Shape: (B)
    
    # 10. Compute Accumulated Opacity
    acc = torch.sum(weights, dim=1) # Shape: (B)
    
    # 11. White Background
    if white_background:
        # Add (1 - acc) * white_background (1.0)
        rgb_final = rgb_final + (1.0 - acc.unsqueeze(-1))
        
    return rgb_final, depth_final, acc
```

## Subtopics

### [Depth-Guided Sampling](7490-depth-guided-sampling.md)
Focuses sampling on estimated surface regions using depth priors to improve quality and reduce samples.

### [Depth-Prior Guided Stratified Sampling](11292-depth-prior-guided-stratified-sampling.md)
Uses predicted depth priors and confidence bounds to define sampling bounds, with stratified randomization within those bounds.

### [Progressive Blur Estimation](14151-progressive-blur-estimation.md)
Models blur as a distribution of rays using a two-stage implicit function module to estimate defocus and motion blur.

### [MipNerfModel](14207-mipnerfmodel.md)
Approximates integrated positional encoding by treating rays as cones/cylinders to solve aliasing artifacts.

### NeRF Rendering Pipeline
Iterates over camera poses to render a sequence of images. For each pose, rays are cast and rendered. If a `render_factor` is specified, the image dimensions and focal length are downsampled by that factor for speed. The rendered RGB and depth are stored. If ground truth images are available and no downsampling is used, PSNR is computed. Results can be saved as 8-bit PNGs. Typical `render_factor` values are 4 or 8 for previews. The `chunk` parameter controls the number of rays processed in parallel (default 32768) to manage GPU memory [S15, unofficial].

### Rendered Depth-Guided Warping
Generates a warped image from a source view to a target view using rendered depth and viewpoint difference. Rays are sampled on a strided grid (e.g., stride 2) to create low-resolution depth maps, which are then upsampled via bilinear interpolation to full resolution for inverse warping. This enables consistency regularization without full-resolution rendering. The pixel coordinate transformation is $p_{j\to i} \sim K R_{j\to i} D_j(p_j) K^{-1} p_j$ [S16, unofficial].

### NeRF-XL Multi-GPU Volume Rendering
Scales NeRF rendering across multiple GPUs by partitioning the ray into segments processed by different GPUs. Global transmittance $T_{1 \to i}$ and color $C_{1 \to N+1}$ are maintained. Each GPU $k$ gathers segment data from other GPUs if necessary, updates the global color by adding $T_{1 \to i} \times C_{i \to i+1}$, and updates the global transmittance by multiplying $T_{1 \to i} \times T_{i \to i+1}$. During inference, data gathering is only required on a single GPU (e.g., GPU 0) to render the final image, reducing data transfer [S17].

### NeRF Volume Rendering via Numerical Quadrature
Approximates the volume rendering integral by summing contributions from sampled spheres inside the cone using numerical quadrature. This is used in methods like Tri-MipRF to handle anti-aliasing. The color is calculated as $\sum_i T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i (t_{i+1} - t_i))) c_i$ [S18].

### Raymarcher
A rendering procedure that uses an RM-LSTM to predict a single intersection point and directly outputs RGB values, skipping full volume rendering. It extracts latent features from a CNN encoder, feeds them into the RM-LSTM (512 channels, 10 steps), and predicts step lengths to update depth. The final depth point is passed through the network to get RGB. This is ~80 times faster than baseline volume rendering but has slightly lower quality (PSNR 23.02 vs 23.34) [S19, unofficial].

### 2D Neural Rendering Module
Maps a low-resolution feature map to the final predicted image using upsampling layers and a 2D convolution to alleviate multi-view inconsistent artifacts. It repeats the input feature tensor by factor 4, applies a learnable MLP, adds the results, applies PixelShuffle (scale 2), and applies a 2D Convolution with a fixed blur kernel. A 1x1 convolution is used instead of 3x3 to avoid multi-view inconsistencies. This module enables real-time rendering (>40 fps on NVIDIA 3090) compared to ~5s per frame for vanilla NeRF [S20].

### Neus SDF Volume Rendering
Represents the 3D scene as a Signed Distance Function (SDF) $f(x)$. Density is derived using a logistic distribution $\phi_b(f(x)) = \frac{b e^{-b f(x)}}{(1 + e^{-b f(x)})^2}$, where $b$ is a trainable hyperparameter. The weight $w(t)$ is normalized by the integral of the density to guarantee surface contribution. This allows precise extraction of geometric surfaces via the zero-level set of the SDF [S21].

### Depth Estimation from NeRF
Extracts 3D point-cloud data by estimating depth where transmittance changes significantly. The surface depth $t_{surface}$ is found by minimizing the first-order derivative of the transmittance function $T(t)$ with respect to $t$: $t_{surface} \approx \arg \min_t |\frac{dT(t)}{dt}|$. The 3D point is then $p = o + t_{surface} d$ [S21].

### PixelNeRF Rendering Module
Takes $N$ input images and a target camera pose to produce an approximate rendering. Input images are passed through a 2D U-Net to create feature images. Rays are cast through the target image, and 128 points are sampled along each ray (uniform up to distance 1, then linear in disparity). These 3D points are reprojected into the $N$ input cameras, and features are gathered. Positionally-encoded 3D locations and statistics are appended to form a tensor of size $64 \times 64 \times 131$ (3 RGB + 128 features) [S22].

### NeRF Rendering with Stratified Sampling
Discretizes the integral using stratified sampling. Coarse sampling uses a uniform distribution to determine object positions. Fine sampling uses volume density weights to improve detail representation. The loss is the sum of squared errors for both coarse and fine samples against the ground truth [S23].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P)$, where $N$ is the number of samples per ray and $P$ is the number of rays (pixels) processed in parallel. The neural network evaluation dominates the cost.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N \cdot P)$ to store intermediate tensors (positions, densities, colors, transmittance) for backpropagation. Chunking is often used to reduce peak memory usage [S15, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Rendering speed is highly dependent on the neural network architecture and hardware. Vanilla NeRF is slow (seconds per frame) without acceleration. Optimizations like hash embeddings or grid-based representations can achieve real-time performance (30+ fps) [S20][S19, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Differentiable, allowing end-to-end training from images. Produces high-quality, anti-aliased results with proper sampling.
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive for high-resolution renders. Sensitive to sampling strategies; uniform sampling may miss details or waste samples in empty space.
*   **When to use:** When training a NeRF model or rendering from a trained implicit radiance field.
*   **When not to use:** When real-time rendering is required without acceleration techniques, or when explicit geometry (mesh) is needed directly from the renderer.

## Background
NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) was introduced by Mildenhall et al. in 2020 [S1]. It represents a static scene as a continuous 5D function mapping spatial location and viewing direction to volume density and emitted color. The method uses differentiable volume rendering to optimize the neural network weights from multi-view images. The rendering equation is based on classical volume rendering [22] (Max, 1995) [S1]. The technique has become a foundational method in 3D vision, leading to numerous variants and applications in novel view synthesis, depth estimation, and scene reconstruction [S1, S10].
