# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: End-to-End NeRF Training, HashNeRF Training Loop, HoloINR Training Loop, NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Training

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric difference between rendered images and ground-truth observations by updating the parameters of a neural radiance field [S1][S2, unofficial][S4, unofficial]. It solves the inverse problem of reconstructing a continuous 3D scene representation from a discrete set of 2D images by repeatedly sampling rays, querying the network, and backpropagating gradients [S7, unofficial][S8]. It sets itself apart from [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md) by computing gradients for backpropagation and from [NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)](5124-nerf-rendering-alpha-compositing.md) by including the adaptive sampling and occupancy grid pruning steps specific to the training loop [S1][S2, unofficial][S8].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A dataset of $N$ training images with known camera poses (extrinsics $C2W$ and intrinsics $K$), near/far bounds, and a neural network model $f_\Theta$ (typically an MLP or hash grid) parameterized by $\Theta$.
*   **Outputs:** Optimized parameters $\Theta$ representing the radiance field.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Rays:** A batch of rays is represented as a tensor of shape $[B, 3, 3]$ or $[B, 6]$ depending on implementation, where $B$ is the batch size ($N_{rand}$). The channels typically correspond to:
    1.  Ray Origin $(o_x, o_y, o_z)$
    2.  Ray Direction $(d_x, d_y, d_z)$
    3.  Target RGB Color $(r, g, b)$ [S5, unofficial][S14, unofficial].
*   **Pre-computed Rays:** To avoid repeated camera projection, rays are often pre-computed into a flat array `rays_rgb` of shape $[(N-1) \cdot H \cdot W, 3, 3]$ (for $N$ images of resolution $H \times W$) [S5, unofficial][S14, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint:** A dictionary containing `model` (state dict), `optimizer` (state dict), and `epoch` or `step` [S10, unofficial][S11, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Load training and validation datasets [S8][S10, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the neural network parameters $\Theta$. For growing networks, initialize alpha linear weights of sub-networks to match the parent to avoid instability [S1].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam [S5, unofficial][S6, unofficial][S12] or AdamW [S13, unofficial][S16, unofficial]) and a learning rate scheduler (Exponential Decay [S5, unofficial][S18, unofficial], LambdaLR [S8][S10, unofficial], or Cosine Annealing [S11, unofficial]).
    *   Pre-compute all rays from training images into `rays_rgb` [S5, unofficial][S11, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop:** For step $i$ from 1 to $N_{iters}$:
    *   **Ray Sampling:**
        *   Select a batch of $N_{rand}$ rays from `rays_rgb` [S5, unofficial][S14, unofficial].
        *   If `use_batching` is True, slice the pre-computed array [S5, unofficial][S14, unofficial]. If False, randomly select pixel coordinates from a random image, optionally applying a central crop if $i < precrop\_iters$ [S14, unofficial].
        *   Extract ray origins, directions, and target colors into `batch_rays` and `target_s` [S5, unofficial][S14, unofficial].
    *   **Forward Pass:**
        *   Pass `batch_rays` through the rendering function (e.g., `render_rays`) [S6, unofficial][S11, unofficial].
        *   The renderer samples points along rays, queries the network for density ($\sigma$) and color ($\mathbf{c}$), and integrates them using alpha compositing to produce predicted RGB $\hat{\mathbf{C}}$ [S1][S2, unofficial][S4, unofficial].
        *   If a coarse-to-fine scheme is used, compute both coarse ($\hat{\mathbf{C}}_c$) and fine ($\hat{\mathbf{C}}_f$) predictions [S11, unofficial].
    *   **Loss Computation:**
        *   Compute the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and target colors:
            $$ \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^{B} (\hat{\mathbf{C}}_j - \mathbf{C}_j)^2 $$
            [S14, unofficial][S17, unofficial].
        *   If coarse output is available, add coarse loss: $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \mathcal{L}_{coarse}$ [S6, unofficial][S11, unofficial][S18, unofficial].
        *   Add regularization terms if applicable (e.g., sparsity, TV, Eikonal) [S7, unofficial][S8][S16, unofficial].
    *   **Backpropagation:**
        *   Compute gradients: $\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L}_{total}$ [S18, unofficial].
        *   *Pitfall:* Higher-order derivative logging (gradients, Laplacians) must occur *before* `loss.backward()` as the autograd graph is freed [S3, unofficial].
    *   **Update:**
        *   Step the optimizer: $\Theta \leftarrow \Theta - \eta \nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L}_{total}$ [S18, unofficial].
        *   Update learning rate according to the scheduler [S8][S18, unofficial].
    *   **Logging/Checkpointing:**
        *   Log loss/PSNR every `log_every` steps [S11, unofficial].
        *   Render validation images every `render_every` steps [S11, unofficial].
        *   Save checkpoint every `save_every` steps [S11, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

def nerf_training_loop(model, optimizer, scheduler, dataloader, num_iters, n_rand, device):
    """
    Standard NeRF training loop.
    
    Args:
        model: Neural Radiance Field model (MLP or HashGrid).
        optimizer: Optimizer (e.g., Adam).
        scheduler: Learning rate scheduler.
        dataloader: Iterator yielding (rays, targets) or providing ray generation utilities.
        num_iters: Total number of optimization steps.
        n_rand: Number of rays to sample per batch.
        device: Computation device.
    """
    mse_loss = nn.MSELoss()
    
    # Pre-compute rays if not done by dataloader
    # rays_rgb shape: [Total_Pixels, 3, 3] -> [Origin(3), Dir(3), Target(3)]
    # rays_rgb = precompute_rays_from_dataset(dataloader.dataset) 
    
    i_batch = 0
    
    for step in range(num_iters):
        # 1. Ray Sampling
        # Slice batch from pre-computed rays or generate on fly
        if use_batching:
            # Slice [i_batch : i_batch + n_rand]
            batch_data = rays_rgb[i_batch : i_batch + n_rand]
            i_batch += n_rand
            if i_batch >= rays_rgb.shape[0]:
                i_batch = 0
                # Shuffle rays_rgb to ensure random sampling order across epochs
                rays_rgb = rays_rgb[torch.randperm(rays_rgb.shape[0])]
        else:
            # Randomly pick image and pixels
            batch_data = generate_random_rays(dataloader.dataset, n_rand)
            
        # Split into rays (origin, dir) and targets (rgb)
        # batch_data shape: [n_rand, 3, 3]
        rays = batch_data[:, :2, :].to(device) # [n_rand, 2, 3] -> origin, dir
        targets = batch_data[:, 2, :].to(device) # [n_rand, 3] -> rgb
        
        # 2. Forward Pass
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        
        # Render rays
        # render_rays returns dict with 'rgb', and optionally 'rgb0' (coarse)
        outputs = model.render(rays)
        predicted_rgb = outputs['rgb']
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        loss = mse_loss(predicted_rgb, targets)
        
        # Add coarse loss if available
        if 'rgb0' in outputs:
            loss += mse_loss(outputs['rgb0'], targets)
            
        # Add regularization terms here if defined in model/config
        
        # 4. Backpropagation
        loss.backward()
        
        # 5. Update
        optimizer.step()
        scheduler.step()
        
        # Logging
        if step % 100 == 0:
            psnr = -10.0 * torch.log10(loss.item())
            print(f"Step {step}: Loss={loss.item():.6f}, PSNR={psnr:.2f}")
            
    return model
```

**Parameters with Typical Values:**
*   $N_{rand}$ (Batch Size): 1024 or 2048 rays per step [S5, unofficial][S11, unofficial].
*   Learning Rate ($\eta$): $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for Adam [S11, unofficial].
*   Decay: Exponential decay with `decay_steps` = $args.lrate\_decay \times 1000$ and `decay_rate` = 0.1 [S5, unofficial][S18, unofficial].
*   Positional Encoding Frequencies: 10 for position, 4 for direction [S11, unofficial].
*   Total Iterations: 100,000 to 1,000,000 [S11, unofficial][S14, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Large batches or high-resolution rays can exceed GPU memory. Mitigated by chunking ray batches during the forward pass [S5, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Growing Networks:** Randomly initializing grown sub-networks can cause density to drop to zero. Initialize sub-network weights to match the parent network [S1].
*   **Empty Checkpoints:** Loading a checkpoint with step 0 is an error condition [S15, unofficial][S15, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Graph Freeing:** Computing logging metrics that require gradients (e.g., Laplacian) must happen before `loss.backward()` [S3, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md)
Training Ray Marching and Integration is the differentiable forward pass that synthesizes RGB images from a neural radiance field for gradient-based optimization. It solves the problem of efficiently sampling 3D space along camera rays, querying a neural network for density and color, and integrating these values via alpha compositing to produce a loss-computable image. It sets itself apart from [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md) by computing gradients for backpropagation and from [NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)](5124-nerf-rendering-alpha-compositing.md) by including the adaptive sampling and occupancy grid pruning steps specific to the training loop.

### [Training Configuration](2332-training-configuration.md)
Training Configuration defines the static and dynamic hyperparameters, optimizer settings, and initialization procedures required to optimize a radiance field model. It establishes baseline settings (MLP depth, width, positional encoding frequencies, and hierarchical sampling counts) against which variants adjust specific architectural or optimization details. It differs from [Training Loop Setup](1522-training-loop-setup.md) by focusing on numerical values and mathematical schedules rather than procedural orchestration, and from [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by defining parameters that control sampling density and loss weighting rather than the integration algorithm itself [G].

### [Mip-NeRF 360](3306-mip-nerf-360.md)
Mip-NeRF 360 is a method for synthesizing photorealistic views of unbounded, real-world scenes where the camera may rotate 360 degrees and content exists at arbitrary distances. It solves the problems of aliasing, training instability, and geometric ambiguity inherent in standard NeRF when applied to large-scale environments. It sets itself apart from its parent [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by integrating a non-linear scene parameterization, online distillation between two MLPs, and a distortion-based regularizer, achieving a 57% reduction in mean squared error compared to standard mip-NeRF.

### [Mip-NeRF 360 Sampling and Training](3393-mip-nerf-360-sampling-and-training.md)
Mip-NeRF 360 Sampling and Training extends the Mip-NeRF framework to unbounded, 360-degree scenes by introducing a disparity-based ray parameterization, a contraction function for coordinate space, and a coarse-to-fine training loop with a proposal network. It solves the artifacts of "floaters" and "background collapse" common in unbounded scene reconstruction by regularizing the density distribution and ensuring efficient sampling through online distillation. It differs from standard NeRF by using linear-in-disparity sampling and from basic Mip-NeRF by handling unbounded scenes via coordinate contraction and disparity parameterization.

### [Neural Volume Scene Setup](4168-neural-volume-scene-setup.md)
Constructs a Mitsuba scene containing a volumetric cube and a custom neural radiance field integrator, initializing the density grid and spherical harmonics coefficients. It differs from generic training setups by directly binding the neural network parameters to the scene graph's heterogeneous volume and integrator components.

### [MVSNeRF](6197-mvsnerf.md)
MVSNeRF is a method for fast, generalizable radiance field reconstruction from multi-view stereo (MVS) images. Unlike standard NeRF which requires per-scene optimization, MVSNeRF uses a feedforward neural network to predict the radiance field directly from input images, significantly reducing training time and iteration counts. It sets itself apart by learning a data prior that accelerates convergence, enabling rapid inference without iterative scene-specific optimization.

### [Dynamic and Temporal Modeling](7610-dynamic-and-temporal-modeling.md)
Dynamic and Temporal Modeling extends static Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) to handle scenes that change over time, enabling the synthesis of novel views from video sequences or non-line-of-sight (NLOS) transient data. It solves the problem of temporal coherence and motion representation by either explicitly modeling deformation fields, implicitly tracking correspondence via color statistics, or modeling light transport in the time domain. It differs from static NeRF by introducing a time dimension or temporal dependencies, and from sibling methods like [Mip-NeRF 360](3306-mip-nerf-360.md) which focuses on spherical panoramas, by specifically addressing the 4D $(x, y, z, t)$ or 5D $(x, y, z, \theta, \phi, t)$ nature of dynamic data.

### [Camera Pose and Parameter Optimization](7611-camera-pose-and-parameter-optimization.md)
Camera Pose and Parameter Optimization addresses the problem of inaccurate or unknown initial camera extrinsics and intrinsics in Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) training. Standard NeRF assumes precise ground-truth poses; when these are noisy or missing, reconstruction quality degrades significantly. This method sets itself apart by treating camera parameters as differentiable variables within the training loop, allowing joint optimization with the radiance field. Unlike [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) which assumes fixed poses, this procedure iteratively refines the pose to minimize rendering loss. Key variants include [BARF (Bundle-Adjusting Neural Radiance Fields)](6872-barf-bundle-adjusting-neural-radiance-fields.md) for coarse-to-fine pose refinement and [NeRF with Unknown Camera Parameters](7405-nerf-with-unknown-camera-parameters.md) for joint optimization from sparse views.

### [MegaNeRF](8580-meganerf.md)
Mega-NeRF is a framework for training large-scale 3D scenes that support interactive human-in-the-loop fly-throughs, addressing challenges of modeling thousands of images with varying lighting, large model capacities, and fast rendering. It differs from its siblings by using spatial partitioning to enable data parallelism for scalable training, whereas methods like DeRF and KiloNeRF use partitioning to speed up inference of a pre-trained single network. It improves upon [NeRF++](6197-nerf-pp.md) by modeling the foreground with an ellipsoid that tightly encloses camera poses and adding altitude constraints to skip underground sampling.

### [BlockNeRF](8581-blocknerf.md)
BlockNeRF is a method that decomposes a large-scale scene into a set of spatial blocks for separate NeRF training using a 'total-part-total' approach to solve large-scale city building complex rendering problems. It decouples rendering time from scene size to allow rendering to scale to arbitrarily large environments. It sets itself apart from standard NeRF by partitioning the scene geometry and appearance into localized MLPs, addressing the high-frequency information loss and slow inference inherent in single-model approaches for urban-scale data.

### [Specific Model Architectures](8585-specific-model-architectures.md)
Specific Model Architectures refers to the family of neural network structures used to represent the radiance field in differentiable volume rendering, extending the standard single Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) baseline. These architectures solve problems related to computational efficiency, sparse input reconstruction, dynamic scene modeling, and generative control by modifying the network topology, input encoding, or training objectives. They are distinguished from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by focusing on the internal representation of the scene function $f_\Theta: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \rightarrow (\sigma, \mathbf{c})$ rather than the sampling or integration strategy.

### [Regularization and Loss Functions](8586-regularization-and-loss-functions.md)
Regularization and Loss Functions define the objective landscape for optimizing Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), addressing the severe under-constraint problem where limited input views lead to geometric artifacts like "floaters" or degenerate solutions. This family sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by focusing on the gradient signals derived from the rendered output rather than the sampling strategy itself, and from [Training Configuration](2332-training-configuration.md) by providing the specific mathematical terms that guide the optimizer.

### [Progressive Inpainting-and-Erasing](8658-progressive-inpainting-and-erasing.md)
Progressive Inpainting-and-Erasing (PERF) is a training strategy for single-view NeRF that avoids geometry conflicts between visible and invisible regions by computing a mask of conflicted regions and eliminating them from NeRF training. It sets itself apart from standard single-view NeRF methods by integrating a collaborative RGBD inpainting step with a progressive erasing mechanism to handle the ambiguity of unseen geometry, building on the [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by dynamically expanding the reference view set.

### [NeRFVS](8667-nerfvs.md)
NeRFVS is a method for indoor scene free view synthesis (FVS) that uses a geometry scaffold from an off-the-shelf neural reconstruction method to guide NeRF optimization, reducing distortions and floaters in extrapolation. It enables NeRF to perform free navigation and extrapolation for indoor scenes by using a neural-reconstructed geometric scaffold to guide optimization, boosted by depth and variance constraints with dynamical adjustment based on view coverage. Unlike standard NeRF, which struggles with extrapolation and sparse views, NeRFVS minimizes per-pixel loss to avoid distortions, unlike SVS which suffers from noticeable artifacts.

### [NeRF-FF](8669-nerf-ff.md)
NeRF-FF is a plug-in strategy that mitigates the degradation of Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) reconstruction quality caused by out-of-focus (defocus blurred) regions in input images. It solves the problem of training NeRFs on partially blurred data by estimating binary image masks that identify in-focus regions, allowing the subsequent NeRF model to omit out-of-focus pixels during training. Unlike methods that use dynamic ray generation or blur kernels, NeRF-FF relies on static ray assumptions, making it compatible with runtime-optimized NeRF variants like Instant-NGP, nerfacto, and splatfacto.

### [KNN-NeRF](8672-knn-nerf.md)
KNN-NeRF is a baseline method for training Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) using sparse 3D point clouds as supervision instead of multi-view images. It solves the problem of generating ground-truth color labels for rays that do not pass through existing points by assigning the color of the nearest point in the cloud to the ray. Unlike standard NeRF which relies on pixel colors from images, KNN-NeRF constructs a dense training signal from sparse geometry, though it struggles with sharp object borders due to the low-frequency bias of neural networks and the sparsity of the input.

### [Hard Sample Mining](8673-hard-sample-mining.md)
Hard Sample Mining (HSM) is an optimization procedure for neural radiance fields that improves training efficiency and reconstruction quality by selectively updating the network based on pixel-level error. It solves the problem of uniform computational waste in standard NeRF training, where samples contributing little to the gradient are treated equally to those with high error. Unlike sibling methods such as [Probability-based Ray Sampling with Quadtree Subdivision](7481-probability-based-ray-sampling-with-quadtree-subdivision.md) or [Nerfacc](https://github.com/NVlabs/nerfacc) which prune low-weight samples based on density or transmittance, HSM ranks samples using the propagated pixel loss to subsample a "hard" minibatch that retains most of the update information.

### [Depth and Geometry Supervision](8727-depth-and-geometry-supervision.md)
Depth and Geometry Supervision refers to a family of training procedures for Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) that incorporate explicit geometric constraints—typically derived from monocular depth estimators, Structure-from-Motion (SfM) keypoints, or multi-view consistency errors—to resolve the shape-radiance ambiguity inherent in volume rendering. Unlike standard NeRF, which relies solely on photometric consistency (RGB loss) and often produces plausible colors with incorrect geometry, these methods enforce geometric plausibility by either guiding the sampling intervals along rays, supervising the rendered depth distribution against a prior, or initializing the scene with estimated depth maps. This family is distinct from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) (which defines the integration mechanics) and [Regularization and Loss Functions](8586-regularization-and-loss-functions.md) (which defines general penalties) by specifically targeting the geometric fidelity of the implicit surface representation using external or derived depth information.

### [Sparse NeRF Reconstruction with Geometric and Semantic Initialization](8779-sparse-nerf-reconstruction-with-geometric-and-semantic-initi.md)
This method addresses the blurred geometry, loss of detail, and slow convergence typical of NeRF training when using sparse input (15–20 images). It distinguishes itself from standard NeRF initialization by decoupling geometric structure anchoring via COLMAP point clouds from semantic-guided weight assignment via Mask R-CNN, thereby reducing initial MSE loss and accelerating convergence.

### [NeRF-ID](8867-nerf-id.md)
NeRF-ID (NeRF in detail) is a differentiable volume rendering optimization method that replaces the heuristic coarse-to-fine sampling procedure of vanilla NeRF with a learnable "proposer" module. It solves the problem of suboptimal sample placement by enabling end-to-end training of the coarse network, proposer, and fine network jointly, thereby improving view synthesis quality. Unlike its sibling [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) which relies on handcrafted probability density functions derived from coarse densities, NeRF-ID uses a neural network to directly predict fine sample locations from coarse features.

### [Progressive and Dynamic Scene Growth](8869-progressive-and-dynamic-scene-growth.md)
Progressive and Dynamic Scene Growth addresses the challenge of reconstructing large-scale or complex 3D scenes from limited input data by incrementally expanding the neural representation's capacity or the training dataset. Unlike static NeRF architectures that require pre-defining the entire hierarchy or fixed network size, these methods adaptively grow the model structure or add synthetic reference views during training to resolve occlusions and capture high-frequency details. This family distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [Adaptive Ray Sampling](8870-adaptive-ray-sampling.md) (which focuses on ray selection) and [Sparse NeRF Reconstruction with Geometric and Semantic Initialization](8779-sparse-nerf-reconstruction-with-geometric-and-semantic-initi.md) (which relies on external priors) by actively modifying the model's topology or the set of supervision signals to improve reconstruction quality in complex regions.

### [Adaptive Ray Sampling](8870-adaptive-ray-sampling.md)
Adaptive Ray Sampling refers to a family of strategies in neural radiance field (NeRF) training that dynamically adjust the distribution of sample points along viewing rays to improve reconstruction quality and training efficiency. Unlike uniform sampling, which treats all regions of a ray equally, these methods allocate computational resources to areas of high geometric or photometric complexity, such as object boundaries or high-frequency details. This family addresses the inefficiency of uniform sampling in sparse or complex scenes by clustering samples where they contribute most to the final rendered image.

### ReconFusion
ReconFusion uses a Diffusion Prior to constrain unobserved views during the training loop [S19, unofficial].

### Supersampling Baseline
Supersampling Baseline is a baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S20]. For every pixel, it casts multiple jittered rays sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel, renders each ray with NeRF, and uses the mean of those rendered values as the predicted color in the loss function [S20]. Oracle Scale Information is a pitfall, as the full-resolution supersampling baseline relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets [S20]. Performance: The 16x supersampling baseline performs worse than mip-NeRF and is significantly more expensive during both training and evaluation [S20].

### Two-stage training for proposers
Two-stage training for proposers is a training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S21, unofficial]. Phase 1 trains the learnt proposer to mimic the heuristic proposer by minimizing the L2 matching loss between their proposal sets [S21, unofficial]. Phase 2 trains all network components end-to-end, resetting the optimizer state and performing a linear warmup for 1,000 SGD steps to reduce shock from switching regimes [S21, unofficial]. Training from scratch sometimes works but often fails; two-stage training is consistently effective [S21, unofficial].

### ViP-NeRF
ViP-NeRF introduces visibility priors to supervise density and improve rendering quality under sparse input view conditions (fewer than 50-100 views), preventing artifacts caused by insufficient training data [S22]. It creates a plane scan volume by warping different depth planes of a secondary view image to the main view, compares warped planes with the main view to obtain an error map, identifies the minimum error among all planes, and thresholds this error to obtain a visibility prior map corresponding to the primary view image [S22]. This map is used to supervise the density during NeRF training [S22]. On the RealEstate-10K dataset, ViP-NeRF achieves a PSNR index 3-4 points higher than the latest paper in 2, 3, and 4 input views [S22].

### Coarse-Fine Consistency Loss
Coarse-Fine Consistency Loss is a loss function that enforces consistency between depth estimates of the coarse and fine NeRF models to prevent ineffective hierarchical sampling and blur [S23]. The formula is:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{cfc} = \mathbb{1}_{m_f=1} \odot \|z_c - \hat{\nabla}(z_f)\|^2 + \mathbb{1}_{m_c=1} \odot \|\hat{\nabla}(z_c) - z_f\|^2 $$
where $z_c$ and $z_f$ are coarse and fine depths, and $m_c/m_f$ are reliability masks determined by reprojection [S23].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples})$, where $N_{iters}$ is total iterations, $N_{rand}$ is batch size, and $N_{samples}$ is points per ray [S5, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot C)$ for the forward pass, where $C$ is channel size. Pre-computed rays add $O(N_{images} \cdot H \cdot W)$ [S5, unofficial][S14, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Training on 128x128 images for 250 epochs [S4, unofficial]. On a dataset of 199 images, 5 epochs with batch size 1024 results in ~39,000 total iterations [S17, unofficial].
*   **Scaling:** Batch size trade-offs exist; larger batches (e.g., $2^{14}$) hinder data loading performance, while smaller batches ($2^{10}$) cap iterations per second [S15, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Stochastic optimization allows training on large datasets by sampling subsets of rays [S5, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** VRAM overflow is a common pitfall, requiring chunking [S5, unofficial]. Blurry predictions can result from coarse voxel grid resolution if not using advanced sampling [S17, unofficial].

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop was introduced in the seminal NeRF paper (Mildenhall et al., 2020) [S5, unofficial]. It established the standard paradigm of optimizing a coordinate MLP via stochastic gradient descent on ray samples. Subsequent works like HashNeRF [S7, unofficial], Mip-NeRF 360 [S1], and various regularization techniques have refined this loop, but the core structure of ray sampling, forward rendering, MSE loss, and Adam optimization remains consistent [S1][S2, unofficial][S4, unofficial][S5, unofficial][S6, unofficial][S7, unofficial][S8][S9, unofficial][S10, unofficial][S11, unofficial][S12][S13, unofficial][S14, unofficial][S15, unofficial][S16, unofficial][S17, unofficial][S18, unofficial].
