# Neural SAR Surface Reconstruction via Inverse Rendering

Also called: Differentiable SAR Rendering, Neural DSM Reconstruction

## Overview
This method reconstructs a Digital Surface Model (DSM) and a backscattering coefficient map from Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) images by minimizing the difference between observed SAR intensities and synthesized intensities using a differentiable rendering model [S1]. It models elevation $Z$ and backscattering $B$ within a bounding box using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), reducing sampling degrees of freedom from a 3D volume to a 2D surface [S1]. Compared to standard volume rendering, this surface representation is more constrained, leading to faster and easier reconstruction of surfaces [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ SAR images with known acquisition parameters (azimuth, range, incidence angles). Each image has dimensions $N_A \times N_R$ (azimuth cells $\times$ range cells).
*   **Outputs:** A continuous elevation field $Z(x, y)$ and a backscattering coefficient field $B(x, y)$ defined over a 2D ground plane.

**Data Structures:**
*   **MLP:** A fully-connected network.
    *   **Input:** 2D position $(x, y)$ on the ground.
    *   **Encoding:** The input $(x, y)$ is encoded using Instant NGP’s positional encoding $\gamma_P$ [S1].
    *   **Output:** Two values: Elevation $Z(x, y)$ and Backscattering coefficient $B(x, y)$.
*   **Bounding Box:** A 3D box of side length $L$ defines the search space for the surface [S1].

**Rendering Model:**
The method assumes a surface-based scattering model. For a sample point on the surface, the scattering coefficient $\sigma^0_k$ is calculated as:
$$ \sigma^0_k(\vec{u}_k) = B(P_{k+1/2}) \cdot |\vec{u}_k \cdot \vec{n}_k| $$
where:
*   $B(P_{k+1/2})$ is the backscattering coefficient at the midpoint $P_{k+1/2}$ of the sample interval.
*   $\vec{u}_k$ is the view direction vector (local incidence unit vector pointing from the sensor to the sample).
*   $\vec{n}_k$ is the surface normal vector deduced from the local geometry (e.g., a quadrilateral patch) [S1].

**Training Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Define the bounding box side length $L$ and initialize the MLP parameters.
2.  **Batch Selection:** For each minibatch, select a random subset of $M$ lines across all $N$ views. $M > 80$ is used to ensure adequate sample distribution while meeting memory constraints [S1].
3.  **Sampling:**
    *   Sample $K$ points along the selected ground ranges.
    *   Shift the sampled ground ranges by a random value along their line of sight to avoid over-fitting at specific coordinates [S1].
    *   Apply Nerfies’ windowing on frequency bands on the output of positional encoding $\gamma_P$ to regulate frequency bias during early training [S1].
    *   Determine the target maximum frequency level $l$ for each sample $k$ of ground range $X_k$ such that $l$ corresponds to $|X_{k+1} - X_{k-1}| / 4$ [S1].
4.  **Forward Pass (Differentiable Rendering):**
    *   Query the MLP for $Z(x, y)$ and $B(x, y)$ at the sampled $(x, y)$ coordinates.
    *   Compute the surface normal $\vec{n}_k$ from the elevation field.
    *   Compute the scattering coefficient $\sigma^0_k$ using the formula above.
    *   Render the synthesized intensity $\hat{I}$ by integrating the scattering coefficients along the ray path (standard volume rendering integration adapted for surface samples) [S1].
5.  **Loss Computation:**
    *   Compute the negative log-likelihood loss $L_D$ on the log-intensities:
        $$ L_D(\hat{I}) = \frac{1}{M \cdot N_R} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \sum_{n=1}^{N_R} \left( \frac{I_{m,n}}{\hat{I}_{m,n}} + \log \left( \frac{\hat{I}_{m,n}}{I_{m,n}} \right) \right) $$
        where $I_{m,n}$ is the observed intensity and $\hat{I}_{m,n}$ is the synthesized intensity [S1].
    *   Compute the Total Variation regularization loss $L_{TV}$ on the predicted backscattering coefficients $B$ to penalize over-fitting and favor geometry fitting [S1].
    *   Total Loss: $L = L_D + \lambda L_{TV}$, with $\lambda = 0.1$ [S1].
6.  **Optimization:** Update MLP parameters using the Adam optimizer [S1].
7.  **Coarse-to-Fine Annealing:**
    *   Gradually increase the number of samples $K$ and adjust accuracy $\mu$ by annealing a subsampling factor $\beta$ from an initial value $\beta_0$ to 1.
    *   Parameters are updated as $K = K_f / \beta$ and $\mu = \mu_f \cdot \beta$, where $K_f$ and $\mu_f$ are target final values [S1].

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

class SARSurfaceMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_dim=256, num_layers=3):
        super().__init__()
        # Instant NGP positional encoding is assumed to be applied externally or as a wrapper
        # Here we define the core MLP structure
        layers = []
        for i in range(num_layers):
            layers.append(nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim))
            layers.append(nn.ReLU())
        layers.append(nn.Linear(hidden_dim, 2)) # Output: [B, Z]
        self.net = nn.Sequential(*layers)
        
    def forward(self, x_encoded):
        out = self.net(x_encoded)
        B = torch.exp(out[:, 0:1]) # Exponential activation for positive B
        Z = (L / 8.0) * torch.atan(out[:, 1:2]) # Bounded activation for Z
        return B, Z

def compute_normal(Z, x, y, eps=1e-4):
    """Compute surface normal from elevation grid."""
    # Central differences
    dz_dx = (Z[..., 1:, 1:] - Z[..., :-1, 1:]) / (x[..., 1:, 1:] - x[..., :-1, 1:])
    dz_dy = (Z[..., 1:, 1:] - Z[..., 1:, :-1]) / (y[..., 1:, 1:] - y[..., 1:, :-1])
    # Normal vector [dx, dy, -1] normalized
    normal = torch.stack([dz_dx, dz_dy, torch.ones_like(dz_dx)], dim=-1)
    normal = normal / (torch.norm(normal, dim=-1, keepdim=True) + eps)
    return normal

def render_sar_line(samples_x, samples_y, samples_z, view_direction, B_field, Z_field):
    """
    Render a single SAR line (range profile).
    samples_x, samples_y, samples_z: Coordinates of samples along the ray.
    view_direction: Unit vector u_k.
    B_field, Z_field: Interpolated fields or MLP queries.
    """
    # Query B and Z at sample locations
    # In practice, Z is used to find the surface intersection or samples are on the surface
    # Here we assume samples are on the surface Z(x,y)
    
    # Compute normals at sample points
    # Note: In the paper, normals are deduced from quadrilateral patches or local geometry
    # For simplicity, we assume we have normals n_k pre-computed or interpolated
    normals = compute_normals_at_points(samples_x, samples_y, Z_field)
    
    # Scattering coefficient
    # sigma0 = B * |u . n|
    dot_product = torch.sum(view_direction * normals, dim=-1)
    sigma0 = B_field * torch.abs(dot_product)
    
    # Integration (simplified trapezoidal or Riemann sum along the ray)
    # The paper mentions rendering a row all at once. 
    # Standard volume rendering: I = sum(w_k * sigma0_k)
    # Weights w_k depend on the distance between samples
    # This step requires specific integration logic not fully detailed in the snippet,
    # but typically involves alpha compositing or simple summation for thin surfaces.
    # [G] Assuming simple summation for surface scattering as per "surface representation"
    intensity = torch.sum(sigma0) 
    return intensity

def train_step(mlp, optimizer, batch_data, L, lambda_tv):
    """
    batch_data: dict containing 'images' (I), 'views' (parameters), 'lines_indices'
    """
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    
    synthesized_images = []
    observed_images = []
    
    for m, line_idx in enumerate(batch_data['lines_indices']):
        view_params = batch_data['views'][line_idx]
        # Get ground range samples for this line
        # Shift ground ranges by random value along line of sight
        x_samples, y_samples = get_shifted_ground_samples(view_params)
        
        # Query MLP
        # Apply Nerfies windowing to positional encoding
        pos_enc = get_pos_enc(x_samples, y_samples)
        pos_enc = apply_nerfies_windowing(pos_enc)
        
        B, Z = mlp(pos_enc)
        
        # Compute normals
        normals = compute_normal(Z, x_samples, y_samples)
        
        # Compute scattering
        u_k = view_params['view_direction']
        dot_prod = torch.abs(torch.sum(u_k * normals, dim=-1))
        sigma0 = B * dot_prod
        
        # Integrate to get intensity
        # [G] Specific integration weights w_k are not detailed, assuming uniform or distance-based
        intensity = torch.sum(sigma0) # Simplified
        
        synthesized_images.append(intensity)
        observed_images.append(batch_data['images'][line_idx])
        
    synthesized_images = torch.stack(synthesized_images)
    observed_images = torch.stack(observed_images)
    
    # Loss: Negative Log-Likelihood
    # LD = (1/M*NR) * sum( I/Î + log(Î/I) )
    # Note: The formula in the paper uses I/Î + log(Î/I). 
    # This is equivalent to the Kullback-Leibler divergence for Poisson-like noise.
    loss_d = torch.mean(observed_images / synthesized_images + torch.log(synthesized_images / observed_images))
    
    # Loss: Total Variation on B
    # [G] Standard TV regularization
    loss_tv = torch.mean(torch.abs(B[1:] - B[:-1]))
    
    total_loss = loss_d + lambda_tv * loss_tv
    total_loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    
    return total_loss.item()

**Parameters:**
*   $L$: Side length of the bounding box. Not given in the sources.
*   $\lambda$: Weight for Total Variation regularization, set to 0.1 [S1].
*   $M$: Number of lines per minibatch, $M > 80$ [S1].
*   $\beta$: Subsampling factor, annealed from $\beta_0$ to 1 [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Few Views:** Reconstruction from only ascending/descending passes (2 views) shows higher RMSE due to a grainier heightmap caused by slight over-fitting to speckle, resulting from reduced speckle realizations per spatial position [S1].
*   **Shadows vs. Water:** Cliffs create large shadow areas similar to submerged areas. The model does not confuse them: submerged areas are reconstructed with low backscattering coefficients, while cliff shadows are handled correctly [S1].
*   **Material Complexity:** The method uses a simple cosine hypothesis for backscattering. It may not capture complex material properties (e.g., Mangia model) as precisely as physically-based simulators, but it separates geometry and appearance effectively [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** The method reduces sampling degrees of freedom from 3 (volume) to 2 (surface), leading to faster and easier reconstruction compared to volume rendering [S1].
*   **Performance:**
    *   Forez scene (5 views, 75m resolution, uniform material): RMSE 36.7m [S1].
    *   Forez scene (2 views, 75m resolution, uniform material): RMSE 52.9m [S1].
    *   Island scene (5 views, 0.75m resolution, multiple materials): RMSE 4.72m [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Efficient reconstruction from few views; separates geometry from texture; handles shadows and water correctly; robust to speckle noise via negative log-likelihood loss.
*   **Limitations:** RMSE increases with fewer views; simple backscattering model may not capture complex material interactions; resolution limited by the bounding box and sampling strategy.

## Background
The method was introduced in a paper by [S1] in 2025. It builds on the concept of inverse rendering for SAR, adapting techniques from NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) to the specific domain of Synthetic Aperture Radar. It aims to retrieve DSMs from only a few SAR images, demonstrating the ability to separate geometry and texture. The approach is compared to Radar Fields [14] and EMPRISE® simulations [S1].
