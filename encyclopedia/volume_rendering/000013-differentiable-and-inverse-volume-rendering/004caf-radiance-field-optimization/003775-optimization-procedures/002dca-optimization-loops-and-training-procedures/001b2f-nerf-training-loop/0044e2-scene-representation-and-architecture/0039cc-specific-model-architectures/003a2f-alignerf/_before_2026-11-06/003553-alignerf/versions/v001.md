# AligNeRF

Also called: Alignment-Aware Training for NeRF
## Overview
AligNeRF is a training strategy for Neural Radiance Fields that addresses image misalignment caused by moving objects or camera calibration errors, while simultaneously improving high-frequency detail recovery in high-resolution settings [S1]. It distinguishes itself from standard NeRF training by introducing a convolution-augmented architecture and a staged optimization process that switches from random ray sampling to patch-based sampling with an alignment-aware loss, allowing the model to leverage 2D local neighborhood information [S1].
## How it works
AligNeRF builds on [Mip-NeRF 360 Optimization Setup](3426-mip-nerf-360-optimization-setup.md) by modifying the network architecture and the training loop to support patch-based processing and alignment awareness.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** Camera poses, intrinsics, and images. The method operates on patches of pixels rather than individual rays.
*   **Outputs:** Rendered RGB images. Internally, the network outputs a feature vector of size $N$ per sample point, which is later projected to RGB.

### Data Structures and Network Layout
The core scene representation remains a coordinate-to-value mapping function $F_\Theta: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \rightarrow (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$, but with specific modifications:
1.  **MLP Output:** The final layer of the MLP outputs $N$ channels of feature space data instead of 3 (RGB) channels. $N$ is a hyperparameter (value not explicitly given in sources, but implied to be larger than 3) [S1].
2.  **Convolutional Network:** A 3-layer convolutional network is appended after the volumetric rendering function.
    *   **Kernels:** $3 \times 3$ filters.
    *   **Activations:** ReLU.
    *   **Padding:** "Reflectance" padding is used for inputs to prevent checkerboard artifacts. Padding values are removed from the outputs [S1].
3.  **Projection Layer:** A feed-forward perceptron layer at the end of the CNN converts the feature space representation back to RGB space [S1].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Staged Training Initialization:**
    *   **Stage 1 (Pre-training):** Train the model using the standard "normal" pre-training procedure (as defined in the parent [Mip-NeRF 360 Optimization Setup](3426-mip-nerf-360-optimization-setup.md)) using random ray sampling. This establishes a baseline scene representation [S1].

2.  **Architecture Modification:**
    *   Change the MLP's output channels from 3 to $N$ [S1].
    *   Insert the 3-layer CNN (with reflectance padding and output cropping) after the volumetric rendering step [S1].
    *   Add the final perceptron layer to map features to RGB [S1].

3.  **Switch to Patch-Based Sampling:**
    *   Replace random ray sampling with patch-based sampling.
    *   Gather a local image region (patch) during each iteration.
    *   **Parameter:** The rendered patch size is $32 \times 32$ pixels [S1].

4.  **Feature-Space Integration:**
    *   Perform numerical integration along the ray in the $N$-dimensional feature space rather than in RGB space. This gathers richer representation per ray [S1].

5.  **Alignment-Aware Fine-Tuning:**
    *   **Stage 2 (Fine-tuning):** Resume training with the modified architecture and patch-based sampling.
    *   **Ground Truth Patch Sampling:** Sample a larger ground truth patch $G$ of size $48 \times 48$ pixels for each iteration [S1].
    *   **Rendered Patch:** Render a smaller patch $R$ of size $32 \times 32$ pixels [S1].
    *   **Alignment Search:** Search over every possible subpatch $G_i$ of $G$ to find the best match with the rendered patch $R$. Since $G$ is $48 \times 48$ and $R$ is $32 \times 32$, there are multiple possible alignments (subpatches) within $G$ [S1].
    *   **Loss Calculation:** Compute the loss using the alignment-aware function $L_{PM}(G, R)$ defined below.

### Formulas

The alignment-aware loss function is:

$$
L_{PM}(G, R) = \min_{i=1,...,N} \left( D(G_i, R) + \lambda \cdot D_{coord}(G_i, R) \right)
$$

Where:
*   $G$ is the ground truth patch ($48 \times 48$).
*   $R$ is the rendered patch ($32 \times 32$).
*   $G_i$ is a subpatch of $G$ aligned with $R$.
*   $N$ is the number of possible subpatches (determined by the size difference between $G$ and $R$).
*   $D(G_i, R)$ is the Euclidean distance (L2 norm) between the subpatch and the rendered patch:
    $$
    D(G_i, R) = \| G_i - R \|_2
    $$
*   $D_{coord}(G_i, R)$ is a regularization term based on the Euclidean distance of the coordinate offset:
    $$
    D_{coord}(G_i, R) = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}
    $$
    Where $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are the horizontal and vertical offsets between $G_i$ and $R$ [S1].
*   $\lambda$ is a regularization weight.
    *   **Parameter:** $\lambda = 0.01$ [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
def alignerf_training_loop(model, images, poses, intrinsics, num_iterations):
    """
    AligNeRF training loop.
    model: MLP with N output channels + CNN + Perceptron.
    images: List of ground truth images.
    poses: Camera poses.
    intrinsics: Camera intrinsics.
    """
    
    # Stage 1: Normal Pre-training
    # Use standard random ray sampling and RGB output loss.
    # See [Mip-NeRF 360 Optimization Setup](3426-mip-nerf-360-optimization-setup.md) for details.
    for iteration in range(pre_train_iterations):
        rays = sample_random_rays(poses, intrinsics, batch_size)
        rgb, sigma = model.forward(rays)
        loss = compute_rgb_loss(rgb, rays.gt_rgb)
        optimizer.step(loss)
        
    # Stage 2: Alignment-Aware Fine-Tuning
    for iteration in range(fine_tune_iterations):
        # 1. Patch-Based Sampling
        # Sample a larger ground truth patch G (48x48)
        gt_patch_coords = sample_patch_coordinates(images, size=(48, 48))
        G = extract_patch(images, gt_patch_coords)
        
        # 2. Render Smaller Patch R (32x32)
        # Sample rays corresponding to the 32x32 region within G
        render_coords = get_center_subpatch_coords(gt_patch_coords, size=(32, 32))
        rays = rays_from_coordinates(render_coords, poses, intrinsics)
        
        # 3. Volumetric Rendering in Feature Space
        # Model outputs N-dimensional features
        features, sigma = model.forward(rays)
        
        # 4. Numerical Integration in Feature Space
        # Integrate along ray to get feature vector F
        F = integrate_features(features, sigma, rays)
        
        # 5. CNN Processing
        # Reshape F to image-like tensor for CNN
        F_img = reshape_to_image(F, height=32, width=32)
        
        # Apply 3-layer CNN with 3x3 kernels, ReLU, reflectance padding
        # Remove padding from output to prevent checkerboard artifacts
        F_processed = apply_cnn(F_img)
        
        # 6. Project to RGB
        R = apply_perceptron(F_processed) # R is 32x32 RGB
        
        # 7. Alignment-Aware Loss Calculation
        # Search for best matching subpatch Gi in G
        best_loss = infinity
        best_i = None
        
        # Iterate over all valid subpatches Gi of G that match R's size (32x32)
        # G is 48x48, R is 32x32. Possible offsets allow sliding window.
        for i in range(number_of_subpatches):
            Gi = extract_subpatch(G, index=i, size=(32, 32))
            
            # D(Gi, R) = L2 norm
            dist_rgb = l2_norm(Gi - R)
            
            # D_coord(Gi, R) = Euclidean distance of offset
            offset_x, offset_y = get_offset(i)
            dist_coord = sqrt(offset_x**2 + offset_y**2)
            
            # Lambda = 0.01
            lambda_val = 0.01
            total_loss = dist_rgb + lambda_val * dist_coord
            
            if total_loss < best_loss:
                best_loss = total_loss
                best_i = i
                
        loss = best_loss
        optimizer.step(loss)
```

### Parameters
*   **Patch Size (Rendered):** $32 \times 32$ pixels [S1].
*   **Patch Size (Ground Truth):** $48 \times 48$ pixels [S1].
*   **Regularization Weight ($\lambda$):** $0.01$ [S1].
*   **MLP Output Channels ($N$):** Not given in the sources.
*   **CNN Layers:** 3 layers [S1].
*   **CNN Kernel Size:** $3 \times 3$ [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Checkerboard Artifacts:** The use of convolutional networks can introduce checkerboard artifacts. AligNeRF mitigates this by using "reflectance" padding for inputs and explicitly removing padding values in the outputs [S1].
*   **Multiview Inconsistency:** Because the CNN is shallow and does not perform upsampling, multiview inconsistency is not observed [S1].
*   **Blurry Renderings:** If the rendered patch $R$ is very blurry, it may match multiple subpatches $G_i$ equally well. The coordinate regularization term $D_{coord}$ penalizes large offsets, helping to resolve this ambiguity [S1].
*   **Precision:** Numerical integration is performed in feature space ($N$ channels) rather than RGB space to gather richer representation [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The alignment-aware loss requires searching over all possible subpatches $G_i$ of the ground truth patch $G$ for each rendered patch $R$. This adds a computational overhead proportional to the number of subpatches during the fine-tuning stage.
*   **Memory Complexity:** The method requires storing the larger ground truth patch $G$ ($48 \times 48$) and the rendered patch $R$ ($32 \times 32$) in memory during each iteration, along with the intermediate feature maps for the CNN.
*   **Strengths:** Improves high-frequency detail recovery and handles misalignment from moving objects or camera calibration errors [S1]. It is an easy-to-plug-in component for NeRF-like models [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires a staged training process (pre-training followed by fine-tuning) [S1]. The alignment search adds complexity to the loss computation [S1].
*   **When to use:** When dealing with complex unbounded real-world scenes that may have moving objects or calibration errors, and when high-resolution detail is critical [S1].
*   **When not to use:** If computational resources are extremely limited and the staged training with patch-based sampling is too expensive, or if the scene is static and well-calibrated with no need for high-frequency detail enhancement.

## Background
AligNeRF was introduced by Yifan Jiang, Peter Hedman, Ben Mildenhall, Dejia Xu, Jonathan T. Barron, Zhangyang Wang, and Tianfan Xue in the paper "AligNeRF: High-Fidelity Neural Radiance Fields via Alignment-Aware Training" [S1]. The work was presented at CVPR 2023 [S1]. It builds upon mip-NeRF 360, which is the state-of-the-art NeRF method for complex unbounded real-world scenes at the time [S1]. The method addresses obstacles related to misalignment and low-frequency bias in standard NeRF models [S1].
