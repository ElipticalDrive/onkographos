# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: End-to-End NeRF Training, HashNeRF Training Loop, HoloINR Training Loop, NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Training

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric difference between rendered images and ground-truth observations by updating the parameters of a neural radiance field [S5, S14, unofficial]. It solves the inverse problem of recovering a continuous 3D scene representation from a set of 2D images by sampling rays, querying a coordinate-based MLP for density and color, and backpropagating the loss through the differentiable volume rendering equation [S5, S6, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from evaluation methods by including adaptive sampling strategies (such as coarse-to-fine hierarchical sampling) and occupancy grid pruning to manage computational cost during gradient descent [S1, S8].

## How it works
The training loop optimizes a neural network $\theta$ (typically an MLP or a hash-based structure) to map 3D coordinates $\mathbf{x}$ and viewing directions $\mathbf{d}$ to volume density $\sigma$ and view-dependent color $\mathbf{c}$. The core objective is minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted pixel color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ and the ground-truth color $C(\mathbf{r})$ for a batch of rays $\mathcal{R}$ [S5, S14, unofficial].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A dataset of $N$ images with known camera poses (extrinsics $W_{cw}$ and intrinsics $K$) and dimensions $H \times W$.
*   **Outputs:** Optimized network weights $\theta$ (and potentially camera poses or other auxiliary parameters).

### Data Structures
*   **Rays:** A ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ is defined by origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$. In implementation, rays are often pre-computed and stored in a flat array `rays_rgb` of shape $[(N-1)HW, 3, 3]$ (or $[N_{rays}, 9]$), where the last axis contains $[\mathbf{o}_x, \mathbf{o}_y, \mathbf{o}_z, \mathbf{d}_x, \mathbf{d}_y, \mathbf{d}_z, C_x, C_y, C_z]$ [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Occupancy Grid (Optional):** For acceleration methods like Instant-NGP, a 3D grid stores occupancy probabilities to prune empty space during ray marching [S15, unofficial].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Initialize the neural network weights $\theta$. For MLPs, weights are often initialized using specific schemes (e.g., SIREN initialization or uniform distributions) [S3, S15, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with a learning rate $\eta$ (e.g., $5 \times 10^{-4}$) [S11, S13, unofficial].
    *   Initialize a learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Pre-compute all possible rays from training images into `rays_rgb` and shuffle them [S5, S14, unofficial].

2.  **Ray Sampling:**
    *   Select a batch of $N_{rand}$ rays from `rays_rgb` (e.g., $N_{rand} = 1024$ or $2048$) [S5, S14, unofficial].
    *   For each ray, sample $N$ points along the segment $[t_{near}, t_{far}]$.
        *   **Uniform Sampling:** Sample $t_i$ linearly in depth or disparity [S5, unofficial].
        *   **Perturbation:** Add random jitter to $t_i$ to break symmetry and improve generalization [S5, unofficial].
        *   **Hierarchical Sampling (Coarse-to-Fine):** If using a coarse network, sample additional points around the opaque regions identified by the coarse pass to form a fine set of samples [S1][S5, unofficial].

3.  **Forward Pass (Differentiable Volume Rendering):**
    *   Query the network for each sample point $i$ on ray $\mathbf{r}$:
        $$ [\sigma_i, \mathbf{c}_i] = \text{MLP}(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{d}_i; \theta) $$
        where $\sigma_i$ is the volume density and $\mathbf{c}_i$ is the color [S5, unofficial].
    *   Compute the transmittance $T_i$ and weight $w_i$ for each sample:
        $$ T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right), \quad \delta_j = t_j - t_{j-1} $$
        $$ w_i = T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) $$
    *   Composite the final color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ and depth $\hat{D}(\mathbf{r})$:
        $$ \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \mathbf{c}_i $$
        $$ \hat{D}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i t_i $$
    *   If a coarse network was used, store its output $\hat{C}_0(\mathbf{r})$ for auxiliary loss [S5, S11, unofficial].

4.  **Loss Computation:**
    *   Compute the primary photometric loss using MSE:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{N_{rays}} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \| \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r}) \|^2 $$
    *   If hierarchical sampling is used, add the coarse loss:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{coarse} = \frac{1}{N_{rays}} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \| \hat{C}_0(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r}) \|^2 $$
    *   Total loss: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \lambda_{coarse} \mathcal{L}_{coarse} + \mathcal{L}_{reg}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ includes optional terms like sparsity or total variation [S7, S11, S16, unofficial].

5.  **Backpropagation and Update:**
    *   Compute gradients $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ via automatic differentiation [S5, S18, unofficial].
    *   Update weights: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ (using Adam or SGD) [S5, S17, unofficial].
    *   Update learning rate according to the scheduler [S5, S11, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
def nerf_training_loop(
    model, 
    optimizer, 
    scheduler, 
    rays_rgb, 
    num_iterations, 
    batch_size=1024, 
    near=2.0, 
    far=6.0, 
    num_samples=128, 
    use_coarse=False
):
    """
    Standard NeRF training loop.
    
    Args:
        model: Neural Radiance Field MLP (or HashNeRF).
        optimizer: Optimizer instance (e.g., Adam).
        scheduler: Learning rate scheduler.
        rays_rgb: Pre-computed array of shape [N_total_rays, 9].
                  Columns 0-2: Ray origin (x,y,z).
                  Columns 3-5: Ray direction (x,y,z).
                  Columns 6-8: Ground truth RGB color.
        num_iterations: Total number of gradient steps.
        batch_size: Number of rays per iteration (N_rand).
        near: Near plane distance for ray sampling.
        far: Far plane distance for ray sampling.
        num_samples: Number of points sampled per ray.
        use_coarse: Boolean flag for coarse-to-fine training.
    """
    # Initialize ray index cursor
    ray_idx = 0
    n_total_rays = rays_rgb.shape[0]
    
    # Shuffle rays initially
    indices = np.arange(n_total_rays)
    np.random.shuffle(indices)
    rays_rgb_shuffled = rays_rgb[indices]

    for step in range(num_iterations):
        # 1. Ray Sampling
        # Slice batch from shuffled rays
        end_idx = ray_idx + batch_size
        batch_data = rays_rgb_shuffled[ray_idx:end_idx]
        
        # Extract origins, directions, and targets
        # batch_data shape: [batch_size, 9]
        rays_o = batch_data[:, 0:3]
        rays_d = batch_data[:, 3:6]
        targets = batch_data[:, 6:9]
        
        # Increment cursor and shuffle if exhausted
        ray_idx += batch_size
        if ray_idx + batch_size > n_total_rays:
            ray_idx = 0
            np.random.shuffle(indices)
            rays_rgb_shuffled = rays_rgb[indices]

        # 2. Forward Pass
        # Sample points along rays
        # t_vals: linearly spaced values from near to far
        t_vals = np.linspace(near, far, num_samples)
        # Add jitter for robustness
        t_rand = np.random.uniform(0, 1, t_vals.shape)
        t_vals = t_vals + t_rand * (far - near) / num_samples
        
        # Compute sample positions
        # pts shape: [batch_size, num_samples, 3]
        pts = rays_o[:, None, :] + rays_d[:, None, :] * t_vals[:, :, None]
        
        # Query Network
        # raw shape: [batch_size, num_samples, 4] (RGB + Density)
        raw = model(pts, rays_d)
        
        # Volume Rendering (Alpha Compositing)
        # Convert raw density to alpha
        # sigma = raw[..., 3]
        # dists = t_vals[:, 1:] - t_vals[:, :-1]
        # alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * dists)
        # weights = alpha * cumprod(1 - alpha)
        # rgb_pred = sum(weights * rgb)
        
        # Note: Implementation details of raw2outputs depend on the specific library,
        # but the mathematical operation is standard alpha compositing.
        rgb_pred, extras = render_volume_rendering(raw, t_vals, rays_d)
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        # Primary MSE Loss
        loss_rgb = torch.mean((rgb_pred - targets) ** 2)
        loss = loss_rgb
        
        # Coarse Loss (if hierarchical)
        if use_coarse and 'rgb_coarse' in extras:
            loss_coarse = torch.mean((extras['rgb_coarse'] - targets) ** 2)
            loss = loss + loss_coarse
            
        # Optional Regularization (e.g., Sparsity, TV)
        # loss += lambda_reg * compute_regularization(extras, step)

        # 4. Backpropagation
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        
        # 5. Update
        optimizer.step()
        scheduler.step()
        
        # Logging (optional)
        if step % 100 == 0:
            psnr = -10 * torch.log10(loss_rgb)
            print(f"Step {step}, Loss: {loss.item():.6f}, PSNR: {psnr.item():.2f}")

    return model
```

### Parameters and Typical Values
*   **Learning Rate ($\eta$):** $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for Adam [S11, unofficial].
*   **Batch Size ($N_{rand}$):** 1024 or 2048 rays [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   **Samples per Ray ($N$):** 128 for coarse, 256 for fine [S5, unofficial].
*   **Near/Far Bounds:** Scene-dependent. For synthetic Blender scenes, $near=2.0, far=6.0$ [S5, S13, unofficial]. For real-world LLFF, bounds are computed from camera poses [S2, unofficial].
*   **Positional Encoding Frequencies:** $\log_2$ max frequency for position: 10; for direction: 4 [S2, S11, unofficial].
*   **Decay:** Exponential decay with rate 0.1 every 100,000 steps, or Cosine Annealing over 1,000,000 steps [S5, S11, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Rendering all rays at once exceeds GPU memory. Mitigation: Chunk the ray batch during the forward pass [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Freeing:** Higher-order derivatives (e.g., for gradient-based logging) must be computed *before* calling `loss.backward()`, which frees the autograd graph [S3, unofficial].
*   **Resuming Training:** When loading a checkpoint, the scheduler's last epoch must be set to `start_epoch - 1` to maintain correct decay schedules [S9, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Dynamic Growth:** Directly initializing grown sub-networks randomly causes density collapse. Initialize new weights to match the parent network's weights [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md)
The differentiable forward pass that synthesizes RGB images from a neural radiance field for gradient-based optimization. It solves the problem of efficiently sampling 3D space along camera rays, querying a neural network for density and color, and integrating these values via alpha compositing to produce a loss-computable image. It sets itself apart from [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md) by computing gradients for backpropagation and from [NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)](5124-nerf-rendering-alpha-compositing.md) by including the adaptive sampling and occupancy grid pruning steps specific to the training loop.

### [Training Configuration](2332-training-configuration.md)
Training Configuration defines the static and dynamic hyperparameters, optimizer settings, and initialization procedures required to optimize a radiance field model. It establishes baseline settings (MLP depth, width, positional encoding frequencies, and hierarchical sampling counts) against which variants adjust specific architectural or optimization details. It differs from [Training Loop Setup](1522-training-loop-setup.md) by focusing on numerical values and mathematical schedules rather than procedural orchestration, and from [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by defining parameters that control sampling density and loss weighting rather than the integration algorithm itself [G].

### [Dynamic Growth Parameters](2968-dynamic-growth-parameters.md)
Dynamic Growth Parameters is an adaptive training strategy for Neural Radiance Fields that expands network capacity based on local scene complexity and prediction uncertainty. Unlike static architectures, it clusters high-uncertainty query points and grows parallel network branches to refine predictions in those regions, enabling coarse-to-fine representation without pre-defining the entire hierarchy.

### [Mip-NeRF 360](3306-mip-nerf-360.md)
Mip-NeRF 360 is a method for synthesizing photorealistic views of unbounded, real-world scenes where the camera may rotate 360 degrees and content exists at arbitrary distances. It solves the problems of aliasing, training instability, and geometric ambiguity inherent in standard NeRF when applied to large-scale environments. It sets itself apart from its parent [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by integrating a non-linear scene parameterization, online distillation between two MLPs, and a distortion-based regularizer, achieving a 57% reduction in mean squared error compared to standard mip-NeRF.

### [Mip-NeRF 360 Sampling and Training](3393-mip-nerf-360-sampling-and-training.md)
Mip-NeRF 360 Sampling and Training extends the Mip-NeRF framework to unbounded, 360-degree scenes by introducing a disparity-based ray parameterization, a contraction function for coordinate space, and a coarse-to-fine training loop with a proposal network. It solves the artifacts of "floaters" and "background collapse" common in unbounded scene reconstruction by regularizing the density distribution and ensuring efficient sampling through online distillation. It differs from standard NeRF by using linear-in-disparity sampling and from basic Mip-NeRF by handling unbounded scenes via coordinate contraction and disparity parameterization.

### [Coarse-to-Fine NeRF](3406-coarse-to-fine-nerf.md)
Coarse-to-Fine NeRF is a hierarchical sampling strategy that improves the learning of high-frequency details in neural radiance fields by allocating computational resources adaptively along rays. It solves the problem of uniform sampling being inefficient for sparse or complex geometry by using a coarse network to identify opaque regions and a fine network to densely sample those specific areas. Unlike standard uniform sampling which distributes points evenly across the entire ray segment, this method clusters additional samples near the object boundaries identified by the coarse pass, thereby improving detail reconstruction without a proportional increase in coarse sampling cost.

### [Neural Volume Scene Setup](4168-neural-volume-scene-setup.md)
Constructs a Mitsuba scene containing a volumetric cube and a custom neural radiance field integrator, initializing the density grid and spherical harmonics coefficients. It differs from generic training setups by directly binding the neural network parameters to the scene graph's heterogeneous volume and integrator components.

### [MVSNeRF](6197-mvsnerf.md)
MVSNeRF is a method for fast, generalizable radiance field reconstruction from multi-view stereo (MVS) images. Unlike standard NeRF which requires per-scene optimization, MVSNeRF uses a feedforward neural network to predict the radiance field directly from input images, significantly reducing training time and iteration counts. It sets itself apart by learning a data prior that accelerates convergence, enabling rapid inference without iterative scene-specific optimization.

### [Probability-based Ray Sampling with Quadtree Subdivision](7481-probability-based-ray-sampling-with-quadtree-subdivision.md)
This method accelerates radiance field training by dynamically adjusting ray distribution through context-based probability sampling and adaptive quadtree subdivision. It reduces training time by 15–40% while maintaining or improving accuracy compared to baseline methods like NeRF, Plenoxels, and Instant-NGP. Unlike uniform sampling or fixed-block approaches (e.g., iMap), it subdivides image regions based on rendering error, allowing complex areas to receive more rays while marking converged simple regions to reduce computational load.

### [Dynamic and Temporal Modeling](7610-dynamic-and-temporal-modeling.md)
Dynamic and Temporal Modeling extends static radiance field optimization to handle non-rigid motion and time-varying scenes, solving the problem of novel view synthesis for dynamic environments. It distinguishes itself from static siblings like [Coarse-to-Fine NeRF](3406-coarse-to-fine-nerf.md) by introducing temporal dependencies, either through explicit deformation fields, implicit transient modeling, or on-the-fly state updates.

### [Camera Pose and Parameter Optimization](7611-camera-pose-and-parameter-optimization.md)
Camera Pose and Parameter Optimization addresses the problem of inaccurate or unknown initial camera extrinsics and intrinsics in Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) training. Standard NeRF assumes precise ground-truth poses; when these are noisy or missing, reconstruction quality degrades significantly. This method sets itself apart by treating camera parameters as differentiable variables within the training loop, allowing joint optimization with the radiance field. Unlike [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) which assumes fixed poses, this procedure iteratively refines the pose to minimize rendering loss. Key variants include [BARF (Bundle-Adjusting Neural Radiance Fields)](6872-barf-bundle-adjusting-neural-radiance-fields.md) for coarse-to-fine pose refinement and [NeRF with Unknown Camera Parameters](7405-nerf-with-unknown-camera-parameters.md) for joint optimization from sparse views.

### [MegaNeRF](8580-meganerf.md)
MegaNeRF is a method for scalable construction of large-scale neural radiance fields (NeRFs) for virtual fly-throughs. It solves the problem of rendering massive scenes by dividing the environment into distinct regions, where each region is represented by its own MLP network. This approach enables parallel training and significantly accelerates rendering compared to traditional single-MLP NeRF implementations.

### [BlockNeRF](8581-blocknerf.md)
BlockNeRF is a method that decomposes a large-scale scene into a set of spatial blocks for separate NeRF training using a 'total-part-total' approach to solve large-scale city building complex rendering problems. It decouples rendering time from scene size to allow rendering to scale to arbitrarily large environments. It sets itself apart from standard NeRF by partitioning the scene geometry and appearance into localized MLPs, addressing the high-frequency information loss and slow inference inherent in single-model approaches for urban-scale data.

### [Specific Model Architectures](8585-specific-model-architectures.md)
This family encompasses modifications to the core Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) architecture of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) to address specific limitations such as rendering speed, sparse input quality, geometric ambiguity, and high-frequency detail recovery. While the standard NeRF architecture uses a single MLP to map 3D coordinates and viewing directions to density and color, these variants introduce structural changes including network splitting, depth expansion, convolutional post-processing, and explicit geometric constraints. They differ from sibling articles on training loops and sampling by focusing on the internal representation and connectivity of the neural network itself.

### [Regularization and Loss Functions](8586-regularization-and-loss-functions.md)
Regularization and Loss Functions define the objective landscape for optimizing Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), addressing the severe under-constraint problem where limited input views lead to geometric artifacts like "floaters" or degenerate solutions. This family sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) by focusing on the gradient signals derived from the rendered output rather than the sampling strategy itself, and from [Training Configuration](2332-training-configuration.md) by providing the specific mathematical terms that guide the optimizer.

### [Progressive Inpainting-and-Erasing](8658-progressive-inpainting-and-erasing.md)
Progressive Inpainting-and-Erasing (PERF) is a training strategy for single-view NeRF that avoids geometry conflicts between visible and invisible regions by computing a mask of conflicted regions and eliminating them from NeRF training. It sets itself apart from standard single-view NeRF methods by integrating a collaborative RGBD inpainting step with a progressive erasing mechanism to handle the ambiguity of unseen geometry, building on the [NeRF Training Loop](970-nerf-training-loop.md) by dynamically expanding the reference view set.

### [Progressive View Synthesis Training](8666-progressive-view-synthesis-training.md)
Progressive View Synthesis Training (PERF) is a training strategy for Panoramic Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) that reconstructs a 3D scene from a single input panorama by progressively adding synthetic reference views to resolve occlusions and improve semantic coherence. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [DS-NeRF](8679-single-view-nerf-training-with-depth-map.md) and [DietNeRF](8669-nerf-ff.md) by replacing generic depth constraints or semantic regularization with a collaborative RGBD inpainting and progressive inpainting-and-erasing pipeline, which explicitly synthesizes plausible content for occluded regions rather than relying on geometric priors or feature matching alone.

### [NeRFVS](8667-nerfvs.md)
NeRFVS is a method for indoor scene free view synthesis (FVS) that uses a geometry scaffold from an off-the-shelf neural reconstruction method to guide NeRF optimization, reducing distortions and floaters in extrapolation. It enables NeRF to perform free navigation and extrapolation for indoor scenes by using a neural-reconstructed geometric scaffold to guide optimization, boosted by depth and variance constraints with dynamical adjustment based on view coverage. Unlike standard NeRF, which struggles with extrapolation and sparse views, NeRFVS minimizes per-pixel loss to avoid distortions, unlike SVS which suffers from noticeable artifacts.

### [NeRF-FF](8669-nerf-ff.md)
NeRF-FF is a plug-in strategy that mitigates the degradation of Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) reconstruction quality caused by out-of-focus (defocus blurred) regions in input images. It solves the problem of training NeRFs on partially blurred data by estimating binary image masks that identify in-focus regions, allowing the subsequent NeRF model to omit out-of-focus pixels during training. Unlike methods that use dynamic ray generation or blur kernels, NeRF-FF relies on static ray assumptions, making it compatible with runtime-optimized NeRF variants like Instant-NGP, nerfacto, and splatfacto.

### [L2G-NeRF](8671-l2g-nerf.md)
L2G-NeRF is a bundle-adjusting method that jointly optimizes neural field parameters and camera poses by combining a local photometric alignment objective with a global geometric alignment objective. It distinguishes itself from global registration methods like BARF by computing gradients with respect to local poses, which is more efficient and tolerates larger noise perturbations before divergence.

### [KNN-NeRF](8672-knn-nerf.md)
KNN-NeRF is a baseline method for training Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) using sparse 3D point clouds as supervision instead of multi-view images. It solves the problem of generating ground-truth color labels for rays that do not pass through existing points by assigning the color of the nearest point in the cloud to the ray. Unlike standard NeRF which relies on pixel colors from images, KNN-NeRF constructs a dense training signal from sparse geometry, though it struggles with sharp object borders due to the low-frequency bias of neural networks and the sparsity of the input.

### [Hard Sample Mining](8673-hard-sample-mining.md)
Hard Sample Mining (HSM) is an optimization procedure for neural radiance fields that improves training efficiency and reconstruction quality by selectively updating the network based on pixel-level error. It solves the problem of uniform computational waste in standard NeRF training, where samples contributing little to the gradient are treated equally to those with high error. Unlike sibling methods such as [Probability-based Ray Sampling with Quadtree Subdivision](7481-probability-based-ray-sampling-with-quadtree-subdivision.md) or [Nerfacc](https://github.com/NVlabs/nerfacc) which prune low-weight samples based on density or transmittance, HSM ranks samples using the propagated pixel loss to subsample a "hard" minibatch that retains most of the update information.

### [NoPe-NeRF](8705-nope-nerf.md)
NoPe-NeRF is a method for training Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) without precomputed camera poses, addressing the challenge of optimizing relative poses between consecutive frames using undistorted monocular depth priors. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like BARF and iNeRF by explicitly generating a dense point cloud from a monocular depth estimation network and unprojecting undistorted depth maps to constrain pose optimization via point cloud and photometric losses.

### [Depth and Geometry Supervision](8727-depth-and-geometry-supervision.md)
Depth and Geometry Supervision refers to a family of training procedures for Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) that incorporate explicit geometric constraints—typically derived from monocular depth estimators, Structure-from-Motion (SfM) keypoints, or multi-view consistency errors—to resolve the shape-radiance ambiguity inherent in volume rendering. Unlike standard NeRF, which relies solely on photometric consistency (RGB loss) and often produces plausible colors with incorrect geometry, these methods enforce geometric plausibility by either guiding the sampling intervals along rays, supervising the rendered depth distribution against a prior, or initializing the scene with estimated depth maps. This family is distinct from sibling methods like [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) (which defines the integration mechanics) and [Regularization and Loss Functions](8586-regularization-and-loss-functions.md) (which defines general penalties) by specifically targeting the geometric fidelity of the implicit surface representation using external or derived depth information.

### [AltNeRF](8763-altnerf.md)
A framework that creates resilient Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) representations using self-supervised monocular depth estimation (SMDE) from monocular videos, without relying on known camera poses. It uses an alternating algorithm to harmonize NeRF outputs with SMDE.

### [Sparse NeRF Reconstruction with Geometric and Semantic Initialization](8779-sparse-nerf-reconstruction-with-geometric-and-semantic-initi.md)
A full-process reconstruction method for sparse input (15-20 images) that addresses blurred geometry, loss of detail, and slow convergence by using COLMAP sparse point clouds for geometric priors and Mask R-CNN for semantic-guided weight initialization.

### ReconFusion
Uses a Diffusion Prior to constrain unobserved views [S19, unofficial].

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S20]. For every pixel, cast multiple jittered rays sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel. Render each ray with NeRF. Use the mean of those rendered values as the predicted color in the loss function [S20]. The full-resolution supersampling baseline relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets [S20]. The 16x supersampling baseline performs worse than mip-NeRF and is significantly more expensive during both training and evaluation [S20].

### Two-stage training for proposers
A training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S21, unofficial]. Phase 1: Train the learnt proposer to mimic the heuristic proposer by minimizing the L2 matching loss between their proposal sets [S21, unofficial]. Phase 2: Train all network components end-to-end. At the start of this phase, reset the optimizer state and perform a linear warmup for 1,000 steps to reduce shock from switching regimes [S21, unofficial]. Training from scratch sometimes works but often fails; two-stage training is consistently effective [S21, unofficial].

### CityNeRF
A progressive learning paradigm that synchronously grows the NeRF model and training set [S22]. Starts by fitting distant views with shallow basic blocks. New blocks are continuously attached as training iterates to adapt to details in far-near views. Effectively activates high-frequency channels in position encoding and unfolds more complex details as training progresses [S22].

### ViP-NeRF
A method that introduces visibility priors to supervise density and improve rendering quality under sparse input view conditions (fewer than 50-100 views), preventing artifacts caused by insufficient training data [S23]. 1) Visibility regularization: Regularize NeRF with visibility priors. 2) Visibility prior: Estimate whether each pixel in a main view is visible in a secondary view. 3) Effective prediction of visibility: Compute visibility using planar scanned volumes without pre-training. Create a plane scan volume by warping different depth planes of a secondary view image to the main view. Compare the warped planes with the main view image to obtain an error map. Identify the minimum error among all planes. Threshold the minimum error of all planes to obtain a visibility prior map corresponding to the primary view image. Use this map to supervise the density during NeRF training [S23]. On the RealEstate-10K dataset, ViP-NeRF achieves a PSNR index 3-4 points higher than the latest paper in 2, 3, and 4 input views [S23].

### Coarse-Fine Consistency Loss
A loss function that enforces consistency between depth estimates of the coarse and fine NeRF models to prevent ineffective hierarchical sampling and blur [S24].
$$ \mathcal{L}_{cfc} = \mathbb{1}_{m_f=1} \odot \| z_c - \hat{\nabla}(z_f) \|^2 + \mathbb{1}_{m_c=1} \odot \| \hat{\nabla}(z_c) - z_f \|^2 $$
where $z_c$ and $z_f$ are coarse and fine depths, and $m_c/m_f$ are reliability masks determined by reprojection [S24].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples})$, where $N_{iters}$ is the number of iterations, $N_{rand}$ is the batch size (rays per step), and $N_{samples}$ is the number of points per ray [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples})$ for the forward pass buffers, plus the memory required for the neural network weights and any auxiliary structures (e.g., occupancy grids) [S5, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Standard NeRF training on synthetic Blender scenes (800x800) takes approximately 1,000,000 iterations, which can take several hours on a single GPU [S11, S14, unofficial].
*   **Scaling:** Performance scales with batch size, but larger batches hinder data loading performance and prevent full GPU occupancy; smaller batches cap iterations per second. A batch size of $2^{10}$ (1024) is often chosen as a trade-off [S15, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** End-to-end differentiable; handles complex geometry and appearance; produces high-quality novel views.
*   **Limitations:** Slow convergence (requires many iterations); prone to "floaters" and geometric ambiguity without regularization; high memory usage for large scenes [S1][S17, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When high-fidelity novel view synthesis is required and ground-truth camera poses are known.
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications (use [Instant-NGP](link) or [3D Gaussian Splatting](link)); for scenes with unknown or highly inaccurate camera poses (use [Camera Pose and Parameter Optimization](7611-camera-pose-and-parameter-optimization.md)).

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop was introduced by Mildenhall et al. in "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" (2020) [S5, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of coordinate-based neural networks for implicit scene representation [S3, unofficial]. The method was initially validated on synthetic datasets (Blender, DeepVoxels) and later extended to real-world datasets (LLFF, Tanks and Temples) [S2, S5, unofficial]. The loop structure has become the standard for subsequent NeRF variants, including Mip-NeRF, Instant-NGP, and 3D Gaussian Splatting (which adapts the loss and sampling but retains the iterative optimization paradigm) [S1, S8][S15, unofficial].
