# NeRV (Neural Reflectance and Visibility Fields)

Also called: Neural Reflectance and Visibility Fields

## Overview
NeRV is a differentiable inverse rendering method that reconstructs a 3D scene representation from images under unconstrained, known lighting, enabling view synthesis and relighting with indirect illumination [S1]. It extends the Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) framework by decomposing the scene into volumetric density and material properties, and introduces a **Neural Visibility Field** to approximate light transport efficiently [S1]. Unlike sibling methods that rely on brute-force raymarching or are limited to single point lights, NeRV uses learned neural approximations for visibility and surface intersections to reduce the complexity of direct and indirect lighting calculations from quadratic/cubic to linear/double-spherical integrals [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A set of $N$ images $\{I^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ of a scene, along with the corresponding camera poses and known environment lighting maps $E(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega})$ [S1].
**Outputs:** Two Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs): a shape MLP $\theta$ and a reflectance MLP $\psi$, which together define the scene's geometry, material, and visibility properties [S1].

### Data Structures
The scene is represented by two neural networks:
1.  **Shape MLP ($\theta$):**
    *   Input: 3D position $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
    *   Output: Volume density $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].
2.  **Reflectance MLP ($\psi$):**
    *   Input: 3D position $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
    *   Output: Material parameters $\mathbf{m} = (\mathbf{a}, \gamma)$, where $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the diffuse RGB albedo and $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$ is the scalar specular roughness [S1].
3.  **Visibility MLP ($\phi$):**
    *   Input: 3D position $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and direction $\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \mathbb{S}^2$.
    *   Output: A tuple $(\tilde{V}, \tilde{t})$, where $\tilde{V} \in [0, 1]$ is the estimated visibility (transmittance) and $\tilde{t} \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the estimated distance to the first surface intersection (expected termination depth) in direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ [S1].

*Note: Surface normals $\mathbf{n}$ are not stored but computed on-the-fly as the negative normalized gradient of the shape MLP output:*
$$ \mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \sigma(\mathbf{x})}{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \sigma(\mathbf{x})\|} $$
*This requires automatic differentiation during training [S1].*

### Rendering Equation
The radiance $L(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o)$ observed from camera center $\mathbf{c}$ in direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_o$ is computed by integrating along the camera ray $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{c} + t\boldsymbol{\omega}_o$:
$$ L(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) = \int_0^\infty V(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{c}) \sigma(\mathbf{x}(t)) L_r(\mathbf{x}(t), \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) \, dt $$
where $V(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{c}) = \exp\left(-\int_0^t \sigma(\mathbf{x}(s)) \, ds\right)$ is the volume transmittance [S1].

The outgoing radiance $L_r$ is decomposed into direct and indirect components:
$$ L_r(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) = L_{\text{direct}} + L_{\text{indirect}} $$

**1. Direct Lighting Component:**
Direct lighting comes from the environment map $E$. The incident radiance is attenuated by visibility. NeRV replaces the true visibility integral with the neural visibility approximation $\tilde{V}_\phi$:
$$ L_{\text{direct}}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) = \int_S \tilde{V}_\phi(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i) E(\mathbf{x}, -\boldsymbol{\omega}_i) R(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i $$
where $S$ is the unit sphere, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ is the incoming direction, and $R$ is the microfacet BRDF [S1].

**2. Indirect Lighting Component:**
To approximate one-bounce indirect illumination, NeRV treats the volume as a hard surface located at the expected termination depth $t_0$. The accumulated radiance is approximated by a single point evaluation at $\mathbf{x}(t_0)$:
$$ L_{\text{indirect}}(\mathbf{x}(t_0), \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) = \int_S L(\mathbf{x}(t_0), -\boldsymbol{\omega}_i) R(\mathbf{x}(t_0), \boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}_o) \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i $$
Here, $L(\mathbf{x}(t_0), -\boldsymbol{\omega}_i)$ is the incoming radiance from direction $-\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$, which itself is computed using the direct lighting formula (Eq. 9) at that point [S1].

**Reflectance Function (BRDF):**
The reflectance $R$ is a standard microfacet BRDF parameterized by albedo $\mathbf{a}$, roughness $\gamma$, and normal $\mathbf{n}$ [S1].

### Training Procedure
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize MLPs $\theta, \psi, \phi$ with random weights [S1].
2.  **Sampling:** For each training iteration, sample a batch of pixels from input images. For each pixel, sample $K$ points along the camera ray using stratified sampling [S1].
3.  **Forward Pass:**
    *   Evaluate $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})$ for all sampled points.
    *   Compute transmittance $V$ along the camera ray using the density samples [S1].
    *   For each point $\mathbf{x}$, query the visibility MLP $\phi(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i)$ for a set of sampled environment directions $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ to get $\tilde{V}_\phi$ and $\tilde{t}$ [S1].
    *   Compute $L_{\text{direct}}$ using $\tilde{V}_\phi$ and the environment map [S1].
    *   Compute $L_{\text{indirect}}$ by evaluating the direct lighting integral at the point $\mathbf{x}(t_0)$, where $t_0$ is derived from the density integration (not the visibility MLP's $\tilde{t}$) [S1].
    *   Compute total radiance $L_r$ and integrate along the camera ray to get predicted pixel color [S1].
4.  **Supervision:**
    *   **Color Loss:** Minimize the difference between rendered and observed pixel colors (e.g., MSE) [S1].
    *   **Visibility Consistency:** The visibility MLP $\phi$ is supervised to be consistent with the volume density samples. Specifically, the predicted visibility $\tilde{V}_\phi$ should match the true transmittance computed from the density MLP along the ray segment [S1]. The expected termination depth $\tilde{t}$ is supervised to match the depth where the accumulated transmittance drops below a threshold (e.g., $1/e$) based on the density samples [S1].
5.  **Optimization:** Update all MLP weights ($\theta, \psi, \phi$) using gradient descent (e.g., Adam) [S1].

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

class NeRV:
    def __init__(self, shape_mlp, reflectance_mlp, visibility_mlp):
        self.shape_mlp = shape_mlp      # MLP_theta
        self.reflectance_mlp = reflectance_mlp # MLP_psi
        self.visibility_mlp = visibility_mlp   # MLP_phi

    def compute_normal(self, x, sigma):
        """Compute surface normal as negative normalized gradient of sigma."""
        x.requires_grad_(True)
        sigma = self.shape_mlp(x)
        grad = torch.autograd.grad(sigma.sum(), x, create_graph=True)[0]
        normal = -grad / (torch.norm(grad, dim=-1, keepdim=True) + 1e-8)
        return normal

    def get_brdf_params(self, x):
        """Get albedo and roughness from reflectance MLP."""

        albedo = params[:, :3]
        roughness = params[:, 3]
        return albedo, roughness

        Render a single pixel.
        """
        # Sample points along the camera ray

        x_vals = camera_ray_origin + t_vals.unsqueeze(1) * camera_ray_direction # (num_samples, 3)
        
        # Get density
        sigma = self.shape_mlp(x_vals) # (num_samples,)
        
        # Compute transmittance V(t)
        # V(t) = exp(-int_0^t sigma(s) ds)
        # Approximate integral using trapezoidal rule or simple sum
        dt = t_vals[1] - t_vals[0]
        accumulated_sigma = torch.cumsum(sigma * dt, dim=0)
        transmittance = torch.exp(-accumulated_sigma)
        # Add transmittance at t=0 (1.0)
        transmittance = torch.cat([torch.ones(1).to(x_vals.device), transmittance[:-1]], dim=0)
        
        # Get normals and material
        normal = self.compute_normal(x_vals, sigma) # (num_samples, 3)
        albedo, roughness = self.get_brdf_params(x_vals) # (num_samples, 3), (num_samples,)

            # L_indirect = integral over sphere of L_incoming * R d omega_i

        # Integrate along camera ray
        # L = sum( T(t) * sigma(t) * L_r(t) * dt )
        weights = transmittance * sigma * dt # (num_samples,)
        color = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(1) * total_radiance, dim=0)
        
        return color

    def train_step(self, batch, optimizer):
        """
        One training step.
        """
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        
        rendered_colors = []
        for ray in batch.rays:
            color = self.render_pixel(ray.origin, ray.direction, batch.env_map)
            rendered_colors.append(color)
        
        rendered_colors = torch.stack(rendered_colors)
        target_colors = batch.target_colors
        
        # Color Loss
        loss = torch.mean((rendered_colors - target_colors) ** 2)

            # Get density

            accumulated_sigma = torch.cumsum(sigma * dt, dim=0)
            true_transmittance = torch.exp(-accumulated_sigma)

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Visibility MLP Consistency:** The visibility MLP must be supervised to match the density samples. If the density changes rapidly during optimization, the visibility MLP may lag, leading to incorrect visibility estimates [S1].
*   **Indirect Lighting Approximation:** The hard surface approximation for indirect lighting assumes that the volume can be treated as a surface at the expected termination depth. This may fail for highly transparent or volumetric scenes where light scatters deeply [S1].
*   **Normal Computation:** Computing normals via automatic differentiation of the density MLP can be noisy if the density field is not smooth. This affects the BRDF evaluation [S1].
*   **Complexity:** While NeRV reduces complexity, it is still more expensive than standard NeRF due to the additional visibility queries and indirect lighting calculations [S1].

## Subtopics

### [NeRV Evaluation Variants](10273-nerv-evaluation-variants.md)
NeRV Evaluation Variants are inference-time strategies for the NeRV representation that modify how visibility and material properties are queried during rendering. NeRV with Neural Visibility Fields (NVF) uses the same visibility approximations learned during training at test time, while NeRV with Test-time Tracing (Trace) replaces the learned visibility with brute-force ray tracing to estimate visibility to point light sources for sharper shadows. Both variants reuse the same trained model. A third approach defers ray queries to diffuse intersections to enable rendering for non-diffuse materials, accepting sampling-related noise as a trade-off.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Direct lighting complexity is reduced from quadratic to linear in samples per ray by using neural visibility approximations instead of line integrals [S1]. Indirect lighting complexity is reduced from cubic to a double spherical integral by using hard surface approximations at expected termination depths [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing three MLPs (shape, reflectance, visibility) and their weights. The memory footprint is comparable to NeRF but slightly higher due to the additional visibility MLP [S1].
*   **Performance:** NeRV enables rendering with arbitrary environment lighting and one-bounce indirect illumination, which was intractable with previous methods like Neural Reflectance Fields that required single point lights [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Supports unconstrained known lighting, indirect illumination, and efficient visibility queries during optimization [S1].
*   **Limitations:** The hard surface approximation for indirect lighting may not capture complex volumetric scattering. The visibility MLP requires careful supervision to remain consistent with the density field [S1].

## Background
NeRV was introduced by Pratul P. Srinivasan, Boyang Deng, Xiuming Zhang, Matthew Tancik, Ben Mildenhall, and Jonathan T. Barron in 2021 [S1]. It extends the Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) framework and builds on Neural Reflectance Fields, which was limited to single point lights and direct illumination [S1]. The key innovation is the use of a neural visibility field to approximate light transport, making inverse rendering with global illumination tractable [S1].
