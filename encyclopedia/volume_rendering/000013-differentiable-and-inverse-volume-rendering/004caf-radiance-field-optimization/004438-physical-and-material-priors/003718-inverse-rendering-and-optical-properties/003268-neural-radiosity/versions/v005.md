# Neural Radiosity

Also called: Full-gradient method, Residual-based optimization

## Overview
Neural Radiosity is an inverse rendering technique that solves the rendering equation by representing the outgoing radiance field as a neural network and optimizing its parameters to minimize the residual between the left-hand side (LHS) and right-hand side (RHS) of the equation [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. Unlike [Volumetric Inverse Rendering with Differentiable Path Tracing](1523-volumetric-inverse-rendering-with-differentiable-path-tracin.md), which relies on differentiable path tracing to compute gradients, Neural Radiosity treats the rendering equation as a self-supervised loss function, allowing for direct optimization of scene properties without explicit light transport simulation per step [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. It differs from [NeRV (Neural Reflectance and Visibility Fields)](10246-nerv-neural-reflectance-and-visibility-fields.md) by optimizing the radiance residual directly rather than learning separate reflectance and visibility fields [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Scene geometry (meshes), material properties (BRDF parameters, diffuse/specular reflectance), and initial network parameters $\theta$ [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:** A trained neural network $L_\theta(x, \omega_o)$ representing the outgoing radiance field [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Neural Network:** A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with 6 fully-connected layers and 512 neurons per hidden layer [S3, unofficial].
*   **Network Inputs:**
    *   Surface location $x$.
    *   Outgoing direction $\omega_o$.
    *   Surface normal at $x$.
    *   Diffuse and specular reflectance at $x$ [S3, unofficial].
    *   Multi-resolution learnable feature vectors from sparse grids (optional, for high-frequency details) [S3, unofficial].
*   **Sampling Buffers:**
    *   Location samples $\{x_j\}_{j=1}^N$ uniformly distributed on scene surfaces [S3, unofficial].
    *   Outgoing direction samples $\{\omega_{o,j}\}_{j=1}^N$ uniformly distributed on the unit hemisphere (or sphere for two-sided BSDFs) [S3, unofficial].
    *   Incident direction samples $\{\omega_{i,j,k}\}_{k=1}^M$ uniformly distributed on the hemisphere for each $(x_j, \omega_{o,j})$ [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Initialize:** Set network parameters $\theta$ and learning rate $\eta$ [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Sample:** For each optimization step, sample $N$ location vectors $\{x_j\}$ uniformly on all scene surfaces and $N$ outgoing directions $\{\omega_{o,j}\}$ uniformly over the hemisphere [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
3.  **Sample Incident Directions:** For each pair $(x_j, \omega_{o,j})$, sample $M$ incident directions $\{\omega_{i,j,k}\}_{k=1}^M$ uniformly over the hemisphere [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Compute Residual:** Calculate the residual $r_\theta(x_j, \omega_{o,j})$ for each sample [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. The residual is the difference between the LHS and RHS of the rendering equation [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial]:
    $$
    r_\theta(x, \omega_o) = L_\theta(x, \omega_o) - E(x, \omega_o) - \int_{H^2} f_r(x, \omega_i, \omega_o) L_\theta(x'(x, \omega_i), -\omega_i) (n \cdot \omega_i) d\omega_i
    $$
    where:
    *   $L_\theta(x, \omega_o)$ is the network's predicted outgoing radiance [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   $E(x, \omega_o)$ is the emitted radiance [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   $f_r(x, \omega_i, \omega_o)$ is the BRDF [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   $x'(x, \omega_i)$ is the closest surface intersection of the ray originating at $x$ with direction $\omega_i$ [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
    *   $n$ is the surface normal.
    *   The integral is estimated via Monte Carlo integration using the $M$ incident samples [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
5.  **Compute Loss:** Estimate the loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ as the mean squared norm of the residual over all samples [S3, unofficial]:
    $$
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{r_\theta(x_j, \omega_{o,j})^2}{p(x_j, \omega_{o,j})}
    $$
    where $p(x_j, \omega_{o,j})$ is the sampling density [S3, unofficial]. For uniform sampling, $p$ is constant and absorbed into the learning rate [G].
6.  **Compute Gradient:** Calculate the gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)$ using automatic differentiation [S3, unofficial]. The gradient of the residual is:
    $$
    \nabla_\theta r_\theta(x, \omega_o) = \nabla_\theta (\partial_p L_\theta(x, \omega_o)) - \int_{H^2} f_r(x, \omega_i, \omega_o) \nabla_\theta (\partial_p L_\theta(x'(x, \omega_i), -\omega_i)) d\omega_i
    $$
    where $\partial_p$ denotes the derivative with respect to scene parameters $p$ (if applicable) [S3, unofficial]. The gradient of the loss is then:
    $$
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{2 r_\theta(x_j, \omega_{o,j}) \nabla_\theta r_\theta(x_j, \omega_{o,j})}{p(x_j, \omega_{o,j})}
    $$
    [S3, unofficial]. Note: The baseline estimator includes the term $\nabla_\theta \langle R_\theta \rangle$, which pulls the RHS towards the LHS [S2, unofficial].
7.  **Update:** Update parameters $\theta$ using a gradient-based optimizer (e.g., Adam) [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
8.  **Repeat:** Steps 2-7 until convergence [S1, unofficial].

            omega_o: Outgoing direction (N, 3).

            diffuse: Diffuse reflectance (N, 3).

        network_input = torch.cat([x, omega_o, normals, diffuse, specular], dim=-1)
        lhs = self.network(network_input)
        
        emitted = self.get_emitted_radiance(x, omega_o)
        reflected = torch.zeros_like(lhs)
        M = omega_i_samples.shape[1]
        
        for k in range(M):
            omega_i = omega_i_samples[:, k, :]
            # Trace ray to find intersection x'
            x_prime, n_prime = self.trace_ray(x, omega_i)
            
            # Evaluate BRDF at x
            f_r = self.evaluate_brdf(x, omega_i, omega_o)
            
            # Evaluate L_theta at x' with direction -omega_i (outgoing from x')
            incident_input = torch.cat([x_prime, -omega_i, n_prime, diffuse, specular], dim=-1)
            incident_radiance = self.network(incident_input)
            
            # Monte Carlo estimate: (1/M) * sum( f_r * L_theta(x', -omega_i) )
            reflected += f_r * incident_radiance
            
        reflected /= M
        return lhs - emitted - reflected

    def train_step(self, N=1000, M=32):
        """Perform one training step."""
        self.optimizer.zero_grad()
        
        # 1. Sample locations and outgoing directions
        x, normals = self.sample_surface_points(N)
        omega_o, omega_i_samples = self.sample_hemisphere(N, M)
        
        # 2. Material properties (assumed constant per sample for simplicity)
        diffuse = torch.ones_like(x) * 0.8
        specular = torch.zeros_like(x)
        
        # 3. Compute residual
        residual = self.compute_residual(x, omega_o, normals, diffuse, specular, omega_i_samples)
        
        # 4. Compute loss (Mean Squared Error, density p is constant for uniform sampling)
        loss = torch.mean(residual ** 2)
        
        # 5. Backpropagate and update
        loss.backward()
        self.optimizer.step()
        
        return loss.item()

**Parameters:**
*   **Network Architecture:** MLP with 6 fully-connected layers, 512 neurons per hidden layer [S3, unofficial].
*   **Sampling:** $N$ surface samples, $M$ incident direction samples per surface sample. Typical values for $M$ are not explicitly given, but [S3, unofficial] mentions using 8 spp for LHS and 2048 for RHS in related experiments, implying high sample counts for stability.
*   **Optimizer:** Adam [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Bias and Variance:** The baseline gradient estimator is biased and has high variance due to the stochasticity of Monte Carlo integration in the RHS and its noisy derivatives [S2, unofficial]. This leads to slow training and occasionally darkened renders [S2, unofficial].
*   **Correlation Bias:** Using the same Monte Carlo samples for both factors in the gradient estimation leads to $E(ab) \neq E(a)E(b)$, a known issue with generic AD in inverse rendering [S3, unofficial].
*   **Memory Intensity:** Storing the full computation graph of Monte Carlo rendering algorithms is memory intensive, making it prohibitive for complex scenes [S3, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Differentiable Neural Radiosity](7410-differentiable-neural-radiosity.md)
A variant that pre-trains a neural network to cache the differential radiance distribution, enabling view-independent gradient computation with constant time and memory complexity relative to path length. It avoids the quadratic time complexity of Radiative Backpropagation and the memory overhead of Path Replay Backpropagation.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method requires tracing rays for each sample to compute the RHS integral, leading to a time complexity proportional to $N \times M$ per optimization step, where $N$ is the number of surface samples and $M$ is the number of incident direction samples [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage is dominated by the storage of the neural network parameters and the computation graph for automatic differentiation, which can be prohibitive for complex scenes [S3, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** The baseline method suffers from slow convergence and potential darkening of renders due to biased and high-variance gradients [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Lightweight neural networks can model global radiance fields effectively [S1, unofficial]. It does not require explicit light transport simulation per step [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** High variance and bias in gradients can hinder convergence [S2, unofficial]. Memory intensive for complex scenes [S3, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** Suitable for scenes where global illumination effects are important and computational resources allow for high sample counts [S1, unofficial].
*   **When Not to Use:** Not suitable for real-time applications or very complex scenes due to memory and time constraints [S3, unofficial].

## Background
Neural Radiosity was introduced by Hadadan et al. in 2021 [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial]. It addresses the complexity of solving the rendering equation by leveraging neural networks to predict outgoing radiance [S1, unofficial]. The method has been implemented in Mitsuba2 with Python bindings, utilizing its differentiable megakernel for automatic differentiation [S3, unofficial].
