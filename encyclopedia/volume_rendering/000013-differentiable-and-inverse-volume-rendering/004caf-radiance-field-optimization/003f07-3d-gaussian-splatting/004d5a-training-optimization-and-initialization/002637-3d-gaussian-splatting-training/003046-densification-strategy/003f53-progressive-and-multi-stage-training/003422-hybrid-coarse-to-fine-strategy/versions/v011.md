# Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy

Also called: Hybrid coarse-to-fine training, Multi-stage point cloud refinement

## Overview
The Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy is a training methodology for point-based radiance field representations that decouples optimization into distinct temporal stages to manage scene complexity [S1]. Unlike single-stage training, which often struggles to converge on high-frequency details or suffers from instability, this approach first establishes a coarse global structure and then progressively densifies the representation to capture fine details [S1]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Curriculum Learning with Slice and Voxel Supervision](12921-curriculum-learning-with-slice-and-voxel-supervision.md) by focusing on the structural density of the primitives themselves rather than the supervision signal, and from [Fine-tuning](9138-fine-tuning.md) by integrating densification directly into the primary optimization loop rather than as a post-processing step.

## How it works
The method operates on a set of 3D points (or mesh vertices) that are optimized to minimize rendering error. The core mechanism is the iterative insertion of new primitives based on error metrics, followed by re-triangulation or re-initialization of features.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of input images (and optionally depth/normal maps), an initial sparse set of primitives (points or mesh vertices), and hyperparameters for densification frequency and thresholds.
*   **Outputs:** A dense, optimized set of primitives with associated attributes (position, color, opacity, etc.) that can be rendered via differentiable rasterization or ray-marching.

### Data Structures
The primary data structure is a list of primitives. In the specific implementation of Coarse-to-Fine Densification [S2], this is a mesh defined by:
*   **Vertices ($V$):** A list of 3D positions and associated feature vectors (e.g., RGB, spherical harmonics coefficients).
*   **Triangles ($T$):** A list of indices referencing three vertices, forming the surface geometry.
*   **Pixel Mapping:** A mapping from each triangle to the set of pixels it covers during rasterization, used for error aggregation.

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Generate an initial sparse mesh or point cloud.
    *   *For Mesh-based initialization [S2]:* Compute Sobel gradient magnitudes on a downsampled target image. Sample initial vertex positions proportionally to edge strength, applying a small uniform floor to ensure coverage. Create a Delaunay triangulation of these points, adding boundary vertices to ensure full image coverage.
    *   *For Point-based initialization [S1]:* Initialize a sparse point cloud, often using camera poses and depth estimates.

2.  **Coarse Training:**
    *   Render the current primitives to generate images.
    *   Compute the loss (e.g., L1, SSIM) between rendered and target images.
    *   Update primitive attributes via gradient descent.
    *   Continue until a warm-up period or iteration threshold is met.

3.  **Densification (Iterative):**
    *   **Scoring:** Periodically (e.g., every 500 iterations) score each primitive $t$ based on rendering error and spatial extent.
        *   *Formula [S2]:*
            $$ \text{score}_t = \overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t \cdot |\text{pixels}_t|^\alpha $$
            Where:
            *   $\overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t$ is the mean per-pixel Structural Dissimilarity (1 - SSIM) of triangle $t$.
            *   $|\text{pixels}_t|$ is the number of pixels covered by triangle $t$.
            *   $\alpha = 0.75$ is an area-weighting exponent [S2].
        *   *Constraint [S2]:* Exclude triangles with fewer than 3 pixels from scoring to prevent noise-driven splitting.
    *   **Selection:** Identify the top-scoring primitives.
    *   **Insertion:**
        *   *For Mesh [S2]:* Insert centroid vertices for the top-scoring triangles. The number of new vertices is capped at $0.35 \times V$, where $V$ is the current vertex count. Initialize new vertex features by averaging the features of the parent triangle's vertices. Re-run Delaunay triangulation on the full vertex set.
        *   *For Points [S1]:* Clone or split high-error points. New points are typically initialized with attributes interpolated from parent points.
    *   **Pruning (Optional):** Remove low-opacity or redundant primitives to maintain efficiency.

4.  **Fine Training:**
    *   Resume gradient descent on the now denser set of primitives.
    *   Repeat steps 3 and 4 until convergence or a maximum iteration count is reached.

### Parameters
*   **Densification Interval:** Frequency of densification steps (e.g., every 500 iterations) [S2].
*   **Start/End Iterations:** Densification may be restricted to a specific window (e.g., iterations 1,500 to 15,000) [S2].
*   **Area Weighting Exponent ($\alpha$):** Controls the influence of primitive size on splitting decisions (typically 0.75) [S2].
*   **Split Ratio:** Maximum fraction of new vertices relative to current count (e.g., 0.35) [S2].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Degeneracy:** Mesh-based methods may encounter degenerate triangles (e.g., colinear vertices). Detection and re-meshing are required, though rare in practice [S2].
*   **Small Triangle Noise:** Triangles covering very few pixels may have high error due to noise. Excluding triangles with fewer than 3 pixels prevents over-splitting in noisy regions [S2].
*   **Precision:** Gradient computation must be stable across varying primitive densities. Using differentiable rasterizers with custom CUDA kernels helps maintain performance and stability [S2].

## Subtopics

### Coarse-to-Fine Densification
A specific implementation of the hybrid strategy using mesh-based primitives [S2]. It differs from point-based variants by explicitly managing surface topology via Delaunay triangulation. It is chosen when surface continuity and explicit geometry are prioritized over volumetric density. The method scores triangles using a combination of DSSIM and pixel area, inserting centroids for high-scoring triangles and re-triangulating [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time increases with the number of primitives. However, the coarse-to-fine approach often converges faster than single-stage training because the initial coarse stage provides a good initialization [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage scales with the number of primitives. The hybrid strategy allows for a more efficient distribution of primitives, avoiding the need for an excessively dense initial cloud [S1].
*   **Performance:** On the Blender dataset, removing the hybrid strategy reduces PSNR from 30.3 dB to 26.6 dB, SSIM from 0.945 to 0.925, and increases LPIPS from 0.078 to 0.163 [S1]. This demonstrates a significant quality improvement.
*   **Strengths:** Better handling of high-frequency details; more stable convergence; efficient use of primitives.
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of densification parameters (interval, thresholds); mesh-based variants may suffer from topological issues if not handled [S2].
*   **When to Use:** When rendering quality is critical and the scene contains complex high-frequency details.
*   **When Not to Use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and coarse approximations are acceptable.

## Background
The Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy originates from research into differentiable point-based radiance fields, which aim to provide an alternative to volumetric neural rendering [S1]. It was introduced to address the limitations of single-stage training, which often fails to capture fine details or converges to suboptimal solutions. The method builds on the principle of progressive densification, where the density of primitives is increased iteratively [S1, S2]. The mesh-based variant, Coarse-to-Fine Densification, was further developed to leverage explicit surface representations for higher quality reconstruction [S2].
