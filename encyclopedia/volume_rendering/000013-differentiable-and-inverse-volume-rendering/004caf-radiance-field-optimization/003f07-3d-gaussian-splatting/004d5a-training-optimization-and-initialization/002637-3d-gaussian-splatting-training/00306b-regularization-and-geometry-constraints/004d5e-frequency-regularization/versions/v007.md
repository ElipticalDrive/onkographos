# Frequency Regularization
Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Geometry Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time technique that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. It solves the problem where standard pixel-wise losses (e.g., L1) fail to differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions, leading to redundant Gaussian creation and blurred details [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise losses, FR increases pixel gradients in over-reconstructed regions by explicitly disentangling frequency components, enabling adaptive densification [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, and their corresponding frequency representations.
*   **Outputs:** Regularization loss terms $\mathcal{L}_{amplitude}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{phase}$ (or combined discrepancy), which are added to the total loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Frequency Domain:** Complex-valued matrices $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$ per channel.
*   **Amplitude/Phase:** Real-valued matrices $|F(u, v)|$ and $\angle F(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$ per channel.

**Steps:**
1.  **Fourier Transform:** Compute the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) for the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth $I$ to obtain frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$.
    $$ F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
    where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value [S2].
2.  **Decomposition:** For each frequency component $(u, v)$, decompose the complex frequency value $F(u, v)$ into amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$:
    $$ |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2} $$
    $$ \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right) $$
    where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$ [S2].
3.  **Discrepancy Calculation:** Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ using the Euclidean metric over all pixels and frequencies:
    $$ d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2} $$
    $$ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2} $$
    where $F$ and $\hat{F}$ are the ground truth and rendered frequency values, respectively [S2].
4.  **Loss Formulation:** The regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ is typically a weighted sum of $d_a$ and $d_p$. In the base FreGS formulation, these discrepancies are used directly. Advanced variants may apply frequency annealing (progressive regularization from low to high frequencies) to guide coarse-to-fine densification [S2].
5.  **Optimization:** The gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ flow back through the differentiable rasterizer to update Gaussian parameters $\theta$ (centers $\mu$, scales $s$, rotations $q$, opacities $\alpha$, and colors $c$) [Parent]. The Adam optimizer is used for training [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimizer:** Adam [S2].
*   **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
*   **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS [S2].
*   **Typical Values:** Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Over-reconstruction Bias:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can bias the optimization towards undesirable artifacts and restrict Gaussian densification [S2].
*   **Gradient Vanishing:** In well-reconstructed regions, pixel-wise L1 loss yields small gradients, preventing densification. FR mitigates this by maintaining significant gradients in over-reconstructed regions [S2].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.fft

def frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image: torch.Tensor, ground_truth_image: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the amplitude and phase discrepancy loss between rendered and ground truth images.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C).
        ground_truth_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C).
        
    Returns:
        Scalar loss tensor.
    """
    # Ensure inputs are float32 for FFT stability
    rendered_image = rendered_image.float()
    ground_truth_image = ground_truth_image.float()
    
    # Compute 2D FFT. Shift zero frequency to center is not strictly necessary for magnitude/phase diff
    # but standard FFT output is used here as per formula definition.
    # torch.fft.fft2 expects input shape (..., H, W). If C is last, we need to handle channels.
    # Assuming single channel or processing per channel.
    
    # For simplicity, assume single channel (H, W) or reshape. 
    # If multi-channel, compute loss per channel and average.
    
    # Compute FFT
    F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image)
    F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth_image)
    
    # Compute Amplitude
    amp_rendered = torch.abs(F_rendered)
    amp_gt = torch.abs(F_gt)
    
    # Compute Phase
    phase_rendered = torch.angle(F_rendered)
    phase_gt = torch.angle(F_gt)
    
    # Compute Discrepancies (Euclidean distance averaged over H, W)
    # Note: The formula uses sum over x,y. We average over H*W.
    # If batch dimension exists, mean over batch as well.
    
    # Amplitude Discrepancy
    diff_amp = amp_rendered - amp_gt
    d_a = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(diff_amp ** 2))
    
    # Phase Discrepancy
    diff_phase = phase_rendered - phase_gt
    d_p = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(diff_phase ** 2))
    
    # Combine losses (weights not specified in base definition, assume equal or configurable)
    loss = d_a + d_p
    
    return loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time technique that constrains the intrinsic scales of 4D Gaussians during deformation to prevent excessive dilation and improve anti-aliasing in video reconstruction. It solves the problem where standard Gaussian Splatting methods suffer from artifacts or loss of detail when tested at sampling rates different from training, by jointly regulating the maximum sampling frequency relative to the Gaussian's scale constraint. Unlike [Mip-Splatting](52-mip-splatting.md), which uses a fixed expansion scale, this method dynamically adapts frequency constraints, outperforming fixed-scale approaches in monocular video reconstruction.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The addition of 2D FFT and inverse operations per iteration increases computational overhead compared to standard L1 loss. The complexity is dominated by the FFT, $O(HW \log(HW))$ per image per channel.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing frequency domain representations for rendered and ground truth images, adding $O(HW)$ memory overhead per channel.
*   **Measured Performance:** FreGS shows superior capability in revealing over-reconstruction regions compared to L1 loss, leading to better Gaussian densification [S2]. It achieves performance comparable to or better than state-of-the-art NeRF-based methods and 3D-GS on datasets like Mip-NeRF360 and Tank&Temple, with a similar number of Gaussians [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Effectively mitigates over-reconstruction, improves detail recovery in high-frequency regions, and guides adaptive densification.
*   **Limitations:** Naive application can lead to artifacts if low/high frequency components are not handled separately (e.g., via annealing). Computationally more expensive than spatial domain losses.
*   **When to use:** When the scene exhibits over-reconstruction artifacts, blurry details, or when adaptive densification is required to capture high-frequency structures.
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are severely constrained and the scene is low-frequency, or when simpler regularization (e.g., depth) suffices.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced as a direction for improving 3D GS optimization to address convergence speed, visual artifacts, and improper Gaussians [S1]. The specific method "FreGS" (3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization) was proposed to alleviate over-reconstruction by minimizing the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth [S2]. It builds on the observation that pixel-wise L1 loss fails to differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions [S2]. The technique has been further adapted for sparse-view scenarios (e.g., DWTGS) using wavelet transforms [S4, unofficial].
