# Gaussian Representation and Truncation

Also called: Gaussian Truncation, Gaussian Pruning, Gaussian Density Control

## Overview
Gaussian Representation and Truncation encompasses the mechanisms for defining the spatial extent of 3D Gaussian primitives, culling those with negligible contribution to the rendered image, and managing their lifecycle (creation, splitting, merging, and removal) during optimization. It solves the problem of computational inefficiency and visual artifacts caused by redundant or overly large Gaussians that extend beyond the visible region or contribute minimally to the final pixel color. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by focusing on the **spatial decay profile** (e.g., Inverse Multiquadric vs. Gaussian), the **truncation threshold logic** (opacity-independent vs. opacity-dependent), and the **structural constraints** (atomization, voxel-fixing, mixed-primitives) applied to the Gaussian primitives themselves, rather than just the rendering pipeline or compression.

## Shared principles
The core mechanism shared by all methods in this family is the evaluation of a Gaussian primitive's contribution to a pixel based on its distance from the pixel center in screen space.

1.  **Projection:** A 3D Gaussian defined by mean $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$ (parameterized by scale $s$ and rotation $R$), and opacity $\omega$ is projected into 2D screen space. The 2D covariance $\Sigma'$ is approximated using the Jacobian of the projective transformation [S1].
2.  **Conic Matrix:** The inverse of the 2D covariance, $\Sigma'^{-1}$, is computed to form the conic matrix $C$. This matrix allows for efficient evaluation of the Mahalanobis distance.
3.  **Distance Evaluation:** For a pixel at position $p$, the displacement vector $d = p - \mu_{2D}$ is computed. The squared Mahalanobis distance (often called the "ellipse term") is calculated as:
    $$ \text{ellipse} = d^T \Sigma'^{-1} d $$
4.  **Culling:** If the ellipse term exceeds a certain threshold, the Gaussian is considered too distant to contribute significantly and is skipped. This threshold is typically derived from a standard deviation count (e.g., $3\sigma$) or a fixed value (e.g., 9.0 for standard Gaussian, 9.0 for Inverse Multiquadric).
5.  **Alpha Computation:** If the Gaussian passes the culling test, its alpha value $\alpha$ is computed by combining the spatial decay function with the opacity parameter $\omega$.

## Subtopics

### [Inverse Multiquadric Splatting](9910-inverse-multiquadric-splatting.md)
Replaces the standard Gaussian radial basis function with an inverse multiquadric function ($G = \frac{1}{\text{ellipse} + 1}$) to alter the decay profile. It solves the problem of Gaussian tails extending too far or decaying too slowly, which causes visual artifacts and inefficient memory usage in large scenes. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting by using a rational function rather than an exponential, and from [Opacity-Independent Gaussian Truncation](10266-opacity-independent-gaussian-truncation.md) by retaining opacity weighting but modifying the spatial decay kernel. It uses a correction factor of $1.38$ to scale the covariance matrices to compensate for the faster decay.

### [Opacity-Independent Gaussian Truncation](10266-opacity-independent-gaussian-truncation.md)
Modifies the standard culling condition to evaluate the geometric Gaussian response *before* applying the opacity scalar. This addresses the limitation in the original implementation where low-opacity Gaussians are effectively invisible and receive no gradients, preventing consistent truncation below approximately 3.03 standard deviations. It enables the investigation of opacity gradients that are otherwise ignored when the total transparency $\alpha$ is zero.

### [Atomized Proliferation](12924-atomized-proliferation.md)
A training strategy that enhances geometric precision by constraining Gaussians representing fine details into "Atom Gaussians" (isotropic spheroids with uniform size) and prioritizing their proliferation to align with scene geometry, followed by pruning to merge large smooth surfaces. It addresses the instability of standard densification and the tendency of Gaussians to form noisy, elongated structures that do not adhere to underlying geometric structures, leading to blurry artifacts and slower convergence. Unlike standard 3DGS, which struggles to stabilize densification, AtomGS uses a warm-up strategy that aggressively densifies initially, then merges, leading to a declining and stabilizing trend in Gaussian count.

### [Voxel-based Gaussian Splatting](13071-voxel-based-gaussian-splatting.md)
A specialized volume representation that places isometric 3D Gaussians at the fixed centroids of a regular 3D voxel grid to enable efficient Computed Tomography (CT) volume extraction and differentiable X-ray rendering. Unlike standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) which optimizes primitive positions and uses spherical harmonics for view-dependent color, VoxGS fixes Gaussian positions to voxel indices and removes color coefficients, retaining only opacity, scale, and rotation to model isotropic density. This design solves the instability of optimizing continuous positions for discrete volume data and allows direct, fast indexing of the density field without trilinear interpolation.

### [Vertex-level Pruning in Mixed-Primitive Gaussian Splatting](13983-vertex-level-pruning-in-mixed-primitive-gaussian-splatting.md)
A reduction mechanism for mixed-primitive Gaussian Splatting that removes redundant vertices from Gaussian triangles and lines during training to refine the geometric representation. It operates in conjunction with standard primitive-level opacity pruning, converting high-vertex primitives into lower-vertex equivalents (ellipses or lines) when their spatial configuration becomes degenerate. This method distinguishes itself from sibling truncation techniques by targeting the internal vertex structure of mixed primitives rather than discarding entire primitives or modifying the alpha blending function.

### [SplatQ](14415-splatq.md)
A post-training Gaussian pruning framework that partitions Gaussians by spatial proximity and appearance similarity into size-limited blocks, smooths importance-based block retention quotas to prevent budget concentration, and constructs a quadratic approximation of joint deletion effects to jointly select Gaussians under a fixed budget. It differs from global Top-K methods by decomposing the selection into subproblems of specific size and solving a combinatorial optimization problem within each block, rather than ranking all Gaussians globally by independent scores.

### [3D Gaussian Particle Approximation of VDB Datasets](14723-3d-gaussian-particle-approximation-of-vdb-datasets.md)
Converts OpenVDB volumetric datasets into a set of non-overlapping 3D Gaussian particles to enable memory-efficient, lossy representation with Level of Detail (LOD) support, rendered via forward ray marching using NVIDIA OptiX. It differs from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting by treating Gaussians as volumetric particles representing local opacity distributions rather than surface splats, and by deriving primitives directly from VDB leaf nodes rather than optimizing from images. Unlike [Inverse Multiquadric Splatting](9910-inverse-multiquadric-splatting.md) which modifies the basis function, this method retains the standard Gaussian density function but constrains the geometry to non-overlapping voxel regions.

### POG (Potentially Over-large Gaussians)
POG is a variant discussed in the context of Hard Gaussian Splatting (HGS). It involves growing Gaussians based on potentially over-large regions. Ablation results on Tanks&Temples show POG yields PSNR 26.26, SSIM 0.879, LPIPS 0.121, whereas Hard Gaussians (HG) yield PSNR 26.36, SSIM 0.880, LPIPS 0.126 [S1]. POG improves LPIPS slightly but degrades PSNR significantly because it overgrows already learned areas, making them susceptible to redundant Gaussians [S1].

### AtomGS
AtomGS implements Atomized Proliferation. It constrains ellipsoid Gaussians of various sizes into uniformly-sized Atom Gaussians and applies a progressive split schedule to provide better coverage of scene details, emphasizing densification in accordance with scene details [S2, unofficial]. It retains larger anisotropic Gaussians to represent the background or geometric areas with few features, while smaller anisotropic Gaussians are constrained into Atom Gaussians [S2, unofficial]. It also integrates Geometry-Guided Optimization using an Edge-Aware Normal Loss, which incorporates weights from a 2D edge detector into the curvature map to impose stricter constraints on Gaussian positioning aligned with flat surfaces while allowing flexibility for irregularly shaped areas [S2, unofficial]. During pruning, it merges Gaussians covering large, less detailed surfaces, resulting in a similar or even reduced number of Gaussians compared to original 3DGS while retaining competitive rendering quality [S2, unofficial]. Unlike standard 3DGS which freely drifts and splits, AtomGS provides precise guidance on where to focus Gaussians for better 3D geometry optimization, balancing visual and geometric accuracy by refining underlying geometry alignment [S2, unofficial].

### Vertex Pruning
Vertex pruning is a mechanism designed to support mixed-primitive-based learning, as standard pruning is incompatible with mixed primitives [S3, unofficial]. It prunes vertices of the mixed primitives to optimize the representation [S3, unofficial].

### Gaussian Reduction with Learnable Mask
This method reduces the number of Gaussians using a learnable mask during optimization, targeting small-sized kernels for removal [S4]. It applies binary masks $M$ to the opacities and scale attributes of the kernels at every densification step [S4]. It uses residual vector quantization with $L$ stages of vector quantization for geometric attributes like scale and rotation [S4]. It represents view-dependent colors via a grid-based neural field using hash grids for input positions, features, and view direction, fed into an MLP [S4]. The end-to-end loss is $L = L_{ren} + \lambda_m L_m + L_r + L_s$, where $L_{ren}$ is L1+SSIM loss, $L_m$ is masking loss ($\frac{1}{N} \sum \sigma(m_n)$), and $L_r, L_s$ are rotation and scale objective functions [S4]. The masking threshold $\epsilon$ is used in the sigmoid function for the learnable mask, and $\lambda_m$ is the weight for the masking loss term, typically $5 \times 10^{-4}$ [S4].

### 3DGS Visibility Pruning
This is the application of visibility-based pruning to 3D Gaussian Splatting, using Gaussian centroids as reference points for visibility calculation [S4]. It represents each Gaussian kernel by its centroid position, scaling matrix $S$, and rotation matrix $R$ defining the covariance $\Sigma$ [S4]. It uses the Gaussian centroids as the 3D points $p_i$ in the visibility mask computation algorithm [S4].

## Background
The concept of truncating Gaussian contributions to optimize rendering performance originates from the original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper [S1]. The standard approach uses a fixed threshold (typically corresponding to $3\sigma$) to cull distant splats. Subsequent research has explored various ways to improve this process, including modifying the decay function (Inverse Multiquadric), decoupling opacity from truncation thresholds, and introducing structural constraints like atomization or voxel grids. The field has also seen the development of post-training pruning techniques like SplatQ and visibility-based pruning to reduce model size without significant quality loss.
