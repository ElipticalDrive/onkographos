# Negative Gaussian Splatting

Also called: Diff-Gaussian

## Overview
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) is a variant of 3D Gaussian Splatting that extends the representation of 3D scenes by incorporating "negative Gaussians"—ellipsoids with negative color values—alongside standard positive Gaussians [S1, S2]. This method solves the limitation of standard Gaussian Splatting, which relies on simple ellipsoid shapes that struggle to model complex nonlinear structures and high-frequency color transitions without a prohibitive increase in the number of primitives [S2]. By allowing Gaussians with inverse colors to cancel each other out, NegGS provides greater flexibility in shape representation and color modulation, enabling the approximation of complex distributions (such as donut or moon-shaped datasets) and improving the rendering of shadows and high-frequency details [S1, S2]. It differs from its siblings by explicitly utilizing negative opacity contributions within the alpha-blending pipeline, whereas siblings like Scaffold-GS or GeoGaussian modify the geometry or kernel shape but maintain positive-only contributions [Parent].

## How it works
The method extends the standard Gaussian Splatting framework [Parent] by allowing the color attribute $c_i$ of a Gaussian primitive to take negative values. The rendering pipeline remains identical to the parent method, but the set of optimized primitives is a joint family of positive and negative components.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of camera poses and corresponding 2D images; initial 3D point cloud (typically from Structure-from-Motion); hyperparameters for initialization and optimization.
*   **Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussian primitives $G_{NeGS}$, each defined by mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, opacity $\alpha$, and color $\mathbf{c}$ (where $\mathbf{c}$ can be negative); rendered images for any novel view.

### Data Structures
The scene is represented by a joint family of Gaussians:
$$ G_{NeGS} = G_{GS} \cup G_{-GS} $$
where:
*   $G_{GS}$ contains tuples with positive color values ($c_i > 0$).
*   $G_{-GS}$ contains tuples with negative color values ($c_i < 0$) [S1].

Each Gaussian primitive $G_i$ retains the standard attributes from the parent method:
*   $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Mean position.
*   $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix (parameterized via rotation $\mathbf{R}$ and scale $\mathbf{S}$).
*   $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
*   $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Color (RGB). In NegGS, $\mathbf{c}_i$ can be negative.

To preserve rendering speed and leverage the existing implementation, negative Gaussians are tracked via a binary mask within the standard Gaussian Splatting data structure [S1].

### The Diff-Gaussian Concept
The theoretical basis for NegGS is the "Diff-Gaussian" distribution, defined as the ratio of two Gaussian probability density functions (PDFs) [S2]:
$$ f_{Diff}(w) = \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(w)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(w)} $$
where $f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}$ and $f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}$ are two standard Gaussian densities [S1]. This ratio can approximate complex nonlinear structures that single Gaussians cannot [S2].

The optimal coefficient $c_{opt}$ for a Diff-Gaussian distribution is the minimum value of this ratio:
$$ c_{opt} := \min_{x} \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(x)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(x)} = \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(w)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(w)} $$
This minimum is achieved if and only if the vector $w$ satisfies:
$$ \Sigma_1^{-1}(w - m_1) = \Sigma_0^{-1}(w - m_0) \quad \text{(Equation 6 in [S1])} $$

To find $w$, we minimize the logarithmic difference, which reduces to minimizing a quadratic function $q(x)$:
$$ q(x) = x^T A x + 2b^T x $$
where:
*   $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1}$
*   $b = \Sigma_0^{-1} m_0 - \Sigma_1^{-1} m_1$

Note: The source [S1] states $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1}$ in the proof derivation (Equation 7), but the summary notes list $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0$. The proof derivation in [S1] is mathematically consistent with $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1}$ derived from $\log f_0 - \log f_1$. We follow the proof derivation [S1]. The condition for the minimum to exist is that $A$ is positive semi-definite, which implies $\Sigma_0 - \Sigma_1$ is positive semi-definite (i.e., $\Sigma_0 \succeq \Sigma_1$) [S1].

For diagonal covariance matrices $\Sigma_0 = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_n^2)$ and $\Sigma_1 = \text{diag}(h_1 \sigma_1^2, \dots, h_n \sigma_n^2)$ with $0 < h_i \le 1$, the coordinates $w_i$ of the null density vector (where the ratio is minimized) are:
1.  $w_i = \frac{m_{1,i} - h_i m_{0,i}}{1 - h_i}$ if $h_i < 1$
2.  $w_i \in \mathbb{R}$ if $h_i = 1$ and $m_{1,i} = m_{0,i}$ [S1]

### Optimization Steps
The optimization follows the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting training loop [Parent], with the following modifications:

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Initialize a set of Gaussians from the SfM point cloud.
    *   Assign a binary mask to each Gaussian indicating if it is positive ($G_{GS}$) or negative ($G_{-GS}$).
    *   **Hyperparameter:** The initial number of negative Gaussians is critical. It is typically set to approximately 20% of the total Gaussians [S1]. This is determined via grid search/cross-validation [S1].

2.  **Rasterization:**
    *   Project all Gaussians ($G_{GS}$ and $G_{-GS}$) to 2D.
    *   Sort by depth.
    *   Composite using alpha blending. The color contribution of a negative Gaussian is subtracted from the accumulated color, effectively acting as a "negative opacity" or color cancellation [S1].
    *   *Note:* The standard alpha blending equation $\mathbf{C} = \sum c_i \alpha_i \prod_{j < i} (1 - \alpha_j)$ applies, where $c_i$ can be negative [Parent, S1].

3.  **Loss Computation:**
    *   Compute photometric loss (e.g., L1 loss) between the rendered image and the ground truth image [S1].

4.  **Backpropagation:**
    *   Backpropagate the loss to all parameters: $\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, \alpha, \mathbf{c}$ for both positive and negative Gaussians [S1].

5.  **Adaptive Control (Pruning and Densification):**
    *   **Pruning:** Gaussians with low opacity are removed, regardless of whether they are positive or negative [S1].
    *   **Densification:** New Gaussians are introduced into empty 3D regions (areas with high gradient or low coverage) [S1].
    *   The optimization dynamically adapts the number of components. Experiments show that negative components allow the method to require fewer total Gaussians to achieve similar or better quality than standard GS [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Precision:** The subtraction of colors can lead to cancellation artifacts if positive and negative Gaussians are not well-balanced. However, the alpha blending ensures that the final color is bounded if opacities are clamped [G].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The condition $\Sigma_0 \succeq \Sigma_1$ (positive semi-definite difference) is required for the Diff-Gaussian minimum to be well-defined in the theoretical derivation. In practice, the optimization handles arbitrary covariances, but the theoretical justification relies on this [S1].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If two Gaussians have identical parameters and inverse colors, they cancel each other out completely. This is a feature, not a bug, as it allows for efficient representation of complex shapes [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** NegGS preserves the original Gaussian Splatting rendering speed because it leverages the same rasterization framework with only a binary mask overhead [S1]. Training time is comparable to standard GS, as the optimization loop is identical [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage is slightly higher due to the storage of the binary mask and potentially more complex covariance interactions, but the total number of Gaussians required is often lower than standard GS for complex scenes, offsetting this [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset, NegGS-30K achieved an average PSNR of 27.39, SSIM of 0.812, and LPIPS of 0.219, compared to GS-30K's 27.21 PSNR, 0.815 SSIM, and 0.214 LPIPS [S1].
    *   On the NeRF Synthetic dataset, NegGS showed slight improvements in PSNR and SSIM over GS [S1].
    *   NegGS is particularly effective on datasets with high-frequency elements and rapid color transitions, such as Shiny Blender and Tanks and Temples [S1, S2].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Models complex nonlinear structures (e.g., donut shapes) that standard Gaussians struggle with [S2].
    *   Improves representation of shadows and high-frequency details [S2].
    *   Requires fewer total Gaussians to achieve comparable or better quality [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   The initial number of negative Gaussians is a critical hyperparameter (typically ~20% of total) [S1].
    *   May introduce artifacts if the balance between positive and negative components is poor [G].
*   **When to use:** Use NegGS when standard Gaussian Splatting fails to capture high-frequency details, complex nonlinear geometries, or accurate shadowing, and when computational resources allow for the initial hyperparameter tuning.
*   **When not to use:** Use standard GS if simplicity and speed are paramount, or if the scene is relatively simple and does not require the additional flexibility of negative components.

## Background
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) was introduced as the first method to extend the simple ellipsoid shapes of Gaussian Splatting to more complex nonlinear structures [S2]. It is based on the theoretical concept of "Diff-Gaussians," which are formed by dividing the PDFs of two Gaussians [S2]. The method was evaluated on several standard datasets, including NeRF Synthetic, Mip-NeRF360, Tanks and Temples, Deep Blending, and Shiny Blender [S1]. It was developed to address the limitation that standard Gaussians, due to their linear nature, require a multitude of components to reflect complex nonlinear structures, leading to increased time complexity [S2].
