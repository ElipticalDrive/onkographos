# Negative Gaussian Splatting

Also called: NegGS, Diff-Gaussian

## Overview
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting framework by incorporating "negative Gaussians"—ellipsoids with negative color values—into the optimization process [S1, S2]. This allows the representation of high-frequency elements, rapid color transitions, and complex nonlinear structures (such as shadows or donut-shaped densities) that are difficult to model with positive Gaussians alone [S1, S2]. Unlike standard Gaussian Splatting, which requires many components to approximate complex shapes, NegGS uses the cancellation effect between positive and negative Gaussians to achieve greater flexibility with fewer total primitives [S1, S2].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A set of camera poses and corresponding 2D images of a scene.
**Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussian primitives (means, covariances, opacities, and colors) representing the scene.

**Data Structures:**
The method maintains a joint family of Gaussians $G_{NeGS} = G_{GS} \cup G_{-GS}$ [S1].
*   $G_{GS}$: A list of Gaussian tuples with positive color values $c_i$.
*   $G_{-GS}$: A list of Gaussian tuples with negative color values $c_i$.
*   Each Gaussian tuple contains:
    *   Mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$
    *   Covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ (parameterized via rotation $\mathbf{R}$ and scale $\mathbf{s}$)
    *   Opacity $\alpha \in [0, 1]$
    *   Color $\mathbf{c}$ (scalar or spherical harmonics coefficients). For $G_{-GS}$, the effective color contribution is $-\mathbf{c}$ or the color value itself is negative.
*   A binary mask tracks which Gaussians belong to $G_{GS}$ and which to $G_{-GS}$ to preserve rendering speed [S1].

**Rendering Kernel:**
The rendering process remains based on alpha blending of projected 2D Gaussians onto the image plane, identical to standard Gaussian Splatting [S1, S2]. The key difference is that Gaussians in $G_{-GS}$ contribute negative color values to the accumulated color buffer during the blending step.

**Optimization Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize the total number of Gaussians $N$. A critical hyperparameter is the initial proportion of negative Gaussians. The sources indicate that approximately 20% of the total Gaussians should be initialized as negative ($G_{-GS}$) for optimal efficacy [S1]. The remaining 80% are positive ($G_{GS}$) [S1].
2.  **Differentiable Rasterization:** Render the current set of Gaussians $G_{NeGS}$ to generate images.
3.  **Loss Computation:** Compute the rendering loss (e.g., L1, SSIM) between the rendered images and the ground truth images [S1, S2].
4.  **Gradient Descent:** Update all Gaussian attributes ($\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, \alpha, \mathbf{c}$) using gradient descent to minimize the loss [S1, S2].
5.  **Dynamic Adaptation:**
    *   **Pruning:** Gaussians with low opacity are reduced (pruned) from both $G_{GS}$ and $G_{-GS}$ [S1].
    *   **Densification:** New Gaussians are introduced into empty 3D regions to adapt the number of components [S1].
    *   Note: The initial number of negative Gaussians is treated as a hyperparameter adapted via grid search during cross-validation, as the final count of negative components is typically significantly lower than positive ones [S1].

**Theoretical Basis (Diff-Gaussian):**
The method can be interpreted as using "Diff-Gaussians," defined by the ratio of two Gaussian probability density functions (PDFs) [S2]. Let $f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}$ and $f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}$ be two Gaussian densities. The Diff-Gaussian distribution is formed by their ratio [S2]. The optimal coefficient $c_{opt}$ for this distribution is defined as:

$$ c_{opt} := \min_x \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(x)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(x)} = \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(w)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(w)} $$

if and only if $w$ satisfies [S1]:

$$ \Sigma_1^{-1}(w - m_1) = \Sigma_0^{-1}(w - m_0) $$

This condition is derived by minimizing the function:

$$ q(x) = x^T A x + 2b^T x $$

where $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1}$ and $b = \Sigma_0^{-1} m_0 - \Sigma_1^{-1} m_1$, assuming $A$ is positive semi-definite [S1].

For diagonal covariance matrices $\Sigma_0 = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_n^2)$ and $\Sigma_1 = \text{diag}(h_1 \sigma_1^2, \dots, h_n \sigma_n^2)$ with $0 < h_i \le 1$, the coordinates $w_i$ of the null density vector (where the ratio is minimized) are [S1]:
1.  $w_i = \frac{m_{1,i} - h_i m_{0,i}}{1 - h_i}$ if $h_i < 1$
2.  $w_i \in \mathbb{R}$ if $h_i = 1$ and $m_{1,i} = m_{0,i}$

This theoretical framework explains how two Gaussians with identical parameters and inverse colors can reduce each other, creating null-density regions or complex shapes [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method leverages the original Gaussian Splatting framework implementation, extending it with negative Gaussians tracked via a binary mask, thereby preserving the original GS rendering speed [S1]. However, it alleviates the time complexity increase associated with using many Gaussian components to reflect complex nonlinear structures in standard GS [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Performance:**
    *   On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset, NegGS-30K achieved an average PSNR of 27.39, SSIM of 0.812, and LPIPS of 0.219, compared to GS-30K's 27.21 PSNR, 0.815 SSIM, and 0.214 LPIPS [S1].
    *   On the NeRF Synthetic dataset, NegGS showed slight improvements or comparable performance to GS (e.g., Chair: 35.88 vs 35.82 PSNR) [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Models high-frequency elements with rapid color transitions [S1, S2].
    *   Improves the representation of shadows [S2].
    *   Allows for the modeling of complex nonlinear structures (e.g., donut, moon-shaped datasets) [S2].
    *   Requires fewer total Gaussians than standard GS to achieve similar or better results [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   The initial number of negative Gaussians is a critical hyperparameter; incorrect initialization can lead to suboptimal optimization [S1].
    *   Requires careful tuning of the ratio of negative to positive Gaussians (typically ~20% negative) [S1].
*   **When to use:** When the scene contains high-frequency details, sharp color transitions, or complex nonlinear geometries (like shadows or thin structures) that are poorly represented by standard positive Gaussians.
*   **When not to use:** For simple scenes where standard Gaussian Splatting performs well, as the added complexity of managing negative Gaussians may not provide significant benefits.

## Background
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) was introduced as the first paper to extend the simple ellipsoid shapes of Gaussian Splatting to more complex nonlinear structures [S2]. It is based on the concept of "Diff-Gaussians," which are distributions formed by dividing the PDFs of two Gaussians [S2]. The method was evaluated on several datasets including NeRF Synthetic, Mip-NeRF360, Tanks and Temples, Deep Blending, and Shiny Blender [S1].
