# Negative Gaussian Splatting

Also called: NegGS, Diff-Gaussian

## Overview
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) is a variant of 3D Gaussian Splatting that incorporates "negative Gaussians"—ellipsoids with negative color values—into the optimization process to model high-frequency elements and complex nonlinear structures [S1, S2]. Unlike standard Gaussian Splatting, which requires many components to approximate complex shapes, NegGS uses the subtraction of Gaussian densities (Diff-Gaussians) to achieve greater flexibility in shape representation and color modulation with fewer primitives [S1, S2].

## How it works
NegGS extends the standard Gaussian Splatting framework by allowing the color coefficient $c_i$ of a Gaussian primitive to be negative [S1]. This effectively models the scene as a difference of Gaussians (Diff-Gaussian), where the rendering equation remains the standard alpha compositing formula, but the contribution of a primitive can be subtractive [S1, S2].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of camera poses and corresponding 2D images; an initial set of 3D Gaussian primitives.
*   **Outputs:** Optimized Gaussian parameters (mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, opacity $\alpha$, color $\mathbf{c}$) representing the 3D scene.

### Data Structures
The method operates on a joint family of Gaussians $G_{NeGS}$, partitioned into two subsets:
$$ G_{NeGS} = G_{GS} \cup G_{-GS} $$
*   $G_{GS}$: Tuples with positive color values $c_i$.
*   $G_{-GS}$: Tuples with negative color values $c_i$ [S1].

To preserve the rendering speed of the original Gaussian Splatting implementation, negative Gaussians are tracked via a binary mask [S1]. The data structure for each Gaussian primitive $i$ includes:
*   $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Mean position.
*   $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix (parameterized by rotation and scale).
*   $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
*   $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Color (can be negative).
*   $m_i \in \{0, 1\}$: Binary mask indicating if the Gaussian is negative ($m_i=1$) or positive ($m_i=0$) [S1].

### Optimization and Initialization
The optimization strategy follows the standard Gaussian Splatting loop:
1.  **Pruning:** Gaussians with low opacity are removed.
2.  **Densification:** New Gaussians are introduced into empty 3D regions [S1].
3.  **Gradient Descent:** Minimize photometric loss (e.g., L1/L2) between rendered and ground-truth images [S1, S2].

**Initialization:**
The initial number of negative Gaussians is a critical hyperparameter. The optimal efficacy is achieved when approximately 20% of the total initial Gaussians are negative [S1]. The remaining 80% are initialized as positive Gaussians [S1].

### Mathematical Formulation: Diff-Gaussian
The core theoretical contribution is the "Diff-Gaussian," defined as the ratio of two Gaussian probability density functions (PDFs) [S2]. Let $f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}$ and $f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}$ be two Gaussian densities. The Diff-Gaussian density is proportional to:
$$ f_{diff}(w) \propto \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(w)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(w)} $$
The optimal coefficient $c_{opt}$ that minimizes this ratio (creating a "null density" or zero-crossing) is defined as [S1]:
$$ c_{opt} := \min_{x} \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(x)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(x)} = \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(w)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(w)} $$
This minimum is achieved if and only if the vector $w$ satisfies [S1]:
$$ \Sigma_1^{-1}(w - m_1) = \Sigma_0^{-1}(w - m_0) $$
This condition is equivalent to minimizing the quadratic function $q(x) = x^T A x + 2b^T x$, where [S1]:
$$ A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1} \quad \text{and} \quad b = \Sigma_0^{-1} m_0 - \Sigma_1^{-1} m_1 $$
Assuming $A$ is positive semi-definite, $w$ is the solution to the linear system derived from the gradient of $q(x)$ [S1].

**Diagonal Case:**
For diagonal covariance matrices $\Sigma_0 = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_n^2)$ and $\Sigma_1 = \text{diag}(h_1 \sigma_1^2, \dots, h_n \sigma_n^2)$ with $0 < h_i \le 1$, the coordinates $w_i$ of the null density vector are [S1]:
1.  $w_i = \frac{m_{1,i} - h_i m_{0,i}}{1 - h_i}$ if $h_i < 1$.
2.  $w_i \in \mathbb{R}$ if $h_i = 1$ and $m_{1,i} = m_{0,i}$ [S1].

This allows the imposition of a null density on a $k$-dimensional affine space, enabling the approximation of complex nonlinear structures like donuts or moons [S1, S2].

### Pseudocode
The following pseudocode outlines the NegGS training loop, extending the standard Gaussian Splatting rasterizer.

```python
def train_neggs(cameras, images, initial_gaussians):
    # 1. Initialization
    # Split initial_gaussians into positive and negative sets
    # Set ~20% of Gaussians as negative (color c_i < 0)
    # Track negative Gaussians with a binary mask is_negative[i]
    
    gaussians = initial_gaussians
    
    for step in range(num_steps):
        # 2. Rasterization
        # Project Gaussians to 2D screen space
        # Sort by depth
        # Render image using alpha compositing
        # Note: Color contribution c_i can be negative
        rendered_image = rasterize(gaussians, cameras[step])
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        gt_image = images[step]
        loss = compute_loss(rendered_image, gt_image) # e.g., L1 + SSIM
        
        # 4. Backpropagation
        # Compute gradients for mu, Sigma, alpha, c, and is_negative
        gradients = compute_gradients(loss, gaussians)
        
        # 5. Optimization Step
        update_parameters(gaussians, gradients, learning_rate)
        
        # 6. Dynamic Adaptation
        # Prune low-opacity Gaussians
        gaussians = prune_low_opacity(gaussians, threshold)
        
        # Densify: Clone and split Gaussians in high-gradient regions
        gaussians = densify_gaussians(gaussians, gradients, step)
        
        # Ensure mask consistency if parameters change significantly
        # [G] In practice, the sign of c_i determines the mask.
        # If c_i becomes positive, it moves to G_GS; if negative, to G_-GS.
        update_masks(gaussians)
        
    return gaussians
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Precision:** The ratio of two Gaussians can lead to numerical instability if the denominator Gaussian has very low variance or is far from the evaluation point. The condition $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1}$ being positive semi-definite is required for the minimization theorem to hold [S1].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** If $h_i = 1$ and $m_{1,i} = m_{0,i}$, the null density coordinate $w_i$ is undefined (any real number), implying a flat direction in the density ratio [S1].
*   **Initialization Sensitivity:** The number of negative Gaussians is critical. Too few may fail to capture high-frequency details; too many may lead to optimization instability or cancellation artifacts [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** NegGS preserves the original Gaussian Splatting rendering speed by leveraging the existing framework with a binary mask [S1]. However, training may require more iterations to converge due to the increased flexibility of the representation [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Slightly higher than standard GS due to the storage of the binary mask and potentially more complex gradient tracking for negative components, but the total number of Gaussians required is often lower [S1, S2].
*   **Performance:** On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset, NegGS-30K achieved an average PSNR of 27.39, SSIM of 0.812, and LPIPS of 0.219, compared to GS-30K's 27.21 PSNR, 0.815 SSIM, and 0.214 LPIPS [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Better modeling of high-frequency elements with rapid color transitions and improved shadow representation [S2]. Requires fewer total Gaussians to represent complex nonlinear structures compared to standard GS [S1, S2].
*   **Limitations:** Sensitive to the initial proportion of negative Gaussians [S1]. May introduce artifacts if the negative and positive components are not well-balanced during optimization [S1].

## Background
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) was introduced as the first method to extend the simple ellipsoid shapes of Gaussian Splatting to more complex nonlinear structures [S2]. It is based on the theoretical concept of Diff-Gaussians, which approximate structures like donuts and moon-shaped datasets by dividing the PDFs of two Gaussians [S2]. The method was evaluated on NeRF Synthetic, Mip-NeRF360, Tanks and Temples, Deep Blending, and Shiny Blender datasets [S1].
