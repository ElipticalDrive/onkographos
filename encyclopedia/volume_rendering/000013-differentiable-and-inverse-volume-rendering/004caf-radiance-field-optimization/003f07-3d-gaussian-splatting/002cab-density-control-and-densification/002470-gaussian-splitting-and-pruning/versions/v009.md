# Gaussian Splitting and Pruning

Also called: Deterministic splitting, Splitting Gaussians

## Overview
Gaussian Splitting and Pruning is a deterministic density control method that modifies the number of 3D Gaussian primitives in a radiance field by splitting those with high reconstruction responsibility and removing those with minimal contribution, based on statistical criteria [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of under-reconstructed or blurry regions (by splitting) and redundant or invisible primitives (by pruning) after an initial convergence phase [S1, unofficial]. Unlike the gradient-based sibling [Adaptive Densification](3034-adaptive-densification.md) which operates continuously during training, this method is typically applied periodically or after initial convergence, using a statistical analysis of rendering weights and losses to make decisions [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of 3D Gaussians $G = \{G_i\}$ with converged parameters (position $\mathbf{p}_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$, opacity weight $\lambda_i$, color $c_i$); a random batch of rays $R_{batch}$ (typically ~5% of the dataset); loss function $L(\cdot)$; z-scores $z_\lambda$ and $z_{\bar{l}}$.
*   **Outputs:** A modified set of Gaussians $G'$ with some removed and some split into two.

**Data Structures:**
*   Gaussian $G_i$: Contains position $\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, covariance matrix $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ (or rotation/scale), opacity weight $\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$, and color $c_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
*   Ray Batch: List of rays, each with associated per-ray loss $L_j$ and the set of weights $\{w_{i,j}\}$ contributed by each Gaussian $G_i$ to that ray.

**Steps:**

1.  **Pruning (Remove Minimal Contributors):**
    *   Compute the mean $\mu_\lambda$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\lambda$ of the opacity weights $\lambda_i$ across all Gaussians.
    *   Identify Gaussians to remove where:
        $$ \lambda_i \leq \mu_\lambda - z_\lambda \sigma_\lambda $$
        where $z_\lambda$ is a z-score, typically set to 2 [S1, unofficial].
    *   Remove these Gaussians from the scene.

2.  **Splitting Criteria (Identify High Responsibility):**
    *   Select a random batch of $M$ rays (e.g., 5% of the dataset) [S1, unofficial].
    *   For each ray $j$ in the batch, compute the per-ray loss $L_j$ (using any reconstruction loss, e.g., Eq. 14 in [S1, unofficial]) and the set of computed weights $w_{i,j}$ for each Gaussian $G_i$ (derived from Eqs. 7 and 9 in [S1, unofficial]).
    *   Estimate the average loss associated with each Gaussian $G_i$ over the batch:
        $$ \bar{l}_i = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^M L_j \cdot w_{i,j} $$
    *   Compute the mean $\mu_{\bar{l}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\bar{l}}$ of these average losses $\bar{l}_i$.
    *   Identify Gaussians to split where:
        $$ \bar{l}_i \geq \mu_{\bar{l}} + z_{\bar{l}} \sigma_{\bar{l}} $$
        where $z_{\bar{l}}$ is a z-score, typically set to 1 [S1, unofficial].

3.  **Splitting Operation:**
    *   For each Gaussian $G_i$ selected for splitting:
        *   Compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of its covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$. Let $\sigma_1$ be the dominant eigenvalue and $\vec{v}_1$ be the corresponding dominant eigenvector [S1, unofficial].
        *   Generate two new Gaussians, $G_a$ and $G_b$.
        *   **Positions:** Shift the original mean $\mu$ (position $\mathbf{p}_i$) in opposite directions along $\vec{v}_1$ by a factor derived from the half-normal distribution:
            $$ \mu_{a,b} = \mu \pm \sigma_1 \vec{v}_1 \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} $$
            The scaling factor $\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \approx 0.8$ [S1, unofficial].
        *   **Covariance:** Set identical covariance matrices for $G_a$ and $G_b$, reconstructed from the initial eigenvalues and eigenvectors, but with the dominant eigenvalue scaled:
            $$ \sigma_{1_{a,b}} = \sigma_1 \sqrt{1 - \frac{2}{\pi}} $$
            The scaling factor $\sqrt{1 - \frac{2}{\pi}} \approx 0.6$ [S1, unofficial]. The other eigenvalues remain unchanged.
        *   **Weights:** Replicate the initial weight $\lambda$, but add noise in the unconstrained parameterization space (log-space) to avoid coupled gradients:
            $$ \log(\lambda_{a,b}) = \log(\lambda) + \mathcal{N}(0, \epsilon_w) $$
            where $\epsilon_w = 0.1$ [S1, unofficial].
        *   **Colors:** Replicate the initial color $c$, but add noise in the unconstrained parameterization space (inverse-sigmoid space):
            $$ \sigma^{-1}(c_{a,b}) = \sigma^{-1}(c) + \mathcal{N}(0, \epsilon_c) $$
            where $\epsilon_c = 0.1$ [S1, unofficial].
        *   Remove the original Gaussian $G_i$ and add $G_a$ and $G_b$.

4.  **Optimization:**
    *   Repeat the optimization process with the modified set of Gaussians [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def gaussian_splitting_and_pruning(gaussians, rays_batch, z_lambda=2.0, z_lbar=1.0, epsilon_w=0.1, epsilon_c=0.1):
    """
    Perform deterministic splitting and pruning of 3D Gaussians.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List of Gaussian objects with attributes:
                   - p: position (3,)
                   - cov: covariance matrix (3,3)
                   - lambda: opacity weight (scalar)
                   - c: color (3,)
        rays_batch: List of ray objects with attributes:
                    - loss: per-ray loss (scalar)
                    - weights: dict mapping gaussian_index to weight (scalar)
        z_lambda: Z-score for pruning threshold.
        z_lbar: Z-score for splitting threshold.
        epsilon_w: Noise scale for log-weights.
        epsilon_c: Noise scale for inverse-sigmoid colors.
        
    Returns:
        new_gaussians: List of updated Gaussian objects.
    """
    new_gaussians = []
    gaussians_to_remove = set()
    gaussians_to_split = []
    
    # Step 1: Pruning
    lambdas = [g.lambda_val for g in gaussians]
    mu_lambda = np.mean(lambdas)
    sigma_lambda = np.std(lambdas)
    prune_threshold = mu_lambda - z_lambda * sigma_lambda
    
    for i, g in enumerate(gaussians):
        if g.lambda_val <= prune_threshold:
            gaussians_to_remove.add(i)
            
    # Step 2: Splitting Criteria
    # Compute average loss per Gaussian over the batch
    M = len(rays_batch)
    avg_losses = np.zeros(len(gaussians))
    
    for ray in rays_batch:
        L_j = ray.loss
        for idx, w in ray.weights.items():
            avg_losses[idx] += L_j * w
            
    avg_losses /= M
    
    mu_lbar = np.mean(avg_losses)
    sigma_lbar = np.std(avg_losses)
    split_threshold = mu_lbar + z_lbar * sigma_lbar
    
    for i, l_bar in enumerate(avg_losses):
        if l_bar >= split_threshold:
            gaussians_to_split.append(i)
            
    # Step 3: Splitting Operation
    for i in gaussians_to_split:
        if i in gaussians_to_remove:
            continue # Already pruned, or handle priority? Source implies separate criteria.
                      # Assuming split takes precedence if both criteria met, or they are disjoint.
                      # Source doesn't specify overlap handling. We'll split if selected.
        
        g = gaussians[i]
        
        # Eigen decomposition
        eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(g.cov)
        # Sort by eigenvalue descending to get dominant
        idx_sort = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
        eigenvalues = eigenvalues[idx_sort]
        eigenvectors = eigenvectors[:, idx_sort]
        
        sigma_1 = eigenvalues[0]
        v_1 = eigenvectors[:, 0]
        
        # Scaling factors from half-normal distribution
        scale_pos = np.sqrt(2.0 / np.pi)
        scale_cov = np.sqrt(1.0 - 2.0 / np.pi)
        
        # New positions
        mu_a = g.p + sigma_1 * v_1 * scale_pos
        mu_b = g.p - sigma_1 * v_1 * scale_pos
        
        # New covariance: scale dominant eigenvalue
        new_eigenvalues = eigenvalues.copy()
        new_eigenvalues[0] = sigma_1 * scale_cov
        
        # Reconstruct covariance matrix
        # cov = V * diag(eigenvalues) * V^T
        cov_a = eigenvectors @ np.diag(new_eigenvalues) @ eigenvectors.T
        cov_b = cov_a.copy()
        
        # New weights (log-space)
        log_lambda_a = np.log(g.lambda_val) + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_w)
        log_lambda_b = np.log(g.lambda_val) + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_w)
        lambda_a = np.exp(log_lambda_a)
        lambda_b = np.exp(log_lambda_b)
        
        # New colors (inverse-sigmoid space)
        # Assuming sigmoid inverse is logit: logit(c) = log(c / (1-c))
        # Note: Source uses sigma^-1(c). Implementation depends on color representation.
        # Assuming c is in [0,1].
        inv_c_a = np.log(g.c / (1 - g.c + 1e-8)) + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_c, 3)
        inv_c_b = np.log(g.c / (1 - g.c + 1e-8)) + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_c, 3)
        
        # Sigmoid to get back to [0,1]
        c_a = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-inv_c_a))
        c_b = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-inv_c_b))
        
        # Create new Gaussian objects
        g_a = Gaussian(p=mu_a, cov=cov_a, lambda_val=lambda_a, c=c_a)
        g_b = Gaussian(p=mu_b, cov=cov_b, lambda_val=lambda_b, c=c_b)
        
        new_gaussians.append(g_a)
        new_gaussians.append(g_b)
        gaussians_to_remove.add(i)
        
    # Step 4: Collect remaining gaussians
    for i, g in enumerate(gaussians):
        if i not in gaussians_to_remove:
            new_gaussians.append(g)
            
    return new_gaussians

class Gaussian:
    def __init__(self, p, cov, lambda_val, c):
        self.p = p
        self.cov = cov
        self.lambda_val = lambda_val
        self.c = c
```

**Parameters:**
*   $z_\lambda$: Z-score for pruning. Typical value: 2 [S1, unofficial].
*   $z_{\bar{l}}$: Z-score for splitting. Typical value: 1 [S1, unofficial].
*   $\epsilon_w$: Noise scale for log-weights. Typical value: 0.1 [S1, unofficial].
*   $\epsilon_c$: Noise scale for inverse-sigmoid colors. Typical value: 0.1 [S1, unofficial].
*   Batch size: Typically 5% of the dataset [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Coupled Gradients:** Noise is added in unconstrained parameterization space (log for weights, inverse-sigmoid for colors) to prevent coupled gradients during subsequent optimization [S1, unofficial].
*   **Initial Convergence:** The method is designed to be applied after the initial model has converged, not continuously during early training [S1, unofficial].
*   **Loss Function:** Any reconstruction loss is viable for $L_j$, but the specific loss used in the source is referenced as Eq. 14 in [S1, unofficial] (likely a combination of L1 and SSIM).

## Subtopics

### [Octree refinement](8792-octree-refinement.md)
Octree refinement is a spatial subdivision technique used to reorder Gaussian splats for progressive rendering, ensuring that sparsely captured areas of a scene are reconstructed earlier than they would be by global contribution metrics alone. It differs from simple global sorting by enforcing a minimum representation per spatial region, thereby improving background quality at the cost of slight foreground degradation.

### [Gaussian Pruning and Sampling](9219-gaussian-pruning-and-sampling.md)
Gaussian Pruning and Sampling is a density control strategy that reduces the number of 3D Gaussian primitives in a radiance field by removing those with minimal contribution to the rendered image. It solves the problem of redundant Gaussian representation and inefficient spatial distribution, which leads to high storage costs and slow optimization times. Unlike opacity-based pruning, which can cause noticeable quality drops or require aggressive thresholds, weight-based pruning evaluates the accumulated blending weights of Gaussians across views, balancing rendering quality with computational efficiency.

### [Blur Split](9237-blur-split.md)
Blur Split is a densification technique for 3D Gaussian Splatting that addresses "under-reconstruction" and blur artifacts in regions with smooth color transitions where gradient-based methods fail. It identifies Gaussians with excessively large maximum contribution areas in screen space and splits them to improve local detail. Unlike standard pruning which removes large Gaussians, Blur Split actively refines them to resolve blurry regions.

### Spatial Splitting with Error Moments
A densification technique that splits Gaussians with error above a threshold $\tau_{space}$ by placing a splitting plane through the Gaussian's center, oriented based on the error moments rather than the Gaussian's longest axis [S2]. For each Gaussian $g$ with error $> \tau_{space}$, the splitting plane normal $\mathbf{n}$ is calculated as the normalized product of the inverse error covariance matrix $\mathbf{S}_g^c$ and the first moment vector $\mathbf{r}_g$:
$$ \mathbf{n} = \frac{(\mathbf{S}_g^c)^{-1} \mathbf{r}_g}{\left\| (\mathbf{S}_g^c)^{-1} \mathbf{r}_g \right\|} $$
Unlike FCC* 24 and DDL* 25b which use a plane perpendicular to the Gaussian's longest axis, this method uses the error moments to determine orientation to avoid Gaussians becoming too small too quickly [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The pruning step is $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of Gaussians. The splitting criteria computation is $O(M \cdot N)$ where $M$ is the batch size. Splitting itself is $O(K)$ where $K$ is the number of Gaussians to split. Overall, it is linear in the number of Gaussians per densification step.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ for storing Gaussians. The batch of rays requires $O(M \cdot N)$ memory if all weights are stored, but can be computed on the fly or aggregated to $O(N)$ if only the average loss per Gaussian is stored.
*   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** Deterministic and statistically grounded; avoids the instability of gradient-based methods by operating after convergence; effectively removes redundant primitives and refines blurry regions.
*   **Limitations:** Requires a batch of rays and loss computation; may not adapt as quickly to dynamic changes as continuous gradient-based methods; relies on the choice of z-scores and batch size.
*   **When to use:** When a stable, high-quality reconstruction is needed after initial training, or when gradient-based methods fail to resolve certain artifacts.
*   **When not to use:** In real-time applications where densification must be continuous and low-latency; when the scene is highly dynamic and requires frequent re-evaluation.

## Background
Originating from the paper "Flexible Techniques for Differentiable Rendering with 3D Gaussians" [S1, unofficial], this method was developed as an alternative to the heuristic merging and splitting techniques explored in the original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper [33]. It provides a deterministic, statistically driven approach to density control. The method is cited in [S1, unofficial] as allowing for increasing detail in an iterative way.
