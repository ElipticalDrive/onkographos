# Scale Decay


## Overview
Scale Decay is a regularization technique applied during the training of 3D Gaussian Splatting representations to control the final size of Gaussians and reduce computational overhead. It addresses the problem of redundant or overly large Gaussians by gradually shrinking their scales throughout the optimization process, thereby encouraging the pruning of occluded or out-of-view primitives. It sets itself apart from standard training by introducing a multiplicative decay factor to the Gaussian covariance parameters, trading a slight decrease in reconstruction quality for a significant increase in rendering speed and reduced memory footprint.

## How it works
Scale Decay operates by applying a multiplicative decay factor to the scale parameters of all active Gaussians at each training iteration. This process is distinct from the adaptive scale control used in rasterization (such as in [Progressive training by scales of Gaussians](9871-progressive-training-by-scales-of-gaussians.md)); instead, it permanently modifies the learned geometry to favor smaller, more efficient primitives.

### Inputs and Outputs
- **Input**: The current set of $N$ Gaussians, each defined by position $\mathbf{p}$, rotation $\mathbf{q}$, scale $\mathbf{s}$ (typically a 3D vector or diagonal covariance), opacity $\alpha$, and spherical harmonics coefficients.
- **Output**: The updated set of Gaussians with scaled-down $\mathbf{s}$ values.

### Data Structures
The Gaussian representation typically stores scale as a 3D vector $\mathbf{s} = [s_x, s_y, s_z]$ or as the diagonal elements of a covariance matrix. The decay is applied directly to these scale components.

### Parameters
- **$\eta_{decay}$**: The scale decay factor, a scalar value strictly less than 1.0.
  - Typical value: $0.999875$ [S1].
  - Tested values: $0.999875, 0.999900, 0.999925, 0.999950$ [S1].
  - Disabled state: "NoDecay" or $\eta_{decay} = 1.0$ [S1].

### Algorithm
The decay is applied after the gradient update step for each iteration. The pseudocode below assumes a standard training loop where `gaussians` is a list or tensor of Gaussian objects/parameters.

```python
def apply_scale_decay(gaussians, eta_decay):
    """
    Applies scale decay to all active Gaussians.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List or tensor of Gaussian parameters.
                   Each element must have a 'scale' attribute (3D vector).
        eta_decay: Float, the multiplicative decay factor (e.g., 0.999875).
    """
    for gaussian in gaussians:
        # Update scale components
        # Note: In some implementations, scale is stored as log-scale.
        # If log-scale is used, the operation is additive:
        # gaussian.scale_log -= (1.0 - eta_decay)
        # However, the source implies direct multiplication on the scale magnitude.
        # We assume direct multiplication on the scale vector s.
        gaussian.scale = gaussian.scale * eta_decay
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
- **Log-Scale Storage**: Many 3D Gaussian Splatting implementations store scales in log-space to ensure positivity during optimization. If the implementation uses log-scale $\mathbf{s}_{log} = \log(\mathbf{s})$, the decay must be applied as an additive shift: $\mathbf{s}_{log}^{new} = \mathbf{s}_{log}^{old} + \log(\eta_{decay})$. The source notes describe the effect as "scaled down by a factor," which is mathematically equivalent to multiplying the linear scale by $\eta_{decay}$ [S1].
- **Pruning Interaction**: Scale Decay works in conjunction with pruning heuristics (e.g., removing Gaussians with low opacity or large scale). By reducing scale, Gaussians may fall below visibility thresholds or become occluded, triggering pruning. This interaction is critical for the observed reduction in Gaussian count [S1].
- **Quality Degradation**: Aggressive decay (lower $\eta_{decay}$) can lead to under-reconstruction of fine details, as evidenced by lower PSNR and higher LPIPS scores [S1].

## Subtopics

### NoDecay
The baseline configuration where $\eta_{decay} = 1.0$. Results in larger final Gaussians, higher Gaussian counts, and lower FPS, but slightly better quality metrics in some configurations [S1].

### High Decay
Configurations with $\eta_{decay} < 0.999875$. Not explicitly tested in the provided sources, but implied to further reduce size and increase FPS at the cost of quality [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: The decay operation itself is $O(N)$ per iteration, where $N$ is the number of Gaussians. This is negligible compared to rasterization and gradient computation.
- **Memory Complexity**: By reducing the final number of Gaussians through pruning, Scale Decay reduces the memory footprint of the final model.
- **Performance Metrics**:
  - **FPS**: Significantly improved. For example, increasing $\eta_{decay}$ from disabled to $0.999875$ increased FPS from 157 to 248 [S1].
  - **Gaussian Count**: Reduced from 1.73M (NoDecay) to 1.52M (with decay) [S1].
  - **Average Scale**: Reduced from 0.0211 (NoDecay) to 0.016 (with decay) [S1].
  - **Quality**: Slight degradation. PSNR decreased from 26.60 (NoDecay) to 26.47 (with decay), and LPIPS increased from 0.231 to 0.236 [S1].
- **Strengths**: Effective lever for balancing quality and speed; encourages pruning of redundant Gaussians.
- **Limitations**: Trade-off in visual quality; requires careful tuning of $\eta_{decay}$ to avoid excessive detail loss.

## Background
Scale Decay was introduced in the GRay paper as a method to optimize the efficiency of 3D Gaussian Splatting. It was found to be highly effective in controlling final Gaussian sizes and encouraging the pruning of occluded or out-of-view Gaussians, leading to noticeable improvements in final FPS with minimal impact on quality [S1].
