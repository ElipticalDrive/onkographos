# Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy

Also called: Coarse-to-fine training strategy

## Overview
The Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy is a multi-stage training approach for point-based radiance field representations that refines the point cloud geometry and attributes iteratively, rather than optimizing a static set of points in a single stage [S1]. It addresses the optimization landscape difficulty of initializing and densifying point clouds simultaneously by decoupling coarse initialization from fine-grained refinement [S1]. Unlike single-stage training, this strategy yields significantly higher rendering quality, particularly in preserving high-frequency details and structural consistency [S1].

## How it works
The method operates by initializing a sparse representation and progressively densifying it based on error metrics, while maintaining differentiability for end-to-end optimization [S1, S2].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of calibrated images (source views), camera poses, and an initial sparse geometric structure (e.g., a mesh or sparse point cloud).
*   **Outputs:** A refined set of primitives (points or triangles) with optimized attributes (position, scale, rotation, color/features) and a rendered image sequence.

### Data Structures
The specific implementation in [S2] uses a triangular mesh structure:
*   **Vertices:** A list of 3D positions and associated feature vectors (e.g., spherical harmonic coefficients or RGB values).
*   **Triangles:** A list of indices referencing three vertices, defining the connectivity.
*   **Pixel Mapping:** A mapping from triangles to the pixels they cover in the target image, used for error calculation.

### Steps
1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Compute Sobel gradient magnitudes on a downsampled target image [S2].
    *   Sample initial vertex positions proportionally to edge strength, applying a small uniform floor to ensure coverage [S2].
    *   Create a Delaunay triangulation of the sampled points, adding boundary vertices to ensure full image coverage [S2].

2.  **Iterative Optimization and Densification:**
    *   Render the current primitive set to generate images [S1, S2].
    *   Compute the loss between rendered and target images [S1].
    *   **Densification Check:** Every 500 iterations (starting at iteration 1,500 and ending at 15,000), perform densification [S2]:
        *   **Score Triangles:** For each triangle $t$, calculate a score based on structural dissimilarity and area [S2]:
            $$ \text{score}_t = \overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t \cdot |\text{pixels}_t|^\alpha $$
            Where:
            *   $\overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t$ is the mean DSSIM (per-pixel structural dissimilarity) of triangle $t$.
            *   $|\text{pixels}_t|$ is the number of pixels covered by triangle $t$.
            *   $\alpha = 0.75$ is the area-weighting exponent [S2].
        *   **Filtering:** Exclude triangles with fewer than 3 pixels from scoring [S2].
        *   **Insertion:** Identify the top-scoring triangles. Insert centroid vertices for these triangles. The number of new vertices is limited to $0.35 \times V$, where $V$ is the current vertex count [S2].
        *   **Feature Initialization:** Initialize the features of new centroid vertices by averaging the features of the parent triangle's vertices [S2].
        *   **Re-triangulation:** Re-run Delaunay triangulation on the full updated vertex set [S2].
        *   **Degeneracy Check:** Detect if the mesh becomes degenerate (e.g., colinear vertices) and re-mesh if necessary [S2].

3.  **Attribute Optimization:**
    *   Update primitive attributes (position, scale, rotation, color/features) via gradient descent using the computed loss [S1, S2].

### Parameters
*   **Densification Interval:** 500 iterations [S2].
*   **Densification Start/End:** Iterations 1,500 to 15,000 [S2].
*   **Area-weighting exponent ($\alpha$):** 0.75 [S2].
*   **Vertex Insertion Ratio:** 0.35 (new vertices per existing vertex) [S2].
*   **Minimum Pixels for Scoring:** 3 pixels [S2].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Degeneracy:** The mesh may become degenerate due to vertices becoming colinear during densification. A re-meshing step is required, though this is noted as rare in practice [S2].
*   **Tiny Triangles:** Without area weighting ($\alpha$), tiny triangles with high error might be repeatedly split, leading to uneven vertex distribution. The exponent $\alpha=0.75$ mitigates this [S2].
*   **Precision:** The method relies on accurate gradient computation for both the rendering loss and the densification scoring [S1, S2].

## Subtopics

### Coarse-to-Fine Densification
A specific variant where a sparse mesh is progressively refined by inserting centroid vertices for high-scoring triangles and re-running Delaunay triangulation [S2]. It is chosen when a mesh-based representation is preferred over a point cloud, offering structured connectivity [S2]. It differs from point-based densification by maintaining explicit triangle topology and using DSSIM-based scoring on triangle areas [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is increased due to the iterative densification steps and re-triangulation. However, it remains efficient compared to volumetric methods. For example, training on an RTX A6000 takes between 1-3 minutes for the final stages [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage scales with the number of primitives. The densification strategy increases the number of primitives over time, requiring more memory for attributes and connectivity [S2].
*   **Performance:** On the Blender dataset, removing the hybrid strategy ('w/o hybrid') reduces PSNR from 30.3 dB to 26.6 dB, SSIM from 0.945 to 0.925, and increases LPIPS from 0.078 to 0.163 [S1]. This demonstrates a significant quality improvement over single-stage training [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Better rendering quality, especially for high-frequency details; stable optimization landscape; efficient compared to neural volumetric methods [S1, S2].
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of densification parameters (interval, ratio, scoring metrics); potential for mesh degeneracy [S2].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality view synthesis is required and the scene geometry is complex, benefiting from progressive refinement [S1, S2].
*   **When not to use:** When real-time training speed is critical and single-stage optimization is sufficient, or when the scene is very simple and does not benefit from densification [S1].

## Background
The Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy originates from research into differentiable point-based radiance fields, which aim to provide an alternative to volumetric neural rendering methods [S1]. It was introduced to address the challenges of optimizing point clouds directly, which can suffer from poor initialization and unstable densification [S1]. The strategy builds on the principles of temporal decoupling and progressive densification common in multi-stage training approaches [S1]. The specific mesh-based variant (Coarse-to-Fine Densification) was further developed in the context of Neural Harmonic Textures, leveraging Delaunay triangulation for structured refinement [S2].
