# Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy

Also called: Multi-stage point cloud refinement

## Overview
The Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy is a training methodology for point-based radiance field representations that divides the optimization process into distinct temporal stages to improve rendering quality and convergence stability. Unlike single-stage training, which optimizes the point cloud uniformly from initialization, this approach establishes a coarse global structure first and progressively refines it through densification. It sets itself apart from siblings like [Fine-tuning](9138-fine-tuning.md) by integrating densification directly into the optimization loop rather than applying it post-convergence, and from [Curriculum Learning with Slice and Voxel Supervision](12921-curriculum-learning-with-slice-and-voxel-supervision.md) by focusing on geometric primitive density rather than supervisory signal hierarchy.

## How it works
The method operates by initializing a sparse representation and iteratively increasing its density based on error metrics, ensuring that new primitives are added only where necessary to reduce reconstruction error.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** Target images (or video frames), camera poses, initial sparse point cloud or mesh, and hyperparameters for densification frequency and thresholds.
*   **Outputs:** Optimized point cloud (or mesh) with positions, colors, opacities, and potentially spherical harmonic coefficients, ready for differentiable rendering.

### Data Structures
The core data structure is a set of primitives (points or mesh vertices).
*   **Point Cloud:** Each point $p_i$ contains position $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, color $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (or SH coefficients), opacity $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$, and scale $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
*   **Mesh (Variant):** Vertices $\mathcal{V}$ and triangles $\mathcal{T}$. Each vertex has features (color, etc.). Triangles track pixel coverage and error scores.

### Numbered Steps
1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Generate an initial sparse representation. For point-based methods, this may involve random sampling or edge-aware sampling. For mesh-based variants, compute Sobel gradient magnitudes on a downsampled target image and sample initial vertex positions proportionally to edge strength with a small uniform floor for coverage [S2].
    *   Create a Delaunay triangulation of sampled points, ensuring boundary vertices cover the full image extent [S2].
2.  **Coarse Training:**
    *   Optimize the initial sparse representation using standard differentiable rendering losses (e.g., L1, SSIM) for a predefined number of iterations or until global structure converges.
3.  **Densification Loop:**
    *   At specific intervals (e.g., every 500 iterations, starting at iteration 1,500 and ending at 15,000), evaluate the error distribution across the representation [S2].
    *   **Scoring:** For each primitive $t$ (triangle or region), compute a score based on error and spatial impact.
        *   For mesh variants: $\text{score}_t = \overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t \cdot |\text{pixels}_t|^\alpha$ [S2].
        *   Here, $\overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t$ is the mean per-pixel structural dissimilarity of primitive $t$, $|\text{pixels}_t|$ is the number of pixels covered by $t$, and $\alpha = 0.75$ is an area-weighting exponent [S2].
        *   Exclude primitives with fewer than 3 pixels from scoring to prevent noise-driven densification [S2].
    *   **Selection:** Identify the top-scoring primitives. The number of new primitives to insert is capped at $0.35 \times V$, where $V$ is the current count of primitives [S2].
    *   **Insertion:**
        *   For mesh variants: Insert centroid vertices for the selected triangles. Initialize their features by averaging the parent triangle's vertex features. Re-run Delaunay triangulation on the full vertex set [S2].
        *   For point-based variants: Split high-error points into multiple points with slightly perturbed positions and averaged features.
    *   **Pruning (Optional):** Remove primitives with low opacity or high error that do not contribute to the image, though the sources primarily emphasize the coarse-to-fine *growth* [S1, S2].
4.  **Refinement:**
    *   Continue training with the densified representation, allowing the optimizer to adjust the new primitives and refine the existing ones.
    *   Repeat steps 3-4 until the maximum iteration count is reached or convergence criteria are met.

### Formulas
*   **Triangle Score:**
    $$ \text{score}_t = \overline{\text{DSSIM}}_t \cdot |\text{pixels}_t|^\alpha $$
    Where $\alpha = 0.75$ [S2].
*   **Vertex Insertion Limit:**
    $$ N_{\text{new}} \le 0.35 \times V $$
    Where $V$ is the current vertex count [S2].

### Parameters
*   **Densification Interval:** Every 500 iterations [S2].
*   **Start Iteration:** 1,500 [S2].
*   **End Iteration:** 15,000 [S2].
*   **Area-weighting exponent ($\alpha$):** 0.75 [S2].
*   **Insertion Ratio:** 0.35 (35% of current count) [S2].
*   **Minimum Pixel Coverage:** 3 pixels [S2].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Degeneracy:** Mesh-based implementations must detect degenerate triangles (e.g., colinear vertices) and re-mesh, although this is rare in practice [S2].
*   **Tiny High-Error Triangles:** Without area weighting ($\alpha < 1$), tiny triangles with high error might be repeatedly split, leading to uneven vertex distribution. The exponent $\alpha=0.75$ mitigates this [S2].
*   **Noise:** Triangles with very few pixels are excluded from scoring to prevent densification driven by noise rather than structural error [S2].

## Subtopics

### Coarse-to-Fine Densification
A specific variant of the hybrid strategy applied to mesh-based representations. It initializes a sparse mesh via edge-aware sampling and progressively refines it by inserting centroid vertices for high-scoring triangles and re-running Delaunay triangulation. It is chosen when a structured mesh representation is preferred over unstructured points, offering better topological consistency. It differs from point-based densification by maintaining explicit connectivity and requiring triangulation updates [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time increases with the number of densification steps. Each densification step involves scoring all primitives and re-computing connectivity (for meshes), adding overhead. However, the coarse-to-fine approach often converges faster overall than single-stage training on dense data because it avoids optimizing unnecessary primitives early on.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage grows linearly with the number of primitives. The hybrid strategy results in a denser final representation than a purely coarse initialization, but sparser than a fully dense initialization, balancing memory and quality.
*   **Measured Performance:** On the Blender dataset, removing the hybrid strategy ('w/o hybrid') reduces PSNR from 30.3 dB to 26.6 dB, SSIM from 0.945 to 0.925, and increases LPIPS from 0.078 to 0.163 [S1]. This demonstrates a significant quality improvement.
*   **Strengths:** Improves rendering quality by focusing computational resources on areas of high error; prevents over-smoothing in complex regions; stabilizes training by starting with a simpler problem.
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of densification intervals and thresholds; adds implementation complexity compared to single-stage training; may introduce artifacts if densification is too aggressive or poorly timed.
*   **When to Use:** When high-fidelity view synthesis is required and the scene has varying levels of detail; when single-stage training leads to poor convergence or blurring.
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications where training time is critical and quality can be sacrificed; for scenes with uniform detail where densification provides little benefit.

## Background
The Hybrid Coarse-to-Fine Strategy originates from research into differentiable point-based radiance fields, which seek to provide an alternative to volumetric neural rendering by using points as the primary representation. The strategy was introduced to address the limitations of single-stage training, which often fails to capture fine details or converges to suboptimal solutions. The mesh-based variant, Coarse-to-Fine Densification, was developed in the context of Neural Harmonic Textures, aiming to combine the flexibility of point-based methods with the structural benefits of meshes. The approach builds on the principle that a hierarchical refinement process is more effective for learning complex radiance fields than uniform optimization [S1, S2].
