# Iterative Reconstruction (SIRT via Autodiff)
Also called: Gradient Descent Reconstruction, SIRT

## Overview
This method performs tomographic reconstruction by treating the volume estimation as an optimization problem solved via gradient descent using automatic differentiation. It minimizes the mean squared error between simulated projections (computed via a differentiable forward projector) and measured projections. Unlike traditional algebraic methods that solve linear systems directly, this approach leverages modern deep learning frameworks to handle complex, non-linear, or physics-based forward models that may not have closed-form adjoints. It is distinct from [SIRT (Simultaneous Iterative Reconstruction Technique)](3101-sirt-simultaneous-iterative-reconstruction-technique.md) in that it uses numerical gradients via backpropagation rather than explicit algebraic updates, and from [ACID (Adversarial Calibration Iterative Deep learning)](16417-acid-adversarial-calibration-iterative-deep-learning.md) which combines neural priors with residual calibration, whereas this method relies purely on data fidelity minimization.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Measured projections: A tensor of shape `[num_angles, H, W]` containing the input sinogram data.
- Geometry parameters: Source-to-Object Distance (DSO), Source-to-Detector Distance (DSD), detector pixel dimensions, and projection angles.
- Volume dimensions: $D \times D \times D$ (typically cubic).
- Optimization parameters: Learning rate ($\eta$), number of iterations ($N_{iters}$).

**Outputs:**
- Reconstructed volume: A 3D tensor of shape `[D, D, D]` representing the estimated attenuation coefficients.

**Data Structures:**
- `volume`: A PyTorch tensor of shape `[D, D, D]` initialized with zeros or a prior estimate. It must have `requires_grad=True` to enable automatic differentiation.
- `optimizer`: An Adam optimizer instance configured with the `volume` parameters and learning rate $\eta$.

**Algorithm Steps:**
1. **Initialization**: Create a tensor `volume` of shape `[D, D, D]` with `requires_grad=True`. Initialize an Adam optimizer with `volume` as the parameter and learning rate $\eta = 10^{-2}$.
2. **Iteration Loop**: For $k = 1$ to $N_{iters}$:
   a. **Zero Gradients**: Call `optimizer.zero_grad()` to clear accumulated gradients from the previous step.
   b. **Forward Projection**: Compute the simulated projections $P_{sim}$ by applying the differentiable forward projector $\mathcal{F}$ to the current volume estimate:
      $$ P_{sim} = \mathcal{F}(volume) $$
      The projector $\mathcal{F}$ maps the 3D volume to 2D projections based on the specified cone-beam geometry (DSO, DSD, angles).
   c. **Loss Computation**: Calculate the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss $L$ between the simulated projections $P_{sim}$ and the measured projections $P_{meas}$:
      $$ L = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} (P_{sim, i} - P_{meas, i})^2 $$
      where $M$ is the total number of pixels across all projections.
   d. **Backpropagation**: Compute gradients of the loss with respect to the volume parameters:
      $$ \nabla_{volume} L = \frac{\partial L}{\partial volume} $$
      This is performed automatically by the framework's autograd engine.
   e. **Optimizer Step**: Update the volume parameters using the Adam optimizer:
      $$ volume \leftarrow volume - \eta \cdot \text{AdamStep}(\nabla_{volume} L) $$

**Parameters:**
- `iters`: Number of iterations. Default is 50 [S1, unofficial].
- `lr`: Learning rate. Default is $10^{-2}$ [S1, unofficial].
- `D`: Volume dimension. Typically 256 or 512 depending on resolution requirements.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision**: Floating-point precision in the forward projector implementation affects gradient accuracy. Use `float32` or `float64` consistently.
- **Boundaries**: The forward projector must handle rays passing outside the volume bounds gracefully, typically by returning zero or ignoring them.
- **Degenerate Input**: If measured projections are all zeros or constant, the gradient may vanish or lead to trivial solutions. Normalization of input data is recommended.

## Subtopics

### [ACID (Adversarial Calibration Iterative Deep learning)](16417-acid-adversarial-calibration-iterative-deep-learning.md)
ACID is an iterative reconstruction framework that stabilizes unstable deep learning-based tomographic reconstruction by combining a data-driven neural network prior with Compressed Sensing (CS)-based sparsity constraints and an explicit residual calibration step. It solves the problem of underdetermined inverse problems (where the number of measurements is less than the unknowns) where standard sparsity-promoting algorithms fail to recover null-space components and standalone deep networks produce unstable, artifact-ridden outputs. Unlike [Iterative Reconstruction (SIRT via Autodiff)](12326-iterative-reconstruction-sirt-via-autodiff.md), which relies on gradient descent through a differentiable forward model to minimize data fidelity, ACID uses a closed-form block coordinate descent update that alternates between calibrating projection-domain residuals and applying a sparsity-filtered neural network update, ensuring convergence even when the neural network is imperfect.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: $O(N_{iters} \cdot C \cdot D^3)$, where $C$ is the cost of the forward projection per iteration. The forward projection cost depends on the number of rays and detector pixels.
- **Memory Complexity**: $O(D^3)$ for storing the volume and gradients. The optimizer state adds a constant factor overhead.
- **Measured Performance**: In the source [S1, unofficial], the method is demonstrated on synthetic phantoms with 180 angles and 256 detector pixels. The reconstruction quality is compared against FDK and FDK+DL. The iterative method provides better quality under noise and sparse views but is computationally more expensive than FDK.
- **Strengths**: Handles complex forward models (e.g., non-linear physics, scatter) if they are differentiable. No need for explicit adjoint derivation.
- **Limitations**: Computationally intensive due to repeated forward projections and backpropagation. Convergence is not guaranteed for non-convex loss landscapes. Sensitive to learning rate and initialization.
- **When to Use**: When the forward model is complex and differentiable, and traditional algebraic methods are difficult to implement or converge slowly. Suitable for research and prototyping.
- **When Not to Use**: When real-time reconstruction is required, or when the forward model is not differentiable.

## Background
This method is derived from the application of automatic differentiation in deep learning frameworks to tomographic reconstruction. It leverages the ability of frameworks like PyTorch to compute gradients of complex computational graphs, allowing for end-to-end optimization of the reconstruction volume. The approach is part of the broader trend of "differentiable rendering" and "differentiable physics" in computer graphics and scientific computing. The specific implementation described in [S1, unofficial] uses a cone-beam forward projector implemented from scratch in PyTorch.
