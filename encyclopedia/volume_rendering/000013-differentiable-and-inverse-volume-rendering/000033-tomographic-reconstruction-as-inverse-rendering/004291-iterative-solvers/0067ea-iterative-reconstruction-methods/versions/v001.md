# Iterative Reconstruction Methods

Also called: Méthodes itératives de reconstruction tomographique

## Overview
Iterative Reconstruction (IR) methods solve the tomographic inverse problem by iteratively refining an estimate of the object to reduce the discrepancy between simulated and measured projections [S1, unofficial]. Unlike direct analytical methods such as Filtered Back Projection (FBP), which can be unstable or produce artifacts under low-dose or sparse-view conditions, IR methods model the acquisition process and noise statistics to produce more accurate reconstructions [S2, unofficial]. They are chosen when data is noisy, incomplete, or when system physics (e.g., focal spot blur, detector response) must be explicitly modeled, accepting higher computational cost and potential over-smoothing as trade-offs [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Measured projection data (sinogram) $P$, system geometry/forward projection operator $\mathcal{R}$, initial guess $f^{(0)}$.
- Output: Reconstructed volume estimate $f^{(N)}$ after $N$ iterations.

**Data Structures:**
- $f$: The image/volume estimate, typically a 2D slice or 3D grid of voxel values. Initialized to a constant (e.g., zero or mean attenuation) [S1, unofficial].
- $P$: The acquired projection data vector or matrix.
- $\tilde{P}$: The estimated projections, computed via forward projection of the current estimate $f^{(n-1)}$ using operator $\mathcal{R}$ [S1, unofficial].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1. **Initialization:** Set the initial object estimate $f^{(0)}$ to a constant value [S1, unofficial].
2. **Forward Projection:** Calculate the estimated projections $\tilde{P}$ in the same directions as the real acquisition using the current estimate $f^{(n-1)}$:
   $$ \tilde{P} = \mathcal{R}(f^{(n-1)}) $$
   [S1, unofficial]
3. **Error Calculation:** Compute the projection error $\epsilon_P$ by comparing estimated projections $\tilde{P}$ to acquired projections $P$. The form depends on the method type:
   - Additive methods: $\epsilon_P = P - \tilde{P}$ [S1, unofficial]
   - Multiplicative methods: $\epsilon_P = P / \tilde{P}$ [S1, unofficial]
4. **Back-Projection:** Back-project the projection error $\epsilon_P$ to obtain the tomography error $\epsilon_f$:
   $$ \epsilon_f = \mathcal{R}^T(\epsilon_P) $$
   where $\mathcal{R}^T$ is the back-projection operator [S1, unofficial].
5. **Update:** Update the object estimate $f^{(n)}$ based on the tomography error $\epsilon_f$:
   - Additive methods: $f^{(n)} = f^{(n-1)} + \epsilon_f$ [S1, unofficial]
   - Multiplicative methods: $f^{(n)} = f^{(n-1)} \times \epsilon_f$ [S1, unofficial]
6. **Convergence Check:** Repeat steps 2–5 until the difference between simulated and real projections is sufficiently low, or a maximum number of iterations is reached [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
- **Regularization via Early Stopping:** The process is often interrupted after a certain number of iterations to avoid reconstructing noise-dominated high frequencies, effectively acting as a low-pass filter [S1, unofficial].

**Pitfalls:**
- **Divergence due to Noise:** While iterative methods converge toward the solution, they eventually diverge due to noise amplification. High frequencies (details and noise) are represented more in later iterations, causing the solution to move away from the true object [S1, unofficial].
- **Over-smoothing:** Iterative reconstruction can produce over-smoothed images that may appear "plastic," losing fine detail [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Iterative 3D Modeling
More primitive methods that aim to degrade image quality by reducing noise and artifacts through an iterative process [S3, unofficial]. The image is repeatedly updated based on comparing the final reconstructed image with the measured projection data [S3, unofficial]. These methods are noted to lead to more inaccurate results compared to more advanced techniques [S3, unofficial].

### Domain Iterative Reconstruction
An iterative reconstruction approach that iterates over a cycle of the forward and inverse Radon transform, applying constraints in each domain to converge on an optimal solution [S4]. The steps involve: 1) Iterating over a cycle of the forward Radon transform, 2) Applying constraints in the Radon domain, 3) Iterating over a cycle of the inverse Radon transform, and 4) Applying constraints in the image domain [S4]. Previously not commonly used due to large computational demands, it becomes practical when both forward and inverse steps are accelerated with a single piece of hardware [S4].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Iterative methods are computationally expensive, requiring multiple forward and back-projection steps per iteration [S2, unofficial].
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the image volume, projection data, and intermediate error terms.
- **Performance:** Can reduce dose or clean streaks compared to FBP [S2, unofficial]. However, they increase compute time significantly [S2, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Handles noisy or sparse data better than FBP; can incorporate system physics and noise statistics [S2, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** High computational cost; risk of over-smoothing ("plastic" look); prone to divergence if not regularized (e.g., via early stopping) [S1, unofficial][S2, unofficial].
- **When to Use:** Low-dose CT, sparse-view CT, or when system modeling is critical.
- **When Not to Use:** Real-time applications requiring speed, or when high-frequency detail preservation is paramount and noise is low.

## Background
Iterative reconstruction methods originated as a solution to the ill-posed nature of tomographic reconstruction, where direct inversion of the projection matrix is unstable and impractical [S1, unofficial]. The term "Méthodes itératives de reconstruction tomographique" is commonly used in French literature [S1, unofficial]. The general principle involves starting with a guess, forward-projecting, comparing to measured data, and updating, a process formalized in early tomography literature [S1, unofficial]. The divergence due to noise amplification in later iterations is a general phenomenon in ill-posed inverse problems [S1, unofficial].
