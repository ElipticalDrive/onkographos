# Tomographic Image Synthesis for Layered 3D

Also called: Attenuation-based Light Field Synthesis, Layered 3D, Tomographic Image Synthesis for Multi-Layer HDR Displays, Tomographic Optimization, Tomographic reconstruction for attenuation displays

## Overview
Tomographic Image Synthesis for Layered 3D is a method to optimally construct volumetric or layered attenuators to emit a target light field by minimizing the difference between emitted and target light fields subject to physical constraints on attenuation, using iterative tomographic reconstruction [S1]. It solves the inverse problem of determining the attenuation map $\mu(x,y)$ that, when backlit, produces a desired 4D light field $l(u,a)$ [S1]. Unlike heuristic methods that blur target images to rear layer resolution, this method formulates the synthesis as a constrained linear least-squares optimization problem, proving that existing dual-layer architectures are near-optimal in the least-squares sense [S1]. It extends conventional parallax barriers to multiple disjoint layers, increasing spatial resolution, depth of field, and brightness for 3D displays, and enabling high dynamic range (HDR) for 2D displays [S1].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Target light field $l(u, a)$, parameterized on a relative two-plane system where $u$ is the spatial coordinate on the slab bisector and $a$ is the ray orientation slope [S1].
*   Display geometry: number of layers $N_l$, layer thickness/separation, and spatial resolution (e.g., 384x512 pixels per layer) [S1].
*   Physical constraints: limited layer contrast (attenuation bounds) [S1].

**Outputs:**
*   Attenuation maps $\mu_n(x, y)$ for each layer $n \in \{1, \dots, N_l\}$, defined as continuously-varying functions nonzero only within the slab dimensions $|x| < w/2, |y| < h/2$ [S1].

### Data Structures
*   **Light Field:** Stored as a 4D array $l(u, a)$ with spatial resolution (e.g., 384x512) and angular resolution (e.g., 7x7 views) [S1].
*   **Attenuation Map:** Represented using a series expansion into a set of normalized linear basis functions rather than a discrete voxel grid to ensure smoother reconstructions and mitigate discrete ray sampling artifacts [S1].
*   **Projection Matrix:** A sparse matrix $A$ mapping the basis function coefficients to the light field samples. The matrix is stored directly in memory, limiting the maximum display dimensions and layer count by available system RAM [S1].

### Mathematical Formulation
The forward model is based on the Beer-Lambert law. For a single slab, the transmitted intensity $I$ of a ray along path $C$ is:
$$
I = I_0 e^{-\int_C \mu(\mathbf{r}) d\mathbf{r}}
$$
where $I_0$ is incident intensity and $\mu(\mathbf{r})$ is the attenuation map [S1]. The logarithm of the normalized intensity $\bar{l}$ is defined as:
$$
\bar{l} = \ln\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right) = -\int_C \mu(\mathbf{r}) d\mathbf{r}
$$
This integral is the Radon transform $p(u, a)$ of the attenuation map [S1]. Thus, the emitted light field is:
$$
l(u, a) = -p(u, a)
$$
For layered displays, the total attenuation is the sum of the attenuations of individual layers (assuming negligible scattering and reflection between layers) [S1]. The inverse problem is to find $\mu$ such that the emitted light field matches the target. This is solved as a constrained linear least-squares optimization:
$$
\min_{\mathbf{x}} \| A \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b} \|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{x}_{min} \le \mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{x}_{max}
$$
where $\mathbf{x}$ represents the coefficients of the basis functions for the attenuation maps, $A$ is the projection matrix (Radon transform), and $\mathbf{b}$ is the target light field (log-intensity) [S1]. The constraints $\mathbf{x}_{min}, \mathbf{x}_{max}$ enforce physical limits on layer contrast (attenuation values) [S1].

### Parameters
*   **Light Field Resolution:** 384x512 spatial pixels by 7x7 angular views [S1].
*   **Layer Count ($N_l$):** Typically 3-5 layers in demonstrations [S1].
*   **Field of View:** ±5 degrees [S1].
*   **Basis Functions:** Normalized linear basis functions [S1].

### Steps
1.  **Preprocessing:** Convert the target light field $l_{target}(u, a)$ to log-intensity space: $\mathbf{b} = \ln(l_{target})$. Evaluate color channels independently [S1].
2.  **Matrix Construction:** Construct the sparse projection matrix $A$ that maps the chosen basis function coefficients to the light field samples. This involves computing line integrals through the basis functions for each ray $(u, a)$ [S1].
3.  **Optimization:** Solve the constrained linear least-squares problem using a sparse, constrained, large-scale trust region method (e.g., Coleman and Li 1996) [S1].
    *   Initialize $\mathbf{x}$ (e.g., zeros or heuristic guess).
    *   Iterate until convergence (typically 8-14 iterations) [S1].
    *   Enforce constraints $\mathbf{x}_{min} \le \mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{x}_{max}$ at each step [S1].
4.  **Post-processing:** Convert the optimized coefficients $\mathbf{x}$ back to attenuation maps $\mu_n(x, y)$ for each layer [S1].
5.  **Rendering (Verification):** Render the synthesized light field using POV-Ray as 7x7 oblique projections. Apply a depth-dependent 4D antialiasing filter by rendering each view with a limited depth of field (finite aperture camera) to approximate combined anti-aliasing and display prefiltering [S1].

### Pseudocode
```python
def tomographic_synthesis(target_light_field, num_layers, spatial_res, angular_res, basis_functions, constraints):
    """
    Synthesize layered attenuation maps for a target light field.
    
    Args:
        target_light_field: 4D array [u, v, theta, phi] or flattened vector b.
        num_layers: Number of attenuator layers N_l.
        spatial_res: (width, height) of each layer.
        angular_res: (num_views_u, num_views_a) of light field.
        basis_functions: Set of normalized linear basis functions phi_i(x,y).
        constraints: Tuple (x_min, x_max) for attenuation bounds.
        
    Returns:
        attenuation_maps: List of N_l 2D arrays representing mu_n(x,y).
    """
    
    # 1. Preprocessing: Convert target to log-intensity
    # Assuming target_light_field is in linear intensity space
    b = log(target_light_field)
    
    # 2. Construct Projection Matrix A
    # A maps basis coefficients to light field samples.
    # A is sparse. Each row corresponds to a ray (u, a).
    # Each column corresponds to a basis function coefficient.
    # A_ij = integral of basis_function_j along ray_i
    A = construct_projection_matrix(spatial_res, angular_res, basis_functions)
    
    # 3. Solve Constrained Least-Squares
    # Minimize ||Ax - b||^2 subject to x_min <= x <= x_max
    # Using sparse constrained trust-region method (Coleman & Li)
    
    x_opt = solve_constrained_lsqr(A, b, constraints.x_min, constraints.x_max)
    
    # 4. Reconstruct Attenuation Maps
    # x_opt contains coefficients for all layers combined
    # Split x_opt into N_l segments corresponding to each layer's basis functions
    attenuation_maps = []
    for layer_idx in range(num_layers):
        layer_coeffs = x_opt[layer_idx * num_basis_per_layer : (layer_idx + 1) * num_basis_per_layer]
        mu = reconstruct_from_basis(layer_coeffs, basis_functions)
        attenuation_maps.append(mu)
        
    return attenuation_maps

def construct_projection_matrix(spatial_res, angular_res, basis_functions):
    """
    Construct the sparse Radon transform matrix A.
    """
    # Determine all rays (u, a) in the light field parameterization
    rays = generate_rays(spatial_res, angular_res)
    num_rays = len(rays)
    num_basis = len(basis_functions)
    
    # Initialize sparse matrix
    A = SparseMatrix(num_rays, num_basis)
    
    for i, ray in enumerate(rays):
        for j, phi in enumerate(basis_functions):
            # Compute line integral of basis function phi along ray
            integral = compute_line_integral(phi, ray)
            A[i, j] = integral
            
    return A

def compute_line_integral(basis_function, ray):
    """
    Compute the integral of a basis function along a specific ray path.
    Ray is defined by u (position on slab) and a (slope).
    """
    # Integrate basis_function over the path C defined by the ray
    # In flatland (2D cross-section), this is a 1D integral
    path = ray.path
    return integrate(basis_function, path)
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Color Crosstalk:** The standard implementation evaluates color channels independently, neglecting color crosstalk between layers. Incorporating crosstalk increases memory requirements and computation time significantly [S1].
*   **Flip Animations:** For uncorrelated multi-view imagery (flip animations), direct application of the optimization leads to significant crosstalk. A spatial downsampling constraint must be added to yield accurate results with enhanced contrast [S1].
*   **Filtering Artifacts:** Linear reconstruction filters applied to the logarithm of the light field do not correspond to the same filters applied to the light field directly [S1].
*   **Memory Limits:** Storing the sparse projection matrix directly in memory limits the maximum display dimensions, image resolution, and number of layers by system capacity [S1]. Matrix-free optimization can mitigate this but may increase computation time [S1].
*   **Physical Assumptions:** Scattering and reflection losses are assumed negligible. Interreflections between layers are ignored, though in practice they may not lead to visual artifacts if ambient lighting is controlled [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The optimization requires solving a large, sparse system of equations. It is not real-time; typical optimization takes 12 minutes for 5 layers on a 2.4 GHz Intel Core 2 workstation with 8 GB RAM [S1]. Convergence is typically achieved in 8-14 iterations [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Proportional to the size of the sparse projection matrix, which depends on the number of light field samples and basis functions. Limited by system RAM [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Produces qualitatively optimal decompositions for dual-layer HDR displays, confirming existing heuristics. Extends naturally to multiple disjoint layers, increasing spatial resolution, depth of field, and brightness compared to parallax barriers [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive; not suitable for dynamic content without significant acceleration (e.g., GPU, precomputed inverses) [S1]. Limited by physical constraints of layer contrast and separation [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Static images or pre-rendered content for multi-layer HDR or 3D attenuation displays [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** Real-time dynamic applications without hardware acceleration; scenes with significant inter-layer scattering or reflection that cannot be neglected [S1].

## Background
*   **Origin:** Proposed by Wetzstein et al. in 2011 [S1].
*   **History:** The method builds on tomographic reconstruction principles [Herman 1995] and light field parameterization [Chai et al. 2000]. It was developed to address the limitations of heuristic image synthesis methods for layered displays, such as parallax barriers and integral imaging, by providing a mathematically optimal solution in the least-squares sense [S1].
*   **Usage:** Demonstrated on hardware prototypes consisting of five printed transparency layers separated by clear acrylic sheets, illuminated by a backlight [S1].
