# Iterative Reconstruction with Basis Expansion

Also called: Linear Combination Basis Method, Non-negative Linear Least-Squares Optimization

## Overview
This method models an attenuation map as a linear combination of non-negative basis functions to solve the inverse problem of synthesizing attenuation-based displays from target light fields. It addresses the limitations of filtered back-projection [Filtered Back-Projection (FBP)](11308-filtered-back-projection-fbp.md) and the Discrete Inverse Radon Transform [Discrete Inverse Radon Transform](14348-discrete-inverse-radon-transform.md) when projections are inconsistent or limited in angle. By formulating the reconstruction as a convex non-negative linear least-squares optimization, it guarantees consistent views in the least-squares sense, unlike as-rigid-as-possible deformations used in visual hull reconstruction.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Target discrete logarithmic light field $\bar{l}$ (vector).
- Number of basis functions $N_b$.
- Definition of basis functions $\bar{\phi}_k(x, y)$ for $k=1 \dots N_b$.
- Ray parameters for discretization (indices $i, j$).

**Outputs:**
- Coefficient vector $\alpha$ (size $N_b$), where $\alpha_k \ge 0$.
- Reconstructed attenuation map $\mu(x, y)$.

**Data Structures:**
- **Basis Functions:** $\bar{\phi}_k(x, y)$ can be conventional voxels [Drebin et al. 1988] or general functions with compact/extended support.
- **Projection Matrix $P$:** Dimensions $N_r \times N_b$, where $N_r$ is the number of rays in the discrete light field. Element $P_{ij}^{(k)}$ represents the line integral of basis function $k$ along ray $(i, j)$.
- **Logarithmic Light Field $\bar{l}$:** Vector of size $N_r$, where $\bar{l}_{ij} = -\ln(t_{ij})$ and $t_{ij}$ is the target transmittance.

**Formulas:**
The attenuation map is modeled as:
$$ \mu(x, y) = \sum_{k=1}^{N_b} \alpha_k \bar{\phi}_k(x, y) \quad (7) $$

The forward model for the discrete logarithmic light field is:
$$ \bar{l}_{ij} = - \sum_{k=1}^{N_b} \alpha_k P_{ij}^{(k)} \quad (9) $$

The projection matrix element $P_{ij}^{(k)}$ is defined by the line integral:
$$ P_{ij}^{(k)} = \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} \int_{-w/2}^{w/2} \bar{\phi}_k(x, y) \delta(d_r(x - (i\Delta u)) - (j\Delta a)y) \, dx \, dy \quad (10) $$
where $d_r$ is the display depth, $\Delta u$ and $\Delta a$ are the spatial and angular steps, and $h, w$ are the display dimensions.

The reconstruction solves the following convex optimization problem:
$$ \arg \min_{\alpha} \| \bar{l} + P\alpha \|_2^2, \quad \text{subject to } \alpha \ge 0 \quad (11) $$

**Parameters:**
- $N_b$: Number of basis functions. Not given in the sources.
- Basis function type: Voxels or general functions. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** The formulation relies on the Weber-Fechner law, assuming the human visual system responds linearly to logarithmic changes in illumination [Reinhard et al. 2010]. Deviations from this assumption may affect perceptual quality.
- **Boundaries:** The integration limits in Equation (10) are $[-h/2, h/2]$ and $[-w/2, w/2]$. Rays intersecting the display boundaries must be handled correctly to avoid artifacts.
- **Degenerate Input:** If the target light field is inconsistent (e.g., due to limited angles), the least-squares solution provides the best fit but may not perfectly reconstruct the target.

## Subtopics

### Multi-Layer Attenuation Model
A model representing the attenuator as a finite number $N_l$ of discrete attenuation layers (masks) separated by distances $d_k$, where each mask controls spatially-varying transmittance. It extends the basis expansion method to physically realizable stacks of LCD panels or semi-transparent slides. The forward model for the logarithmic light field is:
$$ \bar{l}(u, a) = \sum_{k=1}^{N_l} \ln t_k(u + (d_k/d_r)a) = - \sum_{k=1}^{N_l} a_k(u + (d_k/d_r)a) \quad (13) $$
where $t_k(\xi)$ is the transmittance of mask $k$ and $a_k(\xi) = -\ln t_k(\xi)$ is the absorbance. This leads to a linear system similar to Equation (9), where the projection matrix accounts for the specific layer positions. In the prototype, layers are evenly-spaced over a total display thickness of 1.25 cm, with a resolution of 171 dots per inch (149 µm pixels) [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Higher than filtered back-projection due to the iterative optimization required to solve the linear system [S1]. The complexity depends on the number of basis functions $N_b$, the number of rays $N_r$, and the convergence rate of the NNLS solver.
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the projection matrix $P$ of size $N_r \times N_b$. For large $N_b$ and $N_r$, this can be memory-intensive.
- **Strengths:** Handles inconsistent projections and limited angles better than transform methods. Guarantees consistent views in the least-squares sense. Non-negativity constraint ensures physically plausible attenuation maps.
- **Limitations:** Computationally expensive. Requires careful selection of basis functions. Performance degrades if the basis functions do not adequately represent the target attenuation map.
- **When to use:** When using FBP or discrete inverse Radon transform results in artifacts due to limited angles or inconsistent data, and when a physically realizable (non-negative) attenuation map is required.
- **When not to use:** When real-time performance is critical and the projection data is consistent and complete.

## Background
Originated in the context of tomographic image synthesis for attenuation-based light field and high dynamic range displays [S1]. The method builds on the work of Herman [1995] regarding iterative reconstruction algorithms. It was developed to address the limitations of traditional transform methods in synthesizing displays that require non-negative attenuation coefficients. The approach leverages the Weber-Fechner law to justify the use of logarithmic light fields and linear least-squares optimization.
