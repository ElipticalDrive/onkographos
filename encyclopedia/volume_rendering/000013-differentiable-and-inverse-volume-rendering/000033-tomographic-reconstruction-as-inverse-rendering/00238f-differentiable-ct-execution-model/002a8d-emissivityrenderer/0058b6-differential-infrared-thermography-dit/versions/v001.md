# Differential Infrared Thermography (DIT)
Also called: DIT thermography

## Overview
Differential Infrared Thermography (DIT) is a technique used to estimate spray transmittance ($e^{-\delta_{max}}$) from the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) to avoid direct inversion of the ill-posed integral source term [S1, unofficial]. It simplifies the 3-D spray structure as a thin slab layer [S1, unofficial]. DIT measures the local attenuation coefficient of a transmissive target by comparing measurements with two different radiating backgrounds, assuming the target's temperature/radiance remains constant between measurements [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Two raw IR images of the spray ($I_{meas,1}, I_{meas,2}$) acquired under two different background radiance conditions ($I_{bg,1}, I_{bg,2}$). The spray field must remain statistically consistent between measurements [S1, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:** Integrated slab emissivity ($\varepsilon_{DIT}$), total optical depth ($\delta_{max}$), and local extinction coefficient profile ($\alpha(r)$) [S1, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Images:** 2D arrays of radiance values.
*   **Kernel Matrix ($K$):** A matrix where element $K_{ij}$ corresponds to the path length of the $i$-th ray passing through the $j$-th concentric ring in the discretized spray cross-section [S1, unofficial].
*   **Vectors:**
    *   $\Delta$: Vector of Total Optical Depth (TOD) values derived from $\delta_{max}$ [S1, unofficial].
    *   $A$: Unknown vector of radial extinction coefficients $\alpha(r)$ [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) relating measured radiance to local properties is:
$$ I_{meas} = I_{bg} e^{-\delta_{max}} + \int_{0}^{S_{max}} j(s) e^{-\delta(s)} ds $$
Where:
*   $I_{meas}$: Observed radiance [S1, unofficial].
*   $I_{bg}$: Background radiance [S1, unofficial].
*   $j(s)$: Local emission source term [S1, unofficial].
*   $s$: Coordinate along the ray path of total length $S_{max}$ (background at $s=0$, detector at $s=S_{max}$) [S1, unofficial].
*   $\delta(s)$: Local optical depth (LOD), i.e., cumulative attenuation from position $s$ to the detector [S1, unofficial].
*   $\delta_{max}$: Total optical depth (TOD) of the medium along the entire line of sight [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Acquire Measurements:** Acquire two raw IR images, $I_{meas,1}$ and $I_{meas,2}$, corresponding to two different background conditions $I_{bg,1}$ and $I_{bg,2}$, while the spray field remains statistically consistent [S1, unofficial].

2.  **Calculate Integrated Emissivity:** Compute the integrated spray emissivity $\varepsilon_{DIT}$ using the four measured images (two spray, two background) and chamber-noise contributions:
    $$ \varepsilon_{DIT} = \frac{I_{meas,2} - I_{meas,1}}{I_{bg,2} - I_{bg,1}} $$
    [S1, unofficial]

3.  **Determine Total Optical Depth:** Calculate the maximum optical depth $\delta_{max}(x)$ from the emissivity using the Beer-Lambert law approximation:
    $$ \delta_{max}(x) = -\ln(1 - \varepsilon_{DIT}(x)) $$
    [S1, unofficial]

4.  **Recover Extinction Coefficient:** Recover the local extinction coefficient profile $\alpha(r)$ from $\delta_{max}(x)$ via the Abel transform. Under the assumption of axisymmetry:
    $$ \delta_{max}(x) = 2 \int_{x}^{r_{max}} \frac{\alpha(r) r}{\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}} dr $$
    [S1, unofficial]
    Discretize the spray cross-section into concentric rings. Approximate the continuous integral by a finite sum, written in linear algebraic form:
    $$ \Delta = K A $$
    Where $\Delta$ is the vector of TOD, $A$ is the unknown vector of radial extinction coefficient $\alpha(r)$, and $K$ is the geometric kernel matrix [S1, unofficial].

5.  **Solve for Extinction:** Solve the linear system $A = K^{-1} \Delta$ (or using a regularized solver if $K$ is ill-conditioned) to obtain the radial extinction coefficient profile $\alpha(r)$ [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def differential_infrared_thermography(I_meas_1, I_meas_2, I_bg_1, I_bg_2):
    """
    Perform Differential Infrared Thermography to estimate spray transmittance
    and derive extinction coefficients.
    
    Args:
        I_meas_1: 2D array, raw IR image 1 with background 1.
        I_meas_2: 2D array, raw IR image 2 with background 2.
        I_bg_1: 2D array, background radiance image 1 (no spray).
        I_bg_2: 2D array, background radiance image 2 (no spray).
        
    Returns:
        epsilon_DIT: 2D array, integrated slab emissivity.
        delta_max: 2D array, total optical depth.
        alpha_r: 1D array, radial extinction coefficient profile (after Abel inversion).
    """
    # Step 1: Calculate Integrated Emissivity
    # Avoid division by zero if backgrounds are identical
    denom = I_bg_2 - I_bg_1
    epsilon_DIT = np.where(denom != 0, (I_meas_2 - I_meas_1) / denom, 0.0)
    
    # Step 2: Determine Total Optical Depth
    # Clamp epsilon_DIT to [0, 1) to avoid log(0) or negative arguments
    epsilon_clamped = np.clip(epsilon_DIT, 0.0, 0.999999)
    delta_max = -np.log(1.0 - epsilon_clamped)
    
    # Step 3: Recover Extinction Coefficient via Abel Transform
    # This requires axisymmetry and discretization into concentric rings.
    # The specific implementation of the Abel inversion depends on the 
    # chosen discretization scheme (e.g., van Loan, Sneddon).
    # Here we assume a helper function exists for the linear algebraic form Delta = K * A.
    
    # Flatten delta_max to a vector of measurements along projection lines
    # For axisymmetric sprays, typically only one radial profile is needed 
    # if the spray is perfectly axisymmetric, or multiple profiles are averaged.
    # Assuming delta_max is already reduced to a 1D vector of TOD values 
    # along the radial coordinate x for the axisymmetric case.
    
    # Construct geometric kernel matrix K
    # K_ij is the path length of the i-th ray passing through the j-th concentric ring.
    # This depends on the specific discretization of the cross-section.
    # Not given in the sources: Exact construction of K for arbitrary discretization.
    # Standard Abel inversion kernels are used here.
    K = construct_abel_kernel_matrix(delta_max.shape[0])
    
    # Solve linear system Delta = K * A for A (extinction coefficients)
    # Using least squares or regularization if K is ill-conditioned
    try:
        alpha_r = np.linalg.solve(K, delta_max)
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
        # Fallback to pseudo-inverse if singular
        alpha_r = np.linalg.lstsq(K, delta_max, rcond=None)[0]
        
    return epsilon_DIT, delta_max, alpha_r

def construct_abel_kernel_matrix(n_rings):
    """
    Constructs the geometric kernel matrix K for Abel inversion.
    K_ij corresponds to the path length of the i-th ray passing through the j-th ring.
    This is a simplified placeholder for the specific discretization scheme.
    """
    K = np.zeros((n_rings, n_rings))
    for i in range(n_rings):
        x = i + 1  # Projection coordinate
        for j in range(n_rings):
            r = j + 1  # Radial coordinate
            if r >= x:
                # Path length through ring j at projection x
                # For a thin ring approximation or specific discretization,
                # this formula varies. Standard Abel kernel involves sqrt terms.
                # Source [S1, unofficial] states K_ij is the path length.
                # A common discretization for Abel transform:
                # K_ij = 2 * (sqrt(r_j^2 - x_i^2) - sqrt(r_{j-1}^2 - x_i^2)) if r_j > x_i
                # This is a generic implementation detail not fully specified in [S1, unofficial].
                # We use a simplified geometric path length for demonstration.
                K[i, j] = 2 * np.sqrt(r**2 - x**2)
            else:
                K[i, j] = 0.0
    return K
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Background Conditions:** Must be significantly different to ensure numerical stability in the denominator ($I_{bg,2} - I_{bg,1}$) [S1, unofficial].
*   **Statistical Consistency:** The spray structure must not change significantly between the two measurements [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Temperature Interpretation:** DIT temperature does not represent local spray temperature because the 3-D structure is simplified as a thin slab. Instead, it represents an effective apparent temperature associated with the region where the Local Optical Depth (LOD) is of the order of unity, based on the Eddington–Barbier approximation [S1, unofficial].
*   **Optically Thick Cold Vapor:** For optically thick cold vapor sprays in a warm environment, the DIT temperature is an effective temperature associated mainly with the outer spray region where the line-of-sight depth (LOD) is of the order of unity [S1, unofficial].
*   **Uncertainty:** If the spray structure or chamber radiation changes significantly between the two DIT measurements, the estimated emissivity and subsequent reconstruction contain additional uncertainty [S1, unofficial].
*   **Scattering:** The method neglects scattering effects produced by dispersed sprays [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The core DIT calculation (emissivity and optical depth) is $O(N)$ per pixel, where $N$ is the number of pixels. The Abel inversion step is typically $O(N^2)$ or $O(N \log N)$ depending on the algorithm used for the linear system solution.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the input images, the kernel matrix $K$ ($O(N^2)$ for dense representation), and the output vectors.
*   **Strengths:** Avoids direct inversion of the ill-posed integral source term in the RTE. Provides a robust estimate of transmittance and extinction coefficients for axisymmetric sprays [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Assumes axisymmetry. The "temperature" derived is an effective slab temperature, not a true local volumetric temperature. Sensitive to changes in spray structure or background radiation between measurements [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** Suitable for axisymmetric sprays where a first-order estimate of extinction and effective temperature is needed, or as a preprocessing step for more complex tomographic reconstructions [S1, unofficial].
*   **When Not to Use:** For non-axisymmetric sprays, or when precise local volumetric temperature distribution is required without further tomographic processing [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Differential Infrared Thermography (DIT) was originally developed for measuring the local attenuation coefficient of transmissive targets [S1, unofficial]. It has been applied in spray studies to avoid the direct inversion of the ill-posed integral term in the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) [S1, unofficial]. Key references include Lamanna et al. (2013), Golzke et al. (2016), and Sharkov (2003) [S1, unofficial]. The technique relies on the Eddington–Barbier approximation to interpret the resulting temperature field [S1, unofficial].
