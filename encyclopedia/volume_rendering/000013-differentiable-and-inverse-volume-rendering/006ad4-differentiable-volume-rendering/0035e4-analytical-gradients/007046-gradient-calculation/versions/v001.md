# Gradient Calculation

Also called: Gradient of a scalar field, spatial derivative of density

## Overview
Gradient calculation computes the vector field of partial derivatives for a scalar field (such as density or distance), pointing in the direction of the greatest rate of decrease [S1, unofficial]. In volume rendering, this vector is used for shading to simulate surface-like lighting effects on volumetric data and for feature extraction [S1, unofficial]. It is distinguished from numerical finite-difference approximations by its potential for analytical derivation when the field is defined by a differentiable function or transformation, ensuring consistency with differentiable rendering pipelines [S1, unofficial][S2].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A scalar field function $f(x, y, z)$ or a transformed field defined by parameters; a query point $\mathbf{p} = (x, y, z)$.
**Outputs:** The gradient vector $\nabla f$ at $\mathbf{p}$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Scalar Field:** Represented as a function $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ or a discrete grid where interpolation is applied.
*   **Gradient Vector:** A 3D vector $\mathbf{g} = (g_x, g_y, g_z)$.

**Standard Cartesian Gradient:**
For a scalar field $f$, the gradient is defined as:
$$
\nabla f = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right)
$$
where $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}$ are the partial derivatives with respect to the spatial coordinates [S1, unofficial].

        A vector (gx, gy, gz) representing the gradient.
    """
    x, y, z = point

**Möbius Transformation of Gradients:**
When the scalar field is defined in a kernel space and transformed to world space via a Möbius transformation $L$, the gradient must be transformed accordingly [S2].

**Inputs:**
*   $q$: Point in kernel space.
*   $g$: Gradient at $q$ in kernel space (a pure quaternion or vector).
*   $L$: Möbius transformation defined by quaternions $a, b, c, d$.
*   $q_w$: Corresponding point in world space, $q_w = L(q)$.

**Outputs:**
*   $g_w$: Gradient at $q_w$ in world space.

**Steps:**
1.  Define the gradient $g_w$ as the differential change of $q_w$ in response to a differential change of $q$ along $g$:
    $$
    g_w = \frac{d\mathcal{L}(q+g\epsilon)}{d\epsilon} \bigg|_{\epsilon=0}
    $$
2.  Expand the Möbius transformation $L(q) = [a q + b][c q + d]^{-1}$ [S2].
3.  Apply the product rule and substitute $\epsilon = 0$ [S2].
4.  Apply the reciprocal rule for quaternions: $\frac{df^{-1}(x)}{dx} = -f^{-1}(x) \frac{df(x)}{dx} f^{-1}(x)$ [S2].
5.  Factor the result to isolate $g$ [S2].

**Formula:**
The general form is:
$$
g_w = \left[ a - (aq + b)(cq + d)^{-1}c \right] g(cq + d)^{-1}
$$
If $q_w$ is known, this simplifies to:
$$
g_w = (a - q_w c) g(cq + d)^{-1} \quad (3)
$$
where $q_w = [a q + b][c q + d]^{-1}$ [S2].

**Pitfalls:**
*   **Normalization:** The resulting gradient $g_w$ must be normalized to unit length if it represents a pure direction, as the transformation may scale it [S2].
*   **Non-commutativity:** Quaternion multiplication is non-commutative; the order of terms in the formula is critical [S2].
*   **Singularity:** The transformation is undefined if $cq + d = 0$ [S2].

**Usage:**
Used to obtain world-space gradients from kernel-space gradients when $L$ transforms from kernel space to world space [S2].

## Subtopics

### Möbius Transformation of Gradients
A method to compute the gradient $g_w$ of a transformed distance field at point $q_w = L(q)$, given the gradient $g$ at point $q$ in kernel space and the Möbius transformation $L$ [S2]. It sets itself apart by handling non-linear conformal mappings of spatial derivatives, essential for kernel-based rendering techniques [S2]. Choose it when rendering volumes defined by kernel functions that are transformed via Möbius mappings to world space [S2]. Link: [Kernel-Reflection Sequences](https://doi.org/10.2312/stag.20161364).

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per query point for analytical gradients, assuming the derivative functions are available. For numerical approximations, it depends on the stencil size.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ for storing the gradient vector at a point.
*   **Strengths:** Provides exact derivatives for differentiable rendering, avoiding approximation errors inherent in finite differences [S1, unofficial]. Efficient for shading calculations [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires the scalar field to be differentiable and the derivatives to be known or computable [S1, unofficial]. For discrete data, numerical methods introduce noise and depend on grid resolution [G].
*   **When to use:** When shading volumetric data to enhance visual realism or when integrating gradients into a differentiable rendering pipeline [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When the field is non-differentiable or only available as discrete samples without a smooth underlying function, unless numerical differentiation is acceptable [G].

## Background
Gradient calculation is a fundamental concept in vector calculus applied to computer graphics. In volume rendering, it was identified as a trivial but effective method for shading scalar fields by mapping density to opacity and velocity to color, allowing insights into fluid flow patterns [S1, unofficial]. The specific application of Möbius transformations to gradients in the context of kernel-based volume rendering was detailed in work on Kernel-Reflection Sequences [S2].
