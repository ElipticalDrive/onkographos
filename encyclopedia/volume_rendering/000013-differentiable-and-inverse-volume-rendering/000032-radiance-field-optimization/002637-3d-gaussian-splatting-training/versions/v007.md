# 3D Gaussian Splatting Training

Also called: 3DGS Learning, G Splats

## Overview
3D Gaussian Splatting Training is a non-deep learning optimization process that adjusts a set of 3D Gaussian primitives to minimize the difference between rendered images and ground truth images, using camera information and initial 3D Gaussian estimates [S6, unofficial]. It solves the problem of reconstructing complex 3D scenes from sparse or dense image collections by optimizing geometric attributes (position, scale, rotation) and shading attributes (opacity, spherical harmonic coefficients) [S5, unofficial]. Unlike NeRF, which optimizes a neural network, 3DGS training operates on explicit geometric primitives, offering significantly faster training and inference speeds while achieving comparable image quality [S2]. It builds on [Radiance-field optimization](44-radiance-field-optimization.md) by using a differentiable rasterization operator instead of volume integration, allowing for efficient gradient computation through the rendering pipeline [S6, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of input photos taken at different angles (or extracted from a video as an image sequence) [S5, unofficial]; Camera poses (rotation $R_{cw}$, translation $t_{cw}$) and intrinsics ($K$) derived from Structure from Motion (SfM) [S6, unofficial]; Ground truth images $\gamma^{gt}$ [S6, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:** A set of optimized 3D Gaussian parameters: positions $p_w$, rotations $q$, scales $s$, spherical harmonic coefficients $h$, and opacities $\alpha$ [S6, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
Each Gaussian $i$ is defined by:
*   **Position:** $p_w \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S6, unofficial].
*   **Rotation:** Unit quaternion $q = (q_r, q_i, q_j, q_k)$ [S2].
*   **Scale:** $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (standard deviations) [S2].
*   **Opacity:** $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ [S2].
*   **Color:** Spherical Harmonic (SH) coefficients $h$ [S2].
*   **Covariance:** $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, computed from $q$ and $s$ [S2].

**Initialization:**
1.  **Positions ($p_w$):** Initialized from sparse point clouds generated by SfM tools like COLMAP [S2][S6, unofficial].
2.  **Scales ($s$):** Initialized uniformly between 0 and 1 [S2].
3.  **Rotations ($q$):** Initialized as unit quaternions (identity rotation) [S2].
4.  **Opacities ($\alpha$):** Initialized as constants (typically 0.1 or similar, though sources vary, the process initializes them as constants) [S6, unofficial].
5.  **Colors ($h$):** Initialized from the average color of the input images at the corresponding position [S2].

**Forward Rendering (Rasterization):**
1.  **3D to 2D Projection:** Each 3D Gaussian is projected onto the 2D image plane. The 3D covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is transformed into the camera space and then projected to 2D, resulting in a 2D covariance matrix $\Sigma'$ [S6, unofficial].
2.  **Splatting:** The 2D Gaussian is evaluated at each pixel. The weight $g$ is computed based on the Mahalanobis distance in the projected space:
    $$ g = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (u_i - x_j)^T \Sigma'^{-1} (u_i - x_j)\right) $$
    where $u_i$ is the projected center and $x_j$ is the pixel coordinate [S6, unofficial].
3.  **Effective Opacity:** The effective opacity $\alpha'_{ij}$ for Gaussian $i$ at pixel $j$ is:
    $$ \alpha'_{ij} = g \cdot \alpha_i $$
    [S6, unofficial].
4.  **Alpha Blending:** Gaussians are sorted by depth. The pixel color $\gamma_j$ is computed via alpha blending:
    $$ \gamma_j = \sum_{i \in N} \alpha'_{ij} c_i \tau_{ij}, \quad \tau_{ij} = \prod_{k=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha'_{kj}) $$
    where $c_i$ is the color of Gaussian $i$ (evaluated from SH coefficients $h$ based on view direction) [S6, unofficial].

**Loss Function:**
The training minimizes a combination of L1 loss and D-SSIM loss [S6, unofficial]:
$$ \mathcal{L} = (1 - \lambda) \mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{D-SSIM} $$
where $\lambda$ is a weight (default 0.2) [S4, unofficial].

**Backpropagation and Gradient Computation:**
Gradients are computed using the chain rule through the 5 forward steps of image generation [S6, unofficial]. The Jacobians for the parameters are:
*   **Rotation ($q$):**
    $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial \sigma'_i} \frac{\partial \sigma'_i}{\partial \sigma_i} \frac{\partial \sigma_i}{\partial q_i} $$
    [S6, unofficial].
*   **Scale ($s$):**
    $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial \sigma'_i} \frac{\partial \sigma'_i}{\partial \sigma_i} \frac{\partial \sigma_i}{\partial s_i} $$
    [S6, unofficial].
*   **SH Coefficients ($h$):**
    $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial c_i} \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial h_i} $$
    [S6, unofficial].
*   **Opacity ($\alpha$):**
    $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \alpha_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial \alpha'_{ij}} \frac{\partial \alpha'_{ij}}{\partial \alpha_i} $$
    [S6, unofficial].
*   **Position ($p_w$):**
    $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p_{w,i}} = \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial \alpha'_{ij}} \frac{\partial \alpha'_{ij}}{\partial u_i} \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial p_{c,i}} \frac{\partial p_{c,i}}{\partial p_{w,i}} + \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial c_i} \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial p_{w,i}} + \sum_j \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma_j} \frac{\partial \gamma_j}{\partial \alpha'_{ij}} \frac{\partial \alpha'_{ij}}{\partial \sigma'_i} \frac{\partial \sigma'_i}{\partial p_{c,i}} \frac{\partial p_{c,i}}{\partial p_{w,i}} $$
    [S6, unofficial].

**Adaptive Density Control (Densification):**
Performed periodically (e.g., every 100-500 steps) [S2][S4, unofficial].
1.  **Pruning:** If $\alpha < \epsilon$ (e.g., 0.005) or the Gaussian is too large (screen size > threshold), remove the Gaussian [S2].
2.  **Densification Check:** If the positional gradient magnitude $\|\nabla_{p} \mathcal{L}\| > \tau_p$ (e.g., 0.0002) [S2][S4, unofficial]:
    *   **Over-reconstruction:** If the scale norm $\|S\| > \tau_S$ (e.g., 0.01) [S2][S4, unofficial], split the Gaussian into two. The new positions are offset from the original by a small random vector scaled by the covariance [S2].
    *   **Under-reconstruction:** If $\|S\| \le \tau_S$, clone the Gaussian [S2].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def train_3dgs(input_images, camera_poses, initial_points, num_iters=30000):
    # 1. Initialization
    gaussians = []
    for p in initial_points:
        g = Gaussian()
        g.position = p
        g.rotation = unit_quaternion()
        g.scale = random_uniform(0, 1)
        g.opacity = 0.1
        g.color = average_color_of_input_images_at(p)
        gaussians.append(g)
    
    optimizer = Adam(gaussians.parameters())
    
    # 2. Training Loop
    for step in range(1, num_iters + 1):
        # Camera Selection
        cam = select_camera(step, camera_poses)
        gt_image = get_ground_truth(cam, step)
        
        # Forward Pass
        rendered_image = rasterize(gaussians, cam)
        
        # Loss Computation
        loss = compute_loss(rendered_image, gt_image)
        
        # Backward Pass
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        
        # Adaptive Density Control (every refine_every steps)
        if step % refine_every == 0:
            densify_and_prune(gaussians, step)
            
        # SH Degree Scheduling (optional, if sh_degree > 0)
        if step % sh_degree_interval == 0:
            increase_sh_degree(gaussians)
            
    return gaussians

def densify_and_prune(gaussians, step):
    for g in gaussians:
        # Pruning
        if g.opacity < 0.005 or g.screen_size > 0.05:
            g.mark_for_removal()
            
        # Densification
        if g.gradient_magnitude > 0.0002:
            if g.scale_norm > 0.01:
                # Split
                new_g = split_gaussian(g)
                gaussians.append(new_g)
            else:
                # Clone
                new_g = clone_gaussian(g)
                gaussians.append(new_g)
                
    gaussians.remove_marked()
```

### HUGS
A method for animating virtual human avatars using 3DGS. It creates a coarse-to-fine deformation module using linear blend skinning and local learning-based refinement. It utilizes a tri-plane representation to factorize the canonical space. It reconstructs the person and scene from monocular video (50-100 frames) within 30 minutes and achieves state-of-the-art human neural rendering performance at 20 FPS [S1].

### Wolf et al. Stereo Method
Trains a 3DGS to render stereo-calibrated novel views. It applies stereo depth estimation on the rendered views and fuses the estimated dense depth maps using a Truncated Signed Distance Function (TSDF) to form a triangular mesh [S1].

### Pseudo Ground Truth Acquisition for Gaussians
A labeling pipeline that generates Gaussian-specific pseudo ground truth labels from original point cloud labels, accounting for the densification and pruning processes of 3D-GS optimization [S8, unofficial].

### SH Degree Scheduling
A training strategy to gradually increase the Spherical Harmonics (SH) degree. During coarse training, the degree increases from 0 to 1. During fine training, it increases from 1 to the target degree $n$, with each increase occurring after 10% of the total fine training iterations have completed. The default SH degree is 1, and the target $n$ is scene-dependent [S9, unofficial].

### Disk-Based Training Image Loading
Loads ground truth images from disk every iteration instead of storing them in VRAM. This enables training on consumer hardware with limited VRAM for ultra-large-scale datasets, at the cost of reduced training performance compared to standard VRAM storage [S9, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training is significantly faster than NeRF. Typical training times are under 1 hour for standard datasets (e.g., Mip-NeRF 360) [S5, unofficial]. The original implementation uses 30,000 iterations [S2][S4, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage scales with the number of Gaussians. The number of Gaussians can grow up to 5,000,000 or more depending on scene complexity [S3, unofficial]. Memory management is critical for large scenes [S9, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Achieves real-time rendering speeds (100+ FPS) on modern GPUs [S5, unofficial]. Training speed is comparable to or faster than other radiance field methods [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Fast training, real-time rendering, high visual fidelity [S5, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Can produce floating artifacts if not regularized [S1]. Requires careful densification control to avoid over-reconstruction [S2]. Large scene training can be memory-intensive [S9, unofficial].
*   **Hardware:** Optimized for GPU (CUDA/Metal). CPU execution is ~100x slower [S3, unofficial].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 [S2]. It builds on the idea of using explicit Gaussian primitives for radiance field representation, differing from the implicit neural representations of NeRF [S5, unofficial]. The method was designed to address the slow training and rendering speeds of NeRF while maintaining high visual quality [S2]. It has since become a popular baseline for novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction [S1].
