# Regularization and Geometry Constraints


## Overview
Regularization and Geometry Constraints in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) training address the instability of photometric optimization by enforcing geometric consistency, spectral fidelity, and robustness to transient artifacts. Unlike standard training which minimizes pixel-wise color differences, this family introduces auxiliary losses and preprocessing steps to constrain the learned scene representation, preventing over-smoothing, smearing artifacts, and pose drift. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Physical Parameter Optimization](4111-physical-parameter-optimization.md) by focusing on structural and spectral priors rather than physical light transport models, and from [Densification Strategy](6234-densification-strategy.md) by refining existing Gaussians rather than spawning new ones.

## Shared principles
The core mechanism involves augmenting the standard photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{rgb}$ with additional terms or preprocessing steps that encode prior knowledge about the scene's geometry or frequency content. These constraints typically operate on the rendered image space or the feature space to guide the optimization of Gaussian parameters $\theta = \{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{r}, \alpha, \mathbf{c}\}$.

Common mathematical forms include:
1.  **Spectral Regularization:** Penalizing discrepancies in specific frequency bands (e.g., low-frequency energy) to enforce sharpness or stability.
2.  **Geometric Consistency:** Using depth or normal maps to ensure that the rendered geometry aligns with estimated surface structures.
3.  **Feature Alignment:** Matching high-level appearance features from source images to the Gaussian representation to improve extrapolation and robustness.

The general loss function is formulated as:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \sum_i \lambda_i \mathcal{L}_{reg}^{(i)} $$
where $\lambda_i$ are weighting hyperparameters for each regularization term $\mathcal{L}_{reg}^{(i)}$. The gradients of these terms are backpropagated through the differentiable renderer to update the Gaussian parameters.

## Subtopics

### [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md)
Frequency regularization mitigates the "blurriness" and low-frequency artifacts common in standard photometric optimization by penalizing low-frequency variations in the scene representation. Unlike depth or normal regularization, it operates in the spectral domain to enhance edge sharpness and texture fidelity. It solves the problem where Gaussians tend to over-smooth scene details.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Input:** Rendered image $I_{render}$, ground truth image $I_{gt}$, standard photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{rgb}$.
- **Output:** Augmented loss $\mathcal{L}_{total}$.

**Method Steps:**
1.  **Render Image:** Generate $I_{render}$ using current Gaussians.
2.  **Transform to Frequency Domain:** Apply a frequency transform (e.g., Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) or Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)) to obtain frequency coefficients $F(I_{render})$ [G: DWT is explicitly cited for variants like DWTGS; FFT is a standard alternative].
3.  **Compute Frequency Loss:** Calculate a penalty term based on low-frequency components. This is typically the $L_1$ or $L_2$ distance between the low-pass filtered versions of $I_{render}$ and $I_{gt}$, or a penalty on the magnitude of low-frequency coefficients in $I_{render}$ [G: Minimizing the difference in low-frequency energy prevents over-smoothing].
4.  **Augment Loss:** Combine with photometric loss:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \lambda_{freq} \mathcal{L}_{freq} $$
    where $\lambda_{freq}$ is a weighting parameter [G: Typical values are small, e.g., $0.1$ to $1.0$].
5.  **Backpropagate:** Compute gradients and update parameters.

**Parameters:**
- $\lambda_{freq}$: Weight for frequency regularization. Not given in the sources. [G: Start with $0.1$].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** High-frequency components are sensitive to noise; ensure numerical stability in transforms.
- **Boundaries:** Edge effects in transforms (e.g., FFT) may introduce artifacts; use appropriate padding or windowing functions [G: Zero-padding or symmetric padding is common].
- **Degenerate Input:** Inherently low-frequency scenes (e.g., plain walls) may see minimal benefit or increased noise.

```python
def compute_frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image, gt_image, lambda_freq, transform_type='dwt'):
    """
    Computes the frequency regularization loss.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, 3), rendered RGB image.
        gt_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, 3), ground truth RGB image.
        lambda_freq: Float, weight for frequency loss.
        transform_type: String, 'dwt' for Discrete Wavelet Transform, 'fft' for Fourier.
        
    Returns:
        Float, the total augmented loss.
    """
    # Standard photometric loss
    l_rgb = torch.mean(torch.abs(rendered_image - gt_image))
    
    # Frequency transform
    if transform_type == 'dwt':
        # Apply DWT to get low-frequency (LL) and high-frequency components
        # Note: PyWavelets (pywt) is a common library, but we write out the logic
        # For simplicity, assume a function that returns low-pass filtered image
        rendered_low_freq = apply_low_pass_filter(rendered_image)
        gt_low_freq = apply_low_pass_filter(gt_image)
    elif transform_type == 'fft':
        # Apply FFT and zero out high frequencies
        rendered_fft = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image)
        gt_fft = torch.fft.fft2(gt_image)
        # Create a mask to keep only low frequencies (e.g., center 25%)
        mask = create_low_pass_mask(rendered_fft.shape)
        rendered_low_freq = torch.real(torch.fft.ifft2(rendered_fft * mask))
        gt_low_freq = torch.real(torch.fft.ifft2(gt_fft * mask))
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unsupported transform type")
        
    # Compute frequency loss (e.g., L1 distance between low-frequency components)
    l_freq = torch.mean(torch.abs(rendered_low_freq - gt_low_freq))
    
    # Total loss
    total_loss = l_rgb + lambda_freq * l_freq
    
    return total_loss

def apply_low_pass_filter(image):
    """
    Applies a low-pass filter to the image.
    For DWT, this would be the LL subband.
    For simplicity, we use a Gaussian blur as an approximation of low-frequency content.
    """
    # Gaussian blur kernel
    kernel_size = 5
    sigma = 1.0
    kernel = create_gaussian_kernel(kernel_size, sigma)
    # Apply convolution
    filtered_image = torch.nn.functional.conv2d(image.permute(2, 0, 1).unsqueeze(0), kernel, padding=2).squeeze()
    return filtered_image.permute(1, 2, 0)

def create_gaussian_kernel(size, sigma):
    """Creates a 2D Gaussian kernel."""
    coords = torch.arange(size) - size // 2
    x, y = torch.meshgrid(coords, coords, indexing='ij')
    kernel = torch.exp(-(x**2 + y**2) / (2 * sigma**2))
    kernel = kernel / kernel.sum()
    return kernel.unsqueeze(0).unsqueeze(0)

def create_low_pass_mask(shape):
    """Creates a mask to keep low frequencies in the center."""
    h, w = shape
    mask = torch.zeros(h, w)
    center_h, center_w = h // 2, w // 2
    radius = min(h, w) // 4
    y, x = torch.ogrid[:h, :w]
    mask[(y - center_h)**2 + (x - center_w)**2 <= radius**2] = 1.0
    return mask
```

### Wild-GS
**Definition:**
Wild-GS aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects and constrain geometry [S1]. It differs from standard 3DGS by explicitly modeling transient occlusions and photometric variations through these auxiliary constraints.

### Source Feature Extrapolation
**Definition:**
A technique to improve extrapolation capabilities beyond source view frustums by padding source images and adding positional encoding channels for padded regions, preventing smearing artifacts in regions not visible by source images [S2].

**Step:**
Apply border padding to source images $I^{(i)}$ [S2].

**Formula:**
Create a channel $\Gamma$ containing pixel-wise positional encodings for padded regions:
$$ \Gamma[u,v] = \begin{cases} \gamma(u,v) & \text{if } (u,v) \notin I^{(i)} \\ 0 & \text{if } (u,v) \in I^{(i)} \end{cases} $$
Concatenate $I_{pad}$ and $\Gamma$ to form $I'(i) = \text{concatenate}(I_{pad}, \Gamma)$ [S2].

**Parameter:**
$\gamma(\cdot)$ denotes positional encoding with exponential frequencies [S2].

### Smoothed 2D Supervision
**Definition:**
A technique to blur 2D training images with a parallel set of scheduled 2D Gaussian kernels to produce smoothed image gradients, stabilize camera alignment, and restrict the spectrum of the learned 3D scene [S3, unofficial].

**Step:**
Apply a Gaussian schedule for smoothing 2D training images similar to that of the 3D radiance fields [S3, unofficial].

## Background
Regularization techniques in 3DGS have evolved to address the inherent instability of unstructured Gaussian optimization. Frequency regularization was introduced to combat the blurriness caused by standard photometric losses [S1]. Geometric constraints like those in Wild-GS were developed to handle dynamic and transient scenes by leveraging depth and visibility maps [S1]. Source feature extrapolation methods, such as those in DINER, were proposed to improve generalization to unseen viewpoints by addressing border artifacts [S2]. Smoothed 2D supervision was introduced to stabilize joint pose optimization by restricting the frequency spectrum of the learned scene [S3, unofficial]. These methods are widely used in high-fidelity 3D reconstruction, novel view synthesis, and dynamic scene modeling.
