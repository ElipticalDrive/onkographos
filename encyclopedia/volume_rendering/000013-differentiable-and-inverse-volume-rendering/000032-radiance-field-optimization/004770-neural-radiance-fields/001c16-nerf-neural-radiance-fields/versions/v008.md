# NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields)

Also called: NeRF model, Neural Radiance Field (NeRF), Original NeRF, Radiance Fields (NeRF)

## Overview
NeRF represents a static 3D scene as a continuous volumetric function that maps a 3D spatial position and a 2D viewing direction to an emitted RGB color and a volume density scalar [S1][S2, S3, unofficial][S5][S8, S11, S12, S13, S16, S18, S22, S24, unofficial][S26][S29, unofficial][S30]. It solves the problem of novel view synthesis by replacing explicit geometric primitives (meshes or point clouds) with an implicit neural representation encoded in network weights, allowing the generation of photorealistic images from arbitrary camera viewpoints not seen during training [S1][S3, S6, S13, S15, S17, S21, unofficial][S27][S29, unofficial]. It distinguishes itself from earlier implicit methods by explicitly incorporating the viewing direction into the network input to correctly model view-dependent effects such as specularities and transparency, which require angular variation in the output color [S2, S3, unofficial][S5][S8, unofficial][S10][S12, S22, unofficial][S25].

## How it works
NeRF optimizes a continuous 5D radiance field function $F_\Theta$ using a set of posed 2D images and their corresponding camera poses. The method relies on differentiable volume rendering to compute gradients from the photometric error between rendered and ground-truth images, updating the network parameters via gradient descent [S3, S13, S15, S19, S22, unofficial][S27].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ training images $\{I_i\}$ with known camera poses (extrinsics $R_i, t_i$ and intrinsics $K_i$).
*   **Outputs:** A trained neural network $F_\Theta$ that approximates the scene's radiance field.

### Data Structures
The core data structure is a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) parameterized by weights $\Theta$. The original NeRF implementation uses two distinct MLP branches within a single network architecture:
1.  **Density Network ($F_{\Theta_1}$):** Takes encoded 3D position as input. Outputs a scalar volume density $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}^+$ and an intermediate feature vector $h \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ [S5, S25, S30].
2.  **Color Network ($F_{\Theta_2}$):** Takes the feature vector $h$ and the encoded viewing direction as input. Outputs an RGB color vector $c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S5, S25, S30].

The network architecture consists of 8 hidden layers with 256 units each, using ReLU activation functions [S23, unofficial][S25, S30]. Skip connections are added from the input to layers 4 and 8 to address signal attenuation with depth [S2, S23, unofficial].

### Positional Encoding
To enable the MLP to represent high-frequency details, input coordinates are mapped to a higher-dimensional space using Fourier features (positional encoding) [S1][S14, S20, S23, unofficial][S25][S28, S29, unofficial]. For an input vector $\mathbf{p}$ and frequency levels $L$, the encoding $\gamma(\mathbf{p})$ is defined as:
$$
\gamma(\mathbf{p}) = (\sin(2^0 \pi \mathbf{p}), \cos(2^0 \pi \mathbf{p}), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1} \pi \mathbf{p}), \cos(2^{L-1} \pi \mathbf{p}))
$$
The 3D position $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z)$ is encoded with $L_x=10$ levels, resulting in a 63-dimensional vector [S11, S14, S29, unofficial]. The viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ is encoded with fewer levels ($L_d < L_x$, typically $L_d=4$) because color varies more smoothly with direction than position [S3, S14, unofficial].

### Ray Sampling
For each pixel in the target image, a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ is cast, where $\mathbf{o}$ is the camera center and $\mathbf{d}$ is the unit viewing direction [S7, S9, S10][S12, S18, S29, unofficial]. The ray is sampled at $N$ points within the interval $[t_n, t_f]$ (near and far planes) [S9][S29, unofficial].
*   **Stratified Sampling:** The interval is divided into $N$ equal bins. During training, one sample is drawn uniformly at random from each bin (with a perturbation term) to ensure better coverage than fixed grid sampling [S4][S12, S16, unofficial].
*   **Hierarchical Sampling:** NeRF uses two networks, a "coarse" network and a "fine" network, optimized simultaneously [S4][S23, unofficial][S25]. The coarse network samples $N_c$ points. The weights derived from the coarse rendering are used to form a probability distribution, and $N_f$ additional points are sampled via inverse-CDF sampling to focus on regions of high density [S4][S12, S23, unofficial].

### Volume Rendering
The color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ for a ray is computed by discretizing the volume rendering integral [S3, unofficial][S7, S10][S12, S16, S29, unofficial]:
$$
\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i (1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)) \mathbf{c}_i
$$
where:
*   $\sigma_i$ is the volume density at sample $i$.
*   $\mathbf{c}_i$ is the emitted color at sample $i$.
*   $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ is the distance between adjacent samples.
*   $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ is the opacity of the segment.
*   $T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$ is the accumulated transmittance, representing the probability that the ray travels from the camera to sample $i$ without being absorbed [S3, unofficial][S7][S12, S16, S29, unofficial].

### Training Objective
The network parameters are optimized by minimizing the mean squared error (L2 loss) between the rendered images $\hat{I}$ and the ground-truth training images $I$ [S3, unofficial][S9, S26][S29, unofficial]:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|R|} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in R} \|\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2
$$
where $R$ is the set of rays sampled from the training images [S7][S29, unofficial]. The total loss includes contributions from both the coarse and fine networks [S4]:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \left[ \|\hat{C}_{\text{coarse}}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2 + \|\hat{C}_{\text{fine}}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2 \right]
$$

### Pseudocode

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class PositionalEncoding(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, num_freq_bands):
        super().__init__()
        self.num_freq_bands = num_freq_bands

    def forward(self, x):
        # x shape: (..., 3) for position or (..., 3) for direction
        # Apply sin/cos at exponential frequencies
        sines = torch.cat([torch.sin((2**i) * torch.pi * x.unsqueeze(-1)) 
                           for i in range(self.num_freq_bands)], dim=-1)
        cosines = torch.cat([torch.cos((2**i) * torch.pi * x.unsqueeze(-1)) 
                             for i in range(self.num_freq_bands)], dim=-1)
        return torch.cat([x, sines, cosines], dim=-1)

class NeRF(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, D=8, W=256, input_ch=3, input_ch_dir=3, 
                 skips=[4], use_viewdirs=True, freq_pos=10, freq_dir=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.D = D
        self.W = W
        self.input_ch = input_ch
        self.input_ch_dir = input_ch_dir
        self.skips = skips
        self.use_viewdirs = use_viewdirs
        
        # Positional Encoders
        self.pos_enc = PositionalEncoding(freq_pos)
        self.dir_enc = PositionalEncoding(freq_dir)
        
        # Input dimension after encoding
        # Pos: 3 + 3*2*freq_pos = 3 + 60 = 63
        # Dir: 3 + 3*2*freq_dir = 3 + 24 = 27
        self.input_ch_enc = 3 + 2 * input_ch * freq_pos
        self.input_ch_dir_enc = 3 + 2 * input_ch_dir * freq_dir
        
        # Density Network (takes position only)
        self.layers_xyz = nn.ModuleList()
        self.layers_xyz.append(nn.Linear(self.input_ch_enc, W))
        for i in range(1, D):
            if i in skips:
                self.layers_xyz.append(nn.Linear(W + self.input_ch_enc, W))
            else:
                self.layers_xyz.append(nn.Linear(W, W))
        
        # Density output layer
        self.sigma_layer = nn.Linear(W, 1)
        
        # Color Network (takes feature vector + direction)
        if use_viewdirs:
            self.feature_layer = nn.Linear(W, W)
            self.input_ch_dir_total = W + self.input_ch_dir_enc
            self.layers_dir = nn.ModuleList()
            self.layers_dir.append(nn.Linear(self.input_ch_dir_total, W // 2))
            self.sigma_dir_layer = nn.Linear(W // 2, 1)
            self.rgb_layer = nn.Linear(W // 2, 3)
        else:
            self.rgb_layer = nn.Linear(W, 3)

    def forward(self, x):
        # x shape: (..., 6) -> (..., 3) position, (..., 3) direction
        xyz = x[..., :3]
        dirs = x[..., 3:]
        
        # Encode inputs
        xyz_enc = self.pos_enc(xyz)
        dirs_enc = self.dir_enc(dirs)
        
        # Density Network
        h = xyz_enc
        for i, layer in enumerate(self.layers_xyz):
            h = F.relu(layer(h))
            if i in self.skips:
                h = torch.cat([h, xyz_enc], -1)
        
        sigma = F.relu(self.sigma_layer(h))
        
        # Extract feature vector for color network
        h_feature = h
        if self.use_viewdirs:
            h_feature = F.relu(self.feature_layer(h))
            h_color = torch.cat([h_feature, dirs_enc], -1)
            for layer in self.layers_dir:
                h_color = F.relu(layer(h_color))
            sigma_dir = F.relu(self.sigma_dir_layer(h_color))
            rgb = self.rgb_layer(h_color)
            return torch.cat([rgb, sigma_dir, sigma], -1)
        else:
            rgb = self.rgb_layer(h_feature)
            return torch.cat([rgb, sigma], -1)

def render_ray(ray_origins, ray_directions, near, far, nerf_model, N_samples=64, N_importance=64):
    """
    Renders a batch of rays using NeRF.
    ray_origins: (B, 3)
    ray_directions: (B, 3)
    near: float
    far: float
    """
    B = ray_origins.shape[0]
    
    # 1. Stratified Sampling for Coarse Network
    t_vals = torch.linspace(0., 1., N_samples).to(ray_origins.device)
    # Stratified sampling: add random noise in [0, 1) to each t_val
    t_vals = t_vals + torch.rand_like(t_vals) / N_samples
    t_vals = near * (1. - t_vals) + far * t_vals
    
    # Query Coarse Network
    # Points shape: (B, N_samples, 3)
    points = ray_origins.unsqueeze(1) + ray_directions.unsqueeze(1) * t_vals.unsqueeze(2)
    # Concatenate directions: (B, N_samples, 6)
    queries = torch.cat([points, ray_directions.unsqueeze(1).repeat(1, N_samples, 1)], dim=-1)
    
    # Forward pass
    outputs_coarse = nerf_model(queries.reshape(-1, 6)) # (B*N, 4)
    outputs_coarse = outputs_coarse.reshape(B, N_samples, 4)
    
    rgb_coarse = outputs_coarse[..., :3]
    sigma_coarse = outputs_coarse[..., 3]
    
    # Compute weights for importance sampling
    deltas = t_vals[..., 1:] - t_vals[..., :-1] # (B, N-1)
    deltas = torch.cat([deltas, torch.tensor([1e3]).to(deltas.device)], dim=-1)
    
    alpha_coarse = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma_coarse * deltas)
    T_coarse = torch.cumprod(1.0 - alpha_coarse + 1e-10, dim=-1)
    T_coarse = torch.cat([torch.ones_like(T_coarse[..., :1]), T_coarse[..., :-1]], dim=-1)
    weights_coarse = T_coarse * alpha_coarse
    
    # 2. Importance Sampling for Fine Network
    # Sample based on coarse weights
    t_vals_fine, _ = torch.sort(torch.rand(B, N_importance).to(ray_origins.device) * (far - near) + near, dim=-1)
    # Inverse CDF sampling would go here, simplified to random for brevity in this generic impl
    # In practice: use torch.multinomial or inverse CDF on weights_coarse
    
    # Re-query with fine samples
    points_fine = ray_origins.unsqueeze(1) + ray_directions.unsqueeze(1) * t_vals_fine.unsqueeze(2)
    queries_fine = torch.cat([points_fine, ray_directions.unsqueeze(1).repeat(1, N_importance, 1)], dim=-1)
    
    outputs_fine = nerf_model(queries_fine.reshape(-1, 6))
    outputs_fine = outputs_fine.reshape(B, N_importance, 4)
    
    rgb_fine = outputs_fine[..., :3]
    sigma_fine = outputs_fine[..., 3]
    
    # 3. Volume Rendering (Fine)
    # Combine coarse and fine samples (simplified: use only fine for final render in this snippet)
    # Full implementation combines both sets of samples and re-computes weights
    return rgb_fine.mean(dim=1) # Placeholder return
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Precision:** The exponential function in transmittance calculation can underflow or overflow if density values are extremely high or low. A small epsilon ($10^{-10}$) is added to prevent division by zero or log of zero [S12, S29, unofficial].
*   **Boundaries:** The near and far planes ($t_n, t_f$) must be carefully chosen. If $t_f$ is too large, the ray samples empty space, wasting computation and potentially introducing noise. If $t_n$ is too large, the camera center is excluded, causing artifacts [S5, S9].
*   **Degenerate Input:** The method assumes calibrated camera poses. Incorrect poses lead to inconsistent geometry and blurry renderings [S15, S22, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training is slow, typically requiring 10–20 hours on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU for standard scenes [S1, S5]. Rendering is also slow, taking approximately 30 seconds per novel view at 1080p resolution [S1, S5]. This is due to the need to query the deep MLP at hundreds of points per ray for hundreds of thousands of rays per image [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** The model weights are compact (~5 MB for the MLP parameters) [S8, unofficial]. However, training requires storing intermediate states for backpropagation through the volume rendering integral, leading to high memory usage proportional to the number of samples and pixels [44-radiance-field-optimization.md].
*   **Performance:** On the NeRF Synthetic (Blender) dataset, NeRF achieves a PSNR of approximately 31.0 dB [S1]. It provides high-quality novel view synthesis but suffers from poor surface accuracy compared to SDF-based methods like NeuS [S1].
*   **Strengths:** High photorealism, handles complex geometry and view-dependent effects (specularity, transparency) well, no explicit mesh required.
*   **Limitations:** Slow training and rendering, poor surface reconstruction accuracy, sensitive to camera pose errors, struggles with unbounded scenes without specific parameterizations [S1][S3, unofficial][S5].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality novel view synthesis is the primary goal and computational resources allow for long training times.
*   **When not to use:** When real-time rendering or fast training is required (use Instant NGP or 3D Gaussian Splatting), or when accurate surface geometry is needed (use NeuS).

## Background
NeRF was introduced by Mildenhall et al. in 2020 at ECCV [S1, S5]. It established the field of neural radiance fields by demonstrating that a simple fully connected network could represent a 3D scene as a continuous 5D function, enabling photorealistic novel view synthesis from sparse images [S1][S3, unofficial][S5]. The method builds on the concept of Inverse Rendering and Differentiable Volume Rendering [44-radiance-field-optimization.md]. Since its publication, NeRF has inspired a vast body of work addressing its limitations in speed, surface accuracy, and generalization, leading to variants like Instant NGP, Mip-NeRF, and 3D Gaussian Splatting [S1, S5].
