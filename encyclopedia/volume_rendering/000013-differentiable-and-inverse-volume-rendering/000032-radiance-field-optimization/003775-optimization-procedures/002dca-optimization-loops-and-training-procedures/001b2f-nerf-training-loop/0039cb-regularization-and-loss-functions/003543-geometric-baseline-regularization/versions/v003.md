# Geometric Baseline Regularization

Also called: Geometric Baseline, Geometric Loss

## Overview
Geometric Baseline Regularization is a composite loss function used to stabilize the optimization of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), particularly in few-view settings where standard photometric loss leads to degenerate solutions [S1]. It augments the standard photometric reconstruction loss with three geometric terms: foreground completeness, frustum consistency, and depth distribution compactness [S1]. Unlike sibling methods that rely on external priors (e.g., diffusion models [7381-diffusion-regularized-nerf-training.md]) or explicit depth supervision [8582-regnerf.md], this method enforces geometric plausibility directly through the volume rendering weights and ray sampling structure [S1].

## How it works
The method computes a total loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{geom}}$ by combining the photometric loss with three regularization terms. The optimization targets the density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ fields to minimize this loss via stochastic gradient descent [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
- **Inputs**: Training images $I_i$, camera poses, ray origins $\mathbf{o}$ and directions $\mathbf{d}$, sampled depths $t_i$ along rays, and hyperparameters $\lambda_{\text{fg}}, \lambda_{\text{fr}}, \lambda_{\text{dist}}$.
- **Outputs**: Gradient updates for $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$ fields.

### Data Structures and Ray Sampling
For each ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$, $N$ depth samples are taken at positions $t_1, \dots, t_N$. The rendering equation computes the weight $w_i$ for each sample:
$$ w_i = \alpha_i \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \alpha_j\right) $$
where $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ and $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ [S1]. The rendered color $\mathbf{C}$ is:
$$ \mathbf{C} = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \mathbf{c}_i $$
The expected depth $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r})$ is computed as:
$$ \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N w_i t_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N w_i} $$
[S1]

### Regularization Terms

1.  **Photometric Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}$)**:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\sigma, \mathbf{c}) = \sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{I}} ||I_i - \mathbf{C}_i||_2 $$
    where $\mathcal{I}$ is the number of training views [S1].

2.  **Foreground Completeness ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{fg}}$)**:
    Encourages the sum of weights along a ray to equal 1, ensuring the ray is fully absorbed by scene geometry [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{fg}} = \left( 1 - \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \right)^2 $$
    [S1]

3.  **Frustum Consistency ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{fr}}$)**:
    Prevents degenerate solutions where density is placed in only one view frustum (e.g., copying training images). It penalizes weights located in fewer than two training view frustums [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{fr}} = \sum_i w_i \mathbf{1}(n_i \le 1) $$
    where $n_i$ is the number of training view frustums containing the point $\mathbf{r}(t_i)$, and $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function [S1].

4.  **Distribution Compactness ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}$)**:
    Encourages weights to have a compact distribution around the expected depth. This formulation deviates from the original by dividing by $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r})$ to increase regularization strength for geometry close to the camera [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r})} \left( \sum_{i,j} w_i w_j \left| \frac{t_i + t_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{t_j + t_{j+1}}{2} \right| + \frac{1}{3} \sum_{i=1}^N w_i^2 (t_{i+1} - t_i) \right) $$
    Note: The source uses indices $i,j$ for the first sum and $i,k$ in the notes; the standard interpretation for pairwise distance between intervals is $i,j$ [S1].

### Total Loss
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{geom}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}} + \lambda_{\text{fg}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{fg}} + \lambda_{\text{fr}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{fr}} + \lambda_{\text{dist}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} $$
[S1]

### Parameters
- $\lambda_{\text{fg}}, \lambda_{\text{fr}}$: Not explicitly given in the sources. [G] Typically set to 1.0 or similar small constants in baseline implementations, but exact values are not provided in [S1].
- $\lambda_{\text{dist}}$: Scheduled over training steps.
    - For the first 2500 steps, $\lambda_{\text{dist}} = 0$ [S1].
    - From step 2500 to 8000, it linearly increases [S1].
    - Maximum value at 8000 steps:
        - DTU dataset: $1 \times 10^{-4}$ [S1].
        - LLFF dataset: $1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ [S1].

```python
def compute_geometric_baseline_loss(
    training_images, 
    camera_poses, 
    ray_origins, 
    ray_directions, 
    depth_samples, 
    density_field, 
    color_field,
    lambda_fg, 
    lambda_fr, 
    lambda_dist, 
    step, 
    max_steps=8000,
    start_step=2500,
    dtu_max_dist=1e-4,
    llff_max_dist=1.5e-5
):
    """
    Computes the Geometric Baseline Loss for NeRF optimization.
    
    Args:
        training_images: List of ground truth images.
        camera_poses: Camera pose parameters.
        ray_origins: Array of ray origins [num_rays, 3].
        ray_directions: Array of ray directions [num_rays, 3].
        depth_samples: Array of sampled depths [num_rays, N].
        density_field: Function returning density sigma for points.
        color_field: Function returning color c for points.
        lambda_fg: Weight for foreground completeness loss.
        lambda_fr: Weight for frustum consistency loss.
        lambda_dist: Weight for distribution compactness loss.
        step: Current training step.
        max_steps: Total training steps (default 8000).
        start_step: Step where lambda_dist starts increasing (default 2500).
        dtu_max_dist: Max lambda_dist for DTU dataset.
        llff_max_dist: Max lambda_dist for LLFF dataset.
        
    Returns:
        Total loss scalar.
    """
    # Determine lambda_dist based on dataset and step
    # Note: The source does not specify how to detect dataset type automatically.
    # Implementer must choose dtu_max_dist or llff_max_dist based on their dataset.
    # We assume a flag 'is_dtu' is passed or inferred. For this generic function,
    # we use dtu_max_dist as default, but in practice, this must be selected.
    max_lambda_dist = dtu_max_dist 
    
    if step < start_step:
        current_lambda_dist = 0.0
    else:
        # Linear interpolation from 0 to max_lambda_dist
        progress = (step - start_step) / (max_steps - start_step)
        current_lambda_dist = progress * max_lambda_dist

    total_loss = 0.0
    num_training_views = len(training_images)
    
    # Precompute frustum counts for each sample point if necessary for L_fr
    # This requires checking which camera frustums contain each point r(t_i)
    # This is computationally expensive and not detailed in the source pseudocode.
    # We assume a helper function get_frustum_counts exists.
    # frustum_counts[i, j] = number of frustums containing sample j of ray i
    frustum_counts = compute_frustum_counts(ray_origins, ray_directions, depth_samples, camera_poses)

    for view_idx in range(num_training_views):
        # 1. Render the view
        # Get rays for this view
        rays = get_rays_for_view(view_idx, camera_poses)
        gt_image = training_images[view_idx]
        
        # Sample points along rays
        points = ray_origins + ray_directions * depth_samples.unsqueeze(-1)
        
        # Query density and color
        sigmas = density_field(points)
        colors = color_field(points)
        
        # Compute weights w_i
        # alpha_i = 1 - exp(-sigma_i * delta_i)
        # delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i
        deltas = depth_samples[:, 1:] - depth_samples[:, :-1]
        # Pad deltas for the last sample if needed, or handle boundary
        # Standard NeRF rendering:
        alphas = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigmas * deltas)
        
        # Compute cumulative product for transmittance
        # T_i = exp(-sum_{j=1}^{i-1} alpha_j * delta_j)
        # Weights w_i = alpha_i * T_i
        # Implementation detail: use cumulative sum of log(1-alpha) or similar
        # For simplicity, assuming a standard volume rendering function is available
        # or implementing it explicitly:
        
        # Cumulative product of (1 - alpha)
        # (1 - alpha) is transmittance between samples
        transmittance = torch.cumprod(1.0 - alphas, dim=1)
        # Prepend 1.0 for the first sample
        transmittance = torch.cat([torch.ones_like(transmittance[:, :1]), transmittance], dim=1)
        
        weights = alphas * transmittance[:, :-1] # w_i corresponds to sample i
        
        # Rendered color C
        rendered_color = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * colors, dim=1)
        
        # 2. Photometric Loss
        photo_loss = torch.mean((rendered_color - gt_image) ** 2)
        
        # 3. Foreground Completeness Loss
        # Sum of weights should be 1
        weight_sum = torch.sum(weights, dim=1)
        fg_loss = torch.mean((1.0 - weight_sum) ** 2)
        
        # 4. Frustum Consistency Loss
        # Penalize weights where n_i <= 1
        # frustum_counts shape: [num_rays, N]
        # mask: 1 if count <= 1, else 0
        frustum_mask = (frustum_counts[:, 1:] <= 1).float() # Exclude first sample if needed, or align indices
        # Note: depth_samples has N points. frustum_counts should match.
        # Assuming frustum_counts is aligned with depth_samples
        frustum_mask = (frustum_counts <= 1).float()
        fr_loss = torch.sum(weights * frustum_mask) / num_rays # Normalize by number of rays
        
        # 5. Distribution Compactness Loss
        # Compute expected depth D(r)
        # D(r) = sum(w_i * t_i) / sum(w_i)
        # Avoid division by zero
        sum_weights = torch.sum(weights, dim=1)
        sum_weights = torch.clamp(sum_weights, min=1e-8)
        expected_depth = torch.sum(weights * depth_samples, dim=1) / sum_weights
        
        # Term 1: Pairwise distance between interval centers
        # Centers: (t_i + t_{i+1}) / 2
        # Note: The formula uses w_i * w_j. This is O(N^2) per ray.
        # Implementer should vectorize this.
        centers = (depth_samples[:, :-1] + depth_samples[:, 1:]) / 2.0
        # Expand dims for broadcasting
        # dist_matrix[i, j] = |center_i - center_j|
        dist_matrix = torch.abs(centers.unsqueeze(1) - centers.unsqueeze(2))
        # Weighted sum: sum_{i,j} w_i w_j |c_i - c_j|
        # weights shape [num_rays, N-1]
        # dist_matrix shape [num_rays, N-1, N-1]
        weighted_dist = torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(2) * weights.unsqueeze(1) * dist_matrix, dim=(1, 2))
        
        # Term 2: Variance-like term
        # 1/3 * sum_i w_i^2 * (t_{i+1} - t_i)
        # deltas shape [num_rays, N-1]
        variance_term = torch.sum(weights ** 2 * deltas, dim=1)
        
        # Combine terms
        dist_term = weighted_dist + (1.0/3.0) * variance_term
        
        # Divide by expected depth
        # expected_depth shape [num_rays]
        dist_loss = torch.mean(dist_term / expected_depth)
        
        # Accumulate losses
        total_loss += photo_loss + lambda_fg * fg_loss + lambda_fr * fr_loss + current_lambda_dist * dist_loss

    return total_loss / num_training_views
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
- **Division by Zero**: The distribution loss divides by expected depth $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r})$. If the sum of weights is zero (empty ray), this leads to division by zero. Guard against this by clamping the denominator or skipping such rays [S1].
- **Frustum Counting**: Computing $n_i$ (number of frustums containing a point) is computationally expensive. It requires projecting each 3D sample point into every training camera's 2D image plane and checking if it lies within the image bounds [S1].
- **Few-View Collapse**: Without $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fr}}$, NeRFs in few-view settings may collapse to a solution where each camera sees a copy of its own training image. This term specifically penalizes density that is visible in only one frustum [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity**: The distribution loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}$ involves a pairwise sum over samples ($O(N^2)$ per ray), which can be computationally expensive for large $N$. The frustum consistency loss requires projecting points into all training views ($O(N \cdot V)$ where $V$ is the number of views).
- **Memory Complexity**: Requires storing weights and depths for all samples. The frustum counting step may require temporary storage for projection results.
- **Performance**: Provides a strong baseline for few-view NeRF optimization, preventing degenerate solutions [S1]. It is effective in both DTU and LLFF datasets [S1].
- **Strengths**: Simple to implement as it relies only on standard volume rendering outputs. Does not require external depth maps or diffusion models.
- **Limitations**: The $O(N^2)$ term in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}$ can be slow. The frustum counting is also costly.

## Background
Geometric Baseline Regularization was introduced in the context of DiffusioNeRF, a method that regularizes NeRFs with Denoising Diffusion Models [S1]. The authors refer to this combination of losses as their "geometric baseline" because it provides a strong starting point for optimization, particularly in few-view scenarios [S1]. It builds on the idea that geometric priors (compactness, completeness, multi-view consistency) can stabilize learning without explicit supervision [S1].
