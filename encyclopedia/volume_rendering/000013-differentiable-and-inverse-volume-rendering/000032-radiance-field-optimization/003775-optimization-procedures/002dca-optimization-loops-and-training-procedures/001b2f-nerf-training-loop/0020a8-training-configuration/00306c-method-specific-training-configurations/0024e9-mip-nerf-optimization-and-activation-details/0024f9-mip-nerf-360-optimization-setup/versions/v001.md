# Mip-NeRF 360 Optimization Setup

Also called: Mip-NeRF 360 Training Procedure

## Overview
This article defines the specific hyperparameters, network architectures, and loss functions used to train the Mip-NeRF 360 model. It solves the problem of stabilizing the optimization of unbounded, anti-aliased radiance fields by combining a multi-stage proposal sampling strategy with a robust reconstruction loss. It differs from standard NeRF training by enforcing a strict separation between proposal network optimization and NeRF optimization via stop-gradient operations, and by using a Charbonnier loss instead of MSE to handle outliers in "in-the-wild" imagery [S1].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**: A batch of rays $\mathbf{t}$, each defined by origin and direction, and their corresponding ground-truth pixel colors $\mathbf{C}^*$.
*   **Outputs**: Optimized weights for the proposal MLP ($\Theta_{\text{prop}}$) and the NeRF MLP ($\Theta_{\text{NeRF}}$).

### Data Structures
The method relies on two Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs):
1.  **Proposal MLP**:
    *   Architecture: 4 layers, 256 hidden units per layer.
    *   Activations: ReLU for hidden layers, Softplus for the final density output $\tau$.
    *   Parameters: $\Theta_{\text{prop}}$.
2.  **NeRF MLP**:
    *   Architecture: 8 layers, 1024 hidden units per layer.
    *   Activations: ReLU for hidden layers, Softplus for the final density output $\tau$.
    *   Parameters: $\Theta_{\text{NeRF}}$.

### Numbered Steps
1.  **Proposal Sampling Stage 0**: Evaluate the proposal MLP using 64 samples along each ray. Resample these samples to produce the first set of weighted intervals $(\hat{s}_0, \hat{w}_0)$ [S1].
2.  **Proposal Sampling Stage 1**: Evaluate the proposal MLP again using 64 samples, resampled based on the previous stage. Produce the second set of weighted intervals $(\hat{s}_1, \hat{w}_1)$ [S1].
3.  **NeRF Sampling**: Evaluate the NeRF MLP using 32 samples, resampled based on the proposal histograms. Produce the final set of weighted intervals $(s, w)$ [S1].
4.  **Loss Computation**: Compute the total loss $\mathcal{L}$ as the sum of reconstruction, distribution, and proposal losses, averaged over all rays in the batch [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{t}), \mathbf{C}^*) + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{w}) + \sum_{k=0}^1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{prop}}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{w}, \hat{\mathbf{s}}^k, \hat{\mathbf{w}}^k) $$
    *   $\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$: Charbonnier loss between rendered color $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{t})$ and ground truth $\mathbf{C}^*$.
    *   $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}$: Regularizer minimizing the weighted size of individual intervals.
    *   $\mathcal{L}_{\text{prop}}$: Interlevel loss between NeRF and proposal histograms.
    *   $\lambda$: Hyperparameter balancing data and regularization terms.
5.  **Gradient Application**: Apply stop-gradient to $\mathcal{L}_{\text{prop}}$ before backpropagating to $\Theta_{\text{NeRF}}$. This ensures the optimization of $\Theta_{\text{prop}}$ is independent of $\Theta_{\text{NeRF}}$ [S1].
6.  **Optimizer Update**: Update both networks using the Adam optimizer [S1].

### Formulas
*   **Reconstruction Loss** ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$): Charbonnier loss [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \sqrt{(x - x^*)^2 + \epsilon^2} $$
    Where $x$ is the predicted color, $x^*$ is the ground truth color, and $\epsilon = 0.001$ [S1].
*   **Total Loss** ($\mathcal{L}$): As defined in Step 4 above [S1].

### Parameters
*   **Optimizer**: Adam [S1].
    *   $\beta_1 = 0.9$
    *   $\beta_2 = 0.999$
    *   $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$
*   **Learning Rate Schedule**: Annealed log-linearly from $2 \times 10^{-3}$ to $2 \times 10^{-5}$ [S1].
    *   Warm-up phase: 512 iterations [S1].
*   **Gradient Clipping**: Norm of $10^{-3}$ [S1].
*   **Training Iterations**: 250,000 [S1].
*   **Batch Size**: $2^{14}$ rays [S1].
*   **Regularization Weight** ($\lambda$): 0.01 [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Z-aliasing**: The proposal MLP may learn a non-smooth mapping from input coordinates to volumetric densities, causing rays to "skip" scene content. This is particularly visible when the camera translates along the z-axis or when using high-capacity backends like iNGP [S2].
*   **Interlevel Loss Artifacts**: The standard Mip-NeRF 360 interlevel loss assigns equivalent loss to NeRF and proposal histogram bins regardless of overlap extent. It only penalizes complete failures to overlap, leading to non-smooth density maps [S2].

def train_mipnerf360(batch_rays, batch_gt_colors, num_iterations=250000):
    # Initialize networks
    proposal_mlp = MLP(layers=4, hidden_units=256, activation='relu', density_activation='softplus')
    nerf_mlp = MLP(layers=8, hidden_units=1024, activation='relu', density_activation='softplus')
    
    # Initialize optimizer
    optimizer = Adam(params=[proposal_mlp.parameters(), nerf_mlp.parameters()], 
                     lr=2e-3, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, eps=1e-6)
    
    # Learning rate schedule parameters
    lr_start = 2e-3
    lr_end = 2e-5
    warmup_iters = 512
    
    for iteration in range(1, num_iterations + 1):
        # 1. Proposal Sampling Stage 0
        # Sample 64 points along rays using proposal_mlp
        samples_0 = sample_along_rays(batch_rays, num_samples=64, network=proposal_mlp)
        # Resample to produce histogram (s_hat_0, w_hat_0)
        s_hat_0, w_hat_0 = resample_histogram(samples_0)
        
        # 2. Proposal Sampling Stage 1
        # Sample 64 points along rays using proposal_mlp, guided by previous histogram
        samples_1 = sample_along_rays(batch_rays, num_samples=64, network=proposal_mlp, 
                                      histogram=(s_hat_0, w_hat_0))
        # Resample to produce histogram (s_hat_1, w_hat_1)
        s_hat_1, w_hat_1 = resample_histogram(samples_1)
        
        # 3. NeRF Sampling
        # Sample 32 points along rays using nerf_mlp, guided by proposal histograms
        # Note: The source specifies one stage of evaluation of NeRF MLP using 32 samples.
        # It implies resampling based on the proposal output.
        samples_nerf = sample_along_rays(batch_rays, num_samples=32, network=nerf_mlp, 
                                         histogram=(s_hat_1, w_hat_1))
        # Produce final histogram (s, w)
        s, w = resample_histogram(samples_nerf)
        
        # 4. Compute Losses
        # Render color from NeRF samples
        rendered_colors = render_volume(s, w, nerf_mlp)
        
        # Reconstruction Loss (Charbonnier)
        epsilon = 0.001
        recon_loss = mean(sqrt((rendered_colors - batch_gt_colors)**2 + epsilon**2))
        
        # Distribution Loss (Regularizer)
        dist_loss = compute_interval_size_regularization(s, w)
        
        # Proposal Loss (Interlevel)
        # Compute loss between NeRF histogram (s, w) and Proposal histograms
        prop_loss_0 = compute_interlevel_loss(s, w, s_hat_0, w_hat_0)
        prop_loss_1 = compute_interlevel_loss(s, w, s_hat_1, w_hat_1)
        total_prop_loss = prop_loss_0 + prop_loss_1
        
        # Total Loss
        lambda_reg = 0.01
        total_loss = recon_loss + lambda_reg * dist_loss + total_prop_loss
        
        # 5. Gradient Application
        # Stop-gradient on proposal loss for NeRF optimization
        # In practice, this means detaching the proposal loss from the NeRF's gradient graph
        # or ensuring the proposal loss is not backpropagated through the NeRF.
        # The source states: "stop-gradient used in Lprop makes the optimization of Theta_prop 
        # independent from the optimization of Theta_NeRF".
        # This implies L_prop is used to update Theta_prop, but NOT Theta_NeRF.
        # However, the total loss formula includes L_prop. 
        # Standard interpretation: L_prop is computed for both, but gradients for NeRF 
        # are zeroed out for the L_prop term.
        
        # Calculate gradients
        grads = compute_gradients(total_loss, [proposal_mlp.parameters(), nerf_mlp.parameters()])
        
        # Apply stop-gradient to NeRF gradients for the proposal loss component
        # This is a conceptual step; implementation depends on autograd framework.
        # We assume the framework allows selective gradient stopping.
        # If not, we might compute two separate backward passes or use a custom loss wrapper.
        # For the purpose of this pseudocode, we assume a mechanism exists to stop gradients 
        # from L_prop flowing into nerf_mlp.
        grads_nerf_prop_loss = compute_gradients(total_prop_loss, nerf_mlp.parameters())
        # Zero out these specific gradients
        for p in nerf_mlp.parameters():
            if p.grad is not None:
                p.grad -= grads_nerf_prop_loss # Conceptual subtraction to remove influence
        
        # 6. Optimizer Update
        optimizer.step(grads)
        
        # Update learning rate
        if iteration < warmup_iters:
            current_lr = lr_start * (iteration / warmup_iters)
        else:
            # Log-linear annealing
            progress = (iteration - warmup_iters) / (num_iterations - warmup_iters)
            current_lr = lr_start * (lr_end / lr_start) ** progress
        optimizer.set_lr(current_lr)
        
        # Gradient Clipping
        clip_norm = 1e-3
        clip_gradients(optimizer, clip_norm)

## Subtopics

### [Anti-Aliased Interlevel Loss](3457-anti-aliased-interlevel-loss.md)
The Anti-Aliased Interlevel Loss is a proposal supervision loss function that measures the distance between a NeRF histogram and a proposal histogram by blurring the NeRF histogram into a piecewise-linear spline and resampling it into the proposal's interval endpoints. Unlike the piecewise constant loss used in Mip-NeRF 360, which suffers from aliasing when NeRF histogram bins are narrow or translate relative to the proposal, this method produces a smooth loss surface that prevents aliasing artifacts during training. It is used in Zip-NeRF to resolve "z-aliasing" artifacts where the proposal network learns non-smooth mappings, causing rays to skip scene content.

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity**: The method uses a multi-stage sampling approach (2 proposal stages + 1 NeRF stage), increasing the number of forward passes per ray compared to single-stage NeRF. However, the proposal network is smaller (4 layers, 256 units) than the NeRF (8 layers, 1024 units), mitigating the cost.
*   **Memory Complexity**: Memory usage is dominated by the storage of the two MLPs and the intermediate histograms for the batch. The batch size of $2^{14}$ rays requires significant memory for ray data and intermediate tensors.
*   **Measured Performance**: The method was evaluated on 9 scenes (5 outdoors, 4 indoors) with complex central objects and detailed backgrounds [S1]. It improves speed and rendering quality compared to prior strategies of learning one or multiple NeRFs supervised using image reconstruction [S2].
*   **Strengths**: The use of Charbonnier loss provides more stable optimization than MSE, especially for "in-the-wild" imagery with outliers [S1]. The stop-gradient on proposal loss ensures stable, independent optimization of the proposal network [S1].
*   **Limitations**: The standard interlevel loss can lead to z-aliasing artifacts, particularly with high-capacity backends like iNGP [S2]. The method requires careful tuning of the learning rate schedule and gradient clipping [S1].
*   **When to Use**: Use this setup when training unbounded, anti-aliased radiance fields on "in-the-wild" imagery where stability and robustness to outliers are critical.
*   **When Not to Use**: Avoid if computational resources are extremely limited, as the multi-stage sampling increases forward pass count. Also, if z-aliasing is a major concern and the iNGP backend is used, consider the Anti-Aliased Interlevel Loss variant [S2].

## Background

The Mip-NeRF 360 optimization setup was introduced in the paper "Mip-NeRF 360: Unbounded Anti-Aliased Neural Radiance Fields" [S1]. It builds on the original Mip-NeRF method [3] and addresses challenges in training on unbounded, "in-the-wild" datasets. The method was designed to improve training stability and robustness without significantly changing accuracy or speed [Parent]. The issue of z-aliasing in proposal networks was further analyzed in "Zip-NeRF: Anti-Aliased Grid-Based Neural Radiance Fields" [S2], which proposed the Anti-Aliased Interlevel Loss as a solution.
