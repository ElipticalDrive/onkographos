# Zip-NeRF


## Overview
Zip-NeRF is a method that integrates scale-aware anti-aliased neural radiance fields with fast grid-based training to achieve lower error rates and faster convergence than prior techniques [S1]. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts in both spatial rendering and the loss function during online distillation, which are particularly prominent when rendering scenes across multiple scales or with sparse sampling [S1]. It sets itself apart from its parent [Mip-NeRF 360](3306-mip-nerf-360.md) by replacing the large MLP with an Instant-NGP (iNGP) grid structure and introducing specific anti-aliasing mechanisms (multisampling, downweighting, and interlevel loss) to handle the high-frequency details of grid-based representations [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A set of posed images [S1].
*   **Output:** A radiance field model capable of rendering anti-aliased images at various scales [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Instant-NGP Structure ($G_\theta$):** A pyramid of voxel grids and hashes replacing the large MLP used in mip-NeRF 360 [S1].
*   **Feature Codes:** Stored in the grids/hashes. Each level $\ell$ has a resolution $n_\ell$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Proposal Networks:** Separate iNGP and MLP structures for each round of proposal sampling [S1].
*   **NeRF MLP:** Uses a larger view-dependent branch than the standard iNGP [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Sampling Budget:** Two rounds of proposal sampling with 64 samples each, followed by 32 samples in the final NeRF sampling round [S1].
*   **Anti-aliased Interlevel Loss Rectangular Pulse Width ($r$):** $r = 0.03$ in the first proposal round; $r = 0.003$ in the second round [S1].
*   **Anti-aliased Interlevel Loss Multiplier:** $0.01$ [S1].
*   **Normalized Weight Decay Multiplier:** $0.1$ (unless otherwise stated) [S1].
*   **Scale Factors:** $[1, 2, 4, 8]$ for multiscale dataset creation [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Multiscale Dataset Creation:**
    For each input image, generate four additional images by bicubically downsampling by scale factors $s \in [1, 2, 4, 8]$. These act as proxy training/test views with zoomed-out cameras [S1].

2.  **Initialization:**
    Initialize the Instant-NGP grid pyramid, proposal networks, and NeRF MLP parameters [G].

3.  **Multiscale Training Loop:**
    For each training iteration:
    a.  **Ray Sampling:** Cast rays through the multiscale images.
    b.  **Downweighting:** When querying the Instant-NGP grid at level $\ell$ with resolution $n_\ell$ for a query with filter variance $\sigma$, apply the downweighting factor $\phi(\sigma)$ [S2, unofficial]:
        $$ \phi(\sigma) = \text{erf}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\sigma^2 n_\ell^2}}\right) $$
        This convolves the output with a Gaussian, reducing the impact of higher-resolution features for larger queries [S2, unofficial].
    c.  **Multisampling:** Use the anti-aliased multisampling strategy (hexagonal pattern or similar) to sample within the sub-frusta defined by mip-NeRF 360 [S1].
    d.  **Proposal Sampling:** Perform two rounds of proposal sampling (64 samples each) using separate proposal iNGPs/MLPs [S1].
    e.  **Final Sampling:** Perform the final NeRF sampling round (32 samples) [S1].
    f.  **Loss Calculation:**
        i.  **Data Term:** Multiply the standard data term (reconstruction loss) by the scale factor $s$ of the current ray/image [S1].
        ii. **Normalized Weight Decay:** Calculate the loss $\ell$ for feature codes in the pyramid:
            $$ \ell = \text{mean}(V_\ell^2) $$
            where $V_\ell$ is the set of grid/hash values at pyramid level $\ell$. Sum this mean across levels. This penalizes coarse scales more heavily than fine scales [S1]. Apply multiplier $0.1$ [S1].
        iii. **Anti-aliased Interlevel Loss:** Impose the interlevel loss on both proposal rounds with pulse width $r$ ($0.03$ for round 1, $0.003$ for round 2) and multiplier $0.01$ [S1].
    g.  **Optimization:** Update parameters using JAX [S1].

4.  **Evaluation:**
    At test time, evaluate each scale factor $s \in [1, 2, 4, 8]$ separately [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math

def train_zip_nerf(images, poses, num_iterations=100000):
    # Initialize Instant-NGP grid pyramid, proposal networks, and NeRF MLP
    grid_pyramid = initialize_ngp_pyramid()
    proposal_net_1 = initialize_proposal_network()
    proposal_net_2 = initialize_proposal_network()
    nerf_mlp = initialize_nerf_mlp(view_dependent_branch_size='large')

    # Multiscale dataset: bicubically downsample by [1, 2, 4, 8]
    images_s = [bicubic_downsample(img, s) for s in [1, 2, 4, 8]]

    for iteration in range(num_iterations):
        for scale_factor in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
            img_s = images_s[scale_factor - 1]
            rays = cast_rays(img_s, poses)

            for ray in rays:
                # Helper: Downweight Instant-NGP query
                def query_ngp_downweighted(coord, sigma):
                    value = 0.0
                    for l in range(grid_pyramid.num_levels):
                        n_l = grid_pyramid.resolution(l)
                        raw_val = grid_pyramid.query(coord, level=l)
                        phi = math.erf(1.0 / math.sqrt(8 * sigma**2 * n_l**2))
                        value += raw_val * phi
                    return value

                # Proposal Sampling Round 1 (64 samples)
                samples_1 = sample_proposal(proposal_net_1, ray, num_samples=64)
                # Compute anti-aliased interlevel loss over samples (formula not specified in sources)
                loss_prop1 = compute_interlevel_loss(samples_1, r=0.03)

                # Proposal Sampling Round 2 (64 samples)
                samples_2 = sample_proposal(proposal_net_2, ray, num_samples=64)
                loss_prop2 = compute_interlevel_loss(samples_2, r=0.003)

                # Final NeRF Sampling (32 samples)
                samples_final = sample_nerf(nerf_mlp, ray, num_samples=32)

                # Data Loss with scale weighting
                rendered_color = render_ray(samples_final, nerf_mlp, query_ngp_downweighted)
                data_loss = mean_squared_error(rendered_color, img_s) * scale_factor

                # Normalized Weight Decay
                weight_decay_loss = 0.0
                for l in range(grid_pyramid.num_levels):
                    V_l = grid_pyramid.get_values(level=l)
                    weight_decay_loss += mean(V_l**2)
                weight_decay_loss *= 0.1

                # Total Loss
                total_loss = data_loss + 0.01 * (loss_prop1 + loss_prop2) + weight_decay_loss

                update_parameters(total_loss)
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Z-aliasing:** Disabling the anti-aliased interlevel loss causes z-aliasing artifacts in video results, though it may have little effect on single-image metrics [S1].
*   **Sample Count:** The baseline loss (without anti-aliasing) fails catastrophically if fewer than 16 samples are used, whereas the anti-aliased loss degrades gracefully [S1].
*   **Weight Decay:** Using non-normalized weight decay performs poorly; disabling it reduces accuracy significantly [S1].
*   **View-Dependent Branch:** Using iNGP’s small view-dependent MLP decreases accuracy; a larger branch is required [S1].

## Subtopics

### Zip-NeRF Downweighting
A querying scheme to mitigate aliasing where the output of instant-NGP is convolved with a Gaussian [S2, unofficial]. It downweights higher resolution features for larger sized queries [S2, unofficial]. The downweighting factor is $\phi(\sigma) = \text{erf}\left(1/\sqrt{8\sigma^2 n_\ell^2}\right)$, where $\sigma$ is the filter variance and $n_\ell$ is the grid resolution of level $\ell$ [S2, unofficial]. This is combined with a regularizer that pulls all features towards 0 to reduce the impact of higher resolution features for larger sized queries [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
*   **Training Time:** Zip-NeRF trains 24x faster than mip-NeRF 360 (0.89 hrs vs 21.69 hrs on the 360 dataset) [S1]. It is ~6x slower to train than iNGP (0.89 hrs vs 0.15 hrs) [S1].
*   **Render Time:** 0.9s per image, compared to 7.4s for mip-NeRF 360 and 0.2s for a naive mip-NeRF 360 + iNGP baseline [S1].

**Measured Performance:**
*   **360 Dataset (Table 2):** PSNR 28.54, SSIM 0.828, LPIPS 0.189 [S1]. Outperforms mip-NeRF 360 (PSNR 27.57, SSIM 0.793, LPIPS 0.234) and iNGP (PSNR 25.68, SSIM 0.705, LPIPS 0.302) [S1].
*   **Multiscale 360 Dataset (Scale 1x):** PSNR 28.25, SSIM 0.822, LPIPS 0.198 [S1].
*   **Multiscale 360 Dataset (Scale 8x):** PSNR 32.52, SSIM 0.954, LPIPS 0.037 [S1].
*   **Error Reduction:** 11% reduction in RMSE, 17% in DSSIM, 19% in LPIPS vs mip-NeRF 360 on the 360 dataset [S1]. 28% reduction in RMSE, 42% in DSSIM, 37% in LPIPS vs iNGP on the 360 dataset [S1].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Significantly faster training than mip-NeRF 360; superior image quality compared to iNGP; robust to low sample counts; handles multiscale generalization well [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Slower than raw iNGP; render time is higher than iNGP [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When high-quality, anti-aliased rendering is required for unbounded scenes, and training time is a constraint compared to pure MLP-based NeRFs, but quality is more important than the absolute fastest training time of iNGP [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When absolute minimum training time is required (use iNGP) or when rendering single images with high sample counts where z-aliasing is not a concern (mip-NeRF 360 may suffice, though Zip-NeRF is still better) [S1].

## Background
Zip-NeRF was introduced in the paper "Zip-NeRF: Anti-Aliased Grid-Based Neural Radiance Fields" [S1]. It builds on the mip-NeRF 360 codebase [S1] and incorporates Instant-NGP's grid-based acceleration [S1]. The method addresses the divergence between scale-aware anti-aliased NeRFs and fast grid-based NeRF training, unifying them to handle aliasing in both spatial and loss domains [S1].
