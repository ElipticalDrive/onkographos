# MegaNeRF

Also called: Altitude-constrained Raycasting, Ellipsoid Partitioning, Mega-NeRF, Mega-NeRF Foreground/Background Decomposition

## Overview
MegaNeRF is a framework for training large-scale 3D scenes (spanning $150,000$ to $1,300,000 \text{ m}^2$) that supports interactive human-in-the-loop fly-throughs from thousands of images with varying lighting [S1]. It addresses the infeasibility of training a single monolithic NeRF on such scales by decomposing the scene into spatial cells, each modeled by a separate NeRF submodule [S1]. Unlike [BlockNeRF](8581-blocknerf.md) and [KiloNeRF](8581-blocknerf.md) which use spatial partitioning primarily to speed up inference of a pre-trained network, MegaNeRF uses partitioning to enable data parallelism for scalable training [S1]. It distinguishes itself from [NeRF++](8581-blocknerf.md) by modeling the foreground volume as an ellipsoid (rather than a unit sphere) and using camera altitude constraints to skip underground sampling [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ training images with known camera poses (position $\mathbf{t}$ and orientation $\mathbf{R}$) and intrinsics. For altitude constraints, camera altitude measurements $h$ are required [S1].
*   **Outputs:** $K$ independent NeRF submodules $\{\mathcal{M}_k\}_{k=1}^K$, where each $\mathcal{M}_k$ consists of an MLP predicting density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ for points within its assigned spatial cell $V_k$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Spatial Partitioning:** The scene volume $\mathcal{V}$ is divided into $K$ non-overlapping spatial cells $\{V_k\}_{k=1}^K$. The sources describe this as "spatial Voronoi partitioning" or "geometric clustering" [S1]. The centroids of these cells are fixed in height relative to the scene, exploiting the small variance in camera altitude compared to latitude/longitude variations [S1].
2.  **Foreground/Background Decomposition:** The scene is further subdivided into a foreground volume $V_{fg}$ and a background volume $V_{bg}$ [S1].
    *   $V_{fg}$: An ellipsoid that tightly encloses all camera poses and relevant foreground detail [S1].
    *   $V_{bg}$: The complement of $V_{fg}$, modeled using the same 4D outer volume parameterization as NeRF++ (inverse sphere parameterization) [S1].
3.  **Pixel Partitioning Map:** A mapping from each training pixel to the set of cells it intersects. This is computed by sampling points along the camera ray for each training image and assigning the pixel to the trainset of only those spatial cells the ray intersects [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Scene Decomposition:**
    Define the foreground volume $V_{fg}$ as an ellipsoid enclosing all camera poses. Define the background volume $V_{bg}$ as the region outside $V_{fg}$ [S1].

2.  **Visibility-Based Data Partitioning:**
    For each training image $i$ with ray $\mathbf{r}_i$:
    a. Sample points $\mathbf{x}$ along $\mathbf{r}_i$.
    b. Identify which spatial cells $V_k$ contain any point $\mathbf{x}$ on the ray segment within the scene bounds [S1].
    c. Assign pixel $i$ to the training set of each identified cell $V_k$ [S1].
    *Note:* This reduces the size of each submodule's training set by approximately 10x compared to using all pixels [S1].

3.  **Parallel Training:**
    Train each submodule $\mathcal{M}_k$ independently and in parallel using only its assigned partitioned pixel data [S1]. The MLP $\mathcal{M}_k$ predicts $\sigma_k(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{c}_k(\mathbf{x})$ for $\mathbf{x} \in V_k$ [S1].

4.  **Inference (Raycasting):**
    For a query ray $\mathbf{r}$:
    a. Determine the intersection segments of $\mathbf{r}$ with each cell $V_k$ [S1].
    b. For each sample point $\mathbf{x}_i$ along $\mathbf{r}$, identify the cell $V_k$ containing $\mathbf{x}_i$.
    c. Query the corresponding submodule $\mathcal{M}_k$ to obtain $\sigma_i = \sigma_k(\mathbf{x}_i)$ and $\mathbf{c}_i = \mathbf{c}_k(\mathbf{x}_i)$ [S1].
    d. Compute the standard volume rendering integral:
       $$ C(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i} T_i \left( 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i) \right) \mathbf{c}_i $$
       where $T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$ [S1, S3].

5.  **Altitude-Constrained Raycasting (Optimization):**
    During inference, use camera altitude measurements to constrain sampling bounds [S1].
    a. Terminate rays near ground level (altitude $h_{ground}$) to avoid querying underground regions [S1].
    b. This prevents unnecessary queries to empty or irrelevant space below the scene [S1].

6.  **Interactive Rendering (Temporal Coherence):**
    To achieve near-interactive view generation:
    a. Cache occupancy and color values from nearby previous views [S1].
    b. Exploit temporal coherence to skip redundant computations for pixels with minimal change between frames [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def train_mega_nerf(training_images, scene_bounds, num_cells):
    """
    training_images: List of dicts with keys 'image', 'camera_pose', 'altitude'
    scene_bounds: Bounding box of the entire scene
    num_cells: Number of spatial cells K
    """
    # Step 1: Spatial Partitioning
    # Initialize K cells using spatial Voronoi partitioning
    # Centroids are fixed in height based on scene statistics [S1]
    cells = voronoi_partition(scene_bounds, num_cells)
    
    # Step 2: Visibility-Based Data Partitioning
    # Map each pixel to the cells it intersects
    pixel_to_cells = {}
    for img in training_images:
        ray = generate_ray(img['camera_pose'], img['image'].shape)
        # Sample points along the ray
        points = sample_along_ray(ray, scene_bounds)
        # Find intersecting cells
        intersecting_cells = []
        for p in points:
            for k, cell in enumerate(cells):
                if point_in_cell(p, cell):
                    intersecting_cells.append(k)
        # Assign pixel to these cells
        pixel_to_cells[img['id']] = set(intersecting_cells)
        
    # Step 3: Parallel Training
    # Initialize K independent MLPs
    modules = [MLP() for _ in range(num_cells)]
    
    # Train each module in parallel using its partitioned data
    for k in range(num_cells):
        trainset = []
        for img_id, cell_set in pixel_to_cells.items():
            if k in cell_set:
                trainset.append(training_images[img_id])
        # Train module k on its specific trainset [S1]
        modules[k] = train_module(modules[k], trainset)
        
    return modules

def render_mega_nerf(modules, cells, query_ray, altitude_threshold):
    """
    Render a single ray using the trained modules and altitude constraints [S1]
    """
    # Step 4 & 5: Raycasting with Altitude Constraint
    # Determine intersection segments with cells
    segments = get_ray_cell_intersections(query_ray, cells)
    
    # Apply altitude constraint: terminate near ground level
    # If ray goes below altitude_threshold, clip the segment [S1]
    segments = clip_by_altitude(segments, altitude_threshold)
    
    # Standard Volume Rendering
    color = 0.0
    transmittance = 1.0
    delta = 0.02 # Fixed step size or adaptive
    
    for segment in segments:
        # Sample points within the segment
        for x in sample_segment(segment, num_samples=8):
            # Identify which cell contains x
            k = find_cell_for_point(x, cells)
            
            # Query the corresponding submodule [S1]
            sigma, c = modules[k](x)
            
            # Accumulate color
            alpha = 1.0 - exp(-sigma * delta)
            color += transmittance * alpha * c
            transmittance *= (1.0 - alpha)
            
    return color
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Number of Cells ($K$):** Not given in the sources. Determined by scene size and desired granularity.
*   **Ellipsoid Parameters:** Defined by the tightest fit around all camera poses [S1].
*   **Altitude Threshold:** Ground level altitude, derived from camera altitude measurements [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Underground Sampling:** Without altitude constraints, rays may query empty space below the scene, wasting computation [S1].
*   **Boundary Continuity:** Transmittance $T_i$ accumulates across the entire ray, requiring careful handling of transmittance continuity at block boundaries [S3].
*   **Visibility Gaps:** If a cell has no training pixels assigned (due to occlusion or sparse coverage), its module may not train effectively [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training is data-parallel, reducing the training set size per module by ~10x [S1]. This improves training speed by 3x compared to baseline approaches [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Scales with the number of cells $K$ and the size of each submodule. Each submodule is smaller than a full NeRF, allowing larger total scene capacity [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   Achieves a 40x speedup over conventional NeRF rendering [S1, S3].
    *   Remains within 0.8 dB in PSNR quality compared to conventional NeRF [S1, S3].
    *   Improves PSNR by 12% compared to baseline approaches on existing datasets (Quad 6k and UrbanScene3D) [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Enables training on scenes orders of magnitude larger than prior work (up to $1,300,000 \text{ m}^2$) [S1].
    *   Supports interactive fly-throughs via temporal coherence caching [S1].
    *   More efficient sampling than NeRF++ due to ellipsoid bounds and altitude constraints [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Requires geometric clustering and visibility analysis for data partitioning [S1].
    *   Performance depends on the quality of the spatial partitioning and ellipsoid fitting [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Large-scale urban scenes with thousands of images where monolithic NeRF training is infeasible [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** Small-scale scenes where a single NeRF suffices, or scenes with significant vertical variation in camera altitude that breaks the fixed-height centroid assumption [S1].

## Background
*   **Origin:** MegaNeRF was introduced in 2022 by [S1].
*   **History:** It was developed to address the challenges of modeling thousands of images with varying lighting, large model capacities, and fast rendering for large-scale urban environments [S1]. It builds on the visibility analysis of large-scale scenes, noting that only a small fraction of training images are visible from any particular scene point [S1].
*   **Comparison to NeRF++:** Unlike NeRF++ which uses a unit sphere for the foreground, MegaNeRF uses an ellipsoid for tighter bounds and adds altitude constraints to skip underground sampling [S1].
*   **Use Cases:** Search-and-rescue operations, where drones survey large areas quickly [S1].
