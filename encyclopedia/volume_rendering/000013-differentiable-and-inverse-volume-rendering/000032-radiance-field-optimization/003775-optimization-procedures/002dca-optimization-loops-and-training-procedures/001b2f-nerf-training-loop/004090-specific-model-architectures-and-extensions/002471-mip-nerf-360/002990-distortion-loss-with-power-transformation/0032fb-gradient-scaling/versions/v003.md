# Gradient Scaling

Also called: Sampling density based gradient scaling, Transmittance-based gradient scaling

## Overview
Gradient Scaling is a technique used in differentiable volume rendering to mitigate near-camera overfitting and the formation of "floaters" (spurious geometry artifacts) during radiance field optimization [S1]. It addresses the problem of gradient imbalance along rays, where samples far from the camera receive significantly smaller gradients than those near the camera due to the exponential decay of transmittance [S1]. Unlike [Distortion Loss with Power Transformation](4560-distortion-loss-with-power-transformation.md), which modifies the loss function to penalize metric distance, Gradient Scaling modifies the optimization process itself by rescaling gradients during backpropagation to ensure uniform gradient magnitude across all samples on a ray [S1].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Ray $\mathbf{r}$ defined by origin and direction [G].
    *   Set of $N$ samples along the ray, indexed $i=1 \dots N$ [G].
    *   For each sample $i$: volume density $\sigma_i$, distance to adjacent samples $\delta_i$ (typically constant step size), Spherical Harmonic (SH) coefficients $(k_\ell^m)_i$ for color representation, and transmittance $T_i$ up to sample $i$ [S1].
    *   Current rendered color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ and target color $C(\mathbf{r})$ for photometric loss computation [G].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Scaled gradients for SH coefficients $\nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i} \mathcal{L}$ ready for the optimizer step [S1].

### Data Structures
*   **Transmittance $T_i$**: A scalar value for each sample $i$, representing the probability of light passing through the volume from the camera to sample $i$ [G].
    *   $T_1 = 1$ (assuming the first sample is at the camera origin or the start of the integration range) [S1].
    *   $T_{i+1} = T_i \cdot (1 - \alpha_i)$ [S1].
*   **Opacity $\alpha_i$**: Scalar value for each sample $i$ [G].
    *   $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ [S1].

### Formulas
The core mechanism relies on the observation that the gradient of the rendered color with respect to the color component $\mathbf{c}_i$ (derived from SH coefficients) is proportional to the transmittance $T_i$ [S1]:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i} \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = T_i \alpha_i
$$

Since the SH coefficients $(k_\ell^m)_i$ determine the color $\mathbf{c}_i$ via the SH basis function $Y_\ell^m$, the gradient with respect to the SH coefficients is:

$$
\nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i} \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = Y_\ell^m \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i} \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = Y_\ell^m \cdot T_i \alpha_i
$$

The photometric loss gradient $\nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i} \mathcal{L}_{MSE}$ is proportional to $\nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i} \hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ [S1]. Consequently, without scaling, the gradient magnitude decays exponentially with distance from the camera because $T_i$ decays exponentially [S1].

**Gradient Scaling Rule:**
To counteract this, the gradients for the SH coefficients are multiplied by the reciprocal of the transmittance $1/T_i$ [S1]:

$$
\nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i}^{\text{scaled}} \mathcal{L}_{MSE} = \frac{1}{T_i} \cdot \nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i} \mathcal{L}_{MSE}
$$

Substituting the proportionality yields:

$$
\nabla_{(k_\ell^m)_i}^{\text{scaled}} \mathcal{L}_{MSE} \propto \frac{1}{T_i} \cdot (Y_\ell^m \cdot T_i \alpha_i) = Y_\ell^m \cdot \alpha_i
$$

This results in gradients that are proportional only to the opacity $\alpha_i$ and the SH basis value, effectively balancing the gradient magnitude across samples regardless of their depth [S1].

**Sampling Density Based Gradient Scaling (Alternative/Complementary):**
The source also describes a related technique based on sampling density $\rho(\mathbf{p})$, which is inversely proportional to the square of the distance $\zeta_{\mathbf{p}}^i$ from the camera origin [S1]:

$$
\rho(\mathbf{p}) \approx \sum_{i=1}^n v_i(\mathbf{p}) \times \frac{1}{(\zeta_{\mathbf{p}}^i)^2}
$$

where $n$ is the number of cameras and $v_i(\mathbf{p})$ is a visibility function (1 if point $\mathbf{p}$ is in the $i$-th camera's field of view, 0 otherwise) [S1]. This scaling affects both volume density $\sigma$ and SH coefficients $k$, whereas transmittance scaling primarily affects SH coefficients [S1].

### Pseudocode
The following pseudocode implements the transmittance-based gradient scaling for the SH coefficients during the optimization step. It assumes the standard volume rendering pipeline has computed $T_i$ and $\alpha_i$.

```python
def apply_gradient_scaling(sh_coeff_gradients, transmittances, epsilon=1e-7):
    """
    Applies transmittance-based gradient scaling to SH coefficient gradients.
    
    Args:
        sh_coeff_gradients: List of gradient tensors for SH coefficients for each sample i.
                            Shape: [N, num_coeffs].
        transmittances: List of transmittance values T_i for each sample i.
                        Shape: [N].
        epsilon: Small constant to prevent division by zero for far samples [G].
        
    Returns:
        Scaled gradients for SH coefficients.
    """
    N = len(transmittances)
    scaled_gradients = []
    
    for i in range(N):
        T_i = transmittances[i]
        # Guard against division by zero if T_i is extremely close to 0 [G]
        scale_factor = 1.0 / max(T_i, epsilon)
        
        # Multiply the raw gradient by 1/T_i to counteract transmittance decay [S1]
        current_grad = sh_coeff_gradients[i]
        scaled_grad = current_grad * scale_factor
        
        scaled_gradients.append(scaled_grad)
        
    return scaled_gradients
```

### Parameters
*   **Scaling Factor**: $1/T_i$ [S1].
*   **Application Stage**: Early stage of training [S1].
*   **Target**: SH coefficients $(k_\ell^m)_i$ [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
1.  **Division by Zero**: If $T_i$ becomes extremely small (near 0) for samples far from the camera, $1/T_i$ can cause numerical instability or NaNs [G]. A small epsilon floor should be applied to $T_i$ before inversion [G].
2.  **Initial Density**: If the initial volume density $\sigma$ is very small, $T_i$ hardly attenuates (remains close to 1) [S1]. In this case, the scaling factor $1/T_i \approx 1$, and the gradient imbalance is not corrected, potentially leading to slow optimization or non-convergence [S1].
3.  **Sampling Density vs. Transmittance**: Sampling density based scaling affects both $\sigma$ and $k$, while transmittance-based scaling primarily affects $k$ (SH coefficients) because transmittance attenuation primarily influences SH coefficient gradients at the beginning [S1].
4.  **Uniform Sampling**: Near-camera overfitting persists even on datasets with uniform sampling density (e.g., LLFF) due to view transformation, contradicting the hypothesis that sampling imbalance alone causes the issue [S1]. Transmittance decay is the primary culprit [S1].

## Subtopics

### Early-stage transmittance gradient scaling
A gradient modification technique applied during the initial phase of training to prevent uniform initial densities from causing gradients to be dominated by transmittance rather than spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients [S1]. For each sample $i$ along a ray $\mathbf{r}$, the accumulated transmittance $T_i$ is computed and the gradient of the SH coefficients is multiplied by $1/T_i$ [S1]. Formally, $\nabla_{\text{SH}, i}^{\text{scaled}} = \nabla_{\text{SH}, i} \cdot \frac{1}{T_i}$, where $\nabla_{\text{SH}, i}$ is the raw gradient for the $i$-th sample's SH coefficients and $T_i$ is the transmittance up to that sample [S1]. The initial value at the first sample ($i=1$) is exactly $1$ [S1]. At initialization, all densities in the grid share common initial values, making the opacity of all samples along a ray approximately equal, which causes the gradient to be primarily driven by transmittance instead of the SH coefficients, steering optimization [S1]. The reciprocal transmittance scaling counteracts this by normalizing the gradient contribution per sample [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: Negligible extra computational cost, as transmittance $T_i$ is already computed during the forward pass of volume rendering and the scaling is a simple element-wise multiplication [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity**: Negligible extra memory, as transmittance is already computed during the forward pass [S1].
*   **Performance**: On the LLFF dataset (3-view), Plenoxels with Transmittance-based Gradient Scaling achieved PSNR 17.90, SSIM 0.585, LPIPS 0.393, compared to baseline Plenoxels PSNR 16.83, SSIM 0.521, LPIPS 0.438 [S1].
*   **Strengths**: Effectively reduces near-camera floaters, generalizable to other radiance field representations, and has low computational overhead [S1].
*   **Limitations**: Primarily effective in the early stages of training and requires careful handling of numerical stability for $T_i \approx 0$ [S1].

## Background
Gradient Scaling was proposed in the context of few-shot view synthesis, specifically in the F2Plenoxels method [S1]. Previous works attributed near-camera overfitting to sampling density imbalance [S1]. However, F2Plenoxels demonstrated that this issue persists even with uniform sampling density, identifying exponential transmittance decay as the root cause [S1]. The method was validated on synthetic (Blender) and real (LLFF, Mip-NeRF 360) datasets [S1].
