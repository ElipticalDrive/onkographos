# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: IPE (Interatomic Positional Encoding), Interatomic Positional Encoding, NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Training

## Overview

The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric error between rendered images and ground-truth training views by updating the parameters of a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) [S5, S6, S14, unofficial]. It solves the inverse rendering problem by treating the scene as a continuous volumetric function represented by a neural network, optimizing this representation via stochastic gradient descent [S5, S14, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from its siblings by serving as the foundational execution engine for coordinate-based volume rendering optimization, distinct from [MVSNeRF](6197-mvsnerf.md) which avoids per-scene optimization, and [Stage-based Differentiable Volume/Radiance Field Training](4241-stage-based-differentiable-volume-radiance-field-training.md) which introduces explicit phase separation [S11, S22, unofficial].

## How it works

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ training images with known camera poses (extrinsics $C2W$ and intrinsics $K$) [S5, S11, unofficial]. The NeRF model $f_\theta$, typically a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) mapping 3D coordinates $\mathbf{x}$ and viewing directions $\mathbf{d}$ to density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:** Optimized network weights $\theta$ and a trained radiance field capable of synthesizing novel views [S5, S11, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Rays:** A ray is defined by an origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$. Training rays are generated by sampling pixel coordinates $(u, v)$ from training images and back-projecting through the camera matrix [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   **Ray Batch (`rays_rgb`):** A pre-computed or on-the-fly array of shape $[B, 3, 3]$ (or flattened $[B, 9]$), where $B$ is the batch size (number of rays) [S5, S14, unofficial].
    *   Axis 0: Channel type (0: ray origin $\mathbf{o}$, 1: ray direction $\mathbf{d}$, 2: target RGB color $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$) [S5, S14, unofficial].
    *   Axis 1: Batch index (ray ID) [S5, S14, unofficial].
    *   Axis 2: Component (x, y, z for origin/direction; r, g, b for color) [S5, S14, unofficial].
    *   Data type: `float32` [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint:** A dictionary containing:
    *   `'model'` or `'coarse'`/`'fine'`: State dictionary of the neural network weights [S10, S11, unofficial].
    *   `'optimizer'`: State dictionary of the optimizer (e.g., Adam moments) [S10, S11, unofficial].
    *   `'step'` or `'epoch'`: Integer tracking training progress [S10, S11, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Load training and validation datasets [S8][S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the NeRF model $f_\theta$ (e.g., weights from a specific distribution, often small random values or SIREN initialization) [S15, S3, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with learning rate $\eta$ [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Pre-compute all possible rays from training images into a flat array `rays_rgb` of shape $[(N-1) \cdot H \cdot W, 3, 3]$ and shuffle it [S5, S14, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop:** For iteration $i$ from 1 to $N_{iters}$:
    *   **Ray Sampling:**
        *   If batching is enabled, slice the pre-computed `rays_rgb` array for indices $[i_{batch}, i_{batch} + N_{rand})$ [S5, S14, unofficial].
        *   Transpose the slice to shape $[3, N_{rand}, 3]$ (Channels, Batch, Components) [S5, S14, unofficial].
        *   Split into `batch_rays` (origins and directions) and `target_s` (ground truth RGB) [S5, S14, unofficial].
        *   Increment $i_{batch}$. If $i_{batch}$ exceeds the array length, shuffle `rays_rgb` and reset $i_{batch}$ to 0 [S5, S14, unofficial].
        *   *Alternative (No Batching):* Randomly select a training image, generate all rays, optionally apply a central crop (precrop) for the first $P$ iterations, and sample $N_{rand}$ unique pixel coordinates without replacement [S14, unofficial].
    *   **Forward Pass (Rendering):**
        *   Sample $K$ points along each ray in the batch using stratified sampling between near and far bounds [S5, S11, unofficial].
        *   Query the NeRF model $f_\theta$ at these points to obtain density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ [S5, S11, unofficial].
        *   Compute the rendered color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ using the standard emission-absorption model (Differentiable Volume Rendering) [S1][S5, S11, unofficial]:
            $$
            \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{k=1}^K T_k (1 - \exp(-\sigma_k \delta_k)) \mathbf{c}_k
            $$
            where $\delta_k$ is the distance between samples, and $T_k = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$ is the transmittance [S1][S5, S11, unofficial].
        *   If a coarse-fine training scheme is used, render a coarse image $\hat{C}_{coarse}$, then sample additional points based on the coarse weights (importance sampling) and render a fine image $\hat{C}_{fine}$ [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   **Loss Computation:**
        *   Compute the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the rendered color $\hat{C}$ and the target color $\mathbf{c}_{gt}$ [S5, S11, S14, unofficial]:
            $$
            \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{N_{rand}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{rand}} \| \hat{C}_j(\mathbf{r}_j) - \mathbf{c}_{gt,j} \|^2
            $$
        *   If coarse output $\hat{C}_{coarse}$ is available, add its MSE loss to the total loss [S6, S11, S18, unofficial]:
            $$
            \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb, fine} + \lambda_{coarse} \mathcal{L}_{rgb, coarse}
            $$
        *   *Note:* Other losses (eikonal, sparsity, TV) may be added depending on the specific variant [S7, unofficial][S8][S16, unofficial].
    *   **Backpropagation and Update:**
        *   Zero the gradients of the optimizer [S18, S17, unofficial].
        *   Compute gradients via backpropagation: $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{total}$ [S18, S17, unofficial].
        *   Update model parameters: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \cdot \text{Adam}(\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{total})$ [S18, S17, unofficial].
        *   Update the learning rate scheduler [S18, S11, unofficial].
    *   **Logging and Checkpointing:**
        *   Log loss and PSNR every $I_{print}$ steps [S2, S11, unofficial].
        *   Render validation images every $I_{img}$ steps [S2, S11, unofficial].
        *   Save checkpoint every $I_{weights}$ steps [S2, S11, unofficial].

**Parameters with Typical Values:**
*   $N_{rand}$ (Batch Size): 1024 or 2048 rays per iteration [S5, S11, S14, unofficial].
*   $N_{iters}$ (Total Iterations): 100,000 to 1,000,000 [S11, S14, unofficial].
*   Learning Rate ($\eta$): $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for Adam [S11, unofficial].
*   Positional Encoding Frequency ($L$): 10 for position, 4 for direction [S11, unofficial][S20].
*   Decay Rate: 0.1 [S6, S18, unofficial].
*   Decay Steps: $args.lrate\_decay \times 1000$ [S5, S18, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Rendering large batches can exceed GPU memory. Mitigated by chunking rays during the forward pass (`chunk=args.chunk`) [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Graph Freeing:** Higher-order derivatives (e.g., for gradient logging) must be computed *before* calling `loss.backward()`, as the backward pass frees the autograd graph [S3, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint Resumption:** When resuming, the learning rate scheduler's last epoch must be set to `start_epoch - 1` to ensure correct scheduling [S9, unofficial]. If a checkpoint is loaded but the step is 0, it is treated as an empty checkpoint and may cause errors [S15, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Growth:** In dynamic growth methods (e.g., Recursive-NeRF), randomly initializing sub-networks causes instability; grown sub-networks must inherit alpha linear weights from the parent [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader

def train_nerf_loop(model, train_dataset, val_dataset, config):
    """
    Main training loop for NeRF.
    
    Args:
        model: Neural Radiance Field model (MLP).
        train_dataset: Dataset containing images, poses, and intrinsics.
        val_dataset: Validation dataset.
        config: Configuration object with hyperparameters.
    """
    # 1. Initialization
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=config.lr)
    scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR(optimizer, gamma=config.decay_rate)
    
    # Pre-compute rays: shape [TotalPixels, 3, 3] -> [Origin, Direction, TargetRGB]
    rays_rgb = precompute_rays(train_dataset) 
    rays_rgb = rays_rgb.view(-1, 9) # Flatten to [TotalPixels, 9]
    total_pixels = rays_rgb.shape[0]
    
    n_iters = config.n_iters
    batch_size = config.batch_size # N_rand
    
    for i in range(n_iters):
        # 2. Ray Sampling
        if i + batch_size < total_pixels:
            indices = torch.arange(i, i + batch_size)
        else:
            # Shuffle and restart if exhausted
            indices = torch.randperm(total_pixels)[:batch_size]
            rays_rgb = rays_rgb[indices] # Re-shuffle logic handled by dataset or pre-computation
        
        # Extract batch components
        # rays_rgb[:, 0:3] = Origin, rays_rgb[:, 3:6] = Direction, rays_rgb[:, 6:9] = Target RGB
        batch_rays_o = rays_rgb[indices, 0:3]
        batch_rays_d = rays_rgb[indices, 3:6]
        target_rgb = rays_rgb[indices, 6:9]
        
        # 3. Forward Pass (Rendering)
        # Sample points along rays
        z_vals = sample_z_vals(batch_rays_o, batch_rays_d, config.near, config.far, config.n_samples)
        pts = batch_rays_o[:, None, :] + batch_rays_d[:, None, :] * z_vals[:, :, None]
        
        # Query NeRF
        # model returns (sigma, rgb) for each point
        sigma, rgb = model(pts, batch_rays_d)
        
        # Volume Rendering
        # weights = alpha * transmittance
        # alpha = 1 - exp(-sigma * delta)
        deltas = z_vals[:, 1:] - z_vals[:, :-1]
        deltas = torch.cat([deltas, torch.tensor([1e10], device=deltas.device)], dim=-1)
        
        alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * deltas)
        T = torch.cumprod(torch.exp(-sigma * deltas), dim=1)
        T = torch.cat([torch.ones_like(T[:, :1]), T[:, :-1]], dim=1)
        
        weights = alpha * T
        rendered_rgb = torch.sum(weights[..., None] * rgb, dim=1)
        
        # 4. Loss Computation
        loss = F.mse_loss(rendered_rgb, target_rgb)
        
        # 5. Backpropagation and Update
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        
        # 6. Logging
        if i % config.log_interval == 0:
            psnr = -10.0 * torch.log10(loss)
            print(f"Iter {i}: Loss {loss.item():.6f}, PSNR {psnr.item():.2f}")
            
        scheduler.step()
```

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S21]. For every pixel, multiple jittered rays are cast uniformly at random through the spatial footprint, rendered, and averaged [S21]. Relies on 'oracle' scale information not available for most datasets [S21]. The 16x supersampling baseline performs worse than mip-NeRF and is significantly more expensive [S21].

### Two-stage training for proposers
A training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S22, unofficial]. Phase 1 trains the proposer to mimic a heuristic proposer by minimizing L2 matching loss between proposal sets [S22, unofficial]. Phase 2 trains all components end-to-end, resetting the optimizer and performing a 1,000-step linear warmup [S22, unofficial]. Consistently effective where training from scratch fails [S22, unofficial].

### Multi-view SDS
A plug-and-play method creating multi-view supervision during SDS optimization to address the Janus problem [S23]. Renders images from 4 sampled views, stitches them into a 2x2 image, and calculates gradients under multi-view normal map conditions [S23]. Converts 3D assets into differentiable representations (NeRF, 3DGS, DMTet) via rendering supervision [S23].

### Guided NeRF Optimization for MVS
A multi-view depth estimation method utilizing conventional reconstruction (sparse SfM+MVS) and learning-based priors over NeRF [S24]. Runs COLMAP to acquire sparse depth maps, then adapts a monocular depth network via finetuning on these maps using scale-invariant loss [S24].

### Surface-aware density formulation
A method to extract high-quality surfaces from NeRF by modeling geometry as a distance/occupancy field and converting output to density using a PDF [S25]. Incorporates an eikonal term to enforce gradient norm of SDF equal to one [S25]. Density distribution: $\Phi_S(x) = \frac{se^{-sx}}{1+e^{-sx}}$, where $s$ is scaling and $x$ is SDF/occupancy [S25].

### Dynamically-informed iNeRF Estimator
A state estimation method using an iNeRF initialized with a dynamics prior, optimized only via photometric loss, acting as a recursive Bayesian filter [S26]. Process loss set to 0 [S26]. An iNeRF estimator initialized without a dynamics prior will very quickly diverge [S26].

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot K \cdot D)$, where $N_{iters}$ is total iterations, $N_{rand}$ is batch size, $K$ is samples per ray, and $D$ is network depth/width [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_{rand} \cdot K \cdot D)$ for the forward pass graph, plus optimizer state $O(D_{params})$ [S5, S14, unofficial]. VRAM usage is a primary constraint, mitigated by chunking rays [S5, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Training on 128x128 images typically takes 250 epochs or ~100,000 iterations [S4, S11, unofficial]. PSNR increases from ~10 dB to ~19 dB over 20 epochs in small-scale tests [S12].
*   **Scaling:** Performance scales with batch size, but larger batches hinder data loading and reduce throughput; 1024 rays is a common trade-off [S15, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** End-to-end differentiable, handles complex geometry and appearance, produces high-quality novel views [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Slow training convergence, high memory usage, prone to floaters and overfitting in sparse views [S1][S17, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When high-fidelity novel view synthesis is required and computational resources are sufficient [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications or when training data is extremely sparse without regularization [S1][S17, unofficial].

## Background

The NeRF Training Loop originates from the "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" paper by Mildenhall et al. (2020) [S5, S11, unofficial]. It was developed to address the limitations of traditional 3D reconstruction methods by using a coordinate-based MLP to implicitly represent a scene's volumetric density and view-dependent color [S5, S11, unofficial]. The method leverages differentiable volume rendering to backpropagate gradients from 2D image loss to 3D scene parameters [S5, S11, unofficial]. It has become a foundational technique in computer vision and graphics, inspiring numerous variants for dynamic scenes, large-scale environments, and efficient training [S1, S25].
