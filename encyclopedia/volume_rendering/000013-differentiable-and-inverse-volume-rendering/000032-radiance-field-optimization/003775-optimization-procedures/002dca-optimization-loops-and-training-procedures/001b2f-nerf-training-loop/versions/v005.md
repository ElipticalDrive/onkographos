# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: End-to-End NeRF Training, HashNeRF Training Loop, HoloINR Training Loop, NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training, Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Training

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric difference between rendered images and ground-truth training views by updating the parameters of a neural radiance field [S5, S6, S13, unofficial]. It solves the problem of learning a continuous 3D scene representation from 2D images by combining differentiable volume rendering with stochastic gradient descent [S5, S13, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from [NeRF Evaluation and Metric Calculation](3079-nerf-evaluation-and-metric-calculation.md) by computing gradients for backpropagation and from [NeRF Rendering (Alpha Compositing)](5124-nerf-rendering-alpha-compositing.md) by including adaptive sampling and occupancy grid pruning steps specific to the training loop [S1][S2, unofficial][S8].

## How it works
The training loop optimizes a coordinate MLP (or other field representation) to reconstruct images by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and observed RGB colors [S3, S13, unofficial]. The process is stochastic, sampling random rays per iteration rather than rendering full images, which reduces compute per step and enables efficient optimization [S5, S14, unofficial].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**: A dataset of $N$ training images with known camera poses (extrinsics $c2w$) and intrinsics ($K$). Each image has height $H$, width $W$, and focal length $f$.
*   **Outputs**: Optimized neural network weights (density $\sigma$ and color $c$ parameters), and optionally checkpoints of the model state.

### Data Structures
*   **Rays Bundle**: A tensor of shape $[B, 2, 3]$ or $[B, 6]$ containing ray origins $\mathbf{o}$ and directions $\mathbf{d}$ for a batch of $B$ rays [S7, S13, unofficial].
*   **Target Colors**: A tensor of shape $[B, 3]$ containing the ground-truth RGB values sampled from the training images at the corresponding pixel coordinates [S7, S13, unofficial].
*   **Pre-computed Rays Array**: In some implementations, all possible rays from all training images are pre-computed into a flat array of shape $[(N-1)HW, 3, 3]$ (origin, direction, target RGB) to allow fast random sampling without reloading images [S5, S13, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint**: A dictionary containing `model` (state dict), `optimizer` (state dict), and `epoch` or `step` counter [S10, S11, unofficial].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Initialization**:
    *   Load training and validation datasets [S8][S10, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the neural network model (e.g., MLP with positional encoding) [S10, S14, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with a learning rate $lr$ [S5, S10, S16, unofficial].
    *   Initialize a learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, unofficial][S8][S11, unofficial].
    *   If using a coarse-to-fine scheme, initialize both coarse and fine networks [S11, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop** (for $i = 1$ to $N_{iters}$):
    *   **Ray Sampling**:
        *   Randomly select $N_{rand}$ pixel coordinates from the training images [S7, S13, unofficial].
        *   Convert pixel coordinates to 3D ray origins and directions using camera intrinsics [S4, S13, unofficial].
        *   Sample ground-truth RGB colors at these coordinates using bilinear interpolation [S4, S10, unofficial].
        *   *Pitfall*: If using pre-computed rays, shuffle the array when the batch index exceeds the total number of rays to ensure random sampling order [S11, unofficial].
    *   **Forward Pass**:
        *   Pass the ray bundle through the differentiable volume rendering function (see [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md)) [S6, S13, unofficial].
        *   The renderer outputs predicted RGB colors $\hat{C}$, and optionally depth, accumulation, and raw network outputs (density $\sigma$, color $c$) [S6, S13, unofficial].
        *   *Pitfall*: If VRAM is limited, split the ray batch into chunks during the forward pass [S5, unofficial].
    *   **Loss Computation**:
        *   Compute the primary photometric loss: $\mathcal{L}_{MSE} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^{B} \| \hat{C}_j - C_j \|^2$ [S13, S16, unofficial].
        *   If a coarse network is used, compute the coarse loss $\mathcal{L}_{coarse}$ and add it to the total loss: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{fine} + \mathcal{L}_{coarse}$ [S11, S17, unofficial].
        *   Add regularization terms if applicable (e.g., Eikonal, Total Variation, Sparsity) [S7, unofficial][S8][S15, unofficial].
    *   **Backpropagation**:
        *   Zero the gradients of the optimizer [S17, S16, unofficial].
        *   Compute gradients: $\nabla \mathcal{L}$ via backpropagation [S17, S16, unofficial].
        *   *Pitfall*: If logging higher-order derivatives (gradient, Laplacian) for diagnostics, compute them *before* calling `loss.backward()` because the backward pass frees the autograd graph [S3, unofficial].
        *   Update weights: `optimizer.step()` [S17, S16, unofficial].
    *   **Scheduling**:
        *   Update the learning rate based on the current step or epoch using the scheduler [S8][S17, unofficial].
        *   Example Exponential Decay: $lr_{new} = lr_{init} \times \text{decay\_rate}^{(step / \text{decay\_steps})}$ [S17, unofficial].
    *   **Logging and Checkpointing**:
        *   Log loss and PSNR ($\text{PSNR} = -10 \log_{10}(\text{MSE})$) at regular intervals [S11, S13, unofficial].
        *   Render validation images at regular intervals [S11, unofficial].
        *   Save model checkpoints at regular intervals [S11, S14, unofficial].

### Formulas
*   **MSE Loss**:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{MSE} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^{B} (\hat{C}_j - C_j)^2 $$
    Where $\hat{C}_j$ is the predicted color and $C_j$ is the ground-truth color for ray $j$ [S13, S16, unofficial].

*   **PSNR**:
    $$ \text{PSNR} = -10 \log_{10}(\mathcal{L}_{MSE}) $$
    [S13, unofficial]

*   **Exponential Learning Rate Decay**:
    $$ lr_{step} = lr_{init} \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor step / S \rfloor} $$
    Where $\gamma$ is the decay rate (default 0.1) and $S$ is the decay step interval (e.g., $args.lrate\_decay \times 1000$) [S5, S17, unofficial].

### Parameters
*   **$N_{rand}$ (Batch Size)**: Number of rays sampled per iteration. Typical values: 1024 or 2048 [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   **$lr$ (Learning Rate)**: Initial learning rate for Adam. Typical value: $5 \times 10^{-4}$ [S11, unofficial].
*   **$N_{iters}$ (Iterations)**: Total number of optimization steps. Default: 100,000 [S11, unofficial].
*   **$chunk$**: Maximum number of rays processed per forward pass to limit VRAM [S5, unofficial].
*   **$decay\_rate$**: Factor for learning rate decay. Default: 0.1 [S6, S17, unofficial].
*   **$decay\_steps$**: Interval for decay. Calculated as $args.lrate\_decay \times 1000$ [S5, S17, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **VRAM Overflow**: Rendering full images or large batches can exceed GPU memory. Mitigation: Use chunking in the render function [S5, unofficial] or smaller batch sizes (e.g., $2^{10}$ instead of $2^{14}$) [S14, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Dynamic Growth**: When growing sub-networks, initialize their alpha linear weights to match the parent network to avoid density dropping to zero [S1].
*   **Empty Checkpoints**: If loading a checkpoint with step 0, treat it as invalid and log an error [S14, unofficial].
*   **Scheduler Resumption**: When resuming training, set the scheduler's last epoch to `start_epoch - 1` to ensure correct scheduling [S9, unofficial].
*   **Gradient Graph Destruction**: Higher-order derivative computations for logging must occur before `loss.backward()` [S3, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
def train_nerf(model, optimizer, scheduler, train_loader, num_iters, chunk_size=1024):
    """
    Main training loop for NeRF.
    
    Args:
        model: Neural Radiance Field model (MLP + renderer).
        optimizer: Optimizer (e.g., Adam).
        scheduler: Learning rate scheduler.
        train_loader: DataLoader yielding (rays, targets) batches.
        num_iters: Total number of training iterations.
        chunk_size: Number of rays to process per forward pass chunk.
    """
    model.train()
    mse_loss_fn = torch.nn.MSELoss()
    
    for step in range(1, num_iters + 1):
        # 1. Ray Sampling (handled by DataLoader in this simplified view)
        # In practice, rays are randomly sampled from training images.
        # batch_rays: [B, 2, 3] (origins, directions)
        # target_s: [B, 3] (ground truth RGB)
        batch_rays, target_s = next(train_loader)
        
        # Move to device
        batch_rays = batch_rays.to('cuda')
        target_s = target_s.to('cuda')
        
        # 2. Forward Pass
        # The model's render function handles ray marching and volume integration.
        # It may internally chunk rays if batch size > chunk_size.
        # output contains 'rgb' (predicted color), and optionally 'rgb0' (coarse)
        output = model.render(batch_rays, chunk=chunk_size)
        
        predicted_rgb = output['rgb']
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        # Primary photometric loss
        loss = mse_loss_fn(predicted_rgb, target_s)
        
        # Optional: Coarse-to-fine loss
        if 'rgb0' in output:
            loss_coarse = mse_loss_fn(output['rgb0'], target_s)
            loss = loss + loss_coarse
            
        # Optional: Regularization terms (e.g., Eikonal, TV)
        # loss += regularization_weight * compute_regularization(output, model)
        
        # 4. Backpropagation
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        
        # 5. Update Weights
        optimizer.step()
        
        # 6. Scheduling
        scheduler.step()
        
        # 7. Logging
        if step % 100 == 0:
            psnr = -10.0 * torch.log10(loss)
            print(f"Step {step}: Loss={loss.item():.6f}, PSNR={psnr.item():.2f}")
            
        # 8. Checkpointing (pseudo-code)
        # if step % 10000 == 0:
        #     save_checkpoint(model, optimizer, step)
```

## Subtopics

### [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md)
The differentiable forward pass that synthesizes RGB images from a neural radiance field for gradient-based optimization. It sets itself apart by computing gradients for backpropagation and including adaptive sampling specific to training.

### [Training Configuration](2332-training-configuration.md)
Defines the static and dynamic hyperparameters, optimizer settings, and initialization procedures. It differs by focusing on numerical values and schedules rather than procedural orchestration.

### [Dynamic Growth Parameters](2968-dynamic-growth-parameters.md)
An adaptive strategy that expands network capacity based on local scene complexity and prediction uncertainty, enabling coarse-to-fine representation without pre-defining the hierarchy.

### [Mip-NeRF 360](3306-mip-nerf-360.md)
A method for unbounded, 360-degree scenes using non-linear parameterization, online distillation, and distortion-based regularization to solve aliasing and floaters.

### [Mip-NeRF 360 Sampling and Training](3393-mip-nerf-360-sampling-and-training.md)
Extends Mip-NeRF to unbounded scenes using disparity-based parameterization, contraction, and a proposal network for efficient sampling.

### [Coarse-to-Fine NeRF](3406-coarse-to-fine-nerf.md)
A hierarchical sampling strategy that uses a coarse network to identify opaque regions and a fine network to densely sample them, improving high-frequency detail reconstruction.

### [Neural Volume Scene Setup](4168-neural-volume-scene-setup.md)
Constructs a Mitsuba scene graph binding neural network parameters to the integrator, enabling end-to-end gradient flow through the ray marching process.

### [Neural Transient Field (NeTF)](4692-neural-transient-field-netf.md)
A framework for NLOS imaging parametrizing data on spherical wavefronts using MCMC and two-stage training to handle sparse sampling.

### AdaNeRF
Trains a sampling network to predict all weights for points on a line in one go, reducing the number of sample points [S18, unofficial].

### MVSNeRF
Uses a feedforward model to learn data prior, which accelerates convergence [S18, unofficial].

### SinNeRF
Uses a Depth map to constrain unobserved views via Adversarial loss and Perceptual loss [S18, unofficial].

### ReconFusion
Uses a Diffusion Prior to constrain unobserved views [S18, unofficial].

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S19]. For every pixel, it casts multiple jittered rays sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel, renders each ray with NeRF, and uses the mean of those rendered values as the predicted color in the loss function [S19]. However, it relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets, and the 16x supersampling baseline performs worse than mip-NeRF while being significantly more expensive [S19].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples})$, where $N_{iters}$ is the number of iterations, $N_{rand}$ is the batch size (rays per step), and $N_{samples}$ is the number of points sampled per ray [S5, S13, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity**: $O(N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot \text{model\_size})$. VRAM usage is dominated by the activation memory during the forward pass of the ray batch [S5, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance**: Training on 128x128 images typically takes 250 epochs or ~100,000 iterations [S4, S11, unofficial]. With a batch size of 1024, this results in ~7,800 iterations per epoch for a dataset of ~200 images [S16, unofficial].
*   **Scaling**: Performance scales with batch size, but larger batches hinder data loading performance and prevent full GPU occupancy; smaller batches cap iterations per second [S14, unofficial]. A batch size of $2^{10}$ is often a trade-off [S14, unofficial].
*   **Strengths**: Stochastic sampling allows training on large datasets without loading all rays into memory simultaneously. Differentiable rendering enables end-to-end optimization.
*   **Limitations**: Convergence can be slow due to the non-convex nature of the optimization. Blurry results can occur if the voxel grid resolution is too coarse or advanced sampling is not used [S16, unofficial].
*   **When to Use**: When optimizing a continuous radiance field from a set of calibrated images.
*   **When Not to Use**: When real-time inference is required without pre-training, or when the scene is static and can be represented by explicit geometry (e.g., meshes) more efficiently.

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop originates from the "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" paper by Mildenhall et al. (2020) [S5, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of coordinate-based neural networks for implicit scene representation [S3, unofficial]. The loop was refined in subsequent works like HashNeRF [S7, unofficial], Mip-NeRF [S19], and Mip-NeRF 360 [S1] to address issues like aliasing, training efficiency, and unbounded scene handling. It is widely used in computer vision for novel view synthesis, 3D reconstruction, and augmented reality [S1][S11, unofficial].
