# NeRF Training Loop

Also called: IPE (Interatomic Positional Encoding), Interatomic Positional Encoding, NeRF Optimization Loop, NeRF Training Procedure, Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Training

## Overview
The NeRF Training Loop is the iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the photometric error between rendered images and ground-truth training views by updating the parameters of a coordinate-based neural network [S5, S6, S14, unofficial]. It differs from standard volume rendering by treating the scene representation as a differentiable function $f_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ optimized via stochastic gradient descent, rather than a static geometric mesh or voxel grid [S5, S14, unofficial]. Unlike [Training Ray Marching and Integration](2199-training-ray-marching-and-integration.md) which defines the forward pass, the training loop orchestrates data loading, ray sampling, loss computation, and weight updates [S8][S11, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ training images with known camera poses (extrinsics $C_i$ and intrinsics $K_i$) and pixel coordinates. A neural network model (typically an MLP) parameterized by $\theta$.
*   **Outputs:** Updated network weights $\theta$ that minimize the reconstruction loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Ray Bundle:** A tensor of shape $[B, 3, 3]$ or $[B, 6]$ containing batched ray origins, directions, and target RGB colors. In some implementations, rays are pre-computed into a flat array `rays_rgb` of shape $[(N-1) \cdot H \cdot W, 3, 3]$ where axis 1 is $[origin, direction, target\_rgb]$ and axis 2 is the XYZ/RGB components [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Optimizer State:** Maintained by the optimizer (e.g., Adam), storing exponential moving averages of gradients [S11, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint:** A dictionary containing `model` (state dict), `optimizer` (state dict), and `epoch` or `step` [S11, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Load training and validation datasets [S8][S11, unofficial].
    *   Initialize the NeRF model (MLP) with random weights. For progressive growth methods, initialize alpha linear weights of grown sub-networks to match the parent to avoid instability [S1].
    *   Initialize the optimizer (typically Adam) with a learning rate $lr$ (default $5 \times 10^{-4}$ or $0.01$) [S11, S17, unofficial].
    *   Initialize a learning rate scheduler (e.g., Exponential Decay or Cosine Annealing) [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   Precompute ray bundles if batching is enabled: stack ray origins, directions, and target RGBs from all training images, reshape to a flat batch, and shuffle [S5, S14, unofficial].

2.  **Iteration Loop (for $i = 1$ to $N_{iters}$):**
    *   **Ray Sampling:**
        *   If using batching, slice the pre-computed `rays_rgb` array for the current batch index [S14, unofficial].
        *   If not batching, randomly select $N_{rand}$ pixel coordinates from a random training image (or a central crop if $i < precrop\_iters$) [S14, unofficial].
        *   Convert pixel coordinates to ray origins and directions using camera intrinsics [S4, S14, unofficial].
        *   Gather target RGB colors at these coordinates [S4, S14, unofficial].
    *   **Forward Pass (Rendering):**
        *   Sample points along the rays. For standard NeRF, sample $N$ points uniformly or stratified between near and far bounds [S5, unofficial].
        *   Query the NeRF model at these points to obtain volume density $\sigma$ and color $\mathbf{c}$ [S5, unofficial].
        *   Compute the rendered color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ using the emission-absorption model (see Parent article) [S5, S6, unofficial].
        *   If using a coarse-fine two-stage model, render coarse predictions first, then sample additional points based on coarse weights for fine predictions [S5, S11, unofficial].
    *   **Loss Computation:**
        *   Compute the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted RGB $\hat{C}$ and target RGB $C_{gt}$:
            $$ \mathcal{L}_{rgb} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \| \hat{C}_b - C_{gt,b} \|^2 $$
            [S14, S17, unofficial].
        *   If coarse predictions are available, add coarse loss: $\mathcal{L}_{coarse} = \text{MSE}(\hat{C}_{coarse}, C_{gt})$ [S11, S18, unofficial].
        *   Add regularization terms if applicable (e.g., sparsity, Total Variation, Eikonal) [S7, unofficial][S8].
        *   Total Loss: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \lambda_{coarse}\mathcal{L}_{coarse} + \dots$ [S11, S18, unofficial].
    *   **Backpropagation:**
        *   Zero gradients: `optimizer.zero_grad()` [S18, unofficial].
        *   Compute gradients: `loss.backward()` [S18, unofficial].
        *   Update weights: `optimizer.step()` [S18, unofficial].
    *   **Learning Rate Update:**
        *   Update learning rate according to the scheduler. For Exponential Decay: $lr_{new} = lr_{old} \times \text{decay\_rate}^{(i / \text{decay\_steps})}$ [S18, unofficial].
    *   **Logging and Checkpointing:**
        *   Log loss and PSNR ($\text{PSNR} = -10 \log_{10}(\text{MSE})$) at intervals [S14, unofficial].
        *   Render validation images and save checkpoints at specified intervals [S11, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

def train_nerf_loop(model, train_dataset, optimizer, scheduler, num_iters, batch_size, render_fn, device):
    """
    Standard NeRF training loop.
    
    Args:
        model: Neural Radiance Field MLP.
        train_dataset: Dataset yielding (images, poses, intrinsics).
        optimizer: Optimizer instance.
        scheduler: Learning rate scheduler.
        num_iters: Total number of optimization steps.
        batch_size: Number of rays per batch (N_rand).
        render_fn: Function that takes rays and model, returns rendered RGB.
        device: 'cuda' or 'cpu'.
    """
    model.train()
    
    # Pre-compute rays if desired, or sample on the fly
    # For simplicity, we sample on the fly from the dataset
    
    for i in range(num_iters):
        # 1. Data Loading and Ray Sampling
        # Sample a batch of rays from training images
        # Assuming train_dataset yields batches of (rays_o, rays_d, target_rgb)
        # In practice, this often involves picking random pixels from random images
        rays_o, rays_d, target_rgb = sample_rays_from_dataset(train_dataset, batch_size)
        
        rays_o = rays_o.to(device)
        rays_d = rays_d.to(device)
        target_rgb = target_rgb.to(device)
        
        # 2. Forward Pass
        # Render the batch of rays
        # render_fn handles ray marching, model query, and alpha compositing
        pred_rgb = render_fn(model, rays_o, rays_d)
        
        # 3. Loss Computation
        # MSE Loss
        loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(pred_rgb, target_rgb)
        
        # Optional: Add coarse loss if two-stage training
        # if 'rgb_coarse' in extras:
        #     loss += nn.functional.mse_loss(extras['rgb_coarse'], target_rgb)
        
        # 4. Backpropagation
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        
        # 5. Learning Rate Update
        scheduler.step()
        
        # 6. Logging
        if i % 100 == 0:
            mse = loss.item()
            psnr = -10.0 * torch.log10(torch.tensor(mse))
            print(f"Iter {i}: Loss {mse:.6f}, PSNR {psnr:.2f}")
            
        # 7. Checkpointing (omitted for brevity)
```

**Parameters:**
*   $N_{rand}$ (Batch Size): Number of rays sampled per iteration. Typical values: 1024 or 2048 [S5, S11, unofficial].
*   $lr$ (Learning Rate): Default $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for Adam [S11, unofficial].
*   $N_{iters}$ (Iterations): Total training steps. Default 1,000,000 [S14, unofficial].
*   $N_{samples}$: Number of points sampled per ray. Default 128 [S17, unofficial].
*   $near, far$: Near and far clipping planes. For Blender scenes, near=2.0, far=6.0 [S13, unofficial]. For LLFF, computed from bounding depths [S2, unofficial].
*   $perturb$: Jitter factor for stratified sampling. 0.0 for no jitter, 1.0 for full jitter [S2, unofficial].
*   $multires$: Log2 of max frequency for positional encoding of 3D location. Default 10 [S2, unofficial].
*   $multires\_views$: Log2 of max frequency for positional encoding of viewing direction. Default 4 [S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **VRAM Overflow:** Rendering all pixels at once exceeds GPU memory. Mitigated by chunking rays or sampling a small batch $N_{rand}$ [S5, S14, unofficial].
*   **Instability in Growing Networks:** Directly growing a randomly initialized sub-network causes density to drop to zero. Initialize grown sub-network weights to match the parent [S1].
*   **Gradient Freeing:** Higher-order derivatives (e.g., for logging gradients) must be computed before `loss.backward()` as the autograd graph is freed [S3, unofficial].
*   **Checkpoint Resumption:** When resuming, the scheduler's last epoch must be set to `start_epoch - 1` to ensure correct scheduling [S9, unofficial].
*   **Empty Checkpoints:** If a checkpoint is loaded but the step is 0, it is considered empty and should trigger an error [S15, unofficial].

### Supersampling Baseline
A baseline method for evaluating NeRF on multiscale data by training on full-resolution images and using brute-force supersampling during evaluation, or training on multiscale data with supersampling during both training and evaluation [S21]. For every pixel, multiple jittered rays are sampled uniformly at random through the spatial footprint of the pixel, rendered with NeRF, and averaged to predict the color in the loss function [S21]. This method relies on 'oracle' scale information that does not exist for most datasets and performs worse than mip-NeRF while being significantly more expensive [S21].

### Two-stage training for proposers
A training schedule split into two equal-duration phases to stabilize learning of a trainable proposer network in NeRF-ID [S22, unofficial]. Phase 1 trains the learnt proposer to mimic the heuristic proposer by minimizing the L2 matching loss between their proposal sets. Phase 2 trains all network components end-to-end, resetting the optimizer state and performing a linear warmup for 1,000 SGD steps to reduce shock from switching regimes [S22, unofficial]. This two-stage approach is consistently effective where training from scratch often fails [S22, unofficial].

### Multi-view SDS
A plug-and-play method that creates multi-view supervision during SDS optimization to address the Janus problem caused by one-by-one view sampling [S23]. Rendered images from 4 sampled views are stitched into a large 2x2 image, and gradients are calculated under the condition of the multi-view normal map. This converts a 3D asset into differentiable representations (NeRF, 3DGS, DMTet) optimized through rendering supervision [S23].

### Guided NeRF Optimization for MVS
A multi-view depth estimation method that utilizes conventional reconstruction (sparse SfM+MVS) and learning-based priors over NeRFs [S24]. It performs test-time optimization directly over implicit volumes to eliminate the need for accurate pixel matching across views, addressing the shape-radiance ambiguity of NeRF in indoor scenes. Step 1 runs COLMAP to acquire per-view sparse depth maps. Step 2 adapts a monocular depth network to the target scene by finetuning it on the sparse depth maps using a scale-invariant loss [S24].

### Surface-aware density formulation
A method to extract high-quality surfaces from NeRF by modeling geometry as a distance function or occupancy field and converting its output to density using a probability density function [S25]. It incorporates an eikonal term to encourage geometric consistency, enforcing that the gradient norm of the learned SDF is equal to one. The density distribution is modeled as $\Phi_S(x) = \frac{se^{-sx}}{1+e^{-sx}}$, where $s$ is a scaling parameter and $x$ is the SDF or occupancy value [S25].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N_{iters} \cdot N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot D)$, where $D$ is the MLP depth/width. Training is iterative and can take hours to days for high-quality results [S14, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_{rand} \cdot N_{samples} \cdot D)$ for the forward pass, plus optimizer state storage. VRAM usage is managed by chunking rays [S5, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Standard NeRF training on Blender scenes (800x800) takes ~100,000 iterations [S11, unofficial]. PSNR typically increases from ~10 dB to ~18-20 dB over 20 epochs in simplified setups [S12].
*   **Strengths:** High-quality novel view synthesis, continuous representation, easy to integrate with differentiable renderers.
*   **Limitations:** Slow training and inference, requires many views for good results, prone to floaters and overfitting without regularization [S1][S17, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When high-fidelity novel views are required and computational resources are available.
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications or sparse view scenarios without specialized extensions [S1, S21].

## Background
The NeRF Training Loop was introduced in the seminal paper "NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis" by Mildenhall et al. (2020) [S5, S18, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of coordinate-based neural networks for implicit scene representation [S3, unofficial]. The method was initially implemented in TensorFlow [S5, unofficial] and later ported to PyTorch [S6, S11, unofficial]. It has become the standard baseline for inverse volume rendering and radiance field optimization [S14, unofficial].
