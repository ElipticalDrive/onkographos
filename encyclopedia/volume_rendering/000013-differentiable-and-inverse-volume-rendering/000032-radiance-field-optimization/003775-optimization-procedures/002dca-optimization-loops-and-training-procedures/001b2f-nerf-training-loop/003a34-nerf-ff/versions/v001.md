# NeRF-FF

Also called: Focus Frustums, NeRF-FF + iNGP, NeRF-FF + nerfacto, NeRF-FF + splatfacto, Neural Radiance Fields with Focus Frustums

## Overview
NeRF-FF is a plug-in strategy that mitigates the degradation of Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) reconstruction quality caused by out-of-focus (defocus blurred) regions in input images. It solves the problem of training NeRFs on partially blurred data by estimating binary image masks that identify in-focus regions, allowing the subsequent NeRF model to omit out-of-focus pixels during training. Unlike methods that use dynamic ray generation or blur kernels, NeRF-FF relies on static ray assumptions, making it compatible with runtime-optimized NeRF variants like Instant-NGP, nerfacto, and splatfacto.

## How it works
NeRF-FF operates as a preprocessing pipeline that generates per-image binary masks. These masks are derived from "Focus Frustums" (FFs), which are pyramidal volumes in scene space representing the visible, in-focus region for each camera view. The method consists of geometry estimation, in-focus indicator detection, initial frustum estimation, frustum refinement, and mask generation.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ partially defocus-blurred input images $\{I_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where $I_i \in [0, 255]^{3 \times W \times H}$ is the color image for view $i$ with width $W$ and height $H$. Camera intrinsics and extrinsics are required for ray casting.
*   **Outputs:** A set of binary masks $\{Mask_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where $Mask_i(x,y) \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether pixel $(x,y)$ in image $i$ is in-focus.

### Data Structures
1.  **Preliminary NeRF Model:** A trained NeRF instance (e.g., Instant-NGP) used to estimate scene geometry.
2.  **Depth Maps:** For each image $i$, a dense depth map $D_i \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times H}$, where $D_i(x,y)$ is the depth of the nearest surface along the ray for pixel $(x,y)$.
3.  **Indicator Pixels:** A set $P_i$ for each image $i$, containing coordinates $(x,y)$ identified as sharp edges.
4.  **Focus Frustum (FF):** Defined by a camera view frustum bounded by a near plane depth $n_i$ and a far plane depth $f_i$. The volume is the intersection of the view frustum and the slab $[n_i, f_i]$.
5.  **Refined Focus Frustum (RFF):** The updated frustum for image $i$, defined by near plane $\hat{n}_i$ and far plane $\hat{f}_i$.
6.  **Scene Points:** A set $S$ of randomly sampled 3D points representing the scene geometry.

### Parameters
*   $\tau$: Threshold for the discrete Laplace operator to distinguish in-focus edges. Typical value: 80 [S1].
*   $\epsilon$: Hyperparameter controlling the trade-off between scene coverage and reconstruction quality. It defines the minimum fraction of refined frustums that must encompass each sampled point in $S$. Range: $[0, 1.0]$. Typical value: 0.4 [S1]. $\epsilon=0$ omits refinement; $\epsilon=1$ implies no masking (training on all pixels).

### Steps

1.  **Preliminary Geometry Estimation:**
    Train a preliminary NeRF model (e.g., Instant-NGP) on the blurry input images $\{I_i\}$ for a fixed number of iterations (e.g., 2,000 iterations). Use this model to render per-view depth maps $D_i$. Note that while appearance is degraded, the estimated geometry is sufficient for mask generation [S1].

2.  **Identify In-Focus Indicators:**
    For each image $I_i$:
    a. Convert $I_i$ to grayscale.
    b. Apply the discrete Laplace operator $\square$ to the grayscale image.
    c. Identify indicator pixels $P_i$ where the Laplace value exceeds $\tau$:
       $$ P_i = \{ (x, y) \mid \square(I_i)(x, y) > \tau \} $$
       Sharp edges in in-focus regions yield high Laplace values, while defocus blur reduces them [S1].

3.  **Initial Focus Frustum Estimation:**
    For each image $i$:
    a. Sample the depth map $D_i$ at the coordinates in $P_i$ to obtain a set of depth values $\{d_k\}$.
    b. Perform statistical analysis on $\{d_k\}$ to estimate the focus depth and depth of field (DoF).
    c. Set initial near plane $n_i$ and far plane $f_i$ based on the statistical bounds (e.g., mean $\pm$ standard deviation or percentiles) of the sampled depths. This defines the initial Focus Frustum $FF_i$ [S1].

4.  **Focus Frustum Refinement:**
    Refine the near and far planes ($\hat{n}_i, \hat{f}_i$) to ensure robust scene coverage.
    a. Sample a set of 3D points $S$ from the scene geometry (e.g., via random ray sampling or voxel grid).
    b. Determine the refined planes $\hat{n}_i, \hat{f}_i$ for each image $i$ such that:
       i. Every point $p \in S$ is encompassed by at least a fraction $\epsilon$ of the refined frustums $\{RFF_i\}$. A point $p$ is encompassed by $RFF_i$ if $p$ lies within the view frustum of image $i$ and its depth $d(p)$ satisfies $\hat{n}_i \le d(p) \le \hat{f}_i$.
       ii. The accumulated change in depth $R$ is minimized. $R$ is calculated only for frustums whose view frustum contains $p$:
           $$ R = \sum_{i: p \in \text{ViewFrustum}_i} | \hat{n}_i - n_i | + | \hat{f}_i - f_i | $$
    c. This step expands the frustums to capture more geometry while minimizing the inclusion of out-of-focus regions [S1].

5.  **Mask Generation:**
    For each image $i$ and pixel $(x,y)$:
    a. Retrieve the depth $D_i(x,y)$ from the preliminary depth map.
    b. Generate the binary mask $Mask_i(x,y)$:
       $$ Mask_i(x,y) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } D_i(x,y) \in [\hat{n}_i, \hat{f}_i] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
    c. The mask identifies pixels whose depth falls within the refined in-focus volume [S1].

6.  **Final NeRF Training:**
    Train the final NeRF model (e.g., Instant-NGP, nerfacto, or splatfacto) using the input images $\{I_i\}$ and masks $\{Mask_i\}$. During training, only pixels where $Mask_i(x,y) = 1$ are used for loss computation. Pixels with $Mask_i(x,y) = 0$ are ignored [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
def nerff_pipeline(input_images, camera_intrinsics, camera_extrinsics, tau=80, epsilon=0.4):
    """
    input_images: List of images I_i, shape (H, W, 3), values [0, 255]
    camera_intrinsics: List of K_i matrices
    camera_extrinsics: List of W2c matrices
    tau: Laplace threshold
    epsilon: Minimum fraction of frustums covering each scene point
    """
    N = len(input_images)
    
    # Step 1: Preliminary Geometry Estimation
    # Train a preliminary NeRF (e.g., Instant-NGP) on blurry images
    preliminary_nerf = train_nerf(input_images, iterations=2000)
    
    # Render depth maps for all views
    depth_maps = []
    for i in range(N):
        # Render depth along rays defined by K_i and W2c_i
        D_i = render_depth(preliminary_nerf, camera_intrinsics[i], camera_extrinsics[i])
        depth_maps.append(D_i)
        
    # Step 2: Identify In-Focus Indicators
    indicator_pixels = []
    for i in range(N):
        I_i = input_images[i]
        # Convert to grayscale
        I_gray = rgb_to_grayscale(I_i)
        # Apply discrete Laplace operator
        laplace_map = apply_laplace_operator(I_gray)
        # Threshold to find sharp edges
        P_i = []
        for y in range(H):
            for x in range(W):
                if laplace_map[y, x] > tau:
                    P_i.append((x, y))
        indicator_pixels.append(P_i)
        
    # Step 3: Initial Focus Frustum Estimation
    initial_frustums = []
    for i in range(N):
        P_i = indicator_pixels[i]
        D_i = depth_maps[i]
        
        # Sample depths at indicator pixels
        sampled_depths = [D_i[y, x] for (x, y) in P_i]
        
        if not sampled_depths:
            # Fallback if no indicators found: use full depth range or scene bounds
            n_i = min_scene_depth
            f_i = max_scene_depth
        else:
            # Statistical analysis to estimate DoF
            # Example: Mean +/- 2*StdDev, or percentiles
            mean_d = mean(sampled_depths)
            std_d = std(sampled_depths)
            n_i = max(min_scene_depth, mean_d - 2 * std_d)
            f_i = min(max_scene_depth, mean_d + 2 * std_d)
            
        initial_frustums.append({'n': n_i, 'f': f_i})
        
    # Step 4: Focus Frustum Refinement
    # Sample scene points S
    S = sample_scene_points(preliminary_nerf, num_points=100000)
    
    refined_frustums = []
    for i in range(N):
        # Optimize n_hat, f_hat to satisfy epsilon constraint and minimize R
        # This is an optimization problem. A simple iterative expansion or 
        # convex optimization can be used.
        
        # Initialize with initial frustum
        n_hat = initial_frustums[i]['n']
        f_hat = initial_frustums[i]['f']
        
        # Iteratively expand until coverage condition is met
        # Check coverage: count how many frustums cover each point p in S
        # For each p, count i such that p is in view frustum i AND n_hat_i <= depth(p) <= f_hat_i
        
        while not check_coverage_constraint(S, refined_frustums, epsilon):
            # Expand frustums
            # Strategy: Expand n_hat downwards and f_hat upwards
            # Or solve for minimal R subject to constraint
            n_hat = n_hat - expansion_step
            f_hat = f_hat + expansion_step
            
        refined_frustums.append({'n': n_hat, 'f': f_hat})
        
    # Step 5: Mask Generation
    masks = []
    for i in range(N):
        D_i = depth_maps[i]
        n_hat = refined_frustums[i]['n']
        f_hat = refined_frustums[i]['f']
        
        Mask_i = np.zeros((H, W), dtype=bool)
        for y in range(H):
            for x in range(W):
                d = D_i[y, x]
                if n_hat <= d <= f_hat:
                    Mask_i[y, x] = True
        masks.append(Mask_i)
        
    # Step 6: Final NeRF Training
    # Train final NeRF using masks to ignore out-of-focus pixels
    final_nerf = train_nerf_masked(input_images, masks, iterations=4000)
    
    return final_nerf, masks

def check_coverage_constraint(S, frustums, epsilon):
    """
    Check if every point in S is covered by at least epsilon fraction of frustums.
    """
    N = len(frustums)
    min_coverage = 0.0
    for p in S:
        coverage_count = 0
        for i, ff in enumerate(frustums):
            if is_point_in_frustum(p, camera_intrinsics[i], camera_extrinsics[i], ff['n'], ff['f']):
                coverage_count += 1
        coverage = coverage_count / N
        if coverage < min_coverage:
            min_coverage = coverage
    return min_coverage >= epsilon

def is_point_in_frustum(p, K, W2c, n, f):
    """
    Check if 3D point p is within the view frustum and depth slab [n, f].
    """
    # Project p to camera space
    p_cam = W2c @ p_homogeneous(p)
    if p_cam[2] <= 0:
        return False # Behind camera
    
    # Project to image coordinates
    uv = K @ p_cam
    u, v = uv[0]/uv[2], uv[1]/uv[2]
    
    # Check if within image bounds
    if not (0 <= u < W and 0 <= v < H):
        return False
        
    # Check depth
    depth = p_cam[2]
    return n <= depth <= f
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Low $\tau$:** Setting $\tau$ too low causes weak signals (texture in out-of-focus regions) to be identified as in-focus indicators. This leads to erroneous initial frustums and poor performance [S1].
*   **High $\tau$:** Setting $\tau$ too high excludes sharp edges in in-focus regions. Performance declines slowly because the refinement step can compensate for smaller initial frustums [S1].
*   **Fully Blurred Images:** NeRF-FF is not suited for datasets containing fully blurred images. The preliminary NeRF reconstruction suffers from "floater artifacts" (volumetric density with no geometric counterpart), leading to inferior depth maps and erroneous masks [S1].
*   **Floater Artifacts:** Even with partial blur, Instant-NGP may produce floater artifacts. These artifacts result in aberrated depth maps, which impurify the generated masks and persist in the final reconstruction, reducing image quality depending on the camera position [S1].
*   **Synthetic Datasets:** On synthetic datasets with fully blurred images, NeRF-FF underperforms state-of-the-art methods due to low-quality dense reconstruction in the preliminary stage [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method adds a preliminary NeRF training step and a refinement step. However, the final NeRF training is accelerated because it uses optimized variants (Instant-NGP, nerfacto, splatfacto) and static rays.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing depth maps for all views and the preliminary NeRF model. The refinement step requires sampling scene points $S$.
*   **Performance:** On the Ma et al. real-world defocus dataset, NeRF-FF + Instant-NGP reduces training time to under 1 minute (average 45 seconds) on an NVIDIA RTX 3090. This is a two orders of magnitude acceleration compared to state-of-the-art methods like PDRF, DP-NeRF, and Deblur-NeRF [S1].
*   **Quality:** NeRF-FF + iNGP achieves PSNR on par with SOTA methods and consistently outperforms them in SSIM. It trails reference deblurring strategies slightly in LPIPS, but the difference is considered negligible given the speedup [S1].
*   **Compatibility:** Compatible with Instant-NGP, nerfacto, and splatfacto. Applying NeRF-FF masks to nerfacto and splatfacto improves PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS with negligible impact on training duration [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Not suitable for fully blurred images. Quality depends heavily on the accuracy of the preliminary depth maps.

## Background
NeRF-FF was proposed by T. Wirth et al. in 2024 [S1]. It addresses the limitation of existing NeRF methods for defocus blur, which often rely on dynamic ray generation and are incompatible with static-ray-optimized NeRFs like Instant-NGP. The method leverages the discrete Laplace operator to detect in-focus regions and estimates Focus Frustums to mask out-of-focus pixels during training. It is designed to be a plug-in strategy compatible with various NeRF variants, including those in Nerfstudio [S1].
