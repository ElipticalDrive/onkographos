# Drone-NeRF


## Overview
Drone-NeRF is a method for large-scale 3D scene reconstruction from drone-captured imagery that addresses the specific challenges of overhead drone views, including exposure variations, shadow artifacts from vertical surfaces, and computational efficiency in large scenes [S1]. It differs from standard NeRF approaches by employing a block-parallel training strategy where sub-scenes are trained independently and then merged, while explicitly handling exposure discrepancies via appearance embeddings and removing occlusion shadows through an expanded bounding box projection technique [S1]. Unlike methods that treat the entire scene as a single volume, Drone-NeRF partitions the space into sub-bounding boxes, optimizing them separately before a final merging phase that ensures color uniformity and depth continuity across block boundaries [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of drone-captured images with known camera poses (initially estimated), intrinsics, and extrinsics. The scene is partitioned into a grid of sub-bounding boxes.
*   **Outputs:** A merged radiance field model capable of rendering high-quality RGB images and depth maps for any viewpoint within the reconstructed volume, specifically optimized for overhead drone perspectives [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Sub-Bounding Boxes:** The scene is divided into a grid of 3D bounding boxes. Each box contains an independent NeRF model.
*   **Expanded Sub-Bounding Box:** A larger bounding box used during the merging phase to capture projection data from vertical faces to mitigate shadow artifacts.
*   **NeRF Model:** A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that takes position, direction, and appearance embeddings as input and outputs color and density [S1].

**Model Architecture:**
The core MLP consists of:
1.  Input: Concatenation of position encoding, direction encoding, and appearance embedding.
2.  Hidden Layers: 8 fully connected layers with ReLU activation, each having 256 hidden units.
3.  Output Layers: 2 fully connected layers with 128 channels. The final layer outputs the RGB color (3 channels) and density (1 channel) [S1].

**Position and Direction Encoding:**
*   Position Encoding: Highest frequency set to 64.
*   Direction Encoding: Highest frequency set to 4.
*   Appearance Embedding: Used to resolve discrepancies in images arising due to variations in camera exposure [S1].

**Training Procedure (Per Sub-Block):**
1.  **Initialization:** Each sub-block is initialized independently. In the initial stage, hash coding is used in conjunction with a small fusion MLP for rapid query scenarios to accelerate training [S1].
2.  **Ray Sampling:** Hierarchical sampling is employed. For each ray, 128 coarse samples and 256 fine samples are generated. A batch size of 1024 rays is processed per iteration [S1].
3.  **Forward Pass:**
    *   Sample points along the ray.
    *   Encode positions and directions.
    *   Concatenate with appearance embedding.
    *   Pass through the MLP to get density $\sigma$ and color $C$.
    *   Compute volume rendering integral for color $C_r$ and depth $D_r$ [S1].
4.  **Loss Calculation:** Compare rendered color $C_r$ with ground truth image color $C_{gt}$. Compute loss (typically MSE or LPIPS/SSIM based, though specific loss function formula is not detailed, metrics are reported) [S1].
5.  **Optimization:**
    *   Update MLP weights using Adam Optimizer with initial learning rate 0.005 [S1].
    *   Optimize camera poses by back-propagating the loss gradient into the input pose computation. The learning rate for camera optimization is set to 0.0005 [S1].
6.  **Iteration:** Repeat for 5,000 or 30,000 iterations per block [S1].

**Merging Procedure:**
After training individual sub-blocks, the scene is merged to create a coherent overhead view.
1.  **Determine Essential Blocks:** Calculate intersections between the rays of the desired rendering area and the sub-bounding boxes. Only essential sub-bounding boxes are used for rendering to reduce computation [S1].
2.  **Shadow Mitigation:**
    *   Shadows are caused by occlusions from the vertical faces of the sub-bounding boxes.
    *   Create an **expanded sub-bounding box** larger than the original sub-bounding box.
    *   Capture RGB values corresponding to the projection areas generated by vertical surface ray irradiation from the smaller bounding box using the enlarged sub-bounding box.
    *   Replace shadow occlusion regions in the original render with these captured RGB values to eliminate shadow influence [S1].
3.  **Color Uniformization:**
    *   The expanded sub-bounding boxes overlap with adjacent blocks.
    *   In overlapping areas, the scene color is uniformized (averaged or blended) to ensure consistency across block boundaries [S1].
4.  **Depth Merging:**
    *   Use depths falling within the scope of the previous larger (expanded) sub-bounding box for merging.
    *   In overlapping regions, average the depths pertaining to each sub-bounding box.
    *   Compare depth value averages across the same overlapping regions to adjust the overall depth of each sub-bounding box, ensuring smoother continuity and coherence [S1].

```python
def train_drone_nerf_subblock(sub_block, images, poses, intrinsics, iterations=5000):
    """
    Trains a single sub-block of the Drone-NeRF model.
    """
    # Initialize MLP: 8 layers of 256 units (ReLU), followed by 2 layers of 128 units
    mlp = MLP(hidden_dims=[256]*8 + [128]*2, 
              pos_freq=64, 
              dir_freq=4, 
              use_appearance_embedding=True)
    
    # Initialize camera poses for optimization
    optimized_poses = copy(poses)
    
    # Optimizers
    mlp_optimizer = Adam(mlp.parameters(), lr=0.005)
    pose_optimizer = Adam(optimized_poses.parameters(), lr=0.0005)
    
    for iteration in range(iterations):
        # Sample rays
        rays = sample_rays(images, optimized_poses, intrinsics, batch_size=1024)
        
        # Hierarchical sampling: 128 coarse, 256 fine
        coarse_samples = hierarchical_sampling(rays, mlp, num_samples=128)
        fine_samples = hierarchical_sampling(rays, mlp, coarse_samples, num_samples=256)
        
        # Forward pass
        # Get position, direction, and appearance embeddings
        pos_enc = encode_position(fine_samples.positions, freq=64)
        dir_enc = encode_direction(fine_samples.directions, freq=4)
        app_emb = get_appearance_embedding(rays.image_indices) # Assuming per-ray appearance
        
        inputs = concatenate(pos_enc, dir_enc, app_emb)
        
        # MLP predicts density and color
        density, color = mlp(inputs)
        
        # Volume rendering
        rendered_color = volume_render_color(rays, density, color)
        rendered_depth = volume_render_depth(rays, density, fine_samples.distances)
        
        # Compute Loss
        # Note: Specific loss function not detailed, but metrics PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS are used for evaluation
        loss = compute_loss(rendered_color, rays.gt_color)
        
        # Backpropagation
        mlp_optimizer.zero_grad()
        pose_optimizer.zero_grad()
        
        loss.backward()
        
        # Update MLP
        mlp_optimizer.step()
        
        # Optimize Camera Poses
        # Back-propagate loss gradient into input pose computation
        # This requires differentiable rendering with respect to poses
        pose_loss = compute_pose_loss(rendered_color, rays.gt_color, optimized_poses)
        pose_loss.backward()
        pose_optimizer.step()
        
        # Update optimized poses
        optimized_poses = update_poses(optimized_poses, pose_optimizer)

    return mlp, optimized_poses

def merge_subblocks(sub_blocks, expanded_sub_blocks, rendering_area):
    """
    Merges trained sub-blocks into a coherent scene, handling shadows and depth continuity.
    """
    # Determine essential sub-bounding boxes based on ray intersections
    essential_blocks = []
    for block in sub_blocks:
        if intersects(rendering_area.rays, block.bounding_box):
            essential_blocks.append(block)
            
    merged_color_map = {}
    merged_depth_map = {}
    
    for block in essential_blocks:
        # Render using the trained model
        color, depth = block.render(rendering_area.rays)
        
        # Shadow Mitigation
        # Use expanded sub-bounding box to capture projection areas from vertical faces
        expanded_block = get_expanded_block(block)
        shadow_rgb = expanded_block.render_projection_areas(block.bounding_box)
        
        # Replace shadow occlusion regions with captured RGB values
        color = replace_shadows(color, shadow_rgb)
        
        # Store for merging
        merged_color_map[block.id] = color
        merged_depth_map[block.id] = depth
        
    # Color Uniformization in Overlapping Areas
    # Average colors in overlapping regions of expanded sub-bounding boxes
    final_color = uniformize_colors(merged_color_map)
    
    # Depth Merging
    # Average depths in overlapping regions
    final_depth = average_overlapping_depths(merged_depth_map)
    
    # Adjust overall depth for smoother continuity
    final_depth = adjust_depth_continuity(final_depth)
    
    return final_color, final_depth
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training is performed in parallel for each sub-block, reducing total wall-clock time compared to training a single monolithic model. The number of iterations is limited (5,000 or 30,000) to prevent over-fitting, as the model fits the scene quickly [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** VRAM usage is constrained by the sub-block size. The method was evaluated on an Nvidia 4070ti GPU with 12GB VRAM, indicating it fits within moderate consumer hardware limits per block [S1].
*   **Performance:** On the Building Scene (Mill 19 dataset), Drone-NeRF achieved an average PSNR of 17.401 (5k iterations) and outperformed MipNeRF (16.637) and Instant-NGP (17.179) in PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS metrics [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles exposure variations via appearance embeddings; removes shadow artifacts from vertical surfaces; efficient rendering by selecting only essential sub-blocks; suitable for large-scale drone surveys.
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful partitioning of the scene into sub-bounding boxes; merging process adds complexity; performance is sensitive to the number of iterations (over-fitting occurs if too high) [S1].
*   **When to use:** Large-scale outdoor scenes captured by drones, particularly where overhead views are required and shadow artifacts from vertical structures are problematic.
*   **When not to use:** Small-scale indoor scenes or scenes without significant vertical occlusion shadows where standard NeRF variants may suffice.

## Background
Drone-NeRF was introduced in the paper "Drone-NeRF: Efficient NeRF based 3D scene reconstruction for large-scale drone survey" [S1]. It was developed to address the limitations of existing NeRF methods when applied to large-scale aerial imagery, specifically dealing with the unique geometric and lighting conditions of drone-captured data. The method builds upon the principles of NeRF but introduces block-based parallel training and a specialized merging pipeline to handle scale and artifacts [S1].
