# NeRF-FF

Also called: Focus Frustums, NeRF-FF + iNGP, NeRF-FF + nerfacto, NeRF-FF + splatfacto, Neural Radiance Fields with Focus Frustums

## Overview
NeRF-FF is a plug-in strategy that mitigates the degradation of Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) reconstruction quality caused by out-of-focus (defocus blurred) regions in input images [S1]. It estimates binary image masks based on "Focus Frustums"—the visible volume in scene space that is in-focus—and uses these masks to omit out-of-focus pixels during the training of a subsequent NeRF model [S1]. Unlike methods that employ dynamic ray generation or explicit blur kernels, NeRF-FF relies on static ray assumptions, making it compatible with high-performance, runtime-optimized NeRF variants such as Instant-NGP, nerfacto, and splatfacto [S1].

## How it works
NeRF-FF operates as a preprocessing pipeline that generates per-image binary masks. These masks are applied during the training of a target NeRF model (e.g., Instant-NGP) to restrict optimization to in-focus regions [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Set of $M$ defocus-blurred input images $\{I_i\}_{i=1}^M$, where $I_i \in [0, 255]^{3 \times W \times H}$ represents the color information for image $i$ with width $W$ and height $H$ [S1].
    *   Hyperparameters:
        *   $\tau$: Threshold for the discrete Laplace operator (Typical value: 80) [S1].
        *   $\epsilon$: Fraction of refined frustums each sampled point must be encompassed by (Typical value: 0.4) [S1].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   A set of binary masks $\{Mask_i\}_{i=1}^M$, where $Mask_i(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether pixel $(x, y)$ in image $i$ is in-focus [S1].

### Data Structures
1.  **Preliminary NeRF Model:** An Instant-NGP instance trained on the raw blurred images to estimate scene geometry [S1].
2.  **Depth Maps:** For each image $i$, a dense depth map $D_i \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times H}$, where $D_i(x, y)$ is the depth value at pixel $(x, y)$ derived from the preliminary NeRF inference [S1].
3.  **Indicator Pixels:** A set $P_i$ for each image $i$, containing coordinates of pixels identified as in-focus [S1].
4.  **Focus Frustums (FF):** For each image $i$, a volume defined by the camera view frustum clipped by a near plane depth $n_i$ and a far plane depth $f_i$ [S1].
5.  **Refined Focus Frustums (RFF):** Updated volumes defined by near plane $\hat{n}_i$ and far plane $\hat{f}_i$ [S1].
6.  **Scene Points:** A set $S$ of high-density points sampled from the scene geometry [S1].

### Algorithm Steps

1.  **Preliminary Geometry Estimation:**
    Train a preliminary Instant-NGP model on the input images $\{I_i\}$ to obtain per-view depth maps $\{D_i\}$ [S1]. Note: The appearance may suffer from blur, but the estimated geometry is adequate for depth estimation [S1].

2.  **Identify In-Focus Indicator Pixels:**
    For each image $I_i$:
    a. Convert $I_i$ to grayscale.
    b. Apply the discrete Laplace operator $\square$ to the grayscale image.
    c. Identify indicator pixels $P_i$ where the Laplace value exceeds threshold $\tau$:
    $$
    P_i = \{(x, y) \mid \square(I_i)(x, y) > \tau\}
    $$
    where $\square(I_i)(x, y)$ is the Laplace value at pixel $(x, y)$ [S1].

3.  **Initial Focus Frustum Estimation:**
    For each image $i$:
    a. Sample the depth map $D_i$ at the coordinates in $P_i$ to obtain a set of depth values.
    b. Perform statistical analysis on these depth values to estimate the focus depth and range (Depth of Field, DoF) [S1].
    c. Define the initial Focus Frustum $FF_i$ with near plane $n_i$ and far plane $f_i$ based on the estimated DoF [S1].

4.  **Focus Frustum Refinement:**
    Refine the near and far planes of each Focus Frustum to $\hat{n}_i$ and $\hat{f}_i$ to ensure scene coverage while minimizing volume expansion [S1].
    a. Sample a set of high-density scene points $S$ from the preliminary NeRF geometry [S1].
    b. Determine updated planes $\hat{n}_i, \hat{f}_i$ such that:
        *   Any spatial position $p \in S$ is part of at least a fraction $\epsilon$ of the refined Focus Frustums $\{RFF_i\}$ [S1].
        *   The accumulated change in depth $R$ is minimized [S1].
    c. The accumulated change $R$ is calculated only for Focus Frustums whose view frustum encompasses $p$ [S1].
    d. The hyperparameter $\epsilon \in [0, 1.0]$ controls this trade-off. $\epsilon = 0$ omits refinement; $\epsilon = 1$ implies no NeRF-FF masking [S1].

5.  **Mask Generation:**
    For each image $i$ and pixel $(x, y)$:
    a. Retrieve the depth $D_i(x, y)$ from the preliminary depth map.
    b. Generate the binary mask $Mask_i(x, y)$:
    $$
    Mask_i(x, y) = \begin{cases} 
    1, & \text{if } D_i(x, y) \in [\hat{n}_i, \hat{f}_i] \\
    0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
    $$
    Note: Other boundaries of the Focus Frustum (horizontal/vertical) are not considered because all pixels in the depth map are visible in their corresponding image [S1].

6.  **Subsequent NeRF Training:**
    Train the final NeRF model (e.g., Instant-NGP) using the input images $\{I_i\}$, but only consider pixels where $Mask_i(x, y) = 1$ [S1].

        depth_maps.append(d_map)
        
    # Step 2: Identify In-Focus Indicator Pixels
    indicator_pixels = []
    for i, img in enumerate(input_images):
        # Convert to grayscale
        gray_img = to_grayscale(img)
        # Apply discrete Laplace operator
        laplace_img = apply_laplace(gray_img)
        # Threshold to find sharp edges

        for y in range(H):

        indicator_pixels.append(pi)
        
    # Step 3: Initial Focus Frustum Estimation
    initial_ffs = []
    for i, pi in enumerate(indicator_pixels):
        # Sample depths at indicator pixels
        depths = [depth_maps[i][y, x] for (x, y) in pi]
        # Statistical analysis to estimate DoF (min/max or mean/std)
        # This provides initial near n_i and far f_i
        n_i, f_i = estimate_dof(depths)
        initial_ffs.append({'n': n_i, 'f': f_i})
        
    # Step 4: Focus Frustum Refinement
    # Sample high-density scene points S from preliminary geometry
    scene_points = sample_scene_points(preliminary_nerf)
    
    refined_ffs = []
    for i, initial_ff in enumerate(initial_ffs):
        # Determine optimal n_hat and f_hat
        # Constraints:
        # 1. Each point p in scene_points is covered by >= epsilon fraction of all refined FFs

        n_hat, f_hat = refine_planes(initial_ff['n'], initial_ff['f'],

    masks = []
    for i, ref_ff in enumerate(refined_ffs):

        masks.append(mask)

    # Train final NeRF using only masked pixels

### Parameters and Edge Cases
*   **$\tau$ (Laplacian Threshold):**
    *   Typical value: 80 [S1].
    *   Low $\tau$: Includes weak signals/textures in out-of-focus regions as in-focus indicators, leading to bad performance [S1].
    *   High $\tau$: Excludes sharp edges in in-focus regions, causing slowly decreasing performance. The decline is small because initial frustums are refined [S1].
*   **$\epsilon$ (Coverage Fraction):**
    *   Typical value: 0.4 maximizes PSNR and SSIM [S1].
    *   $\epsilon = 0$: Omits refinement step.
    *   $\epsilon = 1$: Equivalent to training without NeRF-FF [S1].
*   **Pitfalls:**
    *   **Fully Blurred Images:** NeRF-FF underperforms on datasets with fully blurred images because the preliminary NeRF produces low-quality dense reconstructions and floater artifacts (high optical density with no ground truth counterpart), leading to erroneous depth maps and masks [S1].
    *   **Floater Artifacts:** Even with partial blur, Instant-NGP may produce floaters, which result in aberrated depth maps and impure masks, reducing image quality depending on camera position [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is reduced to under 1 minute on end-consumer hardware (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 3090) for the combined NeRF-FF execution and Instant-NGP training [S1]. This constitutes an acceleration of two orders of magnitude compared to state-of-the-art methods like PDRF, DP-NeRF, or Deblur-NeRF, which may take up to nearly one day [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Performance:**
    *   On real-world defocus datasets (Ma et al.), NeRF-FF + Instant-NGP achieves PSNR on par with SOTA methods and consistently outperforms them in SSIM by a fair margin, though it trails reference deblurring strategies slightly in LPIPS [S1].
    *   On synthetic datasets with fully blurred images, it underperforms SOTA due to inferior depth map quality from the preliminary reconstruction [S1].
    *   On the Wu et al. dataset, it produces better quality images than DoF-NeRF [S1].
*   **Compatibility:** Compatible with Instant-NGP, nerfacto, and splatfacto (3D Gaussian Splatting) [S1]. Applying NeRF-FF masks to nerfacto and splatfacto improves PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS with negligible impact on training duration [S1].
*   **Strengths:** High speed, compatibility with static-ray optimized NeRFs, robustness to partial blur.
*   **Limitations:** Unsuitable for fully blurred images; sensitive to preliminary NeRF geometry quality; potential for floater artifacts in subsequent reconstruction [S1].

## Background
NeRF-FF was proposed by T. Wirth et al. in the paper "NeRF-FF: a plug-in method to mitigate defocus blur for runtime optimized neural radiance fields" [S1]. It addresses the limitation that standard NeRF reconstruction quality suffers significantly from out-of-focus regions in input images [S1]. Existing methods often use dynamic ray generation or blur kernels, which are incompatible with the static ray assumptions of performance-optimized NeRFs like Instant-NGP [S1]. NeRF-FF fills this gap by providing a static-mask-based approach [S1].
