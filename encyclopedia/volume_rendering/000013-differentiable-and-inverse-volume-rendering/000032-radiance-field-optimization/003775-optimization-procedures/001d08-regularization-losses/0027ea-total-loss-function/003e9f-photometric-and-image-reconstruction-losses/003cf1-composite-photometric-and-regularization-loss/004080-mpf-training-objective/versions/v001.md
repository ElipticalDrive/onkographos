# MPF Training Objective

Also called: Training Objective

## Overview
The MPF Training Objective is a composite loss function designed to optimize the parameters of a Metric-Phase Field (MPF) network for multi-view image reconstruction. It combines standard photometric supervision with geometric regularizers that exploit rendering weights as soft indicators for near-surface and free-space regions, addressing the lack of ground-truth 3D geometry in this setting [S1, unofficial]. Unlike the original MPF formulation which relies on explicit point cloud supervision, this method adapts the metric-phase decomposition constraints to be implicit and differentiable through the rendering process [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs:** A set of camera rays $\mathcal{R}$ sampled from training images; ground truth colors $C^*(\mathbf{r})$; optional foreground masks $A^*(\mathbf{r})$; the MPF network parameters (defining composite field $\varphi$, phase field $\theta$, and metric field $r$); the sharpness parameter $s$; and the phase scale $\beta$.
*   **Outputs:** A scalar total loss value $\mathcal{L}$ used for gradient-based optimization.

**Data Structures**
*   **Rays:** Each ray $\mathbf{r}$ consists of an origin and direction.
*   **Samples:** Along each ray, $n$ samples are taken at positions $\mathbf{r}(t_i)$.
*   **Fields:** The network outputs scalar values for $\varphi(\mathbf{x})$, $\theta(\mathbf{x})$, and $r(\mathbf{x})$ at any 3D position $\mathbf{x}$.
*   **Gradients:** Spatial gradients $\nabla \varphi$, $\nabla \theta$, and $\nabla r$ are computed via automatic differentiation.
*   **Rendering Weights:** $\{w_i\}$ are the standard volumetric rendering weights indicating the contribution of sample $i$ to the final color.

**Parameters**
*   $\alpha_f = 50$: Sharpness parameter for the far loss barrier [S1, unofficial].
*   $T_{\text{warm}} = 50,000$: Number of warm-up iterations [S1, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_{\text{anchor}}, \lambda_{\text{surf}}, \lambda_{\text{align}}, \lambda_{\text{eik}}, \lambda_{\text{far}}, \lambda_{\text{mask}}$: Loss weights. The source notes that these are chosen such that the four groups of terms contribute comparable gradient magnitudes [S1, unofficial]. Specific values for $\lambda$ are not given in the sources.

**Initialization [G]**
1.  Initialize the phase branch $\theta$ (and thus the composite field $\varphi$) to approximate the signed distance of a coarse sphere bounding the scene.
2.  Initialize the metric branch $r$ as a nonnegative, distance-like field, e.g., proportional to $|\theta|$ [S1, unofficial].
3.  If coarse geometric priors (visual hull, SfM points, or depth) are available, initialize $\theta$ from the corresponding coarse implicit surface instead of a sphere [S1, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps**

1.  **Compute Rendering Weights:** For each ray $\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}$, compute the rendering weights $\{w_i\}_{i=1}^n$ using the standard volume rendering equation and the sharpness parameter $s$ [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Compute Free-Space Indicators:** For each sample $i$ on ray $\mathbf{r}$, compute the free-space weight:
    $$ w_{\text{free}, i} = 1 - \exp(-100 |r(\mathbf{r}(t_i))|) $$
    This accounts for occluded surfaces by identifying regions where the metric field $r$ is large [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Compute Photometric Loss:** Calculate the $\ell_1$ difference between rendered color $\widehat{C}(\mathbf{r})$ and ground truth $C^*(\mathbf{r})$:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{rgb}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \|\widehat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C^*(\mathbf{r})\|_1 $$
4.  **Compute Near-Surface Regularization:** Apply losses weighted by $w_i$ to samples near the surface.
    *   **Near-surface Eikonal Loss:** Encourages unit gradients for $\varphi$ and $\theta$:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{surf}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}| n} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \left( (\|\nabla \varphi(\mathbf{r}(t_i))\|_2 - 1)^2 + (\|\nabla \theta(\mathbf{r}(t_i))\|_2 - 1)^2 \right) $$
    *   **Gradient Alignment Loss:** Aligns normalized gradients of $r$ and $\theta$ to prevent degenerate decompositions:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}| n} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \left( 1 - \langle \widehat{\nabla} r_i, \widehat{\nabla} \theta_i \rangle^2 \right) $$
        where $\widehat{\nabla} r_i = \frac{\nabla r(\mathbf{r}(t_i))}{\|\nabla r(\mathbf{r}(t_i))\|_2 + \epsilon}$ and $\widehat{\nabla} \theta_i$ is defined analogously [S1, unofficial].
    *   **Anchor Loss:** Encourages $\varphi$ and $r$ to be small at near-surface samples:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{anchor}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}| n} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \left( |\varphi(\mathbf{r}(t_i))| + r(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) \right) $$
5.  **Compute Free-Space Regularization:** Apply losses weighted by $w_{\text{free}, i}$ to samples away from the surface.
    *   **Free-Space Eikonal Loss:** Encourages $r$ to behave like an unsigned distance field:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{eik}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}| n} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n w_{\text{free}, i} (\|\nabla r(\mathbf{r}(t_i))\|_2 - 1)^2 $$
    *   **Free-Space Far Loss:** Prevents large regions with near-zero UDF values:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{far}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n w_{\text{free}, i} \exp(-\alpha_f (r(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) + |\varphi(\mathbf{r}(t_i))|)) $$
6.  **Compute Optional Silhouette Loss:** If foreground masks $A^*(\mathbf{r})$ are available, compute the $\ell_1$ loss on accumulated opacity $\widehat{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i$:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{mask}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|} \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \|\widehat{A}(\mathbf{r}) - A^*(\mathbf{r})\|_1 $$
7.  **Compute Total Loss:** Combine all terms:
    $$ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{rgb}} + \lambda_{\text{anchor}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{anchor}} + \lambda_{\text{surf}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{surf}} + \lambda_{\text{align}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} + \lambda_{\text{eik}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{eik}} + \lambda_{\text{far}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{far}} + \lambda_{\text{mask}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{mask}} $$

**Pseudocode**

```python
def mpf_training_loss(rays, gt_colors, gt_masks, network, s, beta, alpha_f=50, Twarm=50000, iteration=0):
    """
    Computes the MPF training loss.
    
    Args:
        rays: List of Ray objects, each with origin and direction.
        gt_colors: Tensor of ground truth colors for the rays.
        gt_masks: Tensor of ground truth masks (optional, None if not available).
        network: MPF network object with methods query(x) -> (phi, theta, r).
        s: Sharpness parameter.
        beta: Phase scale parameter.
        alpha_f: Far loss sharpness parameter (default 50).
        Twarm: Warm-up iteration count (default 50000).
        iteration: Current training iteration.
        
    Returns:
        Total loss scalar.
    """
    # 1. Sample rays and query network
    # Assume sample_rays returns positions t, and we query the network at these positions
    # This step depends on the specific ray sampling strategy (e.g., uniform, importance)
    # For this implementation, we assume a standard NeuS-style sampling is handled externally
    # or is part of the 'rays' structure. Here we assume 'rays' contains pre-sampled points
    # or we sample them. Let's assume we sample n points per ray.
    
    n_samples = 128 # Typical value, not explicitly given but required for implementation
    positions = sample_positions(rays, n_samples) # [num_rays, n_samples, 3]
    
    # Query network for fields
    phi, theta, r = network(positions) # [num_rays, n_samples]
    
    # Compute gradients using automatic differentiation
    # grad_phi, grad_theta, grad_r are gradients w.r.t. spatial coordinates
    grad_phi = compute_gradient(phi, positions)
    grad_theta = compute_gradient(theta, positions)
    grad_r = compute_gradient(r, positions)
    
    # Compute rendering weights w_i
    # This requires the standard volume rendering alpha calculation using s and beta
    # alpha_i = 1 - exp(-s * beta * ... ) # Simplified, actual NeuS alpha formula used
    # We assume a function render_weights that computes w_i given the fields and s
    weights = compute_rendering_weights(phi, theta, r, s, beta) # [num_rays, n_samples]
    
    # 2. Compute Free-Space Indicators
    # w_free_i = 1 - exp(-100 * |r|)
    abs_r = torch.abs(r)
    w_free = 1.0 - torch.exp(-100.0 * abs_r)
    
    # 3. Photometric Loss
    rendered_colors = volume_render(rays, weights, network, s, beta) # [num_rays, 3]
    l_rgb = torch.mean(torch.abs(rendered_colors - gt_colors))
    
    # 4. Near-Surface Regularization
    # L_surf
    norm_grad_phi = torch.norm(grad_phi, dim=-1) # [num_rays, n_samples]
    norm_grad_theta = torch.norm(grad_theta, dim=-1)
    l_surf = torch.mean(weights * ((norm_grad_phi - 1.0)**2 + (norm_grad_theta - 1.0)**2))
    
    # L_align
    # Normalize gradients
    eps = 1e-8
    norm_grad_r = torch.norm(grad_r, dim=-1)
    norm_grad_theta_val = torch.norm(grad_theta, dim=-1)
    
    grad_r_hat = grad_r / (norm_grad_r.unsqueeze(-1) + eps)
    grad_theta_hat = grad_theta / (norm_grad_theta_val.unsqueeze(-1) + eps)
    
    # Dot product
    dot_product = torch.sum(grad_r_hat * grad_theta_hat, dim=-1) # [num_rays, n_samples]
    l_align = torch.mean(weights * (1.0 - dot_product**2))
    
    # L_anchor
    l_anchor = torch.mean(weights * (torch.abs(phi) + r))
    
    # 5. Free-Space Regularization
    # L_eik
    l_eik = torch.mean(w_free * (norm_grad_r - 1.0)**2)
    
    # L_far
    # exp(-alpha_f * (r + |phi|))
    l_far = torch.mean(w_free * torch.exp(-alpha_f * (r + torch.abs(phi))))
    
    # 6. Optional Silhouette Loss
    accumulated_opacity = torch.sum(weights, dim=-1) # [num_rays]
    if gt_masks is not None:
        l_mask = torch.mean(torch.abs(accumulated_opacity - gt_masks))
    else:
        l_mask = 0.0
        
    # 7. Total Loss with Scheduling
    # Warm-up schedule: ramp lambda_align and lambda_far from 0 to target
    # The source does not specify the target lambda values or the exact ramp function.
    # We assume linear ramp to target values lambda_align_target and lambda_far_target.
    # [G] Placeholder for target lambda values which are not given in sources.
    lambda_align_target = 1.0 
    lambda_far_target = 1.0
    
    if iteration < Twarm:
        lambda_align = 0.0
        lambda_far = 0.0
    else:
        # Linear ramp from 0 to target over some period? 
        # Source says "progressively ramp... from 0 to target values". 
        # It does not specify the duration of the ramp. 
        # [G] Assuming ramp completes by 2*Twarm for implementation completeness.
        ramp_progress = min(1.0, (iteration - Twarm) / Twarm)
        lambda_align = lambda_align_target * ramp_progress
        lambda_far = lambda_far_target * ramp_progress
        
    # [G] Other lambdas are assumed to be constant or handled by the user.
    # The source says "choose lambda such that the four groups... contribute comparable gradient magnitudes".
    # We use placeholders for the other lambdas.
    lambda_anchor = 1.0
    lambda_surf = 1.0
    lambda_eik = 1.0
    lambda_mask = 1.0
    
    total_loss = (l_rgb + 
                  lambda_anchor * l_anchor + 
                  lambda_surf * l_surf + 
                  lambda_align * l_align + 
                  lambda_eik * l_eik + 
                  lambda_far * l_far + 
                  lambda_mask * l_mask)
                  
    return total_loss
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Trivial Solutions:** The network may reshape the weight distribution to reduce regularization penalties. Mitigation: Stop gradients through the rendering weights $w_i$ when they are used inside the regularization losses [S1, unofficial].
*   **Degenerate Decompositions:** Without alignment, the metric and phase fields may collapse or dominate each other. The gradient alignment loss prevents this [S1, unofficial].
*   **Sign Ambiguity:** The squared inner product in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$ makes the constraint orientation-invariant, which is desirable for sign-ambiguous regions where a consistent global orientation may not exist [S1, unofficial].
*   **Surface Interference:** The far loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{far}}$ is not applied exactly on the surface to prevent it from disrupting the actual surface reconstruction [S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The loss computation is dominated by the network forward pass and the computation of gradients via backpropagation. The additional regularization terms add linear overhead with respect to the number of samples $n$ and rays $|\mathcal{R}|$.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the fields $\varphi, \theta, r$ and their gradients for all samples. The memory footprint is proportional to $|\mathcal{R}| \times n$.
*   **Strengths:** Provides robust geometric regularization without requiring ground-truth 3D data. The use of rendering weights as soft indicators allows the regularizers to adapt to the learned surface location dynamically.
*   **Limitations:** Relies on the assumption that rendering weights concentrate near the zero level set, which may be noisy early in training. The warm-up schedule is critical for stability.
*   **When to Use:** Multi-view image reconstruction where ground-truth 3D geometry is unavailable, and thin structures or sign-ambiguous geometry are present.
*   **When Not to Use:** When explicit ground-truth point clouds are available, the original MPF formulation may be more direct.

## Background
The MPF Training Objective was introduced in the context of the "Volume Rendering with Metric--Phase Fields for Thin Structures and Sign-Ambiguous Geometry" paper [S1, unofficial]. It adapts the original MPF method, which was designed for surface reconstruction from known point clouds, to the more challenging setting of multi-view image reconstruction [S1, unofficial]. In the original MPF, near-surface and far-away points were easily separated using ground-truth point clouds, and losses like $\mathcal{L}_{\text{anchor}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{surf}}$ acted as explicit supervision. In the multi-view setting, the surface location is dynamic and unknown, necessitating the use of rendering weights as soft selectors [S1, unofficial]. The method follows the standard NeuS training pipeline for optimization and ray sampling [S1, unofficial].
