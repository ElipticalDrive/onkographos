# Low Volume Resolution-based Prediction

Also called: LVRP

## Overview
Low Volume Resolution-based Prediction (LVRP) estimates the rendering time of a high-resolution volume by rendering the same data at a significantly lower resolution and applying a linear transformation to the measured time [S1, unofficial]. Unlike [Histogram Spectra for LOD Selection](11687-histogram-spectra-for-lod-selection.md), which predicts sampling error based on statistical density differences, LVRP directly predicts computational cost (time) based on resolution scaling [S1, unofficial]. It differs from [ENTIRE](17899-entire.md) by using a simple linear model rather than a deep learning network, making it hardware-agnostic but less capable of capturing non-linear performance factors [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Volume dataset, rendering parameters (transfer function, camera, ray step size), and a pre-computed linear model (slope and intercept).
- Output: Predicted rendering time for the target high resolution.

**Data Structures:**
- Linear Model: A pair of floating-point values $(m, c)$ representing the slope and bias of the regression line.

**Steps:**
1. **Low-Resolution Rendering:** Render the volume at a fixed low resolution (typically $64^3$ voxels) using the exact rendering parameters intended for the high-resolution target. Measure the actual rendering time $t_{low}$ [S1, unofficial].
2. **Linear Scaling:** Apply the pre-computed linear scaling coefficient $m$ and bias $c$ to the measured time to predict the high-resolution time $t_{high}$ [S1, unofficial].

**Formula:**
$$ t_{high} = m \cdot t_{low} + c $$

Where:
- $t_{high}$ is the predicted rendering time for the high resolution.
- $t_{low}$ is the measured rendering time at the low resolution ($64^3$).
- $m$ is the scaling coefficient.
- $c$ is the bias term.

**Parameter Determination:**
The coefficients $m$ and $c$ are determined offline by rendering the same scenes at both low and high resolutions with identical parameters and fitting a linear regression model to the relationship between $t_{low}$ and $t_{high}$ [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def predict_rendering_time(volume, low_res_time, slope, bias):
    """
    Predicts high-resolution rendering time based on low-resolution measurement.
    
    Args:
        volume: The volume dataset (used to verify resolution context if needed).
        low_res_time: Measured time to render the volume at 64^3 resolution.
        slope: Pre-computed linear scaling coefficient (m).
        bias: Pre-computed linear bias term (c).
        
    Returns:
        float: Predicted high-resolution rendering time.
    """
    # Apply linear transformation
    predicted_time = slope * low_res_time + bias
    return predicted_time
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Fine-Scale Features:** Volume downsampling discards fine-scale structural features that significantly influence raycasting performance. This leads to limited predictive capability for complex datasets where small structures dominate rendering cost [S1, unofficial].
- **Complex Timing Behavior:** In datasets with high variability or complex timing behavior (e.g., cosmology simulations), the linear assumption may fail, resulting in higher Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ for the prediction step after the low-resolution render. The low-resolution render itself is $O(N_{low})$, where $N_{low}$ is the number of voxels at low resolution.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ additional memory for the linear model parameters.
- **Accuracy:** Performance is limited by the loss of fine-scale structural information during downsampling. It performs worse than learning-based methods like [ENTIRE](17899-entire.md) on complex datasets [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Simple to implement; does not require training data or neural networks; decouples feature extraction from prediction implicitly by using raw time [S1, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** Assumes a linear relationship between resolution and time, which may not hold for all transfer functions or hardware; sensitive to the loss of fine details [S1, unofficial].
- **When to use:** When a simple, fast-to-implement baseline is needed and the volume data does not have extreme fine-scale complexity that dominates rendering time.
- **When not to use:** For complex datasets with significant fine-scale structures or when high accuracy is required across varying hardware and rendering methods [S1, unofficial].

## Background
LVRP was introduced as a baseline method for optimizing rendering workloads in the context of in situ visualization and interactive exploration [S1, unofficial]. It exploits the correlation between rendering times at different volume resolutions to estimate final rendering time without full high-resolution rendering [S1, unofficial].
