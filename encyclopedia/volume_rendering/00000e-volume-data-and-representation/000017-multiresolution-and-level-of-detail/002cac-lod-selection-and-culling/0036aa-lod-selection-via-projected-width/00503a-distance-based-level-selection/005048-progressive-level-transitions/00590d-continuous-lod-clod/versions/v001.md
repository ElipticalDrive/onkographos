# Continuous LOD (CLOD)

Also called: Continuous Levels of Detail (LOD) Nodes, LOD Nodes, Levels of Detail, Smooth LOD Blending

## Overview
Continuous LOD (CLOD) is a technique that replaces a high-resolution implicit function with a more efficient low-resolution expression by interpolating between them based on the viewer's position, ensuring the region of influence depends on the viewer rather than the object center [S1]. Unlike traditional LOD which selects a single model based on distance to the object center, CLOD adapts the implicit surface continuously based on the distance between the viewer and the query point, straddling or embedding the surface [S1]. It sets itself apart from proxy nodes by allowing the transition region to intersect the surface and from discrete LOD methods by eliminating sudden visual changes through smooth blending [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** High-resolution signed distance field (SDF) $h$, low-resolution SDF $l$, viewer position $e$, transition start radius $r$, transition width $\delta_r$.
*   **Outputs:** A continuous effective SDF $f_e(p)$ for any query point $p$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Implicit Nodes:** Each node stores references to its high-resolution child $h$ and low-resolution child $l$.
*   **Mesh Vertices (for rasterization):** For transition regions, vertices must store a "parent vertex" from the parent chunk's mesh to enable blending in the vertex shader [S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1.  **Compute Distance:** Calculate the Euclidean distance $d(p, e) = \|p - e\|$ between the query point $p$ and the viewer $e$ [S1].
2.  **Determine Blend Factor:** Compute the interpolation weight $\alpha(p)$ based on the distance relative to the transition zone boundaries $r$ and $r + \delta_r$ [S1]:
    $$
    \alpha(p) = \begin{cases} 
    0 & \text{if } \|p - e\| \le r \\
    \frac{\|p - e\| - r}{\delta_r} & \text{if } r < \|p - e\| < r + \delta_r \\
    1 & \text{if } \|p - e\| \ge r + \delta_r 
    \end{cases}
    $$
3.  **Interpolate SDF:** Evaluate the effective signed distance field $f_e(p)$ using the weighted average of the high and low-resolution fields, normalized by a factor $\lambda$ [S1]:
    $$
    f_e(p) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \left( (1 - \alpha(p)) h(p) + \alpha(p) l(p) \right)
    $$
    *Note: The sources define $\lambda$ in the formula but do not specify its value or calculation method [S1].*
4.  **Mesh Blending (Rasterization):** If rendering a mesh, vertices in the transition region possess two positions: one from the high-resolution LOD and one from the low-resolution LOD. The vertex shader blends these positions using weights $W_0$ and $W_1$ such that $W_0 + W_1 = 1$. At the boundary between the high-resolution LOD and the transition, weights are $(1, 0)$. At the boundary between the transition and the low-resolution LOD, weights are $(0, 1)$ [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   $r$: The radius defining the start of the transition zone from the viewer. Inside this radius, the high-resolution model is used ($\alpha=0$) [S1].
*   $\delta_r$: The width of the transition zone. Interpolation occurs in the range $r \le d(p, e) \le r + \delta_r$ [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Performance:** In the transition zone, computational cost is higher because both high and low-resolution functions must be evaluated [S1].
*   **Memory:** Storing parent vertices for blending requires significantly more memory [S2, unofficial].
*   **GPU Updates:** Mesh data requires more frequent updates on the GPU to maintain correct blending states [S2, unofficial].
*   **Non-manifold Geometry:** Surface nets used to extract meshes from SDFs can produce non-manifold geometry near pseudo-singularities, which may break normal-based lighting [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Higher in the transition zone due to dual function evaluation [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Increased due to the storage of parent vertices for mesh blending [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Provides seamless, continuous visual transitions without popping artifacts; adapts to viewer position rather than object center [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Higher computational cost in transition regions; increased memory footprint and GPU update frequency for mesh-based rendering [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When visual continuity is critical and the cost of dual evaluation in transition zones is acceptable.
*   **When not to use:** When memory bandwidth is severely constrained or when the transition zone cost is prohibitive for the target hardware.

## Background
Continuous LOD nodes were introduced by P. Hubert-Brierre, E. Guérin, A. Peytavie, and E. Galin in their work on accelerating signed distance functions [S1]. The technique is part of a broader family of multiresolution representations for implicit surfaces, building upon the concept of LOD selection via projected width and progressive level transitions [S1]. It addresses the limitations of proxy nodes and traditional distance-based LOD selection by allowing the region of influence to depend on the viewer position [S1].
