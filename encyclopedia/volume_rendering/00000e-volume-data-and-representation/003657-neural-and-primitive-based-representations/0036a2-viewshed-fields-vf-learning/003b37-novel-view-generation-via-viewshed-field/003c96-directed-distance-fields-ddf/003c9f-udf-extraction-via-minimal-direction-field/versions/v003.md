# UDF Extraction via Minimal Direction Field

Also called: Minimal Direction Field (MDF), UDF Extraction, v* optimization

## Overview
This method extracts an Unoriented Distance Field (UDF) from a Directed Distance Field (DDF) by optimizing a field $v^*: B \rightarrow S^2$, where $B$ is the bounding box and $S^2$ is the unit sphere [S1]. The resulting Minimal Direction Field (MDF), $v^*$, points to the closest point on the surface $S$ [S1]. Unlike directly fitting a UDF, this approach requires handling local minima for $v^*$ and non-visible directions [S1]. It serves as the final step in the Viewshed Field pipeline, converting the DDF representation into a robust UDF estimate [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A trained Directed Distance Field (DDF) representation, which provides visibility $\xi$ and depth information [S1].
*   **Output:** A field $v^*(p)$ for every point $p \in B$, representing the direction to the closest surface point, and the derived UDF value $UDF(p) = d(p, v^*(p))$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Direction Field $v^*$:** A field mapping each query point $p$ to a unit vector $v \in S^2$ [S1]. In implementation, this is typically stored as a neural network (e.g., SIREN) or a lookup table outputting 3 values constrained to unit length [G].

**Steps:**
1.  **Initialize:** Initialize the direction field $v^*(p)$ for all $p \in B$ [G].
2.  **Optimize Objective:** Optimize $v^*$ by minimizing an objective function that prefers high visibility and low depth [S1]. The objective encourages $v^*(p)$ to point towards directions where the DDF indicates high visibility $\xi$ and low depth $d$ [S1].
3.  **Compute UDF:** Once $v^*(p)$ is optimized, compute the UDF value at $p$ as the distance along the direction $v^*(p)$ [S1]:
    $$ UDF(p) = d(p, v^*(p)) $$
    where $d(p, v)$ is the depth value provided by the DDF for direction $v$ at point $p$ [S1].

**Formulas:**
*   **UDF Definition:**
    $$ UDF(p) = d(p, v^*(p)) $$
    [S1]
*   **Normal Relation:** When surface normals $n$ exist, the optimal direction is the negative of the normal:
    $$ v^*(p) = -n(p, v^*(p)) $$
    [S1]

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimization Hyperparameters:** Not given in the sources. Typical gradient descent parameters (learning rate, iterations) would apply [G].
*   **Temperature $\eta_T$ and Inverse Depth Scale $\epsilon_s$:** These are mentioned in the context of weighting contributions during DDF fitting, upweighting small distances with high visibility [S1]. Their specific values for UDF extraction are not given [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Local Minima:** The optimization for $v^*$ is prone to local minima, particularly if initialized poorly [S1].
*   **Non-visible Directions:** Directions with low visibility $\xi$ should be avoided or handled carefully, as they do not correspond to surface points [S1].
*   **Discontinuities:** Discontinuities in $v^*$ occur at the surface $S$ and on the medial surface of $S$ in $B$. At medial axis points, there are multiple valid values of $v^*$, which can cause ambiguity in the direction field [S1].
*   **Noise:** Directly computing $v^*$ via the gradient of a fitted UDF (backward pass) is noisier than explicit optimization [S1].

## Subtopics

### [UDF Extraction via Candidate Directions](17903-udf-extraction-via-candidate-directions.md)
This method estimates the UDF value and the optimal direction $v^*$ at a query point $p$ by using a small SIREN network to generate $K_c$ candidate directions, computing associated distances and visibility, and aggregating them with softmax weights. It serves as the final step in the Viewshed Field pipeline, converting the DDF representation into a robust UDF estimate. Unlike computing $v^*$ via the gradient of the UDF (backward pass), which is noisier, this fitting approach provides a stable direction estimate by explicitly optimizing a set of candidate directions.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources. The optimization step for $v^*$ is iterative and depends on the number of optimization steps and the resolution of the field [G].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources. Depends on the storage of the direction field $v^*$ [G].
*   **Strengths:** Provides a robust UDF estimate from a DDF. Explicitly optimizing candidate directions (in variants) can be more stable than gradient-based methods [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Prone to local minima. Discontinuities at medial axes can complicate the direction field [S1].
*   **When to use:** When a UDF is required from a DDF representation, especially when robustness to noise is needed [G].
*   **When not to use:** When the medial axis structure is complex and discontinuities in $v^*$ are problematic for downstream applications [G].

## Background
*   **Origin:** The method is described in "Probabilistic Directed Distance Fields for Ray-Based Shape Representations" [S1].
*   **History:** It is part of the Viewshed Field (VF) learning pipeline, specifically for novel view generation and shape representation [S1].
*   **Usage:** Used in single-image 3D reconstruction (SI3DR) and scene composition [S1].
