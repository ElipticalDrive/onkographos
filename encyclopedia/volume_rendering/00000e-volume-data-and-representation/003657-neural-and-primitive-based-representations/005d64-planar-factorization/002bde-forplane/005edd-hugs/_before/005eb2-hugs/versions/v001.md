# HuGS

Also called: Human Gaussian Splatting, HuGS(Ours)

## Overview
HuGS is a method for creating and animating virtual human avatars using 3D Gaussian Splatting, addressing the problem of high-fidelity, real-time rendering of dynamic humans with complex clothing. It distinguishes itself from NeRF-based formulations by bypassing inverse skinning through a coarse-to-fine deformation algorithm that combines forward Linear Blend Skinning (LBS) with local learning-based refinement via a pose-dependent Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) [S1]. Unlike methods that rely on template mesh weights, HuGS learns per-Gaussian skinning weights, allowing adaptation to arbitrary body shapes and correction of pose estimation errors [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of training images with known camera poses and human poses (joint angles); a canonical 3D Gaussian representation (position, covariance, opacity, spherical harmonics coefficients for color).
*   **Outputs:** A trained model capable of rendering novel views and novel poses of the human avatar.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Gaussian Attributes:** For each Gaussian $i$:
    *   $\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Canonical position.
    *   $\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Rotation matrix (or quaternion).
    *   $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Scaling vector.
    *   $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$: Opacity.
    *   $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$: Spherical Harmonics coefficients (color).
    *   $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^J$: Learned skinning weights (where $J$ is the number of joints).
    *   $\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$: Per-Gaussian latent code.
2.  **MLP:** A small Multi-Layer Perceptron that takes $\mathbf{z}_i$ and pose parameters as input and outputs deformation offsets.

**Deformation Process:**
The transformation from canonical space to observation space for a Gaussian $i$ given a pose $\theta$ is computed in two stages:

1.  **Coarse Deformation (LBS):**
    Compute the rigid body transformation based on joint rotations. Let $\mathbf{T}_j(\theta)$ be the transformation matrix of joint $j$ in the canonical frame given pose $\theta$. The LBS transformation $\mathbf{T}_{LBS}(\theta)$ for Gaussian $i$ is:
    $$ \mathbf{T}_{LBS}(\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_{i,j} \mathbf{T}_j(\theta) $$
    where $w_{i,j}$ are the learned skinning weights for Gaussian $i$ and joint $j$, normalized such that $\sum_j w_{i,j} = 1$. This yields a transformed position $\mathbf{p}'_i$ and rotation $\mathbf{R}'_i$.

2.  **Fine Deformation (MLP Refinement):**
    The MLP predicts a residual translation $\Delta \mathbf{t}_i$ and rotation $\Delta \mathbf{R}_i$ to model non-rigid details (e.g., garment wrinkles). The input to the MLP is the latent code $\mathbf{z}_i$ and the current pose $\theta$.
    $$ [\Delta \mathbf{t}_i, \Delta \mathbf{R}_i] = \text{MLP}(\mathbf{z}_i, \theta) $$
    The final transformation is applied to the LBS result:
    $$ \mathbf{p}''_i = \mathbf{p}'_i + \Delta \mathbf{t}_i $$
    $$ \mathbf{R}''_i = \mathbf{R}'_i \cdot \Delta \mathbf{R}_i $$
    [G] The rotation composition $\Delta \mathbf{R}_i$ is typically applied as a matrix multiplication or via quaternion multiplication depending on the implementation's representation of $\mathbf{R}$.

**Training Steps:**
1.  Initialize Gaussians in canonical space.
2.  Initialize learned skinning weights $\mathbf{w}_i$ uniformly or based on proximity to SMPL vertices (though learned weights are preferred).
3.  Initialize latent codes $\mathbf{z}_i$ for each Gaussian.
4.  For each training iteration:
    a. Sample a training image with pose $\theta$ and camera pose.
    b. For each Gaussian, compute $\mathbf{T}_{LBS}(\theta)$ using $\mathbf{w}_i$.
    c. Compute MLP output $[\Delta \mathbf{t}_i, \Delta \mathbf{R}_i]$ using $\mathbf{z}_i$ and $\theta$.
    d. Apply final transformation to get screen-space Gaussians.
    e. Rasterize Gaussians to generate rendered image.
    f. Compute loss (e.g., L1 loss + D-SSIM loss) between rendered and ground truth image.
    g. Backpropagate to update Gaussian attributes, $\mathbf{w}_i$, $\mathbf{z}_i$, and MLP parameters.

**Parameters:**
*   Latent code size: 16 floats per Gaussian [S1].
*   Rendering resolution: 512x512 pixels [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Sparse Cameras:** Sparse camera setups degrade 3D reconstruction quality due to overfitting on training observations, resulting in poor novel pose synthesis [S1].
*   **Extrapolation:** The MLP does not extrapolate novel deformations; it only fits observed garment deformations and replicates them for novel poses [S1].
*   **Independence:** Each Gaussian is optimized and deformed independently, ignoring relations between Gaussians in local neighborhoods [S1].
*   **Shading Component:** Removing the shading component (translation/rotation from MLP) forces the model to learn duplicate Gaussians with different colors for shaded areas, leading to overfitting [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Rendering Time:** ~12ms (80fps) on a Tesla V100 GPU for 512x512 resolution. Transformation computation takes 10ms; Gaussian rasterization takes 2ms [S1].
*   **Training Time:** 5 to 20 hours on a Tesla V100, depending on dataset size [S1].
*   **Performance (ZJU-MoCap Novel Poses):**
    *   HuGS: PSNR 23.69 dB, SSIM 0.896 [S1].
    *   SLRF: PSNR 23.61 dB, SSIM 0.905 [S1].
    *   Neural Body: PSNR 21.60 dB, SSIM 0.870 [S1].
    *   Ani-NeRF: PSNR 21.29 dB, SSIM 0.860 [S1].
*   **Ablation Results:**
    *   **Skinning Weights (Novel View Synthesis):** Learned weights PSNR 31.99 vs Template weights PSNR 30.97 (>1dB improvement) [S1].
    *   **MLP Input (Novel Pose Synthesis on THuman4):** Latent code PSNR 32.49 vs Position PSNR 32.20 [S1].
    *   **Shading Component (Novel Pose Synthesis on THuman4):** Without shading, PSNR 29.72 dB, SSIM 0.978, LPIPS 0.027 [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Real-time rendering speed; bypasses inverse skinning; adapts to loose clothing and unusual topology; generalizes well to novel poses compared to NeRF-based methods [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Poor performance with sparse camera setups; inability to extrapolate unseen deformations; lack of local neighborhood constraints between Gaussians [S1].

## Background
HuGS (Human Gaussian Splatting) was introduced in the paper "Human Gaussian Splatting: Real-Time Rendering of Animatable Avatars" [S1]. It represents a shift from NeRF-based human rendering, which often requires complex inverse skinning or volume integration, to a rasterization-based approach that leverages the speed of Gaussian Splatting while maintaining animatability through learned deformations.
