# DyNFL

Also called: Dynamic LiDAR Re-simulation Using Compositional Neural Fields

## Overview
DyNFL is a neural field-based approach for high-fidelity re-simulation of LiDAR scans in dynamic driving scenes [S1]. It processes LiDAR measurements and bounding boxes of moving objects to construct an editable neural field comprising separately reconstructed static background and dynamic objects, allowing modification of viewpoints, object positions, and addition/removal of objects [S1]. Unlike static neural fields, DyNFL enables the composition of multiple neural assets through a ray drop test to manage occlusions and transparent surfaces, ensuring physical accuracy by modeling two-way transmittance [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of LiDAR scans $X = \{X_t\}_{t=1}^T$ compensated for ego-motion, and tracked bounding boxes $B = \{B^v_t\}_{v=1}^N$ for $N$ moving vehicles [S1]. Each scan $X_t$ consists of $n_t$ rays. Each ray $r$ is described by the tuple $(o, d, \zeta, e, p_d)$, where $o$ is the origin, $d$ is the direction, $\zeta$ is the range, $e$ is the intensity, and $p_d \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates if the ray is dropped due to insufficient returned radiant power [S1].
*   **Outputs:** A compositional neural field representation that can render LiDAR scans $X^{\text{tgt}}_t$ from novel viewpoints $T^{\text{tgt}}$, support object trajectory alteration, and allow insertion or removal of objects [S1].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Static Neural Field ($F_{\text{static}}$):** Maps $(x, d) \to (s, e, p_d)$.
    *   $x \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Point coordinates.
    *   $d \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Ray direction.
    *   $s \in \mathbb{R}$: Signed distance.
    *   $e \in \mathbb{R}$: Intensity.
    *   $p_d \in [0, 1]$: Ray drop probability.
2.  **Dynamic Neural Fields ($\{F^v\}_{v=1}^N$):** One field per dynamic vehicle $v$.
    *   Operates in a canonical coordinate frame.
    *   Inputs are transformed points and directions relative to the vehicle's canonical pose.
3.  **Bounding Boxes:** $B^v_t \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 8}$, representing the 8 corners of the bounding box for vehicle $v$ at time $t$ in the world coordinate frame [S1].

**Method Steps:**

1.  **Scene Decomposition:** Decompose the scene into a static background and $N$ dynamic vehicles [S1].
2.  **Static Background Encoding:**
    *   For each point $x$ in the static background, compute the positional feature $f_{\text{pos}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ using Multi-Resolution Hash Encoding (MRH) [S1].
    *   Compute the directional feature $f_{\text{dir}}$ by projecting the view direction $d$ onto the first 16 coefficients of the spherical harmonics basis [S1].
    *   Construct the ray feature $f_{\text{ray}} \in \mathbb{R}^{31}$ by concatenating the geometric feature $f_{\text{geo}}$ and $f_{\text{dir}}$ [S1].
    *   Use three Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) $f_s, f_e, f_{\text{drop}}$ to estimate properties:
        $$ (s, f_{\text{geo}}) = f_s(f_{\text{pos}}) $$
        $$ e = f_e(f_{\text{ray}}) $$
        $$ p_d = f_{\text{drop}}(f_{\text{ray}}) $$
        Here, $f_{\text{geo}} \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$ is the per-point geometric feature [S1].
3.  **Dynamic Vehicle Reconstruction:**
    *   Assume each dynamic object undergoes rigid motion [S1].
    *   For each vehicle $v$, identify scans $\{X^v_t\}_{t=1}^T$ and bounding boxes $\{B^v_t\}_{t=1}^T$ [S1].
    *   Define the first bounding box $B^v_1$ as the canonical box [S1].
    *   Estimate relative transformations $\{T_t \in SE(3)\}_{t=2}^T$ between subsequent bounding boxes and the canonical box [S1].
    *   Align points from all time steps to the canonical frame using these transformations.
    *   Reconstruct the neural field $F^v$ in the canonical coordinate frame using the aligned points [S1].
4.  **Neural Field Composition (Rendering):**
    *   To render a ray $r$ from a target viewpoint, trace the ray through the scene [S1].
    *   Evaluate the static field $F_{\text{static}}$ and all dynamic fields $F^v$ [S1].
    *   Apply the **ray drop test**: Use the predicted drop probability $p_d$ to manage occlusions and transparent surfaces. If a ray is dropped by a preceding surface (static or dynamic), it does not contribute to the final intensity or range from subsequent surfaces [S1].
    *   Compose the results from individual fields to generate the final LiDAR measurement (range, intensity, drop status) [S1].
    *   For dynamic vehicles at time $t$, apply the inverse of the transformation $T_t$ to the ray origin and direction before querying $F^v$, effectively rendering the vehicle in its current pose [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Ego-motion Compensation:** LiDAR scans must be compensated for ego-motion before decomposition [S1].
*   **Rigid Motion Assumption:** Dynamic vehicles are assumed to undergo rigid motion; non-rigid deformation is not supported [S1].
*   **Ray Drop Probability:** The interpretation of $p_d$ as a probability for occlusion/transparent surfaces requires careful integration during rendering to maintain physical accuracy [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:** DyNFL outperforms baseline models in range and intensity estimates. Synthetic outputs show higher agreement with real scans on object detection and segmentation tasks [S1].
*   **Strengths:** High-fidelity re-simulation, editable neural field (viewpoint, object position, add/remove objects), physical accuracy (two-way transmittance, beam width) [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Limited to dynamic driving scenes with rigidly moving objects. Requires tracked bounding boxes for dynamic objects [S1].
*   **When to use:** When high-fidelity LiDAR re-simulation in dynamic environments with editing capabilities is required [S1].
*   **When not to use:** For static scenes only (use NFL) or when physical sensor properties like two-way transmittance are not critical (UniSim may suffice) [S1].

## Background
DyNFL was introduced by Hanfeng Wu, Xingxing Zuo, Stefan Leutenegger, Or Litany, Konrad Schindler, and Shengyu Huang in 2024 [S1]. It builds upon NFL (Neural LiDar Fields) by adding a method for compositing neural fields to handle dynamic scenes, whereas NFL is limited to static scenes [S1]. It addresses the gap between physical fidelity and flexible editing in dynamic scene simulation [S1].
