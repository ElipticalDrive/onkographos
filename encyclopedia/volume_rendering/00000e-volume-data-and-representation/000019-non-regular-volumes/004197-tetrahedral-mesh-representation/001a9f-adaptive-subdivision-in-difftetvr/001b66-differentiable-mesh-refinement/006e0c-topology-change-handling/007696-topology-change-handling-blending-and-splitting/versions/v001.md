# Topology Change Handling (Blending and Splitting)

Also called: Dynamic Topology Management, Surface Blending, Surface Splitting

## Overview
This method handles topological changes (merging and separating) in dynamic implicit surface rendering by detecting changes on a high-resolution geometric mesh and applying corresponding modifications to a coarse mechanical mesh [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of maintaining stable physics while allowing high-detail rendering of surfaces that merge or split, decoupling the mechanical simulation from the geometric representation [S1, unofficial]. Unlike mass-spring systems which struggle with inverted elements and stability limits, this approach uses a robust Finite Element Method (FEM) for mechanics and adaptive refinement for geometry, enabling larger time steps and finer visual detail [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
The method operates on two coupled meshes: a coarse **mechanical mesh** (used for physics simulation via FEM) and a fine **geometric mesh** (used for rendering and topology detection) [S1, unofficial]. Topology changes are detected on the geometric mesh; when detected, the mechanical mesh is modified, and the geometric mesh is regenerated [S1, unofficial].

### Data Structures
*   **Mechanical Mesh**: A triangulation of vertices and triangles. Vertices are constrained to the implicit surface.
*   **Geometric Mesh**: A refined triangulation derived from the mechanical mesh.
*   **Border List**: A linked list of border edges (edges belonging to only one triangle), representing a hole in the triangulation.

### Common Procedures
1.  **ERASE(τ)**: Removes triangle $\tau$ from the mechanical triangulation [S1, unofficial].
2.  **FINDBORDERS()**: Searches the triangulation for border edges among the edges of triangles that have been removed. Neighboring border edges are grouped into linked lists, where each list represents one hole. Returns a set of border lists [S1, unofficial].
3.  **COLLAPSE(E, p)**: Collapses a set of edges $E$ and places the resulting single point at position $p$ [S1, unofficial].

### Parameters
*   `minsplit`: Minimum number of quasi-critical points required to trigger splitting. Default: 4 [S1, unofficial].
*   `αr`: Max angle between normals of geometric points for refinement. User-defined [S1, unofficial].
*   `E`: Young’s modulus for FEM. Range: [100, 3000] [S1, unofficial].
*   `ν`: Poisson’s ratio for FEM. Value: 0.3 [S1, unofficial].
*   `emin`: Maximal edge compression. Value: 1 [S1, unofficial].
*   `emax`: Maximal edge stretching. Value: 2 [S1, unofficial].
*   `ê`: Desired mechanical edge length. User-defined [S1, unofficial].
*   `εe`: Limit for test on edge length variation. Value: 0.1 [S1, unofficial].

### Blending Algorithm
Blending detects interpenetration between two disconnected meshes sampling the same surface.

1.  **Detect**: Identify colliding triangles $T$ using `DETECTCOLLIDINGTRIANGLES()`. Collision detection is performed on geometric triangles to maximize precision [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Erase**: For each triangle $\tau \in T$:
    *   `ERASE(NEIGHBORS(τ))`
    *   `ERASE(τ)`
    This removes the colliding triangles and their neighbors from the mechanical mesh [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Find Borders**: `(B1, B2) = FINDBORDERS()`. This returns two border lists corresponding to the holes created in the two merging surfaces [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Adjust**: `(B1, B2) = ADJUST(B1, B2)`. If the border lists have different edge counts, collapse edges from the larger list until they are equal [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Merge**: Weld the borders together.
    *   Calculate barycenters $b_1 = \text{barycenter}(B_1)$ and $b_2 = \text{barycenter}(B_2)$.
    *   Define normal $u = b_1 - b_2$.
    *   Project lists onto a plane with normal $u$.
    *   Select starting points as the closest points in this projection.
    *   Walk both lists in rotating order and weld corresponding points.
    *   The resulting point is placed at the barycenter of the two original points and projected onto the implicit surface [S1, unofficial].

### Splitting Algorithm
Splitting manages the separation of a single surface into two.

1.  **Check Threshold**: Let $n$ be the number of quasi-critical points. If $n < \text{minsplit}$, abort. A **quasi-critical point** is a geometric point that could not be projected onto the implicit surface [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Calculate Splitting Plane**:
    *   Let $p_1 \dots p_n$ be the quasi-critical points.
    *   Let $\tau_1 \dots \tau_m$ be the mechanical triangles containing these points (quasi-critical triangles).
    *   Let $n_1 \dots n_m$ be the normals of these triangles.
    *   Center $p = \text{barycenter}(p_1 \dots p_n)$.
    *   Initialize normal $n = (0,0,0)$.
    *   For each pair $(i, j)$ where $1 \le i < j \le m$:
        *   $n_{ij} = \text{normalize}(n_i \times n_j)$
        *   $n = n + \text{sign}(n \cdot n_{ij}) \cdot n_{ij}$
    *   $n = \text{normalize}(n)$ [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Define Plane**: Define plane $P$ with normal $n$ passing through $p$ [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Cut**: Find ring $R$ of mechanical triangles crossing plane $P$ that contain at least one quasi-critical triangle using `RING(P, t_1 \dots t_m)`. Erase $R$ from the mechanical mesh [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Close Holes**:
    *   `(B1, B2) = FINDBORDERS()`
    *   $b_1 = \text{PROJECTPOSITION}(\text{barycenter}(B_1))$
    *   $b_2 = \text{PROJECTPOSITION}(\text{barycenter}(B_2))$
    *   `COLLAPSE(B1, b1)`
    *   `COLLAPSE(B2, b2)` [S1, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
def blending_algorithm():
    # Step 1: Detect colliding triangles on geometric mesh
    T = detect_colliding_triangles()
    
    # Step 2: Erase colliding triangles and their neighbors from mechanical mesh
    for tau in T:
        erase(neighbors(tau))
        erase(tau)
        
    # Step 3: Find borders of resulting holes
    B1, B2 = find_borders()
    
    # Step 4: Adjust border lists to have equal edge counts
    B1, B2 = adjust_borders(B1, B2)
    
    # Step 5: Merge borders
    merge_borders(B1, B2)

def splitting_algorithm():
    # Step 1: Check if enough quasi-critical points exist
    n = count_quasi_critical_points()
    if n < minsplit:
        return

    # Step 2: Calculate splitting plane normal
    p_list = get_quasi_critical_points()
    tau_list = get_quasi_critical_triangles()
    m = len(tau_list)
    
    p = barycenter(p_list)
    n_vec = Vector(0, 0, 0)
    
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(i + 1, m):
            ni = tau_list[i].normal
            nj = tau_list[j].normal
            nij = normalize(cross(ni, nj))
            sign_val = 1 if dot(n_vec, nij) >= 0 else -1
            n_vec = n_vec + sign_val * nij
            
    n_vec = normalize(n_vec)
    
    # Step 3: Define splitting plane
    plane_P = Plane(normal=n_vec, point=p)
    
    # Step 4: Cut mechanical mesh
    R = find_ring_crossing_plane(plane_P, tau_list)
    erase(R)
    
    # Step 5: Close holes
    B1, B2 = find_borders()
    
    b1 = project_to_surface(barycenter(B1))
    b2 = project_to_surface(barycenter(B2))
    
    collapse(B1, b1)
    collapse(B2, b2)

def adjust_borders(B1, B2):
    # Collapse edges from the largest list until sizes match
    while len(B1) != len(B2):
        if len(B1) > len(B2):
            collapse_edge(B1)
        else:
            collapse_edge(B2)
    return B1, B2

def merge_borders(B1, B2):
    b1 = barycenter(B1)
    b2 = barycenter(B2)
    u = b1 - b2
    
    # Project lists to plane with normal u to find starting points
    # Select closest points in projection
    start1, start2 = find_closest_points_in_projection(B1, B2, u)
    
    # Walk lists in rotating order and weld points
    # Resulting point is barycenter of original points, projected to surface
    weld_list(B1, B2, start1, start2)
```

## Subtopics

### [Twinned meshes for dynamic triangulation of implicit surfaces](23526-twinned-meshes-for-dynamic-triangulation-of-implicit-surface.md)
Decouples mechanical simulation and geometric rendering into two separate dynamic triangulations to achieve stable physics and high-detail rendering simultaneously.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity**: Collision detection is implemented using a naive $O(n^2)$ algorithm in the source, though faster algorithms are noted as available for real-time approaches [S1, unofficial].
*   **Performance**: Tested on a Pentium M 2GHz with 2GB RAM and nVidia Quadro FX Go1400 [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths**:
    *   Accuracy of blending depends on geometric mesh resolution, not the field function itself, allowing high precision [S1, unofficial].
    *   Coarse mechanical mesh resolution does not impact topology management, only geometric resolution matters [S1, unofficial].
    *   Allows larger time steps and finer visual detail without increasing computational strain on the particle system [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations**:
    *   **Blending**: Cannot trivially handle three objects blending at the exact same location, as it requires welding three border lists. A solution involves welding two and closing the third's hole, then re-performing blending [S1, unofficial].
    *   **Splitting**: Requires high geometric refinement (high $\alpha_r$) to detect triangles crossing the splitting plane, as mechanical points are constrained to the surface [S1, unofficial].
    *   **Splitting Detection**: Depends on the projection method (e.g., Newton-Raphson), which is not guaranteed for all field functions, especially $C^0$ continuous ones [S1, unofficial].
    *   **Multiple Splitting**: Extending splitting to multiple locations is harder than blending, requiring clustering of quasi-critical points (e.g., k-means) [S1, unofficial].

## Background
This method was introduced in the paper "Dynamic Triangulation of Implicit Surfaces: towards the handling of topology changes" [S1, unofficial]. It addresses the limitations of mass-spring systems, which often fail to recover from inverted elements and are constrained by stability issues that limit triangulation detail [S1, unofficial]. By using a robust Finite Element Method (FEM) for mechanics and adaptive refinement for geometry, it allows for stable simulation of complex topological changes [S1, unofficial].
