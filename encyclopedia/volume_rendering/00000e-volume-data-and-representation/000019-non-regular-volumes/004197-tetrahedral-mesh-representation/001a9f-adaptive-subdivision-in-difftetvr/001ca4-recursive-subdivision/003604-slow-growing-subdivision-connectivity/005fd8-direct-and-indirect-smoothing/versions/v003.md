# Direct and Indirect Smoothing

Also called: Stationary smoothing rule, Vertex averaging

## Overview
Direct and Indirect Smoothing is a stationary averaging rule coupled with combinatorial subdivision rules to determine the actual positions of mesh vertices [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of defining geometric continuity and function values on refined non-regular meshes by distinguishing between the placement of new vertices (direct smoothing) and the repositioning of existing vertices (indirect smoothing) [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like Generalized Linear B-Spline Wavelets by using explicit vertex averaging rather than wavelet bases to define continuity and function values [G].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A mesh with vertex positions, connectivity information, and a smoothing coefficient $\alpha$ (denoted as $a$ or $d$ in the sources) [S1, unofficial].
*   **Output:** A refined mesh with updated vertex positions $V_{new}$ and new vertices inserted at cell centers [S1, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
Not given in the sources [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **New Vertex Placement (Direct Smoothing):**
    For each cell or face being subdivided, a new vertex $v$ is inserted at the center [S1, unofficial]. Its position is the arithmetic average of the vertices defining that cell or face [S1, unofficial].
    $$ v = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i $$
    Where $N$ is the number of vertices in the cell or face, and $v_i$ are their positions [S1, unofficial].

2.  **Old Vertex Repositioning (Indirect Smoothing):**
    Each existing vertex $v$ is repositioned by linearly combining its old position with the average of its edge-connected neighbors [S1, unofficial].
    $$ V_{new} = \alpha V_{old} + (1 - \alpha) w $$
    Where $V_{old}$ is the original position of the vertex, $w$ is the average position of the vertices edge-connected to $v$, and $\alpha$ is the smoothing coefficient [S1, unofficial].

3.  **Boundary and Sharp Feature Handling:**
    For vertices on boundaries or associated with sharp features, the neighborhood used to compute the average $w$ is restricted to the vertices belonging to the specific feature or boundary [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def direct_and_indirect_smoothing(vertices, neighbors, cells, alpha):
    """
    Applies direct and indirect smoothing to a mesh.
    
    Args:
        vertices: List of 3D coordinate arrays for existing vertices.
        neighbors: List of lists, where neighbors[i] contains indices of 
                   vertices edge-connected to vertex i.
        cells: List of lists, where cells[j] contains indices of vertices 
               defining cell j.
        alpha: Smoothing coefficient (float).
        
    Returns:
        new_vertices: List of 3D coordinate arrays for newly inserted vertices.
        updated_vertices: List of 3D coordinate arrays for repositioned existing vertices.
    """
    # Step 1: New Vertex Placement (Direct Smoothing)
    new_vertices = []
    for cell in cells:
        cell_positions = [vertices[idx] for idx in cell]
        center = sum(cell_positions) / len(cell_positions)
        new_vertices.append(center)
    
    # Step 2: Old Vertex Repositioning (Indirect Smoothing)
    updated_vertices = []
    for i, v_old in enumerate(vertices):
        neighbor_indices = neighbors[i]
        if len(neighbor_indices) > 0:
            neighbor_positions = [vertices[n_idx] for n_idx in neighbor_indices]
            w = sum(neighbor_positions) / len(neighbor_positions)
        else:
            w = v_old
        v_new = alpha * v_old + (1 - alpha) * w
        updated_vertices.append(v_new)
        
    # Boundary/Sharp Feature Handling:
    # For boundary cases and sharp features, the neighborhoods for computing 
    # the average w are restricted to the features themselves [S1, unofficial].
    
    return new_vertices, updated_vertices
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** Floating-point precision errors may accumulate in deep subdivisions [G].
*   **Boundaries:** Failure to restrict neighborhoods for boundary vertices can lead to incorrect smoothing artifacts [S1, unofficial].
*   **Extraordinary Points:** In 3D, continuity analysis at extraordinary points is complex due to local connectivity structure, not just vertex degree [S1, unofficial].

## Background
The method is part of the Slow-Growing Subdivisions framework [S1, unofficial]. Velho and Zorin proved that slow subdivision surfaces with $\alpha = 0.5$ have $C^1$ continuity everywhere at regular points, with the proof based on the Zwart-Powell element box spline basis [S1, unofficial]. The scheme was generalized to dimensions higher than two to smooth functions defined on the mesh [S1, unofficial]. In 2D, this rule is equivalent to those reported in [29] because the edge-connected vertices with weight zero in the vertex masks are exactly those removed by the merge stage of the recursive subdivision [S1, unofficial].
