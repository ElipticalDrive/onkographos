# Central Difference Stencils

Also called: Central Finite Differences, Central Difference Gradient

## Overview
Central difference stencils approximate the partial derivative of a scalar field at a voxel center by computing the difference between field values at symmetric neighbors, divided by twice the step size. This method is second-order accurate, offering higher precision than forward or backward differences for smooth fields, at the cost of requiring samples from both sides of the evaluation point. It is the standard baseline for gradient computation in volume rendering when analytic gradients are unavailable or too expensive to compute.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family approximate the partial derivative $\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i}$ at a voxel center $\mathbf{p}$ using a linear combination of scalar field values $V(\mathbf{p}_k)$ at neighboring positions $\mathbf{p}_k$. The general form is:
$$ G_i = \sum_{k} w_k V(\mathbf{p}_k) $$
where $w_k$ are weights determined by the stencil geometry and step size $h$ (voxel spacing). For isotropic grids with $h=1$, the weights are typically integers or simple fractions. The gradient vector is assembled as $\nabla V = (G_x, G_y, G_z)$. Boundary handling is required when stencil neighbors fall outside the volume domain; common strategies include clamping, zero-padding, or extrapolation [G].

### Clamped Central Differences
**Definition**
A variant of central differencing for gradient computation that clamps offset positions to the current region to avoid additional ray traversals [S1].

**Steps**
1. Compute six additional basis reconstruction samples at positions offset from the current sample by a distance proportional to the region's sample rate [S1].
2. Clamp the offset positions to the current region before evaluating them [S1].

**Pitfalls**
Gradients are not necessarily continuous at level boundaries because the offset size can change [S1].

**Comparison**
Accurate central differencing requires tracing 6 additional rays per sample via infinitesimal rays to find containing regions, which is expensive. The clamped version removes ray traversals but reduces quality [S1].

### Curl Grid Computation
**Definition**
Computes the curl $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ at every active voxel of a vector grid using central differences [S2, unofficial].

**Steps**
1. Initialize a `GradStencil` with the grid tree [S2, unofficial].
2. Iterate over all active voxels $(c, \_)$ in the grid tree [S2, unofficial].
3. Move the stencil to the current coordinate $c$ [S2, unofficial].
4. Compute the x, y, and z components of the curl using the formulas and store the tuple $(c_x, c_y, c_z)$ in a dictionary keyed by coordinate [S2, unofficial].
5. Build the result grid using the computed data, a background value of zero vector $(0,0,0)$, and the original grid's voxel size [S2, unofficial].

**Formulas**
$$ c_x = (v[4][3] - v[5][3] - v[6][2] + v[7][2]) \times 0.5 $$
$$ c_y = (v[6][1] - v[7][1] - v[2][3] + v[3][3]) \times 0.5 $$
$$ c_z = (v[2][2] - v[3][2] - v[4][1] + v[5][1]) \times 0.5 $$
where $v$ represents the stencil values and $0.5$ is the scalar multiplier representing $1/(2 \times \text{voxel\_size})$ in index space [S2, unofficial].

### Hessian-Vector Product via Central Differences
**Definition**
An approximation of the Hessian-vector product $H(\theta)\mathbf{v}$ that avoids computing the full Hessian matrix [S3]. It estimates the directional gradient of the gradient, resulting in an $n$-dimensional vector [S3].

**Steps**
Evaluate the first-order gradient estimator at positions shifted from the current solution along the direction $\mathbf{v}$ by $\pm \epsilon$ [S3].

**Formulas**
$$ H(\theta)\mathbf{v} \approx \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{g(\theta + \epsilon \mathbf{v}) - g(\theta - \epsilon \mathbf{v})}{2\epsilon} $$
The smooth HVP kernel is estimated using directional central differences of the gradient estimator:
$$ D_{HVP} \kappa(\tau) = \frac{D_G \kappa(\tau - \epsilon \mathbf{v}) - D_G \kappa(\tau + \epsilon \mathbf{v})}{2\epsilon} $$
[S3]

**Complexity**
Time complexity is $O(n)$ function evaluations without importance sampling (IS), $O(n)$ with IS, and $O(1)$ with Aggregate IS. Space complexity for Aggregate IS is $O(n)$ [S3].

## Background
Central difference stencils are a fundamental numerical analysis technique widely used in scientific computing and computer graphics. In the context of volume rendering, they are employed to estimate surface normals or density gradients from discrete voxel data when analytic derivatives are not available. The method is standard in ray tracing of structured AMR data, where it is compared against analytic gradient approaches for basis reconstruction [S1]. It is also used in differentiable rendering for estimating higher-order derivatives like the Hessian-vector product, where it serves as a baseline for stochastic gradient estimation [S3]. The technique is implemented in various graphics libraries and research codebases for tasks ranging from curl grid computation to Jacobian estimation [S2, unofficial][S3].
