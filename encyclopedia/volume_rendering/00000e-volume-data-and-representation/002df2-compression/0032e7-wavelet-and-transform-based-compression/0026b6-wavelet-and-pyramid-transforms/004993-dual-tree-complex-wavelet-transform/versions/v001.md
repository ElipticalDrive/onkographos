# Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Transform

Also called: DTCWT

## Overview
The Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Transform (DTCWT) is a wavelet transform that employs two separate real Discrete Wavelet Transforms (DWTs) to compute the real and imaginary parts of the complex coefficients [S1]. Unlike standard real-valued wavelet transforms, DTCWT provides approximate shift invariance and enhanced directional selectivity in 2D and 3D by constructing complex wavelets from pairs of real wavelets [S1]. It is distinguished from its siblings, such as [Laplacian Pyramid Volume Rendering](2261-laplacian-pyramid-volume-rendering.md) and [3D Discrete Wavelet Transform](6083-3d-discrete-wavelet-transform.md), by its ability to generate oriented complex subbands that better capture directional features while maintaining near-shift invariance, which is critical for high-fidelity volume data compression and representation [S1].

## How it works
The method decomposes input data into complex coefficients using a dual-tree structure. The core mechanism involves constructing two real DWTs with distinct filter banks, where the second tree's wavelets are approximately the Hilbert transform of the first tree's wavelets [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
- **Input:** A discrete signal or volume $V$. For 2D/3D separable implementation, this is processed axis by axis.
- **Output:** A set of complex wavelet coefficients $C$. In 2D, this typically includes one approximation subband and six oriented detail subbands (three real, three imaginary) per decomposition level [S1].

### Data Structures
The transform relies on two parallel filter banks.
1.  **Upper Tree Filters:** Low-pass filter $h_0(n)$ and high-pass filter $h_1(n)$.
2.  **Lower Tree Filters:** Low-pass filter $g_0(n)$ and high-pass filter $g_1(n)$ [S1].
3.  **Wavelet Definitions:**
    -   Real wavelet from upper tree: $\psi_h(t)$ derived from $\{h_0, h_1\}$.
    -   Real wavelet from lower tree: $\psi_g(t)$ derived from $\{g_0, g_1\}$ [S1].
    -   Complex 1D wavelet: $\psi(t) = \psi_h(t) + j\psi_g(t)$, where $\psi_g(t)$ is approximately the Hilbert transform of $\psi_h(t)$ [S1].

### Numbered Steps
1.  **Construct Filter Banks:** Define two distinct real DWT filter banks (upper and lower) [S1].
2.  **Compute 1D Complex Wavelets:** For each axis, compute the complex wavelet $\psi(x) = \psi_h(x) + j\psi_g(x)$ and the complex scaling function $\phi(x) = \phi_h(x) + j\phi_g(x)$ [S1].
3.  **Form 2D Complex Wavelets:** Create separable 2D complex wavelets by tensor product: $\Psi(x,y) = \psi(x)\psi(y)$ [S1].
4.  **Expand Complex Wavelets:** Expand the product to separate real and imaginary parts. The expansion is given by:
    $$ \Psi(x, y) = [\psi_h(x) + j\psi_g(x)][\psi_h(y) + j\psi_g(y)] $$
    $$ = \psi_h(x)\psi_h(y) - \psi_g(x)\psi_g(y) + j[\psi_g(x)\psi_h(y) + \psi_h(x)\psi_g(y)] $$
    *(Note: Source [S1] presents two expansions. One expansion yields $\psi_h(x)\psi_h(y) + \psi_g(x)\psi_g(y)$ for the real part in a specific context, but the standard complex product expansion shown in Eq 11 of [S1] uses the minus sign for the real part of $\psi(x)\psi(y)$. The text states: "The spectrum of $\psi_h(x)\psi_h(y) - \psi_g(x)\psi_g(y)$ which corresponds to the real part of $\psi(x,y)$...". Another expansion in the text for a different combination yields a plus sign. We use the standard complex multiplication result for $\psi(x)\psi(y)$ as defined in Eq 11.)* [S1].
5.  **Define Separable Bases:** Define two separable 2D wavelet bases $\Psi_{1,i}(x,y)$ and $\Psi_{2,i}(x,y)$ for $i=1,2,3$ using combinations of $\phi_h, \phi_g, \psi_h, \psi_g$ [S1]:
    -   $i=1$: $\Psi_{1,1} = \phi_h(x)\psi_h(y)$, $\Psi_{2,1} = \phi_g(x)\psi_g(y)$
    -   $i=2$: $\Psi_{1,2} = \psi_h(x)\phi_h(y)$, $\Psi_{2,2} = \psi_g(x)\phi_g(y)$
    -   $i=3$: $\Psi_{1,3} = \psi_h(x)\psi_h(y)$, $\Psi_{2,3} = \psi_g(x)\psi_g(y)$ [S1].
    -   For the imaginary part bases $\Psi_{3,i}, \Psi_{4,i}$:
    -   $i=1$: $\Psi_{3,1} = \phi_g(x)\psi_h(y)$, $\Psi_{4,1} = \phi_h(x)\psi_g(y)$
    -   $i=2$: $\Psi_{3,2} = \psi_g(x)\phi_h(y)$, $\Psi_{4,2} = \psi_h(x)\phi_g(y)$
    -   $i=3$: $\Psi_{3,3} = \psi_g(x)\psi_h(y)$, $\Psi_{4,3} = \psi_h(x)\psi_g(y)$ [S1].
6.  **Compute Oriented Real Wavelets:** Construct six oriented real 2D wavelets from the real and imaginary parts using sum/difference operations with normalization factor $\sqrt{1/2}$ [S1]:
    -   From Real Part ($\Psi_{1,i}, \Psi_{2,i}$):
        $$ \psi_i(x, y) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} (\Psi_{1,i}(x, y) - \Psi_{2,i}(x, y)) $$
        $$ \psi_{i+3}(x, y) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} (\Psi_{1,i}(x, y) + \Psi_{2,i}(x, y)) $$
        for $i=1, 2, 3$ [S1].
    -   From Imaginary Part ($\Psi_{3,i}, \Psi_{4,i}$):
        $$ \psi_i(x, y) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} (\Psi_{3,i}(x, y) + \Psi_{4,i}(x, y)) $$
        $$ \psi_{i+3}(x, y) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} (\Psi_{3,i}(x, y) - \Psi_{4,i}(x, y)) $$
        for $i=1, 2, 3$ [S1].
7.  **Apply Transform:** Convolve the input volume with these filters and downsample by 2 at each level to produce the coefficient tree [S1].

    """
    Performs a 2D Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Transform.
    
    Args:
        volume: 2D array of input data.
        upper_filters: Tuple (h0, h1) for low-pass and high-pass of upper tree.
        lower_filters: Tuple (g0, g1) for low-pass and high-pass of lower tree.

    """
    # Step 1: Filter and Downsample Upper Tree
    # h0 is low-pass, h1 is high-pass
    upper_low = downsample(convolve(volume, h0))
    upper_high = downsample(convolve(volume, h1))
    
    # Step 2: Filter and Downsample Lower Tree
    # g0 is low-pass, g1 is high-pass
    lower_low = downsample(convolve(volume, g0))
    lower_high = downsample(convolve(volume, g1))
    
    # Step 3: Construct 1D Complex Wavelets (Conceptual)
    # In practice, the 2D transform is separable.
    # We apply the 1D complex filter bank along rows, then columns.
    
    # Apply 1D DTCWT along rows
    # Row Low: Upper Low + j * Lower Low
    # Row High: Upper High + j * Lower High
    row_low_real = upper_low
    row_low_imag = lower_low
    row_high_real = upper_high
    row_high_imag = lower_high
    
    # Apply 1D DTCWT along columns of the row-transformed data
    # This creates complex coefficients.
    # Let C be the complex coefficient matrix after row transform.
    # C = C_real + j * C_imag
    
    # Column Transform on Real Part
    col_low_real = downsample(convolve(row_low_real, h0))
    col_high_real = downsample(convolve(row_low_real, h1))
    
    # Column Transform on Imaginary Part
    col_low_imag = downsample(convolve(row_low_imag, g0))
    col_high_imag = downsample(convolve(row_low_imag, g1))
    
    # Combine to get final complex coefficients
    # Approximation (Low-Low)
    approx_real = col_low_real
    approx_imag = col_low_imag

    # For implementation simplicity matching [S1]'s basis definition:
    # We need to compute the specific combinations defined in steps 5-6.
    
    # Define the 6 basis functions based on the filter outputs
    # Note: This pseudocode assumes the filters h0,h1,g0,g1 are designed to satisfy
    # the half-sample shift property for approximate Hilbert transform.
    
    # Return the complex coefficients
    return {
        'approx': approx_real + 1j * approx_imag,

        # from the row and column transforms.

def convolve(data, kernel):
    # Standard 2D convolution
    return scipy.signal.convolve2d(data, kernel, mode='same')

def downsample(data):
    # Keep every second element
    return data[::2, ::2]

The exact mapping from the 4 basic complex subbands (LL, LH, HL, HH) to the 6 oriented real subbands described in [S1] requires specific linear combinations of the real and imaginary parts of these subbands as detailed in the formulas. A full implementation would compute the 4 complex subbands first, then apply the $\sqrt{1/2}$ sum/difference operations to generate the 6 real oriented bands.*

### Parameters
-   **Wavelet Level:** Set to 1 in experiments to balance performance, training time, and model size [S1]. Increasing levels does not significantly improve performance but increases training time and model size [S1].
-   **Basis Numbers ($R_1, R_2, R_3$):** Used for direction-aware representation in planes (XY-ZT, XZ-YT, YZ-XT).
    -   Appearance: $R_1=48, R_2=48, R_3=48$ [S1].
    -   Opacity: $R_1=24, R_2=24, R_3=24$ [S1].
-   **Coordinate Boundaries:** Scene modeled using Normalized Device Coordinates (NDC) with min boundaries $[-2.5, -2.0, -1.0]$ and max boundaries $[2.5, 2.0, 1.0]$ [S1].
-   **Mask Loss Weight ($\lambda_m$):** Controls training data sparsity. Tested values include $-10, -11, 0$ [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
-   **Filter Design:** The lower tree filters must be designed such that $\psi_g(t)$ is approximately the Hilbert transform of $\psi_h(t)$. Failure to do so results in loss of shift invariance and directional selectivity [S1].
-   **Boundary Effects:** Standard DWT boundary handling (extension) can introduce artifacts. The complex nature of DTCWT requires consistent boundary handling across both trees to maintain the phase relationship [S1].
-   **Precision:** The use of complex arithmetic doubles the computational cost compared to real DWT. Precision issues in the imaginary part can affect the orthogonality of the wavelets [S1].

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time Complexity:** Higher than real DWT due to the dual-tree structure and complex arithmetic. The separation into two real DWTs means roughly 2x the filtering operations, plus the combination steps [S1].
-   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing coefficients for both trees. The complex coefficients effectively store twice the information of a real coefficient, but the redundancy is used for shift invariance [S1].
-   **Performance:** In the context of dynamic scene representation (DaReNeRF), DTCWT-based methods achieved PSNR of 30.294 (Flame Salmon) and 32.630 (Cook Spinach) on the Plenoptic Video Dataset, and 25.71 (Hell Warrior) and 34.08 (Bouncing Balls) on the D-NeRF Dataset [S1].
-   **Scalability:** Increasing the wavelet level beyond 1 does not significantly improve performance but substantially increases training time and model size [S1].
-   **Strengths:** Enhanced directional selectivity (6 orientations in 2D vs 3 in standard DWT) and approximate shift invariance [S1].
-   **Limitations:** Higher computational cost and memory usage compared to real DWT. Filter design is more complex [S1].

## Background
The Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Transform (DTCWT) was introduced to address the limitations of the standard Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), specifically its lack of shift invariance and limited directional selectivity in 2D and 3D [S1]. The method employs two real DWTs to compute the real and imaginary parts of the transform, allowing for the construction of complex wavelets that are approximately analytic [S1]. It has been applied in various signal processing tasks and, more recently, in volume data representation for dynamic scenes, such as in the DaReNeRF framework [S1].
