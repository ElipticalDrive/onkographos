# Point-wise Inverse Discrete Wavelet Transform (PW-IDWT) via Generalized Projection

Also called: GP of sample, Generalized Projection (GP), PW-IDWT, Point-wise Inverse Discrete Wavelet Transform

## Overview
The Point-wise Inverse Discrete Wavelet Transform (PW-IDWT) via Generalized Projection (GP) is a method to reconstruct a single signal sample (or a set of samples) from a wavelet decomposition by determining the minimal set of subband coefficients required [S1]. It solves the problem of identifying which coefficients at a coarser level contribute to a specific sample at a finer level during synthesis, exploiting the recursive nature of the lifting scheme [S1]. Unlike methods that decode entire subbands, this approach calculates the exact "Generalized Projection" of a sample into the transformed domain, minimizing the number of subband images to decode [S1]. It builds on [Wavelet-based Volume Rendering](1052-wavelet-based-volume-rendering.md) by providing an efficient, sample-specific inverse transform mechanism.

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Wavelet decomposition levels $L$ (where $L=0$ is the coarsest).
*   Target sample position $k$ in the finest resolution signal domain (level $L$).
*   Filter type parameters: causal length $l_s$ (or $t_l$), anti-causal length $l_s^+$ (or $l_t^+$), and step type $s$ (update) or $t$ (prediction).
*   Parity of sample $k$: $a=0$ for even, $a=1$ for odd.

**Outputs:**
*   The set of coefficient indices required for reconstruction, defined by boundaries $b_j^{a,-}, b_j^{a,+}, b_j^{d,-}, b_j^{d,+}$ for each subband $j$ (approximation $a$ or detail $d$) at each level.

### Data Structures
The method tracks boundaries for the Generalized Projection (GP) at each lifting step.
*   $\hat{b}_a^{i,-}, \hat{b}_a^{i,+}$: Lower and upper boundary indices for the approximation subband at lifting step $i$.
*   $\hat{b}_d^{i,-}, \hat{b}_d^{i,+}$: Lower and upper boundary indices for the detail subband at lifting step $i$.
*   $b_j^{a,-}, b_j^{a,+}, b_j^{d,-}, b_j^{d,+}$: Final boundaries for subband $j$ after $m$ lifting steps.

### Numbered Steps
1.  **Initialize**: For the target sample position $k$ at level $L$, determine if $k$ is even or odd ($a=0$ or $a=1$). This determines the specific sequence of lifting steps (prediction/update) in the synthesis chain [S1]. Initialize the GP boundaries for the finest level. Typically, for a single sample, the initial range is $[k, k]$ for the approximation subband at the target level, but the algorithm climbs *back* from the coarsest level. The source describes climbing back from the coarsest scale $l=L$ down to $0$ [S1].
2.  **Iterate Coarsest to Finest**: Iterate for $l = L-1$ down to $0$. At each finer resolution, there is a set of samples to be recovered $\text{GP}(l, j)_k$ instead of a single one [S1].
3.  **Process Lifting Steps**: For each lifting step $i$ in the synthesis chain (from coarsest to finest), update the boundary indices $\hat{b}_a$ and $\hat{b}_d$ using min/max updating rules based on the filter type (prediction or update) and filter lengths [S1].
    *   If the step is an **Update step** (type $s$):
        *   $\hat{b}_a^{i+1,-} = \hat{b}_a^{i,-}$
        *   $\hat{b}_a^{i+1,+} = \hat{b}_a^{i,+}$
        *   $\hat{b}_d^{i+1,-} = \min\{\hat{b}_d^{i,-}, \hat{b}_a^{i,-} + l_s\}$
        *   $\hat{b}_d^{i+1,+} = \max\{\hat{b}_d^{i,+}, \hat{b}_d^{i,+} + l_s^+\}$ [S1]
    *   If the step is a **Prediction step** (type $t$):
        *   $\hat{b}_a^{i+1,-} = \min\{\hat{b}_a^{i,-}, \hat{b}_d^{i,-} + t_l\}$
        *   $\hat{b}_a^{i+1,+} = \max\{\hat{b}_a^{i,+}, \hat{b}_d^{i,+} + l_t^+\}$
        *   $\hat{b}_d^{i+1,-} = \hat{b}_d^{i,-}$
        *   $\hat{b}_d^{i+1,+} = \hat{b}_d^{i,+}$ [S1]
4.  **Finalize Boundaries**: After completing all $m$ lifting steps, assign the final boundaries $b_j^{a,-}, b_j^{a,+}, b_j^{d,-}, b_j^{d,+}$ based on the final values of $\hat{b}_j^{m}$ and $\hat{b}_j^{m+1}$ [S1]:
    *   $b_j^{a,-} = \hat{b}_j^{m,-}$
    *   $b_j^{a,+} = \hat{b}_j^{m,+}$
    *   $b_j^{d,-} = \hat{b}_j^{m+1,-}$
    *   $b_j^{d,+} = \hat{b}_j^{m+1,+}$ [S1]
5.  **Set of Samples**: To find the GP for a set of samples, recursively compute the GP for individual samples in the approximation subband and take the union of their resulting coefficient sets [S1]:
    $$ \text{GP}^\mu(l+1, j) = \cup_k \text{GP}^\mu(l+1, j)_k \quad \forall k \in \text{GP}(l, j)^a $$
    where $\text{GP}(l, j)^a$ is the set of samples in the approximation subband at level $l$ [S1].

### Formulas
**IDWT Reconstruction:**
The approximation subband at finer level $l-1$ is reconstructed from coarser level $l$ coefficients:
$$ a(l-1, k) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} h(k-2n)a(l, n) + \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} g(k-2n)d(l, n) $$
where $a$ and $d$ are approximation and detail subbands, and $h, g$ are synthesis filters [S1].

**Total Subbands for 3D:**
For 3D reconstruction (filtering on x, y, z, assuming 2-D images stacked along z), the total number of subband images $N_k$ needed for reconstruction of image $k$ is:
$$ N_k = 4 \times [\text{GP}(L, a)_k + \text{GP}(L, d)_k] + \sum_{l=L-1}^1 [3 \times \text{GP}(l, a)_k + 4 \times \text{GP}(l, d)_k] $$
[ S1]

**Boundary Updating Rules:**
*   Update Step ($s$):
    $$ \hat{b}_d^{i+1,-} = \min\{\hat{b}_d^{i,-}, \hat{b}_a^{i,-} + l_s\} $$
    $$ \hat{b}_d^{i+1,+} = \max\{\hat{b}_d^{i,+}, \hat{b}_d^{i,+} + l_s^+\} $$
    [S1]
*   Prediction Step ($t$):
    $$ \hat{b}_a^{i+1,-} = \min\{\hat{b}_a^{i,-}, \hat{b}_d^{i,-} + t_l\} $$
    $$ \hat{b}_a^{i+1,+} = \max\{\hat{b}_a^{i,+}, \hat{b}_d^{i,+} + l_t^+\} $$
    [S1]

### Parameters
*   **Filter Choice**: The 5-3 filter (interpolating, two-step chain) is recommended to minimize the number of subband images to decode [S1].
*   **Parity Index $a$**: Distinguishes between even ($a=0$) and odd ($a=1$) positions, as the set of lifting steps differs [S1].
*   **Filter Lengths**: $l_s, l_s^+$ for update steps; $t_l, l_t^+$ for prediction steps [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Integer DWT Rounding Noise**: Integer DWT introduces rounding noise proportional to the number of rounding operations (decomposition depth and lifting chain length), which degrades rate/distortion performance and causes PSNR oscillation along the axis [S1].
*   **Boundary Conditions**: Special handling is required for signal boundaries. The source notes a sign inversion $b_d = -b_d$ for detail subband boundaries during prediction steps in some contexts [S1].
*   **Periodicity**: The number of samples required in each subband is a periodic function of the sample position $k$ with period 2 [S1].

### Pseudocode
```python
def compute_gp_boundaries(sample_k, decomposition_level_L, filter_params):
    """
    Computes the boundaries of the Generalized Projection (GP) for a single sample.
    
    Args:
        sample_k: int, position of the sample in the finest resolution signal.
        decomposition_level_L: int, total number of decomposition levels.
        filter_params: dict, containing 'l_s', 'l_s_plus', 't_l', 't_l_plus', 
                       and 'steps' (list of step types: 's' for update, 't' for prediction).
    
    Returns:
        dict: Boundaries { 'a_minus', 'a_plus', 'd_minus', 'd_plus' } for each level.
    """
    # Determine parity
    parity = 0 if sample_k % 2 == 0 else 1
    
    # Initialize boundaries for the coarsest level (level L)
    # The GP starts as a single point at the coarsest level for the approximation subband
    # Note: The source implies climbing back from coarsest to finest.
    # At level L, we only have the approximation subband coefficient corresponding to k.
    # However, the algorithm tracks boundaries through the lifting steps.
    # Let's assume we start with the boundaries at the output of the coarsest level synthesis.
    
    # Initialize current boundaries for approximation and detail subbands
    # At the coarsest level L, the "sample" is just the coefficient itself.
    # The boundaries are effectively [k, k] for the approximation subband at level L.
    # The detail subband boundaries are not yet relevant or are empty.
    
    current_a_minus = sample_k
    current_a_plus = sample_k
    current_d_minus = None # Not applicable at coarsest level for a single sample start
    current_d_plus = None
    
    # Store boundaries for each level
    gp_boundaries = {}
    
    # Iterate from coarsest level L-1 down to 0
    # The source says: "Iterate the procedure for l = L-1, down to 0"
    # And "climb back the synthesis filter-bank from its output"
    # The synthesis chain has m steps.
    
    steps = filter_params['steps']
    m = len(steps)
    
    # We need to track boundaries through each lifting step
    # Let's simulate the lifting steps for the current level transition
    
    for l in range(L - 1, -1, -1):
        # For each level, we process the lifting steps
        # The boundaries are updated through the lifting steps
        
        # Initialize step boundaries
        step_a_minus = current_a_minus
        step_a_plus = current_a_plus
        step_d_minus = current_d_minus
        step_d_plus = current_d_plus
        
        for i in range(m):
            step_type = steps[i]
            
            if step_type == 's': # Update step
                # Update approximation boundaries (unchanged)
                new_a_minus = step_a_minus
                new_a_plus = step_a_plus
                
                # Update detail boundaries
                if step_d_minus is not None:
                    new_d_minus = min(step_d_minus, step_a_minus + filter_params['l_s'])
                else:
                    new_d_minus = step_a_minus + filter_params['l_s']
                    
                if step_d_plus is not None:
                    new_d_plus = max(step_d_plus, step_d_plus + filter_params['l_s_plus'])
                else:
                    new_d_plus = step_d_plus + filter_params['l_s_plus'] # Placeholder if d was None
                    
                step_a_minus = new_a_minus
                step_a_plus = new_a_plus
                step_d_minus = new_d_minus
                step_d_plus = new_d_plus
                
            elif step_type == 't': # Prediction step
                # Update detail boundaries (unchanged)
                new_d_minus = step_d_minus
                new_d_plus = step_d_plus
                
                # Update approximation boundaries
                if step_d_minus is not None:
                    new_a_minus = min(step_a_minus, step_d_minus + filter_params['t_l'])
                else:
                    new_a_minus = step_a_minus # Or handle appropriately
                    
                if step_d_plus is not None:
                    new_a_plus = max(step_a_plus, step_d_plus + filter_params['t_l_plus'])
                else:
                    new_a_plus = step_a_plus # Or handle appropriately
                    
                step_a_minus = new_a_minus
                step_a_plus = new_a_plus
                step_d_minus = new_d_minus
                step_d_plus = new_d_plus
        
        # After all lifting steps for this level, assign final boundaries
        # The source says: b_j^{a,-} = \hat{b}_j^{m,-}, etc.
        # Here, step_a_minus corresponds to \hat{b}_j^{m,-}
        
        gp_boundaries[l] = {
            'a_minus': step_a_minus,
            'a_plus': step_a_plus,
            'd_minus': step_d_minus,
            'd_plus': step_d_plus
        }
        
        # Update current boundaries for the next iteration (finer level)
        current_a_minus = step_a_minus
        current_a_plus = step_a_plus
        current_d_minus = step_d_minus
        current_d_plus = step_d_plus
        
    return gp_boundaries

def compute_gp_for_set(samples, decomposition_level_L, filter_params):
    """
    Computes the GP for a set of samples by taking the union of individual GPs.
    """
    all_boundaries = {}
    for k in samples:
        boundaries = compute_gp_boundaries(k, decomposition_level_L, filter_params)
        for l, b in boundaries.items():
            if l not in all_boundaries:
                all_boundaries[l] = {'a_minus': float('inf'), 'a_plus': float('-inf'), 
                                     'd_minus': float('inf'), 'd_plus': float('-inf')}
            all_boundaries[l]['a_minus'] = min(all_boundaries[l]['a_minus'], b['a_minus'])
            all_boundaries[l]['a_plus'] = max(all_boundaries[l]['a_plus'], b['a_plus'])
            if b['d_minus'] is not None:
                all_boundaries[l]['d_minus'] = min(all_boundaries[l]['d_minus'], b['d_minus'])
            if b['d_plus'] is not None:
                all_boundaries[l]['d_plus'] = max(all_boundaries[l]['d_plus'], b['d_plus'])
    return all_boundaries
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: The method is efficient for sparse reconstruction as it only computes the necessary coefficients. The complexity is proportional to the number of lifting steps and the depth of decomposition, which is minimal for short filters like 5-3 [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity**: Low, as it does not require storing or decoding entire subbands, only the specific coefficients identified by the GP [S1].
*   **Performance**: The 5-3 filter requires significantly fewer coefficients than the 9/7 filter. For L=3, the 5-3 filter requires 9-15 samples per 2D slice, while 9/7 requires 19-37. This reduces decoding time and power consumption [S1].
*   **Strengths**: Minimizes the number of subband images to decode, reducing computational load and power consumption. Ideal for applications where only specific samples or regions of interest need to be reconstructed [S1].
*   **Limitations**: Integer DWT introduces rounding noise proportional to the number of rounding operations, which can degrade quality, especially for deep decompositions [S1]. The method is sensitive to the position of the sample due to periodicity and boundary effects [S1].

## Background
The Point-wise Inverse Discrete Wavelet Transform (PW-IDWT) via Generalized Projection (GP) was introduced in the context of three-dimensional encoding/two-dimensional decoding of medical data [S1]. The method exploits the recursive nature of the wavelet transform and the separability of the transform to map the problem to a one-dimensional case [S1]. It is particularly useful for applications where computational resources are limited, such as mobile or embedded systems, due to its efficiency in decoding only the necessary coefficients [S1]. The choice of the 5-3 filter is motivated by its short length and two-step chain, which minimizes the number of subband images to decode [S1].
