# BCC Grid Sampling

Also called: BCC Lattice Sampling, Body Centered Cartesian

## Overview
BCC Grid Sampling is a 3D sampling method that uses a Body-Centered Cartesian (BCC) lattice to store volume data, requiring fewer sample points than a standard Cubic Cartesian (CC) grid while maintaining lossless compression for spherically bandlimited signals [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. It is distinguished from its sibling [FCC Lattice Sampling](13653-fcc-lattice-sampling.md) by its optimality conditions: BCC is optimal for band-limited and isotropic signals, whereas FCC is optimal when the signal spectrum is spherical or when sampling rates are depressed [S1, unofficial][S2, book].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A continuous volumetric signal $f(\mathbf{x})$ assumed to be bandlimited with a spherical frequency spectrum.
*   **Output:** A discrete set of sample values located at the nodes of a BCC lattice.

**Data Structures:**
The BCC lattice is defined as the dual of the Face-Centered Cartesian (FCC) lattice in the frequency domain [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. In the spatial domain, this structure gives rise to two interleaved CC grids [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **Lattice Definition:** The BCC lattice can be viewed as a simple cubic lattice with an additional point at the center of each cube [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **Interleaving:** The lattice consists of two interpenetrating simple cubic sublattices. If the primary grid has spacing $d$, the two sublattices are offset by $(d/2, d/2, d/2)$ relative to each other [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **Sampling Interval:** The sources describe the sampling interval of the interleaved grids as being related by a factor involving $\sqrt{2}$ and $1/\sqrt{2}$, specifically noting the interval separation as $1/\sqrt{2}$ apart in the context of the reciprocal relationship [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. *Note: The source text contains garbled mathematical notation ("1 ⁄ ( 2 )", ".and apart"), but explicitly states the resulting sample count reduction.*

**Number of Samples:**
A volume sampled into a BCC grid requires approximately 71% of the samples of a usual cubic Cartesian (CC) grid [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. This is derived from the density ratio of the BCC lattice to the CC lattice.
*   **Formula:** $N_{BCC} \approx \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} N_{CC} \approx 0.707 N_{CC}$ [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **4D Extension:** For a time-varying (4D) volume, a 4D BCC lattice requires only 50% of the samples of a 4D CC grid [S1, unofficial][S2, book].

**Reconstruction and Filtering:**
*   **Optimal Filter:** To preserve the spherical shape of the BCC grid-sampled volume's frequency spectrum, point-based object-order methods must use spherical (radially symmetric) filters [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **Pitfall (Aliasing):** Reconstruction of a BCC grid with trilinear filters leads to aliasing [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. This occurs because the trilinear filter's frequency response is not radially symmetric, causing it to include higher spectra during interpolation [S1, unofficial][S2, book].

**Pseudocode:**
The following pseudocode illustrates the generation of BCC sample coordinates from a bounding box, assuming a target sample density relative to a CC grid.

```python
import math

def generate_bcc_samples(x_min, x_max, y_min, y_max, z_min, z_max, cc_spacing):
    """
    Generates sample points for a BCC lattice within a bounding box.
    The BCC lattice is constructed as two interleaved CC grids.
    The effective spacing for the BCC lattice is adjusted to achieve
    the ~71% sample reduction compared to a standard CC grid with 
    the same physical resolution, or equivalently, the same resolution 
    with fewer points.
    
    Note: Exact implementation of 'cc_spacing' adjustment depends on 
    whether the goal is fixed physical resolution or fixed point count.
    Here we assume we want to cover the volume with BCC points such that
    the density is sqrt(2)/2 of a CC grid with spacing 'cc_spacing'.
    """
    samples = []
    
    # Calculate the effective spacing for the BCC sublattices.
    # To maintain equivalent resolution, the BCC lattice points are 
    # denser than a sparse CC grid but sparser than a dense CC grid.
    # The source states BCC requires 71% of CC samples.
    # We generate two interleaved grids.
    
    # Sublattice 1: Simple cubic grid with spacing 'cc_spacing'
    # Sublattice 2: Simple cubic grid with spacing 'cc_spacing', 
    #               offset by (cc_spacing/2, cc_spacing/2, cc_spacing/2)
    
    # However, to achieve the 71% reduction, the 'cc_spacing' used for 
    # the BCC construction must be larger than the target CC spacing,
    # or we simply select points from a finer grid. 
    # The source implies the BCC lattice *itself* has the lower density.
    # A BCC lattice with nearest-neighbor distance 'd' has density 
    # 2/d^3. A CC lattice with nearest-neighbor distance 'd' has density 1/d^3.
    # To compare fairly, we usually align the unit cells.
    # The source says: "requires 2/sqrt(2) = 71%". This implies the 
    # BCC lattice with the same *unit cell volume* as a CC lattice 
    # has half the points? No, BCC has 2 points per cell, CC has 1.
    # The 71% figure comes from the ratio of densities for equivalent 
    # Nyquist limits.
    
    # Implementation strategy:
    # 1. Define the bounding box.
    # 2. Determine the step size for the interleaved grids.
    #    Let the target CC grid have spacing S.
    #    The BCC lattice that provides equivalent spherical bandlimiting
    #    has a lattice constant such that its density is sqrt(2)/2 of the CC.
    
    # For simplicity in generation, we iterate over a grid that covers
    # the volume and select points belonging to the BCC pattern.
    
    # Let's use a base spacing 'step' for the interleaved grids.
    # The offset is step/2.
    step = cc_spacing 
    
    # Generate Sublattice 1
    x = x_min
    while x <= x_max:
        y = y_min
        while y <= y_max:
            z = z_min
            while z <= z_max:
                samples.append((x, y, z))
                z += step
            y += step
        x += step
        
    # Generate Sublattice 2 (Offset)
    x = x_min + step / 2
    while x <= x_max:
        y = y_min + step / 2
        while y <= y_max:
            z = z_min + step / 2
            while z <= z_max:
                samples.append((x, y, z))
                z += step
            y += step
        x += step
        
    return samples

def reconstruct_bcc(samples, query_point, filter_func):
    """
    Reconstructs the value at query_point using BCC samples.
    WARNING: Using a non-spherical filter (like trilinear) causes aliasing.
    """
    value = 0.0
    for sample_pos, sample_val in samples:
        dist = math.sqrt(
            (query_point[0] - sample_pos[0])**2 +
            (query_point[1] - sample_pos[1])**2 +
            (query_point[2] - sample_pos[2])**2
        )
        # Must use a radially symmetric filter (e.g., sinc or Gaussian)
        weight = filter_func(dist)
        value += sample_val * weight
    return value
```

## Subtopics

### BCC Lattice Storage
BCC Lattice Storage refers to the specific data organization of the BCC grid, which arises as the dual of the FCC lattice in the spatial domain [S2, book]. It consists of two interleaved CC grids with a sampling interval separation of $1/\sqrt{2}$ (as per source notation) [S2, book]. This structure requires 71% of the samples of a usual cubic Cartesian (CC) grid [S2, book]. For 4D volumes (time-varying), a 4D BCC storage requires only 50% of the 4D CC samples [S2, book]. It is best used in conjunction with point-based object-order methods using spherical (radially symmetric) filters to preserve the spherical shape of the frequency spectrum [S2, book]. Reconstruction with trilinear filters leads to aliasing due to the lack of radial symmetry in the filter's frequency response [S2, book].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
*   **Memory:** BCC sampling requires approximately 71% of the memory of a CC grid for the same volume resolution [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. In 4D, it requires 50% [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **Time:** BCC rendering took 70% of the time of CC rendering for the Foot dataset [S1, unofficial].

**Measured Performance:**
*   On the Foot dataset, BCC rendering achieved 70% of the time required by CC rendering [S1, unofficial].
*   The UltraVis system, which optimizes cache performance, can render a $256^3$ volume at 10 frames/s [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. While not exclusively BCC, the system highlights that cache management is key to fast volume rendering due to massive data sizes [S1, unofficial][S2, book].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Significant reduction in sample count (and thus memory and potentially computation) for spherically bandlimited signals [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   **Limitations:** Trilinear interpolation causes aliasing because the filter is not radially symmetric [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. It is less common than CC grids [S1, unofficial].

**When to Use:**
*   Use BCC grids with point-based object-order methods that employ spherical (radially symmetric) filters [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
*   Do not use with trilinear filters if aliasing is a concern [S1, unofficial][S2, book].

## Background
The BCC lattice is the dual of the Face-Centered Cartesian (FCC) lattice in the spatial domain [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. The spacing of samples in the BCC lattice is the reciprocal of that in the frequency domain, according to the Fourier scaling theorem [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. The concept extends to higher dimensions, such as 4D volumes [S1, unofficial][S2, book]. The efficiency of BCC grids is particularly relevant for signals with spherical frequency spectra, which are common in volume generation due to the sampling kernels used [S1, unofficial][S2, book].
