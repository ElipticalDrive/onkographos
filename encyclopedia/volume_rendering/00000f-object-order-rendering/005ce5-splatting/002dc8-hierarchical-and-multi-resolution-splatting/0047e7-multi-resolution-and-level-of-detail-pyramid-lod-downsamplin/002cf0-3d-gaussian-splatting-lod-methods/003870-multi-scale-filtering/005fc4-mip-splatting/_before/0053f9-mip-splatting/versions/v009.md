# MIP-Splatting

Also called: Alias-free 3D Gaussian Splatting, Anti-Aliased 3D Gaussian Splatting, MS 3DGS, MS-GS, Mip 3D Gaussian Splatting, Multi-Scale Gaussians, Multi-scale 3D Gaussian Splatting

## Overview
MIP-Splatting is an extension of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that eliminates aliasing artifacts and dilation/erosion effects caused by resolution changes, focal length variations, or camera distance changes [S1][S8, unofficial]. It addresses the mismatch between the discrete sampling of the image sensor and the continuous representation of 3D Gaussians by introducing a 3D smoothing filter and a 2D Mip filter [S8, unofficial]. Unlike standard 3DGS, which suffers from high-frequency artifacts when zooming out or changing focal length, MIP-Splatting constrains the representation frequency to the Nyquist limit of the training data and approximates the physical box filter of a camera sensor during rendering [S4][S8, unofficial].

## How it works
MIP-Splatting operates in two phases: a training phase that constructs a multi-scale representation and applies frequency constraints, and a rendering phase that applies screen-space filtering.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of calibrated images with known camera poses $(R, t)$ and intrinsics; an initial point cloud.
*   **Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussians $\{G_k\}$, each defined by center $p_k$, covariance $\Sigma_k$, opacity $\alpha_k$, and spherical harmonics coefficients for color $c_k$.

### Data Structures
Each Gaussian $G_k$ stores:
*   $p_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Center position.
*   $\Sigma_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix, parameterized as $\Sigma_k = O_k S_k S_k^T O_k^T$, where $O_k$ is a rotation matrix (from quaternion) and $S_k = \text{diag}(s_{k,x}, s_{k,y}, s_{k,z})$ is a scaling vector [S8, unofficial].
*   $\alpha_k \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
*   $c_k$: Color coefficients (Spherical Harmonics).
*   $l_k \in \{1, \dots, L\}$: Level of detail index, where $L=4$ levels are typically used (1x, 4x, 16x, 64x downsampled resolution) [S3].
*   $S_{k,\max}, S_{k,\min}$: Maximum and minimum pixel coverage thresholds for selective rendering [S3].

### 3D Smoothing Filter (Training)
To prevent the optimization from creating Gaussians with frequencies higher than the training data can support, a 3D low-pass filter is applied to the 3D Gaussian primitives [S8, unofficial].

1.  **Frequency Bound Derivation:** For each Gaussian $k$, derive the maximal sampling frequency $\hat{\nu}_k$ induced by the training views. The world space sampling interval $\hat{T}$ at depth $d$ for focal length $f$ is $\hat{T} = d/f$. The sampling frequency is $\hat{\nu} = f/d$ [S8, unofficial].
2.  **Filter Application:** Convolve the 3D Gaussian $G_k$ with a 3D Gaussian low-pass filter $G_{low}$ having covariance $\hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I$, where $s$ is a scalar hyperparameter controlling filter size (typical value $s=0.2$) [S8, unofficial].
3.  **Regularized Covariance:** The regularized 3D Gaussian $G_k(x)_{reg}$ has covariance $\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I$:
    $$ G_k(x)_{reg} = \frac{\sqrt{|\Sigma_k|}}{\sqrt{|\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I|}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-p_k)^T (\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I)^{-1} (x-p_k)} $$
    This constrains the maximal frequency of the 3D representation to meet the Nyquist limit [S8, unofficial]. The maximal sampling rate is recomputed every $m$ iterations (typical $m=100$) [S8, unofficial].

### Multi-Scale Aggregation (Training)
To handle aliasing at low resolutions without losing detail, small Gaussians are aggregated into larger ones at coarser levels [S3].

1.  **Initialization:** All Gaussians start at level $l=1$. Densification (splitting/cloning) inherits the level [S3].
2.  **Warm-up:** Training proceeds for the first 1,000 iterations without aggregation [S3].
3.  **Aggregation Trigger:** After warm-up, for each coarse level $l_m$ from 2 to $L$:
    a.  Render all Gaussians from levels $[1, l_m-1]$ at $4^{l_m-1}$ times downsampled resolution for all training cameras to calculate pixel coverage $S_k$ [S3].
    b.  Select "small" Gaussians where $S_k < S_T$ (threshold $S_T=2$ pixels) [S3].
    c.  Bin selected Gaussians into a voxel grid of resolution $(400/l_m)^3$ [S3].
    d.  For each voxel, perform average pooling on position, rotation, SH features, opacity, and scaling of contained Gaussians to create a new Gaussian $G_{new}$ [S3].
    e.  Calculate average pixel coverage $S_{avg}$ of the aggregated Gaussians. Enlarge the scaling of $G_{new}$ by factor $S_T / S_{avg}$ so its pixel coverage targets $S_T$ [S3].
    f.  Assign $G_{new}$ to level $l_m$ [S3].
4.  **Multi-Scale Training:** Train the model on both original and downsampled images [S3].

### Selective Rendering (Training/Inference)
Gaussians are selected based on their pixel coverage $S_k$ relative to their stored bounds $S_{k,\max}$ and $S_{k,\min}$ [S3].

1.  **Update Bounds:** If the rendering downsample scale equals the scale at which $G_k$ was created, update bounds:
    $$ S_{k,\max}' = \max(\lambda_1 S_{k,\max}, S_k), \quad S_{k,\min}' = \min(\lambda_2 S_{k,\min}, S_k) $$
    where $\lambda_1 = 0.95$ and $\lambda_2 = 1.05$ [S3].
2.  **Selection Condition:** A Gaussian $G_k$ is rendered if:
    $$ \frac{S_k}{S_{k,\max}} \le S_{rel,\max} \quad \land \quad \left( \frac{S_k}{S_{k,\min}} \ge S_{rel,\min} \lor S_k \ge S_T \right) $$
    where $S_{rel,\max} = 1.5$ and $S_{rel,\min} = 0.5$ [S3]. This filters out Gaussians that are too large (causing blur) or too small (causing aliasing) for the current resolution [S3].

### 2D Mip Filter (Rendering)
During rendering, the standard 2D dilation operation in 3DGS is replaced by a 2D Mip filter that approximates the physical box filter of a pixel [S8, unofficial].

1.  **Projection:** Transform Gaussian to camera coordinates: $p'_k = R p_k + t$, $\Sigma'_k = R \Sigma_k R^T$. Project to ray space: $\Sigma''_k = J_k \Sigma'_k J_k^T$, where $J_k$ is the Jacobian of the projection [S8, unofficial].
2.  **Regularization:** Add variance $s$ to the projected 2D covariance $\Sigma_{k2D}$ (derived from $\Sigma''_k$) to cover a single pixel in screen space (typical $s=0.1$) [S8, unofficial]:
    $$ \Sigma_{reg} = \Sigma_{k2D} + s I $$
3.  **Opacity Compensation:** Scale opacity to preserve integral:
    $$ \alpha_{new} = \alpha_{old} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{|\Sigma_{k2D}|}}{\sqrt{|\Sigma_{reg}|}} $$
4.  **Splatting:** Render the regularized Gaussian using the standard alpha blending pipeline [S8, unofficial].

import numpy as np

def aggregate_small_gaussians(gaussians, levels, threshold=2.0, voxel_res_base=400):
    """
    Aggregates small Gaussians into larger ones for coarser levels.
    gaussians: List of Gaussian objects with attributes: pos, scale, rot, opacity, sh, level, S_k
    levels: List of level indices [2, 3, 4]
    threshold: S_T, pixel coverage threshold
    voxel_res_base: Base resolution for voxel grid
    """
    new_gaussians = []
    
    for lm in levels:
        # 1. Calculate pixel coverage at downsampled resolution
        # Render all Gaussians from levels [1, lm-1] at 4^(lm-1) downsampled resolution
        # This step requires a renderer to compute S_k for each Gaussian
        for g in gaussians:
            if g.level < lm:
                g.S_k = compute_pixel_coverage(g, scale_factor=4**(lm-1))
        
        # 2. Select small Gaussians
        small_gaussians = [g for g in gaussians if g.S_k < threshold]
        
        # 3. Voxelization and Aggregation
        voxel_size = voxel_res_base / lm
        voxel_grid = create_voxel_grid(voxel_size)
        
        for g in small_gaussians:
            voxel_grid.insert(g)
            
        for voxel in voxel_grid:
            if not voxel.has_gaussians():
                continue
                
            # Average pooling
            avg_pos = mean([g.pos for g in voxel.gaussians])
            avg_scale = mean([g.scale for g in voxel.gaussians])
            avg_rot = mean_quaternions([g.rot for g in voxel.gaussians])
            avg_opacity = mean([g.opacity for g in voxel.gaussians])
            avg_sh = mean([g.sh for g in voxel.gaussians])
            
            # Calculate average pixel coverage
            S_avg = mean([g.S_k for g in voxel.gaussians])
            
            # Enlarge scaling
            # Scale factor = S_T / S_avg
            scale_factor = threshold / S_avg
            enlarged_scale = avg_scale * scale_factor
            
            # Create new Gaussian
            new_g = Gaussian(
                pos=avg_pos,
                scale=enlarged_scale,
                rot=avg_rot,
                opacity=avg_opacity,
                sh=avg_sh,
                level=lm
            )
            new_gaussians.append(new_g)
            
    return new_gaussians

def selective_render(gaussians, current_scale, S_rel_max=1.5, S_rel_min=0.5, S_T=2.0):
    """
    Selects Gaussians for rendering based on pixel coverage bounds.
    """
    selected = []
    
    for g in gaussians:
        # Update bounds if rendering at creation scale
        if g.level == current_scale:
            g.S_max = max(0.95 * g.S_max, g.S_k)
            g.S_min = min(1.05 * g.S_min, g.S_k)
            
        # Check selection criteria
        cond1 = (g.S_k / g.S_max) <= S_rel_max
        cond2 = (g.S_k / g.S_min) >= S_rel_min
        cond3 = g.S_k >= S_T
        
        if cond1 and (cond2 or cond3):
            selected.append(g)
            
    return selected

def render_gaussian_2d_mip(g, R, t, J, s=0.1):
    """
    Renders a single Gaussian with 2D Mip filter.
    """
    # Project to camera space
    p_cam = R @ g.pos + t
    Sigma_cam = R @ g.Sigma @ R.T
    
    # Project to ray space
    Sigma_ray = J @ Sigma_cam @ J.T
    
    # Regularize with 2D Mip filter
    Sigma_reg = Sigma_ray + s * np.eye(2)
    
    # Opacity compensation
    det_orig = np.linalg.det(Sigma_ray)
    det_reg = np.linalg.det(Sigma_reg)
    
    # Guard against zero determinant
    if det_orig <= 0 or det_reg <= 0:
        alpha = 0.0
    else:
        alpha = g.opacity * np.sqrt(det_orig / det_reg)

## Subtopics

### [2D Mip Filter](9593-2d-mip-filter.md)
The 2D Mip Filter mitigates aliasing artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting when rendering from camera distances or focal lengths larger than those encountered during training. It extends the standard 2D Gaussian filter by introducing a view-dependent opacity compensation factor that prevents excessive blurring as Gaussians become smaller relative to the pixel grid. Unlike the basic 2D Gaussian filter which dilates the covariance matrix uniformly, this method dynamically adjusts opacity based on the ratio of the original to the dilated covariance determinant.

### LinPrim Anti-Aliasing
An anti-aliasing strategy combining a 3D smoothing filter and a bounding box expansion technique to limit high frequencies and prevent aliasing/dilation artifacts [S11]. It applies a 3D smoothing strategy to limit how small primitives can become based on visibility from training views, ensuring each primitive is visible from at least one pixel [S11]. It also expands the bounding box vertices outward, parallel to the screen, to enlarge the footprint and increase the minimum appearance size, acting as a coarse band-limiting approximation [S11]. It cannot use Mip-Splatting's 2D Mip filter because LinPrim primitives do not rely on modifying the screen space Gaussian distribution [S11].

### Anti-Aliasing via Kernel Adaptation
Adapts the kernel bandwidth to prevent aliasing when the volume is undersampled relative to the screen (low magnification or perspective) [S12, unofficial]. It calculates the Jacobian $J$ of the local perspective distortion for each point and computes the ray-space ellipsoid $G_{JMV}$ using $J$ to stretch the kernel appropriately [S12, unofficial]. Swan '97 and Mueller '98 suggest linearly increasing splat kernel size and linearly decreasing splat amplitude [S12, unofficial].

### Integration with Mip-Splatting and Scaffold-GS
Mip-Splatting and Scaffold-GS employ conventional projection of 3D-GS, resulting in larger approximation error compared to optimal projection methods [S13]. The proposed optimal projection method can be seamlessly integrated with them to reduce their projection error [S13].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering time is not linearly reduced at very low resolutions because all Gaussians must be splatted before selective filtering, causing overhead [S3]. However, selective rendering reduces the number of Gaussians processed per pixel at low resolutions, improving speed compared to vanilla 3DGS [S3].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Increases slightly due to the storage of multiple levels of Gaussians and per-Gaussian coverage bounds ($S_{k,\max}, S_{k,\min}$) [S3]. The number of added coarse Gaussians is typically less than 5% of the total [S3].
*   **Performance:** On the MipNeRF360 dataset at 64x scale, MIP-Splatting achieves PSNR 25.35 vs 15.23 (3DGS), LPIPS 0.066 vs 0.256, and Time 4.9ms vs 103.3ms [S3]. On the Blender dataset, it achieves PSNR 32.81 at Full Res, 34.49 at 1/2 Res, 35.45 at 1/4 Res, and 35.50 at 1/8 Res [S8, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Eliminates aliasing and dilation artifacts when zooming or changing resolution; improves rendering speed at low resolutions; robust to focal length variations [S1][S8, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires multi-scale training; naive filtering of small Gaussians causes missing parts if not aggregated [S3]; rendering time does not scale linearly with resolution decrease due to splatting overhead [S3].
*   **When to use:** Novel view synthesis where out-of-distribution generalization (different sampling rates) is required, such as in VR, cinematography, or robotics [S8, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When training and testing resolutions are identical and fixed, as the overhead may not be justified [S3].

## Background
MIP-Splatting was introduced to address aliasing artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting caused by discrete sampling [S2]. It builds on the observation that standard 3DGS systematically underestimates Gaussian scale, leading to erosion when zooming in and dilation when zooming out [S8, unofficial]. The method was developed to enable alias-free renderings across various sampling rates by introducing a 3D smoothing filter and a 2D Mip filter [S8, unofficial]. It is used for Novel View Synthesis (NVS) in virtual reality, cinematography, and robotics [S8, unofficial].
