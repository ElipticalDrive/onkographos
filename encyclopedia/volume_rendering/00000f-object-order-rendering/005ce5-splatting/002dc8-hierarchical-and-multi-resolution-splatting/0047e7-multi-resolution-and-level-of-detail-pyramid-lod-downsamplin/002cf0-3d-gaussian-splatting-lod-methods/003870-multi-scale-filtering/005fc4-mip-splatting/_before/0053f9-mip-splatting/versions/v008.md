# MIP-Splatting

Also called: Alias-free 3D Gaussian Splatting, Anti-Aliased 3D Gaussian Splatting, MS 3DGS, MS-GS, Mip 3D Gaussian Splatting, Multi-Scale Gaussians, Multi-scale 3D Gaussian Splatting

## Overview
MIP-Splatting is an extension of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that eliminates aliasing artifacts and dilation/erosion effects caused by resolution changes, focal length variations, or camera distance changes [S1][S8, unofficial]. It addresses the mismatch between the discrete sampling of the image sensor and the continuous representation of 3D Gaussians by introducing two complementary mechanisms: a 3D smoothing filter to constrain the maximal frequency of the 3D representation during training, and a 2D Mip filter during rendering to approximate the physical box filter of a camera sensor [S8, unofficial]. Unlike standard 3DGS which suffers from high-frequency artifacts when zooming out or changing focal length, MIP-Splatting ensures alias-free rendering across various sampling rates [S8, unofficial]. It also introduces a multi-scale representation where Gaussians are aggregated into larger primitives to maintain low-frequency signal integrity when small Gaussians are filtered out at low resolutions [S3].

## How it works
MIP-Splatting operates in two phases: a training phase that constructs a multi-scale representation and constrains frequency, and a rendering phase that applies screen-space filtering and selective rendering.

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   A set of $N$ calibrated images with known camera poses $(R, t)$ and intrinsics.
*   Initial 3D Gaussian primitives $\{G_k\}$ derived from a point cloud (e.g., COLMAP), each defined by center $p_k$, covariance $\Sigma_k$, opacity $\alpha_k$, and spherical harmonics coefficients for color $c_k$.
*   Training resolution scale $S_{train}$ (typically 1x).

**Outputs:**
*   A set of multi-scale 3D Gaussians $\{G_k\}$, where each Gaussian has an associated level of detail $l \in [1, l_{max}]$.
*   Rendered images at arbitrary resolutions and camera distances without aliasing.

### Data Structures
Each Gaussian primitive $G_k$ stores:
*   $p_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Center position.
*   $\Sigma_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: 3D covariance matrix, parameterized as $\Sigma_k = O_k S_k S_k^T O_k^T$, where $O_k$ is a rotation matrix (from quaternion) and $S_k = \text{diag}(s_{k,x}, s_{k,y}, s_{k,z})$ is a scaling vector [S8, unofficial].
*   $\alpha_k \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
*   $c_k$: Spherical harmonics coefficients.
*   $l_k \in \{1, \dots, l_{max}\}$: Level of detail index.
*   $S_{k,max}, S_{k,min} \in \mathbb{R}$: Maximum and minimum pixel coverage thresholds for selective rendering [S3].

### Parameters
*   $l_{max}$: Maximum number of detail levels. Typically 4, corresponding to 1x, 4x, 16x, and 64x downsampled resolutions [S3].
*   $s_{3D}$: Scalar hyperparameter for the 3D smoothing filter variance. Typical value: 0.2 [S8, unofficial].
*   $s_{2D}$: Scalar hyperparameter for the 2D Mip filter variance, chosen to cover a single pixel in screen space. Typical value: 0.1 [S8, unofficial].
*   $S_T$: Selective rendering threshold (pixel coverage). Gaussians with coverage $S_k < S_T$ are considered too small. Typical value: 2 pixels [S3].
*   $\sigma_T$: Opacity threshold for calculating pixel coverage. Typical value: $1/255$ [S3].
*   $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$: Decay coefficients for updating coverage ranges. $\lambda_1 = 0.95$, $\lambda_2 = 1.05$ [S3].
*   $S_{rel,max}, S_{rel,min}$: Relative coverage bounds for selection. $S_{rel,max} = 1.5$, $S_{rel,min} = 0.5$ [S3].
*   $V_{res}$: Voxel grid resolution for aggregation. $(400/l_m)^3$ for level $l_m$ [S3].
*   $m$: Iteration interval for recomputing maximal sampling rate. Typical value: 100 [S8, unofficial].
*   $T_{warmup}$: Warm-up stage duration. Typical value: 1,000 iterations [S3].

### Method Steps

#### 1. 3D Smoothing Filter (Training)
To prevent the 3D Gaussians from becoming too small (high frequency) relative to the training data's sampling rate, a 3D low-pass filter is applied during optimization [S8, unofficial].

1.  **Derive Sampling Frequency:** For each Gaussian $G_k$, determine the maximal sampling frequency $\hat{\nu}_k$ induced by the training views. The world space sampling interval $\hat{T}$ at depth $d$ for focal length $f$ is $\hat{T} = d/f$. The sampling frequency is $\hat{\nu} = 1/\hat{T} = f/d$ [S8, unofficial].
2.  **Regularize 3D Covariance:** Convolve the 3D Gaussian $G_k$ with a 3D Gaussian low-pass filter $G_{low}$ having variance $\hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I$, where $s$ is the filter size hyperparameter ($s_{3D}$). The regularized covariance is:
    $$ \Sigma_{k, reg} = \Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I $$
    The regularized Gaussian is:
    $$ G_k(x)_{reg} = \frac{\sqrt{|\Sigma_k|}}{\sqrt{|\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I|}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-p_k)^T (\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I)^{-1} (x-p_k)} $$
    This constrains the maximal frequency of the 3D representation to meet the Nyquist limit of the training data [S8, unofficial].
3.  **Recompute Frequency:** Every $m$ iterations, recompute $\hat{\nu}_k$ for all Gaussians, as centers remain relatively stable [S8, unofficial].

#### 2. Multi-Scale Aggregation (Training)
To handle low-resolution rendering without holes, small Gaussians are aggregated into larger ones at coarser levels [S3].

1.  **Initialization:** All Gaussians start at level $l=1$. Densification (splitting/cloning) inherits level 1 [S3].
2.  **Warm-up:** For the first $T_{warmup}$ iterations, train normally [S3].
3.  **Aggregation Trigger:** After warm-up, identify "too small" Gaussians. Render all Gaussians from levels $[1, l_m-1]$ at $4^{l_m-1}$ times downsampled resolution for all training cameras. Calculate pixel coverage $S_k$ for each. Select $G_{small} = \{G_k \mid S_k < S_T\}$ [S3].
4.  **Voxelization:** Bin $G_{small}$ into a voxel grid of resolution $(400/l_m)^3$ [S3].
5.  **Average Pooling:** For each voxel $n$, average the position, rotation, scaling, opacity, and color of all Gaussians $G_n$ within it to create a new Gaussian $G_{new,n}$ [S3].
6.  **Enlargement:** Calculate the average pixel coverage $S_{avg}$ of the aggregated Gaussians. Enlarge the scaling of $G_{new,n}$ by a factor $S_T / S_{avg}$ so its pixel coverage targets $S_T$. Assign $G_{new,n}$ to level $l_m$ [S3].
7.  **Repeat:** Repeat steps 3-6 for all coarse levels $l_m \in [2, l_{max}]$ [S3].
8.  **Multi-Scale Training:** Train the model on both original images and downsampled images to optimize the multi-scale representation [S3].

#### 3. Selective Rendering (Inference)
During rendering, Gaussians are selected based on their pixel coverage relative to the current resolution [S3].

1.  **Calculate Coverage:** For each Gaussian $G_k$, calculate its pixel coverage $S_k$ (the size of the splatted 2D Gaussian in pixels, measured at opacity threshold $\sigma_T$) [S3].
2.  **Update Ranges:** If the rendering resolution scale matches the scale at which $G_k$ was created, update its bounds:
    $$ S_{k,max}' = \max(\lambda_1 S_{k,max}, S_k) $$
    $$ S_{k,min}' = \min(\lambda_2 S_{k,min}, S_k) $$
    Otherwise, keep existing bounds [S3].
3.  **Selection:** A Gaussian $G_k$ is rendered if:
    $$ \frac{S_k}{S_{k,max}} \le S_{rel,max} \quad \land \quad \left( \frac{S_k}{S_{k,min}} \ge S_{rel,min} \lor S_k \ge S_T \right) $$
    This filters out Gaussians that are too large (causing blur) or too small (causing aliasing/holes) [S3].

#### 4. 2D Mip Filter (Rendering)
Replace the standard 2D dilation filter with a 2D Mip filter to approximate the camera's pixel box filter [S8, unofficial].

1.  **Project to Screen Space:** Transform $G_k$ to camera coordinates ($p'_k, \Sigma'_k$) and then to ray space via local affine transformation $J_k$:
    $$ \Sigma''_k = J_k \Sigma'_k J_k^T $$
    Let $\Sigma_{k2D}$ be the top-left $2 \times 2$ submatrix of $\Sigma''_k$ [S8, unofficial].
2.  **Regularize 2D Covariance:** Add variance $s_{2D} I$ to $\Sigma_{k2D}$ to cover a single pixel:
    $$ \Sigma_{k2D, mip} = \Sigma_{k2D} + s_{2D} I $$
3.  **Opacity Compensation:** Scale opacity to preserve integral:
    $$ C = \frac{\sqrt{|\Sigma_{k2D}|}}{\sqrt{|\Sigma_{k2D, mip}|}} $$
    $$ \alpha_{new} = \alpha_{old} \cdot C $$
4.  **Rasterize:** Render the Gaussian using $\Sigma_{k2D, mip}$ and $\alpha_{new}$ [S8, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
import numpy as np

def compute_pixel_coverage(gaussian, camera, resolution_scale):
    """
    Calculate pixel coverage Sk for a Gaussian.
    gaussian: dict with 'cov_2d' (2x2 matrix), 'opacity'
    camera: dict with 'intrinsics', 'extrinsics'
    resolution_scale: float, 1.0 for 1x, 0.25 for 4x, etc.
    """
    # Project 3D Gaussian to 2D screen space
    # 1. Transform to camera coordinates
    p_cam = camera['R'] @ gaussian['p'] + camera['t']
    cov_cam = camera['R'] @ gaussian['cov_3d'] @ camera['R'].T
    
    # 2. Project to ray space (simplified for pseudocode)
    # J is the Jacobian of the projection
    J = compute_jacobian(p_cam, camera['intrinsics'])
    cov_ray = J @ cov_cam @ J.T
    
    # 3. Extract 2D covariance
    cov_2d = cov_ray[:2, :2]
    
    # 4. Calculate coverage Sk
    # Sk is the size at opacity threshold sigma_T
    # Solving e^(-0.5 * x^T * Sigma^-1 * x) = sigma_T for x
    # For isotropic approximation, Sk ~ sqrt(2 * ln(1/sigma_T) * trace(cov_2d))
    # More precisely, use the eigenvalues of cov_2d
    eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(cov_2d)
    # Sk is the max extent in pixels
    Sk = np.sqrt(2 * np.log(1.0 / 0.00392) * np.max(eigenvalues)) / resolution_scale
    
    return Sk, cov_2d

def aggregate_gaussians(gaussians, level, threshold_ST):
    """
    Aggregate small Gaussians into larger ones for level lm.
    """
    # 1. Render at downsampled resolution to find small Gaussians
    # Render all Gaussians from levels [1, level-1] at 4^(level-1) downsampled res
    # Calculate Sk for each
    small_gaussians = []
    for g in gaussians:
        if g['level'] < level:
            Sk, _ = compute_pixel_coverage(g, camera, 4**-(level-1))
            if Sk < threshold_ST:
                small_gaussians.append(g)
    
    # 2. Voxelize
    voxel_size = 400 / level
    voxels = {}
    for g in small_gaussians:
        voxel_idx = tuple((g['p'] / voxel_size).astype(int))
        if voxel_idx not in voxels:
            voxels[voxel_idx] = []
        voxels[voxel_idx].append(g)
    
    # 3. Aggregate and Enlarge
    new_gaussians = []
    for voxel_idx, group in voxels.items():
        # Average pooling
        avg_p = np.mean([g['p'] for g in group], axis=0)
        avg_cov = np.mean([g['cov_3d'] for g in group], axis=0)
        avg_opacity = np.mean([g['opacity'] for g in group])
        avg_color = np.mean([g['color'] for g in group], axis=0)
        
        # Calculate average coverage Savg
        Savg = np.mean([g['Sk'] for g in group])
        
        # Enlarge scaling
        # Scaling factor ST / Savg
        # Note: cov_3d contains scaling info. We need to scale the covariance matrix
        # to achieve the target pixel coverage.
        # If Sk ~ sqrt(cov), then cov_new ~ cov_old * (ST/Savg)^2
        scale_factor = threshold_ST / Savg
        new_cov = avg_cov * (scale_factor ** 2)
        
        new_g = {
            'p': avg_p,
            'cov_3d': new_cov,
            'opacity': avg_opacity,
            'color': avg_color,
            'level': level,
            'Sk': threshold_ST # Target coverage
        }
        new_gaussians.append(new_g)
        
    return new_gaussians

def render_mip_splatting(gaussians, camera, resolution_scale):
    """
    Render using MIP-Splatting logic.
    """
    # 1. Select Gaussians
    selected = []
    for g in gaussians:
        Sk, cov_2d = compute_pixel_coverage(g, camera, resolution_scale)
        
        # Update ranges if at creation scale
        if g['level'] == resolution_scale_level: # Simplified check
            g['Sk_max'] = max(0.95 * g['Sk_max'], Sk)
            g['Sk_min'] = min(1.05 * g['Sk_min'], Sk)
            
        # Selection condition
        cond1 = (Sk / g['Sk_max']) <= 1.5
        cond2 = (Sk / g['Sk_min']) >= 0.5 or Sk >= 2.0
        
        if cond1 and cond2:
            # 2. Apply 2D Mip Filter
            # Regularize 2D covariance
            s_2d = 0.1 # Typical value
            cov_2d_mip = cov_2d + s_2d * np.eye(2)
            
            # Opacity compensation
            det_orig = np.linalg.det(cov_2d)
            det_mip = np.linalg.det(cov_2d_mip)
            C = np.sqrt(det_orig / det_mip)
            alpha_new = g['opacity'] * C
            
            selected.append({
                'cov_2d': cov_2d_mip,
                'opacity': alpha_new,
                'color': g['color'],
                'p': g['p']
            })
            
    # 3. Rasterize selected Gaussians
    # Standard alpha blending rasterization
    image = rasterize(selected, camera, resolution_scale)
    return image
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Naive Filtering:** Filtering out small Gaussians without aggregation causes missing parts (holes) in the rendered image [S3].
*   **Performance Overhead:** All Gaussians must be splatted to calculate coverage before filtering, preventing linear speedup at very low resolutions [S3].
*   **Dilation Artifacts:** Standard 3DGS applies a fixed dilation filter, which causes dilation artifacts when zooming out because the projected Gaussians shrink but the dilation remains constant [S8, unofficial]. MIP-Splatting's 2D Mip filter scales with pixel size, avoiding this [S8, unofficial].
*   **Erosion Artifacts:** Standard 3DGS underestimates scale during optimization, causing erosion when zooming in. The 3D smoothing filter mitigates this by constraining the minimal frequency [S8, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [2D Mip Filter](9593-2d-mip-filter.md)
The 2D Mip Filter mitigates aliasing artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting when rendering from camera distances or focal lengths larger than those encountered during training. It extends the standard 2D Gaussian filter by introducing a view-dependent opacity compensation factor that prevents excessive blurring as Gaussians become smaller relative to the pixel grid. Unlike the basic 2D Gaussian filter which dilates the covariance matrix uniformly, this method dynamically adjusts opacity based on the ratio of the original to the dilated covariance determinant. It approximates the physical box filter of a camera sensor.

### LinPrim Anti-Aliasing
An anti-aliasing strategy combining a 3D smoothing filter and a bounding box expansion technique to limit high frequencies and prevent aliasing/dilation artifacts [S11]. It applies a 3D smoothing strategy to limit how small primitives can become based on visibility from training views, ensuring each primitive is visible from at least one pixel [S11]. It also expands the bounding box used for tiling outward, parallel to the screen, which enlarges the footprint and increases the minimum size at which the primitive appears, acting as a coarse band-limiting approximation [S11]. It cannot use Mip-Splatting's 2D Mip filter because LinPrim primitives do not rely on modifying the screen space Gaussian distribution [S11].

### Anti-Aliasing via Kernel Adaptation
Adapts the kernel bandwidth to prevent aliasing when the volume is undersampled relative to the screen (low magnification or perspective) [S12, unofficial]. It calculates the Jacobian $J$ of the local perspective distortion for each point and computes the ray-space ellipsoid $G_{JMV}$ using $J$ to stretch the kernel appropriately [S12, unofficial]. Swan '97 and Mueller '98 suggest linearly increasing splat kernel size and linearly decreasing splat amplitude [S12, unofficial].

### Integration with Mip-Splatting and Scaffold-GS
Mip-Splatting and Scaffold-GS employ conventional projection of 3D-GS, resulting in larger approximation error compared to optimal projection strategies [S13]. The proposed optimal projection method can be seamlessly integrated with them to reduce their projection error [S13].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering time is not linearly proportional to resolution decrease because all Gaussians must be splatted to calculate coverage before filtering [S3]. However, selective rendering reduces the number of Gaussians contributing to alpha blending at low resolutions, offering significant speedups compared to vanilla 3DGS [S3].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Increases due to the multi-scale representation. The number of added coarse-level Gaussians is typically fewer than 5% of the total [S3].
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   On MipNeRF-360 at 64x scale: PSNR 25.35 vs 15.23 (3DGS), LPIPS 0.066 vs 0.256 (3DGS), Time 4.9ms vs 103.3ms (3DGS) [S3].
    *   On Blender dataset at 1/8 Res: PSNR 35.50, outperforming 3DGS and 3DGS+EWA [S8, unofficial].
    *   On Tank and Temples at 64x scale: PSNR 19.67 vs 13.88 (3DGS), Time 5.9ms vs 82.6ms (3DGS) [S3].
    *   On Deep Blending at 64x scale: PSNR 25.70 vs 17.75 (3DGS), Time 3.4ms vs 59.7ms (3DGS) [S3].
*   **Strengths:** Eliminates aliasing and dilation/erosion artifacts at arbitrary resolutions and focal lengths. Improves rendering speed at low resolutions.
*   **Limitations:** Requires multi-scale training. Performance overhead from calculating coverage for all Gaussians.
*   **When to use:** When rendering at resolutions or focal lengths different from training data, or when zooming in/out is required.
*   **When not to use:** When training and testing resolutions are identical and fixed, as the overhead may not be justified.

## Background
MIP-Splatting was introduced to address aliasing artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting caused by discrete sampling [S2, S5]. Standard 3DGS samples the scene at pixel centers, ignoring the pixel footprint, which leads to aliasing when changing resolution or focal length [S5]. The method draws inspiration from Mip-NeRF, which uses multi-scale images for supervision, but MIP-Splatting determines filter size based on pixel size, allowing alias-free rendering at scales unobserved during training [S8, unofficial]. It also relates to EWA-Splatting, which limits frequency bandwidth empirically, whereas MIP-Splatting's 3D filter is determined by training data and tackles the reconstruction problem via inverse rendering [S8, unofficial]. It is used for Novel View Synthesis (NVS) in virtual reality, cinematography, and robotics, specifically addressing out-of-distribution generalization [S8, unofficial].
