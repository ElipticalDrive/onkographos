# EAGLES

Also called: Efficient Accelerated 3D Gaussians with Lightweight EncodingS

## Overview
EAGLES is a method for optimizing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) representations by reducing memory footprint, training time, and storage size while maintaining or improving reconstruction quality [S2]. It achieves this through attribute quantization, progressive coarse-to-fine training, and aggressive pruning [S2]. Unlike standard 3D-GS, which stores full-precision attributes for millions of Gaussians, EAGLES compresses color, rotation, and opacity into latent vectors decoded by a small MLP, and reduces the Gaussian count via influence-based pruning [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling LOD methods like H-3DGS by focusing on attribute-level compression and training-time acceleration rather than hierarchical spatial structures [S2].

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Sparse point cloud from Structure from Motion (SfM) [S2].
*   Training images and camera poses [S2].
*   Hyperparameters: SH degree $f$, latent dimension $l$, decoder output dimension $k$, progressive scaling start/end scales, densification interval, and pruning thresholds [S2].

**Outputs:**
*   Quantized latent vectors $q$ for each Gaussian [S2].
*   Trained MLP decoder $D$ [S2].
*   Non-quantized attributes: position $p$ and scale $s$ (stored in full precision) [S2].
*   Rendered novel views [S2].

### Data Structures
Each Gaussian $i$ stores:
1.  **Position** $p_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: 32-bit float [S2].
2.  **Scale** $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: 32-bit float [S2].
3.  **Latent Vector** $q_i \in \mathbb{Z}^l$: Integer vector of dimension $l$ [S2].
4.  **Base Color** $c_{base, i} \in \mathbb{R}^3$: 32-bit float (optional, if not fully quantized) [S2].

The MLP decoder $D: \mathbb{Z}^l \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^k$ maps the latent vector to the decoded attributes $\boldsymbol{a}_i = \{r_i, o_i, c_{SH, i}\}$, where $r_i$ is rotation, $o_i$ is opacity, and $c_{SH, i}$ are spherical harmonic coefficients [S2]. The dimension $k$ depends on the SH degree $f$, where $d = 3f^2$ coefficients are required for color [S2].

### Steps

1.  **Initialization:** Initialize Gaussians from the SfM point cloud. Set position $p$, scale $s$, and initialize latent vectors $q$ [S2].
2.  **Attribute Quantization (Training):**
    *   For each Gaussian, maintain a continuous approximation $\hat{q}$ of the integer latent vector $q$ [S2].
    *   Compute decoded attributes using the Straight-Through Estimator (STE):
        $$ \boldsymbol{a} = D(\text{STE}(\hat{q})) $$
        where $\text{STE}(\hat{q})$ rounds $\hat{q}$ to the nearest integer for the forward pass but passes gradients through $\hat{q}$ in the backward pass [S2].
    *   Reconstruct the 3D Gaussian covariance matrix $\Sigma$ from decoded rotation $r$ and stored scale $s$:
        $$ \Sigma = RSS^TR^T $$
        where $S$ is the diagonal scale matrix from $s$, and $R$ is the rotation matrix from quaternion $r$ [S2].
    *   Project $\Sigma$ to 2D for the current camera view:
        $$ \Sigma' = JP\Sigma P^TJ^T $$
        where $P$ is the world-to-camera projective transform and $J$ is the Jacobian of the affine approximation of the projective transform [S2].
    *   Compute opacity $\alpha$ and color $c$ for rasterization. Opacity $\alpha$ is derived from the 2D Gaussian value at the pixel multiplied by the decoded scalar opacity $o$ [S2]. Color $c$ is computed from the decoded SH coefficients $c_{SH}$ [S2].
    *   Render the image and compute loss:
        $$ \mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}} $$
        with $\lambda = 0.2$ [S2].
    *   Update $\hat{q}$, $p$, and $s$ via Stochastic Gradient Descent [S2].

3.  **Progressive Coarse-to-Fine Training:**
    *   Start rendering at a reduced resolution scale (e.g., 0.3 of original dimensions) [S2].
    *   Gradually increase the rendering resolution to 1.0 (full scale) over a cosine schedule spanning 70% of the total training iterations [S2].
    *   Alternatively, use Gaussian filtering (e.g., 5x5, 7x7, or 15x15 kernel) or mean filtering during this phase to stabilize optimization [S2]. Downsampling provides the largest training time gains [S2].

4.  **Densification and Pruning:**
    *   **Warmup:** Do not prune or densify for the initial phase [S2].
    *   **Pruning:** Every 100 iterations, remove Gaussians with opacity $\alpha < \alpha_{\text{thresh}}$ [S2].
    *   **Densification:** Every 100 iterations, split large Gaussians (covariance larger than geometry) and clone small Gaussians. Only perform this on Gaussians with positional gradients above a threshold $\tau_{\text{thresh}}$ [S2].
    *   **Interval Adjustment:** Increase the densification interval to 175 iterations to reduce the final Gaussian count without quality loss [S2].
    *   **Final Pruning:** After densification stabilizes, apply a final pruning stage to remove Gaussians with the least influence on the full reconstruction, further reducing count and storage [S2].

5.  **Post-Training Compression:**
    *   Round $\hat{q}$ to the nearest integer $q$ [S2].
    *   Apply entropy coding to store $q$ efficiently [S2].
    *   Store the MLP decoder $D$ and the non-quantized attributes ($p, s$) [S2].

### Parameters
*   **SH Degree $f$:** Typically 4. Color coefficients ($3f^2$) constitute >80% of attribute dimensions [S2].
*   **Latent Dimension $l$:** Dimension of $q \in \mathbb{Z}^l$ [S2].
*   **Decoder Output Dimension $k$:** Dimension of $D: \mathbb{Z}^l \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^k$ [S2].
*   **Progressive Scaling Start:** 0.3 (30% resolution) [S2].
*   **Progressive Scaling End:** 1.0 (100% resolution) [S2].
*   **Progressive Scaling Duration:** 70% of total iterations [S2].
*   **Densification Interval:** 175 iterations (increased from standard 100) [S2].
*   **Gaussian Filter Size:** 5x5, 7x7, or 15x15 if using Gaussian filtering instead of downsampling [S2].

### Pitfalls
*   **Opacity Quantization:** Quantizing opacity is critical to prevent erroneous Gaussians from saturating quickly, which reduces floaters and visual artifacts [S1, S2].
*   **Densification Interval:** Increasing the interval beyond 175 causes a sharp drop in reconstruction quality [S2].
*   **Quantization Drop:** Quantization causes a small drop in PSNR, but opacity quantization can actually improve visual quality by reducing artifacts [S2].
*   **Position/Scale Quantization:** EAGLES does *not* quantize position and scale to avoid overlapping Gaussians and rasterization inaccuracies [S1, S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Memory Reduction:** EAGLES reduces storage by 10-20x compared to 3D-GS [S2]. For example, on the Deep Blending dataset, storage drops from 676 MB (3D-GS) to 52 MB (EAGLES) [S2]. On the Train scene (Tanks & Temples), it drops from 262 MB to 21 MB [S2].
*   **Training Speed:** Training time is significantly reduced. On Deep Blending, EAGLES takes 21m50s vs 36m2s for 3D-GS [S2]. On the Bicycle scene, GPU memory usage drops from 17.4 GB (3D-GS) to 10 GB (EAGLES) [S2].
*   **Rendering Speed:** Rendering FPS is comparable or slightly lower than 3D-GS. On Deep Blending, EAGLES achieves 130 FPS vs 137 FPS for 3D-GS [S2]. EAGLES-Small achieves 160 FPS [S2].
*   **Quality:** PSNR is on par or slightly better. On Deep Blending, EAGLES achieves 29.86 PSNR vs 29.41 PSNR for 3D-GS [S2]. On MipNeRF360, the drop is minimal (0.22 dB) [S2].
*   **Complexity:** The method adds complexity due to the MLP decoder and quantization logic, but reduces the number of Gaussians, which lowers rasterization cost.

## Background
EAGLES (Efficient Accelerated 3D Gaussians with Lightweight EncodingS) was proposed by Sharath Girish, Kamal Gupta, and Abhinav Shrivastava from the University of Maryland, College Park [S2]. It builds upon the seminal 3D Gaussian Splatting work [S2]. The method addresses the high memory and training costs of 3D-GS by introducing attribute quantization, progressive training, and pruning [S2].
