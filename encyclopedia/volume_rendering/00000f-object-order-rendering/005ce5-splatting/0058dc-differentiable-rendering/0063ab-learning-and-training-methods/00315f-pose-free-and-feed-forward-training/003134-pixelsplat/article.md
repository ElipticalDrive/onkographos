# pixelSplat

Also called: 3D Gaussian Splats from Image Pairs, Epipolar Transformer Gaussian Splatting

## Overview
pixelSplat is a feed-forward model that reconstructs a 3D radiance field parameterized by 3D Gaussian primitives from a pair of input images, enabling real-time and memory-efficient rendering [S1]. It solves the problem of local minima inherent to sparse, locally supported representations in generalizable novel view synthesis, where non-differentiable pruning or splitting (used in single-scene optimization) cannot be applied because gradients must back-propagate through the representation [S1]. Unlike light-field transformers which produce black-box ray colors, pixelSplat generates an explicit, editable 3D representation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A pair of images $I_1, I_2$ and their associated camera parameters (poses $T$ and intrinsics $K$) [S1].
*   **Output:** A set of 3D Gaussian primitives, each defined by mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, opacity $\alpha$, and scale $S$ (or spherical harmonics coefficients for color) [S1].

**Data Structures:**
The model predicts a dense probability distribution over 3D space for each pixel. The Gaussian parameters are predicted per pixel $u$ in the reference image.
*   **Features:** Pixel-aligned features $\mathbf{F}[u]$ are generated by summing outputs from a ResNet-50 and a ViT-B/8 vision transformer, both pre-trained with a DINO objective [S1].
*   **Gaussian Parameters:** For each pixel $u$, a neural network $f$ predicts $\phi, \boldsymbol{\delta}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, \mathbf{S}$ [S1]. These are mapped to the final Gaussian attributes:
    *   Mean: $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \text{ray}(u) + \boldsymbol{\delta}$ (where $\text{ray}(u)$ is the camera ray direction/origin for pixel $u$) [S1].
    *   Covariance: $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is predicted directly or derived from scale and rotation [S1].
    *   Opacity: $\alpha$ is predicted [S1].
    *   Scale: $S$ is predicted [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Feature Extraction:** Pass each reference image through a ResNet-50 and a ViT-B/8 (DINO-pretrained). Sum their pixel-wise outputs to generate initial features [S1].
2.  **Epipolar Transformer:** Perform two rounds of epipolar cross-attention to encode features between the two views. This step uses frequency-based positional encoding of depth to resolve scale ambiguity [S1]. The transformer infers a per-scene scale factor $s_i$ to handle the arbitrary scale of SfM poses [S1].
3.  **Probability Distribution Prediction:** Predict a dense probability distribution over 3D space (specifically along the camera rays) conditioned on the epipolar features [S1].
4.  **Differentiable Sampling (Reparameterization Trick):** Sample Gaussian means $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ from the predicted dense probability distribution. The sampling operation is made differentiable via a reparameterization trick that couples the density of a sampled Gaussian primitive to the probability of that location [S1].
    *   *Mechanism:* When a gradient increases the opacity of a Gaussian at a 3D location, the model increases the probability that a Gaussian will be sampled at that location in future training steps, implicitly spawning or deleting primitives [S1].
5.  **Attribute Prediction:** Use a neural network $f$ conditioned on the feature $\mathbf{F}[u]$ for each pixel $u$ to output the remaining Gaussian parameters: mean offset $\boldsymbol{\delta}$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, opacity $\alpha$, and scale $S$ [S1].
    $$ \phi, \boldsymbol{\delta}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, \mathbf{S} = f(\mathbf{F}[u]) \quad (8) $$
6.  **Rendering:** Render the predicted Gaussians using standard differentiable Gaussian splatting rasterization [S1].
7.  **Loss Computation:** Compute the loss between the rendered image and the target image using a combination of Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Perceptual Distance (LPIPS) [S1].
8.  **Optimization:** Update network weights using the Adam optimizer [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Training Resolution:** $256 \times 256$ pixels [S1].
*   **Loss Function:** Mean Squared Error (MSE) + Perceptual Distance (LPIPS) [S1].
*   **Optimizer:** Adam [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Scale Ambiguity:** SfM poses are scaled by an arbitrary unknown scalar $s_i$. The model handles this by inferring the scale via an epipolar transformer that finds cross-view pixel correspondences [S1]. Without this, ghosting artifacts occur [S1].
*   **Local Minima:** Gaussian primitives have local support; gradients vanish if the distance to the correct location exceeds a few standard deviations. The probabilistic sampling avoids this by allowing gradients to flow through the probability distribution, implicitly spawning/deleting primitives [S1]. Without this, speckling artifacts occur [S1].
*   **Out-of-Distribution Poses:** Reflective surfaces may appear transparent, and Gaussians may appear billboard-like when viewed from out-of-distribution camera poses [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Inference cost is approximately 650 times less than the next-fastest baseline for encoding one scene and rendering 100 images [S1]. Rendering is accelerated by 2.5 orders of magnitude compared to state-of-the-art light field transformers [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory-efficient rendering due to rasterization-based volume rendering of Gaussians, significantly less memory per ray at training time compared to ray-marching NeRFs [S1].
*   **Performance:** Benchmarked on wide-baseline novel view synthesis on RealEstate10k and ACID datasets. Outperforms baselines like pixelNeRF, GPNR, and Du et al.'s light field transformer in PSNR and LPIPS [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Real-time rendering, editable 3D representation, fast 3D reconstruction at inference, handles scale ambiguity, avoids local minima via differentiable sampling [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Struggles with reflective surfaces (transparency) and out-of-distribution views (billboard effect) [S1]. Requires paired images with known poses (though scale is handled) [S1].

## Background
pixelSplat was introduced by Charatan, Li, Tagliasacchi, and Sitzmann from MIT and Simon Fraser University/University of Toronto [S1]. It was presented at CVPR 2024 [S1]. The method addresses the limitations of previous generalizable novel view synthesis methods, which were either slow (ray-marching NeRFs) or lacked explicit 3D representations (light-field transformers) [S1]. It builds on the success of 3D Gaussian Splatting for single-scene optimization [19] and adapts it for the generalizable, feed-forward setting [S1].
