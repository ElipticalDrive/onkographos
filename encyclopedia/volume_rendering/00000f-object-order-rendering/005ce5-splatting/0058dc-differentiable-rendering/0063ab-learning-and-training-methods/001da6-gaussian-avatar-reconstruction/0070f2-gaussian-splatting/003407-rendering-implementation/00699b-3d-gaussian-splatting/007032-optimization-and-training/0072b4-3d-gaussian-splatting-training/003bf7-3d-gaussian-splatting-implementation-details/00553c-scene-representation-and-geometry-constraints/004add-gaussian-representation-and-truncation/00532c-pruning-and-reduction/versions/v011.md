# Pruning and Reduction

Also called: Gaussian Pruning, Scene Reduction, Visibility Filtering

## Overview
Pruning and reduction methods aim to decrease the storage footprint and computational cost of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representations by removing redundant or low-impact Gaussians, or by simplifying their attributes, without significantly degrading rendering quality. Unlike the parent method which focuses on the geometric truncation and representation of individual Gaussians, this family focuses on the global or local optimization of the scene graph size and complexity. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Inverse Multiquadric Splatting](9910-inverse-multiquadric-splatting.md) (which changes the kernel shape) and [Directed Distance Field (DDF) for Gaussian Splatting](12854-directed-distance-field-ddf-for-gaussian-splatting.md) (which adds geometric constraints) by directly reducing the number of primitives or their attribute dimensions.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting representation, where the scene is defined by a set of $N$ 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$. Each Gaussian $G_i$ is defined by its mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$, covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ (derived from rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$ and scale $\mathbf{S}_i$), opacity $\alpha_i$, and view-dependent color $\mathbf{c}_i(\omega)$.

The rendering process evaluates the Gaussian density along a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\omega$ and integrates contributions. The Gaussian density function is:
$$ G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right) $$

The core variation across methods lies in the definition, storage, and optimization of $\mathbf{c}_i(\omega)$ and potentially $\alpha_i$, moving beyond the fixed SH coefficients used in base 3DGS. Common strategies include:
1.  **Post-Processing Transformation:** Applying a learned affine transformation to the rendered image based on 3D position and view embedding [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
2.  **Factorized Illumination:** Separating scene lighting (via a tensor grid) from material properties (via BRDF features) to model specular effects [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
3.  **Image-Conditioned Modulation:** Using an MLP and per-image embeddings to modulate Gaussian color and opacity, enabling transient object removal and appearance synthesis [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
4.  **Neural Field Substitution:** Replacing per-Gaussian SH with a shared grid-based neural field to reduce parameters and improve generalization [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
5.  **Volumetric Attenuation:** Modifying the alpha-blending equation to incorporate medium properties, such as direct attenuation $B_d$ and backscatter parameters $B_\infty, B_b$, to model light transport in non-vacuum media [15148-physics-and-optical-modeling.md].

## Subtopics

### [Vertex-level Pruning in Mixed-Primitive Gaussian Splatting](13983-vertex-level-pruning-in-mixed-primitive-gaussian-splatting.md)
Vertex-level pruning is a refinement mechanism for Mixed-Primitive Gaussian Splatting (MP-GS) that removes redundant vertices from Gaussian triangles and lines during training to reduce storage overhead and simplify the scene representation. Unlike standard 3D Gaussian Splatting which only performs primitive-level opacity pruning, this method dynamically converts primitives into lower-vertex forms (ellipses or lines) based on geometric proximity and collinearity. It complements [Attribute Pruning](18516-attribute-pruning.md) and [SplatQ](14415-splatq.md) by addressing topological redundancy in mixed primitives rather than attribute quantization or opacity thresholds alone.

### [SplatQ](14415-splatq.md)
SplatQ is a post-training pruning framework for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that addresses spatial quality imbalance and ignores joint deletion effects inherent in global Top-K selection methods. It partitions Gaussians into size-limited blocks based on spatial and appearance similarity, allocates retention quotas to balance regional budgets, and uses a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) model to jointly select Gaussians within each block, thereby accounting for pairwise interactions and overlapping contributions.

### [Attribute Pruning](18516-attribute-pruning.md)
Attribute pruning selectively reduces the storage and computational overhead of per-Gaussian attributes, primarily spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients, to optimize memory usage and rendering efficiency. Unlike [Pruning and Reduction](14909-pruning-and-reduction.md) which focuses on removing entire primitives, attribute pruning retains the Gaussian primitives but simplifies their representation. It differs from [SplatQ](14415-splatq.md) by focusing on the dimensionality of the attribute vectors rather than quantization of the entire data structure, and from [Vertex-level Pruning in Mixed-Primitive Gaussian Splatting](13983-vertex-level-pruning-in-mixed-primitive-gaussian-splatting.md) by operating on the standard Gaussian representation without changing the primitive type.

#### Vertex Pruning
- **Definition:** A pruning mechanism designed to support mixed-primitive-based learning, as standard pruning is incompatible with mixed primitives [S1, unofficial].
- **Step:** Prune vertices of the mixed primitives to optimize the representation [S1, unofficial].

#### Gaussian Reduction with Learnable Mask
- **Definition:** A method to reduce the number of Gaussians using a learnable mask during optimization, targeting small-sized kernels for removal [S2].
- **Step:** Apply binary masks $M$ to the opacities and scale attributes of the kernels at every densification step [S2]. Use residual vector quantization with $L$ stages of vector quantization for geometric attributes like scale and rotation [S2]. Represent view-dependent colors via a grid-based neural field using hash grids for input positions, features, and view direction, fed into an MLP [S2].
- **Formula:** The end-to-end loss is: $L = L_{ren} + \lambda_m L_m + L_r + L_s$, where $L_{ren}$ is L1+SSIM loss, $L_m$ is masking loss ($\frac{1}{N} \sum \sigma(m_n)$), and $L_r, L_s$ are rotation and scale objective functions [S2].
- **Parameter:** $\epsilon$: Masking threshold for the sigmoid function in the learnable mask [S2]. $\lambda_m$: Weight for the masking loss term, typically $5 \times 10^{-4}$ [S2].

#### 3DGS Visibility Pruning
- **Definition:** Application of visibility-based pruning to 3D Gaussian Splatting, using Gaussian centroids as reference points for visibility calculation [S2].
- **Step:** Represent each Gaussian kernel by its centroid position, scaling matrix $S$, and rotation matrix $R$ defining the covariance $\Sigma$ [S2]. Use the Gaussian centroids as the 3D points $p_i$ in the visibility mask computation algorithm [S2].

#### Visibility List-Based Rendering
- **Definition:** A post-processing step that filters out Gaussians not visible to the current camera view to increase rendering speed without affecting image quality [S3].
- **Step:** Perform visibility filtering as a post-processing step on the Gaussian representation before rendering [S3].
- **Number:** On MipNeRF360 scenes, achieves up to 10% mean FPS speedup (e.g., 410 FPS vs 373 FPS without filtering) [S3]. On Zip-NeRF scenes, achieves up to 45% improvement in rendering speed (e.g., 630 FPS vs 435 FPS without filtering) [S3].
- **Comparison:** Provides significant speedup on large-scale scenes (Zip-NeRF) compared to central object-focused scenes (MipNeRF360), making it particularly important for large scenes [S3].

## Background
Pruning and reduction techniques in 3D Gaussian Splatting emerged as the method scaled to larger scenes and higher resolutions, revealing that the number of Gaussians required for high-fidelity rendering could exceed practical memory and bandwidth limits. Early work focused on visibility-based filtering to skip rendering of occluded Gaussians [S3]. Subsequent research introduced more sophisticated pruning strategies, such as learnable masks to remove small kernels during training [S2], and block-wise optimization to handle joint deletion effects [S1, unofficial]. Mixed-primitive approaches also introduced vertex-level pruning to handle non-standard Gaussian shapes like triangles and lines [S1, unofficial]. These methods are widely used in real-time rendering applications, mobile AR/VR, and large-scale scene reconstruction where storage and frame rate are critical constraints.
