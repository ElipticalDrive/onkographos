# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which fails to differentiate over-reconstructed regions from well-reconstructed ones and results in small gradients in over-reconstructed areas, FR increases pixel gradients in these regions, enabling adaptive densification [S2]. It explicitly disentangles frequency components by regularizing both amplitude and phase discrepancies in the Fourier domain [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Image dimensions $H, W$.
- **Outputs:** Scalar regularization loss values $d_a$ (amplitude discrepancy) and $d_p$ (phase discrepancy).

**Data Structures:**
- Complex frequency values $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$, where $u \in [0, H-1]$ and $v \in [0, W-1]$ are frequency coordinates.
- Real and imaginary components $Re(u, v)$ and $Im(u, v)$.

**Steps:**
1. **Compute 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** Convert the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth image $I$ into their frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$. For a given image $I$, the DFT is computed as [S2]:
   $$
   F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)}
   $$
   where $(x, y)$ are spatial image coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value at $(x, y)$.

2. **Compute Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency component $F(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$ [S2]:
   $$
   |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{Re(u, v)^2 + Im(u, v)^2}
   $$
   $$
   \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{Im(u, v)}{Re(u, v)}\right)
   $$
   where $Re(u, v)$ and $Im(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$.

3. **Calculate Amplitude Discrepancy ($d_a$):** Compute the Euclidean distance between the amplitudes of the ground truth and rendered frequency spectra, averaged over all pixels [S2]:
   $$
   d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2}
   $$

4. **Calculate Phase Discrepancy ($d_p$):** Compute the Euclidean distance between the phases of the ground truth and rendered frequency spectra, averaged over all pixels [S2]:
   $$
   d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2}
   $$

5. **Optimization:** The total frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of $d_a$ and $d_p$. This loss is backpropagated to update the Gaussian parameters (centers, scales, opacities, colors) using an optimizer (e.g., Adam) [S2].

**Parameters:**
- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
- **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
- **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are used for rendering [S2].
- **Frequency Annealing:** Advanced implementations (e.g., FreGS) use an annealing technique with a low-pass filter $H_l$ and dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ to progressively leverage low-to-high frequencies, achieving coarse-to-fine densification [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Over-reconstruction:** Without FR, L1 loss leads to redundant Gaussians in well-reconstructed regions because it cannot distinguish them from under-reconstructed ones [S2].
- **Artifacts:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can bias the splatting towards undesirable artifacts and restrict densification [S2].
- **Resolution:** Training often starts at lower resolution and upsamples after a certain number of iterations (e.g., 500 iterations) to stabilize optimization [S2].

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The addition of 2D DFT computations per iteration increases the computational overhead compared to standard L1 loss. The complexity of DFT is $O(HW \log(HW))$ using FFT, or $O(H^2 W^2)$ for direct computation.
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing frequency representations $F$ and $\hat{F}$, which are complex-valued matrices of size $H \times W$.
- **Performance:** FR significantly increases pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions, leading to superior Gaussian densification compared to methods using only L1 loss [S2]. It allows for a trade-off between memory and performance by enabling training with a similar number of Gaussians as 3D-GS while maintaining or improving PSNR/SSIM [S2].
- **Strengths:** Effectively mitigates over-reconstruction, improves densification in high-frequency regions, and handles scenes with complex structures better than spatial-domain losses alone [S2].
- **Limitations:** Computationally more expensive than spatial losses. Naive application without frequency annealing can lead to artifacts [S2].
- **When to use:** When the scene exhibits over-reconstruction artifacts or when standard densification fails to capture high-frequency details. Particularly useful in conjunction with progressive frequency annealing [S2].
- **When not to use:** When computational resources are severely limited and the scene is simple, or when sparse-view inputs require wavelet-based approaches (like DWTGS) instead of Fourier-based ones [S4, unofficial].

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced to address the limitations of raw optimization methods in 3D Gaussian Splatting, which can lead to over-reconstruction in some regions and underrepresentation in others, resulting in blur and visual inconsistencies [S1]. The technique was formalized in the context of FreGS (3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization), which aims to boost 3D Gaussian splatting from a frequency perspective by minimizing the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth [S2]. It builds on the observation that amplitude and phase capture different information, allowing for more accurate assessment of reconstruction errors [S2]. The method is part of a broader trend of improving 3D GS optimization through additional regularization, including geometry and frequency constraints [S1].
