# Optimization Schedule and Densification

Also called: Densification Scheduling, Primitive Growth Control

## Overview
This family of methods addresses the inefficiency and instability of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) optimization by controlling the rate of primitive creation (densification) and the resolution at which the scene is rendered during training. Standard 3DGS uses a fixed schedule for splitting and cloning Gaussians, which often leads to redundant primitives, poor convergence in late stages, or excessive computational cost. These methods distinguish themselves by decoupling densification from fixed iteration counts, using dynamic resolution scaling, or employing learned networks to determine when and where to add primitives. They solve the problem of balancing rendering fidelity against training time and memory usage.

## Shared principles
The core mechanism shared by these methods is the modification of the standard 3DGS optimization loop, specifically the densification phase. In standard 3DGS, densification occurs at fixed intervals (e.g., every 100 iterations) based on gradient magnitude thresholds. These methods introduce:
1.  **Dynamic Resolution:** Rendering at a lower resolution for early iterations to fit low-frequency structures, then increasing resolution to fit high-frequency details. This reduces the computational load during the initial, most expensive phase of optimization.
2.  **Adaptive Primitive Budgeting:** Instead of a fixed maximum number of Gaussians, the budget is determined dynamically based on scene complexity, momentum of previous growth, or specific scheduling functions (e.g., concave-up growth curves).
3.  **Decoupled Densification Operations:** Separating the "split" operation (global spread) from the "clone" operation (local refinement) and applying them at different stages or resolutions to prevent premature clustering.
4.  **Learned or Heuristic Densification:** Using neural networks or specific gradient-based criteria (e.g., normal gradients) to decide densification, rather than simple view-space gradient thresholds.

## Subtopics

### [Error-Driven 2D Gaussian Training](13985-error-driven-2d-gaussian-training.md)
A densification strategy that initializes supplementary 3D Gaussians by first optimizing 2D Gaussian kernels in image space. It solves the problem of initializing view-dependent Gaussian kernels for complex specular radiance by leveraging pre-rendered images as fixed backgrounds, distinguishing it from standard 3DGS densification which operates directly in 3D space. It allocates kernels proportional to rendering loss and back-projects optimized 2D primitives to 3D using WPCA.

### [DashGaussian](15742-dashgaussian.md)
A scheduling scheme that accelerates training by reformulating optimization as progressive fitting of higher frequency components via dynamic rendering resolution. It produces a concave-up primitive growth curve that spans the entire optimization process, synchronizing primitive growth with dynamically increasing rendering resolution. Unlike methods that peak in primitive count at half-iteration, it uses momentum-based budgeting to adapt to scene complexity.

### [Training Configuration and Hyperparameters](16385-training-configuration-and-hyperparameters.md)
Defines standardized hyperparameters for downstream tasks (classification, segmentation, GAN integration). It differs by focusing on fixed-attribute Gaussian encodings and specific dataset protocols rather than dynamic densification schedules. It complements this family by providing static configuration values when densification is disabled or handled via decoder-specific constraints.

### QuickSplat Optimization Process
An optimization framework that uses learned networks for initialization, densification, and parameter updates to accelerate surface reconstruction [S1]. It iterates for $t = 0$ to $T-1$, accumulating gradients $\nabla G_t$ over all images. It generates new Gaussians $\hat{G}_t$ using a densifier network $f_D$ on current Gaussians $G_t$, accumulated gradients $\nabla G_t$, and time step $t$: $\hat{G}_t \leftarrow f_D(G_t, \nabla G_t, t)$ [S1].

### BlitzGS Optimization Schedule
Disables the Level-of-Detail (LOD) gate during the density-control window (iterations 2,000–32,000) and engages it after the first importance-scoring pass ($T_1$) [S2, unofficial]. After $T_1$, it reweights density-control gradients using the per-Gaussian visibility factor $\phi_i$ for the remainder of the window [S2, unofficial]. Density control runs every 1,000 steps from iteration 2,000 up to 32,000 [S2, unofficial]. Extending training iterations (e.g., 500k–1000k) does not significantly improve quality on aerial scenes, indicating deficits stem from representation structure rather than insufficient budget [S2, unofficial].

### Normal Gradient Based Densification
A densification criterion to improve normal recovery in thin regions by introducing an additional criterion on the gradient of normals, beyond the vanilla 3DGS gradient of view space points [S3]. It densifies Gaussians whose normal gradient exceeds a threshold $T_n$ [S3]. The typical value for $T_n$ is $2 \times 10^{-9}$ [S3].

### Global-to-local densification
A strategy that decouples split and clone operations across densification phases to accelerate optimization. It uses split for global spread at low resolution and suppresses clone, then reintroduces clone for local refinement at full resolution [S4, unofficial]. It calculates the norm of the average position gradient of each Gaussian primitive every 100 iterations [S4, unofficial]. If the gradient norm exceeds a threshold, it checks the maximum scale: if the scale exceeds a threshold, it splits; otherwise, it clones [S4, unofficial]. This prevents premature local clustering and redundant primitives [S4, unofficial].

### Resolution Guided Primitive Scheduler
Uses a momentum-based budgeting scheme where the final primitive count $P_{fin}^{(i)}$ is calculated as $\max(P_{fin}^{(i-1)}, \gamma \cdot P_{fin}^{(i-1)} + \eta \cdot P_{add})$, where $P_{add}$ is the number of primitives densified in step $i$ [S5]. $P_{fin}^{(0)}$ is initialized as $5 \cdot P_{init}$ [S5]. The power factor in primitive growth starts at 2 and gradually decreases to 1 to suppress early growth and encourage mid-stage growth [S5]. Hyperparameters are $\gamma = 0.98$ and $\eta = 1$ [S5]. Resolution modulation uses $\lfloor r(i) \rfloor$ to encourage faster growth [S5]. Too much growth at the end results in poor convergence, mitigated by this scheduling [S5].

### 3D Refinement View Sampling Strategy
A staged sampling schedule for supervising 3D refinement that balances fitting the current refined view with preserving rendering ability in original and previously refined views [S6]. It divides total 3D refinement steps into three equal phases: in the first third, every 3 steps are 'current-refine steps'; in the subsequent third, every 5 steps; in the final third, every 8 steps [S6]. For non-current-refine steps, views are randomly sampled from the training set $S_{train}$ and previous refined set $F_{i-1}$, with lower selection weight for $F_{i-1}$ [S6].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [G] introduced a novel explicit scene representation using 3D Gaussians, enabling real-time novel view synthesis. However, the original training procedure involves a fixed densification schedule that can be inefficient, leading to either under-representation of complex scenes or excessive memory usage. Subsequent works have focused on optimizing this schedule. DashGaussian [S5] demonstrated that dynamic resolution and concave-up primitive growth could significantly reduce training time. BlitzGS [S2, unofficial] addressed city-scale rendering by managing density control windows and LOD gates. QuickSplat [S1] proposed learned networks for densification. Other works like Global-to-local [S4, unofficial] and Normal Gradient [S3] refined the criteria for when to split or clone primitives. These methods collectively form the "Optimization Schedule and Densification" family, aiming to make 3DGS training faster, more stable, and more memory-efficient.
