# Cross-scale geometric adaptation

Also called: Lgeo, Reprojection error supervision, Reprojection-based cross-scale geometric adaptation

## Overview
Cross-scale geometric adaptation is a self-adaptive regularization mechanism that guides geometry learning in few-shot neural rendering by utilizing reprojection errors across multiple voxel scales [S1]. It solves the problem of geometric inconsistency and floating artifacts in sparse-view scenarios by selecting render depths with minimal reprojection error across different scales as pseudo-labels, ensuring geometric integrity without requiring explicit depth ground truth [S1]. It sets itself apart from single-scale depth priors by progressively adapting to scene frequencies, using low-resolution voxels for coarse geometry early in training and high-resolution voxels for detail refinement later, thereby avoiding the scale mismatches that cause single-scale methods to suffer from higher final error [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Training views (frames $i$ and $j$), camera intrinsics $K_i, K_j$, camera poses (rotation $R_i, R_j$ and translation $t_i, t_j$), multi-scale voxel representations (low, mid, high resolution), and rendered RGB maps $C_i, C_j$ and depths $D_{il}$ at scale $l$ for each frame.
*   **Outputs:** Geometric adaptation loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ and pseudo-ground truth depth $D'(r_{train})$ for each training ray $r_{train}$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Multi-scale Voxels:** A hierarchy of voxel grids at $L$ scales (e.g., low, mid, high). Each scale $l$ stores density and color attributes used for volume rendering to produce depth $D_{il}(p_i)$ and color $C_i(p_i)$ [S1].
*   **Ray Data:** For each training ray $r_{train}$, the 2D pixel coordinate $p_i$ in frame $i$ and its homogeneous augmentation $\tilde{p}_i$ are stored [S1].

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Reproject to 3D (Frame $i$):** For a given pixel $p_i$ in frame $i$ and scale $l$, compute the 3D point in camera coordinates $x_{li}$ using the rendered depth $D_{il}(p_i)$ and camera intrinsics $K_i$:
    $$ x_{li} = D_{il}(p_i) K_i^{-1} \tilde{p}_i $$
    [S1]
2.  **Transform to World Coordinates:** Transform $x_{li}$ to world coordinates $x_l$ using frame $i$'s rotation $R_i$ and translation $t_i$:
    $$ x_l = R_i x_{li} + t_i $$
    [S1]
3.  **Reproject to Frame $j$:** Transform the world coordinate point $x_l$ to the camera coordinate system of frame $j$ using frame $j$'s rotation $R_j$ and translation $t_j$:
    $$ x_{li \to j} = R_j^T (x_l - t_j) $$
    [S1]
4.  **Project to 2D (Frame $j$):** Project $x_{li \to j}$ back to the 2D pixel coordinate system of frame $j$ using the projection function $\pi([x, y, z]^T) = [x/z, y/z]^T$ and intrinsics $K_j$:
    $$ \tilde{p}_{li \to j} = \pi(K_j x_{li \to j}) $$
    [S1]
5.  **Compute Reprojection Error:** Calculate the reprojection error $e_l(p_i)$ for scale $l$ as the difference between the original RGB pixel $C_i(p_i)$ and the warped pixel $C_j(\tilde{p}_{li \to j})$ from frame $j$:
    $$ e_l(p_i) = C_i(p_i) - C_j(\tilde{p}_{li \to j}) $$
    [S1]
6.  **Select Pseudo-Ground Truth:** Identify the scale $l$ with the minimum reprojection error for the ray $r_{train}$:
    $$ D'(r_{train}) = \arg \min_l (e_l(r_{train})) $$
    [S1]
7.  **Compute Loss:** Calculate the geometric adaptation loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the rendered depth $D_{il}(r_{train})$ at each scale and the selected pseudo-ground truth depth $D'(r_{train})$, summed over all training rays $R_{train}$:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{geo}(r_{train}) = \sum_{l=0}^{L} \| \hat{D}_l(r_{train}) - D'(r_{train}) \|^2 $$
    [S1]

def project_2d(K, x_cam):
    """Project 3D camera coordinate to 2D pixel coordinate."""
    x, y, z = x_cam
    if z <= 0:
        return None
    u = K[0,0] * (x/z) + K[0,2]
    v = K[1,1] * (y/z) + K[1,2]
    return np.array([u, v])

def is_in_bounds(p, resolution):
    """Check if pixel coordinate is within image bounds."""
    return 0 <= p[0] < resolution[0] and 0 <= p[1] < resolution[1]

**Parameters:**
*   **Number of Scales ($L$):** Typically 3 (low, mid, high) [S1].
*   **Loss Weight ($\lambda$):** Not given in the sources.
*   **Training Schedule:** Low-resolution voxels are used primarily in early training; mid- and high-resolution voxels refine details as training advances [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Invalid Projections:** If the reprojected point $\tilde{p}_{li \to j}$ falls outside the image bounds of frame $j$, the error is considered infinite or the scale is discarded for that frame [S1].
*   **Occlusions:** Reprojection assumes visibility. If a surface is occluded in frame $j$ but visible in frame $i$, the reprojection error will be high, potentially leading to incorrect scale selection. The method relies on the assumption that the geometry is consistent across views [S1].
*   **Sparse Views:** In scenarios with significant viewpoint changes or very sparse training views, the model may face challenges in generalization, as accurate camera poses are required [S1].

## Subtopics

### Frequency Regularization
Penalizes discrepancies between Fourier spectra to guide high-frequency detail. It differs by operating in the frequency domain rather than using spatial reprojection consistency. Choose when high-frequency noise is the primary issue [6130-frequency-regularization.md].

### Smoothed 2D Supervision
Uses smoothed 2D labels for supervision. It differs by relying on 2D image-space smoothing rather than 3D reprojection consistency. Choose when 2D priors are more reliable than 3D geometry [7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md].

### 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization
Enforces half-ellipsoid covariance structures. It differs by constraining the shape of the primitives directly rather than supervising depth via reprojection. Choose when preserving opacity and color stability during deformation is critical [9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md].

### Hard and Soft Depth Regularization
Uses explicit depth priors (Ld). It differs by relying on ground truth or estimated depth maps rather than self-supervised reprojection. It is less robust to scale mismatches compared to cross-scale adaptation [9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md].

### FreGS
Uses frequency-guided Gaussian unpooling. It differs by focusing on densification strategies based on frequency rather than geometric consistency across scales [9212-fregs.md].

### Geometry Regularization
A general term for geometric constraints. It differs by potentially lacking the specific multi-scale adaptive mechanism of cross-scale adaptation [14222-geometry-regularization.md].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds overhead for reprojection calculations for each scale and each training ray. Requires computing 3D points and warping pixels for multiple scales and potentially multiple frame pairs.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing multi-scale voxel representations and intermediate 3D points for all scales during the loss computation.
*   **Measured Performance:** Significantly reduces floaters and improves convergence quality across scales compared to training without geometric adaptation [S1]. It achieves lower final depth error than single-scale depth priors ($L_d$) because it avoids scale mismatches [S1]. Combining $L_{geo}$ with $L_d$ yields the best results [S1].
*   **Scaling:** The method is designed for few-shot scenarios where data is limited, making the self-supervised nature advantageous. It adapts to diverse scenes [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Self-adaptive, no complex scheduling required, robust to geometric noise compared to feature-warping methods like GeCoNeRF [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on accurate camera poses. Performance may degrade in scenarios with significant viewpoint changes or very sparse training views (e.g., 360° datasets) [S1].

## Background
Cross-scale geometric adaptation was proposed in the **FrugalNeRF** framework by the authors of [S1] in 2025. It was developed to address the challenges of few-shot novel view synthesis, specifically to accelerate training and regularize geometry without external priors. The method leverages weight-sharing voxels across scales to capture varying scene frequencies and uses reprojection errors to guide multi-scale geometry learning [S1]. It is used in the context of neural rendering and few-shot view synthesis.
