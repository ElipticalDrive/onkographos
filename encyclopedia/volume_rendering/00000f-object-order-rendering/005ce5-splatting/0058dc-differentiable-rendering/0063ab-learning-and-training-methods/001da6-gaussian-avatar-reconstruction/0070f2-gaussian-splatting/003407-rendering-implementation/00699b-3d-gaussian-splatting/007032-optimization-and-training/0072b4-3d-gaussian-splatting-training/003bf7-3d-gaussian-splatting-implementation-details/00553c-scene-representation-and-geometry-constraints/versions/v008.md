# Scene Representation and Geometry Constraints

Also called: Geometry-aware Gaussian Splatting, Surface-constrained 3DGS

## Overview
This family of methods modifies the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation to improve geometric fidelity, enforce surface alignment, or extract explicit geometry, addressing the limitation that standard 3DGS produces soft, volumetric primitives that blur sharp boundaries and lack precise surface definitions. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [DeSplat](12925-desplat.md) and [360 Gaussian Splatting](12929-360-gaussian-splatting.md) by focusing on the structural integrity of the scene representation (geometry, normals, and boundaries) rather than training efficiency, compression, or perceptual wrappers.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting representation, where the scene is defined by a set of $N$ 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$. Each Gaussian $G_i$ is defined by its mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$, covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ (derived from rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$ and scale $\mathbf{S}_i$), opacity $\alpha_i$, and view-dependent color $\mathbf{c}_i(\omega)$.

The rendering process evaluates the Gaussian density along a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\omega$ and integrates contributions. The Gaussian density function is:
$$ G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right) $$

The core variation across methods lies in the definition, storage, and optimization of $\mathbf{c}_i(\omega)$ and potentially $\alpha_i$, moving beyond the fixed SH coefficients used in base 3DGS. Common strategies include:
1.  **Post-Processing Transformation:** Applying a learned affine transformation to the rendered image based on 3D position and view embedding [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
2.  **Factorized Illumination:** Separating scene lighting (via a tensor grid) from material properties (via BRDF features) to model specular effects [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
3.  **Image-Conditioned Modulation:** Using an MLP and per-image embeddings to modulate Gaussian color and opacity, enabling transient object removal and appearance synthesis [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
4.  **Neural Field Substitution:** Replacing per-Gaussian SH with a shared grid-based neural field to reduce parameters and improve generalization [14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md].
5.  **Volumetric Attenuation:** Modifying the alpha-blending equation to incorporate medium properties, such as direct attenuation $B_d$ and backscatter parameters $B_\infty, B_b$, to model light transport in non-vacuum media [15148-physics-and-optical-modeling.md].

## Subtopics

### [Gaussian Representation and Truncation](12855-gaussian-representation-and-truncation.md)
This family modifies the standard 3DGS representation to improve geometric fidelity, rendering efficiency, or physical accuracy. It sets itself apart by addressing specific limitations of the base method: [Inverse Multiquadric Splatting](9910-inverse-multiquadric-splatting.md) changes the radial basis function for better tail behavior; [Opacity-Independent Gaussian Truncation](10266-opacity-independent-gaussian-truncation.md) decouples visibility from opacity to allow aggressive culling; [Content-Aware Texturing](12606-content-aware-texturing-for-gaussian-splatting.md) adds dynamic textures to capture high-frequency details; [Directed Distance Field](12854-directed-distance-field-ddf-for-gaussian-splatting.md) provides a neural oracle for secondary rays; and [Atomized Proliferation](12924-atomized-proliferation.md) constrains Gaussian sizes for precise geometry.

### [Discontinuity-aware Gaussian Splatting](13138-discontinuity-aware-gaussian-splatting.md)
Discontinuity-aware Gaussian Splatting (DisC-GS) enables 3DGS to represent and render sharp boundaries and discontinuities, addressing the limitation of standard 3DGS which tends to blur such features. It differs from sibling methods like [Planar Gaussian Splatting](16706-planar-gaussian-splatting.md) by explicitly modeling boundaries as cubic Bézier curves within the Gaussian's local subspace and applying a "pre-scissoring" operation during rendering, rather than relying solely on geometric constraints or mesh extraction.

**Inputs:** A set of $N$ 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where each Gaussian $G_i$ is defined by mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$, rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$, scale $\mathbf{S}_i$, opacity $\alpha_i$, and color $\mathbf{c}_i$. For 2D Gaussian Splatting, the last column of $\mathbf{S}_i$ is fixed to zero. The input also includes the camera projection matrix $\mathbf{P}$ and viewing transformation matrix $\mathbf{W}$, and a user-defined hyperparameter $M$ (number of Bézier curves per Gaussian).

**Outputs:** A rendered image $C(p)$ for each pixel $p$, where discontinuities are preserved.

**Data Structures:**
Each Gaussian $G_i$ is augmented with an additional attribute $\mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}} \in \mathbb{R}^{4M \times 2}$.
*   **Layout:** $\mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}}$ stores $4M$ points in the 2D coordinate system defined by the Gaussian's center $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ (origin), rotation matrix column $\mathbf{r}_{1,i}$ (x-axis), and rotation matrix column $\mathbf{r}_{2,i}$ (y-axis).
*   **Interpretation:** These points serve as control points for $M$ cubic Bézier curves representing the boundaries of the Gaussian's projection.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Local to World Transformation:** For each Gaussian $G_i$, transform the control points from its local 2D subspace to the 3D world space. Let $\mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}}[i, :]$ denote the $i$-th point in the local attribute (for $i \in \{0, \dots, 4M-1\}$). The 3D control points $\mathbf{c}_{\text{3D\_curve}}[i]$ are computed as:
    $$ \mathbf{c}_{\text{3D\_curve}}[i] = \boldsymbol{\mu}_i + \mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}}[i, 0] \times \mathbf{r}_{1,i} + \mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}}[i, 1] \times \mathbf{r}_{2,i} $$
    Note: $\mathbf{r}_{1,i}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{2,i}$ are unit vectors, so normalization is omitted.

2.  **Projection to Image Plane:** Project the 3D control points onto the image plane to obtain the 2D control points $\mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[i]$:
    $$ \mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[i] = \mathbf{P} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{c}_{\text{3D\_curve}}[i] $$
    where $\mathbf{P}$ is the projective transformation matrix and $\mathbf{W}$ is the viewing transformation matrix.

3.  **Implicit Curve Representation:** For each of the $M$ cubic Bézier curves defined by four ordered control points $\boldsymbol{\omega}_0, \boldsymbol{\omega}_1, \boldsymbol{\omega}_2, \boldsymbol{\omega}_3$ (where $\boldsymbol{\omega}_k = \mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[4j+k]$ for curve index $j$), compute the implicit representation $B_{\text{imp}}(x, y)$:
    $$ B_{\text{imp}}(x, y) = \gamma_{xxx} x^3 + \gamma_{xxy} x^2 y + \gamma_{xyy} x y^2 + \gamma_{yyy} y^3 + \gamma_{xx} x^2 + \gamma_{xy} xy + \gamma_{yy} y^2 + \gamma_x x + \gamma_y y + \gamma_0 = 0 $$
    The coefficients $\gamma$ are obtained via $O(1)$ arithmetic operations on the control point coordinates.

4.  **Indicator Function Construction:** Define the single-curve indicator function $g_{\text{sc}}$ for a pixel $p=(x_p, y_p)$:
    $$ g_{\text{sc}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_0, \boldsymbol{\omega}_1, \boldsymbol{\omega}_2, \boldsymbol{\omega}_3; p) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } B_{\text{imp}}(x_p, y_p) > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
    For $M > 1$ curves, the combined indicator function $g(p)$ is the product of individual indicators:
    $$ g(p) = \prod_{j=0}^{M-1} g_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[4j], \mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[4j+1], \mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[4j+2], \mathbf{c}_{\text{2D\_curve}}[4j+3]; p) $$
    A pixel is "scissored out" ($g(p)=0$) if it is outside at least one of the $M$ curves.

5.  **Discontinuity-Aware Rendering:** Compute the color $C(p)$ using a modified $\alpha$-blending function:
    $$ C(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{c}_i \beta_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \beta_j) $$
    where the effective opacity $\beta_i$ for the $i$-th Gaussian is:
    $$ \beta_i = \alpha_i g_i(p) \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{i,2D})^\top (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,2D})^{-1} (\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{i,2D})\right) $$
    Here, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i,2D}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,2D}$ are the projected mean and covariance of the $i$-th Gaussian, and $g_i(p)$ is the indicator function for the $i$-th Gaussian.

6.  **Gradient Approximation:** During backpropagation, use a Bézier-boundary gradient approximation strategy to enable gradients to pass through the discontinuous indicator function $g(p)$, maintaining differentiability for learning $\mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}}$.

**Parameters:**
*   $M$: User-defined hyperparameter representing the number of Bézier curves per Gaussian distribution.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Non-differentiability:** The indicator function $g(p)$ is piecewise constant, making the forward rendering process non-differentiable. Naive integration breaks backpropagation.
*   **Zero Gradients:** Even in differentiable segments, the gradient of $g(\cdot)$ with respect to its inputs is zero, which fails to guide the learning of $\mathbf{c}_{\text{curve}}$ without the Bézier-boundary gradient approximation strategy.
*   **Viewpoint Inconsistency:** Using 3D Gaussians can result in viewpoint inconsistency. DisC-GS fixes the last column of the scale matrix $\mathbf{S}$ to be a zero vector for 2D Gaussians to mitigate this.

### [Mesh Extraction and Representation](14095-mesh-extraction-and-representation.md)
This family of methods converts the volumetric, implicit representation of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) into explicit geometric structures, primarily triangular meshes or dense point clouds, to enable compatibility with standard 3D graphics pipelines, mesh editing tools, and physics simulations. Unlike standard 3DGS, which renders via alpha-blended splats, these methods extract a discrete surface or point set that approximates the radiance field, allowing for operations such as texture mapping, rigging, and collision detection. The key distinction lies in whether the extraction is driven by surface-alignment regularization during training (e.g., SuGaR) or by post-hoc probabilistic sampling of the trained density field (e.g., Point Cloud Conversion).

### [Appearance and Physical Modeling](15766-appearance-and-physical-modeling.md)
Appearance and Physical Modeling extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation to handle complex lighting, material properties, and non-vacuum media by modifying the definition, storage, and optimization of view-dependent color $\mathbf{c}_i(\omega)$ and opacity $\alpha_i$. It solves the limitations of static Spherical Harmonics (SH) coefficients, which struggle with high-frequency specularities, complex lighting variations, and volumetric light transport in participating media. This family sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Gaussian Representation and Truncation](12855-gaussian-representation-and-truncation.md) and [Mesh Extraction and Representation](14095-mesh-extraction-and-representation.md) by focusing on the radiometric and physical properties of the primitives rather than their geometric density or surface extraction. It distinguishes between [Color and Appearance Modeling](14740-color-and-appearance-modeling.md), which synthesizes appearance in clear space, and [Physics and Optical Modeling](15148-physics-and-optical-modeling.md), which models volumetric attenuation in scattering media.

### [Planar Gaussian Splatting](16706-planar-gaussian-splatting.md)
Planar Gaussian Splatting (PGS) is an unsupervised neural rendering framework that reconstructs 3D geometry and explicitly parses 3D planar surfaces from multiple RGB images without requiring 3D labels or depth supervision. It extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting representation by introducing discriminative 3D plane descriptors and enforcing local planar alignment constraints. Unlike sibling methods that focus on high-fidelity rendering or generic mesh extraction, PGS is designed to identify distinct 3D plane instances through a hierarchical Gaussian Mixture Tree (GMT). It sets itself apart by using 2D segmentation priors from foundation models (like SAM) to guide the learning of these descriptors, enabling robust planar reconstruction across different scenes.

PGS operates on a set of $N$ 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$. Each Gaussian $G_i$ retains the standard parameters: mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ (derived from rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$ and scale $\mathbf{S}_i$), opacity $\alpha_i$, and view-dependent color $\mathbf{c}_i(\omega)$. Additionally, each Gaussian carries two new parameters: a surface normal vector $\mathbf{n}_i$ and a learnable plane descriptor vector $\mathbf{z}_i$. The descriptor $\mathbf{z}_i$ is constrained to unit norm ($\|\mathbf{z}_i\|_2 = 1$) and serves to identify distinct 3D planes.

#### Data Structures
*   **Gaussian Field:** A list of $N$ Gaussians. Each Gaussian stores:
    *   $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Position.
    *   $\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Rotation matrix.
    *   $\mathbf{S}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Scale vector.
    *   $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$: Opacity.
    *   $\mathbf{c}_i$: Spherical Harmonics coefficients (Degree 2) [S1].
    *   $\mathbf{n}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Surface normal (unit vector).
    *   $\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$: Plane descriptor (unit vector, $d=3$ in experiments) [S1].
*   **KNN Structure:** For each Gaussian, a list of $K$ nearest neighbor indices is maintained to compute local covariance and smoothing [S1].
*   **Region Adjacency Graph (RAG):** A graph where nodes are 2D segments and edges represent adjacency, used to merge segments belonging to the same plane [S1].

#### Initialization
1.  **Gaussian Placement:** Initialize Gaussian locations and colors using a sparse point cloud obtained by running Structure-from-Motion (SfM) on the training images [S1].
2.  **Leaf Node Generation (Optional/Alternative):** Instead of initializing all Gaussians randomly, leaf nodes of the Gaussian Mixture Tree can be generated by:
    *   Prompting SAM with a $32 \times 32$ grid on input images to get masks [S1].
    *   Ignoring segments with high normal vector variance [S1].
    *   Lifting boundary points of valid SAM masks into 3D space using rendered depth maps and camera parameters [S1].
    *   Computing the mean and covariance of these 3D boundary points to form initial Gaussian distributions [S1].

#### Optimization Steps
The training runs for 15,000 iterations on a single GPU [S1]. The core loop involves standard 3DGS densification (cloning, splitting, culling) and the following specialized steps:

1.  **Rendering:** Render the scene to obtain photometric loss. Use Spherical Harmonics of degree 2 [S1].
2.  **Plane Descriptor Learning (Linear Regression):**
    *   Render a 2D descriptor image where each pixel is the blended 3D descriptor $\mathbf{z}$ of the Gaussians visible at that pixel [S1].
    *   Let $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times d}$ be the matrix of rendered descriptors for $P$ pixels, augmented with a column of 1s.
    *   Let $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times m}$ be the one-hot label matrix for the $m$ segments in the current view (derived from SAM) [S1].
    *   Solve for weights $\hat{\mathbf{W}}$ using the closed-form solution:
        $$ \hat{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{Z}^\top \mathbf{Z})^{-1} \mathbf{Z}^\top \mathbf{Y} $$
    *   Compute the prediction $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = [\mathbf{z}_i | 1] \hat{\mathbf{W}}$ for each pixel [S1].
    *   Compute segmentation loss:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{seg} = \sum_{i} \| \mathbf{y}_i - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_i \|_1 $$
    *   Backpropagate $\mathcal{L}_{seg}$ to update $\mathbf{z}_i$ [S1].
    *   *Note:* This regression is recomputed for each camera view. The number of segments $m$ varies per image [S1].
3.  **Segment Merging (RAG):**
    *   Construct a RAG from the 2D segments [S1].
    *   Cut edges in the RAG if the planar distance between segments exceeds 10 cm or the surface normal cosine distance exceeds 20 degrees [S1].
    *   Merge remaining connected components to form consistent 2D plane labels for the current view [S1].
4.  **Local Planar Alignment:**
    *   Applied every 500 iterations [S1].
    *   For each Gaussian, find its $K=30$ nearest neighbors (KNN) [S1].
    *   Compute the $3 \times 3$ covariance matrix of the KNN Gaussian centers [S1].
    *   Perform Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on the covariance matrix [S1].
    *   Identify the local tangent plane using the two eigenvectors corresponding to the two largest eigenvalues [S1].
    *   Project the Gaussian center $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ onto this local tangent plane [S1].
    *   Apply Laplacian smoothing to $\mathbf{n}_i$ and $\mathbf{z}_i$ by averaging over the features of the KNN neighbors [S1].
5.  **Holistic Separability (Mean-Shift):**
    *   Applied once after 2,000 iterations, then every 100 iterations [S1].
    *   Goal: Enhance separability of descriptors $\mathbf{z}$ for distinct planes [S1].
    *   Update rule for the descriptor matrix $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$:
        $$ \mathbf{Z} \leftarrow \mathbf{Z} \cdot (\eta \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot \mathbf{D}^{-1} + (1-\eta) \cdot \mathbf{I}) $$
        Where:
        *   $\mathbf{K}$ is the pairwise distance kernel using the von Mises-Fisher distribution [S1].
        *   $\mathbf{D}$ is the diagonal matrix of row sums of $\mathbf{K}$ [S1].
        *   $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix [S1].
        *   $\eta$ is a mixing parameter [S1].
        *   $\gamma = 60$ is the kernel parameter (used in von Mises-Fisher) [S1].
    *   *Efficient Implementation:* Computing $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is infeasible for large $N$. Instead:
        1.  Estimate $\mathbf{K}$ on a random subset of $M$ points ($M \ll N$) without replacement [S1].
        2.  Propagate and share the estimated kernel and update vectors across all KNN samples within the chosen set [S1].
        3.  Repeat sampling until an update vector is computed for all samples or their neighbors [S1].
        4.  Perform 10 update steps per run [S1].
        5.  This process takes approximately 1 second on GPU [S1].
6.  **KNN Recomputation:** Recompute KNN indices after any refinement to the Gaussian field (cloning, splitting, culling) [S1].

#### Parameters
*   **Training Iterations:** 15,000 [S1].
*   **Hardware:** Single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti [S1].
*   **Gradient Densification Threshold:** 0.001 (increased to limit cloning/splitting) [S1].
*   **Spherical Harmonics Degree:** 2 [S1].
*   **RAG Thresholds:** Planar distance 10 cm, Normal cosine distance 20 degrees [S1].
*   **Mean-Shift Frequency:** Once after 2k iterations, then every 100 iterations [S1].
*   **Mean-Shift Steps:** 10 per run [S1].
*   **Mean-Shift Gamma ($\gamma$):** 60 [S1].
*   **KNN Neighbors ($K$):** 30 [S1].
*   **Local Planar Alignment Frequency:** Every 500 iterations [S1].

#### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Kernel Computation:** Computing the full kernel $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is quadratic in memory and infeasible for millions of points [S1]. The sampling strategy is required [S1].
*   **SAM Ambiguity:** SAM may return subparts, parts, or entire objects for a single point. High-variance normal segments are ignored as invalid [S1].
*   **Multi-view Association:** SAM masks lack semantic information, making 2D-3D lifting difficult due to unknown mask associations and variable numbers of segments [S1]. The linear regression approach handles this by solving for weights per view [S1].

#### Model-based depth estimation for volume parameter update
- [S1] During optimization, use Eq. (6) to extract depth values $z$ from the current scene estimate. These depth values are then used to update the medium-parameter estimates ($B_\infty, B_b$).
- [S1] Depth extraction from 3DGS: $z = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^M d_i \alpha_i T_i}{\sum_{i=1}^M \alpha_i T_i}$. Here $d_i$ is the depth of Gaussian $i$. This is computed at each pixel during rendering.

#### Scale Constrained by Mesh Geometry
- [S2] A constraint to prevent discrepancies between Gaussian Splat scales and the scales of corresponding triangular mesh faces by bounding the relative scale coefficients.
- [S2] $s = s_{rel} \odot s_0$, where $s$ is the final scale, $s_{rel} = (s_{rel,1}, s_{rel,2}) \in [s_{min}, s_{max}] \times [s_{min}, s_{max}]$ is the coefficient vector, $s_0$ is the predefined scale derived from the mesh faces, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.
- [S2] $s_{min}, s_{max}$: The lower and upper bounds for the relative scale coefficients $s_{rel}$.

#### Normal Estimation via Back-propagation
- [S3] A method to solve for the normal attribute $n$ for each 3D Gaussian by optimizing it from an initial random vector via back-propagation, perceiving the depth of all Gaussians along a ray as a distribution.
- [S3] Determine the normal for each pixel by estimating the expectation of the depth distribution along the ray.
- [S3] Normal consistency loss: $\mathcal {L}_{n}=\|\mathcal {N}-\tilde {\mathcal {N}}\|_2$.

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 as a real-time explicit scene representation method [G]. Subsequent research focused on enhancing its geometric properties, leading to families of methods that address its soft volumetric nature, lack of sharp boundaries, and limited physical modeling capabilities.
