# Explicit Camera Jacobian Derivation

Also called: Analytical Jacobians for 3DGS, Camera Pose Jacobian Derivation (Lie Group), Lie Algebra Jacobian for 3DGS

## Overview
This method derives analytical Jacobians of the SE(3) camera pose with respect to 3D Gaussian primitives to enable efficient, differentiable camera tracking without the overhead of automatic differentiation. It solves the problem of computationally expensive gradient computation in SLAM systems that use 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as the scene representation. Unlike prior ray-intersection methods that assume fewer Gaussians, this approach provides the first analytical Jacobians specifically for EWA splatting and 3DGS, ensuring minimal parameterization via Lie algebra. It builds on [Spherical Harmonics Optimization](14206-spherical-harmonics-optimization.md) by providing the geometric gradient backbone required for pose optimization.

## How it works
The method computes the gradient of the rendered pixel color (and depth) with respect to the camera pose $T_{CW} \in SE(3)$. It avoids automatic differentiation by explicitly deriving the chain rule terms on the Lie manifold.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Input:** Camera pose $T_{CW}$ (SE(3)), 3D Gaussian parameters in world coordinates ($\mu_W, \Sigma_W, \alpha, \mathbf{c}$), camera intrinsic matrix $K$.
- **Output:** Jacobian matrices $\frac{\partial \mu_I}{\partial T_{CW}}$ and $\frac{\partial \Sigma_I}{\partial T_{CW}}$ required for gradient descent updates.

**Data Structures:**
- $T_{CW}$: $4 \times 4$ homogeneous transformation matrix.
- $\mu_C$: $3 \times 1$ vector, Gaussian mean in camera coordinates.
- $\Sigma_I$: $2 \times 2$ matrix, 2D covariance in image space.
- $\tau$: $6 \times 1$ vector in Lie algebra $se(3)$, representing infinitesimal perturbation.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Define Manifold Derivative:**
    The derivative of a function $f(\mathbf{T})$ with respect to $\mathbf{T} \in SE(3)$ is defined using the Lie algebra logarithm map [S1, S1, unofficial]:
    $$
    \frac{\mathcal{D}(f(\mathbf{T}))}{\mathcal{D}\mathbf{T}} \triangleq \lim_{\tau \rightarrow 0} \frac{\text{Log}(f(\text{Exp}(\tau) \circ \mathbf{T}) \circ f(\mathbf{T})^{-1})}{\tau}
    $$
    where $\circ$ is group composition, $\text{Exp}: se(3) \to SE(3)$, and $\text{Log}: SE(3) \to se(3)$.

2.  **Compute Mean Jacobian:**
    Apply the chain rule to the Gaussian mean in camera coordinates $\mu_C = T_{CW} \mu_W$ (homogeneous multiplication):
    $$
    \frac{\partial \mu_I}{\partial T_{CW}} = \frac{\partial \mu_I}{\partial \mu_C} \frac{\mathcal{D}\mu_C}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}}
    $$
    The manifold derivative $\frac{\mathcal{D}\mu_C}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}}$ is derived as [S1, S1, unofficial]:
    $$
    \frac{\mathcal{D}\mu_C}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}} = [I \quad -\mu_C^\times]
    $$
    where $I$ is the $3 \times 3$ identity matrix, $\mu_C^\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix of $\mu_C$, and the result is a $3 \times 6$ matrix. The derivation uses the approximation $\text{Exp}(\tau) \approx I + \tau^\wedge$ for small $\tau$ [S1, S1, unofficial].

3.  **Compute Covariance Jacobian:**
    The 2D covariance $\Sigma_I$ depends on the projection Jacobian $J$ and the rotation component $W$ of the camera pose. The chain rule yields [S1, S1, unofficial]:
    $$
    \frac{\partial \Sigma_I}{\partial T_{CW}} = \frac{\partial \Sigma_I}{\partial J} \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mu_C} \frac{\mathcal{D}\mu_C}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}} + \frac{\partial \Sigma_I}{\partial W} \frac{\mathcal{D}W}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}}
    $$
    The term $\frac{\mathcal{D}W}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}}$ is derived as [S1, S1, unofficial]:
    $$
    \frac{\mathcal{D}W}{\mathcal{D}T_{CW}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -W_{:,1}^\times \\ 0 & -W_{:,2}^\times \\ 0 & -W_{:,3}^\times \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    where $W_{:,i}$ is the $i$-th column of the rotation matrix $W$. This results in a $9 \times 6$ matrix (flattened covariance derivative).

4.  **Skew-Symmetric Matrix Definition:**
    For a vector $\mathbf{v} = [v_x, v_y, v_z]^T$, the skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{v}^\times$ is:
    $$
    \mathbf{v}^\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -v_z & v_y \\ v_z & 0 & -v_x \\ -v_y & v_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}
    $$

**Pseudocode:**
The following Python-like pseudocode implements the derivation of the geometric Jacobians for a single Gaussian. It assumes standard vector/matrix operations.

```python
import numpy as np

def skew_symmetric(v):
    """Converts a 3D vector to its skew-symmetric matrix."""
    return np.array([
        [0, -v[2], v[1]],
        [v[2], 0, -v[0]],
        [-v[1], v[0], 0]
    ])

def compute_camera_jacobians(mu_C, W, J_partial_sigma, sigma_partial_J):
    """
    Computes the analytical Jacobians of the 2D Gaussian parameters 
    with respect to the camera pose T_CW (6 DOF).
    
    Args:
        mu_C: numpy array (3,), Gaussian mean in camera coordinates.
        W: numpy array (3,3), Rotation matrix component of T_CW.
        J_partial_sigma: numpy array (2,2,3), dSigma/dJ * dJ/dmu_C. 
                         Note: In practice, this term is often pre-computed 
                         or combined with the projection Jacobian.
                         Here we assume the user provides the combined 
                         term dSigma/dmu_C for the first part of the chain rule.
        sigma_partial_J: numpy array (2,2,2,2), dSigma/dJ. 
                         (Included for completeness if J is explicit, 
                         but typically absorbed into J_partial_sigma).
    
    Returns:
        d_mu_dT: numpy array (3, 6), Jacobian of mean w.r.t. pose.
        d_sigma_dT: numpy array (4, 6), Jacobian of flattened covariance w.r.t. pose.
    """
    
    # 1. Jacobian of Mean w.r.t. Camera Pose
    # d_mu_C / d_T_CW = [I, -mu_C^x]
    mu_C_skew = skew_symmetric(mu_C)
    I_3 = np.eye(3)
    d_mu_C_dT = np.hstack([I_3, -mu_C_skew]) # Shape (3, 6)
    
    # If we need the full chain rule for color/depth, multiply by d_mu_I / d_mu_C
    # d_mu_I_dT = d_mu_I_d_mu_C @ d_mu_C_dT
    # For this method, we return the geometric part.
    
    # 2. Jacobian of Rotation Component W w.r.t. Camera Pose
    # d_W / d_T_CW
    # Construct the 9x6 matrix
    # The derivative is block column based on the 3 columns of W
    d_W_dT = np.zeros((9, 6))
    
    for i in range(3):
        W_col_i = W[:, i]
        W_col_skew = skew_symmetric(W_col_i)
        
        # The term is [0; -W_col_i^x] stacked vertically? 
        # Source says: [ 0      -W_{:,1}^x ]
        #              [ 0      -W_{:,2}^x ]
        #              [ 0      -W_{:,3}^x ]
        # This notation implies a 3x3 block structure where the first block col is 0
        # and the second block col is -W_{:,i}^x.
        # Flattened, this corresponds to indices in the 9-vector.
        
        # Let's map to the 9x6 matrix directly.
        # The source formula (6) shows a 3x6 matrix? No, W is 3x3.
        # The derivative of a 3x3 matrix w.r.t. 6 DOF is a 9x6 matrix.
        # The source notation:
        # [ 0      -W_{:,1}^x ]
        # [ 0      -W_{:,2}^x ]
        # [ 0      -W_{:,3}^x ]
        # This looks like 3 rows of blocks. Each block is 3x6? No.
        # Let's look at Eq 31: dW/d_theta_x = e1^x W.
        # This is a 3x3 matrix.
        # The Jacobian dW/dT_CW stacks these 3x3 matrices as columns.
        
        # Column j of dW/dT_CW corresponds to derivative w.r.t. j-th DOF.
        # DOFs 0,1,2 are rotation, 3,4,5 are translation.
        # W does not depend on translation. So cols 3,4,5 are 0.
        
        # For rotation DOFs k=0,1,2:
        # dW/d_theta_k = e_k^x W
        # e_0^x = [[0,0,0],[0,0,-1],[0,1,0]]
        # e_1^x = [[0,0,1],[0,0,0],[-1,0,0]]
        # e_2^x = [[0,-1,0],[1,0,0],[0,0,0]]
        
        # Let's compute the 3 rotation columns explicitly to be safe and clear.
        pass

    # Re-implementing d_W_dT explicitly for clarity and correctness
    d_W_dT = np.zeros((9, 6))
    
    # Basis vectors for skew matrices
    e1 = np.array([1, 0, 0])
    e2 = np.array([0, 1, 0])
    e3 = np.array([0, 0, 1])
    
    # Derivatives w.r.t. rotation components (first 3 DOFs)
    # dW/d_theta_x = e1^x W
    dW_d_theta_x = skew_symmetric(e1) @ W
    d_W_dT[:, 0] = dW_d_theta_x.flatten()
    
    # dW/d_theta_y = e2^x W
    dW_d_theta_y = skew_symmetric(e2) @ W
    d_W_dT[:, 1] = dW_d_theta_y.flatten()
    
    # dW/d_theta_z = e3^x W
    dW_d_theta_z = skew_symmetric(e3) @ W
    d_W_dT[:, 2] = dW_d_theta_z.flatten()
    
    # Derivatives w.r.t. translation components (last 3 DOFs) are zero
    # d_W_dT[:, 3:] = 0
    
    # 3. Combine for Covariance
    # d_Sigma_I / d_T_CW = (d_Sigma_I / d_J * d_J / d_mu_C) * d_mu_C / d_T_CW + (d_Sigma_I / d_W) * d_W / d_T_CW
    
    # Term 1: d_Sigma_I / d_mu_C * d_mu_C / d_T_CW
    # d_Sigma_I / d_mu_C is typically (2,2,3) -> flattened to (4,3)
    # If J_partial_sigma is (4,3):
    term1 = J_partial_sigma @ d_mu_C_dT # (4, 6)
    
    # Term 2: d_Sigma_I / d_W * d_W / d_T_CW
    # d_Sigma_I / d_W is (4, 9)
    # d_W / d_T_CW is (9, 6)
    # We need d_Sigma_I / d_W. This is derived from the projection of the 3D covariance.
    # For the purpose of this article, we assume the user has computed d_Sigma_I / d_W.
    # Let's assume sigma_partial_W is (4, 9).
    # Note: The source does not provide the explicit formula for d_Sigma_I / d_W, 
    # only d_W / d_T_CW. The implementer must derive d_Sigma_I / d_W from the 
    # standard 3DGS covariance projection equations.
    
    # Placeholder for d_Sigma_I / d_W multiplication
    # term2 = sigma_partial_W @ d_W_dT
    
    # Final Jacobian
    # d_sigma_dT = term1 + term2
    
    return d_mu_C_dT, d_W_dT
```

**Parameters:**
- $\mu_C \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Gaussian center in camera coordinates.
- $T_{CW} \in SE(3)$: Camera-to-world pose.
- $W$: Rotation matrix component of $T_{CW}$.
- $\theta$: Rotation vector (Lie algebra element).
- $\tau$: Infinitesimal parameter in $se(3)$.
- $\wedge$: Hat operator mapping vector to skew-symmetric matrix.
- At least 50 iterations of gradient descent per frame are required for accurate tracking [S1, S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** The derivation relies on the first-order approximation of the Lie group exponential map. For large perturbations, higher-order terms may be needed, but for gradient descent steps, the first-order Jacobian is sufficient.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The skew-symmetric matrix is singular if the vector is zero. Ensure $\mu_C$ and $W$ columns are non-zero.
- **Degenerate Input:** If the camera is at the origin or the Gaussian is at the origin, $\mu_C$ may be zero, simplifying the mean Jacobian but requiring care in the covariance term.

## Subtopics

### Photometric and Geometric Tracking
This variant uses the derived Jacobians to optimize only the current camera pose without updating the map representation. It minimizes photometric and/or geometric residuals.
- **Definition:** Optimizes $T_{CW}$ by minimizing the difference between rendered and observed images/depths.
- **Photometric Residual:** $E_{pho} = \| I(\mathcal{G}, \mathbf{T}_{CW}) - \bar{I} \|_1$, where $I$ renders Gaussians $\mathcal{G}$ from pose $\mathbf{T}_{CW}$ and $\bar{I}$ is the observed image [S1, S1, unofficial].
- **Geometric Residual:** $E_{geo} = \| D(\mathcal{G}, \mathbf{T}_{CW}) - \bar{D} \|_1$, where $D$ is depth rasterization and $\bar{D}$ is observed depth [S1, S1, unofficial].
- **Depth Rasterization:** Per-pixel depth is computed via alpha-blending: $\mathcal{D}_p = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} z_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)$, where $z_i$ is the distance to the mean $\mu_W$ of Gaussian $i$ along the camera ray [S1, S1, unofficial].
- **Parameters:** Optimizes affine brightness parameters for exposure variation and penalizes non-edge or low-opacity pixels. Uses a weighted combination $\lambda_{pho} E_{pho} + (1-\lambda_{pho}) E_{geo}$ [S1, S1, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(N)$ per frame, where $N$ is the number of visible Gaussians. The explicit Jacobian derivation avoids the overhead of automatic differentiation, which is significant for large $N$.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store Gaussian parameters and intermediate Jacobian terms.
- **Measured Performance:** The system operates at 3.2 FPS with monocular input and 2.5 FPS with depth on the Replica dataset [S1, S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Enables real-time SLAM with 3DGS; minimal parameterization reduces redundant computations; applicable to EWA splatting and 3DGS.
- **Limitations:** Requires at least 50 iterations of gradient descent per frame for accurate tracking, which can be computationally demanding [S1, S1, unofficial]. Assumes offline application or requires careful keyframe management for online use.
- **When to Use:** When accurate camera tracking is required in a 3DGS-based SLAM system and automatic differentiation is too slow.
- **When Not to Use:** When the number of Gaussians is very small (ray-intersection methods may be simpler) or when offline optimization is acceptable and real-time tracking is not needed.

## Background
The method was introduced in "Gaussian Splatting SLAM" [S1, S1, unofficial]. Prior methods for camera pose optimization using 3D Gaussians assumed a smaller number of Gaussians and relied on ray-intersection rather than splatting, making them inapplicable to standard 3DGS. This work provides the first analytical Jacobians for SE(3) camera pose with respect to 3D Gaussians in EWA splatting, enabling efficient tracking and mapping in a unified SLAM framework.
