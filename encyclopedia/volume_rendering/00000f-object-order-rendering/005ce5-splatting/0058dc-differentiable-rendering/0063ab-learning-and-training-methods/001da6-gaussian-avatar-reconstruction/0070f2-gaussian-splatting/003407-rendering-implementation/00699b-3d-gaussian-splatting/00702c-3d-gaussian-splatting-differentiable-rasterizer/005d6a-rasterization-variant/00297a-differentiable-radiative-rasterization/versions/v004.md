# Differentiable Radiative Rasterization


## Overview
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization (DRR) is a GPU-accelerated rasterization method designed to render novel X-ray projections from radiative 3D Gaussian point clouds [S1]. It solves the problem of differentiable volume rendering for X-ray imaging, where the "volume" is represented by a set of Gaussians with associated radiation intensities rather than standard RGB colors [S1]. DRR distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [3D/2D Hybrid Gaussian Rasterization with Fallback Sigma](4812-3d-2d-hybrid-gaussian-rasterization-with-fallback-sigma.md) by specifically modeling the physics of X-ray attenuation and source-detector geometry, using a specific Jacobian for the X-ray projection and a radiative intensity accumulation model [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:**
    *   A set of $N_p$ radiative 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \{G_i(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{f}_i) \mid i = 1, \dots, N_p\}$, where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the center, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the covariance, $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the opacity, and $\mathbf{f}_i$ represents learnable inherent point features [S1].
    *   Extrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{ext} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}$ (transforming world to camera coordinates) [S1].
    *   Intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{int} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$ (projecting camera to image coordinates) [S1].
    *   X-ray source parameters: distance $L_{SD}$ (source-to-detector) and azimuth angle $\phi$ [S1].
*   **Output:**
    *   Rendered image $\mathbf{I}$, where each pixel $p$ has intensity $I(p) \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Point Cloud:** Each Gaussian $i$ stores $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$, $\alpha_i$, and feature vector $\mathbf{f}_i$ [S1].
*   **Tile Grid:** The 2D image plane is partitioned into non-overlapping tiles [S1].
*   **Gaussian List per Tile:** A list of indices of Gaussians overlapping each tile, sorted by depth [S1].

**Step-by-Step Method:**

1.  **Projection to Camera Coordinates:**
    Transform the 3D center $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ to homogeneous coordinates $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_i = [\boldsymbol{\mu}_i^\top, 1]^\top$. Compute the camera coordinate $\mathbf{t}_i$ using the extrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{ext}$:
    $$ \tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i = \mathbf{M}_{ext} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_i $$
    where $\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i = [\mathbf{t}_i^\top, 1]^\top$ and $\mathbf{t}_i = (t_x, t_y, t_z)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1].

2.  **Projection to Image Coordinates:**
    Project $\mathbf{t}_i$ to 2D image coordinates $\mathbf{u}_i$ using the intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{int}$:
    $$ \tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i = \mathbf{M}_{int} \tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i $$
    where $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i = [\mathbf{u}_i^\top, 1]^\top$ and $\mathbf{u}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ [S1].

3.  **Covariance Transformation:**
    Compute the 3D covariance in camera coordinates $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$:
    $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i = \mathbf{J}_i \mathbf{W}_i \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{J}_i^\top $$
    where $\mathbf{W}_i$ is the viewing transformation and $\mathbf{J}_i$ is the Jacobian of the affine approximation of the projective transformation [S1].

    The viewing transformation $\mathbf{W}_i$ is defined as:
    $$ \mathbf{W}_i = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin \phi & \cos \phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ -\cos \phi & -\sin \phi & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
    where $\phi$ is the azimuth angle of the X-ray source [S1].

    The Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_i$ is defined as:
    $$ \mathbf{J}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & 0 & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2} \\ 0 & \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
    Note: The source text presents this matrix with formatting errors in column separators, but the terms are explicitly listed as above [S1]. $L_{SD}$ is the distance between the X-ray source and detector [S1].

    Derive the 2D covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}''_i \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ by removing the third row and third column of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i$ [S1].

4.  **Tile Assignment and Sorting:**
    Partition the 2D image plane into non-overlapping tiles [S1]. Assign each Gaussian $i$ to tiles that overlap with its 2D bounding box, derived from $\mathbf{u}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}''_i$ [S1]. Within each tile, sort the assigned Gaussians by their distance to the 2D detector (i.e., by $t_z$) [S1].

5.  **Pixel-wise Radiative Accumulation:**
    For each pixel $p$, identify the set $\mathcal{N}$ of ordered 3D intersection points $\mathbf{x}_j$ (where $j \in \mathcal{N}$) formed by the X-ray ray passing through $p$ and intersecting the Gaussians in the corresponding tile [S1]. The intensity $I(p)$ is computed as:
    $$ I(p) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{i}_j $$
    where $\mathbf{i}_j$ is the radiation intensity of the $j$-th intersection point [S1].

    The radiation intensity $\mathbf{i}_j$ is calculated using the opacity $\alpha_j$ and the probability density $P$ of the $j$-th Gaussian at the intersection point $\mathbf{x}_j$:
    $$ \mathbf{i}_j = \alpha_j P(\mathbf{x}_j | \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j) $$
    The probability value $P$ is defined as:
    $$ P(\mathbf{x} | \boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)\right) $$
    where $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the 3D point position [S1].

    *Note on Accumulation Logic:* The source provides a table illustrating the blending process for ordered points $j=1 \dots 10$. It shows terms like $\sigma_j$ and $\prod_{k=1}^{j-1} (1 - \sigma_k)$. However, the explicit formula given is a simple sum $I(p) = \sum \mathbf{i}_j$. In standard radiative transfer (Beer-Lambert law), attenuation is multiplicative. The term $\sigma_j$ in the table likely represents the *contribution* or *opacity* of the $j$-th slice. If the standard alpha-blending model is implied by the table structure despite the summation notation, the intensity contribution of the $j$-th Gaussian to the pixel is weighted by the transmittance of all preceding (closer) Gaussians. Given the explicit formula $I(p) = \sum \mathbf{i}_j$ and the definition $\mathbf{i}_j = \alpha_j P(...)$, the implementation should follow the summation of these weighted probabilities unless the context of "X-ray" strictly requires exponential attenuation $e^{-\sum \sigma}$. The source states "blending N ordered points... as $I(p) = \sum \mathbf{i}_j$" [S1]. We follow the explicit summation formula provided [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math
import numpy as np

def differentiable_radiative_rasterization(
    gaussians: list, 
    M_ext: np.ndarray, 
    M_int: np.ndarray, 
    L_SD: float, 
    phi: float, 
    image_width: int, 
    image_height: int, 
    tile_size: int
) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Renders an X-ray image from radiative 3D Gaussians using Differentiable Radiative Rasterization.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List of dicts, each containing 'mu' (3,), 'Sigma' (3,3), 'alpha' (float), 'f' (vector).
        M_ext: 4x4 extrinsic matrix.
        M_int: 4x3 intrinsic matrix.
        L_SD: Distance between X-ray source and detector.
        phi: Azimuth angle of the X-ray source.
        image_width: Width of the output image.
        image_height: Height of the output image.
        tile_size: Size of each tile in pixels.
        
    Returns:
        I: Rendered image (image_height, image_width).
    """
    
    # 1. Initialize Image and Tile Data Structures
    I = np.zeros((image_height, image_width))
    num_tiles_x = math.ceil(image_width / tile_size)
    num_tiles_y = math.ceil(image_height / tile_size)
    
    # Tile -> List of Gaussian Indices
    tile_gaussians = {}
    for ty in range(num_tiles_y):
        for tx in range(num_tiles_x):
            tile_gaussians[(ty, tx)] = []

    # 2. Project Gaussians and Assign to Tiles
    projected_gaussians = []
    
    # Precompute W_i (Viewing Transformation) - same for all if phi is global
    sin_phi = math.sin(phi)
    cos_phi = math.cos(phi)
    W = np.array([
        [-sin_phi, cos_phi, 0],
        [0, 0, -1],
        [-cos_phi, -sin_phi, 0]
    ])

    for i, g in enumerate(gaussians):
        mu = g['mu']
        Sigma = g['Sigma']
        alpha = g['alpha']
        
        # Homogeneous coordinates
        mu_h = np.append(mu, 1.0)
        
        # Transform to camera coordinates
        t_h = M_ext @ mu_h
        t = t_h[:3] # tx, ty, tz
        
        # Project to image coordinates
        u_h = M_int @ t_h
        u = u_h[:2] # ux, uy
        
        # Transform Covariance
        # Sigma_prime = J * W * Sigma * W^T * J^T
        W_Sigma_Wt = W @ Sigma @ W.T
        
        # Compute Jacobian J
        tx, ty, tz = t
        if abs(tz) < 1e-6:
            continue # Skip if behind camera or at infinity
            
        # J matrix components
        # Note: Source formula for J has a potential typo in the last column term t_y/t_z^2 vs t_y/t_z.
        # We follow the explicit text: - (L_SD / t_z^2) * (t_y / t_z^2)
        # However, standard projective Jacobians usually have 1/t_z^2. 
        # The source text says: - L_SD / t_z^2 * t_y / t_z^2. 
        # Let's stick to the source's explicit terms.
        
        term1 = L_SD / tz
        term2 = (L_SD / (tz**2)) * (ty / (tz**2))
        
        J = np.array([
            [term1, 0, -term2],
            [0, term1, -term2],
            [0, 0, 0]
        ])
        
        Sigma_prime = J @ W_Sigma_Wt @ J.T
        
        # Get 2D Covariance by skipping 3rd row/col
        Sigma_2d = Sigma_prime[:2, :2]
        
        # Calculate 2D bounding box for tile assignment
        # Approximate bounding box from eigenvalues of Sigma_2d
        eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Sigma_2d)
        # Use 3 sigma for bounding box
        radius_x = 3.0 * math.sqrt(max(eigenvalues[0], 1e-6))
        radius_y = 3.0 * math.sqrt(max(eigenvalues[1], 1e-6))
        
        min_x = u[0] - radius_x
        max_x = u[0] + radius_x
        min_y = u[1] - radius_y
        max_y = u[1] + radius_y
        
        # Determine tile indices
        min_tx = max(0, math.floor(min_x / tile_size))
        max_tx = min(num_tiles_x - 1, math.floor(max_x / tile_size))
        min_ty = max(0, math.floor(min_y / tile_size))
        max_ty = min(num_tiles_y - 1, math.floor(max_y / tile_size))
        
        for ty in range(min_ty, max_ty + 1):
            for tx in range(min_tx, max_tx + 1):
                tile_gaussians[(ty, tx)].append(i)
                
        projected_gaussians.append({
            'index': i,
            'mu': mu,
            't': t,
            'u': u,
            'Sigma_2d': Sigma_2d,
            'alpha': alpha,
            'f': g['f']
        })

    # 3. Sort Gaussians in each Tile by Depth (tz)
    for key in tile_gaussians:
        tile_gaussians[key].sort(key=lambda idx: projected_gaussians[idx]['t'][2])

    # 4. Render Pixels
    # Inverse mapping from pixel to tile
    # Iterate over tiles to avoid redundant checks
    for ty in range(num_tiles_y):
        for tx in range(num_tiles_x):
            tile_indices = tile_gaussians[(ty, tx)]
            if not tile_indices:
                continue
                
            # Determine pixel range for this tile
            y_start = ty * tile_size
            y_end = min((ty + 1) * tile_size, image_height)
            x_start = tx * tile_size
            x_end = min((tx + 1) * tile_size, image_width)
            
            for y in range(y_start, y_end):
                for x in range(x_start, x_end):
                    # Pixel coordinate in image space
                    px, py = float(x), float(y)
                    
                    # Find intersecting Gaussians for this pixel
                    # In a full implementation, we would check if the pixel is within the Gaussian's 2D extent
                    # For simplicity and based on the "sorted list" description, we iterate the tile's list
                    # and compute contribution.
                    
                    intensity_sum = 0.0
                    
                    for idx in tile_indices:
                        pg = projected_gaussians[idx]
                        u_g = pg['u']
                        Sigma_2d = pg['Sigma_2d']
                        alpha_g = pg['alpha']
                        mu_3d = pg['mu']
                        
                        # Check if pixel is roughly within Gaussian extent (optimization)
                        # Calculate Mahalanobis distance in 2D
                        diff = np.array([px - u_g[0], py - u_g[1]])
                        try:
                            dist_sq = diff @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma_2d) @ diff
                        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
                            dist_sq = float('inf')
                            
                        if dist_sq > 10.0: # Threshold for practical zero contribution
                            continue
                            
                        # Calculate 3D intersection point x_j
                        # The source defines x_j as the intersection of the X-ray ray and the Gaussian.
                        # In standard rasterization, we evaluate the Gaussian at the pixel's 2D location.
                        # However, the formula uses P(x_j | mu, Sigma). 
                        # If we assume the ray is perpendicular to the detector (orthographic projection approximation for the PDF evaluation)
                        # or if x_j is the point on the Gaussian closest to the ray, 
                        # the source simplifies this by using the probability density.
                        # The source formula: i_j = alpha_j * P(x_j | mu_j, Sigma_j)
                        # And P is defined in 3D.
                        # To implement P(x_j), we need x_j.
                        # In many Gaussian splatting variants, the 2D projection is used directly.
                        # However, DRR explicitly uses 3D covariance and 3D probability.
                        # We approximate x_j as the point on the ray at the Gaussian's depth tz.
                        # Ray direction is from source to detector. 
                        # For simplicity, if we assume the camera coordinate system aligns with the ray direction (z-axis),
                        # then x_j = (u_x, u_y, tz) in camera coordinates? 
                        # No, u is in image coordinates.
                        # Let's reconstruct the 3D point on the ray corresponding to pixel (px, py) at depth tz.
                        # This requires inverse projection.
                        
                        # Reconstruct 3D point x_j from pixel (px, py) and depth tz of the Gaussian
                        # This is an approximation. The source does not give the exact ray-tracing step for x_j.
                        # It says "x_j is the j-th intersection 3D point".
                        # We will use the Gaussian center's depth tz for the intersection depth.
                        
                        # Inverse project (px, py) to camera coordinates at depth tz
                        # u_h = M_int @ t_h => t_h = M_int^-1 @ u_h (pseudo-inverse if needed)
                        # This part is complex. 
                        # Alternative: The source might imply evaluating the 3D Gaussian at the point 
                        # where the ray intersects the Gaussian's center plane?
                        
                        # Given the ambiguity of x_j in the source text for implementation,
                        # and the fact that P is a 3D function, we must estimate x_j.
                        # A common approach in such rasterizers is to use the projected 2D Gaussian 
                        # which effectively integrates the 3D Gaussian along the ray.
                        # But DRR uses P(x_j).
                        
                        # Let's assume x_j is the point on the ray at the Gaussian's center depth tz.
                        # Map pixel (px, py) to camera coordinates at depth tz.
                        # This requires the inverse of the intrinsic matrix.
                        # Let's assume standard pinhole: x_cam = (px - cx) * tz / fx, y_cam = (py - cy) * tz / fy
                        # We don't have fx, fy, cx, cy explicitly, but M_int contains them.
                        
                        # Construct homogeneous image point
                        u_pixel_h = np.array([px, py, 1.0])
                        
                        # We need to solve M_int @ [x, y, tz, 1]^T = [px, py, 1, w]^T ?
                        # M_int is 4x3. It maps [x, y, z, 1] (homogeneous 3D) to [u, v, 1] (homogeneous 2D)?
                        # The source says M_int is 4x3. This is unusual. Usually 3x4.
                        # "M_int \in R^{4x3}". 
                        # If M_int is 4x3, it maps a 3-vector to a 4-vector?
                        # "tilde{u}_i = M_int tilde{t}_i". tilde{t} is 4x1. M_int must be 4x4 or 3x4.
                        # Source says M_int is 4x3. This is likely a typo in the source or a specific format.
                        # Assuming standard projection: u = K * [R|t] * X.
                        
                        # Due to the ambiguity of M_int dimensions and the lack of explicit inverse projection formula in the source,
                        # we will use the 2D probability density as a proxy if 3D reconstruction is not feasible from the text alone.
                        # However, the source explicitly defines P in 3D.
                        
                        # Let's assume the 2D covariance Sigma_2d already accounts for the integration/projection.
                        # The term P(x_j) in the source might be a simplification for P_2d(u_pixel).
                        # If we use the 2D Gaussian PDF:
                        # P_2d(u) = exp(-0.5 * (u - u_g)^T Sigma_2d^-1 (u - u_g))
                        
                        # We will use the 2D PDF evaluated at the pixel, as this is consistent with the rasterization paradigm
                        # and the availability of Sigma_2d.
                        
                        pdf_2d = math.exp(-0.5 * dist_sq)
                        i_j = alpha_g * pdf_2d
                        intensity_sum += i_j
                        
                    I[y, x] = intensity_sum

    return I
```

**Parameters:**
*   $L_{SD}$: Distance between X-ray source and detector. Typical values depend on the specific X-ray scanner setup [S1].
*   $\phi$: Azimuth angle of the X-ray source. Typical values depend on the scanner geometry [S1].
*   Tile Size: Not specified in the sources. [G] A common choice is $16 \times 16$ or $32 \times 32$ pixels for GPU efficiency.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Singular Covariance:** If $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ is singular, the inverse in the probability formula is undefined. [G] Add a small regularization term (e.g., $10^{-6} \mathbf{I}$) to the covariance matrix before inversion.
*   **Depth Sorting:** Incorrect sorting leads to incorrect blending order. Ensure sorting is stable and based on the correct depth metric ($t_z$) [S1].
*   **Matrix Dimensions:** The source specifies $\mathbf{M}_{int} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$, which is non-standard for projecting a 4D homogeneous vector. [G] Verify the actual dimensions of the input matrices; if $\mathbf{M}_{int}$ is $3\times4$, adjust the multiplication accordingly.
*   **Jacobian Formula:** The source text contains formatting errors in the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}_i$. [G] Use the explicit terms provided: $J_{00} = L_{SD}/t_z$, $J_{02} = -(L_{SD}/t_z^2)(t_y/t_z^2)$, etc., assuming the diagonal terms are as listed.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot T)$, where $N$ is the number of Gaussians and $T$ is the average number of Gaussians per tile/pixel. The sorting step is $O(N \log N)$ globally, but per-tile sorting is faster. The rasterization step is linear in the number of active pixels and overlapping Gaussians.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N + P)$, where $P$ is the number of pixels. Storage for Gaussians, tile lists, and the output image.
*   **Measured Performance:** The source states that the forward and backward processes of X-Gaussian (which uses DRR) are "much faster" than those of original 3DGS due to the avoidance of complex spherical harmonic (SH) function computations [S1].
*   **Strengths:** GPU-friendly CUDA implementation; fast rendering by avoiding SH evaluations; differentiable for training.
*   **Limitations:** Specific to X-ray imaging; requires accurate knowledge of X-ray source parameters ($L_{SD}, \phi$); relies on the radiative Gaussian point cloud model.
*   **When to Use:** When rendering novel X-ray views from a 3D Gaussian point cloud representation of an object.
*   **When Not to Use:** For standard RGB novel view synthesis (use [3D Gaussian Splatting](3d-gaussian-splatting.md) or its variants); for scenes where X-ray physics are not the primary concern.

## Background
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization (DRR) was introduced in the paper "Radiative Gaussian Splatting for Efficient X-Ray Novel View Synthesis" [S1]. It is part of the X-Gaussian framework, which extends 3D Gaussian Splatting to the domain of X-ray imaging. The method was developed to enable efficient training and rendering of X-ray projections without relying on traditional volume rendering or complex SH functions, thereby reducing training time [S1]. The initialization of the Gaussian point clouds is handled by an Angle-pose Cuboid Uniform Initialization (ACUI) strategy, which uses X-ray scanner parameters to set up the camera matrices and uniformly sample points within a bounding cuboid [S1].
