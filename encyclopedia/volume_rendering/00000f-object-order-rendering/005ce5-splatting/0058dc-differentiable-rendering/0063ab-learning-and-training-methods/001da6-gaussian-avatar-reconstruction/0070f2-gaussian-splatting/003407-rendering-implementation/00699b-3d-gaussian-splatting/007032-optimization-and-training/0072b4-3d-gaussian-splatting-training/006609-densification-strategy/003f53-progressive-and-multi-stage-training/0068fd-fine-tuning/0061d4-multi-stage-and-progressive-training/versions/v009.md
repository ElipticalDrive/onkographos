# Multi-stage and Progressive Training


## Overview
Multi-stage and Progressive Training refers to a family of optimization strategies in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that decompose the training process into sequential or incremental phases to stabilize convergence, handle dynamic scene expansion, or manage representation complexity [S1, unofficial]. Unlike monolithic single-stage optimization, these methods partition the learning of geometry, appearance, deformation, or compression into distinct steps, preventing the model from getting stuck in poor local minima or overfitting to initial views [S1, unofficial].

## Shared principles
The core mechanism across all variants is the sequential initialization of Gaussian parameters or training objectives. Instead of optimizing all parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color, and potentially deformation or material properties) simultaneously from a random or sparse initialization, the process is split into stages where subsets of parameters are fixed or optimized in a specific order [S1, unofficial]. This decoupling allows the model to establish a robust baseline representation (e.g., static geometry) before introducing complexity (e.g., dynamic deformation, material decomposition, or compact latent coding) .

## Subtopics

### [Two-stage Optimization](14857-two-stage-optimization.md)
Two-stage optimization partitions 3D Gaussian training into sequential phases to stabilize convergence and improve reconstruction quality. It addresses the difficulty of simultaneously optimizing geometric structure, appearance, and compression or material properties in a single monolithic loop. Specific implementations vary by domain:
*   **Low-Resolution to Full-Resolution (CT Reconstruction):** Used in 3DGR-CT, Stage 1 optimizes Gaussians on a $1/2 \times 1/2 \times 1/2$ low-resolution grid to place them in approximately correct positions by minimizing the L2 norm between projected data and measurements. Stage 2 initializes from Stage 1 and refines parameters on the full-resolution volume.
*   **Initial Geometry/Appearance then Material Decomposition (Inverse Rendering):** Used in GS-IR, Stage 1 (30,000 iterations) optimizes geometry ($\mathbf{T}$) and appearance ($\mathbf{A}$) using color and normal losses. Stage 2 (10,000 iterations) fixes $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ to optimize material parameters ($\mathbf{M}$) using a decomposition loss.
*   **Codec-Free then Codec-Aware (Compression):** Used in CodecSplat, Stage 1 trains the Gaussian generation pipeline without the feature codec. Stage 2 initializes from Stage 1, enables the codec, and optimizes codec-related modules while keeping the backbone fixed, using a combined rendering and rate-distortion loss.

### [On-the-Fly GS Progressive Training](17502-on-the-fly-gs-progressive-training.md)
On-the-Fly GS Progressive Training is a method for incrementally optimizing a 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) field as new images are acquired in real-time or near real-time. It solves the problem of maintaining rendering quality and training efficiency when the scene geometry expands dynamically, preventing the model from overfitting to initial views while avoiding the computational cost of retraining the entire scene from scratch. It differs from static 3DGS training by partitioning optimization into three phases (initial, progressive, final) and using hierarchical image weighting to prioritize local updates, and from other incremental methods by employing a load-balancing loss and splatting-based parallel gradient accumulation to stabilize Gaussian distribution and accelerate convergence.

### [Warm-up stage](18447-warm-up-stage.md)
The warm-up stage is a training adaptation in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that improves distractor suppression and stabilizes convergence for dynamic scene reconstruction. It serves as a preliminary optimization phase before full dynamic training, preventing the model from getting stuck in poor local minima when dealing with non-unique inverse mappings or complex deformation fields. Unlike standard single-stage optimization, it decouples the learning of static geometry from dynamic deformation, allowing the model to establish a robust baseline representation first. It is particularly critical when Structure-from-Motion (SfM) point initialization is absent or insufficient.

[Warm-up stage](18447-warm-up-stage.md)

### [POD Pretraining](19686-pod-pretraining.md)
POD Pretraining is a distillation-based amortized optimization procedure that stabilizes the training of multi-stage residual Gaussian predictors by supervising them with locally refined Gaussian targets instead of unstable image-space gradients. It solves the problem of training collapse caused by the non-unique inverse mapping from residual images to Gaussian parameters, which is amplified across stages through error accumulation. Unlike direct image-space supervision, which leads to degenerate solutions, POD stabilizes stage-wise training by refining predicted Gaussian increments with a short-horizon optimizer and distilling the refined result back into the predictor as a regression target.

#### Progressive Stage-wise Prediction
*   **Definition:** A prediction mode where Gaussian primitives are accumulated progressively across multiple stages rather than predicted in a single shot, allowing for controllable trade-offs between reconstruction quality and representation compactness [S1, unofficial].
*   **Parameter:** Patch size ($ps$): The spatial resolution of the input patches. For the one-shot baseline, $ps=7$; for the stage-wise model, $ps=14$ [S1, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** On DIV2K, the stage-wise predictor ($ps=14$) reaches 26.54 dB PSNR with 12,690 Gaussians at Stage 4, compared to the one-shot baseline ($ps=7$) which reaches 26.58 dB PSNR with 14,528 Gaussians [S1, unofficial]. In the stage-wise model on DIV2K, PSNR increases from 21.91 dB (Stage 1) to 26.54 dB (Stage 4), while the number of Gaussians increases from 3,658 to 12,690 [S1, unofficial].
*   **Comparison:** Compared to one-shot prediction, stage-wise prediction does not clearly improve final reconstruction quality but enables explicit control over the number of activated Gaussians, allowing adjustment of the quality-compactness trade-off [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Multi-stage and progressive training strategies have emerged as essential components of 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization to address the instability and inefficiency of direct end-to-end training, particularly in complex scenarios such as inverse rendering, compression, dynamic scenes, and incremental reconstruction. The concept of decoupling optimization stages is rooted in general deep learning practices but has been specifically adapted for the unique differentiable rendering pipeline of 3DGS.

The **Two-stage Optimization** approach has been applied to diverse domains. In CT reconstruction (3DGR-CT), it addresses the ill-posed nature of sparse-view reconstruction by starting with a coarse geometric prior [S1, unofficial]. In inverse rendering (GS-IR), it separates the well-posed problem of geometry and color from the ill-posed problem of material decomposition . In compression (CodecSplat), it prevents the instability of learning a compact latent space and Gaussian generation simultaneously .

**Progressive Training** for dynamic and incremental scenes addresses the non-stationarity of the data distribution. **On-the-Fly GS** introduces hierarchical weighting and load-balancing losses to handle the continuous influx of new views without catastrophic forgetting or overfitting [S1, unofficial]. The **Warm-up stage** was developed specifically for 4D Gaussian Splatting to mitigate the numerical instability caused by optimizing deformation fields from scratch, especially when SfM initialization is poor .

**POD Pretraining** and **Progressive Stage-wise Prediction** are associated with the AIR framework, which aims to amortize the optimization process for feed-forward 2D Gaussian Splatting. These methods tackle the "non-unique inverse mapping" problem, where image-space gradients do not provide stable directions in Gaussian parameter space, leading to training collapse in multi-stage residual predictors [S1, unofficial].
