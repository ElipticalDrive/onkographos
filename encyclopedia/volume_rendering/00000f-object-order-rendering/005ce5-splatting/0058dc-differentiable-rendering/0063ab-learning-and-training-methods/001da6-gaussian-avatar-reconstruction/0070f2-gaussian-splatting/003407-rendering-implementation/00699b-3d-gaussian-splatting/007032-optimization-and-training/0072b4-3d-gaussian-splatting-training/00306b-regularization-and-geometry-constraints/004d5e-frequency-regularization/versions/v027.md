# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which results in small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR increases pixel gradients in these regions by explicitly disentangling frequency components [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md) by focusing on spectral fidelity rather than surface alignment or depth consistency [S2, 9212-fregs.md].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$.
- **Outputs:** Frequency regularization loss terms $d_a$ (amplitude discrepancy) and $d_p$ (phase discrepancy).

**Data Structures:**
- **Image Buffers:** Standard 2D arrays for $\hat{I}$ and $I$.
- **Frequency Domain:** Complex-valued arrays $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$ per channel.

**Steps:**
1. **Compute 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** Convert $\hat{I}$ and $I$ into the frequency domain. For a single channel, the DFT is computed as:
   $$F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)}$$
   where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $F(u, v)$ is the complex frequency value [S2].
2. **Compute Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency component $(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude and phase for both rendered and ground truth spectra:
   $$|F(u, v)| = \sqrt{Re(u, v)^2 + Im(u, v)^2}$$
   $$\angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{Im(u, v)}{Re(u, v)}\right)$$
   where $Re(u, v)$ and $Im(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$ [S2].
3. **Calculate Discrepancies:** Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ using the Euclidean metric over all pixels and frequencies:
   $$d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2}$$
   $$d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2}$$
   where $F$ and $\hat{F}$ are ground truth and rendered frequency values [S2].
4. **Optimization:** The discrepancies are added to the total loss. The Adam optimizer is used for training [S2].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image, ground_truth_image):
    """
    Computes amplitude and phase discrepancy losses between rendered and ground truth images.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image (Tensor): Rendered image of shape (H, W, C).
        ground_truth_image (Tensor): Ground truth image of shape (H, W, C).
        
    Returns:
        float: Total frequency regularization loss (d_a + d_p).
    """
    # Step 1: Compute 2D DFT for each channel
    # Note: In practice, use FFT libraries. Here we assume a function fft2 exists.
    F_rendered = fft2(rendered_image) # Shape (H, W, C) complex
    F_gt = fft2(ground_truth_image)   # Shape (H, W, C) complex
    
    # Step 2: Compute Amplitude and Phase
    amp_rendered = abs(F_rendered)
    phase_rendered = angle(F_rendered)
    
    amp_gt = abs(F_gt)
    phase_gt = angle(F_gt)
    
    # Step 3: Calculate Discrepancies
    # Mean squared error for amplitude
    mse_amp = torch.mean((amp_rendered - amp_gt) ** 2)
    d_a = torch.sqrt(mse_amp)
    
    # Mean squared error for phase
    mse_phase = torch.mean((phase_rendered - phase_gt) ** 2)
    d_p = torch.sqrt(mse_phase)
    
    # Step 4: Return combined loss
    return d_a + d_p
```

**Parameters:**
- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
- **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
- **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are kept [S2].
- **Typical Values:** Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Over-reconstruction:** Naive L1 loss fails to differentiate over-reconstructed regions from well-reconstructed ones, leading to redundant Gaussians. FR specifically targets this by increasing gradients in over-reconstructed regions [S2].
- **Bias towards artifacts:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can restrict Gaussian densification and bias the splatting towards undesirable artifacts [S2]. This is addressed in variants like FreGS using frequency annealing.

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The addition of 2D DFT and inverse operations per iteration increases computational overhead compared to standard L1 loss. The complexity is dominated by the FFT operation, typically $O(N \log N)$ for an image of size $N$.
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing frequency domain representations of the rendered and ground truth images, adding $O(H \times W \times C)$ memory per channel.
- **Measured Performance:** In the 'Bicycle' scene, FR significantly increases pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to the method without FR (equivalent to base 3D-GS) [S2].
- **Scaling:** FR enables adaptive densification by revealing over-reconstruction regions that L1 loss misses [S2].
- **Strengths:** Alleviates over-reconstruction, improves Gaussian densification, and pushes Gaussians toward high-frequency regions [S2][S3, unofficial].
- **Limitations:** Naive application can bias splatting towards artifacts if low/high frequencies are not handled separately (addressed by Frequency Annealing in FreGS) [S2].
- **When to use:** When over-reconstruction is prevalent and standard densification fails to create new Gaussians in those areas.
- **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and the overhead of FFT is prohibitive, or when the scene is very low frequency and spectral discrepancies are negligible.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced to address convergence speed, visual artifacts, and improper Gaussians in 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization [S1]. It was notably implemented in FreGS (3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization) [S2]. The method builds on the observation that pixel-wise L1 loss is insufficient for guiding densification in over-reconstructed regions [S2]. It is part of a broader family of regularization techniques including geometry and depth constraints [S1].
