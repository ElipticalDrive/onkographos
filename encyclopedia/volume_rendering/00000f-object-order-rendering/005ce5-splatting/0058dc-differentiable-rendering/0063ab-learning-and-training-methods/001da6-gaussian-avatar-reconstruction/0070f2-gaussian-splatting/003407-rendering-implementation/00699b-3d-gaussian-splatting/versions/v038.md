# 3D Gaussian Splatting

Also called: 3DGS

## Overview
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction method that represents a scene as a collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by position, covariance, opacity, and view-dependent color [S1][S6]. It solves the trade-off between the high-quality but slow volumetric rendering of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and the geometric limitations of mesh-based rendering by using explicit, differentiable particle-based discretization [S6][S18, unofficial]. Unlike NeRF, which requires expensive ray marching through a continuous MLP, 3DGS projects 3D Gaussians to 2D screen-space ellipses and rasterizes them using tile-based alpha compositing, enabling real-time rendering speeds (>100 FPS) and rapid training convergence [S6][S18, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering (CPU-Sorted, Instanced)](2058-3d-gaussian-splatting-rendering-cpu-sorted-instanced.md) by utilizing a highly optimized, parallelized GPU rasterization pipeline with global depth sorting and tile-based compositing [S6][S12].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   A set of $N$ 3D Gaussian primitives, each defined by a mean position $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, a covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, an opacity scalar $\alpha \in [0, 1]$, and spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients for view-dependent color [S1][S8, unofficial].
*   Camera parameters: intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$ (focal lengths $f_x, f_y$, principal point $c_x, c_y$) and extrinsic view matrix $\mathbf{V}$ [S8, unofficial][S12].
*   Image dimensions $W \times H$ and tile size (typically $16 \times 16$ pixels) [S12].

**Outputs:**
*   An RGB image $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ and optionally depth/normal maps [S13].

### Data Structures
The scene is stored as a list of Gaussian attributes [S14]. A typical layout for a single Gaussian $i$ includes:
*   `xyz`: `float32[N, 3]` – World-space mean position [S14].
*   `rot`: `float32[N, 4]` – Unnormalized quaternion representing rotation [S14].
*   `scale`: `float32[N, 3]` – Unnormalized log-scale vector [S14].
*   `opacity`: `float32[N, 1]` – Unnormalized opacity scalar [S14].
*   `sh`: `float32[N, SH_dim]` – Spherical harmonics coefficients, where $SH\_dim = 3 \times (n+1)^2$ for degree $n$ (typically $n=3$, so 48 coefficients) [S8, unofficial][S14].

During rendering, intermediate structures include:
*   `means_2D`: `float32[N, 2]` – Screen-space mean coordinates [S15].
*   `cov_2D`: `float32[N, 3, 3]` – 2D screen-space covariance matrix [S13].
*   `conic`: `float32[N, 3]` – Inverse of the upper-left $2 \times 2$ submatrix of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}$, stored as $[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1}(0,0), \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1}(0,1), \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1}(1,1) ]$ for efficient rasterization [S14].
*   `radii`: `int32[N, 2]` – Bounding box radius in pixels for each Gaussian [S13].

### Steps

1.  **Parameter Activation**:
    *   Normalize rotations: $\mathbf{q}_{norm} = \mathbf{q} / \|\mathbf{q}\|$ [S14].
    *   Exponentiate scales to ensure positivity: $\mathbf{s}_{eff} = \exp(\mathbf{s})$ [S14].
    *   Sigmoid opacities: $\alpha_{eff} = \sigma(\alpha)$ [S14].
    *   Construct 3D covariance: $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{3D} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{S} \mathbf{R}^\top$, where $\mathbf{R}$ is the rotation matrix derived from the normalized quaternion and $\mathbf{S} = \text{diag}(s_{\text{eff}})$ [S11].

2.  **Camera Space Transformation**:
    *   Transform means to camera space: $\boldsymbol{\mu}' = \mathbf{V} \boldsymbol{\mu}$ [S14].
    *   Transform covariance to camera space using the upper-left $3 \times 3$ submatrix of the view matrix $\mathbf{V}$ (denoted $\mathbf{V}_{1:3, 1:3}$):
        $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{V}_{1:3, 1:3} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{3D} \mathbf{V}_{1:3, 1:3}^T $$
        [Parent][S8, unofficial].

3.  **Projection to Screen Space**:
    *   Compute the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ of the projective projection at $\boldsymbol{\mu}'$:
        $$ \mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{f_x}{\mu'_z} & 0 & -\frac{f_x \mu'_x}{\mu'_z^2} \\ 0 & \frac{f_y}{\mu'_z} & -\frac{f_y \mu'_y}{\mu'_z^2} \end{pmatrix} $$
        [Parent][S8, unofficial].
    *   Compute the 2D screen-space covariance:
        $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D} = \mathbf{J} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}' \mathbf{J}^T $$
        [Parent][S12].
    *   Compute the 2D mean:
        $$ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{f_x \mu'_x}{\mu'_z} + c_x \\ \frac{f_y \mu'_y}{\mu'_z} + c_y \end{pmatrix} $$
        [Parent][S8, unofficial].
    *   Compute the conic matrix (inverse of the $2 \times 2$ top-left block of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}$) for rasterization:
        $$ \mathbf{C}_{2D} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}(0,0) & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}(0,1) \\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}(0,1) & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}(1,1) \end{pmatrix}^{-1} $$
        Stored as vector $[ \mathbf{C}_{2D}(0,0), \mathbf{C}_{2D}(0,1), \mathbf{C}_{2D}(1,1) ]$ [S14].
    *   Compute the bounding radius in pixels (typically $3\sigma$):
        $$ r = \lceil 3.0 \times \max(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}(0,0)}, \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}(1,1)}) \rceil $$
        [S14].

4.  **Culling and Sorting**:
    *   Cull Gaussians if $\mu'_z < z_{near}$ (default 0.01) or if the bounding box is outside the image boundaries [Parent][S14].
    *   Sort remaining Gaussians by camera-space depth $\mu'_z$ in ascending order (front-to-back) [Parent][S8, unofficial]. This global sort is typically performed using a radix sort on the GPU [S6][S16, unofficial].

5.  **Tile Assignment and Rasterization**:
    *   Divide the image into tiles (e.g., $16 \times 16$ pixels) [S12].
    *   Assign each Gaussian to the tiles its bounding box intersects [S1].
    *   For each tile, iterate over the assigned Gaussians in depth order [S1].
    *   For each pixel $(u, v)$ in the tile, compute the offset $\mathbf{x} = [u - \mu_{2D,x}, v - \mu_{2D,y}]^T$ from the Gaussian mean [S1].
    *   Compute the alpha contribution:
        $$ \alpha_{pixel} = \alpha_{eff} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1} \mathbf{x}\right) $$
        [Parent][S1].
    *   Compute the view-dependent color $\mathbf{c}$ using the spherical harmonics evaluated at the view direction $\mathbf{d} = \text{normalize}(\boldsymbol{\mu}' - \mathbf{cam\_pos})$ [S13][S8, unofficial].

6.  **Alpha Compositing**:
    *   Initialize accumulated color $C_{acc} = 0$ and transmittance $T = 1$ [S1].
    *   For each Gaussian $i$ in depth order:
        $$ C_{acc} \leftarrow C_{acc} + T \cdot \alpha_{pixel} \cdot \mathbf{c}_i $$
        $$ T \leftarrow T \cdot (1 - \alpha_{pixel}) $$
        [Parent][S1].
    *   Early termination: If $T < \tau$ (threshold, e.g., 0.05), stop processing further Gaussians for this pixel [Parent][S12].

### Pseudocode

```python
def render_3dgs(gaussians, camera, image_width, image_height, tile_size=16):
    """
    gaussians: Dict with keys 'xyz', 'rot', 'scale', 'opacity', 'sh' (arrays of shape [N, ...])
    camera: Dict with 'K' (3x3), 'V' (4x4), 'near_plane', 'cam_pos'
    """
    import numpy as np
    
    N = len(gaussians['xyz'])
    
    # 1. Activation
    q_norm = gaussians['rot'] / np.linalg.norm(gaussians['rot'], axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    s_eff = np.exp(gaussians['scale'])
    alpha_eff = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-gaussians['opacity']))
    
    # Construct 3D Covariance
    # Quaternion to rotation matrix R
    def quat_to_rotmat(q):
        w, x, y, z = q[:, 0], q[:, 1], q[:, 2], q[:, 3]
        R = np.zeros((N, 3, 3))
        R[:, 0, 0] = 1 - 2*(y**2 + z**2)
        R[:, 0, 1] = 2*(x*y - w*z)
        R[:, 0, 2] = 2*(x*z + w*y)
        R[:, 1, 0] = 2*(x*y + w*z)
        R[:, 1, 1] = 1 - 2*(x**2 + z**2)
        R[:, 1, 2] = 2*(y*z - w*x)
        R[:, 2, 0] = 2*(x*z - w*y)
        R[:, 2, 1] = 2*(y*z + w*x)
        R[:, 2, 2] = 1 - 2*(x**2 + y**2)
        return R

    R = quat_to_rotmat(q_norm)
    cov_3d = R @ (s_eff[:, None, None] * R)
    
    # 2. Camera Space
    means_3d_hom = np.concatenate([gaussians['xyz'], np.ones((N, 1))], axis=1) # [N, 4]
    means_cam = (camera['V'] @ means_3d_hom.T).T[:, :3] # [N, 3]
    
    V_sub = camera['V'][:3, :3]
    cov_cam = V_sub @ cov_3d @ V_sub.T
    
    # 3. Projection
    fx, fy = camera['K'][0, 0], camera['K'][1, 1]
    cx, cy = camera['K'][0, 2], camera['K'][1, 2]
    
    mu_z = means_cam[:, 2]
    mu_x = means_cam[:, 0]
    mu_y = means_cam[:, 1]
    
    # Jacobian J
    J = np.zeros((N, 2, 3))
    J[:, 0, 0] = fx / mu_z
    J[:, 0, 2] = -fx * mu_x / (mu_z ** 2)
    J[:, 1, 1] = fy / mu_z
    J[:, 1, 2] = -fy * mu_y / (mu_z ** 2)
    
    # 2D Covariance
    cov_2d = J @ cov_cam @ J.T # [N, 2, 2]
    
    # 2D Means
    means_2d = np.zeros((N, 2))
    means_2d[:, 0] = fx * mu_x / mu_z + cx
    means_2d[:, 1] = fy * mu_y / mu_z + cy
    
    # Conic (Inverse of 2x2 top-left)
    det = cov_2d[:, 0, 0] * cov_2d[:, 1, 1] - cov_2d[:, 0, 1] ** 2
    conic = np.zeros((N, 3))
    conic[:, 0] = cov_2d[:, 1, 1] / det
    conic[:, 1] = -cov_2d[:, 0, 1] / det
    conic[:, 2] = cov_2d[:, 0, 0] / det
    
    # Radius
    radii = np.ceil(3.0 * np.maximum(np.sqrt(cov_2d[:, 0, 0]), np.sqrt(cov_2d[:, 1, 1]))).astype(int)
    
    # 4. Culling and Sorting
    valid = (mu_z > camera['near_plane']) & (radii > 0)
    indices = np.argsort(mu_z[valid]) # Sort by depth
    sorted_gaussians = {k: v[valid][indices] for k, v in gaussians.items()}
    sorted_gaussians['means_2d'] = means_2d[valid][indices]
    sorted_gaussians['alpha_eff'] = alpha_eff[valid][indices]
    sorted_gaussians['conic'] = conic[valid][indices]
    sorted_gaussians['means_cam'] = means_cam[valid][indices]
    
    # 5. Tile Rasterization
    image = np.zeros((image_height, image_width, 3))
    tiles = [(y, y+tile_size, x, x+tile_size) for y in range(0, image_height, tile_size) for x in range(0, image_width, tile_size)]
    
    for y_start, y_end, x_start, x_end in tiles:
        # Filter gaussians in tile
        tile_mask = (sorted_gaussians['means_2d'][:, 0] + radii > x_start) & \
                    (sorted_gaussians['means_2d'][:, 0] - radii < x_end) & \
                    (sorted_gaussians['means_2d'][:, 1] + radii > y_start) & \
                    (sorted_gaussians['means_2d'][:, 1] - radii < y_end)
        tile_gaussians = {k: v[tile_mask] for k, v in sorted_gaussians.items()}
        
        for y in range(y_start, min(y_end, image_height)):
            for x in range(x_start, min(x_end, image_width)):
                T = 1.0
                C_acc = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0])
                
                for i in range(len(tile_gaussians['means_2d'])):
                    if T < 0.05: break
                    
                    dx = x - tile_gaussians['means_2d'][i, 0]
                    dy = y - tile_gaussians['means_2d'][i, 1]
                    
                    # Alpha: exp(-0.5 * x^T Sigma^-1 x)
                    alpha = tile_gaussians['alpha_eff'][i] * np.exp(-0.5 * (
                        tile_gaussians['conic'][i, 0]*dx*dx + 
                        2*tile_gaussians['conic'][i, 1]*dx*dy + 
                        tile_gaussians['conic'][i, 2]*dy*dy
                    ))
                    
                    if alpha < 1e-4: continue
                    
                    # Color from SH (simplified evaluation)
                    view_dir = tile_gaussians['means_cam'][i] - camera['cam_pos']
                    view_dir = view_dir / np.linalg.norm(view_dir)
                    # Assuming SH degree 0 for simplicity in this reference implementation
                    color = tile_gaussians['sh'][i, :3] 
                    
                    C_acc = C_acc + T * alpha * color
                    T = T * (1 - alpha)
                
                image[y, x] = C_acc
                
    return image
```

### Parameters
*   **Tile Size**: $16 \times 16$ pixels is standard [S12].
*   **Bounding Box Multiplier**: $3.0$ (representing $3\sigma$ extent) [S14].
*   **Near Plane**: $0.01$ [S14].
*   **Far Plane**: $100$ (or $10^{10}$ for large scenes) [S14][S13].
*   **Spherical Harmonics Degree**: Typically 3, resulting in 48 coefficients per Gaussian (3 channels $\times$ 16 SH coefficients) [S8, unofficial].
*   **Early Termination Threshold**: Transmittance $T < 0.05$ or similar small value [S12].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Singular Covariance**: The 2D covariance matrix may become singular (e.g., if a Gaussian is viewed edge-on or is behind the camera) [S14]. The inverse computation in the conic matrix requires a non-singular matrix [S14]. A small epsilon or regularization may be needed [G].
*   **Jacobian Approximation**: The projection uses a first-order Jacobian approximation, which is not projectively exact [S19, unofficial]. This can cause artifacts for large Gaussians or extreme viewing angles [S19, unofficial].
*   **Sorting Bottleneck**: Sorting millions of Gaussians is a major computational bottleneck [S16, unofficial]. Efficient implementations use CUDA radix sort (e.g., CUB) [S16, unofficial].
*   **Initialization**: Random initialization leads to unstable convergence. Initializing from a sparse Structure-from-Motion (SfM) point cloud is recommended [S23].

## Subtopics

### [CoR-GS](1486-cor-gs.md)
CoR-GS improves sparse-view reconstruction by defining two independent reconstructions and comparing them to discard inaccurate Gaussians. It differs from feed-forward network approaches by relying on a consistency check between dual optimization paths to prune erroneous primitives. [CoR-GS](1486-cor-gs.md)

### [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md)
The 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer is a screen-space rendering method that projects a set of 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D ellipses and alpha-composites them to produce an RGB image, while providing exact gradients for all Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) and camera poses. It differs from volumetric ray-marching methods like NeRF by using discrete, point-based splatting and from standard OpenGL rasterization by computing analytic gradients for the non-linear projection and compositing operations, enabling end-to-end differentiable training. It builds on [3D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](2812-3d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization.md) by implementing the specific forward and backward passes required for optimization. [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md)

### [Optical Flow Loss for 3D Gaussian Reconstruction](3303-optical-flow-loss-for-3d-gaussian-reconstruction.md)
This method improves the 3D shape reconstruction of static objects from video sequences by incorporating an optical flow loss term into the optimization objective. It leverages the temporal prior inherent in video data to resolve concavity/convexity ambiguities that are present when relying solely on silhouette and color loss terms. Unlike standard photometric approaches, this technique uses optical flow as a local estimator of surface correspondence, which is robust to long-term lighting changes and autoexposure variations common in user-captured videos. [Optical Flow Loss for 3D Gaussian Reconstruction](3303-optical-flow-loss-for-3d-gaussian-reconstruction.md)

### [EffGS](3365-effgs.md)
EffGS is an acceleration framework for 3D Gaussian Splatting that improves training and rendering efficiency while maintaining high-fidelity reconstruction in both bounded and large-scale scenes. It sets itself apart from standard 3DGS and other acceleration methods by combining frequency-aware guidance, localized density control, and adaptive primitive scale modulation to prevent quality degradation in complex environments. [EffGS](3365-effgs.md)

### [3D Gaussian Splatting MVP](3410-3d-gaussian-splatting-mvp.md)
3D Gaussian Splatting MVP is a self-contained, pure-PyTorch implementation of the core 3D Gaussian Splatting rendering algorithm, designed as a teaching reference rather than a production renderer. It solves the problem of understanding the mathematical foundations of differentiable Gaussian rasterization by implementing the full pipeline—camera projection, covariance transformation, depth sorting, and alpha compositing—using standard tensor operations without custom CUDA kernels. It sets itself apart from production implementations like [3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering (Software)](2805-3d-gaussian-splatting-rendering-software.md) by explicitly skipping tile-based optimization, spherical harmonics, and adaptive density control, favoring readability and explicit Python loops over vectorized GPU performance. [3D Gaussian Splatting MVP](3410-3d-gaussian-splatting-mvp.md)

### [Ghost Ellipsoids for Chunked Training](3559-ghost-ellipsoids-for-chunked-training.md)
Ghost Ellipsoids for Chunked Training resolves boundary artifacts in chunked volume training by including ellipsoids from neighboring chunks in the loss computation without updating their parameters, providing boundary context without requiring a global volume or merged model. Unlike chunk-based 3DGS methods that resolve boundary inconsistencies by merging chunks into a global coarse model or through post-training stages, this method requires boundary support during training itself without access to a global volume. [Ghost Ellipsoids for Chunked Training](3559-ghost-ellipsoids-for-chunked-training.md)

### [MaskGaussian](4242-maskgaussian.md)
MaskGaussian is a differentiable pruning method for 3D Gaussian Splatting that models Gaussians as probabilistic entities rather than deterministically removing them based on static importance scores. It solves the problem of sub-optimal reconstruction caused by early pruning by introducing **masked-rasterization**, which allows gradients to flow to "masked-out" (inactive) Gaussians, enabling them to be re-evaluated and potentially reactivated as the scene evolves. Unlike sibling methods like [Compact3DGS](1398-compact3dgs.md) which use deterministic pruning or multiply masks with opacity/scale (blocking gradients), MaskGaussian applies masks during the alpha-blending stage, decoupling Gaussian existence from its geometric attributes. [MaskGaussian](4242-maskgaussian.md)

### [Substitutive LOD with K-wise Moment-Matched Merging](4801-substitutive-lod-with-k-wise-moment-matched-merging.md)
Substitutive LOD with K-wise moment-matched merging is a technique for reducing the number of 3D Gaussians in a scene by merging groups of $K$ splats into a single representative Gaussian, thereby boosting rendering performance and managing memory constraints. It addresses the problem of excessive Gaussian counts and associated aliasing artifacts by replacing small or redundant Gaussians with larger, merged ones. Unlike methods that modify Gaussians during reconstruction or prune based on static importance, this method operates on the final set of Gaussians using cost metrics to guide the merging process, allowing for runtime LOD selection. It sets itself apart from siblings like [CoR-GS](1486-cor-gs.md) and [EffGS](3365-effgs.md) by focusing on substitutive merging via moment matching rather than density control or vector quantization. [Substitutive LOD with K-wise Moment-Matched Merging](4801-substitutive-lod-with-k-wise-moment-matched-merging.md)

### [2D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](4815-2d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization.md)
This method renders a scene represented by 2D Gaussian primitives (surfels) into RGB, alpha, normal, and depth maps using a custom rasterizer. It differs from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting by operating on surfels projected into screen space, accumulating color and geometric attributes via front-to-back alpha compositing [Parent]. It serves as the Tier-1 NumPy reference implementation for 2D Gaussian Splatting, performing ray-surfel intersection per pixel using the projection matrix. [2D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](4815-2d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization.md)

### [Density Control and Densification](5337-density-control-and-densification.md)
Density Control and Densification is the mechanism in 3D Gaussian Splatting that dynamically adjusts the number, position, and scale of 3D Gaussian primitives during optimization to improve reconstruction quality [3034-adaptive-densification.md]. It addresses the fundamental limitation of standard gradient descent, which can only move, stretch, or fade existing Gaussians but cannot create new ones in empty regions or cleanly remove useless ones. By periodically inspecting view-space positional gradients, the method identifies under-reconstructed regions (requiring cloning) and over-reconstructed regions (requiring splitting), thereby allowing model capacity to follow image error. It is distinguished from sibling methods like [OTS+SAtn Densification](1488-ots-satn-densification.md) by its reliance on raw positional gradient magnitudes rather than opacity-weighted or attention-guided metrics, and from [Fixed-Pool Relocation](3094-fixed-pool-relocation.md) by its ability to increase the total primitive count rather than maintaining a fixed budget. [Density Control and Densification](5337-density-control-and-densification.md)

### [Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting](5703-dynamic-3d-gaussian-splatting.md)
Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting extends static 3D Gaussian Splatting to handle time-varying scenes by assigning time-varying position and rotation attributes to each Gaussian primitive. It solves the problem of temporal flicker and storage explosion inherent in per-frame baselines by enforcing physical plausibility through local-rigid and As-Rigid-As-Possible (ARAP) or isometry constraints. Unlike [4D Gaussian Splatting](7992-4d-gaussian-splatting.md) which uses implicit neural fields or planes to predict deformation offsets from a canonical space, this method explicitly tracks the motion of each Gaussian particle, offering a more direct representation of dynamic geometry. [Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting](5703-dynamic-3d-gaussian-splatting.md)

### [Epipolar 3D Gaussian Splatting](6070-epipolar-3d-gaussian-splatting.md)
Epipolar 3D Gaussian Splatting is a method that incorporates an epipolar transformer to generalize features between two views, pairing this with a probabilistic prediction of Gaussians aligned along camera-to-pixel rays. [Epipolar 3D Gaussian Splatting](6070-epipolar-3d-gaussian-splatting.md)

### [3D Gaussian Splatting with Transformer-based Parameterization](6072-3d-gaussian-splatting-with-transformer-based-parameterizatio.md)
This method is a feed-forward neural network approach that reconstructs a 3D Gaussian Splatting scene from a small set of input images by leveraging a Transformer architecture. It solves the problem of sparse-view reconstruction by tokenizing input views and camera parameters, processing them through a Transformer block, and directly decoding the resulting tokens into 3D Gaussian parameters. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting [2812-3d-gaussian-splatting.md] by replacing iterative optimization with a direct inference pipeline, and from pixel-aligned methods like MVSplat by operating in a global token space rather than per-pixel ray alignment. [3D Gaussian Splatting with Transformer-based Parameterization](6072-3d-gaussian-splatting-with-transformer-based-parameterizatio.md)

### [SAGA](6154-saga.md)
SAGA is a 2D promptable segmentation method lifted to 3D, leveraging the Segment Anything Model (SAM) for efficient segmentation within 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). It differs from sibling methods like [Gaussian Grouping](2074-gaussianset-data-structure.md), which uses identity encodings for grouping, and [Feature-3DGS](13465-application-specific-extensions.md), which uses CLIP/LSeg language features, by relying on SAM's 2D segmentation capabilities to guide 3D Gaussian assignment. [SAGA](6154-saga.md)

### [Quantile Rendering](7396-quantile-rendering.md)
Quantile Rendering (Q-Render) is a sparse, transmittance-guided sampling strategy designed to efficiently render high-dimensional feature maps from 3D Gaussians. It solves the computational bottleneck of dense volume rendering, where accumulating high-dimensional features along every ray scales poorly with feature dimensionality. Unlike conventional volume rendering, which processes all rasterized Gaussians, Q-Render shifts the Riemann partition from the spatial domain to the transmittance domain, sampling only the most representative "quantile Gaussians" that dominate the ray's transmittance profile. This approach reduces complexity from $O(N C)$ to $O(N + K C)$, enabling real-time rendering of high-dimensional data (e.g., 512-D CLIP embeddings) while maintaining segmentation accuracy. [Quantile Rendering](7396-quantile-rendering.md)

### [4D Gaussian Splatting](7992-4d-gaussian-splatting.md)
4D Gaussian Splatting (4DGS) extends 3D Gaussian Splatting to model dynamic scenes by treating space and time as a unified 4D continuum, enabling real-time rendering of arbitrary time slices without per-frame optimization. Unlike methods that treat time as an independent weighting factor or rely on MLP-based deformation fields, 4DGS explicitly parameterizes temporal opacity envelopes and coherent 4D rotations for each primitive, allowing for smooth interpolation of geometry and appearance between discrete keyframes. [4D Gaussian Splatting](7992-4d-gaussian-splatting.md)

### [Taming 3DGS](8058-taming-3dgs.md)
Taming 3DGS is a resource-efficiency method for 3D Gaussian Splatting that imposes an explicit cap on the total number of Gaussians to enable high-quality rendering on consumer-grade GPUs. It steers the densification process to spend a fixed budget where it helps most, trading peak quality for faster training and smaller model size. Unlike standard 3DGS which allows unbounded growth, this approach formulates optimization as a mixed-integer problem to maintain a constant Gaussian count. [Taming 3DGS](8058-taming-3dgs.md)

### [Analytic-Splatting](8084-analytic-splatting.md)
Analytic-Splatting is a rendering method that replaces the point-sampling of 2D Gaussians in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) with an analytical approximation of the integral of the Gaussian signal over the pixel window area to reduce aliasing artifacts during zooming or resolution changes. Unlike [3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering (Software)](2805-3d-gaussian-splatting-rendering-software.md) which treats pixels as isolated points, or [Mip-Splatting](3410-3d-gaussian-splatting-mvp.md) which uses a hybrid filtering mechanism that suppresses high-frequency components, Analytic-Splatting preserves high-quality details by analytically integrating the signal using a conditioned logistic function approximation of the Gaussian CDF. [Analytic-Splatting](8084-analytic-splatting.md)

### [Rendering Pipelines and Extensions](8587-rendering-pipelines-and-extensions.md)
This family encompasses the differentiable rendering mechanisms and architectural extensions that convert 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D images, enabling both standard novel view synthesis and advanced inverse rendering tasks [G]. It solves the problem of efficiently rasterizing soft, view-dependent volumetric primitives while supporting complex appearance models such as physical lighting and volume data visualization [G]. What sets these methods apart from standard [3D Gaussian Splatting](1795-3d-gaussian-splatting.md) is the decoupling of geometric representation from appearance, allowing for explicit material properties (BRDF) or scalar density fields (VEG) rather than baked spherical harmonics [G]. [Rendering Pipelines and Extensions](8587-rendering-pipelines-and-extensions.md)

### [Projection Geometry and Coordinate Systems](9313-projection-geometry-and-coordinate-systems.md)
This family defines the mathematical framework for transforming 3D Gaussian primitives, defined by mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, into 2D distributions suitable for rasterization [G]. It sets apart from standard perspective projection by supporting optimal tangent-plane projections [3293-optimal-gaussian-splatting.md] and specialized geometries such as cone-beam X-ray scanning [3458-3d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization-cone-beam-scanner-space.md]. [Projection Geometry and Coordinate Systems](9313-projection-geometry-and-coordinate-systems.md)

### [Texture and Appearance Representation](9528-texture-and-appearance-representation.md)
This family extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting primitive representation to model high-frequency spatial details and view-dependent optical effects that static isotropic color vectors (Spherical Harmonics) cannot capture. It replaces or augments the per-Gaussian color attribute with explicit textures, neural networks, or directional basis functions, enabling the reconstruction of complex materials, specularities, and fine surface patterns. Unlike the base [3D Gaussian Splatting](2812-3d-gaussian-splatting.md) method which assumes uniform color per primitive, these methods decouple geometry from appearance or enhance the appearance model with directional sensitivity. [Texture and Appearance Representation](9528-texture-and-appearance-representation.md)

### [Point-Based Ray Tracing for Visibility](10934-point-based-ray-tracing-for-visibility.md)
Point-Based Ray Tracing for Visibility serves as a sparse visibility oracle that evaluates transmittance along sampled light directions using a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) of Gaussian proxies, enabling evaluation from any point in space in any direction. Unlike the camera-tied rasterizer, it decouples visibility estimation from screen-space projection, allowing it to act as a drop-in companion to standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by reusing the same EWA-splatting formulation for consistent blending without requiring training mismatch. [Point-Based Ray Tracing for Visibility](10934-point-based-ray-tracing-for-visibility.md)

### [Primitive Representation](11838-primitive-representation.md)
Primitive Representation defines the geometric and photometric data structures used to approximate a 3D scene in Gaussian Splatting methods [G]. It solves the problem of representing continuous geometry and appearance with discrete, differentiable primitives that can be efficiently rasterized [G]. It sets itself apart from parent methods by explicitly defining the kernel shape (e.g., isotropic, half-space, or hybrid Bézier) and the associated attribute storage (e.g., opacity, normals, control points) that determine how light interacts with the primitive during rendering [G]. [Primitive Representation](11838-primitive-representation.md)

### [Shading and Lighting Models](11924-shading-and-lighting-models.md)
Shading and Lighting Models extend the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization pipeline to support physically based rendering (PBR), self-occlusion, and anti-aliased forward shading [G]. While standard splatting assumes a fixed, uniform lighting environment and simple alpha blending, this family introduces mechanisms for deferred shading pipelines, light-space rasterization for shadows, and analytic integration approximations for multiscale fidelity [Lighting, Shadowing & Relighting](11894-lighting-shadowing-relighting.md), [Analytic-Splatting Forward Shading](3459-analytic-splatting-forward-shading.md), [Transparency and Blending Models](9274-transparency-and-blending-models.md). It differs from sibling methods like [Analytic-Splatting Forward Shading](3459-analytic-splatting-forward-shading.md) by focusing on material attributes and light interaction rather than just pixel-area integration, and from [Transparency and Blending Models](9274-transparency-and-blending-models.md) by explicitly handling light source geometry and PBR material buffers. [Shading and Lighting Models](11924-shading-and-lighting-models.md)

### [Reflective Gaussian Splatting](12321-reflective-gaussian-splatting.md)
Reflective Gaussian Splatting (Ref-Gaussian) is a framework for the real-time, high-quality rendering of reflective objects with inter-reflection effects, addressing the limitations of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting in modeling complex surfaces and multi-view inconsistencies. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [3D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](2812-3d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization.md) by utilizing 2D Gaussian primitives for view-consistent geometry and employing physically based deferred rendering with split-sum approximation to stabilize optimization and enable efficient inter-reflection modeling without heavy Monte Carlo sampling. [Reflective Gaussian Splatting](12321-reflective-gaussian-splatting.md)

### [Ray-Splat Intersection Algorithms](13401-ray-splat-intersection-algorithms.md)
Ray-splat intersection algorithms evaluate the contribution of 3D Gaussians by intersecting viewing rays with the Gaussian primitives directly, rather than projecting them onto the image plane. This family solves the perspective distortion and depth-sorting artifacts inherent in tile-based rasterization. Unlike standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) which approximates the projection as an affine 2D Gaussian, these methods provide exact or analytically derived intersections, enabling correct handling of semi-transparency and degenerate splats. [Ray-Splat Intersection Algorithms](13401-ray-splat-intersection-algorithms.md)

### [Application-Specific Extensions](13465-application-specific-extensions.md)
Application-Specific Extensions modify the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) framework to handle non-RGB, non-photometric data or to extract explicit geometry from the implicit volumetric representation [4971-radiative-and-tomographic-gaussians.md], [5621-domain-specific-adaptations.md], [8498-surface-and-mesh-extraction.md]. While standard 3DGS optimizes for novel-view synthesis of RGB scenes using calibrated camera poses and photometric losses, these adaptations replace the projection target with volumetric supervision, voxel-space rasterization, or 3D geometric intersections [5621-domain-specific-adaptations.md], [8498-surface-and-mesh-extraction.md]. They solve the problem of reconstructing and visualizing high-dimensional or physically distinct data fields where traditional camera-based rendering is inapplicable or insufficient [G]. [Application-Specific Extensions](13465-application-specific-extensions.md)

### [Rasterization Pipeline Optimization](15109-rasterization-pipeline-optimization.md)
Rasterization Pipeline Optimization refers to the family of algorithmic and hardware techniques designed to accelerate the rendering of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by reducing the computational overhead of tile intersection, sorting, and alpha compositing. It solves the bottleneck where standard rasterization overestimates Gaussian extents, processes non-intersecting pixels, and suffers from serial blending latency. What sets this family apart from sibling methods like [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) (which focuses on the differentiable math) or [3D Gaussian Splatting MVP](3410-3d-gaussian-splatting-mvp.md) (which focuses on memory layout) is the explicit restructuring of the rendering pipeline stages—specifically moving culling to parallel preprocess phases, replacing coarse bounding-box tests with precise geometric checks, and co-designing hardware units for projection and sorting. [Rasterization Pipeline Optimization](15109-rasterization-pipeline-optimization.md)

### [Semantic and Structural Extensions](17384-semantic-and-structural-extensions.md)
This family extends 3D Gaussian Splatting by attaching high-dimensional feature vectors (semantic, language, or latent) to Gaussian primitives or by modeling animatable human and articulated animal avatars from multi-view data. It differs from standard rendering by accumulating these feature vectors via differentiable alpha-blending rather than just color, and from sibling methods by leveraging 2D vision-language models to distill features without explicit 2D-to-3D lifting networks during inference. For structural extensions, it solves dynamic appearance and geometry reconstruction by maintaining a canonical 3D Gaussian field and applying differentiable deformation to map primitives to target poses, distinguished by its focus on geometric articulation rather than semantic feature accumulation. [Semantic and Structural Extensions](17384-semantic-and-structural-extensions.md)

## Operating characteristics

**Time Complexity:**
*   **Rendering:** $O(N \cdot T)$, where $N$ is the number of Gaussians and $T$ is the number of tiles. The dominant cost is sorting $N$ Gaussians ($O(N \log N)$) and rasterizing them per tile [S6][S16, unofficial].
*   **Training:** Depends on the number of iterations (typically 30,000) and the densification frequency [S8, unofficial]. Each iteration involves forward rendering, loss computation, and backward gradient propagation [S16, unofficial].

**Memory Complexity:**
*   **Storage:** Each Gaussian requires $\approx 30-60$ parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, SH coefficients) [S23]. A scene with millions of Gaussians can exceed 1GB on disk [S16, unofficial].
*   **VRAM:** Training requires significant VRAM (4GB to view, 12GB to train for typical scenes) due to storing gradients and intermediate tensors for millions of primitives [S16, unofficial].

**Measured Performance:**
*   **Rendering Speed:** Real-time rendering (>30 FPS, up to 100+ FPS) at 1080p resolution with millions of Gaussians on modern GPUs [S6][S23].
*   **Training Time:** Several minutes to tens of minutes for high-quality reconstruction, comparable to InstantNGP but with Mip-NeRF360 class quality [S23].

**Strengths:**
*   Real-time rendering speeds [S6].
*   High-quality novel view synthesis [S1].
*   Explicit representation allows for easy editing and compression [S1].
*   No neural networks required for rendering (unlike NeRF) [S18, unofficial].

**Limitations:**
*   Scene-specific; does not generalize to unknown scenes without re-training [S23].
*   Artifacts may appear in out-of-domain views (unobserved regions) [S23].
*   High memory usage for large scenes [S23].
*   Standard Gaussians struggle with complex nonlinear structures (e.g., holes) without extensions like negative Gaussians [S4].

## Background

3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 at SIGGRAPH [S1][S6]. It emerged as a response to the computational bottlenecks of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), which rely on continuous MLPs and expensive ray marching [S6][S18, unofficial]. The method was inspired by the efficiency of triangle rasterization in traditional graphics pipelines, adapting it for soft, volumetric primitives [S16, unofficial]. The original implementation utilized CUDA kernels for efficient sorting and rasterization, achieving real-time performance [S6][S16, unofficial]. Since its introduction, it has become a foundational representation for 3D reconstruction and generative 3D tasks [S1], as well as dynamic scene rendering [S7].
