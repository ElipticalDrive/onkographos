# Neural SDF Inference via Gaussian Pulling

Also called: Center Loss, Disk Loss, Disk Loss / Center Loss, Neural Signed Distance Function Inference through Splatting 3D Gaussians Pulled on Zero-Level Set, Neural-Pull, Pulling to Centers, Pulling to Disks

## Overview
Neural SDF Inference via Gaussian Pulling (often referred to as GS-Pull) is a method for reconstructing continuous 3D geometry from multi-view RGB images by jointly optimizing a set of 3D Gaussians and a neural Signed Distance Function (SDF) [S1]. It solves the problem of inferring accurate surface geometry from 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), which typically suffers from discrete, sparse, and off-surface Gaussian distributions that do not naturally represent continuous surfaces [S1]. Unlike standard 3DGS which renders color directly, or methods that add separate volume rendering branches, this approach dynamically aligns Gaussians to the SDF's zero-level set via a differentiable "pulling" operation and constrains them to be thin disks tangent to the surface, enabling robust surface extraction using marching cubes [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Posed multi-view RGB images $\{v_i\}_{i=1}^I$ [S1].
**Outputs:** A neural SDF network $f$ and a set of optimized 3D Gaussians $\{g_j\}_{j=1}^J$ [S1].

### Data Structures
1.  **3D Gaussians $\{g_j\}$:** Each Gaussian $g_j$ is defined by:
    *   Mean position $\mu_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1].
    *   Covariance matrix $\Sigma_j \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, parameterized by variances $r_1, r_2, r_3$ along principal axes and a rotation matrix [S1].
    *   Opacity $o_j \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].
    *   Color $c_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$ [S1].
    *   Normal $n_j$: Defined as the unit vector along the axis with the minimum variance $\bar{r} = \min\{r_1, r_2, r_3\}$ [S1].
2.  **Neural SDF $f$:** An MLP with 8 layers, 256 hidden units, and ReLU activation [S1]. Initialized to represent a sphere [S1]. It takes a query point $q \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and outputs a signed distance $s = f(q)$ [S1].
3.  **Pulled Gaussians $\{g'_j\}$:** Temporary structures used during optimization. They share the same attributes ($c_j, o_j, \Sigma_j$) as $g_j$ but have updated centers $\mu'_j$ [S1].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Initialize:** Initialize the set of 3D Gaussians $\{g_j\}$ and the SDF network $f$ [S1].
2.  **Pull Gaussians to Zero-Level Set:** For each Gaussian $g_j$ with center $\mu_j$:
    *   Query the SDF for the signed distance: $s_j = f(\mu_j)$ [S1].
    *   Query the SDF gradient: $\nabla f(\mu_j)$ [S1].
    *   Update the center to lie on the zero-level set:
        $$ \mu'_j = \mu_j - s_j \frac{\nabla f(\mu_j)}{\|\nabla f(\mu_j)\|} $$
        [S1]
    *   Form the pulled Gaussian $g'_j$ with center $\mu'_j$ and original attributes [S1].
3.  **Render and Compute Rendering Loss:**
    *   Render the pulled Gaussians $\{g'_j\}$ into images $v'_i$ using differentiable rasterization [S1].
    *   The color $C'(u,v)$ at pixel $(u,v)$ is computed via volume rendering:
        $$ C'(u, v) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} c_j \cdot o_j \cdot p_j(u, v) \prod_{k=1}^{j-1} (1 - o_k \cdot p_k(u, v)) $$
        where $p_j(u,v)$ is the 2D projection probability of the $j$-th Gaussian [S1].
    *   Compute the rendering loss $L_{Splatting}$ against ground truth images $v_i$:
        $$ L_{Splatting} = 0.8 \cdot L_1(v'_i, v_i) + 0.2 \cdot L_{D-SSIM}(v'_i, v_i) $$
        [S1]
4.  **Compute Geometry Constraints:**
    *   **Thin Disk Loss ($L_{Thin}$):** Encourage Gaussians to be flat disks by minimizing the smallest variance:
        $$ L_{Thin} = \|\min\{r_1, r_2, r_3\}\|_1 $$
        [S1]
    *   **Tangent Loss ($L_{Tangent}$):** Align the Gaussian normal $n_j$ with the SDF gradient at the pulled center $\mu'_j$:
        $$ L_{Tangent} = 1 - \frac{\nabla f(\mu'_j)}{\|\nabla f(\mu'_j)\|} \cdot n_j $$
        [S1]
    *   **Pulling Probability Loss ($L_{Pull}$):** For query points $q$ in the neighborhood, pull them to the zero-level set ($q' = q - f(q)\frac{\nabla f(q)}{\|\nabla f(q)\|}$) and maximize their probability of belonging to the nearest pulled Gaussian $\bar{g}$ (determined by $\bar{g} = \arg\min_{g'_j} \|\mu'_j - q\|^2$). Minimize the negative log-probability:
        $$ L_{Pull}(q'; \bar{\mu}) = -\log \left( \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (q' - \bar{\mu})^T P_{\bar{g}}^{-1} (q' - \bar{\mu})\right) \right) $$
        where $P_{\bar{g}}$ is the covariance of the nearest pulled Gaussian [S1].
    *   **Orthogonal Pulling Loss ($L_{Orthogonal}$):** Ensure the SDF gradient at query $q$ aligns with the normal $\bar{n}$ of the nearest pulled Gaussian disk:
        $$ L_{Orthogonal} = 1 - \frac{\nabla f(q)}{\|\nabla f(q)\|} \cdot \bar{n} $$
        [S1]
5.  **Joint Optimization:** Update $\{g_j\}$ and $f$ by minimizing the total objective:
    $$ \min_{\{g_j\}, f} L_{Splatting} + \alpha L_{Thin} + \beta L_{Tangent} + \gamma L_{Pull} + \delta L_{Orthogonal} $$
    [S1]
6.  **Surface Extraction:** After optimization, extract the mesh from the learned SDF $f$ using the marching cubes algorithm [S1]. For small scenes, use resolution 800; for large scenes, split into parts and reconstruct each with resolution 800 [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Discreteness and Sparseness:** 3D Gaussians are discrete and may be sparsely distributed, making SDF inference difficult [S1]. The "pulling to disks" approach is specifically designed to handle this by providing higher tolerance for off-center queries compared to "pulling to centers" [S1].
*   **Off-surface Drift:** Gaussians may drift off the surface during standard 3DGS optimization. The pulling operation dynamically corrects this by aligning them to the SDF zero-level set [S1].
*   **Gradient Diminishing:** As a query point approaches a Gaussian center, the gradient of the disk loss diminishes quadratically, reducing the driving force. This prevents overfitting to individual Gaussian centers, which occurs with constant gradients in "pulling to centers" [S1].
*   **Large Scenes:** Memory limitations require splitting large scenes into parts for marching cubes reconstruction [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training takes approximately 21.8 minutes on the DTU dataset and 37.6 minutes on the Tanks and Temples dataset [S1]. This is comparable to 2DGS (20.5m on DTU) and significantly faster than NeuS (~12h on TNT) [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:**
    *   **DTU Dataset:** Mean Chamfer Distance of 0.52, outperforming 2DGS (0.54) and GSurfels (0.56) [S1].
    *   **Tanks and Temples Dataset:** Mean F-score of 0.84, surpassing NeuS (0.54) [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Recovers accurate, smooth, and complete surfaces with more geometry details than previous methods. Avoids artifacts like large sky spheres common in TSDF or screened Poisson fusion by learning neural SDFs directly [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on multi-view consistency. Performance may degrade with very sparse or non-uniformly distributed Gaussians if not properly constrained, though the disk loss mitigates this [S1].
*   **When to use:** When surface reconstruction is required from 3DGS, especially when avoiding background artifacts and achieving high geometric accuracy is prioritized over pure rendering speed [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When only novel view synthesis is needed without explicit mesh extraction, or when computational resources are extremely limited for large-scale scenes requiring splitting [S1].

## Background
The method was proposed by Wen-Yuan Zhang et al. in the paper "Neural Signed Distance Function Inference through Splatting 3D Gaussians Pulled on Zero-Level Set" [S1]. It builds upon 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [30] and Neural-Pull [45], addressing the limitations of 3DGS in inferring continuous signed distance functions due to the discreteness and off-surface drift of Gaussians [S1]. The method was published in 2024 [S1].
