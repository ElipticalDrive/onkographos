# FlexGS

Also called: Flexible 3D Gaussian Splatting, Elastic Inference for 3DGS

## Overview
FlexGS is an elastic inference method for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that enables a single trained model to adapt to varying computational constraints at inference time by selecting and transforming a subset of Gaussians based on a desired model size ratio, without requiring additional fine-tuning [S1]. It solves the problem of standard 3DGS requiring significant GPU memory, which limits deployment on devices with restricted resources (e.g., smartphones, VR headsets) by decoupling model size from fixed pruning ratios [S1]. Unlike post-hoc compression methods that require separate fine-tuning runs for each target ratio, FlexGS jointly trains a learnable selector and transformation field to dynamically balance quality and cost [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A pre-trained 3DGS model consisting of $N$ Gaussians, each characterized by position $X \in \mathbb{R}^3$, scaling vector $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$, rotation quaternion $q \in \mathbb{R}^3$, opacity $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, and color $c$ defined by spherical harmonics coefficients $SH \in \mathbb{R}^{(d+1)}$ [S1]. A target ratio $e \in (0, 1]$ representing the desired percentage of remaining Gaussians [S1].
*   **Output:** A rendered image using a subset of the original Gaussians, where the selected Gaussians are transformed to compensate for missing details [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Set:** A collection of $N$ primitives. Each primitive $i$ stores:
    *   Mean position: $X_i$
    *   Scaling vector: $s_i$
    *   Rotation quaternion: $q_i$
    *   Opacity: $\alpha_i$
    *   Spherical Harmonics coefficients: $SH_i$
*   **Selector Output:** A binary mask vector $\lambda \in \{0, 1\}^N$, where $\lambda_i = 1$ if Gaussian $i$ is selected, and $0$ otherwise.
*   **Transformation Field:** A module that outputs displacement and scaling adjustments for selected Gaussians [S1].

**Core Formulas:**
Each Gaussian is defined by a mean value $X$ and a covariance matrix $\Sigma$:
$$ G(X) = e^{-\frac{1}{2} X \Sigma^{-1} X} $$
The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is decomposed into a scaling matrix $S$ and a rotation matrix $R$ for differentiable optimization:
$$ \Sigma = R S S^\top R^\top $$
where $S$ is derived from the scaling vector $s$ and $R$ from the quaternion $q$ [S1].

Rendering blends $N$ ordered points overlapping a pixel using color $c_i$ and opacity $\alpha_i$:
$$ C = \sum_{i \in N} c_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
[ S1 ]

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Train the base 3DGS model using standard differentiable rendering optimization [S1].
2.  **Selector Training:** During training, for each batch, sample a target ratio $e$ from a distribution of desired ratios [S1].
3.  **Adaptive Selection:** Predict a binary scaler $\lambda_i$ for each Gaussian $i$ based on its attributes and the target ratio $e$. The selection must satisfy the constraint:
    $$ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i = eN $$
    [ S1 ]
    To handle the discrete nature of $\lambda_i$, the method uses a learnable module (e.g., Gumbel-Softmax relaxation during training) to allow gradient descent [S1].
4.  **Transformation:** For the selected Gaussians ($\lambda_i = 1$), apply a ratio-dependent transformation field. This field displaces the Gaussians and adjusts their attributes to compensate for the missing details caused by the reduction in Gaussian count [S1].
5.  **Rendering:** Render the scene using the transformed subset of Gaussians [S1].
6.  **Inference:** At inference time, input any ratio $e$. The trained selector determines the subset, the transformation field adjusts them, and rendering proceeds without further training [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   **Target Ratio ($e$):** The desired percentage of Gaussians to retain. Typical values range from 0.01 (1%) to 1.0 (100%) [S1].
*   **Number of Gaussians ($N$):** The total number of Gaussians in the initial 3DGS model [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Extreme Ratios:** Fixed heuristic selection rules may fail to generalize in extreme cases, such as when selecting only 1% of Gaussians, because importance scores must be frequently re-computed as Gaussian attributes evolve during training [S1]. FlexGS addresses this by jointly training the selector [S1].
*   **Discrete Optimization:** The binary nature of selection requires special handling (e.g., Gumbel-Softmax) to enable gradient-based training [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Inference time is proportional to the number of selected Gaussians, which is $eN$. The selection and transformation steps add negligible overhead compared to rendering [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage is proportional to the number of selected Gaussians, $eN$. The selector and transformation networks are lightweight [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** Experiments on ZipNeRF, MipNeRF, and Tanks&Temples scenes demonstrate effectiveness in maintaining rendering performance while reducing model size [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Single model can be deployed on diverse hardware constraints (smartphones, VR headsets) [S1].
    *   No additional fine-tuning required for different ratios [S1].
    *   Adapts to arbitrary ratios during inference [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Requires joint training of selector and transformation modules, which may increase training complexity compared to standard 3DGS [S1].
    *   Performance at extreme ratios (e.g., 1%) may still be challenging, though improved over heuristics [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When deploying 3DGS models on devices with varying computational resources and avoiding the need for multiple model versions or retraining is critical [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When the highest possible quality at very low ratios is required and the overhead of joint training is prohibitive [S1].

## Background
FlexGS was introduced by Hengyu Liu, Yuehao Wang, Chenxin Li, Ruisi Cai, Kevin Wang, Wuyang Li, Pavlo Molchanov, Peihao Wang, and Zhangyang Wang from The Chinese University of Hong Kong, University of Texas at Austin, and Nvidia [S1]. The paper "FlexGS: Train Once, Deploy Everywhere with Many-in-One Flexible 3D Gaussian Splatting" was published in CVPR 2025 (arXiv:2506.04174v1, June 4, 2025) [S1]. It addresses the limitations of standard 3DGS in terms of memory overhead and the lack of adaptability for different devices in previous compression methods [S1].
