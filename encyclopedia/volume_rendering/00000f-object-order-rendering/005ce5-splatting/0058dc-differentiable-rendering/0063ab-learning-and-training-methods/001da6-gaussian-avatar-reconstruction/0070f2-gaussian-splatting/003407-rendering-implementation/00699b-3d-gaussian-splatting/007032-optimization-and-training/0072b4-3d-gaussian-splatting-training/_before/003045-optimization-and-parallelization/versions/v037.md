# Optimization and Parallelization

Also called: 3D Gaussian Splatting Training Optimization, Distributed 3DGS Training

## Overview
This family encompasses the algorithmic strategies and distributed systems required to train 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) models efficiently. It solves the computational bottlenecks of differentiable rasterization and the memory limits of single-GPU training when handling billions of primitives. It is distinguished from sibling methods by its focus on system-level parallelization (multi-GPU partitioning, warp-level backprop optimization) and high-level training pipeline orchestration, rather than parameter-specific learning rate schedules or fused kernel implementations.

## Shared principles
The methods in this family share the following foundational mechanisms:

1.  **Differentiable Rasterization Core**: All methods rely on the differentiable alpha-blending rasterizer. The forward pass computes pixel color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x})$ via depth-sorted alpha blending, and the backward pass computes gradients $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}$ with respect to Gaussian parameters $\theta$ [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].
2.  **Gradient Accumulation**: Gradients are accumulated over multiple training steps or views before an optimizer update. In distributed settings, gradients or intermediate rendering states are synchronized across devices [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].
3.  **Adaptive Representation**: Many optimization variants modify the Gaussian representation or its distribution to improve efficiency or fidelity, requiring corresponding adjustments to the gradient computation or data flow [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].
4.  **Hardware Acceleration**: Methods leverage GPU parallelism, often using CUDA kernels, Tensor Cores (via GEMM reformulation), or half-precision arithmetic to accelerate the rasterization and gradient computation [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].

## Subtopics

### [MaskGaussian Gradient Computation](4254-maskgaussian-gradient-computation.md)
A method for computing the gradient of the loss with respect to probabilistic masks $\mathcal{M}_i$ applied to 3D Gaussians. It distinguishes itself by explicitly accounting for the gradient contribution of the mask on both the foreground Gaussian color accumulation and the background transmittance path, solving the backpropagation problem through masked alpha-blending. Choose this when using mask-based regularization or segmentation-aware training.

### [Training Pipeline](5325-training-pipeline.md)
The iterative optimization loop that integrates dynamic Gaussian management (pruning, densification, cloning) within the training loop. It distinguishes itself by adapting the representation density to scene complexity via axis-slice rendering and gradient-based refinement checks. Choose this as the baseline training architecture for standard 3DGS reconstruction.

### [Parallelization for Backpropagation](5989-parallelization-for-backpropagation.md)
A training optimization that replaces the standard per-pixel backward pass with a per-splat (per-Gaussian) approach. It distinguishes itself by iterating over Gaussians to update pixel gradients, addressing the performance bottleneck of atomic contention in the per-pixel approach. Choose this to reduce training time by 4–5× while maintaining numerical equivalence.

### [Grendel-GS](7349-grendel-gs.md)
A distributed optimization framework that partitions the Gaussian primitive list across multiple GPUs. It distinguishes itself from single-machine streaming methods (like TideGS) by distributing the parameter table across a GPU cluster, enabling training on scenes with billions of primitives. Choose this when single-device VRAM limits prevent training on large-scale scenes.

### [3D Gaussian Splatting Implementation Details](9128-3d-gaussian-splatting-implementation-details.md)
The reference implementation for the core 3DGS training pipeline, detailing specific algorithmic steps, data structures, and hyperparameters. It distinguishes itself by focusing on the baseline configuration, low-level kernel behaviors (e.g., half-precision fetches), and standard threshold schedules rather than high-level architectural modifications. Choose this for implementing the standard 3DGS baseline.

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 . The initial implementation focused on real-time rendering and single-GPU training. As the method gained popularity for large-scale scene reconstruction, limitations in training efficiency and memory scalability became apparent, leading to the development of the sub-methods described above. Grendel-GS  and other parallelization techniques address the scaling challenges inherent in the $O(N)$ complexity of Gaussian primitives.
