# Surface Geometry Constraints


## Overview
Surface Geometry Constraints are optimization and initialization techniques applied to 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) to enforce geometric fidelity, ensuring that the volumetric Gaussian representation aligns with underlying surface structures such as meshes, planes, or physical boundaries [S1, S2][S3, unofficial][S4]. They differ from sibling methods like [Mesh Extraction and Representation](14095-mesh-extraction-and-representation.md) by integrating constraints directly into the training loop rather than post-processing, and from [Appearance and Physical Modeling](15766-appearance-and-physical-modeling.md) by focusing on spatial geometry rather than radiance [S1, S2, S4].

## Subtopics

### [Planar Gaussian Splatting](16706-planar-gaussian-splatting.md)
[Planar Gaussian Splatting](16706-planar-gaussian-splatting.md) is an unsupervised neural rendering framework that reconstructs 3D geometry and explicitly parses 3D planar surfaces from multiple RGB images without requiring 3D labels or depth supervision. It extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting representation by introducing discriminative 3D plane descriptors and enforcing local planar alignment constraints. Unlike sibling methods that focus on high-fidelity rendering or generic mesh extraction, PGS is designed to identify distinct 3D plane instances through a hierarchical Gaussian Mixture Tree (GMT). It sets itself apart by using 2D segmentation priors from foundation models (like SAM) to guide the learning of these descriptors, enabling robust planar reconstruction across different scenes.

### Scale Constrained by Mesh Geometry
A constraint to prevent discrepancies between Gaussian Splat scales and the scales of corresponding triangular mesh faces by bounding the relative scale coefficients [S1]. The final scale $s$ is computed as $s = s_{rel} \odot s_0$, where $s_{rel} = (s_{rel,1}, s_{rel,2}) \in [s_{min}, s_{max}] \times [s_{min}, s_{max}]$ is the coefficient vector, $s_0$ is the predefined scale derived from the mesh faces, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication [S1]. The bounds $s_{min}$ and $s_{max}$ define the allowable range for the relative scale coefficients [S1].

### Tangent Plane Constrained Gaussian Splatting
An extension of 3D Gaussian Splatting where Gaussians are constrained to a 2D tangent plane to resolve the conflict between volumetric Gaussians and surface representation, enabling accurate normal map estimation [S2]. A point $P$ on the 2D Gaussian plane with coordinates $(u, v)$ is defined as $P(u, v) = p_k + s_u t_u u + s_v t_v v = H(u, v, 1, 1)^T$, where $H = [p_k, t_u, t_v, t_w]$ is the transformation matrix [S2]. Normal rendering is performed by splatting normals in the same manner as color splatting to generate a normal map [S2].

### Manual Collision Geometry
A solution where a second invisible layer of geometry (meshes, primitives) is built separately around the Gaussian scene to define physical boundaries, as Gaussian splats lack solid surfaces by default [S3, unofficial]. This involves manually positioning various primitives (boxes, simple polygons) around Gaussians to construct surfaces for navigation and pointing [S3, unofficial]. It is used in early 3DGS development via heavy game engines (Unity, Unreal Engine) or web-based solutions like Spline [S3, unofficial]. A major pitfall is that creating collision geometry object by object for large buildings, industrial environments, or complex outdoor scans is very time-consuming [S3, unofficial].

### Constrained 3D Gaussian Splatting Optimization
First stage of optimization reconstructing the background using segmented background images, background point cloud, spheres' radii, and shell center [S4]. During this pass, Gaussians are constrained to remain inside a spherical shell defined by an inner radius $R_i$ and outer radius $R_o$ centered at $O$ [S4]. The shell loss is defined as:
$$ L_{shell} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \max(0, \|p_i - O\|^2 - R_o) + \max(0, R_i - \|p_i - O\|^2) \right)^2 $$
where $N$ is the total number of Gaussians, $p_i$ is the position of the $i$-th Gaussian [S4]. A planarity loss is also applied:
$$ L_{planarity} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( 1 - \left| \frac{p_i - O}{\|p_i - O\|} \cdot (\text{Rot}_i \cdot a_{local_i}) \right| \right) \frac{s_{max_i}}{s_{min_i} + \epsilon} $$
where $\text{Rot}_i$ is the rotation matrix, $a_{local_i}$ is the shortest axis basis vector, $s_{max}/s_{min}$ are scaling factors, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant [S4]. The total loss is $L = L_{render} + \lambda_{shell} L_{shell} + \lambda_{planarity} L_{planarity}$, with $\lambda_{shell} = 1.0$ and $\lambda_{planarity} = 0.1$ [S4].

## Background
The need for surface geometry constraints in 3D Gaussian Splatting arises from the method's inherent volumetric nature, which can lead to "floaters" and ambiguous surface boundaries when reconstructing sharp geometric features [S2]. Early implementations relied on manual collision geometry to provide physical boundaries for navigation, as the splats themselves did not constitute solid surfaces [S3, unofficial]. Subsequent research introduced explicit constraints, such as planar alignment using foundation model priors [S1], mesh-adherent scale bounding [S1], and tangent plane parameterization for normal estimation [S2]. More recent approaches utilize spherical shell constraints for outdoor background reconstruction to prevent divergence [S4]. These methods collectively aim to bridge the gap between differentiable volumetric rendering and explicit geometric surface representation [S1, S2, S4].
