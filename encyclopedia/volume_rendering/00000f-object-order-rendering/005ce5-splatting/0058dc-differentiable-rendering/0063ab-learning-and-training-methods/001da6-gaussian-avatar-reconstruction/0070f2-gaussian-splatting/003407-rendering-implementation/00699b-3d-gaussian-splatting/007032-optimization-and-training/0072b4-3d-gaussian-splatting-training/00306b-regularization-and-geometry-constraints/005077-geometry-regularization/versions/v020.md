# Geometry Regularization

Also called: Gaussian Flattening, Normal Consistency and Flattening

## Overview
Geometry regularization improves surface smoothness and reduces floating artifacts ("floaters") by constraining the geometric shape of the Gaussians, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains their spatial location [S1, S2, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating directly on the Gaussian covariance parameters and rendered normals rather than image frequency spectra, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by enforcing local surface orientation and flatness rather than global depth consistency [S1, S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Per-Gaussian scaling factors $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$, per-Gaussian normals $n_g$, rendered depth map $D$, rendered normal map $N_r$, and ground truth or pseudo-ground truth depth/normal maps.
*   **Outputs:** Regularization loss terms $\mathcal{L}_{flatten}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{normal}$ added to the total loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Scaling:** Vector $s = [s_x, s_y, s_z]$ representing the scale along the principal axes of the Gaussian.
*   **Normals:** Unit vectors $n_g$ for each Gaussian and $N_r$ for the rendered surface.

**Steps:**
1.  **Flattening Constraint:** Minimize the smallest component of the scaling factor vector $s$ towards zero. This forces the Gaussian to become a flat "surflet" aligned with the surface, where the shortest axis corresponds to the surface normal direction [S2, unofficial].
2.  **Normal Consistency:** Align the per-Gaussian normal $n_g$ with the normal $N_r$ derived from the rendered depth map. This ensures the local orientation of the Gaussian matches the global surface orientation inferred from depth [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The flattening loss minimizes the minimum scale component:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{flatten} = \min(s_x, s_y, s_z) $$
Ideally, this term is minimized such that $\min(s) \to 0$, effectively flattening the Gaussian into a 2D surfel [S2, unofficial].

The normal consistency loss aligns the Gaussian normal $n_g$ with the rendered normal $N_r$:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{normal} = 1 - |n_g \cdot N_r| $$
This penalizes misalignment between the Gaussian's intrinsic orientation and the surface geometry derived from depth [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   `use_normal_loss`: A boolean flag (e.g., in `OptimizationParams`) that enables the geometry regularization module [S1, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_{reg}$: The weighting factor for the combined regularization term in the total loss $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photometric} + \lambda_{reg} (\mathcal{L}_{flatten} + \mathcal{L}_{normal})$ [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Depth Refitting:** Depth maps must be refitted against a prior point cloud to ensure consistent scale before use. Depth maps that fail this refitting process are discarded and not used for supervision [S1, unofficial].
*   **Precision:** Minimizing scale components to zero can lead to numerical instability if not regularized; however, the sources do not specify a lower bound epsilon [S2, unofficial].
*   **Floater Trade-off:** While geometry regularization reduces floaters, aggressive flattening may reduce the number of remaining Gaussians, potentially compromising reconstruction quality in complex regions [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Scaling Loss for Geometry Modeling
A specific implementation of geometry regularization that flattens Gaussian kernels into 3D surfels aligned with the surface by minimizing the smallest component of the scaling factor $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$ towards zero [S2, unofficial]. This aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge as the normal direction [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when explicit normal supervision is unavailable or when surfel-like geometry is desired to reduce floaters [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds minimal overhead during training as it involves simple vector operations on scaling factors and dot products for normals.
*   **Memory Complexity:** No additional large data structures required beyond existing Gaussian attributes.
*   **Strengths:** Significantly improves surface smoothness and reduces floating artifacts by enforcing surfel-like geometry [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** May reduce the number of Gaussians if not balanced with other regularization terms, potentially losing detail in high-frequency areas [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When photometric loss alone results in noisy geometry or floaters, and surface smoothness is prioritized over fine geometric detail [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Originating from the need to improve the geometric quality of 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructions, this method was formalized in implementations such as GaussianSplatting.jl and frameworks like GauStudio [S1, S2, unofficial]. It addresses the limitation of photometric loss alone, which is insufficient to distinguish valid surface geometry from noise in empty space [S2, unofficial]. The technique builds on the concept of representing surfaces with flattened Gaussians (surfels) [S2, unofficial].
