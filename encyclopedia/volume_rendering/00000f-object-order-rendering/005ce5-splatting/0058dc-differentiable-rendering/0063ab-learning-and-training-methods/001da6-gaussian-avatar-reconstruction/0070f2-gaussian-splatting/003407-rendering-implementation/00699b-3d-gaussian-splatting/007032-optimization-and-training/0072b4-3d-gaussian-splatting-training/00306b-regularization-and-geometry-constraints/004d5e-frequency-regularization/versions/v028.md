# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which results in small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR explicitly disentangles frequency components to increase pixel gradients in these regions, enabling adaptive densification [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial domain, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by targeting spectral fidelity rather than geometric depth priors.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Image dimensions $H, W$.
- **Outputs:** Regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{FR}$, which is added to the total loss.

**Data Structures:**
- Complex frequency values $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ for each frequency coordinate $(u, v)$.
- Amplitude values $|F(u, v)|$ and $|\hat{F}(u, v)|$.
- Phase values $\angle F(u, v)$ and $\angle \hat{F}(u, v)$.

**Numbered Steps:**
1. **Compute 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** Convert the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth image $I$ into their frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$. For each channel, compute:
   $$ F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
   where $(x, y)$ are image coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value, and $F(u, v)$ is the complex frequency value [S2].

2. **Compute Amplitude:** Calculate the magnitude of the complex frequency value:
   $$ |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2} $$
   where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$ [S2].

3. **Compute Phase:** Calculate the angle of the complex frequency value:
   $$ \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right) $$
   [S2].

4. **Compute Amplitude Discrepancy ($d_a$):** Calculate the root mean square error between the amplitudes of the ground truth and rendered frequency spectra:
   $$ d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{u=0}^{H-1} \sum_{v=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2} $$
   [S2].

5. **Compute Phase Discrepancy ($d_p$):** Calculate the root mean square error between the phases of the ground truth and rendered frequency spectra:
   $$ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{u=0}^{H-1} \sum_{v=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2} $$
   [S2].

6. **Compute Total Loss:** The frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of $d_a$ and $d_p$, or used directly depending on the specific variant (e.g., FreGS uses both) [S2].

**Parameters:**
- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
- **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
- **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are retained [S2].
- **Typical Values:** Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Over-smoothing:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can bias the splatting towards undesirable artifacts and restrict Gaussian densification [S2].
- **Precision:** The use of `arctan` for phase calculation requires handling cases where $\text{Re}(u, v) \approx 0$ to avoid division by zero or incorrect quadrant determination [G].
- **Boundaries:** The DFT assumes periodic boundary conditions, which may introduce artifacts at image borders if not handled [G].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image: torch.Tensor, ground_truth_image: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the frequency regularization loss based on amplitude and phase discrepancies.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C)
        ground_truth_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C)
        
    Returns:
        Scalar loss tensor
    """
    # Ensure inputs are float32
    rendered_image = rendered_image.float()
    ground_truth_image = ground_truth_image.float()
    
    # Compute 2D DFT for each channel
    # F(u, v) = sum(I(x,y) * exp(-i*2*pi*(u*x/H + v*y/W)))
    # PyTorch's fft2 computes this efficiently
    F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image, dim=(-2, -1))
    F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth_image, dim=(-2, -1))
    
    # Compute Amplitude
    amp_rendered = torch.abs(F_rendered)
    amp_gt = torch.abs(F_gt)
    
    # Compute Phase
    # Use atan2 for robust phase calculation handling quadrants and zero real parts
    phase_rendered = torch.atan2(F_rendered.imag, F_rendered.real)
    phase_gt = torch.atan2(F_gt.imag, F_gt.real)
    
    # Compute Amplitude Discrepancy (da)
    # da = sqrt( (1/HW) * sum( (|F| - |F_hat|)^2 ) )
    # Note: The source formula sums over u, v. fft2 output size is H x W.
    # We average over spatial dimensions (H, W) and channels (C) if present.
    # The source formula implies averaging over H*W. If C > 1, we typically average over C as well or sum.
    # Assuming standard RGB, we average over H, W, and C.
    h, w = rendered_image.shape[-2], rendered_image.shape[-1]
    num_elements = h * w
    if rendered_image.dim() == 4: # Batched
        num_elements *= rendered_image.shape[0]
        
    # Calculate MSE for amplitude
    mse_amp = torch.mean((amp_rendered - amp_gt) ** 2)
    da = torch.sqrt(mse_amp)
    
    # Calculate MSE for phase
    mse_phase = torch.mean((phase_rendered - phase_gt) ** 2)
    dp = torch.sqrt(mse_phase)
    
    # Total loss is typically a combination. FreGS uses both.
    # The source defines da and dp separately. We return their sum or weighted sum.
    # Default weight 1.0 for both as not specified.
    loss = da + dp
    
    return loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The 2D DFT operation is $O(HW \log(HW))$ per image per channel. This adds computational overhead to each training step compared to simple L1 loss.
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the frequency representations $F$ and $\hat{F}$, which are complex tensors of size $H \times W \times C$. This doubles the memory requirement for the image buffers during the loss computation step.
- **Measured Performance:** In the "Bicycle" scene, FR significantly increases pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to L1 loss, enabling better densification [S2].
- **Scaling:** Performance scales with image resolution due to the FFT complexity.
- **Strengths:** Effectively identifies over-reconstructed regions that L1 loss misses, leading to more efficient Gaussian densification and reduced redundancy [S2].
- **Limitations:** Can introduce artifacts if low and high frequencies are not handled separately (e.g., via annealing) [S2]. Computationally more expensive than spatial domain losses.
- **When to use:** When over-reconstruction is prevalent and standard densification fails to prune redundant Gaussians.
- **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited, or when the scene is very low frequency and wavelet/FFT artifacts might dominate.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced to address the limitations of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization, specifically the issues of over-reconstruction and improper Gaussian densification [S1]. The method was formalized in the FreGS paper, which proposed minimizing the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth [S2]. It builds on the observation that L1 loss in the spatial domain is insufficient for guiding densification in over-reconstructed areas [S2]. The technique has since been adapted for sparse-view scenarios using wavelet transforms (DWTGS) [S4, unofficial].
