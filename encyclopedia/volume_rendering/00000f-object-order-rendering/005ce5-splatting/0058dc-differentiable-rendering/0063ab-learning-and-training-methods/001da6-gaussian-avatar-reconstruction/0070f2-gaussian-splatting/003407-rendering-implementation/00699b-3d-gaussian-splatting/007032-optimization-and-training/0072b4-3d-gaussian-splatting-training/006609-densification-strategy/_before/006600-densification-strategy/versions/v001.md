# Densification Strategy
Also called: Adaptive Density Control (ADC), Gaussian Densification

## Overview
Densification strategies in 3D Gaussian Splatting dynamically adjust the number and distribution of Gaussian primitives during training to balance reconstruction fidelity against computational cost. They solve the problem of static Gaussian counts, which either under-reconstruct complex geometry or over-reconstruct flat areas, leading to redundancy and slow rendering. This family is distinguished from sibling methods like [Gaussian Pruning](18664-gaussian-pruning.md) (which removes primitives) and [Regularization and Loss Functions](19512-regularization-and-loss-functions.md) (which constrain optimization) by actively *creating* new Gaussians based on error or gradient signals. It differs from [Selective Gaussian Densification](18688-selective-gaussian-densification.md) by using broader criteria (e.g., pixel error, budget constraints) rather than solely view-space position gradient norms.

## Shared principles
All densification strategies in this family operate within the iterative training loop of 3D Gaussian Splatting. The core mechanism involves three phases:
1.  **Evaluation:** Assessing the current state of Gaussians using a metric derived from rendering error, gradients, or scene complexity.
2.  **Decision:** Determining which Gaussians require modification (splitting, cloning, or noise injection) based on a threshold or score.
3.  **Execution:** Generating new primitives. Common operations include:
    *   **Splitting:** Replacing a Gaussian with multiple smaller Gaussians, typically sampled from a scaled random Gaussian distribution centered at the original position [S1, unofficial].
    *   **Cloning:** Creating a copy of a Gaussian with a slightly perturbed position [S1, unofficial].
    *   **Noise Injection:** Adding controlled spatial noise to positions to increase diversity in under-reconstructed regions [S3].

The strategies share the goal of maintaining a bounded Gaussian count while ensuring high-frequency details are captured. They often rely on the same underlying rasterization outputs (pixel coverage, blending weights) as the base renderer.

## Subtopics

### [Selective Gaussian Densification](18688-selective-gaussian-densification.md)
Uses the sum of individual gradient norms ($S_g = \sum \|\nabla_p I\|$) to distinguish converged from unconverged Gaussians, replacing the vanilla 3DGS sum-of-vectors metric to prevent gradient cancellation. Choose this when memory efficiency and reducing redundant Gaussians are priorities.

### [Selective Gaussian Densification](18688-selective-gaussian-densification.md)
Integrates occlusion-aware filtering and contribution-based normalization into the StopThePop framework to remove popping artifacts and keep occlusion boundaries intact. Choose this for large-scale scenes with heavy occlusion where standard view-count normalizers fail.

### [Pixel-Level Error Densification]
Uses pixel-level error as a densification criterion and revises opacity settings during cloning to stabilize training [S2, unofficial]. Choose this when gradient-based methods are unstable or insufficient for capturing fine textures.

### Noise-Based Densification
Adds controlled spatial noise ($\mu'_i = \mu_i + \epsilon, \epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$) to Gaussians in under-reconstructed areas to increase coverage and prevent over-clustering [S3]. Choose this for scenes with complex textures where deterministic cloning leads to redundancy.

### Budget-constrained densification
Uses score-based sampling to select Gaussians for densification, controlling Gaussian count, model size, and training time [S4]. Choose this when strict computational or memory budgets are required.

## Background
The concept of Adaptive Density Control (ADC) was introduced with the original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper [Kerbl et al., 2023], which used view-space position gradients to split or clone Gaussians. Subsequent work identified limitations in this approach, such as gradient cancellation (where opposing gradients sum to near zero, preventing split) and over-densification in steady-state regions. [Ye et al.] highlighted gradient collision issues [S4]. Later methods like [Selective Gaussian Densification] replaced the vector sum with a sum of norms [S1, unofficial]. [Occlusion-Aware Densification] integrated with StopThePop to handle occlusion [S1, unofficial]. [Bulò et al.] introduced pixel-level error criteria [S2, unofficial]. [Noise-Based Densification] addressed over-clustering via noise injection [S3]. [Budget-Constrained Densification] focused on resource limits [S4].
