# Training Objective and Densification

Also called: MeshGS Training, Tightly/Loosely-bound Training

## Overview
This method defines the optimization objective and densification strategy for **MeshGS** (Adaptive Mesh-Aligned Gaussian Splatting), which integrates explicit mesh geometry into the 3D Gaussian Splatting pipeline [S1]. It solves the problem of Gaussian primitives drifting away from the underlying surface or expanding excessively, which degrades rendering quality on complex geometry [S1]. It sets itself apart from standard 3DGS by decoupling primitives into **tightly-bound** ($G^t$) and **loosely-bound** ($G^l$) sets, applying mesh-aligned constraints and specialized densification only to the former, while retaining standard 3DGS behavior for the latter [S1].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   A set of 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \{g_1, \dots, g_N\}$, where each Gaussian $g_i$ has position $P_i$, rotation $R_i$, scale $S_i = (s_1, s_2, s_3)$, opacity $\alpha_i$, and spherical harmonic coefficients [S1].
*   A reference mesh $M$ defined by a set of faces $F$, where each face $f_j$ has vertices $v_i$ and a face normal $n_f$ [S1].
*   A distance threshold $d_{th} = 0.01$ to distinguish tightly-bound from loosely-bound splats [S1].
*   A threshold $\rho = 0.1$ for scale regularization [S1].
*   Loss weights: $\lambda_{img} = 0.001$, $\lambda_{nc} = 0.1$, $\lambda_{min} = 0.1$, $\lambda_{max} = 10$, $\lambda_{proj} = 50$ [S1].
*   Training duration: 30,000 iterations [S1].

**Outputs:**
*   Optimized Gaussian parameters $\mathcal{G}$ and the rendered image $\hat{I}_i$ for a given camera view [S1].

### Data Structures
The Gaussian set is logically partitioned into two subsets based on the distance to the mesh surface:
1.  **Tightly-bound splats ($G^t$):** Gaussians within distance $d_{th}$ of the mesh. These are subject to mesh-aligned losses and specialized densification [S1].
2.  **Loosely-bound splats ($G^l$):** Gaussians outside distance $d_{th}$. These follow the original 3DGS densification strategy [S1].

### Steps

1.  **Initialization and Classification:**
    Initialize Gaussians (e.g., from a point cloud or mesh vertices). For each Gaussian $g_i$ with center $P_i$, compute the distance to the mesh surface. If the distance is $\le d_{th}$, classify $g_i \in G^t$; otherwise, $g_i \in G^l$ [S1].

2.  **Rendering:**
    Render the image $\hat{I}_i$ from the current Gaussian set using standard differentiable volume rendering [S1].

3.  **Loss Computation:**
    Compute the total loss $L$ as the sum of image loss, normal consistency loss, scale regularization loss, and projection loss [S1]:
    $$ L = L_{img} + \lambda_{nc} L_{nc} + L_{scale} + \lambda_{proj} L_{proj} $$

    *   **Image Loss ($L_{img}$):**
        Combines L1 loss and D-SSIM between the rendered image $\hat{I}_i$ and ground truth $I_i$ [S1]:
        $$ L_{img} = (1 - \lambda_{img}) L_1(\hat{I}_i, I_i) + \lambda_{img} SSIM(\hat{I}_i, I_i) $$

    *   **Normal Consistency Loss ($L_{nc}$):**
        Encourages tightly-bound splats to align their normals with the mesh face normals [S1].
        1.  Estimate the normal $n_i$ for each tightly-bound Gaussian $g_i \in G^t$. Identify the minimum scale axis among $S_i = (s_1, s_2, s_3)$. Apply the rotation matrix $R_i$ to this axis to transform it into world coordinates [S1].
        2.  Compute the cosine similarity between $n_i$ and the normal $n_f$ of the closest mesh face $f \in F$ [S1]:
            $$ L_{nc} = \sum_{i \in G^t, f \in F} (1 - n_i \cdot n_f) $$

    *   **Scale Regularization Loss ($L_{scale}$):**
        Prevents tightly-bound splats from expanding excessively or becoming too small [S1].
        1.  Let $S_i = (s_1, s_2, s_3)$ be the scale parameters of $g_i \in G^t$ [S1].
        2.  Compute the minimum and maximum scale values: $\min(s_1, s_2, s_3)$ and $\max(s_1, s_2, s_3)$ [S1].
        3.  Apply the regularization:
            $$ L_{scale} = \sum_{i \in G^t} \left( \lambda_{min} |\min(s_1, s_2, s_3)| + \lambda_{max} |\max(s_1, s_2, s_3) - \rho| \right) $$

    *   **Projection Loss ($L_{proj}$):**
        Ensures tightly-bound splats remain close to the mesh surface [S1].
        1.  Project the Gaussian center $P_i$ to the closest point $s_i$ on the mesh surface defined by face $f_j$ and vertex $v_i$ [S1]. The projection is calculated as:
            $$ s_i = P_i + ((P_i - v_i) \cdot f_j) \cdot f_j $$
            *(Note: The source formula uses $f_j$ as the projection direction/normal in the dot product, implying $f_j$ is treated as a unit vector in this context, or represents the face normal/edge vector depending on specific mesh representation. The source explicitly states "closest point $s_i$ on the mesh surface defined by face $f_j$ and vertex $v_i$" and gives the formula above [S1].)*
        2.  Compute the distance:
            $$ L_{proj} = \sum_{i \in G^t} \| s_i - P_i \|_2 $$

4.  **Optimization Step:**
    Perform gradient descent on the total loss $L$ to update Gaussian parameters [S1].

5.  **Densification Strategy:**
    Periodically (e.g., every 500 iterations, as implied by standard 3DGS schedules, though exact interval for MeshGS is not explicitly stated beyond "every 500" in the context of mesh updates [S1]), perform densification based on the Gaussian type [S1]:
    *   **For Loosely-bound Splats ($G^l$):** Follow the original 3DGS densification strategy (splitting and cloning based on position and scale gradients) [S1].
    *   **For Tightly-bound Splats ($G^t$):**
        1.  Identify the mesh triangle close to the Gaussian [S1].
        2.  Conduct point sampling from the triangle surface [S1].
        3.  Use the sampled position to initialize new Gaussian splats [S1].
        4.  **Cloning:** Copy additional attributes (opacity, color, rotation) from the parent Gaussian to the new Gaussian [S1].
        5.  **Splitting:** Divide the scale of the new Gaussians by a factor of 1.6 [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
def meshgs_training_step(gaussians, mesh, camera, gt_image, iteration):
    """
    One training step for MeshGS.
    gaussians: List of Gaussian objects with attributes P, R, S, alpha, SH
    mesh: Mesh object with faces F, vertices V, normals
    camera: Camera parameters
    gt_image: Ground truth image
    iteration: Current training iteration number
    """
    
    # 1. Classify Gaussians
    G_t = [] # Tightly-bound
    G_l = [] # Loosely-bound
    d_th = 0.01
    
    for g in gaussians:
        # Find closest point on mesh surface to g.P
        closest_point, closest_face = mesh.get_closest_point(g.P)
        dist = distance(g.P, closest_point)
        
        if dist <= d_th:
            G_t.append(g)
        else:
            G_l.append(g)
            
    # 2. Render Image
    rendered_image = render(gaussians, camera)
    
    # 3. Compute Losses
    # Image Loss
    l1_loss = compute_l1(rendered_image, gt_image)
    ssim_loss = compute_ssim(rendered_image, gt_image)
    lambda_img = 0.001
    L_img = (1 - lambda_img) * l1_loss + lambda_img * ssim_loss
    
    # Normal Consistency Loss (for G_t)
    L_nc = 0.0
    lambda_nc = 0.1
    for g in G_t:
        # Estimate normal: min scale axis rotated by R
        min_scale_idx = argmin(g.S) # s1, s2, or s3
        local_normal = unit_vector([0, 0, 0])
        local_normal[min_scale_idx] = 1.0
        world_normal = g.R @ local_normal
        
        # Find closest face normal
        _, closest_face = mesh.get_closest_point(g.P)
        face_normal = closest_face.normal
        
        L_nc += (1 - dot(world_normal, face_normal))
    L_nc *= lambda_nc
    
    # Scale Regularization Loss (for G_t)
    L_scale = 0.0
    lambda_min = 0.1
    lambda_max = 10
    rho = 0.1
    for g in G_t:
        s_min = min(g.S)
        s_max = max(g.S)
        L_scale += lambda_min * abs(s_min) + lambda_max * abs(s_max - rho)
        
    # Projection Loss (for G_t)
    L_proj = 0.0
    lambda_proj = 50
    for g in G_t:
        # Project P to mesh surface
        # Note: Source formula: s_i = P_i + ((P_i - v_i) . f_j) * f_j
        # This implies projecting onto the plane defined by face normal f_j
        # or edge vector. Assuming f_j is the face normal for surface projection.
        _, closest_face = mesh.get_closest_point(g.P)
        v_i = closest_face.vertex # Representative vertex
        f_j = closest_face.normal # Face normal
        
        # Vector from vertex to point
        v_p = g.P - v_i
        # Projection offset
        offset = dot(v_p, f_j) * f_j
        s_i = g.P + offset
        
        L_proj += norm(s_i - g.P)
    L_proj *= lambda_proj
    
    # Total Loss
    L = L_img + L_nc + L_scale + L_proj
    
    # 4. Backpropagate and Update
    L.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    
    # 5. Densification (if periodic)
    if iteration % 500 == 0: # Assumed interval based on source context
        densify(gaussians, G_t, G_l, mesh)
        
    return rendered_image, L

def densify(gaussians, G_t, G_l, mesh):
    """
    Perform densification for tightly and loosely bound splats.
    """
    # Loosely-bound: Standard 3DGS densification
    # (Split/Clone based on gradients, not detailed here as it's standard 3DGS)
    standard_3dgs_densify(G_l)
    
    # Tightly-bound: Mesh-aligned densification
    new_gaussians = []
    for g in G_t:
        # Find close mesh triangle
        triangle = mesh.get_closest_triangle(g.P)
        
        # Sample point from triangle
        sample_pos = sample_triangle(triangle)
        
        # Create new Gaussian
        new_g = Gaussian()
        new_g.P = sample_pos
        
        # Cloning: Copy attributes
        new_g.R = g.R
        new_g.alpha = g.alpha
        new_g.SH = g.SH
        
        # Splitting: Scale divided by 1.6
        # Note: The source says "during splitting, their scale is divided by a factor of 1.6"
        # This implies the new Gaussians created via splitting have smaller scales.
        # If the operation is a split, we might create two new Gaussians.
        # If cloning, we create one.
        # The source distinguishes cloning and splitting.
        # We assume a decision is made to clone or split based on standard 3DGS criteria.
        
        if is_split_candidate(g):
            new_g.S = [s / 1.6 for s in g.S]
            # Create a second copy for split? Standard 3DGS splits into two.
            # Source says "utilize this position to initialize new Gaussian splats"
            # and "during splitting, their scale is divided by a factor of 1.6".
            # It does not explicitly state creating two, but splitting implies duplication.
            # We will create one new Gaussian with reduced scale for now, 
            # assuming the caller handles duplication if needed.
            new_gaussians.append(new_g)
        else:
            # Cloning
            new_gaussians.append(new_g)
            
    gaussians.extend(new_gaussians)
```

## Subtopics

### [Budget-Centered Training](9682-budget-centered-training.md)
Initializes and constrains the primitive count to the "knee" of the scene's capacity floor curve ($N^*$), replacing gradient-based pruning with a redundancy map and treating the budget as a hard constraint.

### [Coarse-to-fine multi-resolution densification](15569-coarse-to-fine-multi-resolution-densification.md)
Dynamically allocates optimization iterations across different image resolutions based on frequency energy density, decoupling global coverage from local refinement.

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** The addition of mesh proximity queries and normal consistency calculations increases the per-iteration cost compared to standard 3DGS. The projection loss requires finding the closest point on the mesh for each tightly-bound Gaussian, which is $O(N \cdot M)$ or $O(N \log M)$ with spatial acceleration structures, where $N$ is the number of Gaussians and $M$ is the number of mesh faces [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the mesh geometry and potentially spatial acceleration structures for mesh queries. The Gaussian set size is managed by the densification strategy, which may lead to a higher initial count if not pruned effectively, but the scale regularization helps control excessive growth [S1].
*   **Performance:** Evaluated on Mip-NeRF360 and Deep Blending datasets. Trained for 30,000 iterations [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Improves rendering quality on complex geometry by preventing Gaussian drift and excessive expansion. Aligns Gaussians with mesh normals for better surface fidelity [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on the quality of the input mesh. The mesh-aligned densification may be less effective in areas with high curvature or if the mesh is not well-aligned with the true surface [S1].
*   **When to use:** When reconstructing scenes with explicit mesh geometry is desired, or when standard 3DGS Gaussians drift away from the surface.
*   **When not to use:** For scenes without a reliable mesh representation, or when computational resources are limited due to the added mesh query overhead [S1].

## Background

MeshGS (Adaptive Mesh-Aligned Gaussian Splatting) was introduced in 2024 by the authors of the paper "MeshGS: Adaptive Mesh-Aligned Gaussian Splatting for High-Quality Rendering" [S1]. It builds upon the original 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) method [S1] and aims to improve geometry reconstruction by integrating explicit mesh constraints. The method distinguishes itself from prior works like SuGaR by using a distance-based threshold to separate tightly-bound and loosely-bound splats, applying mesh-aligned losses and densification only to the former [S1].
