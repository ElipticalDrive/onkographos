# Gaussian Splitting and Pruning

Also called: Deterministic splitting, Splitting Gaussians

## Overview
Gaussian Splitting and Pruning is a deterministic density control method for 3D Gaussian Splatting that modifies the primitive count based on statistical analysis of reconstruction loss and blending weights [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of under-reconstructed or blurry regions by splitting Gaussians with high reconstruction responsibility, while removing those with minimal contribution [S1, unofficial]. Unlike the gradient-based adaptive densification used in standard 3D Gaussian Splatting, this method operates on converged models using a batch of rays to estimate per-Gaussian loss statistics, allowing for iterative detail refinement without relying on view-space positional gradients [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A converged set of 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}$, where each Gaussian $G_i$ has position $\mu_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$ (defined by eigenvalues $\sigma_{1,i}, \sigma_{2,i}, \sigma_{3,i}$ and eigenvectors $\vec{v}_{1,i}, \vec{v}_{2,i}, \vec{v}_{3,i}$), opacity weight $\lambda_i$, and color $c_i$.
*   **Input:** A random batch of rays $\mathcal{R}$ (typically $\approx 5\%$ of the dataset).
*   **Input:** A reconstruction loss function $L(\cdot)$ (e.g., L1 or LPIPS).
*   **Output:** A modified set of Gaussians with updated counts, positions, scales, and attributes.

**Data Structures:**
*   Gaussian attributes are stored in arrays. $\lambda_i$ is the opacity weight. $c_i$ is the color in spherical harmonics or RGB.
*   Covariance $\Sigma_i$ is typically stored in a compact form (e.g., quaternion for rotation and log-scale for dimensions) but decomposed into eigenvalues/vectors for splitting.

**Parameters:**
*   $z_\lambda$: Z-score for pruning threshold. Typical value: $2$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $z_{\bar{l}}$: Z-score for splitting threshold. Typical value: $1$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $\epsilon_w$: Noise scale for log-weights. Typical value: $0.1$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $\epsilon_c$: Noise scale for inverse-sigmoid colors. Typical value: $0.1$ [S1, unofficial].
*   $\sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.8$: Scaling factor for mean shift [S1, unofficial].
*   $\sqrt{1 - 2/\pi} \approx 0.6$: Scaling factor for dominant eigenvalue [S1, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Compute Weight Statistics:**
    Calculate the mean $\mu_\lambda$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\lambda$ of the opacity weights $\lambda_i$ across all Gaussians.
    $$ \mu_\lambda = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i $$
    $$ \sigma_\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (\lambda_i - \mu_\lambda)^2} $$

2.  **Prune Low-Contribution Gaussians:**
    Remove any Gaussian $G_i$ where:
    $$ \lambda_i \leq \mu_\lambda - z_\lambda \sigma_\lambda $$
    [S1, unofficial]

3.  **Estimate Per-Gaussian Loss:**
    For the batch of $M$ rays $\mathcal{R}$:
    a. Render the batch and compute the per-ray loss $L_j$ for each ray $j \in \{1, \dots, M\}$.
    b. For each ray, retrieve the set of blending weights $w_{i,j}$ assigned to each Gaussian $G_i$ during compositing.
    c. Estimate the average loss $\bar{l}_i$ associated with each Gaussian $G_i$ over the batch:
    $$ \bar{l}_i = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^M L_j \cdot w_{i,j} $$
    [S1, unofficial]

4.  **Compute Loss Statistics:**
    Calculate the mean $\mu_{\bar{l}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\bar{l}}$ of the average losses $\bar{l}_i$.
    $$ \mu_{\bar{l}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \bar{l}_i $$
    $$ \sigma_{\bar{l}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (\bar{l}_i - \mu_{\bar{l}})^2} $$

5.  **Split High-Loss Gaussians:**
    Identify Gaussians where $\bar{l}_i \geq \mu_{\bar{l}} + z_{\bar{l}} \sigma_{\bar{l}}$. For each such Gaussian $G_i$:
    a. Compute the dominant eigenvector $\vec{v}_1$ and eigenvalue $\sigma_1$ of the covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$.
    b. Generate two new Gaussians, $G_a$ and $G_b$.
    c. **Positions:** Shift the original mean $\mu_i$ along the dominant eigenvector $\vec{v}_1$ by $\pm \sigma_1 \sqrt{2/\pi}$:
    $$ \mu_{a,b} = \mu_i \pm \sigma_1 \vec{v}_1 \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} $$
    [S1, unofficial]
    d. **Covariance:** Set the covariance matrices for $G_a$ and $G_b$ to be identical, reconstructed from the original eigenvectors but with the dominant eigenvalue scaled by $\sqrt{1 - 2/\pi}$:
    $$ \sigma_{1_{a,b}} = \sigma_1 \sqrt{1 - \frac{2}{\pi}} $$
    The other eigenvalues $\sigma_{2,i}, \sigma_{3,i}$ remain unchanged.
    [S1, unofficial]
    e. **Attributes:** Replicate the original weight $\lambda$ and color $c_i$, adding noise in the unconstrained parameterization space:
    $$ \log(\lambda_{a,b}) = \log(\lambda_i) + \mathcal{N}(0, \epsilon_w) $$
    $$ \sigma^{-1}(c_{a,b}) = \sigma^{-1}(c_i) + \mathcal{N}(0, \epsilon_c) $$
    where $\mathcal{N}(0, \epsilon)$ denotes Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\epsilon$.
    [S1, unofficial]
    f. Remove the original Gaussian $G_i$.

6.  **Re-optimize:**
    Repeat the optimization process (gradient descent) on the modified set of Gaussians [S1, unofficial].

    # Step 4: Compute Loss Statistics
    mu_lbar = np.mean(l_bars)
    sigma_lbar = np.std(l_bars)
    
    # Step 5: Split High-Loss Gaussians

            # Get dominant eigenvector and eigenvalue

            # Eigenvalues are sorted ascending; dominant is the last one
            idx = np.argmax(eigenvalues)
            sigma_1 = eigenvalues[idx]
            v_1 = eigenvectors[:, idx]
            
            # Create two new Gaussians
            # Position shift
            shift = sigma_1 * v_1 * np.sqrt(2 / np.pi)
            
            # New mean
            mu_a = g.mu + shift
            mu_b = g.mu - shift
            
            # New covariance: scale dominant eigenvalue
            new_eigenvalues = eigenvalues.copy()
            new_eigenvalues[idx] = sigma_1 * np.sqrt(1 - 2 / np.pi)
            cov_a = eigenvectors @ np.diag(new_eigenvalues) @ eigenvectors.T
            cov_b = cov_a.copy()
            
            # New attributes with noise
            log_lambda_a = np.log(g.lambda_val) + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_w)
            log_lambda_b = np.log(g.lambda_val) + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_w)

            c_b = sigmoid(inv_sigmoid_c + np.random.normal(0, epsilon_c))

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Degenerate Covariance:** If a Gaussian has a near-zero dominant eigenvalue, the split shift becomes negligible. Guard against division by zero or numerical instability when computing eigenvectors.
*   **Coupled Gradients:** Noise must be added in the unconstrained parameterization space (log-weights, inverse-sigmoid colors) to prevent coupled gradients during subsequent optimization [S1, unofficial].
*   **Batch Size:** The loss estimation relies on a random batch. If the batch is too small, the statistics $\mu_{\bar{l}}$ and $\sigma_{\bar{l}}$ may be noisy, leading to incorrect splitting/pruning decisions [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Spatial Splitting with Error Moments
A densification technique that splits Gaussians with error above a threshold $\tau_{space}$ by placing a splitting plane through the Gaussian's center, oriented based on the error moments rather than the Gaussian's longest axis [S2]. Unlike FCC* 24 and DDL* 25b which use a plane perpendicular to the Gaussian's longest axis, this method uses the error moments to determine orientation to avoid Gaussians becoming too small too quickly [S2]. The splitting plane normal $\mathbf{n}$ is calculated as the normalized product of the inverse error covariance matrix $\mathbf{S}_g^c$ and the first moment vector $\mathbf{r}_g$:
$$ \mathbf{n} = \frac{(\mathbf{S}_g^c)^{-1} \mathbf{r}_g}{\left\| (\mathbf{S}_g^c)^{-1} \mathbf{r}_g \right\|} $$
[S2]

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method requires an additional forward pass (rendering a batch of rays) to compute losses and weights, followed by a statistical analysis pass over all Gaussians. Splitting increases the number of Gaussians, which increases the cost of subsequent rendering and optimization steps.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage increases as Gaussians are split. Pruning reduces memory usage for low-contribution Gaussians. The batch of rays requires temporary storage for losses and weights.
*   **Strengths:** Deterministic and based on actual reconstruction error rather than heuristic gradients. Allows for iterative refinement of converged models. Effective at resolving blurry regions where gradients might be small or misleading.
*   **Limitations:** Requires rendering a batch of rays, which adds overhead. Sensitive to the choice of z-scores and batch size. May not adapt as quickly to dynamic scenes as gradient-based methods if not re-run frequently.
*   **When to use:** When high-fidelity detail is required in static scenes, or when gradient-based methods fail to capture fine details due to smooth color transitions or sparse views.
*   **When not to use:** In real-time applications where the overhead of batch rendering and statistical analysis is prohibitive. In highly dynamic scenes where the model does not converge between splits.

## Background
Gaussian Splitting and Pruning was introduced as a deterministic alternative to the heuristic splitting methods in the original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper [S1, unofficial]. It leverages statistical criteria based on reconstruction loss and blending weights to control density. The method is detailed in "Flexible Techniques for Differentiable Rendering with 3D Gaussians" [S1, unofficial]. Spatial Splitting with Error Moments is a variant proposed in "Adaptive Spatio‐Temporal 3D Gaussian Splatting for Scenes with Oscillatory Motion" [S2].
