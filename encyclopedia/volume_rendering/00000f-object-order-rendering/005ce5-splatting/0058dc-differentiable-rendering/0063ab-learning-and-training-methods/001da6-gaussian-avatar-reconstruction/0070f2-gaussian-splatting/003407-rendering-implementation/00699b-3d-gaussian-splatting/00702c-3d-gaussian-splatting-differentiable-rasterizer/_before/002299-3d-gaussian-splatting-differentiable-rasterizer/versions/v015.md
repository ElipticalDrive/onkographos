# 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer

Also called: 3D Gaussian Rasterization and Rendering, 3DGS Renderer, 3DGS rasterization pipeline, Differentiable Gaussian Rasterization, differentiable splatting

## Overview
The 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer renders a scene represented by a set of 3D Gaussian primitives into a 2D image by projecting them into screen space, sorting them by depth, and alpha-compositing their contributions [S2, S3][S12, unofficial]. It solves the problem of efficiently generating differentiable views of a 3D Gaussian field, enabling gradient-based optimization of scene parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) via backpropagation through the rendering pipeline [S3][S12, unofficial]. Unlike NeRF’s volumetric ray marching, which queries a continuous neural field along rays, this method uses discrete point-based alpha blending, estimating opacity by evaluating 2D Gaussians via rasterization rather than integral volume rendering [S7]. It differs from its siblings [CoR-GS](1486-cor-gs.md) and [EffGS](3365-effgs.md) by relying on the standard EWA splatting formulation with linearized projection, whereas variants like [3D Gaussian Unscented Transform](9154-3d-gaussian-unscented-transform-3dgut.md) replace this with sigma-point approximations to handle non-linear camera distortions [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:**
    *   `means3D`: 3D positions of $N$ Gaussians, shape $(N, 3)$ [S11, unofficial].
    *   `cov3D_precomp` (or `scales`/`rotations`): 3D covariance matrices, shape $(N, 3, 3)$, or decomposed scale $(N, 3)$ and rotation quaternions $(N, 4)$ [S3][S6, unofficial].
    *   `colors_precomp` (or `shs`): Precomputed RGB colors $(N, 3)$ or Spherical Harmonics coefficients [S6, S9, unofficial].
    *   `opacities`: Opacity values $o \in \mathbb{R}$, shape $(N,)$ [S11, unofficial].
    *   `viewmatrix`: $4 \times 4$ view matrix [S6, unofficial].
    *   `projmatrix`: $4 \times 4$ projection matrix [S6, unofficial].
    *   `tanfovx`, `tanfovy`: Tangents of half horizontal and vertical field of view [S9, unofficial].
    *   `bg`: Background color tensor, shape $(3)$, on GPU [S6, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   `rendered_image`: RGB image, shape $(H, W, 3)$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   `rendered_opacity`: Alpha mask, shape $(H, W)$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   `radii`: Radius of each Gaussian in pixels, shape $(N)$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   `num_rendered`: Number of visible Gaussians [S6, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **GaussianRasterizationSettings:** A configuration struct containing `image_height`, `image_width`, `tanfovx`, `tanfovy`, `cx`, `cy`, `bg`, `scale_modifier`, `viewmatrix`, `projmatrix`, `sh_degree`, `campos`, `prefiltered`, `backward_geometry`, `computer_pseudo_normal`, `debug` [S6, S9, unofficial].
*   **Context (for Autograd):** Stores `xys` (2D means), `conics` (inverse 2D covariance), `colors`, `opacity`, `background`, `cov2d`, `camDepths`, `finalTs` (transmittance), `px2gid` (pixel-to-Gaussian ID map, int64_t), `imgWidth`, `imgHeight`, `errorMap`, `edgeMap`, `densificationInfo`, `xyAbsGrad` [S5, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Projection:** Project 3D Gaussians to 2D screen space.
    *   Compute 2D mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D}$ using perspective projection:
        $$ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D, x} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3D, x} \cdot f_x}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3D, z}} + c_x, \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D, y} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3D, y} \cdot f_y}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3D, z}} + c_y $$
        where $f_x, f_y$ are focal lengths derived from `tanfovx`, `tanfovy` and image dimensions, and $c_x, c_y$ are principal points [S10, unofficial].
    *   Compute 2D covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}$ by linearizing the projection. Let $J$ be the Jacobian of the projection transformation at $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3D}$:
        $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D} = J \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{3D} J^\top $$
        The Jacobian $J$ is approximated using the first-order Taylor expansion at pose $P$: $J = \frac{1}{d} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -\tilde{\mu}_x/d \\ 0 & 1 & -\tilde{\mu}_y/d \end{bmatrix}$, where $d$ is depth and $\tilde{\mu}$ are camera-space coordinates [S11, unofficial].
    *   Compute the conic matrix (inverse covariance) $\boldsymbol{C} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1}$ [S5, unofficial].

2.  **Bounding Box and Binning:**
    *   Compute the axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) for each Gaussian based on the 99th percentile ellipse (3 standard deviations) of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}$ [S11, unofficial].
    *   Bin Gaussians into $16 \times 16$ tiles if their AABB intersects the tile [S11, unofficial].

3.  **Sorting:**
    *   Sort Gaussians within each tile by depth (front-to-back) using a tile-sorting algorithm [S11, unofficial]. Global sorting by depth is also performed to ensure correct compositing order across tiles [S10, unofficial].

4.  **Rasterization (Alpha Compositing):**
    *   For each pixel, iterate through the sorted Gaussians overlapping that pixel.
    *   Compute the displacement vector $\boldsymbol{d}$ from the pixel center to the Gaussian mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D}$ [S10, unofficial].
    *   Compute the exponent for the 2D Gaussian density:
        $$ \text{power} = -0.5 \left( d_x^2 C_{00} + d_y^2 C_{11} + 2 d_x d_y C_{01} \right) $$
        where $C_{ij}$ are elements of $\boldsymbol{C}$ [S10, unofficial].
    *   Compute the blending coefficient $\alpha_i$:
        $$ \alpha_i = o_i \cdot \exp(\text{power}) $$
        Clamp $\alpha_i$ to a maximum of 0.99 to prevent numerical instability [S10, unofficial].
    *   Compute transmittance $T_i$:
        $$ T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad T_1 = 1 $$
        [S11, unofficial].
    *   Update pixel color $\hat{C}$ and transmittance $T$:
        $$ \hat{C} = \sum_{i \in N} c_i \alpha_i T_i $$
        $$ T_{new} = T_{old} \cdot (1 - \alpha_i) $$
        [S3][S10, unofficial].

5.  **Backpropagation:**
    *   Compute gradients of the loss with respect to the output image.
    *   Propagate gradients back through the alpha-compositing loop to compute gradients for `xys`, `conics`, `colors`, and `opacity` [S5, unofficial].
    *   The gradients for 3D means are computed by chaining the Jacobian of the projection with the screen-space mean gradients [S12, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch

def rasterize_gaussians(means3D, cov3D_precomp, colors_precomp, opacities, 
                        viewmatrix, projmatrix, tanfovx, tanfovy, bg, 
                        image_height, image_width, sh_degree=3, campos=None):
    """
    Differentiable rasterization of 3D Gaussians.
    """
    # 1. Projection
    # Transform 3D means to camera space
    means_cam = torch.matmul(viewmatrix[:3, :3], means3D.t()).t() + viewmatrix[:3, 3]
    
    # Perspective projection to 2D
    # f_x, f_y derived from tanfovx, tanfovy and image dimensions
    f_x = image_width / (2 * tanfovx)
    f_y = image_height / (2 * tanfovy)
    cx = image_width / 2
    cy = image_height / 2
    
    means2D_x = (means_cam[:, 0] * f_x / means_cam[:, 2]) + cx
    means2D_y = (means_cam[:, 1] * f_y / means_cam[:, 2]) + cy
    means2D = torch.stack([means2D_x, means2D_y], dim=1)
    
    # Project covariance
    # J is the Jacobian of the projection
    # Simplified Jacobian for perspective projection
    d = means_cam[:, 2]
    J = torch.zeros((means3D.shape[0], 2, 3), device=means3D.device)
    J[:, 0, 0] = f_x / d
    J[:, 1, 1] = f_y / d
    J[:, 0, 2] = -means_cam[:, 0] * f_x / (d**2)
    J[:, 1, 2] = -means_cam[:, 1] * f_y / (d**2)
    
    # Sigma_2D = J * Sigma_3D * J^T
    cov2D = torch.bmm(torch.bmm(J, cov3D_precomp), J.transpose(1, 2))
    
    # Compute conic (inverse covariance)
    # Add small epsilon for stability
    eps = 1e-6
    det = cov2D[:, 0, 0] * cov2D[:, 1, 1] - cov2D[:, 0, 1] * cov2D[:, 1, 0]
    conic = torch.zeros((means3D.shape[0], 2, 2), device=means3D.device)
    conic[:, 0, 0] = cov2D[:, 1, 1] / det
    conic[:, 1, 1] = cov2D[:, 0, 0] / det
    conic[:, 0, 1] = -cov2D[:, 0, 1] / det
    conic[:, 1, 0] = -cov2D[:, 1, 0] / det
    
    # 2. Bounding Box and Binning (Simplified: assume all Gaussians are visible for this example)
    # In practice, compute AABB and bin into 16x16 tiles
    
    # 3. Sorting (Simplified: assume sorted by depth)
    # Sort by z-depth (means_cam[:, 2])
    sorted_indices = torch.argsort(means_cam[:, 2], descending=True)
    
    # 4. Rasterization
    # Initialize output buffers
    out_img = torch.zeros((image_height, image_width, 3), device=means3D.device)
    out_alpha = torch.zeros((image_height, image_width), device=means3D.device)
    transmittance = torch.ones((image_height, image_width), device=means3D.device)
    
    # Iterate over Gaussians (front-to-back)
    for i in sorted_indices:
        # Get Gaussian parameters
        mu = means2D[i]
        c = conic[i]
        color = colors_precomp[i]
        opacity = opacities[i]
        
        # Compute bounding box for this Gaussian (3 sigma)
        # For simplicity, we iterate over a grid around the mean
        # In production, this is done via tile-based iteration
        
        # Compute displacement for all pixels (vectorized)
        # This is a simplified dense computation for clarity
        y_coords, x_coords = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(image_height), torch.arange(image_width), indexing='ij')
        d_x = x_coords - mu[0]
        d_y = y_coords - mu[1]
        
        # Compute exponent
        power = -0.5 * (d_x**2 * c[0, 0] + d_y**2 * c[1, 1] + 2 * d_x * d_y * c[0, 1])
        
        # Compute alpha
        alpha = torch.clamp(torch.exp(power) * opacity, max=0.99)
        
        # Update image and transmittance
        # Only update pixels where alpha > 0 (optimization: use mask)
        mask = alpha > 0
        out_img[mask] += color * alpha[mask].unsqueeze(-1) * transmittance[mask].unsqueeze(-1)
        transmittance[mask] *= (1 - alpha[mask])
        out_alpha[mask] += alpha[mask] * transmittance[mask] # Note: transmittance here is before update
        
    # Final alpha is 1 - transmittance
    out_alpha = 1 - transmittance
    
    # Add background
    out_img = out_img + bg.unsqueeze(-1).unsqueeze(-1) * (1 - out_alpha).unsqueeze(-1)
    
    return out_img, out_alpha
```

## Subtopics

### [Gaussian Splat Rasterization (Vertex Shader)](3253-gaussian-splat-rasterization-vertex-shader.md)
Implements the vertex shader stage of a GPU-based rasterizer for 3D Gaussian Splatting, transforming 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D screen-space ellipses for instanced rendering. It differs from fragment-based or compute-shader approaches by performing all geometric projection, covariance transformation, and ellipse basis computation within the vertex shader, passing only the final vertex positions and attributes to the rasterizer. It builds on [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) by handling the specific data unpacking and frustum culling required for efficient vertex-driven splatting.

### [Opacity Rendering with SH Colors](7480-opacity-rendering-with-sh-colors.md)
Performs a rendering pass that uses the model's actual opacity values and precomputes colors from Spherical Harmonics (SH) in Python before rasterization, allowing the rasterizer to use precomputed colors. It differs from rasterizers with built-in SH evaluation by deferring directional color computation to the Python host layer rather than evaluating SH inside the GPU kernel. The source code initializes the color tensor to ones, indicating that actual SH evaluation is expected to be performed externally or as a simplification in the provided snippet.

### [3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT)](9154-3d-gaussian-unscented-transform-3dgut.md)
3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT) is a rasterization-based rendering technique for 3D Gaussian particles that replaces the standard Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) splatting formulation with the Unscented Transform (UT). It approximates 3D Gaussian particles using a set of sigma points to enable exact projection under arbitrary nonlinear camera projections, such as those with distortion or rolling shutter effects, while retaining the efficiency of rasterization. Unlike its sibling [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md), which relies on linearization via Jacobians, 3DGUT avoids Jacobian derivation and approximation errors by approximating the particle itself. This alignment with ray-tracing formulations also supports secondary rays for phenomena like reflections and refraction.

### [RoofGS Quadratic Form Expansion and Coefficient Folding](10258-roofgs-quadratic-form-expansion-and-coefficient-folding.md)
RoofGS Quadratic Form Expansion and Coefficient Folding is a rasterization optimization that expands the Gaussian quadratic form to separate pixel-dependent terms from Gaussian-dependent terms, allowing pixel coordinates to be hoisted out of the primitive loop and reducing instruction overhead in the innermost rendering loop. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization by pre-computing and folding opacity and scaling factors into the quadratic coefficients, enabling a streamlined FMA (Fused Multiply-Add) sequence for exponent evaluation.

### [Transmittance of Gaussian Splatting](10263-transmittance-of-gaussian-splatting.md)
This method establishes that the image formation model of Gaussian Splatting (alpha-blending) is equivalent to a ray-marching volumetric rendering model under assumptions of statistical independence and non-overlapping Gaussian primitives. It defines a continuous transmittance function and a view-dependent attenuation coefficient that yield the same color output as the standard discrete alpha-compositing rasterizer.

### [Rendering Pipeline and Configuration](11406-rendering-pipeline-and-configuration.md)
The Rendering Pipeline and Configuration family manages the translation of high-level scene state (3D Gaussians, camera matrices) into the specific numerical formats and validation checks required by the underlying CUDA rasterization backend. It solves the problem of undefined behavior, CUDA kernel crashes, and incorrect gradient flow caused by inconsistent tensor states, mismatched devices, or ambiguous input representations (e.g., providing both precomputed colors and Spherical Harmonics coefficients). It sets itself apart from the actual rasterization logic by focusing strictly on pre-computation checks, data normalization, and camera parameter extraction rather than the pixel-wise projection and blending operations.

### [Differentiable Backpropagation Mechanisms](11407-differentiable-backpropagation-mechanisms.md)
This family defines the mathematical and algorithmic mechanisms for computing gradients of the rendering loss with respect to 3D Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) during the training of Gaussian Splatting models [G]. It solves the problem of enabling end-to-end optimization of scene representations by providing differentiable paths from the final pixel color to the underlying 3D attributes [G]. It differs from standard rasterization by explicitly deriving chain-rule gradients for alpha compositing and basis function evaluations, and from non-differentiable methods by supporting arbitrary gradient estimation strategies including per-splat parallelization and stochastic estimation [G].

### [Multi-view Mini-batch Rasterization](13386-multi-view-mini-batch-rasterization.md)
Multi-view mini-batch rasterization is a training strategy that renders multiple viewpoints simultaneously within a single mini-batch to reduce gradient variance and improve convergence speed. It modifies the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) rasterizer pipeline by introducing view-specific frustum culling and a composite sorting ID, allowing the rasterizer to process multiple camera views in parallel while significantly reducing memory consumption compared to naive multi-view rendering. Unlike single-view training, this method increases performance metrics (PSNR/SSIM) but introduces higher memory overhead and preprocessing time.

### [Rasterization Variant](17388-rasterization-variant.md)
This family defines the specific algorithmic variations of the differentiable rasterization pipeline used to render 3D Gaussians, extending the standard alpha-compositing logic of [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md). It addresses specialized requirements such as dynamic pruning gradients, X-ray physics, hybrid sigma fallbacks, and importance scoring, which the base rasterizer does not natively support.

### Gaussian Rasterization Kernel Fusion
Skips the concatenation of the two SH coefficient buffers (view-independent and view-dependent bands) and passes these buffers separately to the rasterization backend, fusing the concatenation into the rasterization kernel [S13, unofficial]. Fuses the activation functions for the scales, rotations, and opacities of the Gaussians into the rasterizer to avoid PyTorch-related overhead [S13, unofficial]. Fuses the gradient computations required for the added benefits during training [S13, unofficial].

### Isotropic Loss
A regularisation term applied during RGB-D optimization to prevent Gaussians from over-stretching along the viewing axis, particularly in textureless regions [S14, unofficial]. Standard rasterisation does not account for elongation along the viewing axis, causing Gaussians to over-stretch without isotropic regularisation [S14, unofficial]. On TUM RGB-D, ATE RMSE changes from 1.43 cm (w/o $E_{iso}$) to 1.47 cm (Ours). On Replica RGB-D, Ours achieves 2.25 cm average ATE RMSE [S14, unofficial]. On TUM RGB-D, isotropic regularisation yields only marginal performance difference. On Replica RGB-D, it significantly improves camera tracking [S14, unofficial].

### Gaussian Normal Estimation
Deriving per-pixel normal estimates directly from the geometry of Gaussians during backpropagation, where adjustments to scale and rotation parameters update normal estimates without additional learnable parameters [S3]. Transforms normals into camera space using the current camera transform and alpha-composites them according to the rendering equation to provide a single per-pixel normal estimate: $\hat{\boldsymbol{N}} = \sum_{i \in N} \hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_i \alpha_i T_i$ [S3]. Incorrect normal orientation is handled by flipping the direction of normals at the beginning of training if the dot product between the current camera viewing direction and the Gaussian normal is negative [S3].

### Normal Prediction from Gaussians
Approximating the normal direction of a Gaussian using the axis with the minimum scaling coefficient, assuming Gaussians become flat, disc-like during optimization [S3]. Geometric normal for Gaussian $i$: $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_i = R \cdot \text{OneHot}(\arg\min(s_1, s_2, s_3))$, where $R \in SO(3)$ is the rotation matrix from quaternion $q$, and $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are scaling coefficients [S3]. Rotation matrix $R$ from quaternion $q=(w,x,y,z)^\top$: $R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 2(y^2+z^2) & 2(xy-wz) & 2(xz+wy) \\ 2(xy+wz) & 1 - 2(x^2+z^2) & 2(yz-wx) \\ 2(xz-wy) & 2(yz+wx) & 1 - 2(x^2+y^2) \end{bmatrix}$ [S3].

### Parallel N-Dimensional Gaussian Rasterization
A point-based alpha-blending technique for rasterizing feature maps alongside RGB, maintaining matching sizes for both to mitigate inconsistent spatial resolution inherent in tile-based rasterization. Dimensions are flexible to adapt to various foundational models [S15]. Rasterize features using ParallelRasterizer with inputs X, C, Σ, A, F, V to produce rendered feature map Fs [S15]. If refinement step, iterate over Gaussians (x, q, c, α, f): if opacity α < ε or Gaussian is too large, remove it [S15]. Learning rate for feature rendering optimization using Adam optimizer [S15]. Learning rate for the Speed-up Module feature decoder network using Adam optimizer [S15]. Image resolution strategy: render at 4 times lower resolution and upsample twice after 250 and 500 iterations [S15]. Standard exponential decay scheduling used similar to reference [11] [S15].

### Simplified Gaussian Rasterization
Rasterizes Gaussians to compute accumulated weights and max counts without full color/SH evaluation [S16, unofficial]. Pack args (omits `flag_max_count`, includes `culling`) [S16, unofficial]. Call `_C.rasterize_gaussians_simp(*args)` [S16, unofficial]. Return `color, radii, accum_weights_ptr, accum_weights_count, accum_max_count` [S16, unofficial].

### Depth-aware Gaussian Rasterization
Rasterizes Gaussians while computing depth, discriminants, and group indices for depth-based rendering [S16, unofficial]. Pack args (omits `flag_max_count`, includes `culling`) [S16, unofficial]. Call `_C.rasterize_gaussians_depth(*args)` [S16, unofficial]. Return dict with keys: render, out_pts, rendered_depth, discriminants, gidx, accum_alpha [S16, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot H \cdot W)$ in naive implementation, but optimized to $O(N + H \cdot W)$ with tiled rasterization and sorting, where $N$ is the number of Gaussians, and $H, W$ are image dimensions [S12, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N + H \cdot W)$ for storing Gaussian parameters and intermediate buffers [S12, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Real-time rendering (30+ FPS) on consumer GPUs for scenes with millions of Gaussians [S12, unofficial].
*   **Scaling:** Scales well with the number of Gaussians due to efficient tile-based sorting and rasterization [S12, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High rendering speed, differentiability, explicit scene representation [S2][S12, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Memory intensive for very large scenes, requires careful tuning of Gaussian parameters for optimal quality [S10, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Real-time novel view synthesis, training of 3D Gaussian fields [S2][S12, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** Scenes with complex lighting effects that require volumetric integration, or when memory is severely constrained [S7][S10, unofficial].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 [S12, unofficial]. It represents a scene as a set of differentiable 3D Gaussian primitives, optimizing their parameters to minimize the difference between rendered and ground truth images [S3]. The method builds on earlier work in differentiable rendering and point-based rendering [S12, unofficial]. It is widely used in novel view synthesis, 3D reconstruction, and dynamic scene modeling [S2, S3].
