# 3D Gaussian Splatting

Also called: 3D Gaussian Splatting Pipeline, 3DGS Rendering, 3DGS Rasterization, 3D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization, 3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering Pipeline, Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering, Kerbl et al. pipeline, Original 3DGS

## Overview
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction method that represents a scene as a collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives, each defined by position, covariance, opacity, and view-dependent color [S1][S6, unofficial]. It solves the problem of real-time, high-fidelity rendering by projecting these 3D Gaussians into 2D screen-space ellipses and rasterizing them using a tile-based, differentiable software rasterizer [S6, unofficial][S14, unofficial]. Unlike volumetric ray-marching methods like NeRF, 3DGS uses discrete, point-based splatting, and unlike standard OpenGL rasterization, it computes analytic gradients for the non-linear projection and compositing operations, enabling end-to-end differentiable training [S6, unofficial][S10]. It sets itself apart from its siblings by using a specific tile-based rasterization pipeline with object-level depth sorting and early termination, optimized for GPU memory bandwidth [S1][S10].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ 3D Gaussian primitives. Each primitive $i$ is defined by raw parameters: mean $\mu_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, rotation quaternion $q_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$, scale $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, opacity $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$, and spherical harmonics coefficients $c_i$ (typically degree 3, $\mathbb{R}^{3 \times 16}$). Camera parameters: view matrix $V \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$, projection intrinsics $K$ (focal lengths $f_x, f_y$, principal point $c_x, c_y$), image dimensions $H \times W$, near plane $z_{near}$, and far plane $z_{far}$ [S6, unofficial][S11, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:** An RGB image $C \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$, optionally with depth, normals, or alpha channels [S11, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
The scene is stored as a list of Gaussian attributes. A typical layout for a Gaussian $i$ includes:
*   `xyz`: `float32[N, 3]` (World space mean) [S12, unofficial].
*   `rot`: `float32[N, 4]` (Quaternion, unnormalized) [S12, unofficial].
*   `scale`: `float32[N, 3]` (Log-scale, unexponentiated) [S12, unofficial].
*   `opacity`: `float32[N, 1]` (Logit, un-sigmoided) [S12, unofficial].
*   `sh`: `float32[N, SH_dim]` (Spherical Harmonics coefficients) [S12, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Activation and Covariance Construction:**
    *   Normalize rotations: $q_{norm} = q / \|q\|$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   Exponentiate scales to ensure positivity: $s_{eff} = \exp(s)$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   Construct the 3D covariance matrix from the rotation matrix $R$ (derived from $q_{norm}$) and scaling matrix $S = \text{diag}(s_{eff})$:
        $$ \Sigma_{3D} = R S S^T R^T $$
        [S6, unofficial][S7].

2.  **Camera Space Transformation:**
    *   Transform means to camera space: $\mu' = V \mu$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   Transform covariance to camera space using the upper-left $3 \times 3$ submatrix of the view matrix $V$ (denoted $V_{1:3, 1:3}$):
        $$ \Sigma' = V_{1:3, 1:3} \Sigma_{3D} V_{1:3, 1:3}^T $$
        [S6, unofficial].

3.  **Projection to Screen Space:**
    *   Compute the Jacobian $J$ of the projective projection at $\mu'$:
        $$ J = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{f_x}{\mu'_z} & 0 & -\frac{f_x \mu'_x}{\mu'_z^2} \\ 0 & \frac{f_y}{\mu'_z} & -\frac{f_y \mu'_y}{\mu'_z^2} \end{pmatrix} $$
        [S6, unofficial]. Note: The Jacobian contains identically zero terms, allowing hardware to skip zero-multiplications [S10].
    *   Compute the 2D screen-space covariance:
        $$ \Sigma_{2D} = J \Sigma' J^T $$
        [S6, unofficial].
    *   Compute the 2D mean:
        $$ \mu_{2D} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{f_x \mu'_x}{\mu'_z} + c_x \\ \frac{f_y \mu'_y}{\mu'_z} + c_y \end{pmatrix} $$
        [S6, unofficial].

4.  **Culling and Sorting:**
    *   Cull Gaussians if they are behind the near plane ($\mu'_z < z_{near}$) or outside the view frustum [S6, unofficial][S10].
    *   Sort remaining Gaussians by camera-space depth $\mu'_z$ to enable correct front-to-back alpha compositing. This object-level sorting is an approximation to avoid prohibitively slow per-pixel sorting [S6, unofficial][S10].

5.  **Rasterization and Compositing:**
    *   Divide the image into tiles (typically $16 \times 16$ pixels) [S10].
    *   Assign projected 2D Gaussians to tiles based on their bounding box (typically $3\sigma$ extent) [S12, unofficial].
    *   For each pixel, iterate over Gaussians in the assigned tile in depth order.
    *   Compute the alpha contribution at a pixel offset $\mathbf{x}$ from the mean:
        $$ \alpha_{pixel} = \alpha_{eff} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{x}^T \Sigma_{2D}^{-1} \mathbf{x}\right) $$
        where $\alpha_{eff} = \sigma(\alpha)$ is the sigmoided opacity [S6, unofficial].
    *   Accumulate color using front-to-back blending:
        $$ C_{acc} \leftarrow C_{acc} + T \cdot \alpha_{pixel} \cdot c $$
        $$ T \leftarrow T \cdot (1 - \alpha_{pixel}) $$
        [S6, unofficial].
    *   Early termination is applied when transmittance $T$ drops below a threshold $\tau$ (e.g., $1/255$) [S10].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def render_3dgs(gaussians, camera, image_size, tile_size=16):
    """
    gaussians: dict with keys 'xyz' (N,3), 'rot' (N,4), 'scale' (N,3), 
               'opacity' (N,1), 'sh' (N, SH_dim)
    camera: dict with 'view_matrix' (4,4), 'fx', 'fy', 'cx', 'cy', 
            'width', 'height', 'near_plane', 'far_plane'
    """
    N = gaussians['xyz'].shape[0]
    H, W = image_size
    C = torch.zeros(H, W, 3)
    T = torch.ones(H, W, 1)
    
    # 1. Activation
    q_norm = gaussians['rot'] / torch.norm(gaussians['rot'], dim=1, keepdim=True)
    s_eff = torch.exp(gaussians['scale'])
    alpha_eff = torch.sigmoid(gaussians['opacity'])
    
    # 2. Covariance Construction
    R = quat_to_rotmat(q_norm) # Helper: converts quaternion to 3x3 rotation matrix
    S = torch.diag_embed(s_eff)
    cov_3d = R @ S @ S.transpose(-1, -2) @ R.transpose(-1, -2)
    
    # 3. Camera Space
    V = camera['view_matrix']
    V_3x3 = V[:3, :3]
    mu_prime = (V_3x3 @ gaussians['xyz'].transpose(0, 1)).transpose(0, 1) + V[:3, 3]
    cov_prime = V_3x3 @ cov_3d @ V_3x3.transpose(-1, -2)
    
    # 4. Projection
    fx, fy, cx, cy = camera['fx'], camera['fy'], camera['cx'], camera['cy']
    mu_z = mu_prime[:, 2:3]
    
    # Jacobian
    J = torch.zeros(N, 2, 3)
    J[:, 0, 0] = fx / mu_z
    J[:, 0, 2] = -fx * mu_prime[:, 0:1] / (mu_z ** 2)
    J[:, 1, 1] = fy / mu_z
    J[:, 1, 2] = -fy * mu_prime[:, 1:2] / (mu_z ** 2)
    
    cov_2d = J @ cov_prime @ J.transpose(-1, -2)
    
    # 2D Mean
    mu_2d_x = fx * mu_prime[:, 0] / mu_z + cx
    mu_2d_y = fy * mu_prime[:, 1] / mu_z + cy
    mu_2d = torch.stack([mu_2d_x, mu_2d_y], dim=1)
    
    # 5. Culling
    valid = (mu_z > camera['near_plane']) & (mu_z < camera['far_plane'])
    # Further frustum culling would go here
    
    # 6. Sorting
    depths = mu_z[valid].squeeze()
    indices = torch.argsort(depths)
    sorted_gaussians = {k: v[valid][indices] for k, v in gaussians.items()}
    sorted_gaussians['mu_2d'] = mu_2d[valid][indices]
    sorted_gaussians['cov_2d'] = cov_2d[valid][indices]
    sorted_gaussians['alpha_eff'] = alpha_eff[valid][indices]
    
    # 7. Rasterization (Simplified per-pixel for clarity, usually tile-based)
    # In practice, this is done via tile-based accumulation in CUDA
    for i in range(sorted_gaussians['xyz'].shape[0]):

        #   alpha = alpha_eff[i] * exp(-0.5 * x.T @ inv(cov_2d[i]) @ x)

        #   T = T * (1 - alpha)

    return C
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Tile Size:** $16 \times 16$ pixels [S10].
*   **Bounding Box Multiplier:** $3.0$ (representing $3\sigma$ extent) [S12, unofficial].
*   **Near Plane:** $0.01$ [S12, unofficial].
*   **Far Plane:** $100$ (or $1e10$ in some implementations) [S12, unofficial][S11, unofficial].
*   **Early Termination Threshold ($\tau$):** Not given in the sources.
*   **Spherical Harmonics Degree:** Typically 3 [S6, unofficial][S7].
*   **Opacity Learning Rate:** $0.025$ (halved from $0.05$) [S6, unofficial].
*   **Training Iterations:** $30,000$ [S6, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Covariance Inversion:** The 2D covariance matrix must be invertible. Degenerate splats (singular covariance) require special handling or regularization [S12, unofficial][S19].
*   **Perspective Approximation:** The Jacobian is a local affine approximation of the projective transformation. This causes perspective distortion artifacts at close distances or for large Gaussians [S16][S19].
*   **Sorting Bottleneck:** Sorting millions of Gaussians is a primary bottleneck, typically handled by CUB device radix sort in CUDA implementations [S14, unofficial].
*   **Memory Usage:** High VRAM usage (4GB to view, 12GB to train) and large disk size (1GB+ for a scene) [S14, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md)
The 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer is a screen-space rendering method that projects a set of 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D ellipses and alpha-composites them to produce an RGB image, while providing exact gradients for all Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) and camera poses. It differs from volumetric ray-marching methods like NeRF by using discrete, point-based splatting and from standard OpenGL rasterization by computing analytic gradients for the non-linear projection and compositing operations, enabling end-to-end differentiable training. It builds on [3D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](2812-3d-gaussian-splatting.md) by implementing the specific forward and backward passes required for optimization.

### [3D Gaussian Splatting MVP](3410-3d-gaussian-splatting-mvp.md)
3D Gaussian Splatting MVP is a self-contained, pure-PyTorch implementation of the core 3D Gaussian Splatting rendering algorithm, designed as a teaching reference rather than a production renderer. It solves the problem of understanding the mathematical foundations of differentiable Gaussian rasterization by implementing the full pipeline—camera projection, covariance transformation, depth sorting, and alpha compositing—using standard tensor operations without custom CUDA kernels. It sets itself apart from production implementations like [3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering (Software)](2805-3d-gaussian-splatting-rendering-software.md) by explicitly skipping tile-based optimization, spherical harmonics, and adaptive density control, favoring readability and explicit Python loops over vectorized GPU performance.

### [Ghost Ellipsoids for Chunked Training](3559-ghost-ellipsoids-for-chunked-training.md)
Ghost Ellipsoids for Chunked Training resolves boundary artifacts in chunked volume training by including ellipsoids from neighboring chunks in the loss computation without updating their parameters, providing boundary context without requiring a global volume or merged model. Unlike chunk-based 3DGS methods that resolve boundary inconsistencies by merging chunks into a global coarse model or through post-training stages, this method requires boundary support during training itself without access to a global volume.

### [2D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](4815-2d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization.md)
This method renders a scene represented by 2D Gaussian primitives (surfels) into RGB, alpha, normal, and depth maps using a custom rasterizer. It differs from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting by operating on surfels projected into screen space, accumulating color and geometric attributes via front-to-back alpha compositing [Parent]. It serves as the Tier-1 NumPy reference implementation for 2D Gaussian Splatting, performing ray-surfel intersection per pixel using the projection matrix.

### [Taming 3DGS](8058-taming-3dgs.md)
Taming 3DGS is a resource-efficiency method for 3D Gaussian Splatting that imposes an explicit cap on the total number of Gaussians to enable high-quality rendering on consumer-grade GPUs. It steers the densification process to spend a fixed budget where it helps most, trading peak quality for faster training and smaller model size. Unlike standard 3DGS which allows unbounded growth, this approach formulates optimization as a mixed-integer problem to maintain a constant Gaussian count.

### [RadSplat](8059-radsplat.md)
RadSplat is a method for initializing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) scenes by leveraging a pre-trained Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) to generate a dense, topologically correct point cloud, which is then converted into Gaussians. It solves the problem of poor initialization quality and topological artifacts inherent in raw Structure-from-Motion (SfM) point clouds, enabling high-quality rendering with unprecedented speed. Unlike feed-forward methods like MVSplat that regress parameters directly, RadSplat uses a hybrid approach that bypasses the per-scene gradient descent loop for initialization.

### [Analytic-Splatting](8084-analytic-splatting.md)
Analytic-Splatting is a rendering method that replaces the point-sampling of 2D Gaussians in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) with an analytical approximation of the integral of the Gaussian signal over the pixel window area to reduce aliasing artifacts during zooming or resolution changes. Unlike [3D Gaussian Splatting Rendering (Software)](2805-3d-gaussian-splatting-rendering-software.md) which treats pixels as isolated points, or [Mip-Splatting](3410-3d-gaussian-splatting-mvp.md) which uses a hybrid filtering mechanism that suppresses high-frequency components, Analytic-Splatting preserves high-quality details by analytically integrating the signal using a conditioned logistic function approximation of the Gaussian CDF.

### [3D Gaussian Ray Tracing](8101-3d-gaussian-ray-tracing.md)
3D Gaussian Ray Tracing is a rendering method that replaces the tile-based rasterization of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting with hardware-accelerated ray tracing to correctly handle semi-transparent Gaussians and complex lighting. Unlike rasterization, which evaluates all Gaussians overlapping a screen tile for every pixel (linear scaling in dense scenes), this method uses a Bounding Volume Hierarchy (BVH) to traverse only the Gaussians intersected by each ray, enabling logarithmic scaling and correct volumetric transparency. It solves the problem of implausible transparency effects and the violation of physical absorption-emission models inherent in standard alpha-blending splatting.

### [Semantic and Language Embeddings](8130-semantic-and-language-embeddings.md)
This family extends 3D Gaussian Splatting [2812-3d-gaussian-splatting.md] by attaching high-dimensional feature vectors to Gaussian primitives, enabling the rendering of semantic, language, or latent feature maps alongside color. It solves the problem of integrating 2D vision-language models (VLMs) into 3D representations, allowing for real-time open-vocabulary semantic segmentation, phrase grounding, and 3D visual question answering. It differs from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting by accumulating feature vectors via differentiable alpha-blending instead of just color, and from sibling methods like [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) by focusing on the semantic attribute representation rather than the rasterization engine itself.

### [Human and Articulated Avatars](8209-human-and-articulated-avatars.md)
This family extends 3D Gaussian Splatting to reconstruct and render animatable avatars of humans and articulated non-human models from multi-view video. It solves the problem of simulating geometrically changing bodies in continuous movements while rendering rich textures, addressing the limitation of standard 3DGS which assumes static scenes. It sets itself apart from static rendering methods by integrating deformation pipelines (such as mesh binding, tetrahedral cages, or neural motion fields) that drive canonical Gaussians into target poses, enabling real-time novel view synthesis of dynamic subjects.

### [AdR-Gaussian](8365-adr-gaussian.md)
AdR-Gaussian is a rendering acceleration method for 3D Gaussian Splatting that optimizes the rasterization pipeline by moving serial culling operations to a parallel preprocessing stage and balancing computational loads across pixel threads. It achieves significant speedups without reducing the total number of Gaussians, thereby avoiding the quality degradation associated with pruning-based acceleration methods.

### [Projection Geometry and Coordinate Systems](9313-projection-geometry-and-coordinate-systems.md)
This family defines the mathematical framework for transforming 3D Gaussian primitives, defined by mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, into 2D distributions suitable for rasterization [G]. It sets apart from standard perspective projection by supporting optimal tangent-plane projections [3293-optimal-gaussian-splatting.md] and specialized geometries such as cone-beam X-ray scanning [3458-3d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization-cone-beam-scanner-space.md].

### [Texture and Appearance Representation](9528-texture-and-appearance-representation.md)
This family extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting primitive representation to model high-frequency spatial details and view-dependent optical effects that static isotropic color vectors (Spherical Harmonics) cannot capture. It replaces or augments the per-Gaussian color attribute with explicit textures, neural networks, or directional basis functions, enabling the reconstruction of complex materials, specularities, and fine surface patterns. Unlike the base [3D Gaussian Splatting](2812-3d-gaussian-splatting.md) method which assumes uniform color per primitive, these methods decouple geometry from appearance or enhance the appearance model with directional sensitivity.

### [Point-Based Ray Tracing for Visibility](10934-point-based-ray-tracing-for-visibility.md)
Point-Based Ray Tracing for Visibility serves as a sparse visibility oracle that evaluates transmittance along sampled light directions using a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) of Gaussian proxies, enabling evaluation from any point in space in any direction. Unlike the camera-tied rasterizer, it decouples visibility estimation from screen-space projection, allowing it to act as a drop-in companion to standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by reusing the same EWA-splatting formulation for consistent blending without requiring training mismatch.

### [Primitive Representation](11838-primitive-representation.md)
Primitive Representation defines the geometric and photometric data structures used to approximate a 3D scene in Gaussian Splatting methods [G]. It solves the problem of representing continuous geometry and appearance with discrete, differentiable primitives that can be efficiently rasterized [G]. It sets itself apart from parent methods by explicitly defining the kernel shape (e.g., isotropic, half-space, or hybrid Bézier) and the associated attribute storage (e.g., opacity, normals, control points) that determine how light interacts with the primitive during rendering [G].

### [Shading and Lighting Models](11924-shading-and-lighting-models.md)
Shading and Lighting Models extend the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization pipeline to support physically based rendering (PBR), self-occlusion, and anti-aliased forward shading [G]. While standard splatting assumes a fixed, uniform lighting environment and simple alpha blending, this family introduces mechanisms for deferred shading pipelines, light-space rasterization for shadows, and analytic integration approximations for multiscale fidelity [Lighting, Shadowing & Relighting](11894-lighting-shadowing-relighting.md), [Analytic-Splatting Forward Shading](3459-analytic-splatting-forward-shading.md), [Transparency and Blending Models](9274-transparency-and-blending-models.md). It differs from sibling methods like [Analytic-Splatting Forward Shading](3459-analytic-splatting-forward-shading.md) by focusing on material attributes and light interaction rather than just pixel-area integration, and from [Transparency and Blending Models](9274-transparency-and-blending-models.md) by explicitly handling light source geometry and PBR material buffers.

### [Reflective Gaussian Splatting](12321-reflective-gaussian-splatting.md)
Reflective Gaussian Splatting (Ref-Gaussian) is a framework for the real-time, high-quality rendering of reflective objects with inter-reflection effects, addressing the limitations of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting in modeling complex surfaces and multi-view inconsistencies. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods like [3D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](2812-3d-gaussian-splatting.md) by utilizing 2D Gaussian primitives for view-consistent geometry and employing physically based deferred rendering with split-sum approximation to stabilize optimization and enable efficient inter-reflection modeling without heavy Monte Carlo sampling.

### [Ray-Splat Intersection Algorithms](13401-ray-splat-intersection-algorithms.md)
Ray-splat intersection algorithms compute the contribution of 3D Gaussians to a pixel by analytically evaluating the Gaussian density along the viewing ray, rather than projecting the 3D Gaussian onto the 2D image plane. This approach solves the problem of numerical instability and geometric inaccuracy that arises when splats become degenerate or when perspective-correct depth estimation is required [3403-perspective-correct-3d-gaussian-splatting-via-pl-cker-coordi.md][5992-gaussian-opacity-fields-gof.md]. Unlike standard rasterization which approximates the 3D Gaussian as a 2D ellipse, these methods treat the Gaussian as a volumetric primitive, enabling accurate depth extraction and surface reconstruction [5992-gaussian-opacity-fields-gof.md].

### [Rasterization Pipeline Optimization](15109-rasterization-pipeline-optimization.md)
Rasterization Pipeline Optimization refers to techniques that accelerate the rendering of 3D Gaussians by improving the efficiency of the rasterization stage, addressing the bottleneck of processing non-intersecting pixels and managing memory bandwidth. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by focusing on the computational pipeline (sorting, hardware acceleration, and culling) rather than the primitive representation or shading models.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering is $O(N \cdot T)$ where $N$ is the number of Gaussians and $T$ is the number of tiles, but effectively linear in the number of active Gaussians per tile due to culling and early termination [S1][S10]. Sorting is $O(N \log N)$ [S14, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ for storing Gaussian attributes. High VRAM usage (4GB to view, 12GB to train) and large disk size (1GB+ for a scene) [S14, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Baseline for AR/VR and desktop GPU rendering; originally targets desktop GPUs but faces memory/bandwidth limits on mobile/AR-VR edge devices [S10].
*   **Strengths:** Real-time rendering speeds (high FPS), high visual fidelity, differentiable training, explicit scene representation.
*   **Limitations:** High memory usage, perspective approximation errors, sorting bottleneck, difficulty in representing complex nonlinear structures (e.g., donuts) without negative Gaussians [S4][S14, unofficial][S16].
*   **When to use:** Real-time novel view synthesis, 3D reconstruction from images, applications requiring differentiable rendering.
*   **When not to use:** Scenes with extreme perspective distortion where affine approximation fails, or when hardware acceleration for ray tracing is preferred for complex transparency [S16][S19].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 [S6, unofficial]. It represents a breakthrough in static scene rendering by representing scenes as collections of 3D Gaussian primitives, enabling real-time rendering rates while maintaining high visual fidelity [S7]. The method was initially released with an implementation in C++/CUDA [S6, unofficial]. It has since been extended to dynamic scenes (4DGS), view-dependent effects (6DGS), and various other domains [S7]. The method is used for novel view synthesis, 3D reconstruction, and has become a baseline for many subsequent works in differentiable rendering [S1][S10].
