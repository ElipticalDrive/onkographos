# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to mitigate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification by minimizing the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which provides small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and fails to distinguish them from well-reconstructed ones, FR increases pixel gradients in these specific areas, enabling more effective adaptive densification [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial domain, explicitly disentangling low-frequency structure from high-frequency detail to guide the optimization process [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, where $H$ and $W$ are image height and width.
**Outputs:** Regularization loss terms $d_a$ (amplitude discrepancy) and $d_p$ (phase discrepancy).

**Data Structures:**
The method operates on 2D image grids. No complex data structures are required beyond standard image buffers and frequency domain representations.

**Steps:**
1.  **Frequency Transformation:** Convert both the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth $I$ into their frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$ using the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). For a single channel, the DFT is computed as:
    $$
    F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)}
    $$
    where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $F(u, v)$ is the complex frequency value [S2]. This step is repeated for each color channel.

2.  **Amplitude and Phase Extraction:** For each frequency component $F(u, v)$, decompose the complex value into amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$:
    $$
    |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2}
    $$
    $$
    \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right)
    $$
    where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$ [S2]. Perform this for both $\hat{F}$ and $F$.

3.  **Discrepancy Calculation:** Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ using the Euclidean metric averaged over all pixels and frequencies:
    $$
    d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2}
    $$
    $$
    d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2}
    $$
    Note: The summation indices in the source formulas [S2] use spatial coordinates $(x,y)$ for the averaging denominator but frequency coordinates $(u,v)$ for the spectral difference. In implementation, the sum is over all frequency bins $(u,v)$ corresponding to the image dimensions [S2].

4.  **Loss Integration:** The total frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of $d_a$ and $d_p$, added to the total training loss. In the base implementation (FreGS), this is applied alongside the standard photometric loss [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
*   **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
*   **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are retained for the forward pass [S2].
*   **Frequency Annealing (Optional Variant):** To prevent bias towards undesirable artifacts and restricted densification, a progressive frequency annealing technique can be used. This incorporates a low-pass filter $H_l$ and a dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ in Fourier space, gradually leveraging low-to-high frequencies to achieve coarse-to-fine densification [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Over-reconstruction Bias:** Naively applying amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can bias the splatting towards undesirable artifacts and restrict Gaussian densification [S2].
*   **Gradient Vanishing:** In regions that are already well-reconstructed, the frequency gradient may become small, similar to L1 loss, if not properly weighted or annealed [S2].
*   **Implementation Note:** The arctangent function should handle quadrant ambiguity correctly (e.g., using `atan2` in standard libraries) to ensure accurate phase calculation [G].

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
The method adds the computational cost of 2D Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) to each training iteration. FFT complexity is $O(N \log N)$ where $N = H \times W$. Memory usage increases by the storage of the frequency domain representations for both rendered and ground truth images, which is $O(N)$ per channel [S2].

**Measured Performance:**
In the FreGS implementation, FR significantly increases pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to the baseline 3D-GS method, as demonstrated in the 'Bicycle' scene [S2]. This leads to superior Gaussian densification in complex areas. Experiments on Mip-NeRF360 and Tank&Temple datasets show that FreGS achieves comparable or better performance to 3D-GS and other NeRF-based methods while maintaining a similar number of Gaussians [S2].

**Scaling:**
The method scales with image resolution due to the FFT cost. It is compatible with the standard 3D-GS training pipeline, including the custom CUDA rasterization kernels [S2].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Effectively identifies and corrects over-reconstruction regions; improves densification quality; disentangles frequency components for better control [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Adds computational overhead per iteration; naive application without annealing can lead to artifacts [S2].

**When to Use:**
Use when the baseline 3D-GS optimization results in over-reconstruction (blurry or redundant Gaussians in simple areas) and under-reconstruction (missing details in complex areas). It is particularly useful when adaptive densification needs to be guided by spectral information rather than just spatial gradients [S2].

**When Not to Use:**
Avoid if computational resources are extremely limited and the overhead of FFT per iteration is prohibitive. Also, if the scene is very simple and does not suffer from over-reconstruction, the benefit may be marginal [S2].

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced in the context of improving 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization, specifically in the work "FreGS: 3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization" [S2]. It addresses the limitations of raw optimization methods that may lead to over-reconstruction in some regions while underrepresenting others, resulting in blur and visual inconsistencies [S1]. The method builds on the observation that pixel-wise L1 loss fails to differentiate between over-reconstructed and well-reconstructed regions, whereas frequency-based metrics can highlight these discrepancies [S2]. It is part of a broader family of regularization techniques including geometry constraints and anchor points [S1].
