# Regularization and Geometry Constraints

Also called: Geometric Regularization, Depth Constraints, Frequency Constraints

## Overview
Regularization and Geometry Constraints are training-time techniques applied to 3D Gaussian Splatting to mitigate over-reconstruction, floating artifacts, and geometric instability by enforcing physical or spectral consistency between rendered outputs and ground truth or priors [S2, S3]. Unlike sibling methods that modify the optimization schedule or learning rates, this family explicitly constrains the Gaussian primitives' geometry (position, scale, orientation) or the frequency content of the rendered images to ensure the resulting radiance field represents a valid surface rather than an unstructured density cloud [S3]. It sets itself apart from [Physical Parameter Optimization](4111-physical-parameter-optimization.md) by focusing on geometric fidelity and artifact reduction rather than physical plausibility of material properties, and from [Densification Strategy](6234-densification-strategy.md) by acting as a constraint on existing primitives rather than a mechanism for generating new ones.

## Shared principles
The shared mechanism across all methods in this family is the addition of a regularization term $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ to the total training loss $\mathcal{L}_{total}$, which penalizes deviations from a desired geometric or spectral property. The total loss is typically defined as:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photometric} + \lambda_{reg} \mathcal{L}_{reg} $$
where $\mathcal{L}_{photometric}$ is the standard rendering loss (e.g., L1 or LPIPS) and $\lambda_{reg}$ is a weighting factor [S1, S2, S3].

The core principle is that photometric loss alone is insufficient for recovering high-quality geometry, often leading to "floaters" (Gaussians in empty space) or blurred surfaces [S3]. Regularization methods fall into two broad categories:
1.  **Geometric Constraints:** These enforce consistency with 3D structure, such as depth maps, surface normals, or covariance shapes. They often involve rendering auxiliary outputs (depth, normals) and computing losses against priors or self-consistency checks [S1, S2, S3].
2.  **Spectral/Feature Constraints:** These enforce consistency in the frequency domain or feature space, ensuring that the rendered image matches the ground truth in terms of high-frequency details or semantic features, thereby preventing overfitting to noise [S2, S3].

A common implementation detail is the decoupling of optimization variables. For instance, when applying depth constraints, scale and rotation parameters are often frozen to prevent the primitive geometry from deforming to fit smooth depth estimates, which would conflict with complex color appearance [S1]. Similarly, frequency regularization operates on the 2D image domain, affecting gradients for all parameters but specifically targeting regions where standard L1 loss fails to provide sufficient gradient signal [S2].

## Subtopics

### [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md)
Frequency Regularization (FR) mitigates over-reconstruction by minimizing the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth. It operates in the Fourier domain, disentangling frequency components to increase pixel gradients in over-reconstructed regions where naive L1 loss yields small gradients. It sets itself apart from [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial domain, and from [FreGS](9212-fregs.md) by serving as the foundational mechanism that FreGS extends with progressive annealing.

### [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md)
Smoothed 2D Supervision enhances optimization robustness by applying explicit spatial blurring to the 2D supervision signal. It addresses instability in camera alignment and overfitting to high-frequency noise during early training stages. It sets itself apart from [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by applying explicit spatial blurring to the target image rather than adjusting positional encoding frequencies, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by operating in the 2D image domain rather than enforcing geometric depth constraints.

### [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md)
This method modifies the covariance matrix of 3D Gaussian primitives to enforce a "half-Gaussian" shape, creating half-ellipsoids that better approximate surface boundaries and reduce volume artifacts in empty space. It solves the problem of Gaussians extending unnaturally into free space. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by explicitly altering the primitive's geometric shape via covariance transformation rather than relying solely on external depth supervision or spectral constraints.

### [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md)
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by encouraging spatial reshaping (movement of centers) rather than deforming Gaussian shapes to fit smooth monocular depth estimates. It sets itself apart from standard depth regularization methods by freezing the scale and rotation parameters of the Gaussians during the regularization step, thereby avoiding the conflict where primitive geometry is forced to conform to smooth depth at the expense of complex color appearance. It builds on [Regularization and Geometry Constraints](6269-regularization-and-geometry-constraints.md) by decoupling the optimization of position and opacity from shape deformation.

### [FreGS](9212-fregs.md)
FreGS is a training-time regularization method that prevents overfitting and high-frequency artifacts by enforcing spectral consistency between rendered and ground-truth images. It addresses "over-reconstruction" by operating directly in the frequency domain. Unlike sibling methods that modify primitive geometry or use spatial gradients, FreGS focuses on stabilizing the optimization landscape by penalizing frequency discrepancies.

### [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md)
Cross-scale geometric adaptation is a self-adaptive regularization technique that uses reprojection errors across multiple voxel scales to guide geometry learning without complex scheduling or explicit depth ground truth. It selects render depths with minimal reprojection error across different scales as pseudo labels, ensuring geometric integrity and significantly reducing floaters compared to training without geometric adaptation. Unlike sibling methods that may rely on pre-trained feature extractors or complex scheduling, this method leverages low-frequency components from low-resolution voxels to guide coarse geometry early in training, with mid- and high-resolution voxels refining details as training advances.

### [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md)
Geometry regularization improves geometric fidelity and visual quality by constraining the shape and orientation of Gaussian primitives, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains location. It addresses issues such as floating artifacts and poor surface smoothness by enforcing that Gaussians align with the underlying surface geometry. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by focusing on spatial shape constraints rather than spectral content, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by modifying the primitive's anisotropy rather than just its position.

#### Wild-GS
- [S1] A method that aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects and constrain geometry.

#### Source Feature Extrapolation
- [S2] A technique to improve extrapolation capabilities beyond source view frustums by padding source images and adding positional encoding channels for padded regions, preventing smearing artifacts in regions not visible by source images.
- [S2] Apply border padding to source images $I^{(i)}$.
- [S2] Create a channel $\Gamma$ containing pixel-wise positional encodings for padded regions: $\Gamma[u,v] = \gamma(u,v)$ if $(u,v)$ is not in $I^{(i)}$, else 0. Concatenate $I_{pad}$ and $\Gamma$ to form $I'(i) = \text{concatenate}(I_{pad}, \Gamma)$.
- [S2] $\gamma(\cdot)$ denotes positional encoding with exponential frequencies.

#### SDF-Regularized 3DGS
- [S3] Methods to improve geometric fidelity in 3DGS by imposing physical constraints, such as Signed Distance Fields (SDFs), monocular depth priors, or multi-view geometric constraints, during optimization.
- [S3] Introduce a regularization term to minimize the difference between an ideal SDF and the SDF calculated from Gaussians.
- [S3] Use a dual-branch framework with GS- and SDF-branches, jointly optimized to provide mutual guidance.
- [S3] Incorporate geometric regularization such as monocular depth priors or multi-plane Gaussian optimization.

#### MVG-Splatting
- [S4] A method that employs a depth map to enforce geometric consistency, specifically applying geometric consistency only in the near and far regions of the scene to effectively mitigate under-reconstruction in these critical regions while avoiding the risk of over-reconstruction.

#### Shape Energy Constraint
- [S5] An energy term defined as the combination of opacity energy and boundary energy, used to preserve the shape, opacity, and unitized colors (spherical harmonics) of Gaussian radiance fields during deformation.
- [S5] Relying solely on feature energy is insufficient to preserve opacity and unitized colors; gray Gaussians with SH values close to zero appear and their size becomes uncontrolled because they cannot be constrained by feature energy alone.
- [S5] Without $E_{shape}$, the optimization process becomes unstable and artifacts appear; with $E_{shape}$, the process stabilizes and these artifacts are mitigated.

## Background
The problem of geometric fidelity in 3D Gaussian Splatting originated from the observation that optimizing solely for photometric loss (L1/LPIPS) results in unstructured density clouds that do not represent valid surfaces, leading to floating artifacts and poor geometry [S3]. Early works like the original 3DGS paper focused on rendering speed and quality but did not explicitly enforce surface constraints [S3]. Subsequent research introduced various regularization techniques to address these limitations.

Depth-based regularization was explored to guide the placement of Gaussians, with methods like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) and [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md) leveraging monocular depth estimates or multi-view consistency [S1]. Frequency-based regularization, such as [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [FreGS](9212-fregs.md), was introduced to handle high-frequency details and prevent overfitting to noise, addressing the limitations of spatial-domain losses [S2, S3].

The integration of Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) and other physical constraints, as seen in [SDF-Regularized 3DGS](9212-fregs.md) variants, represents a trend towards enforcing stronger geometric priors [S3]. Additionally, methods like [Wild-GS](9212-fregs.md) and [Source Feature Extrapolation](9212-fregs.md) address specific challenges in casual captures and extrapolation, using visibility maps and positional encodings to constrain geometry and appearance [S1, S2]. The [Shape Energy Constraint](9212-fregs.md) further refines this by preserving opacity and color during deformation [S5].
