# Regularization and Geometry Constraints

Also called: Geometric Priors, Surface Constraints, Frequency Regularization

## Overview
Regularization and Geometry Constraints are training-time techniques for 3D Gaussian Splatting that impose physical or structural priors to mitigate artifacts such as "floaters," volumetric noise, and over-reconstruction. While standard 3DGS optimizes primarily for photometric fidelity, these methods constrain the geometry (position, shape, orientation) or the frequency spectrum of the rendered output to ensure the resulting Gaussian cloud represents a coherent surface rather than an unstructured density cloud. They set themselves apart from sibling methods like [Physical Parameter Optimization](4111-physical-parameter-optimization.md) or [Base 3DGS Optimization](7549-base-3dgs-optimization.md) by explicitly modifying the loss landscape or primitive definitions to enforce geometric coherence, rather than relying solely on adaptive densification or learning rate schedules.

## Shared principles
The children of this family share the goal of improving geometric fidelity and reducing artifacts caused by the unconstrained optimization of Gaussian primitives. However, they operate in distinct domains:
- **Frequency Domain:** Methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [FreGS](9212-fregs.md) operate by comparing the Fourier spectra of rendered and ground-truth images, penalizing high-frequency discrepancies to guide densification [S2].
- **Spatial/Shape Domain:** Methods like [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md) and [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md) modify the covariance matrices or scaling factors of Gaussians to enforce planarity, normal alignment, or half-ellipsoid shapes [S1].
- **Depth/Geometric Consistency:** Methods like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) and [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md) use depth estimates (monocular or multi-view reprojection) to constrain Gaussian centers and opacities [S1].
- **Supervision Smoothing:** [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) blurs the target images to stabilize gradients during early training [S2].

There is no single shared mathematical formula across all children; the commonality is the application of a constraint term or modification to the standard photometric loss to enforce geometric regularity.

## Subtopics

### [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md)
Frequency Regularization (FR) minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which results in small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR increases pixel gradients in these regions by explicitly disentangling frequency components, thereby guiding adaptive densification toward meaningful high-frequency details. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) or [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial or geometric domain.

### [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md)
Smoothed 2D Supervision is a regularization technique that enhances the robustness of joint optimization in differentiable rendering by smoothing the 2D supervision signal. It solves the problem of noisy gradients and unstable camera alignment during training by blurring training images with a scheduled set of 2D Gaussian kernels. Unlike frequency regularization methods that operate in the Fourier domain [6130-frequency-regularization.md] or covariance transformations that modify primitive shapes [9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md], this method operates directly on the image plane supervision targets. It stabilizes optimization by restricting the spectrum of the learned 3D scene and producing smoother image gradients.

### [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md)
3D Half-Gaussian Splatting (3D-HGS) transforms standard volumetric 3D Gaussians into half-ellipsoids to better model sharp surface boundaries and reduce volumetric artifacts. It achieves this by projecting the Gaussian covariance onto a plane defined by the surface normal, effectively discarding the volume behind the surface. This method consistently outperforms original 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) in PSNR and rendering efficiency across standard datasets.

### [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md)
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization is a geometric constraint strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by decoupling the optimization of Gaussian centers from their opacities. It solves the problem where standard depth regularization forces anisotropic Gaussian shapes to conform to smooth monocular depth estimates, which conflicts with complex color appearance. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md) by explicitly freezing shape parameters (scale and rotation) and using two distinct depth rendering passes to independently update positions ("hard") and opacities ("soft").

### [FreGS](9212-fregs.md)
FreGS addresses the over-reconstruction and high-frequency artifacts common in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by applying progressive frequency regularization to the Gaussian primitives. Unlike sibling methods that rely on spatial smoothing or depth constraints, FreGS operates in the frequency domain, penalizing discrepancies between the Fourier transforms of the rendered and ground-truth images to guide densification toward meaningful details while suppressing noise.

### [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md)
Cross-scale geometric adaptation is a self-adaptive regularization method that uses reprojection errors across multiple voxel scales to guide geometry learning without complex scheduling or explicit depth ground truth. It selects render depths with minimal reprojection error across different scales as pseudo labels, ensuring geometric integrity and significantly reducing floaters compared to training without geometric adaptation. It provides a stronger and more robust constraint than standard depth priors, achieving lower final depth error by progressively refining geometry from coarse to fine scales.

### [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md)
Geometry regularization improves the geometric coherence and smoothness of 3D Gaussian splats by constraining their shape and orientation, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains position. It addresses the issue of "floaters" and volumetric artifacts by enforcing planarity and normal alignment. It sets itself apart from siblings like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by focusing on surface topology rather than just depth error, and from [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating in spatial/shape space rather than the Fourier domain.

### Wild-GS
- [S1] A method that aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects and constrain geometry.

### Source Feature Extrapolation
- [S2] A technique to improve extrapolation capabilities beyond source view frustums by padding source images and adding positional encoding channels for padded regions, preventing smearing artifacts in regions not visible by source images.
- [S2] Apply border padding to source images $I^{(i)}$.
- [S2] Create a channel $\Gamma$ containing pixel-wise positional encodings for padded regions: $\Gamma[u,v] = \gamma(u,v)$ if $(u,v)$ is not in $I^{(i)}$, else 0. Concatenate $I_{pad}$ and $\Gamma$ to form $I'(i) = \text{concatenate}(I_{pad}, \Gamma)$.
- [S2] $\gamma(\cdot)$ denotes positional encoding with exponential frequencies.

### SDF-Regularized 3DGS
- [S3] Methods to improve geometric fidelity in 3DGS by imposing physical constraints, such as Signed Distance Fields (SDFs), monocular depth priors, or multi-view geometric constraints, during optimization.
- [S3] Introduce a regularization term to minimize the difference between an ideal SDF and the SDF calculated from Gaussians.
- [S3] Use a dual-branch framework with GS- and SDF-branches, jointly optimized to provide mutual guidance.
- [S3] Incorporate geometric regularization such as monocular depth priors or multi-plane Gaussian optimization.

### MVG-Splatting
- [S4] A method that employs a depth map to enforce geometric consistency, specifically applying geometric consistency only in the near and far regions of the scene to effectively mitigate under-reconstruction in these critical regions while avoiding the risk of over-reconstruction.

### Shape Energy Constraint
- [S5] An energy term defined as the combination of opacity energy and boundary energy, used to preserve the shape, opacity, and unitized colors (spherical harmonics) of Gaussian radiance fields during deformation.
- [S5] Relying solely on feature energy is insufficient to preserve opacity and unitized colors; gray Gaussians with SH values close to zero appear and their size becomes uncontrolled because they cannot be constrained by feature energy alone.
- [S5] Without $E_{shape}$, the optimization process becomes unstable and artifacts appear; with $E_{shape}$, the process stabilizes and these artifacts are mitigated.

## Background
The problem of "floaters" and unstructured density clouds in 3D Gaussian Splatting arises because the original method optimizes primarily for photometric loss, often resulting in geometries that do not represent valid surfaces [S3]. To recover high-quality geometry, recent methods impose physical constraints during optimization, such as integrating Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) or using monocular depth priors [S3]. Wild-GS [29] aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects [S1]. MVG-Splatting [29] and Mini-Splatting [13] employ depth maps to enforce geometric consistency, with MVG-Splatting applying this only in near and far regions [S4]. Shape Energy constraints [S5] are used in deformation contexts to preserve the shape and colors of Gaussian radiance fields.
