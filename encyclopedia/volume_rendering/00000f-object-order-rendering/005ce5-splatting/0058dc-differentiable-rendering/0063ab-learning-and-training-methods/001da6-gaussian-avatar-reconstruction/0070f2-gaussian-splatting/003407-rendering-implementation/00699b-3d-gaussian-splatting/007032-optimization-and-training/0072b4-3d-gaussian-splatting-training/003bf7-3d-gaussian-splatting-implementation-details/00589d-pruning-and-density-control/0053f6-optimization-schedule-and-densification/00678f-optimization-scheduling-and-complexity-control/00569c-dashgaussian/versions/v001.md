# DashGaussian

Also called: Computational resource allocation for 3DGS

## Overview
DashGaussian is a scheduling scheme for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) optimization that accelerates training by progressively fitting Gaussians to higher frequency components and synchronizing primitive growth with rendering resolution [S1]. It reformulates optimization as a frequency component fitting task, cooperatively allocating rendering resolution and primitive count to strip redundant complexity [S1]. Unlike vanilla 3DGS or methods with concave-down growth curves, DashGaussian produces a concave-up primitive growth curve spanning the entire optimization process, yielding fewer primitives and faster convergence while preserving or improving rendering quality [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Training views (images), camera poses, initial set of 3D Gaussian primitives (position, covariance, opacity, spherical harmonics coefficients), total optimization iterations $T_{total}$.
- **Outputs:** Optimized set of 3D Gaussian primitives.

**Data Structures:**
- **Gaussian Primitives:** Each primitive $i$ is defined by position $\mathbf{p}_i$, covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}_i$ (or rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$ and scale $\mathbf{s}_i$), opacity $\alpha_i$, and color coefficients $c_i$ (typically spherical harmonics).
- **Rendering Resolution:** A scalar factor $r(t)$ or integer resolution $(H_t, W_t)$ at iteration $t$.
- **Primitive Count:** The number of active primitives $P_t$ at iteration $t$.

**Formulas:**
The rendering equation for a pixel $u$ remains standard [S1]:
$$ C(u) = \sum_{i=1}^P T_i(u) \cdot w_i(u) \cdot c_i $$
where $T_i(u) = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - w_j(u))$ is the transmittance, $w_i(u) = o_i G_{i2D}(u)$ is the weight, $o_i$ is opacity, and $G_{i2D}(u)$ is the 2D Gaussian projected to the image plane.

**Parameters:**
- **Target Optimization Time:** 200 seconds (achieved with Taming-3DGS backbone on consumer-grade GPU) [S1].
- **Resolution Downsampling Factor:** $r(t)$, a continuous scalar modulated to integer $\lfloor r(t) \rfloor$ in practice [S1].

**Steps:**

1.  **Frequency Formulation:**
    Reformulate the optimization process as progressively fitting the 3DGS representation to higher levels of frequency components in the training views. This is based on the observation that increasing rendering resolution is equivalent to fitting higher frequency residuals [S1].

2.  **Dynamic Rendering Resolution Scheduling:**
    Define a resolution scheduler $r(t)$ that starts low and increases over time.
    -   At iteration $t$, the rendering resolution is determined by $r(t)$.
    -   To avoid 3D aliasing artifacts when downsampling, apply an anti-aliasing low-pass filter to the training images before downsampling [S1].
    -   The resolution factor $r(t)$ is scene-adaptive, meaning it adjusts based on the frequency information present in the specific scene [S1].
    -   In implementation, the continuous factor is modulated: $r_{int}(t) = \lfloor r(t) \rfloor$ [S1].

3.  **Primitive Growth Scheduling:**
    Schedule the growth of Gaussian primitives to synchronize with the rendering resolution.
    -   The primitive count $P(t)$ follows a **concave-up** curve over the optimization timeline $[0, T_{total}]$ [S1].
    -   This contrasts with existing methods that use concave-down curves or peak at half the iterations [S1].
    -   The growth is coupled with the resolution via a numerical constraint to balance computational redundancy and fitting quality [S1].

4.  **Momentum-Based Primitive Budgeting:**
    Adaptively estimate the maximum number of primitives $P_{max}$ required for the growth curve.
    -   This method avoids relying on dataset priors [S1].
    -   It uses a momentum-based approach to predict the final primitive count needed to represent the scene at full resolution [S1].

5.  **Optimization Loop:**
    For each iteration $t$ from 1 to $T_{total}$:
    a. Determine current resolution $H_t, W_t$ using the resolution scheduler.
    b. Determine target primitive count $P_t$ using the primitive scheduler.
    c. Perform densification/splitting/clamping to adjust the number of primitives to $P_t$ [S1].
    d. Render the image at resolution $H_t \times W_t$ using the current Gaussian set.
    e. Compute loss against the downsampled training image.
    f. Back-propagate gradients and update Gaussian parameters.

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def dash_gaussian_optimize(training_views, initial_gaussians, total_iterations):
    """
    DashGaussian optimization loop.
    :param training_views: List of (image, camera_pose) tuples.
    :param initial_gaussians: Initial set of 3D Gaussian primitives.
    :param total_iterations: Total number of optimization steps.
    :return: Optimized Gaussian primitives.
    """
    gaussians = initial_gaussians
    current_primitive_count = len(gaussians)
    
    # Momentum-based budgeting to estimate max primitives
    # [G] Implementation detail: This typically involves tracking the 
    # growth rate of primitives during early iterations to predict P_max.
    max_primitives = estimate_max_primitives_momentum(gaussians, training_views, initial_iterations=100)
    
    for t in range(1, total_iterations + 1):
        # 1. Schedule Resolution
        # r(t) is a scene-adaptive continuous scalar.
        # Modulate to integer for actual rendering.
        resolution_factor = get_resolution_factor(t, total_iterations, scene_frequency_info)
        render_height = int(original_height / resolution_factor)
        render_width = int(original_width / resolution_factor)
        
        # 2. Schedule Primitive Count
        # Concave-up growth curve.
        # Coupled with resolution to balance redundancy and quality.
        target_primitive_count = get_primitive_count(t, total_iterations, max_primitives)
        
        # 3. Adjust Primitive Count (Densification/Pruning)
        # [G] Standard 3DGS densification logic (split, clone, prune) 
        # is guided to reach target_primitive_count.
        gaussians = adjust_primitive_count(gaussians, target_primitive_count)
        
        # 4. Render and Optimize
        # Select a random training view
        view_image, view_pose = sample_training_view(training_views)
        
        # Apply anti-aliasing low-pass filter if downsampling is significant
        # [G] Standard box filter or Gaussian blur before downsampling.
        target_image = downsample_with_antialiasing(view_image, render_height, render_width)
        
        # Render current gaussians at reduced resolution
        rendered_image = render_gaussians(gaussians, view_pose, render_height, render_width)
        
        # Compute loss (e.g., L1 + SSIM)
        loss = compute_loss(rendered_image, target_image)
        
        # Back-propagate and update
        loss.backward()
        update_gaussians(gaussians)
        
    return gaussians

def get_resolution_factor(t, total_iterations, scene_frequency_info):
    """
    Returns a continuous resolution factor r(t) >= 1.
    Starts low (e.g., 2.0) and increases to 1.0 (full resolution).
    Scene-adaptive based on frequency content.
    """
    # [G] Example schedule: Linear or cosine annealing from high factor to 1.0
    # The exact function is not specified in [S1] beyond "scene-adaptive" and "continuous".
    # A common approach is inverse scaling.
    progress = t / total_iterations
    # Placeholder for specific scheduling function derived from scene frequency
    # S1 mentions "scene-adaptive", implying the schedule might vary per scene.
    # We assume a base schedule modulated by scene complexity.
    base_factor = 1.0 + (2.0 - 1.0) * (1 - progress) # Starts at 2.0, ends at 1.0
    return base_factor

def get_primitive_count(t, total_iterations, max_primitives):
    """
    Returns the target number of primitives at iteration t.
    Follows a concave-up curve.
    """
    # [G] Concave-up implies slow growth initially, accelerating later.
    # Example: Quadratic growth.
    progress = t / total_iterations
    # Scale from initial count to max_primitives
    # Using a power > 1 for concave-up shape
    growth_factor = progress ** 2 
    initial_count = len(initial_gaussians) # Assumed known
    return int(initial_count + (max_primitives - initial_count) * growth_factor)
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
-   **3D Aliasing:** Simply reducing resolution without anti-aliasing causes 3D aliasing, sacrificing fidelity at the original resolution [S1].
-   **Low-Frequency Disturbance:** Anti-aliasing downsampling must apply a low-pass filter before downsampling to avoid disturbing recoverable low-frequency components [S1].
-   **Primitive Budgeting:** Relying on dataset priors for primitive counts can be suboptimal; the momentum-based method is preferred for adaptivity [S1].

## Operating characteristics
-   **Time Complexity:** Reduces per-iteration elapsed time for forward rendering, gradient back-propagation, and primitive optimization by lowering resolution and primitive count in early stages [S1].
-   **Memory Complexity:** Uses fewer primitives overall compared to vanilla 3DGS, reducing memory footprint [S1].
-   **Measured Performance:**
    -   Average acceleration rate across tested backbones: 45.7% [S1].
    -   Optimization time reduction for Taming-3DGS backbone: 41.3% [S1].
    -   Achieves optimization in ~200 seconds on consumer-grade GPU with Taming-3DGS [S1].
-   **Strengths:**
    -   Plug-and-play integration with existing 3DGS backbones (3DGS, Mip-Splatting, Revising-3DGS, Taming-3DGS) [S1].
    -   Preserves or improves rendering quality (PSNR, SSIM) while using fewer primitives [S1].
    -   Scene-adaptive scheduling handles diverse frequency information across scenes [S1].
-   **Limitations:**
    -   Requires careful tuning of the resolution and primitive schedulers for optimal performance [S1].
    -   Performance depends on the underlying backbone's efficiency (e.g., Taming-3DGS vs. vanilla 3DGS) [S1].
-   **When to Use:** When fast training of 3DGS is required without significant quality loss, especially for large-scale or time-intensive reconstruction tasks [S1].
-   **When Not to Use:** When absolute maximum rendering quality is required regardless of training time, or when hardware constraints prevent the dynamic resolution changes (though this is unlikely) [S1].

## Background
-   **Origin:** Proposed by Youyu Chen, Junjun Jiang, Kui Jiang, et al. from Harbin Institute of Technology and Huawei Noah’s Ark Lab [S1].
-   **Paper:** "DashGaussian: Optimizing 3D Gaussian Splatting in 200 Seconds" [S1].
-   **Date:** Published in CVPR 2025 (arXiv:2503.18402v2, March 26, 2025) [S1].
-   **History:** Builds on the observation that optimization complexity (resolution + primitive count) dominates 3DGS training time. Previous works accelerated optimization by refining rendering pipelines or pruning primitives post-densification [S1]. DashGaussian introduces a novel frequency-based scheduling approach to dynamically allocate these resources [S1].
-   **Usage:** Integrated as a backbone enhancement for various 3DGS methods, including Mip-Splatting, Revising-3DGS, and Taming-3DGS [S1].
