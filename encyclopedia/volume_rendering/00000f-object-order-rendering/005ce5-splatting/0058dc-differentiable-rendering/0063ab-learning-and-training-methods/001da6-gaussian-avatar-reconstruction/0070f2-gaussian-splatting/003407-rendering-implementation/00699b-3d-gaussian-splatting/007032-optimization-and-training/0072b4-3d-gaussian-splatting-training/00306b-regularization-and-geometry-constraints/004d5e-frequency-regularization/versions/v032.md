# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which results in small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR increases pixel gradients in these regions by explicitly disentangling frequency components [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial or depth domain, and from [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md) by not enforcing specific covariance structures [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, and a frequency annealing schedule (optional, for progressive regularization) [S2].
*   **Outputs:** A scalar regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{FR}$ used to update Gaussian parameters via gradient descent [S2].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Frequency Domain Representation:** Complex-valued matrices $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$ per channel, storing real and imaginary components [S2].
*   **Amplitude and Phase Matrices:** Real-valued matrices of size $H \times W$ storing magnitude and angle for each frequency component [S2].

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Fourier Transform:** Compute the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) for the ground truth image $I$ and the rendered image $\hat{I}$ to obtain their frequency representations $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ [S2].
    $$ F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
    $$ \hat{F}(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} \hat{I}(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
    where $(x, y)$ are image coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $H, W$ are image height and width [S2].

2.  **Compute Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency component $(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude and phase for both $F$ and $\hat{F}$ [S2]:
    $$ |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2} $$
    $$ \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right) $$
    where $\text{Re}$ and $\text{Im}$ are the real and imaginary components of the complex frequency value [S2].

3.  **Calculate Discrepancies:** Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ using the Euclidean metric over all pixels and channels [S2]:
    $$ d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2} $$
    $$ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2} $$
    Note: The summation indices in the source formula [S2] use $(x,y)$ but the values depend on $(u,v)$. In implementation, this implies summing over the spatial domain which maps to the frequency domain values. Effectively, it is the root mean square error between the amplitude/phase spectra [S2].

4.  **Combine Loss:** The frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of amplitude and phase discrepancies. While the exact weighting is not specified in the primary source, the total discrepancy drives the gradient [S2].
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{FR} = \lambda_a d_a + \lambda_p d_p $$
    where $\lambda_a$ and $\lambda_p$ are hyperparameters [G].

5.  **Frequency Annealing (Optional):** To prevent bias towards undesirable artifacts and restricted densification, a progressive frequency regularization technique can be applied [S2]. This involves incorporating a low-pass filter $H_l$ and a dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ in Fourier space to gradually leverage low-to-high frequencies [S2].
    *   Initialize with low-pass filtering to capture global structures.
    *   Gradually increase the cutoff frequency of the high-pass filter over training iterations to introduce high-frequency details [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
*   **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
*   **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are retained [S2].
*   **Typical Values:** Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Over-reconstruction vs. Well-reconstruction:** L1 loss fails to differentiate between these regions, leading to redundant Gaussians in well-reconstructed areas. FR increases gradients specifically in over-reconstructed regions, enabling adaptive densification [S2].
*   **Artifact Bias:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without frequency annealing can bias the optimization towards undesirable artifacts and restrict Gaussian densification [S2].
*   **Precision:** The arctangent function in phase calculation requires handling quadrants correctly (e.g., using `atan2`) to avoid discontinuities [G].

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The addition of 2D FFT and inverse FFT operations per iteration increases computational cost compared to standard L1 loss. FFT complexity is $O(N \log N)$ where $N = H \times W$ [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing complex frequency representations, doubling the memory requirement for image buffers during the loss computation step [S2].
*   **Measured Performance:** In the 'Bicycle' scene, FR significantly increased pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to the baseline method without FR, enabling better densification [S2].
*   **Scaling:** The method scales with image resolution due to the FFT operations.
*   **Strengths:** Effectively alleviates over-reconstruction, improves Gaussian densification by revealing over-reconstructed regions, and disentangles frequency components [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Naive application without annealing can bias optimization towards undesirable artifacts and restrict densification [S2].
*   **When to use:** When over-reconstruction is prevalent and standard densification criteria fail to create new Gaussians in those regions [S2].
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are severely limited, or when the scene is very low frequency and high-frequency regularization is unnecessary [G].

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced as part of the FreGS method to improve 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization [S2]. It addresses the problem of over-reconstruction and improper Gaussian distribution by operating in the Fourier domain [S1]. The technique builds on the observation that L1 loss in the spatial domain is insufficient for guiding densification in over-reconstructed areas [S2].
