# Geometry Regularization

Also called: Gaussian Flattening, Normal Consistency and Flattening

## Overview
Geometry regularization is a training constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that improves surface smoothness and reduces volumetric artifacts (floaters) by enforcing geometric priors on the Gaussian kernels [S1, S2, unofficial]. Unlike depth supervision, which constrains only the spatial location of the surface, this method constrains the shape and orientation of the Gaussians themselves [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from siblings like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating directly on the 3D covariance structure rather than the image frequency spectrum, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by focusing on normal alignment and flattening rather than depth value consistency [S1, S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Per-Gaussian scaling factors $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$, per-Gaussian normals $n_g$, rendered depth map $D_{rendered}$, and rendered normal map $N_{rendered}$ derived from $D_{rendered}$.
*   **Outputs:** Gradient updates for Gaussian parameters (specifically scaling and orientation) that minimize the regularization loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Parameters:** Each Gaussian $i$ has a scaling vector $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ representing the lengths of the principal axes. The covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$ is constructed from $s_i$ and a rotation matrix $R_i$ derived from the Gaussian's orientation quaternion.
*   **Normals:** $n_g$ is the normal vector associated with the Gaussian's orientation. $N_{rendered}$ is the surface normal estimated from the gradient of the rendered depth map.

**Steps:**
1.  **Enable Check:** Verify if geometry regularization is enabled via the `use_normal_loss` parameter [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Depth Normal Consistency:**
    *   Compute the normal map $N_{rendered}$ from the rendered depth buffer [S1, unofficial].
    *   For each pixel, identify the contributing Gaussian(s).
    *   Calculate the alignment loss between the rendered normal $n_{rendered}$ and the Gaussian's intrinsic normal $n_g$ [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Flattening Surface (Scaling Loss):**
    *   For each Gaussian, identify the smallest component of the scaling factor vector $s_i$. Let $s_{min} = \min(s_{i,x}, s_{i,y}, s_{i,z})$ [S2, unofficial].
    *   Minimize $s_{min}$ towards zero during optimization [S2, unofficial]. This forces the Gaussian to flatten into a 2D surfel aligned with the surface [S2, unofficial].
4.  **Gradient Update:** Combine the depth normal consistency loss and the flattening loss to update the Gaussian parameters [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The total regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ is composed of two terms:

1.  **Depth Normal Consistency Loss:**
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{normal} = \sum_{p} (1 - n_{rendered, p} \cdot n_{g, p}) $$
    where $p$ indexes pixels, $n_{rendered, p}$ is the normal from the rendered depth map, and $n_{g, p}$ is the normal of the Gaussian contributing to pixel $p$ [S1, unofficial].

2.  **Flattening Loss (Scaling Loss):**
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{flatten} = \sum_{i} s_{min, i} $$
    where $s_{min, i}$ is the smallest component of the scaling factor $s_i$ for Gaussian $i$ [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   `use_normal_loss`: A boolean flag (default `false`) that enables this regularization [S1, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_{geo}$: Weighting coefficient for the total regularization loss. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Sky/Distant Background:** Geometry regularization may pull geometry onto itself if applied to distant background elements. The source suggests using a "Sky Dome" (frozen shell of Gaussians) to disentangle sky from foreground, as regularization on the dome can make the ground less opaque [S1, unofficial].
*   **Depth Refitting:** Depth maps must be refitted against a prior point cloud to the same scale before use. Depth maps that fail refitting are discarded [S1, unofficial].
*   **Floater Trade-off:** While flattening reduces floaters, aggressive regularization may reduce the number of remaining points, potentially compromising reconstruction quality [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Scaling Loss for Geometry Modeling
A specific variant of geometry regularization that focuses exclusively on flattening Gaussian kernels into 3D surfels aligned with the surface [S2, unofficial]. It minimizes the smallest component of the scaling factor $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$ towards zero [S2, unofficial]. This aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when the primary goal is to enforce planar surface constraints without necessarily relying on rendered normal alignment [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds overhead proportional to the number of Gaussians and pixels for normal computation and loss calculation. Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing rendered depth and normal maps. Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** Improves geometry smoothness, reduces floaters, and enforces planar surface constraints [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** May reduce the number of remaining points if not balanced with pruning; can negatively affect distant background if not handled with techniques like Sky Dome [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality, smooth surface reconstruction is required and floaters are a significant issue.
*   **When not to use:** When the scene contains significant non-planar structures or when computational resources are extremely limited.

## Background
Originating from improvements to 3D Gaussian Splatting training pipelines, this technique was introduced to address the arbitrary spatial distribution and anisotropic kernels of tiny 3D Gaussians [S2, unofficial]. It builds on the idea that representing surfaces with flattened Gaussians (surfels) provides better geometric priors [S2, unofficial]. The method is implemented in libraries such as GaussianSplatting.jl [S1, unofficial] and GauStudio [S2, unofficial].
