# Geometry Regularization

Also called: Gaussian Flattening, Normal Consistency and Flattening

## Overview
Geometry regularization is a training technique for 3D Gaussian Splatting that improves surface smoothness and reduces floating artifacts by constraining the geometric shape of the Gaussians, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains location [S1, S2, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating directly on the Gaussian covariance parameters rather than the image frequency spectrum, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by focusing on surface orientation and anisotropy rather than depth value alignment.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Per-Gaussian scaling factors $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$, per-Gaussian normals $n_g$, and rendered depth maps $D_{rendered}$ from training views.
*   **Outputs:** Gradient updates to the Gaussian parameters (specifically scale and orientation) to minimize geometric inconsistency.

**Data Structures:**
*   `Gaussian`: Contains fields for position, rotation (quaternion), scale ($s$), opacity, and SH coefficients. The scale $s$ is typically stored as a 3D vector representing the lengths of the principal axes.
*   `RenderedDepth`: A 2D array of depth values corresponding to the camera view.

**Steps:**
1.  **Enable Flag:** The method is controlled by a boolean parameter `use_normal_loss` in the optimization configuration [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Depth Normal Consistency:**
    *   Compute the surface normal $n_d$ at each pixel from the rendered depth map $D_{rendered}$ using spatial gradients (e.g., Sobel operators or finite differences) [S1, S2, unofficial].
    *   Retrieve the per-Gaussian normal $n_g$ associated with the Gaussian contributing to that pixel.
    *   Compute the alignment loss between $n_d$ and $n_g$.
3.  **Flattening Surface (Scaling Loss):**
    *   For each Gaussian, identify the smallest component of the scaling factor vector $s$. Let $s_{min} = \min(s_x, s_y, s_z)$ [S2, unofficial].
    *   Minimize $s_{min}$ towards zero during optimization. This forces the Gaussian to flatten along its smallest axis, effectively turning volumetric blobs into surface-aligned "surfels" [S2, unofficial].
    *   This flattening aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge as the surface normal direction [S2, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The total regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ combines normal consistency and flattening:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{geo} = \lambda_n \mathcal{L}_{normal} + \lambda_f \mathcal{L}_{flatten}
$$
Where:
*   $\mathcal{L}_{normal} = 1 - |n_d \cdot n_g|$ (or similar cosine similarity metric) [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   $\mathcal{L}_{flatten} = s_{min}$ or a function penalizing non-zero $s_{min}$ [S2, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_n, \lambda_f$ are hyperparameters.

**Parameters:**
*   `use_normal_loss`: Boolean flag to enable the technique [S1, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_n, \lambda_f$: Weights for normal consistency and flattening terms. Values not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Depth Refitting:** Depth maps from different frames may vary in scale. They must be refitted against a prior point cloud to the same scale before use. Frames that fail refitting are discarded [S1, unofficial].
*   **Precision:** Computing normals from depth maps can be noisy in flat regions or near depth discontinuities.
*   **Degenerate Input:** If a Gaussian is perfectly spherical ($s_x=s_y=s_z$), the "smallest axis" is ambiguous. The flattening loss encourages anisotropy, resolving this.

## Subtopics

### Scaling Loss for Geometry Modeling
A specific variant focusing on flattening Gaussian kernels into 3D surfels aligned with the surface by minimizing the smallest component of the scaling factor $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$ towards zero [S2, unofficial]. This aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when surface alignment is critical and floaters are prevalent. It is a core component of the broader Geometry Regularization method [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds overhead for depth normal computation (gradient calculation on depth buffer) and per-Gaussian scaling checks. Generally low compared to photometric loss.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing rendered depth maps and computing normals, adding minimal memory overhead.
*   **Performance:** Improves geometry smoothness and reduces floaters [S1, S2, unofficial]. May reduce the number of remaining points if combined with pruning, potentially compromising detail if not balanced [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Effective for surface reconstruction, reduces volumetric artifacts.
*   **Limitations:** Requires reliable depth maps; sensitive to depth noise.
*   **When to use:** When reconstructing surfaces with clear geometry.
*   **When not to use:** When reconstructing transparent or volumetric scenes where surfel assumption fails.

## Background
Originated in the context of improving 3D Gaussian Splatting for surface reconstruction. The concept of using scaling loss to flatten Gaussians into surfels is cited from prior work [5] in [S2, unofficial]. The implementation details regarding depth normal consistency and the `use_normal_loss` flag are documented in unofficial sources [S1, S2, unofficial].
