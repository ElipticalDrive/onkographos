# 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization

Also called: 3D-HGS

## Overview
This method transforms standard 3D Gaussian kernels into "half-Gaussians" to better model surface geometry and sharp transitions between object surfaces and empty space, addressing the volumetric blob artifacts inherent in standard 3D Gaussian Splatting [S1]. It achieves this by regularizing the covariance matrix to align with local surface normals, effectively cutting the Gaussian ellipsoid in half [S1]. Unlike [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) which targets spectral artifacts, or [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) which uses external depth priors, this method enforces geometric consistency directly through the kernel's shape parameters during training [S1].

## How it works
The method modifies the covariance matrix of each Gaussian kernel to represent a half-ellipsoid aligned with a splitting plane. This transformation is applied periodically during training to enforce surface-like geometry.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** The original 3D covariance matrix $\Sigma$ (3x3 symmetric positive definite) and the estimated surface normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ for the kernel's position.
*   **Output:** A regularized covariance matrix $\sigma$ representing a half-Gaussian.

### Data Structures
*   **Covariance Matrix ($\Sigma$):** A 3x3 symmetric matrix.
*   **Basis Matrix:** A 3x3 matrix constructed from two tangent vectors and the normal vector.
*   **Regularized Covariance ($\sigma$):** A 3x3 matrix with specific zero constraints in the normal-aligned basis.

### Algorithm Steps
The transformation is performed every 5000 iterations [S1].

1.  **Normalize the normal vector:**
    $$ \mathbf{n} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{n}}{\|\mathbf{n}\|} $$

2.  **Construct the local basis:**
    Define two orthogonal tangent vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ that, along with $\mathbf{n}$, form an orthonormal basis.
    *   If $n_x = 0$ and $n_y = 0$:
        $$ \mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0, 0) $$
        $$ \mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1, 0) $$
    *   Otherwise:
        $$ \mathbf{v}_1 \leftarrow \frac{(n_y, -n_x, 0)}{\|(n_y, -n_x, 0)\|} $$
        $$ \mathbf{v}_2 \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{v}_1}{\|\mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{v}_1\|} $$
    *   Construct the basis matrix $B$ by concatenating these vectors as columns:
        $$ B = [\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{n}] $$

3.  **Transform covariance to basis space:**
    Project the original covariance $\Sigma$ into the local basis:
    $$ \text{cov} \leftarrow B^T \cdot \Sigma \cdot B $$

4.  **Compute modified diagonal and off-diagonal elements:**
    Calculate the Schur complement-like terms for the tangent plane components:
    $$ a' \leftarrow \text{cov}[0, 0] - \frac{\text{cov}[0, 2]^2}{\text{cov}[2, 2]} $$
    $$ b' \leftarrow \text{cov}[0, 1] - \frac{\text{cov}[0, 2] \cdot \text{cov}[1, 2]}{\text{cov}[2, 2]} $$
    $$ c' \leftarrow \text{cov}[1, 1] - \frac{\text{cov}[1, 2]^2}{\text{cov}[2, 2]} $$

5.  **Construct the regularized covariance in basis space:**
    Create a new matrix $\sigma_{\text{basis}}$ where the tangent-tangent block is defined by $a', b', c'$, the normal-tangent and tangent-normal blocks are zero, and the normal-normal block is scaled down:
    $$ \sigma_{\text{basis}} = \begin{bmatrix} a' & b' & 0 \\ b' & c' & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \min(a', c') / 100 \end{bmatrix} $$
    *Note: The element at $[2,2]$ is set to $\min(a', c') / 100$ to effectively flatten the Gaussian along the normal direction, creating the "half" effect [S1].*

6.  **Transform back to world space:**
    $$ \sigma \leftarrow B \cdot \sigma_{\text{basis}} \cdot B^T $$

### Parameters
*   **Regularization Interval:** 5000 iterations [S1].
*   **Opacity Threshold for Densification/Pruning:** Increased to 0.01 [S1].
*   **Opacity Reset Value for Half-Gaussians:** 0.02 [S1].

### Pseudocode
```python
def transform_covariance_to_half_gaussian(cov_matrix: np.ndarray, normal: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Transforms a 3D Gaussian covariance matrix into a half-Gaussian covariance matrix
    aligned with the given surface normal.
    
    Args:
        cov_matrix: 3x3 symmetric covariance matrix Sigma.
        normal: 3D surface normal vector n.
        
    Returns:
        Regularized 3x3 covariance matrix sigma.
    """
    # 1. Normalize normal
    n = normal / np.linalg.norm(normal)
    
    # 2. Construct basis
    nx, ny, nz = n
    if nx == 0 and ny == 0:
        v1 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
        v2 = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
    else:
        v1 = np.array([ny, -nx, 0.0])
        v1 = v1 / np.linalg.norm(v1)
        v2 = np.cross(n, v1)
        v2 = v2 / np.linalg.norm(v2)
        
    # Basis matrix B = [v1, v2, n]
    B = np.column_stack((v1, v2, n))
    
    # 3. Transform to basis space
    cov_basis = B.T @ cov_matrix @ B
    
    # 4. Compute modified elements
    # cov_basis is 3x3. Indices are [row, col]
    cov_00 = cov_basis[0, 0]
    cov_01 = cov_basis[0, 1]
    cov_02 = cov_basis[0, 2]
    cov_11 = cov_basis[1, 1]
    cov_12 = cov_basis[1, 2]
    cov_22 = cov_basis[2, 2]
    
    # Avoid division by zero if cov_22 is near zero, though typically positive definite
    if cov_22 < 1e-8:
        cov_22 = 1e-8
        
    a_prime = cov_00 - (cov_02 ** 2) / cov_22
    b_prime = cov_01 - (cov_02 * cov_12) / cov_22
    c_prime = cov_11 - (cov_12 ** 2) / cov_22
    
    # 5. Construct regularized basis covariance
    # Top-left 2x2: [[a', b'], [b', c']]
    # Off-diagonals with normal: 0
    # Normal-normal: min(a', c') / 100
    min_ac = min(a_prime, c_prime)
    sigma_basis = np.array([
        [a_prime, b_prime, 0.0],
        [b_prime, c_prime, 0.0],
        [0.0, 0.0, min_ac / 100.0]
    ])
    
    # 6. Transform back to world space
    sigma = B @ sigma_basis @ B.T
    
    return sigma
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Degenerate Normal:** If the normal vector is zero or near-zero, the basis construction fails. The code handles the specific case where $n_x=0$ and $n_y=0$ explicitly. For other near-zero normals, the normalization step should include a small epsilon guard to prevent NaNs [G].
*   **Positive Definiteness:** The original covariance $\Sigma$ must be positive definite. The transformation relies on $\text{cov}[2,2]$ being non-zero. If $\text{cov}[2,2]$ is extremely small, numerical instability may occur in the division steps [G].
*   **Frequency of Application:** The transformation is applied every 5000 iterations. Applying it too frequently may hinder convergence, while applying it too infrequently may not enforce the geometric constraint effectively [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Performance:** On the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset, 3D-HGS with an Efficient Rasterizer achieves 125 FPS and 29.66 PSNR using 3.8M kernels, compared to 3DGS which uses 3.2M kernels [S1]. On Tank & Dengin, it achieves 160 FPS and 24.45 PSNR with 1.8M kernels [S1]. On Tank & Blending, it achieves 126 FPS and 29.76 PSNR with 2.6M kernels [S1].
*   **Quality:** 3D-HGS consistently outperforms original 3D Gaussian Splatting across all metrics (PSNR, etc.) on evaluated datasets [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Better modeling of sharp transitions between object surfaces and empty space; cleaner representations by eliminating dust and noise via adjusted opacity thresholds [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires estimation of surface normals for the transformation; increased kernel count compared to standard 3DGS in some datasets (e.g., Mip-NeRF 360) [S1].
*   **When to use:** When surface geometry fidelity is critical and volumetric artifacts are undesirable.
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and the slight quality gain does not justify the additional complexity or kernel count.

## Background
3D Half-Gaussian Splatting (3D-HGS) was introduced to address the limitations of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting in representing sharp geometric boundaries. The method was evaluated against architectures including Scaffold, mip-splatting, and MCMC [S1]. The approach leverages the idea of splitting Gaussians along a plane defined by the surface normal to create half-ellipsoids, which more accurately model surface elements (surfels) rather than volumetric blobs [S1].
