# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which results in small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR increases pixel gradients in these regions by explicitly disentangling frequency components, thereby guiding adaptive densification toward meaningful high-frequency details [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) or [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial or geometric domain [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Image dimensions $H, W$.
*   **Outputs:** Scalar regularization loss $L_{FR} = d_a + d_p$ (or weighted sum), where $d_a$ is amplitude discrepancy and $d_p$ is phase discrepancy.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Frequency Representation:** Complex-valued matrices $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$.
*   **Amplitude/Phase Matrices:** Real-valued matrices $|F(u, v)|, \angle F(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$.

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Compute 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** Convert the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth image $I$ into their frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$. For a single channel, the DFT at frequency coordinates $(u, v)$ is computed as [S2]:
    $$
    F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)}
    $$
    where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value. This is performed for all channels or on luminance.

2.  **Decompose into Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency component $F(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$ [S2]:
    $$
    |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2}
    $$
    $$
    \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right)
    $$
    where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$. Perform this for both $\hat{F}$ and $F$.

3.  **Compute Amplitude Discrepancy ($d_a$):** Calculate the root-mean-square error between the amplitudes of the ground truth and rendered frequency spectra [S2]:
    $$
    d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2}
    $$
    Note: The summation indices in the source [S2] use $x, y$ for the spatial domain of the DFT output, but logically correspond to the frequency grid indices $(u, v)$. We use $(u, v)$ for clarity in implementation.

4.  **Compute Phase Discrepancy ($d_p$):** Calculate the root-mean-square error between the phases of the ground truth and rendered frequency spectra [S2]:
    $$
    d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2}
    $$

5.  **Compute Total Loss:** Combine the discrepancies. The base FR loss is typically the sum:
    $$
    L_{FR} = d_a + d_p
    $$
    [S2] notes that naive adoption can bias splatting towards artifacts; thus, advanced implementations (like FreGS) use **Frequency Annealing**. This involves applying a low-pass filter $H_l$ and a dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ to progressively regularize low-to-high frequencies, achieving coarse-to-fine densification [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimizer:** Adam [S2].
*   **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
*   **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are retained [S2].
*   **Annealing Schedule:** Not given in the sources. Requires implementation of low-pass/high-pass filters and a schedule to transition from low to high frequency regularization.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Phase Wrapping:** The $\arctan$ function is discontinuous at $\pm \pi$. When computing phase differences, care must be taken to handle the wrap-around (e.g., using circular distance) to avoid large errors near the branch cut [G].
*   **Over-Regularization:** Naively applying FR without frequency annealing can bias 3D Gaussian splatting towards undesirable artifacts and restrict densification [S2].
*   **Computational Cost:** Computing DFT for every training iteration adds significant overhead compared to spatial L1 loss.

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image: torch.Tensor, ground_truth: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the Frequency Regularization loss based on amplitude and phase discrepancy.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (C, H, W). Assumes normalized values.
        ground_truth: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (C, H, W).
        
    Returns:
        Scalar loss tensor.
    """
    # Ensure consistent shape: (C, H, W)
    if rendered_image.dim() == 3 and rendered_image.shape[0] != 3:
        rendered_image = rendered_image.permute(2, 0, 1)
    if ground_truth.dim() == 3 and ground_truth.shape[0] != 3:
        ground_truth = ground_truth.permute(2, 0, 1)
        
    H, W = rendered_image.shape[1], rendered_image.shape[2]
    
    # Compute 2D DFT for each channel
    # torch.fft.fft2 returns complex tensor of shape (C, H, W)
    F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image)
    F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth)
    
    # Compute Amplitude and Phase
    # Amplitude: sqrt(Re^2 + Im^2)
    amp_rendered = torch.abs(F_rendered)
    amp_gt = torch.abs(F_gt)
    
    # Phase: atan2(Im, Re) is more robust than atan(Im/Re)
    phase_rendered = torch.angle(F_rendered)
    phase_gt = torch.angle(F_gt)
    
    # Compute Amplitude Discrepancy (d_a)
    # RMSE over all pixels and channels
    # Source [S2] sums over H*W. We average over C, H, W for multi-channel consistency.
    diff_amp = amp_rendered - amp_gt
    d_a = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(diff_amp ** 2))
    
    # Compute Phase Discrepancy (d_p)
    # Note: Simple subtraction of angles can be problematic due to wrapping.
    # For implementation, we follow the source formula directly, but in practice,
    # circular distance is preferred. Here we implement the source's Euclidean metric on angles.
    diff_phase = phase_rendered - phase_gt
    d_p = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(diff_phase ** 2))
    
    # Total Loss
    loss = d_a + d_p
    
    return loss
```

## Subtopics

### Frequency Annealing
A variant introduced in FreGS that applies low-pass and dynamic high-pass filters in the Fourier space to progressively regularize from low to high frequencies, achieving coarse-to-fine Gaussian densification and avoiding artifacts associated with naive FR [S2].

### DWTGS
A method for sparse-view 3DGS that applies a Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) on the rendered image and penalizes the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges, rethinking frequency regularization for sparse data [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Computing the 2D DFT is $O(HW \log(HW))$ per image per iteration. This is significantly slower than the $O(1)$ pixel-wise L1 loss, potentially slowing down training convergence time [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing complex frequency matrices of size $H \times W$ for both rendered and ground truth images, plus intermediate amplitude/phase matrices. This is $O(HW)$ additional memory per iteration [S2].
*   **Measured Performance:** In the 'Bicycle' scene, FR significantly increased pixel gradients in over-reconstruction regions compared to the baseline 3D-GS method, enabling better densification [S2]. It alleviates over-reconstruction where L1 loss fails to differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Explicitly addresses over-reconstruction; improves Gaussian densification by revealing over-reconstructed regions via increased gradients; disentangles frequency components to guide high-frequency detail recovery [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Naive application can cause artifacts and restrict densification; computationally expensive; requires careful annealing schedules for optimal results [S2].
*   **When to use:** When the scene suffers from over-reconstruction (blurry or bloated Gaussians) and standard densification fails to remove redundant primitives.
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and training speed is the primary constraint, or when the scene is very low frequency and high-frequency regularization is unnecessary.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced in the context of 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization improvements, specifically in the work "FreGS: 3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization" [S2]. It addresses the limitation of the original 3D-GS optimization, which relies on pixel-wise L1 loss and can lead to over-reconstruction in some regions while underrepresenting others, resulting in blur and visual inconsistencies [S1, S2]. The method builds on the observation that frequency domain metrics can better capture structural discrepancies than spatial domain metrics alone [S2].
