# FreGS

Also called: Frequency-aware regularization for 3DGS, Progressive frequency regularization

## Overview
FreGS is a regularization method for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that prevents overfitting and high-frequency artifacts during training by penalizing discrepancies between the frequency spectra of rendered and ground-truth images [S1, unofficial][S2]. It addresses the problem of "over-reconstruction," where the model captures noise or fine details that do not generalize, by operating in the Fourier domain rather than raw pixel space [S2]. Unlike sibling methods that constrain geometry directly (e.g., [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md)) or use spatial gradients (e.g., [ABSGS](14222-geometry-regularization.md)), FreGS enforces spectral consistency, ensuring the rendered output matches the ground truth in terms of frequency content [S1, unofficial][S2].

## How it works
FreGS modifies the standard 3DGS training loop by adding a frequency-domain loss term. It operates on the rendered image and the corresponding ground-truth image.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $I_{render}$, Ground-truth image $I_{gt}$, current training iteration $t$, total iterations $T_{max}$, regularization weight $\lambda_{freq}$.
*   **Outputs:** Gradient updates for Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) derived from the total loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   Images are represented as 2D arrays of pixel values (RGB).
*   Frequency domain representations are complex-valued 2D arrays obtained via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Render:** Compute the rendered image $I_{render}$ using the current set of 3D Gaussians and the rasterization pipeline.
2.  **Transform to Frequency Domain:** Compute the 2D Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) of both the rendered image and the ground-truth image:
    $$ F_{render} = \text{FFT}(I_{render}) $$
    $$ F_{gt} = \text{FFT}(I_{gt}) $$
3.  **Compute Frequency Discrepancy:** Calculate the difference between the magnitude spectra of the rendered and ground-truth images. The magnitude spectrum $M$ is defined as:
    $$ M_{render}(u, v) = |F_{render}(u, v)| $$
    $$ M_{gt}(u, v) = |F_{gt}(u, v)| $$
    The frequency loss $\mathcal{L}_{freq}$ is the L1 norm of the difference between these magnitude spectra:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{freq} = \sum_{u, v} | M_{render}(u, v) - M_{gt}(u, v) | $$
    *Note: Some implementations may use a weighted sum to emphasize low frequencies or normalize by the ground-truth magnitude to prevent scale issues [S1, unofficial][S2].*
4.  **Progressive Application:** Apply the regularization progressively. The weight $\lambda_{freq}$ or the frequency band considered may be annealed over training iterations to introduce high-frequency constraints gradually, preventing early-stage instability [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Total Loss:** Combine with the photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{photo}$ (e.g., L1 + SSIM):
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photo} + \lambda_{freq} \mathcal{L}_{freq} $$
6.  **Backpropagate:** Compute gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{total}$ with respect to the Gaussian parameters and update them using an optimizer (e.g., Adam).

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_fregs_loss(rendered_image, gt_image, lambda_freq, iteration, max_iterations):
    # Step 1: Transform to frequency domain
    F_render = fft2(rendered_image)
    F_gt = fft2(gt_image)
    
    # Step 2: Compute magnitude spectra
    M_render = abs(F_render)
    M_gt = abs(F_gt)
    
    # Step 3: Compute frequency discrepancy (L1 loss on magnitudes)
    freq_loss = sum(abs(M_render - M_gt))
    
    # Step 4: Progressive weighting (example: linear annealing)
    # lambda_freq may increase over time to enforce high-frequency details later
    progress = iteration / max_iterations
    effective_lambda = lambda_freq * (0.5 + 0.5 * progress)
    
    return effective_lambda * freq_loss
```

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_{freq}$: Weight for the frequency loss. Typical values are not specified in the sources [S1, unofficial][S2].
*   Annealing schedule: Not specified in the sources [S1, unofficial][S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** FFT operations are sensitive to numerical precision; ensure images are normalized to a consistent range before transformation [G].
*   **Boundaries:** The FFT assumes periodic boundary conditions; artifacts may arise at image edges if not handled [G].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If the ground-truth image is constant or has no frequency content, the loss may vanish or become unstable [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The dominant operation is the 2D FFT, which is $O(N^2 \log N)$ for an image of size $N \times N$. This adds a non-negligible overhead to each training step compared to standard 3DGS [S1, unofficial][S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the frequency domain representations of the rendered and ground-truth images, doubling the memory for image buffers during the loss computation [S1, unofficial][S2].
*   **Performance:** FreGS reduces overfitting and high-frequency artifacts, leading to more stable training and potentially better generalization [S1, unofficial][S2]. However, it does not explicitly accelerate training; in fact, it may slow it down due to the FFT overhead [S1, unofficial][S2].
*   **Strengths:** Effective at controlling high-frequency detail recovery and preventing noise capture [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to FFT; may require careful tuning of $\lambda_{freq}$ and annealing schedules [S1, unofficial][S2].
*   **When to use:** When the model exhibits over-reconstruction or high-frequency artifacts, and computational resources allow for the additional FFT overhead [S1, unofficial][S2].
*   **When not to use:** When training speed is critical, or when the scene is low-frequency and does not benefit from spectral regularization [S1, unofficial][S2].

## Background
FreGS was introduced as a method to address over-reconstruction in 3D Gaussian Splatting by regularizing frequency discrepancies [S2]. It is part of a broader trend in differentiable rendering to use frequency-domain constraints for better control over detail recovery [S1, unofficial][S2]. The method is cited in surveys of 3DGS regularization and compression techniques [S1, unofficial][S2].
