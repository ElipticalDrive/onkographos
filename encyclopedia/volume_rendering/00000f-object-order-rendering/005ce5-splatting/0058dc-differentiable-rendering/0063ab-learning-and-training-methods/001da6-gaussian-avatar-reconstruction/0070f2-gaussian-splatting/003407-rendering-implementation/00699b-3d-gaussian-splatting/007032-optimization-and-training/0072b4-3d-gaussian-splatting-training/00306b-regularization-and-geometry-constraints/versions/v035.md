# Regularization and Geometry Constraints

Also called: Geometric Priors, Surface Constraints, Frequency Regularization

## Overview
Regularization and Geometry Constraints are training-time techniques for 3D Gaussian Splatting that impose physical or spectral priors to mitigate artifacts such as volumetric noise, floating geometry, and over-reconstruction [S1, S2]. Unlike standard photometric optimization which minimizes pixel-wise color differences, these methods constrain the geometric structure (via depth, normals, or covariance shapes) or the frequency spectrum of the rendered output to ensure the resulting Gaussian primitives form a coherent surface rather than an unstructured density cloud [S1, S2]. This family addresses the instability of unconstrained optimization by guiding densification and parameter updates toward physically plausible configurations [S1].

## Shared principles
All methods in this family modify the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting training loop by introducing additional loss terms or constraints that operate on geometric or spectral properties rather than raw pixel intensity alone.

1.  **Decoupling of Photometric and Geometric Optimization:** Many methods decouple the optimization of appearance (color/opacity) from geometry (position/shape). For instance, [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) freezes scale and rotation to prevent shape deformation while optimizing centers and opacities independently against depth priors [S1].
2.  **Constraint on Gaussian Primitives:** The regularization acts directly on the Gaussian parameters:
    *   **Covariance/Shape:** Methods like [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md) and [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md) enforce specific covariance structures (e.g., half-ellipsoids or flattened surflets) to align Gaussians with local surface normals [S1, S2].
    *   **Position/Depth:** Methods like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) and [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md) use depth maps (ground truth or pseudo-ground truth) to constrain the 3D positions of Gaussian centers [S1].
    *   **Frequency:** Methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [FreGS](9212-fregs.md) operate in the Fourier domain, penalizing discrepancies between the frequency spectra of rendered and ground-truth images to control high-frequency detail recovery [S2].
3.  **Iterative or Progressive Application:** Regularization is often applied progressively. [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) reduces the blur kernel size over iterations to transition from coarse to fine supervision [S4]. [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) may use frequency annealing to introduce high frequencies gradually [S2]. [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md) applies covariance transformation periodically (e.g., every 5000 iterations) [S1].
4.  **Loss Formulation:** The total loss $\mathcal{L}_{total}$ typically combines a photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{photo}$ (e.g., L1, SSIM) with a regularization term $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ weighted by a hyperparameter $\lambda$:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photo} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{reg} $$
    The specific form of $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ depends on the child method (e.g., amplitude/phase discrepancy in frequency domain, depth L1 loss, or covariance transformation error) [S1, S2].

## Subtopics

### [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md)
Frequency Regularization (FR) minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectra of rendered and ground-truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which fails to differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions, FR increases pixel gradients in over-reconstructed areas, enabling adaptive densification by explicitly disentangling frequency components. It addresses the problem of improper Gaussians and visual inconsistencies caused by unconstrained optimization.

### [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md)
Smoothed 2D Supervision enhances the robustness of joint optimization by smoothing the 2D supervision signal. It addresses the instability in camera alignment and the overfitting to high-frequency noise by blurring 2D training images with a parallel set of scheduled 2D Gaussian kernels. Unlike [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) which operates in the Fourier domain, this method applies spatial-domain smoothing to the target images. It sets itself apart from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by constraining the photometric signal's spectrum rather than geometric proxies.

### [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md)
This method regularizes 3D Gaussian kernels to represent half-ellipsoids rather than full ellipsoids, enforcing surface-like geometry by cutting Gaussians along their local normal direction. It solves the problem of volumetric noise and "dust" in Gaussian Splatting by ensuring that opacity is concentrated on one side of the surface, thereby improving reconstruction fidelity and training stability. It differs from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by operating directly on the covariance structure to enforce a half-space constraint, rather than relying on external depth supervision or frequency-domain penalties.

### [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md)
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization is a geometric constraint strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by decoupling the optimization of Gaussian centers from their opacities. It sets itself apart from standard depth regularization methods (which often deform Gaussian shapes to fit smooth monocular depth) by freezing scale and rotation parameters, thereby encouraging spatial reshaping of primitives rather than shape deformation. This approach balances coarse depth fitting with fine color appearance preservation.

### [FreGS](9212-fregs.md)
FreGS addresses the over-reconstruction and high-frequency artifacts common in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) training by applying progressive frequency regularization to the Gaussian primitives. Unlike sibling methods that focus on spatial geometry or pixel-level gradients, FreGS operates in the frequency domain, penalizing discrepancies between the Fourier spectra of rendered and ground-truth images to guide high-frequency detail recovery while preventing noise. It builds on [Regularization and Geometry Constraints](6269-regularization-and-geometry-constraints.md) by defining $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ specifically through spectral analysis.

### [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md)
Cross-scale geometric adaptation is a self-adaptive regularization mechanism that guides geometry learning in few-shot neural rendering by utilizing reprojection errors across multiple voxel scales. It solves the problem of geometric inconsistency and floating artifacts in sparse-view scenarios by selecting render depths with minimal reprojection error across different scales as pseudo-labels, ensuring geometric integrity without requiring explicit depth ground truth. It sets itself apart from single-scale depth priors by progressively adapting to scene frequencies, using low-resolution voxels for coarse geometry early in training and high-resolution voxels for detail refinement later, thereby avoiding the scale mismatches that cause single-scale methods to suffer from higher final error.

### [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md)
Geometry regularization is a training technique that improves surface smoothness and reduces floating artifacts by constraining the geometric shape of 3D Gaussians, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains position. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating directly on the spatial covariance structure rather than the image frequency spectrum, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by enforcing orientation and aspect-ratio constraints rather than just depth value consistency.

#### Wild-GS
- [S1] A method that aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects and constrain geometry.

#### Source Feature Extrapolation
- [S2] A technique to improve extrapolation capabilities beyond source view frustums by padding source images and adding positional encoding channels for padded regions, preventing smearing artifacts in regions not visible by source images.
- [S2] Apply border padding to source images I(i).
- [S2] Create a channel $\Gamma$ containing pixel-wise positional encodings for padded regions: $\Gamma[u,v] = \gamma(u,v)$ if $(u,v)$ is not in $I(i)$, else 0. Concatenate $I_{pad}$ and $\Gamma$ to form $I'(i) = \text{concatenate}(I_{pad}, \Gamma)$.
- [S2] $\gamma(\cdot)$ denotes positional encoding with exponential frequencies.

#### SDF-Regularized 3DGS
- [S3] Methods to improve geometric fidelity in 3DGS by imposing physical constraints, such as Signed Distance Fields (SDFs), monocular depth priors, or multi-view geometric constraints, during optimization.
- [S3] Introduce a regularization term to minimize the difference between an ideal SDF and the SDF calculated from Gaussians.
- [S3] Use a dual-branch framework with GS- and SDF-branches, jointly optimized to provide mutual guidance.
- [S3] Incorporate geometric regularization such as monocular depth priors or multi-plane Gaussian optimization.

#### MVG-Splatting
- [S4] A method that employs a depth map to enforce geometric consistency, specifically applying geometric consistency only in the near and far regions of the scene to effectively mitigate under-reconstruction in these critical regions while avoiding the risk of over-reconstruction.

#### Shape Energy Constraint
- [S5] An energy term defined as the combination of opacity energy and boundary energy, used to preserve the shape, opacity, and unitized colors (spherical harmonics) of Gaussian radiance fields during deformation.
- [S5] Relying solely on feature energy is insufficient to preserve opacity and unitized colors; gray Gaussians with SH values close to zero appear and their size becomes uncontrolled because they cannot be constrained by feature energy alone.
- [S5] Without $E_{shape}$, the optimization process becomes unstable and artifacts appear; with $E_{shape}$, the process stabilizes and these artifacts are mitigated.

## Background
The problem of "floating artifacts" and unstructured density clouds in 3D Gaussian Splatting arises because the original optimization focuses solely on photometric consistency, which does not enforce surface-like geometry [S3]. To recover high-quality geometry, recent methods impose physical constraints during optimization, such as integrating Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) or using monocular depth priors [S3]. Wild-GS [29] aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects [S1]. MVG-Splatting [29] and Mini-Splatting [13] employ depth maps to enforce geometric consistency, with MVG-Splatting applying it specifically in near and far regions [S4]. Shape Energy constraints [S5] have been introduced to preserve the shape and colors of Gaussian radiance fields during deformation, preventing instability caused by unconstrained optimization [S5].
