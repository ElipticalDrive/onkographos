# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectra of rendered and ground-truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which fails to differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions, FR increases pixel gradients in over-reconstructed areas, enabling adaptive densification by explicitly disentangling frequency components [S2]. It addresses the problem of improper Gaussians and visual inconsistencies caused by unconstrained optimization [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground-truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, and hyperparameters for regularization weight $\lambda$ and frequency annealing schedule.
- **Outputs:** Regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{FR}$ used to update Gaussian parameters (position, covariance, opacity, color) via an optimizer (typically Adam) [S2].

**Data Structures:**
- Images are represented as 2D arrays of pixel values.
- Frequency representations are complex-valued 2D arrays resulting from the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT).
- Amplitude and Phase are scalar fields derived from the complex frequency values.

**Steps:**
1. **Compute 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** Convert the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground-truth image $I$ into their frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$. For a single channel, the DFT is computed as [S2]:
   $$ F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
   where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, $H$ and $W$ are image height and width, and $i$ is the imaginary unit. This is applied to all $C$ channels.

2. **Compute Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency component $(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$ [S2]:
   $$ |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2} $$
   $$ \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right) $$
   where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary parts of $F(u, v)$.

3. **Calculate Discrepancies:** Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ between the ground truth and rendered frequency representations [S2]:
   $$ d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2} $$
   $$ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2} $$
   Note: The summation indices in the source [S2] use $(x,y)$ but the formula context implies summation over frequency coordinates $(u,v)$. The standard interpretation for spectral discrepancy is summing over the frequency domain indices. The source text explicitly writes $\sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1}$ inside the square root for $d_a$ and $d_p$, but the terms inside are functions of $F(u,v)$. Given the normalization factor $1/HW$, this is an RMS error over the frequency spectrum. We follow the source's notation but interpret the domain of summation as the frequency grid corresponding to the image size.

4. **Combine Loss:** The frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of amplitude and phase discrepancies:
   $$ \mathcal{L}_{FR} = \lambda_a d_a + \lambda_p d_p $$
   where $\lambda_a$ and $\lambda_p$ are weights. The total training loss becomes $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photo} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{FR}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{photo}$ is the photometric loss (e.g., L1 or SSIM) [S1, S2].

5. **Frequency Annealing (Optional/Advanced):** To prevent bias towards undesirable artifacts and enable coarse-to-fine densification, a frequency annealing technique can be applied [S2]. This involves incorporating a low-pass filter $H_l$ and a dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ in Fourier space. The regularization is applied progressively, starting with low frequencies and gradually incorporating high frequencies [S2].

**Parameters:**
- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
- **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
- **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are used [S2].
- **Annealing:** Progressive application of frequency bands [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Over-reconstruction vs. Under-reconstruction:** Naive FR without annealing may still suffer from restricted densification and bias towards artifacts if it does not distinguish between low and high frequencies [S2].
- **Gradient Vanishing:** In well-reconstructed regions, L1 loss yields small gradients, preventing densification. FR increases gradients in over-reconstructed regions, but care must be taken not to destabilize optimization in already converged areas [S2].
- **Complex Arithmetic:** Implementation requires careful handling of complex number operations for DFT and phase unwrapping if necessary (though arctan2 is standard).

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.fft

def compute_frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image, ground_truth_image, lambda_a=1.0, lambda_p=1.0):
    """
    Computes the Frequency Regularization loss based on amplitude and phase discrepancies.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image (torch.Tensor): Rendered image of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C).
        ground_truth_image (torch.Tensor): Ground truth image of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C).
        lambda_a (float): Weight for amplitude discrepancy.
        lambda_p (float): Weight for phase discrepancy.
        
    Returns:
        torch.Tensor: Scalar loss value.
    """
    # Ensure inputs are float32 and normalized if necessary
    # Assume inputs are in range [0, 1] or similar, consistent with DFT expectations
    
    # Compute 2D FFT for all channels
    # torch.fft.fft2 expects input shape (..., H, W)
    # If input is (B, H, W, C), we need to move C to the last dimension or handle per-channel
    
    # Let's assume input is (B, H, W, C)
    # Move C to last dim for FFT if not already
    if rendered_image.dim() == 4:
        # (B, H, W, C) -> (B, C, H, W) for FFT? No, fft2 works on last two dims.
        # So (B, H, W, C) -> fft2 along H, W. Result (B, H, W, C) complex.
        F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image, dim=(-2, -1))
        F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth_image, dim=(-2, -1))
    elif rendered_image.dim() == 3:
        # (H, W, C)
        F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image, dim=(-2, -1))
        F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth_image, dim=(-2, -1))
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unsupported tensor dimensions")

    # Compute Amplitude and Phase
    # Amplitude: sqrt(Re^2 + Im^2)
    amp_rendered = torch.abs(F_rendered)
    amp_gt = torch.abs(F_gt)
    
    # Phase: arctan2(Im, Re)
    phase_rendered = torch.angle(F_rendered)
    phase_gt = torch.angle(F_gt)
    
    # Compute Discrepancies
    # RMS error over spatial dimensions (H, W) for each channel and batch
    # Source [S2] uses sum over x,y divided by HW. This is RMS over the image/frequency grid.
    
    # Amplitude discrepancy d_a
    # Note: Source [S2] formula sums over x,y but uses F(u,v). 
    # In DFT domain, the indices are frequency indices. 
    # The formula in [S2] is: sqrt(1/HW * sum_{x,y} (|F| - |F_hat|)^2)
    # This implies summing over the frequency grid indices (u,v) which have size HxW.
    
    diff_amp = amp_rendered - amp_gt
    # Mean squared error over H, W, C (and Batch if present)
    # We want the average over the frequency spectrum.
    # If B is present, we might average over B as well or sum. 
    # Standard loss reduction is mean.
    
    mse_amp = torch.mean(diff_amp ** 2) # Mean over all dims
    d_a = torch.sqrt(mse_amp)
    
    # Phase discrepancy d_p
    diff_phase = phase_rendered - phase_gt
    mse_phase = torch.mean(diff_phase ** 2)
    d_p = torch.sqrt(mse_phase)
    
    # Combine
    loss = lambda_a * d_a + lambda_p * d_p
    
    return loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
- Computing the 2D DFT for an image of size $H \times W$ takes $O(HW \log(HW))$ time using FFT.
- Memory usage increases by the storage of complex frequency arrays, which is $O(HW)$ per channel.
- The overhead is added per training iteration.

**Measured Performance:**
- In the 'Bicycle' scene, FR significantly increased pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to L1 loss alone [S2].
- FR alleviates over-reconstruction and improves Gaussian densification [S2].

**Scaling:**
- FR scales with image resolution due to the FFT complexity.
- Progressive frequency regularization (annealing) allows for coarse-to-fine optimization, potentially reducing the number of Gaussians needed for high fidelity [S2].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
- **Strengths:** Effectively identifies over-reconstructed regions that L1 loss misses, leading to better densification and sharper details [S2]. Disentangles frequency components to guide high-frequency detail [S1].
- **Limitations:** Naive adoption without frequency annealing can lead to restricted densification and bias towards undesirable artifacts [S2]. Adds computational overhead per iteration [S2].

**When to Use:**
- Use when standard L1/SSIM loss leads to over-reconstruction or blurry details.
- Use when adaptive densification is needed to capture high-frequency structures [S2].

**When Not to Use:**
- Avoid if computational resources are extremely limited and the overhead of FFT per iteration is prohibitive.
- Avoid if the scene is very low frequency and simple photometric loss is sufficient.

## Background
**Origin:**
- Frequency Regularization was introduced as part of the FreGS method (3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization) [S2].
- It is cited as a direction for improving 3D GS optimization, alongside geometry-aware approaches [S1].

**History and Development:**
- The method addresses the limitation of pixel-wise L1 loss in 3D Gaussian Splatting, which cannot differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions [S2].
- It builds on the idea that amplitude and phase in the Fourier domain capture different information, allowing for more precise regularization [S2].
- The concept of using frequency domain constraints for optimization is extended in variants like DWTGS, which uses Discrete Wavelet Transform for sparse-view scenarios [S4, unofficial].

**Where it is Used:**
- Used in 3D Gaussian Splatting training pipelines to improve visual quality and densification [S2].
- Applied in datasets like Mip-NeRF360 and Tank&Temple [S2].
