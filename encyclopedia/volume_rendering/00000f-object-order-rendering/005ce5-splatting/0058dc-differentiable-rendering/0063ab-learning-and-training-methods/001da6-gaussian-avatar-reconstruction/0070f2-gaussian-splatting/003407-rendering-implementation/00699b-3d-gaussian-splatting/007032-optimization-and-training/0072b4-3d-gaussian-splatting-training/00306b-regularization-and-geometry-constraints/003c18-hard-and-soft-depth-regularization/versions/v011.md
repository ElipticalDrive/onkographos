# Hard and Soft Depth Regularization

Also called: Spatial Reshaping Regularization

## Overview
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization is a training strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by constraining geometry through the movement of Gaussian centers rather than the deformation of their shapes [S1]. It solves the conflict that arises when standard depth regularization forces anisotropic Gaussians to conform to smooth monocular depth estimates, which contradicts complex color appearance [S1]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating in spatial parameter space (centers/opacities) rather than image frequency space, and from [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md) by explicitly decoupling shape deformation from depth fitting [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Current set of 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G}$, each defined by center $\mu$, scale $s$, rotation quaternion $q$, opacity $\alpha$, and spherical harmonics coefficients for color.
*   A monocular depth estimate $D_{mono}$ for the current view.
*   Camera parameters for the current view.

**Outputs:**
*   Gradient updates for Gaussian centers $\mu$ and opacities $\alpha$.
*   Scale parameters $s$ and rotations $q$ remain frozen during this specific regularization step.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian State:** Standard 3DGS representation.
*   **Depth Maps:** Two distinct depth maps are rendered from the current Gaussian state:
    1.  **Hard Depth Map ($D_{hard}$):** Computed using the weighted average of Gaussian depths, weighted by opacity and alpha-blending weights. This map represents the primary geometric surface.
    2.  **Soft Depth Map ($D_{soft}$):** Computed using a different weighting scheme, typically emphasizing the contribution of Gaussians with higher opacity or specific depth intervals to capture fine color appearance details [S1]. *Note: The exact weighting for $D_{soft}$ is not explicitly defined in the source beyond "rendering two distinct depth maps" [S1].*

**Steps:**
1.  **Freeze Shape Parameters:** Set gradients for scale $s$ and rotation $q$ to zero for all Gaussians. This prevents the Gaussian ellipsoids from deforming to fit the depth map [S1].
2.  **Render Depth Maps:**
    *   Render $D_{hard}$ from the current Gaussian state.
    *   Render $D_{soft}$ from the current Gaussian state.
3.  **Compute Hard Depth Loss:** Calculate the loss $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ between $D_{hard}$ and the monocular depth estimate $D_{mono}$. This loss drives the coarse geometric alignment.
4.  **Compute Soft Depth Loss:** Calculate the loss $\mathcal{L}_{soft}$ between $D_{soft}$ and $D_{mono}$. This loss drives the fine detail preservation.
5.  **Compute Gradients:**
    *   Compute gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ with respect to Gaussian centers $\mu$.
    *   Compute gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{soft}$ with respect to Gaussian opacities $\alpha$.
    *   *Note: The source states gradients update "only the center positions" for the first map and "only the opacities" for the second [S1]. In practice, this implies masking the gradient computation or zeroing out irrelevant gradients before the optimizer step.*
6.  **Apply Updates:** Update $\mu$ using gradients from $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ and $\alpha$ using gradients from $\mathcal{L}_{soft}$.

**Formulas:**
*   **Total Regularization Loss:**
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{reg} = \lambda_{hard} \mathcal{L}_{hard}(D_{hard}, D_{mono}) + \lambda_{soft} \mathcal{L}_{soft}(D_{soft}, D_{mono}) $$
    where $\lambda_{hard}$ and $\lambda_{soft}$ are weighting factors [S1].
*   **Loss Functions:** The specific form of $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{soft}$ (e.g., L1, L2, Scale-Invariant Log) is not explicitly defined in the source, but typically aligns with standard depth loss metrics [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_{hard}, \lambda_{soft}$: Not given in the sources.
*   Freeze $s, q$: Boolean, always true during this step [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Detail Loss:** Applying depth regularization directly to NeRF-style MLPs or un-frozen Gaussian shapes forces primitive geometry to conform to smooth monocular depth, conflicting with complex color appearance and causing blurred results [S1].
*   **Sensitivity:** Gaussian radiance fields are more sensitive to small depth errors than NeRFs, potentially leading to noisy primitive distributions in texture-rich regions [S1].
*   **Scale Invariance:** Standard scale-invariant depth losses may overlook minor depth variations if not properly normalized, though this specific issue is addressed by the sibling method [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md) [S1].

## Subtopics

### Geometric Regularization via Bilateral Filter
A regularization loss that enforces coherent motion by grouping Gaussians in image space using a bilateral filter approach, avoiding the dampening of independent motion caused by spatial KNN [S2]. It sets itself apart from standard bilateral filters by not dividing the loss by the sum of weights, as the goal is comparison rather than energy conservation [S2].
*   **When to choose:** When dealing with scenes containing oscillatory or independent motion (e.g., leaves on a tree) where spatial proximity does not imply motion coherence [S2].
*   **Link:** [Adaptive Spatio‐Temporal 3D Gaussian Splatting](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.70410) [S2].
*   **Method:**
    1.  For a training image $I$ at time $t_I$, sample another time $t'$.
    2.  Compute the 6D screw-axis representation of the transformation between $t_I$ and $t'$ for each Gaussian.
    3.  Blend these vectors to form an image.
    4.  For each pixel $p$, compute the weighted sum of squared distances between the splatted screw-axis representations of $p$ and neighboring pixels $p'$.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}} = \sum_{p' \in \mathcal{N}(p)} k_{c,c'} k_{d,d'} k_{p,p'} \|s_p - s_{p'}\|^2 $$
    where $k_{c,c'}$ is color similarity, $k_{d,d'}$ is depth similarity, $k_{p,p'}$ is spatial distance weight, and $s_p, s_{p'}$ are screw-axis representations [S2].
*   **Pitfall:** Do not divide by the sum of weights [S2].

### Isotropic Regularization
Maintains coherent 3D structure and optimizes newly inserted Gaussians using current and random past keyframes to prevent forgetting [S3, unofficial]. It sets itself apart by penalizing elongation along the viewing direction to avoid artifacts in continuous SLAM scenarios [S3, unofficial].
*   **When to choose:** In continuous SLAM or sparse-view scenarios where Gaussians tend to become highly elongated along the viewing direction, causing tracking challenges [S3, unofficial].
*   **Link:** [Gaussian Splatting SLAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06741) [S3, unofficial].
*   **Method:**
    1.  Use keyframes in current window $W_k$ and two random past keyframes $W_r$ per iteration.
    2.  Compute the mean scaling $\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_i$ for each Gaussian.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ E_{iso} = \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{G}|} \|\mathbf{s}_i - \tilde{\mathbf{s}}_i \cdot \mathbf{1}\|_1 $$
    where $\mathbf{s}_i$ are scaling parameters, $\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_i$ is their mean, and $\mathbf{1}$ is a vector of ones [S3, unofficial].
*   **Pitfall:** None explicitly stated, but the method is designed to prevent artifacts from viewing-direction elongation [S3, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of Gaussians and the cost of rendering two depth maps. Rendering depth maps is $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of Gaussians. Gradient computation is $O(N)$.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing two additional depth maps per view, adding $O(W \times H)$ memory per view, where $W, H$ are image dimensions.
*   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Scaling:** Scales linearly with the number of Gaussians.
*   **Strengths:** Prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by preserving Gaussian shape; balances coarse depth fitting with fine color appearance [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of $\lambda_{hard}$ and $\lambda_{soft}$; sensitive to noise in monocular depth estimates [S1].
*   **When to use:** When training 3D Gaussian Splatting with monocular depth priors and observing blurred details or shape distortion [S1].
*   **When not:** When high-frequency geometric details are less important than color fidelity, or when computational resources are extremely limited [S1].

## Background
Originated in the context of optimizing sparse-view 3D Gaussian Radiance Fields, specifically addressing the limitations of NeRF-style depth regularization when applied to Gaussian splatting [S1]. The method was proposed to resolve the geometry conflict where smooth monocular depth estimates force Gaussian shapes to deform, leading to quality degradation [S1]. It is used in conjunction with other regularization techniques like [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md) to improve detailed geometry reshaping [S1].
