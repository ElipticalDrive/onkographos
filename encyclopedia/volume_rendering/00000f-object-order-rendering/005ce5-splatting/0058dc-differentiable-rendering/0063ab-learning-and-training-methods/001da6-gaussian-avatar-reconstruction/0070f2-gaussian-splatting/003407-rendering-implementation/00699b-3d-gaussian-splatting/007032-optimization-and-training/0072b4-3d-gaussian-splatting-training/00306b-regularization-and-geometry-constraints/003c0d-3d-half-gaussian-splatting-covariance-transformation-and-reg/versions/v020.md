# 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization

Also called: 3D-HGS

## Overview
This method regularizes the covariance matrices of 3D Gaussians to enforce a "half-Gaussian" geometry, effectively modeling sharp surface boundaries by cutting ellipsoids along their tangent planes. It solves the problem of "floaters" and blurred surfaces common in standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by penalizing covariance components that extend into empty space. It sets itself apart from siblings like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by operating directly on the primitive's geometric shape in 3D space rather than on rendered image features or depth maps.

## How it works
The method modifies the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ of each Gaussian to constrain its shape to a half-ellipsoid aligned with the local surface normal. This transformation is applied periodically during training.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** The original 3D covariance matrix $\Sigma$ (3x3 symmetric positive definite), and the estimated surface normal vector $n$ for the Gaussian.
*   **Output:** The regularized covariance matrix $\sigma$ (3x3), which represents a half-Gaussian.

### Data Structures
The covariance matrix is stored as a 3x3 symmetric matrix. In implementation, this is often represented by 6 unique values or a full 3x3 matrix with symmetry constraints. The basis matrix is a 3x3 orthogonal matrix.

### Algorithm Steps
The regularization is performed every 5000 iterations [S1].

1.  **Normalize the normal vector:**
    $$ n \leftarrow \frac{n}{\|n\|} $$

2.  **Construct the basis matrix:**
    Define an orthonormal basis $\{v_1, v_2, n\}$ where $v_1$ and $v_2$ span the tangent plane.
    *   If $n_x = 0$ and $n_y = 0$:
        $$ v_1 = (1, 0, 0) $$
        $$ v_2 = (0, 1, 0) $$
    *   Else:
        $$ v_1 \leftarrow \frac{(n_y, -n_x, 0)}{\|(n_y, -n_x, 0)\|} $$
        $$ v_2 \leftarrow \frac{n \times v_1}{\|n \times v_1\|} $$
    *   Concatenate to form the basis matrix $B$:
        $$ B = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ v_1 & v_2 & n \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix} $$

3.  **Transform covariance to basis space:**
    $$ cov \leftarrow B^T \Sigma B $$
    Here, $cov$ is the representation of $\Sigma$ in the local coordinate system defined by $B$.

4.  **Compute modified diagonal elements:**
    Calculate the Schur complement-like terms for the tangent plane components:
    $$ a' \leftarrow cov[0, 0] - \frac{cov[0, 2]^2}{cov[2, 2]} $$
    $$ b' \leftarrow cov[0, 1] - \frac{cov[0, 2] \cdot cov[1, 2]}{cov[2, 2]} $$
    $$ c' \leftarrow cov[1, 1] - \frac{cov[1, 2]^2}{cov[2, 2]} $$

5.  **Construct the regularized covariance in basis space:**
    Create a new matrix $\sigma_{local}$ where the tangent-tangent block is modified, and the normal-normal component is heavily penalized:
    $$ \sigma_{local} = \begin{bmatrix} a' & b' & 0 \\ b' & c' & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \min(a', c') / 100 \end{bmatrix} $$
    Note: The off-diagonal terms connecting the tangent plane to the normal direction ($[0,2]$ and $[1,2]$) are set to 0. The normal component $[2,2]$ is reduced to $\min(a', c') / 100$, effectively flattening the Gaussian along the normal axis to create a half-ellipsoid shape [S1].

6.  **Transform back to world space:**
    $$ \sigma \leftarrow B \sigma_{local} B^T $$

### Parameters
*   **Regularization Interval:** 5000 iterations [S1].
*   **Opacity Threshold for Densification/Pruning:** Increased to 0.01 [S1].
*   **Opacity Reset for Half-Gaussians:** Set to 0.02 [S1].

### Pseudocode
```python
def regularize_covariance(cov_world: Matrix3x3, normal: Vector3) -> Matrix3x3:
    """
    Transforms a 3D Gaussian covariance into a half-Gaussian covariance
    aligned with the given surface normal.
    
    Args:
        cov_world: 3x3 symmetric covariance matrix in world space.
        normal: 3D vector representing the surface normal.
        
    Returns:
        Regularized 3x3 covariance matrix.
    """
    # 1. Normalize normal
    n = normal / np.linalg.norm(normal)
    
    # 2. Construct basis
    if n[0] == 0 and n[1] == 0:
        v1 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
        v2 = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
    else:
        v1 = np.array([n[1], -n[0], 0.0])
        v1 = v1 / np.linalg.norm(v1)
        v2 = np.cross(n, v1)
        v2 = v2 / np.linalg.norm(v2)
        
    B = np.column_stack((v1, v2, n))
    
    # 3. Transform to basis space
    cov_basis = B.T @ cov_world @ B
    
    # 4. Compute modified elements
    # Ensure cov_basis[2,2] is not zero to avoid division by zero
    denom = cov_basis[2, 2]
    if denom < 1e-8:
        denom = 1e-8
        
    a_prime = cov_basis[0, 0] - (cov_basis[0, 2]**2) / denom
    b_prime = cov_basis[0, 1] - (cov_basis[0, 2] * cov_basis[1, 2]) / denom
    c_prime = cov_basis[1, 1] - (cov_basis[1, 2]**2) / denom
    
    # 5. Construct regularized matrix in basis space
    min_ac = min(a_prime, c_prime)
    sigma_local = np.array([
        [a_prime, b_prime, 0.0],
        [b_prime, c_prime, 0.0],
        [0.0, 0.0, min_ac / 100.0]
    ])
    
    # 6. Transform back to world space
    sigma_world = B @ sigma_local @ B.T
    
    return sigma_world
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Division by Zero:** If $cov[2, 2]$ (the variance along the normal in basis space) is zero or near-zero, the division in step 4 will fail. A small epsilon guard is required [G].
*   **Degenerate Normals:** If the normal vector is zero, normalization fails. The basis construction assumes a valid normal [G].
*   **Precision:** The reduction of the normal component by a factor of 100 is aggressive. If the original covariance is ill-conditioned, this may lead to numerical instability in the Cholesky decomposition used for sampling or rendering [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The transformation involves matrix multiplications ($3 \times 3$) and is applied every 5000 iterations. The overhead per iteration is negligible, but the periodic application adds a small computational cost during those specific steps.
*   **Memory Complexity:** No additional memory is required beyond the existing covariance and normal storage.
*   **Performance:** On the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset, 3D-HGS with an Efficient Rasterizer achieves 125 FPS and 29.66 PSNR using 3.8M kernels, compared to 3DGS which uses 3.2M kernels [S1]. On Tank & Dengin, it achieves 160 FPS and 24.45 PSNR with 1.8M kernels [S1]. On Tank & Blending, it achieves 126 FPS and 29.76 PSNR with 2.6M kernels [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Consistently outperforms original 3DGS in PSNR and other metrics [S1]. Effectively eliminates dust and noise by adjusting opacity thresholds [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires accurate surface normals for the transformation. The method is evaluated against Scaffold, mip-splatting, and MCMC, showing superiority over 3DGS but specific comparisons to other regularization methods depend on the baseline [S1].

## Background
The method originates from the paper "3D-HGS: 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting" [S1]. It was developed to address the limitations of full ellipsoidal Gaussians in representing sharp geometric boundaries. By cutting the Gaussian along the tangent plane, it better models the transition between object surfaces and empty space, as visualized by the alignment of splitting plane normals with scene surface normals [S1].
