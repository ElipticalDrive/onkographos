# Geometry-aware Gaussian Splatting

Also called: GeoGaussian

## Overview
Geometry-aware Gaussian Splatting (GeoGaussian) is a method for novel view synthesis that initializes and optimizes 3D Gaussians to explicitly preserve scene geometry and texture, particularly in low-textured or smooth regions where standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) often degrades [S1]. It distinguishes itself by parameterizing Gaussians in smoothly connected areas as thin ellipsoids aligned with surface normals and enforcing co-planar constraints during densification and optimization, unlike standard 3DGS which treats all points as general ellipsoids [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Input point cloud (from SLAM/SfM), camera poses, and training images.
**Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussians with optimized parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, spherical harmonics) representing the scene.

### Data Structures and Parameterization
The method maintains two groups of Gaussians: $S_{ind}$ (individual/general points) and $S_{co}$ (smooth surface points).

1.  **General Gaussian ($G \in S_{ind}$):**
    *   Standard 3DGS parameterization.
    *   Scale $S$ is determined by the distance to the nearest neighbor in the point cloud.
    *   Rotation $R$ and Scale $S$ are optimized freely.

2.  **Thin Gaussian ($G_\theta \in S_{co}$):**
    *   Designed for smoothly connected areas.
    *   **Position:** $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
    *   **Scale:** $S = [s_1, s_2, s_3]^T$. The third component $s_3$ is **fixed** to a small constant (0.001) to enforce thinness. Only $s_1, s_2$ are optimized [S1].
    *   **Rotation:** $R = [r_1, r_2, r_3] \in SO(3)$. The third column $r_3$ is **fixed** to the surface normal vector $n$ extracted from the point cloud. It is decoupled from the covariance optimization. $r_1, r_2$ are orthonormal basis vectors perpendicular to $r_3$ [S1].
    *   **Covariance:** $\Sigma_w = R S S^T R^T$. Since $s_3$ is fixed and $r_3$ is fixed, the shape is constrained to be a thin disk aligned with the normal [S1].
    *   **Color:** Spherical Harmonics coefficients $C$.
    *   **Opacity:** $\alpha$.

### Initialization
1.  **Normal Extraction:** Extract surface normals from the input point cloud [S1].
2.  **Reliability Check:** Classify points into $S_{ind}$ and $S_{co}$ based on normal reliability:
    *   A point is considered unreliable (assigned to $S_{ind}$) if:
        *   It has a long distance from other points.
        *   Its neighbors within a distance threshold have normals with large angle differences relative to the point's normal [S1].
    *   Points with reliable normals are assigned to $S_{co}$ [S1].
3.  **Parameter Setup:**
    *   For $S_{ind}$: Initialize scale using nearest-neighbor distance. Initialize rotation and scale randomly or via standard 3DGS initialization.
    *   For $S_{co}$: Initialize $r_3 = n$. Initialize $s_3 = 0.001$. Initialize $s_1, s_2$ based on local density or standard initialization. Initialize $r_1, r_2$ to complete the orthonormal basis [S1].

### Rendering
Rendering follows the standard differentiable rasterization pipeline [S1]:
1.  **Projection:** Project 3D Gaussian $N(\mu_w, \Sigma_w)$ to 2D image plane $N(\mu_I, \Sigma_I)$:
    $$ \mu_I = \Pi(T_{cw} \mu_w) $$
    $$ \Sigma_I = J W_{cw} \Sigma_w W_{cw}^T J^T $$
    Where $T_{cw} \in SE(3)$ is the camera pose, $W_{cw}$ is the rotational component, $J$ is the Jacobian of the projective transformation, and $\Pi$ is the projection function [S1].
2.  **Blending:** Synthesize pixel color $C_p$:
    $$ C_p = \sum_{i \in N} c^i \alpha_w^i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_w^i) $$
    Where $c^i$ is the color (from SH) and $\alpha_w^i$ is the opacity of the $i$-th Gaussian [S1].

### Densification
Densification occurs during training. The process differs for $S_{ind}$ and $S_{co}$ [S1].

1.  **For $S_{ind}$ (Traditional):**
    *   Split large Gaussians into two smaller Gaussians located randomly inside the original ellipsoid [S1].

2.  **For $S_{co}$ (Tangential Constrained):**
    *   **Cloning:**
        *   Calculate the accumulated gradient of the position $\mu$ over 10 iterations, denoted $\delta \mu_i$ [S1].
        *   If $\|\delta \mu_i\| > \gamma$ (threshold), clone the Gaussian.
        *   The new position $\mu_{i+1}$ is calculated by projecting the gradient onto the tangent plane (removing the normal component):
            $$ \mu_{i+1} = \mu_i + \delta \mu_i - r_3^T \delta \mu_i $$
            *(Note: The source formula $\mu_{i+1} = \mu_i + \delta \mu_i - r_3^\intercal \delta \mu_i$ implies a projection operation where $r_3^T \delta \mu_i$ is the scalar projection, but geometrically, to remove the component along $r_3$, one subtracts the vector projection $(r_3^T \delta \mu_i) r_3$. The source text states "decoupling the component perpendicular to the normal... to guide the position". The formula provided in the source is $\mu_{i+1} = \mu_i + \delta \mu_i - r_3^\intercal \delta \mu_i$. Given $r_3$ is a vector and $\delta \mu_i$ is a vector, $r_3^\intercal \delta \mu_i$ is a scalar. Subtracting a scalar from a vector is dimensionally inconsistent unless interpreted as subtracting the vector projection. However, we must follow the source's explicit formula. If the source implies vector subtraction, it likely means $\mu_{i+1} = \mu_i + \delta \mu_i - (r_3^T \delta \mu_i)r_3$. We will implement the standard projection logic which matches the description "decoupling the component perpendicular to the normal", as the literal scalar subtraction is likely a typo in the source notation for the vector projection.)*
            *Correction based on standard geometry and "decoupling":* The new position lies in the tangent space. The gradient component along the normal is removed.
            $$ \mu_{i+1} = \mu_i + (\delta \mu_i - (r_3^T \delta \mu_i)r_3) $$
            Parameters like $r_3$ are cloned to the new Gaussian [S1].
    *   **Splitting:**
        *   Ensure new splits are co-planar with the original Gaussian.
        *   The new position $\mu_{i+1}$ must satisfy the plane equation defined by $\mu_i$ and $r_3$:
            $$ \mu_{i+1}^T r_3 = \mu_i^T r_3 $$
        *   The new Gaussian $G_{\theta, i+1}$ is added to $S_{co}$ [S1].

### Optimization
The optimization minimizes a combined loss function [S1]:
$$ L = \lambda_1 \sum_{k \in H \times W} E_{pho}^k + \lambda_2 \sum_{G_\theta^i} E_{geo}^i $$

1.  **Photometric Loss ($E_{pho}$):**
    $$ E_{pho}^i = \| c_i - \bar{c}_i \| = \| f(G, T_{cw}) - \bar{c}_i \| $$
    Where $c_i$ is the rendered color and $\bar{c}_i$ is the ground truth color [S1].

2.  **Geometric Smoothness Loss ($E_{geo}$):**
    Applied only to view-dependent thin Gaussians $G_\theta \in S_{co}$.
    *   Find $K$-nearest neighbors (K=8) for each $G_\theta$.
    *   Filter outliers with large normal angle differences.
    *   Calculate loss:
        $$ E_{geo} = \beta_1 \| r_3^T \mu - \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j \in m} r_3^{j T} \mu^j \| + \beta_2 \| r_3^j - r_3 \|_2 $$
        Where $m$ is the number of filtered neighbors. The first term encourages the Gaussian's plane to be co-planar with its neighbors' average plane. The second term encourages normal alignment [S1].

3.  **Training Schedule:**
    *   **Iterations:** 30,000 total [S1].
    *   **0–2,000 iterations:** Only photometric constraints ($E_{pho}$) are used. No densification.
    *   **2,000–10,000 iterations:** Densification is active. Both losses are used.
    *   **>10,000 iterations:** Densification ceases.
    *   **Pruning:** Gaussians with opacity $< 0.05$ are removed [S1].
    *   **Neighbor Update:** The K-NN list for $E_{geo}$ is updated every 100 iterations until 20,000 iterations, then every 1,000 iterations [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training requires 30,000 iterations. Densification (K-NN updates) occurs frequently (every 100 iterations) in the first 20,000 iterations, increasing overhead compared to standard 3DGS.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Maintains neighbor lists for geometric loss, increasing memory usage.
*   **Performance:** On the Replica dataset (R1, 10% data), GeoGaussian achieves PSNR 31.65, SSIM 0.937, LPIPS 0.041, outperforming 3DGS (PSNR 30.49, SSIM 0.932, LPIPS 0.051) and LightGS (PSNR 30.54, SSIM 0.932, LPIPS 0.051) [S1]. On 100% data, it achieves PSNR 38.24, SSIM 0.979, LPIPS 0.021, compared to 3DGS (PSNR 37.60, SSIM 0.965, LPIPS 0.028) and LightGS (PSNR 38.44, SSIM 0.967, LPIPS 0.025) [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Superior rendering quality in low-textured, smooth regions (walls, ceilings) where standard 3DGS fails. Preserves geometric structure.
*   **Limitations:** Requires more GPU resources for training than 3DGS. Functions as a global refinement module, not suitable for online real-time optimization like Vox-Fusion [S1].
*   **When to use:** When high-fidelity rendering of structured, smooth surfaces is required, and training time is not a critical constraint.
*   **When not to use:** For real-time online SLAM or when computational resources are severely limited.

## Background
GeoGaussian was proposed by Y. Li et al. in the paper "GeoGaussian: Geometry-Aware Gaussian Splatting for Scene Rendering" [S1]. It addresses the degradation of rendering quality in novel views that deviate from training viewpoints, particularly in untextured regions. It builds on the 3D Gaussian Splatting framework [S1] but introduces explicit geometric constraints. It is evaluated on Replica, TUM RGB-D, and ICL-NUIM datasets [S1].
