# GS-IR

Also called: 3D Gaussian Splatting for Inverse Rendering

## Overview
GS-IR is an inverse rendering approach based on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) that leverages forward mapping volume rendering to achieve photorealistic novel view synthesis and relighting results [S1]. It estimates scene geometry, surface material, and environment illumination from multi-view images captured under unknown lighting conditions [S1]. Unlike NeRF-based inverse rendering methods that use implicit neural representations and ray tracing (backward mapping), which suffer from low expressive power and high computational complexity, GS-IR uses 3D Gaussians and forward mapping splatting for real-time rendering efficiency [S1]. It addresses the specific challenges of applying 3DGS to inverse rendering: the lack of native plausible normal estimation and the inability of forward mapping to trace occlusion for indirect lighting [S1].

## How it works
The GS-IR framework consists of three stages: an initial stage for geometry reconstruction, a second stage for occlusion baking, and a final stage for material and illumination optimization [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:** Multi-view images captured under unknown lighting conditions [S1].
**Outputs:** Optimized 3D Gaussians representing scene geometry, material properties (Albedo, Metallic, Roughness, Ambient Occlusion), and environment illumination [S1].

### Data Structures
The method extends the standard 3D Gaussian representation. Each Gaussian $i$ retains the mean position $\mu_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$ [S1]. To support inverse rendering, additional attributes are stored per Gaussian:
*   **Normal:** A normal vector $\mathbf{n}_i$ [S1].
*   **Material Properties:** Albedo, Metallic, Roughness, and Ambient Occlusion (AO) [S1].
*   **Illumination:** Coefficients for environment illumination, modeled using Spherical Harmonics (SH) [S1].

### Steps

#### Stage 1: Geometry and Normal Optimization
The first stage optimizes 3D Gaussians for geometry reconstruction using differentiable splatting [S1].

1.  **Differentiable Splatting:** Render depth maps and color images using the standard front-to-back alpha compositing equation [15745-inner-3d-gaussian-splatting.md].
2.  **Depth-Derivation Regularization:** To address the challenge that 3DGS does not produce plausible normals natively, GS-IR introduces a regularization strategy [S1].
    *   Compute the pseudo-normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_D$ from the gradient of the rendered depth map $\hat{D}$:
        $$ \hat{\mathbf{n}}_D = \nabla_{uv} \hat{D} $$
        where $\nabla_{uv}$ denotes the gradient in the image plane [S1].
    *   Use $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_D$ to supervise the optimization of the normals $\mathbf{n}$ stored within the 3D Gaussians [S1].
3.  **Optimization:** Update Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity) to minimize the difference between rendered and input images, guided by the depth-derived normal supervision [S1].

#### Stage 2: Occlusion Baking
Forward mapping cannot trace occlusion like backward mapping (ray tracing), making it difficult to calculate occlusion for indirect lighting [S1]. GS-IR addresses this by baking occlusion into volumes [S1].

1.  **Precompute Occlusion:** Based on the learned geometry (depth and normal) from Stage 1, precompute occlusion information [S1].
2.  **Cache Storage:** Store the occlusion data in a spherical harmonics-based architecture to model indirect illumination [S1]. This approach is inspired by the "Indirect Lighting Cache" used in real-time rendering [S1].

#### Stage 3: Material and Illumination Optimization
The final stage combines differentiable splatting with a physically-based rendering (PBR) pipeline [S1].

1.  **PBR Pipeline Integration:** Integrate the baked occlusion and material properties into the rendering equation [S1].
2.  **Joint Optimization:** Optimize the illumination (environment lighting) and material-aware 3D Gaussians (Albedo, Metallic, Roughness, AO) simultaneously [S1].
3.  **Relighting:** The optimized parameters allow for photorealistic relighting and novel view synthesis under arbitrary lighting conditions [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Normal Estimation:** 3DGS does not support producing plausible normals natively [S1]. Its adaptive control of Gaussian density may lead to loose geometry, making it difficult to estimate accurate scene normals [S1]. GS-IR mitigates this with depth-derivation-based regularization [S1].
*   **Occlusion Calculation:** Forward mapping (rasterization and splatting) cannot trace occlusion like backward mapping (ray tracing) [S1]. GS-IR handles this via baking rather than direct ray tracing [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Efficiency:** GS-IR achieves real-time rendering efficiency by replacing ray tracing with differentiable forward mapping volume rendering [S1].
*   **Expressiveness:** The flexible and expressive 3DGS representation allows for fast and compact geometry reconstruction [S1].
*   **Scalability:** Unlike TensoIR, which is limited to object-level scenes, GS-IR handles occlusion via baking and is available for both objects and unbounded scenes [S1].
*   **Performance:** The method demonstrates superiority over baseline methods (including NeRF-based approaches) in qualitative and quantitative evaluations of various challenging scenes [S1].

## Background
GS-IR is the first work to introduce the 3DGS technique for inverse rendering [S1]. It was proposed to overcome the limitations of previous works that used implicit neural representations (e.g., NeRF) and volume rendering, which suffer from low expressive power and high computational complexity [S1]. The method builds on the remarkable performance in quality and speed of 3DGS [S1].
