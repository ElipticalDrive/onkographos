# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which fails to distinguish between well-reconstructed regions and over-reconstructed noise, FR explicitly disentangles frequency components to push Gaussians toward high-frequency regions where detail is missing [S2][S3, unofficial]. This method addresses the problem that photometric loss alone is insufficient to distinguish valid surface geometry from noise, leading to overfitting in empty space [S2][S3, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, where $H$ and $W$ are height and width.
*   **Outputs:** Regularization loss terms $\mathcal{L}_{FR}$ (amplitude and phase discrepancies) added to the total loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   Complex frequency values $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ stored as real and imaginary components.
*   Amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and Phase $\angle F(u, v)$ derived from the complex spectrum.

**Steps:**
1.  **Fourier Transform:** Convert the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth image $I$ into the frequency domain using the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) [S2].
2.  **Compute Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency coordinate $(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude and phase for both the rendered and ground truth spectra [S2].
3.  **Calculate Discrepancies:** Compute the Euclidean distance between the amplitudes and the phases of the ground truth and rendered images [S2].
4.  **Aggregate Loss:** Sum the amplitude and phase discrepancies to form the frequency regularization term [S2].

**Formulas:**
The 2D Discrete Fourier Transform is defined as [S2]:
$$ F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
where $(x, y)$ are image coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value, and $F(u, v)$ is the complex frequency value.

The amplitude and phase of a frequency component $F(u, v)$ are calculated as [S2]:
$$ |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2} $$
$$ \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right) $$
where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$, respectively.

The total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ are computed as [S2]:
$$ d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2} $$
$$ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2} $$
where $F$ and $\hat{F}$ denote the ground truth and rendered frequency values, respectively.

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
*   **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
*   **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are retained [S2].
*   **Typical Values:** Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Over-reconstruction:** Naive adoption of amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can bias the optimization towards undesirable artifacts and restrict Gaussian densification [S2].
*   **Gradient Vanishing:** In over-reconstructed regions, pixel-wise L1 loss results in small gradients, preventing densification. FR increases these gradients, revealing over-reconstruction regions [S2].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.fft

def compute_frequency_regularization(rendered_image: torch.Tensor, ground_truth: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the frequency regularization loss based on amplitude and phase discrepancies.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) representing the rendered image.
        ground_truth: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) representing the ground truth image.
        
    Returns:
        Scalar tensor representing the total frequency regularization loss.
    """
    # Ensure inputs are float32 for FFT stability
    rendered_image = rendered_image.float()
    ground_truth = ground_truth.float()
    
    # Move to CPU if necessary for FFT, or use torch.fft on GPU if supported
    # Note: torch.fft.fft2 works on GPU tensors in PyTorch
    H, W, C = rendered_image.shape
    
    # Compute 2D FFT for each channel
    # F(u, v) shape: (H, W, C)
    F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth, dim=(0, 1))
    F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image, dim=(0, 1))
    
    # Compute Amplitude and Phase
    # Amplitude: |F(u, v)|
    amp_gt = torch.abs(F_gt)
    amp_rendered = torch.abs(F_rendered)
    
    # Phase: angle(F(u, v))
    phase_gt = torch.angle(F_gt)
    phase_rendered = torch.angle(F_rendered)
    
    # Compute Discrepancies
    # d_a: Root Mean Square Error of Amplitudes
    # d_p: Root Mean Square Error of Phases
    # Note: The source formula uses sum over x,y divided by HW, then sqrt.
    # This is equivalent to RMSE.
    
    # Calculate Mean Squared Error for Amplitude
    mse_amp = torch.mean((amp_gt - amp_rendered) ** 2)
    d_a = torch.sqrt(mse_amp)
    
    # Calculate Mean Squared Error for Phase
    mse_phase = torch.mean((phase_gt - phase_rendered) ** 2)
    d_p = torch.sqrt(mse_phase)
    
    # Total Loss (sum of discrepancies)
    # The source defines d_a and d_p as the discrepancies. 
    # The total loss is typically a weighted sum. 
    # Here we return the sum of the two discrepancies as the core FR term.
    loss = d_a + d_p
    
    return loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The addition of FFT operations increases computational overhead per iteration compared to standard L1 loss. The complexity is dominated by the 2D FFT, which is $O(HW \log(HW))$ per channel.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the frequency domain representations of the rendered and ground truth images, adding $O(HW \cdot C)$ memory overhead per iteration.
*   **Measured Performance:** In the "Bicycle" scene, FR significantly increased pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to the baseline method without FR [S2].
*   **Scaling:** The method is effective for adaptive densification, allowing Gaussians to be created in regions where high-frequency details are missing [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Effectively distinguishes over-reconstructed regions from well-reconstructed ones, enabling better Gaussian densification [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Naive application without frequency annealing can lead to restricted densification and artifacts [S2].
*   **When to use:** When over-reconstruction and noise are prevalent during training, or when standard densification fails to capture high-frequency details.
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are severely constrained, or when the scene is low-frequency and simple L1 loss suffices.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced to address the limitations of pixel-wise L1 loss in 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization. The raw optimization method can lead to over-reconstruction in some regions while underrepresenting others, resulting in blur and visual inconsistencies [S1]. The method was detailed in the paper "FreGS: 3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization" [S2]. It is part of a broader set of improvements including geometry-aware approaches and optimization procedure enhancements [S1].
