# Hard and Soft Depth Regularization

Also called: Spatial Reshaping Regularization

## Overview
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization is a training strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that prevents geometric detail loss and blurred appearance by decoupling the optimization of Gaussian center positions from their shape parameters. It solves the problem where standard depth regularization forces anisotropic Gaussian primitives to conform to smooth monocular depth estimates, conflicting with complex color appearance [S1]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating directly on the spatial distribution of primitives rather than image-space frequency spectra, and from [Isotropic Regularization](10092-isotropic-regularization.md) by explicitly freezing shape parameters to enforce movement rather than penalizing anisotropy [S1].

## How it works
The method constrains 3D Gaussian geometry by encouraging spatial reshaping (movement of centers) rather than deforming Gaussian shapes, thereby preventing the detail loss caused by forcing anisotropic Gaussians to fit smooth monocular depth [S1].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Current set of 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}$, each defined by position $\mu_i$, scale $s_i$, rotation quaternion $q_i$, opacity $\alpha_i$, and spherical harmonics coefficients; monocular depth map $D_{mono}$; camera parameters.
*   **Output:** Updated Gaussian positions $\mu_i$ and opacities $\alpha_i$; frozen scales $s_i$ and rotations $q_i$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian State:** A list of objects or parallel arrays containing:
    *   $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Center position.
    *   $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Scale factors (log-space or linear).
    *   $q \in \mathbb{R}^4$: Rotation quaternion.
    *   $\alpha \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
    *   $c \in \mathbb{R}^k$: Color coefficients (SH).
*   **Depth Maps:** Two rendered depth maps, $D_{hard}$ and $D_{soft}$, generated from the current Gaussian state.

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Freeze Shape Parameters:** During the depth regularization step, freeze the scale parameters $s$ and rotation quaternion $q$ of all Gaussians. This prevents the Gaussian shapes from deforming to fit the depth prior [S1].
2.  **Render Two Depth Maps:** Render two distinct depth maps from the current Gaussian state:
    *   $D_{hard}$: A depth map used to compute gradients for updating center positions $\mu$. This typically corresponds to a "hard" assignment or the depth of the primary contributing Gaussian.
    *   $D_{soft}$: A depth map used to compute gradients for updating opacities $\alpha$. This typically corresponds to the alpha-blended depth (weighted average of depths along the ray).
3.  **Compute Position Gradients (Hard):** Calculate the loss between $D_{hard}$ and the monocular depth prior $D_{mono}$. Compute gradients with respect to the center positions $\mu$ only.
4.  **Compute Opacity Gradients (Soft):** Calculate the loss between $D_{soft}$ and $D_{mono}$. Compute gradients with respect to the opacities $\alpha$ only.
5.  **Update Parameters:** Apply the gradients independently to update $\mu$ and $\alpha$. This balances coarse depth fitting (via position updates) with fine color appearance preservation (via opacity updates) [S1].

**Formulas:**
Let $\mathcal{L}_{depth}$ be the depth loss (e.g., L1 or scale-invariant loss) between the rendered depth and $D_{mono}$.
The total regularization loss is split into two components:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{reg} = \lambda_{hard} \mathcal{L}_{hard} + \lambda_{soft} \mathcal{L}_{soft} $$
where $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ is computed using $D_{hard}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{soft}$ is computed using $D_{soft}$.

The gradient updates are:
$$ \mu \leftarrow \mu - \eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{hard}}{\partial \mu} $$
$$ \alpha \leftarrow \alpha - \eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{soft}}{\partial \alpha} $$
where $\eta$ is the learning rate. The scales $s$ and rotations $q$ are not updated in this step:
$$ s \leftarrow s $$
$$ q \leftarrow q $$

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_{hard}, \lambda_{soft}$: Weights for the hard and soft depth losses. Typical values are not specified in the source but are tuned to balance depth accuracy and color fidelity [S1].
*   $M$: Number of Gaussians considered for depth calculation (implicit in the rendering process).

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Detail Loss:** Applying depth regularization directly to NeRF-style MLPs or un-frozen Gaussian shapes forces the primitive geometry to conform to smooth monocular depth estimates, which conflicts with complex color appearance and causes detail loss and blurred appearance [S1].
*   **Sensitivity:** Gaussian radiance fields are more sensitive to small depth errors compared to NeRF, which may result in a noisy distribution of primitives and failures in regions with complex textures [S1].
*   **Scale Invariance:** Existing scale-invariant depth losses often align depth maps to a fixed scale, which may overlook minor depth variations [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def hard_and_soft_depth_regularization(gaussians, mono_depth, camera, lambda_hard, lambda_soft):
    """
    Perform hard and soft depth regularization on 3D Gaussians.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List of Gaussian objects with attributes mu, s, q, alpha, c.
        mono_depth: Monocular depth map (H, W).
        camera: Camera parameters for rendering.
        lambda_hard: Weight for hard depth loss.
        lambda_soft: Weight for soft depth loss.
        
    Returns:
        Updated gaussians.
    """
    # Step 1: Freeze shape parameters
    # In an autograd framework, this is achieved by detaching s and q from the computation graph
    frozen_s = gaussians.s.detach()
    frozen_q = gaussians.q.detach()
    
    # Step 2: Render two depth maps
    # D_hard: Depth of the primary Gaussian or hard assignment
    # D_soft: Alpha-blended depth (weighted average)
    D_hard = render_hard_depth(gaussians.mu, frozen_s, frozen_q, gaussians.alpha, gaussians.c, camera)
    D_soft = render_soft_depth(gaussians.mu, frozen_s, frozen_q, gaussians.alpha, gaussians.c, camera)
    
    # Step 3: Compute Hard Loss (for position update)
    # Example: L1 loss between rendered hard depth and monocular depth
    loss_hard = torch.mean(torch.abs(D_hard - mono_depth))
    
    # Step 4: Compute Soft Loss (for opacity update)
    loss_soft = torch.mean(torch.abs(D_soft - mono_depth))
    
    # Step 5: Compute gradients
    # Gradients for mu from loss_hard
    grad_mu = torch.autograd.grad(loss_hard, gaussians.mu, retain_graph=True)[0]
    
    # Gradients for alpha from loss_soft
    grad_alpha = torch.autograd.grad(loss_soft, gaussians.alpha)[0]
    
    # Step 6: Update parameters
    # Update mu using grad_mu
    gaussians.mu = gaussians.mu - 0.01 * grad_mu # 0.01 is a placeholder learning rate
    
    # Update alpha using grad_alpha
    gaussians.alpha = gaussians.alpha - 0.01 * grad_alpha
    
    # s and q remain unchanged (frozen)
    
    return gaussians
```

## Subtopics

### [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md)
Global-Local Depth Normalization is a depth regularization technique for 3D Gaussian Splatting that prevents scale-invariant losses from overlooking minor depth variations, thereby improving detailed geometry reshaping in texture-rich regions. It sets itself apart from standard scale-invariant losses by combining global scale preservation with local scale amplification, addressing the issue where existing methods align depth maps to a fixed global scale and ignore small local errors.

### [Depth Reinitialization](9233-depth-reinitialization.md)
Depth Reinitialization is a regularization strategy that alleviates 'overlapping' and 'under-reconstruction' artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting by leveraging dense depth information to reorganize the spatial distribution of Gaussians. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by rejecting standard alpha-blended depth maps in favor of geometric mid-point depths to generate clean, artifact-free point clouds for reinitialization.

### [Isotropic Regularization](10092-isotropic-regularization.md)
Isotropic Regularization is a geometric constraint used in continuous SLAM-based Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of highly elongated Gaussian primitives along the viewing ray direction. While standard differentiable rendering imposes no constraint on Gaussian scaling along the viewing ray, this lack of constraint causes artifacts and tracking instability in continuous SLAM scenarios where viewpoints are not carefully selected. It sets itself apart from its parent [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by explicitly penalizing anisotropy (stretching) rather than just enforcing depth consistency, and from [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md) by operating on the scale parameters directly rather than depth values.

### [Position Regularization](11049-position-regularization.md)
Position Regularization prevents floating artifacts and dramatic geometry shifts in 3D Gaussian Splatting by constraining Gaussian centers to remain close to a reference surface or their initial positions. It operates as a geometric prior constraint, distinct from depth-consistency methods in its parent category [9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md].

### [Coherent 3D Gaussian Optimization](14045-coherent-3d-gaussian-optimization.md)
Coherent 3D Gaussian Optimization is a regularization strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that enforces spatial coherence in Gaussian positions and opacities during training, specifically addressing artifacts in sparse-view novel view synthesis. It sets itself apart from standard 3DGS by replacing independent per-pixel residual updates with a learned implicit decoder that produces smooth, coherent residual fields for depth and opacity, supplemented by multiview total variation and optical flow constraints. This prevents the "semi-transparent" artifacts and detail loss common when optimizing Gaussians against sparse input views.

### Geometric Regularization via Bilateral Filter
A regularization loss that enforces coherent motion by grouping Gaussians in image space using a bilateral filter approach, avoiding the dampening of independent motion caused by spatial KNN [S2]. For a training image $I$ at time $t_I$, sample another time $t'$. Compute the 6D screw-axis representation of the transformation between $t_I$ and $t'$ for each Gaussian. Compute the geometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ as the weighted sum of squared distances between screw-axis representations of neighboring pixels. The geometric loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}} = \sum_{p' \in \mathcal{N}(p)} k_{c,c'} k_{d,d'} k_{p,p'} \|s_p - s_{p'}\|^2$, where $k_{c,c'}$ is color similarity, $k_{d,d'}$ is depth similarity, $k_{p,p'}$ is spatial distance weight, and $s_p, s_{p'}$ are screw-axis representations [S2]. Unlike standard bilateral filters, the loss is not divided by the sum of the weights because the goal is comparison, not energy conservation [S2].

### Near-Far Regularization
A regularization term applied alongside depth distortion to constrain Gaussians to aggregate at object surfaces, ensuring robust 3D structure reconstruction [S3]. It narrows the distance between the weighted depth of the nearest and farthest $M$ Gaussians on each ray [S3]. Extract subset $N$ (nearest $M$ Gaussians) and $F$ (farthest $M$ Gaussians, where blending weight approaches 1) on each ray. Render depth maps $d_N, d_F$ and blending weight maps $T_N, T_F$ for subsets $N$ and $F$. The regularization term is $R_{nf} = T_N \cdot T_F \cdot |d_N - d_F|$, where $T$ is the sum of blending weights $\omega_i$ [S3]. Used in LE3D to improve structural reconstruction quality for downstream tasks [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method adds two rendering passes (hard and soft depth) per regularization step, effectively doubling the rendering cost for the depth loss computation. The gradient computation is linear in the number of Gaussians and pixels.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing two additional depth maps per regularization step. No significant additional memory overhead for Gaussian parameters as shapes are frozen.
*   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Scaling:** Scales with the number of Gaussians and image resolution. The sensitivity to depth errors may require careful tuning of learning rates for complex textures [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by decoupling position and shape optimization. Balances coarse depth fitting with fine color appearance [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of $\lambda_{hard}$ and $\lambda_{soft}$. May be sensitive to noisy monocular depth estimates [S1].
*   **When to use:** When training 3D Gaussian Splatting with monocular depth priors and observing blurred appearance or detail loss due to shape deformation [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When high-fidelity shape deformation is required (e.g., for non-rigid deformation where shape changes are expected) or when monocular depth is unreliable [S1].

## Background
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization was proposed in the context of optimizing sparse-view 3D Gaussian radiance fields. It addresses the limitations of existing depth regularization strategies for NeRFs, which commonly employ depth to regularize the entire model, creating geometry conflicts in Gaussian fields [S1]. The method was introduced to enable spatial reshaping by encouraging movement among the primitives, considering that the basis of scene geometry lies in the position of the Gaussian primitives rather than their shape [S1].
