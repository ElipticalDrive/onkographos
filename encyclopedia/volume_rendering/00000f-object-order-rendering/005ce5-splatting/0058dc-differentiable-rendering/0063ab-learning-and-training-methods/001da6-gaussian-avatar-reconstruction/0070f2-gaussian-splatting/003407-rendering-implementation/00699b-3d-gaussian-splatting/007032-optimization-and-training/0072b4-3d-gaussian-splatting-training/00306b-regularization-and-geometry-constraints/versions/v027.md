# Regularization and Geometry Constraints

Also called: Geometric Priors, Surface Regularization, Frequency Constraints

## Overview
Regularization and Geometry Constraints are training-time techniques for 3D Gaussian Splatting that enforce physical plausibility, spectral consistency, or multi-view geometric coherence to mitigate artifacts such as floaters, over-reconstruction, and noisy gradients. Unlike standard photometric optimization which minimizes pixel-wise color differences, these methods introduce auxiliary losses or structural modifications to the Gaussian primitives (centers, covariances, opacities) to guide the optimization toward valid surface representations. They set themselves apart from sibling methods like [Physical Parameter Optimization](4111-physical-parameter-optimization.md) by focusing on geometric and spectral priors rather than physical light transport models, and from [Densification Strategy](6234-densification-strategy.md) by actively constraining the existing primitives rather than just adding new ones.

## Shared principles
The family shares the goal of improving the geometric fidelity and visual quality of the reconstructed scene by modifying the optimization landscape. Common mechanisms include:
1.  **Spectral Consistency:** Enforcing that the frequency content of the rendered image matches the ground truth, preventing high-frequency noise or over-smoothing [S2, S3].
2.  **Geometric Priors:** Using external signals (monocular depth, surface normals) or internal consistency (multi-view reprojection) to constrain the 3D structure of the Gaussians [S1, S3].
3.  **Primitive Modification:** Altering the shape or orientation of the Gaussian kernels (e.g., flattening to surfels, half-ellipsoids) to better approximate surfaces [S1].
4.  **Gradient Stabilization:** Smoothing supervision signals or decoupling parameter updates to prevent unstable convergence during early training stages [S2, S3].

## Subtopics

### [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md)
Frequency Regularization (FR) minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction. Unlike naive L1 loss, which provides small gradients in over-reconstructed regions, FR increases gradients by disentangling frequency components, pushing Gaussians toward high-frequency details. It uses amplitude and phase discrepancies in the Fourier domain.

### [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md)
Smoothed 2D Supervision enhances robustness by blurring the 2D supervision signal with a scheduled set of 2D Gaussian kernels. It solves the problem of unstable camera alignment and noisy gradients in early training by restricting the learned scene spectrum to low frequencies initially, stabilizing convergence before gradually introducing higher frequencies. It is crucial for successful pose refinement.

### [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md)
3D Half-Gaussian Splatting (3D-HGS) replaces full ellipsoids with half-ellipsoids bounded by a splitting plane to address volumetric artifacts. It enforces geometric plausibility by aligning the splitting plane normal with estimated surface normals, modeling sharp transitions between surfaces and empty space. It modifies the fundamental shape of the primitive rather than just penalizing the output.

### [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md)
This method prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by decoupling the optimization of Gaussian centers from their shapes. It freezes scale and rotation parameters, using two distinct rendered depth maps to independently update center positions ("hard" depth fitting) and opacities ("soft" color appearance balancing), avoiding the deformation of primitives to fit smooth monocular depth.

### [FreGS](9212-fregs.md)
FreGS prevents overfitting and high-frequency artifacts by enforcing spectral consistency between rendered images and ground truth. It operates in the frequency domain to penalize frequency discrepancies, addressing the specific issue of over-reconstruction. It applies progressive frequency regularization to guide optimization from coarse to fine details.

### [Cross-scale geometric adaptation](10796-cross-scale-geometric-adaptation.md)
Cross-scale geometric adaptation is a self-adaptive method that uses reprojection errors across multiple voxel scales to guide geometry learning without explicit depth ground truth. It selects render depths with minimal reprojection error as pseudo labels, leveraging low-frequency components from low-resolution voxels for coarse geometry early in training, while higher resolutions refine details later.

### [Geometry Regularization](14222-geometry-regularization.md)
Geometry regularization improves surface smoothness and reduces floaters by enforcing geometric priors on the Gaussian kernels. It constrains the shape and orientation of the Gaussians themselves (normal alignment and flattening) rather than just the spatial location of the surface, operating directly on the 3D covariance structure.

### Wild-GS
Wild-GS aligns pixel appearance features from image triplanes directly to 3D Gaussians and uses depth regularization and visibility maps to mitigate transient effects and constrain geometry [S1].

### Source Feature Extrapolation
Source Feature Extrapolation improves extrapolation capabilities beyond source view frustums by padding source images and adding positional encoding channels for padded regions, preventing smearing artifacts in regions not visible by source images [S2]. It applies border padding to source images $I^{(i)}$ and creates a channel $\Gamma$ containing pixel-wise positional encodings for padded regions: $\Gamma[u,v] = \gamma(u,v)$ if $(u,v)$ is not in $I^{(i)}$, else 0. The modified image is $I'(i) = \text{concatenate}(I_{pad}, \Gamma)$, where $\gamma(\cdot)$ denotes positional encoding with exponential frequencies [S2].

### SDF-Regularized 3DGS
SDF-Regularized 3DGS improves geometric fidelity by imposing physical constraints such as Signed Distance Fields (SDFs), monocular depth priors, or multi-view geometric constraints during optimization [S3]. It introduces a regularization term to minimize the difference between an ideal SDF and the SDF calculated from Gaussians, often using a dual-branch framework with GS- and SDF-branches jointly optimized for mutual guidance [S3].

### MVG-Splatting
MVG-Splatting employs a depth map to enforce geometric consistency, specifically applying geometric consistency only in the near and far regions of the scene to effectively mitigate under-reconstruction in these critical regions while avoiding the risk of over-reconstruction [S4].

### Shape Energy Constraint
Shape Energy Constraint is an energy term defined as the combination of opacity energy and boundary energy, used to preserve the shape, opacity, and unitized colors (spherical harmonics) of Gaussian radiance fields during deformation [S5]. Relying solely on feature energy is insufficient to preserve opacity and unitized colors; gray Gaussians with SH values close to zero appear and their size becomes uncontrolled because they cannot be constrained by feature energy alone [S5]. Without $E_{shape}$, the optimization process becomes unstable and artifacts appear; with $E_{shape}$, the process stabilizes and these artifacts are mitigated [S5].

## Background
The need for regularization in 3D Gaussian Splatting arises from the method's reliance on photometric loss, which often results in unstructured density clouds that do not represent valid surfaces, leading to floaters and geometric inaccuracies [S3]. Early works like DINER introduced depth-aware sampling and source feature extrapolation to improve reconstruction quality and handle regions outside source view frustums [S2]. Subsequent research focused on imposing physical constraints, such as SDFs and monocular depth priors, to recover high-quality geometry [S3]. Methods like Wild-GS and MVG-Splatting further refined this by integrating visibility maps and targeted geometric consistency checks [S1, S4]. The family of regularization techniques continues to evolve, addressing specific issues like over-reconstruction [S2], transient effects [S1], and deformation stability [S5].
