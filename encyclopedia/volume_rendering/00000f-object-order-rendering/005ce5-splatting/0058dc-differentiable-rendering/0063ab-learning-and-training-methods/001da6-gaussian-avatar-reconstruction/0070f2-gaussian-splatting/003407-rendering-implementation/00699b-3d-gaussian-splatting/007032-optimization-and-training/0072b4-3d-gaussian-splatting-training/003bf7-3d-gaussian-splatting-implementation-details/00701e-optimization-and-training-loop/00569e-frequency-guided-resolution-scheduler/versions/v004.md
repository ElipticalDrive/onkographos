# Frequency Guided Resolution Scheduler

Also called: Frequency-based resolution scheduling

## Overview
The Frequency Guided Resolution Scheduler is a training strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that accelerates optimization by dynamically adjusting the rendering resolution based on the frequency content of the training data [S1]. It reduces optimization complexity by allocating more iterations to high-resolution (HR) rendering only when the model has sufficiently captured the low-frequency structure of the scene [S1]. Unlike static resolution schedules, this method uses the Fourier energy of the input images to determine the optimal switching points between different resolution levels, ensuring that high-frequency details are fitted only after the coarse geometry is established [S1].

## How it works
The method schedules the rendering resolution $r(k)$ as a function of the iteration index $k$ throughout the total training budget $S$. It operates by analyzing the frequency domain representation of the training views to determine how much information is contained at different scales.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**:
    *   $N$: Number of training views.
    *   $S$: Total number of optimization iterations.
    *   $\{I_n\}_{n=1}^N$: Set of training images at full resolution $(H, W)$.
    *   $a$: Hyperparameter controlling the minimum frequency threshold (default $a=4$) [S1].
*   **Outputs**:
    *   A schedule mapping each iteration $k \in [1, S]$ to a rendering resolution factor $r(k)$, where the rendered image size is $(H/r(k), W/r(k))$ [S1].

### Data Structures
No persistent data structures are required beyond the standard Gaussian parameters and the pre-computed frequency energy values. The scheduler computes scalar values for each resolution level.

### Steps
1.  **Compute Frequency Energy**: For each training view $I_n$, compute its Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) $F_n$. Calculate the total frequency energy $\mathcal{X}(F)$ of the full-resolution images:
    $$ \mathcal{X}(F) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{i=1}^{h} \sum_{j=1}^{w} ||F_n(i, j)||^2 $$
    where $(h, w) = (H, W)$ [S1].

2.  **Determine Resolution Levels**: Define a set of downsampling factors $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m$ such that $1 = r_m < r_{m-1} < \dots < r_1$.
    *   The maximum downsampling factor $r_m$ (lowest resolution) is determined by the condition:
        $$ \mathcal{X}(F_{r_m}) = \frac{1}{a} \mathcal{X}(F) $$
        where $\mathcal{X}(F_{r_m})$ is the frequency energy of the images downsampled by factor $r_m$ [S1].
    *   Intermediate energy levels $\mathcal{X}(F_{r_1}), \dots, \mathcal{X}(F_{r_{m-1}})$ are linearly sampled between $\mathcal{X}(F)$ and $\mathcal{X}(F_{r_m})$ [S1].
    *   The corresponding resolution factors $r_i$ are solved for these energy levels. Note that downsampling must be performed carefully to avoid aliasing; a common approach is center-cropping the DFT and scaling values by $1/r^2$ before inverse transforming [S1].

3.  **Calculate Switching Iterations**: For each resolution transition from $r_i$ to $r_{i-1}$, calculate the iteration index $s_{r_i}$ where the switch occurs:
    $$ s_{r_i} = S \cdot \frac{\mathcal{X}(F_{r_i})}{\mathcal{X}(F)} $$
    This formula distributes iterations proportionally to the frequency energy contained in the lower resolution [S1].

4.  **Interpolate Resolution**: For any iteration $k$, determine the current resolution $r(k)$. If $k$ falls between switching points $s_{r_i}$ and $s_{r_{i-1}}$, interpolate the resolution factor. The sources specify that the resolution $r(k)$ is interpolated with the inverse square of $r_i$ and $r_{i-1}$ [S1]. Specifically, if we define the "inverse square resolution" metric, the interpolation ensures smooth transitions in the effective pixel density.

def compute_frequency_energy(images):
    """
    Computes the average frequency energy of a set of images.
    images: List of numpy arrays of shape (H, W, C) or (H, W).
    Returns: Scalar float representing total energy.
    """
    total_energy = 0.0
    n = len(images)
    for img in images:
        # Compute 2D FFT
        f_transform = np.fft.fft2(img)
        # Shift zero frequency to center (optional for visualization, not strictly needed for energy sum)
        # f_shifted = np.fft.fftshift(f_transform)
        
        # Calculate energy: sum of squared magnitudes
        energy = np.sum(np.abs(f_transform)**2)
        total_energy += energy
        
    return total_energy / n

def binary_search_factor(images, target_energy, low=1.0, high=10.0, tol=0.1):
    """
    Finds the downsampling factor r such that energy(F_r) approx target_energy.
    """
    while high - low > tol:
        mid = (low + high) / 2
        e = get_downsampled_energy(images, mid)
        if e > target_energy:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    return (low + high) / 2

### Parameters
*   $a$: Hyperparameter controlling the starting significance level for the lowest resolution. Default value is 4 [S1].
*   $S$: Total number of iterations.
*   $N$: Number of training views.

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Aliasing**: Simple spatial downsampling (e.g., nearest-neighbor subsampling) causes aliasing, which disturbs recoverable low-frequency components and introduces artifacts [S1]. The method requires anti-aliasing downsampling, such as center-cropping the DFT and scaling values by $1/r^2$ before inverse transforming [S1].
*   **Precision**: The frequency energy calculation involves summing squared magnitudes, which can be sensitive to numerical precision if not normalized correctly.
*   **Degenerate Input**: If the training images have very low frequency content (e.g., uniform color), the energy difference between resolutions may be negligible, leading to rapid switching. The hyperparameter $a$ helps mitigate this by setting a floor.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity**: The scheduling itself adds negligible overhead. The primary benefit is a reduction in total optimization time by reducing the number of high-resolution rendering steps [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity**: No additional memory is required beyond the training images and Gaussian parameters.
*   **Performance**: DashGaussian reports significant speedups (e.g., optimizing in 200 seconds) while maintaining or improving quality [S1].
*   **Strengths**: Reduces optimization complexity by focusing high-resolution computation only when necessary.
*   **Limitations**: Requires pre-computation of frequency energies, which adds a small upfront cost.

## Background
The Frequency Guided Resolution Scheduler was introduced in the "DashGaussian" paper [S1]. It addresses the high computational cost of 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization by recognizing that early-stage optimization primarily fits low-frequency components. By scheduling resolution based on frequency energy, it accelerates training without sacrificing final image quality.
