# Oriented Gaussian Splatting

Also called: Definition 2, Oriented Gaussian, Reciprocal Oriented Gaussian

## Overview
Oriented Gaussian Splatting augments the standard 3D Gaussian primitive with a learnable normal vector $n_i$ to create a reciprocal attenuation model, enabling the derivation of explicit geometric fields (normal and occupancy) via the Objects as Volumes (OaV) framework [S1]. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting by restoring reciprocity ($\sigma(x, w) = \sigma(x, -w)$), which is broken by standard isotropic attenuation, thereby allowing for consistent surface wrapping and mesh extraction [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A set of $N$ 3D Gaussians, each defined by center $\mu_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$, opacity $\alpha_i$, color $c_i$, and a learnable oriented normal vector $n_i \in S^2$. Training images with corresponding camera poses.
*   **Outputs:** Rendered RGB images, depth maps, oriented normal maps, and optionally a 3D mesh extracted from the geometric fields.

**Data Structures:**
Each Gaussian $i$ stores:
*   $\mu_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Center position.
*   $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Covariance matrix (typically parameterized via rotation $R_i$ and scaling $S_i$).
*   $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
*   $c_i$: Color (SH coefficients or RGB).
*   $n_i \in S^2$: Unit normal vector.

**Method Steps:**

1.  **Oriented Attenuation Calculation:**
    For a query point $\mathbf{x}$ and view direction $\mathbf{w}$, compute the oriented attenuation coefficient $\bar{\sigma}_i(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})$ for each Gaussian $i$ [S1]:
    $$ \bar{\sigma}_i(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{1}_{n_i^T(\mathbf{x}-\mu_i) \geq 0} |\mathbf{w} \cdot \nabla \log(1 - G_i(\mathbf{x}))| $$
    where $G_i(\mathbf{x}) = \alpha_i \exp(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \mu_i)^T \Sigma_i^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \mu_i))$ is the standard 3D Gaussian kernel, and $\mathbf{1}$ is the indicator function ensuring attenuation only occurs on the side of the Gaussian center pointed to by $n_i$ [S1].

2.  **Rendering:**
    Render RGB, depth, and normals using a differentiable rasterizer. The depth is rendered as the exact 0.5-isosurface of the geometric field [S1]. The normals $N(p)$ are the expected orientation of Gaussians along the ray [S1].

3.  **Geometric Field Derivation (Vacancy and Normal Fields):**
    To extract geometry, compute the vacancy field $v(\mathbf{x})$ and the normal field $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})$ [S1].
    
    *   **Vacancy Field:** Approximated by iterating over all training camera rays $\mathcal{T}_c$ to find the maximum product of unobstructed transmittance to point $\mathbf{x}$ [S1]:
        $$ v(\mathbf{x}) = \max_{(\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{w}) \in \mathcal{T}_c} \left\{ \prod_{i=1}^N \left( 1 - \bar{G}_{\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{w}}^{(i)}(t) \right) : \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{w}, t > 0 \right\} $$
        where $\bar{G}_{\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{w}}^{(i)}(t) = G_i(\mathbf{o} + \min(t, t_{G_i}^{\mathbf{o},\mathbf{w}})\mathbf{w})$ and $t_{G_i}^{\mathbf{o},\mathbf{w}} := \arg \max_{t \geq 0} G_i(\mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{w})$ [S1].
        
    *   **Normal Field:** The Gaussian vector field $V(\mathbf{x})$ is the gradient of the log-vacancy, computed as the sum of gradients of Gaussian components oriented towards the query point [S1]:
        $$ V(\mathbf{x}) := \nabla \log v(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}_{n_i^T(\mathbf{x}-\mu_i) \geq 0} \nabla \log(1 - G_i(\mathbf{x})) $$
        The normal field is the normalized vector field [S1]:
        $$ \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}) := \frac{V(\mathbf{x})}{\|V(\mathbf{x})\|} $$

4.  **Optimization Losses:**
    The total loss combines photometric, depth-normal consistency, normal alignment, and multi-view losses [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{RGB}} + \lambda_{\text{DN}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{DN}} + \lambda_{\text{N}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{N}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{MV}} $$
    *   **Normal Alignment Loss ($\mathcal{L}_N$):** Enforces surface wrapping by aligning rendered normals with depth gradients [S1]:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_N = \sum_p 1 - N(p) \cdot \nabla D(p) $$
        where $N(p)$ is the rendered oriented normal at pixel $p$ and $\nabla D(p)$ is the image-space gradient of the rendered depth [S1].
    *   **Loss Weights:** $\lambda_{DN} = 0.05$, $\lambda_{N} = 0.05$, $\lambda_{pc} = 0.6$, $\lambda_{gc} = 0.02$ [S1].

5.  **Densification:**
    Every $K$ iterations, compute errors $\mathcal{L}_N(p)$ across all training views and propagate them to individual Gaussians via their blending weights [S1]. High-error Gaussians are cloned with flipped normals to close holes in the wrapping shell [S1].

6.  **Mesh Extraction (Optional):**
    Two methods are described for extracting a mesh from the geometric fields [S1]:
    
    *   **Pivot-Based Marching Tetrahedra:**
        1.  Spawn two Delaunay pivots per Gaussian: the center $\mu_i$ and $\mu_i + 3s_i n_i$, where $s_i = \|S_i R_i^T n_i\|_2$ is the ellipsoid scaling along $n_i$.
        2.  Compute occupancy values at each pivot using the vacancy lower bound (Eq. 7).
        3.  Apply Marching Tetrahedra on the resulting Delaunay triangulation.
        4.  Refine vertices to the 0.5-isosurface via binary search.
        
    *   **Primal Adaptive Meshing:**
        1.  **Vertices Initialization:** Initialize vertices by sampling marching tetrahedra mesh faces, weighting each face inversely proportional to its distance from the nearest training camera.
        2.  **Isosurface Refinement:** Project vertices onto the 0.5-isosurface of $v$ via iterative Newton update:
            $$ x_{i+1} = x_i + \frac{1}{2}(0.5 - v(x_i))\mathcal{N}(x_i) $$
        3.  **Filtering:** Remove vertices $x$ with $|0.5 - v(x)| > \epsilon$. Iterate stages 1-3 until no points are removed.
        4.  **Delaunay:** Compute Delaunay tetrahedralization of remaining vertices and classify tetrahedra as inside/outside based on vacancy of sampled interior points.
        5.  **Mesh extraction:** Extract triangle faces separating inside and outside tetrahedra.

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_{DN} = 0.05$
*   $\lambda_{N} = 0.05$
*   $\lambda_{pc} = 0.6$
*   $\lambda_{gc} = 0.02$
*   $K$: Iteration interval for densification (Not given in the sources.)
*   $\epsilon$: Threshold for vertex filtering in Primal Adaptive Meshing (Not given in the sources.)

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Wrapping Assumption:** The formulation assumes Gaussians properly "wrap" the scene geometry. If Gaussians fail to cover all sides of a surface, gaps appear, causing $\mathcal{L}_N(p)$ to rise locally [S1].
*   **Hidden Gaussians:** Gaussians that do not contribute to rendering can remain hidden inside the geometry after optimization, violating the wrapping assumption and producing artifacts if intersected along a ray [S1].
*   **Reciprocity:** The consistency requirement is that the region where transmittance is constant must lie inside the object. This holds when Gaussians properly wrap the surface geometry [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering is similar to standard 3D Gaussian Splatting. Geometric field derivation requires iterating over all training rays for each query point, which is computationally expensive if done naively, but optimized via the max-transmittance approximation [S1]. Mesh extraction adds overhead depending on the method (Pivot-Based vs. Primal Adaptive) [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Adds one normal vector $n_i$ per Gaussian compared to standard 3DGS [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Restores reciprocity, enabling explicit geometric field derivation (normal and occupancy) and high-fidelity mesh extraction without sacrificing surface fidelity [S1]. Produces substantially lighter, fully watertight meshes compared to prior works requiring 9 pivots per Gaussian [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on the "wrapping" assumption; poor wrapping leads to artifacts and requires densification with flipped normals [S1].
*   **When to use:** When explicit surface geometry (mesh, normals) is required from a radiance field representation.
*   **When not to use:** When only image synthesis is needed and computational efficiency is paramount, as standard 3DGS is simpler and faster for rendering alone.

## Background
Oriented Gaussian Splatting was introduced in the paper "From Blobs to Spokes: High-Fidelity Surface Reconstruction via Oriented Gaussians" [S1]. It builds on the Objects as Volumes (OaV) framework [S1] to address the lack of reciprocity in standard 3D Gaussian Splatting attenuation, which prevented the derivation of closed-form vacancy expressions [S1].
