# Hard and Soft Depth Regularization


## Overview
Hard and Soft Depth Regularization is a geometric constraint strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that prevents detail loss and blurred appearance by decoupling the optimization of Gaussian centers and opacities from their shape parameters. It solves the problem where standard depth regularization forces anisotropic Gaussian shapes to conform to smooth monocular depth estimates, which conflicts with complex color appearance [S1]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) and [3D Half-Gaussian Splatting Covariance Transformation and Regularization](9150-3d-half-gaussian-splatting-covariance-transformation-and-reg.md) by explicitly freezing scale and rotation to enforce spatial reshaping (movement of centers) rather than shape deformation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Current set of 3D Gaussians, each defined by center $\mu$, scale $s$, rotation quaternion $q$, opacity $\alpha$, and spherical harmonic coefficients for color.
*   Ground truth depth map $D_{gt}$ and color image $I_{gt}$ for a reference view.
*   Rendered depth map $D_{pred}$ and color image $I_{pred}$ from the current Gaussian state.

**Outputs:**
*   Gradient updates for Gaussian centers $\mu$ and opacities $\alpha$.
*   Updated Gaussian parameters after applying gradients.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Field:** A list or array of Gaussian primitives. Each primitive stores:
    *   $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Center position.
    *   $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Scale (log-space or linear, depending on implementation).
    *   $q \in \mathbb{R}^4$: Rotation quaternion.
    *   $\alpha \in [0, 1]$: Opacity.
    *   $c \in \mathbb{R}^k$: Color coefficients (SH).
*   **Depth Map:** A 2D array $D_{pred}$ of size $H \times W$, storing the weighted average depth of splatted Gaussians at each pixel.
*   **Alpha/Weight Map:** A 2D array $T$ of size $H \times W$, storing the accumulated blending weights (transmittance/opacity contribution) at each pixel.

**Method Steps:**

1.  **Freeze Shape Parameters:**
    During the depth regularization phase, fix the scale $s$ and rotation $q$ of all Gaussians. These parameters are not updated by the gradients derived from the depth loss. Only centers $\mu$ and opacities $\alpha$ are trainable [S1].

2.  **Render Two Depth Maps:**
    Render two distinct depth maps from the current Gaussian state using standard volumetric rendering (alpha blending):
    *   **Hard Depth Map ($D_{hard}$):** The standard weighted average depth. For a pixel $p$, let $N_p$ be the set of Gaussians splatted to $p$, sorted by depth. Let $\omega_i$ be the blending weight of Gaussian $i$ at $p$, and $d_i$ be its depth.
        $$ D_{hard}(p) = \frac{\sum_{i \in N_p} \omega_i d_i}{\sum_{i \in N_p} \omega_i} $$
        Note: The denominator is the accumulated opacity/transmittance factor.
    *   **Soft Depth Map ($D_{soft}$):** A depth map that emphasizes the contribution of Gaussians with higher opacity or specific depth ranges, often implemented by weighting the depth contribution differently or using a different accumulation strategy that is less sensitive to outliers. In the context of DNGaussian, this is used to independently adjust opacities. Specifically, the "soft" aspect refers to using the depth gradient to update opacities, effectively treating depth as a soft constraint on the visibility of primitives rather than just their position. The source describes rendering two types of depth to independently adjust center and opacity [S1]. A common implementation for the "soft" depth used for opacity updates is the same $D_{hard}$ but differentiated with respect to $\alpha$ rather than $\mu$.

3.  **Compute Hard Depth Loss (Center Update):**
    Compute a loss $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ between $D_{hard}$ and $D_{gt}$. This loss is used to compute gradients with respect to the Gaussian centers $\mu$.
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{hard} = \mathcal{L}_{depth}(D_{hard}, D_{gt}) $$
    Common choices for $\mathcal{L}_{depth}$ include L1 loss or scale-invariant losses (though scale-invariant losses may require normalization, see [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md) for improvements).
    Compute gradients:
    $$ \nabla_{\mu} \mathcal{L}_{hard} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{hard}}{\partial D_{hard}} \frac{\partial D_{hard}}{\partial \mu} $$
    Update $\mu$:
    $$ \mu \leftarrow \mu - \eta \nabla_{\mu} \mathcal{L}_{hard} $$
    where $\eta$ is the learning rate.

4.  **Compute Soft Depth Loss (Opacity Update):**
    Compute a loss $\mathcal{L}_{soft}$ between $D_{soft}$ and $D_{gt}$. This loss is used to compute gradients with respect to the Gaussian opacities $\alpha$.
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{soft} = \mathcal{L}_{depth}(D_{soft}, D_{gt}) $$
    Compute gradients:
    $$ \nabla_{\alpha} \mathcal{L}_{soft} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{soft}}{\partial D_{soft}} \frac{\partial D_{soft}}{\partial \alpha} $$
    Update $\alpha$:
    $$ \alpha \leftarrow \alpha - \eta \nabla_{\alpha} \mathcal{L}_{soft} $$
    (Note: Opacity updates may require clamping to $[0, 1]$ or using a sigmoid activation if $\alpha$ is stored in logit space).

5.  **Combine with Photometric Loss:**
    The total loss for the training step includes the photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{photo}$ (e.g., L1 + SSIM) computed on the color image $I_{pred}$ vs $I_{gt}$, and the depth regularization losses.
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{photo} + \lambda_{hard} \mathcal{L}_{hard} + \lambda_{soft} \mathcal{L}_{soft} $$
    The gradients for $\mu$ and $\alpha$ from the depth terms are applied independently, while $\mathcal{L}_{photo}$ provides gradients for all parameters (including $s, q, c$ if not frozen, but in this specific method, $s, q$ are frozen during depth regularization steps, so $\mathcal{L}_{photo}$ gradients for $s, q$ might be ignored or handled in a separate phase). The source states that shape parameters are frozen *during depth regularization* [S1]. This implies a decoupled optimization loop or a conditional gradient application.

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def hard_and_soft_depth_regularization_step(gaussians, gt_depth, gt_color, lr, lambda_hard, lambda_soft):
    """
    Performs one step of Hard and Soft Depth Regularization.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List of Gaussian objects with attributes .mu, .s, .q, .alpha, .color
        gt_depth: Ground truth depth map (H, W)
        gt_color: Ground truth color image (H, W, 3)
        lr: Learning rate
        lambda_hard: Weight for hard depth loss (center update)
        lambda_soft: Weight for soft depth loss (opacity update)
        
    Returns:
        Updated gaussians
    """
    # 1. Freeze shape parameters (s, q) for this step
    # In practice, this means not computing gradients for s and q from depth loss
    # or setting their gradients to zero.
    
    # 2. Render Hard Depth Map
    # D_hard is the weighted average depth
    # T is the accumulated opacity (sum of weights)
    D_hard, T, rendered_color = render_depth_and_color(gaussians)
    
    # 3. Render Soft Depth Map
    # In DNGaussian, the "soft" depth is often the same D_hard but differentiated w.r.t alpha
    # Or it might refer to a depth map that is less sensitive to outliers.
    # For implementation simplicity, we use D_hard for both, but differentiate w.r.t different params.
    D_soft = D_hard 
    
    # 4. Compute Hard Depth Loss (for centers)
    # Use L1 loss as a baseline
    loss_hard = torch.mean(torch.abs(D_hard - gt_depth))
    
    # 5. Compute Soft Depth Loss (for opacities)
    loss_soft = torch.mean(torch.abs(D_soft - gt_depth))
    
    # 6. Compute Photometric Loss (for color, and potentially shape if not frozen)
    # Note: If shape is frozen, we only care about color gradients for appearance
    # But the source says "freeze scale and rotation... to enable spatial reshaping"
    # This implies shape is NOT updated by depth loss. Photometric loss might still update shape?
    # The source says: "freeze the shape parameters... during depth regularization"
    # This suggests a separate phase or a conditional update.
    # We assume here that photometric loss is computed but shape gradients are zeroed out if in depth reg phase.
    loss_photo = photometric_loss(rendered_color, gt_color)
    
    # 7. Backpropagate and Update
    # Zero gradients
    for g in gaussians:
        g.mu.grad = None
        g.alpha.grad = None
        g.s.grad = None
        g.q.grad = None
        g.color.grad = None
        
    # Compute gradients for Hard Loss w.r.t centers
    loss_hard.backward(gradient=torch.ones_like(D_hard), retain_graph=True)
    # The gradient flows through D_hard to mu.
    # We need to ensure only mu gets the gradient from loss_hard.
    # In PyTorch, if we call backward on loss_hard, it computes grads for all leaf tensors.
    # We must zero out grads for s, q, alpha, color from this pass.
    for g in gaussians:
        g.s.grad = None
        g.q.grad = None
        g.alpha.grad = None
        g.color.grad = None
        
    # Compute gradients for Soft Loss w.r.t opacities
    loss_soft.backward(gradient=torch.ones_like(D_soft))
    # Zero out grads for mu, s, q, color from this pass
    for g in gaussians:
        g.mu.grad = None
        g.s.grad = None
        g.q.grad = None
        g.color.grad = None
        
    # Compute gradients for Photometric Loss
    # If we are strictly freezing shape during depth reg, we might skip this or apply it separately.
    # The source implies a balance between depth fitting and color appearance.
    # Let's assume photometric loss is applied to all params except s, q if they are frozen.
    # However, the source says "freeze... during depth regularization".
    # This might mean a separate optimization step.
    # For a combined step, we assume photometric loss updates color and potentially shape,
    # but depth loss only updates mu and alpha.
    # To be safe and follow "freeze shape", we zero out s, q grads from photo loss too.
    loss_photo.backward()
    for g in gaussians:
        g.s.grad = None
        g.q.grad = None
        
    # Update parameters
    for g in gaussians:
        if g.mu.grad is not None:
            g.mu.data -= lr * (lambda_hard * g.mu.grad)
        if g.alpha.grad is not None:
            g.alpha.data -= lr * (lambda_soft * g.alpha.grad)
        # Color is updated by photo loss
        if g.color.grad is not None:
            g.color.data -= lr * g.color.grad
            
    return gaussians
```

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_{hard}, \lambda_{soft}$: Weights for depth losses. Typical values are not given in the sources. [G: Common values in similar works range from 0.1 to 1.0, but must be tuned.]
*   $D_{gt}$: Ground truth depth. Must be aligned with the camera pose of $I_{gt}$.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Smooth Depth Conflict:** Applying depth regularization directly to NeRF-style MLPs or un-frozen Gaussian shapes forces the primitive geometry to conform to smooth monocular depth estimates, which conflicts with complex color appearance and causes detail loss [S1]. This method avoids this by freezing shape.
*   **Scale-Invariant Losses:** Existing scale-invariant depth losses often align depth maps to a fixed scale, which may overlook minor depth variations. This method builds on [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md) to address this [S1].
*   **Noisy Depth:** Gaussian radiance fields are more sensitive to small depth errors than NeRFs, which may result in a noisy distribution of primitives and failures in regions with complex textures [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md)
A depth regularization technique that prevents scale-invariant losses from overlooking minor depth variations, thereby improving detailed geometry reshaping in texture-rich regions. It sets itself apart from standard scale-invariant losses by combining global scale preservation with local scale amplification, addressing the issue where existing methods align depth maps to a fixed global scale and ignore small local errors. It builds on [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) and differs from siblings [Depth Reinitialization](9233-depth-reinitialization.md), [Position Regularization](11049-position-regularization.md), and [Coherent 3D Gaussian Optimization](14045-coherent-3d-gaussian-optimization.md) by explicitly decoupling absolute scale maintenance from local error amplification during gradient computation.

### [Depth Reinitialization](9233-depth-reinitialization.md)
A regularization strategy that alleviates 'overlapping' and 'under-reconstruction' artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting by leveraging dense depth information to reorganize the spatial distribution of Gaussians. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by rejecting standard alpha-blended depth maps in favor of geometric mid-point depths to generate clean, artifact-free point clouds for reinitialization.

### [Isotropic Regularization](10092-isotropic-regularization.md)
A geometric constraint used in continuous SLAM-based Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of highly elongated Gaussian primitives along the viewing ray direction. While standard differentiable rendering imposes no constraint on Gaussian scaling along the viewing ray, this lack of constraint causes artifacts and tracking instability in continuous SLAM scenarios where viewpoints are not carefully selected. It sets itself apart from its parent [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by explicitly penalizing anisotropy (stretching) rather than just enforcing depth consistency, and from [Global-Local Depth Normalization](9179-global-local-depth-normalization.md) by operating on the scale parameters directly rather than depth values.

### [Position Regularization](11049-position-regularization.md)
Prevents floating artifacts and dramatic geometry shifts in 3D Gaussian Splatting by constraining Gaussian centers to remain close to a reference surface or their initial positions. It operates as a geometric prior constraint, distinct from depth-consistency methods in its parent category.

### [Coherent 3D Gaussian Optimization](14045-coherent-3d-gaussian-optimization.md)
A regularization strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that enforces spatial coherence in Gaussian positions and opacities during training, specifically addressing artifacts in sparse-view novel view synthesis. It sets itself apart from standard 3DGS by replacing independent per-pixel residual updates with a learned implicit decoder that produces smooth, coherent residual fields for depth and opacity, supplemented by multiview total variation and optical flow constraints. This prevents the "semi-transparent" artifacts and detail loss common when optimizing Gaussians against sparse input views.

### Geometric Regularization via Bilateral Filter
A regularization loss that enforces coherent motion by grouping Gaussians in image space using a bilateral filter approach, avoiding the dampening of independent motion caused by spatial KNN. For a training image $I$ at time $t_I$, sample another time $t'$. Compute the 6D screw-axis representation of the transformation between $t_I$ and $t'$ for each Gaussian. Compute the geometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ as the weighted sum of squared distances between screw-axis representations of neighboring pixels. The geometric loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}} = \sum_{p' \in \mathcal{N}(p)} k_{c,c'} k_{d,d'} k_{p,p'} \|s_p - s_{p'}\|^2$, where $k_{c,c'}$ is color similarity, $k_{d,d'}$ is depth similarity, $k_{p,p'}$ is spatial distance weight, and $s_p, s_{p'}$ are screw-axis representations. Unlike standard bilateral filters, the loss is not divided by the sum of weights because the goal is comparison, not energy conservation [S2].

### Near-Far Regularization
A regularization term applied alongside depth distortion to constrain Gaussians to aggregate at object surfaces, ensuring robust 3D structure reconstruction. It narrows the distance between the weighted depth of the nearest and farthest $M$ Gaussians on each ray. Extract subset $N$ (nearest $M$ Gaussians) and $F$ (farthest $M$ Gaussians, where blending weight approaches 1) on each ray. Render depth maps $d_N, d_F$ and blending weight maps $T_N, T_F$ for subsets $N$ and $F$. The regularization term is $R_{nf} = T_N \cdot T_F \cdot |d_N - d_F|$, where $T$ is the sum of blending weights $\omega_i$. It prunes Gaussians at the front or back of each ray through opacity supervision when there is a significant disparity between them, and supervises the position of the first and last $M$ Gaussians to be as close as possible. Used in LE3D to improve structural reconstruction quality for downstream tasks [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method adds two rendering passes (or one pass with two outputs) and two backward passes per training step. This increases the computational cost compared to standard 3DGS by a factor of approximately 2-3x, depending on the efficiency of the depth rendering implementation.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing two additional depth maps ($D_{hard}, D_{soft}$) and their gradients, increasing memory usage by $O(H \times W)$ per view.
*   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Scaling:** The method scales with the number of Gaussians and image resolution. The depth regularization is particularly effective in sparse-view scenarios where photometric cues are insufficient [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Prevents detail loss and blurred appearance caused by shape deformation. Decouples geometric and photometric optimization, allowing for better fitting of complex color appearance while maintaining coarse depth consistency.
*   **Limitations:** Requires ground truth depth maps. Sensitive to noisy depth estimates. Freezing shape parameters may limit the ability of Gaussians to adapt to complex geometries if the initial shape is poor.
*   **When to use:** Sparse-view reconstruction, scenes with complex textures where depth regularization is needed to prevent floating artifacts, but shape deformation must be avoided.
*   **When not to use:** Dense-view reconstruction where photometric cues are sufficient, or when ground truth depth is unavailable.

## Background
Originated in the paper "DNGaussian: Optimizing Sparse-View 3D Gaussian Radiance Fields with Global-Local Depth Normalization" [S1]. The method addresses the limitations of existing depth regularization strategies for NeRFs and 3DGS, which often force Gaussian shapes to fit smooth monocular depth, leading to detail loss. By freezing shape parameters and independently updating centers and opacities, the method achieves a balance between depth fitting and color appearance.
