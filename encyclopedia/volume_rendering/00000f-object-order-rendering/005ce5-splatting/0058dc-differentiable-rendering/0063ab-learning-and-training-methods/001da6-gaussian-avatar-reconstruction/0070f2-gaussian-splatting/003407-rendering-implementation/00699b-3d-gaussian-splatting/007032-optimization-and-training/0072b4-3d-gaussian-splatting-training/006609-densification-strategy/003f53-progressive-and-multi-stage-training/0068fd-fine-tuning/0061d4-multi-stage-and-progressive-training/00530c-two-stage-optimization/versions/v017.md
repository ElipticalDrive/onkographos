# Two-stage Optimization

Also called: Full-Resolution Refinement, GS-IR Training Process, Low-Resolution Optimization

## Overview
Two-stage optimization is a training strategy for 3D Gaussian Splatting that decouples the optimization landscape into distinct phases to improve stability, convergence speed, or representation fidelity. It addresses the difficulty of simultaneously optimizing complex geometric parameters, appearance codes, and compression bottlenecks from sparse or random initializations. Unlike single-stage training, it separates coarse initialization or stable generation from fine-grained refinement or rate-distortion optimization, allowing each stage to focus on a specific subset of parameters or resolution levels [S1, S2][S3, unofficial].

## How it works
The method generally follows a sequence of parameter freezing/unfreezing and resolution scaling. While specific implementations vary by application (CT reconstruction, inverse rendering, or compression), the core logic involves an initial stage for stable placement or generation, followed by a second stage for refinement or encoding.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** Multi-view images (or projection data), camera poses, and optionally depth maps.
*   **Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G}$ with optimized parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color/material) and, in compression variants, a compressed bitstream.

### Data Structures
The Gaussian representation $\mathcal{G}$ typically consists of a list of primitives. Each Gaussian $i$ is defined by:
*   Position $\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$
*   Scale $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$
*   Rotation $\mathbf{q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$ (quaternion)
*   Opacity $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$
*   Color/Material coefficients (e.g., SH coefficients or PBR parameters)

In compression variants (CodecSplat), an intermediate feature tensor $\mathbf{F}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C_F \times H \times W}$ is used, which is passed through a feature codec [S3, unofficial].

### Steps

#### Variant 1: Low-Resolution to Full-Resolution (3DGR-CT)
Used for sparse-view CT reconstruction to handle computational burden and ensure correct initial placement [S1].

1.  **Stage 1: Low-Resolution Optimization**
    *   Define a coarse 3D volume grid with resolution $1/2 \times 1/2 \times 1/2$ of the target volume.
    *   Initialize Gaussians within this coarse grid.
    *   Optimize Gaussian parameters to minimize the projection loss. The loss function is the $L_2$ norm between projected data $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ and actual measurements $\mathbf{p}$:
        $$ \min_{\theta_i} \sum_{i=1}^{N} || \hat{\mathbf{p}}[\mathcal{V}(\cdot|\{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^N)] - \mathbf{p} ||_2^2 $$
    *   This stage rapidly places Gaussians in approximately correct positions [S1].

2.  **Stage 2: Full-Resolution Refinement**
    *   Expand the sampling domain to all voxels of the original high-resolution volume.
    *   Use the Stage 1 Gaussians as initialization.
    *   Refine Gaussian parameters (position, scale, etc.) to capture fine-grained details.
    *   Minimize the same projection loss function on the full-resolution grid [S1].

#### Variant 2: Initial Geometry/Appearance then Decomposition (GS-IR)
Used for inverse rendering to separate geometry from material/lighting [S2].

1.  **Stage 1: Initial Optimization**
    *   Optimize geometric parameters $\mathbf{T} = \{\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{q}, \mathbf{n}\}$ (position, scale, rotation, normal) and appearance parameters $\mathbf{A}$ (color).
    *   Loss function:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{init} = \mathcal{L}_c + \mathcal{L}_n $$
    *   $\mathcal{L}_c$ is the $L_1$ color reconstruction loss.
    *   $\mathcal{L}_n$ is the normal loss, defined as:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_n = \mathcal{L}_{n-p} + \lambda_{n-TV} \text{TV}_{normal} $$
    *   Train for 30,000 iterations using the Adam optimizer [S2].

2.  **Stage 2: Decomposition Optimization**
    *   Fix geometric parameters $\mathbf{T}$ and appearance $\mathcal{A}$.
    *   Optimize material parameters $\mathbf{M} = \{a, \rho, m\}$ (albedo, roughness, metalness) and lighting.
    *   Loss function:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_d = \mathcal{L}_{shade} + \lambda_M \text{TV}_{mat} + \lambda_E \text{TV}_{light} $$
    *   Train for 10,000 iterations [S2].

#### Variant 3: Codec-Free to Codec-Aware (CodecSplat)
Used for ultra-compact latent coding to stabilize training of quantized bottlenecks [S3, unofficial].

1.  **Stage 1: Codec-Free Gaussian Generation**
    *   Train the feed-forward Gaussian generation pipeline *without* the feature codec.
    *   Predict Gaussian set $\mathcal{G}_{cf}$ from refined feature $\mathbf{F}_c$ and encoder-side depth $\mathbf{D}_{enc}$.
    *   Render target view $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{tcf} = \text{Render}(\mathcal{G}_{cf}, \mathbf{K}_t, \mathbf{T}_t)$.
    *   Loss function:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{stage1} = \mathcal{D}_{render}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{tcf}, \mathbf{Y}_t) $$
    *   Where $\mathbf{Y}_t$ is the target-view image and $\mathcal{D}_{render}$ is the rendering reconstruction loss [S3, unofficial].

2.  **Stage 2: Codec-Aware Latent Coding**
    *   Initialize from Stage 1 checkpoint.
    *   Enable the feature codec and post-codec refiner.
    *   Optimize codec-related modules while keeping remaining backbone modules fixed.
    *   Render decoded Gaussian set $\hat{\mathcal{G}}$ to target view: $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_t = \text{Render}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}, \mathbf{K}_t, \mathbf{T}_t)$.
    *   Loss function:
        $$ \mathcal{L}_{stage2}(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{max}} \left[ \mathcal{D}_{render}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_t, \mathbf{Y}_t) + \gamma \mathcal{D}_{codec-depth} + \beta R \right] $$
    *   Where $R$ is the estimated rate, $\gamma$ weights the codec-side depth supervision, and $\beta$ controls the rate penalty.
    *   Codec-side depth loss:
        $$ \mathcal{D}_{codec-depth} = || \mathbf{S} - \mathbf{S}_{enc} ||_1 $$
    *   $\mathbf{S}$ is the decoder-side normalized inverse-depth map.
    *   $\mathbf{S}_{enc}$ is the supervision target from encoder-side depth $\mathbf{D}_{enc}$:
        $$ \mathbf{S}_{enc,i}(p) = \frac{\mathbf{D}_{enc,i}(p)^{-1} - f_i^{-1}}{n_i^{-1} - f_i^{-1}} $$
    *   Where $n_i$ and $f_i$ are near and far bounds of context view $i$.
    *   During training, sample $\lambda \in [16, 1024]$. At test time, fix $\lambda$ [S3, unofficial].

### Parameters
*   **3DGR-CT:** No specific learning rate or iteration count given in sources. Uses $L_2$ norm [S1].
*   **GS-IR:**
    *   Optimizer: Adam [S2].
    *   Iterations: 30,000 (Stage 1), 10,000 (Stage 2) [S2].
    *   $\lambda_{n-TV}$: 5.0 [S2].
    *   $\lambda_M$: 1.0 [S2].
    *   $\lambda_E$: 0.01 [S2].
    *   Hardware: Single NVIDIA V100 GPU [S2].
*   **CodecSplat:**
    *   Optimizer: AdamW, LR $10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01 [S3, unofficial].
    *   Hardware: Four NVIDIA RTX 5880 48GB GPUs [S3, unofficial].
    *   $\lambda_{max}$: 1024 [S3, unofficial].
    *   $\gamma$: 0.1 [S3, unofficial].
    *   $\beta$: $10^{-4}$ (DL3DV), $4 \times 10^{-4}$ (RE10K) [S3, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Instability:** Directly training with a quantized bottleneck (CodecSplat) is unstable; the two-stage approach is required to stabilize Gaussian generation before introducing compression artifacts [S3, unofficial].
*   **Resolution Mismatch:** In low-resolution stages, fine details are lost; the second stage must adequately refine these without getting stuck in local minima caused by the coarse initialization [S1].
*   **Parameter Coupling:** In inverse rendering, failing to fix geometry in Stage 2 can lead to material/lighting ambiguity [S2].

## Subtopics

### [Two-stage training procedure for Relightable 3D Gaussians](15262-two-stage-training-procedure-for-relightable-3d-gaussians.md)
Defines the optimization protocol for Relightable 3D Gaussians (R3DG), combining standard 3DGS with PBR. It uses normal gradient-based densification in Stage 1 and PBR-specific losses in Stage 2. Chosen when relighting and material recovery are required from static multi-view inputs.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Generally reduces total training time compared to single-stage high-resolution optimization by allowing rapid convergence in the coarse stage (3DGR-CT) [S1]. However, it adds overhead from the sequential nature of the stages.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Low-resolution stages (3DGR-CT) significantly reduce memory footprint during the initial phase [S1]. CodecSplat reduces final storage via compression but requires GPU memory for the encoder/decoder backbone [S3, unofficial].
*   **Performance:**
    *   **3DGR-CT:** Improves reconstruction quality in sparse-view CT by ensuring correct initial placement [S1].
    *   **GS-IR:** Achieves high-quality inverse rendering results on a single V100 GPU [S2].
    *   **CodecSplat:** Achieves KiB-level scene bitstreams (e.g., 87.29 KiB on DL3DV) while maintaining competitive PSNR (28.52 dB) compared to MiB-level baselines [S3, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Stability, reduced computational burden in early stages, better handling of complex parameter spaces (geometry vs. material, generation vs. compression).
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of stage transitions and loss weights. Not suitable for real-time training where latency is critical.

## Background
*   **Origin:** The concept of multi-stage optimization in 3DGS is not attributed to a single paper but has emerged in various specialized applications.
    *   **3DGR-CT:** Introduced in "3DGR-CT: Sparse-view CT reconstruction with a 3D Gaussian representation" (2025) for medical imaging [S1].
    *   **GS-IR:** Introduced in "GS-IR: 3D Gaussian Splatting for Inverse Rendering" (CVPR 2024) for computer graphics [S2].
    *   **CodecSplat:** Introduced in "CodecSplat: Ultra-Compact Latent Coding for Feed-Forward 3D Gaussian Splatting" (2026 preprint) for compression [S3, unofficial].
*   **History:** As 3DGS expanded from novel view synthesis to inverse rendering, medical imaging, and compression, the need to decouple conflicting optimization objectives (e.g., geometric accuracy vs. compression rate, or geometry vs. material) led to the adoption of two-stage protocols.
