# 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) Preliminaries

Also called: 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS), 3D Gaussian Splatting Representation, Standard 3DGS

## Overview
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is an explicit scene representation method that models a 3D scene as a collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives, replacing the implicit neural fields of NeRF with discrete, differentiable volumetric splats [S1, S2, S4, S5, S10, S12, S16, S19, S20][S25, S27, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial]. It solves the trade-off between the high visual quality of neural radiance fields and the real-time rendering speeds of traditional rasterization by projecting these 3D Gaussians onto the image plane as 2D ellipses and compositing them via alpha blending [S1, S2, S4, S10, S12, S16, S19][S25, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial]. Unlike NeRF, which requires expensive ray marching and network queries, 3DGS enables real-time rendering through GPU-friendly tile-based rasterization [S1, S2, S4, S10, S12, S16, S19][S25, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial]. It sets apart from its siblings by using explicit Gaussian ellipsoids parameterized by mean, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonics, optimized end-to-end from sparse Structure-from-Motion (SfM) point clouds [S1, S2, S4, S5, S12, S19][S27, unofficial][S31][S37, S42, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A set of calibrated RGB images $\{I_m\}_{m=1}^M$ with known camera poses (extrinsics $W_m$ and intrinsics $K_m$), and an initial sparse 3D point cloud estimated via Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [S1, S5, S12][S27, unofficial][S30][S37, S42, unofficial].
**Outputs:** A rendered RGB image $\hat{C}$ for a target camera view, and optimized Gaussian parameters (mean, rotation, scale, opacity, spherical harmonics coefficients) [S1, S2, S5, S12][S28, S32, S42, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
The scene is represented as a set of $N$ 3D Gaussian primitives $G = \{g_i\}_{i=1}^N$. Each primitive $g_i$ is defined by the following attributes [S1, S2, S4, S5][S7, unofficial][S12, S19, S20][S27, S37, S42, S49, unofficial]:
1.  **Mean Position:** $\mu_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, representing the center of the Gaussian [S1, S2, S4, S5][S7, unofficial][S12, S19, S20][S27, S37, S42, S49, unofficial].
2.  **Covariance Matrix:** $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, defining the shape and orientation [S1, S2, S4, S5][S7, unofficial][S12, S19, S20][S27, S37, S42, S49, unofficial]. To ensure positive semi-definiteness and facilitate optimization, $\Sigma_i$ is parameterized by a rotation quaternion $q_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$ (normalized to unit length) and a scale vector $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (typically stored in logarithmic space to ensure positivity) [S2, S5][S7, unofficial][S12, S18][S28, unofficial][S31][S37, unofficial][S43][S49, unofficial]:
    $$ \Sigma_i = R_i S_i S_i^\top R_i^\top $$
    where $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is the rotation matrix derived from $q_i$, and $S_i = \text{diag}(s_{i,x}, s_{i,y}, s_{i,z})$ is the diagonal scaling matrix [S2, S5][S7, unofficial][S12, S18][S28, unofficial][S31][S37, unofficial][S43][S49, unofficial].
3.  **Opacity:** $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$, representing the intrinsic transparency of the primitive [S2, S5][S7, unofficial][S12, S19, S20][S27, S37, S42, S49, unofficial].
4.  **Color:** View-dependent color $c_i(d)$ parameterized by spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients $C_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times (d+1)^2}$, where $d$ is the degree of the SH basis [S2, S4, S5][S7, unofficial][S11, S12, S19, S20][S27, S37, S42, S49, unofficial]. The color for a viewing direction $d$ is computed as $c_i(d) = \text{SH}(d, C_i)$ [S2, S11, S12][S42, S47, unofficial].

**Rendering Pipeline:**
For a given camera view with extrinsic matrix $W$ and intrinsic matrix $K$:

1.  **Projection to Camera Space:**
    Transform the 3D mean $\mu_i$ to camera coordinates:
    $$ \mu'_i = W \mu_i $$
    (Note: Some sources use homogeneous coordinates $[\mu_i, 1]^\top$ and apply $KW$ [S5, S44]. The standard 3DGS approach typically transforms to camera space first, then projects.)

2.  **2D Splatting (Projection to Image Plane):**
    Project the 3D Gaussian onto the 2D image plane. The projected mean $\hat{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is obtained via perspective projection:
    $$ \hat{\mu}_i = \pi(K, W \mu_i) $$
    where $\pi$ denotes the perspective projection function [S16, S31, S44].
    
    The projected 2D covariance matrix $\Sigma'_i \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ is approximated using a first-order Taylor expansion (affine approximation) of the perspective projection at $\mu_i$ [S2, S5, S10][S27, S34, S41, unofficial][S44][S46, unofficial]:
    $$ \Sigma'_i = J W \Sigma_i W^\top J^\top $$
    where $W$ is the world-to-camera transformation matrix, and $J \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}$ is the Jacobian of the perspective projection transformation evaluated at $\mu_i$ [S2, S5, S10][S27, S34, S41, unofficial][S44][S46, unofficial]. Specifically, if $P = KW$ is the projection matrix, and $u = P \mu_i$, the Jacobian $J$ relates small changes in 3D space to changes in 2D image coordinates [S2, S5, S10][S27, S34, S41, unofficial][S44][S46, unofficial].

3.  **Rasterization and Sorting:**
    The 2D Gaussians are rasterized onto the image. To handle overlaps, the contributing Gaussians for each pixel (or tile) are sorted in front-to-back order based on their depth (distance from the camera) [S2, S9, S10][S15, unofficial][S16, S19][S28, unofficial][S30][S32, S38, S42, S49, unofficial]. This is typically implemented using a tile-based rasterizer where tiles (e.g., 16x16 pixels) are processed in parallel [S15, S27, S28, unofficial][S30][S41, unofficial].

4.  **Alpha Compositing:**
    For each pixel $p$, the final color $\hat{C}(p)$ is computed by accumulating the contributions of the sorted Gaussians $i=1 \dots N$ using front-to-back alpha blending [S2, S9][S15, unofficial][S19][S32, S33, S38, unofficial][S39][S42, S47, unofficial]:
    $$ \hat{C}(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i(d_p) \cdot \alpha_i(p) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j(p)) $$
    where:
    *   $c_i(d_p)$ is the view-dependent color of the $i$-th Gaussian for the viewing direction $d_p$ from the camera center to pixel $p$ [S2, S9][S15, unofficial][S19][S32, S33, S38, unofficial][S39][S42, S47, unofficial].
    *   $\alpha_i(p)$ is the effective opacity of the $i$-th Gaussian at pixel $p$, calculated as:
        $$ \alpha_i(p) = \alpha_i \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (p - \hat{\mu}_i)^\top (\Sigma'_i)^{-1} (p - \hat{\mu}_i)\right) $$
        where $\alpha_i$ is the learned scalar opacity, and the exponential term is the Gaussian density at pixel $p$ [S2, S5, S9][S15, unofficial][S31][S33, S42, S49, unofficial].
    *   The product term $\prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j(p))$ represents the accumulated transmittance $T_i(p)$, i.e., the probability that the ray has not been blocked by previous Gaussians [S2, S9][S15, unofficial][S19][S32, S33, S38, unofficial][S39][S42, S47, unofficial].

**Training and Optimization:**
The parameters $\{\mu_i, q_i, s_i, \alpha_i, C_i\}$ are optimized using gradient descent (typically Adam) to minimize a loss function between the rendered image $\hat{C}$ and the ground truth image $I$ [S1, S12][S28, S38, S42, unofficial]. The loss is a weighted combination of L1 loss and D-SSIM (Differentiable Structural Similarity) [S12][S38, S46, unofficial]:
$$ \mathcal{L} = (1 - \lambda) \mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{D-SSIM} $$
where $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter (typically 0.2) [S12][S38, unofficial].

**Adaptive Density Control:**
During training, the number of Gaussians is not fixed. Adaptive density control is applied periodically (e.g., every $K$ iterations) to manage complexity and detail [S1, S9, S12][S27, S28, S37, S42, unofficial]:
1.  **Densification:** Gaussians with high view-space positional gradients (indicating under-reconstruction) are either cloned (if small) or split into two smaller Gaussians (if large) [S1, S9, S12][S27, S28, S37, S42, unofficial].
2.  **Pruning:** Gaussians with opacity below a threshold $\epsilon_\alpha$ (e.g., 0.005) or excessively large scales are removed [S12][S27, S28, S37, S42, unofficial].
3.  **Opacity Reset:** Opacity values are periodically reset to prevent saturation and aid convergence [S1, S12][S27, S42, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def compute_covariance(q, s):
    """
    Compute 3D covariance matrix from quaternion q and scale s.
    q: (4,) unit quaternion [w, x, y, z]
    s: (3,) scale vector
    Returns: (3, 3) covariance matrix Sigma
    """
    # Construct rotation matrix R from quaternion q
    # Standard conversion from quaternion to rotation matrix
    w, x, y, z = q[0], q[1], q[2], q[3]
    R = np.array([
        [1 - 2*(y**2 + z**2), 2*(x*y - w*z),     2*(x*z + w*y)],
        [2*(x*y + w*z),       1 - 2*(x**2 + z**2), 2*(y*z - w*x)],
        [2*(x*z - w*y),       2*(y*z + w*x),     1 - 2*(x**2 + y**2)]
    ])
    
    # Construct diagonal scale matrix S
    S = np.diag(s)
    
    # Sigma = R * S * S^T * R^T
    Sigma = R @ (S @ S.T) @ R.T
    return Sigma

def project_gaussian(mu_3d, W, K, Sigma_3d):
    """
    Project 3D Gaussian to 2D image plane.
    mu_3d: (3,) mean in world coordinates
    W: (4, 4) world-to-camera transformation matrix
    K: (3, 3) camera intrinsic matrix
    Sigma_3d: (3, 3) 3D covariance matrix
    Returns: mu_2d (2,), Sigma_2d (2, 2)
    """
    # Transform mean to camera space
    mu_cam = W[:3, :3] @ mu_3d + W[:3, 3]
    
    # Project mean to 2D
    mu_homogeneous = np.append(mu_cam, 1.0)
    mu_proj_homogeneous = K @ mu_cam # Assuming mu_cam is already in camera space, 
                                     # but standard is K * (R*t + R*mu). 
                                     # Let's use the full projection P = K @ W
    P = K @ W
    mu_proj_homogeneous = P @ np.append(mu_3d, 1.0)
    mu_2d = mu_proj_homogeneous[:2] / mu_proj_homogeneous[2]
    
    # Compute Jacobian J of the projection at mu_3d
    # The projection is u = K * (R * mu + t) / (r3^T * mu + t3)
    # Let v = R * mu + t, and z = r3^T * mu + t3
    # u = K11 * v1 / z + K12 * v2 / z + K13 * v3 / z ...
    # Simplified: J is the 2x3 Jacobian of the perspective projection
    # J = d(u,v)/d(mu)
    
    # Compute v and z
    v = W[:3, :3] @ mu_3d + W[:3, 3]
    z = W[2, :3] @ mu_3d + W[2, 3]
    
    # Compute Jacobian of perspective projection
    # u = (K[0,0]*v[0] + K[0,1]*v[1] + K[0,2]*v[2]) / z
    # v = (K[1,0]*v[0] + K[1,1]*v[1] + K[1,2]*v[2]) / z
    
    # Derivatives of v w.r.t mu are just R (W[:3,:3])
    R_cam = W[:3, :3]
    
    # Jacobian of perspective division
    # d(u)/d(v) = (K[0,:]/z) - (K[0,:] @ v) * (1/z^2) * [0,0,1]
    # This is complex. Standard approximation uses the Jacobian of the affine projection
    # J_affine = K[:2, :3] @ R_cam
    # But 3DGS uses the Jacobian of the perspective projection.
    # J = d(u)/d(mu) = d(u)/d(v) * d(v)/d(mu)
    
    # Let's use the standard formula from the sources: Sigma_2d = J * W * Sigma_3d * W^T * J^T
    # Here W in the formula refers to the world-to-camera rotation/translation part?
    # Source [S2, S5][S27, S41, unofficial][S44][S46, unofficial] say: Sigma' = J * W * Sigma * W^T * J^T
    # where W is the viewing transformation matrix (world-to-camera) and J is the Jacobian of the projective transformation.
    
    # Let's compute J explicitly for perspective projection
    # u = f_x * X/Z + c_x
    # v = f_y * Y/Z + c_y
    # d(u)/d(X) = f_x/Z
    # d(u)/d(Y) = 0
    # d(u)/d(Z) = -f_x*X/Z^2
    # This is for camera coordinates.
    
    # Let's stick to the matrix form provided in sources:
    # Sigma_2d = J * W * Sigma_3d * W^T * J^T
    # Where W is the 3x3 rotation part of the world-to-camera transform? Or the full 4x4?
    # Source [S44] says: Sigma'_i = J W Sigma_i W^T J^T, where W is camera extrinsic.
    # Source [S2] says: Sigma' = J W Sigma W^T J^T, where W is viewing transformation matrix.
    
    # Let's assume W is the 3x3 rotation part for the covariance transformation, 
    # and J is the 2x3 Jacobian of the perspective projection in camera coordinates.
    
    # Jacobian of perspective projection in camera coordinates
    # u = K[0,0]*X/Z + K[0,2]
    # v = K[1,1]*Y/Z + K[1,2]
    # d(u)/d(X) = K[0,0]/Z
    # d(u)/d(Y) = 0
    # d(u)/d(Z) = -K[0,0]*X/Z^2
    # d(v)/d(X) = 0
    # d(v)/d(Y) = K[1,1]/Z
    # d(v)/d(Z) = -K[1,1]*Y/Z^2
    
    J = np.array([
        [K[0,0]/z, 0, -K[0,0]*v[0]/z**2],
        [0, K[1,1]/z, -K[1,1]*v[1]/z**2]
    ])
    
    # W in the formula Sigma' = J W Sigma W^T J^T is likely the rotation part of the world-to-camera transform
    # because Sigma is a 3x3 matrix in world space, and we need to rotate it to camera space before projecting.
    # Source [S44] says W is camera extrinsic. If W is 4x4, we need to extract the 3x3 rotation.
    # Let's assume W_rot is the 3x3 rotation part of W.
    W_rot = W[:3, :3]
    
    Sigma_2d = J @ W_rot @ Sigma_3d @ W_rot.T @ J.T
    
    return mu_2d, Sigma_2d

def render_gaussians(gaussians, camera_params, image_shape):
    """
    Render a set of 3D Gaussians to an image.
    gaussians: List of dicts with keys 'mu', 'q', 's', 'alpha', 'SH_coeffs'
    camera_params: Dict with 'W' (4x4), 'K' (3x3)
    image_shape: (H, W)
    Returns: Rendered image (H, W, 3)
    """
    H, W = image_shape
    image = np.zeros((H, W, 3))
    
    # Precompute projected Gaussians
    projected = []
    for g in gaussians:
        Sigma_3d = compute_covariance(g['q'], g['s'])
        mu_2d, Sigma_2d = project_gaussian(g['mu'], camera_params['W'], camera_params['K'], Sigma_3d)
        
        # Check if Gaussian is in front of camera
        mu_cam = camera_params['W'][:3, :3] @ g['mu'] + camera_params['W'][:3, 3]
        if mu_cam[2] <= 0:
            continue
            
        projected.append({
            'mu_2d': mu_2d,
            'Sigma_2d': Sigma_2d,
            'alpha': g['alpha'],
            'SH_coeffs': g['SH_coeffs'],
            'depth': mu_cam[2]
        })
    
    # Sort by depth (front to back)
    projected.sort(key=lambda x: x['depth'])
    
    # Rasterize
    # For each pixel, accumulate color
    # Optimization: Use tile-based rasterization in practice, but here we do naive per-pixel for clarity
    for y in range(H):
        for x in range(W):
            p = np.array([x, y])
            transmittance = 1.0
            color = np.zeros(3)
            
            for proj_g in projected:
                mu_2d = proj_g['mu_2d']
                Sigma_2d = proj_g['Sigma_2d']
                alpha = proj_g['alpha']
                SH_coeffs = proj_g['SH_coeffs']
                
                # Compute Mahalanobis distance
                diff = p - mu_2d
                try:
                    inv_Sigma = np.linalg.inv(Sigma_2d)
                    dist_sq = diff @ inv_Sigma @ diff
                except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
                    continue # Singular matrix
                
                # Gaussian density
                density = np.exp(-0.5 * dist_sq)
                
                # Effective opacity
                effective_alpha = alpha * density
                
                # View direction for SH
                # Approximate view direction from camera center to pixel
                # This is a simplification; in practice, view direction is from camera center to 3D point
                # But for SH evaluation, we need the direction in camera space
                # Let's assume we have the 3D point for this Gaussian
                # We need to store the 3D point or recompute it
                # For this pseudocode, we assume we can get the view direction
                # Let's assume 'view_dir' is passed or computed from the 3D Gaussian's position
                # Since we don't have the 3D position in 'projected', we need to store it or recompute
                # Let's assume we have access to the original Gaussian's 3D position
                # This is a limitation of the simplified pseudocode
                
                # For the sake of the pseudocode, let's assume we have 'view_dir'
                # In reality, we would iterate over the original gaussians list
                # and match them by index or ID
                
                # Let's assume we have 'view_dir' for this Gaussian
                # view_dir = ... (computed from 3D position and camera center)
                
                # Evaluate SH
                # c = SH(view_dir, SH_coeffs)
                # For simplicity, assume constant color c
                c = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]) # Placeholder for SH evaluation
                
                # Alpha compositing
                color += c * effective_alpha * transmittance
                transmittance *= (1 - effective_alpha)
                
                if transmittance < 1e-4:
                    break
            
            image[y, x] = color
            
    return image
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Singular Covariance:** If the projected 2D covariance matrix $\Sigma'_i$ is singular (e.g., due to degenerate Gaussians or numerical instability), the inverse cannot be computed. Guard against this by checking the determinant or using a pseudo-inverse [S2, S5, S10][S27, S34, S41, unofficial][S44][S46, unofficial].
*   **Precision:** Floating-point precision issues can arise during the accumulation of transmittance, especially for deep scenes. Use double precision for intermediate calculations if necessary [S5].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** Gaussians outside the camera frustum should be culled to save computation [S41, unofficial].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If the initial SfM point cloud is sparse or noisy, the initial Gaussian distribution may be poor, leading to slow convergence or artifacts [S1, S12][S27, unofficial][S30][S37, S42, unofficial].

Defines the hyperparameters and procedural rules for optimizing a set of 3D Gaussians to reconstruct a scene from images. ## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training time is significantly faster than NeRF, typically converging in 30-60 minutes on a single GPU, compared to hours for NeRF [S1][S13, unofficial][S19][S28, S35, unofficial]. Rendering time is real-time, achieving >30 FPS at 1080p on modern GPUs [S1][S13, unofficial][S19][S28, S35, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage scales linearly with the number of Gaussians $N$. Each Gaussian stores $\mu$ (3), $q$ (4), $s$ (3), $\alpha$ (1), and SH coefficients ($3 \times (d+1)^2$). For $d=3$, this is $3 \times 16 = 48$ floats per Gaussian. Typical scenes use millions of Gaussians, requiring several GBs of VRAM [S1][S13, unofficial][S19][S28, S35, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** On the NeRF Synthetic (Blender) dataset, 3DGS achieves PSNR = 33.2 ± 0.4 dB, comparable to Mip-NeRF 360 (32.4 ± 0.4 dB) and better than Instant-NGP (32.6 ± 0.4 dB) and NeRF (31.0 ± 0.6 dB) [S1]. Rendering speed is 68.4 ± 4.2 FPS at 1080p on an A100 GPU [S1].
*   **Scaling:** Performance scales well with GPU parallelism due to tile-based rasterization. However, the number of Gaussians can grow large, leading to memory bottlenecks [S1][S13, unofficial][S19][S28, S35, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Real-time rendering, high visual quality, differentiable optimization, explicit representation allows for easy editing and manipulation [S1, S2, S4, S10, S12, S16, S19][S25, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Can produce redundant Gaussians, leading to inefficient memory usage. SH representation may fail to capture complex lighting interactions or high-frequency textures, resulting in color bleeding or artifacts [S4]. Not specifically designed for volumetric media, which may limit its applicability in certain domains [S8].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 as a novel approach to novel-view synthesis [S1, S2, S4, S5, S10, S12, S16, S19, S20][S25, S27, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial]. It builds on the idea of using explicit 3D primitives for rendering, moving away from the implicit neural fields of NeRF [S1, S2, S4, S10, S12, S16, S19][S25, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial]. The method was developed to address the slow training and rendering speeds of NeRF while maintaining high visual quality [S1, S2, S4, S10, S12, S16, S19][S25, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial]. It has since become a baseline for many subsequent works in 3D reconstruction and rendering [S1, S2, S4, S5, S10, S12, S16, S19, S20][S25, S27, S28, unofficial][S31][S32, S37, S42, S48, unofficial].
