# Negative Gaussian Splatting

Also called: Diff-Gaussian

## Overview
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting representation by allowing Gaussian primitives to have negative color values, enabling the modeling of high-frequency elements and complex nonlinear structures that are difficult to capture with positive-only Gaussians [S1, S2]. Unlike standard Gaussian Splatting, which requires many components to approximate complex shapes, NegGS uses the subtraction of Gaussian densities (Diff-Gaussians) to alleviate time complexity while improving the representation of shadows and rapid color transitions [S2].

## How it works
NegGS modifies the primitive attributes of 3D Gaussian Splatting by introducing a joint family of Gaussians that includes both positive and negative color components [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** A set of camera poses and corresponding 2D images; initial 3D point cloud (typically from Structure-from-Motion); hyperparameters for initialization and optimization.
*   **Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussian primitives $\{G_i\}$, each defined by position $\mathbf{p}_i$, covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ (or rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$ and scale $\mathbf{s}_i$), opacity $\alpha_i$, and color $\mathbf{c}_i$, where $\mathbf{c}_i$ can be negative.

### Data Structures
The method retains the standard Gaussian Splatting data structures but adds a mechanism to track the sign of the color component.
*   **Gaussian Primitive:** Defined by the tuple $(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{s}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{c}_i, \mathbf{SH}_i)$, where $\mathbf{SH}_i$ represents spherical harmonic coefficients.
*   **Sign Mask:** A binary mask or flag associated with each Gaussian primitive to distinguish between positive Gaussians ($G_{GS}$) and negative Gaussians ($G_{-GS}$) [S1].
*   **Joint Family:** The set of all Gaussians is partitioned into $G_{NeGS} = G_{GS} \cup G_{-GS}$, where $G_{GS}$ contains tuples with $\mathbf{c}_i > 0$ and $G_{-GS}$ contains tuples with $\mathbf{c}_i < 0$ [S1].

### Rendering Pipeline
The rendering process is identical to standard 3D Gaussian Splatting, with the exception that the color accumulation step allows for negative contributions [S1].
1.  **Projection:** Each 3D Gaussian is projected to 2D, resulting in a 2D covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i$ and a 2D mean $\mathbf{m}_i$.
2.  **Sorting:** Gaussians are sorted by depth.
3.  **Rasterization:** For each pixel, the alpha blending equation is applied. The opacity contribution of each Gaussian is $\sigma_i = \alpha_i G'_i(x')$, where $G'_i(x')$ is the 2D Gaussian kernel.
4.  **Color Accumulation:** The final color $C(x')$ is computed as:
    $$ C(x') = \sum_{i \in N} \mathbf{c}_i \sigma_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \sigma_j) $$
    Here, $\mathbf{c}_i$ can be negative, allowing negative Gaussians to subtract color from the accumulated result [S1, Parent].

### Optimization and Initialization
The optimization strategy follows the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting pipeline (rasterization, loss computation, gradient descent) but with specific initialization and pruning rules [S1].

1.  **Initialization:**
    *   Generate an initial point cloud from SfM data.
    *   Assign a sign (positive or negative) to each Gaussian.
    *   **Hyperparameter:** The initial number of negative Gaussians is critical. Empirical results suggest setting it to approximately 20% of the total Gaussians for optimal efficacy [S1].
    *   The remaining 80% are initialized as positive Gaussians.

2.  **Training:**
    *   Perform differentiable rendering to compute the loss (e.g., L1 loss, SSIM loss) against ground truth images.
    *   Update parameters ($\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{s}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{c}_i, \mathbf{SH}_i$) using Adam optimizer.
    *   The negative signs are preserved during optimization; the magnitude of $\mathbf{c}_i$ is learned, but the sign is fixed by the initial mask [S1].

3.  **Pruning and Densification:**
    *   **Pruning:** Gaussians with low opacity are removed.
    *   **Densification:** New Gaussians are introduced into empty 3D regions or areas with high gradient magnitude.
    *   When new Gaussians are created, their sign must be assigned. The sources do not specify a deterministic rule for assigning signs to *newly* created Gaussians, only that the initial proportion is critical [S1]. [G: In practice, new Gaussians are often initialized with positive signs, or the sign is randomly assigned based on the target ratio, but the source text does not explicitly define this dynamic rule.]

### Diff-Gaussian Theory
NegGS can be interpreted as using "Diff-Gaussians," which are formed by the ratio of two Gaussian probability density functions (PDFs) [S2]. This allows for the approximation of complex shapes like donuts or moons.

**Theorem 3.2 (Optimal Coefficient):**
Let $f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}$ and $f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}$ be two Gaussian PDFs. Assume $\Sigma_0 - \Sigma_1$ is positive semi-definite. The optimal coefficient $c_{opt}$ that minimizes the ratio is:
$$ c_{opt} := \min_x \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(x)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(x)} = \frac{f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(w)}{f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(w)} $$
if and only if $w$ satisfies:
$$ \Sigma_1^{-1}(w - m_1) = \Sigma_0^{-1}(w - m_0) $$

**Derivation:**
The minimization of the ratio is equivalent to minimizing the log-ratio:
$$ \arg \min_x (\log f_{m_0, \Sigma_0}(x) - \log f_{m_1, \Sigma_1}(x)) $$
$$ = \arg \min_x \left( (x - m_1)^T \Sigma_1^{-1} (x - m_1) - (x - m_0)^T \Sigma_0^{-1} (x - m_0) \right) $$
This reduces to minimizing the quadratic function $q(x) = x^T A x + 2b^T x$, where:
$$ A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1} $$
$$ b = \Sigma_0^{-1} m_0 - \Sigma_1^{-1} m_1 $$
Note: The source text defines $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0$ in the notes, but the derivation in the source text shows $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1}$ implicitly through the log-expansion. However, the source explicitly states: "where $A = \Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0$ and $b = \Sigma_0^{-1} m_0 - \Sigma_1^{-1} m_1$". We follow the source's explicit definition of $A$ and $b$ for implementation, noting the potential typo in the source's derivation vs. statement. [S1]

**Corollary 3.3 (Null Density Vector for Diagonal Covariances):**
For diagonal covariance matrices $\Sigma_0 = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_n^2)$ and $\Sigma_1 = \text{diag}(h_1 \sigma_1^2, \dots, h_n \sigma_n^2)$ with $0 < h_i \le 1$, the coordinates $w_i$ of the null density vector (where the ratio is minimized) are:
1.  $w_i = \frac{m_{1,i} - h_i m_{0,i}}{1 - h_i}$ if $h_i < 1$.
2.  $w_i \in \mathbb{R}$ if $h_i = 1$ and $m_{1,i} = m_{0,i}$.

This implies that by aligning means and variances, a Diff-Gaussian can have a null density on a $k$-dimensional affine space, allowing for more effective approximation of complex scenes [S1].

def initialize_neggs(points, num_total_gaussians, neg_ratio=0.2):
    """
    Initialize Gaussians with a mix of positive and negative colors.
    """
    gaussians = []
    num_neg = int(num_total_gaussians * neg_ratio)
    num_pos = num_total_gaussians - num_neg
    
    # Initialize positions from SfM points
    positions = sample_points(points, num_total_gaussians)
    
    # Initialize scales and rotations (standard GS initialization)
    scales = initialize_scales(positions)
    rotations = initialize_rotations()
    
    # Initialize opacities
    opacities = initialize_opacities()
    
    # Initialize colors
    # First num_pos are positive, next num_neg are negative
    colors = []
    for i in range(num_pos):
        colors.append(positive_random_color())
    for i in range(num_neg):
        colors.append(negative_random_color()) # e.g., -1 * positive_random_color()
        
    # Assign signs
    signs = [1] * num_pos + [-1] * num_neg
    
    for i in range(num_total_gaussians):
        g = Gaussian(
            position=positions[i],
            scale=scales[i],
            rotation=rotations[i],
            opacity=opacities[i],
            color=colors[i],
            sign=signs[i]
        )
        gaussians.append(g)
        
    return gaussians

def render_neggs(gaussians, camera):
    """
    Render using standard alpha blending, allowing negative colors.
    """
    # Project 3D Gaussians to 2D
    projected_gaussians = []
    for g in gaussians:
        proj = project_3d_gaussian(g, camera)
        projected_gaussians.append(proj)
        
    # Sort by depth
    projected_gaussians.sort(key=lambda g: g.depth)
    
    # Rasterize
    image = zeros_like(target_image)
    alpha_accum = zeros_like(target_image)
    
    for g in projected_gaussians:
        # Compute 2D Gaussian kernel at each pixel covered by bounding box
        kernel_values = compute_2d_gaussian_kernel(g)
        
        # Alpha contribution
        sigma = g.opacity * kernel_values
        
        # Update alpha accumulation
        alpha_term = sigma * (1 - alpha_accum)
        
        # Color contribution (can be negative)
        color_term = g.color * sigma * (1 - alpha_accum)
        
        # Accumulate
        image += color_term
        alpha_accum += alpha_term
        
    return image

def optimize_neggs(gaussians, images, cameras, num_iterations):
    """
    Standard optimization loop with pruning/densification.
    """
    for iteration in range(num_iterations):
        # Select random camera
        camera = random.choice(cameras)
        image = random.choice(images)
        
        # Render
        rendered_image = render_neggs(gaussians, camera)
        
        # Compute Loss
        loss = compute_loss(rendered_image, image)
        
        # Backpropagate
        gradients = compute_gradients(loss, gaussians)
        
        # Update parameters
        update_gaussians(gaussians, gradients)
        
        # Pruning and Densification (Standard GS steps)
        # Note: Source does not specify sign assignment for new Gaussians
        gaussians = prune_low_opacity(gaussians, threshold=0.005)
        gaussians = densify_high_gradient(gaussians, cameras, threshold=0.0002)
        
    return gaussians

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** NegGS reduces the time complexity compared to standard Gaussian Splatting for complex nonlinear structures. Standard GS requires many components to approximate complex shapes, increasing computational load. NegGS uses fewer total Gaussians to achieve similar or better results by leveraging negative components [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Similar to standard GS, but potentially lower due to the reduced number of required primitives for complex scenes [S2].
*   **Performance:**
    *   On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset, NegGS-30K achieved an average PSNR of 27.39, SSIM of 0.812, and LPIPS of 0.219, compared to GS-30K's 27.21 PSNR, 0.815 SSIM, and 0.214 LPIPS [S1].
    *   On the NeRF Synthetic dataset, NegGS showed slight improvements in PSNR and SSIM over GS [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Better modeling of high-frequency elements with rapid color transitions [S2].
    *   Improved representation of shadows [S2].
    *   Ability to approximate complex nonlinear structures (e.g., donut, moon shapes) with fewer primitives [S2].
    *   Preserves the original GS rendering speed by using a binary mask to track negative Gaussians [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   The initial number of negative Gaussians is a critical hyperparameter; incorrect initialization can lead to poor optimization [S1].
    *   Requires careful tuning of the ratio of negative to positive Gaussians (typically ~20%) [S1].
*   **When to use:** Use NegGS when the scene contains complex nonlinear structures, high-frequency details, or shadows that are difficult to represent with standard positive Gaussians. It is particularly useful when standard GS requires an excessive number of primitives to achieve acceptable quality [S2].

## Background
Negative Gaussian Splatting (NegGS) was introduced as the first method to extend the simple ellipsoid shapes of Gaussian Splatting to more complex nonlinear structures [S2]. It is based on the concept of Diff-Gaussians, which are formed by dividing the PDFs of two Gaussians [S2]. The method was evaluated on NeRF Synthetic, Mip-NeRF360, Tanks and Temples, Deep Blending, and Shiny Blender datasets [S1]. The key insight is that allowing negative colors enables Gaussians with identical parameters and inverse colors to reduce each other, providing greater flexibility in shape representation and color modulation [S1].
