# Optimization and Parallelization

Also called: Distributed 3DGS Optimization, Parallel Backpropagation for Gaussian Splatting

## Overview
This family encompasses algorithmic strategies to scale the training of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) beyond single-GPU memory limits and to accelerate the differentiable rasterization backward pass. It solves the bottleneck where the number of Gaussians exceeds device VRAM or where the $O(N \cdot P)$ complexity of per-pixel gradient accumulation causes excessive atomic contention. It distinguishes itself from standard optimization by explicitly partitioning the Gaussian primitive list across devices or restructuring the gradient accumulation loop to iterate over splats rather than pixels [5989-parallelization-for-backpropagation], [7349-grendel-gs].

## Shared principles
The methods in this family share the following foundational mechanisms:

1.  **Differentiable Rasterization Core**: All methods rely on the differentiable alpha-blending rasterizer. The forward pass computes pixel color $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x})$ via depth-sorted alpha blending, and the backward pass computes gradients $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}$ with respect to Gaussian parameters $\theta$ [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].
2.  **Gradient Accumulation**: Gradients are accumulated over multiple training steps or views before an optimizer update. In distributed settings, gradients or intermediate rendering states are synchronized across devices [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].
3.  **Adaptive Representation**: Many optimization variants modify the Gaussian representation or its distribution to improve efficiency or fidelity, requiring corresponding adjustments to the gradient computation or data flow [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].
4.  **Hardware Acceleration**: Methods leverage GPU parallelism, often using CUDA kernels, Tensor Cores (via GEMM reformulation), or half-precision arithmetic to accelerate the rasterization and gradient computation [6233-optimization-and-parallelization].

## Subtopics

### [MaskGaussian Gradient Computation](4254-maskgaussian-gradient-computation.md)
A method for computing gradients with respect to probabilistic masks $\mathcal{M}_i$ applied to Gaussians. It explicitly accounts for the gradient contribution of the mask on both the foreground Gaussian color accumulation and the background transmittance path, distinguishing it from standard backpropagation which treats opacity as a fixed scalar [4254-maskgaussian-gradient-computation].

### [Training Pipeline](5325-training-pipeline.md)
The iterative optimization loop that integrates dynamic Gaussian management (pruning, densification, cloning) within the training loop. It distinguishes itself by adapting the representation density to scene complexity via axis-slice rendering and gradient-based refinement checks [5325-training-pipeline].

### [Parallelization for Backpropagation](5989-parallelization-for-backpropagation.md)
Replaces the standard per-pixel backward pass with a per-splat approach. By iterating over Gaussians and updating the gradients of the pixels they cover, it avoids atomic contention bottlenecks, yielding a 4–5× reduction in training time while maintaining numerical equivalence [5989-parallelization-for-backpropagation].

### [Grendel-GS](7349-grendel-gs.md)
A distributed optimization framework that partitions the Gaussian primitive list across multiple GPUs. Unlike single-machine streaming solutions, it enables training on scenes with billions of primitives by leveraging inter-GPU communication to aggregate partial render results [7349-grendel-gs].

### [3D Gaussian Splatting Implementation Details](9128-3d-gaussian-splatting-implementation-details.md)
Defines the specific algorithmic steps, hyperparameters, and data structures (e.g., half-precision storage, GEMM reformulation) required to implement the training and rendering pipeline, including the adaptive densification schedule [9128-3d-gaussian-splatting-implementation-details].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 . The original implementation was limited by single-GPU memory constraints, as the number of Gaussians required for high-fidelity reconstruction often exceeded 10–20 million, surpassing the VRAM of standard consumer GPUs. Subsequent work focused on distributed training (e.g., Grendel-GS) and optimized backward passes to reduce training time from days to hours .
