# Mapping Strategies

Also called: Pixel mapping strategies, Scene alignment strategies

## Overview
Mapping strategies define the correspondence mechanism used to align pixels or features between two distinct scenes or views during radiance field optimization and editing. They solve the problem of establishing spatial alignment when direct geometric priors are insufficient or when transferring attributes between different scene representations. Unlike standard SLAM mapping which optimizes geometry for a single scene, these strategies focus on cross-scene or cross-view correspondence, often leveraging semantic information to handle misalignment that identity-based methods miss.

## How it works
The method operates by establishing a mapping function $M$ that associates pixels or regions in a source scene with those in a target scene. The specific strategy determines how this association is computed.

**Inputs:**
- Source image/scene representation $I_S$
- Target image/scene representation $I_T$
- Optional: Pre-trained feature extractor (e.g., ViT, DINO)

**Outputs:**
- Correspondence map or transformation parameters aligning $I_S$ to $I_T$

**Data Structures:**
- Feature maps: Dense semantic feature vectors extracted from images.
- Segmentation masks: Binary or multi-label masks defining semantically meaningful parts.

**Steps:**
1. **Feature Extraction:** Extract dense semantic features from both scenes using a Vision Transformer (ViT) or self-supervised model like DINO [S1]. Let $F_S$ and $F_T$ be the feature maps.
2. **Correspondence Computation:** Compute similarity between features in $F_S$ and $F_T$.
   - For *Identity Mapping*: Assume pixel $(u,v)$ in $S$ corresponds to $(u,v)$ in $T$. This is computationally trivial but lacks accuracy if scenes are misaligned [S1].
   - For *Semantic Correspondence*: Match features based on semantic similarity. Find $v \in T$ that maximizes similarity $sim(F_S(u), F_T(v))$ [S1].
   - For *Co-segmentation*: Cluster features in $F_S$ and $F_T$ using DINO’s clustering capabilities to identify semantically consistent regions. Align regions rather than individual pixels [S1].
3. **Alignment Application:** Use the computed correspondences to warp, resample, or fuse the source scene into the target space. If geometry alignment is required, use density-based resampling along rays to align key points identified by the mapping [S1].

**Formulas:**
- Identity: $M(u,v) = (u,v)$
- Semantic Similarity: $v^* = \arg\max_{v} \text{sim}(F_S(u), F_T(v))$
- Co-segmentation: Regions $R_S, R_T$ are aligned if their semantic cluster labels match [S1].

**Parameters:**
- Similarity metric: Cosine similarity or Euclidean distance in feature space.
- Clustering algorithm: DINO’s internal clustering mechanism.

**Edge Cases:**
- *Identity Mapping Pitfall*: Fails significantly when scenes have different viewpoints or scales due to lack of spatial alignment accuracy [S1].
- *Semantic Ambiguity*: Objects with similar semantics but different geometries may cause incorrect correspondences.

## Subtopics

### [iMAP](11979-imap.md)
iMAP is the first NeRF-based dense online SLAM model that jointly optimizes camera pose and an implicit scene representation. It solves the problem of simultaneous localization and mapping by treating the scene as a continuous radiance field rather than a discrete mesh or point cloud. It sets itself apart from its siblings by being the foundational method that introduced the iterative two-step approach of tracking and mapping to NeRF-based SLAM, upon which NICE-SLAM and subsequent methods build.

## Operating characteristics
- **Identity Mapping**: Fastest computation ($O(1)$ per pixel), but lowest accuracy for misaligned scenes. Suitable only for perfectly registered inputs.
- **Semantic Correspondence**: Higher computational cost due to feature extraction and matching ($O(N \cdot M)$ for dense matching). Robust to viewpoint changes.
- **Co-segmentation**: Moderate to high cost due to clustering. Provides robust alignment at semantic boundaries, preserving structural fidelity [S1].
- **Strengths**: Enables view-consistent 3D editing and multi-scene fusion by handling misalignment better than geometric-only methods.
- **Limitations**: Relies on the quality of the semantic feature extractor. May fail on textureless or non-semantically distinct regions.

## Background
The concept of mapping strategies in this context originates from research on multi-scene radiance fields and 3D editing, specifically in the work on Generalized TensoRF. The methods were developed to address the challenge of aligning pixels between different scenes for consistent editing and fusion, where traditional geometric registration is insufficient or unavailable [S1].
