# Geometry Regularization

Also called: Gaussian Flattening, Normal Consistency and Flattening

## Overview
Geometry Regularization is a training optimization technique for 3D Gaussian Splatting that improves geometric fidelity and surface smoothness by constraining the shape and orientation of Gaussian kernels, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains their spatial location [S1, S2, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) or [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by directly manipulating the covariance structure (via flattening) and enforcing alignment between rendered geometric cues (normals/depth) and the Gaussian primitives [S1, S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** The current state of the 3D Gaussian scene (positions $\mathbf{p}$, covariances $\Sigma$, opacities $\alpha$, colors $\mathbf{c}$), rendered depth maps $D$, and rendered surface normals $N_{rendered}$ derived from $D$.
*   **Outputs:** Gradient updates to the Gaussian parameters, specifically targeting scale and rotation components of the covariance matrix.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian State:** Each Gaussian $i$ is defined by position $\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, rotation $\mathbf{q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$ (quaternion), scale $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, and opacity $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$.
*   **Covariance Matrix:** $\Sigma_i = R_i S_i S_i^T R_i^T$, where $R_i$ is the rotation matrix derived from $\mathbf{q}_i$ and $S_i = \text{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Rendered Normals:** Computed from the depth buffer by estimating the local surface plane at each pixel [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   `use_normal_loss`: Boolean flag to enable geometry regularization [S1, unofficial].
*   Regularization weights: Not explicitly defined in the provided sources for the specific loss terms, but standard practice applies weights to the normal consistency and flattening losses [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**
1.  **Enable Check:** If `use_normal_loss` is false, skip geometry regularization steps [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Render Geometric Cues:** Render the depth map $D$ and compute the corresponding surface normals $N_{rendered}$ for the current camera view [S1, S2, unofficial].
3.  **Compute Normal Consistency Loss:**
    *   For each pixel, extract the rendered normal $\mathbf{n}_{rendered}$ [S1, unofficial].
    *   Determine the per-Gaussian normal $\mathbf{n}_{gauss}$. This is typically derived from the Gaussian's orientation or the shortest axis of its covariance ellipsoid [S1, S2, unofficial].
    *   Compute the loss $L_{normal}$ as the discrepancy between $\mathbf{n}_{rendered}$ and $\mathbf{n}_{gauss}$ (e.g., using cosine similarity or dot product maximization) [S1, S2, unofficial].
4.  **Compute Flattening Loss:**
    *   Extract the scaling factor vector $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ for each Gaussian [S2, unofficial].
    *   Identify the smallest component of $\mathbf{s}$, denoted as $s_{min} = \min(\mathbf{s})$ [S2, unofficial].
    *   Compute the loss $L_{flatten}$ by minimizing $s_{min}$ towards zero [S2, unofficial]. This forces the Gaussian to flatten along its smallest axis, effectively turning it into a surfel aligned with the surface [S2, unofficial].
5.  **Optimize:** Combine the losses and backpropagate to update the Gaussian parameters [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def geometry_regularization_step(gaussians, rendered_depth, rendered_normals, use_normal_loss):
    if not use_normal_loss:
        return

    total_loss = 0.0

    # Step 3: Normal Consistency
    # Note: Exact derivation of per-Gaussian normal from covariance is implementation-specific.
    # Common approach: The normal is the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue of Sigma.
    # Or, for flattened Gaussians, the normal aligns with the shortest scale axis.
    for gaussian in gaussians:
        # Get rendered normal at the projected pixel location of this Gaussian
        # (This requires mapping Gaussian center to screen space and sampling rendered_normals)
        n_rendered = sample_normal(rendered_normals, gaussian.screen_pos)
        
        # Derive Gaussian normal from its covariance structure
        # Sigma = R * diag(s^2) * R^T
        # The normal is the direction of the smallest scale component
        min_scale_idx = argmin(gaussian.scale)
        n_gauss = get_axis_direction(gaussian.rotation, min_scale_idx)
        
        # Loss: Maximize dot product (minimize angle)
        # L_normal = 1 - dot(n_rendered, n_gauss)
        loss_normal = 1.0 - dot(normalize(n_rendered), normalize(n_gauss))
        total_loss += loss_normal

    # Step 4: Flattening Loss
    for gaussian in gaussians:
        s = gaussian.scale
        s_min = min(s)
        # Loss: Minimize the smallest scale component towards zero
        # L_flatten = s_min
        loss_flatten = s_min
        total_loss += loss_flatten

    # Backpropagate total_loss to update gaussians
    total_loss.backward()
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** Minimizing scale components towards zero can lead to numerical instability if not clamped [S2, unofficial].
*   **Boundaries:** At object boundaries, normal estimation from depth can be noisy, leading to incorrect alignment [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Degenerate Input:** If depth maps fail refitting against the prior point cloud, they should be discarded to avoid corrupting the normal consistency loss [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Scaling Loss for Geometry Modeling
A specific variant of geometry regularization that focuses exclusively on flattening Gaussian kernels into 3D surfels aligned with the surface by minimizing the smallest component of the scaling factor $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ towards zero [S2, unofficial]. This aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when the primary goal is to enforce surfel-like geometry without necessarily enforcing strict normal consistency with rendered depth maps [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds overhead proportional to the number of Gaussians for computing normals and scale-based losses [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing rendered depth and normal buffers [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Improves geometric fidelity and reduces floaters [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Produces smoother surfaces and better-defined geometry compared to unconstrained splatting [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** May reduce the number of remaining points if combined with pruning, potentially compromising detail [S2, unofficial]. Requires accurate depth and normal estimation [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high geometric fidelity is required and depth/normal data is available [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited or depth data is unreliable [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Originating from improvements to 3D Gaussian Splatting training, this technique was highlighted in unofficial implementations and modular frameworks like GaussianSplatting.jl and GauStudio [S1, S2, unofficial]. It addresses the limitation of depth supervision alone by adding shape constraints [S1, unofficial]. The concept of using scaling loss to flatten Gaussians into surfels is cited from prior work [5] in the GauStudio paper [S2, unofficial].
