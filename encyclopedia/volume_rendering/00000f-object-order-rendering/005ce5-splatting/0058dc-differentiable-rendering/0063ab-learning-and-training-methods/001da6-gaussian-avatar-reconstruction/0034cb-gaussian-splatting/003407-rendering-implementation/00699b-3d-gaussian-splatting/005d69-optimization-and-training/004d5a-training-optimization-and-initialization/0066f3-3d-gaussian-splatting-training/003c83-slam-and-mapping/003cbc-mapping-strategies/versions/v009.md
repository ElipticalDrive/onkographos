# Mapping Strategies

Also called: Pixel mapping strategies, Scene alignment strategies

## Overview
Mapping strategies define the correspondence mechanism used to align pixels or features between two distinct scenes, typically for the purpose of transferring visual attributes (such as color or texture) while preserving structural fidelity [S1]. Unlike general SLAM mapping which optimizes geometry and pose jointly, these strategies specifically address the problem of spatial misalignment when fusing or editing radiance fields [S1]. They are distinguished from sibling methods like [Semantic-aware mapping](12053-semantic-aware-mapping.md) by their focus on direct pixel-to-pixel or feature-to-feature correspondence for attribute transfer rather than semantic segmentation for scene understanding, and from [Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Representation](12096-neural-radiance-field-nerf-representation.md) by their role as a post-processing or alignment step rather than the scene representation itself.

## How it works
**Inputs:** Two sets of images or rendered views from different scenes (Source and Target) that share semantic content but may differ in appearance or slight geometric misalignment.
**Outputs:** A correspondence map or transformation parameters that align pixels/features from the Source scene to the Target scene.

The method implements one of three strategies, selected based on the required alignment accuracy and computational constraints [S1]:

1.  **Identity Mapping:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Assumes a direct 1:1 pixel correspondence between the source and target images based on their coordinate indices.
    *   **Limitation:** Lacks spatial alignment accuracy if the scenes are not perfectly registered [S1].
    *   **Use Case:** Baseline comparison or when scenes are pre-aligned with high precision.

2.  **Semantic Correspondence-based Mapping:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Extracts dense semantic features from both scenes using Vision Transformers (ViT) [Dosovitskiy 2020] and DINO [Amir et al. 2021]. These features are used to establish spatial matching between semantically equivalent regions.
    *   **Process:**
        1.  Encode source and target images into feature maps using DINO/ViT.
        2.  Compute similarity metrics (e.g., cosine similarity) between feature vectors to find correspondences.
        3.  Map pixels from source to target based on highest semantic similarity.
    *   **Benefit:** Improves alignment accuracy over identity mapping by leveraging semantic context [S1].

3.  **Co-segmentation-based Mapping:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Refines alignment by segmenting images into semantically meaningful parts using DINO’s clustering capabilities.
    *   **Process:**
        1.  Perform clustering on DINO features to identify coherent semantic regions (superpixels/segments).
        2.  Establish correspondences at the segment level rather than pixel level.
        3.  Map pixels within corresponding segments.
    *   **Benefit:** Yields finer correspondence and maintains semantic boundaries, preventing bleeding of attributes across object edges [S1].

**Resampling Note:** To further align geometry and color, density-based resampling along corresponding rays is often employed. This identifies density peaks in the radiance field to adjust sampling points, ensuring color-geometry consistency [S1].

    """

    """

    elif strategy == "semantic":
        # Extract dense semantic features

        # For each pixel in target, find the most similar feature in source
        correspondence_map = compute_correspondence(source_features, target_features)
        
        # Remap source pixels to target coordinates
        mapped_img = remap(source_img, correspondence_map)

        segment_correspondence = match_segments(source_segments, target_segments)

        mapped_img = remap_within_segments(source_img, segment_correspondence)
        return mapped_img

## Subtopics

### [iMAP](11979-imap.md)
iMAP is the first NeRF-based dense online SLAM model that jointly optimizes camera pose and an implicit scene representation. It solves the problem of simultaneous localization and mapping by treating the scene as a continuous radiance field rather than a discrete mesh or point cloud. It sets itself apart from its siblings by being the foundational method that introduced the iterative two-step approach of tracking and mapping to NeRF-based SLAM, upon which NICE-SLAM and subsequent methods build.

### [RNR-Map Construction](16526-rnr-map-construction.md)
RNR-Map Construction embeds visual information from an RGB-D image into a 2D grid map (RNR-Map) using a pretrained encoder and inverse camera projection. It differs from sibling methods like [iMAP](11979-imap.md) by operating directly on latent codes to bypass the computationally expensive differentiable rendering step required for alignment, enabling high-frequency mapping.

## Operating characteristics
- **Identity Mapping:** Fastest ($O(1)$ per pixel), but fails if scenes are misaligned.
- **Semantic Correspondence:** Moderate computational cost due to feature extraction and similarity computation. Robust to minor geometric misalignments.
- **Co-segmentation:** Highest computational cost due to clustering and segment matching. Provides the best alignment quality for attribute transfer, especially across object boundaries [S1].
- **Resampling:** Adds overhead for ray sampling and density peak detection but improves color-geometry consistency [S1].

## Background
These strategies were introduced in the context of multi-scene radiance fields and view-consistent 3D editing, specifically in the Generalized TensoRF framework [S1]. The work addresses the challenge of recombining compatible density and radiance components from different scenes while maintaining structural fidelity and enabling new visual styles [S1].
