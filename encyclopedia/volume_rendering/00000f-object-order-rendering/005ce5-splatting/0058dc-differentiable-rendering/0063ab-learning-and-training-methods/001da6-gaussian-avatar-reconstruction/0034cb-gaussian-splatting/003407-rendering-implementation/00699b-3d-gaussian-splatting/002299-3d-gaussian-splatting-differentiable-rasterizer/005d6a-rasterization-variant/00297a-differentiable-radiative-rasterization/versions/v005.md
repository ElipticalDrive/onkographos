# Differentiable Radiative Rasterization


## Overview
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization (DRR) is a GPU-accelerated rasterization method designed to render novel X-ray projections from radiative 3D Gaussian point clouds [S1]. It solves the problem of differentiable volume rendering for X-ray imaging by adapting the splatting pipeline to model radiation intensity rather than visible light color [S1]. Unlike standard 3D Gaussian Splatting which renders RGB colors, DRR computes scalar intensity values based on the probability density of Gaussians along the X-ray path, enabling end-to-end training of X-ray scanner parameters and Gaussian attributes [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:**
    *   A set of $N_p$ 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \{G_i(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{f}_i) \mid i = 1, \dots, N_p\}$, where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the mean, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the covariance, $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the opacity, and $\mathbf{f}_i$ represents learnable inherent point features [S1].
    *   Extrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{ext} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}$ [S1].
    *   Intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{int} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$ [S1].
    *   X-ray source parameters: distance $L_{SD}$ and azimuth angle $\phi$ [S1].
*   **Output:**
    *   Rendered image $\mathbf{I}$, where each pixel $p$ holds an intensity value $I(p) \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **3D Gaussian:** Stores $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ (3 floats), $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ (6 floats, symmetric), $\alpha_i$ (1 float), and $\mathbf{f}_i$ (variable size, used for intensity fitting) [S1].
*   **Tile:** Non-overlapping rectangular regions of the 2D image plane. Gaussians are assigned to tiles based on their 2D bounding boxes [S1].
*   **Sorted List:** Within each tile, Gaussians are sorted by their depth (distance to the 2D detector) [S1].

**Formulas:**
1.  **Gaussian Probability Density:** The probability value of the $i$-th Gaussian at a 3D point $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is:
    $$P(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)\right) \quad (6)$$ [S1]

2.  **Projection to Camera Coordinates:**
    Transform the 3D mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ to camera coordinates $\mathbf{t}_i = (t_x, t_y, t_z)^\top$ using homogeneous coordinates:
    $$\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{t}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_{ext} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_i = \mathbf{M}_{ext} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (7)$$ [S1]

3.  **Projection to Image Coordinates:**
    Project $\mathbf{t}_i$ to 2D image coordinates $\mathbf{u}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$:
    $$\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_{int} \tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i = \mathbf{M}_{int} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{t}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (7)$$ [S1]

4.  **Covariance Transformation:**
    Compute the covariance in camera coordinates $\Sigma'_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$:
    $$\Sigma'_i = \mathbf{J}_i \mathbf{W}_i \Sigma_i \mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{J}_i^\top \quad (8)$$ [S1]
    Where:
    *   $\mathbf{W}_i$ is the viewing transformation matrix:
        $$\mathbf{W}_i = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin \phi & \cos \phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ -\cos \phi & -\sin \phi & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (9)$$ [S1]
    *   $\mathbf{J}_i$ is the Jacobian of the affine approximation of the projective transformation:
        $$\mathbf{J}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & 0 & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z} \\ 0 & \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (9)$$ [S1]
        *Note: The source text presents a formatting error in the matrix layout for $\mathbf{J}_i$, listing terms as $\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z}$, $0$, $-\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$ in the first row. However, standard projective Jacobians and the symmetry with the second row suggest the term is $\frac{t_y}{t_z}$ or similar scaling. The source explicitly writes $\frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$ in the text description but the matrix visual is ambiguous. We retain the source's explicit term $\frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$ as written in the formula block, but note the potential typo.* [S1]
        *Correction based on standard derivations and source context:* The source text says $-\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$. This is likely a typo for $-\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} t_y$ or similar. However, adhering strictly to the provided source text for the formula:
        $$\mathbf{J}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & 0 & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2} \\ 0 & \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ [S1]

5.  **2D Covariance:**
    Obtain $\Sigma''_i \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ by removing the third row and column from $\Sigma'_i$ [S1].

6.  **Radiation Intensity Contribution:**
    For a pixel $p$, let $\mathcal{N}$ be the set of ordered 3D points (intersections of X-rays with Gaussians) overlapping the pixel. The intensity $I(p)$ is:
    $$I(p) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{i}_j \quad (10)$$ [S1]
    Where $\mathbf{i}_j$ is the radiation intensity of the $j$-th intersection point $\mathbf{x}_j$. The contribution is weighted by the probability term:
    $$\sigma_j = \alpha_j P(\mathbf{x}_j | \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j) \quad (10)$$ [S1]
    *Note: The source implies $\mathbf{i}_j$ is derived from $\sigma_j$ and potentially the learnable features $\mathbf{f}_j$ via an inner product with basis weights, though the exact combination function for $\mathbf{i}_j$ from $\sigma_j$ and $\mathbf{f}_j$ is not explicitly formulaized in the provided text snippets, only that DRR adopts the inner product to fit radiation intensity [S1].*

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize Gaussian point clouds using the Angle-pose Cuboid Uniform Initialization (ACUI) strategy within a cuboid enclosing the object [S1].
2.  **Projection:** For each Gaussian $i$:
    a. Compute camera coordinates $\mathbf{t}_i$ using $\mathbf{M}_{ext}$ [S1].
    b. Compute image coordinates $\mathbf{u}_i$ using $\mathbf{M}_{int}$ [S1].
    c. Compute $\mathbf{W}_i$ using azimuth angle $\phi$ [S1].
    d. Compute $\mathbf{J}_i$ using $L_{SD}$, $t_y$, $t_z$ [S1].
    e. Compute $\Sigma'_i = \mathbf{J}_i \mathbf{W}_i \Sigma_i \mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{J}_i^\top$ [S1].
    f. Extract $\Sigma''_i$ by dropping the 3rd row/col of $\Sigma'_i$ [S1].
3.  **Tiling:** Partition the image into non-overlapping tiles [S1].
4.  **Assignment:** Assign each Gaussian to tiles whose bounding box overlaps with the Gaussian's 2D projection $(\mathbf{u}_i, \Sigma''_i)$ [S1].
5.  **Sorting:** Sort Gaussians within each tile by their distance to the detector (depth $t_z$) [S1].
6.  **Rasterization:** For each pixel $p$ in each tile:
    a. Identify the ordered set $\mathcal{N}$ of Gaussians overlapping $p$ [S1].
    b. For each $j \in \mathcal{N}$, compute $\sigma_j = \alpha_j P(\mathbf{x}_j | \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)$ [S1].
    c. Compute radiation intensity $\mathbf{i}_j$ based on $\sigma_j$ and learnable features $\mathbf{f}_j$ [S1].
    d. Sum contributions: $I(p) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{i}_j$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import math
import numpy as np

def compute_jacobian(tz, ty, L_SD):
    """
    Computes the Jacobian matrix Ji for the projective transformation.
    Based on source [S1] formula (9).
    Note: Source contains formatting ambiguity; this implementation follows the explicit text terms.
    """
    # Term A: L_SD / tz
    term_a = L_SD / tz
    # Term B: - (L_SD / tz^2) * (ty / tz^2)
    # Source text: -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2}
    term_b = -(L_SD / (tz ** 2)) * (ty / (tz ** 2))
    
    J = np.array([
        [term_a, 0.0, term_b],
        [0.0, term_a, term_b],
        [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
    ])
    return J

def compute_viewing_matrix(phi):
    """
    Computes the viewing transformation matrix Wi.
    Based on source [S1] formula (9).
    """
    sin_phi = math.sin(phi)
    cos_phi = math.cos(phi)
    
    W = np.array([
        [-sin_phi, cos_phi, 0.0],
        [0.0, 0.0, -1.0],
        [-cos_phi, -sin_phi, 0.0]
    ])
    return W

def gaussian_probability(x, mu, Sigma_inv):
    """
    Computes P(x | mu, Sigma).
    Based on source [S1] formula (6).
    """
    diff = x - mu
    exponent = -0.5 * np.dot(diff.T, np.dot(Sigma_inv, diff))
    return math.exp(exponent)

def render_drr(gaussians, M_ext, M_int, L_SD, phi, image_width, image_height, tile_size):
    """
    Differentiable Radiative Rasterization.
    Inputs:
        gaussians: List of dicts with keys 'mu' (3,), 'Sigma' (3,3), 'alpha' (float), 'f' (features)
        M_ext: (4,4) extrinsic matrix
        M_int: (4,3) intrinsic matrix
        L_SD: float, source-detector distance
        phi: float, azimuth angle
        image_width, image_height: int
        tile_size: int, size of rasterization tiles
    Output:
        image: (H, W) float array
    """
    # Initialize output image
    image = np.zeros((image_height, image_width))
    
    # 1. Project Gaussians
    projected_gaussians = []
    for i, g in enumerate(gaussians):
        mu = g['mu']
        Sigma = g['Sigma']
        alpha = g['alpha']
        f = g['f']
        
        # Homogeneous coordinates
        mu_h = np.append(mu, 1.0)
        
        # Camera coordinates
        t_h = np.dot(M_ext, mu_h)
        t = t_h[:3] # tx, ty, tz
        
        # Image coordinates
        u_h = np.dot(M_int, t_h)
        u = u_h[:2] # ux, uy
        
        # Covariance transformation
        W = compute_viewing_matrix(phi)
        J = compute_jacobian(t[2], t[1], L_SD)
        
        Sigma_prime = J @ W @ Sigma @ W.T @ J.T
        
        # 2D Covariance (skip 3rd row/col)
        Sigma_2d = Sigma_prime[:2, :2]
        
        projected_gaussians.append({
            'mu_3d': mu,
            'mu_2d': u,
            'Sigma_2d': Sigma_2d,
            'alpha': alpha,
            'f': f,
            'tz': t[2],
            'ty': t[1]
        })
        
    # 2. Tiling and Sorting
    # Create tiles
    num_tiles_x = (image_width + tile_size - 1) // tile_size
    num_tiles_y = (image_height + tile_size - 1) // tile_size
    
    # Assign Gaussians to tiles
    # Simple bounding box check: Gaussian covers tile if its 2D bounding box overlaps
    # Bounding box can be approximated by mu_2d +/- 3*sqrt(diag(Sigma_2d))
    tiles = {}
    for g in projected_gaussians:
        mu_2d = g['mu_2d']
        Sigma_2d = g['Sigma_2d']
        
        # Approximate bounding box radius (e.g., 3 sigma)
        std_x = 3.0 * math.sqrt(Sigma_2d[0, 0])
        std_y = 3.0 * math.sqrt(Sigma_2d[1, 1])
        
        min_x = mu_2d[0] - std_x
        max_x = mu_2d[0] + std_x
        min_y = mu_2d[1] - std_y
        max_y = mu_2d[1] + std_y
        
        # Determine tile indices
        tile_x_start = int(min_x // tile_size)
        tile_x_end = int(max_x // tile_size)
        tile_y_start = int(min_y // tile_size)
        tile_y_end = int(max_y // tile_size)
        
        for tx in range(tile_x_start, tile_x_end + 1):
            for ty in range(tile_y_start, tile_y_end + 1):
                if 0 <= tx < num_tiles_x and 0 <= ty < num_tiles_y:
                    tile_key = (ty, tx)
                    if tile_key not in tiles:
                        tiles[tile_key] = []
                    tiles[tile_key].append(g)
                    
    # Sort Gaussians in each tile by depth (tz)
    for tile_key in tiles:
        tiles[tile_key].sort(key=lambda g: g['tz'])
        
    # 3. Rasterization
    for ty in range(num_tiles_y):
        for tx in range(num_tiles_x):
            tile_key = (ty, tx)
            if tile_key not in tiles:
                continue
                
            tile_gaussians = tiles[tile_key]
            
            # Pixel coordinates within the tile
            for dy in range(tile_size):
                for dx in range(tile_size):
                    px = tx * tile_size + dx
                    py = ty * tile_size + dy
                    
                    if px >= image_width or py >= image_height:
                        continue
                        
                    # Check which Gaussians overlap this pixel
                    # This is a simplified check; full implementation requires precise ellipse intersection
                    # For the purpose of this article, we assume the source's "overlapping" implies
                    # a check against the 2D Gaussian PDF or bounding box.
                    # We will iterate all Gaussians in the tile and check if pixel is within bounds.
                    
                    intensity_sum = 0.0
                    for g in tile_gaussians:
                        # Check if pixel (px, py) is within the Gaussian's 2D extent
                        # Using a simple bounding box check for implementation completeness
                        mu_2d = g['mu_2d']
                        Sigma_2d = g['Sigma_2d']
                        
                        std_x = 3.0 * math.sqrt(Sigma_2d[0, 0])
                        std_y = 3.0 * math.sqrt(Sigma_2d[1, 1])
                        
                        if abs(px - mu_2d[0]) <= std_x and abs(py - mu_2d[1]) <= std_y:
                            # Compute probability at pixel location (assuming pixel center is the intersection point x_j)
                            # Note: In X-ray, x_j is the intersection of the ray with the Gaussian.
                            # For rasterization, we approximate x_j as the 3D point corresponding to pixel (px, py)
                            # at the depth of the Gaussian? Or simply use the 2D projection probability?
                            # Source [S1] says: "ij is the radiation intensity of xj... sigma_j = alpha_j P(xj | mu_j, Sigma_j)"
                            # And "xj is the j-th intersection 3D point".
                            # In standard splatting, we evaluate the 2D Gaussian at the pixel.
                            # Let's assume x_j in the formula refers to the 3D point on the ray.
                            # However, DRR renders "projections". The intensity is accumulated.
                            # We approximate P(x_j) using the 2D projection probability at the pixel.
                            
                            # Compute 2D Mahalanobis distance
                            diff = np.array([px - mu_2d[0], py - mu_2d[1]])
                            try:
                                Sigma_2d_inv = np.linalg.inv(Sigma_2d)
                                exponent = -0.5 * np.dot(diff.T, np.dot(Sigma_2d_inv, diff))
                                prob = math.exp(exponent)
                            except:
                                prob = 0.0
                                
                            sigma_j = g['alpha'] * prob
                            
                            # Compute radiation intensity i_j
                            # Source says: "adopts the inner product between a learnable vector ... and a set of basis weights"
                            # This part is not fully detailed in the provided text snippets.
                            # We assume i_j is proportional to sigma_j or derived from it.
                            # For a complete implementation, the feature f_j would be used here.
                            # Since the exact function is not given, we use sigma_j as the base intensity component [G].
                            i_j = sigma_j 
                            
                            intensity_sum += i_j
                            
                    image[py, px] = intensity_sum
                    
    return image
```

**Parameters:**
*   $L_{SD}$: Distance between X-ray source and detector [S1].
*   $\phi$: Azimuth angle of the X-ray source [S1].
*   Tile size: Not given in the sources.
*   Number of Gaussians $N_p$: Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Jacobian Singularity:** If $t_z = 0$, the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_i$ involves division by zero. This occurs when a Gaussian center is on the camera plane. [G] Guard against this by clamping $t_z$ to a small epsilon or culling Gaussians with $t_z \le 0$.
*   **Covariance Definiteness:** $\Sigma_i$ must be positive definite. Numerical errors during transformation $\Sigma'_i = \mathbf{J}_i \mathbf{W}_i \Sigma_i \mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{J}_i^\top$ may lead to non-positive definite $\Sigma''_i$. [G] Ensure $\Sigma''_i$ is positive definite before inversion or use Cholesky decomposition with fallback.
*   **Source Text Ambiguity:** The formula for $\mathbf{J}_i$ in the source contains a potential typo in the term $\frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$. Implementers should verify this against the original paper's derivation or standard projective geometry [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of Gaussians $N_p$ and pixels, due to the tile-based sorting and accumulation [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Linear with respect to $N_p$ for storing Gaussian attributes and tile lists [S1].
*   **Performance:** Faster than original 3DGS due to the avoidance of complex Spherical Harmonic (SH) function computations [S1].
*   **Strengths:** GPU-friendly CUDA implementation; efficient for X-ray novel view synthesis; avoids SfM initialization via ACUI [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Specific to X-ray imaging (radiative Gaussians); requires accurate $L_{SD}$ and $\phi$ parameters [S1].

## Background
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization was introduced in the paper "Radiative Gaussian Splatting for Efficient X-Ray Novel View Synthesis" [S1]. It is part of the X-Gaussian framework, which adapts 3D Gaussian Splatting for X-ray imaging. The method was developed to enable efficient training of radiative Gaussian point clouds and X-ray scanner parameters without running SfM algorithms, using the Angle-pose Cuboid Uniform Initialization (ACUI) strategy [S1].
