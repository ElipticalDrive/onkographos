# Geometry Regularization

Also called: Gaussian Flattening, Normal Consistency and Flattening

## Overview
Geometry regularization improves the geometric coherence and smoothness of 3D Gaussian Splatting models by constraining the shape and orientation of the Gaussians, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains their 3D location [S1, S2, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by focusing on surface alignment and anisotropy rather than positional depth errors, and from [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by operating in spatial geometry rather than spectral frequency space. It primarily solves the problem of "floaters" and noisy, blobby geometry by encouraging Gaussians to flatten into surfels aligned with the underlying surface [S1, S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Per-Gaussian parameters (centers $\mathbf{c}$, scales $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, rotations $\mathbf{q}$, opacities $\alpha$), rendered depth map $D$, and optionally a normal map $N$ derived from $D$.
*   **Outputs:** Gradient updates for Gaussian parameters to minimize geometric loss.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian Scales:** A vector $\mathbf{s} = [s_x, s_y, s_z]$ representing the extent of the Gaussian along its local axes.
*   **Rendered Depth/Normal:** A 2D array corresponding to the image resolution, where each pixel contains a depth value and a surface normal vector.

**Steps:**
1.  **Enable Flag:** The method is controlled by a boolean parameter `use_normal_loss` in the optimization configuration [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Depth Normal Consistency:**
    *   Render the depth map $D$ from the current Gaussian scene.
    *   Compute surface normals $N_{rendered}$ from $D$ using spatial gradients (e.g., Sobel operators or finite differences) [S1, S2, unofficial].
    *   For each Gaussian projected onto the image plane, retrieve its local normal $N_{gaussian}$. The local normal is typically defined by the direction of the smallest scale axis (the shortest semi-axis of the ellipsoid) [S2, unofficial].
    *   Compute the alignment loss between $N_{rendered}$ and $N_{gaussian}$ for overlapping pixels [S1, unofficial].
3.  **Flattening Surface (Scaling Loss):**
    *   Identify the smallest component of the scaling vector $\mathbf{s}$ for each Gaussian. Let $s_{min} = \min(s_x, s_y, s_z)$ [S2, unofficial].
    *   Minimize $s_{min}$ towards zero during optimization. This forces the Gaussian to become flat (a surfel) along its shortest axis [S2, unofficial].
    *   This flattening aids in determining stable Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge as the surface normal [S2, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
The total regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{geo}$ combines normal consistency and flattening:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{geo} = \lambda_{norm} \mathcal{L}_{norm} + \lambda_{flat} \mathcal{L}_{flat}
$$
Where:
*   $\mathcal{L}_{norm} = \sum_{i} (1 - \mathbf{n}_i \cdot \mathbf{n}_{rendered, i})$ is the normal consistency loss, measuring the angular deviation between the Gaussian's local normal $\mathbf{n}_i$ and the rendered normal $\mathbf{n}_{rendered, i}$ [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   $\mathcal{L}_{flat} = \sum_{i} s_{min, i}$ is the flattening loss, penalizing non-zero values for the smallest scale component [S2, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_{norm}$ and $\lambda_{flat}$ are weighting factors. Specific values are not given in the sources [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   `use_normal_loss`: Boolean flag to enable the regularization [S1, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_{norm}, \lambda_{flat}$: Weights for the respective loss terms. Values are "Not given in the sources." [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Depth Refitting:** If depth maps vary between frames, they must be refitted against a prior point cloud to the same scale before use. Depth maps that fail refitting are discarded [S1, unofficial].
*   **Sky/Distant Background:** Geometry regularization can pull geometry onto itself in open environments. Using a "Sky Dome" (frozen shell of Gaussians) can help disentangle background, but a full sphere may reduce ground opacity; a hemisphere is preferred in such cases [S1, unofficial].
*   **Floater Trade-off:** While flattening reduces floaters, aggressive regularization may reduce the number of remaining points, potentially compromising detail [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Scaling Loss for Geometry Modeling
A specific variant focusing solely on the anisotropic shape of Gaussians. It minimizes the smallest component of the scaling factor $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ towards zero, effectively flattening Gaussians into 3D surfels aligned with the surface [S2, unofficial]. This aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when surface alignment is critical and normal consistency from depth is unreliable or unavailable.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds overhead proportional to the number of Gaussians for normal computation and loss calculation. Computing normals from the depth map requires spatial gradient operations on the rendered image [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing rendered depth and normal buffers [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Significantly improves surface smoothness and reduces "floaters" (noisy, isolated Gaussians) [S1, S2, unofficial]. It enforces physical plausibility by aligning Gaussians with local surface geometry [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires high-quality depth maps or normal priors. If depth is noisy, normal consistency may propagate errors. Aggressive flattening can lead to over-smoothing or loss of fine geometric details if not balanced with photometric loss [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When the scene has well-defined surfaces and depth supervision is available or can be reliably estimated. Particularly useful for reducing artifacts in complex geometry [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** In scenes with significant transparency, motion blur, or where depth estimation is unreliable.

## Background
Originating from improvements to 3D Gaussian Splatting training pipelines, geometry regularization addresses the inherent noise in unstructured Gaussian distributions [S1, S2, unofficial]. The concept of "scaling loss" to flatten Gaussians into surfels was introduced in prior work [5] cited in [S2, unofficial]. The implementation details, such as the `use_normal_loss` flag and depth refitting strategies, are documented in community-driven implementations like GaussianSplatting.jl [S1, unofficial]. It is used in modular frameworks like GauStudio to enhance the geometric fidelity of reconstructed scenes [S2, unofficial].
