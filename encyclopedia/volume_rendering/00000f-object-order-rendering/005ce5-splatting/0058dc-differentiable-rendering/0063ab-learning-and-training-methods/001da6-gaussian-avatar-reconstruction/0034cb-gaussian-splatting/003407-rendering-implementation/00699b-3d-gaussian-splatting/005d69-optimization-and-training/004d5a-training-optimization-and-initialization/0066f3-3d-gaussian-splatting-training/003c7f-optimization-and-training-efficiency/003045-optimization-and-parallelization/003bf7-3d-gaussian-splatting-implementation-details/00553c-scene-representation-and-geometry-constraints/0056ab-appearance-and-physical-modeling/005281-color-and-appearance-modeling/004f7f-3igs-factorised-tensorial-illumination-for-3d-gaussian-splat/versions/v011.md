# 3iGS (Factorised Tensorial Illumination for 3D Gaussian Splatting)
Also called: Factorised Tensorial Illumination for 3D Gaussian Splatting

## Overview
3iGS enhances 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by modeling outgoing radiance as a function of a continuous local illumination field and individual Gaussian BRDF attributes, rather than optimizing outgoing radiance in isolation [S1]. It solves the problem of inaccurate specular view-dependent effects in reflective scenes by separating scene lighting from material properties, enabling physically plausible shading while maintaining the rapid rendering speeds of rasterization [S1]. Unlike vanilla 3DGS, which uses spherical harmonics for view-dependent color without physical interpretability, 3iGS explicitly models diffuse color and specular highlights via a neural renderer and a factorized tensor illumination grid [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Input:** A set of $N$ 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^N$ initialized from a point cloud (e.g., SfM), camera poses, and training images.
*   **Output:** Rendered image $\hat{\mathbf{C}}$ and optimized scene representation including Gaussian parameters, BRDF features, and the illumination grid.

**Data Structures**
1.  **3D Gaussian $G_i$:**
    *   Mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Position.
    *   Opacity $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$: Transparency.
    *   Covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$: Derived from rotation $\mathbf{R}_i$ and scale $\mathbf{S}_i$ via $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top$ [S1].
    *   Diffuse Color $\mathbf{c}_d \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Constant base color.
    *   BRDF Features $\boldsymbol{\rho}_i$: Repurposed 16x3 SH coefficients from 3DGS plus 4 additional parameters for base color and roughness [S1].
2.  **Illumination Grid $\mathcal{G}_l$:**
    *   Represented using vector-matrix spatial factorization [S1].
    *   Structure: $\mathcal{G}_l = \sum_{r=1}^{R_L} \mathbf{A}_{L,r}^X \circ \mathbf{b}_{3r-2} + \mathbf{A}_{L,r}^Y \circ \mathbf{b}_{3r-1} + \mathbf{A}_{L,r}^Z \circ \mathbf{b}_{3r}$ [S1].
    *   $\mathbf{A}_{L,r}^X, \mathbf{A}_{L,r}^Y, \mathbf{A}_{L,r}^Z$: Feature tensors (matrices) for X, Y, Z dimensions.
    *   $\mathbf{b}$: Feature vectors (appearance mode vectors).
    *   $R_L$: Number of feature channels for illumination.
    *   Resolution: Coarse grid of $150^3$ voxels [S1].

**Numbered Steps**

1.  **Initialize Gaussians:** Fit Gaussians to a point cloud. Initialize $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{S}_i, \mathbf{c}_d$, and $\boldsymbol{\rho}_i$ [S1].
2.  **Initialize Illumination Grid:** Define an initial boundary encapsulating the scene bounding box. Initialize the factorized tensors $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ for $\mathcal{G}_l$ [S1].
3.  **Compute Incoming Illumination:** For each Gaussian $i$ at mean $\mathbf{x}_i$, query the illumination grid $\mathcal{G}_l$ to obtain incoming illumination $L_i$ via interpolation [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{G}_l : \{ \mathbf{x}_i \} \mapsto L_i $$
4.  **Encode Viewing Direction:** Encode the viewing direction $\omega_o$ using Integrated Directional Encoding (IDE) [S1].
5.  **Compute Specular Color:** Pass the Gaussian BRDF features $\boldsymbol{\rho}_i$, the incoming illumination $L_i$, and the encoded viewing direction $\omega_o$ through a neural network $\mathcal{F}$ to compute specular color $\mathbf{c}_s$ [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{F} : \{ \boldsymbol{\rho}_i, L_i, \omega_o \} \mapsto \mathbf{c}_s $$
6.  **Compute Outgoing Radiance:** Sum the diffuse color $\mathbf{c}_d$ and specular color $\mathbf{c}_s$ to get the final radiance $\mathbf{c}(\omega_o)$ [S1]:
    $$ \mathbf{c}(\omega_o) = \mathbf{c}_d + \mathbf{c}_s(\omega_o) $$
7.  **Render:** Rasterize the scene using the standard 3DGS alpha-blending pipeline [S1]. For a pixel $\mathbf{u}$, the color $\hat{\mathbf{C}}$ is:
    $$ \hat{\mathbf{C}} = \sum_{i \in N} T_i g_i(\mathbf{u} | \boldsymbol{\mu}', \boldsymbol{\Sigma}') \alpha_i \mathbf{c}_i, \quad T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - g_j(\mathbf{u} | \boldsymbol{\mu}', \boldsymbol{\Sigma}') \alpha_j) $$
    where $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{J}^\top$ [S1].
8.  **Optimize:** Minimize the loss function $\mathcal{L}$ [S1]:
    $$ \mathcal{L} = (1 - \lambda)\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}} $$
    with $\lambda = 0.2$ [S1]. Optimize $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{S}_i, \mathbf{c}_d, \boldsymbol{\rho}_i$, and the illumination grid parameters [S1].

    Args:
        gaussians: List of Gaussian objects with attributes:
            - mu: (N, 3) mean positions
            - alpha: (N,) opacities
            - R: (N, 3, 3) rotation matrices
            - S: (N, 3) scale vectors
            - c_d: (N, 3) diffuse colors
            - rho: (N, F) BRDF features (16x3 SH + 4 params)
        illumination_grid: Factorized tensor grid object with method query(x)
        camera_pose: Camera transformation matrices W and J
        image_size: (H, W)
        
    Returns:
        rendered_image: (H, W, 3)
    """
    N = gaussians.mu.shape[0]
    
    # 1. Transform Gaussians to camera space
    # Sigma = R * S * S^T * R^T
    Sigma = compute_covariance(gaussians.R, gaussians.S)
    # Sigma' = J * W * Sigma * W^T * J^T
    Sigma_prime = transform_covariance(Sigma, camera_pose.W, camera_pose.J)
    mu_prime = transform_points(gaussians.mu, camera_pose.W, camera_pose.J)
    
    # 2. Rasterization (Simplified 3DGS rasterization)

    # Here we assume a rasterizer function that returns sorted indices and screen-space properties
    screen_properties = rasterize_gaussians(mu_prime, Sigma_prime, gaussians.alpha, image_size)
    
    rendered_image = np.zeros((image_size[0], image_size[1], 3))
    
    for i in screen_properties.sorted_indices:
        # Get screen-space Gaussian properties
        g_i = screen_properties.gaussian_density[i] # g_i(u | mu', Sigma')
        alpha_i = gaussians.alpha[i]
        
        # 3. Compute Illumination
        # Query local illumination field at Gaussian mean
        L_i = illumination_grid.query(gaussians.mu[i])
        
        # 4. Compute Specular Color
        # Encode viewing direction omega_o for this pixel and Gaussian
        omega_o = compute_view_direction(gaussians.mu[i], camera_pose.origin)
        omega_o_encoded = integrate_directional_encoding(omega_o)
        
        # Neural network F maps rho, L, omega_o to c_s

        # 5. Compute Final Radiance
        c_i = gaussians.c_d[i] + c_s
        
        # 6. Alpha Blending
        # T_i is transmittance from previous Gaussians (handled by rasterizer order)
        # Contribution = T_i * g_i * alpha_i * c_i
        # Note: T_i is accumulated by the rasterizer
        contribution = screen_properties.transmittance[i] * g_i * alpha_i * c_i
        rendered_image += contribution
        
    return rendered_image

    """Sigma = R * S * S^T * R^T"""
    S_diag = np.diag(S)
    return R @ (S_diag @ S_diag.T) @ R.T

def transform_covariance(Sigma, W, J):
    """Sigma' = J * W * Sigma * W^T * J^T"""
    return J @ (W @ Sigma @ W.T) @ J.T

**Parameters**
*   **Tensorial Illumination Resolution:** $150^3$ voxels [S1].
*   **BRDF Features:** 16x3 SH coefficients repurposed as feature channels, plus 4 additional parameters (base color, roughness) for IDE [S1].
*   **Loss Weight $\lambda$:** 0.2 [S1].
*   **Training Iterations:** 30,000 steps [S1].
*   **Training Strategy:**
    *   First 3,000 iterations: Train with only diffuse color $\mathbf{c}_d$ [S1].
    *   After 3,000 iterations: Add specular colors $\mathbf{c}_s$ [S1].
*   **Grid Boundary Management:** Initial boundary encapsulates scene bounding box. Midway through training, shrink grid to fit Gaussians and resample with same number of voxels [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Grid Boundary:** If the illumination grid boundary is too small, Gaussians near the edge may receive incorrect illumination. The method mitigates this by shrinking and resampling the grid midway through training [S1].
*   **Specular Stability:** Adding specular colors too early in training can lead to instability. The staged training (diffuse first, then specular) is critical for convergence [S1].
*   **Memory Footprint:** While the illumination grid uses 87.5% less voxels than TensoRF, the number of optimized Gaussians is often an order of magnitude higher, which dominates memory usage [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Physically-Based 3D Gaussian Splatting](16738-physically-based-3d-gaussian-splatting.md)
Physically-Based 3D Gaussian Splatting (PBR 3DGS) is a framework that assigns physically interpretable rendering attributes to Gaussian primitives and evaluates the rendering equation to produce per-Gaussian PBR colors, replacing the view-dependent spherical harmonics used in vanilla 3DGS with a physical Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF). It solves the problem of inverse rendering and relighting by enabling the simulation of complex lighting interactions, such as indirect illumination and shadows, which vanilla 3DGS cannot natively represent due to its lack of physical material definitions. Unlike vanilla 3DGS, which uses $k$-order spherical harmonics for view-dependent effects that lack physical interpretability, this method provides physical interpretability by explicitly modeling albedo, roughness, and metalness.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering speed is comparable to 3DGS because illumination is pre-calculated and stored in the factorized tensor grid, allowing fast evaluation via interpolation [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** The illumination field uses 87.5% less voxels compared to TensoRF on synthetic datasets [S1]. However, the total memory is dominated by the number of Gaussians, which is often an order of magnitude higher than the grid size [S1].
*   **Performance:** On the Shiny Blender dataset, 3iGS achieves PSNR 27.51, SSIM 0.930, LPIPS 0.045 [S1]. It surpasses 3DGS quantitatively and qualitatively on synthetic datasets like NeRF Blender and Shiny Blender, and on real-world datasets like Tanks and Temples [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Significantly enhances rendering quality of specular view-dependent effects; maintains rapid training and rendering speeds; compact illumination representation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Does not model global indirect illumination or shadows natively (relies on local illumination field); requires careful training schedule (diffuse then specular) [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Scenes with reflective surfaces where surface colors change dramatically across viewing angles [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** Scenes where global lighting effects (shadows, indirect bounces) are critical and cannot be approximated by a local field [S1].

## Background
3iGS was introduced by Z.J. Tang and T.J. Cham [S1]. It addresses the limitations of vanilla 3DGS, which optimizes outgoing radiance in isolation without accounting for adjacent Gaussians or scene lighting conditions, resulting in poor view-dependent effects for specular surfaces [S1]. The method is inspired by conventional computer graphics engines that use baking techniques for fast rendering [S1]. It builds on the factorized tensor representation from TensoRF [S1] and adapts it for local illumination in 3DGS.
