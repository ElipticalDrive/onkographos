# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which provides small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR increases pixel gradients in these regions by explicitly disentangling frequency components [S2]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Smoothed 2D Supervision](7213-smoothed-2d-supervision.md) by operating in the Fourier domain rather than the spatial domain, and from [Hard and Soft Depth Regularization](9178-hard-and-soft-depth-regularization.md) by targeting spectral consistency rather than geometric depth alignment [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Image dimensions $H, W$.
*   **Outputs:** Scalar loss values $d_a$ (amplitude discrepancy) and $d_p$ (phase discrepancy), which are combined into the total regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$.

**Data Structures:**
*   Complex frequency array $F(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$, storing real and imaginary components.
*   Amplitude array $|F(u, v)|$ of size $H \times W$.
*   Phase array $\angle F(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$.

**Parameters:**
*   $\lambda_{reg}$: Weighting factor for the frequency regularization term. Not given in the sources.
*   Optimizer: Adam [S2].
*   Framework: PyTorch [S2].
*   Rasterization: Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS [S2].

**Steps:**
1.  Compute the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) for the ground truth image $I$ to obtain $F(u, v)$ and for the rendered image $\hat{I}$ to obtain $\hat{F}(u, v)$.
2.  Calculate the amplitude and phase for both frequency representations.
3.  Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$.
4.  Combine discrepancies into the regularization loss.

**Formulas:**
The 2D DFT is defined as [S2]:
$$
F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)}
$$
where $(x, y)$ are image coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value.

The amplitude and phase are derived from the real ($Re$) and imaginary ($Im$) components of $F(u, v)$ [S2]:
$$
|F(u, v)| = \sqrt{Re(u, v)^2 + Im(u, v)^2}
$$
$$
\angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{Im(u, v)}{Re(u, v)}\right)
$$

The total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and phase discrepancy $d_p$ are calculated as the Euclidean distance averaged over all pixels [S2]:
$$
d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} \left( |F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)| \right)^2}
$$
$$
d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} \left( \angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v) \right)^2}
$$

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.fft

def compute_frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image: torch.Tensor, ground_truth_image: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the frequency regularization loss based on amplitude and phase discrepancies.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (C, H, W). Assumes normalized values.
        ground_truth_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (C, H, W).
        
    Returns:
        Scalar tensor representing the total frequency regularization loss.
    """
    # Ensure inputs are in the correct format for FFT (C, H, W)
    if rendered_image.dim() == 3 and rendered_image.shape[0] != 1:
        # If shape is (H, W, C), permute to (C, H, W)
        rendered_image = rendered_image.permute(2, 0, 1)
        ground_truth_image = ground_truth_image.permute(2, 0, 1)
        
    # Compute 2D FFT for both images
    # torch.fft.fft2 expects input shape (..., H, W)
    F_rendered = torch.fft.fft2(rendered_image)
    F_gt = torch.fft.fft2(ground_truth_image)
    
    # Compute Amplitude and Phase for Rendered Image
    amp_rendered = torch.abs(F_rendered)
    phase_rendered = torch.angle(F_rendered)
    
    # Compute Amplitude and Phase for Ground Truth
    amp_gt = torch.abs(F_gt)
    phase_gt = torch.angle(F_gt)
    
    # Compute Amplitude Discrepancy (da)
    # Formula: sqrt( (1/HW) * sum( (|F| - |F_hat|)^2 ) )
    # Note: The source formula uses sum over x,y. In tensor operations, we sum over spatial dims.
    # H and W are the spatial dimensions.
    H, W = rendered_image.shape[-2], rendered_image.shape[-1]
    
    amp_diff_sq = (amp_rendered - amp_gt) ** 2
    da = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(amp_diff_sq))
    
    # Compute Phase Discrepancy (dp)
    # Formula: sqrt( (1/HW) * sum( (angle(F) - angle(F_hat))^2 ) )
    phase_diff_sq = (phase_rendered - phase_gt) ** 2
    dp = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(phase_diff_sq))
    
    # Combine losses. The source defines da and dp separately. 
    # Typically, they are summed or weighted. The source implies minimizing both.
    # We return the sum as the regularization term.
    total_loss = da + dp
    
    return total_loss
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Phase Wrapping:** The arctangent function is discontinuous at $\pm \pi$. If the phase difference exceeds $\pi$, the squared difference will be large even if the angles are close (e.g., $3.1$ vs $-3.1$). The source does not specify phase unwrapping or circular distance metrics, which is a potential pitfall for implementation [S2].
*   **Zero Amplitude:** If $|F(u, v)| = 0$, the phase is undefined. Division by zero in `arctan(Im/Re)` or direct angle computation may occur. A small epsilon should be added to the denominator or amplitude check [G].
*   **Resolution:** The DFT is computed on the full image resolution. High-resolution images increase computational cost [S2].

## Subtopics

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a variant designed for sparse-view 3D Gaussian Splatting that rethinks frequency regularization by applying a Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) on the rendered image instead of a Fourier Transform [S4, unofficial]. It penalizes the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges, addressing the limitations of standard FR in sparse-view scenarios [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Computing the 2D DFT for an image of size $H \times W$ takes $O(HW \log(HW))$ time using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) [S2]. This is added to the per-step cost of the rasterization and gradient update.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the complex frequency arrays for both rendered and ground truth images, adding $O(HW)$ memory overhead per step [S2].
*   **Performance:** FR significantly increases pixel gradients in over-reconstruction regions compared to L1 loss, enabling more effective adaptive densification [S2]. It mitigates over-reconstruction and reduces the creation of redundant Gaussians in well-reconstructed regions [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Explicitly disentangles frequency components, allowing for targeted densification in high-frequency regions [S2]. It avoids the bias towards undesirable artifacts that can occur with naive frequency regularization by using progressive annealing (low-to-high frequency) [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Requires custom CUDA kernels or efficient FFT libraries (like PyTorch's FFT) which may not be available in all minimal rendering setups [S2]. It may increase training time due to the additional spectral computations [S2].
*   **When to use:** When the scene exhibits over-reconstruction artifacts or when standard densification fails to capture high-frequency details [S2].
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited and FFT overhead is prohibitive, or when the scene is purely low-frequency [S2].

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced in the context of improving 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization, specifically to address convergence speed, visual artifacts, and improper Gaussians [S1]. The method was detailed in "FreGS: 3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization" [S2]. It builds on the observation that pixel-wise L1 loss is insufficient for guiding densification in over-reconstructed areas [S2]. The technique has been further adapted for sparse-view scenarios via DWTGS [S4, unofficial] and is part of a broader trend of incorporating spectral and geometric constraints into 3D GS training [S1].
