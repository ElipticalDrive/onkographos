# Geometry Regularization

Also called: Gaussian Flattening, Normal Consistency and Flattening

## Overview
Geometry regularization improves reconstruction quality by constraining the shape and orientation of the surface, distinct from depth supervision which only constrains location [S1, unofficial]. It addresses the issue of "floaters" and poor geometric smoothness by enforcing that rendered depth normals align with per-Gaussian normals and by flattening Gaussians along their smallest axis [S1, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from siblings like [Frequency Regularization](6130-frequency-regularization.md) by focusing on explicit geometric constraints (normal alignment and anisotropy) rather than frequency-domain smoothing.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Per-Gaussian parameters (center $\mathbf{c}$, scaling vector $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, rotation quaternion $\mathbf{q}$, opacity $\alpha$); rendered depth map $D$; rendered normal map $N_{rendered}$ derived from $D$; per-Gaussian normals $N_{gauss}$ derived from the Gaussian covariance structure.
*   **Outputs:** Updated Gaussian parameters (specifically scaling $\mathbf{s}$ and potentially rotation $\mathbf{q}$) via gradient descent; total loss value.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Gaussian State:** Each Gaussian $i$ has a scaling vector $\mathbf{s}_i = [s_{i,x}, s_{i,y}, s_{i,z}]^T$. The covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$ is constructed from $\mathbf{s}_i$ and rotation $\mathbf{q}_i$.
*   **Normal Derivation:** The normal of a Gaussian is typically defined by the direction of its smallest principal axis (shortest scaled edge) [S2, unofficial]. If $\mathbf{s}_i$ is sorted such that $s_{min}$ is the smallest component, the normal $\mathbf{n}_i$ corresponds to the corresponding axis in the rotated frame.

**Parameters:**
*   `use_normal_loss`: Boolean flag to enable geometry regularization [S1, unofficial].
*   $\lambda_{flatten}$: Weight for the flattening loss. Not given in the sources.
*   $\lambda_{normal}$: Weight for the normal consistency loss. Not given in the sources.

**Steps:**
1.  **Preprocessing:** Refit all depth maps against a prior point cloud to ensure consistent scale. Discard depth maps that fail refitting [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Compute Rendered Normals:** Derive surface normals $N_{rendered}$ from the rendered depth map $D$ using finite differences or a differentiable normal estimation operator.
3.  **Compute Gaussian Normals:** For each Gaussian, identify the smallest component of the scaling vector $\mathbf{s}_i$. The normal $N_{gauss}$ is the unit vector corresponding to the axis associated with this minimum scale, rotated by $\mathbf{q}_i$ [S2, unofficial].
4.  **Calculate Normal Consistency Loss:** Compute the angular difference between $N_{rendered}$ and $N_{gauss}$ at corresponding pixel locations.
5.  **Calculate Flattening Loss:** Minimize the smallest component of $\mathbf{s}_i$ towards zero.
6.  **Update:** Backpropagate the combined loss to update Gaussian parameters.

**Formulas:**
Let $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ be the scaling factor vector for Gaussian $i$.
The flattening loss $\mathcal{L}_{flatten}$ for a single Gaussian is:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{flatten} = \min(\mathbf{s}_i) $$
The goal is to minimize this value towards zero [S2, unofficial].

The normal consistency loss $\mathcal{L}_{normal}$ is typically defined as the dot product or angular distance between the rendered normal $\mathbf{n}_{rendered}$ and the Gaussian normal $\mathbf{n}_{gauss}$:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{normal} = 1 - \mathbf{n}_{rendered} \cdot \mathbf{n}_{gauss} $$
(Exact formulation for $\mathcal{L}_{normal}$ not given in the sources, but "align" implies maximizing dot product or minimizing angle [S1, unofficial]).

## Subtopics

### Scaling Loss for Geometry Modeling
A regularization technique to flatten Gaussian kernels into 3D surfels aligned with the surface by minimizing the smallest component of the scaling factor towards zero [S2, unofficial]. It aids in determining Gaussian normals by selecting the shortest scaled edge [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen when explicit surface alignment and normal estimation are required for the Gaussians [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
*   **Time:** Adds overhead to the training step for computing normals from depth and calculating the flattening loss. Normal derivation from depth is $O(P)$ where $P$ is the number of pixels. Flattening loss is $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of Gaussians.
*   **Memory:** Requires storing rendered depth and normal maps. No additional large data structures for the Gaussians themselves.

**Measured Performance:**
*   Improves geometry and reduces the number of floaters [S1, unofficial].
*   Specific quantitative metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) are not given in the sources.

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Explicitly enforces surface-like properties (flatness, normal consistency) [S1, unofficial]. Helps disentangle sky/distant background when combined with techniques like Sky Dome [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Requires reliable depth maps. Depth maps that fail refitting against a prior point cloud must be discarded [S1, unofficial]. May trade off with other regularization methods like entropy loss for opacity, potentially reducing the number of remaining points if not balanced [S2, unofficial].

**When to Use:**
*   Use when geometric accuracy and smoothness are prioritized over raw photometric fidelity.
*   Use when floaters are a significant issue.
*   Do not use if depth supervision is unreliable or unavailable.

## Background
Originated as a method to improve geometry and smoothness in 3D Gaussian Splatting, distinct from depth supervision [S1, unofficial]. It is implemented in libraries such as GaussianSplatting.jl [S1, unofficial]. The concept of flattening Gaussians into surfels via scaling loss is discussed in the context of GauStudio [S2, unofficial].
