# Hyperparameter Settings

Also called: Optimizer and Regularization Settings, Optimizer settings, Phys3DGS Hyperparameter Settings, Training Hyperparameters

## Overview
This article defines the specific numerical configurations for optimizers, learning rates, and regularization weights used in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) training, particularly within physically-based inverse rendering and dynamic scene optimization contexts. It solves the problem of training instability and convergence failure by providing precise values that balance gradient flow, material ambiguity, and geometric fidelity. It differs from general densification strategies [Densification Strategy](6234-densification-strategy.md) by focusing on the continuous parameter updates (learning rates, optimizer hyperparameters) rather than the discrete primitive management (splitting, pruning) triggered by density metrics.

## How it works
The training process relies on a combination of the Adam optimizer, specific learning rates for different Gaussian attributes, and regularization terms to prevent physical inconsistencies.

**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs:** Gaussian primitives $\mathcal{G}$ with parameters $\{\mu_k, s_k, q_k, \alpha_k, \mathbf{c}_k\}$, scene images, and ground truth (RGB, depth, or normal maps).
*   **Outputs:** Optimized Gaussian parameters and scene representation.

**Data Structures and Layouts**
The learning rates are applied to specific fields of the Gaussian primitive structure. The exact layout of learning rates (LR) for the Phys3DGS model is defined as follows [S2, unofficial]:

| Parameter | Learning Rate |
| :--- | :--- |
| Position ($\mu$) | $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| Color SH (Spherical Harmonics) | $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| Scale ($s$) | $5 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| Opacity ($\alpha$) | $5 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| Base Color | $1 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| Metallic | $1 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| Roughness | $1 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| Normal Rotation | $1 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| Direct Light SH (deg=0) | $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| Direct Light SH (deg>0) | $1 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| Indirect Light SH (deg=0) | $2.5 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| Indirect Light SH (deg>0) | $1 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| Visibility SH | $0.1$ |

For ToRF (Time of the Flight of the Gaussians), the MLP learning rate starts at $\gamma_{MLP} = 0.0008$ and decays exponentially to $1.6 \times 10^{-6}$ over 60,000 iterations [S1]. Reflectivity is initialized to 0.1 with a learning rate $\gamma_r = 0.00016$ [S1].

**Optimizer Configuration**
The Adam optimizer is used with the following hyperparameters [S1][S2, unofficial]:
*   $\beta_1 = 0.9$
*   $\beta_2 = 0.999$
*   $\epsilon = 10^{-15}$

**Regularization Settings**
To maintain physical plausibility and training stability, specific regularization strengths are applied [S2, unofficial]:
*   Smoothness regularizer for Base Color: $\lambda_{smooth} = 0.06$
*   Smoothness regularizer for Roughness: $\lambda_{smooth} = 0.02$
*   Smoothness regularizer for Metallic: $\lambda_{smooth} = 0.02$
*   Other regularizers (opacity, scale, rotation constraints): $\lambda_o = \lambda_{sc} = \lambda_{sr} = 1.0$

In ToRF, the loss function weights are set to $\alpha = 5$ (or $\alpha = 1$ for synthetic scenes) and $\beta = 0.0008$ [S1].

**Pitfalls and Edge Cases**
A critical instability arises if a single Gaussian behaves abnormally, such as being driven to the opposite side of the surface, acquiring an excessively large scale, or extreme opacity [S2, unofficial]. This causes:
1.  Erroneous Gaussians interfering with visibility, occluding global illumination.
2.  The optimizer attempting to compensate by increasing local illumination intensity or decreasing opacity to extreme values.
3.  Gradient overflow in Spherical Harmonic (SH) coefficients or opacity values [S2, unofficial].
The high weights ($\lambda = 1.0$) for non-smoothness regularizers are specifically chosen to mitigate this instability [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Conditional 3DGS Optimization and Rendering](11459-conditional-3dgs-optimization-and-rendering.md)
This method adapts 3DGS training to handle 7-dimensional data (3D spatial, 1D temporal, 3D directional) by converting conditional representations into standard 3DGS formats. It distinguishes itself by leveraging temporal cues for adaptive density control (splitting) and modifying pruning thresholds to account for time and view-direction modulation, allowing seamless integration with existing 3DGS infrastructure.

### [Blended Error Densification and Splitting](13892-blended-error-densification-and-splitting.md)
This strategy grows Gaussian primitives using an auxiliary per-pixel error metric rather than positional gradient magnitudes. It solves the limitation of gradient-based methods being blind to absolute error values in high-frequency areas by redistributing per-pixel errors to primitives proportional to their rendering contribution. It is distinct from sibling methods by explicitly utilizing accumulated L1 error norms as probability weights for splitting.

### [Adaptive Gaussian Selector](16104-adaptive-gaussian-selector.md)
The Adaptive Gaussian Selector is a learnable module within the FlexGS framework that adaptively selects a subset of 3D Gaussians during training based on their attributes and a desired elastic ratio $e$, using a differentiable mask. It sets itself apart by allowing gradients to flow through the selection decision via the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) applied to Gumbel-Softmax outputs, unlike sibling methods that rely on fixed heuristics or non-differentiable hard thresholds.

### GaMeS
GaMeS simplifies mesh generation by utilizing an initial mesh or estimating it in a single-stage training process, introducing a pseudo-mesh of unconnected triangle faces with Gaussian components positioned on these faces to ensure proper alignment [S3]. It realizes deformation by modifying pseudo-mesh positions, with alterations automatically propagating to the Gaussian mesh, enabling real-time animation and scene editing [S3]. However, it exhibits artifacts when dealing with significant deformations in meshes with large faces, leading to inaccuracies [S3].

### Sparse Matching Integration
This approach integrates mid-points derived from feature-matching correspondences with randomly initialized Gaussian points and applies geometric regularization specifically to correspondences that pass through an outlier filter [S4]. Unlike dense matcher-based methods that cause blurriness in high-frequency areas, this sparse matching approach yields more precise correspondences and better high-frequency reconstruction when combined with frequency regularization [S4]. On the LLFF dataset with 3 views, it improves PSNR from 16.50 (Base 3DGS) to 19.90, an improvement of ~3 dB [S4].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The optimization step is dominated by the Adam optimizer updates and the rendering loss computation. The exponential decay of the MLP learning rate in ToRF [S1] requires tracking iteration counts but does not change the per-step complexity.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Memory usage is determined by the number of Gaussians and the storage of SH coefficients. The high learning rate for opacity ($5 \times 10^{-2}$) [S2, unofficial] may require careful gradient clipping to prevent overflow, but does not inherently increase memory usage.
*   **Measured Performance:** In Phys3DGS, the specific learning rates and regularization weights ensure stable convergence for inverse rendering tasks, preventing the gradient overflow issues associated with abnormal Gaussians [S2, unofficial]. In ToRF, the specific reflectivity and MLP learning rates enable stable optimization of depth indirectly in dynamic radiance fields [S1].
*   **Strengths:** The detailed per-parameter learning rates allow fine-grained control over the convergence of different physical properties (e.g., roughness vs. position). The high regularization weights prevent training instability caused by outlier primitives [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** The settings are highly specific to the Phys3DGS and ToRF architectures. Applying these exact values to standard 3DGS may not yield optimal results. The sensitivity to abnormal Gaussians [S2, unofficial] means that initialization and densification strategies must be robust.
*   **When to use:** Use these settings when implementing Phys3DGS for inverse rendering or ToRF for dynamic scene optimization. Do not use blindly for standard static 3DGS without adjusting for the specific loss functions and regularizers of the target method.

## Background
The hyperparameter settings described here originate from two primary papers: "Phys3DGS: Physically-based 3D Gaussian Splatting for Inverse Rendering" [S2, unofficial] and "Time of the Flight of the Gaussians: Optimizing Depth Indirectly in Dynamic Radiance Fields" [S1]. Phys3DGS was developed to address the material-lighting ambiguity in inverse rendering by introducing physically-based regularizers and specific learning rates for PBR parameters [S2, unofficial]. ToRF was developed to optimize depth indirectly in dynamic scenes using C-ToF (Continuous-Time-of-Flight) data, requiring specific MLP and reflectivity learning rates [S1]. These settings have become reference points for subsequent works in physically-based and dynamic 3DGS training.
