# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which fails to differentiate between well-reconstructed and over-reconstructed regions, FR explicitly disentangles frequency components to increase pixel gradients in over-reconstructed areas, thereby guiding adaptive densification toward high-frequency details [S2][S3, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, and the current Gaussian parameters $\theta$ (centers $\mu$, scales $s$, rotations $q$, opacities $\alpha$, and colors $c$).
*   **Outputs:** A scalar regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ (specifically the amplitude and phase discrepancies) and gradients flowing back to $\theta$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Frequency Domain:** Complex-valued matrices $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$ for each color channel.
*   **Amplitude/Phase:** Real-valued matrices $|F(u, v)|$ and $\angle F(u, v)$ of size $H \times W$.

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Render:** Compute the rendered image $\hat{I}$ from the current Gaussian parameters $\theta$ using standard alpha-blending rasterization [S2].
2.  **Transform:** Apply the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) to the ground truth image $I$ and the rendered image $\hat{I}$ to obtain their frequency representations $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ [S2].
    $$ F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
    $$ \hat{F}(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} \hat{I}(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)} $$
    where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $i$ is the imaginary unit [S2].
3.  **Decompose:** For each frequency component $(u, v)$, compute the amplitude and phase for both the ground truth and rendered images [S2]:
    $$ |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(u, v)^2 + \text{Im}(u, v)^2} $$
    $$ \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Im}(u, v)}{\text{Re}(u, v)}\right) $$
    where $\text{Re}(u, v)$ and $\text{Im}(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$ [S2].
4.  **Compute Discrepancies:** Calculate the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ using the Euclidean metric over all pixels [S2]:
    $$ d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} \left( |F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)| \right)^2} $$
    $$ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} \left( \angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v) \right)^2} $$
5.  **Regularize:** The frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of these discrepancies (often combined with a frequency annealing schedule in advanced variants like FreGS, which applies low-pass and high-pass filters to guide coarse-to-fine densification) [S2]. The total loss is:
    $$ \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \lambda_{reg} \mathcal{L}_{reg} $$
    where $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ incorporates $d_a$ and $d_p$ [Parent, S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
*   **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
*   **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS [S2].
*   **Weight $\lambda_{reg}$:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Frequency Annealing:** Advanced implementations use a low-pass filter $H_l$ and dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ to progressively regularize from low to high frequencies [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Over-reconstruction:** Naive L1 loss creates redundant Gaussians in well-reconstructed regions because it cannot distinguish them from under-reconstructed ones. FR mitigates this by increasing gradients specifically in over-reconstructed regions [S2].
*   **Bias to Artifacts:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can restrict Gaussian densification and bias the splatting toward undesirable artifacts [S2]. This is addressed by frequency annealing techniques [S2].
*   **Precision:** The arctangent function for phase calculation requires handling quadrants correctly (e.g., using `atan2`) to avoid discontinuities, though the source formula uses standard arctan [S2].

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The addition of 2D DFT and inverse DFT operations increases computational overhead per iteration compared to standard L1 loss. The complexity is dominated by the FFT, typically $O(N \log N)$ for image size $N=HW$.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing frequency domain representations for both rendered and ground truth images, adding $O(HW)$ memory overhead per channel.
*   **Measured Performance:** In the "Bicycle" scene, FR significantly increased pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to the baseline method (equivalent to 3D-GS with L1 loss) [S2]. It allows for superior Gaussian densification when combined with frequency annealing [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Effectively alleviates over-reconstruction; improves adaptive densification by revealing over-reconstructed regions that L1 loss misses; pushes Gaussians toward high-frequency regions [S2][S3, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Naive application without frequency annealing can lead to restricted densification and artifacts [S2]. Computationally more expensive than spatial domain losses.
*   **When to use:** When the scene suffers from over-reconstruction or when precise high-frequency detail recovery is critical.
*   **When not to use:** When computational resources are severely limited, or when the scene is low-frequency and simple L1 loss suffices.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced to address convergence speed, visual artifacts, and improper Gaussians in 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization [S1]. It is part of a broader trend of additional regularization techniques, including geometry-aware approaches [S1]. The specific formulation using amplitude and phase discrepancy was proposed in the FreGS paper [S2]. It is often cited alongside other optimization enhancements and constraint relaxation methods [S1].
