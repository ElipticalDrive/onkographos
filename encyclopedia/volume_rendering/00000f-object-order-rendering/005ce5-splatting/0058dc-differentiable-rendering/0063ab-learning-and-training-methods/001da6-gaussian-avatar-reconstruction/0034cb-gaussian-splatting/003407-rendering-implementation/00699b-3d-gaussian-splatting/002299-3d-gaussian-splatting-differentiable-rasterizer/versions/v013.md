# 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer

Also called: 3D Gaussian Rasterization and Rendering, 3DGS Renderer, 3DGS rasterization pipeline, Differentiable Gaussian Rasterization, differentiable splatting

## Overview

The 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer is the core rendering engine that projects a set of 3D Gaussian primitives into screen space and composites them into a 2D image, while simultaneously computing gradients for all primitive parameters [S3][S12, unofficial]. It solves the problem of efficiently rendering millions of anisotropic Gaussians with view-dependent color (via Spherical Harmonics) in real-time, enabling end-to-end differentiable optimization of scene geometry, appearance, and camera poses [S2][S11, unofficial]. Unlike volumetric ray-marching methods like NeRF, which integrate density along rays, this method performs discrete alpha-compositing of 2D elliptical kernels in screen space, leveraging tile-based sorting and rasterization primitives for high throughput [S7][S12, unofficial]. It differs from its siblings by providing the specific forward and backward passes required for gradient-based training of Gaussian parameters, often implemented as a custom CUDA kernel to bypass the overhead of standard graphics APIs [S12, unofficial].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs

**Inputs:**
*   **Gaussian Parameters:**
    *   `means3D`: Tensor of shape $(N, 3)$, 3D positions in world space [S11, unofficial].
    *   `scales`: Tensor of shape $(N, 3)$, anisotropic scaling factors [S11, unofficial].
    *   `rotations`: Tensor of shape $(N, 4)$, unit quaternions $q=(w,x,y,z)$ representing orientation [S11, unofficial].
    *   `opacities`: Tensor of shape $(N,)$, scalar opacity values $o_i \in [0, 1]$ [S11, unofficial].
    *   `colors`: Either pre-computed RGB tensor of shape $(N, 3)$ or Spherical Harmonics (SH) coefficients of shape $(N, D, 3)$ where $D$ depends on the SH degree [S6, S9, unofficial].
*   **Camera Parameters:**
    *   `viewmatrix`: $4 \times 4$ world-to-camera transformation matrix [S9, unofficial].
    *   `projmatrix`: $4 \times 4$ projection matrix (combining intrinsic and extrinsic) [S9, unofficial].
    *   `image_width`, `image_height`: Integer resolution of the output image [S9, unofficial].
    *   `tanfovx`, `tanfovy`: Tangents of half the horizontal and vertical field of view [S9, unofficial].
    *   `bg`: Background color tensor of shape $(3)$, typically white or black [S9, unofficial].
*   **Settings:**
    *   `sh_degree`: Degree of Spherical Harmonics used for color [S9, unofficial].
    *   `scale_modifier`: Scalar multiplier applied to Gaussian scales (default 1.0) [S9, unofficial].
    *   `prefiltered`: Boolean flag; if False, SH coefficients are evaluated during rasterization [S9, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   `rendered_image`: Tensor of shape $(H, W, 3)$, the final RGB image [S6, unofficial].
*   `rendered_opacity`: Tensor of shape $(H, W)$, accumulated opacity [S6, unofficial].
*   `radii`: Tensor of shape $(N)$, radius of each Gaussian in pixels (used for culling) [S6, unofficial].
*   `num_rendered`: Count of visible Gaussians [S6, unofficial].
*   Gradients for all input tensors with respect to the loss function [S5, unofficial].

### Data Structures

The rasterizer typically operates on a tiled grid to optimize memory access and sorting.

1.  **Gaussian Attributes:** Stored in contiguous CUDA memory arrays (or PyTorch tensors) for batch processing.
    *   `xys`: 2D screen-space means $(x, y)$ of shape $(N, 2)$ [S5, unofficial].
    *   `conics`: Inverse 2D covariance matrices (or their Cholesky factors) of shape $(N, 3)$, storing the upper triangle of $\Sigma^{-1}$ as $(\Sigma^{-1}_{00}, \Sigma^{-1}_{01}, \Sigma^{-1}_{11})$ [S5, unofficial].
    *   `colors`: View-dependent colors evaluated from SH or pre-computed RGB [S5, unofficial].
    *   `opacity`: Scalar opacities [S5, unofficial].
    *   `cov2d`: 2D covariance matrices of shape $(N, 2, 2)$, used for bounding box calculation [S5, unofficial].
    *   `camDepths`: Depth of each Gaussian center in camera space [S5, unofficial].

2.  **Tile Buffers:**
    *   The image is divided into $16 \times 16$ tiles [S11, unofficial].
    *   Each tile maintains a list of Gaussians that overlap its bounding box.
    *   Sorting is performed per-tile based on Gaussian depth [S11, unofficial].

3.  **Context for Backpropagation:**
    *   `px2gid`: Mapping from pixel index to Gaussian ID, stored as `int64_t` [S5, unofficial].
    *   `finalTs`: Transmittance buffer for each pixel, saved for backward pass [S5, unofficial].
    *   `imgBuffer`: Intermediate rendering buffers (color, opacity, depth) [S6, unofficial].

### Numbered Steps

1.  **Projection:** Transform 3D Gaussian means $\mu_i$ from world space to camera space using the view matrix. Project camera-space means to 2D screen coordinates $(x, y)$ using the perspective projection formula:
    $$
    x = \frac{X_c}{Z_c} f_x + c_x, \quad y = \frac{Y_c}{Z_c} f_y + c_y
    $$
    where $(X_c, Y_c, Z_c)$ are camera-space coordinates, $f_x, f_y$ are focal lengths derived from FOV, and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point [S10, S11, unofficial].

2.  **Covariance Transformation:** Compute the 2D covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$ in screen space. This involves projecting the 3D covariance $\Sigma_{3D}$ using the Jacobian $J$ of the perspective projection:
    $$
    \Sigma_i = J W \Sigma_{3D} W^\top J^\top
    $$
    where $W$ is the view rotation matrix [S11, S12, unofficial]. The 2D covariance is often regularized by adding a small constant to the diagonal to prevent singularities [S12, unofficial].

3.  **Bounding Box and Culling:** Calculate the axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) for each Gaussian in screen space, typically covering $3\sigma$ (99.7% probability) of the 2D ellipse [S11, unofficial]. Frustum cull Gaussians outside the image bounds and those with zero radius [S9, unofficial].

4.  **Tiling and Sorting:** Bin visible Gaussians into $16 \times 16$ tiles based on their AABBs. Within each tile, sort Gaussians by depth (front-to-back) to ensure correct alpha compositing order [S11, unofficial].

5.  **Color Evaluation:** If using Spherical Harmonics, evaluate the SH function for each Gaussian at the viewing direction to obtain the color $c_i$. If `prefiltered` is False, this is done inside the kernel; otherwise, pre-computed colors are used [S6, S9, unofficial].

6.  **Rasterization (Alpha Compositing):** For each pixel, iterate through the sorted list of overlapping Gaussians. Compute the 2D Gaussian density at the pixel center $p$:
    $$
    \alpha_i = o_i \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(p - \mu_i)^\top \Sigma_i^{-1} (p - \mu_i)\right)
    $$
    Clamp $\alpha_i$ to a maximum value (e.g., 0.99) to prevent numerical instability [S10, unofficial]. Update the accumulated transmittance $T_i$ and color $C$:
    $$
    T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad C = \sum_{i \in N} c_i \alpha_i T_i
    $$
    where $N$ is the set of Gaussians overlapping the pixel [S3][S11, unofficial].

7.  **Gradient Computation:** Compute gradients for the backward pass. The rasterizer must differentiate through the projection, sorting, and compositing steps. Gradients flow from the loss to the screen-space means, opacities, colors, and covariances, which are then propagated back to 3D parameters [S5, S12, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
import torch
import math

def gaussian_rasterizer_forward(means3D, scales, rotations, opacities, sh_coeffs, 
                                viewmatrix, projmatrix, tanfovx, tanfovy, 
                                image_height, image_width, bg_color, sh_degree):
    """
    Forward pass for 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization.
    Simplified for clarity; assumes CUDA kernel invocation in production.
    """
    N = means3D.shape[0]
    
    # 1. Project 3D Gaussians to 2D
    # Transform means to camera space
    means_cam = torch.matmul(means3D, viewmatrix[:3, :3].T) + viewmatrix[:3, 3]
    
    # Perspective projection
    fx = image_width / (2 * math.tan(tanfovx))
    fy = image_height / (2 * math.tan(tanfovy))
    cx = image_width / 2
    cy = image_height / 2
    
    means2D_x = (means_cam[:, 0] / means_cam[:, 2]) * fx + cx
    means2D_y = (means_cam[:, 1] / means_cam[:, 2]) * fy + cy
    means2D = torch.stack([means2D_x, means2D_y], dim=1)
    
    # 2. Compute 2D Covariance
    # Construct rotation matrix from quaternion
    R = quaternion_to_rotation_matrix(rotations)
    # Scale matrix
    S = torch.diag_embed(torch.exp(scales)) # Assuming scales are log-space
    
    # 3D Covariance: Sigma_3D = R S S^T R^T
    cov3D = R @ (S @ S.T) @ R.T
    
    # Project to 2D using Jacobian J
    # J = (1/Z) * [[fx, 0, -fx*X/Z], [0, fy, -fy*Y/Z]]
    # Simplified: Sigma_2D = J * W * Sigma_3D * W^T * J^T
    # Note: Full implementation requires careful handling of the Jacobian and view matrix
    cov2D = compute_2d_covariance(cov3D, means_cam, fx, fy)
    
    # 3. Compute Conics (Inverse Covariance)
    # Store upper triangle of inv(cov2D) for efficiency
    inv_cov2D = torch.inverse(cov2D)
    conics = torch.stack([inv_cov2D[:, 0, 0], inv_cov2D[:, 0, 1], inv_cov2D[:, 1, 1]], dim=1)
    
    # 4. Evaluate Colors (SH)
    if sh_coeffs is not None:
        # Evaluate SH at viewing direction
        colors = evaluate_sh(sh_coeffs, means_cam, sh_degree)
    else:
        colors = sh_coeffs # Pre-computed
    
    # 5. Rasterize (Simulated Tile-based Alpha Compositing)
    # In practice, this is done in a CUDA kernel
    rendered_image = torch.zeros((image_height, image_width, 3), device=means3D.device)
    rendered_opacity = torch.zeros((image_height, image_width), device=means3D.device)
    
    # Sort Gaussians by depth
    depths = means_cam[:, 2]
    sorted_indices = torch.argsort(depths, descending=True)
    
    # For each pixel, composite Gaussians
    # This loop is illustrative; production uses parallel tile processing
    for y in range(image_height):
        for x in range(image_width):
            T = 1.0
            color = bg_color
            for idx in sorted_indices:
                mu = means2D[idx]
                dist_sq = (x - mu[0])**2 + (y - mu[1])**2
                
                # Check if pixel is within bounding box (simplified)
                if dist_sq > 9.0: # 3 sigma approx
                    continue
                    
                # Compute alpha
                cov_inv = inv_cov2D[idx]
                power = -0.5 * (dist_sq * cov_inv[0,0] + ... ) # Full quadratic form
                alpha = opacities[idx] * torch.exp(power)
                alpha = torch.clamp(alpha, max=0.99)
                
                c = colors[idx]
                color = color + c * alpha * T
                T = T * (1 - alpha)
                
                if T < 1e-4:
                    break
            
            rendered_image[y, x] = color
            rendered_opacity[y, x] = 1 - T
            
    return rendered_image, rendered_opacity

def quaternion_to_rotation_matrix(q):
    w, x, y, z = q[:, 0], q[:, 1], q[:, 2], q[:, 3]
    R = torch.zeros((q.shape[0], 3, 3), device=q.device)
    R[:, 0, 0] = 1 - 2 * (y**2 + z**2)
    R[:, 0, 1] = 2 * (x*y - w*z)
    R[:, 0, 2] = 2 * (x*z + w*y)
    R[:, 1, 0] = 2 * (x*y + w*z)
    R[:, 1, 1] = 1 - 2 * (x**2 + z**2)
    R[:, 1, 2] = 2 * (y*z - w*x)
    R[:, 2, 0] = 2 * (x*z - w*y)
    R[:, 2, 1] = 2 * (y*z + w*x)
    R[:, 2, 2] = 1 - 2 * (x**2 + y**2)
    return R

def evaluate_sh(coeffs, view_dirs, degree):

    # coeffs: (N, num_bases, 3)
    # view_dirs: (N, 3)
    # Returns: (N, 3)
    # Note: Full SH evaluation involves basis functions
    return coeffs[:, 0, :] # 0th degree only for simplicity
```

### Parameters with Typical Values

*   `sh_degree`: 3 (for full view-dependent color) [S9, unofficial].
*   `scale_modifier`: 1.0 [S9, unofficial].
*   `bg_color`: [1, 1, 1] (white) or [0, 0, 0] (black) [S9, unofficial].
*   `alpha_clamp_max`: 0.99 (to prevent division by zero in gradients) [S10, unofficial].
*   `tile_size`: $16 \times 16$ pixels [S11, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls

*   **Singular Covariance:** If a Gaussian is viewed edge-on, the 2D covariance can become singular. Regularization (adding a small value to the diagonal) is required [S12, unofficial].
*   **Undefined Gradients:** If the alpha channel is not used in the loss, `v_outAlpha` may be undefined. Initialize it to zeros before backward computation [S5, unofficial].
*   **Frustum Culling:** Gaussians outside the view frustum or with zero radius must be excluded from gradient updates to avoid NaNs [S9, unofficial].
*   **Memory Constraints:** Iterating over all Gaussians in a Python loop is slow and memory-intensive. Production implementations use CUDA kernels [S10, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Gaussian Splat Rasterization (Vertex Shader)](3253-gaussian-splat-rasterization-vertex-shader.md)
This method implements the vertex shader stage of a GPU-based rasterizer for 3D Gaussian Splatting, transforming 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D screen-space ellipses for instanced rendering. It differs from fragment-based or compute-shader approaches by performing all geometric projection, covariance transformation, and ellipse basis computation within the vertex shader, passing only the final vertex positions and attributes to the rasterizer. It builds on [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) by handling the specific data unpacking and frustum culling required for efficient vertex-driven splatting.

### [Opacity Rendering with SH Colors](7480-opacity-rendering-with-sh-colors.md)
This method performs a rendering pass that uses the model's actual opacity values and precomputes colors from Spherical Harmonics (SH) in Python before rasterization, allowing the rasterizer to use precomputed colors. It differs from rasterizers with built-in SH evaluation by deferring directional color computation to the Python host layer rather than evaluating SH inside the GPU kernel. The source code initializes the color tensor to ones, indicating that actual SH evaluation is expected to be performed externally or as a simplification in the provided snippet.

### [3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT)](9154-3d-gaussian-unscented-transform-3dgut.md)
3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT) is a rasterization-based rendering technique for 3D Gaussian particles that replaces the standard Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) splatting formulation with the Unscented Transform (UT). It approximates 3D Gaussian particles using a set of sigma points to enable exact projection under arbitrary nonlinear camera projections, such as those with distortion or rolling shutter effects, while retaining the efficiency of rasterization. Unlike its sibling [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md), which relies on linearization via Jacobians, 3DGUT avoids Jacobian derivation and approximation errors by approximating the particle itself. This alignment with ray-tracing formulations also supports secondary rays for phenomena like reflections and refraction.

### [RoofGS Quadratic Form Expansion and Coefficient Folding](10258-roofgs-quadratic-form-expansion-and-coefficient-folding.md)
RoofGS Quadratic Form Expansion and Coefficient Folding is a rasterization optimization that expands the Gaussian quadratic form to separate pixel-dependent terms from Gaussian-dependent terms, allowing pixel coordinates to be hoisted out of the primitive loop and reducing instruction overhead in the innermost rendering loop. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization by pre-computing and folding opacity and scaling factors into the quadratic coefficients, enabling a streamlined FMA (Fused Multiply-Add) sequence for exponent evaluation.

### [Transmittance of Gaussian Splatting](10263-transmittance-of-gaussian-splatting.md)
This method establishes that the image formation model of Gaussian Splatting (alpha-blending) is equivalent to a ray-marching volumetric rendering model under assumptions of statistical independence and non-overlapping Gaussian primitives. It defines a continuous transmittance function and a view-dependent attenuation coefficient that yield the same color output as the standard discrete alpha-compositing rasterizer.

### [Rendering Pipeline and Configuration](11406-rendering-pipeline-and-configuration.md)
The Rendering Pipeline and Configuration family manages the data preparation, validation, and orchestration required to execute the differentiable 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization kernel. It solves the problem of translating high-level scene representations (Gaussian parameters, camera matrices) into the specific tensor layouts and validation states required by the underlying CUDA backend. It sets itself apart from the rasterization kernel itself by handling the "plumbing": normalizing inputs, enforcing mutual exclusivity between parametric and precomputed representations, and configuring the `GaussianRasterizationSettings` that bridge the scene and the kernel [Parent].

### [Differentiable Backpropagation Mechanisms](11407-differentiable-backpropagation-mechanisms.md)
This family defines the mathematical and algorithmic mechanisms for computing gradients of the rendering loss with respect to 3D Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) during the training of Gaussian Splatting models [G]. It solves the problem of enabling end-to-end optimization of scene representations by providing differentiable paths from the final pixel color to the underlying 3D attributes [G]. It differs from standard rasterization by explicitly deriving chain-rule gradients for alpha compositing and basis function evaluations, and from non-differentiable methods by supporting arbitrary gradient estimation strategies including per-splat parallelization and stochastic estimation [G].

### [Multi-view Mini-batch Rasterization](13386-multi-view-mini-batch-rasterization.md)
Multi-view mini-batch rasterization is a training strategy that renders multiple viewpoints simultaneously within a single mini-batch to reduce gradient variance and improve convergence speed. It modifies the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) rasterizer pipeline by introducing view-specific frustum culling and a composite sorting ID, allowing the rasterizer to process multiple camera views in parallel while significantly reducing memory consumption compared to naive multi-view rendering. Unlike single-view training, this method increases performance metrics (PSNR/SSIM) but introduces higher memory overhead and preprocessing time.

### [Rasterization Variant](17388-rasterization-variant.md)
This family comprises variants of the differentiable rasterization process for 3D Gaussian Splatting that modify the standard alpha-compositing accumulation, sigma estimation, or physical attenuation models to address specific rendering artifacts, physical accuracy, or auxiliary output requirements. It differs from the parent [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) by replacing or augmenting the standard screen-space 2D Gaussian projection and alpha blending with specialized intensity summation, hybrid sigma fallbacks, or analytic optical thickness calculations.

### Gaussian Rasterization Kernel Fusion
Skip the concatenation of the two SH coefficient buffers (view-independent and view-dependent bands) and pass these buffers separately to the rasterization backend, fusing the concatenation into the rasterization kernel [S13, unofficial]. Fuse the activation functions for the scales, rotations, and opacities of the Gaussians into the rasterizer to avoid PyTorch-related overhead [S13, unofficial]. Fuse the gradient computations required for the added benefits during training [S13, unofficial].

### Isotropic Loss
A regularisation term applied during RGB-D optimization to prevent Gaussians from over-stretching along the viewing axis, particularly in textureless regions [S14, unofficial]. Standard rasterisation does not account for elongation along the viewing axis, causing Gaussians to over-stretch without isotropic regularisation [S14, unofficial]. On TUM RGB-D, ATE RMSE changes from 1.43 cm (w/o $E_{iso}$) to 1.47 cm (Ours). On Replica RGB-D, Ours achieves 2.25 cm average ATE RMSE [S14, unofficial]. On TUM RGB-D, isotropic regularisation yields only marginal performance difference. On Replica RGB-D, it significantly improves camera tracking [S14, unofficial].

### Gaussian Normal Estimation
Deriving per-pixel normal estimates directly from the geometry of Gaussians during backpropagation, where adjustments to scale and rotation parameters update normal estimates without additional learnable parameters [S3]. Normals are transformed into camera space using the current camera transform and alpha-composited according to the rendering equation to provide a single per-pixel normal estimate: $\hat{\boldsymbol{N}} = \sum_{i \in N} \hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_i \alpha_i T_i$ [S3]. Incorrect normal orientation is handled by flipping the direction of normals at the beginning of training if the dot product between the current camera viewing direction and the Gaussian normal is negative [S3].

### Normal Prediction from Gaussians
Approximating the normal direction of a Gaussian using the axis with the minimum scaling coefficient, assuming Gaussians become flat, disc-like during optimization [S3]. Geometric normal for Gaussian $i$: $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_i = R \cdot \text{OneHot}(\arg\min(s_1, s_2, s_3))$, where $R \in SO(3)$ is the rotation matrix from quaternion $q$, and $s \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are scaling coefficients [S3]. Rotation matrix $R$ from quaternion $q=(w,x,y,z)^\top$: $R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 2(y^2+z^2) & 2(xy-wz) & 2(xz+wy) \\ 2(xy+wz) & 1 - 2(x^2+z^2) & 2(yz-wx) \\ 2(xz-wy) & 2(yz+wx) & 1 - 2(x^2+y^2) \end{bmatrix}$ [S3].

### Parallel N-Dimensional Gaussian Rasterization
A point-based alpha-blending technique for rasterizing feature maps alongside RGB, maintaining matching sizes for both to mitigate inconsistent spatial resolution inherent in tile-based rasterization. Dimensions are flexible to adapt to various foundational models [S15]. Rasterize features using ParallelRasterizer with inputs X, C, Σ, A, F, V to produce rendered feature map Fs [S15]. If refinement step, iterate over Gaussians (x, q, c, α, f): if opacity α < ε or Gaussian is too large, remove it [S15]. Learning rate for feature rendering optimization using Adam optimizer [S15]. Learning rate for the Speed-up Module feature decoder network using Adam optimizer [S15]. Image resolution strategy: render at 4 times lower resolution and upsample twice after 250 and 500 iterations [S15]. Standard exponential decay scheduling used similar to reference [11] [S15].

### Simplified Gaussian Rasterization
Rasterizes Gaussians to compute accumulated weights and max counts without full color/SH evaluation [S16, unofficial]. Pack args (omits `flag_max_count`, includes `culling`) [S16, unofficial]. Call `_C.rasterize_gaussians_simp(*args)` [S16, unofficial]. Return `color, radii, accum_weights_ptr, accum_weights_count, accum_max_count` [S16, unofficial].

### Depth-aware Gaussian Rasterization
Rasterizes Gaussians while computing depth, discriminants, and group indices for depth-based rendering [S16, unofficial]. Pack args (omits `flag_max_count`, includes `culling`) [S16, unofficial]. Call `_C.rasterize_gaussians_depth(*args)` [S16, unofficial]. Return dict with keys: render, out_pts, rendered_depth, discriminants, gidx, accum_alpha [S16, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot T)$ where $N$ is the number of Gaussians and $T$ is the number of tiles, but effectively linear in practice due to culling and sparse overlap [S12, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N + H \cdot W)$ for storing Gaussian attributes and tile buffers [S12, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Real-time rendering (30+ FPS) is achievable on modern GPUs with optimized CUDA kernels [S12, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High rendering speed, differentiability, explicit scene representation [S2][S12, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Memory usage scales with the number of Gaussians; requires careful densification control [S12, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** Real-time novel view synthesis, training of 3D Gaussian scenes [S2].
*   **When Not to Use:** Scenes with extremely high geometric complexity requiring implicit representations, or when hardware lacks CUDA support [S10, unofficial].

## Background

The 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer was introduced in the seminal paper "3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering" by Kerbl et al. (2023) [S12, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of splatting from computer graphics and adapts it for differentiable rendering of 3D Gaussians [S12, unofficial]. The method has become a standard for real-time neural rendering, enabling fast training and inference of 3D scenes from images [S2].
