# Scene Representation and Geometry Constraints


## Overview
This family defines the fundamental data structures, geometric constraints, and physical modeling extensions for 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [15421-scene-representation-and-geometry-constraints.md]. It solves the problem of representing complex scenes with learnable primitives while ensuring geometric consistency, physical accuracy, and temporal coherence. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Sorting and Rendering Pipeline Optimization](12180-sorting-and-rendering-pipeline-optimization.md) by focusing on the semantic and physical definition of the primitives rather than their computational rendering order, and from [Data Compression and Storage Optimization](12943-data-compression-and-storage-optimization.md) by prioritizing representation fidelity over storage efficiency.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family operate on a set of $N$ 3D Gaussian primitives. Each primitive $i$ is defined by learnable parameters describing its geometry, appearance, and position.

**Data Structure:**
Each Gaussian $g_i$ is represented by the following attributes [S1]:
*   **Position ($\mu_i$):** A 3D vector $\mathbb{R}^3$ representing the centroid in world space.
*   **Rotation ($q_i$):** A unit quaternion $\mathbb{R}^4$ representing orientation.
*   **Scale ($s_i$):** A 3D vector $\mathbb{R}^3$ representing anisotropic scaling factors along principal axes.
*   **Opacity ($\omega_i$):** A scalar $\mathbb{R} \in [0, 1]$ representing maximum opacity.
*   **Color ($c_i$):** Represented by Spherical Harmonic (SH) coefficients (typically degree 3, 48 coefficients for RGB) for view-dependent appearance.

**Covariance Matrix:**
The spatial spread is defined by a 3D covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$, constructed from scale and rotation:
$$ \Sigma_i = R_i S_i S_i^T R_i^T $$
where $S_i = \text{diag}(s_i)$ is the scaling matrix and $R_i$ is the rotation matrix derived from $q_i$ [S1].

**Projection to 2D:**
During rendering, the 3D covariance $\Sigma_i$ is projected to the 2D image plane using the Jacobian $J$ of the affine approximation of the projective transformation:
$$ \Sigma' = J \Sigma_i J^T $$
The relevant part for rasterization is the top-left $2 \times 2$ submatrix $\Sigma'_{2 \times 2}$ [S1].

**Spatial Response (Kernel Function):**
The contribution of a Gaussian to a pixel at screen coordinates $x$ is determined by:
$$ R(x) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu')^T (\Sigma'_{2 \times 2})^{-1} (x - \mu')\right) $$
where $\mu'$ is the projected 2D mean [S1].

**Alpha Compositing:**
The final opacity $\alpha$ contributed by a Gaussian is:
$$ \alpha = \omega_i \cdot R(x) $$
The pixel color is computed via alpha blending with primitives sorted back-to-front:
$$ C_{pixel} = \sum_i T_i \alpha_i C_i, \quad T_i = \prod_{j < i} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
where $C_i$ is evaluated using SH coefficients based on view direction [S1].

**Truncation:**
To optimize speed, fragments are discarded if $\alpha < \tau_\alpha$ (typically $1/255$), limiting rendering to a region of approximately $3.33\sigma$ [S1].

**Density Estimation for Mesh Extraction:**
The scene density $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ is the superposition of Gaussian contributions:
$$ \sigma(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}_i)^T \Sigma_i^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}_i)\right) $$
where $\alpha_i$ is opacity, $\mathbf{p}_i$ is the mean, and $\Sigma_i$ is the covariance [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Gaussian Representation and Truncation](12855-gaussian-representation-and-truncation.md)
Defines the fundamental data structure and rendering primitives. It specifies how a scene is represented as a set of $N$ learnable 3D Gaussians, detailing the parameters (position, rotation, scale, opacity, color), the construction of the covariance matrix, and the projection to 2D for rasterization. It also defines the standard truncation logic used to optimize rendering speed by discarding fragments with negligible contribution.

### [Mesh Extraction and Representation](14095-mesh-extraction-and-representation.md)
Converts the volumetric, implicit representation of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) into explicit geometric structures, primarily triangular meshes or point clouds. Unlike standard 3DGS, which renders via alpha-composited splats, these methods extract a discrete surface or point set that approximates the scene geometry. [SuGaR Mesh Extraction](12580-sugar-mesh-extraction.md) achieves this by regularizing Gaussians to align with the surface during training, while [3D Gaussian Splatting to Point Cloud](14068-3d-gaussian-splatting-to-point-cloud.md) samples points from the Gaussian distribution post-training.

### [Appearance and Physical Modeling](15766-appearance-and-physical-modeling.md)
Extends the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation to handle complex lighting, material properties, and non-vacuum media. It solves the limitations of base 3DGS, where view-dependent color is stored as fixed Spherical Harmonics (SH) coefficients, by introducing dynamic mechanisms to compute or modulate outgoing radiance and opacity. This family sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Gaussian Representation and Truncation](12855-gaussian-representation-and-truncation.md) by focusing on appearance synthesis and material-light interaction rather than primitive management, and from [Physics and Optical Modeling](15148-physics-and-optical-modeling.md) by distinguishing between appearance synthesis in clear space and volumetric light transport in participating media.

### [Dynamic Scene Representation](15830-dynamic-scene-representation.md)
Extends static 3D Gaussian Splatting to handle temporal variations, enabling the rendering of novel views from video sequences or time-varying scenes [14584-explicit-4d-gaussian-splatting.md]. Unlike static representations, these methods model the evolution of Gaussian attributes (position, rotation, scale) over time to capture rigid and non-rigid motion [15458-gaussian-deformation-field-gaufre.md]. They are distinguished from static siblings by their ability to interpolate or deform primitives across a temporal domain, solving the problem of rendering coherent video content from sparse or dense input views [14584-explicit-4d-gaussian-splatting.md].

### Model-based depth estimation for volume parameter update
**step**
- [S1] During optimization, use Eq. (6) to extract depth values $z$ from the current scene estimate. These depth values are then used to update the medium-parameter estimates ($B_\infty, B_b$).
**formula**
- [S1] Depth extraction from 3DGS: $z = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^M d_i \alpha_i T_i}{\sum_{i=1}^M \alpha_i T_i}$. Here $d_i$ is the depth of Gaussian $i$. This is computed at each pixel during rendering.

### Scale Constrained by Mesh Geometry
**definition**
- [S2] A constraint to prevent discrepancies between Gaussian Splat scales and the scales of corresponding triangular mesh faces by bounding the relative scale coefficients.
**formula**
- [S2] $s = s_{rel} \odot s_0$, where $s$ is the final scale, $s_{rel} = (s_{rel,1}, s_{rel,2}) \in [s_{min}, s_{max}] \times [s_{min}, s_{max}]$ is the coefficient vector, $s_0$ is the predefined scale derived from the mesh faces, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.
**parameter**
- [S2] $s_{min}, s_{max}$: The lower and upper bounds for the relative scale coefficients $s_{rel}$.

### Normal Estimation via Back-propagation
**definition**
- [S3] A method to solve for the normal attribute $n$ for each 3D Gaussian by optimizing it from an initial random vector via back-propagation, perceiving the depth of all Gaussians along a ray as a distribution.
**step**
- [S3] Determine the normal for each pixel by estimating the expectation of the depth distribution along the ray.
**formula**
- [S3] Normal consistency loss: $\mathcal {L}_{n}=\|\mathcal {N}-\tilde {\mathcal {N}}\|_2$.

## Background
The foundational representation of 3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 [G]. Subsequent work has extended this representation to handle mesh constraints [S2], normal estimation [S3], and volumetric rendering in participating media [S1]. These methods address the limitations of the original static, vacuum-based representation by introducing geometric regularization, physical modeling, and temporal dynamics.
