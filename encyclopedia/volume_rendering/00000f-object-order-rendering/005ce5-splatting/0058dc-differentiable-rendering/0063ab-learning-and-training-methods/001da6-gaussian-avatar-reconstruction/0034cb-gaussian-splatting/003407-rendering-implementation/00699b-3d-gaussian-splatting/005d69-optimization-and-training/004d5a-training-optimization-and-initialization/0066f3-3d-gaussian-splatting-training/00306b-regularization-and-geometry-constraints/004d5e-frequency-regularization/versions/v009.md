# Frequency Regularization

Also called: Amplitude and Phase Discrepancy Regularization, Improved Optimization via Regularization

## Overview
Frequency Regularization (FR) is a training-time constraint for 3D Gaussian Splatting that minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of rendered images and ground truth images to alleviate over-reconstruction and improve Gaussian densification [S2]. Unlike naive pixel-wise L1 loss, which results in small gradients in over-reconstructed regions and prevents effective densification, FR explicitly disentangles frequency components to increase pixel gradients in these regions, enabling adaptive densification [S2]. It builds on [Regularization and Geometry Constraints](6269-regularization-and-geometry-constraints.md) by using frequency-domain information to guide the optimization trajectory [Parent].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- **Inputs:** Rendered image $\hat{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, Ground truth image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, where $H$ and $W$ are height and width.
- **Outputs:** Frequency regularization loss terms $\mathcal{L}_{FR}$ (amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and phase discrepancy $d_p$).

**Data Structures:**
- Complex frequency values $F(u, v)$ and $\hat{F}(u, v)$ for each frequency coordinate $(u, v)$.
- Real and imaginary components $Re(u, v)$ and $Im(u, v)$.

**Steps:**
1. **Compute 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** Convert the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and ground truth image $I$ into their frequency representations $\hat{F}$ and $F$. For a single channel, the DFT is computed as [S2]:
   $$
   F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} I(x, y) \cdot e^{-i2\pi(\frac{u}{H}x + \frac{v}{W}y)}
   $$
   where $(x, y)$ are image coordinates, $(u, v)$ are frequency coordinates, and $I(x, y)$ is the pixel value. This is performed for all channels and combined or treated independently depending on implementation [S2].

2. **Compute Amplitude and Phase:** For each frequency component $F(u, v)$, calculate the amplitude $|F(u, v)|$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$ [S2]:
   $$
   |F(u, v)| = \sqrt{Re(u, v)^2 + Im(u, v)^2}
   $$
   $$
   \angle F(u, v) = \arctan\left(\frac{Im(u, v)}{Re(u, v)}\right)
   $$
   where $Re(u, v)$ and $Im(u, v)$ are the real and imaginary components of $F(u, v)$.

3. **Calculate Discrepancies:** Compute the total amplitude discrepancy $d_a$ and total phase discrepancy $d_p$ between the ground truth and rendered frequency representations using the Euclidean metric [S2]:
   $$
   d_a = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (|F(u, v)| - |\hat{F}(u, v)|)^2}
   $$
   $$
   d_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{HW} \sum_{x=0}^{H-1} \sum_{y=0}^{W-1} (\angle F(u, v) - \angle \hat{F}(u, v))^2}
   $$
   Note: The summation indices in the source [S2] use $x, y$ in the formula for $d_a$ and $d_p$ but refer to frequency coordinates $(u, v)$ in the text. The standard interpretation is summing over all frequency bins $(u, v)$ from $0$ to $H-1$ and $0$ to $W-1$.

4. **Combine Loss:** The total frequency regularization loss is typically a weighted sum of amplitude and phase discrepancies. The specific weighting is not detailed in the provided notes, but the components are $d_a$ and $d_p$ [S2].

**Parameters:**
- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer [S2].
- **Framework:** PyTorch [S2].
- **Rasterization:** Custom CUDA kernels from 3D-GS are used [S2].
- **Frequency Annealing:** An advanced variant uses an annealing technique with a low-pass filter $H_l$ and dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ to progressively leverage low-to-high frequencies, achieving coarse-to-fine densification [S2]. The specific formulas for $H_l$ and $H_h$ are not provided in the notes.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Over-reconstruction:** Naive L1 loss fails to differentiate over-reconstructed regions from well-reconstructed ones, leading to redundant Gaussians. FR addresses this by increasing gradients in over-reconstructed regions [S2].
- **Bias towards artifacts:** Naively adopting amplitude and phase discrepancies without distinguishing between low and high frequencies can restrict Gaussian densification and bias the splatting towards undesirable artifacts [S2]. This is mitigated by frequency annealing [S2].
- **Precision:** The arctangent function for phase calculation requires handling cases where $Re(u, v) = 0$ to avoid division by zero. Standard `atan2` implementations should be used.

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def frequency_regularization_loss(rendered_image: torch.Tensor, ground_truth_image: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Computes the frequency regularization loss between rendered and ground truth images.
    
    Args:
        rendered_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C).
        ground_truth_image: Tensor of shape (H, W, C) or (B, H, W, C).
        
    Returns:
        Scalar loss value.
    """
    # Ensure inputs are in the correct shape and device
    # Assume inputs are (H, W, C) for simplicity, batched if needed
    
    # Convert to float32 for stability
    I = ground_truth_image.float()
    I_hat = rendered_image.float()
    
    # Compute 2D DFT for each channel
    # torch.fft.fft2 expects (H, W) or (..., H, W)
    # We need to handle the channel dimension
    if I.dim() == 3:
        I_fft = torch.fft.fft2(I.permute(2, 0, 1)) # Shape (C, H, W)
        I_hat_fft = torch.fft.fft2(I_hat.permute(2, 0, 1))
    elif I.dim() == 4:
        I_fft = torch.fft.fft2(I.permute(0, 3, 1, 2)) # Shape (B, C, H, W)
        I_hat_fft = torch.fft.fft2(I_hat.permute(0, 3, 1, 2))
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unsupported tensor dimensions")
        
    # Compute amplitude and phase
    # Amplitude: sqrt(Re^2 + Im^2)
    amplitude_gt = torch.sqrt(I_fft.real**2 + I_fft.imag**2)
    amplitude_pred = torch.sqrt(I_hat_fft.real**2 + I_hat_fft.imag**2)
    
    # Phase: atan2(Im, Re)
    phase_gt = torch.atan2(I_fft.imag, I_fft.real)
    phase_pred = torch.atan2(I_hat_fft.imag, I_hat_fft.real)
    
    # Compute discrepancies
    # d_a = sqrt(1/(HW) * sum((|F| - |F_hat|)^2))
    # d_p = sqrt(1/(HW) * sum((angle(F) - angle(F_hat))^2))
    
    # Note: The source [S2] sums over x, y in the formula for d_a and d_p, 
    # but refers to frequency coordinates. We sum over H and W dimensions.
    # If batched, we might want to average over batch as well, but the source 
    # does not specify. We assume per-image loss or mean over batch.
    
    # Calculate mean squared error over spatial dimensions (H, W)
    # For amplitude
    mse_amp = torch.mean((amplitude_gt - amplitude_pred)**2, dim=(-2, -1)) # Mean over H, W
    d_a = torch.sqrt(mse_amp)
    
    # For phase
    mse_phase = torch.mean((phase_gt - phase_pred)**2, dim=(-2, -1)) # Mean over H, W
    d_p = torch.sqrt(mse_phase)
    
    # Combine losses
    # The source does not specify weights. We assume equal weights or a hyperparameter.
    # For this implementation, we return the sum.
    loss = d_a + d_p
    
    # If batched, return mean over batch
    if loss.dim() > 0:
        loss = torch.mean(loss)
        
    return loss
```

## Subtopics

### [Scale Regularization](9855-scale-regularization.md)
Scale regularization is a training-time constraint used in 3D Gaussian Splatting to prevent the formation of excessively large Gaussians, which can degrade rendering quality, cause aliasing artifacts, and destabilize optimization. It specifically addresses issues arising from unconstrained Gaussian scale growth by penalizing scales that exceed a predefined maximum or geometric resolution threshold. Unlike general isotropic regularization which merely encourages equal scaling in all directions, this method ties the scale limit directly to the geometric resolution of the underlying mesh or the maximum sampling frequency of the scene.

### DWTGS
DWTGS is a method that rethinks frequency regularization for sparse-view 3DGS by applying a discrete wavelet transform on the rendered image and penalizing the loss in the wavelet domain to encourage sharper edges [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The addition of 2D DFT and inverse operations (if used for annealing) increases computational overhead per iteration compared to standard L1 loss. The complexity is dominated by the FFT, which is $O(HW \log(HW))$ per image.
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing frequency representations for rendered and ground truth images, adding $O(HW)$ memory overhead per channel.
- **Measured Performance:** In the "Bicycle" scene, FR significantly increased pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions compared to the method without FR (equivalent to 3D-GS with L1 loss) [S2]. This led to superior Gaussian densification [S2].
- **Scaling:** The method is compatible with the standard 3D-GS training pipeline, using Adam optimizer and PyTorch framework [S2]. It can be combined with progressive frequency regularization (annealing) to manage the trade-off between memory and performance [S2].
- **Strengths:** Effectively alleviates over-reconstruction, improves Gaussian densification by revealing over-reconstructed regions, and handles frequency components (amplitude and phase) separately to capture different image information [S2].
- **Limitations:** Naive application without frequency annealing can restrict Gaussian densification and bias the splatting towards undesirable artifacts [S2]. It requires careful tuning of the annealing schedule if used.
- **When to use:** When over-reconstruction is a significant issue, and standard L1 loss fails to trigger densification in those regions. It is particularly useful for scenes with complex high-frequency details.
- **When not to use:** When computational resources are extremely limited, or when the scene is simple and over-reconstruction is not a concern.

## Background
Frequency Regularization was introduced as a method to improve 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization by addressing convergence speed, visual artifacts, and improper Gaussians [S1]. It was proposed in the context of FreGS, which aims to boost 3D Gaussian splatting from a frequency perspective [S2]. The method was developed to overcome the limitations of pixel-wise L1 loss, which cannot differentiate between over-reconstructed and well-reconstructed regions [S2]. It is part of a broader set of techniques including geometry regularization and optimization procedure enhancements [S1].
