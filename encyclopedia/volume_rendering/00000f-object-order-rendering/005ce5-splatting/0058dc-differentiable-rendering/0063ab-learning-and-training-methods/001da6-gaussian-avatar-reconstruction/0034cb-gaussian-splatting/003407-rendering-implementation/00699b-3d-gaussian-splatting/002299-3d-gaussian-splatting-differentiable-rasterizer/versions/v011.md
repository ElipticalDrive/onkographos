# 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer

Also called: 3DGS Renderer, 3DGS rasterization pipeline, Differentiable Gaussian Rasterization, differentiable splatting

## Overview
The 3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer is a screen-space rendering method that projects a set of 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D ellipses and accumulates their color and opacity via alpha compositing [S2, S3, S6]. It solves the problem of real-time, high-fidelity novel view synthesis by replacing volumetric ray marching with a differentiable rasterization pipeline, allowing gradients to flow from the rendered image back to Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) and camera poses [S8, S9, unofficial]. It differs from its siblings, such as [2D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization](4815-2d-gaussian-splatting-rasterization.md) which uses planar primitives, and [Ghost Ellipsoids for Chunked Training](3559-ghost-ellipsoids-for-chunked-training.md) which optimizes memory usage during training, by relying on full 3D covariance projection and tile-based depth sorting to handle arbitrary 3D geometry and occlusion [S3][S9, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:**
    *   `means3D`: 3D positions, shape $(N, 3)$ [S9, unofficial].
    *   `quats`: Rotation quaternions, shape $(N, 4)$ [S9, unofficial].
    *   `scales`: Anisotropic scaling factors, shape $(N, 3)$ [S9, unofficial].
    *   `opacities`: Scalar opacity values, shape $(N,)$ [S9, unofficial].
    *   `colors`: View-dependent colors, typically represented as Spherical Harmonics (SH) coefficients or precomputed RGB tensors, shape $(N, 3)$ or $(N, K, 3)$ [S5, S7, unofficial].
    *   `viewmatrix`: 4x4 camera view matrix [S5, S9, unofficial].
    *   `projmatrix` or `K`: Camera projection/intrinsic matrix [S5, S9, unofficial].
    *   `image_width`, `image_height`: Output resolution [S9, unofficial].
    *   `settings`: Configuration including `tanfovx`, `tanfovy`, `cx`, `cy`, `bg` (background color), `sh_degree`, `scale_modifier`, `prefiltered`, `backward_geometry` [S5, S7, unofficial].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   `rendered_image`: RGB image, shape $(H, W, 3)$ [S5, S7, unofficial].
    *   `rendered_opacity`: Alpha mask, shape $(H, W)$ [S5, unofficial].
    *   `radii`: Number of pixels covered by each Gaussian, shape $(N,)$ [S5, unofficial].
    *   Gradients for all input parameters [S4, S9, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
The rasterizer typically operates on a tiled grid. Gaussians are binned into 16x16 tiles based on their axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABB) in screen space [S9, unofficial]. Each tile maintains a sorted list of Gaussian indices ordered by depth (z-coordinate in camera space) [S3][S9, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Projection:**
    Transform 3D Gaussian centers $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ to camera space. Project them to 2D screen coordinates $\boldsymbol{\mu}'_i$ using the camera intrinsics. The 2D mean in pixel coordinates is computed as [S8, S9, unofficial]:
    $$
    \boldsymbol{\mu}'_i = \begin{bmatrix} f_x \frac{x_c}{z_c} + c_x \\ f_y \frac{y_c}{z_c} + c_y \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    where $(x_c, y_c, z_c)$ are the camera-space coordinates, and $f_x, f_y, c_x, c_y$ are intrinsic parameters.

2.  **Covariance Transformation:**
    Compute the 3D covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ from scale $\mathbf{s}_i$ and rotation $\mathbf{q}_i$ [S3]. Transform $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ to camera space $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_i$ using the rotation matrix $R$ derived from $\mathbf{q}_i$:
    $$
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_i = R \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i R^\top
    $$
    Approximate the projection to 2D image space using the Jacobian $J$ of the perspective projection at the Gaussian center [S9, unofficial]:
    $$
    J = \frac{1}{z_c} \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & -f_x \frac{x_c}{z_c} \\ 0 & f_y & -f_y \frac{y_c}{z_c} \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    The 2D covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i$ is:
    $$
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i = J \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_i J^\top
    $$
    Apply a scale modifier if specified [S7, unofficial].

3.  **Bounding Box and Binning:**
    Compute the AABB for each Gaussian in screen space, typically extending 3 standard deviations from the mean along the eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i$ [S9, unofficial]. Bin Gaussians into 16x16 tiles if their AABB intersects the tile [S9, unofficial].

4.  **Sorting:**
    Sort Gaussians within each tile by their camera-space depth $z_c$ in descending order (back-to-front) or ascending (front-to-back) depending on the compositing implementation. The standard approach sorts front-to-back for alpha compositing [S3][S8, S9, unofficial].

5.  **Rasterization (Alpha Compositing):**
    For each pixel, iterate through the sorted Gaussians that cover it. Compute the 2D displacement $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu}'_i$ where $\mathbf{p}$ is the pixel center [S8, unofficial]. Calculate the exponent:
    $$
    \text{power} = -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{d}^\top (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i)^{-1} \mathbf{d}
    $$
    Compute the blending coefficient (alpha) [S3][S9, unofficial]:
    $$
    \alpha_i = o_i \cdot \exp(\text{power})
    $$
    Clamp $\alpha_i$ to a maximum value (e.g., 0.99) to prevent numerical instability [S8, unofficial].
    Update the accumulated transmittance $T$ (initially 1.0) and color $\hat{C}$ (initially background color) [S3][S8, unofficial]:
    $$
    \hat{C} = \hat{C} + \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i T
    $$
    $$
    T = T (1 - \alpha_i)
    $$
    Stop if $T$ falls below a threshold or all covering Gaussians are processed [S8, unofficial].

6.  **Backward Pass:**
    Compute gradients of the loss with respect to the output image. Propagate gradients back to $\alpha_i$ and $T$. Derive gradients for $\boldsymbol{\mu}'_i$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i$, $o_i$, and $\mathbf{c}_i$. Chain-rule gradients flow through the projection Jacobian $J$ to 3D positions, scales, and rotations [S4, S9, unofficial]. The analytic gradients for position, scale, rotation, and opacity are computed explicitly to ensure efficiency and correctness [S10, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import torch
import math

def rasterize_gaussians(means3D, quats, scales, opacities, colors, viewmatrix, K, width, height, bg_color, scale_modifier=1.0):
    """
    Differentiable rasterization of 3D Gaussians.
    """
    # 1. Transform to camera space
    # Apply viewmatrix to means3D
    means_cam = apply_view_transform(means3D, viewmatrix)
    
    # 2. Project to 2D and compute covariance
    # Extract intrinsics from K
    fx, fy, cx, cy = K[0, 0], K[1, 1], K[0, 2], K[1, 2]
    
    # Compute 2D means
    x_cam, y_cam, z_cam = means_cam[:, 0], means_cam[:, 1], means_cam[:, 2]
    means2D_x = (fx * x_cam / z_cam) + cx
    means2D_y = (fy * y_cam / z_cam) + cy
    means2D = torch.stack([means2D_x, means2D_y], dim=1)
    
    # Compute 3D covariance from scale and rotation
    cov3D = compute_covariance(scales, quats)
    
    # Transform covariance to camera space
    R_cam = compute_rotation_matrix_from_quat(quats)
    cov3D_cam = torch.bmm(torch.bmm(R_cam, cov3D), R_cam.transpose(1, 2))
    
    # Compute Jacobian J for projection
    # J is 2x3 for each Gaussian
    J = torch.zeros(len(means3D), 2, 3)
    J[:, 0, 0] = fx / z_cam
    J[:, 0, 2] = -fx * x_cam / (z_cam ** 2)
    J[:, 1, 1] = fy / z_cam
    J[:, 1, 2] = -fy * y_cam / (z_cam ** 2)
    
    # Project covariance to 2D
    # Sigma_2D = J @ Sigma_cam @ J.T
    cov2D = torch.bmm(torch.bmm(J, cov3D_cam), J.transpose(1, 2))
    
    # Apply scale modifier
    cov2D *= scale_modifier
    
    # 3. Compute bounding boxes and binning (simplified for pseudocode)
    # In practice, this involves tile-based sorting
    # Here we assume a sorted list of Gaussians per pixel is available
    # For simplicity, we iterate all Gaussians and update pixels (slow, for illustration)
    
    out_img = bg_color.repeat(height, width, 1)
    T = torch.ones(height, width, 1)
    
    # Sort Gaussians by depth (front to back)
    sorted_indices = torch.argsort(z_cam, descending=False)
    
    for i in sorted_indices:
        mu = means2D[i]
        cov = cov2D[i]
        alpha = opacities[i]
        color = colors[i]
        
        # Compute inverse covariance
        try:
            inv_cov = torch.inverse(cov)
        except:
            continue
            
        # Determine pixel range covered by Gaussian (3 sigma)
        # Simplified: iterate all pixels (inefficient, but correct logic)
        # In implementation, this is done via tile-based loops
        for y in range(height):
            for x in range(width):
                p = torch.tensor([x, y], dtype=torch.float32)
                d = p - mu
                exponent = -0.5 * torch.dot(d, torch.matmul(inv_cov, d))
                
                # Clamp alpha
                a = torch.clamp(alpha * torch.exp(exponent), max=0.99)
                
                # Composite
                out_img[y, x] = out_img[y, x] * T[y, x] + color * a
                T[y, x] = T[y, x] * (1 - a)
                
                if T[y, x] < 1e-6:
                    break # Optimization: stop if transmittance is negligible
                    
    return out_img

def compute_covariance(scales, quats):
    """Compute 3x3 covariance matrix from scale and quaternion."""
    # Scales are log-space in some implementations, assume linear here
    S = torch.zeros(len(scales), 3, 3)
    for i in range(len(scales)):
        S[i] = torch.diag(scales[i])
        
    R = compute_rotation_matrix_from_quat(quats)
    # Sigma = R * S * S^T * R^T
    cov = torch.bmm(torch.bmm(R, torch.bmm(S, S.transpose(1, 2))), R.transpose(1, 2))
    return cov

def compute_rotation_matrix_from_quat(quats):
    """Convert quaternion (w, x, y, z) to rotation matrix."""
    w, x, y, z = quats[:, 0], quats[:, 1], quats[:, 2], quats[:, 3]
    R = torch.zeros(len(quats), 3, 3)
    R[:, 0, 0] = 1 - 2 * (y**2 + z**2)
    R[:, 0, 1] = 2 * (x*y - w*z)
    R[:, 0, 2] = 2 * (x*z + w*y)
    R[:, 1, 0] = 2 * (x*y + w*z)
    R[:, 1, 1] = 1 - 2 * (x**2 + z**2)
    R[:, 1, 2] = 2 * (y*z - w*x)
    R[:, 2, 0] = 2 * (x*z - w*y)
    R[:, 2, 1] = 2 * (y*z + w*x)
    R[:, 2, 2] = 1 - 2 * (x**2 + y**2)
    return R

def apply_view_transform(means3D, viewmatrix):
    """Apply 4x4 view matrix to 3D points."""
    # Homogeneous coordinates
    ones = torch.ones(len(means3D), 1)
    means_homo = torch.cat([means3D, ones], dim=1)
    means_cam_homo = torch.matmul(means_homo, viewmatrix.transpose(0, 1))
    return means_cam_homo[:, :3]
```

## Subtopics

### [Gaussian Splat Rasterization (Vertex Shader)](3253-gaussian-splat-rasterization-vertex-shader.md)
This method implements the vertex shader stage of a GPU-based rasterizer for 3D Gaussian Splatting, transforming 3D Gaussian primitives into 2D screen-space ellipses for instanced rendering. It differs from fragment-based or compute-shader approaches by performing all geometric projection, covariance transformation, and ellipse basis computation within the vertex shader, passing only the final vertex positions and attributes to the rasterizer. It builds on [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) by handling the specific data unpacking and frustum culling required for efficient vertex-driven splatting.

### [Masked-Rasterization](4366-masked-rasterization.md)
Masked-Rasterization is a technique that allows probabilistically existing Gaussians to receive gradients during the blending process, even when they are masked out of the final image. It solves the problem of "stale" Gaussians in pruning pipelines by ensuring that unsampled entities can still update their existence probability based on their virtual contribution, rather than being permanently discarded due to zero alpha filtering. It differs from sibling methods like [Analytic-Splatting](3025-analytic-splatting.md) by applying masks during the blending stage rather than modifying Gaussian attributes (opacity/scale) prior to rasterization, which prevents the loss of gradient flow.

### [Differentiable Radiative Rasterization](4526-differentiable-radiative-rasterization.md)
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization (DRR) is a GPU-friendly rasterization process implemented in CUDA that renders novel X-ray projections from radiative Gaussian point clouds. It solves the problem of differentiable rendering for X-ray imaging by projecting 3D Gaussians to a 2D detector plane and accumulating radiation intensity per pixel, allowing gradients to flow back to Gaussian parameters (position, covariance, opacity, and radiation intensity). Unlike standard optical Gaussian Splatting which uses alpha compositing for color, DRR sums the radiation intensities of intersection points weighted by the Gaussian probability density, tailored for the physics of X-ray attenuation.

### [3D/2D Hybrid Gaussian Rasterization with Fallback Sigma](4812-3d-2d-hybrid-gaussian-rasterization-with-fallback-sigma.md)
This method renders 3D Gaussians by combining a screen-space 2D Gaussian approximation with a ray-surfel 3D sigma calculation to determine the tighter bounding sigma for alpha attenuation. It solves the problem of inaccurate alpha estimation in standard splatting when Gaussians are viewed at extreme angles or large distances, ensuring robust occlusion and correct opacity accumulation. It differs from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization by explicitly computing a fallback 2D sigma based on pre-computed screen-space means and selecting the minimum of the 3D and 2D sigmas to define the effective spread.

### [Opacity Rendering with SH Colors](7480-opacity-rendering-with-sh-colors.md)
This method performs a rendering pass that uses the model's actual opacity values and precomputes colors from Spherical Harmonics (SH) in Python before rasterization, allowing the rasterizer to use precomputed colors. It differs from standard SH rendering by performing the SH-to-RGB conversion in the Python host code rather than precomputing colors for the rasterizer, allowing the rasterizer to use simpler color inputs while deferring the directional color evaluation to the CPU/GPU Python layer. The source description states that colors are precomputed from SH, but the provided code initializes the color tensor to ones, indicating a potential implementation simplification or external precomputation step.

### [3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT)](9154-3d-gaussian-unscented-transform-3dgut.md)
3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT) is a rasterization-based rendering technique for 3D Gaussian particles that replaces the standard Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) splatting formulation with the Unscented Transform (UT). It solves the problem of approximation errors and Jacobian derivation complexity inherent in linearizing non-linear camera projections (e.g., distorted cameras, rolling shutter) by approximating the 3D Gaussian particle itself using sigma points. Unlike its sibling [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) which relies on first-order Jacobian approximations, 3DGUT enables exact projection under arbitrary non-linear camera models while retaining rasterization efficiency and aligning the rendering formulation with ray-tracing methods to support secondary rays.

### [RoofGS Quadratic Form Expansion and Coefficient Folding](10258-roofgs-quadratic-form-expansion-and-coefficient-folding.md)
RoofGS Quadratic Form Expansion and Coefficient Folding is a rasterization optimization that expands the Gaussian quadratic form to separate pixel-dependent terms from Gaussian-dependent terms, allowing pixel coordinates to be hoisted out of the primitive loop and reducing instruction overhead in the innermost rendering loop. It sets itself apart from standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization by pre-computing and folding opacity and scaling factors into the quadratic coefficients, enabling a streamlined FMA (Fused Multiply-Add) sequence for exponent evaluation.

### [Transmittance of Gaussian Splatting](10263-transmittance-of-gaussian-splatting.md)
This method establishes that the image formation model of 3D Gaussian Splatting (alpha-blending) is mathematically equivalent to a ray-marching volumetric rendering model under specific assumptions. It defines a continuous transmittance function and a view-dependent attenuation coefficient that, when integrated along a ray, produce the same color output as the standard discrete alpha-compositing rasterizer. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by providing the theoretical bridge between the discrete rasterization pipeline and continuous volume rendering, enabling density-based optimizations or alternative sampling strategies that are provably consistent with the original rendering.

### [Rendering Pipeline and Configuration](11406-rendering-pipeline-and-configuration.md)
The Rendering Pipeline and Configuration family manages the data preparation, validation, and orchestration required to execute the differentiable 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization kernel. It solves the problem of translating high-level scene representations (Gaussian parameters, camera matrices) into the specific tensor layouts and validation states required by the underlying CUDA backend. It sets itself apart from the rasterization kernel itself by handling the "plumbing": normalizing inputs, enforcing mutual exclusivity between parametric and precomputed representations, and configuring the `GaussianRasterizationSettings` that bridge the scene and the kernel [Parent].

### [Differentiable Backpropagation Mechanisms](11407-differentiable-backpropagation-mechanisms.md)
This family defines the mathematical and algorithmic mechanisms for computing gradients of the rendering loss with respect to 3D Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) during the training of Gaussian Splatting models. It solves the problem of enabling end-to-end optimization of scene representations by providing differentiable paths from the final pixel color to the underlying 3D attributes. It differs from standard rasterization by explicitly deriving chain-rule gradients for alpha compositing and basis function evaluations, and from non-differentiable methods by supporting arbitrary gradient estimation strategies including per-splat parallelization and stochastic estimation.

### [Multi-view Mini-batch Rasterization](13386-multi-view-mini-batch-rasterization.md)
Multi-view mini-batch rasterization is a training strategy that renders multiple viewpoints simultaneously within a single mini-batch to reduce gradient variance and improve convergence speed. It modifies the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) rasterizer pipeline by introducing view-specific frustum culling and a composite sorting ID, allowing the rasterizer to process multiple camera views in parallel while significantly reducing memory consumption compared to naive multi-view rendering. Unlike single-view training, this method increases performance metrics (PSNR/SSIM) but introduces higher memory overhead and preprocessing time.

### [3D Gaussian Splatting Optimization](15143-3d-gaussian-splatting-optimization.md)
3D Gaussian Splatting Optimization is the iterative training process that minimizes the difference between rendered images and ground-truth observations by adjusting the parameters of a set of 3D Gaussians. It solves the problem of learning scene geometry and appearance from multi-view images by treating the Gaussian parameters as differentiable variables. Unlike sibling methods that focus on rasterization speed or specific rendering artifacts, this method focuses on the gradient computation and parameter update logic, specifically leveraging Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with the Adam optimizer to converge on a high-fidelity scene representation.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot K)$ where $N$ is the number of Gaussians and $K$ is the average number of Gaussians per pixel (typically small due to culling and sorting). The tile-based sorting and rasterization are highly parallelizable on GPUs [S3][S9, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N + H \cdot W)$ for storing Gaussian parameters and the output image/transmittance buffers. The tile-based approach reduces intermediate memory overhead compared to per-pixel lists [S9, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Real-time rendering (30+ FPS) is achievable on modern GPUs for scenes with $10^5-10^6$ Gaussians at 1080p resolution [S8, S9, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High rendering speed, differentiability for end-to-end optimization, high visual fidelity [S2][S8, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Memory intensive for very large scenes; requires careful handling of depth sorting and occlusion; performance degrades with extreme camera angles or very large Gaussians if not properly clamped [S8, S9, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Real-time novel view synthesis, training of 3D Gaussian scenes from images.
*   **When not to use:** Scenes requiring explicit ray-tracing effects (reflections, refractions) without extensions; extremely large-scale outdoor scenes where memory becomes a bottleneck without chunking [S8, S9, unofficial].

## Background
3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Kerbl et al. in 2023 [S3][S9, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of Gaussian splatting from computer graphics, adapting it for differentiable neural rendering. The method represents a scene as a set of 3D Gaussians, optimized from multi-view images. The differentiable rasterizer is a core component, enabling the optimization of Gaussian parameters via gradient descent [S3][S9, unofficial]. Prior work included volumetric rendering (NeRF) and point-based rendering, but 3DGS offered a significant speedup while maintaining quality [S6][S8, unofficial].
