# Dynamic Neural Radiance Field (Dynamic NeRF)

Also called: 4D Facial Avatar Reconstruction, Dynamic Neural Radiance Field (Dynamic NeRF) for Facial Avatars, Dynamic Radiance Field

## Overview
Dynamic Neural Radiance Field (Dynamic NeRF) reconstructs a 4D facial avatar from monocular video by representing the scene as a dynamic neural radiance field conditioned on facial expressions and head pose [S1]. It enables novel view synthesis and explicit expression editing, distinguishing itself from static NeRF variants like [Scene NeRF](12484-scene-nerf.md) by modeling temporal dynamics and from 2D reenactment methods like [FOMM](15318-dynamic-neural-radiance-fields.md) by providing 3D consistency and view-dependent effects [S1]. It builds on [Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) Representation](12096-neural-radiance-field-nerf-representation.md) by extending the static radiance field to a time-varying canonical space.

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Monocular RGB video sequence of a talking human head; a static background image $B$ captured without the subject; per-frame rigid pose matrices $P_i \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$; per-frame facial expression coefficients $\delta_i \in \mathbb{R}^{76}$ from a delta-blendshape basis.
*   **Output:** Rendered RGB images for novel views and edited expressions; foreground-background segmentation (implicit via density).

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Dynamic Radiance Field Network ($D_\theta$):** An MLP with 8 fully-connected layers, each 256 neurons wide, using ReLU activations. It takes encoded position, viewing direction, expression, and latent code as input and outputs $(RGB, \sigma)$.
2.  **Learnable Latent Codes ($\gamma_i$):** A vector $\gamma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ per training frame to compensate for estimation errors in pose and expression.
3.  **Positional Encoding:**
    *   Position $p$: 10 frequencies.
    *   Viewing direction $\tilde{v}$: 4 frequencies.

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Pose and Expression Transformation:**
    For a camera ray $r(t) = c + t\tilde{d}$, transform a 3D point $x_{cam}$ in camera space to the canonical space $p$ using the rigid pose matrix $P_i$:
    $$ p = P_i \cdot x_{cam} $$
    The viewing direction $\tilde{v}$ is transformed similarly to account for head rotation, denoted as $\tilde{v}'$ in the network input context, though the source formula uses $\tilde{v}$ directly in the MLP input $D_\theta(p, \tilde{v}, \delta, \gamma)$ [S1].

2.  **Network Forward Pass:**
    Compute density $\sigma$ and color $RGB$ for a point $p$ given expression $\delta$ and latent code $\gamma$:
    $$ D_\theta(p, \tilde{v}, \delta, \gamma) = (RGB, \sigma) $$
    where $\theta$ represents the weights of the MLP [S1].

3.  **Ray Sampling:**
    *   Sample 2048 viewing rays per training image/iteration.
    *   95% of rays correspond to pixels within the head bounding box (derived from the morphable model).
    *   **Coarse Sampling:** Stratified sampling of 64 points along each ray between $z_{near}$ and $z_{far}$.
    *   **Fine Sampling:** Re-sample 64 points based on the density distribution from the coarse network (importance sampling) [S1].

4.  **Volumetric Rendering:**
    Compute the expected color $C(r)$ for a ray $r$ by integrating along the ray:
    $$ C(r; \theta, P, \delta, \gamma) = \int_{z_{near}}^{z_{far}} \sigma_\theta(r(t)) \cdot RGB_\theta(r(t), \tilde{d}) \cdot T(t) \, dt $$
    where $T(t) = \exp\left(-\int_{z_{near}}^t \sigma_\theta(r(s)) \, ds\right)$ is the accumulated transmittance [S1].
    *Note: The source formula (2) writes $\tilde{d} \cdot T(t)$ inside the integral, but standard volume rendering defines the term as $\sigma \cdot RGB \cdot T$. The provided formula in [S1] is $C(r; \theta, P, \delta, \gamma) = \int_{z_{near}}^{z_{far}} \sigma_\theta(r(t)) \cdot RGB_\theta(r(t), \tilde{d} \cdot T(t)) dt$. This notation implies the color contribution is modulated by transmittance. We follow the standard interpretation where the integrand is $\sigma \cdot RGB \cdot T$ for correctness, as $\tilde{d} \cdot T(t)$ is not a standard vector operation for color modulation. However, strictly adhering to the source text's symbolic representation:*
    $$ C(r; \theta, P, \delta, \gamma) = \int_{z_{near}}^{z_{far}} \sigma_\theta(r(t)) \cdot RGB_\theta(r(t), \tilde{d}) \cdot T(t) \, dt $$
    *(The source [S1] Eq 2 explicitly writes: $\int_{z_{near}}^{z_{far}} \sigma_\theta(r(t)) \cdot RGB_\theta(r(t), \tilde{d} \cdot T(t)) dt$. This is likely a typesetting artifact for $\sigma \cdot RGB \cdot T$. We use the standard volume rendering equation which is mathematically consistent with the description "accumulate the sampled density and RGB values... to compute the final output color" [S1].)*

5.  **Background Decoupling:**
    *   Assume a static background image $B$.
    *   The last sample on the ray $r$ is assumed to lie on the background with color $B[r]$.
    *   The network learns to predict low density for foreground samples if the ray hits a background pixel, and high density for foreground pixels. This achieves self-supervised foreground-background decomposition [S1].

6.  **Loss Calculation:**
    Compute reconstruction loss for both coarse and fine networks:
    $$ L_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^M (L_i(\theta_{coarse}) + L_i(\theta_{fine})) $$
    $$ L_i(\theta) = \sum_{j \in pixels} ||C(r_j; \theta, P_i, \delta_i, \gamma_i) - I_i[j]||^2 $$
    where $M$ is the number of training images, $I_i[j]$ is the pixel value, and $C$ is the rendered color [S1].

7.  **Latent Code Regularization:**
    Apply L2 loss with weight decay $\lambda = 0.05$ to the learnable latent codes $\gamma_i$:
    $$ L_{reg} = \lambda \sum_{i} ||\gamma_i||^2 $$

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Eye Movements:** The morphable model does not explicitly model eye blinks or eye movements. These cannot be directly controlled but are implicitly correlated with other expression parameters [S1].
*   **Data Requirements:** Requires more training data than static NeRF (which works with ~100 images) because the dynamic field must generalize over the space of expression vectors [S1].
*   **Upper Body:** The method focuses on the human head; dynamics of the upper body are not modeled [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training requires 400k iterations with Adam optimization [S1]. Rendering involves two passes (coarse and fine) with 64 samples each, plus importance sampling overhead.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Stores two MLPs (coarse and fine), latent codes per frame, and the background image.
*   **Performance:** On monocular 1920×1080 video, achieves PSNR of 26.85, SSIM of 0.95, and LPIPS of 0.06 (full data) [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Generates 3D consistent results including view-dependent effects (e.g., glasses reflections); allows explicit control over head pose and facial expressions [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Cannot explicitly control eye blinks/movements; requires significant training data; limited to head reconstruction [S1].
*   **When to use:** When 3D-consistent novel view synthesis and expression editing of a human head from monocular video is required.
*   **When not to use:** When full-body animation or explicit eye control is needed, or when training data is extremely limited.

## Background
Dynamic NeRF was introduced by [S1] (Thies et al., CVPR 2021) for monocular 4D facial avatar reconstruction. It extends the static NeRF framework [S1] to handle dynamic scenes by conditioning the radiance field on facial expressions and pose. It is used in applications requiring controllable 3D facial avatars from video, such as virtual meetings, animation, and digital humans.
