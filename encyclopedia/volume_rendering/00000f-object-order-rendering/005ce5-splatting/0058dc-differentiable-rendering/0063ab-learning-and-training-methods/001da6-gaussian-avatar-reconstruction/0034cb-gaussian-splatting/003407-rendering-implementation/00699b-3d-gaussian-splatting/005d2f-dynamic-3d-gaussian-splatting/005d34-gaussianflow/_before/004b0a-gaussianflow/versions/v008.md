# GaussianFlow

Also called: Dual-Domain Deformation Model (DDDM), Guo et al

## Overview
GaussianFlow is a point-based differentiable rendering approach for dynamic 3D scene reconstruction that models a 4D scene as a set of deformable 3D Gaussian particles advected by an explicit 3D velocity field [S1, unofficial][S3]. It solves the problem of efficient dynamic scene reconstruction by introducing a Dual-Domain Deformation Model (DDDM) that explicitly models attribute deformations (position, rotation, radiance) in both time and frequency domains, eliminating the need for implicit neural fields or per-frame optimization [S3]. Unlike [Dynamic 3D Gaussians](5703-dynamic-3d-gaussian-splatting.md) which tracks motion via persistent view synthesis and local constraints, GaussianFlow uses a global flow-based deformation model, achieving 5× faster training than per-frame 3DGS while preserving real-time rendering speeds [S3].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A sequence of images (multi-view or monocular video) with known camera poses [S3].
*   **Outputs:** A set of 3D Gaussians $\{G_i\}$ where each Gaussian $i$ has time-varying attributes: position $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i(t)$, rotation $q_i(t)$, and radiance/color $c_i(t)$ [S3]. The method outputs a 4D representation that can be rendered at any arbitrary time $t$ [G].

**Data Structures:**
Each Gaussian $i$ stores base attributes $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $q_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$, $c_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$ and DDDM deformation parameters: polynomial coefficients $\mathbf{P}_{pos}, \mathbf{P}_{rot}, \mathbf{P}_{color}$ for time-domain fitting, and Fourier coefficients $\mathbf{F}_{pos}, \mathbf{F}_{rot}, \mathbf{F}_{color}$ for frequency-domain fitting [S3].

**Optimization Steps:**
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize Gaussians from a static 3DGS reconstruction at $t_0$ [G]. Initialize DDDM coefficients to zero [G].
2.  **Static Optimization:** Optimize static Gaussian attributes and DDDM coefficients for a subset of frames or the reference frame to establish a baseline [S2].
3.  **Dynamic Optimization Loop:**
    a.  **Forward Pass:** For each training image at time $t_j$:
        i.  Compute deformation residuals $\Delta \mathbf{A}_i(t_j)$ using DDDM [S3].
        ii. Update Gaussian attributes to $\mathbf{A}_i(t_j)$ [G].
        iii. Render the image using differentiable rasterization [S3].
    b.  **Loss Computation:**
        i.  **Image Loss:** $\mathcal{L}_{img} = \lambda_{rgb} \mathcal{L}_{rgb} + \lambda_{ssim} \mathcal{L}_{SSIM}$ between rendered and ground truth images [S2].
        ii. **Temporal Smoothness Loss:** Encourages smooth deformation over time [S2, S3].
        iii. **KNN Rigid Loss:** Regularizes motion trajectory by assuming local neighborhoods of Gaussians move rigidly [S2, S3].
        iv. **Flow Loss:** If 2D flow estimation is used, a bidirectional optical flow loss is introduced to align dynamic objects across frames [S2].
    c.  **Backward Pass:** Compute gradients w.r.t. DDDM coefficients and static attributes [G].
    d.  **Update:** Update parameters using an optimizer [G].
4.  **Iteration:** Repeat step 3 until convergence [S2].

**Formulas:**
The Dual-Domain Deformation Model (DDDM) models the time-dependent residual of each attribute using joint polynomial fitting (time domain) and Fourier series fitting (frequency domain) [S2, S3].
For a Gaussian $i$ and time $t$, the deformation residual $\Delta \mathbf{A}_i(t)$ for attribute $\mathbf{A} \in \{\boldsymbol{\mu}, q, c\}$ is:
$$ \Delta \mathbf{A}_i(t) = \mathbf{P}_i(t) + \mathbf{F}_i(t) $$
where:
*   $\mathbf{P}_i(t)$ is a polynomial function of $t$:
    $$ \mathbf{P}_i(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \mathbf{a}_{k,i} t^k $$
    Here, $\mathbf{a}_{k,i}$ are learnable polynomial coefficients for Gaussian $i$, and $K$ is the polynomial degree [S3].
*   $\mathbf{F}_i(t)$ is a Fourier series approximation:
    $$ \mathbf{F}_i(t) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \left( \mathbf{b}_{m,i} \cos(\omega_m t) + \mathbf{c}_{m,i} \sin(\omega_m t) \right) $$
    Here, $\mathbf{b}_{m,i}, \mathbf{c}_{m,i}$ are learnable Fourier coefficients, $\omega_m$ are fixed angular frequencies (e.g., $\omega_m = \frac{2\pi m}{T}$ where $T$ is the total duration), and $M$ is the number of harmonics [S2, S3].

The final attributes at time $t$ are:
$$ \boldsymbol{\mu}_i(t) = \boldsymbol{\mu}_i + \Delta \boldsymbol{\mu}_i(t) $$
$$ q_i(t) = \text{Normalize}(q_i \otimes \Delta q_i(t)) $$
$$ c_i(t) = c_i + \Delta c_i(t) $$
where $\otimes$ denotes quaternion multiplication [G].

To prevent optimization problems from uniform time division, adaptive timestep scaling is adopted [S2, S3]. The time $t$ is mapped to a scaled time $t'$ where the distribution of $t'$ accounts for the density of keyframes or motion intensity [S3].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def evaluate_ddddm(gaussian, t, poly_coeffs, fourier_coeffs, omega):
    # Polynomial part: sum(a_k * t^k)
    p = sum(c * (t ** k) for k, c in enumerate(poly_coeffs))
    # Fourier part: sum(b_m * cos(omega_m * t) + c_m * sin(omega_m * t))
    f = sum(b * cos(omega_val * t) + c * sin(omega_val * t) 
            for b, c, omega_val in zip(fourier_coeffs[0], fourier_coeffs[1], omega))
    return p + f

def optimize_gaussian_flow(images, poses, initial_gaussians, timestamps, num_iterations):
    gaussians = initial_gaussians
    # Initialize DDDM coefficients to zero [G]
    poly_coeffs = {attr: [zeros(dim) for _ in range(K)] for attr, dim in gaussians.attrs}
    fourier_coeffs = {attr: [zeros(dim), zeros(dim)] for attr, dim in gaussians.attrs}
    omega = [2 * pi * m / T for m in range(1, M + 1)]

    for iteration in range(num_iterations):
        # Alternate static and dynamic optimization [S2]
        if iteration % 2 == 0:
            optimize_static(gaussians, images, poses)
        else:
            for img, t in zip(images, timestamps):
                # Adaptive timestep scaling [S3]
                t_scaled = adaptive_scale(t)
                # Compute deformation residuals [S3]
                delta_pos = evaluate_ddddm(gaussians, t_scaled, poly_coeffs['pos'], fourier_coeffs['pos'], omega)
                delta_rot = evaluate_ddddm(gaussians, t_scaled, poly_coeffs['rot'], fourier_coeffs['rot'], omega)
                delta_col = evaluate_ddddm(gaussians, t_scaled, poly_coeffs['color'], fourier_coeffs['color'], omega)
                # Update attributes [G]
                gaussians.pos = gaussians.pos + delta_pos
                gaussians.rot = normalize(gaussians.rot * delta_rot)
                gaussians.color = gaussians.color + delta_col
                # Render and compute losses [S2, S3]
                rendered = render(gaussians, poses)
                loss = lambda_rgb * l1(rendered, img) + lambda_ssim * ssim(rendered, img)
                loss += lambda_smooth * temporal_smoothness_loss(gaussians, t)
                loss += lambda_rigid * knn_rigid_loss(gaussians)
                # Backward pass [G]
                loss.backward()
            # Update parameters [G]
            update_parameters(gaussians, poly_coeffs, fourier_coeffs)
    return gaussians
```

**Parameters:**
*   Polynomial degree $K$: Not given in the sources. [S3]
*   Fourier harmonics $M$: Not given in the sources. [S3]
*   Learning rate: Not given in the sources. [S3]
*   Loss weights $\lambda_{rgb}, \lambda_{ssim}, \lambda_{smooth}, \lambda_{rigid}$: Not given in the sources. [S3]

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Overfitting to Violent Motion:** Uniform time division can cause the model to overfit to frames with high motion. Adaptive timestep scaling mitigates this [S3].
*   **Precision:** Fourier series may require high $M$ for high-frequency motion, increasing memory. Polynomial fitting may struggle with non-smooth motion [S2].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The method assumes the scene is captured within the time range $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$ [G].

## Subtopics

### Gaussian Splatting Lucas-Kanade
A method that directly applies optical flow estimation to the rendered Gaussians, using the flow to align dynamic objects across frames and improve temporal consistency [S1, unofficial]. It contrasts with GaussianFlow, which treats each Gaussian as a dynamic particle advected by a 3D velocity field and models deformation via DDDM [S1, unofficial][S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Training is 5× faster than per-frame 3DGS modeling [S3]. Rendering speed is comparable to original 3DGS, enabling real-time performance [S3].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Higher than static 3DGS due to storage of DDDM coefficients (polynomial and Fourier) for each Gaussian [S3]. Lower than implicit neural field methods (like NeRF) which store large networks [S3].
*   **Measured Performance:** Achieves superior novel view rendering quality compared to previous leading methods on multi-view and monocular datasets (HyperNeRF, Plenoptic) [S3].
*   **Strengths:** Ultra-fast training and rendering; explicit 4D representation; supports both multi-view and monocular videos; no need for neural fields [S3].
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful tuning of DDDM parameters ($K, M$); may struggle with highly complex, non-rigid deformations if polynomial/Fourier bases are insufficient; adaptive timestep scaling adds complexity [S2, S3].
*   **When to Use:** Dynamic scene reconstruction from videos where real-time rendering and fast training are critical, and the motion is not extremely chaotic [S3].
*   **When Not to Use:** If the scene contains very high-frequency, non-smooth motion that cannot be captured by low-degree polynomials or limited Fourier harmonics, or if per-frame accuracy is more important than global consistency [S2].

## Background
GaussianFlow was introduced by Guo et al. in 2024 [S3]. It builds on the success of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for static scenes and addresses the limitations of existing dynamic methods, which either suffer from slow training/rendering (NeRF-based) or lack global consistency (per-frame 3DGS) [S3]. The method was developed to enable fast dynamic scene reconstruction and real-time rendering from both multi-view and monocular videos [S3]. It is part of the broader family of dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting methods, which aim to extend the explicit, efficient representation of 3DGS to 4D [S2].
