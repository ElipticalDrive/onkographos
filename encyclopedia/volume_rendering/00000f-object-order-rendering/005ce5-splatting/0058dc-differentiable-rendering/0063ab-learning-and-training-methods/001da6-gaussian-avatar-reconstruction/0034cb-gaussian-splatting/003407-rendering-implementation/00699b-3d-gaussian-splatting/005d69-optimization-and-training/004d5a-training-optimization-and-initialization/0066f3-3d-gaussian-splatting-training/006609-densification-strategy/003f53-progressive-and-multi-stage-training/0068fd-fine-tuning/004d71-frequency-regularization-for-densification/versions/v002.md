# Frequency Regularization for Densification

Also called: 3D Gaussian Splatting with Progressive Frequency Regularization, FreGS

## Overview
Frequency Regularization for Densification (FreGS) addresses the over-reconstruction issue in 3D Gaussian Splatting, where high-variance image regions are covered by a few large Gaussians, leading to blur and artifacts [S1]. Unlike standard 3D-GS which relies solely on pixel-wise L1 loss in the spatial domain, FreGS minimizes the discrepancy between the frequency spectrum of the rendered image and the ground truth in the Fourier space [S1]. This approach significantly increases pixel gradients within over-reconstruction regions, enabling a coarse-to-fine densification process guided by progressive frequency annealing [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs:** A sparse point cloud generated by Structure-from-Motion (SfM); ground truth images $I$ for the training views [S1].
*   **Outputs:** Optimized 3D Gaussian parameters (covariance $\Sigma$, center $p$, opacity $\alpha$, spherical harmonics coefficients $c$) with densified geometry [S1].

**Data Structures**
*   **3D Gaussian:** Defined by covariance matrix $\Sigma$, center position $p$, opacity $\alpha$, and spherical harmonics coefficients $c$ [S1]. The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is parameterized by a scaling matrix and a rotation matrix for differentiable optimization [S1].
*   **Frequency Spectrum:** Complex-valued arrays representing the amplitude $F(u, v)$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$ of the image in the Fourier domain [S1].

**Numbered Steps**
1.  **Initialization:** Initialize a set of 3D Gaussians from the sparse point cloud generated by Structure-from-Motion [S1].
2.  **Rendering:** Splat the 3D Gaussians to obtain 2D Gaussians and render the image $\hat{I}$ using standard $\alpha$-blending [S1]. The color $C$ of a pixel is computed by blending $N$ ordered 2D Gaussians overlapping the pixel:
    $$ C = \sum_{i \in N} c_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) $$
    where $c_i$ and $\alpha_i$ are derived from the $i$-th 2D Gaussian's covariance matrix, spherical harmonics coefficients, and opacity [S1].
3.  **Frequency Transformation:** Generate the frequency spectrum $\hat{F}$ of the rendered image $\hat{I}$ and the frequency spectrum $F$ of the ground truth $I$ by applying a Fourier transformation [S1].
4.  **Discrepancy Computation:** Compute the discrepancy in amplitude $F(u, v)$ and phase $\angle F(u, v)$ in the Fourier space between the rendered and ground truth spectra [S1].
5.  **Filtering:** Apply a low-pass filter $H_l$ and a dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ to extract low-to-high frequency components progressively [S1].
6.  **Densification:** Perform Gaussian densification guided by the frequency regularization [S1]:
    *   **Under-reconstruction:** Clone Gaussians in regions with missing geometric features [S1].
    *   **Over-reconstruction:** Divide large Gaussians into multiple smaller Gaussians in large high-variance regions [S1].
7.  **Frequency Annealing:** Use frequency annealing to progressively regularize from low-frequency signals to high-frequency signals, achieving coarse-to-fine Gaussian densification [S1]. Low-frequency signals encode large-scale structures (easier to model), while high-frequency signals encode local details (harder to model) [S1].
8.  **Termination:** Gaussian densification is terminated after the 15,000th iteration, consistent with the standard 3D-GS protocol [S1].

**Pseudocode**
```python
def frequency_regularization_densification(gaussians, ground_truth_images, training_iterations=15000):
    """
    Implements FreGS densification strategy.
    """
    for iteration in range(1, training_iterations + 1):
        # Step 2: Render current scene
        rendered_images = []
        for view_idx in range(len(ground_truth_images)):
            # Splat 3D Gaussians to 2D and alpha-blend
            # C = sum(c_i * product(1 - alpha_j)) for j < i
            rendered_image = render_gaussians(gaussians, view_idx)
            rendered_images.append(rendered_image)

        # Step 3 & 4: Compute Frequency Discrepancy
        total_freq_loss = 0.0
        for rendered_img, gt_img in zip(rendered_images, ground_truth_images):
            # Fourier Transform
            F_hat = np.fft.fft2(rendered_img)
            F_gt = np.fft.fft2(gt_img)
            
            # Extract Amplitude and Phase
            A_hat = np.abs(F_hat)
            Phi_hat = np.angle(F_hat)
            A_gt = np.abs(F_gt)
            Phi_gt = np.angle(F_gt)
            
            # Compute discrepancy
            # Sources state: "regularizing discrepancies of amplitude F(u,v) and phase" [S1]
            amp_loss = np.mean(np.abs(A_hat - A_gt))
            phase_loss = np.mean(np.abs(Phi_hat - Phi_gt))
            total_freq_loss += amp_loss + phase_loss

        # Step 5 & 7: Frequency Annealing and Filtering
        # Progressive regularization from low to high frequency [S1]
        # Exact schedule parameters not given in sources.
        progress = iteration / training_iterations
        cutoff = 0.1 + 0.8 * progress
        # Apply low-pass and dynamic high-pass filters
        freq_spectrum = F_hat
        low_freq = freq_spectrum * (np.abs(np.fft.fftfreq(freq_spectrum.shape[0])) > cutoff)
        high_freq = freq_spectrum * (np.abs(np.fft.fftfreq(freq_spectrum.shape[0])) <= cutoff)
        
        # Step 6: Densification
        # Identify regions for cloning (under-reconstruction) and splitting (over-reconstruction)
        # based on frequency regularization gradients [S1]
        # The sources note that frequency regularization increases pixel gradients 
        # in over-reconstruction regions significantly compared to L1 loss [S1]
        gradients = compute_gradients(rendered_images, ground_truth_images, total_freq_loss)
        densify_gaussians(gaussians, gradients)
        
        # Standard optimization step
        optimize_gaussians(gaussians, total_freq_loss)

    return gaussians
```

**Parameters**
*   **Training Iterations:** 15,000 [S1].
*   **Frequency Annealing Schedule:** Progressive from low to high frequency [S1]. Exact schedule parameters (e.g., start/end frequencies, annealing rate) are not given in the sources.
*   **Filters:** Low-pass filter $H_l$ and dynamic high-pass filter $H_h$ [S1]. Specific cutoff frequencies or kernel sizes are not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Over-reconstruction:** The primary target of FreGS is the case where high-variance regions are covered by few large Gaussians, causing blur [S1].
*   **Under-reconstruction:** Regions with missing geometric features are handled by cloning [S1].
*   **Precision:** The method relies on Fourier transforms, which may be sensitive to image boundaries and padding [G].
*   **Computational Cost:** Computing Fourier transforms and frequency discrepancies adds overhead to the training loop [G].

## Subtopics

### Exponentially Ascending Gradient Threshold
Controls densification by dynamically adjusting the gradient magnitude threshold required for cloning or splitting Gaussians [9684-exponentially-ascending-gradient-threshold.md]. Choose when gradient magnitudes vary significantly across training iterations.

### Hard Gaussian Splatting
Enforces strict geometric constraints on Gaussian placement to prevent invalid overlaps or degenerate shapes [10590-hard-gaussian-splatting.md]. Choose when physical plausibility or strict boundary adherence is required.

### SplatFormer Training Objective & Refinement
Uses a transformer-based architecture to refine the training objective and improve feature alignment [11745-splatformer-training-objective-refinement.md]. Choose when complex spatial dependencies need explicit modeling beyond standard splatting.

### MCMC-based 3DGS / SGLD for 3DGS
Applies Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling and Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics for optimization [11917-mcmc-based-3dgs-sgld-for-3dgs.md]. Choose when uncertainty quantification or sampling-based convergence is preferred over deterministic gradient descent.

### Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting
Specifically targets and removes blur artifacts in the rendered output through dedicated deblurring losses or kernels [12318-deblurring-3d-gaussian-splatting.md]. Choose when motion blur or defocus blur dominates the error metric.

### Supplementary Point Addition for Sparse Point Clouds
Injects additional points into the initialization phase to improve coverage in sparse regions [12323-supplementary-point-addition-for-sparse-point-clouds.md]. Choose when the initial SfM reconstruction is too sparse for effective densification.

### Gaussian Volume Mask
Uses volumetric masks to guide densification and prevent Gaussians from forming in empty or occluded space [12710-gaussian-volume-mask.md]. Choose when scene geometry has large empty volumes that cause noise.

### Error-Guided Gaussian Reallocation
Reallocates Gaussians based on per-pixel rendering error to concentrate resources where reconstruction fails [13012-error-guided-gaussian-reallocation.md]. Choose when error distribution is highly non-uniform across the scene.

### Augmented Radiance Field
Augments the standard radiance field representation with additional features or priors to improve reconstruction quality [13218-augmented-radiance-field.md]. Choose when the base representation lacks sufficient expressiveness for complex scenes.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Adds overhead due to Fourier transforms and frequency discrepancy calculations at each iteration [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing frequency spectra for rendered and ground truth images [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** Experiments on benchmarks like Mip-NeRF360 and Tanks-and-Temples show high-quality Gaussian densification and alleviation of over-reconstruction [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Significantly increases pixel gradients in over-reconstruction regions compared to standard L1 loss, leading to better detail recovery [S1].
*   **Limitations:** The specific implementation details of the filters and annealing schedule are not provided, making exact performance scaling difficult to predict [S1].
*   **When to Use:** When standard 3D-GS exhibits over-reconstruction artifacts (blur in high-variance regions) [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When computational resources are extremely limited, as the frequency domain operations add overhead [G].
