# Triangle Mesh Splatting
Also called: Laur and Hanrahan splatting

## Overview
Triangle Mesh Splatting is a volume rendering technique that approximates continuous splat kernels with a discrete triangle mesh to leverage standard graphics hardware for rapid scan conversion [S1, unofficial]. It distinguishes itself from circular or elliptical footprint methods by replacing analytic integration with rasterization-based accumulation, trading geometric precision for significant rendering speed improvements on GPU architectures [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A set of splat primitives, each defined by a center position, a spread parameter (kernel width), and optical properties (color, opacity).
*   **Output:** A 2D image buffer with accumulated color and opacity.

**Data Structures:**
Each splat $k$ is represented by a triangle mesh $M_k$. The mesh approximates the 2D footprint of the splat kernel.
*   **Vertices:** 3D coordinates $(x, y, z)$ in view space.
*   **Indices:** Define triangular faces.
*   **Attributes:** Per-vertex color and opacity values, interpolated across the triangle.

**Steps:**
1.  **Mesh Approximation:** For each splat, generate a triangle mesh that approximates its 2D footprint. The mesh should cover the area where the splat kernel has significant non-zero values. The specific topology (e.g., a single quad split into two triangles, or a finer polygon) depends on the desired accuracy versus performance trade-off [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Transformation:** Transform the vertices of each triangle mesh from object space to screen space using the Model-View-Projection (MVP) matrix. This maps the 3D splat to its 2D footprint on the image plane.
3.  **Rasterization (Scan Conversion):** Use graphics hardware to rasterize the transformed triangle meshes. This step effectively computes the footprint $F(x,y)$ for each pixel covered by the mesh. The hardware interpolates vertex attributes (color, opacity) across the triangle surface.
4.  **Accumulation:** During rasterization, accumulate the color and opacity of each fragment into the image buffer. This is typically done using alpha blending or a custom shader that performs front-to-back or back-to-front compositing, weighted by the splat's intrinsic density. The rasterization process inherently handles the spatial weighting of the footprint [S1, unofficial].

**Formulas:**
Let $T_k$ be the triangle mesh for splat $k$. The contribution to a pixel $p$ is determined by the rasterization of $T_k$. If pixel $p$ is covered by $T_k$, the fragment color $C_p$ and opacity $\alpha_p$ are interpolated from the triangle's vertices. The final image color $C_{final}$ is computed by compositing these fragments.

**Parameters:**
*   **Mesh Resolution:** The number of triangles used to approximate the splat footprint. Higher resolution yields better accuracy but increases rasterization cost. Not given in the sources.
*   **Splat Spread:** The radius or width of the splat kernel, determining the size of the footprint. Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Precision:** Rasterization artifacts may occur if the triangle mesh does not accurately cover the splat's significant footprint, leading to aliasing or leakage.
*   **Boundaries:** The mesh approximation must be carefully constructed to avoid gaps or overlaps between adjacent splats, which can cause visual artifacts.
*   **Degenerate Input:** If a splat is extremely small or large, the mesh approximation may need adjustment to maintain efficiency and accuracy. Not given in the sources.

## Subtopics

### [Differentiable Triangle Splatting](8761-differentiable-triangle-splatting.md)
Differentiable Triangle Splatting is a real-time differentiable renderer that represents a scene as a soup of unstructured, disconnected 3D triangles and optimizes them via end-to-end gradients by rendering each triangle as a differentiable splat in screen space. It merges the adaptability of Gaussian splatting with the efficiency of triangle primitives, surpassing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS), and 3D Convex Splatting (3DCS) in visual fidelity, training speed, and rendering throughput. Unlike Gaussian methods that require complex depth-dependent covariance adjustments, Triangle Splatting uses a single smoothness parameter $\sigma$ for all triangles, ensuring consistent spatial influence regardless of depth or projected scale.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Dominated by rasterization of the triangle meshes. Complexity is proportional to the number of triangles and the area covered on the screen.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Proportional to the number of splats and the complexity of their mesh approximations.
*   **Measured Performance:** Significantly faster than analytic integration methods due to hardware acceleration. Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** High rendering speed, leverages existing GPU rasterization pipelines, simple implementation.
*   **Limitations:** Approximation error from mesh discretization, potential for aliasing if mesh resolution is insufficient.
*   **When to Use:** Real-time volume rendering where speed is critical and moderate accuracy is acceptable.
*   **When Not to Use:** Applications requiring high geometric precision or analytic integration of complex kernels.

## Background
Triangle Mesh Splatting was introduced by Laur and Hanrahan as a method to improve rendering speed by approximating splats with a triangle mesh and using graphics hardware to quickly scan convert the footprint [S1, unofficial]. This approach builds on earlier work by Westover, who mapped view-dependent footprints to a generic footprint table, and by Crawfis and Max, who used texture mapping hardware to render splats represented as textures mapped to polygons [S1, unofficial].
