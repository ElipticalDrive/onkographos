# EWA Splatting

Also called: EWA Volume Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA splatting

## Overview
EWA Splatting is a high-quality volume rendering technique that eliminates aliasing artifacts by replacing standard footprint functions with a band-limited resampling filter [S1, unofficial][S2][S9, S16, unofficial]. It solves the problem of visual holes and high-frequency noise inherent in basic splatting by combining an elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with a Gaussian low-pass filter [S2][S5, S6, unofficial]. Unlike its siblings [Splatting Without the Blur](1157-splatting-without-the-blur.md) and [Splat Radius Adjustment Based on Normal Angle](1264-splat-radius-adjustment-based-on-normal-angle.md), which focus on geometric extent or radius heuristics, EWA Splatting derives an analytic, perspective-correct 2D footprint via local affine approximation of the projective transformation [S9, S16, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Volume data represented as a set of particles (voxels or samples), each with a position, a 3x3 variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ defining the elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel, and optical properties (extinction $g_k$, emission $c_{\lambda k}$) [S6, S9, unofficial].
*   **Output:** A 2D image where each pixel value is the accumulated color and opacity from overlapping splats [S6, S9, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Particle:** Contains center $\mathbf{p}_k$, variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ (3x3 symmetric), extinction $g_k$, and emission $c_{\lambda k}$ [S6, S9, unofficial].
*   **Resampling Filter ($\rho_k$):** Defined by a 2x2 variance matrix $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h$ and a 2D mean $\mathbf{x}_k$ in screen space [S6, unofficial][S12].
*   **Footprint Table:** A 1D lookup table storing values of the exponential function $e^{-r^2/2}$ or similar, used for efficient rasterization of the Gaussian footprint [S9, S13, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Transform to Camera Space:**
    Map the voxel center $\mathbf{p}_k$ from object space to camera space $\mathbf{u}_k = (u_{k_0}, u_{k_1}, u_{k_2})^T$ using the viewing transformation matrix $\mathbf{W}$ (rotational part) and translation [S6, S9, unofficial].
    $$ \mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{p}_k + \mathbf{t} $$
    The origin $\mathbf{u}=0$ is the center of projection; the image plane is $u_2 = 1$ [S6, unofficial].

2.  **Compute Jacobian:**
    Calculate the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$ of the perspective projection at $\mathbf{u}_k$ [S6, unofficial][S12]:
    $$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & 0 & -\frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & -\frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ \frac{u_{k_0}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_1}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_2}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \end{pmatrix} $$
    where $\|\mathbf{u}_k\| = \sqrt{u_{k_0}^2 + u_{k_1}^2 + u_{k_2}^2}$ [S6, unofficial][S12].

3.  **Transform Variance to Image Space:**
    Transform the 3D object-space variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ to the image space variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k'$ using the Jacobian and viewing rotation [S6, unofficial][S12]:
    $$ \mathbf{V}_k' = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{V}_k \mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T $$
    *Note: Sources use $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ to denote the 2x2 submatrix obtained by skipping the third row and column of the transformed 3x3 matrix [S6, unofficial][S12]. Let $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ be this 2x2 matrix.*

4.  **Project Center to Screen:**
    Perform perspective division to get 2D screen coordinates $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = (x_{k_0}, x_{k_1})^T$:
    $$ x_{k_0} = \frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}}, \quad x_{k_1} = \frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}} $$
    [S9, unofficial].

5.  **Compute Resampling Filter Parameters:**
    Define the low-pass filter variance $\mathbf{V}_h$ (typically the 2x2 identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$) [S6, unofficial][S12].
    Compute the combined inverse covariance matrix $\mathbf{M}_k$:
    $$ \mathbf{M}_k = (\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h)^{-1} $$
    [S6, unofficial][S12].
    Compute the normalization factor $N_k$:
    $$ N_k = \frac{1}{2\pi |\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}| |\mathbf{W}^{-1}| |\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h|^{\frac{1}{2}}} $$
    [S6, unofficial][S12].
    *Note: $|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}|$ is the determinant of the inverse Jacobian. Since $\mathbf{J}$ maps camera to image, its determinant relates to the scaling of the projection.*

6.  **Rasterize Footprint:**
    For each pixel $\mathbf{x}$ within the bounding box of the projected ellipse:
    a. Calculate the squared distance: $r^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k)$.
    b. If $r^2 < r_{cutoff}^2$ (typically $r_{cutoff} \approx 3$), compute the footprint value:
       $$ \rho_k(\mathbf{x}) = N_k e^{-\frac{1}{2} r^2} $$
    c. Accumulate $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ into the image buffer, weighted by extinction and attenuation [S6, S9, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Cutoff radius $r$:** Truncation limit for the Gaussian kernel. Typically $1 \le r \le 3$ [S6, unofficial][S12].
*   **Low-pass filter $\mathbf{V}_h$:** Usually the 2x2 identity matrix [S6, unofficial][S12].
*   **Shininess:** Used in shading passes, default 8.0 [S13, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Singularity:** If $u_{k_2} \to 0$ (point at center of projection), the Jacobian becomes singular. The point should be clipped or handled separately [S6, unofficial].
*   **Non-overlapping Assumption:** Basic splatting assumes non-overlapping kernels, which causes aliasing. EWA handles overlap via the resampling filter, but compositing order (back-to-front) remains critical for correct transparency [S1, S9, unofficial].
*   **Numerical Stability:** The inverse of the 2x2 matrix $\mathbf{M}_k$ can be unstable if the ellipse is degenerate (e.g., viewed edge-on). A safety factor or regularization may be needed [S13, unofficial].
*   **Notation:** A hat symbol ($\hat{\mathbf{V}}$) denotes skipping the third row and column [S6, unofficial][S12].

## Subtopics

### [Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS)](1155-opacity-thin-side-ots.md)
A parameterization scheme for EWA-based splatting that ensures robust gradient-descent optimization by dynamically scaling the learned weight $\theta$ such that the projected amplitude $a'$ equals $\theta$ when viewing the Gaussian along its thinnest side (shortest axis). It differs from standard EWA splatting, which preserves the integral of extinction (mass), and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), which preserves the 2D projected amplitude regardless of view, by preserving the 3D unbounded integral (view-independent mass) while ensuring the 2D projection behaves as an ordinary integral relative to the thinnest view.

### [OTS (EWA-Based Splatting)](1181-ots-ewa-based-splatting.md)
OTS (Opacity-Thin-Side) is a splatting method using unnormalized Gaussians that preserves the 2D amplitude $a'$ (called 'opacity') across projections, unlike EWA Splatting which preserves the weight $w$. It differs from standard EWA Splatting by requiring a Jacobian determinant correction to preserve the integral of the extinction coefficient under perspective projection.

### [GPU-Accelerated Volume Splatting With Elliptical RBFs](1186-gpu-accelerated-volume-splatting-with-elliptical-rbfs.md)
This method renders scattered volumetric data represented as 3D ellipsoidal Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) using a post-shaded, kernel-centric approach. It extends high-quality image-aligned sheet-buffer splatting to irregular grids by slicing kernels into parallel, image-aligned density planes, rasterizing them as 2D footprints, and compositing them. Unlike fragment-centric methods that evaluate Gaussian functions per pixel, this technique leverages hard-wired GPU polygonal rasterization, achieving two orders of magnitude higher frame rates. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by supporting general ellipsoidal kernels and using a flat spatial decomposition with active cell lists rather than screen-aligned spherical impostors.

### [Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing](1290-ellipsoidal-kernel-splatting-with-warped-slicing.md)
Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing is a volume rendering technique that represents point samples as 3D ellipsoids and rasterizes them by slicing the ellipsoid into 2D polygons. It solves the problem of efficiently computing the intersection of general ellipsoids with screen-aligned slicing planes on GPU hardware, where direct analytical slicing requires expensive square roots and conditional statements. The method warps the slicing planes from screen space into the coordinate system of a unit sphere, allowing the use of hard-wired GPU texture mapping and blending on radially symmetric kernel slices. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by explicitly slicing the kernel into discrete polygons for incremental rasterization rather than rasterizing the entire projected footprint, and it avoids the pre-integrated slab approach of earlier image-aligned splatting by using point-sampled slices.

### [Perspective Correct Splat Rasterization with EWA Approximation](2095-perspective-correct-splat-rasterization-with-ewa-approximati.md)
This method is an improved splat rendering pipeline that combines a perspectively correct fast splat rasterization procedure with an approximation of the Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts and visual holes in point-sampled geometry rendering, particularly when the model is viewed from flat angles or shifted from the viewing axis. Unlike its siblings, it uses an affine approximation to the projective mapping to ensure perspective-correct splat shapes without requiring per-point normals or complex anisotropic footprint calculations, trading exact band-limited footprints for a formulation that avoids holes and Moiré patterns. It builds on [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by replacing the standard EWA covariance computation with a faster, perspective-correct affine approximation.

### [Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting](2137-adaptive-ewa-volume-splatting.md)
Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting is a rendering technique that simplifies the computationally expensive Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling filter by classifying volume particles into three cases based on their distance from the viewpoint. It solves the problem of high computational cost in full EWA splatting while maintaining aliasing-free quality. Unlike full EWA splatting, which always computes the combined covariance of the reconstruction and low-pass filters, this method uses only the reconstruction filter for close particles (magnification) and only the low-pass filter for far particles (minification), resorting to full EWA only in the transition zone.

### [EWA Approximation with Tangent Vector Adjustment](2143-ewa-approximation-with-tangent-vector-adjustment.md)
A high-quality filtering technique for point-based rendering that approximates the EWA resampling filter by dynamically adjusting the tangent vectors of isotropic splats during setup to prevent aliasing during minification, without requiring expensive per-fragment convolution. It sets itself apart from the original EWA framework by using projective mapping and tangent adjustment rather than affine mapping and Gaussians for both reconstruction and low-pass filters, and from Botsch et al. [BHZK05] by avoiding per-fragment filter evaluation, thus eliminating the fragment shader bottleneck.

### [EWA Splatting Renderer (Mesh-Free)](4665-ewa-splatting-renderer-mesh-free.md)
A mesh-free rendering pipeline that projects raw point clouds directly to 2D camera views using EWA projection and Z-buffer occlusion, producing RGB images, label maps, and 2D-to-3D index maps without mesh reconstruction. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by using simplified screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff, trading anisotropic, correctly band-limited footprints for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii.

### [Surface Splatting and Lighting](9275-surface-splatting-and-lighting.md)
Surface Splatting and Lighting is a family of rendering techniques that reconstruct continuous, shaded surfaces from unstructured point cloud samples by projecting each point as a spatially extended primitive (a splat) carrying a reconstruction kernel. It solves the problem of sparse dot cloud aliasing and transparency artifacts inherent in raw point rendering by ensuring hole-free reconstructions even for moderate sampling densities. Unlike forward splatting methods that apply lighting during rasterization, variants like Deferred Surface Splatting decouple geometric projection from lighting calculation, allowing multiple light sources to be applied efficiently in screen space without re-projecting geometry.

### [Gathering](9569-gathering.md)
Gathering is a rendering technique where, for each output pixel, contributions are gathered from neighboring pixels to compute the new value. It solves the parallelization inefficiency of splatting on GPUs by ensuring that each iteration writes to a single pixel, allowing for efficient parallel execution. It is the conceptual inverse of splatting, which distributes contributions from source pixels to neighboring target pixels.

### EWA-based Attribute Interpolation
An interpolation method for smooth attribute interpolation of normals and other attributes, based on Elliptical Weighted-Average (EWA) splatting [S17]. It is a post-rasterisation pass to smoothly interpolate normals and other attributes using EWA splatting, because the CGC data structure does not allow random access to neighboring grid cells [S17]. It renders cells as round splats with a weighted footprint and accumulates attributes (normals) to the G-buffer [S17]. It provides smooth attribute interpolation compared to standard point-based methods, improving visual quality [S17]. Unlike Laine et al. [12] who use post-processing Poisson disk averaging (expensive, up to 96 samples, fails on specular surfaces), this method interpolates before shading and provides smoother normals without needing TAA or excessive samples [S17]. Unlike Jablonski et al. [10] who use a 3x3 Gaussian filter, this method uses EWA splatting which provides smoother normals [S17]. Unlike storing additional corner values (e.g., 32 instead of 8) which increases memory significantly, this method uses interpolation [S17].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot A)$, where $N$ is the number of splats and $A$ is the average number of pixels covered by a splat footprint. The computation of the Jacobian and variance transformation is $O(1)$ per splat [S6, S9, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ for storing splat parameters, plus $O(R^2)$ for the footprint table if precomputed, where $R$ is the cutoff radius [S9, S13, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** On an SGI Indigo with 200 MHz, EWA splatting rendered the MRbrain dataset (2602 viewport) in 30 seconds [S1, unofficial]. On a 866 MHz Pentium-III, the Skull dataset rendered in 11 seconds [S1, unofficial]. Hardware acceleration can significantly improve this, but the algorithm is computationally intensive due to the per-pixel Gaussian evaluation and matrix operations [S6, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High image quality, aliasing-free, handles arbitrary elliptical kernels, perspective-correct [S2][S9, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive, requires careful handling of compositing order, sensitive to near-singular projections [S1, S6, S13, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** High-quality offline or interactive volume rendering where aliasing is unacceptable and hardware supports efficient matrix operations [S2][S9, unofficial].
*   **When Not to Use:** Real-time applications with very large datasets and limited GPU compute power, where adaptive approximations or simpler splatting methods are preferred [S6, unofficial].

## Background
EWA Splatting was introduced by Matthias Zwicker, Hanspeter Pfister, Jeroen van Baar, and Markus Gross in 2001 [S9, S16, unofficial]. It is named after Heckbert's Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter for texture mapping, due to the similarity in derivation [S8, abstract only, unofficial][S9, unofficial]. The method was developed to address the aliasing artifacts and visual holes in point-sampled surface and volume rendering [S2, S7]. It builds on the concept of splatting [23-splatting.md] and extends it with a rigorous band-limited resampling filter [S9, unofficial].
