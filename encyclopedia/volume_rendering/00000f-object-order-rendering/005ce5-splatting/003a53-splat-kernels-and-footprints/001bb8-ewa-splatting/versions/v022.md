# EWA Splatting

Also called: Elliptical Weighted Average Splatting, Zwicker EWA splatting, EWA Volume Splatting

## Overview
EWA Splatting is a high-quality, aliasing-free volume rendering technique that projects 3D volumetric particles as 2D elliptical footprints onto the image plane [S2][S8, unofficial][S10, unofficial]. It solves the aliasing artifacts inherent in standard splatting by replacing the basic footprint function with a band-limited **EWA resampling filter**, which combines an elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with a Gaussian low-pass filter [S1, unofficial][S2][S6, unofficial]. Unlike its siblings [Splat Radius Adjustment Based on Normal Angle](1264-splat-radius-adjustment-based-on-normal-angle.md) and [Splat Extent and Projection](1792-splat-extent-and-projection.md), which may use simpler isotropic or ad-hoc projections, EWA Splatting derives the exact 2D footprint shape via a local affine approximation of the perspective projection, ensuring correct anisotropic filtering and hole-free rendering [S2][S8, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A set of volumetric particles (voxels or samples), each defined by a center position $\mathbf{t}_k$ in object space, a 3x3 variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ defining the shape/orientation of the reconstruction kernel, an extinction coefficient $g_k$, and an emission color $c_{\lambda k}$ [S6, unofficial][S13].
*   **Output:** A 2D image where each pixel contains the accumulated color and opacity from overlapping splats [S6, unofficial][S13].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Particle:** Struct containing `center` (vec3), `variance` (mat3), `extinction` (float), `color` (vec3). [G]
*   **Splat Primitive:** Struct containing `screen_center` (vec2), `variance_2x2` (mat2), `normalization_factor` (float), `radius` (float). [G]

**Steps:**

1.  **Interpret Volume as Particles:** Treat the volume data as a set of absorbing and emitting particles [S6, unofficial][S13]. Each particle $k$ is represented by a 3D elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel:
    $$ G_{\mathbf{V}_k}(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{t}_k) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}|\mathbf{V}_k|^{1/2}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{t}_k)^T \mathbf{V}_k^{-1}(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{t}_k)} $$
    The kernel is truncated where $(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{t}_k)^T \mathbf{V}_k^{-1} (\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{t}_k) \leq r^2$, with typical cutoff radius $r \in [1, 3]$ [S6, unofficial][S13].

2.  **Transform to Camera Space:** Apply the viewing transformation to map object space to camera space [S10, unofficial]. Let $\mathbf{W}$ be the rotational part of the viewing transformation [S13]. The camera space center $\mathbf{u}_k$ and variance $\mathbf{V}'_k$ are:
    $$ \mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{t}_k + \mathbf{d} $$
    $$ \mathbf{V}'_k = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_k \mathbf{W}^T $$
    Camera space coordinates are $\mathbf{u} = (u_0, u_1, u_2)$, with the center of projection at $\mathbf{u}=0$ and the image plane at $u_2 = 1$ [S6, unofficial][S13].

3.  **Compute Jacobian of Perspective Projection:** Calculate the 3x3 Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$ at the particle's camera space position $\mathbf{u}_k$:
    $$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & 0 & -\frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & -\frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ \frac{u_{k_0}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_1}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_2}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \end{pmatrix} $$
    where $\|\mathbf{u}_k\| = \sqrt{u_{k_0}^2 + u_{k_1}^2 + u_{k_2}^2}$ [S6, unofficial][S13].

4.  **Transform Variance to Image Space:** Compute the 3x3 variance matrix in image space (ray space approximation) by transforming the camera space variance with the Jacobian:
    $$ \mathbf{V}_k^{\text{img}} = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{V}'_k \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{V}_k \mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T $$
    Extract the 2x2 submatrix $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ by skipping the third row and column of $\mathbf{V}_k^{\text{img}}$ [S6, unofficial][S13].

5.  **Project Center to Screen:** Perform perspective division to get the 2D screen coordinates $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$:
    $$ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \left( \frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}}, \frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}} \right) $$

6.  **Compute EWA Resampling Filter:** The footprint function is convolved with a 2D Gaussian low-pass filter $\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{V}_h}$ to prevent aliasing [S13]. Let $\mathbf{V}_h$ be the 2x2 variance of the low-pass filter (typically the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$) [S13]. The combined variance is $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h$. The EWA resampling filter $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ is a 2D Gaussian:
    $$ \rho_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi |\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}| |\mathbf{W}^{-1}| |\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h|^{\frac{1}{2}}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k)} $$
    where $\mathbf{M}_k = (\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h)^{-1}$ [S6, unofficial][S13].

7.  **Rasterize Splat:** Rasterize the elliptical footprint $\rho_k$ onto the screen [S17, unofficial]. The support of the Gaussian is limited to the cutoff radius $r$ [S13]. The rasterization of the footprint is performed using forward differencing, requiring only one 1D footprint table for all reconstruction kernels and any viewing direction [S8, unofficial][S10, unofficial].

8.  **Composite Back-to-Front:** Sort particles by view-space depth ($u_{k_2}$) from front to back [S6, unofficial][S13]. Accumulate contributions into the image buffer using the volume rendering equation approximation:
    $$ I_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \sum_k c_{\lambda k} g_k \rho_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} (1 - g_j \rho_j(\hat{\mathbf{x}})) $$
    This assumes non-overlapping kernels along the ray and ignores self-occlusion within a single kernel [S8, unofficial][S10, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math

def ewa_splatting(particles, view_matrix, image_width, image_height, cutoff_radius=2.0):
    # Helper classes for vectors and matrices
    class Vec2:
        def __init__(self, x, y): self.x, self.y = x, y
    class Vec3:
        def __init__(self, x, y, z): self.x, self.y, self.z = x, y, z
    class Mat3:
        def __init__(self, data): self.data = data # 3x3 list of lists
        def __matmul__(self, other):
            res = [[0]*3 for _ in range(3)]
            for i in range(3):
                for j in range(3):
                    for k in range(3): res[i][j] += self.data[i][k] * other.data[k][j]
            return Mat3(res)
        def transpose(self):
            return Mat3([[self.data[j][i] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])
        def det(self):
            a,b,c = self.data[0]
            d,e,f = self.data[1]
            g,h,i = self.data[2]
            return a*(e*i-f*h) - b*(d*i-f*g) + c*(d*h-e*g)

    def mat_vec_mul(M, v):
        return Vec3(
            M.data[0][0]*v.x + M.data[0][1]*v.y + M.data[0][2]*v.z,
            M.data[1][0]*v.x + M.data[1][1]*v.y + M.data[1][2]*v.z,
            M.data[2][0]*v.x + M.data[2][1]*v.y + M.data[2][2]*v.z
        )

    splats = []
    for k in particles:
        # Object to Camera
        u_k = mat_vec_mul(view_matrix, k.center)
        W = view_matrix.rotation
        V_prime_k = W @ k.variance @ W.transpose()
        
        u0, u1, u2 = u_k.x, u_k.y, u_k.z
        norm_u = math.sqrt(u0**2 + u1**2 + u2**2)
        
        # Perspective Projection Jacobian
        J = Mat3([
            [1/u2, 0, -u0/(u2**2)],
            [0, 1/u2, -u1/(u2**2)],
            [u0/norm_u, u1/norm_u, u2/norm_u]
        ])
        
        # Image Space Variance (3x3)
        V_img_k = J @ V_prime_k @ J.transpose()
        
        # Extract 2x2 submatrix (skip 3rd row/col)
        V_hat_k_data = [[V_img_k.data[0][0], V_img_k.data[0][1]],
                        [V_img_k.data[1][0], V_img_k.data[1][1]]]
        
        # Screen Center
        x_hat_k = Vec2(u0 / u2, u1 / u2)
        
        # Low-pass filter variance (Identity)
        V_h_data = [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]
        
        # Combined Variance and Inverse (2x2)
        V_combined_data = [[V_hat_k_data[0][0] + V_h_data[0][0], V_hat_k_data[0][1] + V_h_data[0][1]],
                           [V_hat_k_data[1][0] + V_h_data[1][0], V_hat_k_data[1][1] + V_h_data[1][1]]]
        det_V_combined = V_combined_data[0][0]*V_combined_data[1][1] - V_combined_data[0][1]*V_combined_data[1][0]
        M_k_data = [[V_combined_data[1][1]/det_V_combined, -V_combined_data[0][1]/det_V_combined],
                    [-V_combined_data[1][0]/det_V_combined, V_combined_data[0][0]/det_V_combined]]
        
        # Normalization Factors
        det_V_combined_sqrt = math.sqrt(abs(det_V_combined))
        norm_factor = 1.0 / (2 * math.pi * det_V_combined_sqrt)
        
        # Compute bounding box for rasterization
        # Eigenvalues of V_combined determine axis lengths
        trace = V_combined_data[0][0] + V_combined_data[1][1]
        det = det_V_combined
        disc = math.sqrt(max(0, trace**2 - 4*det))
        lambda1 = (trace + disc) / 2
        lambda2 = (trace - disc) / 2
        max_radius = cutoff_radius * math.sqrt(max(lambda1, lambda2))
        
        splats.append({
            'center': x_hat_k,
            'M': M_k_data,
            'norm_factor': norm_factor,
            'extinction': k.extinction,
            'color': k.color,
            'depth': u2,
            'radius': max_radius
        })
    
    # Sort splats back-to-front (by u2, which is depth in camera space)
    splats.sort(key=lambda s: s['depth'], reverse=True)
    
    # Initialize image buffers
    image_color = [[(0.0, 0.0, 0.0) for _ in range(image_width)] for _ in range(image_height)]
    image_opacity = [[0.0 for _ in range(image_width)] for _ in range(image_height)]
    
    for s in splats:
        min_x = max(0, int(s['center'].x - s['radius']))
        max_x = min(image_width, int(s['center'].x + s['radius']))
        min_y = max(0, int(s['center'].y - s['radius']))
        max_y = min(image_height, int(s['center'].y + s['radius']))
        
        for y in range(min_y, max_y):
            for x in range(min_x, max_x):
                dx = x - s['center'].x
                dy = y - s['center'].y
                
                # Evaluate Gaussian: exp(-0.5 * pos^T M pos)
                exponent = -0.5 * (dx * (s['M'][0][0]*dx + s['M'][0][1]*dy) + dy * (s['M'][1][0]*dx + s['M'][1][1]*dy))
                if exponent > -cutoff_radius**2:
                    weight = s['norm_factor'] * math.exp(exponent)
                    
                    current_opacity = image_opacity[y][x]
                    contribution = (s['color'][0] * s['extinction'] * weight,
                                    s['color'][1] * s['extinction'] * weight,
                                    s['color'][2] * s['extinction'] * weight)
                    new_opacity = min(1.0, current_opacity + s['extinction'] * weight)
                    
                    alpha_blend = (1.0 - current_opacity)
                    image_color[y][x] = (
                        image_color[y][x][0] * (1.0 - s['extinction'] * weight) + contribution[0] * alpha_blend,
                        image_color[y][x][1] * (1.0 - s['extinction'] * weight) + contribution[1] * alpha_blend,
                        image_color[y][x][2] * (1.0 - s['extinction'] * weight) + contribution[2] * alpha_blend
                    )
                    image_opacity[y][x] = new_opacity
                    
    return image_color, image_opacity
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Singularity at $u_2=0$:** If a particle is at the center of projection, the Jacobian is undefined.
*   **Precision:** The determinant calculations and matrix inversions can suffer from precision issues if the splat is extremely elongated or small. Use stable linear algebra routines. [G]
*   **Overlap Assumption:** The back-to-front compositing formula assumes kernels do not overlap along the ray.

## Subtopics

### [Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS)](1155-opacity-thin-side-ots.md)
A parameterization scheme for EWA-based splatting that ensures robust gradient-descent optimization by dynamically scaling the learned weight $\theta$ such that the projected amplitude $a'$ equals $\theta$ when viewing the Gaussian along its thinnest side (shortest axis). It differs from standard EWA splatting, which preserves the integral of extinction (mass), and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), which preserves the 2D projected amplitude regardless of view, by preserving the 3D unbounded integral (view-independent mass) while ensuring the 2D projection behaves as an ordinary integral relative to the thinnest view.

### [OTS (EWA-Based Splatting)](1181-ots-ewa-based-splatting.md)
OTS (EWA-Based Splatting) is a volumetric rendering method that adapts the standard EWA splatting framework to support accurate volumetric integration by correcting the primitive weight for perspective projection effects. It solves the problem of incorrect opacity accumulation in standard EWA splatting, where the projected 2D Gaussian's integral is distorted by perspective division, leading to visual inaccuracies in depth-dependent attenuation. It sets itself apart from standard EWA Splatting by explicitly multiplying the splat weight by the determinant of the projection Jacobian, and from 3DGS by retaining the exact volumetric integration model rather than approximating it with affine assumptions.

### [GPU-Accelerated Volume Splatting With Elliptical RBFs](1186-gpu-accelerated-volume-splatting-with-elliptical-rbfs.md)
This method renders scattered volumetric data represented as 3D ellipsoidal Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) using a post-shaded, kernel-centric approach. It extends high-quality image-aligned sheet-buffer splatting to irregular grids by slicing kernels into parallel, image-aligned density planes, rasterizing them as 2D footprints, and compositing them. Unlike fragment-centric methods that evaluate Gaussian functions per pixel, this technique leverages hard-wired GPU polygonal rasterization, achieving two orders of magnitude higher frame rates. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by supporting general ellipsoidal kernels and using a flat spatial decomposition with active cell lists rather than screen-aligned spherical impostors.

### [Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing](1290-ellipsoidal-kernel-splatting-with-warped-slicing.md)
Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing is a volume rendering technique that represents point samples as 3D ellipsoids and rasterizes them by slicing the ellipsoid into 2D polygons. It solves the problem of efficiently computing the intersection of general ellipsoids with screen-aligned slicing planes on GPU hardware, where direct analytical slicing requires expensive square roots and conditional statements. The method warps the slicing planes from screen space into the coordinate system of a unit sphere, allowing the use of hard-wired GPU texture mapping and blending on radially symmetric kernel slices. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by explicitly slicing the kernel into discrete polygons for incremental rasterization rather than rasterizing the entire projected footprint, and it avoids the pre-integrated slab approach of earlier image-aligned splatting by using point-sampled slices.

### [Perspective Correct Splat Rasterization with EWA Approximation](2095-perspective-correct-splat-rasterization-with-ewa-approximati.md)
This method is an improved splat rendering pipeline that combines a perspectively correct fast splat rasterization procedure with an approximation of the Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts and visual holes in point-sampled geometry rendering, particularly when the model is viewed from flat angles or shifted from the viewing axis. Unlike its siblings, it uses an affine approximation to the projective mapping to ensure perspective-correct splat shapes without requiring per-point normals or complex anisotropic footprint calculations, trading exact band-limited footprints for a formulation that avoids holes and Moiré patterns. It builds on [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by replacing the standard EWA covariance computation with a faster, perspective-correct affine approximation.

### [Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting](2137-adaptive-ewa-volume-splatting.md)
Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting is a rendering technique that simplifies the computationally expensive Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling filter by classifying volume particles into three cases based on their distance from the viewpoint. It solves the problem of high computational cost in full EWA splatting while maintaining aliasing-free quality. Unlike full EWA splatting, which always computes the combined covariance of the reconstruction and low-pass filters, this method uses only the reconstruction filter for close particles (magnification) and only the low-pass filter for far particles (minification), resorting to full EWA only in the transition zone.

### [EWA Approximation with Tangent Vector Adjustment](2143-ewa-approximation-with-tangent-vector-adjustment.md)
A high-quality filtering technique for point-based rendering that approximates the EWA resampling filter by dynamically adjusting the tangent vectors of isotropic splats during setup to prevent aliasing during minification, without requiring expensive per-fragment convolution. It sets itself apart from the original EWA framework by using projective mapping and tangent adjustment rather than affine mapping and Gaussians for both reconstruction and low-pass filters, and from Botsch et al. [BHZK05] by avoiding per-fragment filter evaluation, thus eliminating the fragment shader bottleneck.

### [EWA Splatting Renderer (Mesh-Free)](4665-ewa-splatting-renderer-mesh-free.md)
A mesh-free rendering pipeline that projects raw point clouds directly to 2D camera views using EWA projection and Z-buffer occlusion, producing RGB images, label maps, and 2D-to-3D index maps without mesh reconstruction. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by using simplified screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff, trading anisotropic, correctly band-limited footprints for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii.

### [Surface Splatting and Lighting](9275-surface-splatting-and-lighting.md)
Surface Splatting and Lighting is a family of rendering techniques that reconstruct continuous, shaded surfaces from unstructured point cloud samples by projecting each point as a spatially extended primitive (a splat) carrying a reconstruction kernel. It solves the problem of sparse dot cloud aliasing and transparency artifacts inherent in raw point rendering by ensuring hole-free reconstructions even for moderate sampling densities. Unlike forward splatting methods that apply lighting during rasterization, variants like [Deferred Surface Splatting](1072-deferred-surface-splatting.md) decouple geometric projection from lighting calculation, allowing multiple light sources to be applied efficiently in screen space without re-projecting geometry.

### [Gathering](9569-gathering.md)
Gathering is a rendering technique where, for each output pixel, contributions are gathered from neighboring pixels to compute the new value. It fits GPUs better than splatting because it writes to a single pixel per iteration, allowing parallel execution.

### EWA-based Attribute Interpolation
An interpolation method for smooth attribute interpolation of normals and other attributes, based on Elliptical Weighted-Average (EWA) splatting [S19]. It is implemented as a post-rasterisation pass to smoothly interpolate normals and other attributes using EWA splatting, because the CGC data structure does not allow random access to neighboring grid cells [S19]. The method renders cells as round splats with a weighted footprint and accumulates attributes (normals) to the G-buffer [S19]. It provides smooth attribute interpolation compared to standard point-based methods, improving visual quality [S19]. Unlike Laine et al. [12] who use post-processing Poisson disk averaging (expensive, up to 96 samples, fails on specular surfaces), this method interpolates before shading and provides smoother normals without needing TAA or excessive samples [S19]. Unlike Jablonski et al. [10] who use a 3x3 Gaussian filter, this method uses EWA splatting which provides smoother normals [S19]. Unlike storing additional corner values (e.g., 32 instead of 8) which increases memory significantly, this method uses interpolation [S19].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot A)$, where $N$ is the number of particles and $A$ is the average number of pixels covered by a splat. The computation of the EWA filter (matrix inversion, determinant) is done per particle, making it more expensive per particle than simple splatting, but the rasterization is efficient. [S6, unofficial][S13]
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store particle data and splat primitives. The image buffer is $O(W \cdot H)$. [G]
*   **Measured Performance:** Zwicker et al. reported rendering times of 11 seconds for the Skull dataset on an 866 MHz Pentium-III [S1, unofficial]. Adaptive EWA splatting reduces this cost significantly for distant particles [S6, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High image quality, aliasing-free, handles arbitrary elliptical kernels, works with regular and irregular grids [S2][S8, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to matrix operations and per-pixel filter evaluation. Requires sorting particles back-to-front. [S6, unofficial][S13]
*   **When to use:** When high-quality, aliasing-free volume rendering is required, and performance allows for the computational cost. [S13]
*   **When not to use:** For real-time applications with very large datasets where adaptive approximations or simpler splatting methods are necessary. [S6, unofficial]

## Background
EWA Splatting was introduced by Matthias Zwicker, Hanspeter Pfister, Jeroen van Baar, and Markus Gross in 2001 [S8, unofficial]. It is named after Heckbert's EWA filter for texture mapping due to the similarity in derivation [S9, abstract only, unofficial]. The method was developed to address aliasing artifacts in volume splatting caused by incorrect visibility determination and non-overlapping reconstruction kernel assumptions [S1, unofficial][S8, unofficial]. It has been widely used in high-quality volume visualization and has inspired many variants and optimizations, including adaptive EWA splatting [S6, unofficial][S13].
