# Object-order volume rendering

Also called: Kernel-based volume rendering, Point-based volume rendering

## Overview
Object-order volume rendering is a family of techniques that decompose a volume into discrete primitives (points, splats, or cells) and rasterize their contributions to the image plane, rather than casting rays through the volume grid. It solves the problem of efficiently rendering complex, sparse, or intricate volumetric structures by avoiding the computational overhead of traversing empty space inherent in ray-based methods [S1, book]. Unlike its siblings, it prioritizes spatial coherence and primitive-level culling over the continuous sampling of image-order methods, making it distinct from [Chunked Trilinear Volume Rendering](2557-chunked-trilinear-volume-rendering.md) which relies on grid traversal.

## Shared principles
The core mechanism involves three phases: decomposition, projection, and accumulation.

1.  **Decomposition**: The volume data is represented not as a dense grid, but as a collection of basis primitives. These are typically points, tetrahedral cells, or hexagonal cells [S1, book]. Each primitive carries attributes such as position, density, and color.
2.  **Projection**: Each primitive is projected onto the 2D image plane. This projection defines a "footprint" or kernel shape. To ensure proper blending and avoid artifacts, radially symmetric kernels are employed [S1, book].
3.  **Accumulation**: The contributions of all primitives whose footprints overlap a pixel are accumulated. This is typically done via alpha blending or weighted summation during rasterization.

**Mathematical Formulation**:
Let $P$ be the set of primitives. For a pixel $p$, the final color $C(p)$ and opacity $\alpha(p)$ are computed by sorting the overlapping primitives by depth and accumulating:

$$
\alpha(p) = 1 - \prod_{i \in \text{overlap}(p)} (1 - \alpha_i)
$$

$$
C(p) = \sum_{i \in \text{overlap}(p)} C_i \alpha_i \prod_{j < i} (1 - \alpha_j)
$$

where $\alpha_i$ and $C_i$ are the opacity and color contribution of primitive $i$ at pixel $p$, determined by the kernel function $K$ and the primitive's attributes.

**Key Trade-offs**:
*   **Empty Space**: Object-order methods do not spend effort traversing or storing empty space, which is advantageous for objects with intricate shapes and pockets of empty space [S1, book].
*   **Kernel Size**: Radially symmetric kernels must be larger than the trilinear kernels used in raycasting to ensure proper blending. Consequently, the rasterization of a footprint takes more time than the trilinear interpolation of ray samples [S1, book].
*   **Culling**: Early ray termination is not available. The object-order equivalent, early point elimination, is more difficult to achieve than early ray termination [S1, book].
*   **Suitability**: Objects with compact structure are more favorably rendered with image-order methods or hybrid methods [S1, book].

## Subtopics

### Splatting
The most common implementation where point-based primitives are projected as 2D kernels (splats) onto the screen. It is chosen when the data can be effectively approximated by point clouds or when hardware rasterization acceleration is desired. See [Splatting](52-splatting.md).

## Background
The concept emerged as an alternative to raycasting for handling sparse or complex volumetric data. It was notably discussed in the context of volume visualization and graphics, highlighting the trade-offs between spatial coherence exploitation and kernel size overhead [S1, book]. The method allows for the extension of rendering to handle complex geometries without the memory overhead of storing empty voxels.
