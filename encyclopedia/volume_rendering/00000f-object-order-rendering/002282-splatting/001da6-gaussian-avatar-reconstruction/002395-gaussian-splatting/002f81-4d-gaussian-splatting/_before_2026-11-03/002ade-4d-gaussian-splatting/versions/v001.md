# 4D Gaussian Splatting

Also called: 4D-GS

## Overview
4D Gaussian Splatting (4DGS) is a method for modeling dynamic scenes by representing them as a collection of 4D Gaussians, extending the static 3D Gaussian Splatting approach to capture time-varying content [S2, unofficial]. It solves the problem of novel view synthesis for dynamic videos by treating space and time as a coherent integrated 4D manifold, allowing for the efficient rendering of motion and appearance changes [S2, unofficial]. Unlike approaches that treat space and time as independent (e.g., adding a separate 1D Gaussian $p_i(t)$ to 3D Gaussians), 4DGS uses a unified 4D covariance matrix to better capture underlying scene motion, avoiding the jitter and flickering associated with independent temporal weighting [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A sequence of images (video) representing a dynamic scene. For initialization, a colored point cloud (e.g., from COLMAP) or random points in volume are used.
*   **Output:** A set of $N$ 4D Gaussians parameterized by mean $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$, opacity $\alpha$, and 4D Spherical Harmonics (4DSH) coefficients, enabling the rendering of arbitrary views at arbitrary times $t$.

**Data Structures:**
Each Gaussian $i$ is defined by the following parameters:
1.  **Mean:** $\mu_i = (\mu_{x}, \mu_{y}, \mu_{z}, \mu_{t}) \in \mathbb{R}^4$.
2.  **Covariance:** $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$, parameterized via scaling and rotation.
3.  **Opacity:** $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$.
4.  **Color:** Represented by 4DSH coefficients.

**Covariance Parameterization:**
The 4D covariance matrix is decomposed into a scaling matrix $S$ and a rotation matrix $R$:
$$ \Sigma = R S S^T R^T $$
*   **Scaling:** $S = \text{diag}(s_x, s_y, s_z, s_t)$, where $s_x, s_y, s_z$ are spatial scales and $s_t$ is the temporal scale.
*   **Rotation:** A 4D rotation is decomposed into two isotropic rotations represented by quaternions $q_l$ (left) and $q_r$ (right).
    $$ R = L(q_l) R(q_r) $$
    Where $q_l = (a, b, c, d)$ and $q_r = (p, q, r, s)$ are unit quaternions. The matrices $L(q_l)$ and $R(q_r)$ are constructed as:
    $$ L(q_l) = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b & -c & -d \\ b & a & -d & c \\ c & d & a & -b \\ d & -c & b & a \end{pmatrix}, \quad R(q_r) = \begin{pmatrix} p & -q & -r & -s \\ q & p & s & -r \\ r & -s & p & q \\ s & r & -q & p \end{pmatrix} $$
    *(Note: The source [S2, unofficial] provides a specific matrix layout for $L(q_l)R(q_r)$ which may vary in sign conventions depending on quaternion definition; the above follows standard 4D rotation decomposition principles implied by the source's matrix multiplication structure.)*

**Conditional 3D Gaussian Derivation:**
To render at a specific time $t$, the 4D Gaussian is conditioned on $t$. The conditional 3D mean $\mu_{xyz|t}$ and covariance $\Sigma_{xyz|t}$ are derived from the properties of multivariate Gaussians:
$$ \mu_{xyz|t} = \mu_{1:3} + \Sigma_{1:3,4} \Sigma_{4,4}^{-1} (t - \mu_t) $$
$$ \Sigma_{xyz|t} = \Sigma_{1:3,1:3} - \Sigma_{1:3,4} \Sigma_{4,4}^{-1} \Sigma_{4,1:3} $$
Where $\mu_{1:3}$ is the spatial part of the mean, $\Sigma_{1:3,4}$ is the cross-covariance between space and time, and $\Sigma_{4,4}$ is the temporal variance.

**Marginal Time Probability:**
The probability of the Gaussian existing at time $t$ is given by the marginal 1D Gaussian:
$$ p_i(t) = \mathcal{N}(t; \mu_{t,i}, \Sigma_{4,4,i}) $$

**Rendering Equation:**
The color $\mathcal{I}(u, v, t)$ at pixel $(u, v)$ and time $t$ is computed by volume splatting:
$$ \mathcal{I}(u, v, t) = \sum_{i=1}^N p_i(t) p_i(u, v|t) \alpha_i c_i(d, t) \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - p_j(t) p_j(u, v|t) \alpha_j) $$
Where:
*   $p_i(u, v|t)$ is the 2D Gaussian probability density projected onto the image plane at time $t$.
*   $c_i(d, t)$ is the view-dependent color.

**Color Representation (4D Spherical Harmonics):**
View-dependent color and temporal appearance are modeled using 4D Spherical Harmonics (4DSH):
$$ Z_{nl}^m(t, \theta, \phi) = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right) Y_l^m(\theta, \phi) $$
Where:
*   $Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)$ are standard 3D spherical harmonics with degree $l \ge 0$ and order $-l \le m \le l$.
*   $n$ is the Fourier series order for time.
*   $T$ is the period of the temporal basis.

**Initialization:**
*   **Plenoptic Video Dataset:** Initialize Gaussians using a colored point cloud from COLMAP on the first frame. Distribute timestamps $\mu_t$ uniformly across the scene duration.
*   **D-NeRF Dataset:** Select 100,000 random points in volume $[-1.2, 1.2]^3$. Set initial $\mu_t$ to the scene's duration.

**Optimization and Densification:**
*   **Loss:** Rendering loss (e.g., L1 + DSSIM) is used as supervision.
*   **Sampling:** Straightforward batch sampling in time is used to prevent temporal flickering and jitter, processing entire videos end-to-end.
*   **Densification:**
    *   Use average gradients of $\mu_t$ as an additional density control indicator alongside spatial gradients.
    *   In over-reconstruction regions, employ joint spatial and temporal position sampling during Gaussian splitting.

## Subtopics

### Gaussian-splat timelapses
A time-lapse rendering architecture where each timepoint's splats are fitted independently and stored contiguously, with time acting as a hard ordering barrier to prevent LOD rung blending across timepoints [S3, unofficial]. It fits Gaussian splats per timepoint (e.g., 256K per frame) and streams timepoints sequentially, fetching chunks holding the new timepoint as the timeline advances [S3, unofficial]. Time-dependent elements like lineage tracks are rendered by clipping polylines and fading splats in/out based on their extent along the hidden temporal dimension [S3, unofficial].

### Urban Scene Adaptation for 4DGS
Training and rendering modifications for sparse, wide-baseline outdoor scenes to prevent overfitting and model infinite-distance backgrounds [S2, unofficial]. It integrates sparse depth supervision from LiDAR point clouds using an inverse L1 loss function, adopts a cube map as the background model for the sky, and penalizes inverse depth values in sky-masked regions to enforce infinite distance [S2, unofficial]. The sky mask is generated using SegFormer [S2, unofficial]. The temporal coefficient of 4DSH is deactivated (set to zero) during training to reduce overfitting to sparse observations [S2, unofficial]. Unlike indoor methods, it provides a unified representation of dynamic and static regions without explicit object masks [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering is efficient, leveraging tile-based rasterization similar to 3DGS. On the Plenoptic Video dataset, 4DGS achieves 114 FPS [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the number of Gaussians $N$. Each Gaussian stores 4D mean, 4D covariance (parameterized by 4 scales and 2 quaternions), opacity, and 4DSH coefficients.
*   **Performance:** On the Plenoptic Video dataset, 4DGS achieves PSNR 32.01, DSSIM 0.014, and LPIPS 0.055 [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Captures complex motion and appearance changes end-to-end; efficient real-time rendering; unified representation of dynamic and static regions.
*   **Limitations:** May suffer from temporal flickering if batch sampling is not used; independent space-time models struggle with motion representation compared to coherent 4D Gaussians [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Dynamic scene novel view synthesis, video reconstruction, time-lapse visualization.
*   **When not to use:** Static scenes (3DGS is simpler and sufficient), scenes with extremely sparse temporal observations without adaptation (e.g., urban scenes require specific modifications like LiDAR supervision).

## Background
4D Gaussian Splatting was introduced by Wu et al. in 2023 as an extension of 3D Gaussian Splatting to handle dynamic scenes [S2, unofficial]. It addresses the limitations of treating space and time independently by formulating a coherent 4D Gaussian model. The method builds on the efficient rasterization techniques of 3DGS [S2, unofficial] and adapts them to the spatiotemporal domain. It has been applied to datasets like Plenoptic Video and D-NeRF, and adapted for urban scenes with LiDAR supervision [S2, unofficial]. Variants like Gaussian-splat timelapses have been developed for microscopy and scientific data visualization [S3, unofficial].
