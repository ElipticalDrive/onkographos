# Differentiable Radiative Rasterization


## Overview
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization (DRR) is a GPU-friendly rasterization process implemented in CUDA that renders novel X-ray projections from radiative Gaussian point clouds [S1]. It solves the problem of differentiable rendering for X-ray imaging by projecting 3D Gaussians to a 2D detector plane and accumulating radiation intensity per pixel, allowing gradients to flow back to Gaussian parameters (position, covariance, opacity, and radiation intensity) [S1]. Unlike standard optical Gaussian Splatting which uses alpha compositing for color, DRR sums the radiation intensities of intersection points weighted by the Gaussian probability density, tailored for the physics of X-ray attenuation [S1]. It builds on [3D Gaussian Splatting Differentiable Rasterizer](2813-3d-gaussian-splatting-differentiable-rasterizer.md) but adapts the projection and accumulation steps for X-ray geometry.

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Radiative Gaussian point cloud $\mathcal{G} = \{G_i(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i, \alpha_i, \mathbf{f}_i) \mid i = 1, \dots, N_p\}$, where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the center, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the covariance, $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the opacity, and $\mathbf{f}_i$ represents inherent point features used to fit radiation intensity [S1].
- Extrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{ext} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}$ [S1].
- Intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{int} \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$ [S1].
- X-ray source distance $L_{SD}$ (distance between X-ray source and detector) [S1].
- X-ray source azimuth angle $\phi$ [S1].

**Outputs:**
- Rendered image $\mathbf{I}$, where each pixel $p$ has an intensity value $I(p) \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
- **Gaussian Point Cloud:** A list of $N_p$ Gaussians. Each Gaussian $i$ stores:
  - $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$: 3D center position.
  - $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$: $3\times3$ covariance matrix.
  - $\alpha_i$: Scalar opacity.
  - $\mathbf{f}_i$: Feature vector for radiation intensity calculation [S1].
- **Tiles:** The 2D image plane is partitioned into non-overlapping tiles. Each tile maintains a list of indices of Gaussians that overlap its bounding box in screen space [S1].

**Steps:**

1. **Project Centers to Image Coordinates:**
   Transform the 3D Gaussian center $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ to camera coordinates $\mathbf{t}_i$ using homogeneous coordinates and the extrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{ext}$:
   $$ \tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{t}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_{ext} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_i = \mathbf{M}_{ext} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
   where $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_i$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i$ are homogeneous coordinates [S1].
   Project $\mathbf{t}_i$ to image coordinates $\mathbf{u}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ using the intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{M}_{int}$:
   $$ \tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_{int} \tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i = \mathbf{M}_{int} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{t}_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
   where $\mathbf{t}_i = (t_x, t_y, t_z)^\top$ [S1].

2. **Transform Covariance:**
   Compute the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ in camera coordinates:
   $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i = \mathbf{J}_i \mathbf{W}_i \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{J}_i^\top $$
   where $\mathbf{W}_i$ is the viewing transformation and $\mathbf{J}_i$ is the Jacobian of the affine approximation of the projective transformation [S1].

   The viewing transformation $\mathbf{W}_i$ is defined as:
   $$ \mathbf{W}_i = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin \phi & \cos \phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ -\cos \phi & -\sin \phi & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
   where $\phi$ is the azimuth angle of the X-ray source [S1].

   The Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_i$ is defined as:
   $$ \mathbf{J}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & 0 & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z} \\ 0 & \frac{L_{SD}}{t_z} & -\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
   *Note: The source text [S1] contains a formatting error in the matrix layout, listing terms as $\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$ in the notes but the context of standard projective Jacobians and the visual structure of the source equation (9) suggests the term is likely $\frac{t_y}{t_z}$ or similar scaling. However, strictly following the source text's explicit formula in the notes: $-\frac{L_{SD}}{t_z^2} \frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$. We retain the source's explicit notation for implementation fidelity, noting the potential typo.* [S1].
   Here, $L_{SD}$ is the distance between the X-ray source and detector, and $t_y, t_z$ are components of $\mathbf{t}_i$ [S1].

3. **Obtain 2D Covariance:**
   Derive the 2D covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}''_i \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ by skipping the third row and column of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'_i$ [S1].

4. **Tile Assignment:**
   Partition the 2D image plane into non-overlapping tiles. Assign each 3D Gaussian $i$ to the tiles whose bounding boxes overlap with the 2D projection defined by $(\mathbf{u}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}''_i)$ [S1].

5. **Sorting:**
   Sort the 3D Gaussians within each tile by their distance to the 2D detector (i.e., by $t_z$) [S1].

6. **Intensity Calculation:**
   For each pixel $p$, identify the set $\mathcal{N}$ of ordered points (Gaussians) overlapping the pixel in the corresponding tile [S1].
   The probability value of the $i$-th Gaussian distribution at a 3D point position $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is:
   $$ P(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)\right) $$
   [S1].
   The radiation intensity contribution $\mathbf{i}_j$ of the $j$-th intersection point $\mathbf{x}_j$ is derived from the Gaussian's opacity $\alpha_j$, the probability $P(\mathbf{x}_j | \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)$, and the inherent point features $\mathbf{f}_j$ (fitted via inner product with basis weights, though the exact feature-to-intensity function is not detailed in the provided text, it is implied that $\mathbf{i}_j$ is the radiation intensity of $\mathbf{x}_j$) [S1].
   The total intensity $I(p)$ at pixel $p$ is the sum of contributions from all overlapping Gaussians:
   $$ I(p) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{i}_j $$
   where $\mathbf{i}_j$ is the radiation intensity of the $j$-th intersection point $\mathbf{x}_j$ [S1].
   The source also defines a term $\sigma_j = \alpha_j P(\mathbf{x}_j | \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)$ in the context of the blending table, but the final intensity formula is a direct sum $\sum \mathbf{i}_j$, not the alpha-compositing product form used in standard 3DGS [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math
import numpy as np

def differentiable_radiative_rasterization(gaussians, M_ext, M_int, L_SD, phi, image_width, image_height, tile_size):
    """
    Renders X-ray projections from radiative Gaussian point clouds.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List of dicts, each containing:
            'mu': (3,) array, center position
            'Sigma': (3,3) array, covariance matrix
            'alpha': float, opacity
            'f': array, inherent point features
        M_ext: (4,4) array, extrinsic matrix
        M_int: (4,3) array, intrinsic matrix
        L_SD: float, distance between X-ray source and detector
        phi: float, azimuth angle of the source
        image_width: int
        image_height: int
        tile_size: int, size of each tile in pixels
        
    Returns:
        I: (image_height, image_width) array, rendered intensities
    """
    N = len(gaussians)
    
    # Step 1: Project centers to image coordinates
    # Transform to camera coordinates
    # Homogeneous coordinates for mu
    mu_homo = np.hstack([g['mu'], 1.0]) for g in gaussians] # (N, 4)
    t_homo = M_ext @ mu_homo.T # (4, N)
    t = t_homo[:3, :].T # (N, 3)
    
    # Project to image coordinates
    # Note: M_int is 4x3, t_homo is (N, 4). We need to handle dimensions carefully.
    # The source says u_homo = M_int @ t_homo. 
    # If M_int is 4x3 and t_homo is 4x1, result is 4x1.
    # For batch: M_int @ t.T -> (4, N).
    u_homo = M_int @ t.T # (4, N)
    u = u_homo[:2, :].T # (N, 2), image coordinates
    
    # Step 2: Transform Covariance
    # Compute W_i
    sin_phi = math.sin(phi)
    cos_phi = math.cos(phi)
    W = np.array([
        [-sin_phi, cos_phi, 0],
        [0, 0, -1],
        [-cos_phi, -sin_phi, 0]
    ])
    
    Sigma_prime_list = []
    for i in range(N):
        Sigma_i = gaussians[i]['Sigma']
        t_i = t[i]
        tz = t_i[2]
        ty = t_i[1]
        
        # Compute J_i
        # Source formula: J_i = [[L_SD/tz, 0, -L_SD/tz^2 * ty/tz^2], ...]
        # Note: The source text has a potential typo in the exponent of tz in the numerator term.
        # We follow the explicit text: -L_SD/tz^2 * ty/tz^2
        term = - (L_SD / (tz**2)) * (ty / (tz**2))
        J = np.array([
            [L_SD / tz, 0, term],
            [0, L_SD / tz, term],
            [0, 0, 0]
        ])
        
        # Sigma_prime = J @ W @ Sigma @ W.T @ J.T
        Sigma_prime = J @ W @ Sigma_i @ W.T @ J.T
        Sigma_prime_list.append(Sigma_prime)
        
    # Step 3: Obtain 2D Covariance
    Sigma_2d_list = []
    for Sigma_prime in Sigma_prime_list:
        # Skip third row and column
        Sigma_2d = Sigma_prime[:2, :2]
        Sigma_2d_list.append(Sigma_2d)
        
    # Step 4: Tile Assignment
    # Create tiles
    num_tiles_x = int(math.ceil(image_width / tile_size))
    num_tiles_y = int(math.ceil(image_height / tile_size))
    tiles = [[[] for _ in range(num_tiles_x)] for _ in range(num_tiles_y)]
    
    for i in range(N):
        u_i = u[i]
        Sigma_2d_i = Sigma_2d_list[i]
        
        # Determine bounding box in pixels
        # Approximate bounding box using 2 sigma
        # Eigenvalues of Sigma_2d
        eigvals = np.linalg.eigvals(Sigma_2d_i)
        max_std = math.sqrt(max(eigvals)) * 2.0
        
        min_x = max(0, int(u_i[0] - max_std))
        max_x = min(image_width - 1, int(u_i[0] + max_std))
        min_y = max(0, int(u_i[1] - max_std))
        max_y = min(image_height - 1, int(u_i[1] + max_std))
        
        tile_x_min = min_x // tile_size
        tile_x_max = max_x // tile_size
        tile_y_min = min_y // tile_size
        tile_y_max = max_y // tile_size
        
        for ty in range(tile_y_min, tile_y_max + 1):
            for tx in range(tile_x_min, tile_x_max + 1):
                tiles[ty][tx].append(i)
                
    # Step 5: Sorting
    # Sort Gaussians in each tile by distance to detector (tz)
    for ty in range(num_tiles_y):
        for tx in range(num_tiles_x):
            tile_gaussians = tiles[ty][tx]
            # Sort by t[i][2]
            tile_gaussians.sort(key=lambda idx: t[idx][2])
            tiles[ty][tx] = tile_gaussians
            
    # Step 6: Intensity Calculation
    I = np.zeros((image_height, image_width))
    
    for ty in range(num_tiles_y):
        for tx in range(num_tiles_x):
            tile_gaussians = tiles[ty][tx]
            
            # Determine pixel range for this tile
            y_start = ty * tile_size
            y_end = min((ty + 1) * tile_size, image_height)
            x_start = tx * tile_size
            x_end = min((tx + 1) * tile_size, image_width)
            
            for y in range(y_start, y_end):
                for x in range(x_start, x_end):
                    # Check if pixel (x,y) is inside the Gaussian's 2D projection
                    # This is expensive to do naively for every pixel. 
                    # In a real CUDA implementation, this is done via rasterization of the ellipse.
                    # Here we approximate by checking if the pixel is within the bounding box 
                    # and potentially inside the ellipse.
                    
                    # For simplicity in this Python pseudocode, we assume all Gaussians 
                    # in the tile contribute to all pixels in the tile, 
                    # which is incorrect but follows the "sum over N ordered points overlapping the pixel" logic 
                    # if we assume the tile assignment is the only filter.
                    # A correct implementation would check if (x,y) is inside the ellipse defined by u_i and Sigma_2d_i.
                    
                    # Correct approach:
                    total_intensity = 0.0
                    for idx in tile_gaussians:
                        u_i = u[idx]
                        Sigma_2d_i = Sigma_2d_list[idx]
                        
                        # Check if pixel (x,y) is inside the ellipse
                        # Vector from center to pixel
                        v = np.array([x - u_i[0], y - u_i[1]])
                        
                        # Mahalanobis distance squared
                        try:
                            dist_sq = v @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma_2d_i) @ v
                        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
                            dist_sq = float('inf')
                            
                        if dist_sq <= 4.0: # 2 sigma threshold
                            # Calculate probability
                            mu_i = gaussians[idx]['mu']
                            Sigma_i = gaussians[idx]['Sigma']
                            
                            # We need the 3D intersection point x_j.
                            # The source says x_j is the j-th intersection 3D point of the X-ray landing on pixel p.
                            # In X-ray geometry, the ray is from source through pixel p.
                            # The intersection with the Gaussian is approximated by the center projection or ray-Gaussian intersection.
                            # The source uses P(x_j | mu, Sigma). 
                            # If we assume x_j is the point on the ray closest to mu_i or the projection of mu_i onto the ray?
                            # The source formula for I(p) sums i_j, where i_j is radiation intensity of x_j.
                            # And sigma_j = alpha_j * P(x_j | mu_j, Sigma_j).
                            # It seems the "probability" term acts as the weight.
                            
                            # Let's assume x_j is the 3D point corresponding to the pixel p along the ray.
                            # For a pinhole camera model, the ray direction can be computed.
                            # However, the source does not provide the exact formula for x_j.
                            # It defines P(x | mu, Sigma) generally.
                            # In the context of rasterization, often the center mu_i is used if it's close, 
                            # or the intersection is computed.
                            # Given the lack of detail on x_j, we use the probability at the projected center 
                            # as a proxy, or assume x_j is the point on the ray.
                            # Let's use the probability at the projected center for the weight, 
                            # and the feature-based intensity for the magnitude.
                            
                            # Probability at the 3D center mu_i? Or at the intersection?
                            # The source says "P(x_j | mu_j, Sigma_j)".
                            # If we assume the Gaussian is thin, x_j ~ mu_i projected.
                            # Let's calculate P at mu_i for simplicity, as x_j is not explicitly defined.
                            # Actually, the standard 3DGS uses the 2D probability.
                            # Here, the source defines P in 3D.
                            
                            # Let's calculate the 3D probability at the center mu_i.
                            # This is a simplification.
                            try:
                                diff = mu_i - mu_i # 0 vector? No, x_j is the intersection.
                                # If x_j is not defined, we cannot compute P(x_j).
                                # However, the source table shows sigma_j = alpha_j * P(x_j | mu_j, Sigma_j).
                                # And I(p) = sum i_j.
                                # It is likely that i_j = alpha_j * P(x_j | mu_j, Sigma_j) * intensity_from_features.
                                # Let's assume x_j is the 3D point on the ray.
                                # Without the ray equation, we cannot compute x_j.
                                # We will assume the probability is evaluated at the center mu_i for the purpose of this pseudocode,
                                # noting that this is a gap [G].
                                diff = mu_i - mu_i # Placeholder for x_j - mu_i
                                # This is incorrect. 
                                # Let's use the 2D probability as a proxy for the weight, as is common in splatting.
                                # P_2d = exp(-0.5 * v @ Sigma_2d_inv @ v)
                                P_2d = math.exp(-0.5 * dist_sq)
                                
                                # Radiation intensity from features
                                # The source says "inner product between a learnable vector ... and a set of basis weights".
                                # We don't have the basis weights. We assume the intensity is stored or computed from f.
                                # Let's assume i_j = alpha_j * P_2d * intensity(f_j).
                                # Since intensity(f_j) is not defined, we assume it's 1 or part of the feature.
                                # Let's assume the "radiation intensity" i_j is simply alpha_j * P_2d.
                                
                                i_j = gaussians[idx]['alpha'] * P_2d
                                total_intensity += i_j
                            except:
                                pass
                    I[y, x] = total_intensity
                    
    return I
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** The Jacobian calculation involves division by $t_z^2$ and $t_z^3$ (implicitly in the term $\frac{t_y}{t_z^2}$). If $t_z$ is close to zero, numerical instability occurs. Gaussians with $t_z \le 0$ (behind the camera/source) should be discarded [S1].
- **Boundaries:** The bounding box calculation for tile assignment must be clamped to the image boundaries [S1].
- **Degenerate Input:** If $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$ is singular, the inverse in the probability calculation will fail. Regularization or checking for positive definiteness is required [G].
- **X-ray Geometry:** The definition of the intersection point $\mathbf{x}_j$ is not explicitly detailed in the source beyond being the "intersection 3D point of the X-ray landing on pixel p". Implementers must define the ray equation from the X-ray source through the pixel to find $\mathbf{x}_j$ for the probability evaluation [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Linear in the number of Gaussians $N_p$ and the number of pixels, assuming efficient tile-based culling. The sorting step is $O(N_p \log N_p)$ per tile.
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_p)$ to store Gaussian parameters and tile lists.
- **Measured Performance:** The forward and backward processes of the X-Gaussian model (which uses DRR) are much faster than those of the original 3DGS due to the avoidance of complex spherical harmonic (SH) function computations [S1].
- **Strengths:** GPU-friendly CUDA implementation; fast rendering for X-ray novel view synthesis; differentiable for optimization of Gaussian parameters [S1].
- **Limitations:** Specific to X-ray imaging geometry; requires accurate knowledge of $L_{SD}$ and $\phi$; relies on the ACUI initialization strategy for camera calibration and Gaussian placement [S1].
- **When to use:** When rendering novel X-ray projections from a radiative Gaussian point cloud.
- **When not to use:** For standard optical RGB rendering; when X-ray source parameters are unknown.

## Background
Differentiable Radiative Rasterization (DRR) was introduced in the paper "Radiative Gaussian Splatting for Efficient X-Ray Novel View Synthesis" [S1]. It is part of the X-Gaussian framework, which adapts 3D Gaussian Splatting for X-ray imaging. The method was developed to enable efficient training and rendering of X-ray projections without running Structure-from-Motion (SfM) algorithms, using a custom Angle-pose Cuboid Uniform Initialization (ACUI) strategy [S1].
