# 3D Gaussian Splatting Rasterization (Cone-Beam/Scanner Space)
Also called: Cone-beam Gaussian Rasterization, Radiative Gaussian Projection, Transformation module in X-ray rasterization, X-ray Rasterization

## Overview
This method renders X-ray projections from a 3D Gaussian representation by integrating the 3D Gaussian density along cone-beam ray paths, resulting in a sum of 2D Gaussians rather than the alpha-compositing used in standard visible-light rendering [S1]. It solves the problem of simulating X-ray CT projections from learned 3D Gaussian fields by adapting the rasterization pipeline to a pinhole camera model, enabling analytical integration of radiative density [S1]. Unlike standard 3D Gaussian Splatting which composites opacities, this method sums projected 2D densities, fundamentally addressing integration bias issues where learning integrated density in 2D leads to inconsistencies in 3D density retrieval [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A set of $M$ 3D Gaussians defined by position $p_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, covariance $\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, and 3D density $\rho_i \in \mathbb{R}$ [S1].
*   **Input:** Scanner parameters: Detector physical size $(D_x, D_y)$, Source-to-Detector distance $LSD$, Source-to-Object distance $LSO$, and rotation angle $\theta$ [S1].
*   **Output:** A 2D projection image $I_r$ where each pixel value is the integrated density along the corresponding ray [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **3D Gaussian:** Stores $p_i$ (3 floats), $\Sigma_i$ (6 floats, symmetric positive definite), $\rho_i$ (1 float).
*   **Scanner Space:** A coordinate system with origin at the X-ray source and z-axis pointing to the projection center [S1].
*   **Ray Space:** A local coordinate system for each Gaussian where viewing rays are parallel to the third coordinate axis, facilitating analytical integration [S1].

**Steps:**

1.  **Calculate Field of View (FOV):**
    Compute the horizontal and vertical fields of view using the pinhole camera model formulation [S1]:
    $$
    \text{FOV}_x = 2 \cdot \arctan\left(\frac{D_x}{2 \cdot LSD}\right), \quad \text{FOV}_y = 2 \cdot \arctan\left(\frac{D_y}{2 \cdot LSD}\right)
    $$
    where $D_x, D_y$ are the physical detector dimensions and $LSD$ is the source-to-detector distance [S1].

2.  **Transform to Scanner Space:**
    Define the transformation matrix $T$ from world space to scanner space [S1]:
    $$
    T = \begin{bmatrix} W & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    where $W$ and $t$ are defined as [S1]:
    $$
    W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad t = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ LSO \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    Here, $\theta$ is the rotation angle of the X-ray source/detector around the z-axis, and $LSO$ is the distance between the source and the object [S1].
    Transform the Gaussian position:
    $$
    \tilde{p}_i = T \cdot p_i \quad (\text{homogeneous coordinates implied})
    $$
    [S1].

3.  **Local Affine Approximation (Ray Space):**
    Apply a local affine transformation to each Gaussian to map it to ray space. This involves computing the Jacobian $J_i$ of the projective mapping $\phi$ (following Ewa Splatting [69]) [S1].
    Transform the covariance matrix [S1]:
    $$
    \tilde{\Sigma}_i = J_i W \Sigma_i W^\top J_i^\top
    $$
    The new position in ray space is $\tilde{p}_i = \phi(p_i)$ [S1].

4.  **Project to 2D Gaussian:**
    Integrate the 3D Gaussian along the ray path (z-axis in ray space). This is achieved by dropping the third row and column of the transformed position $\tilde{p}_i$ and covariance $\tilde{\Sigma}_i$ to obtain the 2D parameters $\hat{p}_i$ and $\hat{\Sigma}_i$ [S1]:
    $$
    \hat{p}_i = [\tilde{p}_{i,x}, \tilde{p}_{i,y}]^\top, \quad \hat{\Sigma}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{\Sigma}_{i,xx} & \tilde{\Sigma}_{i,xy} \\ \tilde{\Sigma}_{i,yx} & \tilde{\Sigma}_{i,yy} \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    [S1].

5.  **Compute Projected Density:**
    Calculate the central density $\hat{\rho}_i$ of the resulting 2D Gaussian. Unlike standard 3DGS, this method scales the original 3D density $\rho_i$ by a factor $\mu_i$ derived from the determinants of the covariances [S1]:
    $$
    \mu_i = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi |\tilde{\Sigma}_i|}{|\hat{\Sigma}_i|}}
    $$
    $$
    \hat{\rho}_i = \mu_i \rho_i
    $$
    [S1]. This scaling ensures that the method assigns the actual 3D density to the kernel, avoiding the integration bias present in standard 3DGS where the learned density is view-dependent [S1].

6.  **Rasterize:**
    Render the projection by summing the contributions of all 2D Gaussians. The pixel value $I_r(r)$ is the sum of the 2D Gaussian densities [S1]:
    $$
    I_r(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} G_{2i}(\hat{x} | \hat{\rho}_i, \hat{p}_i, \hat{\Sigma}_i)
    $$
    where $G_{2i}$ is the normalized 2D Gaussian distribution [S1]:
    $$
    G_{2i}(\hat{x}) = \frac{\hat{\rho}_i}{2\pi |\hat{\Sigma}_i|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{x} - \hat{p}_i)^\top \hat{\Sigma}_i^{-1} (\hat{x} - \hat{p}_i)\right)
    $$
    [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def cone_beam_rasterization(gaussians, scanner_params, image_shape):
    """
    Rasterize 3D Gaussians into an X-ray projection using cone-beam geometry.
    
    Args:
        gaussians: List of dicts with keys 'p' (3,), 'Sigma' (3,3), 'rho' (float).
        scanner_params: Dict with keys 'Dx', 'Dy', 'LSD', 'LSO', 'theta'.
        image_shape: Tuple (height, width) for the output detector plane.
        
    Returns:
        projection: 2D numpy array of shape (height, width).
    """
    Dx = scanner_params['Dx']
    Dy = scanner_params['Dy']
    LSD = scanner_params['LSD']
    LSO = scanner_params['LSO']
    theta = scanner_params['theta']
    
    # Step 1: Define Transformation Matrix T
    cos_t = np.cos(theta)
    sin_t = np.sin(theta)
    W = np.array([
        [1, 0, 0],
        [0, -cos_t, -sin_t],
        [0, sin_t, -cos_t]
    ])
    t = np.array([0, LSO, 0])
    
    # Initialize projection image
    projection = np.zeros(image_shape)
    
    # Precompute detector pixel coordinates relative to center
    # Assuming origin of image is center, map to physical coordinates
    # Note: Exact pixel-to-physical mapping depends on specific implementation details
    # not fully specified in [S1], but standard pinhole projection is implied.
    # We assume image_shape is (H, W) and we iterate over pixels.
    
    # To optimize, we can iterate over Gaussians and splat to image.
    # However, standard rasterization iterates pixels or uses tile-based approach.
    # Given the analytical nature, we compute 2D Gaussian parameters for each Gaussian
    # and then rasterize them.
    
    projected_gaussians = []
    
    for g in gaussians:
        p_i = np.array(g['p'])
        Sigma_i = np.array(g['Sigma'])
        rho_i = g['rho']
        
        # Step 2: Transform to Scanner Space
        # p_tilde = W * p_i + t
        p_tilde = W @ p_i + t
        
        # Step 3: Local Affine Approximation
        # J_i is the Jacobian of the projective mapping phi.
        # [S1] references Eq. 29 in [69] (Ewa Splatting).
        # The exact formula for J_i in cone-beam geometry is complex and depends on p_tilde.
        # For a pinhole camera, the projection is x = f * X / Z.
        # The Jacobian involves the depth Z.
        # Since [S1] does not provide the explicit formula for J_i, we note it must be computed.
        # [G] Assuming standard pinhole projection Jacobian for local affine approximation:
        # If p_tilde = [X, Y, Z], the projection to image plane at distance LSD is:
        # x_img = LSD * X / Z, y_img = LSD * Y / Z.
        # The Jacobian J maps world derivatives to image derivatives.
        # However, [S1] states J_i is from Eq 29 in [69]. 
        # We will compute Sigma_tilde using the provided formula.
        
        # Compute Sigma_tilde
        # Sigma_tilde = J_i * W * Sigma_i * W.T * J_i.T
        # We need J_i. 
        # [G] In Ewa Splatting [69], J is the Jacobian of the projection function.
        # For a point p in camera space [X,Y,Z], projected to [u,v]:
        # u = fx * X/Z, v = fy * Y/Z.
        # The Jacobian J (2x3) is:
        # [ fx/Z, 0,   -fx*X/Z^2 ]
        # [ 0,   fy/Z, -fy*Y/Z^2 ]
        # We need focal lengths fx, fy derived from FOV.
        
        # Compute Focal Lengths from FOV
        # FOV_x = 2 * arctan(Dx / (2 * LSD))
        # Image width in pixels is W_img.
        # fx = (W_img / 2) / tan(FOV_x / 2) = (W_img / 2) * (LSD / (Dx/2)) = W_img * LSD / Dx
        # Similarly fy = H_img * LSD / Dy
        
        H_img, W_img = image_shape
        fx = W_img * LSD / Dx
        fy = H_img * LSD / Dy
        
        X, Y, Z = p_tilde
        if Z <= 0:
            continue # Gaussian is behind the source
            
        # Compute Jacobian J (2x3)
        J = np.array([
            [fx / Z, 0, -fx * X / (Z**2)],
            [0, fy / Z, -fy * Y / (Z**2)]
        ])
        
        # Compute Sigma_tilde
        Sigma_tilde = J @ W @ Sigma_i @ W.T @ J.T
        
        # Step 4: Project to 2D
        # Drop 3rd row/col
        p_hat = p_tilde[:2]
        Sigma_hat = Sigma_tilde[:2, :2]
        
        # Check if Sigma_hat is valid (positive definite)
        if np.linalg.det(Sigma_hat) <= 0:
            continue
            
        # Step 5: Compute Projected Density
        det_tilde = np.linalg.det(Sigma_tilde)
        det_hat = np.linalg.det(Sigma_hat)
        
        # mu_i = sqrt(2 * pi * |Sigma_tilde| / |Sigma_hat|)
        mu_i = np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * det_tilde / det_hat)
        rho_hat = mu_i * rho_i
        
        projected_gaussians.append({
            'p': p_hat,
            'Sigma': Sigma_hat,
            'rho': rho_hat
        })
        
    # Step 6: Rasterize 2D Gaussians
    # Standard 2D Gaussian rasterization (alpha compositing or density sum)
    # [S1] states: "summing 2D Gaussians instead of alpha-compositing"
    # This implies a density accumulation, not opacity blending.
    
    for g_2d in projected_gaussians:
        p_2d = g_2d['p']
        Sigma_2d = g_2d['Sigma']
        rho_2d = g_2d['rho']
        
        # Compute bounding box for this Gaussian
        # Eigenvalues of Sigma_2d
        eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Sigma_2d)
        # Standard deviation in principal axes
        std_x = np.sqrt(eigenvalues[0])
        std_y = np.sqrt(eigenvalues[1])
        
        # Bounding box radius (e.g., 3 sigma)
        radius_x = 3 * std_x
        radius_y = 3 * std_y
        
        # Map to pixel coordinates
        # Note: p_2d is in physical units? 
        # In the derivation, x_hat is in the image plane coordinates.
        # The Jacobian J maps to image coordinates (pixels) if fx, fy are in pixels.
        # So p_2d is in pixel coordinates.
        
        min_x = int(np.floor(p_2d[0] - radius_x))
        max_x = int(np.ceil(p_2d[0] + radius_x))
        min_y = int(np.floor(p_2d[1] - radius_y))
        max_y = int(np.ceil(p_2d[1] + radius_y))
        
        # Clip to image bounds
        min_x = max(0, min_x)
        max_x = min(W_img, max_x)
        min_y = max(0, min_y)
        max_y = min(H_img, max_y)
        
        if min_x >= max_x or min_y >= max_y:
            continue
            
        # Inverse covariance for Gaussian evaluation
        Sigma_inv = np.linalg.inv(Sigma_2d)
        norm_const = rho_2d / (2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(np.linalg.det(Sigma_2d)))
        
        for y in range(min_y, max_y):
            for x in range(min_x, max_x):
                diff = np.array([x - p_2d[0], y - p_2d[1]])
                exponent = -0.5 * diff @ Sigma_inv @ diff
                value = norm_const * np.exp(exponent)
                projection[y, x] += value
                
    return projection
```

**Parameters:**
*   $LSD$ (Source-to-Detector Distance): Not given in the sources.
*   $LSO$ (Source-to-Object Distance): Not given in the sources.
*   $\theta$ (Rotation Angle): Not given in the sources.
*   $D_x, D_y$ (Detector Size): Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Integration Bias:** If standard 3DGS is used, learning the integrated density in the 2D image plane leads to inconsistency in density retrieval because different views yield different results for the same 3D density [S1]. This method avoids that by scaling $\rho_i$ with $\mu_i$ [S1].
*   **Depth Zero:** If $Z \le 0$ in scanner space, the Gaussian is behind the source and should be skipped [G].
*   **Singular Covariance:** If $\det(\hat{\Sigma}_i) \le 0$, the 2D projection is degenerate and should be skipped [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(M \cdot N)$ where $M$ is the number of Gaussians and $N$ is the number of pixels covered by the Gaussians. The transformation and projection steps are $O(M)$.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(M)$ to store Gaussian parameters. The output image is $O(H \cdot W)$.
*   **Strengths:** Provides physically accurate X-ray projections by integrating density along rays. Solves the integration bias problem of standard 3DGS for tomographic reconstruction [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires accurate scanner geometry parameters. The local affine approximation may introduce errors for large Gaussians or extreme angles [S1].
*   **When to use:** When simulating X-ray CT projections from 3D Gaussian fields.
*   **When not to use:** For standard visible-light rendering where alpha-compositing is appropriate.

## Background
This method was introduced in "R$^2$-Gaussian: Rectifying Radiative Gaussian Splatting for Tomographic Reconstruction" [S1]. It adapts the 3D Gaussian Splatting framework to cone-beam CT geometry, treating the X-ray scanner as a pinhole camera [S1]. The transformation module is based on the Ewa Splatting approach [69] for local affine approximation.
