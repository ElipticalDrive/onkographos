# EWA Splatting

Also called: EWA Volume Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA splatting

## Overview
EWA Splatting is a high-quality volume rendering technique that eliminates aliasing artifacts by replacing the standard footprint function with a band-limited resampling filter [S1, unofficial][S2][S8, S10, unofficial]. It solves the problem of incorrect visibility determination and high-frequency sampling errors inherent in basic splatting, where reconstruction kernels are assumed non-overlapping [S1, S8, unofficial]. Unlike its siblings [Splatting Without the Blur](1157-splatting-without-the-blur.md) and [Splat Radius Adjustment Based on Normal Angle](1264-splat-radius-adjustment-based-on-normal-angle.md), which rely on isotropic or angle-adjusted approximations, EWA Splatting computes anisotropic, perspective-correct 2D Gaussian footprints by convolving a 3D elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with a 2D low-pass filter [S2][S8, S15, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Volume data represented as a set of particles (voxels), each with a position $\mathbf{p}_k$, extinction coefficient $g_k$, emission color $c_{\lambda k}$, and a 3D elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel defined by a variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ in object space. Camera parameters: viewing transformation matrix $\mathbf{W}$ (rotational part) and projection matrix.
**Outputs:** A 2D image with intensity values $I_\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{x}})$.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Particle $k$:**
    *   $\mathbf{p}_k$: 3D position in object space.
    *   $g_k$: Extinction coefficient (scalar).
    *   $c_{\lambda k}$: Emission color (RGB vector).
    *   $\mathbf{V}_k$: 3x3 symmetric positive-definite variance matrix in object space.
*   **Camera/Projection:**
    *   $\mathbf{W}$: 3x3 rotation matrix mapping object space to camera space.
    *   $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$: 3x3 Jacobian matrix of the perspective projection at camera-space point $\mathbf{u}_k$.
*   **Ray Space:**
    *   $\mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1, x_2)^T$: Coordinates where $x_0, x_1$ define the projection plane and $x_2$ is the distance from the center of projection.
    *   $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (x_0, x_1)^T$: 2D screen/image space coordinates.

**Parameters:**
*   $r$: Cutoff radius for Gaussian truncation. Typical value: $1 \leq r \leq 3$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   $\mathbf{V}_h$: 2x2 variance matrix of the low-pass filter. Usually the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   Assumptions: Reconstruction kernels do not overlap along a ray (for attenuation approximation); emission coefficient is constant within the support of each footprint [S8, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Transform to Camera Space:**
    For each particle $k$, compute its position in camera space $\mathbf{u}_k$:
    $$ \mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{W} (\mathbf{p}_k - \mathbf{t}_{cam}) $$
    where $\mathbf{t}_{cam}$ is the camera center (origin in camera space). The image plane is at $u_2 = 1$ [S6, unofficial][S11].

2.  **Compute Jacobian:**
    Calculate the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$ of the perspective projection at $\mathbf{u}_k$:
    $$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & 0 & -\frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & -\frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ \frac{u_{k_0}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_1}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_2}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \end{pmatrix} $$
    where $\|\mathbf{u}_k\| = \sqrt{u_{k_0}^2 + u_{k_1}^2 + u_{k_2}^2}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].

3.  **Transform Variance to Image Space:**
    Transform the object-space variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ to image space $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ (2x2) using the local affine approximation:
    $$ \hat{\mathbf{V}}_k = \text{skip\_third\_row\_col} \left( \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{V}_k \mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T \right) $$
    The notation $\hat{\mathbf{V}}$ denotes the 2x2 matrix obtained by removing the third row and column of the 3x3 result [S6, unofficial][S11][S8, unofficial].

4.  **Compute Resampling Filter Parameters:**
    Combine the transformed reconstruction variance with the low-pass filter variance $\mathbf{V}_h$:
    $$ \mathbf{M}_k = (\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h)^{-1} $$
    Compute the normalization factor:
    $$ \text{norm}_k = \frac{1}{2\pi |\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}| |\mathbf{W}^{-1}| |\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h|^{\frac{1}{2}}} $$
    Note: $|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}|$ is the determinant of the inverse Jacobian, and $|\mathbf{W}^{-1}|$ is the determinant of the inverse rotation matrix [S6, unofficial][S11][S8, unofficial].

5.  **Project to Screen Space:**
    Compute the 2D screen position $\mathbf{x}_k$ of the particle:
    $$ \mathbf{x}_k = \left( \frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}}, \frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}} \right) $$
    This is the perspective division of $\mathbf{u}_k$ [S6, unofficial][S11][S8, unofficial].

6.  **Rasterize the Splat:**
    For each pixel $(x, y)$ within the bounding box of the projected ellipse defined by $\mathbf{M}_k$:
    a. Calculate the distance squared:
       $$ d^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k) $$
    b. If $d^2 \leq r^2$, compute the footprint value:
       $$ \rho_k(x, y) = \text{norm}_k \cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2} d^2} $$
    c. Compute the attenuation factor $o_k$. Assuming self-occlusion is ignored and kernels are ordered front-to-back, this is approximated as constant for the support of the current splat [S8, unofficial].
    d. Compute the contribution to the pixel:
       $$ \Delta I = c_{\lambda k} \cdot g_k \cdot \rho_k(x, y) \cdot o_k $$
    e. Accumulate $\Delta I$ into the pixel buffer using back-to-front compositing (alpha blending) [S6, unofficial][S11][S8, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def ewa_splatting(volume_particles, W, projection_matrix, image_width, image_height, r=2.0, Vh=None):
    """
    Renders volume particles using EWA Splatting.
    
    Args:
        volume_particles: List of dicts with keys:
            'p': (3,) object space position
            'g': scalar extinction
            'c': (3,) emission color
            'V': (3,3) object space variance matrix
        W: (3,3) rotation matrix (object to camera)
        projection_matrix: (4,4) OpenGL-style projection matrix
        image_width, image_height: int
        r: cutoff radius
        Vh: (2,2) low-pass filter variance, default identity
        
    Returns:
        image: (image_height, image_width, 3) float array
    """
    if Vh is None:
        Vh = np.eye(2)
        
    # Initialize image buffer (assume pre-multiplied alpha or simple accumulation for simplicity)
    # Note: Full EWA requires back-to-front sorting and alpha blending.
    # For this implementation, we assume particles are sorted front-to-back in view space.
    image = np.zeros((image_height, image_width, 3))
    
    # Extract camera center from projection matrix if needed, 
    # but typically W handles rotation and translation is separate.
    # Assuming W maps object to camera, and camera origin is at (0,0,0).
    
    for particle in volume_particles:
        p = particle['p']
        g = particle['g']
        c = particle['c']
        V_obj = particle['V']
        
        # 1. Transform to Camera Space
        # Assuming p is relative to camera center or W includes translation?
        # Standard W is rotational part. Let's assume p is already in camera space 
        # or we have a full view matrix. The notes define W as rotational part.
        # Let's assume u_k = W * p for simplicity if p is relative to camera center.
        # If p is world space, we need full view matrix. 
        # Based on notes: "W maps object space to camera space coordinates". 
        # Usually this implies rotation only, but let's assume u_k is computed correctly.
        u_k = W @ p # Simplified, assuming p is relative to camera center or W is full view rotation
        
        u0, u1, u2 = u_k
        
        if u2 <= 0:
            continue # Behind camera
            
        # 2. Compute Jacobian
        norm_u = np.linalg.norm(u_k)
        J = np.array([
            [1.0/u2, 0, -u0/(u2**2)],
            [0, 1.0/u2, -u1/(u2**2)],
            [u0/norm_u, u1/norm_u, u2/norm_u]
        ])
        
        # 3. Transform Variance to Image Space
        # V_img_full = J @ W @ V_obj @ W.T @ J.T
        # Note: The notes say V_k in object space is transformed by J_u_k * W * V_k * W^T * J_u_k^T
        # But W is already applied to get u_k? 
        # Let's follow the formula: V_hat_k = skip(J @ W @ V_obj @ W.T @ J.T)
        # If W is the rotational part of the view matrix, and p is in object space:
        # u_k = W @ p (if p is relative to camera center)
        # The formula in notes: V_hat_k = skip(J_u_k * W * V_k * W^T * J_u_k^T)
        # This implies V_k is in object space, and we transform it to image space.
        
        V_cam = W @ V_obj @ W.T
        V_img_full = J @ V_cam @ J.T
        
        # Skip third row and column
        V_hat_k = V_img_full[:2, :2]
        
        # 4. Compute Resampling Filter Parameters
        M_k = np.linalg.inv(V_hat_k + Vh)
        
        # Determinants
        det_J_inv = 1.0 / np.linalg.det(J)
        det_W_inv = 1.0 / np.linalg.det(W)
        det_V_sum = np.linalg.det(V_hat_k + Vh)
        
        norm_k = 1.0 / (2.0 * np.pi * abs(det_J_inv) * abs(det_W_inv) * np.sqrt(det_V_sum))
        
        # 5. Project to Screen Space
        x_k = np.array([u0 / u2, u1 / u2])
        
        # 6. Rasterize
        # Determine bounding box
        # Eigenvalues of M_k to find extent
        eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvals(M_k)
        # Radius in screen space
        # r_screen = r * sqrt(max(eigenvalues)) ? 
        # The condition is (x-xk)^T M (x-xk) <= r^2
        # Max extent is r * sqrt(1/min(eigenvalues))
        min_eig = np.min(eigenvalues)
        if min_eig <= 0:
            continue
        radius_screen = r / np.sqrt(min_eig)
        
        x_min = int(max(0, x_k[0] - radius_screen))
        x_max = int(min(image_width, x_k[0] + radius_screen))
        y_min = int(max(0, x_k[1] - radius_screen))
        y_max = int(min(image_height, x_k[1] + radius_screen))
        
        for y in range(y_min, y_max):
            for x in range(x_min, x_max):
                x_pix = np.array([x, y])
                diff = x_pix - x_k
                d_sq = diff @ M_k @ diff
                
                if d_sq <= r**2:
                    rho = norm_k * np.exp(-0.5 * d_sq)
                    # Attenuation factor o_k is approximated as constant
                    # In a full pipeline, this would be accumulated.
                    # Here we assume simple accumulation for demonstration of EWA footprint
                    # Real EWA uses back-to-front alpha blending.
                    # Contribution
                    contrib = c * g * rho
                    
                    # Simple accumulation (not correct for transparency, but shows EWA weight)
                    # Correct compositing:
                    # alpha = 1 - exp(-g * rho) ? No, rho is the integrated footprint.
                    # The notes say: I = sum c g q o.
                    # q is the footprint.
                    # We add to image.
                    image[y, x] += contrib
                    
    return image
```

## Subtopics

### [Deferred Surface Splatting](1072-deferred-surface-splatting.md)
Deferred Surface Splatting converts unstructured point clouds into continuous surfaces by projecting points as splats into a deferred pipeline. It differs from EWA Splatting by using screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff rather than anisotropic, correctly band-limited EWA footprints, trading accurate filtering for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii.

### [Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS)](1155-opacity-thin-side-ots.md)
Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS) is a parameterization scheme for EWA-based splatting that ensures robust gradient-descent optimization by dynamically scaling the learned weight $\theta$ such that the projected amplitude $a'$ equals $\theta$ when viewing the Gaussian along its thinnest side. It differs from standard EWA Splatting, which preserves the integral of extinction (mass), by preserving the 3D unbounded integral (view-independent mass) while ensuring the 2D projection behaves as an ordinary integral relative to the thinnest view.

### [OTS (EWA-Based Splatting)](1181-ots-ewa-based-splatting.md)
OTS (Opacity-Thin-Side) is a splatting method using unnormalized Gaussians that preserves the 2D amplitude $a'$ (called 'opacity') across projections, unlike EWA Splatting which preserves the weight $w$. It differs from standard EWA Splatting by requiring a Jacobian determinant correction to preserve the integral of the extinction coefficient under perspective projection.

### [GPU-Accelerated Volume Splatting With Elliptical RBFs](1186-gpu-accelerated-volume-splatting-with-elliptical-rbfs.md)
This method renders scattered volumetric data represented as 3D ellipsoidal Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) using a post-shaded, kernel-centric approach. It extends high-quality image-aligned sheet-buffer splatting to irregular grids by slicing kernels into parallel, image-aligned density planes, rasterizing them as 2D footprints, and compositing them. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by supporting general ellipsoidal kernels and using a flat spatial decomposition with active cell lists rather than screen-aligned spherical impostors.

### [Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing](1290-ellipsoidal-kernel-splatting-with-warped-slicing.md)
Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing is a volume rendering technique that represents point samples as 3D ellipsoids and rasterizes them by slicing the ellipsoid into 2D polygons. It solves the problem of efficiently computing the intersection of general ellipsoids with screen-aligned slicing planes on GPU hardware, where direct analytical slicing requires expensive square roots and conditional statements. The method warps the slicing planes from screen space into the coordinate system of a unit sphere, allowing the use of hard-wired GPU texture mapping and blending on radially symmetric kernel slices. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by explicitly slicing the kernel into discrete polygons for incremental rasterization rather than rasterizing the entire projected footprint, and it avoids the pre-integrated slab approach of earlier image-aligned splatting by using point-sampled slices.

### [Perspective Correct Splat Rasterization with EWA Approximation](2095-perspective-correct-splat-rasterization-with-ewa-approximati.md)
This method is an improved splat rendering pipeline that combines a perspectively correct fast splat rasterization procedure with an approximation of the Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts and visual holes in point-sampled geometry rendering, particularly when the model is viewed from flat angles or shifted from the viewing axis. Unlike its siblings, it uses an affine approximation to the projective mapping to ensure perspective-correct splat shapes without requiring per-point normals or complex anisotropic footprint calculations, trading exact band-limited footprints for a formulation that avoids holes and Moiré patterns. It builds on [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by replacing the standard EWA covariance computation with a faster, perspective-correct affine approximation.

### [Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting](2137-adaptive-ewa-volume-splatting.md)
Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting is a rendering technique that simplifies the computationally expensive Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling filter by classifying volume particles into three cases based on their distance from the viewpoint. It solves the problem of high computational cost in full EWA splatting while maintaining aliasing-free quality. Unlike full EWA splatting, which always computes the combined covariance of the reconstruction and low-pass filters, this method uses only the reconstruction filter for close particles (magnification) and only the low-pass filter for far particles (minification), resorting to full EWA only in the transition zone.

### [EWA Approximation with Tangent Vector Adjustment](2143-ewa-approximation-with-tangent-vector-adjustment.md)
A high-quality filtering technique for point-based rendering that approximates the EWA resampling filter by dynamically adjusting the tangent vectors of isotropic splats during setup to prevent aliasing during minification, without requiring expensive per-fragment convolution. It sets itself apart from the original EWA framework by using projective mapping and tangent adjustment rather than affine mapping and Gaussians for both reconstruction and low-pass filters, and from Botsch et al. [BHZK05] by avoiding per-fragment filter evaluation, thus eliminating the fragment shader bottleneck.

### [Phong Splatting](2802-phong-splatting.md)
Phong Splatting is a surface splatting technique that assigns a linearly varying normal field to each elliptical splat, enabling per-pixel Phong shading without surface connectivity. It solves the piecewise constant shading artifacts of standard point-based rendering by providing smooth, Phong-like shading quality on modern GPUs. Unlike its sibling [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md), which uses a simplified isotropic kernel, Phong Splatting uses elliptical splats with projectively correct per-pixel parameterization, trading the simplicity of isotropic filtering for superior visual quality and exact depth values.

### EWA Surface Splatting
EWA Surface Splatting is a method where EWA volume splats reduce to surface splats suitable for high-quality iso-surface rendering by flattening the 3D Gaussian kernel along the volume gradient [S9, unofficial]. For each point in object-space, quadrilaterals are deformed and texture-mapped with a Gaussian texture to result in the correct screen-space EWA splat after projection [S9, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
**Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot A)$, where $N$ is the number of particles and $A$ is the average number of pixels covered by a splat. The computation of the covariance matrix and its inversion is $O(1)$ per particle, but the rasterization of the elliptical footprint is more expensive than isotropic splatting [S6, unofficial][S11].
**Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store particle data and variance matrices. The sheet buffer requires $O(W \cdot H)$ memory [S8, unofficial].
**Measured Performance:** On an 866 MHz Pentium-III, rendering the Skull dataset took approximately 11 seconds per frame [S1, S8, S10, unofficial]. On a GeForce FX Ultra 5900, a 256^2 x 225 CT scan was rendered in real-time [S9, unofficial].
**Strengths:** Eliminates aliasing artifacts without excessive blurring; handles arbitrary elliptical kernels and perspective projection correctly; high image quality [S2][S8, S15, unofficial].
**Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to the evaluation of the 2D Gaussian footprint and matrix inversions; requires sorting particles back-to-front for correct compositing; numerical instability can occur when the projection approaches a line (condition number of the inverse mapping increases) [S1, S8, S12, unofficial].
**When to use:** When high-quality, alias-free volume rendering is required and performance allows for the computational cost of EWA filtering.
**When not to use:** When real-time performance on low-end hardware is critical and aliasing can be tolerated or handled by simpler methods like [Splatting Without the Blur](1157-splatting-without-the-blur.md).

## Background
EWA Splatting was introduced by Matthias Zwicker, Hanspeter Pfister, Jeroen van Baar, and Markus Gross in 2001 [S8, unofficial]. It is named after Heckbert's Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter for texture mapping due to the similarity in derivation [S7, abstract only, unofficial][S8, unofficial]. The method was developed to address the aliasing artifacts present in earlier splatting techniques, which assumed non-overlapping kernels and ignored the change in sampling rate due to perspective projection [S1, S8, unofficial]. It has been widely used in high-quality volume visualization and has influenced subsequent methods like 3D Gaussian Splatting [S3].
