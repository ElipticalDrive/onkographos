# Splatting
## Overview
Splatting is an object-order volume rendering technique that computes the contribution of each voxel to the image by convolving the voxel with a filter (kernel) that distributes its value to a neighborhood of pixels on the screen [S2, unofficial]. It differs from feed-backward (image-order/ray-casting) methods by mapping direction: feed-forward spreads input information to output (object-order), while feed-backward queries input from output pixels [S5, unofficial]. It offers rendering quality similar to ray casting, and both ray casting and splatting provided in some cases better image qualities than shear-warp [S2, unofficial].

## Shared principles
The core mechanism of splatting involves treating the volume as a continuous function defined by smooth kernels centered at each voxel [S10, unofficial]. The rendering process follows these shared steps:

1.  **Kernel Definition:** Each voxel is represented by a 3D reconstruction kernel, typically a Gaussian distribution with mean $\mu = 0.0$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 1.0$, truncated outside a radius of $2.0$ with a peak value of $1.0$ [S10, unofficial]. The contribution of a voxel at position $D$ to a screen point $(x,y,z)$ is given by:
    $$ \text{contribution}_D(x,y,z) = h_V(x-D_x, y-D_y, z-D_z) \cdot \rho(D) $$
    where $h_V$ is the volumetric reconstruction kernel and $\rho(D)$ is the voxel density [S10, unofficial].

2.  **Projection (Splatting):** The 3D kernel is projected onto the 2D image plane, creating a "footprint" or "splat" [S10, unofficial]. The weight of the splat at pixel $(x,y)$ relative to the voxel's projection $(D_x, D_y)$ is:
    $$ \text{weight}(x,y)_D = \text{footprint}(x-D_x, y-D_y) $$
    The intensity written to the pixel is modulated by the voxel density:
    $$ P(x,y) = \text{weight}(x,y) \cdot \rho(D) $$
    [S10, unofficial].

3.  **Footprint Representation:** For regular grids, the footprint is typically a circle invariant to rotations [S7, book]. To avoid aliasing, the opacity is convolved with a smoothing kernel (e.g., Gaussian) [S7, book]. A splat diameter of 1.6 times the spacing between values is sufficient to avoid aliasing while preventing excessive blurring [S7, book]. For perspective projections, the splat size must be adjusted according to the sample's distance from the viewpoint [S7, book]. Efficient representation often uses a screen-aligned quadrilateral texture-mapped with an opacity-weighted texture [S7, book].

4.  **Compositing:** Splats are composited using alpha blending. Depending on the optical model, they must be rendered in front-to-back or back-to-front order [S7, book]. Compositing in splatting is performed along sheets (all pixels at once for a set of voxels), whereas ray-casting composites along rays (one sample at a time) [S10, unofficial].

5.  **Sheet-Based Traversal:** A common implementation traverses the volume one sheet of voxels at a time, from front to back [S10, unofficial]. The sheet (x-y, x-z, or y-z) nearest to being parallel with the image plane is selected [S10, unofficial]. Each voxel in the sheet is transformed from grid space to screen space, shaded independently to produce an $(R,G,B,\alpha)$ tuple, and projected into an accumulation sheet [S10, unofficial]. The accumulation sheet is composited with the working image after all voxels in the sheet are processed [S10, unofficial].

6.  **Shading:** Shading can be performed via pre-shading (classifying/shading voxels before projection) or post-shading [S10, unofficial]. Post-shading maintains three sheet-buffers (previous, current, next), projects 3D kernels into the next buffer, estimates gradients using central differences on the current buffer based on the next and previous buffers, and shades using the normalized gradient [S10, unofficial].

### [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md)
EWA Splatting is an object-order rendering technique that approximates the volumetric integration of 3D Gaussian primitives by projecting them into 2D elliptical disks (splats) and rasterizing them as screen-space filters. It solves the computational cost of ray-marching by assuming Gaussians do not overlap and are sorted by view-space depth, trading continuous extinction accumulation for efficient, hole-free reconstruction. It differs from standard point rendering by using anisotropic, band-limited elliptical footprints derived from the Gaussian covariance, whereas simplified variants use screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic falloff.

### [Ray-Driven Splatting](1156-ray-driven-splatting.md)
A technique merging splatting with raycasting principles, where splats are suspended in object space and rays are spawned to intersect them, enabling perspective projection efficiency and acceleration methods.

### [Footprint Evaluation](1180-footprint-evaluation.md)
A forward mapping rendering algorithm for regular volumetric grids with potentially different spacings in the three grid directions, calculating image plane footprints for each data sample.

### Early Splat Termination
An optimization for volume splatting that skips processing of voxels when the accumulated opacity at the target screen pixels is already full, analogous to early ray termination in ray casting [S10, unofficial]. It works by projecting two volumes: the density volume and an opacity volume [S10, unofficial]. For each projected voxel, a 2D bounding box is computed around the pixels covering the splat [S10, unofficial]. If all pixels inside the bounding box have full opacity (1.0 or near 1.0), the voxel processing is skipped [S10, unofficial].

### Extinction-based splatting
A rendering solution introduced in [S11] that uses extinction (referred to as 'density' in NeRF literature) instead of the learned opacity value used in 3DGS, to analyze the difference between opacity-based and accurate volumetric rendering. For small numbers of primitives, extinction-based splatting performs better than opacity-based splatting. However, with more primitives, opacity splatting performs best [S11].

### Splatting Without the Blur
A volume rendering algorithm that performs classification and shading after voxels are projected onto the screen to avoid blurring object edges, which occurs when classification is done prior to projection [S12, unofficial]. It projects voxel densities onto the screen using regular splats (radially symmetric interpolation kernels) [S12, unofficial]. It projects gradient volumes onto the screen using gradient splats, OR computes gradients on the projection plane using central differencing on the projected density [S12, unofficial]. It performs per-pixel shading using the computed gradients to classify and color the voxels [S12, unofficial]. An optimization uses lookup tables for shading to handle the added overhead of screen gradients and per-pixel shading [S12, unofficial]. Computing gradients on the projection plane via central differencing reduces the number of footprint rasterizations by a factor of four compared to projecting a separate gradient volume, because only voxel densities need to be projected [S12, unofficial].

### Hierarchical Splatting
A progressive refinement algorithm for volume rendering using a pyramidal volume representation to achieve real-time interactive rendering [S12, unofficial]. It constructs a pyramidal volume representation that stores average values and estimated error for each cell [S12, unofficial]. It fits an octtree to the pyramid based on a user-supplied precision threshold [S12, unofficial]. It draws the octtree using a set of splats (footprints), where each splat is scaled to match the size of the projection of the corresponding volume cell [S12, unofficial]. It approximates the splats with RGBA Gouraud-shaded polygons for efficient rasterization on modern graphics workstations [S12, unofficial].

### Splat Radius Adjustment Based on Normal Angle
A method to adjust splat radii to prevent holes between splats when samples are elevated in the normal direction of the base plane, by scaling the radius based on the angle between the splat normal and the base plane normal [S13, unofficial]. The radius $r$ is calculated as:
$$ r = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}\mu}{0.1 + 0.9\langle n, n_B \rangle} $$
where $r$ is the radius, $\mu$ is the sampling step size of the regular grid, $n$ is the normal vector of the splat, and $n_B$ is the normal vector of the base plane [S13, unofficial]. The constant 0.1 in the denominator establishes scaling and prevents division by zero or excessively large radii when the dot product is small [S13, unofficial]. The constant 0.9 in the denominator controls the maximal radius increase; when $\langle n, n_B \rangle = 1$, the denominator is 1.0, and $r = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}\mu$ [S13, unofficial]. If samples are elevated in the normal direction of the plane, holes may occur between splats if radii are not adjusted [S13, unofficial]. A large angle between the splat normal and the plane normal indicates large variance between neighboring elevation values, resulting in increased spatial gaps, necessitating larger radii [S13, unofficial].

### 4D Gaussian Splatting
A method for modeling dynamic Gaussians for time-varying content [S14, unofficial]. It develops a spatiotemporal structure encoder to capture deformations of both positions and colors over time [S14, unofficial].

### Stop-the-pop
A method that mitigates popping artifacts caused by the sorting procedure in volume splatting [S14, unofficial].

### SpotlessSplats
A method that enhances robustness by detecting outliers in a richer, pre-trained feature space to ignore transient distractors [S14, unofficial].

## Background
Splatting was originally proposed by Lee Westover in 1990 [S10, unofficial]. It is based on the idea of treating each voxel as a fuzzy spherical ball (3D Gaussian) projected onto the screen, determining how every pixel might be affected by that voxel, as opposed to ray-casting which determines how every voxel affects a pixel [S10, unofficial]. The continuous volume is defined as a collection of smooth functions (Gaussians) centered at each voxel, with density assigned at the center and dropping off according to the distribution [S10, unofficial]. The term "splat" refers to the footprint left on the image plane, analogous to throwing a cube of gelatinous material onto the plane [S2, unofficial].

Axis-aligned sheet buffered splatting causes 'popping' artifacts when viewing angles change around 45 degrees because kernels in consecutive sheets are composited together while kernels on the same sheet are added together, and addition/compositing are not equivalent [S10, unofficial]. To mitigate this, image-aligned sheet buffered splatting aligns sheet buffers parallel with the image plane regardless of volume orientation, slicing the volume into thin slabs representing portions of the line integral through the entire kernel [S10, unofficial].

Splatting algorithms suffer from time-consuming computations necessary to compute the view-dependent filters with which to convolute the voxels in the volume [S2, unofficial]. Observations showed that overall splatting offers a rendering quality similar to that of ray casting, and both ray casting and splatting provided in some cases better image qualities than shear-warp [S2, unofficial]. This is because in shear-warp, rendered voxels were interpolated without prior opacity and color being incorporated, whereas splatting incorporates these during the footprint projection [S2, unofficial].
