# EWA Splatting

Also called: EWA Volume Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA splatting

## Overview
EWA Volume Splatting is a high-quality direct volume rendering technique that eliminates aliasing artifacts by replacing the standard footprint function with a band-limited resampling filter [S1, unofficial][S2][S8, unofficial]. It solves the problem of incorrect visibility determination and high-frequency sampling errors inherent in basic back-to-front splatting, where non-overlapping kernel assumptions lead to visual holes and moiré patterns [S1, S8, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from its parent [Splat Kernels and Footprints](8724-splat-kernels-and-footprints.md) by analytically combining an elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with a Gaussian low-pass filter to produce an alias-free footprint, and from alternatives like [Phong Splatting](2802-phong-splatting.md) by prioritizing correct antialiasing over per-pixel surface shading [S2][S15, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Volume data represented as a set of particles (voxels or samples), each with a position, a 3D elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel (defined by a variance matrix $\mathbf{V}$ and weight $w$), and an emission coefficient $c_\lambda$. Camera parameters (viewing transformation $\mathbf{W}$, projection matrix).
*   **Output:** A 2D image where each pixel color is the accumulated contribution of splatted resampling filters [S6, S8, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Particle:** Contains position $\mathbf{p}$, 3x3 variance matrix $\mathbf{V}$, weight $w$, and color/emission $c_\lambda$.
*   **Resampling Filter ($\rho_k$):** Defined by a 2x2 covariance matrix $\mathbf{M}_k^{-1}$ (derived from $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h$) and a center $\mathbf{x}_k$ in screen space.
*   **Footprint Table:** A 1D lookup table storing values of the exponential function $e^{-\frac{1}{2}r}$ to accelerate rasterization [S8, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps:**

1.  **Interpret Volume Data:** Treat volume data as a set of absorbing and emitting particles, each represented by a 3D elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel [S6, S8, unofficial]:
    $$G_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{p}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}|\mathbf{V}|^{1/2}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{p})^T \mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{p})}$$
    The kernel is truncated where $(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p})^T \mathbf{V}^{-1} (\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}) \leq r^2$, with typical cutoff radius $1 \leq r \leq 3$ [S6, unofficial][S11].

2.  **Transform to Camera Space:** Map the particle position $\mathbf{p}$ and variance matrix $\mathbf{V}$ from object space to camera space using the rotational part of the viewing transformation $\mathbf{W}$ [S6, S8, unofficial]:
    $$\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{t}_{cam}$$
    $$\mathbf{V}_{cam} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}^T$$
    Camera space coordinates are $\mathbf{u} = (u_0, u_1, u_2)$, with the origin at the center of projection and the image plane at $u_2 = 1$ [S6, unofficial].

3.  **Compute Jacobian:** Calculate the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$ of the perspective projection at the camera-space position $\mathbf{u}_k$ [S6, unofficial][S11]:
    $$\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & 0 & -\frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & -\frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ \frac{u_{k_0}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_1}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_2}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \end{pmatrix}$$
    where $\|\mathbf{u}_k\| = \sqrt{u_{k_0}^2 + u_{k_1}^2 + u_{k_2}^2}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].

4.  **Transform Variance to Image Space:** Transform the camera-space variance matrix to image space using the Jacobian [S6, unofficial][S11]:
    $$\mathbf{V}_k = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{V}_{cam} \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T$$
    Note: The sources use $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ to denote the 2x2 submatrix obtained by skipping the third row and column of the full 3x3 matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ [S6, unofficial][S11].

5.  **Project Center:** Project the voxel center from camera space to screen coordinates $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = (x_{k_0}, x_{k_1})$ by perspective division:
    $$x_{k_0} = \frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}}, \quad x_{k_1} = \frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}}$$
    (Assuming normalized device coordinates or appropriate scaling to pixel space) [S8, unofficial].

6.  **Compute Resampling Filter:** The EWA volume resampling filter $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ is the convolution of the integrated footprint function and a 2D Gaussian low-pass filter with variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_h$ (typically the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$) [S6, S8, unofficial]:
    $$\rho_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi |\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}| |\mathbf{W}^{-1}| |\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h|^{\frac{1}{2}}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)}$$
    where $\mathbf{M}_k = (\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h)^{-1}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
    *Note: The normalization factor includes determinants of the inverse Jacobian and viewing transformation to preserve energy [S8, unofficial].*

7.  **Rasterize Footprint:** Rasterize the elliptical footprint $\rho_k$ in screen space. This is typically done by evaluating the Gaussian function over the bounding box of the ellipse or using a 1D footprint table with forward differencing for efficiency [S8, unofficial]. The filter is band-limited to respect the Nyquist frequency [S1, unofficial].

8.  **Composite:** Sort particles back-to-front based on view-space depth (or use sheet-buffer compositing to reduce popping artifacts) and accumulate contributions into the image buffer [S6, S8, unofficial]. The final intensity is approximated as:
    $$(I_\lambda \otimes h)(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \approx \sum_k c_{\lambda k} o_k g_k \rho_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}})$$
    where $o_k$ is the attenuation factor, assumed constant within the footprint support [S8, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def ewa_volume_splatting(volume_particles, camera_matrix, projection_matrix, image_width, image_height):
    """
    Renders volume data using EWA Volume Splatting.
    
    Args:
        volume_particles: List of dicts with keys:
            'pos': (3,) array, object space position
            'V': (3,3) array, object space variance matrix
            'color': (3,) array, emission coefficient
            'extinction': float, absorption coefficient
        camera_matrix: (4,4) array, world-to-camera transformation
        projection_matrix: (4,4) array, camera-to-clip transformation
        image_width: int
        image_height: int
        
    Returns:
        image: (image_height, image_width, 3) array, rendered RGB image
    """
    # Initialize image buffer
    image = np.zeros((image_height, image_width, 3))
    
    # Extract rotational part of viewing transformation W
    # Assuming camera_matrix maps world to camera
    W = camera_matrix[:3, :3]
    
    # Precompute inverse determinants for normalization
    # Note: |J^-1| and |W^-1| are constant for all particles if W is rigid rotation
    # |W^-1| = 1 since W is orthogonal rotation
    inv_det_W = 1.0 
    
    # Sort particles back-to-front by view-space depth
    # View-space depth is the z-component in camera space
    for p in volume_particles:
        p['cam_pos'] = W @ p['pos'] + camera_matrix[:3, 3]
        p['depth'] = p['cam_pos'][2]
    
    volume_particles.sort(key=lambda x: x['depth'], reverse=True)
    
    for particle in volume_particles:
        u = particle['cam_pos']
        u0, u1, u2 = u
        V_cam = W @ particle['V'] @ W.T
        
        # Compute Jacobian J at u
        norm_u = np.linalg.norm(u)
        J = np.array([
            [1/u2, 0, -u0/(u2**2)],
            [0, 1/u2, -u1/(u2**2)],
            [u0/norm_u, u1/norm_u, u2/norm_u]
        ])
        
        # Transform variance to image space
        V_img = J @ V_cam @ J.T
        
        # Get 2x2 submatrix (skip 3rd row/col)
        V_hat = V_img[:2, :2]
        
        # Low-pass filter variance (Identity matrix)
        V_h = np.eye(2)
        
        # Compute M_k = (V_hat + V_h)^-1
        M_k = np.linalg.inv(V_hat + V_h)
        
        # Project center to screen
        x_k = np.array([u0/u2, u1/u2])
        
        # Normalization factor
        # |J^-1| is determinant of inverse Jacobian
        # For perspective projection, the relevant scaling for area is 1/u2^2
        # The formula uses |J^-1| |W^-1| |V_hat + V_h|^0.5
        # Note: The source formula has |J^-1| in numerator. 
        # Determinant of J is 1/(u2^2 * norm_u). So |J^-1| = u2^2 * norm_u.
        # However, standard EWA derivations often simplify this. 
        # We follow the source formula strictly.
        det_J_inv = 1.0 / np.linalg.det(J)
        det_sum_V = np.linalg.det(V_hat + V_h)
        norm_factor = 1.0 / (2 * np.pi * abs(det_J_inv) * inv_det_W * np.sqrt(det_sum_V))
        
        # Rasterize the footprint
        # Determine bounding box of the ellipse in screen space
        # The ellipse is defined by (x - x_k)^T M_k (x - x_k) <= r^2
        # We use a cutoff radius r=3 for the Gaussian support
        r_cutoff = 3.0
        
        # Get eigenvalues and eigenvectors of M_k to find extent
        eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(M_k)
        # Major axis length is r_cutoff / sqrt(min_eigenvalue)
        # Minor axis length is r_cutoff / sqrt(max_eigenvalue)
        max_extent = r_cutoff / np.sqrt(np.min(eigenvalues))
        
        # Convert to pixel coordinates (assuming x is in NDC or similar, 
        # need to map to pixel space. For simplicity, assume x is already in pixel space 
        # or scale factors are included in V. 
        # In standard EWA, x is in screen/pixel coordinates. 
        # The Jacobian derivation above assumes normalized image plane u2=1.
        # We must map u0/u2 to pixel coordinates.
        # Let's assume a simple mapping: pixel_x = (x + 1) * width / 2
        # This requires knowing the frustum. For this generic implementation, 
        # we assume x_k is in pixel coordinates or we apply a scale.
        # To be precise, we need the projection matrix to map to pixels.
        # Let's assume the input 'projection_matrix' maps camera to clip, 
        # and we need to map to pixels.
        # For the sake of the algorithm description, we assume x_k is in pixel space.
        
        x_min = int(max(0, x_k[0] - max_extent))
        x_max = int(min(image_width, x_k[0] + max_extent))
        y_min = int(max(0, x_k[1] - max_extent))
        y_max = int(min(image_height, x_k[1] + max_extent))
        
        for y in range(y_min, y_max):
            for x in range(x_min, x_max):
                dx = x - x_k[0]
                dy = y - x_k[1]
                delta = np.array([dx, dy])
                
                # Evaluate Gaussian
                exponent = -0.5 * delta @ M_k @ delta
                
                # Check cutoff
                if exponent > -0.5 * r_cutoff**2:
                    weight = norm_factor * np.exp(exponent)
                    
                    # Accumulate color
                    # Simplified: assume constant attenuation o_k = 1 for this basic version
                    # In full EWA, o_k is precomputed or approximated
                    color = particle['color'] * particle['extinction'] * weight
                    
                    # Additive blending (simplified, no alpha compositing for color)
                    # For proper volume rendering, alpha compositing is needed.
                    # EWA splatting often uses additive blending for density/color 
                    # if alpha is handled separately or if it's density visualization.
                    # The source mentions "composited back-to-front".
                    # Standard alpha blending: C_out = C_in + C_splat * (1 - A_in)
                    # Here we assume simple accumulation for demonstration of the filter.
                    image[y, x] += color

    return image
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Cutoff radius $r$:** Truncation radius for the Gaussian kernel. Typical value $1 \leq r \leq 3$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Low-pass filter variance $\mathbf{V}_h$:** Usually the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Reconstruction Kernel:** 3D Elliptical Gaussian [S9, S15, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Aliasing:** Basic splatting assumes non-overlapping kernels, leading to holes. EWA solves this by overlapping, band-limited filters [S1, S8, unofficial].
*   **Numerical Stability:** The central conics approach can be numerically unstable when the projection approaches a line (condition number of inverse mapping increases) [S12, unofficial].
*   **Perspective Distortion:** The method relies on a local affine approximation (Jacobian) of the perspective projection. This is accurate for small kernels but may introduce errors for very large or distant splats [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Non-spherical Kernels:** If 3D kernels are not radially symmetric (e.g., sheared, anisotropic), additional care is required to ensure contributions sum correctly [S9, unofficial].
*   **Self-occlusion:** The method typically ignores self-occlusion within a single kernel's support [S8, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Deferred Surface Splatting](1072-deferred-surface-splatting.md)
Deferred Surface Splatting is a rendering technique that converts unstructured point clouds into continuous, shaded surfaces by projecting points as splats into a deferred rendering pipeline. It solves sparse dot cloud aliasing and transparency artifacts by assigning each point sample an extent drawn as a small oriented primitive (a splat) carrying a radially decreasing reconstruction kernel. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by using screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff rather than anisotropic, correctly band-limited EWA footprints, trading accurate filtering for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii. It differs from siblings like [Phong Splatting](2802-phong-splatting.md) by deferring the lighting calculation to a final screen-space pass, allowing multiple light sources to be applied efficiently without re-projecting geometry [G].

### [Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS)](1155-opacity-thin-side-ots.md)
Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS) is a parameterization scheme for EWA-based splatting that ensures robust gradient-descent optimization by dynamically scaling the learned weight $\theta$ such that the projected amplitude $a'$ equals $\theta$ when viewing the Gaussian along its thinnest side (shortest axis). It differs from standard EWA splatting, which preserves the integral of extinction (mass), and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), which preserves the 2D projected amplitude regardless of view, by preserving the 3D unbounded integral (view-independent mass) while ensuring the 2D projection behaves as an ordinary integral relative to the thinnest view.

### [OTS (EWA-Based Splatting)](1181-ots-ewa-based-splatting.md)
OTS (Opacity-Thin-Side) is a splatting method using unnormalized Gaussians that preserves the 2D amplitude $a'$ (called 'opacity') across projections, unlike EWA Splatting which preserves the weight $w$. It differs from standard EWA Splatting by requiring a Jacobian determinant correction to preserve the integral of the extinction coefficient under perspective projection.

### [GPU-Accelerated Volume Splatting With Elliptical RBFs](1186-gpu-accelerated-volume-splatting-with-elliptical-rbfs.md)
This method renders scattered volumetric data represented as 3D ellipsoidal Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) using a post-shaded, kernel-centric approach. It extends high-quality image-aligned sheet-buffer splatting to irregular grids by slicing kernels into parallel, image-aligned density planes, rasterizing them as 2D footprints, and compositing them. Unlike fragment-centric methods that evaluate Gaussian functions per pixel, this technique leverages hard-wired GPU polygonal rasterization, achieving two orders of magnitude higher frame rates. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by supporting general ellipsoidal kernels and using a flat spatial decomposition with active cell lists rather than screen-aligned spherical impostors.

### [Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing](1290-ellipsoidal-kernel-splatting-with-warped-slicing.md)
Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing is a volume rendering technique that represents point samples as 3D ellipsoids and rasterizes them by slicing the ellipsoid into 2D polygons. It solves the problem of efficiently computing the intersection of general ellipsoids with screen-aligned slicing planes on GPU hardware, where direct analytical slicing requires expensive square roots and conditional statements. The method warps the slicing planes from screen space into the coordinate system of a unit sphere, allowing the use of hard-wired GPU texture mapping and blending on radially symmetric kernel slices. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by explicitly slicing the kernel into discrete polygons for incremental rasterization rather than rasterizing the entire projected footprint, and it avoids the pre-integrated slab approach of earlier image-aligned splatting by using point-sampled slices.

### [Perspective Correct Splat Rasterization with EWA Approximation](2095-perspective-correct-splat-rasterization-with-ewa-approximati.md)
This method is an improved splat rendering pipeline that combines a perspectively correct fast splat rasterization procedure with an approximation of the Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts and visual holes in point-sampled geometry rendering, particularly when the model is viewed from flat angles or shifted from the viewing axis. Unlike its siblings, it uses an affine approximation to the projective mapping to ensure perspective-correct splat shapes without requiring per-point normals or complex anisotropic footprint calculations, trading exact band-limited footprints for a formulation that avoids holes and Moiré patterns. It builds on [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by replacing the standard EWA covariance computation with a faster, perspective-correct affine approximation.

### [Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting](2137-adaptive-ewa-volume-splatting.md)
Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting is a rendering technique that simplifies the computationally expensive Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling filter by classifying volume particles into three cases based on their distance from the viewpoint. It solves the problem of high computational cost in full EWA splatting while maintaining aliasing-free quality. Unlike full EWA splatting, which always computes the combined covariance of the reconstruction and low-pass filters, this method uses only the reconstruction filter for close particles (magnification) and only the low-pass filter for far particles (minification), resorting to full EWA only in the transition zone.

### [EWA Approximation with Tangent Vector Adjustment](2143-ewa-approximation-with-tangent-vector-adjustment.md)
A high-quality filtering technique for point-based rendering that approximates the EWA resampling filter by dynamically adjusting the tangent vectors of isotropic splats during setup to prevent aliasing during minification, without requiring expensive per-fragment convolution. It sets itself apart from the original EWA framework by using projective mapping and tangent adjustment rather than affine mapping and Gaussians for both reconstruction and low-pass filters, and from Botsch et al. [BHZK05] by avoiding per-fragment filter evaluation, thus eliminating the fragment shader bottleneck.

### [Phong Splatting](2802-phong-splatting.md)
Phong Splatting is a surface splatting technique that assigns a linearly varying normal field to each elliptical splat, enabling per-pixel Phong shading without surface connectivity. It solves the piecewise constant shading artifacts of standard point-based rendering by providing smooth, Phong-like shading quality on modern GPUs. Unlike its sibling [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md), which uses a simplified isotropic kernel, Phong Splatting uses elliptical splats with projectively correct per-pixel parameterization, trading the simplicity of isotropic filtering for superior visual quality and exact depth values.

### [EWA Splatting Renderer (Mesh-Free)](4665-ewa-splatting-renderer-mesh-free.md)
A mesh-free rendering pipeline that projects raw point clouds directly to 2D camera views using EWA projection and Z-buffer occlusion, producing RGB images, label maps, and 2D-to-3D index maps without mesh reconstruction. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by using simplified screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff, trading anisotropic, correctly band-limited footprints for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii.

### [EWA Splatting with kNN Covariance Estimation](4690-ewa-splatting-with-knn-covariance-estimation.md)
A rendering method that uses per-point 3D covariance estimated from k-nearest neighbours to project an elliptical weighted average (EWA) shape onto the 2D image plane for denser coverage than simple disk splatting. It builds on [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by replacing the simplified spherical impostor with an anisotropic footprint derived from local point cloud geometry, trading the need for explicit surface normals for a statistical estimate of local orientation.

### Order Independent EWA Volume Splatting
Order Independent EWA Volume Splatting is a novel order independent EWA volume splatting method that unifies the advantages of previous OI techniques and provides an efficient high-speed approximation by exploiting the analytical expression of elliptical Gaussian kernels used for the noise [S16, unofficial]. It computes a fast view-dependent approximation of accumulated densities along viewing directions using a per-pixel projective volume rendering approach, then projects the spatially bound kernel functions directly (splats them) thanks to their analytic representation [S16, unofficial]. Unlike ray marching which requires hundreds of evaluations per pixel and is computationally demanding for procedural noise, this method provides interactive visualization [S16, unofficial]. Unlike EWA volume splatting which requires time-consuming back-to-front ordering and introduces rasterization overhead due to overlapping ellipses, this OI method gets rid of ordering to trade realism for speed [S16, unofficial].

### EWA Surface Splatting
EWA Surface Splatting is a method where EWA volume splats reduce to surface splats suitable for high-quality iso-surface rendering by flattening the 3D Gaussian kernel along the volume gradient [S9, unofficial]. For each point in object-space, it deforms quadrilaterals texture-mapped with a Gaussian texture to result in the correct screen-space EWA splat after projection [S9, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot A)$, where $N$ is the number of particles and $A$ is the average number of pixels covered by a splat footprint. The computation of the covariance matrix and Jacobian is $O(1)$ per particle, but the rasterization of the elliptical footprint is the dominant cost.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store particle data and footprint tables. The image buffer is $O(W \cdot H)$.
*   **Measured Performance:** Approximately 11 seconds per frame for the UNC CT scan, engine, and foot datasets on an 866 MHz Pentium-III processor [S1, S8, S10, unofficial]. Rendering time for the Skull data set was 11 seconds on the same hardware [S1, unofficial].
*   **Scaling:** Performance degrades with increasing resolution and particle count. Adaptive EWA splatting can improve performance by simplifying the filter for distant particles [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Strengths:** High-quality antialiasing without excessive blurring; handles arbitrary elliptical kernels; suitable for regular, rectilinear, and irregular volume datasets; universal primitive for surface and volume data [S2][S8, S15, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to the evaluation of the resampling filter; requires back-to-front sorting or sheet-buffer compositing; numerical instability can occur with extreme perspective projections [S1, S8, S12, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality, alias-free volume rendering is required, especially for non-spherical kernels or irregular grids.
*   **When not to use:** When interactive frame rates are critical and hardware acceleration for EWA filtering is not available, or when simple, fast approximations are sufficient.

## Background
EWA Volume Splatting was introduced by Matthias Zwicker, Hanspeter Pfister, Jeroen van Baar, and Markus Gross in 2001 [S8, unofficial]. It is named after Heckbert's Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter for texture mapping due to the similarity in derivation [S7, abstract only, unofficial][S8, unofficial]. The method was presented at IEEE Visualization in October 2001 [S8, unofficial]. It addresses the limitations of earlier splatting methods, such as Westover's framework, which did not handle sampling rate changes due to perspective projections, leading to aliasing artifacts [S8, unofficial]. The technique has been widely adopted as a high-quality baseline for volume splatting and has inspired numerous variants and optimizations, including adaptive EWA splatting and GPU-accelerated implementations [S6, unofficial][S11].
