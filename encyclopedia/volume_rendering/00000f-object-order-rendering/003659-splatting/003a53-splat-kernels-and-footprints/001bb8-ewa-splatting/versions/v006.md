# EWA Splatting

Also called: EWA Volume Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA splatting

## Overview
EWA Splatting is a high-quality, aliasing-free volume rendering technique that replaces the standard footprint function with a band-limited resampling filter [S1, S6, unofficial]. It solves the aliasing artifacts inherent in basic splatting by combining an elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with a Gaussian low-pass filter, ensuring that the sampling rate respects the Nyquist frequency of the rasterized image [S1, S3, S6, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from its parent [Splatting](23-splatting.md) and siblings like [Ray-Driven Splatting](1156-ray-driven-splatting.md) by explicitly deriving an analytic expression for the 2D footprint in screen space via a local affine approximation of the perspective projection, thereby handling arbitrary elliptical kernels and perspective distortion without excessive blurring [S6, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Volume data represented as a set of particles (voxels), each defined by a position $\mathbf{p}_k$, extinction coefficient $g_k$, emission color $c_{\lambda k}$, and a 3D variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ defining an elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel. Viewing parameters: Model-View matrix (rotational part $\mathbf{W}$), projection matrix, and viewport dimensions.
**Outputs:** A 2D image buffer with accumulated color and opacity.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Particle:** Struct containing `position` (vec3), `color` (vec3), `extinction` (float), `variance_matrix` (mat3x3).
*   **Footprint Table:** A 1D lookup table storing values of the exponential function $e^{-r^2/2}$ or the final filter kernel, typically size 256 [S6, S8, unofficial].
*   **Sheet Buffer:** A temporary buffer storing accumulated splat contributions for a slab of voxels parallel to the image plane, used for compositing [S6, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   Cutoff radius $r$: Truncation limit for the Gaussian kernel. Typical value $1 \leq r \leq 3$ [S4, unofficial][S7].
*   Low-pass filter variance $\mathbf{V}_h$: 2x2 identity matrix is typical [S4, unofficial][S7].
*   Threshold $c$: Radial index threshold for evaluation, typically $c=4$ [S6, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Interpretation:** Treat volume data as a set of absorbing/emitting particles [S4, unofficial][S7].
2.  **Transformation to Camera Space:** Transform the voxel center $\mathbf{p}_k$ from object space to camera space coordinates $\mathbf{u}_k = (u_{k_0}, u_{k_1}, u_{k_2})$ using the viewing transformation. The origin $\mathbf{u}=0$ is the center of projection, and the image plane is $u_2=1$ [S4, unofficial][S7].
3.  **Variance Transformation:** Compute the 3x3 variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ in camera space by transforming the object-space variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k^{obj}$:
    $$ \mathbf{V}_k = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{V}_k^{obj} \mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T $$
    where $\mathbf{W}$ is the rotational part of the viewing transformation, and $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$ is the Jacobian of the perspective projection at $\mathbf{u}_k$ [S4, unofficial][S7]:
    $$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & 0 & -\frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & -\frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ \frac{u_{k_0}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_1}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_2}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \end{pmatrix} $$
    where $\|\mathbf{u}_k\| = \sqrt{u_{k_0}^2 + u_{k_1}^2 + u_{k_2}^2}$ [S4, unofficial][S7].
4.  **Derive 2D Resampling Filter:**
    *   Obtain the 2x2 variance matrix $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ by skipping the third row and column of $\mathbf{V}_k$ [S4, unofficial][S7, S7].
    *   Compute the combined inverse variance matrix $\mathbf{M}_k$:
        $$ \mathbf{M}_k = (\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h)^{-1} $$
        where $\mathbf{V}_h$ is the 2x2 variance matrix of the Gaussian low-pass filter [S4, unofficial][S7].
    *   Compute the normalization factor and the 2D footprint function (EWA volume resampling filter) $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ for screen coordinates $\mathbf{x}$:
        $$ \rho_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi |\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}| |\mathbf{W}^{-1}| |\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h|^{\frac{1}{2}}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)} $$
        where $\mathbf{x}_k$ is the projected screen position of the voxel [S4, unofficial][S7].
    *   Note: $|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}|$ and $|\mathbf{W}^{-1}|$ are determinants of the inverse Jacobian and inverse rotation matrix respectively [S4, unofficial][S7].
5.  **Rasterization:**
    *   Project the voxel center to screen space $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$ by perspective division of $\mathbf{u}_k$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   Rasterize the footprint $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ over the bounding box of the ellipse defined by $\mathbf{M}_k$.
    *   Use forward differencing to evaluate the exponential term efficiently, often using a precomputed 1D lookup table for $e^{-r^2/2}$ [S6, unofficial].
    *   Evaluate the kernel only where $(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k) \leq c^2$ (typically $c=4$) [S6, unofficial].
6.  **Compositing:**
    *   Sort particles back-to-front based on view-space depth $u_{k_2}$ [S4, unofficial][S7].
    *   Accumulate contributions into the image buffer. The intensity $I_\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{x}})$ is computed as:
        $$ I_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \sum_k c_{\lambda k}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) g_k \rho_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} (1 - g_j \rho_j(\hat{\mathbf{x}})) $$
        assuming the attenuation factor is approximated as constant $o_k$ within the footprint support, simplifying to:
        $$ (I_\lambda \otimes h)(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \approx \sum_k c_{\lambda k} o_k g_k \rho_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) $$
        [S6, unofficial].
    *   Alternatively, accumulate splats into a sheet buffer for planes parallel to the projection plane, then composite sheets back-to-front [S6, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math

def compute_ewa_footprint(u_k, V_k_obj, W, V_h, viewport_width, viewport_height):
    """
    Computes the 2D EWA footprint parameters for a single voxel.
    u_k: Camera space position (u0, u1, u2)
    V_k_obj: 3x3 object space variance matrix
    W: 3x3 rotational viewing matrix
    V_h: 2x2 low-pass filter variance matrix (usually Identity)
    """
    # 1. Compute Jacobian J_u_k
    u0, u1, u2 = u_k
    norm_u = math.sqrt(u0*u0 + u1*u1 + u2*u2)
    
    # J is 3x3
    J = [
        [1.0/u2, 0, -u0/(u2*u2)],
        [0, 1.0/u2, -u1/(u2*u2)],
        [u0/norm_u, u1/norm_u, u2/norm_u]
    ]
    
    # 2. Transform Variance to Camera Space
    # V_k = J * W * V_k_obj * W^T * J^T
    # Note: Matrix multiplication order depends on convention (row/col vectors).
    # Assuming column vectors: V_k = J * (W * V_k_obj * W^T) * J^T
    # Implementation requires a mat3x3 multiply function.
    V_k = mat3x3_multiply(mat3x3_multiply(mat3x3_multiply(J, W), V_k_obj), mat3x3_transpose(W))
    V_k = mat3x3_multiply(V_k, mat3x3_transpose(J))
    
    # 3. Extract 2x2 submatrix V_hat_k
    # Skip 3rd row and column
    V_hat_k = [
        [V_k[0][0], V_k[0][1]],
        [V_k[1][0], V_k[1][1]]
    ]
    
    # 4. Compute M_k = (V_hat_k + V_h)^-1
    V_combined = mat2x2_add(V_hat_k, V_h)
    M_k = mat2x2_inverse(V_combined)
    
    # 5. Compute Normalization Factor
    # Det(J^-1) = 1 / Det(J)
    # Det(W^-1) = 1 / Det(W) = 1 (since W is rotation)
    det_J = mat3x3_determinant(J)
    det_W = mat3x3_determinant(W)
    
    det_V_combined = mat2x2_determinant(V_combined)
    
    norm_factor = 1.0 / (2.0 * math.pi * abs(1.0/det_J) * abs(1.0/det_W) * math.sqrt(det_V_combined))
    
    # 6. Project Center to Screen
    # Screen coords x_hat = (u0/u2, u1/u2) scaled to viewport
    x_screen = (u0/u2) * (viewport_width / 2.0)
    y_screen = (u1/u2) * (viewport_height / 2.0)
    
    return {
        "center": (x_screen, y_screen),
        "M_k": M_k,
        "norm_factor": norm_factor,
        "cutoff": 4.0 # Typical threshold c
    }
```

## Subtopics

### [Deferred Surface Splatting](1072-deferred-surface-splatting.md)
Deferred Surface Splatting is a rendering technique that converts unstructured point clouds into continuous, shaded surfaces by projecting points as splats into a deferred rendering pipeline. It solves sparse dot cloud aliasing and transparency artifacts by assigning each point sample an extent drawn as a small oriented primitive (a splat) carrying a radially decreasing reconstruction kernel. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by using screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff rather than anisotropic, correctly band-limited EWA footprints, trading accurate filtering for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii. It differs from siblings like [Phong Splatting](2802-phong-splatting.md) by deferring the lighting calculation to a final screen-space pass, allowing multiple light sources to be applied efficiently without re-projecting geometry [G].

### [Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS)](1155-opacity-thin-side-ots.md)
Opacity-Thin-Side (OTS) is a parameterization scheme for EWA-based splatting that ensures robust gradient-descent optimization by dynamically scaling the learned weight $\theta$ such that the projected amplitude $a'$ equals $\theta$ when viewing the Gaussian along its thinnest side (shortest axis). It differs from standard EWA splatting, which preserves the integral of extinction (mass), and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), which preserves the 2D projected amplitude regardless of view, by preserving the 3D unbounded integral (view-independent mass) while ensuring the 2D projection behaves as an ordinary integral relative to the thinnest view.

### [OTS (EWA-Based Splatting)](1181-ots-ewa-based-splatting.md)
OTS (Opacity-Thin-Side) is a splatting method using unnormalized Gaussians that preserves the 2D amplitude $a'$ (called 'opacity') across projections, unlike EWA Splatting which preserves the weight $w$. It differs from standard EWA Splatting by requiring a Jacobian determinant correction to preserve the integral of the extinction coefficient under perspective projection.

### [GPU-Accelerated Volume Splatting With Elliptical RBFs](1186-gpu-accelerated-volume-splatting-with-elliptical-rbfs.md)
This method renders scattered volumetric data represented as 3D ellipsoidal Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) using a post-shaded, kernel-centric approach. It extends high-quality image-aligned sheet-buffer splatting to irregular grids by slicing kernels into parallel, image-aligned density planes, rasterizing them as 2D footprints, and compositing them. Unlike fragment-centric methods that evaluate Gaussian functions per pixel, this technique leverages hard-wired GPU polygonal rasterization, achieving two orders of magnitude higher frame rates. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by supporting general ellipsoidal kernels and using a flat spatial decomposition with active cell lists rather than screen-aligned spherical impostors.

### [Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing](1290-ellipsoidal-kernel-splatting-with-warped-slicing.md)
Ellipsoidal Kernel Splatting with Warped Slicing is a volume rendering technique that represents point samples as 3D ellipsoids and rasterizes them by slicing the ellipsoid into 2D polygons. It solves the problem of efficiently computing the intersection of general ellipsoids with screen-aligned slicing planes on GPU hardware, where direct analytical slicing requires expensive square roots and conditional statements. The method warps the slicing planes from screen space into the coordinate system of a unit sphere, allowing the use of hard-wired GPU texture mapping and blending on radially symmetric kernel slices. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by explicitly slicing the kernel into discrete polygons for incremental rasterization rather than rasterizing the entire projected footprint, and it avoids the pre-integrated slab approach of earlier image-aligned splatting by using point-sampled slices.

### [Perspective Correct Splat Rasterization with EWA Approximation](2095-perspective-correct-splat-rasterization-with-ewa-approximati.md)
This method is an improved splat rendering pipeline that combines a perspectively correct fast splat rasterization procedure with an approximation of the Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts and visual holes in point-sampled geometry rendering, particularly when the model is viewed from flat angles or shifted from the viewing axis. Unlike its siblings, it uses an affine approximation to the projective mapping to ensure perspective-correct splat shapes without requiring per-point normals or complex anisotropic footprint calculations, trading exact band-limited footprints for a formulation that avoids holes and Moiré patterns. It builds on [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by replacing the standard EWA covariance computation with a faster, perspective-correct affine approximation.

### [Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting](2137-adaptive-ewa-volume-splatting.md)
Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting is a rendering technique that simplifies the computationally expensive Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling filter by classifying volume particles into three cases based on their distance from the viewpoint. It solves the problem of high computational cost in full EWA splatting while maintaining aliasing-free quality. Unlike full EWA splatting, which always computes the combined covariance of the reconstruction and low-pass filters, this method uses only the reconstruction filter for close particles (magnification) and only the low-pass filter for far particles (minification), resorting to full EWA only in the transition zone.

### [EWA Approximation with Tangent Vector Adjustment](2143-ewa-approximation-with-tangent-vector-adjustment.md)
A high-quality filtering technique for point-based rendering that approximates the EWA resampling filter by dynamically adjusting the tangent vectors of isotropic splats during setup to prevent aliasing during minification, without requiring expensive per-fragment convolution. It sets itself apart from the original EWA framework by using projective mapping and tangent adjustment rather than affine mapping and Gaussians for both reconstruction and low-pass filters, and from Botsch et al. [BHZK05] by avoiding per-fragment filter evaluation, thus eliminating the fragment shader bottleneck.

### [Phong Splatting](2802-phong-splatting.md)
Phong Splatting is a surface splatting technique that assigns a linearly varying normal field to each elliptical splat, enabling per-pixel Phong shading without surface connectivity. It solves the piecewise constant shading artifacts of standard point-based rendering by providing smooth, Phong-like shading quality on modern GPUs. Unlike its sibling [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md), which uses a simplified isotropic kernel, Phong Splatting uses elliptical splats with projectively correct per-pixel parameterization, trading the simplicity of isotropic filtering for superior visual quality and exact depth values.

### [EWA Splatting Renderer (Mesh-Free)](4665-ewa-splatting-renderer-mesh-free.md)
A mesh-free rendering pipeline that projects raw point clouds directly to 2D camera views using EWA projection and Z-buffer occlusion, producing RGB images, label maps, and 2D-to-3D index maps without mesh reconstruction. It differs from its parent [EWA Splatting](1069-ewa-splatting.md) by using simplified screen-aligned spherical impostors with isotropic linear falloff, trading anisotropic, correctly band-limited footprints for a formulation that requires no per-point normals or radii.

### Order Independent EWA Volume Splatting
Order Independent EWA Volume Splatting is a novel order independent EWA volume splatting method that unifies the advantages of previous OI techniques and provides an efficient high-speed approximation by exploiting the analytical expression of elliptical Gaussian kernels used for the noise [S11, unofficial]. It computes a fast view-dependent approximation of accumulated densities along viewing directions using a per-pixel projective volume rendering approach, and projects the spatially bound kernel functions directly (splat them) thanks to their analytic representation [S11, unofficial]. Unlike ray marching which requires hundreds of evaluations per pixel and is computationally demanding for procedural noise, this method provides interactive visualization [S11, unofficial]. Unlike EWA volume splatting which requires time-consuming back-to-front ordering and introduces rasterization overhead due to overlapping ellipses, this OI method gets rid of ordering to trade realism for speed [S11, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The computation of the EWA footprint involves matrix multiplications and inversions per voxel, which is more expensive than basic splatting. Rasterization requires evaluating the quadratic form for each pixel in the footprint. Adaptive EWA splatting reduces this cost by simplifying the filter for distant particles [S4, unofficial][S7].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the volume data, particle attributes, and potentially sheet buffers for compositing. The footprint table is small (1D array) [S6, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Rendering times of approximately 6-11 seconds per frame were reported on an 866 MHz Pentium-III processor for datasets like the zebra texture, UNC CT scan, engine, and foot datasets [S6, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Provides high-quality, aliasing-free rendering even with non-spherical (elliptical) kernels and perspective projections. Handles regular, rectilinear, and irregular volume data sets [S5, abstract only, unofficial][S6, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to the complex footprint calculation. Basic splatting assumptions (non-overlapping kernels) can still cause artifacts if not handled by the EWA filter [S1, S6, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high visual quality and anti-aliasing are required, especially for anisotropic or elliptical volume data.
*   **When not to use:** When real-time performance is critical and the hardware cannot support the per-voxel matrix operations, or when the volume data is highly isotropic and simple splatting suffices.

## Background
EWA Volume Splatting was introduced by Matthias Zwicker, Hanspeter Pfister, Jeroen van Baar, and Markus Gross in 2001 [S6, unofficial]. It is named after Heckbert's Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter for texture mapping due to the similarity in derivation [S5, abstract only, unofficial][S6, unofficial]. The method was implemented in the VTK (Visualization Toolkit) framework [S6, unofficial]. It addresses the limitations of Westover's original splatting framework, which did not account for sampling rate changes due to perspective projections, leading to aliasing artifacts [S6, unofficial].
