# EWA Splatting

Also called: EWA Volume Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting, Elliptical Weighted Average Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA Volume Splatting, Zwicker EWA splatting

## Overview
EWA Splatting is a high-quality volume rendering method that eliminates aliasing artifacts in splat-based rendering by replacing the standard footprint function with a band-limited resampling filter [S1, unofficial][S2][S8, unofficial]. It solves the problem of incorrect visibility determination and high-frequency sampling errors that occur when reconstruction kernels overlap or when perspective projection causes the sampling rate to drop below the Nyquist frequency [S1, S8, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from basic splatting [Splatting](23-splatting.md) by combining an elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with a Gaussian low-pass filter, and from [Splatting Without the Blur](1157-splatting-without-the-blur.md) by explicitly preserving high-frequency detail through adaptive filtering rather than removing blur entirely [S2][S5, S8, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Volume data represented as a set of particles (voxels), each defined by position $\mathbf{p}_k$, density/extinction coefficient $g_k$, color $c_{\lambda k}$, and a 3D elliptical Gaussian reconstruction kernel with variance matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ in object space [S6, S8, unofficial].
*   **Output:** A 2D image where each pixel color is the accumulated contribution of splatted resampling filters [S6, S8, unofficial].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Voxel Data:** For each voxel $k$, store:
    *   $\mathbf{p}_k$: 3D position in object space.
    *   $g_k$: Extinction coefficient (scalar).
    *   $c_{\lambda k}$: Emission color (RGB).
    *   $\mathbf{V}_k$: $3 \times 3$ symmetric positive-definite variance matrix of the reconstruction kernel in object space [S6, S8, unofficial].
*   **Ray Space Coordinates:** $\mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1, x_2)^T$, where $x_0, x_1$ define a point on the projection plane and $x_2$ is the distance from the center of projection [S8, unofficial].
*   **Camera Space Coordinates:** $\mathbf{u} = (u_0, u_1, u_2)^T$, with origin at the center of projection and image plane at $u_2 = 1$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Image Space Coordinates:** $\mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1)^T$ (often denoted $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$) [S8, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Cutoff radius $r$:** Truncates the Gaussian kernel. Typical value $1 \leq r \leq 3$ [S6, unofficial][S11]. The kernel is evaluated only where $(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p})^T \mathbf{V}^{-1} (\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}) \leq r^2$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Low-pass filter variance $\mathbf{V}_h$:** $2 \times 2$ matrix. Usually chosen as the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
*   **Assumptions:**
    1.  Reconstruction kernels have local support and do not overlap along a ray [S8, unofficial].
    2.  Kernels are ordered front-to-back [S8, unofficial].
    3.  Emission coefficient $c_{\lambda k}$ is constant within the support of each footprint [S8, unofficial].
    4.  Attenuation factor is approximately constant $o_k$ within the support of each footprint [S8, unofficial].
    5.  Perspective transformation is linearly approximated by its Jacobian [S6, unofficial][S11].

**Steps:**

1.  **Transform to Camera Space:**
    Apply the viewing transformation to map voxel positions and kernel variances from object space to camera space.
    *   Position: $\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{p}_k + \mathbf{t}$ (where $\mathbf{W}$ is the rotational part of the viewing transformation) [S6, S8, unofficial].
    *   Variance: $\mathbf{V}_k^{cam} = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{V}_k \mathbf{W}^T$ [S8, unofficial].

2.  **Compute Jacobian of Perspective Projection:**
    Calculate the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}$ at the camera-space position $\mathbf{u}_k = (u_{k_0}, u_{k_1}, u_{k_2})^T$ [S6, unofficial][S11]:
    $$
    \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & 0 & -\frac{u_{k_0}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{u_{k_2}} & -\frac{u_{k_1}}{u_{k_2}^2} \\ \frac{u_{k_0}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_1}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} & \frac{u_{k_2}}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \end{pmatrix}
    $$
    where $\|\mathbf{u}_k\| = \sqrt{u_{k_0}^2 + u_{k_1}^2 + u_{k_2}^2}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].

3.  **Transform Variance to Image Space:**
    Compute the transformed variance matrix in image space $\mathbf{V}_k^{img}$ (denoted as $\mathbf{V}_k$ in some sources, but distinct from object space) [S6, unofficial]:
    $$
    \mathbf{V}_k^{img} = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k} \mathbf{V}_k^{cam} \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^T
    $$
    *Note: Some sources denote the result of skipping the third row/column with a hat symbol $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ [S6, unofficial][S11].*

4.  **Derive the Resampling Filter (Footprint Function):**
    The EWA volume resampling filter $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ is a 2D Gaussian defined in image space coordinates $\mathbf{x}$ [S6, S8, unofficial]:
    $$
    \rho_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi |\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{u}_k}^{-1}| |\mathbf{W}^{-1}| |\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h|^{\frac{1}{2}}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)^T \mathbf{M}_k (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k)}
    $$
    Where:
    *   $\mathbf{x}_k$ is the projected screen position of voxel $k$ (obtained by perspective division of $\mathbf{u}_k$) [S8, unofficial].
    *   $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k$ is the $2 \times 2$ submatrix of $\mathbf{V}_k^{img}$ obtained by skipping the third row and column [S6, unofficial][S11].
    *   $\mathbf{V}_h$ is the $2 \times 2$ variance matrix of the low-pass filter (typically $\mathbf{I}$) [S6, unofficial][S11].
    *   $\mathbf{M}_k = (\hat{\mathbf{V}}_k + \mathbf{V}_h)^{-1}$ [S6, unofficial][S11].
    *   The normalization factor includes determinants of the Jacobian inverse and the viewing rotation inverse [S6, S8, unofficial].

5.  **Rasterize the Splat:**
    Rasterize the elliptical footprint $\rho_k(\mathbf{x})$ onto the screen. This is typically done by deforming a screen-space quadrilateral or using a 1D lookup table for the Gaussian exponential with forward differencing [S7, abstract only, unofficial][S8, unofficial]. The contribution to a pixel is weighted by the voxel's extinction $g_k$ and emission $c_{\lambda k}$, modulated by the attenuation factor $o_k$ [S8, unofficial].

6.  **Composite:**
    Accumulate contributions from all splats in back-to-front order to compute the final image intensity [S6, S8, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def ewa_splatting(volume_data, view_matrix, projection_matrix, image_width, image_height):
    """
    volume_data: List of dicts with keys 'p' (3,), 'g' (float), 'c' (3,), 'V' (3x3)
    view_matrix: 4x4 MVP matrix (or separate W and translation)
    image_width, image_height: int
    """
    # Initialize output buffers
    color_buffer = np.zeros((image_height, image_width, 3))
    alpha_buffer = np.zeros((image_height, image_width))
    
    # Extract W (rotation) from view matrix assuming standard OpenGL column-major or similar
    # Assuming view_matrix is 4x4, W is top-left 3x3
    W = view_matrix[:3, :3]
    
    # Sort voxels back-to-front based on camera space depth
    # 1. Transform positions to camera space
    # Assuming view_matrix transforms object to camera space
    # u = W * p + t. We need translation. 
    # If view_matrix is full MVP, we might need to separate. 
    # For simplicity, assume we have camera positions u_k and variances V_k_cam
    
    voxels_cam = []
    for v in volume_data:
        p = v['p']
        # Transform position
        u = W @ p + view_matrix[:3, 3] # Assuming translation is in 4th column
        # Transform variance
        V_cam = W @ v['V'] @ W.T
        voxels_cam.append({
            'u': u,
            'V_cam': V_cam,
            'g': v['g'],
            'c': v['c']
        })
    
    # Sort by u[2] (depth) back-to-front (largest z first if camera looks down -z, 
    # or smallest if +z. Standard OpenGL: camera looks down -Z. 
    # Let's assume u[2] is depth. Back-to-front means largest depth value first if 
    # depth increases away from camera. 
    # EWA assumes front-to-back compositing for transparency.
    # So sort by u[2] ascending (closest first) if u[2] is distance? 
    # Usually u[2] is negative in OpenGL. Let's sort by u[2] descending (closest to 0).
    voxels_cam.sort(key=lambda x: x['u'][2], reverse=True)
    
    for v in voxels_cam:
        u = v['u']
        V_cam = v['V_cam']
        g = v['g']
        c = v['c']
        
        # 1. Compute Jacobian J at u
        u0, u1, u2 = u
        norm_u = np.linalg.norm(u)
        
        J = np.array([
            [1/u2, 0, -u0/(u2**2)],
            [0, 1/u2, -u1/(u2**2)],
            [u0/norm_u, u1/norm_u, u2/norm_u]
        ])
        
        # 2. Transform Variance to Image Space
        V_img = J @ V_cam @ J.T
        
        # 3. Extract 2x2 submatrix V_hat
        V_hat = V_img[:2, :2]
        
        # 4. Low-pass filter variance (Identity)
        V_h = np.eye(2)
        
        # 5. Compute M_k
        M_k = np.linalg.inv(V_hat + V_h)
        
        # 6. Project to Screen Coordinates x_k
        # Perspective division
        x_k = np.array([u0 / u2, u1 / u2])
        
        # 7. Compute Normalization Factor
        # |J^-1| * |W^-1| * |V_hat + V_h|^0.5
        # Note: |J^-1| = 1/|J|
        det_J = np.linalg.det(J)
        det_W = np.linalg.det(W)
        det_V_sum = np.linalg.det(V_hat + V_h)
        
        norm_factor = 1.0 / (2 * np.pi * abs(det_J) * abs(det_W) * np.sqrt(det_V_sum))
        
        # 8. Rasterize Splat
        # Determine bounding box of the ellipse
        # Eigenvalues of M_k give inverse squared semi-axes
        eigenvalues, _ = np.linalg.eigh(M_k)
        # Cutoff radius r (e.g., 3)
        r = 3.0
        # Semi-axes lengths
        a = r / np.sqrt(eigenvalues[0])
        b = r / np.sqrt(eigenvalues[1])
        
        # Bounding box in screen space
        x_min = int(np.floor(x_k[0] - a))
        x_max = int(np.ceil(x_k[0] + a))
        y_min = int(np.floor(x_k[1] - b))
        y_max = int(np.ceil(x_k[1] + b))
        
        # Clip to image bounds
        x_min = max(0, x_min)
        x_max = min(image_width, x_max)
        y_min = max(0, y_min)
        y_max = min(image_height, y_max)
        
        for y in range(y_min, y_max):
            for x in range(x_min, x_max):
                # Screen coordinates relative to center
                dx = x - x_k[0]
                dy = y - x_k[1]
                pos = np.array([dx, dy])
                
                # Evaluate Gaussian
                exponent = -0.5 * pos @ M_k @ pos
                if exponent > 0: # Outside cutoff
                    continue
                    
                weight = norm_factor * np.exp(exponent)
                
                # Attenuation approximation (simplified: assume o_k = 1 for first pass or precomputed)
                # In full EWA, o_k is precomputed or approximated
                o_k = 1.0 
                
                # Contribution
                contribution = g * weight * o_k
                
                # Alpha blending (Front-to-Back)
                # P_new = P_old + (1 - A_old) * C_new * A_new
                # Here weight is the alpha contribution of this splat
                alpha = contribution
                
                if alpha_buffer[y, x] + alpha >= 1.0:
                    # Fully opaque, stop
                    color_buffer[y, x] = c
                    alpha_buffer[y, x] = 1.0
                    break
                else:
                    color_buffer[y, x] += (1.0 - alpha_buffer[y, x]) * c * alpha
                    alpha_buffer[y, x] += alpha * (1.0 - alpha_buffer[y, x]) # Approximation
                    
    return color_buffer
```

## Subtopics

### [Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting](2137-adaptive-ewa-volume-splatting.md)
Adaptive EWA Volume Splatting simplifies the computationally expensive EWA resampling filter by classifying volume particles into three cases based on their distance from the viewpoint. It solves the problem of high computational cost in full EWA splatting while maintaining aliasing-free quality. Unlike full EWA, which always computes the combined covariance of the reconstruction and low-pass filters, this method uses only the reconstruction filter for close particles (magnification) and only the low-pass filter for far particles (minification), resorting to full EWA only in the transition zone.

### EWA Surface Splatting
EWA Surface Splatting is a method where EWA volume splats reduce to surface splats suitable for high-quality iso-surface rendering by flattening the 3D Gaussian kernel along the volume gradient [S9, unofficial]. It differs from EWA Volume Splatting by treating the kernel as a 2D surface element rather than a volumetric particle, enabling anisotropic texture filtering for point-sampled surfaces [S2][S9, unofficial].

### Order Independent EWA Volume Splatting
Order Independent EWA Volume Splatting is a novel method that unifies the advantages of previous OI techniques and provides an efficient high-speed approximation by exploiting the analytical expression of elliptical Gaussian kernels used for the noise [S16, unofficial]. Unlike EWA volume splatting which requires time-consuming back-to-front ordering and introduces rasterization overhead due to overlapping ellipses, this OI method gets rid of ordering to trade realism for speed [S16, unofficial]. It computes a fast view-dependent approximation of accumulated densities along viewing directions using a per-pixel projective volume rendering approach and projects spatially bound kernel functions directly [S16, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \cdot A)$, where $N$ is the number of voxels and $A$ is the average area of the projected splat in pixels. The computation of the Jacobian and matrix inversion is $O(1)$ per voxel but expensive in constants [S6, S8, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store voxel data and $O(W \cdot H)$ for the image buffer [S6, S8, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** Rendering time was approximately 11 seconds per frame for the UNC CT scan (256^2 x 225) on an 866 MHz Pentium-III processor [S1, S8, S10, unofficial]. Hardware acceleration significantly improves this, but interactive high-quality EWA splatting remains difficult due to computational complexity [S6, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High image quality without aliasing artifacts or excessive blurring; handles arbitrary elliptical kernels and perspective projection efficiently; universal primitive for surface and volume data [S2][S8, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive due to matrix operations and Jacobian calculation; assumes non-overlapping kernels which can lead to artifacts if violated; sorting required for correct transparency [S1, S8, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When high-quality, alias-free volume rendering is required and performance is not the primary constraint, or when using hardware acceleration to mitigate cost [S2][S6, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** For real-time applications on low-end hardware without optimization (e.g., Adaptive EWA) or when approximate results are acceptable [S6, unofficial].

## Background
EWA Splatting was introduced by Matthias Zwicker, Hanspeter Pfister, Jeroen van Baar, and Markus Gross in 2001 [S8, unofficial]. It is named after Heckbert's Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter for texture mapping due to the similarity in derivation [S2][S7, abstract only, unofficial][S8, unofficial]. The method addresses the limitations of Westover's original splatting framework, which did not deal with sampling rate changes due to perspective projections, leading to aliasing artifacts [S8, unofficial]. It builds on the concept of resampling filters to combine reconstruction and low-pass filtering [S2][S8, unofficial].
